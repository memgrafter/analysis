# Disentangling Similarity and Relatedness in Topic Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10619  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10619  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Co-occurrence-based topic models (e.g., LDA) lean toward thematic relatedness, while PLM-augmented models (e.g., BERT-KT) lean toward taxonomic similarity; this gap predicts downstream task performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a critical evaluation gap in topic modeling: the failure to distinguish between taxonomic similarity (substitutability) and thematic relatedness (co-occurrence). The authors introduce a neural scorer trained on a large LLM-annotated dataset of word pairs to separately quantify both dimensions. Applied across 13 topic models and six corpora, the analysis reveals a consistent architectural divide—models that rely on word co-occurrence (like LDA) capture thematic relatedness more strongly, whereas models augmented with pretrained language representations (like BERT-KT) capture taxonomic similarity. This distinction correlates with performance on downstream tasks: relatedness-inclined models excel at event monitoring and category retrieval, while similarity-inclined models outperform on synonym retrieval. The framework offers a diagnostic tool for interpreting topic model outputs and aligning model selection with application needs.

## Method Summary
The core method involves training a neural scorer to predict continuous scores (0–1) for both similarity and relatedness on any pair of English words. The training data comprises 51,523 word pairs constructed from WordNet relations (synonyms, co-hyponyms, hypernyms, antonyms), ConceptNet edges (RelatedTo, UsedFor, etc.), BERT-base cosine nearest neighbors, and controlled negatives (cross-domain, cross-POS, high-Zipf). Each pair was annotated using DeepSeek-V3 with a detailed prompt defining the two scales. For each word pair, features include: 300-dimensional GloVe 840B embeddings, four element-wise interaction features (sum, difference, element-wise product, absolute difference), and an 8-dimensional WordNet-derived vector (path similarity, LCH, Wu–Palmer, Lin, JCN, shortest path, same-category flag, synset overlap). The scorer is a two-layer MLP (1208 → 256 → 64 with GELU and dropout 0.4) with two independent sigmoid heads. The loss combines MSE for each dimension plus a gap penalty term (λ=1.0) to encourage divergence. To evaluate topic models, the scorer is applied to the top 5 words of each topic (from k=20 topics), averaging pairwise scores per topic, then aggregating over multiple runs. The primary metric is a “shifted normalized gap” (relatedness minus similarity, normalized per corpus), where positive values indicate relatedness-leaning and negative values indicate similarity-leaning.

## Key Results
- **Architectural divide:** Co-occurrence-based models (LDA, NMF) consistently score higher on relatedness, while PLM-augmented models (BERT-KT, BERTopic) score higher on similarity across corpora.
- **Downstream prediction:** A model’s average gap score correlates with its performance on specific tasks—relatedness-leaning models are better at event monitoring and category retrieval, similarity-leaning models at synonym retrieval.
- **Scorer validity:** The neural scorer achieves Spearman’s ρ ≈ 0.54 (similarity) and 0.34 (relatedness) on the external TxThmNorms benchmark, demonstrating disentanglement capability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The scorer’s design isolates the two semantic dimensions through dedicated output heads and a gap-penalizing loss. Interaction features (e.g., `e1⊙e2`, `|e1−e2|`) capture different aspects of word pair relationships: multiplicative interactions tend to highlight thematic co-occurrence, while absolute differences may encode taxonomic distinctions. The WordNet-derived features inject structured knowledge about hypernymy and synonymy, which anchors the similarity prediction in lexical hierarchies. By training on a diverse mixture of curated sources (WordNet, ConceptNet, BERT neighbors) and challenging negatives, the model learns to separate patterns of substitutability (synonyms, shared hypernyms) from patterns of associative co-occurrence (doctor/hospital, rain/umbrella). Applied to topic models, the averaged pairwise scores reflect whether a topic’s top words are more substitutable (tight semantic cluster) or more associatively linked (thematic co-occurrence). This gap reliably maps to architectural choices: Bag-of-words models optimize for word co-occurrence statistics, naturally yielding thematically coherent but less taxonomically precise topics; PLM-augmented models inherit syntactic and semantic knowledge from pretraining, favoring more taxonomically coherent word sets.

## Foundational Learning
- **Taxonomic similarity:** Measures substitutability within a semantic category (e.g., car/automobile). *Why needed:* Captures whether a topic groups words that could replace each other in context. *Quick check:* Synonyms should yield high similarity (>0.8) but low relatedness (<0.3).
- **Thematic relatedness:** Measures associative co-occurrence across contexts (e.g., doctor/hospital). *Why needed:* Captures whether a topic covers a coherent theme or event. *Quick check:* Complementary or scenario-based pairs should yield high relatedness (>0.8) but low similarity (<0.3).
- **Neural scorer:** A model that outputs continuous scores for both dimensions from word pair features. *Why needed:* Provides a scalable, automated evaluation metric for topic models. *Quick check:* Verify on a small annotated set; expect moderate correlation with human judgments.
- **Topic models:** Unsupervised methods that discover latent themes in corpora. *Why needed:* The target of evaluation; different algorithms produce word groups with varying semantic coherence. *Quick check:* Inspect top-10 words of a topic; do they form a substitutable set or an associative set?
- **WordNet features:** Structured lexical relations (path, LCH, etc.) used in scorer. *Why needed:* Provide symbolic knowledge about taxonomy to ground similarity predictions. *Quick check:* For close synonyms, Wu–Palmer and path similarity should be high; for hypernyms, lower but non-zero.
- **LLM annotation:** Using large language models (DeepSeek-V3) to label semantic dimensions. *Why needed:* Scales dataset creation beyond manual effort. *Quick check:* Sample annotated pairs for consistency; compute intra-annotator agreement if multiple calls are made.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Word Pairs (source: WordNet, ConceptNet, BERT neighbors, negatives) → Feature Extractor (GloVe embeddings + interaction features + WordNet features) → MLP (1208→256→64, GELU, Dropout 0.4) → Output Heads (Similarity, Relatedness, each sigmoid)

**Critical path:**  
1. Construct vocabulary (WordNet ∩ BERT-base) → 2. Generate candidate pairs from multiple sources → 3. Filter and balance dataset → 4. Annotate pairs with DeepSeek-V3 → 5. Compute features for each word pair → 6. Train scorer with gap-penalized loss → 7. Validate on TxThmNorms → 8. Apply fixed scorer to topic model outputs → 9. Compute shifted normalized gap per (model, corpus) → 10. Correlate gap with downstream task performance.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Static GloVe vs. contextual embeddings:* GloVe provides consistent, low-dimensional vectors but lacks polysemy handling; using contextual embeddings could improve coverage but introduce instability.  
- *Fixed “most frequent” synset selection:* Simple heuristic for WordNet features but may misrepresent words with distinct dominant senses in specialized corpora.  
- *LLM annotation cost vs. scale:* DeepSeek-V3 enables large-scale labeling but introduces potential API variability and lacks human interpretability; a smaller human-annotated subset could serve as a gold test set.

**Failure signatures:**  
- External validation (TxThmNorms) Spearman ρ below 0.4 for either dimension indicates poor generalization.  
- Scorer outputs near-identical values for synonym and complement pairs (e.g., car/automobile vs. doctor/hospital) suggests confounding.  
- High OOV rate in topic evaluation (many words without GloVe vectors) leads to degenerate (near-zero) feature vectors and random scores.

**First experiments:**  
1. Ablate WordNet features: retrain scorer without the 8D WordNet vector; expect ~0.225 drop in similarity ρ on TxThmNorms.  
2. Sanity-check scorer on prototypical pairs: e.g., “couch/sofa” (high sim, low rel) and “rain/umbrella” (low sim, high rel); confirm output gap >0.6.  
3. Compute Kendall’s W for model gap rankings across three corpora (e.g., NIPS, ACL, 20News); target W >0.5 to ensure stability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Vocabulary coverage:** The reliance on static GloVe embeddings limits the scorer's applicability to domain-specific corpora (e.g., ACL) with many OOV terms. How can the framework be adapted to handle such terms without degrading disentanglement performance?
- **Annotation consistency:** The use of a single LLM (DeepSeek-V3) for labeling introduces potential noise due to API variability or model drift. What is the impact of annotation randomness on the gap metric's stability, and could multiple annotators or human validation improve robustness?
- **Feature ablation insights:** The paper notes that WordNet features are critical for similarity prediction. Are there other structured knowledge sources or interaction features that could further improve disentanglement, especially for relatedness?
- **Generalization across languages:** The current scorer is English-only. To what extent can the approach be extended to multilingual settings, and how do semantic relations differ across languages?

## Limitations
- **Implementation ambiguities:** Critical details for WordNet feature computation (e.g., “same-category” criterion), ConceptNet edge filtering, and BERT neighbor clustering (“max 3/cluster”) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- **Annotation reliability:** DeepSeek-V3 API specifics (temperature, retries) and potential drift across annotation runs are unreported; single-pass labeling may introduce noise.
- **Vocabulary coverage:** Scorer relies on GloVe static embeddings; domain-specific terms in corpora like ACL may be OOV, forcing default (near-random) scores and distorting gap estimates.

## Confidence
- Scorer disentangles similarity and relatedness: **Medium** (architecture described, but feature and annotation gaps risk external generalization)
- Architectural divide among topic models: **Medium** (evaluation pipeline clear, but scorer imperfections could bias gap distributions)
- Gap predicts downstream task performance: **Medium** (correlations shown, but unmeasured confounders like topic coherence may influence results)
