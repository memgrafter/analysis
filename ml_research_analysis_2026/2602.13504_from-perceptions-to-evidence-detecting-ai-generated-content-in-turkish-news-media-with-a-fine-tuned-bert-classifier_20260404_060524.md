# From Perceptions To Evidence: Detecting AI-Generated Content In Turkish News Media With A Fine-Tuned Bert Classifier

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13504
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13504
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A fine-tuned Turkish BERT classifier detects AI-rewritten news articles with an F1 score of 0.9708 and estimates that ~2.5% of examined Turkish news content from 2023–2026 shows AI revision.

## Executive Summary
This study presents the first empirical computational framework for detecting AI-generated or AI-rewritten content in Turkish news media. Addressing a significant gap, the authors constructed a balanced dataset of 3,600 articles by pairing human-written Turkish news from 2021 with corresponding ChatGPT-generated revisions. A Turkish-specific BERT model (`dbmdz/bert-base-turkish-cased`) was fine-tuned with rigorous regularization on this dataset, achieving an F1 score of 0.9708 on a held-out test set. When applied to over 3,500 unseen articles from three major Turkish outlets spanning 2023–2026, the model revealed stable cross-source and temporally consistent patterns, with a mean prediction confidence exceeding 0.96. The external analysis estimated an average AI-revision prevalence of 2.5% across the examined corpus, suggesting a measurable, albeit modest, integration of large language models into journalistic workflows.

## Method Summary
The core method involves creating a paired dataset for supervised binary classification. Human-written articles scraped from three major Turkish news outlets in 2021 (presumed pre-ChatGPT era) serve as the human baseline. Each article is rewritten by ChatGPT (v5.2) using an outlet-specific prompt designed to preserve factual content, length, and editorial style while improving fluency, yielding the AI-rewritten counterpart. After cleaning and tokenization with a Turkish BERT WordPiece tokenizer, the `dbmdz/bert-base-turkish-cased` model is fine-tuned with a linear classification head. Training employs AdamW (LR 2e-5), aggressive regularization (dropout 0.2, label smoothing 0.1), early stopping, and mixed precision. The final model is applied to a large external corpus of articles from the same outlets published between 2023 and 2026 to estimate the prevalence of AI revision.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuned Turkish BERT classifier achieves an F1 score of **0.9708** on the held-out test set, with nearly symmetric precision (0.9938) and recall (0.9878).
- Application to 3,500+ external articles shows **stable cross-source and temporal patterns**, with the predicted proportion of AI-revised content ranging narrowly from 1.3% to 3.6% across outlets and years.
- The model exhibits **high prediction confidence** on external data, with a mean softmax probability exceeding 0.96 for its assigned class.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** A paired dataset of human-written and AI-rewritten Turkish news articles enables the model to learn stylistic differences rather than superficial cues.
**Mechanism:** Articles from 2021 (presumed pre-ChatGPT human-written) are paired with ChatGPT-generated revisions using a newspaper-specific prompt. The prompt enforces preservation of facts, length, and editorial style while improving fluency, creating a clean signal for the model to learn revision patterns. High cosine similarity (0.9) ensures the pairs are semantically aligned but stylistically distinct.
**Core assumption:** News from 2021 contains no AI involvement, providing a pure human writing baseline.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "construct a labeled dataset of 3,600 news articles... with distinct editorial orientations"; [section 3] "we believe that all news were written by reporters and there was no help from any language models... ChatGPT... to rewrite or revise the gathered news".
**Break condition:** If 2021 articles already contained unreported AI assistance, the human-writing baseline is contaminated, and the learned distinctions may not generalize.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A Turkish-specific BERT model, fine-tuned with aggressive regularization, captures language-specific markers of AI revision without overfitting to the training distribution.
**Mechanism:** The `dbmdz/bert-base-turkish-cased` model, pre-trained on Turkish morphology, is fine-tuned with high dropout (0.2), label smoothing (0.1), and early stopping. This forces the model to learn robust features. The near-symmetric precision/recall (0.9938/0.9878) on the test set indicates balanced learning of both classes.
**Core assumption:** AI revisions introduce detectable, consistent stylistic fingerprints (e.g., syntactic regularity, lexical choice) that differ from human writing in Turkish, and these are learnable by BERT.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "fine-tune a Turkish-specific BERT model... achieves 97.08% F1 score... symmetric precision and recall"; [section 5.4] "dropout with a probability of 0.2... label smoothing with a factor of 0.1... early stopping".
**Break condition:** If the model's high performance relies on dataset-specific artifacts (e.g., newspaper boilerplate text, source codes), performance would collapse on truly external data with different formatting or topics.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Stable cross-source and temporal prediction patterns with high confidence (>0.96) suggest the model detects a persistent phenomenon (AI revision) rather than ephemeral stylistic noise.
**Mechanism:** The model is applied to 3,500+ unseen articles (2023–2026). Consistent class distributions (96–98% human-written) and confidence across outlets/years imply the decision boundary is robust to topic shifts and editorial slant. The estimated 2.5% AI-revised rate is inferred from these stable predictions.
**Core assumption:** The model's predictions on unseen data reflect true AI involvement prevalence, not an unmeasured covariate (e.g., article length, topic) that correlates with the training data.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "consistent cross-source and temporally stable classification patterns... mean prediction confidence exceeding 0.96"; [section 6.1] Tables/figures showing narrow variation (e.g., 96.4%–98.7% human-written) across sources and years.
**Break condition:** If external human annotation reveals the model's "AI-revised" predictions are false positives (e.g., on certain genres like sports or finance), the stable pattern would be an artifact of the model's bias, not a true prevalence estimate.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Transfer Learning with Language Models**
  *Why needed here:* The study relies on fine-tuning a pre-trained Turkish BERT. Understanding that the model's initial weights encode general Turkish syntax/semantics, and fine-tuning adjusts these for the specific task of AI-detection, is key to interpreting the high F1 score.
  **Quick check:** What does the `[CLS]` token representation capture after fine-tuning, and why is it used for classification?

- **Concept: Agglutinative Morphology in Turkish NLP**
  *Why needed here:* Turkish's rich suffixation creates many word forms. The Turkish-specific BERT's WordPiece tokenizer and vocabulary (32k) are designed to handle this, unlike subword models for analytic languages. This affects how the model processes text and learns stylistic cues.
  **Quick check:** How might a multilingual BERT (e.g., mBERT) perform worse on this task compared to `dbmdz/bert-base-turkish-cased`?

- **Concept: Evaluation Under Class Balance and Symmetric Costs**
  *Why needed here:* The dataset is balanced (50/50), and the paper emphasizes symmetric precision/recall. This implies false positives and false negatives are equally costly (e.g., mislabeling human news as AI harms trust; missing AI content fails detection). F1 is thus primary, not accuracy alone.
  **Quick check:** Why might accuracy be misleading here if the external data is heavily imbalanced (e.g., 97.5% human-written)?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Pipeline -> Cleaner -> Paired Generator -> Tokenizer -> Model Core -> Training Loop -> Evaluation Module -> Deployment Script

**Critical path:**
1. Acquire pure human baseline: Scrape 2021 articles (assumed AI-free) → clean → store.
2. Generate AI-revised pairs: Feed each 2021 article to ChatGPT with outlet-specific prompt → verify length similarity & high cosine similarity → label as AI-revised.
3. Preprocess & split: Clean both sets → tokenize → stratified 80/10/10 split (train/val/test).
4. Fine-tune: Train on train set, monitor val F1, early stop → select best checkpoint.
5. Test & validate: Evaluate on test set → if F1 ~0.97, proceed; else revisit data/prompt.
6. External run: Apply to 2023–2026 articles → aggregate predictions per source/year → report distributions & mean confidence.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Sequence length (512 tokens):** Must truncate longer articles (75th percentile >2500 chars). Loses contextual info but avoids custom long-context architectures (e.g., Longformer). Tradeoff: simplicity vs. potential signal loss in lengthy pieces.
- **Prompt design for AI-revision:** Must preserve facts/length but alter style. Over-constraining yields near-identical texts (hard to distinguish); under-constraining changes content. The paper's prompt is a heuristic; no systematic prompt ablation.
- **Single-label binary task:** Treats all AI involvement as one class. Ignores spectrum (e.g., fully generated vs. lightly edited) and may conflate different AI tools.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Low confidence on external data:** Mean confidence drops significantly below 0.96 → model uncertain, possibly out-of-distribution.
- **Source-specific drift:** One outlet's AI % suddenly jumps in a given year without editorial change → possible data artifact (e.g., new AP style) or model failure.
- **Training/validation loss divergence:** Validation loss increases while training loss decreases despite regularization → overfitting to training set quirks.
- **Genre sensitivity:** Error analysis reveals high failure on specific sections (e.g., sports scores, financial data) where AI revision patterns differ.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline comparison:** Train a traditional model (e.g., SVM with TF-IDF) on the same 3,600-article dataset. Expected: BERT significantly outperforms, confirming the value of contextual representations.
2. **Error analysis with SHAP:** Sample 50 misclassified articles (false positives/negatives) from the test set. Identify which tokens/features drove the wrong prediction. Expected: False positives may be human-written with formulaic language; false negatives may be AI-revised with erratic human-like quirks.
3. **Temporal holdout validation:** Train on 2021 data only, test on *unseen* 2022 articles (if available). Measures generalization across years before applying to 2023–2026. Expected: F1 remains >0.90, supporting temporal stability claim.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** How does the fine-tuned Turkish BERT model perform on a temporally and thematically diverse set of human-annotated, unseen articles from a broader range of Turkish media outlets?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state the external validation lacks ground-truth labels and was conducted on only three outlets, noting this limits the ability to quantify true out-of-domain accuracy. They recommend creating "human-annotated external test sets, sampling 100–200 articles per source."
**Why unresolved:** The 2.5% prevalence estimate from the external application could reflect either genuine AI usage or a systematic failure of the model to generalize to new distributions (e.g., different years, topics, or writing styles). Without labeled gold-standard data for the external corpus, this core finding remains conjectural.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A newly created, manually annotated test set of several hundred recent articles from multiple additional Turkish news sources (including diverse political orientations and genres), labeled by multiple independent annotators for AI involvement. Performance metrics (F1, precision, recall) on this held-out, human-labeled set would directly measure external generalizability.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Can the classifier's performance and the estimated prevalence of AI-rewritten content be improved by moving beyond binary classification to detect specific, nuanced forms of AI involvement (e.g., heavy revision vs. light editing) or different bias dimensions introduced?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors identify the binary scheme as a limitation and propose extending toward "multi-class and multi-label formulations that capture distinct bias dimensions (framing, selection, ideological)."
**Why unresolved:** The current binary "human-written" vs. "AI-rewritten/revised" label collapses potentially meaningful gradations of AI contribution. A 2.5% estimate masks variability in *how* AI is used (e.g., drafting entire articles vs. polishing sentences), which has different implications for transparency and bias. The model may also conflate AI's linguistic fingerprints with other editorial biases.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A dataset annotated with fine-grained labels (e.g., "minimal AI assistance," "AI-generated draft heavily edited," "AI-generated draft lightly edited") or labels for specific bias types. Training and evaluating multi-label classifiers on this richer data would quantify the spectrum of AI use and disentangle it from other journalistic biases.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** To what extent does the 512-token truncation requirement of BERT impact detection accuracy for longer Turkish news articles, and do long-context architectures (e.g., Longformer) yield significant improvements?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note that "BERT’s 512-token limit necessitates truncation of longer articles, potentially discarding informative content given that the 75th percentile of text length in the corpus exceeds 2500 characters." They suggest exploring "long-context architectures such as Longformer."
**Why unresolved:** Truncation may remove crucial stylistic cues or structural patterns (e.g., concluding paragraphs, specific sourcing patterns) that distinguish AI from human writing. If these cues are systematically located later in articles, the current model's performance on full-length articles is artificially constrained. The observed high confidence might be an artifact of analyzing only the article's beginning.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative experiments where the same articles are processed without truncation using a Longformer model, versus with truncation using BERT. Performance differences (F1 score, confidence distribution) on a test set of long articles would quantify the impact of truncated context. A detailed analysis of which truncated segments carry the most discriminative signal would also be informative.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How does the proposed Turkish BERT-based detector compare in performance and failure modes to simpler traditional machine learning models (e.g., SVM, Logistic Regression) and other multilingual or English-focused transformer models when applied to Turkish news?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors list as a key limitation the "absence of baseline comparisons with traditional classifiers (e.g., SVM, Logistic Regression) and alternative transformer models (e.g., multilingual BERT)."
**Why unresolved:** The reported 97.08% F1 is contextless without knowing if this high performance is unique to the specific Turkish BERT model or if simpler, more interpretable models achieve similar results. Furthermore, it's unknown if a multilingual model (like mBERT) could perform adequately, which would impact the argument for language-specific model development.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic benchmark evaluation on the same train/test splits, comparing the dbmdz/bert-base-turkish-cased model against: (a) traditional ML models using TF-IDF or handcrafted features, (b) multilingual BERT, and (c) potentially other Turkish-specific models. Performance metrics and error analysis (e.g., confusion matrices, examples where models disagree) would clarify the value-add of the specific architecture and pre-training.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** What are the specific linguistic, stylistic, or structural features that the model uses to distinguish AI-rewritten Turkish news, and what do its misclassifications reveal about the limitations of current detection approaches?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors recommend a "systematic error analysis using interpretability techniques (LIME, SHAP)" to "illuminate the model’s decision boundaries and failure modes."
**Why unresolved:** The model is a black box. Understanding *why* it makes predictions is crucial for trust, debugging, and improving the system. For instance, does it rely on superficial markers (e.g., specific transition phrases common in LLM output) that could be easily adopted by human writers? Are misclassifications concentrated in certain topics (e.g., sports, finance) or article types (e.g., breaking news vs. analysis)?
**What evidence would resolve it:** Applying post-hoc explainability methods (LIME, SHAP) to a sample of correctly classified and misclassified articles. This would identify the most influential words, phrases, or syntactic patterns for each class. A qualitative analysis of high-confidence false positives and false negatives would reveal systematic weaknesses, such as susceptibility to adversarial paraphrasing or confusion with specific human writing styles.

## Limitations
- **Outlet identities & preprocessing specifics** are undisclosed, preventing exact dataset replication and increasing risk of unintended data leakage (e.g., residual source identifiers).
- **Baseline purity assumption**: The 2021 articles are treated as AI-free, but unreported LLM assistance would contaminate the training signal, potentially teaching the model to detect a hybrid rather than pure AI revision.
- **External validity**: The 2.5% AI-revised estimate lacks human verification on the 2023–2026 articles; high model confidence (>0.96) does not confirm ground truth, especially if the model learns dataset-specific artifacts.

## Confidence
- **Internal performance (F1=0.9708):** High. Well-specified training regime, symmetric precision/recall, and held-out test set support robust learning on the curated dataset.
- **Cross-source/temporal stability:** Medium-High. Consistent predictions across outlets/years suggest generalization, but this depends on the training data sufficiently representing the external distribution—an unknown without outlet matching.
- **Prevalence estimate (2.5% AI-revised):** Low. Inference from model predictions alone, without human annotation of external articles, leaves open the possibility of systematic false positives (e.g., on formulaic genres like sports reporting).

## Next Checks
1. **Human annotation audit:** Randomly sample 200 articles from the 2023–2026 set (stratified by outlet and model-predicted label). Have bilingual Turkish annotators review for AI involvement using clear guidelines. Measure agreement and compare to model predictions to estimate false-positive/negative rates.
2. **Temporal holdout test:** If 2022 articles are available, use them as a truly unseen test set (model trained only on 2021 pairs). This checks for temporal drift without any data from the evaluation period, strengthening the stability claim.
3. **Genre-specific error analysis:** Categorize external articles by section (e.g., politics, sports, finance). Compute AI-prediction rates per genre. A significant deviation (e.g., sports at 0% vs. politics at 5%) would indicate the model is sensitive to topical/style variations rather than AI revision per se, questioning the 2.5% aggregate estimate.
