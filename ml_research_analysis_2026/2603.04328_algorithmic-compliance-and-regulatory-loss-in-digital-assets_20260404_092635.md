# Algorithmic Compliance and Regulatory Loss in Digital Assets

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04328
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04328
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Static enforcement thresholds in non‑stationary crypto AML systems cause large excess regulatory loss (mean 68.5–154.3 units) and loss ratios 1.97–2.23, while standard metrics (PR‑AUC, ROC‑AUC) overestimate real‑world effectiveness.

## Executive Summary

This study evaluates machine learning‑based anti‑money laundering (AML) systems for cryptocurrency using forward‑looking and rolling deployments on Bitcoin transaction data. The core insight is that temporal non‑stationarity induces pronounced instability in cost‑sensitive enforcement thresholds, leading to persistent miscalibration even when predictive accuracy remains stable. Consequently, static classification metrics substantially overstate real‑world regulatory effectiveness. Relative to dynamically optimal benchmarks, fixed historical thresholds generate mean excess loss of 68.5 units (CFN/CFP=10) and 154.3 units (CFN/CFP=25), with rolling window loss ratios averaging 1.97 and 2.23—indicating 97% and 123% higher loss, respectively. The failure arises from decision rule instability rather than declining model performance.

## Method Summary

The analysis uses the public Elliptic dataset: 46,564 labeled Bitcoin transactions (9.8% illicit), 165 features, indexed across 49 discrete time steps (with “unknown” labels excluded). The data show a declining illicit prevalence over time, embodying temporal non‑stationarity. Two classifiers are examined: regularized logistic regression (L2) and XGBoost, with features standardized using training‑set moments.

Three evaluation designs are implemented: (1) random stratified split (ignores time), (2) forward split (train on early periods, test on later ones), and (3) rolling deployment (for each test period *t*, train on the previous 10 time steps, then deploy). For each training window, the loss‑minimizing threshold τ* is selected via grid search over training scores to minimize *L(τ) = CFN·FN(τ) + CFP·FP(τ)* for cost ratios CFN/CFP ∈ {10, 25}. The chosen τ* is held fixed during the test period. An infeasible oracle benchmark re‑optimizes τ* on the contemporaneous test data, providing a lower bound on loss. Excess loss (deployed – oracle) and the deployed‑to‑oracle loss ratio are aggregated across test windows, and statistical significance is assessed via moving block bootstrap (block lengths 3, 5, 7; 5 000 replications) to account for serial correlation.

## Key Results

- Mean excess regulatory loss reaches 68.5 units (CFN/CFP=10) and 154.3 units (CFN/CFP=25) under rolling deployment.
- The deployed‑to‑oracle loss ratio averages 1.97 and 2.23 for the two cost ratios, indicating 97% and 123% higher loss than dynamically optimal thresholds.
- Static classification metrics (PR‑AUC, ROC‑AUC) remain stable across forward and rolling splits but mask large, persistent miscalibration of thresholds, leading to severe overestimation of real‑world AML effectiveness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Fixed Thresholds and Non‑Stationarity

**Claim:** Fixed enforcement thresholds cause large excess regulatory loss in non‑stationary AML systems.

**Mechanism:** The loss‑minimizing threshold τ* depends on (i) the distribution of risk scores and (ii) the illicit activity prevalence (base rate). Both change over time in crypto markets. A static τ calibrated on historical data becomes miscalibrated, increasing false positives or false negatives relative to an oracle that re‑optimizes τ each period. The result is persistent over‑ or under‑enforcement even when the model’s ranking ability remains high.

**Core assumption:** Predictive accuracy (e.g., ROC‑AUC) is stable across time, but the cost‑optimal threshold is not.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] “Temporal non‑stationarity induces pronounced instability in cost‑sensitive enforcement thresholds… core failure arises from miscalibration of decision rules rather than from declining predictive accuracy.”
- [section 4.2] Formal loss function *L(τ)=CFN·FN(τ)+CFP·FP(τ)*; τ* is a function of score distribution and prevalence.
- [section 5] Figure 3 shows τ* fluctuating sharply over time; Table 1 reports mean excess loss 68.5–154.3 units.
- [corpus] The Elliptic dataset exhibits a monotonic decline in illicit prevalence (Table A1), providing a concrete source of non‑stationarity.

**Break condition:** If both the score distribution and the illicit prevalence were constant across time, a fixed τ would coincide with the oracle and excess loss would vanish.

### Mechanism 2: Static Metrics Mask Threshold Drift

**Claim:** Static classification metrics (PR‑AUC, ROC‑AUC) grossly overestimate real‑world AML effectiveness because they ignore the economic impact of threshold shifts.

**Mechanism:** PR‑AUC and ROC‑AUC summarize ranking performance irrespective of the chosen operating point. Under temporal drift, the optimal operating point changes, so the same ranking can correspond to very different loss outcomes. Hence static metrics are uninformative about the actual regulatory loss incurred in deployment.

**Core assumption:** Regulatory decision makers rely on a single threshold applied to model scores; they do not continuously re‑calibrate it.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] “Strong static classification metrics substantially overstate real‑world regulatory effectiveness.”
- [section 4.1] “The performance of an AML system is not defined solely by predictive accuracy, but by how effectively it minimizes regulatory loss.”
- [section 5] Table 3: Random split yields PR‑AUC=0.769 and low loss, while forward split (time‑respecting) yields PR‑AUC=0.362 and nearly double loss.
- [corpus] The paper cites Elkan (2001) and Fawcett (2006) to argue that cost‑sensitive thresholds matter and that PR/ROC metrics can be misleading under imbalance.

**Break condition:** If the enforcement threshold were always chosen to be optimal for the current distribution, the mapping from ranking metrics to loss would be stable.

### Mechanism 3: Model‑Agnostic Fragility of Decision Rules

**Claim:** Deployment fragility is a property of the decision rule under non‑stationarity, not a flaw of a particular predictive model; even highly accurate models suffer large excess loss when thresholds are fixed.

**Mechanism:** Improving the model’s ranking accuracy (e.g., using XGBoost instead of logistic regression) does not eliminate threshold instability because τ* still varies with prevalence and score dispersion. In fact, a better model can increase the proportional gap because its oracle loss is lower, making the static threshold’s deficiency appear larger.

**Core assumption:** The optimal threshold τ* varies over time regardless of model fidelity.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [appendix B] Table B1 and Figures B1–B2: XGBoost achieves much higher PR‑AUC (0.978 vs 0.769) under random split, but forward‑split test loss is nearly identical to logistic regression (7,521 vs 7,579 at CFN/CFP=25). Rolling deployment excess loss remains large and the loss ratio is even higher (3.67 vs 1.97).
- [section 6] “Deployment fragility is robust to model choice… indicates that the result is a property of the decision rule under non‑stationarity rather than of any particular model’s predictive limitations.”
- [corpus] The model risk literature (Danielsson et al., 2016) warns that models calibrated in one regime may fail in another, independent of their in‑sample fit.

**Break condition:** If one could perfectly predict the future τ* or if τ* were invariant to distributional changes, a static threshold from any model would suffice.

## Foundational Learning

### Cost‑Sensitive Decision Thresholds
**Why needed:** AML enforcement has asymmetric costs (false negatives far more expensive than false positives). The regulator must choose a threshold on the risk score that minimizes expected loss, not one that maximizes accuracy.
**Quick check:** Given a model that outputs illicit probabilities, a current illicit prevalence of 5 %, and CFN/CFP = 20, how would you compute the threshold that minimizes expected regulatory loss?

### Temporal Non‑Stationarity / Concept Drift
**Why needed:** Bitcoin transaction patterns and illicit prevalence change over time due to market cycles, regulatory actions, and adversarial adaptation. Training data therefore becomes stale, affecting both feature distributions and class priors.
**Quick check:** What statistical signals would you monitor to detect that the optimal enforcement threshold has shifted in a rolling deployment?

### Regulatory Loss as Deployment Metric
**Why needed:** The ultimate goal is to minimize the economic harm from missed illicit activity and unnecessary investigations. This loss is a direct function of false positives/negatives and the chosen cost ratio.
**Quick check:** If a system flags 100 transactions, 20 are truly illicit (false negatives=5, false positives=75) and CFN=10, CFP=1, what is the regulatory loss?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Data ingestion → Predictive model trainer → Threshold optimizer → Deployment engine → Oracle benchmark → Evaluation harness

### Critical path
The end‑to‑end process starts with loading the Elliptic dataset and extracting features. For each rolling time step: a model is trained on the previous 10 periods; the loss‑minimizing threshold τ* is found on the training scores; that τ* is fixed and applied to the current test period; false positives/negatives and regulatory loss are computed; an oracle re‑optimizes τ* on the test period; metrics (excess loss, loss ratio) are aggregated across windows with moving block bootstrap for uncertainty quantification.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Model simplicity vs. capacity:** Simple models (logistic regression) isolate deployment dynamics; complex models (XGBoost) may capture patterns better but still suffer threshold drift.
- **Evaluation horizon:** Random split hides temporal drift and overestimates performance; forward/rolling splits reflect reality but reduce training data and increase variance.
- **Cost ratio selection:** Higher CFN/CFP amplifies sensitivity to threshold error but also scales absolute loss; multiple ratios are tested for robustness.
- **Re‑calibration frequency:** Fixed historical threshold vs. sliding‑window recalibration trade‑off: more frequent recalibration could close the gap but requires contemporary labels (often unavailable).

### Failure signatures
- Large gap between static metrics (PR‑AUC, ROC‑AUC) and deployed regulatory loss.
- Time series plot of τ* shows abrupt jumps or sustained drift.
- Episodic spikes in deployed‑to‑oracle loss ratio, especially during market transitions.
- Training performance remains high while test loss deteriorates sharply.

### First 3 experiments
1. Replicate the main rolling deployment with logistic regression, calculate mean excess loss, loss ratios, and confidence intervals (block bootstrap).
2. Run the same rolling deployment with XGBoost, compare PR‑AUC, deployed loss, oracle loss, and loss ratios to the logistic baseline to confirm robustness of fragility.
3. Perform the feasible recalibration experiment: for each test period, re‑optimize τ using only the most recent *w* ∈ {2,3,5,7} time steps of data; compute the fraction of the deployment gap closed and test whether it differs significantly from zero.

## Limitations

- Exact hyperparameters for logistic regression (regularization strength) and XGBoost are unspecified, potentially affecting replication.
- Threshold search granularity and bootstrap implementation details (overlapping blocks, boundary handling) are not fully described, introducing uncertainty.
- The analysis relies on the Elliptic dataset version with 165 features and 49 time steps; alternate versions could alter prevalence trends and results.

## Confidence

- Fixed Threshold Fragility: High  
  Strong empirical evidence (large, persistent excess loss) and theoretical grounding (τ* depends on prevalence and score distribution, which drift).
- Static Metrics Overestimation: Medium  
  Supported by random vs. forward split comparisons, but the magnitude of overestimation may depend on cost ratios and model distributions.
- Model‑Agnostic Failure: High  
  Robust across logistic regression and XGBoost despite large PR‑AUC gaps; the issue resides in decision rule instability, not predictive accuracy.

## Next Checks

1. Replicate with Specified Hyperparameters: Train logistic regression with tuned *C* via nested cross‑validation within rolling windows and XGBoost with standard hyperparameter search; compare mean excess loss and loss ratios to the paper’s baseline.
2. Test Feasible Recalibration Frequency: Implement sliding‑window τ re‑optimization using the last *w* ∈ {2,3,5,7} periods; compute mean loss ratio improvement and test significance (paired t‑test on per‑window ratios).
3. Isolate Prevalence vs. Distribution Shift: Construct a synthetic experiment holding the score distribution fixed but applying the real illicit prevalence timeline to the loss function; measure excess loss to disentangle the effect of base‑rate drift from covariate shift.
