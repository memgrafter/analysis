# Neutral Prompts, Non-Neutral People: Quantifying Gender and Skin-Tone Bias in Gemini Flash 2.5 Image and GPT Image 1.5

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12133
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12133
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** Both Gemini Flash 2.5 Image and GPT Image 1.5 exhibit a strong "default white" bias (>96% of subjects classified as white) from neutral prompts, but diverge sharply in gender defaults (Gemini: 93.7% female, GPT: 70.6% male), with an intersectional effect where female-presenting subjects are rendered with darker measured skin tones partly due to cosmetic styling.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether semantically neutral prompts (e.g., "a person") yield demographically neutral outputs from two commercial text-to-image models: Gemini Flash 2.5 Image and GPT Image 1.5. By generating 3,200 photorealistic images and analyzing them with a color-normalization pipeline and facial masking, the authors measure skin tone across standardized scales (Monk, PERLA, Fitzpatrick) and perceived gender. Both models show an overwhelming "default white" bias, with over 96% of generated subjects classified as light-skinned. However, their gender distributions oppose each other dramatically—Gemini produces predominantly female-presenting subjects while GPT produces predominantly male-presenting ones. Furthermore, an intersectional pattern emerges where female-presenting subjects are measured with significantly darker skin tones than male-presenting ones, a effect partly attributed to systematic cosmetic styling. The findings demonstrate that neutral prompts do not produce neutral outputs; instead, they reveal model-specific latent demographic priors shaped by training data and alignment interventions.

## Method Summary
The study generated 400 images per model for each of four semantically neutral prompts ("a human being, photorealistic", "a person, photorealistic", "an individual, photorealistic", "someone, photorealistic"), totaling 3,200 images. A hybrid preprocessing pipeline was applied to each detected and aligned face: conversion to CIELAB color space, CLAHE on the L* channel blended equally with the original, and background-referenced white balance using non-facial bright pixels. A skin mask was created from dense facial landmarks (MediaPipe Face Mesh), excluding eyes, nostrils, lips, and eyebrows, with a tapered forehead expansion. The dominant skin color was extracted via k-means clustering (k=4) on masked pixels, aggregating clusters covering at least 36% of the mask, and mapped to Monk Skin Tone (MST), PERLA, and Fitzpatrick Skin Type (FST) using CIEDE2000 distance to reference palettes. Perceived gender and race were classified using DeepFace's attribute estimator. Statistical comparisons across models and prompts used chi-square, t-tests, and Mann-Whitney U tests.

## Key Results
- Both models exhibit a "default white" bias: over 96% of generated subjects are classified as white across MST, PERLA, and FST scales.
- Gender defaults diverge sharply: Gemini Flash 2.5 Image generates 93.7% female-presenting subjects, while GPT Image 1.5 generates 70.6% male-presenting subjects.
- An intersectional effect is observed: female-presenting subjects are measured with significantly darker skin tones than male-presenting ones within each model, partly attributed to consistent cosmetic styling (blush, warm-toned makeup) that increases measured redness/warmth.
- Prompt wording modulates outputs differently per model; notably, the token "someone" inverts GPT's default male bias to a female majority, indicating that even synonymous neutral prompts probe distinct latent associations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Training Data Distribution Imprint**  
Claim: Persistent "default white" prior arises from training on large-scale, uncurated internet imagery where light-skinned individuals are vastly overrepresented. Even semantically neutral prompts activate this prior because the unmarked concept of "person" is shaped by the most frequently seen examples. Evidence: >96% white classification across scales; prior work documents systemic light-skin dominance in web imagery. Break condition: A model trained on a deliberately balanced dataset still yielding a "default white" output under neutral prompting would falsify this.

**Mechanism 2: Model-Specific Alignment Interventions**  
Claim: Opposing gender defaults (Gemini female-skewed, GPT male-skewed) result from different post-training alignment (safety, diversity) applying corrective biases atop base training distributions. The base prior may be male-skewed (reflecting internet demographics); stronger corrective alignment in Gemini over-corrects toward female presentation, while GPT's weaker intervention leaves the original prior dominant. Evidence: Stark gender divergence; the paper hypothesizes alignment differences. Break condition: Consistent same-gender distribution across models after controlling for generation parameters would undermine this.

**Mechanism 3: Systematic Aesthetic Styling as Confound**  
Claim: The measured darker skin tone for female-presenting subjects is partly driven by learned visual conventions: female faces are routinely rendered with blush or warm-toned makeup on cheek regions included in skin masks, while male faces use cooler, flatter lighting. These stylistic choices increase measured redness/warmth in CIELAB space, shifting them to darker MST/PERLA/FST categories independently of base pigmentation. Evidence: Visual inspection and intersectional effect; the paper explicitly attributes part of the tone shift to cosmetic rendering. Break condition: If swapping gendered stylistic attributes (makeup/lighting) while holding base face constant does not produce the predicted skin tone shift, the hypothesis weakens.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Illumination-Aware Colorimetry**  
  *Why needed:* Raw RGB values are dominated by lighting and color grading, not skin pigmentation. Without separating these, skin tone metrics are invalid.  
  *Quick check:* Why is converting to CIELAB and performing background-referenced white balance essential, and what does each step mitigate?

- **Concept: Ordinal vs. Interval Data in Fairness Metrics**  
  *Why needed:* Fitzpatrick Skin Type is ordinal (I-VI), not continuous. Treating it as interval (e.g., calculating means) is statistically inappropriate and can misrepresent bias severity.  
  *Quick check:* Why is reporting distributions and using Mann-Whitney U more appropriate than mean FST here?

- **Concept: Prompt as Diagnostic Probe vs. Control Variable**  
  *Why needed:* The study flips the assumption that neutral prompts are a "control." They are the *independent variable* that reveals latent model biases. Understanding this flip is key to interpreting results.  
  *Quick check:* How can "a person" produce opposite gender outputs from two models, and what does this imply for designing bias audits?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Prompt Input Module -> Generation Orchestration (calls commercial API) -> Preprocessing Pipeline (face detection, alignment, color normalization) -> Skin Mask Generation (dense landmarks, convex hull, subtractive masking, tapered forehead) -> Quantification Engine (k-means clustering, weighted centroid, mapping to scales) -> Demographic Classifier (DeepFace) -> Statistical Aggregator.

**Critical path:** Prompt → Generate Image → Detect Face & Align → Normalize Color → Generate Skin Mask → Cluster & Compute Centroid → Map to Scales → Classify Demographics → Aggregate.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Normalization strength: Aggressive CLAHE/white balance can cause unnatural colors; pipeline uses blending and background-only white balance to preserve naturalism.  
- Mask fidelity: Excluding non-skin features (lips) is crucial but risks losing cheek area if landmarks are inaccurate; tapered forehead expansion compensates.  
- Scale choice: Using multiple scales (MST, PERLA, FST) improves robustness but complicates cross-study comparison.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High variance in skin tone for a single prompt/model indicates stochastic instability in human representation.  
- Prompt inversion effect (e.g., "someone" flips GPT's gender default) suggests the token hits an unintended embedding region.  
- Post-normalization colors look unnatural: over-correction in white balance; check background selection logic.  
- Mask fails on facial hair or extreme poses: landmark detector confidence low; requires fallback heuristics or exclusion.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Reproduction:** Run the exact 4-prompt, 400-samples/each protocol on current API versions. Verify >96% white classification and gender divergence pattern. Goal: Confirm findings are persistent.  
2. **Normalization Efficacy Test:** For a subset (n=200), compute raw vs. normalized (L\*a\*b\*) skin tone metrics. Expect raw values to show less severe "default white" bias due to warm lighting darkening light skin. Goal: Validate the pipeline's necessity.  
3. **Cosmetic Confound Probe:** Manually label 200 images (100 female-presenting, 100 male-presenting) for visible blush/makeup on masked skin regions. Correlate label with PERLA/MST deviation from model's gender-group median. Goal: Empirically link aesthetic stylization to measured skin tone shift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** Do neutral textual prompts consistently yield demographically neutral image outputs across different commercial text-to-image models, or do they systematically activate model-specific demographic priors?  
*Basis:* Explicit research question. *Why unresolved:* Mechanisms causing divergent defaults (training data, alignment) are not disentangled. *What evidence would resolve:* Controlled ablation studies on model internals to identify which components correlate with observed defaults.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent is the observed systematic association between predicted gender and measured skin tone (darker tones for female-presenting subjects) driven by learned aesthetic/stylistic conventions versus a deeper representational bias?  
*Basis:* Intersectional effect attributed partly to cosmetic styling. *Why unresolved:* Methodology measures combined output (pigmentation + aesthetic) but cannot isolate each contribution. *What evidence would resolve:* Experimental pipeline controlling for makeup, lighting, post-processing; analyze skin tone on regions unaffected by cosmetics (forehead, jawline) or use adversarial techniques to remove stylistic artifacts.

**Open Question 3:** Why does the specific lexical token "someone" provoke a dramatic inversion of GPT's default male bias to a female majority, while other near-synonymous neutral prompts do not?  
*Basis:* Explicitly noted anomaly in results. *Why unresolved:* Phenomenon documented but not explained; points to fine-grained, non-semantic linguistic associations. *What evidence would resolve:* Representational analysis (embedding similarity, activation patching) comparing internal representations of "someone," "a person," "an individual" to identify features that differentially activate gender pathways.

**Open Question 4:** How do commercial image generation models' demographic defaults change when explicitly prompted for diversity (e.g., "a diverse group of people"), and does such prompting override defaults or fundamentally alter the distribution to mitigate intersectional biases?  
*Basis:* Limitations note focus on neutral prompts; prior work suggests explicit counter-stereotypical prompting has limited effect, but not tested on these models. *Why unresolved:* Only default behavior is characterized; efficacy of diversity prompting remains unknown. *What evidence would resolve:* Large-scale follow-up study using same models/pipeline with standardized diversity/inclusion prompts, comparing shifts in aggregate distributions, variance, and intersectional patterns.

## Limitations
- **Parameter opacity:** Exact API endpoints, generation settings (sampler, CFG), and color normalization thresholds (CLAHE clip/grid, white balance percentile) are unspecified, risking reproduction variance.
- **Interpretive confounds:** The cosmetic-styling explanation for darker female skin tones is visually plausible but not quantitatively validated; it relies on observed patterns, not controlled experiments isolating pigmentation from aesthetics.
- **Weak corpus evidence:** Neighboring literature supports systemic bias but offers weak direct evidence for vendor-specific alignment divergences or the aesthetic confound mechanism, making some interpretations somewhat speculative.

## Confidence
- **Default white bias:** High – robust across multiple scales (MST/PERLA/FST), with >96% white classification.
- **Opposite gender defaults:** High – stark, statistically significant divergence (93.7% female vs 70.6% male) is clearly reported.
- **Cosmetic confound for skin tone:** Low/Medium – logically consistent and visually observed, but lacks quantitative disentanglement of pigmentation vs. stylistic rendering.

## Next Checks
1. **Baseline persistence test:** Re-run generation with current public API versions (Gemini Flash 2.5, GPT Image 1.5) using exact prompts; confirm >90% white and divergent gender distributions survive parameter standardization.
2. **Normalization impact audit:** For a held-out subset, compute raw vs. normalized skin-tone metrics; quantify how much the pipeline attenuates or amplifies the "default white" effect and gender-skin tone intersection.
3. **Controlled cosmetic confound probe:** Manually annotate 200 generated images for visible blush/makeup in masked skin regions; correlate annotation with PERLA/MST deviation from model-specific gender-group median to test if styling explains the tone shift.
