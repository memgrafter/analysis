# MIND: Unified Inquiry and Diagnosis RL with Criteria Grounded Clinical Supports for Psychiatric Consultation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03677
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03677
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Key result:** PsySim-Std: 71.5% accuracy, 72.5% macro-F1; PsySim-Adapt: 62.5% accuracy, 63.1% macro-F1; support faithfulness 8.6/10; 82% human preference.

## Executive Summary
MIND introduces a unified reinforcement learning framework for psychiatric consultation that systematically addresses unsupported clinical assertions and inquiry drift. It constructs a Criteria-Grounded Psychiatric Reasoning Bank (PRB) to retrieve evidence-aligned clinical supports at each turn, enforces explicit reasoning through rubric-based process rewards, and uses value-aware trajectory rectification to correct low-yield behaviors. Evaluated on two patient simulators (PsySim-Std and PsySim-Adapt), MIND significantly outperforms existing methods in diagnostic accuracy, macro-F1, and support faithfulness, while receiving a strong preference from human evaluators. The work demonstrates a viable path toward more reliable and transparent LLM-based psychiatric assistants, though questions regarding cultural generalizability, simulator fidelity, and computational cost remain.

## Method Summary
MIND’s pipeline comprises four stages. First, a static Criteria-Grounded Psychiatric Reasoning Bank (PRB) is built from de-identified EMRs and clinical guidelines; the PRB stores tuples of (clinical retrieval state, support note, quality score). Second, a two-stage LLM agent generates a compressed retrieval query (`rag_query`) based on the dialogue history, retrieves top‑k supports from the PRB, and then produces a `think` trace (covering symptom analysis, differentials, decision logic) followed by an `answer` (next inquiry or diagnosis). Third, reinforcement learning with GRPO optimizes the policy using a mixture of turn‑level rewards: process reward (`r_proc_t`) from an LLM‑as‑judge scoring the `think` trace, retrieval reward (`r_retr_t`), information gain reward (`r_gain_t`), penalty terms (`r_pen_t`), and a terminal accuracy reward. A KL constraint preserves the supervised initialization. Finally, a trajectory rectifier monitors each turn for low‑utility signals (e.g., repetition, low information gain) and triggers either self‑retry or a PRB‑guided fallback to a reference inquiry, thereby reducing drift and stabilizing long‑horizon behavior.

## Key Results
- Diagnostic accuracy: 71.5% (PsySim-Std), 62.5% (PsySim-Adapt); macro‑F1: 72.5% (Std), 63.1% (Adapt), all significantly above baselines.
- Support faithfulness: 8.6/10, indicating high alignment between responses and retrieved clinical supports.
- Human evaluation: 82% preference for MIND over competing systems.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Criteria‑Grounding Retrieval Bank
**Claim:** A Criteria‑Grounded Psychiatric Reasoning Bank (PRB) reduces unsupported clinical assertions by providing criteria‑aligned supports for each dialogue turn.  
**Mechanism:** The dialogue context is compressed into a factual, keyword‑dense “clinical retrieval state” (`q_t`). This state queries a pre‑built bank of (retrieval state, support note) pairs. Retrieved supports (`r_i`), synthesized from guidelines, explicitly state known facts, missing information, and the next diagnostic step, grounding reasoning in standardized criteria (e.g., duration thresholds).  
**Core assumption:** Direct retrieval from raw dialogue is unreliable; structured intermediate representations and distilled supports provide actionable, decision‑critical cues.  
**Breakdown condition:** If retrieval states omit critical missing fields (e.g., “duration unclear”) or synthesized supports misalign with criteria (low reliability scores), the grounding mechanism fails, potentially leading to missed checks or incorrect inferences.

### Mechanism 2: Rubric‑Based Process Supervision
**Claim:** Rubric‑based process supervision via explicit `think` traces improves clinical reasoning alignment and reduces low‑yield questioning.  
**Mechanism:** The policy generates a two‑stage output per turn: first a `rag_query` (`q_t`), then after retrieval a `think` trace (`z_t`) and an `answer` (`a_t`). The `think` trace must cover symptom analysis, differential considerations, and decision logic. An LLM‑as‑judge scores this trace on three clinical dimensions, yielding a dense turn‑level reward (`r_proc_t`).  
**Core assumption:** Outcome‑only supervision (final diagnosis) is too sparse for multi‑turn credit assignment; fine‑grained supervision on intermediate steps is necessary to teach criterion‑aligned inquiry.  
**Breakdown condition:** If `think` traces become templated, ignore retrieved supports, or are inconsistent with the response, the process reward loses its guiding power, potentially leading to superficial compliance without genuine reasoning.

### Mechanism 3: Value‑Aware Trajectory Rectification
**Claim:** Trajectory rectification mitigates inquiry drift (off‑topic/low‑yield questioning) and improves multi‑turn stability.  
**Mechanism:** A rule‑based monitor checks turn‑level signals (e.g., question duplication, format violations, low information gain). Upon detection, it triggers (a) self‑retry (re‑generate the turn under stricter constraints) or (b) PRB‑guided fallback (retrieve the nearest PRB entry and execute its reference inquiry). This steers the dialogue back to a high‑yield, criteria‑aligned path.  
**Core assumption:** Noisy narratives and sparse rewards cause policy degeneration in long‑horizon RL; lightweight, rule‑based interventions with stable fallbacks are more reliable than purely reward‑based correction.  
**Breakdown condition:** If fallback inquiries are themselves low‑yield or misaligned with current context, or if self‑retry repeatedly fails, rectification may introduce new drift or stall progress.

## Foundational Learning
- **Psychiatric Diagnostic Criteria (ICD‑10/DSM‑5)**  
  *Why needed:* The PRB and reasoning rubric are built around criteria thresholds (e.g., “depressive episode requires low mood/anhedonia ≥2 weeks”). Understanding these rules is essential to grasp “criteria‑grounded” reasoning and evaluate inquiry quality.  
  *Quick check:* For a major depressive episode per ICD‑10, what are the required core symptoms and minimum duration?
  
- **Reinforcement Learning for Sequential Decision‑Making (PPO/GRPO)**  
  *Why needed:* MIND optimizes a multi‑turn policy via RL with mixed rewards. Credit assignment across turns and the role of dense process rewards are key to interpreting the training objective.  
  *Quick check:* How does adding a turn‑level process reward (`r_proc_t`) help mitigate the credit assignment problem in a 10‑turn psychiatric consultation?
  
- **Retrieval‑Augmented Generation (RAG) in Medical Domains**  
  *Why needed:* MIND’s PRB is a specialized RAG system. Understanding standard RAG and its medical challenges (hallucination, ambiguous queries) explains the use of structured `rag_query` and distilled supports.  
  *Quick check:* In medical RAG, why might compressing a patient’s narrative into a keyword‑dense “clinical retrieval state” improve retrieval reliability compared to using the raw narrative as the query?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
PRB Constructor → builds static knowledge base (triplets `(q_i, r_i, m_i)`).  
Dialogue Policy → two‑stage LLM agent: generates `rag_query`, then fetches supports and produces `think` + `answer`.  
Reward Modules → compute `r_proc_t` (LLM judge on `think`), `r_retr_t` (retrieval reliability), `r_gain_t` (information gain), `r_pen_t` (penalties), and terminal `r_term`.  
Trajectory Rectifier → monitors turn utility (repetition, low gain); triggers self‑retry or PRB fallback.  
Patient Simulator → fixed LLM (Qwen3) generates patient response from profile.

### Critical Path (per turn t)
1. Policy generates `rag_query` (`q_t`) from dialogue history `H_t`.  
2. PRB lookup retrieves top‑k supports `S_t`.  
3. Policy generates `think` trace (`z_t`) and `answer` (`a_t`) conditioned on `H_t` and `S_t`.  
4. Simulator returns patient utterance `u_{t+1}`.  
5. Compute turn rewards (`r_proc_t`, `r_retr_t`, `r_gain_t`, `r_pen_t`).  
6. Rectifier checks for low‑utility; if triggered, overrides `a_t` with retry or fallback.  
7. Log trajectory for RL update.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Retrieval depth (k):** Larger k increases chance of relevant supports but adds noise and latency; performance saturates around k=5 (Fig. 6).  
- **Process reward cost:** LLM‑as‑judge scoring is computationally expensive per turn but provides essential dense supervision; ablations show significant drop without it.  
- **Rectification aggressiveness:** Overly frequent fallbacks can make dialogues rigid; the system caps fallbacks per episode to prevent over‑reliance on a single PRB entry.

### Failure Signatures
- High repetition rate (ineffective `r_gain_t` or failed rectification).  
- Low support faithfulness (<8/10) suggests ignoring retrieved supports or generating unsupported claims.  
- Divergence in PsySim‑Adapt performance relative to PsySim‑Std indicates poor generalization to narrative variation.  
- Format violations (missing `think`/`answer`) point to SFT warm‑start issues or KL penalty misconfiguration.

### First 3 Experiments (Reproduction Checks)
1. **PRB Retrieval sanity check:** For a held‑out set of dialogue states, manually verify that top‑k retrieved supports contain the correct next‑step criteria check; measure precision@k (expect >85% at k=5).  
2. **Process reward alignment correlation:** Recruit 3 board‑certified psychiatrists to rate 100 `think` traces on the three rubric dimensions; compute Spearman correlation with LLM‑judge scores (target ρ≥0.7).  
3. **Fallback trigger analysis:** Log conditions triggering rectification; compare the percentage of corrected trajectories that acquire a key diagnostic cue within 2 turns vs. non‑corrected controls.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Atypical/Novel Presentations:** How does MIND handle symptom patterns not well‑represented in the PRB? The PRB is fixed and built from historical data; the “Other” category performance is not dissected, limiting understanding of generalization to rare disorders. Evidence needed: error analysis on “Other” cases and tests with disorders explicitly excluded from PRB construction.
- **Patient Simulator Fidelity:** Do PsySim‑Std and PsySim‑Adapt accurately reflect real patient ambiguity, inconsistency, and narrative drift? Simulated responses may not capture the complexity of real psychiatric interactions, risking overfitting to simulator artifacts. Evidence needed: direct comparison of MIND on simulated vs. real dialogues with clinician raters.
- **Cross‑Cultural Generalizability:** Can MIND transfer to cultural contexts where symptom expression and communication styles differ from Chinese resources? The PRB is built from Chinese guidelines, potentially missing culturally specific presentations (e.g., somatic emphasis). Evidence needed: validation on datasets from diverse regions and adaptation experiments with localized guidelines.
- **Computational Feasibility:** What is the inference latency and memory overhead of the two‑stage retrieval‑reasoning plus rectification process? The PRB contains >100k entries; real‑time use in clinics demands low latency (<2 sec/turn). Evidence needed: benchmarking on clinical hardware and scaling analysis.
- **Reward Hacking:** Does process supervision truly improve reasoning quality, or does it optimize for superficial rubric compliance? LLM judges may introduce bias, leading to gaming. Evidence needed: expert blind evaluation of reasoning traces and ablation studies with varied rubrics.

## Limitations
- **Incomplete methodological details:** Key components such as PRB synthesis prompts and simulator reconstruction from EMRs are not fully described, limiting exact replication.
- **Simulator fidelity uncertainty:** The realism of PsySim‑Std/Adapt relative to actual psychiatric consultations is unknown, which may affect transfer to real clinical settings.
- **Evaluation dependence on LLM judges:** Process rewards and faithfulness rely on LLM‑as‑judge scoring without robust human validation, risking misalignment or reward hacking.
