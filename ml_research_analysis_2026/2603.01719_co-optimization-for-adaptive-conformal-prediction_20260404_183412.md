# Co-optimization for Adaptive Conformal Prediction

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01719
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01719
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Co-optimizes interval center and radius via alternating updates, achieving 13–20% shorter intervals and half the conditional coverage error compared to CQR on skewed data.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces CoCP, a method for adaptive conformal prediction intervals that co-optimizes the center and radius to address inefficiencies under skewed conditional distributions. By alternating between updating the radius via quantile regression on folded absolute residuals and refining the center using a soft-coverage objective whose gradients concentrate near interval boundaries, CoCP naturally shifts the interval toward higher density regions without full density estimation. A final split-conformal calibration step guarantees finite-sample marginal coverage. Theoretical analysis shows asymptotic optimality under unimodality and smoothness, and experiments on synthetic skewed data and seven UCI regression datasets demonstrate substantial improvements in efficiency and conditional reliability over state-of-the-art baselines.

## Method Summary
CoCP employs two neural networks: a center network \(m(x)\) and a radius network \(h(x) > 0\). Training proceeds in an alternating loop over \(T\) iterations: with \(m\) frozen, \(h\) is trained via pinball loss on the folded residuals \(|Y - m(x)|\) at quantile \(\tau = 1-\alpha\); then with \(h\) frozen, \(m\) is trained via a differentiable soft-coverage loss \(-\mathbb{E}[\sigma((h - |Y - m|)/\beta)]\) with small temperature \(\beta\), which generates gradients that imbalance according to endpoint densities. After alternations, \(h\) is fine-tuned. To reduce variance, \(K\)-fold cross-fitting trains separate \((m^{(k)}, h^{(k)})\) pairs; their outputs are averaged to form \( \tilde{m}(x), \tilde{h}(x) \). Finally, split-conformal calibration on a held-out set computes the score \(S = |Y - \tilde{m}(x)| / \tilde{h}(x)\) and its \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile \(\hat{q}\), yielding the interval \(\tilde{m}(x) \pm \hat{q} \tilde{h}(x)\). Standard choices: \(T=5\), \(\beta=0.01\), \(K=5\), MLP backbone (2 hidden layers, 64 ReLU units), Adam optimizer with early stopping.

## Key Results
- On skewed synthetic data (Exponential, LogNormal), CoCP shortens average interval length by 13–20% relative to CQR and reduces conditional coverage error (ConMAE) by half.
- Across seven UCI datasets, CoCP achieves the tightest intervals on five and best conditional diagnostics (MSCE, WSC, \(\ell_1/\ell_2\)-ERT).
- Theory: Under unimodal smooth conditional densities, CoCP asymptotically recovers the highest-density interval and attains conditional coverage as estimation error and temperature vanish.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The efficiency gains stem from a geometric push–pull principle and an alternating co-optimization scheme that avoids full density estimation.

**Mechanism 1: Folding Geometry and Push–Pull Translation**  
For a fixed center \(m\), the minimal radius achieving conditional coverage \(1-\alpha\) is the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile \(\psi_x(m)\) of the folded residual \(|Y-m|\). The derivative \(\psi'_x(m) = -\big(f(m+\psi)-f(m-\psi)\big)/\big(f(m+\psi)+f(m-\psi)\big)\) reveals that if the density at the right endpoint exceeds the left, moving \(m\) right reduces \(\psi_x(m)\). The optimal symmetric interval (highest-density interval) satisfies \(f(m^\star+h^\star) = f(m^\star-h^\star)\). Thus, translating the center toward the denser side shortens the width. This requires a continuous unimodal conditional density to ensure a contiguous interval and differentiability of \(\psi_x\).

**Mechanism 2: Alternating Co‑Optimization**  
CoCP directly implements the push–pull without estimating \(f_x\).  
- Radius step: With \(m\) fixed, train \(h\) to estimate \(\psi_x(m)\) via pinball loss on \(|Y-m|\).  
- Center step: With \(h\) fixed, train \(m\) via the soft‑coverage loss \(-\mathbb{E}[\sigma((h - |Y-m|)/\beta)]\). Its gradient concentrates near boundaries where \(|Y-m| \approx h\) and asymptotically approximates \(f(m+h)-f(m-\psi)\), pushing \(m\) toward the denser endpoint. Alternating iterations refine both parameters. Consistency of quantile regression and a faithful gradient direction are needed; these rely on boundedness, positive density around the score quantile, and sufficient model capacity.

**Mechanism 3: Split‑Conformal Calibration for Marginal Validity**  
Regardless of how \(m\) and \(h\) are learned, applying split‑conformal calibration on the normalized score \(S = |Y-m(X)|/h(X)\) guarantees finite‑sample marginal coverage under exchangeability. On an independent calibration set, \(\hat{q}\) is the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of the scores; the final interval \([m(x)-\hat{q}h(x),\, m(x)+\hat{q}h(x)]\) satisfies \(\mathbb{P}(Y \in \mathcal{I}(X)) \ge 1-\alpha\) exactly by the standard rank argument.

Break conditions: Non‑unimodal densities invalidate the push‑pull optimality; discontinuities or flat regions in \(f_x\) make the derivative ambiguous. Mis‑specified \(h\) misaligns boundary gradients; extreme learning rates or temperatures cause instability. Violating exchangeability (e.g., covariate shift) breaks marginal coverage guarantees.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Conformal Prediction and Split Conformal**  
  *Why needed:* Provides distribution‑free marginal coverage guarantees by calibrating any nonconformity score on an independent dataset via empirical quantiles.  
  *Quick check:* What splitting scheme does split conformal require, and why does the empirical quantile of calibration scores control coverage?

- **Concept: Quantile Regression (Pinball Loss)**  
  *Why needed:* The radius \(h(x)\) is trained as the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of the folded residual \(|Y-m(x)|\) by minimizing \(\mathbb{E}[\rho_\tau(|Y-m| - h)]\).  
  *Quick check:* Show that minimizing \(\mathbb{E}[\rho_\tau(Y - q)]\) yields \(q = F_Y^{-1}(\tau)\) for continuous \(Y\), where \(\rho_\tau(u) = u(\tau - \mathbf{1}_{u<0})\).

- **Concept: Stochastic Gradient Optimization with Surrogate Losses**  
  *Why needed:* The soft‑coverage objective replaces the nondifferentiable interval indicator with a sigmoid window so \(m\) can be updated via backpropagation, with temperature \(\beta\) controlling gradient locality.  
  *Quick check:* How does \(\beta\) affect the magnitude and spatial concentration of gradients from \(\sigma((h - |Y-m|)/\beta)\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Input \(x\) → \(m\_net(x)\) and \(h\_net(x)\) → folded residual \(|y - m\_net(x)|\) → pinball loss for \(h\) and soft‑coverage loss for \(m\) → alternating updates → cross‑fit ensemble \((\tilde{m}, \tilde{h})\) → score \(S = |y - \tilde{m}(x)| / \tilde{h}(x)\) → calibration quantile \(\hat{q}\) → interval \([\tilde{m}(x) - \hat{q}\tilde{h}(x),\, \tilde{m}(x) + \hat{q}\tilde{h}(x)]\).

Critical path:  
1. Split training data into \(D_m\), \(D_h\), and validation \(D_val\).  
2. Warm‑start \(m\_net\) on \(D_m\) with MSE, early stop on \(D_val\).  
3. Alternate \(T=5\) times: (a) train \(h\_net\) on \(D_h\) with \(m\_net\) frozen (pinball loss); (b) train \(m\_net\) on \(D_m\) with \(h\_net\) frozen (soft‑coverage loss).  
4. Fine‑tune \(h\_net\) on \(D_h\) with \(m\_net\) frozen.  
5. Repeat steps 1‑4 for \(K=5\) cross‑fitting folds; average outputs to get \(\tilde{m}, \tilde{h}\).  
6. Compute scores \(S_i = |Y_i - \tilde{m}(X_i)| / \tilde{h}(X_i)\) on calibration set; set \(\hat{q}\) as \(\lceil(1-\alpha)(n_{cal}+1)\rceil\)-th order statistic.  
7. Deploy the interval function.

Design tradeoffs:  
- Alternating iterations \(T\): More iterations (e.g., 5) improve refinement but risk overfitting; 2–4 often suffice on skewed data.  
- Temperature \(\beta\): Smaller \(\beta\) (e.g., 0.01) localizes gradients for precise centering but may cause instability.  
- Cross‑fit folds \(K\): Larger \(K\) (e.g., 5) improves data efficiency at the cost of training \(K\) models.  
- Model capacity: 2 hidden layers, 64 units balances expressiveness and generalization; oversized models may overfit the soft‑coverage surrogate.  
- Calibration size: Larger calibration set reduces \(\hat{q}\) error but reduces training data; 20% split is typical.

Failure signatures:  
- Marginal coverage << 0.9: Check exchangeability of calibration and test data; ensure no leakage between splits.  
- Excessively wide intervals: Monitor \(h\_net\) outputs; verify pinball loss decreasing; increase \(D_h\) size or training epochs.  
- High conditional error (MSCE/WSC/ERT): Likely \(m\_net\) not shifting; try increasing \(T\) or decreasing \(\beta\).  
- Training instability (NaNs/Infs): Ensure \(h\_net\) uses Softplus with positive bias and add epsilon to score denominator.  
- High variance across seeds: Reduce model capacity, increase early‑stopping patience, or add regularization.

First 3 experiments:  
1. Sanity check: After training, compute empirical coverage and average length on a fresh test set; coverage should be ≥0.9 within statistical error.  
2. Push‑pull effect: On synthetic skewed data, compare CoCP intervals to CQR and oracle HDI; visualize that CoCP’s center shifts toward the denser tail, and confirm 13–20% length reduction and ConMAE halving.  
3. Ablation on alternating iterations \(T\): Run CoCP with \(T=0,1,\dots,5\) on skewed data; plot length and ConMAE versus \(T\); show significant improvement even for small \(T\), while \(T=0\) (warm‑start only) behaves like an equal‑tail method.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How does CoCP's boundary‑local gradient approach compare to full conditional density estimation in sample efficiency, robustness to misspecification, and ability to handle complex conditional shapes? The paper does not empirically compare against density‑based baselines under varying sample sizes or multimodal distributions.
- **Question 2:** Does the alternating optimization procedure converge to a stable fixed point, and how sensitive is it to initialization, learning rates, and iterations? Theoretical analysis assumes consistent estimators but does not address the dynamics of the alternating updates.
- **Question 3:** Can the co‑optimization framework be generalized to multi‑dimensional responses to produce efficient, valid prediction regions (e.g., ellipsoids) while maintaining boundary‑local updates? The authors cite this as a natural but open direction due to challenges in geometry and conformal calibration for non‑interval sets.

## Limitations
- The push–pull mechanism relies on unimodal, smooth conditional densities; multimodal or heavy‑tailed distributions may break optimality.
- Temperature \(\beta\) is fixed at 0.01 without tuning guidance; sensitivity is not thoroughly analyzed.
- Data split ratios for \(D_m\) vs \(D_h\) are unspecified, hindering exact reproduction.
- Baseline hyperparameters for methods like C‑HDR and CHR are incompletely described, complicating fair comparisons.

## Confidence
