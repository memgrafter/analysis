# Entropy-Preserving Reinforcement Learning
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.11682
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11682
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Entropy-preserving methods (REPO, ADAPO) and proper numerical handling (FP16) mitigate entropy collapse, yielding state-of-the-art performance on AppWorld and AIME benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Policy gradient reinforcement learning for language models suffers from entropy collapse, where the policy becomes overconfident, reducing exploration and long‑term performance. This paper formally analyzes entropy dynamics of leading objectives (GRPO, PPO) and identifies two main contributors: a correlation between advantages and log‑probabilities that sharpens the policy, and numerical precision issues (BF16 rounding) that invert the intended asymmetric clipping of methods like DAPO. To counter collapse, the authors propose REPO—which rescales advantages to explicitly control entropy—and ADAPO—which adaptively adjusts clipping thresholds based on measured entropy. Both maintain higher cumulative entropy throughout training. Empirical results on AppWorld and AIME show that entropy‑preserving methods consistently outperform baselines. Notably, on‑policy RLOO with careful FP16 training achieves new state‑of‑the‑art results on AppWorld (79% Test Normal, 71% Test Challenge) with Qwen-3-32B. Entropy preservation also improves sequential learning across tasks.

## Method Summary
The paper introduces two entropy‑preserving algorithms. REPO (Rescaling Advantage for Entropy Preservation) adds an adaptive multiplier ζ to the per‑token advantage, effectively injecting a KL penalty relative to a uniform reference without requiring full logits; this explicit control changes the expected entropy per update while using a control variate to reduce variance. ADAPO (Adaptive Clipping for Policy Optimization) monitors running entropy and dynamically adjusts clipping thresholds ε_low and ε_high—boosting ε_high when entropy drops and reducing ε_low otherwise—to preserve a target entropy. Both integrate seamlessly with existing advantage estimators (RLOO, GRPO). The analysis also uncovers a subtle numerical pitfall: computing log‑probabilities in BF16 (with typical FSDP2 output casting) introduces a multiplicative bias that reverses DAPO’s asymmetric clipping, accelerating entropy collapse. Switching to FP16 with fixed rounding restores the intended behavior and yields significant gains. The theoretical foundation is laid by deriving the expected entropy change (Theorem 1) and modeling clipping effects stochastically.

## Key Results
- REPO-R and ADAPO maintain higher cumulative entropy and outperform GRPO and DAPO on AppWorld and AIME.
- On-policy RLOO with FP16 (avoiding BF16 rounding bias) achieves state‑of‑the‑art on AppWorld: 79% Test Normal and 71% Test Challenge with Qwen-3-32B.
- Entropy preservation improves sequential learning: policies trained with REPO/ADAPO transfer better to new tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Entropy collapse arises from a positive correlation between advantages and centered log‑probabilities, weighted by action probability.
- **Mechanism:** The expected entropy change per update is approximately -E[A·L·π], where A is advantage, L is mean‑centered log‑probability, and π is the action probability. When advantages are positively correlated with log‑probabilities (common for calibrated base models), the update sharpens the distribution, reducing entropy.
- **Core assumption:** Action score vectors are approximately orthogonal (uᵀu' ≈ 0 for a≠a'), simplifying gradient covariance to action‑probability weighting.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] formal analysis of entropy dynamics; [section 3] Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 derive the dynamics and simplified form. Corpus evidence is weak; neighboring papers acknowledge entropy collapse but do not derive this covariance relation.
- **Break condition:** If the correlation becomes negative (e.g., via explicit entropy control or asymmetric clipping that boosts low‑prob actions), entropy can increase.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Asymmetric clipping thresholds (ε_low < ε_high) in DAPO/GSPO implicitly preserve entropy by allowing larger upward moves for low‑prob actions (positive advantage) than downward moves for high‑prob actions (negative advantage).
- **Mechanism:** Clipping bounds the policy ratio r. With ε_low < ε_high, upward movement (up to 1+ε_high) exceeds downward movement (down to 1‑ε_low). Over many stochastic updates, this asymmetry yields a net entropy increase, counteracting collapse.
- **Core assumption:** Advantages are often positive for low‑probability (unexplored but correct) actions and negative for high‑probability (overconfident) ones, making the asymmetric bias beneficial on average.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] notes asymmetric clipping helps; [section 3] analyzes DAPO/GSPO bounds and stochastic entropy increase; Agentic Entropy‑Balanced Policy Optimization also supports explicit entropy balancing.
- **Break condition:** If asymmetry is reversed (ε_low > ε_high), clipping becomes entropy‑destroying (as happens with BF16 rounding).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** BF16 quantization of log‑probabilities introduces a multiplicative upward bias in observed importance ratios r, effectively reversing DAPO’s intended asymmetry and accelerating entropy collapse.
- **Mechanism:** Rounding to BF16 causes E[r_obs | r_true] > r_true (Theorem 3), with bias proportional to r_true. For r>1 (positive advantage) the bias pushes r above the upper clip earlier, limiting probability increases; for r<1 (negative advantage) the bias pulls r toward 1, making the lower clip less restrictive. This creates an effective ε_low > ε_high regime.
- **Core assumption:** Quantization errors are independent and uniformly distributed over the rounding interval.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] identifies numerical precision as significant; [section 4.1] Theorem 3 and Figure 2a quantify bias and clipping fractions; [section 4.2] FP16 training reverses collapse (Figure 3).
- **Break condition:** Using full precision (FP32) or careful FP16 handling for log‑prob computations eliminates the bias and restores intended clipping asymmetry.

  - Why needed here: Entropy is the central concept; collapse means reduced diversity of generated outputs, harming pass@k and sequential learning. The paper treats entropy as a first‑class objective.
  - Quick check question: Why might a pre‑trained LM exhibit high pass@1 but low entropy? How does that affect subsequent RL fine‑tuning?
- **Concept: Numerical Precision (BF16 vs FP16) and Its Impact on Gradient Computations**
  - Why needed here: The paper shows numerical issues dramatically affect entropy dynamics; FP16 training with proper log handling stabilizes training and improves results. Ignoring this can cause irreproducibility.
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between BF16 and FP16, and why might FP16 yield more accurate policy gradients despite its smaller dynamic range?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: LLM policy (LoRA adapters) -> vLLM rollout workers -> Environment simulators -> Advantage estimator -> Entropy controller (REPO/ADAPO) -> FSDP2 trainer -> Mixed precision manager -> Updated policy
- **Critical path**:
  1. For each batch of contexts, sample K completions per context using current policy (throughput‑optimized vLLM).
  2. Execute completions in environments to obtain rewards (e.g., unit tests, correctness verifiers).
  3. Compute advantages via chosen estimator (e.g., RLOO: A = R - mean(others); GRPO: normalized by std).
  4. Apply entropy‑preserving modifications: REPO rescales advantages per token; ADAPO adjusts clipping thresholds based on measured entropy.
  5. Compute policy gradient loss (possibly with PPO clipping) using Cut Cross‑Entropy to avoid full logits.
  6. Backpropagate under FSDP2; update parameters with AdamW and gradient clipping.
  7. Periodically evaluate on held‑out test environments and track per‑token entropy.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **On‑policy vs off‑policy**: Strictly on‑policy (RLOO) requires synchronous gradient updates after all rollouts, limiting throughput but avoiding off‑policy drift. Off‑policy (GRPO, DAPO) allows multiple update epochs on the same trajectories, improving GPU utilization but risking entropy collapse and instability.
  - **Memory vs entropy control**: Explicit entropy bonuses require full logits over the vocabulary, increasing memory. REPO avoids this by using only sampled token log‑probs (zero extra memory), at the cost of potentially higher variance (mitigated by control variate effect).
  - **Precision vs stability**: BF16 allows larger models but introduces rounding bias that harms entropy preservation. FP16 with careful scaling improves entropy dynamics but requires gradient scaling to avoid underflow. The choice may be hardware‑dependent.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Entropy collapse**: Rapid decrease in average per‑token entropy (<20% of initial within a few iterations), accompanied by decreasing pass@k and poor sequential training performance.
  - **Numerical asymmetry bias (BF16)**: Fraction of tokens hitting upper clip (ε_high) grows while lower clip (ε_low) becomes rarely triggered, indicating reversed effective clipping.
  - **Off‑policy drift**: Importance ratios r explode beyond 1e3 without clipping; if clipping is too loose, KL divergence from old policy grows unchecked.
  - **Sparse reward stall**: When rewards are very sparse, advantages may all be zero or near zero for many batches, causing gradient starvation. Monitor advantage distribution.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline sanity check**: Run RLOO on a single AppWorld task with a tiny model (e.g., 100M parameters) for 2 iterations. Verify that entropy does not collapse catastrophically and that gradients update LoRA parameters. Check that the average reward increases.
  2. **Entropy controller test**: Run REPO‑D on the same setup. Observe that the adaptive ζ controller boosts entropy when it dips below initial and checkpoints. Manually set ζ to a large positive value and confirm entropy rises at the cost of some reward.
  3. **Numerical precision verification**: Run DAPO in BF16 and FP16 on a small‑scale run (e.g., 10 tasks, 1 iteration each). Measure the fraction of tokens hitting upper vs lower clippers; FP16 should show more lower‑clip hits and a net entropy increase, while BF16 shows the opposite.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**  
  *Can REPO/ADAPO scale to asynchronous training with thousands of GPUs without losing performance benefits?*  
  - **Basis:** The paper states entropy‑preserving methods can help weakly on‑policy methods approach strictly on‑policy performance while maintaining throughput benefits, but evidence is from small clusters (≤8 GPUs).  
  - **Why unresolved:** The throughput vs. accuracy trade‑offs in massive asynchronous systems (1000+ GPUs) remain unverified; scaling could introduce communication bottlenecks or instability.  
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Large‑scale experiments training Qwen‑3 on AppWorld with asynchronous GRPO/DAPO, with and without REPO/ADAPO, compared to synchronous RLOO. Measure throughput and final test scores.

- **Open Question 2**  
  *To what extent have numerical precision choices (BF16, FSDP2 output casting) caused false positives in reporting entropy collapse across RL‑for‑LM literature?*  
  - **Basis:** They show BF16+FSDP2 casting induces collapse while FP16+fix reverses it for DAPO, implying some prior collapse findings may be numerical artifacts.  
  - **Why unresolved:** Most existing RL‑for‑LM studies do not disclose or control numerical handling details; no systematic audit has quantified impact.  
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate key papers (e.g., GRPO, DAPO) under both original BF16+casting and fixed FP16 conditions on standard benchmarks (AppWorld, AIME). Compare entropy trajectories and final test accuracy.

- **Open Question 3**  
  *What specific aspect of preserved entropy (final value, cumulative sum, or exploration diversity) drives improved sequential learning on new tasks?*  
  - **Basis:** Figure 7 shows entropy‑preserving methods outperform GRPO in sequential training, but the paper correlates without isolating the causal factor.  
  - **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether high cumulative entropy directly transfers or merely correlates with another property (e.g., modular representations). Aggregate metrics cannot distinguish.  
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments: train policies to identical final entropy using different schedules (e.g., REPO vs. constant entropy bonus), then measure learning speed on a held‑out task. Additionally, probe first‑task policies for exploration metrics (pass@k, trajectory diversity) and correlate with transfer success.

## Limitations
- Theoretical derivations assume orthogonal action score vectors and independence; these may not hold for large language model policies.
- Empirical results are limited to AppWorld and AIME; generalization to other domains (e.g., open‑ended dialogue, control tasks) is unverified.
- BF16 rounding bias claims depend on hardware and software stacks; reproducibility across different systems is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Entropy collapse mechanism (Theorem 1, Corollary 1): Medium
- Asymmetric clipping preserves entropy: Medium–High
- BF16 quantization reverses effective clipping asymmetry: High
- REPO and ADAPO improve entropy and performance: Medium
- RLOO with FP16 handling achieves SOTA on AppWorld: Medium
