# Quantitative Approximation Rates for Group Equivariant Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20370  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20370  
- **Reference count:** 29  
- **Primary result:** Derivation of quantitative approximation rates for group‑equivariant neural networks; hard‑coded equivariance does not sacrifice asymptotic expressivity. DeepSets achieve the optimal rate \(\Theta((1/\varepsilon)^{nd/(2\alpha)})\) for permutation‑invariant functions, frame‑averaged networks scale with \(nd-\dim(E(d))\), and general bi‑Lipschitz invariant models scale with the Minkowski dimension of the quotient space.

## Executive Summary
This paper derives explicit quantitative approximation rates for several group‑equivariant neural network architectures, focusing on \(\alpha\)-Hölder functions defined on high‑dimensional, group‑invariant domains. The core method reduces the approximation problem to one over lower‑dimensional quotient spaces formed by the group action, enabling a more parameter‑efficient representation. Using shifted partition‑of‑unity constructions and deep ReLU networks, the authors achieve optimal rates that match standard non‑equivariant networks for permutation‑invariant tasks and show that hard‑coded symmetries (permutations, rigid motions) come at no asymptotic cost to expressivity.

## Method Summary
The construction for DeepSets combines a shifted partition‑of‑unity on the input domain with digit‑encoding of cell counts via base‑\((n+1)\) representations. A ReLU‑based bit‑extractor decodes these counts, and a coloring‑inspired network interpolates across the resulting count vectors to approximate the target \(\alpha\)-Hölder function. The depth and width scale logarithmically and polynomially in the discretization parameter \(m \sim 1/\varepsilon\), yielding total parameters proportional to \((1/\varepsilon)^{nd/(2\alpha)}\) up to log factors.

For invariance to both permutations and rigid motions (\(E(d) \times S_n\)), the authors employ frame‑averaging over a carefully chosen subgroup (e.g., rotations). A permutation‑invariant backbone (DeepSets) processes each frame‑transformed point cloud, and the outputs are averaged. Under conditions that the frame is continuous away from a singular set of measure zero, the approximation rate inherits the quotient dimension \(nd - \dim(E(d))\). For more general groups, a bi‑Lipschitz invariant embedding maps orbits into a Euclidean space of dimension comparable to the Minkowski dimension of the quotient, after which a standard MLP is applied.

## Key Results
- For permutation‑invariant \(f\) on \(n\) points in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), DeepSets achieve the optimal parameter rate \(\Theta((1/\varepsilon)^{nd/(2\alpha)})\), matching unrestricted ReLU MLPs.
- For functions invariant to permutations and rigid motions (\(E(d) \times S_n\)), frame‑averaged networks (e.g., rotation frames applied to a DeepSets backbone) attain the optimal rate scaling with \(nd - \dim(E(d))\).
- For any group with a bi‑Lipschitz invariant embedding, the rate scales with the Minkowski dimension of the quotient space; hard‑coded equivariance never reduces asymptotic expressivity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Dimensional reduction via quotient spaces
- **Claim:** Invariant/equivariant approximation reduces to approximating functions on a lower‑dimensional quotient space.  
- **Mechanism:** Group symmetries partition the input space into orbits; the target function is constant along each orbit. The covering number of the quotient space scales with the ambient dimension minus the group's dimension (or Minkowski dimension), enabling more efficient representation.  
- **Core assumption:** The target is invariant/equivariant and \(\alpha\)-Hölder continuous.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: "The core method reduces approximation of invariant/equivariant \(\alpha\)-Hölder functions on high‑dimensional sets to approximation over lower‑dimensional quotient spaces"  
  - [section]: Page 9 Proposition 1 gives covering number bounds for quotient spaces.  
  - [corpus]: Common in related work on dimension reduction via symmetry.  
- **Break condition:** The group action must be free/proper; otherwise the quotient dimension may not reduce as expected (e.g., non‑free \(O(d)\) action).

### Mechanism 2: Neural construction for quotient representation
- **Claim:** Explicit deep ReLU networks can approximate quotient‑space functions at optimal rates.  
- **Mechanism:** Build networks that first discretize the input via shifted partition‑of‑unity, encode point counts per cell using base‑\((n+1)\) digits, extract counts with ReLU bit‑extractors, and interpolate across count vectors using color‑based networks.  
- **Core assumption:** Input domain is bounded; number of points \(n\) is fixed; function \(\alpha\)-Hölder.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: "constructs explicit deep ReLU networks to achieve these rates"  
  - [section]: Theorem 2 (Pages 11‑18) provides the full construction for DeepSets; Section 3.4 generalizes to non‑invariant functions.  
  - [corpus]: Construction resembles Kolmogorov‑Arnold superposition and Han et al. (2022), adapted to neural‑realizable form.  
- **Break condition:** Requires \(n\) fixed and bounded; intermediate layers have exponential width in discretization resolution \(m\) before dimension reduction.

### Mechanism 3: Frame‑averaged networks inherit quotient rates
- **Claim:** Combining an invariant backbone (e.g., DeepSets) with a frame‑averaging operator over a smaller group (e.g., rotations) yields optimal rates scaling with the combined quotient dimension.  
- **Mechanism:** Frame averaging maps input to transformed versions that lie in a subspace where the backbone operates. The backbone’s approximation transfers via the averaging operator, which is continuous on domains avoiding singularities (e.g., distinct singular values for SVD frames).  
- **Core assumption:** Frame‑averaging is well‑defined and continuous on the domain; backbone achieves desired rates on the transformed subspace.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: "frame‑averaged networks ... also achieve the optimal rate scaling with \(nd-\dim(E(d))\)"  
  - [section]: Section 4.2 (Pages 35‑38) for rotation frames; Proposition 18 formalizes.  
  - [corpus]: Frame averaging discussed by Pozdnyakov and Ceriotti (2023); Dym et al. (2024) show continuous canonicalization is impossible → motivates weighted frames.  
- **Break condition:** Frame may be discontinuous at boundary cases (e.g., equal singular values); requires domain restriction (e.g., distinct singular values) or use of continuous frames (not analyzed here).

### Concept: Hölder Continuity and Modulus of Continuity
- **Why needed:** Smoothness measured by the \(\alpha\)‑Hölder condition directly determines approximation rate; the bound depends on \(\alpha\) and the modulus of continuity.  
- **Quick check:** If \(|f|_\alpha \le 1\) with \(\alpha=1/2\), then for \(\|x-y\| = 1/4\), we have \(|f(x)-f(y)| \le (1/4)^{1/2} = 1/2\).

### Concept: Covering Numbers and Minkowski Dimension
- **Why needed:** The number of parameters in the network scales with the covering number of the quotient space. The key insight is that covering numbers of quotient spaces can be much smaller than those of the ambient space.  
- **Quick check:** If \(N(K,r) \le C r^{-d}\) for all \(r\), then the Minkowski dimension of \(K\) is \(d\). The expected approximation rate for an \(\alpha\)-Hölder function on such a set would be \(\Theta((1/\varepsilon)^{d/(2\alpha)})\) parameters.

### Concept: Deep ReLU Networks and Construction Techniques
- **Why needed:** The paper constructs explicit ReLU networks; understanding how to implement piecewise‑linear functions, digit extraction, and median/selection is crucial for the construction.  
- **Quick check:** Approximating \(x \cdot y\) on \([-M,M]^2\) can be done with a two‑layer ReLU network of width \(O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})\)? Actually one can use the identity \(xy = ((x+y)^2 - (x-y)^2)/4\), and approximate squares with ReLU networks of depth 2 and width \(O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})\). More directly, a network with two hidden layers and \(O(1/\varepsilon)\) neurons suffices for uniform error \(\varepsilon\).

### Concept: Gram–Schmidt Stability and Orthogonalization
- **Why needed:** In the \(O(d)\)‑invariant case, the Gram–Schmidt process maps to a subspace of reduced dimension; stability ensures the map can be approximated by neural networks.  
- **Quick check:** If \(u_1,u_2\) are orthonormal and \(v_1,v_2\) are approximations with \(\|u_i - v_i\| \le \eta\), then the projection operator \(p(x;u_1,u_2)\) (e.g., expressing \(x\) in the orthonormal basis) changes by at most \(O(\eta)\); thus \(\|p(x;u)-p(x;v)\| \le C\eta\) for some constant \(C\) depending on \(\|x\|\).

### Concept: Frame Averaging and Invariant Representations
- **Why needed:** Many geometric learning models use frame averaging to achieve invariance to continuous groups; the paper shows these inherit rates from permutation‑invariant backbones.  
- **Quick check:** A "frame" is a set of transformations that cover the group in a way that each output is averaged. Example: For 2D rotations, a frame of \(k\) equally spaced rotations (e.g., \(0°, 90°, 180°, 270°\)) applied to the input before a permutation‑invariant backbone, then average the outputs.

### Concept: Bi‑Lipschitz Invariant Embeddings
- **Why needed:** For groups without a convenient frame, an invariant embedding with bounded distortion can reduce the problem to standard approximation.  
- **Quick check:** An invariant embedding \(E: \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}^m\) is bi‑Lipschitz on the quotient if there exist \(L_1, L_2 > 0\) such that \(L_1 \, d_{quot}([x],[y]) \le \|E(x)-E(y)\| \le L_2 \, d_{quot}([x],[y])\). This preserves metric structure of orbits.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text flow):**  
Input point cloud \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\) -> Choose backbone:  
- Permutation‑invariant case: DeepSets (pointwise \(\phi\), sum, \(\rho\)) -> Output.  
- Permutation‑equivariant case: Sumformer/Transformer (attention yields same sums) -> Output per point.  
If also invariant to a continuous group (e.g., rotations), insert a frame‑averaging module: \(X\) -> set of transformed point clouds \(\{g \cdot X\}_{g \in \text{frame}}\) -> apply same DeepSets backbone to each -> average outputs.  
Alternatively, for a general bi‑Lipschitz invariant model: \(X\) -> fixed invariant embedding \(E\) (e.g., sorted coordinates, max‑filtering) -> standard MLP -> Output.  
All \(\phi\) and \(\rho\) are implemented as deep ReLU networks with widths/depths from the construction.

**Critical path:**  
1. Identify symmetry group (permutation only? permutation+rotation? general?).  
2. Choose architecture: DeepSets for pure permutation invariance; Sumformer/Transformer for permutation equivariance; frame‑averaged DeepSets for combined permutations and rigid motions; bi‑Lipschitz embedding for other groups.  
3. Determine required network size from the rate formula: for quotient dimension \(d_{\text{intr}}\), parameters \(\sim (1/\varepsilon)^{d_{\text{intr}}/(2\alpha)}\) (ignoring logs).  
4. Implement the construction: follow Theorem 2 (shifted partition‑of‑unity, digit encoding, ReLU bit‑extractors, coloring‑based interpolation). Use existing implementations for polynomials, bit extraction, median.  
5. For frame averaging, implement a stable frame (e.g., SVD‑based or simple rotation frames) and apply backbone to each transformed copy; average.  
6. Handle edge cases: restrict domain to avoid frame singularities (e.g., distinct singular values) or add jitter; ensure boundedness assumptions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Frame vs. bi‑Lipschitz embedding:** Frame averaging gives exact invariance but may be discontinuous at degenerate inputs; bi‑Lipschitz embeddings are continuous if well‑conditioned but may only approximate invariance.  
- **Permutation backbone:** DeepSets is simple but lacks pairwise interactions; Sumformer/Transformer can match its rate while modeling interactions, at higher computational cost.  
- **Width vs. depth:** Construction uses moderate width \(O(d n)\) but depth logarithmic in \(1/\varepsilon\); for hardware efficiency one might trade width for depth.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Rate mismatch: Empirical error decays slower than \((1/\varepsilon)^{d_{\text{intr}}/(2\alpha)}\). Check that the target is truly invariant and \(\alpha\)-Hölder, and that the quotient covering number is as expected.  
- Frame discontinuities: Loss spikes when inputs have degenerate configurations (e.g., collinear points in 2D). Workaround: use continuous frames or restrict domain.  
- Over‑parameterization not helping: Increasing capacity yields no improvement; bottleneck may be the embedding (e.g., DeepSets may struggle with certain permutation‑invariant functions with strong pairwise dependencies, though asymptotically it should).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Permutation‑invariant synthetic test:** Target \(f(X) = \sum_i \|x_i\|^2\) (\(\alpha=1\)) on \([0,1]^{d \times n}\). Train DeepSets and MLP with equal parameter counts; both should achieve error scaling like \((1/\varepsilon)^{nd/2}\).  
2. **Rigid motion invariance on 2D point clouds:** Target \(f(X) = \sum_{i<j} \|x_i - x_j\|^2\) (invariant to translations/rotations). Test frame‑averaged DeepSets with a 2‑rotation frame vs. MLP on raw coordinates. Expected scaling exponent \((2n-3)/(2\alpha)\) for \(d=2, n\ge3\). Avoid inputs with degenerate frames (e.g., enforce distinct norms).  
3. **Intrinsic dimension reduction:** Sample point clouds from a low‑dimensional manifold (e.g., points lie on a 1D curve in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)). For a permutation‑invariant \(f\), the effective intrinsic dimension should be \(n \times\) (manifold dimension). Verify error scaling matches \((1/\varepsilon)^{n \cdot d_{\text{curve}}/(2\alpha)}\) and fails to match \(nd/(2\alpha)\) if invariance is ignored.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Derive explicit approximation rates for popular rotation‑equivariant models (EGNN, Dimenet, Vector Neurons, MACE).  
- Establish provable gaps in approximation rates between different equivariant architectures (e.g., DeepSets vs. frame‑averaged models).  
- Determine whether asymptotic approximation theory (ignoring constants) is sufficient to explain performance differences at realistic scales, or if a refined analysis with explicit constants is necessary.  
- Identify domain restrictions under which models like EGNN, Dimenet, etc., are provably universal for approximating invariant/equivariant functions.

## Limitations
- Analysis restricted to \(\alpha\)-Hölder functions on bounded, group‑invariant domains with fixed \(n\); may not reflect real‑world data distributions.  
- Constants in the network construction (width, depth, interpolation bounds) are not explicitly quantified, leading to potentially very large hidden layers.  
- Frame‑averaging requires frame continuity and fails on singular configurations (e.g., collinear points); the paper restricts to a full‑measure set but practical implementations need careful handling.  
- Only ReLU activations are considered; other nonlinearities are not covered.  
- Results concern uniform approximation, not generalization; Minkowski dimension bounds may be loose, leaving constant‑factor gaps.

## Confidence
- **Permutation‑invariant DeepSets rate:** High – explicit construction matches known lower bounds.  
- **Frame‑averaged networks:** Medium – relies on frame continuity and domain avoidance of singularities; constants not tightly controlled.  
- **General bi‑Lipschitz models:** Medium – Minkowski dimension may be a loose bound; embedding choice affects tightness and may introduce additional constants.
