# Attribution-Guided Model Rectification of Unreliable Neural Network Behaviors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.15656  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15656  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: With as few as one cleansed sample, the method reduces attack success rate from 99.94% to 1.34% on CIFAR-10 while preserving high accuracy, outperforming fine-tuning and other baselines.

## Executive Summary
Neural networks often exhibit unreliable behaviors such as backdoors and spurious correlations, which typically require expensive data cleaning and retraining to address. This work proposes an attribution-guided rank-one model editing framework for rectification. The approach is built on two key properties—rectifiability and span-aligned control—that enable correction using minimal cleansed data without sacrificing overall performance. It further introduces an attribution-guided layer localization method to identify the layer most responsible for the unreliable behavior, overcoming layer-dependent editability bottlenecks. A dynamic editing loop iteratively localizes and edits layers within a performance budget. Experiments across neural Trojans, spurious correlations, and feature leakage show drastic mitigation of unreliability (e.g., lowering attack success rate to 1.34% on CIFAR-10 with one sample) while maintaining accuracy, with broad applicability even to real-world skin lesion analysis.

## Method Summary
The rectification method assumes the susceptible model has been trained on both clean and corrupted samples, so that corrupted features lie within the span of training keys. Given a small cleansed dataset, the method computes key statistics \(C = KK^T\) from the activations of a selected layer. For a corrupted-clean pair \((\tilde{x}, x)\), a rank-one update \(W \gets W + \Lambda (C^{-1}k^*)^T\) is applied to the weights of a chosen layer, where \(k^*\) is the corrupted key and \(\Lambda\) aligns the corrupted activation toward the clean target. To choose which layer to edit, integrated gradients are computed from \(\tilde{x}\) to \(x\) for each layer, and the layer whose attribution projected onto \(C^{-1}k^*\) is largest is selected. A dynamic controller iteratively repeats this localization and editing, updating the gap \(\delta^*\) between clean and corrupted outputs and monitoring performance degradation \(\epsilon^*\), until \(\delta^*\) falls below a target or \(\epsilon^*\) exceeds a budget.

## Key Results
- **Drastic reduction in attack success rate**: On CIFAR-10 with a neural Trojan, ASR drops from 99.94% to 1.34% using just one cleansed sample, while overall accuracy remains above 93%.
- **Effective mitigation of spurious correlations**: The method significantly reduces the effect of spurious features on predictions in multi-environment settings, outperforming fine-tuning and other baselines with minimal data.
- **Broad applicability**: Demonstrated success across backdoor attacks, spurious correlations, and feature leakage, including a real-world skin lesion dataset, with consistent retention of clean accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Rectifiability and Span‑Aligned Control
*Claim*: Rectification succeeds with minimal cleansed samples because corrupted features lie in the span of training keys and the model already learns a mapping from corrupted to clean features.  
*Mechanism*: Since the model was trained on corrupted samples, their keys are included in the training set; hence \(C = KK^T\) captures the corrupted key. The rank‑one update \(W' = W + \Lambda (C^{-1}k^*)^T\) uses \(C^{-1}\) to decorrelate the edit from existing key‑value associations, preventing disruption. The small cleansed set refines the pre‑learned mapping without requiring large sample budgets.  
*Core assumption*: Corrupted features are in‑span relative to training keys and the model has low training error on corrupted samples.  
*Evidence anchors*: Abstract mentions rectifiability and span‑aligned control; Section 4.2 and Propositions 4.1–4.2 formalize properties; neighboring knowledge‑editing works (e.g., [3]) do not address these for unreliability.  
*Break condition*: Fails if corrupted features are out‑of‑span (e.g., novel triggers) or if the model did not learn the corrupted‑to‑clean mapping (high training error).

### Mechanism 2: Attribution‑Guided Layer Localization
*Claim*: Integrated gradients combined with the editing direction correctly identify the layer most responsible for unreliability, overcoming layer‑dependent editability.  
*Mechanism*: For each layer \(l\), compute integrated gradients \(M_l\) from corrupted to clean input along a linear path. The score \(\|M_l (C^{-1}k^*)^T\|_F\) approximates the first‑order reduction in the prediction gap achievable by editing that layer. The layer with maximum score is selected.  
*Core assumption*: Integrated gradients faithfully attribute output change to layer features; projection onto \(C^{-1}k^*\) correlates with the true gradient of the gap w.r.t. editing.  
*Evidence anchors*: Abstract states attribution‑guided layer localization; Section 5.1 derives estimator \(\hat{G}_l\); neighboring attribution papers (e.g., [44,55]) focus on explanation, not layer selection for editing.  
*Break condition*: Noisy or saturated activations yield uninformative \(M_l\); ill‑conditioned \(C\) amplifies noise in \(C^{-1}k^*\), mis‑ranking layers.

### Mechanism 3: Dynamic Rectification Framework
*Claim*: Iteratively localizing and editing layers within a performance budget yields better rectification than static one‑shot editing because layer responsibilities shift during editing.  
*Mechanism*: The algorithm loops while the gap \(\delta^* > \delta\) and performance degradation \(\epsilon^* \leq \epsilon\): compute attributions for all layers, select the top‑scoring layer \(l^*\), apply rank‑one editing for \(T\) steps, recompute \(\delta^*\) and \(\epsilon^*\). This adapts to changing layer contributions.  
*Core assumption*: The layer contributing most to unreliability changes as the model is edited; the performance budget preserves overall accuracy.  
*Evidence anchors*: Abstract mentions dynamic framework; Section 5.2 and Algorithm 1; Table 1 and Fig. 4 show dynamic outperforms static.  
*Break condition*: Budget \(\epsilon\) too tight halts loop prematurely; unstable scores across iterations cause oscillation; repeated editing on same layer overfits to the single pair.

## Foundational Learning
- **Rank‑one model editing**  
  *Why needed*: Core operation that modifies weights to associate a corrupted feature (key) with a clean activation (value). Understanding the constrained least‑squares solution and \(C^{-1}\)’s role in preserving existing associations is essential.  
  *Quick check*: In \(W' = W + \Lambda (C^{-1}k^*)^T\), what does \(C^{-1}\) achieve and why is it necessary?

- **Integrated gradients attribution**  
  *Why needed*: Layer localization relies on integrated gradients to quantify each layer’s contribution to the output change from corrupted to clean input. Correct computation of the integral along a path and the completeness axiom is required.  
  *Quick check*: Why does summing \(M_l\) over all features of a layer equal the total output change \(f(x)-f(\tilde{x})\)? Which path is used and why is the reference set to \(\tilde{x}\)?

- **Dynamic editing loop and budget constraints**  
  *Why needed*: The method iteratively selects layers and edits within a performance budget. Grasping the loop conditions and the trade‑off between robustness and accuracy is necessary for deployment.  
  *Quick check*: What are the two conditions in the while loop of Algorithm 1, and what does each control? How is the model updated after each edit?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Model -> Attribution Engine -> Layer Scorer -> Layer Selector -> Rank-one Editor -> Model update; Controller manages loop; Key Statistics Buffer provides \(C\); Evaluation Module computes metrics; Cleansed data feeds Key Statistics Buffer.

- **Critical path**:
  1. Input: trained model \(f\), cleansed sample \(x\), corrupted sample \(\tilde{x}\), performance budget \(\epsilon\), target gap \(\delta\).
  2. Compute initial gap \(\delta^* = f(x) - f(\tilde{x})\) and degradation \(\epsilon^* = 0\).
  3. While \(\delta^* > \delta\) and \(\epsilon^* \leq \epsilon\):
     a. Run Attribution Engine on all layers to get \(M_l\).
     b. Compute scores \(\hat{G}_l = \|Z M_l (C^{-1}k^*)^T\|_F\) with \(Z = C^{-1/2}\) and \(k^* = f^{l-1}(\tilde{x})\).
     c. Select \(l^* = \argmax \hat{G}_l\).
     d. Apply Rank-one Editor to layer \(l^*\) for \(T\) steps: \(W_l \gets W_l + \eta (C^{-1}k^*)^T (v^* - W_l k^*)\).
     e. Update model \(f\).
     f. Recompute \(\delta^*\) and \(\epsilon^*\) using clean validation set.
  4. Return rectified model \(f\).

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Attribution method: Integrated gradients vs. simpler gradients; IG is more accurate but costlier; linear path may miss non‑linearities.
  - Key statistics \(C\): Larger cleansed set improves accuracy but conflicts with minimal‑data goal; regularization (e.g., \(\lambda I\)) needed when data scarce.
  - Editing hyperparameters: Step size \(\eta\), steps \(T\); larger \(T\) may converge faster but risk over‑editing.
  - Budget parameters \(\epsilon\) and \(\delta\): Tight \(\epsilon\) preserves accuracy but may limit rectification; loose \(\epsilon\) may harm accuracy.
  - Dynamic vs. static: Dynamic adapts to shifting layer responsibilities but requires repeated evaluation; static is simpler but may fail if optimal layer changes.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Immediate large accuracy drop after first edit: \(\eta\) too large or \(\epsilon\) too loose; reduce \(\eta\) or tighten \(\epsilon\).
  - No ASR reduction after several iterations: localization failing (scores meaningless) or editing insufficient (T too small); check if selected layer changes.
  - Oscillation in layer selection: unstable attributions; smooth scores or increase cleansed data for \(C\).
  - Overfitting to single pair: clean accuracy degrades significantly; editing too specific; increase data for \(C\) or add regularization.
  - High variance across random pairs: method sensitive to pair choice; consider multiple pairs or ensemble editing.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline replication**: Replicate Table 1 (CIFAR‑10 backdoor) with \(n=1\) cleansed sample. Verify ASR drops from ~99.9% to <2% while overall accuracy stays >93%. If not, debug attribution and layer selection.
  2. **Localization validation**: For a fixed Trojaned model, individually edit each of the 8 convolutional layers and measure ASR reduction. Rank layers by effectiveness. Then run proposed localization on 100 random pairs; compute recall of selected layers in top‑3 effective layers. Expect recall >80% (Table 10).
  3. **Dynamic vs static ablation**: Compare dynamic rectification (proposed) with static (edit final layer only) and random layer editing, using same budget and \(n=1\). Evaluate ASR reduction and overall accuracy. Expect dynamic to achieve lower ASR with comparable overall accuracy.

## Limitations
- **Rectifiability assumption**: Requires corrupted features to be in‑span of training keys; fails for novel triggers not seen during training.
- **Attribution robustness**: Integrated gradients can be noisy or path‑dependent; \(C^{-1}k^*\) estimation may be poor with minimal data, leading to mis‑localization.
- **Dynamic loop stability**: May terminate early under tight budgets; scores can oscillate across iterations; repeated editing risks overfitting to a single pair.
- **Generalization**: Rank‑one updates may be insufficient for complex, multi‑faceted corruptions; computational overhead from repeated attribution and editing.

## Next Checks
1. **Out‑of‑span corruption test**: Train Trojaned models with trigger patterns absent during training. Apply rectification with one cleansed sample. Expect failure to reduce ASR, confirming the span assumption.
2. **Attribution robustness study**: Across architectures and corruption types, compare layer localization using integrated gradients, standard gradients, and Grad‑CAM against actual editing effectiveness (ASR reduction after editing each layer). Quantify correlation to validate scoring heuristic.
3. **Dynamic vs. static ablation**: Systematically vary cleansed sample count (1, 5, 10) and performance budget \(\epsilon\). Measure ASR reduction and clean accuracy stability. Determine if dynamic consistently outperforms static and identify parameter regimes where oscillation occurs.
