# LLMs as Signal Detectors: Sensitivity, Bias, and the Temperature-Criterion Analogy
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14893
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14893
- **Reference count:** 5
- **Primary result:** Temperature scaling in LLMs does not act as a pure response bias shift but simultaneously increases discriminative sensitivity (AUC) and shifts the response criterion, falsifying the direct psychophysical analogy.

## Executive Summary
This study applies the full parametric framework of Signal Detection Theory (SDT) to large language models (LLMs) performing factual question answering. It decomposes model performance into sensitivity (ability to discriminate correct from incorrect) and response criterion (bias toward answering). The core test examined whether adjusting the sampling temperature functions analogously to manipulating payoff matrices in human psychophysics, which should only shift the criterion. The results falsify this analogy for the models tested: increasing temperature increased both sensitivity (AUC) and the conservatism of the criterion. Furthermore, all models exhibited unequal-variance evidence distributions, with instruct-tuned models showing more extreme asymmetry than typical human memory data. The SDT decomposition reveals distinct sensitivity-bias trade-offs between models (e.g., Mistral vs. Llama-3-Instruct) that standard calibration metrics like Expected Calibration Error (ECE) fail to capture, demonstrating SDT's superior diagnostic power for separating fundamental capability from policy.

## Method Summary
The study conducted 168,000 factual question-answering trials across three LLMs (Mistral, Llama-3-Base, Llama-3-Instruct). For each trial, the model's output distribution was used to construct a continuous "evidence" measure (e.g., based on logits or token probabilities). Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were generated by sweeping a decision criterion across this evidence continuum. These ROCs were fit with the parametric SDT model to estimate sensitivity (dʹ or AUC) and response criterion (c), while also testing the equal-variance assumption by estimating the z-ROC slope. Temperature was systematically varied to test if its effect aligned with a pure criterion shift.

## Key Results
*   Standard calibration metrics (e.g., ECE) conflate a model's discriminative ability (sensitivity) with its response policy (bias).
*   Increasing sampling temperature simultaneously **increases** discriminative sensitivity (AUC ↑ 0.07–0.15) and **shifts** the response criterion toward greater conservatism (c ↑), falsifying the temperature-as-pure-criterion-shift analogy.
*   All tested models exhibit significantly unequal-variance evidence distributions (z-ROC slopes = 0.52–0.84), with instruct-tuned models showing more extreme asymmetry than base models or typical human memory experiments.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The work is valid because it imports a mature, quantitative framework from cognitive psychology (Signal Detection Theory) that explicitly separates two orthogonal components of decision-making: sensitivity (dʹ) and criterion (c). By fitting parametric models to ROC curves derived from model outputs, it moves beyond scalar calibration scores that blend these factors. The falsification of the temperature-criterion analogy demonstrates that decoding stochasticity (temperature) does not merely shift a fixed decision threshold but actively alters the underlying representation's signal-to-noise ratio, a finding with implications for how we control generation diversity.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Signal Detection Theory (SDT):** A framework for modeling decision-making under uncertainty, separating an agent's ability to discriminate signals from noise (sensitivity, dʹ) from their willingness to respond (criterion, c). *Why needed: Provides the mathematical lens to decompose LLM performance beyond aggregate accuracy or calibration.*
*   **Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve:** A plot of hit rate vs. false alarm rate as the decision criterion varies. The area under the curve (AUC) measures discriminative performance independent of any single criterion. *Why needed: Empirically reveals the full trade-off between sensitivity and bias.*
*   **criterion (c):** The location of the decision threshold on the evidence continuum. Liberal criteria (low c) yield more hits and false alarms; conservative criteria (high c) yield fewer of both. *Why needed: Quantifies response bias/policy separate from capability.*
*   **dʹ (d-prime):** A normalized measure of sensitivity, the separation between signal and noise distribution means in standard deviation units. *Why needed: A model-agnostic metric of core discriminative power.*
*   **Unequal-Variance SDT:** The standard SDT assumption that noise and signal distributions have equal variance is often violated. The slope of the z-ROC (<1) quantifies the ratio of noise to signal standard deviations. *Why needed: Explains why simple ROC transformations fail and captures a key property of model evidence distributions.*
*   **Expected Calibration Error (ECE):** A popular metric that bins predictions by confidence and measures the gap between confidence and accuracy. *Why needed to contrast: Fails to separate changes in sensitivity from changes in bias, which SDT disentangles.*

**Quick Check:** For a binary classifier, if you only adjust the classification threshold, what happens to the ROC curve? (Answer: It moves you along a fixed ROC curve; AUC does not change.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Input Question -> LLM Forward Pass -> Output Token Logits/Probabilities -> Evidence Extraction (e.g., sum of correct token probs) -> Decision Rule (criterion applied to evidence) -> Binary Response (Answer / Abstain)

**Critical Path:** The study's core path is: (1) Generate model outputs for a factual QA dataset. (2) Define a scalar "evidence" value from the output distribution. (3) Apply a series of thresholds to this evidence to label samples as "positive" (answer given) or "negative" (abstain/incorrect). (4) Compute hit/false alarm rates for each threshold to build an empirical ROC. (5) Fit parametric SDT models (equal/unequal variance) to the ROC to estimate dʹ/AUC and c.

**Design Tradeoffs:** The primary tradeoff is the **mapping from generative output to SDT's "evidence" continuum**. The paper uses aggregated token probabilities or logits, but this assumes these scores form a monotonic, interval-scale representation of the model's internal "belief strength." Alternative mappings (e.g., based on entropy, specific decoding strategies) could yield different SDT parameters. Another tradeoff is **defining "signal" and "noise"**: here, "signal" is a correct answer and "noise" is an incorrect one, but for open-ended tasks, this binary framing is non-trivial.

**Failure Signatures:** 1) If the ROC curve is non-monotonic or non-concave, the parametric SDT fit is invalid, suggesting the chosen evidence metric does not support a unidimensional decision process. 2) If temperature changes only move points along a fixed ROC (AUC constant), the pure criterion shift analogy holds. 3) If z-ROC slope is not significantly <1, the unequal-variance model is unnecessary.

**First Three Experiments:**
1.  **Mapping Validation:** For one model, compare 3 different "evidence" extraction methods (e.g., max token prob, sum of top-k probs, negative log-likelihood of gold answer). Fit SDT to each and compare stability of dʹ and c estimates across temperature.
2.  **Task Generalization Test:** Replicate the full analysis on a non-factual QA task (e.g., logical entailment) using the same models. Compare whether temperature's dual effect on sensitivity and criterion persists.
3.  **Distributional Stress Test:** For the factual QA task, perform a formal model comparison between equal-variance SDT, unequal-variance SDT, and a non-parametric ROC smooth (e.g., using kernel density estimates). Use AIC/BIC to determine the best-fitting representation of the model's evidence distributions.

## Limitations
*   Findings are limited to a small set of three specific LLMs (Mistral, Llama variants) on a single task type (factual QA), raising questions about generalizability to other architectures, training methods, or domains.
*   The mapping of generative outputs to SDT's "evidence" continuum (e.g., using logit sums) is an assumption that may not hold universally across model types or decoding strategies.
*   While unequal-variance is confirmed, other SDT assumptions (e.g., Gaussian evidence distributions) are not formally tested, and external validity to real-world, high-stakes deployment scenarios is unestablished.

## Confidence
*   **Calibration metrics (e.g., ECE) conflate discriminative ability and response bias:** **High**. Direct empirical decomposition across thousands of trials demonstrates this conflation.
*   **Temperature scaling functions as a pure criterion shift (analogy to human psychophysics):** **Low (Falsified)**. Core result shows temperature changes both sensitivity (AUC) and criterion (c), but demonstrated only for these specific models and task.
*   **All models exhibit unequal-variance evidence distributions (z-ROC slopes <1):** **High**. Quantified slopes (0.52–0.84) are explicitly reported and differ from typical human memory data.
*   **SDT decomposition reveals distinct model positions in sensitivity-bias space indiscernible via calibration metrics:** **High**. Direct comparison shows calibration metrics fail to separate models like Mistral (high dʹ, liberal c) from Llama-3-Instruct (lower dʹ, conservative c).
*   **Instruct models show more extreme evidence asymmetry than base models:** **Medium**. Pattern is clear in reported slopes, but sample size (n=3) is small for a broad comparative claim.

## Next Checks
1.  **Expand model and developer diversity:** Replicate the SDT analysis with a broader set of at least 10 models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, open-weight community models) to test if the temperature dual-effect and unequal-variance findings hold across architectures and training paradigms.
2.  **Cross-task generalization:** Apply the identical SDT framework to at least three fundamentally different task domains (e.g., logical reasoning, safety/alignment probes, creative text generation) to determine if the sensitivity-bias decomposition and temperature effects are task-dependent or universal.
3.  **Distributional assumption stress-test:** For each model, formally compare the fit of equal-variance SDT, unequal-variance SDT, and non-parametric ROC analysis (e.g., using likelihood ratio tests or AIC) on the 168k+ trials. This validates whether Gaussian evidence distributions are adequate or if more complex signal/noise models are required.
