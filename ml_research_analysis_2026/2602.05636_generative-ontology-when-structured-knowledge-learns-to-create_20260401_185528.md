# Generative Ontology: When Structured Knowledge Learns to Create

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.05636
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05636
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** A framework that encodes domain ontologies as executable Pydantic schemas to constrain LLM generation, using a multi-agent pipeline and RAG, producing structurally valid tabletop game designs with significantly improved creative quality.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Generative Ontology, a framework that transforms a structured domain ontology into an active generative grammar. By encoding expert knowledge as executable Pydantic schemas, the system constrains large language models (LLMs) to produce formally valid artifacts. This core mechanism is combined with a multi-agent pipeline—where specialized LLM agents assume roles like "Mechanics Architect" and "Balance Critic"—and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounded in exemplars. In an ablation study with 120 tabletop game designs, the full pipeline significantly improved creative quality dimensions (e.g., fun, strategic depth) and eliminated structural errors. Benchmarking against 20 published games showed structural parity but a persistent gap in certain creative dimensions, suggesting the need for iterative human-AI collaboration.

## Method Summary
The method compiles a domain ontology (e.g., for board games) into a Pydantic schema defining classes, fields, and validation rules. Generation is performed via DSPy, which enforces the schema as a typed output. A multi-agent pipeline decomposes the design task: a Mechanics Architect generates core rules using RAG from a filtered corpus of existing games; a Theme Weaver and Component Designer flesh out the theme and parts; parallel Balance Critic and Fun Judge agents evaluate the draft, triggering a Refinement Agent if needed. An ablation study compared four conditions (raw LLM, schema-only, single-agent with full ontology, full multi-agent pipeline) across 10 prompts, generating 3 designs each (N=120). A separate LLM-based evaluator ("Design Coach") scored designs on seven creative dimensions, with its reliability assessed via test-retest.

## Key Results
*   The full multi-agent pipeline (C4) with schema and RAG dramatically improved creative quality versus simpler baselines, with large effect sizes for fun (Cohen's d=1.12) and strategic depth (d=1.59, p<.001).
*   Schema enforcement alone eliminated structural errors with an enormous effect (d=4.78), proving the grammar's efficacy for formal validity.
*   Against 20 published board games, AI-generated designs achieved structural parity but scored lower on fun (7–8 vs. 8–9, d=1.86), with tension/drama and social interaction rated at parity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Ontology as Executable Grammar
*   **Claim:** Encoding domain knowledge as executable Pydantic schemas transforms a passive ontology into an active generative grammar, enabling structurally valid LLM outputs.
*   **Mechanism:** Ontology concepts (e.g., Game, Mechanism) map directly to Pydantic `BaseModel` classes with typed fields, enums, and validation rules. The LLM must conform to this schema during generation, eliminating structural hallucinations. The schema's `Field` descriptions act as a deterministic contract.
*   **Core Assumption:** Domain experts can articulate a complete, unambiguous vocabulary and dependency structure (e.g., "deck building requires cards") that captures all validity conditions for a functional artifact.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract: "encodes domain knowledge as executable Pydantic schemas that constrain LLM generation." Section 4: "The schema is the ontology made executable." Ablation: structural errors drop (d=4.78).
*   **Break Condition:** If validity constraints are not fully codifiable in a schema (e.g., "fun" is emergent from playtesting), the grammar produces formally valid but experientially deficient artifacts.

### Mechanism 2: Multi-Agent Specialization
*   **Claim:** A multi-agent pipeline with domain-specialized LLMs and role-specific "anxieties" improves creative quality by decomposing the generation task and preventing shallow, agreeable outputs.
*   **Mechanism:** Generation is split among agents (Mechanics Architect, Theme Weaver, Balance Critic, etc.), each with a narrow ontological domain and a professional "anxiety" (e.g., "Is there meaningful player agency?"). This architectural specialization forces deeper consideration of each sub-domain and introduces internal critique during creation.
*   **Core Assumption:** Creative quality in structured domains benefits from simulated expert division of labor and internal critique, mirroring human design team dynamics.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract: "multi-agent pipeline... multi-agent specialization significantly improved creative quality." Section 6: "Human design teams do not work this way. Generative Ontology enables the same division of labor." Ablation (Section 9.2): quality gains (fun d=1.12, strategy d=1.59; p<.001).
*   **Break Condition:** If agent roles and anxieties do not map cleanly onto decomposable quality aspects, or if the base LLM cannot sustain coherent personas, specialization may increase cost without quality gain.

### Mechanism 3: RAG Grounded in Precedent
*   **Claim:** Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) grounded in a corpus of exemplars, filtered by the ontology's taxonomy, grounds novelty in precedent and prevents reinvention of known solutions.
*   **Mechanism:** A two-phase retrieval first filters an exemplar corpus (e.g., BoardGameGeek) by ontological mechanism types (e.g., "deck building"), then ranks by semantic similarity to the query theme. Retrieved examples are injected into generation prompts, providing a "vocabulary" of successful instantiations for the LLM to recombine and modify.
*   **Core Assumption:** High-quality creative output in a domain with accumulated exemplars requires awareness of prior solutions to avoid redundancy and leverage proven patterns.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract: "retrieval-augmented generation grounds designs in precedents." Section 7: "RAG transforms... from invention to informed invention. The ontology provides the grammar; the retrieved examples provide the vocabulary." Listed as pipeline component in Section 9.1.
*   **Break Condition:** If the exemplar corpus is too small, biased, or doesn't overlap with the target space, RAG may constrain novelty unproductively. The paper does not quantify RAG's isolated contribution.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Structured Knowledge Representation (Ontology Engineering)**
    *   **Why needed:** The entire framework rests on a formal, machine-readable model of the domain (classes, relationships, constraints). Without this, there is no "grammar" to enforce.
    *   **Quick Check:** Can you identify all required fields (e.g., `goal`, `end_condition`) and their type constraints in the `GameOntology` Pydantic schema, and explain why a field like `strategy_depth` has a minimum length?
*   **Concept: Constrained LLM Generation (Schema-Guided Prompting)**
    *   **Why needed:** This is the operational bridge between the static ontology and fluent LLM creativity. It ensures the LLM's output must map to a predefined, validated structure.
    *   **Quick Check:** How does a DSPy `Signature` with a `GameOntology` output field differ from a standard prompt asking for a "game design"? What happens if the LLM output does not match the schema?
*   **Concept: Multi-Agent System Design**
    *   **Why needed:** The quality gains in the ablation study are attributed to this architectural pattern. Understanding task decomposition, role assignment, and critique injection is key to replicating results.
    *   **Quick Check:** If you were to design a "MusicComposer" generative ontology, what might be three specialized agent roles and their corresponding professional anxieties?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component Map:** Domain Ontology (UML/Pydantic) -> DSPy Module & Signature -> Multi-Agent Pipeline (Sequential Architect/Weaver/Designer + Parallel Critic/Judge) -> Validation Contract (dspy.Assert/custom) -> LLM Evaluator ("Design Coach"). Retrieval Service (ChromaDB/SQLite) interfaces between the Theme Prompt and Mechanics Architect.
*   **Critical Path:** Theme Prompt → Mechanics Architect (with RAG) → Theme Weaver → Component Designer → Parallel critique by Balance Critic & Fun Judge → (If issues) Refinement Agent → Final validated `GameOntology` output → Evaluator scoring.
*   **Design Tradeoffs:**
    *   Cost vs. Quality: Full pipeline uses ~8.5x more tokens than baseline for ~1 point gain in fun.
    *   Generality vs. Expressivity: A fixed `MechanismType` enum limits exploration to known mechanisms but prevents non-sensical outputs. Adding new mechanisms requires ontology/schema update.
    *   Automation vs. Human-in-the-Loop: The system generates specifications playable *in principle*. The creative gap likely requires iterative human playtesting not automated here.
*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   Schema Validation Failure: LLM output cannot be parsed into `GameOntology`. Check output format, increase retries, adjust prompt.
    *   Constraint Violation: Custom validator fails (e.g., `deck_building` without `card_types`). Re-prompt designer agent with the error.
    *   Agent "Anxiety" Failure: Critic agents return only superficial issues. Check agent docstrings and examples for depth of instruction.
*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  **Schema Sanity Check:** Run generation with C2 (Schema only) on a trivial theme ("simple racing game"). Verify all required fields are populated with non-vague text. Goal: Confirm Pydantic constraint works.
    2.  **Single vs. Multi-Agent Comparison:** Generate 5 designs with C3 (single agent with full ontology) vs. C4 (multi-agent). Manually inspect for coherence between theme, mechanics, and components. Goal: Observe the decomposition effect.
    3.  **RAG Ablation:** Run C4 with RAG disabled. Compare mechanism novelty and plausibility against RAG-enabled runs. Goal: Qualitatively assess if designs become more derivative or implausible without exemplar grounding.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1.  **How can LLM-based evaluators be calibrated against human experts?** The paper establishes test-retest reliability (ICC 0.836–0.989) but not validity against expert human judgment on dimensions like elegance or fun. A study correlating LLM scores with experienced human designers is needed.
2.  **Can iterative generation–refinement cycles close the creative quality gap?** The gap (e.g., in replayability, elegance) is attributed to the absence of "iterative playtesting." The effect of multiple refinement iterations on quality metrics remains unknown and is proposed as future work.
3.  **How robust is the pipeline across different LLM architectures?** All generation and evaluation used Claude Sonnet 4. Results may be model-specific. Cross-model evaluation with different LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, open-source models) is required.
4.  **What is the functional role of "professional anxiety" prompts?** The pipeline assigns agents an "anxiety" (e.g., "Is there meaningful player agency?") claimed to prevent shallow outputs, but no ablation isolates its contribution versus role specialization alone. An ablation with neutral prompts is needed.
5.  **Does the pattern transfer to domains beyond tabletop games?** The paper claims generalizability to any domain with "expert vocabulary, validity constraints, and accumulated exemplars," but only games are validated. Implementation and evaluation in novel domains (e.g., recipes, musical forms) are required.

## Limitations
*   The core assumption that all creative validity constraints (e.g., "fun") are fully codifiable in Pydantic schemas is not empirically falsified.
*   Exact agent prompts/signatures and the preprocessed BGG corpus (1,767 games with embeddings) are not provided, blocking faithful recreation of the retrieval component and precise agent behavior.
*   The isolated contribution of RAG (vs. schema or multi-agent effects) is not quantified; the cited benefit is logical but not ablated.

## Confidence
*   **Mechanism 1 (Schema as Grammar): Medium.** Ablation shows structural errors drop (d=4.78), proving enforcement works. Confidence is medium because "complete codifiability" of emergent properties like fun is not tested.
*   **Mechanism 2 (Multi-Agent Specialization): High.** Ablation provides strong evidence (d=1.12 fun, d=1.59 strategy, p<.001). Risk is only in generalizing role definitions.
*   **Mechanism 3 (RAG Grounding): Low-Medium.** Claim is logically sound but no ablation isolates its effect. Confidence lowered by potential corpus bias and lack of quantitative impact.
*   **Benchmark Parity:** Medium.** Structural parity is demonstrated; fun gap is statistically clear but causally ambiguous (model capability vs. missing human loop).

## Next Checks
1.  **Schema Completeness Stress Test:** Audit the `GameOntology` Pydantic schema against a corpus of 50 published board games. For each, attempt to generate a conformant instance. Categorize *schema rejection* reasons (missing fields, constraint violations, vague text) to test the codifiability break condition.
2.  **Agent Role Swap Ablation:** Run the full pipeline (C4) but systematically swap the "anxiety" instructions between two roles (e.g., give Balance Critic’s anxiety to Fun Judge). Measure change in the affected creative dimension. If swapped anxieties transfer improvement to the wrong dimension, it confirms anxiety-role alignment is critical.
3.  **RAG Isolation via Corpus Perturbation:** Rebuild the BGG retrieval corpus with a controlled bias (e.g., filter to only "strategy games"). Run C4 with RAG enabled on neutral themes. Compare novelty (mechanism diversity) and plausibility against runs with a neutral corpus and with RAG disabled. A severe drop with biased corpus would quantify RAG’s grounding role.
