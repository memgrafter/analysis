# Toward an Integrated Cross-Urban Accident Prevention System: A Multi-Task Spatial-Temporal Learning Framework for Urban Safety Management

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05521  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05521  
- **Reference count:** 10  
- **Primary result:** A multi-task framework reduces RMSE by up to 6%, boosts Recall by up to 8%, and raises MAP by over 5% against state-of-the-art baselines, with less than 1% performance degradation under 50% input noise.

## Executive Summary
Accident risk prediction across multiple cities is hindered by data heterogeneity, inconsistent reporting, and noisy, sparse, cyclical records. This paper introduces MLA-STNet, a unified multi-task framework that jointly predicts accident risk for New York City and Chicago by integrating two specialized modules: STG-MA stabilizes temporal dynamics and filters noise from gridded geographical data, while STS-MA transfers knowledge across cities via shared temporal parameters yet preserves city-specific semantic structures derived from road networks and points of interest. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the multi-task approach consistently outperforms single-city models and prior art, with notable robustness to synthetic noise, suggesting a viable path toward scalable cross-urban safety management.

## Method Summary
MLA-STNet operates on two synchronized inputs per city: a 20×20 hourly gridded risk tensor with weather and mobility features, and a node-based road network embedding with three adjacency matrices (road connectivity, accident co-occurrence, POI similarity). The model uses a multi-task learning formulation where all cities share a backbone but retain city-specific spatial graphs and output heads. The core architecture comprises parallel branches: the STG-MA branch processes grid data via a 2D convolution followed by local masked attention (short-term) and a Mamba state-space model (long-term), fused adaptively. The STS-MA branch applies adaptive graph convolutions on heterogeneous city-specific graphs, then similar temporal modeling with shared Mamba/attention parameters. The branch outputs are fused per city via a gated mechanism and passed through a two-layer 1×1 conv head to produce risk maps. Training minimizes summed RMSE loss across cities with early stopping.

## Key Results
- Multi-task MLA-STNet reduces RMSE by up to 6% and increases Recall by up to 8% compared to state-of-the-art baselines on NYC and Chicago.
- Mean Average Precision (MAP) improves by over 5%, demonstrating better ranking of high-risk locations.
- Performance degrades by less than 1% even when 50% Gaussian noise is injected into inputs, confirming notable robustness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** STG-MA stabilizes accident risk predictions by suppressing unstable spatial fluctuations and enhancing long-range temporal dependencies, which is critical for handling clustered, sparse, cyclical, and noisy accident data.
- **Mechanism:** STG-MA processes grid-based features through a 2D convolution to capture local spatial patterns, reshapes the output into spatial trajectories, and then applies two complementary temporal pathways: (i) local masked attention (LMA) for short-term dependencies within a causal window, and (ii) a selective state-space model (Mamba) for efficient long-range modeling. An adaptive channel fusion gate combines these pathways, and only the final timestep is used for prediction.
- **Core assumption:** Accident risk exhibits spatially localized clusters and temporal periodicity, but the signal is corrupted by noise and sparsity; thus, a combination of local spatial filtering and robust temporal modeling can extract stable patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states that STG-MA "suppresses unstable spatio-temporal fluctuations and strengthens long-range temporal dependencies." Section 5.2 details the architecture. Ablation (Table 6) shows STG-MA contributes to lower RMSE and higher MAP.
- **Break condition:** This mechanism may fail if spatial clustering is absent (e.g., completely random accidents) or if noise overwhelms the signal such that even adaptive fusion cannot distinguish true patterns, leading to over-smoothing or misaligned temporal tracking.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The STS-MA module addresses cross-city heterogeneity by enabling knowledge transfer through shared temporal parameters while preserving city-specific semantic structures via heterogeneous graphs, thus allowing the model to generalize across diverse urban environments.
- **Mechanism:** STS-MA takes node-based features on road networks and constructs multiple city-specific adjacency matrices (road connectivity, risk co-occurrence, POI similarity) plus an adaptive matrix. These are used in adaptive graph convolution layers to produce node embeddings. Then, similar to STG-MA, it applies LMA and Mamba to these embeddings, but with shared projection matrices across cities. The output is projected back to each city's grid using a mapping matrix.
- **Core assumption:** Despite differences in infrastructure, reporting, and mobility, cities share underlying accident-inducing temporal dynamics (e.g., commuting peaks) but have unique spatial-semantic relationships; therefore, separating spatial-semantic parameters while sharing temporal processing is beneficial.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract describes STS-MA as leveraging "shared parameters to transfer knowledge across cities yet maintains city-specific semantic spaces." Section 5.3 defines the module. Experiments (Table 5) show multi-task outperforms single-task, and ablation (Table 6) shows removing STS-MA hurts recall significantly.
- **Break condition:** This mechanism could break down if cities are too dissimilar that shared temporal parameters introduce negative transfer, or if one city lacks certain semantic features (e.g., no POI data) causing imbalance that the shared design cannot fully compensate.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The multi-task learning framework jointly optimizes all cities in a single model, which improves overall predictive performance and robustness by exploiting commonalities while allowing city-specific adaptations, outperforming isolated city-specific models.
- **Mechanism:** The dataset combines multiple cities' data into a unified representation with block-diagonal spatial structures (separate grids and graphs). During training, the loss is summed across all cities, and all model parameters (except possibly the output head) are shared. The model outputs separate risk maps for each city. This setup enables parameter sharing for common patterns (e.g., temporal cycles) while city-specific branches (e.g., graph convolutions) adapt to local semantics.
- **Core assumption:** Accident risk prediction tasks for different cities are related but non-identical; joint optimization yields better generalization than separate training due to regularization effects and transfer of useful representations.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract says the model "formulates accident risk prediction as a multi-task learning problem across multiple cities" and shows improvements over single-task baselines. Section 4.2 defines the problem formulation. Tables 4 and 5 compare single-task and multi-task versions; multi-task yields lower RMSE and higher Recall, especially under noise (Tables 8,9). Ablation (Table 6) shows removing the multi-task aspect degrades performance.
- **Break condition:** Negative transfer could occur if cities have fundamentally different data distributions (e.g., one city has very different accident patterns due to unique policies or geography), causing the shared parameters to be pulled in conflicting directions. Also, if one city dominates the loss due to larger sample size, smaller cities may be neglected.

## Foundational Learning

### Multi-task Learning in Spatio-Temporal Contexts
- **Why needed here:** The core innovation of MLA-STNet is to predict accident risk for multiple cities simultaneously. Understanding multi-task learning is essential to grasp why joint optimization improves robustness and generalization across heterogeneous urban datasets.
- **Quick check question:** How does sharing parameters across cities in a multi-task setup help when each city has different spatial structures and reporting standards?

### Selective State Space Models (Mamba)
- **Why needed here:** Mamba replaces standard attention for temporal modeling, offering linear complexity and strong long-range dependency modeling. This is key for handling long sequences of hourly data efficiently while capturing cyclical patterns.
- **Quick check question:** What are the main advantages of using a selective state-space model (Mamba) over Transformer-style attention for accident risk prediction?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Input Embedding -> STG-MA (grid branch) and STS-MA (node branch) -> Gated Fusion -> Output Head.  
Grid branch: 1×1 embedding → Conv2D → reshape → LMA + Mamba → adaptive fusion.  
Node branch: 1×1 embedding → adaptive graph conv → reshape → LMA + Mamba (shared parameters) → fusion → map to grid.

### Critical path
1. Input sequences (grid and node) are embedded separately.
2. Parallel processing: STG-MA handles grid embeddings; STS-MA processes node embeddings with city-specific heterogeneous graphs.
3. Both branches produce city-specific grid representations.
4. Gated fusion merges the two representations per city.
5. Output head generates the final risk map for each city.
Multi-city handling is achieved by concatenating spatial dimensions while keeping city-specific adjacency structures (block-diagonal) and separate output projections.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Shared vs. city-specific parameters:** Sharing most parameters (including Mamba and attention projections) enables transfer learning and reduces overfitting, but city-specific graphs (adjacency matrices) and grid resolutions are preserved to handle heterogeneity. Too much sharing may cause negative transfer; too little loses multi-task benefits.
- **Computational efficiency:** Mamba provides O(T) temporal complexity versus O(T²) for attention, crucial for long sequences. However, graph convolutions and adaptive matrices add overhead. The model balances accuracy (multiple graph supports) with speed (Mamba).
- **Noise handling:** STG-MA's local masked attention and fusion act as a denoising mechanism, but aggressive filtering might lose subtle signals. The adaptive fusion gate learns to balance noise suppression vs. signal retention.

### Failure signatures
- **Over-smoothing in spatial predictions:** Risk maps become too diffuse, failing to capture sharp hotspots. Indicates STG-MA's spatial filtering is too aggressive or LMA window too large.
- **Degraded cross-city transfer:** Performance on one city drops significantly when trained jointly compared to single-task, suggesting negative transfer due to dissimilar distributions or data imbalance.
- **Poor recall of rare events:** Model misses actual hotspots, possibly because noise suppression in STG-MA filters out weak but true signals, or because the loss function does not sufficiently emphasize rare events.
- **Instability under noise:** If performance degrades rapidly with input noise (more than claimed 1%), the robustness mechanisms (e.g., risk mask, adaptive fusion) may be insufficient.

1. **Baseline comparison:** Train and evaluate MLA-STNet (both single-task and multi-task) against SOTA baselines (GSNet, ViT-Traffic, MG-STNet, etc.) on NYC and Chicago datasets for all-day and high-frequency periods. Report RMSE, Recall, MAP (Tables 4,5). Expected: multi-task MLA-STNet outperforms baselines.
2. **Ablation study:** Remove each key component (STG-MA, STS-MA, STM, LMA) one at a time and measure performance drop (Table 6, Figures 13-14). Expected: removing STG-MA hurts RMSE/MAP; removing STS-MA hurts Recall; removing STM hurts temporal stability; removing LMA hurts spatial precision.
3. **Robustness test:** Inject Gaussian noise (levels 0.0 to 0.5) into inputs and evaluate model stability (Tables 8-9, Figures 16-17). Expected: MLA-STNet shows less than 1% variation in metrics, while baselines degrade more.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Adaptive and causal graph structures:** How can adaptive and causal graph structures be designed to dynamically capture evolving urban mobility patterns, social factors, and policy interactions? The current model relies on static adjacency matrices that cannot account for temporal evolution or exogenous shocks.
2. **Federated learning extension:** How can MLA-STNet be extended to federated learning settings where cities cannot share raw data due to privacy regulations and data sovereignty concerns? The current multi-task architecture assumes centralized data access.
3. **Real-time deployment optimizations:** What engineering optimizations (e.g., model pruning, knowledge distillation, risk-aware graph sparsification) are necessary to deploy MLA-STNet in real-time ITS operations with low-latency inference? The model's computational demands for multi-city joint inference at grid-level resolution have not been tested under strict real-time constraints.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameters (embedding dimension, Mamba configuration, graph rank) are not fully disclosed, hindering exact replication.
- Negative transfer risk in multi-task training when cities exhibit divergent patterns is not thoroughly examined.
- Robustness claim (≤1% metric degradation under 50% Gaussian noise) may not hold for real-world noise such as missing reports, outliers, or reporting inconsistencies.

## Confidence
- STG-MA stabilization: Medium. Ablation confirms contribution, but direct evidence of effective noise suppression remains limited.
- STS-MA cross-city transfer: Medium. Multi-task gains are evident, yet the precise transfer mechanism and break conditions are not fully verified.
- Multi-task framework benefit: Medium-High. Consistent improvements across baselines, noise tests, and metrics support the claim, though potential negative transfer for highly dissimilar cities is not ruled out.
- Noise robustness: Low. Evaluation used only synthetic Gaussian noise; broader noise types and intensities are needed to substantiate the strong claim.
