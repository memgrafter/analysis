# Causal Neighbourhood Learning for Invariant Graph Representations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17934
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17934
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CNL-GNN achieves state-of-the-art F1-scores on four datasets (Cora: 93.51, Citeseer: 86.87, PubMed: 90.23, Twitch: 99.41), surpassing baselines by 2–13%, and maintains >84% F1 on unseen language domains.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses spurious correlations in graph data that cause GNNs to overfit and generalize poorly under distribution shifts. CNL-GNN introduces a framework for causal interventions at both structural and feature levels. It generates counterfactual neighbourhoods by linking target nodes to dissimilar non‑neighbours, applies attention‑based edge importance scoring and group‑aware adaptive perturbations, and disentangles node embeddings into invariant and variant components. Evaluated on Cora, Citeseer, PubMed, and Twitch, CNL‑GNN attains F1‑scores of 93.51, 86.87, 90.23, and 99.41, outperforming state‑of‑the‑art baselines by 2–13%. Cross‑domain tests on Twitch subgraphs show >84% F1 on unseen languages, demonstrating robustness. Ablations confirm the contribution of each module and the model remains stable across a range of edge drop rates.

## Method Summary
CNL‑GNN comprises four core modules. The Counterfactual Neighbourhood Generator (CNG) creates perturbed graphs by adding edges between target nodes and randomly sampled dissimilar non‑neighbours, providing alternative structural scenarios for contrastive learning. The Edge Importance Module (EIM) uses attention to score each edge’s relevance, while a group detector identifies latent node groups; together they inform an Adaptive Causal Edge Generator that retains intra‑group edges with high importance and perturbs inter‑group/low‑importance edges via masking or noise injection. Feature Perturbation adds noise to node features. After encoding the perturbed graph with a GATv2 encoder, a Feature Gate splits embeddings into a context vector (causal) and object vector (spurious). Two separate GNN branches refine each component, with orthogonality and mutual information losses encouraging disentanglement. A gated fusion combines the streams. Training jointly optimizes classification loss, a contrastive loss between original and counterfactual representations, and the disentanglement losses, enabling the model to learn representations invariant to spurious structural and feature variations.

## Key Results
- CNL‑GNN achieves F1‑scores of 93.51 (Cora), 86.87 (Citeseer), 90.23 (PubMed), and 99.41 (Twitch), outperforming SOTA baselines by 2–13%.
- Cross‑domain evaluation on Twitch subgraphs yields >84% F1 on unseen language domains.
- Ablation studies confirm the necessity of all modules; the model remains robust across edge drop rates 0.05–0.3.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Counterfactual Neighbourhood Generation
- Claim: Generating counterfactual neighbourhoods by swapping similar neighbours with dissimilar nodes forces the GNN to learn representations that are invariant to spurious structural variations.
- Mechanism: For each target node v, sample k dissimilar non‑neighbours from the graph and add bidirectional edges between v and those nodes, creating a perturbed (counterfactual) graph. The model is trained jointly on original and counterfactual graphs with a contrastive loss that minimizes prediction differences, encouraging stable embeddings.
- Core assumption: That neighbourhoods composed of dissimilar nodes provide a valid counterfactual intervention, i.e., removing the influence of the original neighbourhood’s spurious correlations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] The abstract mentions “counterfactual neighbourhood generation” and “simulating alternative causal scenarios at the connectivity level.”
  - [section] Section V.A details the CNG module, including Equation 2 and the sampling strategies (random, similar, dissimilar), with dissimilar empirically selected.
  - [corpus] Corpus context: Counterfactual interventions are used in representation learning (e.g., “Edvae: Disentangled latent factors models in counterfactual reasoning” appears in references) but typically in feature space, not graph structure, indicating novelty.
- Break condition: If dissimilar sampling fails to produce structurally meaningful perturbations (e.g., in dense graphs where most nodes are already neighbours), the counterfactual may become indistinguishable from the original, reducing its regularizing effect.

### Mechanism 2: Attention‑Based Edge Importance and Adaptive Perturbation
- Claim: Estimating edge relevance via attention and selectively perturbing edges based on group membership and importance scores reduces the impact of spurious connections.
- Mechanism: An edge importance module (EIM) computes scores s_ij = LeakyReLU(⟨a, Wh_i⟩+⟨a, Wh_j⟩) for each edge (i,j) using transformed node features and a shared attention vector a. A group detector identifies latent groups; edges within the same group are retained with high probability, while inter‑group edges are perturbed. Finally, edges with low importance scores are masked or replaced with noise, resulting in a perturbed graph ˜G.
- Core assumption: The attention‑based scores reflect causal relevance, i.e., edges with high scores are more likely to convey causal information for the prediction task.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] The abstract cites “adaptive edge perturbation with learnable importance masking” and “attention‑based edge scoring.”
  - [section] Section V.B describes the EIM and Equation 3; Section V.C details the group‑aware perturbation and masking process (Equation 4).
  - [corpus] The corpus includes multiple works on invariant graph learning that struggle with spurious edges (e.g., “Dissecting the Failure of Invariant Learning on Graphs”), highlighting the need for such selective mechanisms.
- Break condition: If the attention mechanism fails to converge to meaningful edge scores (e.g., due to poor initialization or extreme label imbalance), the perturbation may discard causal edges, harming performance.

### Mechanism 3: Causal Feature Disentanglement and Fusion
- Claim: Splitting node embeddings into invariant (causal) and variant (spurious) components and fusing them adaptively yields robust representations under distribution shifts.
- Mechanism: After encoding the perturbed graph with GATv2, a feature gate produces a context vector x_c (intended to capture invariant features); the object vector x_o is obtained as x - x_c. Two separate GNN branches further process x_c and x_o independently. Orthogonality and mutual information losses encourage disentanglement. The final representation x_f = α·x_c + (1−α)·x_o uses a learned gating coefficient α to balance the two streams.
- Core assumption: Node embeddings contain separable causal and non‑causal factors, and the gating mechanism can learn to weight them appropriately per node.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] The abstract refers to “causal feature disentanglement” and “learning invariant node representations.”
  - [section] Section V.D spells out the disentanglement process, Equation 5, and the associated losses (orthogonality, mutual information).
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is limited; most neighboring papers focus on invariant subgraphs or contrastive objectives rather than explicit feature splitting, suggesting this mechanism is under‑explored.
- Break condition: If the disentanglement losses are too strong, they may force an unnatural separation, degrading the richness of the embeddings; if too weak, causal and spurious features remain entangled, reducing invariance.

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map:
  - Input graph G (nodes V, edges E) and features X.
  - Counterfactual Neighbourhood Generator (CNG) → produces ˜G_c (counterfactual graph).
  - Edge Importance Module (EIM) → computes edge scores S.
  - Group detector → yields group assignments G.
  - Adaptive Causal Edge Generator (ACEG) → uses P_g (group perturbation) and M_i (importance masking) to produce perturbed graph ˜G.
  - Feature Perturbation → yields ˜X.
  - GATv2 encoder → produces node embedding x.
  - Feature Gate → splits into x_c (context) and x_o (object).
  - Two GNN branches: ContextGNN and ObjectGNN → refine x_c and x_o.
  - Fusion: x_f = α·x_c + (1−α)·x_o.
  - Counterfactual intervention on x_c and x_o → enable causal loss terms.
  - Losses: classification loss, contrastive loss (original vs counterfactual graph), orthogonality loss, mutual information loss.
- Critical path: Training iterates over nodes to build counterfactual neighbourhoods (Step 3–6 in Algorithm 1), computes S (7), detects groups (8), applies perturbations to obtain ˜G and ˜X (9–10), runs forward pass through GATv2 and disentanglement (11–16), generates counterfactual features (17), computes total loss and updates parameters (18). The loop runs for T epochs.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Counterfactual sampling strategy (dissimilar vs similar) trades off structural validity vs regularisation strength.
  - Edge drop rate (tuned via sensitivity analysis) balances robustness and information loss.
  - Disentanglement loss weights control the separation of causal and spurious features; excessive weight may harm representational capacity.
  - Group detector quality directly affects perturbation validity; a simple clustering vs sophisticated learned groups involves a speed‑accuracy tradeoff.
- Failure signatures:
  - Abrupt drop in F1 when edge drop rate >0.3 (Fig. 5) indicates over‑perturbation.
  - High variance across folds in ablation without CNG or EIM suggests those modules are essential for stability.
  - If the α gating parameter collapses to 0 or 1, the model is ignoring one branch, indicating possible disentanglement failure.
  - Poor cross‑domain performance on Twitch‑FR (11.2% drop) may signal that the learned groups are not portable across languages.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Benchmark evaluation: Compare CNL‑GNN against SOTA baselines (ICL, ACE, CaNet, CIE, GCIL, CAM) on citation (Cora, Citeseer, PubMed) and Twitch datasets using 5‑fold CV (Section VI.E, Table II).
  2. Ablation study: Remove CNG, EIM, group module, or both EIM+group to quantify contributions (Section VI.G, Fig. 4).
  3. Sensitivity analysis: Vary the edge drop rate (0.05 to 0.4) and measure ΔF1 to assess robustness to structural perturbations (Section VI.H, Fig. 5).

## Limitations
- Counterfactual neighbourhood generation may falter on dense graphs, where dissimilar nodes are scarce, weakening the intervention.
- Group detector assumptions may not transfer across domains, as evidenced by an 11.2% drop on Twitch‑FR.
- Feature disentanglement is weakly validated; no direct evidence demonstrates that the split yields causally meaningful components.

## Confidence
- CNG invariant learning: Medium
- Edge importance as causal: Medium
- Feature disentanglement: Low‑Medium
- Cross‑domain robustness: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Dense-graph test: Apply CNL‑GNN to high‑degree graphs (e.g., social networks) and measure counterfactual efficacy via perturbation sensitivity.
2. Attention interpretability: Evaluate edge scores against ground‑truth causal edges on synthetic graphs or use edge deletion to confirm task relevance of high‑score edges.
3. Group detector ablation: Replace learned groups with random or baseline clustering and measure performance drop; additionally test domain transfer with mismatched group distributions.
