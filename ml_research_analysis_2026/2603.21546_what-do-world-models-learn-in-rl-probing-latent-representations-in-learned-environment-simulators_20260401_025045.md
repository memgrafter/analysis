# What Do World Models Learn in RL? Probing Latent Representations in Learned Environment Simulators

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.21546
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21546
- Reference count: 1
- Primary result: World models develop structured, approximately linear internal representations of game state (e.g., ball/paddle positions, scores) that are both highly decodable and functionally used for predictions.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the internal representations learned by world models—neural simulators that predict future observations given actions. The authors focus on two distinct architectures: IRIS, which uses a discrete token transformer, and DIAMOND, a continuous diffusion UNet, both trained on Atari Breakout and Pong. By applying linear and MLP probes to frozen model activations, they find that key game state variables (object positions, scores) can be decoded with high accuracy (R² > 0.8 for ball position, >0.99 for paddle/score). The marginal improvement from non-linear probes (Δ ≤ 0.06) indicates that these representations are nearly linear. Furthermore, causal interventions that shift activations along the learned probe directions produce strong correlations (r ≥ 0.96) with changes in the model's next-step predictions, demonstrating that these directions are functionally used. Additional analyses of IRIS reveal spatial specialization in attention heads and highly consistent identification of object-containing tokens as critical via ablation. Overall, the study provides evidence that world models across different architectures develop structured, interpretable internal representations.

## Method Summary
The study uses pretrained world models (IRIS and DIAMOND) and a dataset of 10,000 Atari frames per game, with ground-truth state variables extracted from emulator RAM. Activations are extracted from multiple layers: for IRIS, from the VQ-VAE encoder and 10 transformer layers; for DIAMOND, from convolutional encoder/decoder stages, bottleneck, and normalization layers. To probe representations, they train ridge regression (α=1.0) and a 2-layer MLP with 5-fold cross-validation, measuring R² for each state variable. A selectivity gap Δ = R²_MLP - R²_linear quantifies nonlinear benefit. For causal validation, they compute normalized ridge weight vectors for high-R² properties, then shift a layer's activations by α·ŵ for varying α. They compute the KL divergence between patched and original next-token distributions and correlate |α| with KL to obtain r. For IRIS, they also compute attention entropy and spatial selectivity on the 4×4 token grid, and perform token ablation by replacing each of the 16 tokens with zero, the mean code, or a random codebook entry, measuring KL disruption and ranking consistency across baselines via Spearman's ρ.

## Key Results
- State variables (ball/paddle positions, scores) are linearly decodable with high R² (>0.8 for ball x, >0.99 for paddle/score); MLP probes improve R² by ≤0.06, suggesting near-linear structure.
- Activation interventions along probe directions yield a strong monotonic relationship between shift magnitude |α| and prediction KL divergence (r ≥ 0.96), confirming functional use.
- IRIS attention heads show spatial specialization; token ablation consistently ranks tokens containing objects as most critical, with high correlation (ρ > 0.9) across different ablation baselines.

## Why This Works
The high linear decodability indicates that world models organize their latent spaces to align with task-relevant state variables, likely due to the pressure to accurately predict future observations. The minimal gain from non-linear probes suggests that these representations are already nearly linearly separable, which may reflect an efficient coding principle. The causal intervention results demonstrate that the linear directions are not merely readout artifacts but are actively employed in the model's forward predictions, implying that the internal computations leverage these structured features. In IRIS, the spatial selectivity of attention and the critical role of object-containing tokens further suggest that the discrete token discovery process yields an object-centric decomposition of the scene.

## Foundational Learning
- **Linear probing**: A technique to test if latent representations encode target variables in a linearly separable manner. *Why needed*: It provides a simple diagnostic of representation geometry and interpretability. *Quick check*: Does a linear model (e.g., ridge regression) achieve high R² when predicting known state variables from activations?
- **Causal intervention via activation patching**: Shifting activations along a direction to measure impact on model outputs. *Why needed*: To establish whether a representation is functionally used, not just correlated. *Quick check*: Does the magnitude of the shift correlate with changes in predictions (e.g., KL divergence)?
- **Discrete vs. continuous world models**: Comparison of IRIS (discrete tokens) and DIAMOND (continuous diffusion). *Why needed*: To assess whether findings generalize across architectural paradigms. *Quick check*: Do both types exhibit similar linear decodability and causal use?
- **Token ablation**: Removing or replacing specific tokens to assess their contribution. *Why needed*: Identifies critical components of the representation. *Quick check*: Does ablating a token cause a significant increase in prediction error?
- **Attention entropy and spatial selectivity**: Measures of how attention is distributed across spatial tokens. *Why needed*: To interpret the transformer's processing in IRIS. *Quick check*: Are attention patterns concentrated (low entropy) on specific token regions, especially those containing objects?
- **World models**: Learned simulators that predict future observations given actions. *Why needed*: The core subject of study; understanding their internal mechanisms is key to improving interpretability and performance. *Quick check*: Can the model generate plausible next frames conditioned on actions?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:
IRIS: Raw frame (64x64) -> VQ-VAE encoder -> Discrete token grid (16x16) -> Flattened token sequence -> Transformer (predict next token distribution) -> VQ-VAE decoder -> Next frame.
DIAMOND: Raw frame -> CNN encoder -> Latent feature map -> Diffusion UNet (conditioned on action and noisy next frame) -> Denoised next frame.

**Critical path**: For IRIS, the forward pass consists of encoding the current frame into tokens, running the transformer to produce next-token logits, sampling or selecting tokens, and decoding to a frame. For DIAMOND, it involves encoding the current frame, then iteratively denoising a sample of the next frame via multiple UNet steps conditioned on the action and current frame's latent.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- IRIS uses discrete tokens, which provide compression and potential interpretability but risk quantization loss; the transformer handles sequence modeling but may struggle with long-range dependencies. DIAMOND's diffusion produces high-fidelity samples but requires many denoising steps and lacks an explicit discrete structure.
- Discrete models may be more data-efficient and easier to interpret; continuous diffusion models are more expressive but computationally heavier.
- Both trade off sample efficiency vs. generation quality; the choice influences the nature of the latent space and the ease of probing.

**Failure signatures**:
- IRIS: Poor VQ-VAE reconstruction (low SSIM), codebook collapse (many unused tokens), uniform attention maps, and low token ablation impact.
- DIAMOND: High diffusion loss, blurry or incoherent generated frames, lack of action conditioning (similar outputs for different actions), and unstable training (e.g., diverging KL).

**First experiments**:
1. Load a checkpoint and generate next-frame predictions for a few actions; compute reconstruction metrics (e.g., PSNR, SSIM) to validate basic functionality.
2. For IRIS, visualize the VQ-VAE token grid overlaid on frames to confirm tokens correspond to spatial regions; compute codebook usage histogram.
3. For IRIS, extract attention maps for each head and compute entropy and spatial selectivity to see if attention is concentrated and aligned with objects.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Do linearly decodable state representations generalize to 3D environments?
- Can sequence-conditioned probes reveal temporal representations in transformer-based world models?
- Are linear probe directions causally necessary for world model predictions, or merely correlated?

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details (training hyperparameters, data sampling) hinder exact replication.
- Evaluation limited to two Atari games (Breakout, Pong); generalizability to other domains is unknown.
- Probing focused on a small set of state variables; other latent factors remain unexplored.

## Confidence
- Linear decodability of state variables: High
- Functional use of linear directions (causal intervention): High
- Cross-architecture linearity: Medium (only two architectures tested)
- IRIS spatial attention/token importance: Medium (robust but limited to one discrete model)
- Generalization beyond Atari: Low (not tested)

## Next Checks
- Test on a broader set of environments (e.g., more Atari games, 3D domains like DeepMind Lab or MuJoCo) to assess generalizability.
- Probe additional target variables such as velocities, accelerations, and object relationships to capture dynamic state.
- Fine-tune world models on downstream RL tasks and re-probe to determine whether linear representations persist after adaptation.
