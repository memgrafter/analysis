# Synthetic or Authentic? Building Mental Patient Simulators from Longitudinal Evidence

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22704
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22704
- Reference count: 33
- Primary result: DEPROFILE significantly improves dialogue realism, behavioral diversity, and event richness compared to state-of-the-art baselines.

## Executive Summary
Mental health patient simulators often produce homogeneous and incoherent dialogues because they rely on static, snapshot-style prompts. This paper introduces DEPROFILE, a framework that constructs unified patient profiles by integrating clinical assessments, counseling dialogues, and longitudinal social media histories. A Chain-of-Change agent transforms noisy social media records into structured, temporally grounded memory cards with strict citation protocols. Evaluations across multiple LLM backbones show marked gains: MentalBERT similarity up to 0.939, Q-Centroid diversity up to 0.063, and G-Eval event richness up to 4.86, outperforming baselines such as PATIENT-Ψ and EEYORE. Human evaluation by psychiatric experts confirms superior clinical plausibility (mean 3.73/5) and statistically significant win rates (p<0.05).

## Method Summary
DEPROFILE draws on three data sources: D4 diagnostic dialogues (1,340 cases), Client Reaction counseling interactions (300 clients), and Twitter-STMHD social media histories (~37k tweets). It first aligns clinical assessments with counseling dialogues using deterministic demographic and symptom constraints to form “clinical skeletons.” Each skeleton is then enriched with social media timelines of users who share high symptom consistency (>0.8) and personality similarity (>0.8). The selected timelines are processed by a Chain-of-Change agent into memory cards that bundle temporally aggregated symptom/event nodes with relative timestamps. During simulation, a modular prompt combines basic demographics, risk, personality, symptoms, assessment/counseling snippets, and the memory cards. The simulator LLM must cite specific cards when referring to past events, anchoring responses in verifiable episodes. The pipeline is evaluated via automatic metrics (MentalBERT, Q-Centroid, G-Eval) and expert review.

## Key Results
- MentalBERT similarity up to 0.939, indicating far greater dialogue realism.
- Q-Centroid (behavioral diversity) up to 0.063, showing more varied patient behaviors.
- G-Eval event richness up to 4.86; human clinical plausibility mean 3.73/5, with win rates against all baselines statistically significant (p<0.05).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DEPROFILE’s improvements stem from three synergistic mechanisms:

1. **Unified multi-source patient profiles**: The framework first forms “clinical skeletons” by deterministically matching assessment dialogues with counseling dialogues based on identical demographics and absence of symptom conflicts. It then enriches each skeleton by retrieving social media timelines of users with high symptom consistency (>0.8) and personality similarity (>0.8). This two-stage process yields a coherent, multidimensional persona that blends cross-sectional clinical data with longitudinal life-event histories, directly addressing the homogeneity of prior simulators.

2. **Chain-of-Change (CoC) Agent**: Raw social media timelines are transformed into structured “Memory Cards” through three phases: semantic extraction of symptom/event triplets, temporal aggregation into episodes (e.g., 7‑day windows), and deterministic rendering with relative timestamps. The agent also generates a knowledge graph for evaluation. The strict citation protocol forces the simulator to reference specific memory cards when discussing past events, thereby grounding responses in verifiable episodes and curbing hallucination.

3. **Dual Regulatory Mechanism**: The timeline component (T) serves a dual role depending on context. In sparse prompts (e.g., basic profile only), it enriches the simulation with narrative details and event diversity. In full‑context settings with few‑shot demonstrations, it acts as a constraint anchor, preventing the LLM from fabricating details inconsistent with the verified timeline. This trade‑off improves both richness and clinical accuracy.

Break conditions: Matching thresholds set too high limit profile diversity; too low re‑introduce clinical conflicts. CoC extraction errors produce incomplete or misleading memory cards. Non‑compliance with citations reduces temporal grounding. The fixed enrichment/constraint role may not suit all contexts.

## Foundational Learning
**Multi‑Source Data Alignment & Hard Matching**  
*Why needed*: Mental health presentations are inherently multi‑faceted (symptoms, personality, life events). DEPROFILE’s core innovation is bridging disjoint datasets (clinical interviews, counseling, social media) into a single coherent profile. The engineer must understand how deterministic constraints (demographic/symptom matching) ensure clinical validity before expanding with probabilistic timeline matching.

**Temporal Memory Structuring (Episodic Aggregation)**  
*Why needed*: Raw social media timelines are chaotic day‑by‑day streams. The CoC agent’s episode aggregation (e.g., 7‑day windows) reduces context length while preserving change patterns. This is critical for making longitudinal data usable within LLM context windows and for identifying salient periods.  
*Quick check*: Why does the CoC agent bucket nodes into episodes (e.g., by ⌊days_ago/W⌋) instead of feeding every daily post directly into the prompt? What problem does this solve?

**LLM‑as‑a‑Judge for Clinical Dimensions (G‑Eval)**  
*Why needed*: Evaluating patient simulators requires clinical expertise (symptom consistency, event richness). Human evaluation is expensive. DEPROFILE uses a customized G‑Eval prompt (Appendix D) to score generated dialogues against the profile. The engineer must grasp how to design such prompts to align with clinical reasoning.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Input Data (D4, Client Reaction, Twitter‑STMHD) → Preprocessing & Annotation (demographics, Big Five, symptoms, timelines) → Profile Alignment Engine (Stage 1 deterministic counseling‑assessment matching; Stage 2 similarity‑based social media retrieval) → CoC Agent (memory card generation) → Prompt Assembler (B,R,P,S,A,C,T modules) → Simulator (LLM backbone) → Evaluator (MentalBERT, G‑Eval, Human)

**Critical Path:** Data Annotation → Profile Alignment → CoC Processing → Prompt Construction → Simulation → Evaluation. A failure in alignment (Stage 1/2) propagates downstream; a flawed CoC output directly causes hallucination.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Matching Precision vs. Coverage: Symptom/personality thresholds >0.8 ensure coherence but may exclude valid timelines; lower thresholds increase coverage but risk clinical conflict.  
- CoC Determinism vs. Expressiveness: Deterministic template‑based card rendering ensures reproducibility but may lose nuanced phrasing; LLM‑based extraction adds expressiveness but risks noise.  
- Timeline as Enricher vs. Constraint: The system does not dynamically switch; the same timeline module either adds detail (in sparse prompts) or restricts (in full prompts). This is a fixed trade‑off based on the chosen prompt configuration.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Profile‑level: Manual review finds contradictions between matched counseling/assessment symptoms (Stage 1 failure).  
- Timeline‑level: CoC agent outputs cards with “None” for key posts, indicating poor extraction or inauthentic posts not filtered.  
- Simulation‑level: Responses violate the citation protocol (e.g., mention a time point not in any card) or express symptoms not in the positive/negative lists.  
- Evaluation‑level: High MentalBERT similarity but low G‑Eval symptom consistency, suggesting the simulator mimics linguistic style but ignores clinical grounding.

**First Experiments:**  
1. Validate Matching Thresholds: Vary symptom/personality similarity thresholds (e.g., 0.7, 0.8, 0.9) on a held‑out set of 100 skeletons; measure manual coherence and timelines retained; aim for threshold maximizing coherence while keeping coverage (~80+ timelines/skeleton).  
2. Test CoC Extraction Quality: For 50 randomly selected timelines, annotators compare CoC‑extracted symptom/event nodes against original posts; compute precision/recall; target >85% precision on retained nodes.  
3. Ablation of Timeline Module: Using a single backbone (e.g., DeepSeek‑V3.2), run full three‑stage QA with (a) full prompt minus T, (b) full prompt with T; measure G‑Eval Event Richness and Symptom Consistency; expect (a) higher Diversity/Similarity but lower Event Richness; (b) higher Event Richness and potentially lower Diversity if hallucination is curbed.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Mitigating social media bias**: How can we adjust for positivity/outrage bias common on social platforms to further refine the authenticity of simulated patient experiences? The authors propose adding weighting mechanisms in future work.
2. **Incorporating non‑verbal cues**: How can text‑based simulations integrate clinician intuition and non‑verbal signals (e.g., tone, facial expressions) to better reflect psychiatric diagnostic interactions? The current text‑only approach lacks these subtle cues.
3. **Causal inference from timelines**: How can causal relationships between life events and symptom changes be automatically inferred from longitudinal social media to enhance clinical interpretability? The CoC agent builds a graph with temporal edges but does not yet capture trigger/worsens/alleviates relations.
4. **Generalization beyond depression**: To what extent can DEPROFILE be applied to other mental disorders? Evaluation focused solely on depression; different symptom structures and longitudinal patterns may require adjustments.

## Limitations
- Implementation details missing: Big Five extraction, authenticity filter, life‑event/symptom classifiers, CoC hyperparameters, generation settings.
- Rigid similarity threshold (>0.8) without sensitivity analysis may unduly restrict profile diversity or introduce conflicts.
- Reliance on proprietary GPT‑5 for G‑Eval hinders reproducibility; social media dataset biases remain unaddressed.
