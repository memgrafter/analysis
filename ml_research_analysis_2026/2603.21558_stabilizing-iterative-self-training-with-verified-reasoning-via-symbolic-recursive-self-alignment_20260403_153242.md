# Stabilizing Iterative Self-Training with Verified Reasoning via Symbolic Recursive Self-Alignment

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.21558
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21558
- **Reference count**: 5
- **Primary result**: Symbolic Recursive Self-Alignment (NSRSA) stabilizes iterative self‑training by filtering step‑level errors, achieving 91.0% accuracy on GSM8K with Qwen3‑4B‑Thinking after 5 iterations (baseline 80.5%), versus collapse to 73.2% without verification.

## Executive Summary
Recursive self‑training—fine‑tuning a model on its own generations—suffers from error compounding (recursive drift), where small mistakes amplify over iterations, leading to collapse. This paper introduces NSRSA, which embeds symbolic verification (arithmetic via SymPy, logical flow tracking, domain constraints) directly at the reasoning step level. Unlike outcome‑only filters that accept solutions with correct final answers but flawed intermediates (“lucky guesses”), NSRSA discards such solutions before they enter training. On GSM8K, NSRSA raises accuracy from 80.5% to 91.0% over 5 iterations while maintaining stability, and a DPO variant further reaches 91.2%. Transfer to MATH‑500 improves by +5.7 percentage points. The verification rate of correct‑answer solutions rises across iterations, indicating that the model learns to produce more inherently sound reasoning.

## Method Summary
NSRSA operates in an iterative loop: for each training problem, the current model generates multiple reasoning chains. A deterministic symbolic verification subsystem inspects each chain via four checks: (1) final answer correctness, (2) arithmetic correctness of all parseable expressions using SymPy (requiring at least 80% correctness), (3) logical flow consistency (e.g., variable assignments not changing too rapidly), and (4) domain constraints (e.g., non‑negativity for counts). Only solutions passing all checks are retained for fine‑tuning the model via LoRA. A DPO extension constructs preference pairs where the chosen solution passes all checks and the rejected solution has a correct final answer but fails at least one step‑level check; optimizing these pairs further aligns the model toward verifiable reasoning. This step‑level filtration prevents “lucky guesses” from contaminating the training set, thereby stabilizing the recursion.

## Key Results
- NSRSA achieves 91.0% GSM8K accuracy after 5 iterations (from 80.5% baseline), while no verification collapses to 73.2% and outcome‑only filtering plateaus at 85.8%.
- Approximately 34% of correct‑answer solutions that pass outcome checks are rejected by NSRSA due to flawed intermediate reasoning.
- The verification rate (fraction of correct‑answer solutions passing all step checks) increases across iterations, and Self‑BLEU remains low, indicating improved reasoning without mode collapse.
- DPO variant reaches 91.2% accuracy; cross‑task transfer to MATH‑500 gains +5.7 percentage points.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
NSRSA stabilizes recursive self‑training through three interconnected mechanisms.

First, it prevents recursive drift by filtering out “lucky guesses”—solutions with correct final answers but flawed intermediate reasoning—before they enter the training data. The symbolic verification subsystem inspects every reasoning step: it evaluates parseable arithmetic expressions with SymPy (threshold τ_arith=0.8), tracks variable assignments for logical consistency (gap >2 steps and >50% change flagged), and enforces simple domain constraints. Only solutions passing all four checks are used for fine‑tuning. The core assumption is that step‑level symbolic checks can reliably distinguish sound reasoning from coincidentally correct answers. Evidence includes the rejection of ~34% of correct‑answer solutions that pass outcome checks, and the fact that naive outcome‑only filtering still collapses at iteration 2. If parser coverage is too low (many solutions have zero parseable expressions), flawed reasoning may slip through; the fallback to τ_arith=0.5 when acceptance is low mitigates this.

Second, NSRSA’s verification signal teaches the model to prefer sound reasoning via direct preference optimization (DPO). Preference pairs are constructed: the chosen solution passes all NSRSA checks, while the rejected solution has a correct final answer but fails at least one step check. DPO then aligns the model to generate more verifiable reasoning. The core assumption is that this contrast is learnable; the model generalizes to produce better reasoning without external supervision. Evidence: DPO variant reaches 91.2% accuracy (vs 91.0% for SFT‑only), and verification rate increases across iterations. If the model cannot learn from these pairs due to capacity limits, DPO would not improve over SFT.

Third, the iterative loop becomes progressively more stable because the verification rate of correct‑answer solutions rises over iterations, indicating that the model learns to produce inherently more verifiable reasoning. Each iteration trains on a higher‑quality dataset (since NSRSA‑filtered solutions are more likely to be sound), creating a positive feedback loop where subsequent generations are more likely to pass verification. The core assumption is that verification rate is a valid proxy for reasoning quality. Evidence: Figure 3 shows a rising trend for NSRSA, while no‑verification collapses. Self‑BLEU remains low, suggesting diversity is preserved. If the model overfits to verification rules (e.g., satisfying the parser without true soundness), the verification rate could plateau while actual quality stagnates.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Generation Module (base model samples reasoning chains) -> Verification Subsystem (four deterministic checks) -> Training Module (LoRA for SFT; DPO for preferences) -> Iteration Controller (orchestrates loop).

**Critical path**:
1. For each training problem, generate *N* candidate solutions.
2. Parse each solution: extract answer, identify arithmetic expressions, track variable assignments.
3. Run verification: compute arithmetic correctness rate, detect logical inconsistencies, check constraints, confirm answer.
4. Select solutions that pass all checks (or construct preference pairs for DPO).
5. Fine‑tune the previous model on selected data (SFT) or run DPO on pairs.
6. Evaluate on held‑out sets and log metrics; repeat for *K* iterations.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Verification granularity vs. coverage*: Stricter arithmetic thresholds improve precision but reduce acceptance; fallback to τ_arith=0.5 if acceptance too low.
- *Symbolic vs. learned verifiers*: Symbolic tools give hard guarantees but only for well‑defined operations (e.g., arithmetic); a learned process reward model could handle more abstract steps but may inherit its own errors.
- *SFT vs. DPO*: SFT on verified data gives steady gains; DPO adds a small extra boost but requires careful pair construction and hyperparameter tuning.
- *Parser design*: Simple regex‑based extraction is lightweight but may miss natural‑language arithmetic; more sophisticated NLP parsers could increase coverage but add complexity.

**Failure signatures**:
- Accuracy collapse: test accuracy drops below baseline within 2–3 iterations.
- Mode collapse: Self‑BLEU rises steadily (>0.50 by iteration 5).
- Stagnant verification rate: fraction of correct‑answer solutions passing all NSRSA checks plateaus or declines.
- Dataset starvation: acceptance rate becomes extremely low (<20% of generated correct answers), leading to overfitting.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline comparison**: Run 5 self‑training iterations under five conditions (no verification, outcome verification, majority voting, NSRSA symbolic, NSRSA+DPO). Plot accuracy trajectories.
2. **Filtering analysis**: At iteration 1, record acceptance rates per strategy and compute the proportion of correct‑answer solutions rejected by NSRSA but accepted by outcome verification. Report per‑check pass rates.
3. **Verification rate trend**: Track the verification rate across iterations for NSRSA. Correlate with Self‑BLEU to check diversity preservation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Enhancing arithmetic parser for natural language**: How can we capture natural‑language operations and multi‑step derivations without increasing false rejections? The paper notes limitations of regex extraction and suggests more sophisticated NLP parsing could improve coverage, but the stability impact is unknown. *Evidence*: integrate an advanced parser, evaluate coverage, arithmetic pass rate, verification rate, and iterative accuracy.
2. **Generalization to non‑math domains**: Can NSRSA be generalized to domains like code generation where verification requires dynamic execution or approximate checks? Extending requires domain‑specific constraint sets; evaluation is currently limited to math. *Evidence*: develop domain‑specific verifiers (e.g., unit tests for code), run NSRSA self‑training, compare stability and performance against no‑verification baselines.
3. **Optimizing verification thresholds**: What is the impact of thresholds (e.g., τ_arith) and fallback strategies on the trade‑off between data quality and quantity, and how can they be optimized for deeper recursion? Choices are heuristic; systematic study of their effect on iteration dynamics, acceptance rates, and long‑term stability is missing. *Evidence*: controlled experiments varying τ_arith and fallback triggers, plot accuracy trajectories, analyze acceptance/verification rates.
4. **Hybrid symbolic and learned verifiers**: Does combining NSRSA’s hard symbolic checks with learned process reward models yield improvements beyond either approach alone? The paper proposes this as future work; no empirical evaluation exists. *Evidence*: train a PRM on NSRSA‑verified step labels, use its scores to augment verification, compare against pure NSRSA and PRM‑only baselines.

## Limitations
- Parser coverage limited: regex extraction may miss arithmetic expressions, especially in natural language; fallback τ_arith=0.5 could admit noisy samples.
- Heuristic checks: logical flow and constraint modules use simple keywords and assumptions that may not generalize to diverse problems.
- Evaluation scope restricted to GSM8K and MATH‑500 with a single model (Qwen3‑4B‑Thinking); transfer to other models/tasks not established.
