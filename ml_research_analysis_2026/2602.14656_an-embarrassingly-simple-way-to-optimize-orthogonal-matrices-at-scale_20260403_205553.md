# An Embarrassingly Simple Way to Optimize Orthogonal Matrices at Scale

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.14656
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14656
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: POGO achieves orders-of-magnitude speedups over retraction-based methods while maintaining feasibility and matching unconstrained Adam accuracy on vision models.

## Executive Summary
Orthogonal constraints are prevalent in neural network architectures, including attention mechanisms and recurrent layers, yet optimizing parameters under such constraints is notoriously difficult due to the non-convex Stiefel manifold. POGO (Projected Orthogonal Gradient Optimization) introduces an elegantly simple approach: it performs a gradient step within the tangent space of the manifold and then applies a normal step that returns the iterate to the feasible set. This procedure requires only five matrix multiplications per update, making it highly efficient on GPUs and scalable to thousands of orthogonal matrices. Empirical results on vision transformers and convolutional networks demonstrate that POGO attains accuracy comparable to unconstrained Adam while strictly preserving orthogonality. On large-scale benchmarks with tens of thousands of kernels, POGO reduces training time from hours to minutes, representing an order-of-magnitude improvement over prior retraction-based methods.

## Method Summary
POGO operates on parameters \(X\) subject to \(XX^\top = I\) (Stiefel manifold). Given a Euclidean gradient \(\nabla f(X)\) and a base optimizer (e.g., SGD, VectorAdam), it first computes the optimizer's output \(G\). The Riemannian gradient is then obtained as \(G_X = X \cdot \operatorname{Skew}(X^\top G)\), where \(\operatorname{Skew}\) extracts the skew-symmetric part. A tangent-space update yields an intermediate point \(M = X - \eta G_X\). To enforce orthogonality, POGO determines a step size \(\lambda\) either by solving a quartic polynomial derived from the squared manifold distance or by fixing \(\lambda = 1/2\). The final iterate is \(X_{\text{next}} = M + \lambda (I - MM^\top) M\). This algorithm uses only matrix multiplications and no retractions, ensuring computational efficiency.

A minimal reproduction can be accomplished in three steps: (1) clone the official PyTorch repository (`github.com/adrianjav/pogo`); (2) implement a single-matrix benchmark (e.g., online PCA) with dimensions \(p=1500, n=2000\) and use the SGD base optimizer, \(\lambda=1/2\), and \(\eta=0.25\); (3) monitor loss, manifold distance \(\|XX^\top - I\|\), and runtime. However, the paper does not disclose full hyperparameter settings for all baselines nor the precise implementation of the quartic solver, which may hinder exact replication of the large-scale results.

## Key Results
- POGO matches the validation accuracy of unconstrained Adam on vision transformers and convolutional networks while maintaining strict orthogonality.
- On benchmarks with 22,000 orthogonal kernels, POGO trains in minutes versus hours for retraction-based methods, achieving orders-of-magnitude speedups.
- The method scales to thousands of orthogonal matrices with a constant cost of five matrix multiplications per update.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** POGO performs optimization updates within the tangent space of the Stiefel manifold, using a Riemannian gradient that is orthogonal to the manifold constraint.
- **Mechanism:** The Euclidean gradient \(\nabla f(X)\) is mapped to the tangent space via \(G = \text{BaseOptimizer}(\nabla f(X))\) followed by \(G = X \cdot \operatorname{Skew}(X^\top G)\), where \(\operatorname{Skew}\) extracts the skew-symmetric part. This forms a valid tangent vector \(XS\). The intermediate point \(M = X - \eta \cdot XS\) lies in the tangent space direction but generally off the manifold.
- **Core assumption:** The Base Optimizer must be *linear* (Def. 1), i.e., its output is proportional to its input, to preserve equivalence between Euclidean and tangent-space updates.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "POGO extends Landing... enabling the inclusion of modern adaptive optimizers while ensuring that orthogonal constraints are effectively met."
  - [section 3.1]: "Linear optimizers preserve their semantics in TX... The vanilla SGD... is trivially linear. However, the popular Adam optimizer is not... In contrast, Vector Adam (V Adam) satisfies Def. 1."
  - [corpus]: The neighbor "Infeasible Deterministic..." describes infeasible methods like Landing that temporarily relax constraints, providing context but not direct evidence for the linear BO requirement.
- **Break condition:** Using a non-linear optimizer (e.g., standard Adam) breaks the tangent-space equivalence, potentially degrading performance or feasibility.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** POGO computes an optimal step size \(\lambda\) that exactly returns the iterate to the Stiefel manifold after the tangent-space step.
- **Mechanism:** Given the intermediate point \(M\), the landing step is \(X_{\text{next}} = M - \lambda \cdot \nabla N(M)\), where \(\nabla N(M) = (MM^\top - I)M\) is the normal direction. The squared manifold distance \(P(\lambda) = \|X_{\text{next}} X_{\text{next}}^\top - I\|^2\) is a quartic polynomial in \(\lambda\). POGO solves \(P(\lambda)=0\) to find the real root minimizing the distance to \(M\).
- **Core assumption:** The quartic polynomial has real roots; the chosen root selection rule (real part of root with least imaginary part) yields a feasible step.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2]: "The polynomial \(P(\lambda)\) turns out to have a nice symbolic form... is a quartic polynomial w.r.t. \(\lambda\)... the problem \(\min_\lambda P(\lambda)=0\) has a known closed-form solution using radicals."
  - [section 3.4]: "POGO only consists of additions and multiplications... requires 5 matrix multiplications if we set \(\lambda=1/2\), and an additional \(O(p^4 n)\) otherwise [for exact \(\lambda\).]"
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; the neighbor "Infeasible Deterministic..." discusses Landing's adaptive step size but not exact polynomial solving.
- **Break condition:** Numerical instability in solving the quartic, or failure to pick the correct real root (e.g., all roots complex or selection picks a root that overshoots).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Under a bound on the scaled learning rate \(\xi := \eta\|G\| < 1\), fixing \(\lambda=1/2\) yields iterates that remain \(o(\xi^7)\)-close to the manifold without computing the polynomial root.
- **Mechanism:** The bound \(\|MM^\top - I\| \le \eta^2 \|S^2\|\) (Prop. 3.2) combined with \(\xi < 1\) allows proving that \(P(1/2) = o(\xi^7)\) (Prop. 3.3). By induction, starting from \(X_0 \in St(p,n)\), all subsequent iterates satisfy \(\|X_t X_t^\top - I\|^2 = o(\xi^7)\) (Thm. 3.5). This approximates a polar retraction \((MM^\top)^{-1/2} M\) by a first-order Taylor expansion.
- **Core assumption:** 
  1. \(X_0\) is exactly orthogonal.
  2. The base optimizer gradient norm is uniformly bounded by \(L\), and the learning rate \(\eta\) is chosen such that \(\xi < 1\).
  3. The manifold dimension \(p\) is finite (for the \(o(\xi^7)\) bound).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3]: "Remarkably, the bound above depends exclusively on the learning rate \(\eta\) and the relative gradient... if we keep \(\xi < 1\) by e.g. setting the learning rate appropriately, then \(\lambda=1/2\) becomes a great approximation."
  - [section 3.3, Thm. 3.5]: "If \(X_0 \in St(p,n)\), \(\xi := \eta L < 1\) and \(\lambda=1/2\), then for every \(X_t\) produced by Eqs. 9 and 10 it holds that \(P(1/2) = \|X_t X_t^\top - I\|^2 = o(\xi^7)\)."
  - [corpus]: The neighbor "Sequential Adaptive Priors..." discusses orthogonal functions but does not address the specific \(\lambda=1/2\) bound. The neighbor "Clarifying Shampoo..." considers spectral methods but not Stiefel manifold steps.
- **Break condition:** \(\xi \ge 1\) (e.g., \(\eta\) too large or \(\|G\|\) too large) invalidates the bound; the \(o(\xi^7)\) guarantee does not prevent moderate manifold violations in practice if \(\xi\) is near 1. The bound is asymptotic (\(\varepsilon\to0\) in Thm. 3.4), so for large \(\varepsilon\) (early iterations) the guarantee is weaker.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Stiefel manifold (row-orthogonal matrices)
  - *Why needed here:* POGO's entire mechanism operates on the Stiefel manifold \(St(p,n) = \{X \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n} \mid XX^\top = I_p\}\). Understanding its geometry (tangent space structure, retractions) is essential to interpret the tangent-space updates and landing steps.
  - *Quick check question:* Given \(X \in St(p,n)\) and a skew-symmetric \(S\), is \(X + S\) still on the manifold? Why or why not?

- **Concept:** Riemannian gradient and retractions
  - *Why needed here:* POGO's \(XS\) (with \(S = \operatorname{Skew}(X^\top G)\)) is the Riemannian gradient under the Euclidean metric. Retractions (like QR, polar) are alternative ways to return to the manifold. POGO contrasts with retraction-based methods by using a normal step based on \(\nabla N\).
  - *Quick check question:* How does the polar retraction \(X (XX^\top)^{-1/2}\) differ from POGO's normal step \(M - \lambda(MM^\top - I)M\) when \(\lambda=1/2\)? (Hint: Taylor expansion.)

- **Concept:** Proximal methods on manifolds
  - *Why needed here:* POGO's normal step \(-\lambda \nabla N(M)\) is a proximal-like step on the manifold distance \(N(X)\). It is conceptually similar to the Landing field's normal component but with exact/approximate step size computation.
  - *Quick check question:* In Euclidean space, proximal gradient descent for constraint \(\|x\|_2=1\) might use \(x \gets x - \lambda(x\|x\|^2 - x)\). How does this relate to \(\nabla N(X) = (XX^\top - I)X\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - Input: \(X\) (current parameters), \(\nabla f(X)\) (Euclidean gradient), hyperparameters \(\eta\) (learning rate), \(\lambda_{\text{policy}}\) (exact root or fixed 1/2), \(\text{BaseOptimizer}\) (e.g., VectorAdam).
  - Step 1: \(G = \text{BaseOptimizer}(\nabla f(X))\) (may adaptively rescale).
  - Step 2: \(G_{\text{tangent}} = X \cdot \operatorname{Skew}(X^\top G)\) (maps to tangent space, requires \(X^\top G\) → skew-sym).
  - Step 3: \(M = X - \eta \cdot G_{\text{tangent}}\) (tangent-space update).
  - Step 4: Compute \(\lambda\):
    - Exact: Form quartic \(P(\lambda)\) from \(MM^\top - I\) and \(\nabla N(M)\), solve for real root.
    - Approx: Use \(\lambda = 1/2\).
  - Step 5: \(X_{\text{next}} = M + \lambda (I - MM^\top) M\) (normal step; note sign: \(-\lambda \nabla N(M) = \lambda (I - MM^\top) M\)).
  - Output: \(X_{\text{next}}\) (approximately orthogonal).

- **Critical path:** The most expensive operations are matrix multiplications: \(X^\top G\) (\(O(p^2 n)\)), \(X \cdot \operatorname{Skew}(\ldots)\) (\(O(p^2 n)\)), \(MM^\top\) (\(O(p^2 n)\)), and optionally \(\nabla N(M)\) and polynomial coefficient computations (\(O(p^2 n)\)). For exact \(\lambda\), the quartic solve is \(O(1)\) but involves handling complex roots; the \(O(p^4 n)\) mentioned likely refers to forming full matrices for coefficient calculation? Clarify: exact \(\lambda\) requires computing roots of scalar polynomial, so overhead is small (constant factor) but requires careful numerics.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Exact \(\lambda\) vs. \(\lambda=1/2\):* Exact \(\lambda\) ensures \(X_{\text{next}}\) is *exactly* orthogonal (up to numerical precision) and may allow larger \(\eta\). However, it adds code complexity and potential numerical instability.
  - *Base optimizer:* Linear optimizers (VectorAdam, SGD) work; non-linear (Adam) may degrade. Adaptive (VAdam) helps control \(\xi\) automatically, enabling larger \(\eta\).
  - *Feasibility vs. cost:* POGO aims for feasibility with minimal cost (5 matmuls). Retraction methods (QR) are more expensive and numerically unstable. Landing trades feasibility for speed; POGO recovers feasibility.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Manifold distance \(\|XX^\top - I\|\) grows steadily → likely \(\xi \ge 1\) or \(\lambda\) too large. Reduce \(\eta\) or switch to adaptive base optimizer.
  - Training diverges or accuracy drops → non-linear base optimizer, or exact \(\lambda\) choosing a root that overshoots (check root selection logic).
  - High computational cost relative to Landing → using exact \(\lambda\) unnecessarily; consider \(\lambda=1/2\) if \(\xi\) is small.
  - Numerical errors (NaNs) → quartic polynomial solving unstable for large matrices? Scale inputs? Use float64?

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Single-matrix PCA/Procrustes:** Verify convergence speed and manifold distance against Landing/RGD. Use small to medium matrices (e.g., \(500 \times 1000\)). Check that distance stays low (≤ 1e-6) with \(\lambda=1/2\) and VAdam.
  2. **Scalability sanity check:** Train a CNN with orthogonal kernels (thousands of \(3 \times 3\) matrices). Measure throughput (samples/sec) vs. RGD/RSDM. Expect order-of-magnitude speedup.
  3. **Feasibility stress test:** Train an O-ViT with many orthogonal attention matrices. Monitor \(\|XX^\top - I\|\) per layer. Confirm it remains comparable to RGD (retraction method) and far below Landing's fluctuations. Use float64 to rule out precision artifacts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can POGO be extended to manifolds beyond the Stiefel manifold?**  
   The derivation of the landing polynomial relies on the orthogonality constraint \(XX^\top = I\). Extending to Grassmannian, SPD, or symplectic manifolds would require different geometric tools and may not yield a simple closed-form solution.

2. **Is the linearity requirement for the base optimizer fundamental?**  
   Standard Adam is non-linear due to elementwise normalization. It is unclear whether a modification can preserve Adam's adaptive benefits while satisfying the linearity condition essential for tangent-space equivalence.

3. **Does exact λ solving provide tangible benefits?**  
   The paper uses \(\lambda=1/2\) throughout. The asymptotic bound may be conservative; for larger learning rates or early training, the exact quartic solution might reduce manifold drift and improve convergence or final accuracy, but at higher computational cost.

4. **Can POGO start from an arbitrary off-manifold initialization?**  
   The theoretical guarantee assumes \(X_0\) is exactly orthogonal. Practitioners often use random Gaussian initializations. It is unknown whether POGO can safely start from such points or if a dedicated warm-start is needed.

5. **What are the convergence rates under stochastic gradients?**  
   The paper provides deterministic convergence analysis. Stochastic settings (minibatches) are common in deep learning, and the rates of POGO relative to Riemannian SGD or stochastic Landing remain unexplored.

## Limitations
- The guarantee for \(\lambda=1/2\) is asymptotic (\(o(\xi^7)\)); in practice, when \(\xi\) approaches 1, early iterations may exhibit noticeable manifold drift.
- The exact quartic solver is not described in detail, raising concerns about numerical stability and root selection, especially in low precision.
- Hyperparameter values for baseline comparators (e.g., learning rates, momentum) are not fully reported, making it difficult to exactly reproduce the paper's comparative results.
