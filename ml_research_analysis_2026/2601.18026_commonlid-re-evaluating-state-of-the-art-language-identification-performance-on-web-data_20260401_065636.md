# CommonLID: Re-evaluating State-of-the-Art Language Identification Performance on Web Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.18026
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18026
- **Reference count:** 27
- **Primary result:** Evaluation of eight SOTA LID models shows a stark performance drop on the new web-domain benchmark CommonLID (e.g., GlotLID: 60.4% macro-F1) compared to traditional clean benchmarks like FLORES (96.5%), exposing significant overestimation in current evaluations.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces **CommonLID**, a large-scale, community-annotated benchmark for language identification (LID) on authentic web data. Covering 109 languages and 373k lines, it reveals that state-of-the-art LID models, which perform near-perfectly on clean, formal corpora like FLORES, suffer severe performance degradation on noisy web text. This performance gap highlights a critical domain mismatch between existing benchmarks and real-world deployment conditions. The authors openly release the dataset and code to drive progress, particularly for low-resource languages, while acknowledging limitations from pre-annotation bias and single-annotator labeling.

## Method Summary
CommonLID was constructed by sampling documents from Common Crawl (2024-2025) and MADLAD-400 using three existing LID models (fastText, OpenLID, GlotLID) for pre-filtering. Native speakers annotated line-level language labels via a modified Dynabench interface, incentivized by authorship. Post-processing reconciled multiple valid labels (e.g., macro- vs. micro-language), filtered short/non-linguistic lines, and performed per-language audits. The benchmark was used to evaluate eight pre-trained LID models (e.g., GlotLID, FUN-LangID, CLD2) alongside five existing datasets (FLORES+, UDHR-LID, SmolSent, Bible, Social Media). Performance was compared using macro-averaged F1 and false positive rate (FPR), with label normalization to ISO 639-3 and class balancing for skewed datasets.

## Key Results
*   **Stark domain gap:** All eight evaluated models show a massive performance drop on CommonLID vs. clean benchmarks. Top model GlotLID scores 96.5% macro-F1 on FLORES but only 60.4% on CommonLID.
*   **No model exceeds 75% F1:** Even the best-performing model (GlotLID) achieves at most ~75% macro-F1 across all tested datasets when considering full coverage, underscoring the difficulty of the web-domain task.
*   **Coverage vs. accuracy trade-off:** Models with broader language coverage (e.g., FUN-LangID, 1634 languages) often have lower per-language scores on the languages they share with higher-accuracy models, revealing a fundamental evaluation tension.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Standard LID evaluations overestimate real-world performance because they use clean, formal texts that do not match the noisy web domain, leading to a stark drop when models are deployed on web data.
*   **Mechanism:** LID models are typically trained and evaluated on curated corpora (e.g., Wikipedia, Bible translations, legal documents) that lack web-specific noise (HTML artifacts, code-switching, informal language). When tested on authentic web text, performance degrades significantly, as exposed by CommonLID.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract]: "evaluation of eight LID models shows a stark performance drop on CommonLID: GlotLID achieves 60.4% macro-F1 versus 96.5% on FLORES."
    *   [section 2.2]: "Most systems are evaluated on clean, high-quality datasets such as FLORES and UDHR... They do not include many of the artifacts of web data."
    *   [corpus: Table 1]: Shows consistently lower macro-F1 scores on CommonLID compared to other evaluation sets across all eight models.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** The sampling methodology using existing LID models biases CommonLID towards languages and registers already covered by those models, limiting representation of the lowest-resource languages and reinforcing existing coverage gaps.
*   **Mechanism:** To make annotation feasible, the authors pre-filtered Common Crawl documents using three existing LID models. This selection only includes text recognized by these systems, which themselves are biased towards languages with more training data (e.g., religious texts). Consequently, languages absent from these models are severely underrepresented.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 3]: "We used three LID models for selection... Samples are taken... with each of the three LID models... It also biases the data to genres and language registers which match the model’s training data."
    *   [section 7]: "We used three existing models and one existing dataset in select samples to be validated... This inherently biases our dataset to the languages, genres and language registers supported by these models and dataset."
    *   [corpus: Figure 1 & Table 4]: Some languages have very few lines (e.g., ace, pol, grc, gle with 1 line each), indicating incomplete coverage for the lowest-resource languages.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Community-driven annotation by native speakers yields higher-quality labels for many languages, but the lack of multiple annotators per language prevents reliability assessment and may allow subtle errors or dialectal biases to go undetected.
*   **Mechanism:** Annotators were recruited from the NLP community and offered authorship incentives. They annotated full documents in their native language. This leverages deep linguistic knowledge. However, for most languages only one annotator participated, so there is no inter-annotator agreement metric, and latent errors or ambiguities (e.g., macro- vs. micro-language labels, code-switching) may persist.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 3]: "Recruitment: Researchers who speak or work with these languages... We also partnered with grassroots NLP organisations... offering authorship was a key design decision."
    *   [section 4.1]: "we were unable to find more than one volunteer native speaker for many of them. This made it impossible to conduct an inter-annotator agreement study for our data."
    *   [section 4.1]: "We found two categories of annotation where we could not reconcile the distinct labels... The existence of multiple valid labels for a single line is a key limitation..."

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Language Identification (LID)**
    *   *Why needed here:* Understand the fundamental task being benchmarked and why it is particularly challenging for low-resource and web domains.
    *   *Quick check question:* What are the typical features used by LID systems (e.g., character n-grams, subwords), and why might they fail on code-switched or noisy text?
*   **Concept: Evaluation Metrics (Macro-F1, False Positive Rate)**
    *   *Why needed here:* The paper relies on macro-averaged F1 to compare models fairly across languages with vastly different support; FPR indicates false alarms. Understanding these prevents misinterpretation of results.
    *   *Quick check question:* Why is macro-averaging preferred over micro-averaging when evaluating LID across many languages with unequal representation?
*   **Concept: Domain Shift / Covariate Mismatch**
    *   *Why needed here:* The central finding is a performance drop due to domain mismatch between training/evaluation sets and real web data. This concept explains why models fail in new environments.
    *   *Quick check question:* If a model is trained on Bible text but tested on social media, what types of linguistic features (e.g., vocabulary, syntax, orthography) might cause it to fail?
*   **Concept: Low-Resource Language Challenges**
    *   *Why needed here:* CommonLID focuses on expanding evaluation to many low-documentation languages, which face data scarcity and script diversity issues.
    *   *Quick check question:* Why are models like GlotLID, which are trained on religious texts (Bible/Quran), likely to perform poorly on informal web text from the same low-resource languages?
*   **Concept: Annotation Bias**
    *   *Why needed here:* The paper's own sampling method introduces a selection bias that affects which languages and text genres are represented in the final benchmark.
    *   *Quick check question:* How does using an existing LID system to pre-filter data for a new LID benchmark create a circular dependency?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Common Crawl/MADLAD Sampling -> Pre-annotation (fastText/OpenLID/GlotLID) -> Native Speaker Annotation (Dynabench) -> Post-processing (dedup, filter, reconcile labels) -> Evaluation (model inference, metric computation)
*   **Critical path:**
    1.  Recruit sufficient native-speaking annotators for target languages (most time-sensitive).
    2.  Collect annotations through the platform; monitor leaderboard to ensure engagement.
    3.  Perform post-processing: reconcile multiple labels, filter low-quality lines, audit per language.
    4.  Run evaluations and analyze results.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   *Openness vs. bias:* Using pre-annotation enables coverage but reinforces existing model biases.
    *   *Community authorship vs. consistency:* Authorship incentivizes participation but complicates standardization; single annotator per language limits agreement.
    *   *Line-level vs. context:* Line-level labels are simpler but lose document-level context that could resolve ambiguities.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Many languages with <100 lines, indicating poor annotator availability.
    *   High proportion of lines with multiple labels or English-filter exemptions.
    *   Models that excel on FLORES/UDHR but severely underperform on CommonLID (e.g., >30% F1 drop) reveal domain overfitting.
    *   Spurious language classes discovered during audit (e.g., gux, swe, tag removed).
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  *Cross-dataset evaluation:* Run GlotLID, FUN-LangID, and CLD2 on FLORES, SmolSent, and CommonLID; plot F1 distributions to visualize domain gap.
    2.  *Coverage analysis:* For each language in CommonLID, compute the F1 of models that support it; identify clusters of languages with consistently low scores (e.g., certain families or scripts).
    3.  *Pre-annotation bias probe:* Compare language distributions in CommonLID vs. a random sample of Common Crawl (if feasible) to quantify how much pre-filtering skewed representation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1: Fair evaluation across variable coverage.** How can we design a fair evaluation protocol for comparing LID models with vastly different language coverage, especially when high-coverage models may have lower per-language accuracy on low-resource languages? The paper notes the difficulty due to non-overlapping language classes and that a "more reliable but lower-coverage LID model may be preferable depending on the application." A formal metric incorporating a user-specified utility function or cost model for missing languages is needed.
*   **Open Question 2: Annotation methodology for ambiguous web text.** What annotation methodology best captures the inherent ambiguity of multilingual web text (e.g., code-switching, macro/micro-language distinctions) while producing reliable, consistent line-level labels? The authors kept multiple valid labels for some lines, calling this a "key limitation." Evidence from a controlled study comparing protocols (single vs. multi-label, line vs. document context) with multiple annotators is required.
*   **Open Question 3: Bias-free sampling for low-resource languages.** How can we collect representative web-domain text for low-resource languages without relying on existing LID systems, which are biased towards the domains and registers they were trained on (e.g., religious text)? Pre-annotation is a "necessary compromise" that creates a circular dependency. Alternative strategies (URL heuristics, community-sourced lists, unsupervised crawling with verification) must be developed and assessed.
*   **Open Question 4: True generalization of SOTA models.** Do current SOTA LID models (like GlotLID) truly generalize to the diverse, noisy web domain for long-tail languages, or does their high performance on Bible/FLORES data reflect overfitting to a narrow, clean, religious-domain training distribution? The performance drop suggests overfitting, but a multi-domain, large-scale human-annotated benchmark for the long tail is needed to establish true generalization.
*   **Open Question 5: Evaluation reliability for single-annotator data.** How can we reliably evaluate LID for languages where only a single native speaker annotator was available? Without inter-annotator agreement, label noise and ambiguity threaten benchmark validity. Evidence from methodologies to estimate single-annotator quality (e.g., expert re-annotation subsets, probabilistic models) or large-scale efforts to secure multiple annotators for a core set is necessary.

## Limitations
*   **Pre-annotation bias:** Selecting documents via existing LID models likely overrepresents languages and genres already covered, underrepresenting the lowest-resource languages.
*   **Single-annotation reliability:** Most languages have only one native-speaker annotator, preventing inter-annotator agreement analysis and potentially masking dialectal or ambiguous cases.
*   **Incomplete coverage:** Many languages have very few lines (e.g., <100), indicating difficulties in recruitment or data availability, limiting benchmark utility for those languages.

## Confidence
*   **Performance overestimation claim:** **High**. Consistent macro-F1 drops across eight models (e.g., GlotLID: 60.4% on CommonLID vs 96.5% on FLORES) strongly support the claim.
*   **Benchmark bias from pre-annotation claim:** **Medium**. Authors openly acknowledge the issue, but its quantitative impact on the reported performance gap remains unmeasured.
*   **Sufficiency of community annotation claim:** **Medium-Low**. While native-speaker involvement adds credibility, the absence of agreement metrics and the presence of multi-label conflicts leave residual uncertainty about label quality.
*   **Generalization failure of SOTA models claim:** **High**. The stark and consistent drop across all evaluated models provides strong evidence that current SOTA does not generalize to the web domain.

## Next Checks
1.  **Quantify pre-annotation bias:** Sample a random subset of Common Crawl (using uniform language distribution if possible) and compare its language/genre profile to CommonLID to measure selection skew.
2.  **Assess annotator reliability:** Select 5–10 languages and recruit two additional native speakers to re-annotate a random document subset; compute Cohen’s κ to gauge consistency and identify systematic errors.
3.  **Mitigation experiment:** Fine-tune a top model (e.g., GlotLID) on a balanced mix of CommonLID and traditional datasets; evaluate whether the domain gap narrows on an unseen web holdout set.
