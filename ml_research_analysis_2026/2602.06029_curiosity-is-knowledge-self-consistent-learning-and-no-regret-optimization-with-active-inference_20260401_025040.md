# Curiosity is Knowledge: Self-Consistent Learning and No-Regret Optimization with Active Inference
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06029
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06029
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A single sufficient curiosity condition in Active Inference simultaneously guarantees posterior consistency and bounded cumulative regret.

## Executive Summary
Active Inference (AIF) provides a unified decision-making framework that minimizes Expected Free Energy (EFE), naturally balancing epistemic (information-seeking) and pragmatic (goal-oriented) pressures. This paper presents a core theoretical result: a single condition on the curiosity coefficient—ensuring that epistemic value is never overwhelmed—guarantees both posterior consistency (convergence of beliefs to truth) and bounded cumulative regret (asymptotically optimal performance). The analysis identifies dependencies on initial uncertainty, model discriminability, and heuristic alignment. The theory is validated on two real-world tasks: constrained system identification (locating a plume source under sensor saturation) and composite Bayesian optimization (learning and optimizing under an unknown linear preference). The findings position curiosity as the essential mechanism linking coherent learning with efficient optimization.

## Method Summary
The authors implement an Active Inference agent that selects actions by maximizing an acquisition function  
α(x|D_t) = β_t I(s;(x,y)|D_{t-1}) − E_{p(y|x,D_{t-1})}[h_t(y)],  
where I(s;(x,y)|D) is mutual information (epistemic value), h_t(y) is a heuristic encoding the task’s pragmatic cost (e.g., constraint violation or learned preference), and β_t is the curiosity coefficient. After observing y from the environment, the posterior over latent variables is updated. For **constrained system identification**, latent s are discrete parameters, h_t(y)=I(C(y)>0) (saturation penalty), and mutual information is computed exactly from discrete probabilities. For **composite Bayesian optimization**, the function f(x) is modeled by a Gaussian process; the unknown linear preference a is learned via pairwise probit queries, and mutual information is approximated using predictive variance. The sufficient curiosity condition (β large enough) is shown to yield vanishing posterior error mass w_t and sublinear cumulative regret R_T. Experiments fix β and vary it across runs (e.g., β∈{0.05,0.8,2.0} for system ID; β∈{0.1,1,10} for BO).

## Key Results
- **Unified guarantee:** A single sufficient curiosity condition (β large enough to keep epistemic value non‑negligible) simultaneously ensures posterior consistency and bounded cumulative regret, proving that exploration and exploitation are intrinsically linked via curiosity.
- **Dependencies:** The required β depends on initial uncertainty, the model’s ability to distinguish hypotheses (observational distinguishability), and the alignment of heuristic h_t with the true objective/constraint.
- **Empirical validation:** On both tasks, satisfying the sufficient curiosity condition produces decreasing posterior error and sublinear regret, while insufficient β leads to myopic exploitation, stalled learning, and linear regret growth.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The acquisition function α(x) balances two pressures: the epistemic term I(s;(x,y)) drives actions that maximally reduce uncertainty about latent variables, while the pragmatic term E[h_t(y)] steers toward outcomes with low heuristic cost. The curiosity coefficient β scales the epistemic term. If β is too small, pragmatic concerns dominate early, leading to exploitation without improvement: the posterior may stabilize on wrong beliefs and regret accumulates linearly. However, if β is sufficiently large so that the epistemic term never becomes negligible, the agent continuously seeks informative data, making the posterior converge (consistency). The same condition, under reasonable heuristic alignment, also keeps the cumulative pragmatic cost bounded because the agent only occasionally sacrifices optimality for information. Thus β acts as a single knob that simultaneously preserves learning and ensures efficient optimization. The theoretical proof formalizes this by relating β to a lower bound on information gain and a discriminability constant.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Prior(s) → Posterior q_t(s) (updated each step) → Mutual Information I(s;(x,y)|D_{t-1}) and Heuristic h_t(y) → Acquisition α(x|D_t)=β I − E[h] → Action x_t → Environment → Observation y_t → Update D_t (add (x_t,y_t)) → Loop.  
In composite BO: Preference queries → Preference model (probit) → Updated h_t(y) (feeds into acquisition).

**Critical path:**  
1. Start with prior and empty data.  
2. Compute α(x) for candidate x using current posterior, MI estimator, and heuristic h_t.  
3. Select x_t = argmax α.  
4. Execute x_t, observe y_t.  
5. Append (x_t,y_t) to D_t and update posterior.  
6. If h_t is learned, periodically query preferences and update the preference model.  
7. Repeat from step 2.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **β level:** Higher β increases exploration and learning speed but may delay optimization; the theory gives a lower bound, but too large β can waste effort.  
- **MI estimator:** Exact MI is feasible only for small discrete s or GP with closed form; approximations (e.g., nested MC) introduce bias/variance that can break theoretical guarantees.  
- **Heuristic h_t:** Must align with true objective/regret; misalignment (static or learned) can cause high regret even with sufficient β.  
- **Update frequency of h_t:** Updating too often destabilizes acquisition; too infrequently may delay correction of heuristic errors.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Posterior error w_t plateaus → likely β too low or MI estimator failure.  
- Regret grows linearly despite high β → heuristic h_t misaligned or discriminability condition violated.  
- All α(x) values nearly equal → MI estimator returning near‑zero; check predictive variance.  
- Preference model converges quickly but regret remains high → probit noise λ too high or query frequency too low.

**Exactly 3 first experiments:**  
1. Reproduce system identification (plume) results: implement simulator using Table C.1, enforce saturation constraint, run β∈{0.05,0.8,2.0} with 5 seeds, plot w_t vs. iterations (target Fig. 3).

3. Ablate preference learning: vary probit noise λ (e.g., {0.1,1,10}) and query frequency (every 1,5,10 BO steps), record R_T and preference model posterior variance to identify sensitive settings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can the curiosity coefficient β_t be scheduled adaptively over time while still guaranteeing both posterior consistency and bounded cumulative regret?
- **Open Question 2:** How robust are the guarantees of posterior consistency and no-regret optimization to model misspecification (e.g., incorrect kernel, likelihood, or latent space structure)?
- **Open Question 3:** Can the theoretical guarantees be extended beyond discrete latent parameters (Theorem 5.1) and Gaussian process models (Theorem 6.1) to other common Bayesian models such as continuous parameter spaces, Bayesian neural networks, or non-Gaussian likelihoods?
- **Open Question 4:** How can the "observational distinguishability" condition (Assumption ii in Theorem 5.1), which couples the energy function h_t(y) with the true parameter, be checked or enforced in practical applications where h_t(y) is learned or heuristic?
- **Open Question 5:** How do the sample complexity and regret bounds scale with problem dimensionality (e.g., input dimension in Bayesian optimization, number of latent hypotheses), and does the required level of curiosity change in high dimensions?

## Limitations
- The theoretical guarantees assume exact mutual information computation; for high‑dimensional problems (e.g., 40‑D BO) the required MI estimator is unspecified, and approximations may violate the sufficient curiosity condition.
- The composite BO protocol relies on preference learning details (probit noise λ, query frequency, heuristic update schedule) that are not fully described, jeopardizing reproducibility and potentially affecting the pragmatic term’s alignment.
- Observational distinguishability, a key assumption for posterior consistency, lacks a practical diagnostic when h_t is learned, making it difficult to verify in real applications.

## Confidence
- Posterior consistency and no‑regret theoretical guarantees → **High** (rigorous proofs within stated assumptions)
- Experimental reproducibility of real‑world tasks → **Medium‑Low** (missing implementation specifics for MI estimation, preference learning, and heuristic updates)
- Robustness to model misspecification and high‑dimensional scaling → **Low** (unquantified; assumptions may not hold in practice)
- Extending sufficient curiosity to adaptive β schedules → **Low** (identified as open problem)
