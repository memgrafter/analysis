# Variation is the Norm: Embracing Sociolinguistics in NLP

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24222
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24222
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Actively incorporating orthographic variation during training yields more robust NLP models that generalize across standard and non-standard test sets, outperforming models trained on a single variety.

## Executive Summary
Natural Language Processing typically treats language variation—such as non-standard spellings—as noise to be normalized away. This paper challenges that paradigm by arguing that sociolinguistic variation should be embraced as a meaningful signal to improve model robustness. Using Luxembourgish orthographic variation as a case study, the authors manipulate datasets across eight classification tasks to represent standard, non-standard (destandardized), and combined (standard + non-standard) varieties. Fine-tuning BERT-based models (LuxemBERT, mBERT) on the combined dataset consistently yields the highest performance on all test sets, while models trained solely on non-standard data perform worst. The findings demonstrate that normalization alone is insufficient; exposure to multiple realizations during training helps models learn invariant semantic content.

## Method Summary
The study fine-tunes two pre-trained models—LuxemBERT (Luxembourgish-specific) and mBERT (multilingual)—on seven classification tasks (intent classification, part-of-speech tagging, named entity recognition, sentiment classification, comment moderation, topic classification, Winograd NLI). For each task, three training data variants are created: (1) standard, (2) non-standard via a dictionary-based destandardization algorithm (for originally standard tasks) or via a normalization pipeline (for inherently non-standard tasks), and (3) combined (standard + non-standard). Hyperparameters follow a consistent setup (e.g., batch size 16, learning rate 5e-5 or 2e-5, 3–5 epochs). Each model is evaluated on all three corresponding test sets (standard, non-standard, combined) using weighted F1 score, with results averaged over five random seeds.

## Key Results
- Combined-training models significantly outperformed single-variety models on every test set across all eight tasks. For intent classification, LuxemBERT fine-tuned on combined data achieved 68.56% F1 on standard test and 65.81% on non-standard test, versus 57.97% and 46.48% for standard-only training.
- Models trained exclusively on non-standard data performed worst in nearly all tasks, showing large performance drops on both standard and non-standard test sets.
- Normalization (converting non-standard to standard) proved less effective than the combined approach; normalized models did not reliably close the performance gap on authentic non-standard text.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Learning Invariant Representations
- **Claim:** Training on a combined dataset teaches the model to recognize semantic content across orthographic variants, improving out-of-distribution robustness.
- **Mechanism:** Exposure to multiple surface realizations of the same concept forces the encoder to develop representations less sensitive to specific spellings and more focused on shared linguistic and semantic features.
- **Core assumption:** Standard and non-standard Luxembourgish share core linguistic structure, differing primarily at the orthographic level.
- **Evidence anchors:** Cross-variant performance gains are consistent (abstract, section 4.4); neighboring work on Nigerian Pidgin shows similar benefits from multi-variety training.
- **Break condition:** If standard and non-standard varieties diverge in deep syntax/semantics (beyond orthography), the approach may fail without more sophisticated modeling.

### Mechanism 2: Destabilizing Effect of Non-Standard-Only Training
- **Claim:** Models trained solely on non-standard data perform worst because high orthographic diversity impedes stable representation learning.
- **Mechanism:** The non-standard dataset contains a wider distribution of spelling forms, some rare or idiosyncratic, leading to overfitting or failure to converge on a coherent feature space due to increased intra-class variance.
- **Core assumption:** Non-standard data's orthographic diversity acts as a form of label noise when it is the only training signal.
- **Evidence anchors:** Non-standard-only models are worst in nearly all tasks (section 4.4); destandardization introduces high surface-level variance (WER 60–83%, section 4.2).
- **Break condition:** If non-standard data were exceptionally high-quality/volume or used with massive capacity/augmentation, the gap might close.

### Mechanism 3: Normalization Erases Social Meaning
- **Claim:** Normalization alone is insufficient because it discards sociolinguistically meaningful variation; the combined approach preserves this social signal while mitigating destabilizing effects.
- **Mechanism:** Normalization maps all variants to a standard form, erasing indexical information (e.g., informality, identity). The combined approach retains the standard form as an anchor while exposing the model to variation, allowing it to learn a many-to-one mapping from variants to meaning.
- **Core assumption:** Orthographic variation in Luxembourgish carries non-random social meaning relevant for tasks like sentiment or comment moderation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Authors note non-standard variants carry social meaning (section 2.2); on comment moderation, normalized models show minimal difference whereas combined models gain on non-standard test (section 4.4).
- **Break condition:** For tasks where only propositional content matters (e.g., factual extraction), the social signal might be pure noise, reducing the benefit.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Tokenizer -> Pretrained Encoder (LuxemBERT or mBERT) -> Fine-tuning Head -> Training Data Pipeline -> Evaluation
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Data Preparation: For each task, acquire standard dataset; apply manipulation (destandardization or normalization) to create non-standard and combined versions; preserve splits.  
  2. Model Loading: Instantiate task-specific model (sequence or token classification) from Hugging Face using LuxemBERT or mBERT.  
  3. Fine-tuning Loop: For each model & data variant, run supervised fine-tuning for 3–5 epochs with batch size 16 and learning rate 5e-5/2e-5; save best checkpoint on dev set.  
  4. Evaluation: Load each checkpoint and evaluate on all three test set variants (standard, non-standard, combined); compute weighted F1.  
  5. Aggregation: Repeat steps 3–4 with 5 random seeds; report mean ± std.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Combined Data vs. Standard-Only: Combined increases training complexity but aims for better generalization.  
  - LuxemBERT vs. mBERT: LuxemBERT is in-domain pre-trained (likely better), mBERT is multilingual (may have interference).  
  - Destandardization Algorithm: Real dictionary-based destandardization captures authentic patterns but is limited to Spellchecker.lu's variant set.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Catastrophic failure on non-standard test: standard-trained model shows >15% F1 drop on non-standard vs. standard test.  
  - No benefit from combined training: combined model not statistically significantly better than standard-trained on both test sets.  
  - Extreme instability (high std-dev across seeds): fine-tuning setup too sensitive; may need more seeds/warmup/clipping.  
  - Normalization appears "successful": similar performance between normalized-trained and standard-trained models, indicating normalization did not bridge domain gap.
- **First three experiments:**  
  1. Replicate Core Result (Intent Classification): Fine-tune LuxemBERT on standard, non-standard, and combined IC data; evaluate each on all three test sets; expect combined model to excel everywhere.  
  2. Task-Type Analysis: Replicate for a token classification task (POS) and a different sequence classification task (WNLI/TC); verify if combined benefit is stronger for sequence tasks vs. token tasks.  
  3. Normalization Efficacy Check: For Comment Moderation (CM), fine-tune on normalized version and original non-standard; evaluate both on original non-standard test; expect minimal difference, confirming normalization's ineffectiveness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the effectiveness of combined standard and non-standard training generalize to languages with fundamentally different sociolinguistic variation profiles (e.g., diglossic systems like Arabic, pluricentric languages like English, or languages with non-orthographic variation)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state their framework is universally applicable but only demonstrate it on Luxembourgish orthographic variation, noting the need for broader validation.
- **Why unresolved:** The Luxembourgish case involves specific conditions (e.g., ongoing standardization, heavy French/German contact) that may not capture challenges in other variation types (e.g., morphological, syntactic, or code-switching). Without cross-linguistic replication, it is unclear if the combined approach consistently outperforms single-variety training.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi-language study replicating the experimental design (fine-tuning on standard, non-standard, and combined data) across diverse languages representing different variation dimensions, measuring performance differentials and identifying which sociolinguistic factors predict the success of the combined approach.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does combined training yield substantial improvements for sequence classification tasks but only marginal gains for token classification tasks like POS tagging, and what task characteristics mediate the impact of variation?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Results show combined training significantly boosts intent classification (sequence) but only slightly improves POS tagging (token) across models (Tables 2, 3, and Appendix).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze whether token-level tasks are inherently more robust to orthographic noise, whether label granularity reduces sensitivity, or if variation affects contextual representations differently. This gap prevents understanding when variation-inclusive training is most critical.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying task design (e.g., token vs. sequence, fine-grained vs. coarse labels) with matched datasets, error analysis comparing failure modes on variant forms, and probing studies to quantify how variation impacts layer-wise representations for different tasks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do current lexical normalization techniques fail to preserve the social meaning encoded in non-standard orthography, and how can normalization pipelines be redesigned to retain indexical cues while achieving standardization?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note normalization is less effective than combined training and explicitly state that non-standard variants carry social meaning (Section 2.2), which normalization erases.
- **Why unresolved:** While normalization performance is compared, there is no direct evaluation of what social information is lost (e.g., regional identity, formality). Normalization tools like spellux target canonical forms without tracking social-semiotic signals.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Human evaluation studies where annotators judge social attributes (e.g., speaker background, stance) of normalized versus original texts; fine-tuning models on normalized data and testing on tasks requiring social inference (e.g., dialect identification); developing normalization systems that output multiple standard variants annotated with likely social connotations.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What operationalizable set of sociolinguistic criteria should be documented in dataset "language cards" to most effectively predict and mitigate variation-related failures in NLP pipelines?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The framework proposes nine criteria (Section 3.1) and suggests a "sociolinguistic language card" (Section 5) but does not prioritize or validate which criteria are most actionable for NLP practitioners.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear which criteria (e.g., degree of codification, attitudes, contact situation) directly correlate with specific NLP vulnerabilities (e.g., tokenization errors, evaluation bias). Without empirical weighting, the card may be too vague or burdensome.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large-scale correlation studies linking quantified criteria scores (from linguistic surveys, policy documents) to performance drops in diverse NLP tasks across datasets; iterative design and user testing of a minimal checklist with NLP researchers to assess utility in diagnosing robustness issues.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does synthetic destandardization accurately reflect natural orthographic variation, or does it introduce distributional biases that limit the robustness gains from combined training?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The destandardization algorithm uses real frequency data from Spellchecker.lu but the authors acknowledge it may not cover the full variation space (Limitations).
- **Why unresolved:** Synthetic variation is derived from a specific user-generated corpus (spellchecker corrections) which may overrepresent certain error types or user demographics. Real-world non-standard text may include rare variants, context-sensitive choices, or multimedia-influenced orthography not captured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of variant distributions in destandardized data versus naturally occurring non-standard corpora (e.g., social media, forums); performance evaluation of models trained on combined data when tested on authentic non-standard text versus destandardized test sets; user studies to assess naturalness of destandardized texts.

## Limitations
- Exact access mechanisms for the seven datasets (URLs, licensing) are unspecified, hindering faithful replication.
- Implementation details and public availability of the destandardization algorithm (Lutgen et al., 2025) and the Spellchecker.lu variant dictionary are unknown.
- Precise training configurations beyond basic hyperparameters (e.g., optimizer type, warmup steps, gradient clipping) are not fully documented.
- The destandardization process may not capture the full authentic variation space, limiting generalizability of the "non-standard" condition.
- The social-indexical signal hypothesis receives limited direct empirical support; benefits may partly stem from simple data volume increase.

## Confidence
- **Primary Claim (Combined training > single-variety training):** High. Strong, consistent results across eight tasks and two models with clear performance gaps.
- **Mechanism 1 (Invariant semantic learning):** Medium. Cross-variant gains are direct evidence, but the mechanism is inferential; a data volume ablation is missing.
- **Mechanism 2 (Non-standard-only instability):** Medium. Poor performance of non-standard-only models is robust, but attribution solely to "linguistic noise" is post-hoc; data sparsity/quality factors are not fully disentangled.
- **Mechanism 3 (Normalization discards social signal):** Low. Core sociolinguistic premise has weakest direct empirical support; the CM task result is suggestive but not conclusive.
