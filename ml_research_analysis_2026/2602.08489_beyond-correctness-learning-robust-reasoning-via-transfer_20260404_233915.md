# Beyond Correctness: Learning Robust Reasoning via Transfer
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08489
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08489
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: RLTR improves Maj@64 on MATH-500 by 3.6 percentage points over RLVR and achieves RLVR's average accuracy with 2.5× fewer training steps.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Reinforcement Learning with Transferable Reward (RLTR), an extension to Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) that optimizes not only for final answer correctness but also for reasoning process robustness. RLTR adds a transfer reward: a partial reasoning trace from the generator is completed by a separate frozen receiver model, and success is measured by the receiver's final answer correctness. This incentivizes the generator to produce reasoning prefixes that are stable, reusable, and generalizable. On MATH-500, RLTR improves majority@64 by 3.6 percentage points over RLVR and reaches RLVR's average accuracy with only 40% of the training steps. Similar gains in consistency and accuracy appear on harder benchmarks (AIME2024, AMC23) and an out-of-domain science benchmark (GPQA). RLTR also preserves solution diversity (Pass@K) whereas RLVR reduces it, and training dynamics show that transferability correlates strongly with improved majority voting performance.

## Method Summary
RLTR modifies the RLVR framework by incorporating a transfer reward component. During training, for each generated reasoning trace from the generator model (M_gen), a random truncation ratio τ ~ Uniform(0.3, 0.9) is sampled. The prefix up to that point, concatenated with the original problem, is fed to a frozen receiver model (M_rcv) which generates a continuation. The transfer reward R_trans is 1 if the receiver's final answer matches the ground truth, else 0. The total reward for RL is R = a * R_ans + R_fmt + t * R_trans, with weights a=0.1 and t=1.0. The generator is updated using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a KL penalty against a reference policy. The approach uses a training subset of MATH (difficulty ≥3) and evaluates on several benchmarks using majority voting over 64 samples.

## Key Results
- On MATH-500, RLTR improves Maj@64 by 3.6 percentage points over RLVR and achieves RLVR's average accuracy with 2.5× fewer training steps.
- Gains in consistency and accuracy extend to harder benchmarks (AIME2024, AMC23) and out-of-domain tasks (GPQA).
- RLTR preserves solution diversity (Pass@K) whereas RLVR reduces it; transferability during training correlates with Maj@K improvement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The transfer reward incentivizes the generator to produce reasoning prefixes that are *intrinsically robust and reusable*, not just correct at the endpoint.
- **Mechanism:** By truncating the generator's reasoning trace and requiring a separate, frozen receiver model to complete it to a correct answer (Eq. 3), the generator is penalized for producing fragile, generator-specific, or incomplete intermediate reasoning. The loss function (Eq. 5) directly optimizes for this cross-model success probability.
- **Core assumption:** A reasoning prefix that reliably guides a *different* model to the correct solution contains stable, generalizable, and semantically meaningful steps, making the overall reasoning process more robust to perturbations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "operationalizes robustness via transfer reward that tests whether a partial reasoning prefix from one model can be completed by a separate receiver model to reach a verifiably correct answer."
  - [section 3.2] "R_trans(ygen, M_rcv) = 1[answer(y_rcv) = y_gt]" and truncation ratio τ sampled uniformly between 0.3 and 0.9.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., "CLIPO", "From <Answer> to <Think>") identify RLVR's neglect of intermediate reasoning correctness as a key gap RLTR addresses.
- **Break condition:** If the receiver model's capability is too low (e.g., smaller architecture, weaker base), the transfer reward signal becomes uninformative or noisy, weakening the incentive. The generator may then learn to cater to the receiver's limitations rather than produce genuinely robust reasoning.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Optimizing for transferability *implicitly improves multi-sample consistency and sampling robustness* (higher Maj@K), as the same robust prefixes increase the probability that diverse samples converge on the same correct answer.
- **Mechanism:** Reasoning that is transferable must be stable under truncation/continuation by another model. This stability property also means that minor stochastic variations during the generator's own sampling (different reasoning paths) are more likely to contain shared, correct intermediate states, leading to a higher chance that majority voting among samples yields the correct answer.
- **Core assumption:** The property of being a "good" prefix for another model is mathematically correlated with the property of being a "stable" prefix across the generator's own sampling distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.2] Figure 2 shows RLTR's transferability curve tracks its Maj@64 curve closely; RLVR's transferability drops while its Maj@64 degrades in later training.
  - [section 4.1] RLTR's gains in Maj@K are substantially larger than its gains in average accuracy (e.g., MATH-500 Maj@64: +1.6pp vs Avg: +0.8pp over RLVR).
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence weak; surrounding work links process supervision to consistency but RLTR's specific transfer-reward mechanism to Maj@K is novel per the paper.
- **Break condition:** If the correlation between transferability and self-consistency breaks (e.g., on a distribution where receiver and generator have fundamentally different inductive biases), Maj@K improvements may not materialize even if transferability scores rise.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Transfer reward provides a *denser, more generalizable learning signal* than pure outcome reward, leading to faster convergence and better final performance with fewer steps.
- **Mechanism:** Final-answer correctness (R_ans) is a sparse signal that only arrives at the very end of a long generation. The transfer reward (R_trans) is computed on partial trajectories, providing an intermediate feedback signal. More importantly, it is *verifiable* (like R_ans) but focuses on the *process* rather than the outcome, avoiding reward model hacking while offering step-level pressure.
- **Core assumption:** The signal from a successful cross-model continuation is a valid and computationally cheaper proxy for the quality of the intermediate reasoning, compared to training a full Process Reward Model (PRM).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "reaches comparable performance in substantially fewer training steps... with roughly 2.5× fewer training steps."
  - [section 4.2] Figure 2a shows RLTR reaches RLVR's accuracy level in ~60% of the steps.
  - [section 4.4] Table 4 confirms RLTR achieves the same accuracy with fewer total FLOPs despite per-step overhead.
- **Break condition:** If the receiver model is significantly more capable than the generator, the transfer reward may be too easy to satisfy, providing a weak gradient. Conversely, if the receiver is much weaker, the signal becomes unachievable, leading to poor learning.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Generator (M_gen) -> Transfer Rollout Module (truncation + frozen Receiver M_rcv) -> Reward Combiner (R_ans, R_fmt, R_trans) -> RL Optimizer (GRPO) -> Generator

**Critical path**:
1. For each batch of problems, M_gen generates full reasoning trajectories.
2. Compute R_ans (final answer correctness) and R_fmt (format validity).
3. For each trajectory, sample τ, truncate to prefix, and feed prefix+problem to M_rcv to get continuation y_rcv.
4. Compute R_trans from y_rcv's final answer.
5. Form combined reward R_RLTR = a*R_ans + R_fmt + t*R_trans.
6. Perform GRPO update on M_gen with KL regularization vs. reference policy.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Receiver strength:** Stronger receiver yields clearer signal for high-K consistency but increases compute; weaker receiver is cheaper but may limit signal quality.
- **Truncation ratio (τ):** Uniform sampling over [0.3,0.9] encourages robustness to arbitrary interruption vs. overfitting to a fixed point.
- **Reward weights (a, t):** Table 2 shows higher t improves Maj@K. Setting t=0 reverts to RLVR; too high a may neglect final correctness.

**Failure signatures**:
- **Transferability plateau or drop** while average accuracy rises (like RLVR in Fig 2c): Model abandoning robust process for "lucky" final answers.
- **Severe diversity loss (Pass@K drop)** as in RLVR (Table 6): Model overfitting to narrow solution patterns.
- **Receiver overfitting:** If gains disappear with a random receiver, the generator may be exploiting receiver quirks.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline replication:** Reproduce RLVR results (Table 1) to validate implementation before adding transfer components.
2. **Ablation: Receiver model suite:** Train RLTR with receivers of varying sizes (1.5B, 3B, 7B) to verify robustness of gains (Table 3).
3. **Truncation robustness sweep:** Evaluate transfer accuracy across τ in [0.1, 0.9] (Fig 3) to confirm the model learns interruption-robust reasoning, not boundary artifacts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Optimal truncation ratio τ:** The paper uniformly samples τ∈[0.3,0.9] but does not investigate whether an optimal value exists or how it varies with problem difficulty or model capacity.
- **Minimal viable receiver:** The paper tests only three receiver models; it is unclear what properties (e.g., parameter count, pretraining data) are essential for a useful transfer signal, and whether a weaker receiver could suffice.
- **Reasoning trace modularity:** The link between cross-model transfer success and specific reasoning properties (e.g., step independence, abstraction) remains speculative; no trace-level evidence is provided.
- **Out-of-domain generalization:** Gains on GPQA may be due to dataset overlap rather than true domain-agnostic transfer; need training on diverse non-math datasets.
- **Computational trade-offs at scale:** The cost-benefit analysis is limited to a specific model pair and generation length; scaling to longer contexts or larger generators may alter the 2.5× step reduction.

## Limitations
- Exact data filtering criteria for MATH (difficulty ≥3) and format reward implementation are unspecified, risking reproducibility.
- Receiver model choice and its impact on signal quality are not fully explored; optimal size/capability trade-off remains uncertain.
- Generalization to out-of-domain benchmarks like GPQA is promising but limited to one benchmark; broader transferability needs verification.
