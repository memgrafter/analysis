# Reasoning or Rationalization? The Role of Justifications in Masked Diffusion Models for Fact Verification

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.01190  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01190  
- **Reference count:** 5  
- **Primary result:** Masked Diffusion Language Models converge on verdicts early in the diffusion process; forcing a justification-first deliberation degrades accuracy; verdicts are strongly causally dependent on justification quality, indicating rationalization rather than genuine reasoning.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether justifications produced by Masked Diffusion Language Models (MDLMs) for fact verification reflect true reasoning or are post‑hoc rationalizations. Using LLaDA‑8B on the A VeriTeC dataset, the authors conduct a series of interventions—including output order manipulation, delayed verdict unmasking, and interventional tests—to probe the temporal dynamics and causal dependencies between verdicts and justifications. They find that MDLMs typically lock in the binary verdict within the first few diffusion steps, regardless of whether the prompt asks for the verdict before the justification. Forcing the model to generate a justification before revealing the verdict (reasoning‑first) harms accuracy, as accumulated noise in the justification tokens drifts the final verdict away from the early correct prediction. Interventional analyses further reveal that the verdict is highly sensitive to justification quality: corrupting the justification severely degrades accuracy, while the model can readily rationalize forced incorrect verdicts. Together, these results suggest that in MDLMs, extended deliberation is not necessarily beneficial and can even be detrimental, undermining early accurate assessments with noise.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates LLaDA‑8B, an 8‑billion‑parameter masked diffusion model, on the A VeriTeC fact‑verification dataset. Each input consists of a claim and evidence (provided as Q‑A pairs), and the model is prompted to output a JSON object containing a binary verdict ("Supported"/"Refuted") and a free‑text justification, with a fixed generation budget of 64 tokens. The core methodology revolves around three inference‑time interventions: (1) **Output ordering** – swapping the order of the verdict and justification fields in the JSON prompt; (2) **Delayed verdict unmasking** – modifying the decoding scheduler to keep the verdict token masked until a specified fraction of justification tokens has been revealed; (3) **Interventional tests** – an *Integrity Test* that forces an incorrect verdict and then assesses whether the justification aligns with it (rationalization), and a *Reliance Test* that conditions the model on corrupted justifications while allowing only the verdict to be generated, measuring causal dependence. Baseline performance is compared with autoregressive models (LLaMA 3.1 8B, Qwen3‑8B). The results are analyzed via verdict accuracy, token‑level probability trajectories for the verdict, and qualitative error classification.

## Key Results
- **Early verdict convergence:** Regardless of output order (verdict‑first or justification‑first), LLaDA‑8B resolves the verdict within the first few diffusion steps. Accuracy is similar for both orders (86.2% vs. 89.0%).
- **Deliberation harms accuracy:** Enforcing a justification‑first schedule by delaying verdict unmasking degrades performance significantly; accuracy drops from 86.2% to 71.9% when the verdict is unmasked after 25% of justification tokens, a phenomenon they term *refinement drift*.
- **Verdict depends causally on justification:** In the Reliance Test, corrupting the justification lowers accuracy to 57.3%, whereas providing gold justifications yields 97.1%. The Integrity Test shows that when forced to output an incorrect verdict, the model generates a supportive (rationalizing) justification in 56% of cases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Early Verdict Convergence in MDLMs
LLaDA‑8B decides the binary verdict early in the iterative denoising process, treating it as a global anchor that stabilizes before the justification details are complete. The model’s non‑causal, bidirectional architecture refines all tokens simultaneously; high‑confidence global signals (the verdict) converge faster than the more locally nuanced justification tokens, which require more diffusion steps to align with evidence. This explains why output ordering has minimal impact: the verdict is already determined early, irrespective of prompt structure.

### Mechanism 2: Refinement Drift from Noisy Justification Accumulation
When the model is forced to generate justification tokens before the verdict is unmasked, the accumulating justification noise conditions subsequent refinement steps and can override the initially correct verdict. Because the verdict token remains maskable and the model continuously re‑evaluates all tokens jointly, local inconsistencies in the justification gradually shift the verdict distribution away from the correct prediction. This accounts for the accuracy drop observed with delayed verdict unmasking.

### Mechanism 3: Causal Dependence of Verdict on Justification Quality
In MDLMs, all tokens are generated from a joint conditional distribution; thus the verdict’s probability is directly influenced by the state of the justification tokens during diffusion. Interventional tests confirm this dependence: corrupting the justification causes a drastic accuracy decline, while gold justifications boost accuracy near perfection. Moreover, the model can rationalize forced incorrect verdicts, demonstrating that the justification can be shaped to fit a predetermined verdict. These findings indicate that the justification acts as a causal mediator for the final verdict, rather than being a mere afterthought.

  *Quick check:* What causal question does the *Integrity Test* (force verdict, generate justification) answer, and how does it differ from the *Reliance Test* (force justification, generate verdict)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** A VeriTeC Dataset -> LLaDA‑8B Model (with Decoding Scheduler and Intervention Hooks) -> Trajectory Logger

**Critical path:**  
1. Load LLaDA‑8B and tokenizer.  
2. For each sample, format the input prompt with claim and evidence.  
3. Initialize all 64 output tokens to `[MASK]`.  
4. Iterate (e.g., 64 steps):  
   a. Compute logits for all masked positions.  
   b. Decoding scheduler selects next token to unmask (modified during interventions).  
   c. Unmask selected token (argmax or sample).  
   d. Log probabilities for the verdict token’s position.  
5. Parse final JSON, compare verdict to ground truth.  
6. Aggregate token trajectories and justification text for analysis.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Flexibility vs. Stability:* MDLM’s any‑order decoding enables experiments but invites refinement drift as noisy tokens condition later steps.  
- *Intervention Strength:* Forcing a verdict tests rationalization capacity; forcing a justification tests causal reliance, but requires careful masking to avoid leakage.  
- *Task Simplification:* Using provided evidence isolates verification reasoning but may not generalize to end‑to‑end fact‑checking.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Deliberation Harm: Accuracy drops (86.2% → 71.9%) when >25% of justification is mandated before verdict unmasking.  
- High Rationalization Rate: Integrity Test yields supportive justifications for forced incorrect verdicts in 56% of cases.  
- Justification Quality Sensitivity: Accuracy plummets to 57.3% with self‑generated corrupted justifications vs. 97.1% with gold justifications, especially on "Supported" claims (17.2%).

**First 3 experiments to run:**  
1. **Output Ordering Baseline:** Compare accuracy when prompting for V→J vs. J→V.  
2. **Delayed Verdict Unmasking:** Prevent verdict unmasking until X% of justification tokens are revealed (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 90%); plot accuracy vs. threshold.  
3. **Reliance Test:** Condition on corrupted justifications (masked to remove label cues) and allow only verdict generation; compare accuracy to baseline and gold‑justification condition.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to deductive domains**  
   *Question:* Does refinement drift persist in domains requiring strict, multi‑step logical deduction (e.g., advanced mathematics) where intermediate steps are prerequisites rather than rationalizations?  
   *Basis:* Explicitly stated in Limitations.  
   *Why unresolved:* Study used A VeriTeC, which simplifies verification with provided evidence.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Replicate interventions on math benchmarks (e.g., AIME) and compare convergence patterns.

2. **Effect of model scale**  
   *Question:* How does parameter count, pretraining data, or architecture influence the severity of refinement drift and early verdict convergence?  
   *Basis:* Explicitly stated in Limitations.  
   *Why unresolved:* Experiments only on LLaDA‑8B; scaling laws unknown.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Evaluate multiple MDLM sizes (1B, 8B, 70B) on A VeriTeC and measure drift metrics.

3. **Other diffusion‑based LM architectures**  
   *Question:* Do discrete diffusion (e.g., SEDD) or continuous diffusion models exhibit similar refinement drift?  
   *Basis:* Explicitly stated in Limitations.  
   *Why unresolved:* Focused exclusively on masked diffusion (LLaDA); architectural differences may alter dynamics.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Apply same protocol (ordering, delayed unmasking, interventional tests) to SEDD and continuous diffusion models.

4. **Internal mechanisms of early verdict locking**  
   *Question:* What internal mechanisms (e.g., attention patterns, token probability trajectories) cause MDLMs to converge on verdicts within the first few diffusion steps, even when instructed to generate justifications first?  
   *Basis:* Inferred from paper’s observations; not explicitly probed.  
   *Why unresolved:* Analysis relied on external behavior without examining model internals.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Analyze attention weights and hidden states for verdict‑related tokens during early diffusion steps and compare with autoregressive models.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability:** Results are obtained on a single model (LLaDA‑8B) and one fact‑verification dataset; other tasks (e.g., mathematical reasoning) may not exhibit early verdict convergence or refinement drift.
- **Rationalization claim confidence:** The conclusion that models rationalize hinges on an operational definition (justification alignment with forced verdicts); this might capture coherent post‑hoc storytelling rather than deceptive reasoning.
- **Prompt and schedule reproducibility:** Exact prompt phrasing, JSON formatting, and token unmasking scheduler details are unspecified, possibly affecting quantitative outcomes.

## Confidence
- **Internal validity:** High  
  The interventional tests (Integrity/Reliance) robustly demonstrate causal dependence of verdicts on justification quality for LLaDA‑8B on A VeriTeC.
- **Generalizability to other tasks/models:** Medium  
  Extrapolation beyond the studied setting is uncertain; scaling and architectural differences could alter behavior.
- **Rationalization claim:** Medium  
  The evidence strongly supports reliance on justifications, but the term “rationalization” may be debated.
- **Prompt/schedule reproducibility:** Low  
  Missing details could lead to deviations from reported numbers if not faithfully recovered.

## Next Checks
