# Beyond Task Performance: A Metric-Based Analysis of Sequential Cooperation in Heterogeneous Multi-Agent Destructive Foraging

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10685
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10685
- **Reference Count:** 31
- **Primary Result:** A Deep Reinforcement Learning approach achieved superior overall performance, robustness to partner degradation, temporal synchronization, and fairer workload distribution compared to Greedy and Lévy Walk baselines, revealing hidden cooperative dynamics masked by final task metrics.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the lack of general-purpose metrics for analyzing cooperation in heterogeneous multi-agent destructive foraging under partial observability and sequential role dependency. The authors introduce a novel metric suite—categorized as primary, inter-team, and intra-team—and apply it to a two-team aquatic cleaning scenario with dynamic waste. Results demonstrate that a Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) approach outperforms Greedy and Lévy Walk baselines not only in task completion but also in robustness to partner failure, temporal coordination between teams, and equitable workload distribution. The metrics uncover that similar final performance can obscure fundamentally different cooperative strategies and failure modes, such as Greedy's latency tail and Lévy's inefficient independent exploration.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates algorithms in a grid-based environment where a moving waste field must be located by fast scouts with wide sensing and then destroyed by slow foragers with narrow sensing, using a shared global model updated by any agent's observation. Three algorithms are tested: a parameter-sharing DRL policy (Double Deep Q-Learning), a myopic Greedy heuristic, and a hybrid Lévy Walk/Dijkstra approach for scouts and foragers respectively. Performance is measured via a proposed metric suite: primary (e.g., Percentage of Target Achieved, RMSE), inter-team (e.g., Discovery-to-Service Latency, Inter-Team Temporal Lag), and intra-team (e.g., Gini Coefficient, Coverage Overlap). Evaluation involves 100-episode runs per algorithm, with additional degradation analysis where one team's policy is corrupted by stochastic noise.

## Key Results
- DRL achieved the highest overall task performance (PTA) and lowest model error (RMSE).
- DRL showed the lowest sensitivity to partner degradation (SS) and the fairest forager workload distribution (lowest Gini).
- DRL exhibited the tightest temporal synchronization between scout discovery peaks and forager collection peaks (lowest ITL).
- Greedy and Lévy baselines each had distinct trade-offs: Greedy suffered from a latency tail; Lévy showed poor cooperative success ratio and high Gini due to independent forager exploration.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Forager performance is causally dependent on scout reliability, quantified by a degradation slope (SS) under policy corruption.
- **Mechanism:** Foragers require accurate, timely location data from scouts due to limited sensing. Scout policy degradation (ε > 0) reduces discovery quality/timeliness, directly lowering forager collection efficiency (PTA_C) and increasing model error (RMSE). The sensitivity slope (SS) measures this dependency.
- **Break condition:** If foragers can discover targets independently (e.g., via long-range sensing or stochastic exploration like Lévy), the correlation weakens (lower SS), as seen with LéWalks.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.2 (Figures 10, 11, Table 4) shows algorithm-specific SS values; abstract mentions sequential dependencies.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Fair workload distribution (low Gini) emerges from sustained, rapid scout discovery creating a target surplus, not from explicit coordination.
- **Mechanism:** High discovery rate (PTA_D) and low discovery scatter (DSL) generate a steady stream of known targets. With two foragers, abundant targets prevent monopolization, forcing division of labor. Algorithms with slower/erratic discovery (Lévy) show high Gini.
- **Break condition:** Episodic, low-abundance discovery leads to nearest-agent monopoly and high Gini regardless of algorithm.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.3 describes emergent fairness due to rapid continuous discovery; Section 5.1 shows DRL/Greedy's fast PTA_D vs. Lévy's slower pace.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Low Inter-Team Temporal Lag (ITL) in DRL indicates learned anticipation of scout patterns, while baselines show reactive or stochastic decoupling.
- **Mechanism:** DRL foragers learn policies that position them near probable future discovery zones via shared model value, aligning their collection peak with the scout discovery peak. Greedy foragers chase immediate known targets (desynchronization if discovered earlier). Lévy's stochastic movement causes variable timing.
- **Break condition:** Uniformly random static resources eliminate DRL's spatiotemporal prediction advantage, converging ITL distributions toward baselines.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.2 notes DRL's tightly clustered ITL vs. dispersed baselines; Section 5.1's lower RMSE suggests better prediction.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Heterogeneous Multi-Agent Role Specialization**
  - **Why needed:** Asymmetric capabilities (sensing/actuation) create sequential dependencies central to inter-team metrics.
  - **Quick check:** If foragers had the same sensing range as scouts, how would CSR and DSL likely change?
- **Concept: Destructive Foraging & Partial Observability**
  - **Why needed:** Resource removal and limited sensing define the information asymmetry scouts mitigate, underpinning RMSE and IRR.
  - **Quick check:** How would scouts' Coverage Overlap differ if resources were static and known?
- **Concept: Metric Design for Cooperative Dynamics vs. Pure Task Performance**
  - **Why needed:** PTA alone masks coordination quality; Gini/ITL reveal hidden dynamics like emergent fairness vs. inefficiency.
  - **Quick check:** Can two algorithms have identical PTA but different MC profiles? What does that imply?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Environment (grid graph, dynamic waste) -> Agent Teams (Scouts, Foragers) -> Shared Model (Ŷ) -> Algorithm Policies (π_scout, π_forager) -> Metrics Suite (Primary, Inter-Team, Intra-Team)
- **Critical path:** Environment state (positions, Y) -> Agent observations (Θ(p_n)) -> Policy action -> State transition -> Shared model update (Ŷ) -> Metrics computation (using ground truth Y and action logs). Inter-team metrics require logged discovery/service events with timestamps.
- **Design tradeoffs:** PTA is simple but masks coordination; requires pairing with RMSE and Gini. CSSC degradation slope (SS) is powerful but costly (multiple runs with injected noise). CO is time-step sensitive; may need smoothing.
- **Failure signatures:** High final PTA but high RMSE: myopic overfitting without global model. Low CSR (<0.8): foragers not dependent on scouts (inefficient role use). High Gini (>0.3 for 2 agents) with high PTA: one forager bottlenecked; system fragile to its failure.
- **First 3 experiments:** 1) Reproduce Figure 4 & Table 2: Validate primary metrics, confirm DRL's faster PTA rise and lower RMSE. 2) Run CSSC analysis (Figures 10/11): Corrupt one team's policy (ε=0 to 1), plot PTA_C/RMSE, compute SS; expect DRL's SS less steep than Greedy. 3) Ablation for MC (Table 5/6): Remove one scout/forager, measure delta in PTA and RMSE; expect scout removal to hurt PTA_D and RMSE more severely.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Can the metric suite extend to systems with more than two specialized roles? Basis: authors suggest application to "scenarios with more than two functional roles," but validation is only for two-role structure.
- **Open Question 2:** How do realistic communication constraints (latency, packet loss, limited bandwidth) affect the metrics? Basis: future work mentions incorporating these; current framework assumes perfect shared model communication.
- **Open Question 3:** Are the metrics generalizable to other sequential cooperative domains beyond aquatic cleaning? Basis: authors claim transferability but validation is limited to a single grid-based scenario.

## Limitations
- Single-domain validation; generalizability to other heterogeneous MARL tasks unknown.
- DRL hyperparameters and reward design not fully specified; replication relies on external codebase.
- Novel metrics (ITL, CSSC, Gini) lack extensive sensitivity analysis across environmental variations.
- Mechanistic explanations (e.g., fairness from abundance) are correlational; causal relationships not experimentally isolated.

## Confidence
- **DRL outperforms baselines in overall performance and robustness:** High
- **Metric suite reveals hidden cooperative dynamics:** Medium
- **ITL reflects learned anticipation:** Low
- **Fair workload distribution emerges from abundant discovery:** Medium

## Next Checks
1. Cross-domain test: Apply the metric suite to at least two other heterogeneous MARL benchmarks (e.g., cooperative navigation, predator-prey).
2. Controlled ablation of scout discovery rate: Vary scout sensing radius or speed to directly test causal impact on forager Gini and ITL.
3. Include advanced MARL baselines: Compare DRL against QMIX and MADDPG to ensure superiority is not baseline-limited.
