# Relaxed Efficient Acquisition of Context and Temporal Features

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11370
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11370
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: REACT outperforms LAFA baselines on longitudinal biomedical datasets, achieving improved AUROC/AUPRC at lower total acquisition cost; e.g., on ILIADD, AUROC 0.842 at cost 35.739.

## Executive Summary
REACT introduces a novel approach to active feature acquisition in longitudinal biomedical studies, where measurements are taken sequentially and incur costs. The method simultaneously optimizes the selection of static contextual descriptors (acquired once at onboarding) and adaptive temporal feature acquisitions over time. It uses a Gumbel-Sigmoid relaxation with straight-through gradients to train discrete acquisition policies end-to-end under a combined prediction and cost objective. Evaluated on five real‑world datasets (CHEEARS, ILIADD, OAI, ADNI), REACT consistently achieves better predictive performance at lower acquisition costs compared to state‑of‑the‑art LAFA baselines. Ablations demonstrate the importance of the learned context selector and self‑iterative training regime.

## Method Summary
REACT comprises three neural modules: the Context Selector, which outputs binary decisions for which static context features to acquire; the Longitudinal Planner, a policy network that, given the current masked temporal history, acquired context, and time step, predicts a binary mask over future feature‑time acquisitions; and the Predictor, which estimates outcome labels using the accumulated information. The discrete acquisition masks are made differentiable via Gumbel‑Sigmoid relaxation; during the forward pass, hard binary masks are used by thresholding, while the backward pass uses soft samples from the relaxed distribution (straight‑through estimator). Training optimizes a weighted sum of prediction loss and acquisition cost, iteratively generating on‑policy trajectories with the current planner (self‑iterative training) to update all modules jointly. A warm‑up phase with offline reference plans stabilizes early training. The framework is validated on multiple clinical longitudinal datasets, showing superior accuracy‑cost trade‑offs.

## Key Results
- On the ILIADD dataset, REACT attains AUROC 0.842 at a total acquisition cost of 35.739, surpassing all compared LAFA baselines.
- Across five datasets (CHEEARS, ILIADD, OAI, ADNI), REACT achieves higher AUROC and AUPRC than ASAC, RAS, AS, and DIME at comparable or lower measurement costs.
- Ablation studies confirm that the learned context selector and self‑iterative training are essential components; removing either degrades the accuracy‑cost trade‑off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Gumbel‑Sigmoid relaxation with straight‑through gradients enables end‑to‑end training of discrete acquisition masks.
**Mechanism:** Replaces non‑differentiable binary sampling with a continuous Gumbel‑Sigmoid relaxation during the backward pass (using soft samples), while maintaining hard binary masks during the forward pass via thresholding. This creates a differentiable bridge between discrete acquisition decisions and the continuous prediction loss.
**Core assumption:** The straight‑through estimator’s gradient mismatch (continuous proxy in backward, discrete in forward) provides a sufficiently accurate gradient signal for optimizing the discrete acquisition policy.
**Evidence anchors:**
- Abstract states REACT employs Gumbel–Sigmoid relaxation with straight‑through estimation.
- Section 3.4 defines the relaxation and straight‑through estimator formulation (Eqs. 4–7).
- Corpus evidence is weak; neighboring papers do not focus on relaxation techniques.
**Break condition:** If temperature τ is not properly annealed or gradient mismatch causes divergence in policy parameters.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Joint optimization of initial context selection and temporal acquisition planning improves accuracy‑cost tradeoffs by exploiting their synergistic relationship.
**Mechanism:** A unified objective (Eq. 3) combines prediction loss with acquisition costs for both context (one‑time) and temporal features. The context selector learns which static descriptors best support downstream temporal planning, while the planner adapts to the available context.
**Core assumption:** Initial contextual descriptors provide valuable prior information that influences the value of future temporal acquisitions, and this interaction can be captured in a joint optimization framework.
**Evidence anchors:**
- Abstract mentions simultaneous optimization of (i) contextual descriptors and (ii) adaptive acquisition plans.
- Section 1 introduces the LAFA formulation distinguishing one‑time contextual descriptors from sequential measurements.
- Section 4.6 (Ablation, Fig. 8) shows learned context selector outperforms “all” or “none” baselines on most datasets.
**Break condition:** If contextual features are either completely predictive (making temporal decisions irrelevant) or completely uninformative (making context selection moot).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Self‑iterative training with on‑policy rollouts yields better longitudinal acquisition policies than training on static offline reference states.
**Mechanism:** The planner repeatedly rolls out its current policy to generate diverse on‑policy acquisition trajectories, which are then used for gradient updates. This exposes the policy to states it actually induces, rather than only reference states from an initial plan.
**Core assumption:** The state distribution encountered during test time differs meaningfully from the distribution in fixed reference plans, and on‑policy experience reduces this distribution shift.
**Evidence anchors:**
- Section 3.5 describes the self‑iterative training procedure.
- Section 4.7 (Fig. 7) shows self‑iterative training outperforms offline reference on clinical datasets.
- Corpus evidence is weak; neighboring papers do not address policy training procedures.
**Break condition:** If early policy rollouts are so poor that they visit only degenerate states (e.g., always terminate immediately), causing a collapse in state coverage.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Gumbel‑Sigmoid Straight‑Through Estimator**
- **Why needed here:** REACT must optimize discrete binary acquisition decisions via gradient descent while maintaining discrete outputs at inference. The straight‑through estimator provides the differentiable relaxation.
- **Quick check question:** In the forward pass, what mask is used for actual acquisitions? In the backward pass, what mask is used for gradient computation?

**Concept: Longitudinal Active Feature Acquisition (LAFA)**
- **Why needed here:** LAFA extends traditional active feature acquisition to sequential, irreversible decisions with multiple prediction timepoints, capturing temporal coupling essential for REACT’s formulation.
- **Quick check question:** How does LAFA differ from standard AFA in terms of decision structure and constraints?

**Concept: Onboarding vs. Temporal Feature Distinction**
- **Why needed here:** REACT explicitly separates static contextual descriptors (acquired once at t=0) from temporal measurements (acquired adaptively over time), mirroring real clinical workflows.
- **Quick check question:** Why can’t existing LAFA baselines naturally handle this two‑phase structure, requiring the adaptation of concatenating static features to every timestep?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
- **Context Selector (α):** A linear layer producing logits for each of d_s context features; during forward, binary mask m_s = I[σ(α) > 0.5] determines which onboarding descriptors to acquire. Cost incurred: c_s^T m_s.
- **Longitudinal Planner (π_θ):** A 3‑layer MLP (sizes [512, 256, 128]) that takes (i) masked temporal history H_t (T×d matrix), (ii) acquired context ˜s (d_s), and (iii) current time t. It outputs logits over the entire future feature×time matrix; the first non‑zero row identifies the next acquisition time t_next and its corresponding mask m_t_next. The planner emits the full future plan P_{>t} at each step for loss computation.
- **Predictor (f_φ):** A 3‑layer MLP (hidden size 64) that concatenates (i) masked temporal history up to prediction time t' (H_{t'}), (ii) acquired context ˜s, and (iii) a normalized time embedding for t'. It outputs class probabilities for timestep t'.

**Critical path:**
1. **Onboarding (t=0):** Compute ˜s = I[σ(α) > 0.5] ⊙ s, incurring cost c_s^T m_s.
2. **Initialize:** Set H ← 0 (T×d zero matrix), M_prev ← 0 (T×d zero mask).
3. **For each decision step:**
   a. Planner evaluates current state (H, ˜s, t) → produces future plan M = σ(π_θ(H, t, ˜s)) > 0.5.
   b. Extract t_next = first time with non‑zero mask in M.
   c. Acquire features according to m_{t_next} at time t_next; update H and M_prev.
   d. Predict ŷ_t using current H and ˜s.
4. **Termination:** If planner outputs an all‑zero mask for future steps, stop acquiring further features.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Relaxation temperature τ:** Fixed during training (paper does not specify schedule). Higher τ yields smoother gradients but less discrete‑like masks; lower τ yields sharper masks but riskier gradients. Typical moderate value around 1.0.
- **Cost coefficient λ:** Controls the accuracy‑cost tradeoff. Requires sweeping across orders of magnitude to generate Pareto curves. Too high leads to under‑acquisition; too low ignores cost.
- **Warmup strategy:** First 50 batches use offline reference plans (best‑scoring discrete trajectories from exhaustive search on training set); remaining 950 batches use self‑iterative rollouts. This hybrid balances stability and on‑policy coverage.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Planner never terminates:** All future masks are non‑zero. This may indicate λ is too small or reward shaping is insufficient.
- **Context selector degeneracy:** The mask m_s is always all‑ones or all‑zeros. Inspect logits α; if they are all near zero or all large positive, the relaxation may have saturated.
- **Training instability:** Loss spikes or NaN during self‑iterative phase, often due to straight‑through gradient mismatch. Remedy: reduce learning rate or increase τ.
- **Poor generalization:** Validation performance drops significantly compared to training rollouts, suggesting distribution shift between reference states and self‑generated states. Consider more warmup batches or alternative reference plan generation.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Hyperparameter sweep for λ:** Train REACT with multiple λ values (e.g., [0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.003, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05] depending on dataset cost scale). For each λ, obtain test‑set performance‑cost point and plot AUROC vs. total cost to form a Pareto curve. Baseline methods must be swept over their respective cost hyperparameters (μ for ASAC, γ for RAS, fixed Δ for AS, acquisition budget for DIME).
2. **Context selector ablation:** Train three REACT variants: (i) REACT‑learned (original), (ii) REACT‑all (force m_s = all ones), (iii) REACT‑none (force m_s = all zeros). Keep all other components identical. Compare performance‑cost curves to demonstrate the benefit of the learned context selector.
3. **Self‑iterative training ablation:** Compare full REACT (50 warmup + 950 self‑rollout) against: (i) Offline‑reference‑only (train all 1000 batches on reference states), and (ii) Pure online (random initialization, no warmup). Plot performance‑cost curves to show that self‑iterative training outperforms offline‑only, confirming the advantage of on‑policy states.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the Gumbel‑Sigmoid relaxation with straight‑through gradients affect the optimality of the discrete acquisition masks compared to exact discrete optimization?
- **Basis in paper:** The method relies on relaxation to enable backpropagation but does not analyze deviation from true discrete solutions or potential biases.
- **Why unresolved:** The continuous approximation may lead to suboptimal cost‑accuracy trade‑offs; theoretical understanding of the gap introduced by the relaxation and straight‑through estimator is lacking.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare with exact combinatorial optimization on small instances, measure performance differences; systematically vary temperature annealing and analyze stability of learned policies.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can REACT be adapted to handle irregular longitudinal measurements and variable visit intervals without requiring fixed time discretization?
- **Basis in paper:** The formulation assumes discrete timesteps 1,…,T and fixed time indices; real‑world biomedical data often have irregular observation times.
- **Why unresolved:** Irregularities are common; forcing alignment may cause information loss or bias; the current framework lacks explicit time‑awareness beyond fixed indices.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend REACT to incorporate continuous‑time dynamics (e.g., neural ODEs or time embeddings), evaluate on datasets with irregular intervals, and compare performance against the fixed‑time version.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Would a personalized context selector (that adapts to individual characteristics) improve cost‑efficiency compared to the current population‑level selector?
- **Basis in paper:** The context selector outputs a single binary mask applied to all instances; temporal decisions are personalized but context acquisition is not.
- **Why unresolved:** Different patients may have different relevant contextual factors; a uniform mask may over‑acquire for some and under‑acquire for others, missing potential cost savings and predictive gains.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a context selector that conditions on a small set of always‑available features (e.g., age, sex), train jointly with the planner, and compare cost‑accuracy trade‑offs; also assess subgroup performance in clinical validation.

## Limitations
- **Incomplete methodological details:** Exact Gumbel temperature schedule, predictor architecture details, optimizer settings, and training iteration count are not provided, hindering exact replication.
- **Limited diagnostic analysis:** No exploration of sensitivity to relaxation parameters or alternative gradient estimators; potential instability from straight‑through gradient mismatch is not thoroughly examined.
- **Dataset‑specific cost scaling:** λ values were swept but selection criteria for final operating points are unclear; generalizability to other cost weightings is unknown.
