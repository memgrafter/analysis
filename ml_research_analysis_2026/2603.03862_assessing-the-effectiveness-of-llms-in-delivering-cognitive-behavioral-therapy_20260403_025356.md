# Assessing the Effectiveness of LLMs in Delivering Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03862
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03862
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** LLMs generate linguistically coherent CBT-like dialogue but significantly underperform human therapists in therapeutic skills and empathy; RAG offers minimal improvement.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates the ability of large language models (LLMs) to emulate cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) therapists. Using 17 role‑played therapy sessions (average 39 turns each) sourced from YouTube, the authors compare generation‑only and retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) approaches across several models, including GPT‑4o‑mini, Llama3‑8B, Mistral‑7B, Gemma‑7B, and Qwen‑7B.

Automated metrics show high semantic similarity (BERTScore up to 85.82, Mean Consistency 95.17) but low lexical overlap (ROUGE) and reveal a pervasive agreeableness bias and verbosity. More importantly, LLMs score dramatically lower than human therapists on the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) sub‑scales—especially Understanding, Interpersonal Effectiveness, and Strategy—and on empathy dimensions, with zero Interpretations despite high Exploration scores. RAG provides only marginal gains, suggesting that the bottleneck lies in applying therapeutic reasoning rather than lacking knowledge. These findings highlight limited therapeutic fidelity and potential safety concerns for unvalidated LLM use in mental health.

## Method Summary
The study uses 17 anonymized role‑played CBT sessions (∼39 turns per session) as a test set, and 26 CBT guideline tutorial sessions to construct a retrieval corpus. Dialogue turns are formatted as conversation histories. Two pipelines are evaluated: (1) generation‑only, where the LLM is prompted with the conversation history and CBT role instructions; (2) RAG, where the last three turns are used to retrieve the top‑3 relevant guideline passages via dense retrieval (e.g., Sentence‑BERT), which are then concatenated with the instruction and history. Models include GPT‑4o‑mini, Llama3‑8B, Mistral‑7B, Gemma‑7B, and Qwen‑7B. Evaluation comprises: natural language generation metrics (BLEU, ROUGE‑L, METEOR, BERTScore, Distinct‑1); semantic consistency using a DeBERTa‑v3 NLI model (Mean Consistency, Max Contradiction); and therapeutic skill/empathy scoring by an automated GPT‑4o rater based on the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) sub‑scales (0–6) and an empathy framework (0–2). This automated evaluation allows large‑scale analysis of LLM‑generated therapist utterances.

## Key Results
- LLMs produce semantically consistent CBT‑like language (e.g., GPT‑4o‑mini BERTScore 85.82, Mean Consistency 95.17) but show substantially lower ROUGE/BLEU scores than therapist references, indicating stylistic divergence.
- Across all models, CTRS sub‑scale scores (Understanding, Interpersonal Effectiveness, Collaboration, Guided Discovery, Focus, Strategy) are far below human therapist levels, with Strategy particularly low. Empathy scores are low on Interpretations (often 0.00) while Exploration scores are relatively high, suggesting procedural questioning without true understanding.
- RAG yields only minimal improvement over generation‑only, and an agreeableness bias (“Absolutely…”, “It’s completely understandable…”) together with excessive verbosity is evident in generated responses, raising concerns about therapeutic fidelity and safety.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Pattern‑Matching on Therapeutic Language
- **Claim:** LLMs generate linguistically coherent and semantically consistent CBT‑like dialogue by leveraging pattern‑matching on therapeutic language present in their pre‑training data.
- **Mechanism:** The next‑token prediction objective, trained on vast corpora containing therapeutic dialogues and self‑help materials, allows reproduction of surface‑level structural and lexical features of CBT (e.g., Socratic questioning, psychoeducation phrasing) without accessing underlying therapeutic reasoning.
- **Core assumption:** Therapeutic language exhibits detectable statistical patterns that general‑purpose LLMs can replicate.
- **Evidence:** High BERTScore (85.82) and Mean Consistency (95.17) across models; abstract states LLMs generate CBT‑like dialogues.
- **Break condition:** Model fails to maintain logical consistency with the session’s evolving therapeutic focus (as measured by NLI metrics like Maximum Contradiction).

### Mechanism 2: Limited Benefit of RAG
- **Claim:** Retrieval‑Augmented Generation provides minimal improvement because the LLM’s parametric knowledge already contains sufficient CBT procedural language, making external guideline retrieval redundant for surface‑level generation.
- **Mechanism:** The pre‑trained model’s internalized knowledge base encompasses the domain‑specific phrases and structures found in the retrieved CBT guideline passages. The RAG module’s retrieved context does not provide novel, session‑specific strategic insights that the model lacks, thus only marginally influencing the next‑token distribution.
- **Core assumption:** The cognitive steps of therapy (e.g., case conceptualization, individualized strategy selection) are not reliably encoded in text‑based guidelines and are the primary differentiators from human performance.
- **Evidence:** Abstract states “RAG yields minimal improvement.” Section 3 notes that RAG provides limited improvement and that LLMs already possess sufficient CBT‑related knowledge. Retrieval corpus consists of generic guideline passages, not client‑specific case notes.
- **Break condition:** RAG would show significant gains if the retrieved content contained highly specific, situational therapeutic directives or personalized case formulations absent from the model’s training.

### Mechanism 3: Agreeableness Bias and Verbiage
- **Claim:** LLMs exhibit an “agreeableness bias” and excessive verbosity, producing formulaic, affirming responses that simulate affective empathy but fail at cognitive empathy and strategic intervention, due to optimization for user engagement and broad training data distributions.
- **Mechanism:** The model’s training objective (next‑token prediction on diverse internet data) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) prioritize helpfulness, harmlessness, and user satisfaction, which manifest as over‑affirmation (“Absolutely…”), generic reassurance (“It’s completely understandable…”), and verbose exploration. This conflicts with CBT’s need for constructive challenge, precise interpretation, and brevity.
- **Core assumption:** The safety/alignment tuning of LLMs inherently conflicts with therapeutic techniques requiring cognitive confrontation or strategic silence.
- **Evidence:** Section 4.1 shows direct quotes with “Absolutely…” and “It’s completely understandable…”. Table 4 reveals very low Interpretation scores (e.g., 0.00 for GPT‑4o‑mini variants) vs. higher Exploration scores. Section 3 notes that LLMs produce longer responses than human therapists.
- **Break condition:** The model generates a concise, tailored counter‑argument to a client’s maladaptive belief, akin to a high‑scoring human therapist interpretation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Data Pipeline → Retriever (RAG) → Generator → Evaluator Suite

**Critical Path**  
1. Secure and preprocess role‑play transcripts.  
2. Implement generation‑only and RAG pipelines (prompts, retrieval).  
3. Generate responses for all turns across all models.  
4. Run the full evaluation suite (NLG, NLI, CTRS/empathy).  
5. Analyze correlations between linguistic metrics and therapeutic skills.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Metric selection: Automated CTRS/empathy scoring with GPT‑4o is scalable but may lack nuance compared to human experts.  
- RAG design: Fixed window size (3 turns) and top‑k (3) are heuristics; larger contexts or different retrieval models could affect results but increase compute.  
- Model choice: Using small models (7B, GPT‑4o‑mini) limits ceiling performance; findings may not generalize to larger, more capable LLMs.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Semantic: High BERTScore/Consistency but low CTRS/empathy → language mimicry without therapeutic intent.  
- Lexical: Very low ROUGE/BLEU → overly verbose or stylistically divergent responses.  
- Empathy: High Exploration scores with zero Interpretations → procedural questioning without understanding.  
- Qualitative: Frequent use of phrases like “Absolutely…”, “It’s completely understandable…”, code‑switching, and misuse of therapist pronouns (“As I reflect…”).

**First Three Experiments**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Re‑run generation‑only and RAG for all models on the 17‑session test set; replicate key tables and check utterance length distributions.  
2. **RAG sensitivity analysis:** Vary context window *w* (1,3,5) and retrieved passages *k* (1,3,5); measure impact on CTRS Strategy and Focus scores.  
3. **Empathy breakdown:** Manually inspect 50 high‑scoring Exploration examples; code for open vs. closed questions, presence of affect words, and connection to client’s prior utterance to validate the “Interpretations = 0” finding.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The authors highlight several open questions for future research:
- How reliable are automated evaluations (e.g., GPT‑4o scoring) compared to human expert ratings for therapeutic skills and empathy?
- To what extent do unreported RAG design choices (e.g., chunk size, retrieval model) contribute to the observed minimal gains, and could improved retrieval lead to significant improvements?
- Do findings from role‑played sessions generalize to real therapist–client interactions, and what are the safety implications of deploying unvalidated LLMs in mental health contexts?

## Limitations
- Therapeutic skill and empathy assessments rely on GPT‑4o scoring without disclosed prompts/rubrics, which may diverge from expert human judgments.
- RAG implementation details (e.g., guideline chunk size, retrieval model) are unreported, limiting reproducibility and possibly explaining minimal gains.
- The dataset consists of role‑played YouTube sessions, which may not capture the complexity and nuance of real therapist–client interactions.

## Confidence
- **LLMs generate linguistically coherent and semantically consistent CBT‑like text:** High confidence (supported by BERTScore, consistency metrics).
- **RAG yields minimal improvement over generation‑only:** Medium confidence (clear result but may be sensitive to unreported design choices).
- **LLMs exhibit an agreeableness bias and excessive verbosity:** High confidence (directly observed in response analysis).
- **LLMs underperform human therapists on therapeutic skills and empathy:** High confidence (consistent deficits across CTRS sub‑scales and empathy dimensions).
