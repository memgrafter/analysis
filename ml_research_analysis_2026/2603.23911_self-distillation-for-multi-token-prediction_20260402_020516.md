# Self-Distillation for Multi-Token Prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23911  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23911  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** MTP-D improves acceptance rates by 7.5% for four heads while preserving main-head performance; looped extension enables economical scaling to 8–16 heads, yielding up to 220.4% speedup over single-head MTP.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the low acceptance rates and training instability that hinder Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) in large language model inference. The authors introduce MTP-D, a self‑distillation approach that aligns the logit distributions of MTP heads with the main head using gradient‑detached, top‑N logits selection. This modification boosts acceptance rates by 7.5% for four heads without harming the main head’s accuracy. To scale beyond a few heads, the method employs a looped extension strategy: after training a group of MTP heads, their weights are copied to initialize the next group, and continued pretraining (with only 70B tokens) adapts them to new positions. This cost‑effective scaling yields up to 8–16 heads and achieves up to 220.4% inference speedup compared to single‑head MTP. Validated on 2B dense and 10B MoE models across seven benchmarks, MTP-D enhances both efficiency and practicality of MTP deployment.

## Method Summary
MTP-D augments the standard multi‑token prediction objective with a self‑distillation loss for each MTP head. Specifically, for a given MTP head, a KL divergence is computed between its predicted distribution and that of the main head, but only over the top‑N most probable tokens (N=10,000) and with the main head’s logits detached from the gradient. This prevents the MTP supervision from altering the main head’s parameters while teaching the MTP head to mimic its high‑quality distribution. The total loss per head combines this KL term with the usual cross‑entropy on ground‑truth future tokens, weighted by coefficients αₖ and βₖ. To scale to many heads, MTP-D introduces looped extension: once a group of m heads is trained, their weights are duplicated to initialise the next m heads, and continued pretraining (with minimal data) fine‑tunes the new group while keeping all earlier weights fixed. This leverages the architectural consistency of cascaded MTP to propagate learned knowledge across head indices.

## Key Results
- MTP‑D improves per‑head acceptance rate by 7.5% for four heads while preserving main‑head accuracy.
- Looped extension enables cost‑effective scaling to 8–16 heads; with 8 heads, MTP‑D achieves up to 220.4% speedup over single‑head MTP.
- The top‑N selection (N=10,000) balances computational efficiency, numerical stability, and alignment quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Gradient‑detached self‑distillation preserves main‑head performance
- **Claim:** The use of stop‑gradient (sg) on the main head’s logits in the KL loss prevents degradation of the main head’s own accuracy while still aligning MTP head distributions.
- **Mechanism:** The KL loss `L_KL_mtp = KL(Pₖ || sg(Qₜ))` updates only the MTP head’s parameters (Pₖ) because the gradient is blocked from flowing back through the detached teacher logits Qₜ. This allows the MTP head to match the main head’s top‑N distribution without pulling on the main head’s weights.
- **Core assumption:** The main head’s top‑N logits encode a high‑quality, semantically rich target distribution that, if mimicked, will improve MTP head acceptance rates.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [section 3.2] Explicit use of `sg(·)` on main head logits.
  - Ablation “no detach” shows a significant increase in main‑head loss (+0.079), confirming the protective effect.
- **Break condition:** Omitting the stop‑gradient causes the main head’s performance to drop.

### Mechanism 2: Top‑N logit selection focuses on high‑signal tokens
- **Claim:** Restricting the KL divergence to the top‑N highest‑probability logits (N=10,000) avoids the long‑tail of the vocabulary, making training more efficient, numerically stable, and effective.
- **Mechanism:** The vocabulary distribution after softmax is long‑tailed; most tokens have near‑zero probabilities. Computing KL over all tokens is wasteful and can lead to numerical instability (e.g., log(0) or vanishing gradients). Selecting only the top‑N concentrates supervision on tokens with meaningful probabilities, reducing memory/compute and stabilizing loss curves.
- **Core assumption:** The high‑probability region of the main head’s distribution contains the supervisory signal most relevant for improving acceptance rates; low‑probability noise is unhelpful or harmful.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [section C] Analysis of the long‑tailed distribution motivates N=10,000 (covers ≈99.5% probability mass).
  - Ablation (Figure 10) shows full‑vocabulary distillation causes oscillating/vanishing losses, while TopN=10,000 yields stable convergence and best acceptance.
- **Break condition:** If N is too small (e.g., 1 or 100), the distillation signal becomes insufficient; if too large (full vocab), computational cost and instability return.

### Mechanism 3: Looped extension exploits intra‑group similarity for economical scaling
- **Claim:** Copying a trained group of MTP heads to initialise the next group enables cost‑effective scaling to many heads via continued pretraining on far fewer tokens than full pretraining.
- **Mechanism:** In a cascaded MTP architecture, the mapping from a backbone state (augmented with previous ground‑truth embeddings) to the distribution of the next token is relatively consistent across different offsets. A group of m heads (e.g., heads 1–4) trained together therefore learns similar functions. Copying their weights to initialise heads 5–8 (or 9–16) provides a strong starting point already aligned with the main head. A short continued‑pretraining phase (70B tokens vs. 350B for full pretraining) fine‑tunes these new heads to their specific sequential contexts with minimal data.
- **Core assumption:** The parameter and distribution similarity within a trained group allows weight transfer across different head indices; a small amount of additional training adapts the copied heads to the new positions.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [section 3.3] Description of the looped extension algorithm.
  - Training‑free loop experiments show an initial acceptance drop at the junction (e.g., head 5 when extending 4→8) but subsequent heads recover, confirming inherent scalability; with continued pretraining, recovery is faster and more complete.
- **Break condition:** If the initial group is poorly trained (low acceptance), the copied initialization will be weak, leading to rapid collapse when scaling (as seen in training‑free MTP without distillation).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Knowledge Distillation (KD)**  
  *Why needed here:* MTP‑D is fundamentally a self‑distillation method where the main head acts as teacher to the MTP heads (students). Understanding KD’s goal—transferring “dark knowledge” via probability distribution matching—explains the use of KL divergence and stop‑gradient.  
  *Quick check:* Why is stop‑gradient applied to the teacher’s logits? What would happen if gradients flowed back through the teacher?

- **Concept: Cascaded Multi‑Token Prediction Architecture**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper builds on a cascaded MTP design (e.g., DeepSeek‑V3) where each head consumes the output of the previous head. This sequential dependency compounds acceptance rates and makes scaling non‑trivial, necessitating looped extension.  
  *Quick check:* In a K‑head cascaded system, if each head’s acceptance rate is 80%, what is the cumulative acceptance rate for the 3rd token? How does this motivate high individual head acceptance?

- **Concept: Speculative Decoding**  
  *Why needed here:* The entire purpose of MTP is to accelerate inference via speculative decoding. Acceptance rate (AR) and cumulative acceptance rate (CAR) directly link MTP head quality to speedup; without understanding the verification process, the value of MTP‑D’s improvements is unclear.  
  *Quick check:* Define AR and CAR as used in the paper (Eq. 6‑7, Appendix D). Why does CAR matter more than single‑head AR when scaling to many heads?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text flow):**  
Core Transformer → Main Output Head → MTP Head 1 → MTP Head 2 → … → MTP Head K  
*(Each MTP head’s input is the backbone’s final hidden state plus the sum of embeddings of ground‑truth tokens up to the previous position; the head outputs logits for the next future token.)*

**Critical path:**  
*Pretraining phase:* For each batch, compute main head logits Qₜ. For each MTP head k, compute its logits Pₖ on the appropriate state (backbone output plus previous ground‑truth embeddings). Compute L_CEₖ against true token t+k. Compute L_KLₖ using top‑N indices from sg(Qₜ) vs. Pₖ at those indices. Sum weighted losses Lₘₜₚ = Σₖ(αₖ L_CEₖ + βₖ L_KLₖ). Backward updates affect only the MTP heads (and the backbone if it is not frozen); the main head remains untouched by L_KL due to stop‑gradient.  
*Looped extension phase:* Freeze backbone and all existing MTP heads. Initialise new MTP heads by copying weights from the first m trained heads. Continue pretraining on new data with the same Lₘₜₚ loss, now updating only the new heads. Repeat.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- βₖ weighting: Higher βₖ improves MTP acceptance but risks indirect backbone interference; empirically βₖ=0.5 works well for 4‑head models.  
- TopN value: Larger N improves distillation fidelity but increases memory/compute; N=10,000 offers a good balance (≈99.5% probability mass).  
- Looped data budget: 70B tokens suffice for successful extension, far less than the 350B needed for full pretraining, but too little data may leave new heads under‑adapted.  
- Group size m: Smaller groups may be more stable per extension step; larger groups may preserve intra‑group correlations better. The paper uses m=4.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Main‑head accuracy decay:* Monitoring validation loss/accuracy; a significant increase (e.g., +0.079 in the “no detach” ablation) indicates harmful interference.  
- *Sharp acceptance drop at loop junction:* In training‑free or early continued‑pretraining extensions, a dramatic CAR decrease at the first new head (e.g., head 5 when extending 4→8) suggests poor adaptation of copied weights.  
- *Oscillating/vanishing losses:* Especially with full‑vocabulary distillation, loss curves become unstable, signalling numerical issues.  
- *Diminishing returns after ~16 heads:* CAR plummets (<5%), making further heads ineffective due to cascaded error accumulation.

**First 3 experiments (replication attempts):**  
1. **Ablation: Gradient Detachment (`no detach`).** Train a single‑head MTP‑D model but remove the stop‑gradient from the teacher logits. Expect a significant increase in main‑head loss, confirming the need for detachment.  
2. **Ablation: TopN Selection.** Train variants with TopN = 1, 100, 1 000, 10 000, and full vocabulary. Measure MTP‑head loss and acceptance rate. Expect: performance improves up to ~1 000–10 000, then plateaus; full vocabulary shows instability.  
3. **Training‑Free Looped Extension (1→8 and 4→8).** Take a fully trained MTP‑D model (with 1 or 4 heads) and simulate an 8‑head system by copying weights without further training. Measure CAR per head. Expect an initial drop at the junction (e.g., head 5 for 4→8) but recovery in subsequent heads; MTP‑D should exhibit less drop than baseline MTP.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- *Post‑training adaptation:* How can MTP‑D be applied to supervised fine‑tuning or RLHF, and does it retain acceptance‑rate gains in those regimes?  
- *Optimal loss coefficients:* What is the theoretical relationship between the loss weights αₖ (cross‑entropy) and βₖ (distillation) and the number of MTP heads K?  
- *Generalisation:* Does MTP‑D remain effective across diverse training datasets (e.g., code, multilingual) and ultra‑large models (100B+ parameters)?

## Limitations
- **Architectural dependency:** The method is tightly bound to the cascaded MTP design; its efficacy on parallel or non‑sequential multi‑token prediction architectures is uncertain.
- **Unquantified training overhead:** The computational and memory cost of the Top‑N selection and KL computation during training is not systematically reported.
- **Scalability ceiling:** CAR decays sharply beyond 16 heads; the error‑propagation dynamics and potential architectural modifications to extend the useful range are not analyzed.
- **Dataset and scale limitations:** Experiments used a single dataset (FineWeb‑Edu‑350B) and models up to 10B (MoE); sensitivity to data distribution and performance on vastly larger models remain unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Gradient‑detached distillation preserves main‑head performance:** Medium (supported by a clear ablation, but limited external validation).
- **Top‑N selection (N=10,000) balances efficiency and stability:** High (directly validated via multiple ablations with clear reasoning).
- **Looped extension enables economical scaling to 8–16 heads:** Medium (empirical evidence shows recovery within groups, but only up to 16 heads; beyond that is untested).

## Next Checks

2. **Extended Looped Scaling Stress Test.** Apply looped extension to 24 and 32 heads, tracking CAR per head to precisely locate the point where CAR becomes negligible (<2%) and to characterise the decay curve.
