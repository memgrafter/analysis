# Fine-Tune, Don't Prompt, Your Language Model to Identify Biased Language in Clinical Notes
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.10004
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10004
- **Reference count**: 18
- **Primary result**: Supervised fine-tuning with lexically primed inputs significantly outperforms zero-shot and in-context learning for detecting biased language, but models exhibit poor cross-specialty generalizability.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical need to automate detection of stigmatizing and privileging language in clinical notes. The authors construct a bias-detection framework by curating a lexicon of biased terms, extracting anchored text chunks from OB-GYN notes and MIMIC-IV, and obtaining clinician annotations. They compare zero-shot prompting, in-context learning, and supervised fine-tuning using both an encoder-only model (GatorTron) and a generative model (Llama). Fine-tuning consistently achieves superior performance, with GatorTron reaching an F1 score of 0.96 on the OB-GYN test set. However, external validation on the broader MIMIC-IV dataset reveals a substantial 44% performance drop, underscoring that bias detection is highly specialty-specific and context-dependent.

## Method Summary
The study's methodology involves several stages: first, a lexicon of stigmatizing, privileging, and neutral terms is curated from clinical literature and clinician input. This lexicon is used to extract "anchored" text chunks (sentences containing a biased term) from two sources: a dedicated OB-GYN dataset and the MIMIC-IV critical care database. These chunks are then annotated by clinicians for bias classification. Three modeling approaches are evaluated: zero-shot prompting, few-shot in-context learning, and supervised fine-tuning. Both an encoder-only domain-specific model (GatorTron) and a general-purpose generative model (Llama) are tested. A key innovation is "lexical priming," where the biased term from the lexicon is prepended to the input text during fine-tuning to explicitly guide the model.

## Key Results
*   Supervised fine-tuning, especially with lexically primed inputs, substantially outperforms zero-shot and in-context learning paradigms across both GatorTron and Llama models.
*   On the in-domain OB-GYN test set, fine-tuned GatorTron achieves a high F1 score of 0.96.
*   The same OB-GYN fine-tuned model shows poor generalization to MIMIC-IV notes, with performance dropping 44% (F1 < 0.70), demonstrating a severe specialty-specificity problem.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism is that fine-tuning allows the model to learn specialty-specific contextual patterns and emotional valences associated with terms, which are not captured by general-purpose prompting or a static lexicon. A term labeled as "stigmatizing" in one specialty may be "neutral" or even "privileging" in another due to differing clinical norms and communication practices. Prompting lacks the adaptability to learn these nuanced, domain-specific mappings from limited examples.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Bias Lexicon Curation**: A curated list of terms associated with stigma or privilege. *Why needed*: Provides a starting point for identifying potentially biased language and enabling anchored text extraction. *Quick check*: Validate lexicon coverage against a diverse set of clinical notes from multiple specialties.
*   **Anchored Text Extraction**: Retrieving sentences containing a term from the lexicon. *Why needed*: Focuses annotation efforts on relevant snippets and creates a balanced dataset for model training. *Quick check*: Measure the proportion of truly biased instances within the extracted chunks for a random sample.
*   **Zero-Shot Prompting**: Using a model's inherent knowledge without task-specific examples. *Why needed*: Baseline for assessing a model's out-of-the-box capability on a nuanced social task. *Quick check*: Run a consistent classification prompt across multiple model sizes/variants.
*   **In-Context Learning (Few-Shot)**: Providing a few labeled examples within the prompt. *Why needed*: Tests if models can adapt to the task from a small number of demonstrations without weight updates. *Quick check*: Systematically vary the number and selection of in-context examples to assess stability.
*   **Supervised Fine-Tuning**: Adjusting model weights on a labeled dataset. *Why needed*: Teaches the model the specific classification decision boundary for the target task and domain. *Quick check*: Analyze learning curves to ensure the model is not overfitting to lexical cues alone.
*   **Cross-Specialty Generalizability**: Model performance when applied to a different clinical domain than it was trained on. *Why needed*: Evaluates if learned bias patterns are universal or context-bound. *Quick check*: Train on one specialty/hospital system and test on a completely different one (e.g., psychiatry vs. surgery).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Lexicon -> Anchored Text Extraction -> Clinician Annotation -> (Lexical Priming) -> Model Input -> Fine-Tuning -> Evaluation

**Critical Path**: The process is data-centric. The pipeline flows from lexicon creation to targeted data extraction, human validation, and finally model adaptation. The most critical juncture is the annotation phase, as human labels define the learning target. Lexical priming is an inline pre-processing step before model input.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The primary tradeoff is between **specialty-specific performance** and **generalizability**. The lexicon and annotation process are tailored to a source domain (OB-GYN), yielding high in-domain accuracy but poor transfer. Using a broader source dataset (MIMIC-IV) improves generalizability but at the cost of reduced precision, likely due to noisier, more heterogeneous labels and contexts. Another tradeoff is **lexicon dependence vs. contextual learning**: priming helps initially but may cause over-reliance on surface forms if fine-tuning data is limited.

**Failure Signatures**: 1) A sharp drop in F1-score (e.g., >30%) when evaluating on a dataset from a different specialty or hospital system. 2) Low precision on broad datasets, indicating the model makes many false positive bias detections on ambiguous or neutral phrases common in general notes. 3) High disagreement between model predictions and clinician annotations on edge cases involving specialty-specific euphemisms or indirect language.

**First Three Experiments**:
1.  **Replication of core result**: Implement the full pipeline for OB-GYN using GatorTron-base, confirming the F1 >0.95 and the benefit of lexical priming over non-primed fine-tuning.
2.  **Specialty-transfer test**: Take the OB-GYN fine-tuned model and evaluate it *without further training* on a held-out set of psychiatry notes (if available) or a different MIMIC-IV sub-section (e.g., discharge summaries vs. progress notes) to quantify the generalizability gap.
3.  **Ablation of lexical priming**: Fine-tune identical models with and without prepending the biased term to the input text. Compare performance on both in-domain and cross-domain test sets to isolate priming's contribution to in-domain success versus potential hindrance to generalization.

## Limitations
*   **Generalizability gap**: The 44% performance drop on MIMIC-IV vs. OB-GYN indicates the framework is highly specialty-specific, with underlying causes (terminology variance, contextual norms) not fully disentangled.
*   **Annotation subjectivity**: Reliance on clinician annotations introduces potential inter-rater variability and specialty-specific biases that are not quantified.
*   **Lexicon completeness**: The curated lexicon may not capture all manifestations of stigma (e.g., implicit bias) and may overfit to known terms from the OB-GYN domain.

## Confidence
*   **Fine-tuning > prompting**: High (consistent across encoder/generative models, multiple metrics).
*   **Specialty-specific fine-tuning necessity**: Medium (supported by OB-GYN↔MIMIC-IV results, but unverified beyond these domains).
*   **Lexicon priming effectiveness**: Medium (works within specialty, but lexicon's role vs. fine-tuning data is partially conflated).
*   **Cross-specialty poor generalizability**: High (quantified 44% drop, replicated with reversed training).

## Next Checks
1.  **Multi-specialty holdout testing**: Apply the fine-tuned OB-GYN model to at least two new specialties (e.g., emergency medicine, psychiatry) with matched lexicon annotations to quantify generalizability limits.
2.  **Lexicon expansion and inter-specialty calibration**: Augment the lexicon via a Delphi panel with diverse clinicians and linguists; re-annotate a shared subset across specialties to measure context-dependent valence shifts.
3.  **Error-driven context analysis**: Perform fine-grained error analysis on MIMIC-IV misclassifications (e.g., false negatives from specialty-specific euphemisms) to inform context-aware feature engineering or prompt tuning.
