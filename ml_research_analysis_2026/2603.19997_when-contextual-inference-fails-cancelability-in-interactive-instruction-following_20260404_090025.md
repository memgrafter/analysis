# When Contextual Inference Fails: Cancelability in Interactive Instruction Following

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19997
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19997
- **Reference count:** 20
- **Primary result:** Large language models demonstrate strong zero-shot pragmatic inference but fail to adapt their clarification strategies to speaker reliability, revealing a dissociation between uncertainty judgment and goal‑directed action.

## Executive Summary
The paper examines whether large language models (LLMs) can distinguish literal meaning from contextual inference and adjust their interpretations based on a speaker’s reliability. Using a block‑building task with two speaker types—a cooperative pragmatic speaker (Pragmatic Pia) and a literal‑only speaker (Literal Lisa)—the authors introduce the interactive Build What I Mean (BWIM) benchmark. Models resolve ambiguous instructions by either inferring context or asking clarifying questions at a small cost. All tested models correctly applied pragmatic inference, but a key dissociation emerged: while models detected speaker unreliability in explicit confidence ratings, they failed to exploit this information to guide efficient clarification behavior. GPT‑5.1 displayed extreme question‑aversion, capping accuracy at 73.1%; Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude Opus asked questions frequently but at nearly identical rates for both speakers, leading to suboptimal cost management. Humans, in contrast, adaptively questioned the unreliable speaker more. The findings show that pragmatic knowledge alone is insufficient; LLMs struggle to translate uncertainty into cost‑effective, interactive action.

## Method Summary
BWIM is an interactive block‑building environment where an LLM builder receives instructions from either Pragmatic Pia (who omits contextually inferable details) or Literal Lisa (who requires explicit details). Each speaker block contains 20 instructions (8 fully‑specified, 12 underspecified: 6 missing color, 6 missing stack height). The builder can either output a build command or ask a single clarification question per turn; each question incurs a –5 point cost, correct builds +10, incorrect –10. Feedback after each turn indicates whether the built structure matches the target. The builder must learn over the block to tailor its strategy: for Pia, inference is safe; for Lisa, asking is safer. Evaluations compare question‑asking rates between speakers, overall accuracy, and in a separate experiment, explicit confidence ratings (1–4) on underspecified trials. Models are assessed zero‑shot via API calls with a system prompt defining the grid, output format, and scoring; the full conversation history is appended each turn.

## Key Results
- Models showed strong pragmatic inference, correctly interpreting underspecified instructions from Pia, but exhibited extreme questioning behaviors: GPT‑5.1 asked almost no questions and reached only 73.1% accuracy with Lisa; Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude Opus asked questions at nearly identical rates for both speakers (partner‑blind over‑clarification).
- Confidence ratings revealed that models detected unreliability (lower confidence for Lisa on underspecified trials), yet this did not translate into adaptive questioning.
- Humans increased questioning for the unreliable speaker; models did not, demonstrating a failure to align information‑seeking actions with learned partner reliability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Dissociation of Uncertainty Judgment and Action Policy
- **Claim:** Models possess internal representations of speaker‑specific reliability (evidenced by confidence ratings) but fail to integrate this learned uncertainty into their action selection policy for information‑seeking.
- **Mechanism:** The task reveals two partially decoupled processes: (1) a bottom‑up assessment of interpretation confidence based on feedback history, and (2) a top‑down action policy (e.g., “ask or build”) that does not sufficiently weight the confidence signal. The policy may be overly reliant on surface‑level triggers (e.g., encountering an underspecified instruction) rather than a learned model of partner reliability.
- **Core assumption:** Adaptive clarification requires a mapping from internal uncertainty estimates to cost‑sensitive action thresholds. The observed behaviors suggest this mapping is either missing, rigid, or not properly modulated by partner identity.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “while models detected speaker unreliability in explicit confidence ratings… they failed to exploit this information to guide efficient clarification behavior.”  
  - Section 5 (Results): “GPT and Gemini both show patterns of adaptation” in confidence but “Gemini asks an almost identical number of questions from both Pragmatic Pia and Literal Lisa.”
- **Break condition:** If models began showing significant differential questioning rates between speakers (e.g., p<0.05 in a mixed‑effects model of question count ~ speaker), the dissociation would be resolved.

### Mechanism 2: Rigid Pragmatic Enrichment with Impaired Cancellation
- **Claim:** Models apply contextual enrichment (pragmatic inference) as a default strategy but lack a flexible mechanism to suppress or “cancel” this inference when faced with evidence of partner‑specific unreliability.
- **Mechanism:** The high baseline rate of pragmatic interpretation (>70% even for Literal Lisa) indicates that enrichment is strong and automatic. The persistence of this bias, despite negative feedback, suggests the inferred contextual meaning is not sufficiently tagged or prioritized against a competing literal interpretation track. Cancellation may require a separate inhibitory control process not robustly developed.
- **Core assumption:** Successful adaptation requires not just recognizing unreliability but actively overriding a prepotent pragmatic inference. The data shows recognition (lower confidence) without consistent override (continued pragmatic guesses or random noise).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 5 (Results): “In the Literal Lisa condition… Claude maintained a pragmatic choice in 92.4% of cases, while GPT dropped to 79% and Gemini to 70%.”  
  - Section 2 (The adaptation task): Defines cancellability as the key diagnostic: “contextual inferences are defeasible and may fail in contexts where the literal content remains reliable.”
- **Break condition:** If a model’s non‑pragmatic response rate for Literal Lisa rose significantly above chance and correlated with later question‑asking, it would indicate effective cancellation influencing action.

### Mechanism 3: Partner‑Blind Trigger‑Based Clarification
- **Claim:** Models that do ask questions employ a strategy triggered by ambiguity (underspecification) rather than by a posteriori estimate of partner reliability, leading to suboptimal cost management.
- **Mechanism:** The classification of an instruction as “underspecified” likely activates a high‑cost error avoidance heuristic (“when unsure, ask”). This heuristic is not fine‑tuned by the learned speaker model. For Gemini and Claude, the trigger is so strong it overrides the knowledge that one speaker is reliably pragmatic. For GPT, the trigger is absent or suppressed, leading to extreme risk‑taking.
- **Core assumption:** Cost‑effective clarification requires a dynamic threshold that shifts based on the expected value of an inference given the partner. The observed behaviors suggest a static or minimally adaptive threshold.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “suboptimal strategies, such as partner‑blind over‑clarification.”  
  - Section 5 (Results): “Gemini asks an almost identical number of questions from both Pragmatic Pia and Literal Lisa.”  
  - Table 4 (Models’ feedback): Models attribute differences to “omitted information” rather than “reliability of inferences,” suggesting they are not forming a partner model that modulates action policy.
- **Break condition:** A significant interaction in question count between speaker type and model, where at least one model asks significantly more questions to the unreliable speaker.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Cancellability as a Diagnostic**  
  *Why needed here:* The entire experimental design hinges on using the defeasibility of pragmatic inferences to isolate the processing of literal vs. contextual meaning. A learner must understand that a reliable speaker’s omitted information can be safely inferred (non‑cancelable context), while an unreliable speaker’s omissions are not license for inference (cancelable).  
  *Quick check question:* If a speaker says “Build a tower” after a blue block was just placed, what is the literal meaning, and when would you cancel the inference “tower should be blue”?

- **Concept: Interactive Benchmarking vs. Metalinguistic Judgment**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper argues that explicit confidence ratings or multiple‑choice tests probe *knowledge* of pragmatics, but only interactive, cost‑sensitive tasks probe its *deployment* in service of a goal. An engineer must understand why a model can ace an implicature quiz yet fail to act on that knowledge.  
  *Quick check question:* What is a key limitation of evaluating pragmatic competence only through explicit “what was meant?” prompts, according to the paper’s methodology?

- **Concept: Partner‑Specific Adaptation as a Test of Theory of Mind Lite**  
  *Why needed here:* The task requires building a minimal theory of the partner’s communicative competence (pragmatic vs. literal) and using it to tailor one’s own actions. This is a precursor to more complex theory of mind.  
  *Quick check question:* What specific piece of feedback allows an agent to update its belief about whether a speaker is a “Pragmatic Pia” or a “Literal Lisa”?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Speaker Profile Module -> Model Under Test -> {Build: Grid Environment Simulator | Question: Speaker Profile Module} -> Scoring & Cost Engine -> Feedback & History Buffer -> Model Under Test (next turn)
- **Critical path:** The core interactive loop: (Speaker instruction) -> (Prompt construction with history) -> (LLM generation) -> (Parse action: build or question) -> (If question, get answer from speaker module) -> (Execute build, get feedback) -> (Append to history).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Fixed cost vs. variable cost: The –5 penalty is explicit and constant; real‑world costs are implicit (time, social friction).  
  - Binary speaker identity vs. continuous reliability: Speakers are fixed types; real partners lie on a spectrum.  
  - Single ambiguity type: Only color/number repetition from previous structure; real‑world ambiguities are multiple and interacting.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. Partner‑Blind Questioning: Question rate does not differ significantly (p>0.05) between speaker blocks.  
  2. Question Aversion: Overall question rate near zero, accuracy capped well below 100% on underspecified trials with the literal speaker.  
  3. No Confidence Adaptation: No significant drop in confidence ratings for underspecified trials with the literal speaker compared to the pragmatic speaker.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Replication of Confidence Dissociation:** Run the confidence‑rating experiment (Exp 1) to confirm the model detects unreliability before running the interactive benchmark.  
  2. **BWIM Baseline:** Run the full interactive benchmark (Exp 2) to establish the model’s question‑asking strategy and final accuracy; diagnose over‑ or under‑clarification.  
  3. **Ablation: Speaker Order & Carryover:** Test if adaptation is symmetric and if learning about one speaker negatively transfers to the other; compare performance in [Pragmatic then Literal] vs. [Literal then Pragmatic] orders.

## Limitations
- The BWIM benchmark and exact stimulus set are unreleased, preventing stimulus‑controlled replications.
- Results are limited to proprietary model versions (GPT‑5.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude Opus 4.5) that may not generalize to other architectures or future versions.
- The mechanistic link between internal uncertainty representations and the action policy is inferred behaviorally, not directly observed.

## Confidence
- Dissociation of Judgment vs. Action: High
- Rigid Pragmatic Enrichment: Medium
- Partner‑Blind Trigger‑Based Clarification: High

## Next Checks
1. Stimulus‑Controlled Replication: Re‑run BWIM with recreated stimulus sets on accessible model families (e.g., GPT‑4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) to test the dissociation pattern.
2. Action Policy Intervention: Fine‑tune or prompt‑engineer a model to explicitly link confidence estimates to a dynamic questioning threshold; success would show elevated differential questioning.
3. Internal Probe Analysis: Use diagnostic classifiers or attention analysis on model hidden states during BWIM to search for latent signals of speaker reliability; absence in action head would confirm decoupling.
