# Can large language models interpret unstructured chat data on dynamic group decision-making processes? Evidence on joint destination choice

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05582  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05582  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** LLMs reliably extract explicit decision-making entities and coreferences (F1≈1.0) but show significant performance drops on subjective tasks like egocentrism detection and rationale inference.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can automate the extraction of structured decision-making factors—such as participants, alternatives, preferences, and rationales—from unstructured group chat transcripts about joint restaurant choices. Using a four-step sequential prompting framework, the authors evaluate GPT-5 on tasks ranging from objective entity recognition to subjective social inference. Quantitatively, the model excels at surface-level annotation but struggles with nuanced interpretation of group negotiation dynamics. Qualitatively, it exhibits optimism bias and fails to capture implicit social cues. The findings suggest LLMs can assist in preliminary coding of group decision data but require human oversight for deeper analytic tasks involving social nuance.

## Method Summary
The authors implement a four-step prompting framework that processes chat logs sequentially: first, it discovers and categorizes explicit entities (people, options, preferences); second, it resolves coreferences to link pronouns and descriptions to specific entities; third, it extracts perceptions of others’ preferences and egocentric statements; and fourth, it identifies rationales behind stated preferences. Each step builds on the structured output of the previous one, aiming to decompose the complex inference problem into manageable sub-tasks. No human baseline comparisons are included; evaluation relies on F1 scores against a gold-standard annotation set derived from the same corpus.

## Key Results
- Near-perfect F1 scores (~1.0) for explicit entity discovery and coreference resolution.
- Moderate performance on egocentrism detection (F1=0.41–0.67) and perception extraction (F1=0.82).
- Low performance on preference rationale extraction (Positive-F1=0.37–0.40).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The sequential prompting framework works by reducing cognitive load on the LLM: each step focuses on a specific, well-defined annotation task with clear output schemas. This design leverages the model’s strength at following precise instructions for surface-level pattern matching (e.g., entity listing) while containing the complexity of subjective inference to later stages where contextual hints from prior steps are available. The stepwise decomposition also makes errors easier to isolate and debug. Assumption: The chain-of-thought style explicitly structures intermediate representations, minimizing contextual interference between distinct annotation goals.

## Foundational Learning
The framework builds competence through progressive mastery of annotation subtasks:

- **Entity Discovery**: Identifying named participants, alternative options, and explicit preferences. *Why needed*: Forms the foundational set of items to be tracked. *Quick check*: Can the model list all mentioned restaurants and people from a chat snippet without misclassification?
- **Coreference Resolution**: Linking pronouns (e.g., “she”, “they”) or descriptors (e.g., “the vegetarian”) to specific entities. *Why needed*: Prevents fragmentation of the same actor or option across turns. *Quick check*: Does the model consistently map “him” to the correct male participant across multiple messages?
- **Perception Extraction**: Detecting statements about what a participant believes others prefer. *Why needed*: Reveals negotiation and misinformation in group dynamics. *Quick check*: Can the model distinguish between “I think John likes sushi” (perception) and “John likes sushi” (direct fact)?
- **Egocentrism Detection**: Flagging self-centered remarks that prioritize individual over group preference. *Why needed*: Quantifies dominance and cooperation. *Quick check*: Does the model recognize “I don’t care what anyone else wants, I want pizza” as egocentric?
- **Rationale Extraction**: Pulling out explicit justifications for preferences (e.g., “because it’s close”). *Why needed*: Explains decision drivers. *Quick check*: Can the model extract reasons even when they are embedded in longer sentences or conditional clauses?
- **Relation Extraction**: Generalizing from entities and perceptions to preference and rationale links. *Why needed*: Builds the decision graph. *Quick check*: Can the model connect a person to an option with a reason in a structured triple?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Entity Discovery → Coreference Resolution → Egocentrism/Perception Extraction → Rationale Extraction  
**Critical path**: Raw chat text enters Entity Discovery; its output (entity list with mentions) feeds Coreference Resolution, which produces a resolved entity map. This map contextualizes Egocentrism/Perception Extraction, whose outputs (self-focus and belief statements) inform Rationale Extraction to assign reasons to specific preferences.  
**Design tradeoffs**: The sequential design simplifies prompt engineering and debugging but introduces error propagation—early mistakes (e.g., missed entities) cascade. A parallel architecture might reduce this but would require more complex coordination prompts and risk inconsistent references.  
**Failure signatures**: (1) Sudden F1 drops when moving from entity-level to subjective tasks; (2) qualitative reports of “optimism bias” where LLMs interpret ambiguous social signals more positively than human coders; (3) inability to link indirect rationales or detect unspoken negotiation tactics.  
**First three experiments**:  
1. Swap GPT-5 with Claude 3 and Llama 3 using identical prompts and data to measure portability of performance gaps.  
2. Apply the framework to two new corpora (e.g., travel planning chats, project prioritization meetings) to test domain robustness.  
3. Run a human annotation study on the same egocentrism and rationale tasks to establish a performance ceiling and calibrate LLM adequacy thresholds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper primarily documents empirical performance boundaries rather than articulating explicit future research questions. However, the findings implicitly raise several open questions: Is the optimism bias model-specific or inherent to instruction-following on subjective social tasks? How does performance generalize beyond casual restaurant discussions to high-stakes or professional group decisions? Unknown: Whether the sequential framework’s error propagation could be mitigated with confidence-weighted outputs or iterative refinement. The absence of a human baseline leaves practical utility thresholds undefined.

## Limitations
- Generalizability constrained by a narrow corpus focused solely on restaurant choices in a casual setting.  
- Exclusive evaluation with GPT-5 precludes understanding of model-specific biases or strengths across LLM families.  
- Absence of a human baseline for subjective tasks makes it unclear whether reported F1 scores (e.g., 0.41–0.67) are useful in practice.  
- Qualitative claims about optimism bias and implicit dynamics lack systematic measurement and rely on anecdotal examples.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: Claims about explicit entity and coreference extraction performance (F1 ~1.0) are well-supported by objective metrics.
- **Medium Confidence**: Findings on egocentrism detection and perception extraction are moderately supported by F1 scores but unstable across subcategories and lack human comparison.
- **Low Confidence**: Assertions regarding optimism bias and difficulty with implicit social dynamics are based on qualitative anecdotes only; rationale extraction performance (Positive-F1=0.37–0.40) is metric-based but very low, suggesting limited practical reliability.

## Next Checks
1. Conduct cross-model replication using at least two other leading LLMs (e.g., Claude 3, Llama 3) with identical prompts and dataset.
2. Test the framework on at least three new corpora representing different group decision scenarios (e.g., travel planning, project prioritization, conflict resolution).
3. Establish a human-LLM comparative benchmark for subjective tasks (egocentrism, rationale extraction) to define adequate performance thresholds.
