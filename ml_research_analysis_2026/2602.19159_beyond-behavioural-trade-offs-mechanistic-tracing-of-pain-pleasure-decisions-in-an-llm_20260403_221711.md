# Beyond Behavioural Trade-Offs: Mechanistic Tracing of Pain-Pleasure Decisions in an LLM

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19159
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19159
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Identified specific internal loci where valence-like information is represented and causally used, moving beyond behavioral observations.

## Executive Summary
This study bridges behavioral evidence of LLM pain/pleasure trade-offs with mechanistic interpretability by probing and intervening in Gemma-2-9B-it. Using linear probing, the authors find that pain versus pleasure signs are perfectly linearly separable (AUC=1.00) from the earliest layers (0–1) across residual and attention streams, and quantitative intensity can be decoded with R² up to 0.825. The internal valence representation most aligned with the final decision peaks at token position pos-2 in late layers (correlation ≈0.986), suggesting the critical comparison occurs just before output. Activation steering along a data-derived valence direction shifts the 2–3 decision margin by up to ±2.19 logits, with maximal effect at attention output layer 14; head-level analyses indicate a distributed implementation across many heads. Together, these results locate specific internal circuits for valence processing and demonstrate their causal role in trade-offs.

## Method Summary
The authors use a controlled decision task where prompts ask the model to choose a digit (1/2/3) to maximize points; digit 3 is tied to pain, digit 2 to pleasure, with intensities varied quantitatively (1–10) and qualitatively (e.g., “mild”). Neutral control prompts are also included. Activations are extracted from a frozen Gemma-2-9B-it via TransformerLens, targeting residual and attention streams across layers 0–16 and token positions pos-1 (final) to pos-5. Linear probes (logistic for sign, linear for intensity) assess representational availability. Causal interventions include: (1) additive steering—activating a direction vector (μ_pleasure – μ_pain) scaled by ε and measuring the induced change in the 2–3 logit margin; (2) head-level swap and ablation at attention output L14 to probe distribution. The analysis focuses on early layers for sign separability, mid-late layers for intensity decoding, and late layers (especially L10–L15) for decision alignment and steering effects.

## Key Results
- Linear probes achieve perfect (AUC=1.00) separation of pain vs. pleasure signs from layers 0–1 across all stream families (resid_post, attn, mlp).
- Quantitative intensity is decodable with R² up to 0.825 (attention stream, L10); qualitative intensity shows lower but significant Spearman ρ.
- Decision-aligned valence structure peaks at token position pos-2 in late layers (L12–L13), correlating with the 2–3 logit margin at ≈0.986.
- Activation steering along the data-derived valence direction modulates the margin by up to +2.00 / –2.19 logits, strongest at attention output L14; effects exceed those at the final residual stream.
- Head-level patching/ablation at attn_out L14 shows modest individual contributions (heads 2,3) but a strong aggregate effect, suggesting a distributed implementation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Early linear separability of valence sign
**Claim:** A linearly separable valence sign (pain vs. pleasure) representation exists in Gemma-2-9B-it from very early layers (L0–L1).  
**Mechanism:** Linear probes applied to residual and attention stream activations at the final token position achieve perfect (AUC=1.00) classification of pain/pleasure prompts starting at layer 0–1.  
**Core assumption:** Perfect linear separability at early layers reflects a dedicated, decision-relevant valence representation rather than spurious correlation with lexical cues.  
**Evidence:** Abstract and Table 1 show best AUC=1.00 at L0 in resid_post/attn and L1 in resid_pre/mlp; BoW baseline effective AUC≈0.741 falls short but leaves room for lexical confounds.  
**Break condition:** If a lexical baseline (BoW) matched the perfect AUC, the mechanism would reduce to surface-form tracking.

### Mechanism 2: Decision-aligned valence structure peaks at pos‑2
**Claim:** The most decision‑aligned valence structure (graded intensity) peaks slightly before the final token (pos‑2) in late layers, not at the decision position itself.  
**Mechanism:** Scanning the last five token positions reveals that correlation between the data‑derived valence direction (μ_pleasure – μ_pain) and the 2–3 logit margin reaches ≈0.985 at pos‑2 in layers L12–L13.  
**Core assumption:** The pos‑2 peak reflects a genuine comparison/decision computation rather than a template artifact (e.g., the explicit digit list).  
**Evidence:** Abstract reports Corr(logits)≈0.986; Section notes pos‑2 often coincides with the final option token in the response‑format clause.  
**Break condition:** If counterbalanced templates (permuting/removing the digit list) cause the pos‑2 peak to vanish or shift, the mechanism would be template‑dependent.

### Mechanism 3: Causal control via steering and distributed head implementation
**Claim:** Causal control of the 2–3 decision margin via a data‑derived valence direction is strongest when intervening in late‑layer attention outputs (attn_out L14), and this effect is distributed across multiple heads.  
**Mechanism:** Activation steering (h ← h + ε·v_val) produces the largest modulation of the decision margin (Δ up to +2.00 / –2.19) at attn_out L14, exceeding effects at the final residual stream. Head‑level patching/ablation suggests no single head is necessary, but the aggregate effect across heads (0–15) is largest, with heads 2 and 3 showing modest individual contributions.  
**Core assumption:** The steering effect magnitude at a site reflects its functional role in routing valence information; the distributed head pattern suggests a multi‑head implementation.  
**Evidence:** Abstract notes strongest effects at attn_out L14; Table 6 shows attn_out L14 max |Δmargin| > resid_post L15; Table 7 shows vector‑level effects and head‑level contributions.  
**Break condition:** If steering at attn_out L14 fails to generalize to new templates, or if head‑level effects concentrate in a single head under more sensitive metrics, the distributed claim would be weakened.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Linear Probing**  
  - Why needed here: To map *representational availability*—whether pain/pleasure sign and intensity are linearly decodable from internal activations. This establishes a descriptive signature before causal tests.  
  - Quick check question: If a linear probe achieves perfect separation on training data but a simple BoW baseline achieves 0.74 AUC, what does that imply about the probe's result?

- **Concept: Activation Steering & Steering Vectors**  
  - Why needed here: To test *sufficiency*—whether adding a valence‑direction vector at a specific site can causally modulate the decision margin. The dose‑response (ε sweep) shows control strength and monotonicity.  
  - Quick check question: Why might steering along a data‑derived valence direction produce a nonlinear or weak dose‑response compared to steering along the unembedding 2‑3 axis?

- **Concept: Transformer Streams & Token Positions**  
  - Why needed here: To localise *where* and *when* in the forward pass valence information is processed (resid_pre, attn_out, mlp_out, resid_post; pos‑1 vs. pos‑2). The positional scan reveals that decision alignment peaks at pos‑2.  
  - Quick check question: If the valence signal is strongest at pos‑2 but the decision is read at pos‑1, what computational role might pos‑2 play (e.g., option comparison vs. final decision)?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Gemma-2-9B-it transformer blocks (L0‑L42). Analysis focused on first 16 blocks. Key streams per block: resid_pre (input to block), attn_out (attention output), mlp_out (MLP output), resid_post (output summed into residual). Final decision read from resid_post at last token (pos‑1) via unembedding. Token positions counted backward from end (pos‑1 = final).  
- **Critical path:** Prompt → tokenized input → forward pass through L0‑L15 → activation at target site (e.g., attn_out L14 pos‑1) → intervention (steer/patch) → final resid_post pos‑1 → logits → 2‑3 margin / normalized choice probability.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Depth vs. computational cost:* Analysed only first 16 layers (of 42) for tractability; may miss earlier/late contributions.  
  - *Position choice:* Defaulted to pos‑1 for causal interventions to match standard next‑token prediction, though Corr(logits) peaked at pos‑2. This trades positional optimality for consistency with typical readouts.  
  - *Intervention magnitude:* Steering effects were weak near ε=0 and only strong at |ε|≥100, risking off‑manifold perturbations. Tradeoff between detectable effect and ecological validity.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Probing: BoW baseline matches perfect AUC → valence signal is lexical confound.  
  - Steering: Unembedding‑axis sanity check fails (no margin change) → pipeline error (hook location, token indexing, margin calc).  
  - Localisation: Steering effect at attn_out L14 does not replicate across different prompt templates → effect is template‑specific.  
  - Head patching: Single‑head swap/ablation shows large effect (contradicting "distributed" claim) → valence computation is concentrated.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Replicate linear probing** on a held‑out prompt set (same template) to confirm sign AUC=1.00 and intensity R² peaks. Check if BoW still lags.  
  2. **Steering at resid_post L15** with ε∈{-200,…,200} to reproduce Figure 3 curve. Verify monotonicity and compare to unembedding‑axis control. *Action:* Use same prompt pool, compute mean margin vs. ε, fit slope near 0.  
  3. **Head‑level patching at attn_out L14:** Swap each head (0‑15) individually with pleasure/pain class means, compute Δ(pleasure−pain) margin. Ablate each head’s projection onto valence direction. *Action:* Expect small individual effects (<0.1) but all‑heads swap effect > single heads.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** To what extent are valence representations in Gemma‑2‑9B‑it disentangled from surface lexical and template confounds, such as the specific wording "pain" vs. "pleasure" or the explicit digit list in the prompt?  
Basis in paper: The authors identify residual lexical confounding as a key limitation, noting the BoW baseline achieves effective AUC ≈0.741 for sign decoding. They explicitly propose future work using "paraphrase and synonym controls, counterbalanced templates, and ‘symbol‑to‑valence’ remappings."  
Why unresolved: Perfect early‑layer sign separability (AUC=1.00) is compatible with strong surface‑form cues; the current experimental design cannot isolate whether the model constructs an internal, lexical‑independent valence variable.  
What evidence would resolve it: Implement and test with counterbalanced prompt templates that permute or remove the explicit digit list (e.g., replacing {1,2,3} with neutral symbols), use paraphrased prompts with synonyms for "pain"/"pleasure", and train valence directions on one format (e.g., quantitative) and test on another (e.g., qualitative) to assess cross‑format generalization.

**Open Question 2:** What is the complete spatiotemporal circuit through which valence information is routed and integrated into the decision, particularly around the pos‑2 token position where decision‑aligned valence structure peaks?  
Basis in paper: The results show Corr(logits) peaks near pos‑2 in late layers (≈0.985). The authors explicitly state this suggests "the most decision‑aligned representation may occur just prior to the final token" and propose expanding scans to "a fuller stream × layer × position map, especially around positions where decision alignment peaks (e.g., pos‑2)."  
Why unresolved: The current analysis focused primarily on pos‑1 for causal interventions; the pos‑2 peak coincides with the response‑format clause ("…1, 2, or 3."), so it may be template‑specific. The full routing from input tokens through all layers to the decision site remains unmapped.  
What evidence would resolve it: Perform a comprehensive layer‑wise × position (pos‑5 to pos‑1) mapping of valence decodability and causal leverage across all streams. Apply path patching to trace mediation pathways from earlier token positions (e.g., where pain/pleasure is described) through intermediate layers to the final decision site (e.g., L14 attn_out).

**Open Question 3:** Is the distributed implementation of valence across multiple attention heads functionally redundant, or do specific head subsets play distinct, non‑overlapping roles in encoding or routing valence information?  
Basis in paper: Head‑level patching at attn_out L14 shows modest individual effects (heads 2 and 3) but stronger aggregate effects, leading authors to conclude the mechanism is "distributed across multiple heads rather than concentrated in a single unit." They explicitly suggest "leave‑one‑out head necessity (all heads vs. all \ {h})" to quantify marginal contributions.  
Why unresolved: Current head‑level ablation/swap results are noisy and based on small prompt sets without confidence intervals; it is unclear whether heads contribute uniquely or if effects are fully redundant.  
What evidence would resolve it: Conduct systematic leave‑one‑out head ablation and patching with larger, balanced prompt sets and report confidence intervals. Combine head interventions after aligning their valence‑direction projections (sign‑aligning and normalizing) to test for additive vs. subadditive effects. Identify if head contributions are specialized by correlating head activity with pain vs. pleasure or with specific intensity levels.

**Open Question 4:** Do valence representations in Gemma‑2‑9B‑it generalize across different semantic framings of intensity (quantitative numeric scales vs. qualitative verbal descriptors), or are they format‑specific associations?  
Basis in paper: The study uses both quantitative and qualitative intensity scales but analyzes them separately or pools them. The authors explicitly propose "cross‑form generalization" tests: "training valence directions on quantitative prompts and testing on qualitative prompts (and vice versa)" to distinguish robust representations from format‑specific cues.  
Why unresolved: The behavioral screening showed different choice patterns under quantitative vs. qualitative pleasure (model complied with quantitative but not qualitative). It is unknown whether internal valence directions transferred between formats reflect a common underlying variable or separate, format‑bound associations.  
What evidence would resolve it: Extract valence directions (e.g., μ_pleasure - μ_pain) from one format (e.g., quantitative) and apply them as steering vectors to prompts in the other format (qualitative). Test whether steering effects on the 2‑3 margin persist and are dose‑responsive. Perform linear probing to see if intensity decoders trained on one format predict the other.

**Open Question 5:** What is the causal gradient of the valence direction with respect to the decision boundary, and how does it vary across layers? Does the data‑derived valence axis align with the true causal direction that would maximize control efficacy?  
Basis in paper: Steering along the data‑derived valence axis (μ_pleasure - μ_pain) produced modest, non‑monotonic effects near ε=0 and showed sign inversion at L13. The authors note this suggests "partial misalignment between the descriptive μ_pleasure - μ_pain direction and the local causal gradient" and propose calibrating steering "in units of natural activation variance."  
Why unresolved: The observed steering curve (flat near origin, inversion at L13) indicates the descriptive direction is not consistently aligned with the decision gradient. Without knowing the true causal gradient, it is unclear whether the valence axis is off‑manifold, rotated, or if downstream computation nullifies early perturbations.  
What evidence would resolve it: Estimate the local decision gradient via targeted interventions (e.g., using infinitesimal patches or optimization‑based direction search) at each late layer (L10–L15) and compare its orientation to the data‑derived valence direction. Alternatively, use causal mediation analysis to trace how perturbations at upstream sites propagate to the decision margin, revealing the effective causal subspace.

## Limitations
- Analysis restricted to the first 16 of 42 layers; deeper contributions to valence processing remain unexplored.
- Prompt templates include explicit digit lists (e.g., "2: pleasure, 3: pain"), risking positional artifacts such as the pos‑2 peak.
- Steering effects were only robust at large ε (≥100), potentially off‑manifold and ecologically invalid.
- Head‑level patching showed tiny individual effects (Δmargin < 0.1), making the claim of a distributed implementation fragile.
- Findings are based on a single model (Gemma‑2‑9B‑it); broader validity across architectures and sizes is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Early linear separability (Mechanism 1)**: Medium  
- **Decision‑aligned pos‑2 peak (Mechanism 2)**: Medium  
- **Causal steering at attn_out L14 & distributed heads (Mechanism 3)**: Medium‑Low  

## Next Checks
1. **Template counterbalancing:** Remove or permute the explicit digit list in prompts and re‑scan the positional correlation of the valence direction; a vanishing pos‑2 peak would confirm template dependency.
2. **Lexical control probing:** Use scrambled prompt tokens or simple keyword features (e.g., “pain”, “pleasure”) in a linear probe; if these achieve AUC≈1.00, the early separability reduces to surface tracking.
3. **Steering generalization:** Apply the valence direction derived from one prompt template (e.g., quantitative) to a novel template (e.g., qualitative) and measure margin changes; consistent effects across templates validate the causal claim.
