# Harnessing Reasoning Trajectories for Hallucination Detection via Answer-agreement Representation Shaping

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17467
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17467
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieves state-of-the-art hallucination detection, e.g., 86.64% AUROC on TruthfulQA with Qwen3-8B, a 19.79% absolute gain over vanilla embeddings.

## Executive Summary
Large reasoning models (LRMs) generate extensive reasoning traces but still suffer from hallucinations, making detection from raw outputs or standard embeddings unreliable. This paper proposes Answer-agreement Representation Shaping (ARS), a method that perturbs the embedding at the end of the reasoning trace to produce counterfactual answers. By labeling these counterfactuals based on agreement with the original answer, ARS learns a lightweight mapping that reshapes the answer embedding space: embeddings of answers that agree under perturbation are pulled together, while disagreeing ones are separated. This explicit encoding of answer stability yields a powerful hallucination signal. ARS consistently improves detection across four reasoning datasets and two LRM families, achieving state-of-the-art results such as 86.64% AUROC on TruthfulQA with Qwen3-8B (a 19.79% absolute gain). The approach requires no human annotation and adds minimal test-time overhead.

## Method Summary
ARS operates on a frozen LRM. Given a reasoning trace, it extracts the penultimate-layer hidden state \(h\) after the last reasoning token. Gaussian noise is added to \(h\) and decoding resumes, generating \(M\) counterfactual answers \(\tilde{a}_j\). Answer embeddings \(u\) for the original and \(\tilde{u}_j\) for the counterfactuals are labeled as agreeing or disagreeing via an LRM judge or text similarity. A linear projection \(g_\phi\) is trained with a contrastive loss: for each anchor embedding \(z = g_\phi(u)\), it maximizes similarity to an agreeing \(\tilde{z}^+\) and minimizes similarity to a disagreeing \(\tilde{z}^-\). After training, the shaped embedding \(z\) of a single generation can be fed directly to any off-the-shelf embedding-based detector (e.g., supervised probe, CCS, EigenScore). This injects a stability signal without test-time sampling or model updates.

## Key Results
- ARS achieves state-of-the-art hallucination detection on four datasets (TruthfulQA, GSM8K, etc.) and two LRM families (Qwen, DeepSeek).
- On TruthfulQA with Qwen3-8B, ARS reaches 86.64% AUROC, a 19.79% absolute improvement over vanilla embeddings.
- The method is detector-agnostic, generalizes across domains within the same LRM family, and scales to larger models; it outperforms diverse baselines and requires no human annotation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ARS's gains stem from three interconnected mechanisms:

### Mechanism 1: Latent Intervention at Trace Boundary for Counterfactual Generation
The trace-boundary embedding \(h\) captures the model's internal state before answer decoding. Injecting Gaussian noise into \(h\) yields perturbed states \(\tilde{h} = h + \delta\) that, when decoding resumes, produce counterfactual answers \(\tilde{a}\). If the original answer is truthful and supported by a stable state, most perturbations preserve the answer; if the answer arises from a fragile state (hallucination), many perturbations flip the answer. This makes agreement under small latent perturbations a sharp indicator of answer stability. The core assumption is that \(h\) already commits to the final answer's semantic direction, so perturbations primarily affect the answer rather than earlier reasoning. Evidence: Section 4.1 argues that intervening at the trace boundary yields a cleaner stability signal than text-space interventions; Table 4 shows latent noise outperforms deletion/masking.

### Mechanism 2: Agreement-Driven Representation Shaping
Vanilla answer embeddings entangle semantic content with surface variation. ARS learns a linear mapping \(g_\phi\) that transforms an answer embedding \(u\) into a shaped space \(z\). By generating multiple counterfactual embeddings \(\tilde{u}^+\) (agreeing) and \(\tilde{u}^-\) (disagreeing) via latent intervention, the contrastive loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{ARS}}\) maximizes similarity between \(z\) and agreeing \(\tilde{z}^+\) while minimizing similarity to disagreeing \(\tilde{z}^-\). This explicitly encodes answer stability into the embedding geometry: embeddings of answers robust to perturbations cluster together, while fragile ones are isolated. The core assumption is that answer agreement under small perturbations is a reliable, model-intrinsic marker of hallucination risk. Evidence: Table 2 shows ARS-shaped embeddings substantially boost AUROC across detectors (e.g., 66.85 → 86.64 on TruthfulQA for CCS); Figure 3 (right) indicates improvement across layers.

### Mechanism 3: Plug-and-Play Detection with Shaped Embeddings
Once \(g_\phi\) is trained, ARS requires no additional forward passes or interventions at test time. The shaped embedding \(z\) for a single generation can be scored by any off-the-shelf embedding-based detector, treating it as a more truthfulness-aware representation. The stability signal induced by ARS generalizes to heterogeneous scoring functions because the reshaped space enhances clustering, variance, or linear separability of truthful vs. hallucinated outputs. Evidence: Section 4.3 reports that ARS improves performance for all four detectors (CCS, probing, HaloScope, EigenScore); Figure 3 (left) demonstrates cross-dataset transfer (e.g., GSM8K → TriviaQA), supporting detector-agnostic benefits.

## Foundational Learning
### Autoregressive Decoding & Trace Boundary
**Why needed:** ARS intervenes at the penultimate-layer state of the last reasoning token. Understanding that generation proceeds token-by-token and that the answer decoding starts from this boundary is essential to grasp why perturbations there affect only the answer and not earlier steps.  
**Quick check:** If you add noise to a hidden state at layer \(L\) after all reasoning tokens are processed, which part of the output will be primarily altered? (Answer: The subsequent answer tokens, because the reasoning trace is already fixed.)

### Latent Space Interventions for Counterfactuals
**Why needed:** ARS replaces text editing with direct noise injection in the latent representation. This avoids disrupting surface coherence while still affecting decoding outcome. Knowing how to manipulate hidden states to probe model decisions is key to designing stability signals.  
**Quick check:** Why might token deletion in the reasoning trace produce less reliable counterfactual answers than small Gaussian noise at the trace boundary? (Answer: Deletion changes the token sequence and may break syntax/semantics, while latent noise minimally perturbs the trace and lets the model “fill in” naturally.)

### Contrastive Representation Learning
**Why needed:** The ARS loss is a contrastive objective that pulls agreement samples together and pushes disagreement apart. Recognizing this as metric learning explains why shaped embeddings become more separable.  
**Quick check:** If \(\text{sim}(z, \tilde{z}^+)\) is high and \(\text{sim}(z, \tilde{z}^-)\) is low for many triples, what property does the embedding space have with respect to answer stability? (Answer: Embeddings of answers that are robust to perturbations cluster together, while fragile answers are isolated.)

### Probing Classifiers for Hallucination Detection
**Why needed:** ARS-shaped embeddings are evaluated via supervised probes, CCS, etc. Knowing that these detectors assume truthful/hallucinated outputs occupy distinguishable regions in the representation space is necessary to interpret Table 2 results.  
**Quick check:** What does a higher AUROC for HaloScope on ARS embeddings vs. vanilla embeddings indicate about the shaped space? (Answer: The PCA-based subspace used by HaloScope better separates truth from hallucination after ARS shaping.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Frozen LRM (π_θ) → Trace-boundary extractor → Perturbation module → Counterfactual decoder → Answer embedding extractor → Agreement scorer → Shaping head (g_ϕ) → Downstream detector.

### Critical Path
1. Generate (x, r, a) from π_θ.
2. Extract h (trace-boundary) and sample M Gaussian perturbations → M counterfactual answers {ã_j}.
3. Compute answer embeddings u (original) and ũ_j (counterfactuals); label Agr groups (agree/disagree).
4. Train the linear projection g_ϕ with contrastive loss over (z, z̃⁺, z̃⁻) triples to maximize agreement similarity and minimize disagreement similarity.
5. At test time: compute z = g_ϕ(u) for a new generation and feed it to any embedding-based detector; no additional interventions.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Intervention position**: Perturbing at the trace boundary yields a strong stability signal while minimally disrupting the reasoning trace; earlier or later positions weaken the signal (Figure 5a).
- **Noise scale σ**: Too small yields insufficient disagreement; too large produces degenerate counterfactuals. Optimal σ ≈ 1.75 (empirically determined, Figure 5b).
- **Projection head complexity**: A single linear layer suffices for g_ϕ, balancing simplicity and cross-detector compatibility; nonlinear heads (e.g., MLP) may offer gains but increase parameters and risk overfitting.
- **Agreement labeling**: Using the LRM itself as a judge avoids human annotation but may be biased if the model consistently hallucinates; text metrics (ROUGE) provide a cheaper, possibly less biased alternative with minor performance drop (Appendix H).

### Failure Signatures
- **Poor agreement labeling**: Noisy Agr labels (e.g., inconsistent LRM judge) reduce the effectiveness of the contrastive loss, reflected by a low ηϕ in Proposition 4.2.
- **Non-transfer across domains**: If training on a specific dataset (e.g., MATH-500) yields low AUROC on a distinct domain (e.g., TruthfulQA), the stability signal may be dataset-specific; ARS shows cross-dataset transfer within the same LRM family but extreme shifts could break it.
- **Layer mismatch**: Using too early or too late layers for the trace-boundary embedding can degrade performance; intermediate-to-late layers catch answer-related semantics best (Figure 3 right).

### First Three Experiments
1. **Validate intervention position**: Compare detection AUROC when perturbing at various token positions (early trace, mid-trace, boundary, mid-answer). The boundary should yield the highest improvement over baselines, confirming its optimal trade-off.
2. **Ablate perturbation strength**: Sweep the noise standard deviation σ and measure downstream detector AUROC. Expect a peaked curve indicating an optimal signal-to-noise ratio; identify that peak (around 1.75).
3. **Test shaped embedding separability**: Visualize z (e.g., via t-SNE) for truthful and hallucinated samples. ARS should produce tighter clusters for answer-agreeing counterfactuals and a clearer separation between truthful and hallucinated groups compared to vanilla embeddings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: How does reasoning trace length impact ARS detection performance?
- **Basis:** The paper's experiments aggregate over all traces without stratifying by length; Section 5.4 examines fractional positions but not absolute length.
- **Why unresolved:** The stability signal at the boundary might weaken for very long traces (due to noise accumulation) or very short traces (less context), but this is not investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Bin test samples by reasoning trace token count and compute AUROC per bin for ARS vs. baselines; also perform controlled manipulations (truncate/extend traces) and measure performance changes.

### Open Question 2: How sensitive is ARS to the choice of answer agreement function Agr(·,·)?
- **Basis:** ARS uses the same LRM to judge agreement while noting Agr could be textual similarity or an LRM judge; no comparison among these choices is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** Self-agreement may be biased if the model consistently hallucinates; different judges could yield different positive/negative pairs and thus different shaped embeddings.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train ARS with multiple Agr implementations (exact match, ROUGE-L, a stronger external judge, or human judgments on a small set) and compare detection AUROC on a held-out test set.

### Open Question 3: Could a nonlinear shaping head (e.g., a small MLP) further improve ARS's representation separation compared to the linear projection?
- **Basis:** ARS employs a single linear projection for g_ϕ for simplicity and cross-detector compatibility; the theoretical analysis does not restrict linearity, and Section 5.4 ablates output dimension but not the projector's nonlinearity.
- **Why unresolved:** A linear mapping might be insufficient to disentangle agreeing and disagreeing embeddings; nonlinearities could capture more complex patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace the linear g_ϕ with a lightweight MLP (similar parameter count), train under identical settings, and compare AUROC across detectors on all datasets; include embedding visualizations to assess cluster quality.

## Limitations
- Trace segmentation for models without explicit delimiters (e.g., Qwen) relies on heuristics that may affect the quality of the trace-boundary embedding.
- Agreement labeling uses the LRM's self-judge, which could be biased and propagate the model's own hallucinations.
- Generating multiple counterfactuals per sample increases computational overhead during training.

## Confidence
- Boundary Intervention Mechanism: Medium confidence.
- Agreement-Driven Shaping: High confidence.
- Plug-and-Play Generalization: High confidence.
