# Think over Trajectories: Leveraging Video Generation to Reconstruct GPS Trajectories from Cellular Signaling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26610  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26610  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Achieves state-of-the-art Sig2GPS accuracy, reducing mean absolute error by ~30% (e.g., 214.96m vs. 306.12m) and boosting L100 by 25 percentage points, while lowering inference latency to ~30 seconds.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the Sig2GPS problem: reconstructing high-precision GPS trajectories from coarse cellular signaling records. It reframes the task as a map-visual video generation problem: cellular signaling traces are rendered onto map tiles, and a flow-based video model is trained to generate a video where a continuously drawn path depicts the GPS trajectory. The generated video is then refined via a trajectory-aware reinforcement learning method (Traj-GDPO) that optimizes for distance, direction, and continuity rewards. The approach surpasses existing industrial baselines in accuracy across all trajectory scopes, reduces inference time dramatically, and scales favorably with more frames. It also demonstrates strong cross-city transferability for next-GPS prediction.

## Method Summary
The method is a two-stage pipeline. First, in supervised fine-tuning (SFT), a pre-trained flow-based video generator (Wan2.2-TI2V-5B) is fine-tuned with LoRA on pairs of map-conditioning images and target videos. The conditioning image is an OpenStreetMap tile with the signaling polyline overlaid; the target video shows the ground-truth GPS path being progressively drawn over the same map. The flow-matching objective trains the network to predict a velocity field that transforms noise into the video. Second, the reinforcement learning stage (Traj-GDPO) refines the SFT policy. For each conditioning image, multiple stochastic rollouts are generated by converting the deterministic ODE sampler to an SDE sampler. From each rollout video, the final trajectory polyline is extracted and three programmatic rewards are computed: geodesic distance error at anchor frames, directional consistency (dot product of start-end vectors), and continuity (binary penalty for fragmented paths). Advantages are computed via group-relative normalization of each reward component, summed, and batch-normalized. A clipped policy gradient update with a KL penalty anchored to the initial SFT policy improves the generator toward higher-reward trajectories while preventing catastrophic drift.

## Key Results
- **SOTA accuracy:** Reduces mean absolute error (MAE) by 30% on small-scope trajectories (214.96m vs. 306.12m of the best industrial baseline) and increases L100 (error <100m) by up to 25 percentage points across all scopes.
- **Faster inference:** Trajectory generation takes ~30 seconds, compared to >2 minutes for the previous engineered pipeline.
- **Scalable & transferable:** Test-time scaling (more frames) consistently improves accuracy, and the model shows strong cross-city performance on next-GPS prediction tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Map-Visual Alignment with Human Intuition**  
Rendering coarse cellular signaling traces onto a map tile provides a visual interface that aligns with expert human reasoning for inferring plausible GPS routes. The map tile encodes spatial context (road networks, landmarks) missing from raw numeric sequences. A video generation model trained on such pairs learns to respect road topology and spatiotemporal continuity automatically, as the visual domain naturally constrains the output to follow roads. Break condition: if the map style or projection differs between training and deployment, the visual prior may fail, leading to topologically invalid paths (e.g., cutting through buildings).

**Mechanism 2: Flow-Based Video Generation for Implicit Continuity**  
Flow-matching models define a continuous probability path via a learned velocity field. Training with an ℓ₂ loss on interpolated latents forces the network to predict coherent motion vectors that transform noise into a structured video frame sequence. This inherent temporality encourages smoothness and spatial consistency across frames. The map context grounds the generation, allowing the model to attend to road geometry, biasing the velocity field toward on-road motion. Break condition: with extremely sparse signaling (only a few cell connections), the conditioning polyline provides insufficient spatiotemporal anchors, potentially causing globally incorrect paths (e.g., wrong highway exit) due to ambiguous map context.

**Mechanism 3: Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**  
The Traj-GDPO stage directly optimizes trajectory-level metrics poorly captured by the SFT ℓ₂ loss. By sampling diverse rollouts via SDE noise and computing three programmatic rewards (distance, direction, continuity), the policy gradient update refines the flow model’s velocity field. Group-relative normalization (GDPO) handles heterogeneous reward scales, while a KL penalty anchored to the fixed SFT policy prevents collapse. This RL stage corrects fine-grained topological and directional errors left by SFT. Break condition: miscalibrated rewards (e.g., overly strict continuity) could lead to unnatural trajectories (e.g., excessively straight lines) that satisfy programmatic checks but lack plausibility.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Data Pipeline → SFT Module → RL Module

**Critical Path**  
Data collection and rendering quality → successful SFT convergence (baseline video quality) → stable RL training (no mode collapse) → inference scaling (more frames → better accuracy). RL stabilization is the most brittle step.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Fixed KL reference (SFT policy) vs. moving reference: Fixed prevents drift but limits adaptation; moving allows exploration but risks collapse.  
- Decoupled vs. scalarized rewards: GDPO prevents high-magnitude rewards from dominating but introduces hyperparameters (group size K, normalization ε).  
- Video frame count: More frames improve accuracy but increase latency; F=21 is chosen as a trade‑off.

**Failure Signatures**  
- *SFT stage:* Generated videos show correct overall shape but wrong road-level choices (e.g., service road instead of main lane).  
- *RL stage (without proper KL):* Trajectories collapse to repetitive, simplistic paths (e.g., long straight segments) with high reward variance.  
- *RL stage (with poor reward design):* Optimizes one reward at the expense of others (e.g., perfect continuity but large distance error).  
- *Inference:* Degradation on cities with different road conventions (e.g., left-hand driving) if training data is exclusively right-hand.

**First Three Experiments**  
1. **Rendering sanity check:** Visualize a batch of (conditioning image, target video) pairs to ensure alignment and smooth drawing.  
2. **SFT baseline evaluation:** Generate videos for the validation set and compute MAE/RMSE to confirm reasonable performance without RL.  
3. **Single-reward RL ablation:** Run Traj‑GDPO with only the distance reward active to isolate its impact; expect MAE improvement but degradation in continuity/direction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Robustness to sparse signaling:** How does the model perform when cellular connectivity is very sparse (few base station connections)? The paper identifies this as future work.
- **Cross-map generalization:** Can the model handle map tiles from different providers or cartographic styles (e.g., Google Maps, vector tiles) beyond OpenStreetMap? This limitation is noted.
- **Extension to other tasks:** Whether the Think Over Trajectory framework can be adapted for trajectory recovery (filling missing segments) and long‑horizon forecasting remains open.
- **Dataset construction bias:** The heuristic matching criteria used to pair signaling with GPS traces may bias the model toward dense, long‑duration, on‑road taxi trajectories; impact on other mobility patterns is unknown.

## Limitations
- The method relies heavily on the visual map domain; performance may degrade if deployed map style, projection, or road network representation differs from training.
- Very sparse signaling traces (≤3 connections over a long duration) likely cause global misrouting due to insufficient spatial anchors.
- Reward functions in Traj‑GDPO are heuristic and may over‑optimize for programmatic checks, producing unnatural trajectories if miscalibrated.

## Confidence
- **SOTA accuracy claims:** *Medium* – backed by internal benchmarks vs. industrial baselines, but potential leakage or lack of external validation.
- **Map‑visual mechanism:** *Medium* – plausible with case studies, but break conditions (map mismatch) identified; corpus provides no direct validation.
- **Traj‑GDPO RL refinement:** *Medium‑High* – supported by ablations, but reward design lacks real‑world plausibility validation beyond programmatic metrics.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑Map Generalization Test:** Evaluate the trained model on map tiles from different providers (e.g., Mapbox) or with style‑transfer augmentations; measure performance drop and visualize artifacts.
2. **Sparse Signaling Stress Test:** Artificially down‑sample signaling traces in the test set to extreme sparsity (e.g., 1 connection per 5 km) and quantify MAE/L100 degradation; confirm if failure mode matches the “global misrouting” hypothesis.
3. **Reward Sensitivity Sweep:** Systematically vary weights/thresholds of \(R_{cont}\) and \(R_{dir}\) and measure trade‑offs with MAE and human‑rated trajectory naturalness (e.g., via crowdsourced turn plausibility survey).
