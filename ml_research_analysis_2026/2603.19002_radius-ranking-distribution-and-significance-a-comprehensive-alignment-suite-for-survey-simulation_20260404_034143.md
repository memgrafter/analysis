# RADIUS: Ranking, Distribution, and Significance - A Comprehensive Alignment Suite for Survey Simulation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19002
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19002
- **Reference count:** 39
- **Primary result:** Introduces RADIUS, a multi-dimensional alignment suite with significance testing that reveals complementary failure modes in LLM survey simulation, showing ranking alignment is easier than distribution alignment and advocating for joint assessment.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the fragmented evaluation of LLM-based survey simulation by proposing RADIUS, a standardized alignment suite. RADIUS measures both ranking alignment (Top Rank Match, Rank Correlation) and distribution alignment (Total Variation Distance, Distribution Homogeneity), each with statistical significance tests to account for human data sampling variance. Evaluated on over 300 multiple-choice questions from GSS and OpinionQA surveys, the suite demonstrates higher discriminative power than common metrics like Cramer's V or JSD. Key findings indicate that ranking alignment is generally easier to achieve than distribution alignment, while homogeneity testing is the strictest criterion. Crucially, RADIUS exposes cases where high performance on one dimension masks failure on another, underscoring the need for multi-dimensional assessment in survey simulation.

## Method Summary
RADIUS evaluates simulator alignment by framing human and simulated responses as categorical distributions over survey options. For each question, it computes four metrics: Top Rank Match (TRM) uses bootstrapping (n=1000) to derive confidence intervals for human top options, penalizing simulations only if their top choice falls outside the statistically tied group; Rank Correlation (RC) is normalized Spearman’s ρ; Total Variation Distance (TVD) quantifies probability mass shift; and Distribution Homogeneity (DH) applies a chi-square test, returning a binary flag for significant differences. Question-level scores are averaged for survey-level summary, and paired t-tests compare simulator runs. This design prioritizes robustness to human sampling noise and captures orthogonal alignment dimensions.

## Key Results
- RADIUS metrics exhibit wider value ranges (e.g., RC: 0.3–0.9, TVD: 0.3–0.7) than commonly used metrics (Cramer's V, JSD), demonstrating higher discriminative power across diverse survey questions.
- Ranking alignment (TRM/RC) is generally easier to achieve than distribution alignment (TVD/DH) for LLM simulators, with DH being the strictest criterion due to its sensitivity to small consistent shifts.
- Complementary failure modes are prevalent: examples show perfect TRM with high TVD (correct top option but wrong magnitudes), high RC with TRM=0 (correct order but wrong top choice), and DH always significant despite low TVD (statistically detectable small shifts).
- Paired statistical tests (e.g., t-tests) on survey-level scores reliably distinguish simulator performance where single metrics might not.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Multi-dimensional alignment (ranking + distribution) prevents misalignment from being masked, as high performance on one dimension can hide failure on another.
- **Mechanism:** The suite computes orthogonal metrics: ranking metrics (TRM, RC) assess option order preservation, while distribution metrics (TVD, DH) quantify probability mass shifts and statistical distinguishability. Their combination surfaces complementary failure modes.
- **Core assumption:** Ranking and distribution misalignments can occur independently; a simulator might capture correct ordering but wrong magnitudes, or vice versa.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...complementary failure modes where performance on one dimension masks misalignment on another"
  - [section 5] Qualitative examples (Q1–Q6 Table 2) where metrics diverge.
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers focus on single-dimension alignment (e.g., "Distribution Shift Alignment...").

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Statistical significance testing (bootstrap for TRM, chi-square for DH) makes metrics robust to human data sampling variance and ties, preventing false penalization.
- **Mechanism:** For TRM, bootstrap resampling creates confidence intervals to group statistically tied top options; agents are only penalized if they pick outside the human top-group. For DH, chi-square tests if distributions are statistically indistinguishable, not just numerically different.
- **Core assumption:** Human survey distributions are samples from a larger population; evaluation should account for uncertainty rather than treating the observed distribution as ground truth.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1] "bootstrapping... handles ties in human preferences"
  - [section 4.2] "chi-square test of homogeneity... statistically significant differences"
  - [corpus] Not directly addressed in neighbors; they use point metrics (e.g., JSD) without significance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** RADIUS metrics have higher discriminative power than common alternatives (JSD, CV, WD), as evidenced by wider value ranges and clearer separation from non-parametric baselines.
- **Mechanism:** Metrics like TVD and RC are bounded and interpretable (0–1 scale), and their design (e.g., TVD's mass-shift interpretation) makes them more sensitive to categorical mismatches than distributional correlations like Cramer's V.
- **Core assumption:** Existing metrics are compressed in range and fail to capture nuanced misalignments; new metrics will show greater spread across diverse surveys.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5] "Our metrics span a wide range... RC from 0.3 to 0.9, TVD from 0.3 to 0.7... commonly used metrics exhibit narrower ranges"
  - [Table 1] LLM vs. non-parametric baselines: RADIUS metrics often show significant differences where others don't.
  - [corpus] Neighbor "AlignSurvey" also notes limitations of existing metrics but doesn't propose discriminative alternatives.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Categorical response distributions in surveys.
  - **Why needed here:** RADIUS treats each question's human/agent responses as probability distributions over options. Engineers must understand normalization, handling of zero counts, and the difference between ordinal vs. nominal options.
  - **Quick check question:** If a question has 3 options with human counts [100, 200, 300], what is the normalized distribution, and why might treating options as ordinal affect ranking metrics?
- **Concept:** Non-parametric statistical testing (bootstrap, chi-square).
  - **Why needed here:** TRM uses bootstrap to handle ties; DH uses chi-square for homogeneity. Engineers must grasp resampling logic, p-value interpretation, and test assumptions (e.g., chi-square's expected frequency requirements).
  - **Quick check question:** How does increasing bootstrap iterations affect TRM's stability, and what might a chi-square test's result imply if one distribution has a very rare option with count < 5?
- **Concept:** Rank correlation (Spearman) and its limitations.
  - **Why needed here:** RC is a core metric; its calculation (ranks, tied ranks) and interpretation (monotonic vs. linear) are critical. Engineers must know when it can be misleading (e.g., with many ties).
  - **Quick check question:** If human ranks are [1,2,3] and agent ranks are [1,3,2], what is RC, and does it capture that the top choice is correct but the middle two are swapped?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Loader -> Distribution Normalizer -> Metric Calculators (TRM, RC, TVD, DH) -> Aggregator -> Statistical Test Utils
- **Critical path:** Load data → normalize → for each question: compute TRM, RC, TVD, DH → aggregate → statistical comparison between runs. TRM and DH depend on significance testing; others are direct.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Bootstrap iterations*: More = robust but slower; tradeoff between CI stability and compute.
  - *Chi-square min-count*: Aggregating rare options improves test validity but may mask misalignment in tail options.
  - *Metric normalization*: RC is normalized to [0,1]; keep consistent for reporting.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - All TRM = 1 but TVD high → ranking preserved but magnitudes wrong (see Q1 in paper).
  - DH always 0 (significant) but TVD low → small but consistent shifts.
  - RC near 1 but TRM = 0 → order preserved but top choice wrong.
  - Metrics unchanged across baselines (random vs. LLM) → suite not discriminative.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replicate Table 1**: Run RADIUS on provided GSS/OpinionQA data with LLM and baselines; verify TRM/RC/TVD/DH values match within rounding.
  2. **Bootstrap sensitivity test**: Vary `n_bootstrap` (100, 1000, 10000) on questions with close top options; check TRM stability.
  3. **Metric correlation analysis**: Compute Pearson correlation between all metrics (TRM, RC, TVD, DH, JSD, CV) across 300+ questions; expect low correlation between ranking vs. distribution groups, validating complementarity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What are the inherent trade-offs between optimizing for ranking alignment versus distribution alignment in survey simulation, and can these trade-offs be quantified?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Future work could explore the trade-offs between ranking and distribution alignment..."
  - **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates that these are complementary dimensions but does not explore whether improvements in one consistently come at the expense of the other or if they can be jointly optimized.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments where a simulator's architecture or prompting is varied to intentionally shift performance across the RADIUS dimensions, measuring the correlation between metric scores to identify Pareto frontiers or fundamental conflicts.
- **Open Question 2:** How does alignment at the individual-response level (e.g., persona fidelity) relate to population-level alignment (e.g., distribution matching) measured by RADIUS?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Future work could... investigate the relationship between individual- and group-level alignment in surveys."
  - **Why unresolved:** The suite operates at the population level, but many simulation methods (e.g., persona-based agents) are explicitly designed for individual-level realism. The connection between simulating believable individuals and generating accurate aggregates is not established.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Studies that compute both individual-level accuracy/RMSE and RADIUS scores on the same simulator runs, analyzing their correlation across questions and populations to see if one predicts the other.
- **Open Question 3:** How should survey-level RADIUS scores be aggregated and weighted across heterogeneous questions (e.g., different topic difficulty, number of options) to produce a single, meaningful summary metric for comparison?
  - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 4.3 states survey-level alignment is computed by "averaging question-level scores" but provides no justification for this simple mean. The varying strictness and scales of different metrics (TRM vs. TVD) across question types suggest naive averaging may be problematic.
  - **Why unresolved:** The paper advocates for a multi-dimensional suite but then reduces it to scalar comparisons in tables (e.g., Table 1). The methodological choice of aggregation (simple mean vs. weighted, handling of metric scales) is a hidden assumption that could bias simulator comparisons.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of the variance and correlation structure of question-level scores across the GSS/OpinionQA datasets to propose a principled aggregation scheme (e.g., meta-metric, weighting by question reliability or difficulty).
- **Open Question 4:** Can the RADIUS framework and its statistical significance tests be extended to evaluate alignment for more complex survey question formats, such as open-ended responses or multi-select questions?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "this work focuses on multiple-choice single-select questions... leaving open-ended survey simulation to future work." The DH test and ranking metrics are defined for categorical, single-choice data.
  - **Why unresolved:** The value of survey simulation depends on handling diverse real-world survey formats. The core principles (ranking, distribution, significance) are applicable, but the operational definitions (e.g., "top choice," chi-square test) need adaptation for text or sets of options.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and validation of new metric variants for open-ended and multi-select data. This would involve creating embeddings or clustering for open responses, defining meaningful "rankings" or distributions over them, and adapting homogeneity tests for multi-label data.

## Limitations
- **Major unknowns block faithful reproduction:** Exact persona generation method and LLM prompts (only referenced via [23]), LLM model and sampling parameters used for simulation, and bootstrap confidence interval method (percentile vs. BCa).
- **Corpus support weak:** Claims about novelty and superiority over fragmented prior work lack direct citation analysis; neighboring papers focus on single-dimension alignment.
- **Generalization concerns:** Suite validated only on multiple-choice single-select surveys from two sources (GSS, OpinionQA); applicability to other formats (open-ended, multi-select) is untested.

## Confidence
- **Multi-dimensional complementarity:** Medium (supported by qualitative examples but lacks broad correlation analysis across all questions).
- **Significance testing robustness:** Medium (mechanism clear, but break conditions like test sensitivity/insensitivity not systematically evaluated).
- **Discriminative power vs. alternatives:** Low (claims based on limited reported ranges; needs large-scale validation across more survey datasets).
