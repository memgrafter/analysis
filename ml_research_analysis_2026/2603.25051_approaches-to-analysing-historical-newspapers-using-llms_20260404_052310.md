# Approaches to Analysing Historical Newspapers Using LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.25051
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25051
- Reference count: 6
- Primary result: GaMS3-12B-Instruct model best for aspect-level sentiment on OCR-degraded newspapers (69.5% accuracy, weighted F1=0.708).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the application of large language models (LLMs) to the scalable analysis of OCR-degraded historical newspapers, with a focus on representing collective identities. The authors evaluate four instruction-following LLMs for aspect-level sentiment classification on identity mentions, identifying the Slovene-adapted GaMS3-12B-Instruct model as the most suitable, achieving 69.5% accuracy and a weighted F1 score of 0.708. The model excels at detecting neutral sentiment (F1=0.794) but struggles with positive and negative classes.

Applied to a large corpus of early 20th-century Slovene newspapers, the pipeline reveals systematic sentiment variation—e.g., Germans are predominantly portrayed negatively, while Austrians are mostly neutral. By integrating BERTopic topic modeling and entity co-occurrence graphs, the mixed-methods approach uncovers clear ideological differences between the conservative-Catholic *Slovenec* and the liberal-progressive *Slovenski narod*. Despite limitations such as class imbalance, the study demonstrates the value of LLMs for digital humanities on noisy historical corpora.

## Method Summary
The study employs a mixed-methods pipeline tailored to OCR-degraded historical newspapers. First, paragraphs are extracted from the sPeriodika corpus and identity mentions are identified using a manually compiled lexicon of collective identity terms. Each mention is assigned a sentiment label (positive/negative/neutral) by prompting an instruction-following LLM (GaMS3-12B-Instruct) with the target identity marked in XML and a 5-sentence context window. In parallel, BERTopic clusters all paragraphs into topics using multilingual sentence embeddings. These signals—identity mentions, sentiment, topics, and named entities (e.g., locations)—are aggregated at the paragraph level to construct co-occurrence graphs. Graph metrics guide targeted retrieval of source snippets for qualitative close reading and critical discourse analysis. The pipeline is evaluated on a manually annotated sample of 371 identity mentions, and then applied to compare two major Slovene newspapers of the late 19th/early 20th century.

## Key Results
- GaMS3-12B-Instruct achieves 69.5% accuracy and weighted F1=0.708, with strong neutral detection (F1=0.794) but weaker performance on positive and negative sentiment.
- Large-scale analysis reveals that Germans are associated with predominantly negative sentiment, while Austrians receive mostly neutral sentiment.
- Combined use of LLM sentiment, BERTopic, and co-occurrence graphs uncovers clear ideological differences between the conservative-Catholic *Slovenec* and liberal-progressive *Slovenski narod*.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Instruction-following LLMs can perform mention-level sentiment classification on OCR-degraded historical text without supervised fine-tuning.
- **Mechanism:** The model receives a structured prompt containing a target identity mark-up and a 5-sentence context window. It generates a label (positive/negative/neutral) based on its pre-trained knowledge and instruction tuning. The approach leverages the model’s ability to follow complex instructions and its exposure to diverse linguistic patterns during training.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM’s internal representations are sufficiently robust to the orthographic noise and archaic language variants common in OCR-extracted historical text to produce meaningful, if imperfect, sentiment signals.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "evaluate four instruction-following LLMs for aspect-level sentiment classification on identity mentions."
  - [section 4.2] Detailed description of the prompt structure, context window, and label set.
  - [corpus] Weak: The paper does not provide direct evidence about the *linguistic nature* of the OCR degradation (e.g., specific error rates, common misrecognitions). The model's performance is evaluated on a clean, manually annotated sample drawn from the same source, which may not fully represent the noisier parts of the full corpus.
- **Break condition:** Performance collapses on specific error types (e.g., garbled character sequences, severe diacritic loss) or for identities with highly context-dependent sentiment, where the 5-sentence window is insufficient.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Topic modeling (BERTopic) provides a scalable, context-aware method for thematically clustering heterogeneous historical paragraphs, enabling "distant reading" comparison across newspapers.
- **Mechanism:** BERTopic uses transformer-based sentence embeddings ( paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2) to capture semantic similarity, followed by dimensionality reduction (UMAP) and clustering. This neural approach is more sensitive to contextual meaning than bag-of-words models like LDA, better handling OCR noise and archaic phrasing.
- **Core assumption:** Contextual embeddings from multilingual models effectively map semantically related historical paragraphs—even with degraded text—into coherent topic spaces that reflect meaningful historical themes.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Using BERTopic, we identify major thematic patterns and show both shared concerns and clear ideological differences..."
  - [section 4.1] Explicit parameter choices and the comparison to classical models (LDA, NMF), citing Murugaraj et al. (2025) which found BERTopic superior.
  - [corpus] Strong: The corpus description provides paragraph and token counts, confirming the large scale necessitating automated methods.
- **Break condition:** Topics become overly granular (as noted: ~40k-49k topics) or incoherent without significant manual curation of the 500 most-represented topics into 20 themes, indicating the model's output requires substantial human interpretative work to be analytically useful.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Constructing co-occurrence graphs of themes, collective identities, locations, and LLM-predicted sentiment enables a mixed-methods workflow where "distant" network analysis guides "close" qualitative discourse analysis.
- **Mechanism:** Edges are created based on shared paragraph-level co-occurrence of nodes (e.g., a theme node connected to an identity node if they appear together). Node size for identities is proportional to their non-neutral sentiment share. Quantitative network properties (diversity, connectivity) highlight contrasts between newspapers, which researchers then investigate through targeted retrieval and close reading of source paragraphs.
- **Core assumption:** Statistical co-occurrence in paragraph-level data is a valid proxy for discursive association in the historical press, and variations in network metrics reveal meaningful ideological patterns (e.g., othering, belonging) that can be verified textually.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Combined with BERTopic and entity co-occurrence graphs, the mixed-methods approach uncovers clear ideological differences..."
  - [section 4.3] Precise definition of nodes, edge weights (co-occurrence counts), and visual encoding (node size for sentiment).
  - [corpus] Strong: The graph nodes are built directly from the processed corpus data (themes from BERTopic, identities from lexicon, locations from NER).
- **Break condition:** Co-occurrence patterns are driven by genre conventions (e.g., obituaries listing many nationalities) rather than ideological content, or the LLM's sentiment errors (especially over-predicted negativity) systematically distort the graph's sentiment-weighted node sizes, leading close reading to confirm spurious patterns.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Aspect-level/Targeted Sentiment Analysis
  - **Why needed here:** The research goal is sentiment *toward specific collective identities*, not general document sentiment. This requires the model to isolate sentiment for a marked entity within its local context, a more complex task.
  - **Quick check question:** If a sentence says "The German army advanced, but the German people suffered," what should the sentiment labels be for "German army" and "German people"?
- **Concept:** OCR Degradation in Historical NLP
  - **Why needed here:** The entire corpus is OCR-extracted, introducing noise (character misrecognitions, spacing errors, archaic spellings) that standard NLP tools and models may not handle well, necessitating robustness evaluation.
  - **Quick check question:** How might the archaic spelling "＝" (long s) or a common OCR error like "clcse" (for "close") affect a model's tokenization and understanding?
- **Concept:** Mixed-Methods Digital Humanities
  - **Why needed here:** The study explicitly combines scalable computational methods (topic modeling, LLM classification, graph analysis) with traditional qualitative close reading and critical discourse analysis to validate and interpret findings.
  - **Quick check question:** What is a key risk of relying solely on the LLM's aggregated sentiment scores for an identity without subsequent close reading of the source texts?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
1. Data Ingestion & Preprocessing: Load sPeriodika corpus (XML/TEI), extract paragraphs, apply basic cleaning (though OCR noise remains).
2. Lexicon Builder: Compile the manual/automatic lexicon of collective identity lemmas (nominals & adjectivals) and map adjectivals to nominal categories.
3. Identity Mention Extractor: Run lexicon over all paragraphs to find identity mentions, capturing context (sentence ±2).
4. LLM Sentiment Classifier (GaMS3-12B-Instruct): Send structured prompt for each mention (ID, tagged target, 5-sentence context) to model API; parse response to {+, -, 0}. *Critical: Manage API rate limits and costs for ~millions of mentions.*
5. Topic Modeler (BERTopic): Fit separate models per newspaper on all paragraphs; assign each paragraph a topic ID.
6. Graph Builder: Aggregate data at paragraph level: for each paragraph, have {topic, [identities], [locations], [sentiments]}. Create nodes (topics, identities, locations, sentiment). Create weighted edges for every co-occurring pair within a paragraph.
7. Visualization & Query Engine: Render graphs (e.g., Gephi, PyVis) with node size/color encoding. Implement queries to retrieve all paragraphs containing a specific node-pair (e.g., "Nemci" + "State administration").
8. Close Reading & CDA Module: Interface for researchers to query the corpus based on graph findings, retrieve source text snippets, and perform qualitative annotation/analysis.

**Critical path:** Preprocessed Paragraphs → (Lexicon Match) → Identity Mentions → (LLM API) → Sentiment Labels → (Aggregate with Topics/Locations) → Graph → (Researcher Query) → Source Snippets → Close Reading.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Accuracy vs. Scale: GaMS chosen for highest weighted F1/accuracy, not macro-F1, prioritizing performance on the dominant neutral class for large-scale aggregation, at the cost of under-detecting positive sentiment.
- Automation vs. Curation: BERTopic's raw output (~50k topics) is unusable without manual curation into 20 themes; full automation is sacrificed for meaningful thematic comparison.
- Graph Resolution: Using paragraph-level co-occurrence balances detail and computational cost, but may miss longer-range discourse relationships that span paragraphs.

**Failure signatures:**
- Sentiment: Unusually high neutral proportions (>95%) for an identity known to be historically controversial; or a sudden, large shift in predicted sentiment for an identity between two newspapers without textual evidence.
- Topics: Post-curation, a manually defined "theme" contains paragraphs with no coherent semantic link, suggesting the model's output was not salvageable.
- Graphs: A key ideological node (e.g., "Nemci") shows *lower* non-neutral sentiment in the newspaper historically known for stronger anti-German rhetoric, contradicting historiography.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. LLM Sanity Check: Run GaMS on a small, manually verified subset of 50 mentions. Does its confusion matrix align with the paper's reported patterns (strong neutral, weak positive, high-recall negative)? Does it fail catastrophically on garbled OCR strings?
2. Topic Coherence Spot-Check: After BERTopic and manual curation, randomly sample 5 paragraphs from each of the 20 final themes. Can a researcher, without knowing the theme label, consistently identify the theme's core subject? This tests if the neural topics + manual grouping produced coherent, distinct themes.
3. Graph-to-Text Validation: For a high-non-neutral identity (e.g., "Nemˇ curji"), use the graph to retrieve all paragraphs where it co-occurs with a major theme (e.g., "Political life"). Perform close reading: does the retrieved text actually contain evaluative/conflict language, or is the co-occurrence driven by neutral listing? This validates the core assumption of the graph method.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can temporal named entity graphs be constructed and analyzed to capture diachronic shifts in the sentiment and network associations of collective identities across the entire sPeriodika corpus (1771–1914)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the conclusion: "Temporal named entity graphs will be explored to observe the changing prominence and attitudes of collective identities."
- **Why unresolved:** The current study provides a cross-sectional analysis limited to two newspapers at the turn of the century, without integrating temporal dynamics or corpus-wide trends.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Time-animated visualizations and quantitative metrics (e.g., sentiment trajectory clusters, evolving co-occurrence centrality) for key identities, correlated with external historical event timelines to validate interpretive significance.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What architectural or prompting modifications to instruction-following LLMs can reduce the asymmetric performance across sentiment classes—particularly the under-detection of positive sentiment and over-prediction of negative sentiment—in OCR-degraded historical Slovene without compromising neutral sentiment reliability?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper documents GaMS’s "clear asymmetry in class behaviour" and notes that positive sentiment is "under-detected" while negative sentiment is "detected with high sensitivity but less selectively," and this is highlighted as a key limitation for dataset-level aggregation.
- **Why unresolved:** The study evaluates off-the-shelf models in a few-shot setting and selects the best performer but does not investigate error mitigation strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments comparing prompt engineering, post-processing calibration, or lightweight fine-tuning on manually annotated historical subsets, with reported improvements in class-balanced F1 scores and reduced aggregation bias.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do conservative-Catholic (*Slovenec*) and liberal-progressive (*Slovenski narod*) newspapers differ in their linguistic framing—specifically metaphor, modality, and argumentation structures—around contested collective identities (e.g., Germans, Italians) when analyzed via LLM-assisted framing detection?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states future work will "investigate how these periodicals constructed narratives around collective identities" and refers to "linguistic framing." The paper’s discourse analysis section qualitatively notes othering and positive self-presentation but does not systematically compare framing via LLMs.
- **Why unresolved:** The current LLM application is limited to aspect-level sentiment; framing analysis remains manual and thematic, not scaled or model-supported.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** LLM-driven annotation of framing devices (e.g., threat narratives, heroic tropes) across identity mentions, followed by statistical comparison of framing prevalence between newspapers and correlation with sentiment labels to test if framing mediates sentiment expression.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What evaluation framework best assesses the "interpretive validity" of LLM-generated sentiment labels in digital humanities workflows, moving beyond aggregate accuracy to measure alignment with nuanced human critical discourse analysis?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors underscore "a disconnect between technical benchmarks and scholarly needs," arguing that "a model’s F1 score is ultimately less important than its interpretive validity."
- **Why unresolved:** The study relies on standard classification metrics (accuracy, F1) against a small annotated set but does not provide a methodology for validating whether aggregated LLM outputs meaningfully reflect historical discourse complexities as judged by humanities experts.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A protocol where domain experts independently evaluate a sample of LLM-aggregated identity-sentiment distributions against close reading of source contexts, measuring agreement (e.g., Cohen’s κ) on whether the LLM metrics capture salient ideological patterns, with systematic error analysis for false positives/negatives that mislead historical interpretation.

## Limitations
- OCR degradation impact: The evaluation uses a cleaner sample; specific effects of diacritic loss or archaic characters on LLM performance remain unclear.
- Class imbalance: The model's high neutral detection (F1=0.794) but weak positive/negative performance risks distorting aggregated sentiment trends, especially for less prominent identities.
- Topic curation bottleneck: BERTopic produces ~50k topics, requiring manual reduction to 20 themes; this limits full automation and introduces researcher bias.

## Confidence
- **LLM zero-shot sentiment on OCR text** – Medium
- **BERTopic thematic clustering** – High
- **Graph-guided mixed-methods** – Medium

## Next Checks
1. **LLM robustness stress test:** Run GaMS3 on a held-out set of severely degraded OCR paragraphs to quantify failure modes and error types.
2. **Topic coherence quantification:** After manual curation, compute pairwise topic similarity (e.g., via embedding cosine) for the 20 final themes to verify semantic distinctness and reduce researcher bias.
3. **Graph pattern falsification:** For high-non-neutral identity-theme pairs (e.g., "Nemci" + "State administration"), sample 20 co-occurring paragraphs and have historians blind-score whether the association is ideologically charged vs. neutral listing.
