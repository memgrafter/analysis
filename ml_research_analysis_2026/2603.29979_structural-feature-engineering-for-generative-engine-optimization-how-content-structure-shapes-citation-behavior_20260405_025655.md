# Structural Feature Engineering for Generative Engine Optimization: How Content Structure Shapes Citation Behavior

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.29979
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29979
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Primary result:** GEO-SFE increases citation rate by 17.3% and subjective quality by 18.5% across six generative engines, with macro-structure contributing nearly half the gains.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the absence of systematic methods to optimize content structure for AI-powered search engines that generate cited answers. It introduces GEO-SFE, a framework that engineers structural features across three hierarchical levels—document architecture (macro), information chunking (meso), and visual emphasis (micro)—to influence citation behavior while preserving semantic meaning. Evaluated across six generative engines, GEO-SFE yields a consistent 17.3% increase in citation rate and an 18.5% improvement in subjective quality metrics. The findings establish structural optimization as a fundamental pillar of Generative Engine Optimization, with macro-structure alone accounting for nearly half of the observed improvements.

## Method Summary
GEO-SFE decomposes content structure into macro (document-level elements like headings and TOC), meso (section-level chunking and lists), and micro (sentence-level emphasis like bold/italics). It extracts 12+ structural metrics from HTML/DOM, classifies the target generative engine’s architecture (Search-then-Synthesize, Iterative Refinement, Integrated Search-Generation), and applies architecture-specific weights to compute target feature values. Optimization proceeds hierarchically (macro→meso→micro) under strict semantic integrity constraints (sentence embedding similarity >0.95, paragraph similarity >0.70, document JS-divergence <0.15). A cascading fallback mechanism prioritizes partial optimization over semantic corruption, and a validator selects the best transformation based on a composite visibility score.

## Key Results
- 17.3% average increase in citation rate across six generative engines.
- 18.5% improvement in human-evaluated subjective quality metrics.
- Macro-structure optimization contributed approximately 45% of total gains, underscoring the primacy of document-level architecture.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Decomposing content structure into three hierarchical levels allows granular optimization aligned with how LLMs process information at different scales.
- **Mechanism:** Different LLM attention mechanisms operate on distinct structural granularities: macro for global parsing and retrieval relevance; meso for synthesis quality and extractability; micro for token-level attention during generation. Isolating and optimizing each level targets specific LLM processing pathways without cross-interference.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs process hierarchical structure through specialized attention heads; modifying features at a given level primarily influences the corresponding processing stage.
- **Evidence anchors:** Decomposition defined in abstract and section IV.A; neighboring GEO papers focus on semantics, this work’s novelty is systematic structural decomposition (high FMR ~0.53 with related titles).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Architecture-specific feature weighting enables targeted optimization by matching structural preferences to retrieval-generation pipelines.
- **Mechanism:** Generative engines differ in retrieval strategies (batch, iterative, real-time), creating distinct “structural expectations” for cited content. The framework learns feature weights per architecture type (Table II) and adjusts optimization targets accordingly (Equation 16–17).
- **Core assumption:** Citation behavior is not architecture-agnostic; structural features interact with retrieval-generation flow, so a universal optimum does not exist.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section II.A on architecture differences; Table II shows weight variations (e.g., meta-structure clarity weight 0.45 for STS vs. 0.27 for ISG); heading depth target adjustments per architecture (section IV.D).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Semantic preservation constraints enable structural changes without altering meaning, ensuring visibility gains without content corruption.
- **Mechanism:** Structural transformations risk semantic drift. The framework imposes multi-granularity similarity thresholds (sentence: τ_sent=0.95, paragraph: τ_para=0.70, document: JS<0.15). If violated, a cascading fallback (Equation 15) reduces optimization scope—from full to partial to minimal—preventing meaning distortion even if citation gains are reduced.
- **Core assumption:** LLM citation decisions are sensitive to semantic meaning but robust to structural variations, provided meaning is preserved.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section IV.D on constraints and fallback; section V.A semantic verification (mean sentence similarity 0.843 using Bge-m3).

## Foundational Learning
- **Hierarchical Structural Decomposition**
  - Why needed: To isolate which level (document flow, section chunking, sentence emphasis) most influences LLM citation behavior, avoiding attribution to monolithic “structure.”
  - Quick check: If you add a table to a paragraph, does that change macro, meso, or micro structure? Explain why. *(Answer: meso, because it alters section-level organization by introducing a new element within a section.)*
- **Architecture-Specific Feature Weighting**
  - Why needed: Generative engines employ different retrieval strategies; a structural change beneficial for batch retrieval may not help iterative RAG.
  - Quick check: For a real-time retrieval system (Integrated Search-Generation), would you prioritize deeper heading hierarchies or more independent chunks? Why? *(Answer: More independent chunks, because iterative systems benefit from modular, cross-referencable pieces for multi-hop reasoning.)*
- **Semantic Integrity Constraints**
  - Why needed: Optimization must not alter factual meaning or logical flow; constraints ensure gains come from better presentation, not changed information.
  - Quick check: What happens if after optimization the sentence embedding similarity drops below 0.95? What does the framework do? *(Answer: Cascading fallback activates, reducing optimization scope to partial or minimal to prevent semantic corruption.)*

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input content -> Feature Extractor -> Architecture Classifier -> Weight Applier -> Optimizer (with semantic constraints after each hierarchical level) -> Validator -> Output optimized content
- **Critical path:** Feature extraction → Architecture identification → Target computation → Hierarchical optimization (macro→meso→micro with rollback on constraint violation) → Validation → Output. Semantic constraint check occurs after each level.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Feature intensity vs human readability: Excessive formatting (>35% structured elements) boosts LLM parsing but harms humans; framework enforces F_d ∈ [0.25,0.35].
  - Universal vs architecture-specific targets: Universal principles provide baseline safety; architecture-specific corrections add sensitivity but risk overfitting; α_A weights should be set based on platform traffic share.
  - Optimization scope vs semantic risk: Aggressive macro changes yield larger gains but higher violation probability; fallback ensures safety at performance cost.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Semantic collapse: Document JS-divergence >0.15 or sentence similarity <0.95; check fallback activation logs.
  - Feature distribution skew: Any feature outside [SF_min, SF_max] bounds indicates constraint violation.
  - Architecture mismatch: Low citation gain on a platform despite high gain on others suggests misclassification.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Ablation validation: Run full GEO-SFE vs. w/o macro, meso, micro on 50 sample articles. Expect ~45% contribution from macro, ~40% from meso (per Table VI). Replicate to confirm independent effects.
  2. Weight sensitivity: For one architecture (e.g., STS), perturb its feature weights ±20% and measure citation change. Expect strong sensitivity to meta-structure clarity (weight 0.45) but not micro-features (weight sum ~0.25).
  3. Constraint threshold sweep: Vary τ_sent from 0.90 to 0.98, measure citation gain vs. semantic preservation. Identify knee point where gains plateau but similarity remains >0.95 to validate chosen thresholds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do generative engine architectures (e.g., Search-then-Synthesize vs. Integrated Search-Generation) causally differ in their processing of structural features, and can these differences be predicted from architectural design principles?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper identifies architecture-specific weight variations (Table II) but does not investigate underlying mechanistic reasons, attributing differences to “distinct retrieval-generation mechanisms” without causal analysis.
- **Why unresolved:** Without understanding causal pathways, optimization strategies may fail to generalize to new or evolving architectures, limiting long-term applicability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled interpretability studies (e.g., attention head analysis, retrieval ablation) across architectural types to isolate how design choices (e.g., query decomposition, context window) mediate structural feature impact on citation decisions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What are the optimal trade-offs between structural optimization intensity and semantic preservation, and do these trade-offs vary systematically across content domains or query complexities?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper employs fixed semantic similarity thresholds (e.g., τ_sent = 0.95) without exploring whether domain-specific semantics or query types require adaptive constraint tuning.
- **Why unresolved:** Universal thresholds may over-constrain optimization in flexible domains (e.g., creative writing) or under-protect semantics in technical domains, reducing practical effectiveness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large-scale experiments varying semantic constraint thresholds across diverse domains, measuring citation gains alongside human evaluation of semantic drift to derive adaptive thresholding models.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do structural GEO optimizations interact with semantic GEO strategies (e.g., adding statistics, authoritative language) when applied concurrently, and can their combination yield synergistic improvements beyond additive effects?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper isolates structural feature engineering from semantic content modifications, treating them as independent; no experiments test combined interventions, despite acknowledging complementarity.
- **Why unresolved:** Potential interactions—positive synergy or negative interference—are unknown, risking suboptimal outcomes if creators layer strategies naively.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Factorial experimental designs where structural transformations (from GEO-SFE) and semantic modifications (from prior GEO work) are systematically crossed to measure interaction effects on citation rates across platforms.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Is the three-level hierarchical decomposition (macro, meso, micro) of content structure sufficient to capture all relevant signals for citation behavior, or are there domain-specific or novel structural features (e.g., rhetorical devices, discourse markers) that could enhance GEO-SFE?
- **Basis in paper:** The feature set is derived from general web content patterns and may not encompass specialized structures in domains like academic papers or legal documents, or emerging formats.
- **Why unresolved:** Incomplete feature coverage might limit GEO-SFE’s effectiveness in niche contexts, undermining claims of generalizability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of citation patterns in specialized domains to identify missing structural cues, followed by feature augmentation studies measuring incremental gains from added features while controlling for semantics.

## Limitations
- Semantic constraint thresholds (e.g., τ_sent=0.95) assume current LLMs are robust to structural changes but sensitive to semantic drift; as models evolve, these thresholds may become inadequate.
- Architecture-specific weights are derived from six engines and may not generalize to future or niche generative engines with different retrieval-generation pipelines.
- The enforced feature intensity bounds (F_d ∈ [0.25,0.35]) balance LLM parsing and human readability, but the optimal point for real-world user engagement (beyond citations) remains unvalidated.
