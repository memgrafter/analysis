# Modelling the Morphology of Verbal Paradigms: A Case Study in the Tokenization of Turkish and Hebrew

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05648
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05648
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: Tokenization alignment with morphological structure determines learnability; Hebrew requires morpheme-aware tokenization for paradigm learning, Turkish is robust.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how tokenization strategies affect transformer models' ability to represent complex verbal paradigms in Turkish (concatenative morphology) and Hebrew (non-concatenative binyanim). Using the Blackbird Language Matrices (BLM) task, models are evaluated on natural sentences and simplified verb-only data. Results show that for Turkish, both monolingual (BERTurk) and multilingual (Electra) models succeed regardless of tokenization granularity, reflecting the transparency of affixation. In contrast, Hebrew models diverge: AlephBERT with morpheme-aware segmentation achieves high accuracy (F1=0.835), while multilingual Electra with character-level tokenization fails (F1=0.333). Performance improves on verb-only data for all models, indicating that contextual noise hinders paradigm learning, but Hebrew's non-concatenative structure remains challenging for subword tokenization. The study concludes that tokenization acts as a linguistic filter, and its alignment with morphological structure is critical for models to learn systematic verbal paradigms.

## Method Summary
The study uses the BLM task to evaluate paradigm learning. Natural sentences are extracted from Universal Dependencies treebanks for Turkish and Hebrew via queries filtering by voice features. Instances are constructed by grouping four verb forms (same root, different voices) into puzzles with three context pairs and four candidate answers. Both natural and verb-only (VERBONLY) datasets are created, each with 8,000 balanced instances (7,200 train, 800 test). Three pretrained models—BERTurk, AlephBERT, and multilingual Electra—are frozen; sentence embeddings are obtained via mean-pooling of token representations. A feed-forward neural network (FFNN) is trained with a max-margin loss to map concatenated context embeddings to an output embedding that should be close to the correct answer embedding. Evaluation uses per-voice and average F1 scores.

## Key Results
- Turkish models succeed regardless of tokenization strategy (both BERTurk and Electra).
- For Hebrew, AlephBERT (morpheme-aware) performs excellently (avg F1=0.835); multilingual Electra (character-level) fails on natural sentences (F1=0.333).
- On verb-only data, Hebrew Electra improves significantly but still confuses causative and causative-passive forms.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Tokenization granularity as a linguistic filter for non-concatenative morphology**  
Tokenization defines the atomic units a model processes. For templatic morphology like Hebrew binyanim, highly fragmented tokenization (e.g., character-level) breaks the root-pattern relationship, making systematic alternations invisible. Morpheme-aware segmentation preserves these relationships. The model learns morphological generalizations from token distributions; if the root vs. pattern distinction is absent, it must infer from suboptimal signals, which may be impossible. Evidence: Abstract notes the Hebrew monolingual vs. multilingual divergence; Section 5.4 shows character-level representations capture surface similarity but fail to distinguish deeper differences; neighboring work on splitting nonconcatenative languages corroborates.

**Mechanism 2: Resilience for concatenative morphology**  
For concatenative systems like Turkish, affixes attach sequentially. Whether a word is one token or split, the presence and identity of affixal markers remain available in the token sequence. This allows the model to learn form-meaning mappings reliably. Core assumption: functional load is carried by discrete, addable suffixes/prefixes whose surface form survives subword splitting. Evidence: Abstract states both Turkish models succeed; Section 5.4 confirms both atomic and fragmented tokenization are effective; Table 1 shows frequent tokens include clear tense/agreement markers (e.g., `##dı`, `##ıyor`).

**Mechanism 3: Paradigm-level evaluation necessity**  
Standard evaluations may not require distinguishing between verb forms appearing in different sentences. BLM forces the model to encode *relations* between minimally different sentences, exposing whether internal representation geometry respects morphological system structure. Tokenization choices directly impact whether these relational patterns are encoded. Core assumption: a model can produce plausible sentences without encoding abstract paradigm structure; BLM probes the sentence embedding space for systematicity. Evidence: Section 2 describes BLMs as tools for systematic morphological regularities; Sections 5.2–5.3 show performance differences emerge on the paradigm task; neighboring papers provide indirect support.

## Foundational Learning
- **Morphological Typology (Concatenative vs. Non-concatenative)**  
  *Why needed:* The finding hinges on the structural difference between Turkish's agglutinative system and Hebrew's root-and-pattern system.  
  *Quick check:* What is the key structural difference between the Turkish causative `yaz-dır-t-tı` ("made write") and the Hebrew causative `hi-k'tov` ("caused to write") that affects tokenization?

- **Subword Tokenization as a Learned Lexical Feature**  
  *Why needed:* Tokenization is a feature shaping the model's input space; BPE/WordPiece creates a fixed vocabulary where morpheme boundaries are opaque and data-dependent.  
  *Quick check:* How might the vocabulary of a multilingual model like Electra, trained mostly on Latin-script data, represent a Hebrew verb form differently than a monolingual Hebrew model like AlephBERT?

- **Paradigm as a Relational Structure**  
  *Why needed:* BLM tests knowledge of the verb form *system*; success requires embeddings to reflect shared root and differing templates across forms.  
  *Quick check:* In the BLM task, what must the model learn from the three context sentence pairs to correctly choose the fourth?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Universal Dependencies treebanks -> Query Engine (grew.match.fr) -> BLM Generator -> Tokenizers (BERTurk, AlephBERT, Electra) -> Embedding Extractor -> Feed-forward Neural Network -> Evaluator

**Critical path:**  
1. Extract natural sentences from UD treebanks using provided queries.  
2. For each extracted verb, collect all sentences for its four target voices.  
3. Instantiate BLM puzzles (ensure disjoint train/test roots).  
4. For each puzzle, generate sentence embeddings for all 8 sentences using the three models.  
5. For each model, train a separate FFNN on the train set embeddings (context+answer pairs labeled with correct answer voice).  
6. Evaluate FFNN on test set embeddings. Repeat for VERBONLY dataset.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Monolingual vs. Multilingual Models:* Monolingual vocabularies optimize for their language's morphology; multilingual uses a fixed vocabulary skewed to high-resource languages, causing fragmentation for Hebrew. Tradeoff: vocabulary efficiency vs. morphological fidelity.  
- *Natural vs. Synthetic (VERBONLY) Data:* Natural data includes realistic syntactic/semantic noise; VERBONLY isolates verb forms. Tradeoff: ecological validity vs. diagnostic clarity.  
- *Sentence Embedding Method:* Mean-pooling/[CLS] is syntax-agnostic, testing semantic relations; a sequential model could exploit word order cues.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Hebrew Multilingual (Electra) on Natural Data: F1 ~0.333 (chance) with distributed errors (Fig 5), indicating tokenization destroyed relational signal.  
- Persistent Causative Confusion: In VERBONLY, Hebrew Electra confuses `Caus` and `CausPass` (Fig 7), suggesting character-level similarity dominates over templatic distinctions.  
- Token Count Disparity: Hebrew verbs average ~1.3 tokens in AlephBERT vs. ~5 tokens in Electra (Table 2); high fragmentation correlates with failure.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate Tokenization Statistics: Process datasets through tokenizers; tabulate average tokens per verb form per voice; confirm Hebrew Electra ~5.1 tokens and AlephBERT ~1.3.  
2. Run BLM on Natural Data: For each model, extract embeddings, train FFNN, evaluate per-voice F1; verify Turkish succeeds both, Hebrew only with AlephBERT, Electra fails.  
3. Run BLM on VERBONLY Data: Repeat with verb-only sentences; observe lift for Hebrew Electra but remaining causative deficit; quantify confusion matrix shift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How do tokenization strategies interact with morphological typology beyond concatenative and templatic systems?**  
   *Why unresolved:* Limited to two languages; unknown if findings generalize to polysynthetic or fusion languages.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct similar BLM experiments on languages representing a wider typological spectrum (e.g., Swahili, Georgian, Arabic).

2. **What specific representational deficiency in character-level tokenization prevents the multilingual model from distinguishing Hebrew causative from causative-passive forms?**  
   *Why unresolved:* Paper does not isolate whether root pattern, vowel templates, or affixes are most detrimental.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Design controlled minimal pair sentences and probe model representations to identify most attenuated cues.

3. **Can linguistically-informed tokenization (e.g., morpheme-aware BPE) be made compatible with the multilingual training objective?**  
   *Why unresolved:* Failure of Electra could be due to necessity of character-level tokenization for multilingual coverage vs. a suboptimal segmentation algorithm.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Train a multilingual model with a hybrid/morpheme-aware tokenizer and evaluate on Hebrew BLM; or analyze existing multilingual models for incidental morpheme-like subwords.

4. **Does simplifying to verb-only sentences indicate that natural context actively hinders paradigm learning, or merely reduces noise and sequence length?**  
   *Why unresolved:* Paper does not test controlled reintroduction of contextual elements.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Create datasets with gradually increasing contextual complexity and track performance decay.

5. **For Hebrew, is morpheme-aware tokenization sufficient for robust paradigm learning, or are additional architectural inductive biases required?**  
   *Why unresolved:* Success of AlephBERT could also stem from monolingual training data or pretraining objective.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Swap tokenizer in a multilingual model while keeping architecture constant; or ablate specific morphological features in the input.

## Limitations
- **Instance construction ambiguity:** Exact constraints (e.g., lexical diversity beyond verb lemma distinctness) are unspecified, risking uncontrolled variation.
- **Training procedure gaps:** Missing FFNN details (layers, dropout, margin, embedding normalization) require assumptions that may affect reproducibility.
- **Split integrity risk:** Unclear whether train/test disjointness applies to sentences or only instances; potential data leakage could inflate results.

## Confidence
- **Tokenization as a linguistic filter** (Cluster A): **High**  
  Supported by stark performance divergences (Hebrew Electra F1=0.333 vs. AlephBERT F1=0.835) and tokenization statistics (5.1 vs. 1.3 tokens/verb).
- **BLM as a diagnostic for relational knowledge** (Cluster B): **Medium**  
  BLM reveals failures absent in verb-only data, but the necessity claim is inferred, not proven.
- **Typology-driven resilience in concatenative systems** (Cluster C): **High**  
  Differential tokenization impact aligns with known morphological properties of Turkish concatenativity.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate tokenization fragmentation: Process the exact UD treebank sentences through each tokenizer; compute average tokens per verb form per voice. Confirm Hebrew Electra averages ~5.1 tokens and AlephBERT ~1.3. Deviations indicate preprocessing mismatches (e.g., niqqud handling).
2. Implement BLM with strict instance constraints: Enforce that all 7 verb lemmas per instance are distinct and that train/test roots are disjoint. Measure per-voice F1; if Hebrew Electra F1 exceeds 0.4, audit for accidental answer leakage into context pairs.
3. Ablate evaluation design: Train a standard sentence-level voice classifier on the same natural sentences for each model. If Hebrew Electra achieves high accuracy here but fails BLM, it supports the claim that BLM uniquely probes paradigm-level relational structure.
