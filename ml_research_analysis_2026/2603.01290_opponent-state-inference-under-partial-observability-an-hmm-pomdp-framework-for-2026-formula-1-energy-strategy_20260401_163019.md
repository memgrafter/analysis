# Opponent State Inference Under Partial Observability: An HMM-POMDP Framework for 2026 Formula 1 Energy Strategy

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01290
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01290
- **Reference count:** 8
- **Primary result:** A 40-state HMM infers hidden rival states from telemetry, enabling a DQN policy that achieves 96.8% ERS-level accuracy and 96.3% trap detection recall on synthetic data.

## Executive Summary
The 2026 Formula 1 energy strategy problem requires inferring hidden opponent states (ERS charge, Override Mode, tyre degradation) from public telemetry to avoid deceptive counter-harvest traps. This paper proposes a two-layer framework: a 40-state Hidden Markov Model (HMM) maintains a belief distribution over each rival's hidden state using six telemetry signals, with a key decomposition of "Low ERS" into *Lharvest* (deliberate trap) and *Lderate* (physical depletion) via *δ_throttle*. A Deep Q-Network (DQN) policy consumes this belief state to select energy deployment actions. Synthetic evaluations show high inference accuracy (96.8% ERS-level, 89.4% for *Lharvest* vs *Lderate*) and trap detection (96.3% recall). Empirical validation via Baum-Welch calibration on real 2026 telemetry is pre-registered starting at the Australian Grand Prix.

## Method Summary
The method comprises two layers. Layer 1: A discrete 40-state HMM ingests six preprocessed telemetry observables (∆v_trap, ∆t_sector, ∆b_brake, σ²_speed, z_aero, δ_throttle) and updates a belief over the rival's hidden state via the forward algorithm. Emission parameters are analytically defined (e.g., δ_throttle distributions differ markedly between *Lharvest* and *Lderate*), and transition matrices encode physics-based dynamics (e.g., *Lharvest*→*M* under burn vs. *Lderate*→*M* blocked). Layer 2: A DQN with a 66-dimensional input (40-dim belief + own-state features like ERS, Override, tyre, gap) outputs Q-values for burn/harvest actions. The HMM is calibrated on real race telemetry using Baum-Welch, and the DQN is trained on synthetic experiences generated from the calibrated HMM, employing Double DQN, experience replay, and reward shaping.

## Key Results
- HMM inference: 96.8% 4-class ERS-level accuracy, 89.4% accuracy distinguishing *Lharvest* vs *Lderate*.
- Trap detection: 96.3% recall in identifying counter-harvest conditions.
- The *δ_throttle*-driven state split reduces false trap activations compared to prior versions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: HMM-based opponent state inference with strategic decomposition
- **Claim:** The HMM accurately infers hidden rival states by modeling observable telemetry, with a key advance in splitting “Low ERS” into *Lharvest* (deliberate conservation/trap) and *Lderate* (physical depletion) using *δ_throttle*.
- **Mechanism:** A 40-state discrete HMM uses six publicly observable telemetry signals (∆v_trap, ∆t_sector, ∆b_brake, σ²_speed, z_aero, δ_throttle). Emission parameters are analytically defined from physics and domain knowledge (e.g., δ_throttle distributions: ≈0.08 for *Lharvest* vs ≈0.55 for *Lderate*). Beliefs are updated via the forward algorithm; transition matrices encode physics-based dynamics (e.g., *Lharvest*→*M* under burn vs *Lderate*→*M* blocked). Baum‑Welch calibrates parameters on real race telemetry.
- **Core assumption:** (1) Conditional independence of observables given hidden state; (2) Discrete state decomposition (four ERS modes) is sufficient for strategic decision‑making; (3) Rival policies are stationary during inference.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “40‑state Hidden Markov Model infers … *Lharvest*, *Lderate* using *δ_throttle* … 89.4% accuracy distinguishing *Lharvest* vs *Lderate*.”
  - [section 4.2]: Table 2 gives *δ_throttle* emission separation (|0.55−0.08|=0.47) and [section 5.1] describes transition asymmetry critical for trap detection.
  - [corpus]: Related HMM opponent‑modeling work (e.g., Albrecht and Stone survey) provides general methodological support, but the specific 40‑state decomposition for F1 is novel.
- **Break condition:** If *δ_throttle* signal is corrupted or circuit‑dependent mandatory super‑clipping narrows the separation (as predicted for Melbourne), *Lharvest*/*Lderate* accuracy drops; violation of conditional independence leads to over‑confident beliefs unless corrected with bivariate emissions.

### Mechanism 2: Belief‑state driven DQN policy
- **Claim:** Using the HMM belief state as input to a Deep Q‑Network enables superior energy deployment decisions compared to observable‑only threshold baselines.
- **Mechanism:** The DQN takes a 66‑dimensional input: belief state *b_t* (40), own state encodings (ERS, Override, tyre, gap, etc.), and scalar δ_gap. The network (three hidden layers: 256–256–128) learns Q‑values for actions *a*∈{burn, harvest}. Training uses Double DQN, experience replay, and a shaping reward that rewards correct belief tracking and penalizes trap activations.
- **Core assumption:** (1) The belief state is a sufficient statistic of the observation history for optimal control in the POMDP approximation; (2) The DQN can approximate the optimal Q‑function from finite samples; (3) Rival policy is stationary (no adaptation to being observed).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “Deep Q‑Network policy takes this belief state to select energy deployment actions.”
  - [section 5.2]: Describes DQN architecture, input encoding (Table 3), and training hyperparameters.
  - [corpus]: DQN for POMDPs is established (Mnih et al., 2015), but using an explicit HMM belief state as input is less common; recent work on memory‑improvable domains highlights the challenge.
- **Break condition:** If HMM beliefs are miscalibrated (e.g., high ECE) or non‑stationarities in rival strategies occur (e.g., adaptive trap‑setting), the DQN degrades; Oracle baseline (B3) shows upper bound gap.

### Mechanism 3: Counter‑harvest trap detection via joint belief inference
- **Claim:** Detecting the deceptive counter‑arvest trap requires joint inference over ERS level and harvest/derate sub‑mode, not merely single observable thresholds.
- **Mechanism:** The trap condition is formally: *P*(*Lharvest*) > *θ*_trap ∧ z_aero=1 ∧ in activation zone. This uses the *Lharvest* posterior from the HMM, enabled by the δ_throttle‑driven state split. Without this, a car in *Lderate* with Active Aero mimics the trap’s observable signature but represents a genuine attack opportunity.
- **Core assumption:** (1) The trap signature is precisely captured by the triple (low δ_throttle, Active Aero, activation zone); (2) Rivals execute the trap from *Lharvest*; (3) Other observables (e.g., ∆v_trap alone) are insufficient (Proposition 1).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3]: Proposition 1 proves threshold policy on ∆v_trap fails; defines trap condition.
  - [section 7.2]: Synthetic trap detection recall 96.3% vs v1.5’s 95.7%, with reduced false positives from *Lderate* confusion.
  - [corpus]: Opponent modeling under partial observability supports the need for belief tracking to handle deception.
- **Break condition:** If rivals adopt novel deceptive strategies outside the *Lharvest*+z_aero pattern or if Active Aero detection is noisy, trap detection recall drops, especially at low‑regen circuits.

## Foundational Learning

### Hidden Markov Models (HMM)
- **Why needed here:** To infer hidden rival states from a sequence of noisy telemetry observations; the forward algorithm maintains a belief distribution.
- **Quick check question:** What are the three fundamental problems of HMM and how does the Baum‑Welch algorithm address the parameter estimation problem?

### Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDP)
- **Why needed here:** The energy‑strategy problem is a POMDP because the ego agent must act based on belief states formed from observations of a hidden rival state. The belief state is a sufficient statistic for optimal control.
- **Quick check question:** In a POMDP, why is the belief state (probability distribution over states) a sufficient statistic for the optimal policy, whereas raw observations are not?

### Deep Q‑Networks (DQN)
- **Why needed here:** To learn a mapping from high‑dimensional belief states and own‑car features to energy deployment actions, using reinforcement learning on synthetic experience.
- **Quick check question:** What are the key stabilizing techniques in DQN (e.g., experience replay, target network) and why are they necessary?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Data ingestion (FastF1) → Observable derivation (6 features from 5‑lap baselines) → HMM inference (forward algorithm) → Belief state (40‑dim) → DQN policy (66‑dim input) → Action selection. Calibration loop: after each race, Baum‑Welch updates HMM parameters (E, T) and DQN retrains on synthetic experience.

### Critical path
1. **Telemetry retrieval:** Fetch lap/sector data via FastF1.
2. **Baseline establishment:** Compute per‑driver 5‑lap rolling averages for speed, sector time, etc.
3. **Feature computation:** Derive ∆v_trap, ∆t_sector, ∆b_brake, σ²_speed, z_aero (speed residual in activation zones), δ_throttle (throttle/speed condition).
4. **HMM forward pass:** Multiply previous belief by transition T, apply emission E given discretized observables, normalize.
5. **Policy query:** Assemble 66‑dim input, run DQN forward pass, select action (ε‑greedy during training).
6. **Action logging:** Record chosen action for later evaluation and replay buffer storage (if training).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Discrete HMM vs. continuous particle filter:** Discrete HMM enables closed‑form Baum‑Welch; continuous state would require approximate inference (SMC‑EM) less stable with limited race data (~3,500 sectors/race).
- **Conditional independence:** Simplifies emission model but induces over‑confidence (ECE ≈ 0.006 synthetic lower bound); mitigated later with bivariate emissions or temperature scaling.
- **State expansion (v1→v1.5→v2):** Adding δ_throttle observable and splitting L into Lharvest/Lderate improves trap detection but increases model complexity (40 states); identifiability relies on strong signal separation.
- **Stationary opponent assumption:** Reduces problem to single‑agent POMDP; violated in adversarial equilibria, addressed in follow‑up work.

### Failure signatures
- **Calibration drift:** High ECE (>0.1) on held‑out real telemetry indicates belief over‑confidence; check emission correlation violations (∆v_trap vs ∆t_sector).
- **Circuit‑specific degradation:** Trap detection recall drops significantly at low‑regen circuits (Melbourne, recharge ratio 1.0); monitor per‑circuit metrics.
- **Override Mode ambiguity:** When live timing gap data is missing, Override recovery relies on prior P(gap<1 s)≈0.25; high error may indicate upstream data issues.
- **DQN collapse:** Q‑values saturate or diverge; check replay buffer diversity and Huber loss spikes.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Synthetic HMM accuracy:** Run v2 HMM on 20 synthetic races (5 profiles ×4). Expect 96.8% ERS‑level accuracy, 89.4% Lharvest vs Lderate accuracy, 96.3% trap recall. Compare to v1.5 baseline.
2. **Baum‑Welch convergence:** After Australian GP data, run EM for 10 iterations. Check log‑likelihood increase and parameter stability. Compare calibrated emission means against pre‑set values; expect shift toward data‑driven estimates.
3. **Policy advantage test:** Evaluate DQN vs. baselines (B1–B3) on a held‑out synthetic race set with known ground truth. Measure average ∆position per race; DQN should significantly outperform B1/B2 and approach B3 (oracle). Check advantage comes from trap avoidance (fewer false burns in Lderate+z_aero sectors).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do rival strategies adapt when they reason about being observed by the HMM belief state, moving beyond the stationary opponent assumption?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state: "The stationary opponent assumption... is precisely what Kleisarchaki [2026b] breaks." They define their POMDP solution as "the correct baseline—the floor, not the ceiling, of performance under adversarial conditions."
- **Why unresolved:** The current framework assumes rivals use fixed, non‑adaptive policies. Real opponents will infer the team's HMM and deliberately manipulate its beliefs (e.g., by executing more sophisticated traps or feints), creating a partially observable stochastic game (POSG) the current POMDP cannot solve.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementation and empirical comparison of the game‑theoretic extension ([2026b]) in live 2026 races, measuring performance delta against the stationary baseline under conditions where traps are expected.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the actual empirical accuracy of the HMM's 40‑state inference on real 2026 telemetry, particularly for distinguishing Lharvest from Lderate at circuits with low regeneration ratios like Melbourne?
- **Basis in paper:** "Baum‑Welch calibration on 2026 race telemetry begins with the Australian Grand Prix (8 March 2026); results will be incorporated before final submission." They predict: "trap‑detection recall at Melbourne will be lower than the 96.3% synthetic baseline."
- **Why unresolved:** Synthetic results are an upper bound. The pre‑registered empirical validation has not yet occurred. Critical parameters like the circuit‑specific shift in the δ_throttle prior for Lderate at Melbourne (blended mean 0.268 vs. 0.08/0.55) must be learned from real data.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Post‑Melbourne Baum‑Welch calibration results: (a) ERS‑level and Lharvest/Lderate classification accuracy on held‑out real telemetry, (b) trap detection recall/FPR in actual race conditions, (c) comparison of learned vs. analytic emission parameters.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the structural over‑confidence in beliefs (caused by the conditional independence assumption between ∆v_trap, ∆t_sector, and ∆b_brake) be corrected by incorporating state‑dependent correlations in the emission model, and does this improve downstream DQN policy performance?
- **Basis in paper:** "The conditional independence assumption... produces systematically over‑confident posteriors." They note: "A structural correction—replacing the conditional independence assumption with bivariate Gaussian emissions... is introduced in Kleisarchaki [2026b]."
- **Why unresolved:** The current ECE=0.006 is a synthetic lower bound. Temperature scaling is a post‑hoc fix, but the root‑cause violation of the correlated emission physics remains. The promised structural correction in follow‑up work has not been empirically evaluated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train and compare DQN policies using (i) current conditionally independent HMM beliefs, (ii) temperature‑scaled beliefs, and (iii) correlated emission HMM beliefs on a common synthetic testbed with known correlation structure, then validating the best approach on post‑Melbourne real data.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the exclusion of Boost Mode as a latent dimension affect the optimality of the Burn/Harvest policy, and can its usage be inferred from the current observation set without explicit state modelling?
- **Basis in paper:** "Boost Mode exclusion... is deliberate... unconstrained Boost usage is expected to be identifiable primarily through additional non‑∆v_trap observables... which are outside the present emission architecture." They reserve its formalisation for subsequent work.
- **Why unresolved:** Boost Mode is a high‑impact, unconstrained deployment tool. If rivals use it frequently, the policy's Burn/Harvest decisions may be systematically mis‑scheduled because the HMM misattributes the speed signature to other ERS states. The claim that it is "outside the present emission architecture" is an assumption needing validation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of post‑race 2026 telemetry to (a) quantify Boost Mode usage frequency per circuit, (b) test if Boost events produce observation sequences that are statistically distinguishable from the current 40‑state model's emissions, (c) measure correlated policy errors against a Boost‑aware model.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What is the minimum volume of real race telemetry required for Baum‑Welch to produce a stable, generalisable HMM parameter set, and how does circuit heterogeneity affect the global transition matrix?
- **Basis in paper:** "The model should be treated as empirically validated only from Race 4 onwards." They note: "Circuit‑conditioned transition matrices are the correct fix and are deferred."
- **Why unresolved:** With ~560 effective parameters and ~3,480 observations per rival per race, the data regime is tight. The global T matrices average over track geometry, but recharge availability and activation zone density vary circuit‑to‑circuit (1.0× to 2.2× recharge ratio). A single global T may be misspecified for specific tracks.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** (a) Track per‑race convergence of Baum‑Welch log‑likelihood on a held‑out validation set to empirically determine the Race N where improvements plateau, (b) learn circuit‑specific transition matrices using a hierarchical Bayesian model with pooling, and compare prediction accuracy on out‑of‑circuit races against the global matrix.

## Limitations
- **Telemetry preprocessing:** Exact algorithms for computing ∆b_brake (monotone‑descent detection), σ²_speed (detrending), and z_aero (activation zone membership) are only high‑level; inconsistent handling of missing/noisy channels could degrade feature quality.
- **Calibration specifics:** Baum‑Welch initialization (Dirichlet priors, EM iterations), circuit‑specific adjustments (e.g., Melbourne’s mandatory super‑clipping offset), and convergence criteria are unspecified; poor calibration may yield biased parameters and mis‑calibrated beliefs.
- **Model assumptions:** The HMM assumes conditional independence of the six observables given state, yet real telemetry exhibits correlations (e.g., ∆v_trap ↔ ∆t_sector) that can cause over‑confident beliefs. The discrete 40‑state decomposition might be too coarse for continuous ERS dynamics, and rival policies are assumed stationary—unlikely in a competitive F1 environment.
