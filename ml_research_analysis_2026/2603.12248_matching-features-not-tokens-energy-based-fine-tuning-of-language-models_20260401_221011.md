# Matching Features, Not Tokens: Energy-Based Fine-Tuning of Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12248
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12248
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** EBFT matches/surpasses RLVR and SFT on coding (HumanEval pass@16 0.771) and translation (COMET 0.725) while lowering validation cross-entropy and feature-matching loss, without requiring a task-specific verifier.

## Executive Summary
Current fine-tuning of language models via teacher-forcing cross-entropy aligns the training distribution but not the rollout distribution used at inference, causing a distribution shift. This paper introduces Energy-Based Fine-Tuning (EBFT), a method that uses a frozen feature network to provide dense semantic rewards for generated completions. EBFT combines a feature-matching objective with a cross-entropy regularizer and employs REINFORCE with RLOO baseline and strided block-parallel sampling for efficiency. Evaluated on Q&A coding with Qwen2.5-1.5B and translation with Llama3.2-1B, EBFT achieves strong downstream task performance, improves distributional metrics (lower validation cross-entropy and feature-matching loss), operates without any task-specific reward, and demonstrates better out-of-distribution generalization than both supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and conventional reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR).

## Method Summary
EBFT trains a generator model *pθ* while keeping a copy of the initial model as a frozen feature network *ϕ*. For each context *c*, the method samples *n* completions of fixed length *G* using strided block-parallel decoding, which reuses intermediate activations to generate multiple rollouts in only *G* forward passes. The reward for a completion *ŷ* is computed from the whitened activations of *ϕ(c:ŷ)* and *ϕ(c:y)* (the ground‑truth completion). Specifically, the reward combines an alignment term (inner product of normalized whitened features) and a diversity penalty (pairwise inner products among the sampled completions). The whitening transformation decorrelates and normalizes features per context using the empirical covariance of the *n* samples. The generator is updated via REINFORCE with an RLOO baseline, and the overall objective *L = L_FM + γ L_CE* includes an auxiliary cross-entropy loss on the rollouts to stabilize training. The feature network extracts high-dimensional representations by concatenating L2‑normalized activations from the 25%, 50%, and 75% layers.

## Key Results
- **Coding (Qwen2.5-1.5B):** HumanEval pass@16: EBFT 0.771 vs RLVR 0.752 vs SFT 0.728. Validation cross-entropy: EBFT 0.207 vs SFT 0.289 vs RLVR 0.774. Feature‑matching loss: EBFT 0.258 vs SFT 0.315 vs RLVR 0.442.
- **Translation (Llama3.2-1B):** COMET: EBFT 0.725 vs SFT 0.717 vs RLVR 0.697. Cross‑entropy (BLEU surrogate): EBFT 1.670 vs SFT 1.782 vs RLVR 2.454.
- EBFT requires no task-specific verifier and shows stronger OOD generalization compared to baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
EBFT aligns the rollout distribution with the data distribution by rewarding completions that occupy similar regions in a rich, frozen feature space. Because the feature network was pretrained on diverse data, its activations capture high‑level semantics; matching feature moments thus minimizes a divergence that correlates with task performance. The whitening step ensures each feature dimension contributes equally and reduces collinearity, stabilizing the signal. Combining the feature‑matching reward with on‑policy cross‑entropy regularization prevents the model from collapsing to low‑entropy outputs, maintaining diversity. The strided block‑parallel generation makes the on‑policy sampling efficient enough to train with REINFORCE. Together, these design choices yield an implicit KL‑regularized objective that improves both accuracy and distributional quality, avoiding the trade‑off observed in RLVR where task gains come at the cost of degraded cross‑entropy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Energy‑Based Models:** Interpret the generator’s distribution as *pθ ∝ exp(−Eθ)*. EBFT’s objective can be seen as adjusting *θ* to lower the energy of high‑reward completions. *Why needed:* Provides theoretical grounding for using reinforcement learning to align distributions. *Quick check:* Can you derive the gradient of the KL divergence between *pθ* and an energy‑based target?
- **REINFORCE with Baselines:** Policy gradient method that uses rewards from sampled rollouts. The RLOO baseline reduces variance by comparing two rollouts from the same prompt. *Why needed:* Enables training with only the dense reward signal from the feature network. *Quick check:* Write the REINFORCE update with a baseline and explain how the baseline is estimated in RLOO.
- **Intermediate Layer Feature Extraction:** Using activations from multiple depths (25%, 50%, 75%) and concatenating L2‑normalized vectors. *Why needed:* Captures both low‑level and high‑level representations needed for semantic similarity. *Quick check:* For a given model, how would you extract these features for a sequence?
- **Whitening Transform:** Decorrelates features by transforming with *Σ̂^−½*, where *Σ̂* is the empirical covariance of sampled completions. *Why needed:* Prevents dimensions with larger variance from dominating the inner product and improves numerical stability. *Quick check:* Given a set of feature vectors, describe the steps to compute the whitening matrix and apply it.
- **Strided Block‑Parallel Decoding:** Custom attention mask that reuses keys/values from previous blocks to generate *G* completions in *G* forward passes instead of *n·G*. *Why needed:* Makes on‑policy sampling with multiple rollouts computationally feasible. *Quick check:* Sketch how the attention mask is constructed to allow each token to attend only to its block and previous blocks.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Context encoder → Feature network *ϕ* (frozen) & Generator *pθ* (trainable) → Strided block‑parallel decoder → *n* rollouts per prompt → Whitening module → Reward computation (alignment + diversity) → REINFORCE + CE loss → Parameter update for *pθ*
- **Critical path:** For each minibatch of contexts, generate *n* completions in one parallelism step, compute *ϕ* activations for all *(c:ŷ)* and *(c:y)*, whiten per‑context, compute rewards, evaluate baselines, compute loss (reward – baseline + γ·CE), backpropagate to update *pθ*. *ϕ* remains fixed throughout.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Rollout length *G*: larger *G* better aligns with long‑horizon tasks but increases memory and reduces parallelism efficiency.
  - Feature network size: using the same architecture as generator ensures compatibility but larger *ϕ* (e.g., 7B) offers marginal gains and higher cost.
  - Alignment bias *α* (diversity term weight): lower *α* encourages diversity but can harm convergence; *α*=1 used in main results.
  - *γ* (CE weight): balances stability vs. speed of feature‑matching; too small leads to divergence.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - CE loss spikes or diverges → *γ* too low or *α* < 1 causing instability.
  - Rewards become near zero → whitening over‑normalized or features collinear; check covariance rank.
  - Generated tokens do not correspond to intended nested contexts → strided mask implementation error; validate by inspecting a few examples.
  - Out‑of‑memory during rollout → block‑parallel not implemented correctly; ensure *G* forward passes, not *n·G*.
- **First three experiments:**
  1. Implement strided block‑parallel generation and verify that for a batch of contexts, the *n* rollouts indeed correspond to the same prefix hierarchy (e.g., visualize first few tokens).
  2. Compute whitened features on a small batch and confirm that the covariance of transformed vectors is close to identity; add a small ridge if rank‑deficient.
  3. Train on a tiny subset (e.g., 100 examples) with *γ*=0.03, *α*=1 for a few steps; check that validation CE does not explode and that feature‑matching loss decreases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Feature network scaling and choice:** How does using larger or different architectures for *ϕ* affect EBFT’s performance, especially for very large generators? Current ablations show little benefit from scaling up *ϕ* on 1.5B models.
- **Theoretical guarantee of distribution matching:** When does matching feature moments guarantee matching the full data distribution? The richness condition is assumed but not analyzed.
- **Effect of rollout horizon:** Why do improvements from short‑horizon EBFT (*G*=4–8) persist and even grow at longer test‑time generations? The generalization mechanism across horizons is unclear.
- **Absence of performance–distribution trade‑off:** Why does EBFT avoid the degradation in cross‑entropy that RLVR suffers? Understanding the role of the KL‑regularized objective and feature‑matching gradient could clarify.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** What are robust guidelines for setting *α* (alignment bias) and *γ* (CE weight) across tasks? Sweeps exist for one task but general principles are missing.

## Limitations
- Incomplete reporting of baseline hyperparameters (learning rates, KL penalties, rollout batch sizes) hampers exact replication.
- The strided block‑parallel generation mechanism is underspecified beyond a reference, leading to potential implementation errors.
- Selection of feature extraction layers (25%, 50%, 75%) is ambiguous across model architectures.
- Missing explicit values for *γ* and *α* used in main results; only sweep ranges are provided.
- The whitening step with *n*=4 samples may be numerically unstable in high‑dimensional feature space.
- Validation feature‑matching loss at *G*=32 is computed after training with *G*=8; the exact procedure (e.g., multi‑stage generation) is not described.
