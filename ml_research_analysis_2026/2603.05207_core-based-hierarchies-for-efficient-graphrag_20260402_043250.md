# Core-based Hierarchies for Efficient GraphRAG
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05207
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05207
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Replacing Leiden with k‑core decomposition yields deterministic, density‑aware hierarchies; proposed heuristics improve answer comprehensiveness and diversity by 5–10 percentage points and cut token usage by up to 40% over Leiden‑based GraphRAG.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses instability in Leiden-based GraphRAG on sparse knowledge graphs by proving that modularity optimization admits exponentially many near-optimal partitions, making results non-reproducible. As a solution, the authors replace Leiden with k‑core decomposition, a linear‑time deterministic method that produces a density‑aware hierarchy. They introduce heuristics (RkH, M2hC, MRC) to construct size‑bounded, connectivity‑preserving communities and a token‑budget‑aware sampling strategy (RRTC) to reduce LLM costs. Across three datasets and three LLMs, k‑core methods achieve statistically significant gains of 5–10 percentage points in comprehensiveness and diversity and reduce token consumption by up to 40%.

## Method Summary
The GraphRAG pipeline begins with document chunking (600‑token windows, 100‑token overlap) and LLM‑based extraction of entities, relationships, and claims to build a knowledge graph. Instead of Leiden, the authors compute k‑core decomposition to obtain a deterministic hierarchy where each node’s core number indicates its density‑aware centrality. From this hierarchy, heuristics such as RkH (recursive splitting within k‑core levels) and its post‑processors M2hC/MRC (merging small clusters) generate communities whose sizes are bounded to fit LLM context limits. For query‑time answering, community summaries are produced hierarchically, and RRTC selects a representative set of edges across k‑shells in round‑robin order under a token budget. The overall workflow replicates standard GraphRAG but with a stable, linear‑time community detection and token‑efficient retrieval.

## Key Results
- k‑core‑based GraphRAG improves answer comprehensiveness and diversity by 5–10 percentage points over Leiden baselines (p < 0.005).
- Token consumption is reduced by up to 40% using the RRTC sampling strategy.
- Improvements are consistent across three diverse datasets (podcast transcripts, news articles, earnings calls) and three LLMs (GPT‑3.5‑turbo, GPT‑4o‑mini, GPT‑5‑mini).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Leiden's Instability on Sparse Graphs via Modularity Degeneracy
- **Claim:** Modularity optimization on sparse knowledge graphs admits exponentially many near-optimal partitions, making Leiden-based communities inherently non-reproducible and unstable.
- **Mechanism:** In sparse graphs (constant average degree, many low-degree nodes), the modularity score is nearly flat across a vast space of partitions because low-degree nodes contribute negligibly to the objective. This creates a degeneracy where numerous distinct partitions achieve similar modularity scores, allowing stochastic optimization (like Leiden) to select arbitrarily among them based on initialization or tie-breaking.
- **Core assumption:** Knowledge graphs are sparse (average degree Θ(1)) and contain a linear fraction of nodes with degree ≤ d for some constant d, such that n_{≤d} = Θ(n). This holds for typical knowledge graphs where most entities have few relations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] States: “we prove that on sparse knowledge graphs… modularity optimization admits exponentially many near-optimal partitions, making Leiden-based communities inherently non-reproducible.”
  - [section 3] Provides Theorem 1 and proof showing D(ε) ≥ 2^{n_{≤d}/(d+1)} for sparse graphs, deriving the exponential lower bound.
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., Edge et al. 2024) use Leiden for GraphRAG but do not address its degeneracy; this paper’s proof is the first formal treatment of that instability in this context.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the knowledge graph is dense (average degree ω(1)), where low-degree nodes are a vanishing fraction and modularity’s optimum becomes sharp.

### Mechanism 2: Deterministic Density-Aware Hierarchy via k‑Core Decomposition
- **Claim:** Replacing Leiden with k‑core decomposition yields a deterministic, linear‑time hierarchy that naturally captures progressively denser subgraphs, avoiding Leiden’s instability.
- **Mechanism:** K‑core decomposition recursively peels nodes with degree < k, producing nested subgraphs (k‑cores) where each node’s core number is the largest k for which it belongs. This process is deterministic, runs in O(|E|), and creates a hierarchy where higher k‑cores correspond to regions with higher minimum degree—interpreted as semantically central in knowledge graphs.
- **Core assumption:** The k‑core hierarchy meaningfully reflects topical centrality in knowledge graphs: entities connected by multiple distinct relational paths (higher k) are more topically central than peripheral nodes with few connections.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] States: “we propose replacing Leiden with k‑core decomposition, which yields a deterministic, density‑aware hierarchy in linear time.”
  - [section 4] Explains why k‑core sidesteps modularity’s degeneracy and discusses the semantic interpretation of k‑cores in knowledge graphs (e.g., “multiple relational paths between entities signal genuine topical relatedness”).
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers do not apply k‑core to GraphRAG; this paper is the first to propose it as a replacement for Leiden in this setting.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism may fail if the knowledge graph is triangle‑rich and truss‑based decomposition would be more appropriate (as noted in the remark), or if the graph is so sparse that high‑k cores are empty or trivial.

### Mechanism 3: Heuristics for Size‑Bounded Communities and Token‑Efficient Retrieval
- **Claim:** Heuristics built on the k‑core hierarchy (RkH, M2hC, MRC) construct size‑bounded, connectivity‑preserving communities, while RRTC reduces token usage by sampling representative edges under a budget, without sacrificing answer quality.
- **Mechanism:** 
  - RkH processes each k‑core level, splitting dense components into size‑bounded clusters (via a greedy Split procedure) and handling residuals (including 2‑hop connected singletons) separately to avoid distorting core structure.
  - M2hC and MRC post‑process RkH’s output to merge small clusters (size 2) into larger ones when connectivity exists, reducing fragmentation.
  - RRTC selects edges per community by descending k‑shell, using round‑robin across communities until a token budget is met, prioritizing prominent edges (by endpoint degree).
- **Core assumption:** Size‑bounded clusters align with LLM context limits and preserve retrieval quality; merging small clusters improves coherence; edge sampling can discard redundant information without losing essential signals for global sensemaking.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Mentions: “We introduce a set of lightweight heuristics that leverage the k‑core hierarchy to construct size‑bounded, connectivity‑preserving communities… along with a token‑budget–aware sampling strategy that reduces LLM costs.”
  - [section 4] Details Algorithms 1–4 (RkH, M2hC/MRC, Split, Split‑2hop) and RRTC, explaining their design choices (e.g., handling residuals, 2‑hop connectivity, anchor sets).
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers address token efficiency in GraphRAG (e.g., Deep GraphRAG, LEGO‑GraphRAG) but not via k‑core‑guided sampling; this paper’s RRTC is a novel budget‑aware strategy.
- **Break condition:** The heuristics may fail if community size bounds are too strict (fragmenting meaningful topics) or too loose (exceeding LLM context). RRTC may degrade quality if the budget is too aggressive or if the edge ranking (by endpoint degree) fails to capture informative edges in sparse regions.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - Indexing pipeline: Document chunking → LLM‑based entity/relation extraction → Knowledge graph construction → k‑core decomposition → Community construction (RkH/M2hC/MRC) → Hierarchical summarization (leaf → parent)
  - Query pipeline: User query → Retrieve relevant leaf‑level community summaries (optionally using RRTC for token budgeting) → Map‑Reduce intermediate answers → Final answer generation
  - Evaluation module: Head‑to‑head comparison using multiple LLM judges
- **Critical path:** The most sensitive path is indexing: graph construction → k‑core → community heuristics, because errors here propagate to all queries. Query‑time retrieval is stateless given the index.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Determinism vs. expressiveness: k‑core gives reproducibility but may coarsely group nodes compared to modularity’s finer partitions in dense graphs.
  - Cluster size control: Tight bounds ensure LLM context compliance but may split semantically cohesive groups if M is too small.
  - Token reduction (RRTC): Saves cost but risks losing niche perspectives if the budget is too low.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Excessive tiny clusters (size 1–2) after RkH indicate poor residual handling.
  - Token usage far above Leiden baselines suggests RRTC wasn’t applied or edge ranking is ineffective.
  - Degraded win rates (especially diversity) on sparse datasets signal that k‑core’s density focus may have over‑merged peripheral but thematically important nodes.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce modularity degeneracy: On a small sparse graph (average degree ≤ 3), run Leiden with 10 random seeds; measure partition variability (e.g., adjusted Rand index) and confirm near‑optimal modularity scores cluster tightly.
  2. Validate k‑core hierarchy determinism: Compute k‑core decomposition on the same graph multiple times; verify identical core numbers and hierarchy structure.
  3. End‑to‑end quality check: On a tiny dataset (e.g., 10 documents), run full GraphRAG with Leiden C2 vs. M2hC LF; confirm M2hC produces more size‑bounded clusters and that head‑to‑head win rate (using a judge LLM) favors M2hC on a few sample queries.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the k‑core‑based GraphRAG framework be efficiently adapted to handle dynamic knowledge graphs where nodes and edges are frequently added or removed?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states "Future work includes extending the framework to dynamic knowledge graphs..."
- **Why unresolved:** The current approach recomputes k‑core decomposition from scratch on a static graph; dynamic updates require incremental algorithms to maintain the hierarchy efficiently.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and evaluate incremental k‑core update methods (e.g., batch or streaming) on datasets with temporal edges, measuring update latency and resulting community quality.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the k‑core‑based approach generalize to retrieval‑augmented generation tasks that do not require global sensemaking, such as factoid QA or single‑document summarization?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion mentions "exploring its applicability beyond global sensemaking tasks."
- **Why unresolved:** Global sensemaking leverages hierarchical summarization across many documents; other tasks may need finer‑grained retrieval or different aggregation strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the same GraphRAG pipeline to standard QA benchmarks (e.g., Natural Questions) and compare accuracy, token usage, and latency against vector‑based RAG and Leiden GraphRAG.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are the heuristics RkH, M2hC, and MRC optimal for converting the k‑core hierarchy into size‑bounded communities, or can alternative partitioning strategies improve the trade‑off between coverage and granularity?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper proposes these heuristics but does not compare them with other k‑core‑based community extraction methods or formal objectives.
- **Why unresolved:** The heuristics use greedy splitting and 2‑hop connectivity; they may not optimize for properties like conductance or modularity within k‑core layers, leaving room for improvement.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design heuristics that explicitly minimize community conductance or maximize internal density within core levels, then evaluate against the proposed methods on comprehensiveness, diversity, and token efficiency.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive is the performance advantage of k‑core over Leiden to the knowledge graph construction pipeline (e.g., entity extraction, relation weighting)?
- **Basis in paper:** The evaluation uses a single pipeline from Edge et al. [6]; the paper attributes Leiden's instability to graph sparsity but does not vary construction parameters.
- **Why unresolved:** Graph sparsity and structure depend on extraction choices; different pipelines might yield denser graphs where Leiden performs better, or exacerbate sparsity where k‑core excels.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Construct multiple knowledge graphs from the same corpus using varied chunk sizes, extraction LLMs, or edge weighting schemes, then measure the relative performance of k‑core vs. Leiden across a spectrum of graph densities.

## Limitations
- Graph construction reproducibility relies on external prompts and extraction code from a separate repository; any changes there could affect the sparse‑graph conditions critical for the theoretical guarantees.
- The community size bound `M` is derived from token limits and average node tokens, but its precise tuning per dataset is not thoroughly explored, risking over‑ or under‑merging.
- Evaluation is limited to three specific datasets and three LLMs; it remains unclear whether the reported gains generalize to other domains, denser graphs, or newer LLM judges.

## Confidence
- Modularity degeneracy on sparse graphs: High.
- k‑core hierarchy determinism & density‑awareness: High.
- Heuristic efficacy (RkH/M2hC/MRC + RRTC) in improving quality & reducing tokens: Medium.
