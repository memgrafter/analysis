# Explainable Token-level Noise Filtering for LLM Fine-tuning Datasets
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14536
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14536
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: XTF improves downstream accuracy by up to 13.7% (medicine) and 13.3% (math) over standard fine-tuning across seven LLMs and three tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses token-level noise in sentence-level fine-tuning datasets for large language models (LLMs). It introduces XTF, an explainable framework that decomposes token value into three measurable attributes—reasoning importance (RI), knowledge novelty (KN), and task relevance (TR)—and masks gradients of tokens identified as noisy. By filtering out uninformative or misleading tokens during supervised fine-tuning, XTF aligns the training gradient more closely with the ideal risk gradient. Evaluated on math, code, and medicine tasks across seven LLMs, XTF yields substantial accuracy gains, demonstrating that token-level dataset optimization is critical for effective LLM fine-tuning.

## Method Summary
XTF operates in three phases: scoring, filtering, and masked training. For each output token in a training sample, it computes three scores using the base (unfine-tuned) model: RI via average attention over the token in the input+output context; KN as 1 minus the model’s predicted probability of the token (PCP), reflecting whether the model already knows it; and TR via semantic distance from a dataset-level domain vector (average embedding of “expert words”). Thresholds are derived from score distributions (IQR for RI, PCP>0.95 for KN, Multi-Otsu for TR). Tokens failing any threshold are deemed noisy; their labels are set to -100 during fine-tuning, masking their gradients. A conservative union of the three filtered sets ensures broad noise coverage. The approach is model-agnostic and can be combined with full fine-tuning or PEFT methods like LoRA.

## Key Results
- XTF improves accuracy by up to 13.7% on PubMedQA (medicine) and 13.3% on GSM8K (math) compared to standard fine-tuning.
- Gains are consistent across seven LLMs (DeepSeek, Llama, Mistral) and three tasks, significantly outperforming sample-level data enhancement and other token-level baselines.
- Ablation studies confirm that all three attributes contribute complementarily; the optimal attribute combination varies by task and model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Masking gradients of tokens lacking reasoning importance, knowledge novelty, or task relevance prevents the model from learning from uninformative tokens. During fine-tuning, XTF identifies such tokens via scoring functions (attention, prediction confidence, semantic distance) and sets their labels to -100, excluding them from the loss. This directs optimization toward tokens that genuinely contribute to the target task, improving alignment between the training gradient and the ideal risk gradient. The core assumption is that token-level noise exists in sentence-level datasets and can be reliably captured by the three attributes. Evidence: The paper defines noise as the union of tokens with low RI, high KN (low novelty), or low TR (Eq. 1). Over-filtering or under-filtering risks occur if thresholds are mis-calibrated or if the base model is misaligned.

**Mechanism 2:** Decomposing token value into three explicit, measurable attributes makes the filtering process explainable and auditable. Each attribute uses a distinct, interpretable scoring method: RI via attention weights, KN via prediction confidence, TR via embedding distance. Thresholds are derived from data distributions (IQR, fixed percentile, Multi-Otsu), providing transparent criteria. This contrasts with black-box token selection. The core assumption is that these three dimensions sufficiently capture token contributions to fine-tuning. Limitations: Attributes may not cover all aspects (e.g., syntactic role), and important tokens could be filtered if they lack one attribute.

**Mechanism 3:** A conservative union of the three attribute-based filters captures a broad spectrum of noisy tokens with minimal overlap, leading to robust noise removal. The paper reports ≤58.3% overlap among filtered sets (Fig. 4), indicating complementarity: tokens ambiguous under one attribute are often clearly identified by another. Strict thresholds (extreme tails) minimize false positives. The core assumption is that a token noisy in any respect is detrimental regardless of other qualities. Evidence: Ablation (Table 2) shows using all three attributes yields the best performance, and optimal combinations vary by task/model. Break condition: If attributes are highly correlated, the union adds little value; poor calibration of one attribute could introduce false positives.

## Foundational Learning
- **Token-level training in LLMs:** Why needed: LLMs optimize per-token losses via teacher forcing; not all label tokens equally contribute to task performance. Quick check: Why does training on a sentence-level target result in some tokens being “noise” for fine-tuning?
- **Attention mechanism:** Why needed: Reasoning Importance (RI) uses average attention weights as a proxy for a token’s role in the model’s generative reasoning. Quick check: How does average attention over an output token indicate its reasoning importance?
- **Prediction confidence (softmax probability):** Why needed: Knowledge Novelty (KN) uses 1 - PCP; high confidence means the model already knows the token, offering little new learning signal. Quick check: Why would a high-confidence token be considered low in knowledge novelty?
- **Embedding vectors and semantic similarity:** Why needed: Task Relevance (TR) measures distance from a dataset domain vector; large distance indicates low alignment with the task domain. Quick check: How does the centroid of all token embeddings represent the task domain, and why does a large distance imply low relevance?
- **Gradient masking via label ignoring:** Why needed: Filtered tokens are excluded by setting their labels to -100, which PyTorch’s cross-entropy loss ignores, masking their gradients. Quick check: What effect does setting a label to -100 have on loss calculation and backpropagation?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Data -> Scoring Block (computes RI, KN, TR per token) -> Filtering Block (applies thresholds, unions noisy sets) -> Training Block (standard fine-tuning with -100 labels for masked tokens).
- **Critical path:** Data loading → Scoring (1–2 forward passes through base model) → Thresholding → Mask generation → Training loop (loss on unmasked tokens only) → Validation → Model selection.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Computational cost: Scoring adds inference-level passes, increasing preprocessing time/memory.
  - Conservative filtering: Union reduces false negatives but may increase false positives; strict thresholds mitigate over-filtering but risk under-filtering.
  - Model dependence: Scores rely on base model’s representations; weak or misaligned scorers yield unreliable filters.
  - Threshold adaptivity: Distribution-based thresholds (IQR, Otsu) reduce manual tuning but assume expected score shapes.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Extreme mask ratio (>50% output tokens masked) suggests overly aggressive thresholds.
  - No performance gain over normal fine-tuning may indicate clean data, poor threshold calibration, or weak base model.
  - Score distribution anomalies (e.g., no clear clusters for TR) imply threshold method failure.
  - Training instability (loss spikes) could result from masking important tokens.
  - Base model mismatch (different tokenization/architecture) breaks scoring validity.
- **First experiments:**
  1. Main benchmarking: Evaluate XTF vs. baselines on GSM8K, CodeExercise-Python-27k, PubMedQA across seven LLMs; measure accuracy gains.
  2. Ablation study: Remove each attribute individually and in combinations to assess complementary contributions.
  3. Threshold sensitivity: Perturb RI (IQR multiplier), KN (PCP cutoff), TR (Multi-Otsu clusters) and measure impact on mask ratio and accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What is the optimal set of attributes for token-level noise filtering across diverse LLM fine-tuning tasks, and can these be automatically learned rather than manually specified?
2. How does XTF's gradient masking approach interact with modern parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like (Q)LoRA, and does token-level filtering provide complementary benefits or interfere with low-rank adaptation mechanisms?
3. Can the token filtering thresholds derived from static score distributions be replaced with dynamic, training-aware thresholding that adapts as the model learns, rather than preprocessing once before training?
4. Does XTF's improvement primarily come from removing actual noise or from regularizing the training process by effectively reducing sequence length, and would a simple random token masking approach achieve similar gains?
5. Do the three scoring mechanisms measure independent dimensions of token value, or are they highly correlated, and could a single more-computationally-efficient metric suffice?

## Limitations
- Ambiguous definitions: “expert words” for domain vector; Multi-Otsu cluster count; attention aggregation method.
- Missing details: LoRA target modules; validation set size and early stopping criteria.
- Risks: Over/under-filtering due to threshold mis-calibration; base model dependence; three attributes may not capture all token value aspects.
- Generalizability: Evaluated on only three tasks (math, code, medicine); unknown performance on other domains.
- Computational overhead: Additional forward passes for scoring increase preprocessing cost, especially for large models/datasets.

## Confidence
- Gradient masking of noisy tokens improves fine-tuning: High (consistent gains across 7 LLMs, 3 tasks, supported by ablations).
- Three-attribute decomposition yields explainable filtering: High (transparent scoring functions and distribution-based thresholds).
- Conservative union captures complementary noise: Medium (overlap ≤58.3% and union generally best, but optimal combination varies; union may introduce false positives).
- Threshold robustness: Medium (sensitivity analysis shows chosen thresholds near-optimal but not universally stable).
- Attribute independence: Low (no correlation analysis provided; cannot rule out redundancy).

## Next Checks
1. **Threshold robustness sweep:** Systematically vary RI (IQR multiplier), KN (PCP cutoff 0.90–0.99), and TR (Multi-Otsu clusters 2–5) on a subset of tasks. Plot mask ratios vs. accuracy to identify stable regions and confirm default choices are near-optimal.
2. **Base model scoring generalizability:** Compute attribute scores using base models of different sizes (1B vs. 7B) and pretraining distributions (code-only vs. general). Evaluate XTF performance when fine-tuning a fixed target model to test scorer independence.
3. **Attribute complementarity quantification:** For each dataset, compute pairwise Jaccard similarity of filtered tokens per attribute. Train models with single attributes and all pairwise combinations, compare to union. Validate that union outperforms any subset and quantify overlap.
