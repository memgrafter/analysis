# Concept Heterogeneity-aware Representation Steering

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.02237  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02237  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: CHaRS consistently outperforms global steering baselines, achieving up to 7% higher Attack Success Rate on jailbreaking tasks while preserving utility.

## Executive Summary
Standard representation steering in large language models uses a single global direction (difference-in-means) to alter model behavior, assuming homogeneous concept representations. However, real representations are highly non‑homogeneous, exhibiting clustered, context‑dependent structure, which renders global steering brittle. This paper introduces Concept Heterogeneity‑aware Representation Steering (CHaRS), which models source and target activations as Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) and formulates steering as a discrete optimal transport problem between semantic clusters. The resulting input‑dependent steering map (via barycentric projection) adapts to local structure. Experiments across jailbreaking, toxicity mitigation, and image style control show CHaRS consistently outperforms global steering baselines, achieving up to 7% improvement in Attack Success Rate on jailbreaking tasks while preserving utility.

## Method Summary
CHaRS operates in two phases. In the **offline** phase, activation datasets are collected for source and target concepts at a chosen layer. K‑means clustering yields centroids and mixture weights; isotropic covariances are assumed. A cost matrix is built from pairwise Euclidean distances between centroids, and entropy‑regularized optimal transport (Sinkhorn) computes a soft coupling matrix \(P^*\). Optionally, PCA on translation vectors defines a low‑dimensional subspace (CHaRS‑PCT). In the **online** phase, for an input activation \(x\), kernel similarities to source centroids produce gating weights. The steering vector \(\hat{v}(x)\) aggregates cluster‑level translations \(b_j - a_i\) weighted by \(P^*_{ij}\) and the gating. This vector is applied via activation addition (ActAdd) or directional ablation (DirAbl) with strength \(\alpha\). The approach is inference‑time, deterministic, and differentiable.

## Key Results
- **Jailbreaking**: Up to +7% Attack Success Rate over global steering baselines across models (3B–32B), without utility loss on tinyBenchmark.
- **Toxicity mitigation**: Greater reduction in toxic generations compared to prior causal methods, with no increase in perplexity and maintained MMLU performance.
- **Image style control**: Improved style induction (CLIP zero‑shot) and content preservation (CLIPScore) on diffusion models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Capturing Concept Heterogeneity via Mixture Modeling
Global steering fails because concept representations are heterogeneous and clustered; CHaRS addresses this by modeling activations as Gaussian mixtures. Using k‑means, it estimates source and target distributions as GMMs; each centroid corresponds to a semantic subregion. Steering for an input becomes a weighted combination of cluster‑level translations, with weights based on proximity to centroids. This relies on the assumption that the activation space naturally partitions into semantically meaningful clusters recoverable via unsupervised clustering. Evidence: [Abstract] highlights the non‑homogeneous structure; [Section 2.2] shows difference‑in‑means corresponds to unimodal OT; [Figure 1] visualizes clusters; related work also notes contextual effects. Limitations: poor clustering (bad \(K\), noise, non‑convexity) can misrepresent the concept; if the concept is truly unimodal, CHaRS adds unnecessary overhead.

### Mechanism 2: Smooth, Input‑Dependent Steering via Barycentric Projection
The barycentric projection of the GMM‑OT coupling yields a deterministic, continuous steering map that varies smoothly across the activation manifold. For input \(x\), the steering vector is a kernel‑weighted average of cluster translation vectors \(v_{ij}=b_j-a_i\), with weights combining \(x\)'s similarity to source centroids and the coupling \(P^*\). This approximates the true OT map because the conditional target distribution under the Mixture coupling has low variance; isotropic covariances simplify estimation while preserving multimodality. Evidence: [Section 3.1] derives the barycentric map; [Section 3.2] details the isotropic approximation; empirical results (Tables 1–3, Figures 2–3) confirm gains. Limitations: kernel bandwidth \(\sigma\) must be tuned—too small induces noise, too large causes over‑smoothing; highly anisotropic covariances may lead to inaccurate translations under the isotropic assumption.

### Mechanism 3: Robust Cluster Matching via Entropic Optimal Transport
CHaRS uses entropy‑regularized optimal transport (Sinkhorn) to compute a soft coupling between source and target clusters. This yields a stable, differentiable alignment that scales to high dimensions and is robust to noise. The regularization parameter \(\lambda\) balances fidelity and numerical stability; the kernel matrix encodes pairwise cluster affinities. Evidence: [Section 3.2] outlines Sinkhorn; [Appendix D.3] gives algorithmic details. Limitations: mis‑specifying \(\lambda\)—too large blurs matching, too small causes instability—and high cluster imbalance may cause the coupling to be dominated by large clusters, ignoring smaller yet important ones.

## Foundational Learning
### Optimal Transport
- **Why needed here**: CHaRS is built on optimal transport between distributions; understanding couplings, Wasserstein distance, and barycentric projection is essential.
- **Quick check question**: What does the barycentric projection of a coupling compute? (Answer: The conditional expectation of the target given the source under the coupling.)

### Gaussian Mixture Models and Clustering
- **Why needed here**: CHaRS models concept representations as GMMs; clustering (k‑means) learns mixture components from activation data.
- **Quick check question**: How does CHaRS approximate component covariances in its practical implementation? (Answer: By assuming isotropic covariances, i.e., spherical Gaussians with variance estimated per cluster.)

### Activation Steering
- **Why needed here**: The paper addresses limitations of existing steering methods (ActAdd, DirAbl) which rely on difference‑in‑means. Understanding these baselines is necessary to appreciate CHaRS’s extension.
- **Quick check question**: What is the steering vector used in standard difference‑in‑means steering? (Answer: The vector obtained by subtracting the mean activation of the source set from the mean activation of the target set.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Offline: Activation extraction → Clustering → Cost matrix → Sinkhorn coupling → (Optional) PCA. Online: Input activation \(x\) → Compute kernel similarities to source centroids → Compute gating weights → Assemble steering vector via barycentric projection → Apply steering (ActAdd or DirAbl).

**Critical path:** Clustering → Cost matrix → Sinkhorn → kernel weighting → steering vector generation. The quality of clustering and coupling \(P^*\) is pivotal.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Number of clusters \(K\): Higher \(K\) captures finer heterogeneity but increases memory (\(O(K^2)\) cost matrix) and risk of overfitting.
- Entropy regularization \(\lambda\): Smaller \(\lambda\) yields sparser, more accurate matching; larger \(\lambda\) yields smoother but potentially over‑blurred coupling.
- Kernel bandwidth \(\sigma\): Fixed vs adaptive (median heuristic). Adaptive helps generalize across input density variations.
- Isotropic vs full covariance: Full covariance captures directional spread but is unstable with small clusters; isotropic is robust but may miss correlations.
- CHaRS‑PCT: Choosing number of principal components \(L\) trades off dimensionality reduction/denoising against information retention; too small \(L\) may drop relevant directions.

**Failure signatures:**
- No gain over baseline: Verify \(K>1\) and that clustering yields meaningful separation (e.g., silhouette score >0); check \(\lambda\) not too high.
- Utility degradation: Monitor perplexity/accuracy on downstream tasks; adjust \(\alpha\) downward.
- Instability across seeds: Average over multiple k‑means seeds; consider consensus clustering.
- Out‑of‑memory: Reduce \(K\) or increase \(\lambda\); optionally pre‑PCA activations.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Jailbreaking: Apply CHaRS with ActAdd/DirAbl to induce harmful outputs; measure Attack Success Rate and preserve utility on tinyBenchmark.
2. Toxicity mitigation: Sequential steering across layers on Gemma2‑2B / Llama3‑8B / Qwen2.5‑7B; evaluate toxicity reduction via classifier and zero‑shot judge, and utility via perplexity and MMLU.
3. Image style control: Use CHaRS sequentially on FLUX.1 diffusion model for style transfer; evaluate style induction (CLIP zero‑shot) and content preservation (CLIPScore).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Anisotropic covariances**: Would allowing anisotropic covariance structures in the GMM improve steering effectiveness? The authors note their isotropic assumption may miss directional nuances.
- **Alternative clustering**: Can clustering algorithms beyond k‑means (e.g., HDBSCAN, spectral clustering) yield better cluster assignments and improve CHaRS?
- **Feature weighting**: Does incorporating feature‑weighting mechanisms (e.g., per‑dimension scaling) enhance the transport map’s ability to capture directional nuances?

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters (cluster count \(K\), entropy \(\lambda\), kernel \(\sigma\), intervention layer \(\ell\)) are not fully reported, hindering exact replication.
- Clustering quality in high‑dimensional activation spaces is unvalidated; poor recovery of true modes could degrade performance.
- The method relies on isotropic covariances and k‑means, which may not capture the true semantic structure of concepts.
