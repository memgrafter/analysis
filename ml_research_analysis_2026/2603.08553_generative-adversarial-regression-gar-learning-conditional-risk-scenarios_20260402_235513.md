# Generative Adversarial Regression (GAR): Learning Conditional Risk Scenarios

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.08553
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08553
- Reference count: 16
- Primary result: GAR with Encoder-LSTM achieved a joint VaR-ES score of -3.929 on S&P 500 data, outperforming baselines and maintaining robustness under worst-case policies.

## Executive Summary
Generative Adversarial Regression (GAR) is a framework for conditional scenario generation that aligns generated outcomes with downstream risk measures, specifically Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES). It leverages the elicitability property to cast conditional risk estimation as a regression problem, and introduces an adversarial policy within a minimax game to ensure robustness across a broad class of decision policies. Tested on a dataset of 9 S&P 500 stocks with 10-day horizons, GAR (Encoder-LSTM variant) achieved a joint VaR-ES score of -3.929, significantly better than competing baselines. Crucially, when evaluated under worst-case policies, GAR's performance remained stable, while fixed-policy models degraded sharply, demonstrating the value of the adversarial approach. The VaR violation rate was 6.6%, close to the 5% target, compared to 14.8% for DCC-GARCH.

## Method Summary
GAR formulates conditional risk alignment as a minimax optimization problem. A generator network produces future return scenarios conditioned on historical context (past returns). The generator's parameters are trained to minimize a strictly consistent scoring function (e.g., joint VaR-ES score) between the conditional risk estimated from the generated scenarios and the risk estimated from real outcomes. To guarantee robustness to the choice of downstream policy, an adversarial policy network (e.g., GRU) is introduced to maximize this scoring discrepancy over a parameterized class of policies. This forces the generator to align with the worst-performing policy within the class. The conditional risk is estimated via Monte Carlo sampling from the generator for each context. Training alternates between updating the adversarial policy (maximizing the score) and updating the generator (minimizing the score). The method is evaluated on financial data using VaR and ES as risk measures, with empirical results showing superior performance and robustness.

## Key Results
- GAR (Encoder-LSTM) achieved a joint VaR-ES score of -3.929 on test data, outperforming unconditional GAN (-3.765), DCC-GARCH (-3.774), and direct linear (-3.760).
- Under worst-case policies, adversarially trained GAR remained stable (score -3.888), while fixed-policy counterparts degraded sharply (e.g., Encoder-Linear dropped to -1.349).
- VaR violation rate was 6.6%, near the 5% target, while DCC-GARCH had 14.8%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Conditional Risk as a Regression Problem
- Claim: Under an elicitable risk functional, the policy-induced conditional risk 𝜌(𝐿 Π|𝐶=𝑐) can be learned by minimizing the expected strictly consistent score over a predictor 𝑎(𝐶).
- Mechanism: The paper leverages the elicitability property (Assumption 1). For any policy-induced outcome 𝐿 Π, the conditional risk function 𝑐 ↦ 𝜌(𝐿 Π|𝐶=𝑐) is the unique minimizer of the pointwise conditional expectation of a strictly consistent score 𝑆(𝑎(𝐶), 𝐿 Π) (Equation 3). This transforms the abstract goal of "risk-aligned generation" into a concrete regression objective.
- Core assumption: The primary risk functional (e.g., VaR/ES pair) is elicitable, and a strictly consistent scoring function 𝑆 exists.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "GAR builds on a regression characterization of conditional risk for elicitable functionals..."
  - [section 2.2]: The derivation from Equation (3) to (4) shows the regression characterization is a direct consequence of the law of iterated expectations and strict consistency.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct support; cited work (Gneiting, 2011) establishes elicitability theory, but this paper's specific application to conditional generative modeling is novel.
- Break condition: If the risk functional of interest is not elicitable (e.g., spectral risk measures without a corresponding score), this regression foundation does not apply. The paper's empirical focus on jointly elicitable (VaR, ES) avoids this.

### Mechanism 2: Adversarial Policy for Robustness to Policy Shift
- Claim: A minimax formulation with an adversarial policy ensures the learned generator preserves conditional risk uniformly across an admissible policy class, not just a fixed benchmark set.
- Mechanism: The generator is trained to minimize the score discrepancy against the worst-performing policy within a parameterized class Π 𝜙 (Equation 8). The adversary (policy) maximizes this discrepancy, forcing the generator to eliminate risk misalignments that would be exposed by any policy in the class, not just a pre-selected few. This is a robust optimization approach to policy ambiguity.
- Core assumption: The adversarial policy class Φ is sufficiently rich to approximate "worst-case" policies relevant in deployment. The optimization dynamics converge to a stable min-max equilibrium.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "...adopts a minimax formulation in which an adversarial policy identifies worst-case discrepancies... preserves alignment... across a broad class of policies."
  - [section 2.3]: Explicitly defines the min-max objective (8) as replacing a finite benchmark set with a "rich admissible policy class."
  - [corpus]: Weak direct support; robust optimization against a policy adversary is a novel instantiation in this context.
- Break condition: If the adversarial policy class Φ is too restrictive (e.g., limited model capacity, overly constrained by normalization), it may fail to find truly worst-case policies, leading to a false sense of robustness. Table 2 shows degradation for fixed-policy models under worst-case evaluation, implying the adversarial mechanism was necessary for that resilience.

### Mechanism 3: Monte Carlo Estimation of Generator-Implied Conditional Risk
- Claim: The conditional risk 𝜌(Π(𝐺(𝑍,𝐶))|𝐶=𝑐) induced by a generative model can be estimated via Monte Carlo sampling from the generator for a given context 𝑐.
- Mechanism: For a fixed context 𝑐, multiple latent samples 𝑍 → 𝑌 𝜃 = 𝐺(𝑍, 𝑐) are generated. The policy-induced outcomes Π(𝑌 𝜃) form an empirical distribution. The plug-in estimator 𝜌(⋅) applied to this sample (e.g., empirical quantile for VaR, tail average for ES) yields an estimate of the generator's conditional risk. This estimate is the input to the scoring function.
- Core assumption: The Monte Carlo sample size 𝑁 𝑀𝐶 is large enough for the plug-in risk estimate to be accurate for the optimization gradients. The policy Π 𝜙 and aggregator 𝐴 are deterministic.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2]: "We then estimate the generator-implied conditional risk... by a plug-in estimator computed from the empirical distribution... For (VaR𝛼,ES𝛼) this corresponds to the empirical quantile and tail average."
  - [Algorithm 1, lines 6-11 & 19-24]: The training procedure explicitly loops over Monte Carlo samples to compute ˆ𝑎 𝑖 for each minibatch instance.
  - [corpus]: Weak; this is a standard technique for estimating distributional properties from generative models, not a novel claim of the paper.
- Break condition: If the generator is poorly calibrated or has high variance for a given context, the empirical estimate of its conditional risk may be noisy, leading to high-variance gradient estimates and unstable training. This is a general challenge for any differentiable programming approach involving sampling.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Elicitability**
  - Why needed here: It provides the theoretical foundation to treat conditional risk estimation as a regression problem using strictly consistent scoring rules. Without elicitability, there is no guarantee that minimizing the expected score recovers the true conditional risk functional.
  - Quick check question: If you wanted to extend GAR to a coherent but non-elicitable risk measure (e.g., a spectral risk measure with a non-linear distortion function not satisfying elicitability), what fundamental change to the objective would be required? (Answer: You could not use the regression characterization; you'd need a different alignment objective, potentially based on a distance between distributions or a different evaluation metric).

- **Concept: Conditional Scenario Generation**
  - Why needed here: The entire objective is to generate scenarios that are conditioned on observable context 𝐶. This distinguishes GAR from unconditional simulation. The conditioning information is the bridge between the historical data distribution and the specific, context-dependent risk assessment required for decision-making.
  - Quick check question: In the paper's financial application, what two components constitute the conditioning information 𝐶, and what does the generated scenario 𝑌 represent? (Answer: 𝐶 is the preceding 5 days of returns for 9 assets; 𝑌 is the subsequent 10-day return trajectory.)

- **Concept: Adversarial Training (min-max optimization)**
  - Why needed here: To achieve robustness against policy shift. Training against a fixed set of policies (as in related work) leads to models that are brittle when the deployed policy changes. The adversarial policy acts as a "stress tester" within a class, forcing the generator to be correct under a wide range of decision rules.
  - Quick check question: In GAR's min-max game, which component is being *maximized* (the adversary) and which is being *minimized* (the generator)? What is the quantity being optimized? (Answer: The policy parameters 𝜙 are maximized to increase the score discrepancy; generator parameters 𝜃 are minimized to decrease it. The quantity is the expected strictly consistent score between real and generator-implied conditional risk.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Context Encoder -> Latent Sampler -> Scenario Decoder -> (generated scenario) -> Policy Network -> Policy-Induced Outcome -> Risk Estimator -> Scoring Function. Adversary updates policy network; generator updates encoder/decoder.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Input minibatch of (𝐶, 𝑌) pairs.  
  2. Adversary step: For each (𝑐ᵢ, 𝑦ᵢ), run policy Π𝜙 on real 𝑦ᵢ to get real outcome 𝑙ᵢ. For each 𝑐ᵢ, generate 𝑁 𝑀𝐶 samples 𝑦̂ᵢ,𝑠 = 𝐺(𝑧ₛ, 𝑐ᵢ), run Π𝜙 on each to get 𝑙̂ᵢ,𝑠, compute empirical risk 𝑎̂ᵢ, then score 𝑆(𝑎̂ᵢ, 𝑙ᵢ). Average score → gradient → update 𝜙.  
  3. Generator step: Repeat Monte Carlo process to get 𝑎̂ᵢ and 𝑙ᵢ, compute gradient wrt 𝜃 to minimize average score, update 𝜃.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Generator expressivity vs. training stability: LSTM vs linear.  
  - Adversarial policy capacity vs. robustness: more powerful adversary enforces stronger robustness but may cause instability.  
  - Monte Carlo sample size (𝑁 𝑀𝐶) vs. computation: larger size improves risk estimate accuracy but increases cost.  
  - Smoothing of indicators (𝑘) vs. score fidelity: smoothing aids backprop but introduces approximation error.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Training divergence/NaN: likely due to unbalanced learning rates, poor smoothing, or gradient issues.  
  - High VaR violation rate: indicates underestimation of tail risk, possibly due to inadequate adversarial pressure or generator limitations.  
  - Large performance gap between fixed and adversarial under worst-case: expected sign that adversarial is needed; absence may indicate adversary not powerful enough.  
  - Generator overfits conditioning: sensitive to minor context changes, suggests unstable encoder representation.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Reproduce core result: Train all three generators (Simple-linear, Encoder-linear, Encoder-LSTM) with fixed-policy objective and compare test joint VaR-ES score and VaR violation rate against baselines. Expect Encoder-LSTM best (~ -3.93).  
  2. Demonstrate adversarial robustness: Train each architecture with both fixed-policy and adversarial objectives; evaluate on worst-case policy. Expect adversarially trained models to show less degradation.  
  3. Ablation: Policy class capacity: Train GAR (Encoder-LSTM) with adversarial policies of varying capacity (GRU layers) and evaluate robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** Can GAR be extended to non-elicitable risk functionals, and what alternative regression targets could be used?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The framework relies on elicitability for the regression characterization (Assumption 1).  
**Why unresolved:** Non-elicitable risks are common but not considered; the paper does not address this limitation.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Propose modifications to the scoring function or optimization objective for non-elicitable cases, and empirically validate on examples like distortion risk measures.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How does the expressiveness of the adversarial policy class Φ impact GAR's robustness, and is the GRU-based parameterization with gross exposure constraint adequate?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 3.3 uses a specific GRU policy with a constraint, but no justification for this choice or analysis of policy class richness.  
**Why unresolved:** The minimax robustness is only over Φ; if Φ is narrow, worst-case policies might be missed, undermining guarantees.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Test GAR with varied policy architectures (e.g., linear, deeper RNNs) and without constraints, then evaluate under adversarial policies not seen during training.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What are the computational efficiency and scalability characteristics of GAR, especially for high-dimensional assets or longer horizons?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper reports results on 9 assets and 10-day horizons but omits computational metrics; no discussion on scalability.  
**Why unresolved:** GAR's alternating min-max with Monte Carlo sampling could be costly; scalability is critical for real-world applications.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct experiments scaling the number of assets and horizon lengths, measuring training time, memory, and convergence, and compare with simpler baselines.

## Limitations
- Major uncertainties: fixed benchmark policy definitions and key hyperparameters (latent dimension, sigmoid sharpness, learning rates) are unreported, relying on assumptions.
- Data period: paper uses future data (up to 2025) which is unavailable; using earlier data may introduce distributional shifts.
- Implementation details for baselines (DCC-GARCH, direct linear) are vague, hindering fair comparison.

## Confidence
- Conditional risk as regression: **Medium** (theoretically sound for elicitable functionals, but conditional application novel).
- Adversarial robustness: **Medium** (empirical gap supports claim, but depends on policy class richness).
- Empirical performance: **Low** (superior scores reported, but unreported details and data-period issues undermine reproducibility).
