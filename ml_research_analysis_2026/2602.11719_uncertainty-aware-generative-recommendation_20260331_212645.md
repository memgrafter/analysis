# Uncertainty-aware Generative Recommendation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11719
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11719
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** UGR achieves state-of-the-art performance (e.g., +3.5% HR@5 on Office versus the strongest baseline) while stabilizing training and enabling risk-aware applications.

## Executive Summary
Generative recommendation reframes item recommendation as sequence generation, but existing methods suffer from uncertainty blindness—treating all errors equally—leading to unstable training and unquantifiable risks. This paper introduces Uncertainty-aware Generative Recommendation (UGR), which combats uncertainty blindness via three synergistic mechanisms: an uncertainty-weighted reward that penalizes confident hallucinations more heavily, difficulty-aware optimization dynamics that focus learning on hard samples, and explicit confidence alignment that teaches the model to output interpretable risk scores. Validated on three datasets (Office Products, Amazon Industrial/Scientific, Yelp) with a two‑stage pipeline (SFT + UGR alignment), UGR yields significant gains (e.g., +3.5% HR@5 on Office) and notably stabilizes training by preventing candidate collapse. The learned confidence further supports risk‑aware downstream tasks like confidence‑based re‑ranking and selective recommendation, demonstrating the practical value of uncertainty‑aware generative models.

## Method Summary
UGR extends generative recommendation with an uncertainty‑aware preference alignment stage. After initial supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) of a large language model (Qwen3‑8B with LoRA) on next‑item prediction using semantic IDs (SIDs), the model is refined via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Each training instance produces a candidate set of size G=16 via constrained beam search. The core GRPO loss is modulated by two innovations: (1) an uncertainty‑weighted reward that normalizes advantages within the candidate group based on the cumulative logit intensity of each item—higher intensity on an incorrect item leads to a more negative reward—and (2) a difficulty‑aware weight λ_diff that scales the loss according to the rank of the ground‑truth item in the candidate set (harder samples receive larger weight). An auxiliary loss forces explicit confidence scores (derived from appended [C_HIGH]/[C_LOW] tokens) to respect a hierarchical error taxonomy derived from SID structure (partial vs. complete mismatch). The total loss combines the weighted GRPO loss and the confidence ranking loss, trained for two epochs with a low learning rate. This design yields stable optimization and well‑calibrated confidence estimates.

## Key Results
- UGR consistently outperforms state‑of‑the‑art baselines on three datasets, with a peak gain of +3.5% HR@5 on Office Products.
- Training stability is markedly improved: candidate collapse—a rapid decline in beam diversity during standard GRPO—is prevented, as shown by sustained HR@16 scores.
- Confidence scores are well‑calibrated and enable practical risk‑aware applications (re‑ranking, truncation, user‑side rejection) with controllable trade‑offs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** UGR assigns adaptive rewards that penalize confident hallucinations more than tentative errors, providing denoised training signals that reflect error severity.
- **Mechanism:** During rollout, the model generates a candidate set via constrained beam search. For each incorrect candidate, its cumulative logit intensity *s(y)* is computed. The reward is defined as *r(y_i) = -exp(s(y_i)) / Σ_{y_j∈N} exp(s(y_j))* for incorrect items, and 1.0 for the correct item. This normalizes within‑group advantages (GRPO) to focus optimization on highly confident errors.
- **Core assumption:** The model’s logit intensity on incorrect items reflects its degree of misconception; higher logits indicate more confident hallucinations that require stronger corrective signals.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] “an uncertainty‑weighted reward to penalize confident errors more heavily”
  - [section 3.2] Detailed formulation of the reward function and its integration with GRPO.
  - [corpus] Limited direct corpus evidence; neighboring works like UCPO (arXiv:2601.22648) use uncertainty in policy optimization but for different contexts (general LLM alignment).
- **Break condition:** If the model’s logits are poorly calibrated (i.e., high logits do not correlate with confident errors), this weighting may misattribute severity, amplifying noise instead of denoising.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** UGR dynamically reweights per‑sample loss based on the ground truth’s ranking within beam candidates, suppressing gradients from easy samples and amplifying those from hard ones to maintain exploration.
- **Mechanism:** The difficulty weight *λ_diff(x) = α_min + (1−α_min) * (rank(y*) / (G−1))* is computed, where *rank(y*)* is the position of the ground truth in the sorted candidate set (with missing ground truth assigned max rank). This weight multiplies the GRPO loss, down‑weighting easy samples (high rank) and up‑weighting hard ones.
- **Core assumption:** The relative ranking of the ground truth in the beam search output serves as a proxy for sample difficulty; a high rank indicates the model finds the sample easy, while a low or missing rank indicates difficulty.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] “difficulty‑aware optimization dynamics to prevent premature convergence”
  - [section 3.3] Definition of the dynamic weight and its integration into the loss.
  - [corpus] Weak; Active3D (arXiv:2511.20050) uses uncertainty for active view selection in 3D reconstruction—analogous to focusing on uncertain regions—but not in recommendation optimization.
- **Break condition:** If beam search candidate diversity is low or the ranking does not reflect true semantic difficulty (e.g., due to model bias), the proxy may misidentify sample difficulty, leading to improper gradient scaling.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** UGR aligns explicit confidence scores (via special tokens) with a hierarchical error taxonomy (partial vs. complete wrongness), enabling interpretable risk signals and acting as semantic regularization.
- **Mechanism:** The vocabulary is expanded with [C_HIGH] and [C_LOW] tokens. After generating a candidate sequence *y*, the confidence score *c(y) = P([C_HIGH]|x, y) / (P([C_HIGH]|x, y) + P([C_LOW]|x, y))* is computed via an append‑and‑query forward pass. A ranking loss enforces *c(y*) > c(y_partial) > c(y_wrong)* with stronger margin for complete wrongness (*γ₂ > γ₁*).
- **Core assumption:** The hierarchical structure of SIDs (semantic IDs) naturally defines error granularity: shared prefix indicates partial correctness (hard negatives), while root mismatch indicates complete wrongness. Confidence should reflect this hierarchy.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] “explicit confidence alignment to empower the model with confidence expression capabilities”
  - [section 3.4] Confidence tokenization and hierarchical alignment loss.
  - [corpus] Directly related work “Uncertainty‑Aware Semantic Decoding for LLM‑Based Sequential Recommendation” (arXiv:2508.07210) uses confidence for decoding, but UGR integrates it into preference optimization—a distinct application.
- **Break condition:** If the SID hierarchy does not capture meaningful semantic similarity (e.g., due to poor RQ‑VAE training), the taxonomy becomes arbitrary, and confidence scores may not correlate with actual recommendation quality.

- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
  **Why needed here:** UGR builds upon GRPO as its base reinforcement learning framework. GRPO’s group‑wise advantage normalization and critic‑free design are adapted to incorporate uncertainty signals. Without grasping GRPO, the modifications in UGR lack context.

- **Implicit vs. Explicit Uncertainty in LLMs**  
  **Why needed here:** UGR leverages both implicit uncertainty (logit‑based, used for reward and difficulty) and explicit confidence (token‑based, used for alignment). Distinguishing these concepts is critical to understanding UGR’s dual‑uncertainty modeling.  
  **Quick check:** Why is explicit confidence more suitable for risk‑aware decision‑making than implicit uncertainty (e.g., token entropy) in recommendation?

- **Semantic IDs (SIDs) and Hierarchical Structure**  
  **Why needed here:** UGR relies on SIDs’ tree structure to define partial correctness and to make confidence scores interpretable. The fixed‑length discrete nature of SIDs also enables comparable logits across candidates.  
  **Quick check:** Why are SIDs preferred over free‑text item titles in generative recommendation for UGR’s confidence alignment?

### Design tradeoffs
- **SID representation vs. text generation:** SIDs provide discrete, fixed‑length, hierarchical structure enabling fine‑grained contrast and confidence alignment, but require pre‑trained RQ‑VAE and limit expressiveness to the codebook.
- **Beam search candidate diversity:** Larger *G* yields more diverse negatives for better contrast but increases computational cost. Constrained beam search balances diversity and high‑likelihood sampling.
- **Loss weighting:** Direct summation of *L_Main* and *L_Aux* avoids extra hyperparameters but assumes scale compatibility; imbalance could cause one objective to dominate.
- **Confidence token simplicity:** Using two tokens ([C_HIGH]/[C_LOW]) makes confidence a single scalar, but loses granularity (e.g., no medium confidence). More tokens could provide finer calibration but increase complexity.

### Failure signatures
- **Training instability:** Rapid degradation in HR@16 (candidate coverage) indicates candidate collapse from overconfidence on easy samples; monitor diversity metrics.
- **Confidence misalignment:** Confidence scores do not correlate with accuracy (e.g., low‑confidence items often correct); check calibration plots.
- **Reward noise:** Uncertainty‑weighted rewards exhibit high variance across steps; monitor *μ_r, σ_r* and consider temperature smoothing.
- **Auxiliary loss domination:** *L_Aux* gradients are significantly larger than *L_Main*; monitor gradient norms per loss component.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Ablation study:** Remove each component (UW‑reward, difficulty‑aware, confidence alignment) from UGR and compare HR@5/NG@5. Expect: SFT baseline > GRPO (binary) > +UW‑reward > +difficulty‑aware > full UGR.
2. **Candidate collapse analysis:** Track the number of unique valid items in the beam search output (*G*=16) over training steps. Standard GRPO should show rapid decline; UGR should maintain diversity (as in Figure 5).
3. **Confidence calibration:** Bin test candidates by confidence score (e.g., deciles) and compute hit ratio per bin. Expect monotonic increase: higher confidence → higher hit ratio. Also evaluate risk‑aware applications (user rejection/item truncation) as in Section 4.5.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Adapting UGR beyond GRPO** – The authors note that while validated within GRPO, the core insight of uncertainty‑aware optimization may be generalizable to other preference alignment strategies like DPO. However, adapting the specific uncertainty‑weighted reward and difficulty‑aware dynamics to DPO’s static pairwise loss is non‑trivial and requires methodological changes.
2. **Fine‑grained uncertainty decomposition** – Current uncertainty modeling uses logits and a binary confidence head. A more granular decomposition (e.g., epistemic vs. aleatoric) could offer richer interpretability and further enhance risk‑aware decisions, but scaling such approaches to large LLMs for sequence generation remains challenging.
3. **Online interactive recommendation** – UGR is evaluated offline; its extension to a live setting where the model must dynamically balance exploration and exploitation using confidence signals is unexplored. Evidence from an online A/B test would be needed to assess long‑term engagement and cumulative regret.

## Limitations
- Unreported key hyperparameters (*α_min*, *γ₁*, *γ₂*) hinder exact reproduction of the reported results.
- Missing details on RQ‑VAE training and SID decoding affect item representation and candidate generation.
- Implementation specifics for constrained beam search and prompt formatting are not provided, which could impact candidate diversity and reward calculation.
