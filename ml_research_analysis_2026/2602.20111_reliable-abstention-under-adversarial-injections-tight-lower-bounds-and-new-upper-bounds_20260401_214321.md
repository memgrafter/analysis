# Reliable Abstention under Adversarial Injections: Tight Lower Bounds and New Upper Bounds

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20111
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20111
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A tight Ω(√T) lower bound for VC-1 classes and new algorithms achieving ˜O(T^{1-1/k}) for classes with finite inference dimension, and a ˜O(T^{2/3}) bound for 2D halfspaces.

## Executive Summary
We study online learning with adversarial injections: data is mostly i.i.d. from an unknown distribution D but may contain adversarially chosen clean-labeled points; the learner can abstain, paying for mistakes and for abstentions on i.i.d. rounds. Key contributions include: (1) a tight Ω(√T) lower bound for VC-1 classes, separating distribution-agnostic from known-D learning; (2) a potential-based algorithm using robust witnesses that, when instantiated with inference dimension k, achieves ˜O(T^{1-1/k}) combined error, and with certificate dimension yields similar rates, notably ˜O(T^{2/3}) for 2D halfspaces—the first agnostic bound despite prior impossibility without abstention; (3) recovery of prior ˜O(√T) for VC-1 and ˜O(d√T) for axis-aligned rectangles. This work demonstrates that abstention enables learning against adversarial injections for a broader class of concepts and introduces combinatorial parameters that govern achievable rates.

## Method Summary
The adversarial injection model combines i.i.d. data with a limited number of adversarially chosen clean-labeled points. The learner’s objective is to minimize combined error—mistakes on i.i.d. rounds plus abstentions on i.i.d. rounds. The core algorithmic innovation is a potential-based method driven by a robust score function f defined on k-subsets of labeled examples and version spaces. For each round, the algorithm computes the potential change for each possible label; if the maximum change exceeds a threshold α, it predicts the label causing the larger drop, otherwise it abstains. The score function must be monotone (to bound potential increase) and m‑robust (to limit attackable points). This framework yields general bounds: mistakes ≤ c·(T choose k)/α and abstentions ≤ O(m·α^{1/k} log T). By carefully selecting f using combinatorial parameters (inference dimension k or certificate dimension), the method achieves sublinear rates: ˜O(T^{1-1/k}) for finite inference dimension, and specifically ˜O(T^{2/3}) for 2D halfspaces. The analysis crucially relies on exchangeability of i.i.d. samples and a trade‑off between α and the robustness parameters.

## Key Results
- A tight Ω(√T) lower bound for VC-1 classes, separating distribution-agnostic from known-D learning.
- A potential-based algorithm using robust witnesses; instantiated with inference dimension k it achieves ˜O(T^{1-1/k}) combined error, and with certificate dimension (new) yields similar rates. For 2D halfspaces, certificate dimension 3 gives ˜O(T^{2/3}), the first agnostic bound, despite prior impossibility without abstention.
- The framework recovers prior ˜O(√T) for VC-1 and ˜O(d√T) for axis-aligned rectangles.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:**  For any VC-1 class, every distribution-agnostic learner incurs expected combined error Ω(√T) in the adversarial injection model with clean labels.
- **Mechanism:**  The adversary builds a deep tree (depth ~√T) where each concept is a root-to-leaf path. It uniformly selects a secret path θ and splits time into √T blocks. In block i, the adversary either injects samples uniform over the children of node θ_i (the i-th node on θ) or, for exactly one randomly chosen block, draws i.i.d. from that distribution. The learner’s uncertainty persists until the unique positive sample in that distribution appears. On each round in a block, unless the learner abstains (paying 1/B) or eventually sees the +1 (low probability), the misclassification probability is at least 1/√T. Summing over √T blocks yields the lower bound.
- **Core assumption:**  The learner does not know D (distribution-agnostic) and cannot distinguish blocks; Yao’s principle for oblivious adversaries applies.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] – States the Ω(√T) lower bound for VC-1 and the separation between known-D and unknown-D regimes.  
  [section 3] – Details the tree construction, block structure, and coupon-counting argument.
- **Break condition:**  If the learner had oracle access to D (known-D regime), the lower bound fails. Also, the adversarial strategy is oblivious; adaptive strategies might tighten the bound but the claim already shows a fundamental gap.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:**  The potential-based algorithm (Algorithm 2) with an (m, c, k)-resilient score function f achieves expected combined error ~O( (c/(k!))^{1/(k+1)} (mT log T)^{k/(k+1)} ) for any horizon T.
- **Mechanism:**  The learner maintains a leave‑k‑out potential ρ_f(S) = Σ_{|U|=k} f(U; V(S\U)). On round t, for the two possible labels b, it computes Δ_b = ρ_f(S_{t-1}) – ρ_f(S_{t-1} ∪ {(x_t, b)}). If max_b Δ_b ≥ α, it predicts the opposite label to guarantee a drop ≥ α when wrong; otherwise it abstains. Mistakes cause a net drop of at least α each, while the total potential increase over T rounds is bounded by c·(T choose k) using monotonicity and boundedness of f. For abstentions, m‑robustness of f implies that any realizable set of size m contains a witness (U, x) such that flipping x’s label reduces f(U; ·) by ≥1; this limits the number of attackable points (samples that could be made to incur an abstention by adversarial extensions) to ≤ em (α + cT^{k–1})^{1/k}. Exchangeability of i.i.d. samples then bounds the expected charged abstentions.
- **Core assumption:**  f is (m, c, k)-resilient: (i) monotonicity (restricting V cannot increase f), (ii) m‑robustness (existence of witness in every realizable m‑set). Robustness is essential to bound attackable samples.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] – Introduces the potential framework driven by robust witnesses and the (m,c,k)-resilient notion.  
  [section 4] – Defines the potential, resilient function properties, and proves Theorem 4.3 bounding misclassification and abstention errors; derives the combined error by balancing α.
- **Break condition:**  If f violates monotonicity, the potential increase bound breaks. If f is not m‑robust for the target class, there may exist histories with many attackable points, causing high abstention error. The framework also assumes the learner can compute or approximate f efficiently.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:**  For classes with finite inference dimension k, the framework yields combined error ~O(T^{1–1/k}). For 2‑dimensional halfspaces, certificate dimension 3 yields ~O(T^{2/3}), the first distribution‑agnostic bound despite prior impossibility without abstention.
- **Mechanism:**  Inference dimension guarantees that for any (k–1)‑set of context points, one of them is inferred by its comparison‑query transcript under any hypothesis. This yields a certificate function σ (using the transcript) whose range size is ≤ 2^{k–1}k!. Setting f(U;V) = number of distinct σ values realizable by V makes f (k, 2^{k–1}k!, k–1)-resilient. Certificate dimension relaxes this by allowing a fixed alphabet size n and a window size m; the corresponding f is (m, n, k–1)-resilient. For 2D halfspaces, a pairwise lexicographic comparison of two points (caching their weighted inner‑product order) with n=3 and m=7 suffices to certify any third point given 7 realizable samples, giving certificate dimension 3.
- **Core assumption:**  The concept class admits a finite inference or certificate dimension. For halfspaces, the geometric property (convexity) allows such certificates. The construction assumes the learner can compute the certificate mapping σ efficiently.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] – States the inference‑dimension instantiation and the certificate‑dimension result for R² halfspaces.  
  [section 5] – Defines inference and certificate dimensions, provides the resilience proofs (Lemmas 5.2, 5.4) and the specific analysis for 2D halfspaces (Theorem 5.5, Lemma D.1).
- **Break condition:**  If inference dimension is infinite (e.g., halfspaces in ℝ^d, d≥3), the direct instantiation fails to give sublinear rates, leaving the learnability open. Certificate dimension may be infinite or require large m, n, which weakens the rate. Also, the α‑balancing assumes c, m, n are manageable; large constants may affect practical performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input -> score function f and threshold α -> State: history S_{t-1}, version spaces V(S_{t-1}\U) for all k-subsets U -> Core Procedure: compute Δ_b for b in {+1,-1}, predict if max Δ ≥ α else abstain -> Update S_t after observing label.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Receive x_t.  
  2. If V^{x_t → +1} = ∅, predict –1; if V^{x_t → –1} = ∅, predict +1 (forced predictions).  
  3. Otherwise, for each b ∈ {+1, –1}, simulate potential change Δ_b = ρ_f(S_{t-1}) – ρ_f(S_{t-1} ∪ {(x_t, b)}).  
  4. If max_b Δ_b ≥ α, output ŷ_t = – argmax_b Δ_b; else ŷ_t = ⊥.  
  5. Observe y_t, update S_t = S_{t-1} ∪ {(x_t, y_t)}.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *k vs. rate:* Larger k reduces mistake bound (c/(α)·T^k) but increases abstention bound (m α^{1/k} log T). Optimal α balances, giving overall ~O( (c^{1/(k+1)} (m T log T)^{k/(k+1)} ).  
  - *Choice of f:* Must be monotone and m‑robust. Monotonicity ensures bounded increase per step; robustness limits attackable points.  
  - *Computation:* ρ_f requires summing over (|S| choose k) subsets; need efficient representation of f to avoid exponential blow‑up (e.g., rectangles and inference-dimension classes allow efficient computation via coordinate independence or transcript compression).
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Potential does not drop on mistakes (violation of monotonicity or prediction rule) → mistake bound blows up.  
  - Many attackable points → abstention error high (failure of m‑robustness).  
  - Inefficient f computation → algorithm intractable for large T or k.  
  - Wrong α → unbalanced errors; α too small → many mistakes; α too large → many abstentions.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Implement Algorithm 2 with the VC‑1 score function (f_seg) on a threshold class on [0,1]; compare empirical combined error to the ~O(√T) bound and to an oblivious tree adversary.  
  2. Use the rectangle score function (f_rect) for axis‑aligned rectangles in ℝ^d; verify ~O(d√T) dependence and the recovery of Goel et al.’s bound.  
  3. For 2D halfspaces, use the certificate σ (lexicographic pair comparison); test the ~O(T^{2/3}) rate against random i.i.d. data with varying fractions of injected rounds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can an adaptive adversary force larger combined error than Ω(√T) for VC-1 or other natural classes, or is the oblivious construction essentially worst-case?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "An interesting direction is to understand whether adaptive injection strategies can force larger error for other natural classes (beyond those where ˜O(√T) is already known to be tight), or whether the oblivious construction is essentially worst-case for the combined objective."
- **Why unresolved:** The lower bound uses an oblivious adversarial strategy; adaptivity might increase the required error.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Either a matching adaptive lower bound or an algorithm achieving the oblivious bound even against adaptive adversaries.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can we break the exchangeability-based analysis to achieve polynomially faster rates for some concept classes?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "It is plausible that for some classes, restricting the adversary (or strengthening the learner's information) could yield faster rates, but doing so likely requires new techniques for controlling charged abstentions beyond the current exchangeability-based approach."
- **Why unresolved:** All current upper bounds rely on exchangeability of i.i.d. samples; this may be inherently limiting.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A new algorithm with provably faster dependence on T that uses fundamentally different abstention control.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are halfspaces in ℝ^d (d≥3) learnable with sublinear combined error? Is infinite inference dimension a true barrier or just a limitation of current techniques?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "While we show halfspaces in ℝ^2 are learnable with sublinear combined error, the status of halfspaces in ℝ^d for d≥3 remains open. In particular, inference dimension is infinite in higher dimensions, but it is unclear whether this reflects a real barrier in the adversarial injection model with abstention, or merely a limitation of inference-dimension-based instantiations."
- **Why unresolved:** Inference dimension fails for d≥3; certificate dimension or other approaches may still yield sublinear rates.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A positive upper bound (e.g., O(T^{1-1/(d+1)})) or a superlinear lower bound for halfspaces in ℝ^d.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What single combinatorial parameter characterizes learnability and optimal rates in the adversarial injection model with abstention?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "A central open problem is to find a combinatorial parameter that characterizes learnability (and rates) in this model, analogous to VC dimension in the stochastic setting and Littlestone dimension in the fully adversarial setting."
- **Why unresolved:** VC dimension does not distinguish the known-D vs unknown-D regimes; inference and certificate dimensions only capture specific classes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A parameter that (i) is finite iff the class is learnable in this model, (ii) determines the optimal rate to within poly-logarithmic factors, and (iii) applies broadly.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How many samples from the unknown distribution D are needed to approximate the known-D guarantees? Do polynomially many samples suffice for broad VC classes?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Understanding the sample complexity needed to approximate the known-D guarantees of Goel et al. [2024a] (and whether polynomially many samples suffice for broad VC classes) is an appealing direction."
- **Why unresolved:** Known-D results assume an oracle providing exact D-queries; tradeoffs with finite sample access are unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A sample Complexity theorem: for any VC class, O(poly(d,1/ε)) D-samples achieve error comparable to the known-D rate.

## Limitations
- Certificate dimension is newly introduced; its value for many natural classes (e.g., halfspaces in ℝ^d, d≥3) remains unknown and could be large or infinite, limiting the claim that similar rates apply broadly.
- The algorithm’s per‑round complexity scales as O(|S_t| choose k), which becomes infeasible for larger k (needed to beat √T) unless the score function f admits an efficient incremental update.
- The lower bound is only proved for VC‑1 classes; it is unclear whether Ω(√T) extends to general VC‑d, creating a gap between existing upper bounds (e.g., O(d√T) for rectangles) and lower bounds.
