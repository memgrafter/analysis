# Self-Improving World Modelling with Latent Actions

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.06130
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06130
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** SWIRL improves world model performance by 14-28% over supervised fine-tuning across visual and textual benchmarks without requiring action-labelled trajectories.

## Executive Summary
SWIRL addresses the challenge of learning internal world models for large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) without costly action-labelled trajectories. The framework treats actions as latent variables and iteratively optimises two components: a Forward World Model (FWM) that predicts next states and an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) that infers actions from state transitions. Optimisation is performed via Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO), where each model alternately acts as a policy and as a reward model for the other. The method is theoretically grounded in alternating variational mutual information maximisation and ELBO maximisation.

Empirically, SWIRL demonstrates consistent gains over SFT baselines on multiple benchmarks, including up to +28% on ByteMorph and +14% on StableToolBench. The iterative process creates a virtuous cycle between the FWM and IDM, improving both components. The approach is data-efficient and shows robustness in long-horizon predictions, offering a practical path to self-supervised world model learning.

## Method Summary
SWIRL begins with a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) warm‑up on a small set of action‑annotated transitions to give both models basic competence. The core algorithm then alternates two phases. In Phase I (FWM update), the IDM is frozen. For each state transition (x, y) from unlabelled data, a latent action z is sampled from the IDM’s posterior Q_φ(z|x,y). The FWM generates G rollouts ŷ conditioned on (x, z). Each rollout is rewarded by the IDM’s log‑probability of reconstructing the same z from (x, ŷ). The FWM is updated with GRPO to maximise a group‑relative advantage estimate of the log‑likelihood while staying close (via KL penalty) to a reference policy (the model checkpoint at the start of the iteration). In Phase II (IDM update), the FWM is frozen. For the same (x, y), the IDM draws G candidate actions z, each rewarded by the FWM’s log‑likelihood of producing y from x and z. The IDM is updated similarly with KL regularisation. The two phases iterate, with the reference policy refreshed each iteration. Separate model weights for FWM and IDM are recommended; choosing a group size G (e.g., 8 for VLMs, 64 for LLMs) and KL coefficient β (typically 0.1) are key hyperparameters. This coordinate ascent scheme provably improves lower bounds on conditional mutual information and the marginal likelihood, enabling self‑improvement from state‑only data.

## Key Results
- Visual dynamics benchmarks: **+16%** on AURORABench, **+28%** on ByteMorph, **+16%** on WorldPredictionBench.
- Textual environments (tool calling): **+14%** on StableToolBench.
- Iterative cycles yield mutual improvements in FWM and IDM scores, especially when using separate‑weight models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
We present the three mechanisms underlying SWIRL’s effectiveness.

### Mechanism 1: Identifiability via FWM optimisation
SWIRL’s FWM optimisation improves *world model identifiability*—the ability to produce distinct next states for different latent actions—by maximising a variational lower bound on conditional mutual information. In Phase I, a frozen IDM provides latent actions z from real data. The FWM generates rollouts ŷ conditioned on z, and each rollout receives a reward equal to the IDM’s log‑probability of reconstructing the same z from (x, ŷ). This objective encourages the FWM to generate futures that retain information about the latent action, making z recoverable from ŷ. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) shows that this reward is a lower bound on I(Z; Ŷ|X). If the IDM’s posterior Q_φ becomes uninformative or the reward collapses (e.g., all z become equally probable), the learning signal vanishes and further optimisation stalls.

### Mechanism 2: Data fidelity via IDM optimisation
SWIRL’s IDM optimisation improves *data fidelity*—the ability to infer actions that explain observed state transitions under the current FWM—by maximising an Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). In Phase II, with the FWM frozen, the IDM samples candidate actions z from a reference policy for real pairs (x, y). Each candidate is rewarded by the FWM’s log‑likelihood log P_θ(y|x,z). This encourages the IDM to select actions that the FWM considers plausible explanations for the observed transition. Theorem 3.2 establishes that this update maximises an ELBO for the marginal log P_θ(y|x). Miscalibration of the FWM (e.g., over‑confidence on invalid transitions) or poorly set KL penalties can cause the IDM to learn incorrect mappings.

### Mechanism 3: Virtuous cycle through reciprocal rewards
The alternating, reciprocal cycle between FWM and IDM, mediated by GRPO, enables *self‑improvement* from state‑only data by creating a virtuous loop: improvement in one model yields a better learning signal for the other. The system performs coordinate ascent, with Phase I maximising a CMI lower bound w.r.t. θ and Phase II maximising an ELBO w.r.t. ϕ. Because each phase’s reward depends on the other model’s current parameters, the joint optimisation drives the two models toward mutual consistency. Empirically, separate‑weight setups show monotonic gains in both FWM and IDM scores across iterations (Section 4.5), while shared weights lead to gradient interference and performance collapse. The cycle can break if one model drifts too far (e.g., reward hacking), if the IDM’s posterior collapses, or if the FWM’s log‑likelihoods become unreliable.

## Foundational Learning
Several core concepts underpin SWIRL.

### Variational Inference (ELBO & Mutual Information Bounds)
Why needed: SWIRL’s theoretical foundation is the derivation of its two optimisation phases as maximising valid lower bounds: an ELBO for the IDM and a variational conditional mutual information bound for the FWM. Understanding these bounds clarifies why the objectives are sound and how they relate to the true data distribution.
Quick check: “Why is using Q_φ(z|x, ŷ) as a reward for the FWM a valid way to encourage the generated ŷ to retain information about the latent action z?” (Answer: It lower‑bounds the negative conditional entropy −H(Z|Ŷ,X), which is part of the CMI.)

### Policy Gradient Methods (GRPO) for Off‑Policy Correction
Why needed: Both FWM and IDM are optimised as policies via GRPO. GRPO’s group‑relative advantage estimation stabilises learning when the reward model (the frozen counterpart) changes slowly between phases, by removing bias from overall reward scale.
Quick check: “How does the group‑relative advantage in GRPO help mitigate the non‑stationarity of the reward model during training?” (Answer: By subtracting the group mean, it makes updates robust to shifts in the absolute quality of the frozen model’s scores.)

### Latent Variable Modelling for Inverse Problems
Why needed: The core challenge is to learn dynamics from state‑only transitions (x, y) without observed actions. Treating actions as latent variables and learning an inverse model Q_φ(z|x,y) is the standard variational inference approach. SWIRL’s innovation lies in using the *product* of this inverse model and the forward model in a reciprocal loop.
Quick check: “Why is learning an inverse dynamics model Q_φ(z|x,y) from (x,y) pairs alone generally ill‑posed, and how does SWIRL’s reciprocal loop address this?” (Answer: Many z can produce the same (x,y); SWIRL’s FWM generates diverse ŷ for a sampled z, and rewards the FWM when the IDM can recover that z, encouraging z to become identifiable from ŷ.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- **Unlabelled data:** State‑only transitions (x, y)
- **Forward World Model (P_θ):** Generates next state ŷ conditioned on state x and latent action z.
- **Inverse Dynamics Model (Q_φ):** Takes a transition (x, y) and outputs a distribution over z.
- **GRPO Trainer:** Alternates two roles: (1) policy = FWM, reward = log Q_φ(z|x, ŷ); (2) policy = IDM, reward = log P_θ(y|x, z). Updates use group‑relative advantages and KL penalty to a reference policy.
- **Reference Policy (π_ref):** Model checkpoint at the start of the current iteration, used for regularisation.

### Critical path
1. **SFT warm‑up:** Fine‑tune base model on a small set of action‑labelled trajectories to initialise both FWM and IDM with basic competence.
2. **Phase I (FWM update):** Freeze IDM. For each batch (x, y), sample z ∼ Q_φ(z|x, y). Generate G rollouts ŷ ∼ P_θ(·|x, z). Compute reward r = log Q_φ(z|x, ŷ). Compute group‑relative advantages A_f. Update θ to maximise (1/G) Σ A_f log P_θ(ŷ|x, z) − β KL(π_ref_FWM ∥ P_θ).
3. **Phase II (IDM update):** Freeze θ. For each batch (x, y), sample G actions z ∼ Q_φ(·|x, y). Compute reward r = log P_θ(y|x, z). Compute advantages A_i. Update ϕ similarly with KL to π_ref_IDM.
4. **Iterate:** Set new π_ref to the updated models before the next iteration. Typical hyperparameters: rollout size G=8 (VLM) or G=64 (LLM); temperature 0.75; top‑p 0.96; learning rates 2e‑7 (VLM) or 5e‑5 (LLM); β=0.1.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Separate vs. shared weights:** Separate weights (θ ≠ ϕ) yield stable monotonic improvement; shared weights (θ = ϕ) cause gradient interference, leading to early peaks then degradation (Figure 2, Table 1).
- **Group size G:** Larger G improves advantage estimates and final performance but increases compute/memory. G=16 is a sweet spot (paper uses 8 for VLM, 64 for LLM).
- **KL coefficient β:** Balances reward maximisation against staying close to π_ref. Sensitive to environment; typically β=0.1 for VLM, β=0.1 or 1.0 for LLM IDM.
- **Iteration count:** Not all tasks benefit from multiple iterations. For long‑horizon WorldPredictionBench, the first iteration (IDM→FWM) is often best; additional iterations may overfit.

### Failure signatures
- **Reward collapse:** IDM’s log‑prob becomes nearly uniform across z for generated ŷ; monitor intra‑group reward variance.
- **Action token degeneration:** Generated z become short, repetitive, or nonsensical; track average action length and perplexity (Figure 6).
- **Gradient interference (shared weights):** FWM score increases while IDM score decreases in the same iteration.
- **Compounding error in long‑horizon:** FWM score drops sharply over autoregressive steps, indicating poor dynamics generalisation; see Table 3 for liquid‑SFT.

### First 3 experiments (reproduction)
1. **SFT warm‑up baseline:** Train base model (Liquid‑7B or Qwen‑2.5‑3B‑Instruct) on environment‑specific action‑labelled data (e.g., AURORA train set, half of StableToolBench). Verify non‑trivial performance on the primary benchmark (e.g., >3.5 GPT‑4o score on AURORA, >12 BLEU on StableToolBench ID‑Medium).
2. **Single‑phase RL (IDM→FWM):** Initialise from SFT. Run only Phase I (FWM update) for N steps with frozen IDM. Evaluate FWM on validation; expect improvement over SFT baseline, confirming the FWM identifiability mechanism.
3. **Full iterative SWIRL:** Initialise from SFT. Run at least 2 full iterations (Phase I then Phase II) with separate weights. Track FWM validation score and IDM accuracy (e.g., via GPT‑4o judge on AURORA captions). Expect a virtuous cycle (both metrics improve). Compare with shared weights to observe instability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Camera control on ByteMorph:** Why does SWIRL fail to improve camera dynamics, and how can this be overcome? The paper suggests static unlabelled videos provide weak supervision; a dataset with diverse camera motions or auxiliary losses may help.
2. **Shared‑weight instability:** What causes gradient interference between FWM and IDM when parameters are shared, and can we retain parameter efficiency without degradation? Architectural modifications (e.g., adapters) could resolve this.
3. **Long‑horizon improvement:** Why doesn’t iterative self‑improvement consistently benefit long‑horizon world modelling, and how to mitigate error accumulation? Compounding errors and diminishing reward signal might require contrastive rewards or intermediate consistency checks.
4. **Theoretical guarantees:** What are the convergence guarantees for alternating GRPO optimisation under neural function approximation, and how does sample complexity scale? Current analysis provides lower bounds but no convergence rates; empirical sampling studies are needed.

## Limitations
- **Implementation gaps:** Missing details (prompt templates, GRPO specifics, video preprocessing) hinder faithful replication.
- **Theoretical assumptions:** No convergence analysis for neural models; shared‑weight instability indicates objective conflict.
- **Dependence on SFT warm‑up:** Performance is sensitive to the quality and quantity of initial action‑labelled data; the paper lacks sensitivity analysis.

## Confidence
- **Empirical gains:** High (consistent improvements across five benchmarks)
- **Theoretical derivation:** Medium (bounds are correct but rely on ideal variational proxies)
- **Self‑improvement loop:** Low (plausible but lacks ablations on necessity of both phases; break conditions observed)

## Next Checks
1. **Single‑phase ablation:** Run only Phase I after SFT; if gains persist, the FWM mechanism is primary; if not, the full cycle is necessary.
2. **Weight separation stress test:** Train with separate weights for 5+ iterations on AURORA‑BENCH; verify whether gains continue monotonically or plateau, refining stopping criteria.
3. **Reward‑hacking probe:** Monitor action diversity (unique %), average length, and IDM entropy during iterations. If diversity drops >30% or entropy halves, increase β or G to stabilise.
