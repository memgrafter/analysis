# Accurate Failure Prediction in Agents Does Not Imply Effective Failure Prevention

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03338
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03338
- **Reference count:** 23
- **Primary result:** Despite high offline critic accuracy (AUROC 0.94), execution-time intervention can degrade success on high-performing tasks (+2.8pp gain only on high-failure ALFWorld), but a small pilot test reliably predicts when intervention will help vs. harm via the disruption-recovery tradeoff threshold p* = d/(r+d).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether using an LLM critic to intervene mid-trajectory improves the reliability of LLM agents. It formalizes a disruption-recovery tradeoff: intervention can convert a failing trajectory into a success (recovery) but can also disrupt a trajectory that would have succeeded. A pre-deployment test using a small pilot (e.g., 50 tasks) estimates the baseline failure rate (p), recovery rate (r), and disruption rate (d), computing a threshold p* = d/(r+d). Intervention is beneficial only if p > p*. Experiments show that while critics achieve high offline accuracy (AUROC up to 0.94), intervention causes severe degradation on high-success tasks (up to -26 percentage points) for some models, while yielding modest gains on the high-failure ALFWorld benchmark. Crucially, the pilot test correctly anticipated these outcomes, demonstrating its value in identifying when not to intervene and preventing large regressions.

## Method Summary
The method equips an LLM agent with a fine-tuned critic that monitors execution trajectories and triggers an intervention (rollback or append) when a failure probability exceeds a threshold. To decide whether to deploy this system, the authors propose a pilot study: run the agent with intervention enabled on a small, representative set of tasks, compare outcomes to a baseline run without intervention, and compute empirical estimates of p (baseline failure rate), r = C/F (recovery rate: proportion of baseline failures turned into successes), and d = B/S (disruption rate: proportion of baseline successes turned into failures). The threshold p* = d/(r+d) determines if net benefit is expected. This approach shifts evaluation focus from the critic's offline discriminative power to the intervention's net effect on the agent's success rate in deployment.

## Key Results
- High offline critic accuracy (AUROC up to 0.94) did not translate to deployment gains; on HotPotQA and GAIA, intervention caused severe regressions (up to -26 percentage points) for models like MiniMax-M2.1.
- On the high-failure ALFWorld benchmark, intervention yielded a modest but statistically significant improvement (+2.8 percentage points, p=0.014).
- The pre-deployment pilot test (≈50 tasks) reliably predicted these outcomes by estimating p, r, d and computing p*, demonstrating its utility in avoiding detrimental interventions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The net effect of execution-time intervention is governed by a disruption-recovery tradeoff, quantified by the condition `p > d/(r+d)`, where `p` is the baseline failure rate, `r` is the recovery rate, and `d` is the disruption rate.
- **Mechanism:** Intervention can convert a baseline failure into a success (recovery, count `C`) but can also convert a baseline success into a failure (disruption, count `B`). The expected change in success rate is `ΔSuccess = p*r - (1-p)*d`. Intervention is beneficial only when this difference is positive.
- **Core assumption:** The rates `r` and `d` are intrinsic to the agent's response to mid-trajectory correction and are relatively stable across similar tasks, allowing estimation from a small pilot.
- **Break condition:** If the agent's behavior is highly unstable such that `r` and `d` vary significantly across individual tasks or minor prompt changes, a single pilot estimate becomes unreliable for deployment decisions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The disruption-to-recovery ratio (`d/r`) is model-dependent and determines the ceiling for intervention benefits, not the critic's offline accuracy (e.g., AUROC).
- **Mechanism:** A critic with high AUROC may still trigger interventions on trajectories the agent could have completed successfully. The agent's inherent sensitivity to disruption (high `d`) or poor recovery capability (low `r`) sets a low ceiling for net gains. Models like MiniMax-M2.1 exhibit a high `d/r` ratio (7.3:1), leading to catastrophic regressions, while GLM-4.7 has a lower ratio (1.5:1) and experiences near-neutral effects.
- **Core assumption:** `r` and `d` are properties of the base agent's decision-making process under perturbation and are largely independent of the critic's discriminative power once a trigger threshold is set.
- **Break condition:** If a novel intervention mechanism (e.g., richer feedback) fundamentally alters the agent's recovery dynamics (`r` increases substantially without increasing `d`), the model's historical `d/r` ratio may no longer predict deployment outcomes.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A small pre-deployment pilot study (e.g., 50 tasks) can reliably estimate `p`, `r`, and `d` and predict whether full-scale intervention will help or harm, preventing severe regressions.
- **Mechanism:** By running the agent with the intervention mechanism enabled on a small, representative pilot set and comparing outcomes to a matched baseline, one can compute empirical estimates of `p`, `r` (C/F), and `d` (B/S). The derived threshold `p* = d/(r+d)` indicates the minimum baseline failure rate required for net benefit.
- **Core assumption:** The pilot sample is representative of the target deployment distribution, and the `d` and `r` rates are stable enough that estimates from 50 tasks have acceptable variance.
- **Break condition:** If the pilot tasks are not i.i.d. from the deployment distribution (e.g., different difficulty skew) or if the intervention mechanism's effect size is below the pilot's statistical power, the prediction may be inaccurate.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Binary Classification Metrics (AUROC, F1, Calibration)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper uses the critic's AUROC to argue that high offline accuracy is insufficient. Understanding these metrics prevents misinterpreting classifier performance as deployment safety.
  - *Quick check:* If a critic has AUROC=0.94 but a disruption rate `d=0.35` and recovery rate `r=0.12`, what is the minimum baseline failure rate `p` needed for intervention to be beneficial? (Answer: `p > 0.745`).
- **Concept: 2x2 Contingency Table for Causal Inference**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire analysis rests on comparing paired baseline vs. intervention outcomes (cells A, B, C, D) to compute `r` and `d`. This is the foundation for the tradeoff equation.
  - *Quick check:* In a study with 100 tasks, baseline fails on 60, intervention succeeds on 15 of those (recovery). Baseline succeeds on 40, intervention fails on 10 of those (disruption). What are `r` and `d`? (Answer: `r=15/60=0.25`, `d=10/40=0.25`).
- **Concept: Statistical Power and Effect Size Estimation**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's pilot recommendation must account for the detectable effect size given a limited number of pilot tasks (e.g., ±4pp for ALFWorld). Engineers need to size pilots appropriately.
  - *Quick check:* If you run a 50-task pilot and observe `d=0.2`, `r=0.1`, what is `p*` and what is the 95% CI width for `p*` assuming binomial variance? (Answer: `p*=0.667`; CI depends on counts of B and S).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Base Agent -> LLM Critic -> Intervention Mechanism -> Pilot Estimator
- **Critical path:** Deploy decision flow: (a) Choose target agent, benchmark, and intervention mechanism. (b) Run small pilot (N=50-100) to collect paired outcomes. (c) Compute `p`, `r = C/F`, `d = B/S` from pilot. (d) Calculate `p* = d/(r+d)`. (e) Deploy intervention only if `p > p*` (with safety margin).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Intervention Mechanism Simplicity vs. Disruption:** Simpler mechanisms (APPEND, ROLLBACK) have higher `d`. More complex, context-aware corrections might lower `d` but increase latency and complexity.
  - **Pilot Size vs. Responsiveness:** A 50-task pilot is cheap but may have high variance in `d/r` estimates for low-failure regimes (`S` small). Larger pilots reduce variance but incur cost.
  - **Threshold Tuning (`τ`) vs. Cascade Risk:** Lower `τ` increases intervention frequency, potentially increasing both `C` and `B`. Very high `τ` may avoid early disruptions but also miss recoverable failures.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Early-step disruption:** Interventions at step 0-1 on trajectories that would have succeeded immediately. This is the dominant failure mode in high-success regimes (HotPotQA, GAIA).
  - **Intervention cascade:** A single trigger leads to subsequent triggers (e.g., rollback causes new action that also triggers), exhausting the step budget and causing no-answer failures. Evident in MiniMax-M2.1.
  - **Calibration mismatch:** Temperature scaling suppresses intervention rate differently per model (e.g., 71% reduction for GLM vs. 3% for MiniMax), affecting `d` and `r`.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replicate pilot estimation on target domain:** Use calibrated `ROLLBACK` on 50 in-distribution tasks. Compute `p`, `r`, `d`. Verify `p > p*`? If not, do not deploy.
  2. **Threshold sensitivity sweep:** Vary `τ` (e.g., 0.4 to 0.9) on the same pilot to see if any threshold flips the sign of `ΔSuccess`. If no threshold yields `ΔSuccess>0`, intervention is likely harmful regardless of tuning.
  3. **Minimum-step constraint test:** Add `min_step >= 2` rule to prevent early-step interventions. Rerun pilot. Does `d` decrease significantly? Does `ΔSuccess` improve? This tests if early disruption is the primary harm source.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Can frontier-scale LLM critics (e.g., 100B+ parameters) improve intervention outcomes by reducing disruption rates or increasing recovery rates, despite the lack of improvement observed when scaling from 0.6B to 14B?
- **Question 2:** Can more sophisticated intervention mechanisms (e.g., step-specific corrections, planning-aware backtracking) lower the disruption rate d while maintaining or increasing recovery rate r?
- **Question 3:** Do the observed disruption-recovery dynamics (i.e., d/r ratios and the threshold p* = d/(r+d)) generalize across different agent frameworks (e.g., LangChain, ReAct) and diverse task domains (e.g., code generation, scientific reasoning)?
- **Question 4:** Can a pilot study conducted on a source domain reliably predict whether intervention will be beneficial on a target domain, or must estimation of d and r be performed within each deployment domain?

## Limitations
- **Generalizability:** Evidence drawn from 3 LLM models and two simple interventions (ROLLBACK, APPEND); may fail for other agents or advanced correction methods.
- **Pilot reliability:** 50-task pilots yield noisy `d`/`r` estimates, especially when baseline success is high, risking wrong `p*` predictions.
- **Rate stability:** The constancy of `r` and `d` across tasks/domains is untested; instability would undermine a single‑pilot `p*`.

## Confidence
- **Tradeoff condition** (`p > d/(r+d)`): **High** (theoretically sound, validated on three benchmarks).
- **Pilot‑based prediction** (≈50 tasks): **Medium** (small sample, limited domains).
- **d/ r ratio as ceiling** vs. AUROC: **Medium** (empirical across models but narrow settings).

## Next Checks
1. **Broad generalizability:** Run 100‑task pilots for 5+ new agents (GPT‑4, Claude‑3, Llama‑3) across math, code, embodied domains. Compare predicted vs. actual ΔSuccess to set pilot size standards.
2. **Rate stability:** On HotPotQA and GAIA, stratify tasks by baseline success quartiles. Compute `r` and `d` per quartile; test homogeneity. If heterogeneous, adopt stratified `p*` or larger pilots.
3. **Rich intervention:** Replace ROLLBACK with critic‑generated hints on MiniMax in high‑success benchmarks. Measure `d`/`r` to check if the `d/r` ceiling can be lowered.
