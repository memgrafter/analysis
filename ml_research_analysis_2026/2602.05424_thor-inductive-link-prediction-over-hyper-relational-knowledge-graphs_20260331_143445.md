# THOR: Inductive Link Prediction over Hyper-Relational Knowledge Graphs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05424
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05424
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** THOR achieves substantial relative improvements (66.1%, 55.9%, 20.4%) over state-of-the-art rule-based, semi-inductive, and fully-inductive baselines on hyper-relational KG link prediction, plus 32.5% in cross-domain settings.

## Executive Summary
THOR addresses the challenge of inductive link prediction over hyper-relational knowledge graphs (HKGs), where models must generalize to entirely unseen entities and relations during inference. Existing methods fail in this setting due to their reliance on vocabulary-specific embeddings. THOR introduces two foundation graphs—relation and entity—that model fundamental, position-wise interactions (e.g., head-to-head-relation, value-to-head-entity) agnostic to specific symbols. These graphs are processed by parallel NBFNet-based encoders conditioned on the query, and a transformer decoder with edge-biased attention scores candidate entities without negative sampling. Evaluated on 12 HKG datasets, THOR significantly outperforms baselines, demonstrating the efficacy of its architecture and the masked training paradigm for inductive reasoning in hyper-relational contexts.

## Method Summary
THOR constructs a **relation foundation graph** whose nodes are relation types and edges encode role-based interactions (e.g., `h2hr`, `r2kr`) from hyper-relational facts. An **entity foundation graph** connects entities via position-wise edges (e.g., `h2te`, `v2ve`). For a query fact, both graphs are initialized with an indicator vector (1 for nodes in the query, 0 otherwise) and processed by separate NBFNet encoders, producing query-conditioned representations for relations and entities. A transformer decoder then takes these representations, orders query elements, and applies self-attention with learned biases for specific element pairs (head-relation, relation-key, key-value, etc.). The decoder’s `[MASK]` token output directly scores all entities in the entity foundation graph, enabling training without negative sampling. This design allows THOR to capture transferable structural patterns independent of any specific entity or relation vocabulary.

## Key Results
- Relative improvements: 66.1% over best rule-based, 55.9% over best semi-inductive, and 20.4% over best fully-inductive baselines.
- Cross-domain generalization: 32.5% improvement.
- Ablation studies confirm the critical roles of qualifier-driven edges (`r2kr`, `h2ve`, etc.) and the masked training objective; removing either causes significant performance drops.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Foundation graphs capture structural invariance transferable across HKGs by modeling position-wise interactions agnostic to specific entity/relation IDs.
THOR defines two graphs: (1) a relation foundation graph with edges like `h2hr`, `r2kr` connecting relations based on their semantic roles; (2) an entity foundation graph with edges like `h2te`, `v2he` connecting entities within and across facts. These interactions are defined purely by positions (head, tail, value, key) rather than concrete symbols. The core assumption is that the topology of these position-based interactions encodes fundamental patterns consistent across different HKGs, enabling generalization to unseen vocabularies. Evidence includes the abstract’s statement on modeling fundamental interactions agnostic to specific relations and entities, formal definitions in Section 3.1, and the trend in neighboring work (e.g., ULTRA, MOTIF) toward foundation graphs—THOR extends this to hyper-relational facts via qualifier-driven edges. Adding extra interactions (e.g., `k2kr` or cross-fact value sharing) degrades performance, indicating the selected set balances expressiveness and noise.

### Conditional message passing via NBFNet with a labeling trick yields query-dependent node representations that are more expressive for inductive reasoning than standard GNNs.
For a given query, all nodes in the foundation graphs are initialized with an indicator: 1 if the node appears in the query, 0 otherwise. NBFNet then performs message passing conditioned on this query, propagating information to produce representations tailored to the prediction task. The core assumption is that the query serves as a conditioning signal that focuses the model on substructures relevant to the missing entity. Evidence: the abstract mentions NBFNet-based encoders; Section 3.2 formalizes the initialization and message passing; and prior work establishes NBFNet for inductive link prediction on triple KGs. The all-ones indicator (rather than learnable embeddings) is crucial for generalization in inductive settings; using embeddings causes overfitting.

### Transformer decoder with edge-biased attention enables efficient masked training without negative sampling while modeling intra-fact interactions.
The decoder receives entity and relation representations from the encoders, constructs a sequence of query elements, and computes self-attention with learnable biases for specific position pairs (e.g., `(h,r)`, `(r,k)`, `(k,v)`). The `[MASK]` token representation directly scores all entities in the entity foundation graph, eliminating the need to corrupt negatives. The core assumption is that intra-fact positional relationships are critical for predicting missing elements and can be captured via edge biases, while entity representations from the foundation graph are sufficiently discriminative. Evidence: the abstract highlights masked training without negative sampling; Section 3.2 defines the five bias types; and edge-biased transformers are used in transductive hyper-relational models (e.g., GRAN, HAHE). A ULTRA-alike variant with negative sampling underperforms by 57.9%, confirming the superiority of masked training.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Relation Foundation Graph Encoder -> Entity Foundation Graph Encoder -> Transformer Decoder
- **Critical path:** Query fact → build both foundation graphs → parallel NBFNet encoders (query-conditioned) → concatenate/reconcile representations → transformer decoder with edge-biased attention → entity scores.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - All-ones indicator vs. learnable embeddings: all-ones improves generalization but may limit capacity; adopted for inductive settings.  
  - Parallel encoders vs. sequential (e.g., ULTRA-alike): parallel avoids information bottleneck and negative sampling but increases computation (2–3× slower).  
  - Edge bias set: limited to five types; adding more (e.g., key-to-key) hurts performance (ablation).
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Incorrect foundation graph edge construction (verify edges per fact).  
  - NBFNet labeling error (query nodes must be 1, others 0).  
  - Decoder edge bias misassignment (ensure five bias types map correctly to element pairs).  
  - Overfitting (training MRR high, validation low): reduce model size or increase dropout.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Baseline replication: Run THOR and inductive baselines on WD20K100(V1) and JFFI(V1) to reproduce Table 2 improvements.  
  2. Relation foundation graph ablation: Remove `R2K` (`r2kr`, `k2rr`) or `Prim` (cross-fact primary interactions) to confirm necessity (Table 4 rows “noR2K”, “noPrim”).  
  3. Entity foundation graph ablation: Remove `P2V` (`h2ve`, `v2he`, `t2ve`, `v2te`) or `V2V` (`v2ve`) to quantify impact (Table 4 rows “noP2V”, “noV2V”).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can fundamental interactions in THOR's foundation graphs be extended to accommodate more complex hyper-relational motifs beyond the currently identified position-wise interactions?
- Why do graph motifs that improve performance on triplet-based KGs (like 3-path and 3-star) degrade performance when applied to hyper-relational knowledge graphs?
- What is the theoretical explanation for THOR's architecture significantly outperforming the ULTRA-alike variant (57.9% improvement)?
- Why does THOR show performance degradation on transductive domain-specific datasets compared to its strong inductive performance, and can hybrid inductive-transductive approaches mitigate this?
- How can the community establish standardized benchmark datasets and evaluation protocols for inductive link prediction over hyper-relational knowledge graphs, given the current lack of consensus?

## Limitations
- Key hyperparameters (NBFNet layers, embedding dimension, transformer depth, dropout) and training details are not reported, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Computational cost is 2–3× higher than some baselines, limiting scalability.
- Foundation graph edge definitions are hand-crafted and may not capture all invariant patterns across domains.
