# When Understanding Becomes a Risk: Authenticity and Safety Risks in the Emerging Image Generation Paradigm

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.24079  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24079  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: MLLMs generate more unsafe content and produce harder-to-detect fake images compared to diffusion models, especially with richer prompts; detector fine-tuning on one MLLM fails to generalize to others.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates safety and authenticity risks in emerging image generation paradigms, comparing multimodal large language models (MLLMs) with traditional diffusion models. Evaluating seven models (two diffusion, five MLLMs) across multiple benchmarks, the study finds that MLLMs generate significantly more unsafe content (unsafe scores up to 0.613 vs. diffusion's 0.280) because they accurately interpret abstract and non-English prompts that diffusion models fail to comprehend, leading to corrupted outputs that are incorrectly labeled safe. Moreover, MLLM-generated images are harder to detect as synthetic; detector accuracy for MLLMs drops substantially when prompts are enriched with descriptive details, while diffusion models remain stable. Fine-tuning detectors on MLLM-specific data improves performance for that model but generalizes poorly to other MLLMs, highlighting a critical gap in current defenses.

The findings underscore that the enhanced semantic understanding of MLLMs, while beneficial for legitimate use, introduces heightened risks for generating harmful content and evading detection. The study calls for more robust, generalizable safeguards that can address the diverse artifacts produced by different MLLM architectures and the evolving nature of prompt-based attacks.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a comprehensive evaluation of seven image generation models: two diffusion models (SD3.5 Large, SD3.5 Large Turbo) and five MLLMs (Bagel, Janus, Janus Pro, TokenFlow, VILA-U). For safety assessment, they use a suite of unsafe prompt datasets, including Lexica, 4chan, Template, I2P, and a Chinese-translated TemplateLong, alongside benign prompts from MSCOCO and Flickr30k. All models generate images with safety mechanisms disabled. The generated images are labeled for safety using the Moderation API, with human validation on a subset. For authenticity detection, they employ four detectors: two commercial (Winston.Ai, Illuminarty) and two open-source (DE-FAKE, AIorNot-SigLIP2). They also experiment with prompt enrichment (creating v1 and v2 versions with more detail) and fine-tune detectors on data from individual MLLMs to test generalization.

The evaluation follows a structured pipeline: prompts are fed to each model to produce images, which are then scored by safety classifiers or detectors. Key metrics are the unsafe score (proportion of images labeled unsafe) and detection accuracy. The study analyzes differences in generation quality (e.g., distortion rates) and correlates them with safety labels. It also examines how prompt richness affects detection and how fine-tuning on one model's data transfers to others.

## Key Results
- MLLMs generate significantly more unsafe images than diffusion models; unsafe scores for MLLMs reach up to 0.613, while diffusion models peak at 0.280, primarily because MLLMs interpret abstract and non-English prompts accurately.
- MLLM-generated images are consistently harder to detect as fake; with richer prompts, detection accuracy for MLLMs drops substantially (e.g., TokenFlow from 0.934 to 0.799), whereas diffusion models remain unaffected.
- Fine-tuning detectors on data from a single MLLM improves accuracy on that model but generalizes poorly to other MLLMs and diffusion models, indicating strong negative transfer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** MLLMs generate more unsafe images than diffusion models partly because their stronger semantic understanding allows them to successfully interpret abstract or non-English unsafe prompts, whereas diffusion models frequently fail and produce damaged, low-fidelity outputs that are classified as safe.
- **Mechanism:** Diffusion models rely on surface-level token matching and struggle with semantically complex prompts, leading to generation failures (garbled pixels, text artifacts). These corrupted outputs are inherently nonsensical and thus labeled safe by classifiers. MLLMs, with integrated language reasoning, parse abstract meaning and generate coherent, contextually aligned images—including harmful ones—avoiding the "failure as safety" artifact.
- **Core assumption:** The Moderation API's safe/unsafe labels primarily reflect semantic content, not image artifacts; distorted images are correctly labeled safe regardless of prompt intent.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "MLLMs accurately interpret abstract and non-English prompts"; [section 3.3] "Diffusion models tend to produce damaged images due to weaker prompt comprehension... These failures are characterized by random black patches... These images are usually labeled as safe by the classifier, which artificially lowers measured unsafe scores."; [section 3.4] Case study showing MLLMs generating realistic decayed scenes from figurative prompts while diffusion models produce literal "text-like" or meaningless outputs.
- **Break condition:** If diffusion models achieve comparable semantic parsing to MLLMs for abstract prompts without increasing unsafe content generation, the mechanism fails. Conversely, if MLLMs' safe generations also increase under abstract prompts, the artifact explanation weakens.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Images generated by MLLMs are harder for existing fake image detectors to identify as synthetic compared to diffusion model outputs because MLLMs produce more photorealistic and contextually coherent images via richer prompt utilization.
- **Mechanism:** Current detectors (trained predominantly on diffusion data) learn artifacts specific to that paradigm's generation process. MLLMs' architecture—unified multimodal reasoning—produces different visual artifacts (e.g., smoother context integration, better texture consistency) that fall outside the detector's learned decision boundary. When prompts are enriched, MLLMs leverage their comprehension to fill in realistic details, further narrowing the real vs. synthetic gap.
- **Core assumption:** Detection accuracy is primarily driven by learned visual artifacts; generalization gaps arise from distribution shifts between training (diffusion) and test (MLLM) data.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "MLLM-generated outputs are consistently harder to identify; with richer, more descriptive prompts, detection accuracy for MLLMs drops substantially"; [section 4.2] "Across both datasets and all four detectors, we observe a consistent pattern: images produced by MLLMs generally yield lower detection accuracy compared to those generated by diffusion models."; [section 4.4] "For MLLMs, detection accuracy decreases as prompt richness increases (v0→v2), whereas diffusion models remain nearly unaffected."
- **Break condition:** If detectors trained on diverse multi-paradigm data achieve uniform high accuracy across all models regardless of prompt richness, the mechanism is mitigated. If MLLMs' realism plateau does not improve with prompt detail, the "prompt enrichment" sub-mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The elevated safety risks of MLLMs are not adequately addressed by current defenses because (a) external safeguards (like NSFW detectors) remain insufficient against MLLM outputs, and (b) fine-tuned detectors generalize poorly across different MLLMs due to architectural diversity.
- **Mechanism:** MLLMs' outputs occupy a distinct manifold in image-space compared to diffusion models. A detector fine-tuned on one MLLM's data learns model-specific fingerprints but fails to capture the broader MLLM family's variations. Commercial black-box detectors lack adaptation capability, leaving a persistent blind spot. The semantic richness of prompts also creates a moving target: richer inputs push MLLM outputs toward greater realism, continuously challenging static detectors.
- **Core assumption:** Model-specific generation artifacts dominate over shared synthetic indicators; architectural differences (e.g., tokenization, training objectives) cause significant inter-MLLM variation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Fine-tuning detectors with MLLM-specific data improves accuracy but generalizes poorly across different MLLMs."; [section 4.3] "DE-FAKE achieves near-perfect accuracy on Janus but generalizes poorly to other MLLMs and diffusion models, indicating strong negative transfer."; [section 4.4] "MLLMs can evade detection by enriching textual details in the prompt—a behavior not observed in diffusion models."
- **Break condition:** If a single detector configuration achieves >95% accuracy across all tested MLLMs and prompt variations, the generalization problem is solved. If prompt enrichment no longer degrades detection for MLLMs, the dynamic evasion vector is closed.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Diffusion Model Mechanics
- **Why needed here:** To understand the baseline paradigm (iterative denoising, CLIP-guided) against which MLLMs are contrasted, and why its failure modes (prompt misunderstanding) lead to artificially low unsafe scores.
- **Quick check question:** What architectural aspect of diffusion models typically causes them to produce "text-like" or corrupted images on complex prompts?

### Concept: Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) Architecture
- **Why needed here:** Central to the paper's thesis; their unified autoregressive framework and visual tokenization enable semantic parsing and coherent generation from rich prompts, directly causing the observed risks.
- **Quick check question:** How does decoupling visual encoding for understanding vs. generation (as in Janus) potentially affect safety profile compared to a fully unified model like Bagel?

### Concept: Safety Evaluation Metrics (Unsafe Score, Detection Accuracy)
- **Why needed here:** The paper's core comparisons rely on these quantitative measures; understanding their definitions (proportion of NSFW images, classifier accuracy) is crucial to interpret results.
- **Quick check question:** Why might the "unsafe score" for diffusion models be artificially depressed on certain prompts, and how does the paper attempt to control for this?

### Concept: Detector Training Paradigms (Single-source vs. Multi-source Fine-tuning)
- **Why needed here:** Explains why existing detectors fail and what data is needed to fix them; the negative transfer result is a key finding about MLLM heterogeneity.
- **Quick check question:** What is the likely outcome if you fine-tune a detector on images from only VILA-U and then test it on Janus Pro?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Generative Models (2 Diffusion: SD3.5 Large, SD3.5 Large Turbo; 5 MLLMs: Bagel, Janus, Janus Pro, TokenFlow, VILA-U) -> Prompt Datasets (Unsafe: Lexica, 4chan, Template, I2P, TemplateLong[Chinese]; Benign: MSCOCO, Flickr30k + enriched v1/v2) -> Safety Classifier (Moderation API, validated with human labels) / Fake Detectors (Commercial: Winston.Ai, Illuminarty; Open-source: DE-FAKE, AIorNot-SigLIP2) -> Evaluation Pipeline (Prompt → Model (safeguards disabled) → Image → Classifier/Detector → Score)

### Critical path
1. **Unsafe Generation:** Select unsafe prompt subset → Generate 10 images per model per prompt → Score via Moderation API → Compute per-prompt unsafe score → Aggregate & compare across models/datasets.
2. **Fake Detection:** Sample benign prompts → Generate 1 image per model → Run through detectors → Compute accuracy per model-detector pair → Fine-tune/train detectors on paradigm-inclusive data → Re-evaluate → Test prompt extension effect (v0→v1→v2).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Internal vs. External Safeguards:** Paper disables model-internal filters to measure base model propensity. Tradeoff: measures inherent risk but not real-world deployed risk.
- **Detector Choice:** Mix of commercial (real-world relevance) and open-source (adaptable) provides breadth but adds complexity in interpreting performance gaps (may reflect training data vs. inherent detectability).
- **Prompt Augmentation:** Manual creation of TemplateLong/v1/v2 introduces subjectivity but is necessary to test semantic richness effects beyond existing datasets.

### Failure signatures
- **High distortion rate** in diffusion model outputs on complex prompts → indicates prompt comprehension failure, not safety alignment.
- **Sharp accuracy drop** for MLLMs on enriched prompts → signals detector brittleness to semantic realism.
- **Poor cross-model generalization** after single-source fine-tuning → reveals MLLM-specific fingerprints; detector is overfitting.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate RQ1 core:** For TemplateLong dataset, generate 10 images per model (7) per prompt (30) → label with Moderation API → compute mean unsafe score. Expect MLLMs > 0.3, diffusion < 0.3. Verify distortion rate difference via human labeling of 50 random prompts.
2. **Prompt extension replication:** Use v0/v1/v2 prompts (20 from MSCOCO) → generate from 1 MLLM (e.g., Janus) and 1 diffusion (SD3.5 Large) → run trained-from-scratch DE-FAKE → record accuracy drop. Expect significant drop for MLLM, near-zero for diffusion.
3. **Single-source fine-tuning test:** Create balanced dataset: 200 real + 200 images from *one* MLLM (e.g., Bagel). Fine-tune DE-FAKE. Test on all 7 models' images (14,000 from main paper). Expect high accuracy on Bagel (>0.9) but low on other MLLMs (~0.6) and diffusion (~0.7), confirming negative transfer.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the higher unsafe generation propensity of MLLMs generalize across a wider range of model architectures and training configurations beyond the seven models evaluated? (Basis: Section 5 acknowledges limited model coverage; needs broader evaluation.)
2. To what extent do MLLMs' multilingual capabilities translate into unsafe generation risks in languages beyond Chinese, particularly for low-resource or culturally specific prompts? (Basis: Chinese test suggests cross-lingual risk but other languages unexplored.)
3. Can fake image detectors be designed to generalize effectively across diverse MLLM architectures without requiring per-model retraining or massive paradigm-inclusive datasets? (Basis: Single-source fine-tuning shows strong negative transfer; need architecture-agnostic features.)
4. Is there a saturation point in prompt richness beyond which MLLM-generated images cease to become harder to detect, or does detection difficulty increase indefinitely? (Basis: v0→v2 shows accuracy drop but plateau possible; need further enrichment study.)

## Limitations
- The Moderation API is a black-box safety labeler and may misclassify distorted images, conflating artifacts with semantic safety.
- Manual prompt enrichment (v1/v2) introduces subjectivity, potentially limiting the generalizability of the prompt richness effect.
- Detector evaluation is limited to four models; other architectures could exhibit different artifact patterns, affecting the observed generalization gaps.

## Confidence
- MLLMs generate more unsafe content: **High**
- MLLM outputs are harder to detect, especially with rich prompts: **Medium-High**
- Fine-tuned detectors generalize poorly across MLLMs: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the unsafe assessment using an alternative open-source safety classifier (e.g., `clip-NSFW`) to test robustness to labeling variance.
2. Quantify the visual artifact shift between diffusion and MLLM images using feature-space distances (e.g., DINOv2 embeddings) to validate the detection gap mechanism.
