# Prompt Injection as Role Confusion

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.12277
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12277
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Prompt injection exploits role confusion: models assign roles based on textual style rather than source tags; CoT Forgery achieves 60% average success on StrongREJECT and 61% on agent exfiltration.

## Executive Summary
This paper demonstrates that the fundamental vulnerability behind prompt injection attacks is role confusion: models assign roles based on textual style rather than the structural tags that define message sources. The authors develop linear role probes to measure internal role perception and introduce CoT Forgery, an attack that injects fabricated chain-of-thought reasoning mimicking a privileged role. Across six frontier models, CoT Forgery attains 60% average success on the StrongREJECT jailbreak benchmark and 61% on an agent exfiltration task, while baseline attacks succeed at most 4%. Critically, the degree of internal role confusion—quantified as CoTness or Userness via the probes—predicts attack success before generation, with success rates climbing from single-digit percentages to over 90% across quantiles. This establishes that style dominates tags in the model’s latent space, rendering interface-level security boundaries ineffective.

## Method Summary
The authors train layer‑specific linear classifiers (role probes) on hidden states of neutral text wrapped in different role tags (system, user, chain‑of‑thought, assistant, tool). The probes learn to predict the role solely from tag‑induced geometric shifts, enabling measurement of role perception on arbitrary text. To evaluate prompt injection, they devise CoT Forgery: an auxiliary language model generates a fake chain‑of‑thought in the target model’s style, which is appended to a harmful query and submitted as a single user message or embedded in tool output. Attack success is judged by an LLM evaluator on the StrongREJECT benchmark and a Wikipedia‑based agent exfiltration scenario. The probe‑derived CoTness/Userness scores for the injected content correlate strongly with success rates, confirming the role confusion hypothesis.

## Key Results
- Role probes reveal that models misassign roles based on textual style rather than source tags, effectively overriding architectural boundaries.
- CoT Forgery achieves 60% average success on StrongREJECT and 61% on agent exfiltration across six frontier models, while baseline attacks remain ≤4%.
- Success rates increase monotonically with probe‑measured CoTness/Userness, rising from single digits to >90%, demonstrating the predictive power of internal role confusion.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Models process text in a continuous latent space where role identity is not dictated solely by structural tags (e.g., `<system>`, `<user>`) but by stylistic features. When a prompt mimics the style of a privileged role—such as chain‑of‑thought reasoning—the model’s internal representations shift toward that role’s subspace. This geometric overlap causes the injected content to be treated as if it originated from the privileged role, thereby bypassing intended security boundaries and leading to harmful compliance or exfiltration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Role tags**: Special markers (e.g., system, user, assistant, CoT, tool) that define message source and purpose in chat templates.
  *Why needed*: They are the intended mechanism for separating privileges; confusion arises when style overrides them.
  *Quick check*: Can you identify the role tags used by a given model (e.g., gpt‑oss‑20b) and explain their hierarchy?

- **Latent space**: The high‑dimensional internal representations of tokens; roles occupy distinct subspaces.
  *Why needed*: Role confusion is a geometric phenomenon; probes operate here to measure perception.
  *Quick check*: If you average hidden states of tokens from the same role, do they cluster? How would you test this empirically?

- **Linear probes**: Simple classifiers (e.g., logistic regression) trained on hidden states to predict an attribute; used here for role diagnosis.
  *Why needed*: They quantify role separation and allow measuring role perception on arbitrary text without altering model behavior.
  *Quick check*: Train a probe on hidden states from role‑tagged neutral text. What validation accuracy indicates successful role separation?

- **CoT Forgery**: An attack that generates a fake chain‑of‑thought in the target model’s style and injects it into a user prompt or tool output.
  *Why needed*: It exploits the model’s tendency to trust stylistic cues over tags, achieving high attack success.
  *Quick check*: Given a harmful query, how would you generate a forged CoT? What style elements must be mimicked?

- **CoTness / Userness**: Scores from role probes indicating how strongly a text fragment activates the chain‑of‑thought or user role subspace.
  *Why needed*: They serve as continuous predictors of attack success, enabling pre‑generation risk assessment.
  *Quick check*: If a piece of text has high CoTness, what does that imply about its role classification according to the model?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Neutral text → Role tagging wrapper → Model → Layer-wise hidden states → Linear role probes → Role probabilities.  
Attack pipeline: Harmful query + Auxiliary LLM (style mimicry) → Forged CoT → User message/tool output → Target model → CoTness measurement → Response → Success judgment.

**Critical Path**  
1. Prepare a neutral text dataset with constant content but different role tags.  
2. Train per‑layer linear probes to predict role from hidden states.  
3. Validate probe separation on held‑out neutral text and real conversations.  
4. Generate forged CoT for attack queries using an auxiliary LLM.  
5. Submit attack payloads to the target model; record full transcripts.  
6. Apply probes to compute CoTness/Userness and correlate with LLM‑judged success.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Linear probes trade expressivity for simplicity and interpretability; yet they achieve high accuracy, suggesting near‑linear separability of roles.  
- Focus on chat models with CoT reasoning limits generalizability but aligns with frontier systems.  
- Using an auxiliary LLM for forgery introduces variability but demonstrates robustness; however, exact prompting strategies are not standardized.  
- Benchmarks (StrongREJECT, agent exfiltration) cover harmful compliance and tool misuse but may not capture all injection vectors.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Probe accuracy <90% on held‑out neutral text indicates contamination (e.g., position or content artifacts).  
- Low ASR despite high CoTness suggests the model ignores CoT style or the forged CoT is ineffective; check destyling.  
- In agent exfiltration, high Userness but no exfiltration attempt points to tool‑loop filtering or sandbox issues.  
- Destyled forgeries retaining high CoTness mean style markers were not fully removed; refine auxiliary prompt.

**Three First Experiments**  
1. Train role probes on an accessible open model (e.g., gpt‑oss‑20b) and report layer‑wise role prediction accuracy; confirm high CoTness for genuine CoT in real conversations.  
2. Implement CoT Forgery for 50 StrongREJECT queries using GPT‑4o as auxiliary; compute ASR and average CoTness; compare with a destyled baseline.  
3. Bin attacks by CoTness quantile and plot success rate to reproduce the monotonic correlation (Figure 9).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Role probe diagnostics for defenses**: How can role probes empirically test whether defensive interventions reshape the model’s latent role geometry rather than merely adding superficial patterns? Current defenses are evaluated behaviorally; it is unclear if they address the deep representational flaw.  
2. **Unifying injection families**: Do all families of prompt injection attacks (beyond CoT Forgery and agent hijacking) exploit the same underlying role confusion mechanism? Only specific attack types have been tested; other vectors (multimodal, long‑context) may differ.  
3. **Architectural separation**: What architectural or training modifications can enforce clean separation of role representations in latent space, preventing style from overriding explicit tags? No existing method reliably achieves this.  
4. **Real‑time detection**: Can discrepancies between the architecturally intended role (from parsing) and the probe‑measured role serve as an effective real‑time detector of prompt injection? This concept remains untested for reliability, timeliness, and generalizability.

## Limitations
- Chat template specifics (special tokens, nesting) vary across models and are often undocumented, risking probe training on artifacts rather than true role geometry.
- Access to frontier models (GPT‑5 family, o4‑mini) is limited; reported ASRs rely on best‑effort API calls with unknown generation parameters.
- The geometric interpretation of style dominating tags is inferred from probe behavior, not direct mechanistic evidence.

## Confidence
- Core role‑confusion vulnerability: Medium
- CoT Forgery attack effectiveness (open models): Medium
- Predictive power of CoTness/Userness: Medium
- Geometric flaw (style over tags): Low

## Next Checks
1. **Probe robustness to template variations**: Train probes on multiple open models with deliberately altered chat templates (different tag positions, added system prompts) and measure consistency of role separation.
2. **Auxiliary prompt ablation**: Standardize a set of style examples from genuine CoT; systematically vary auxiliary LLM instructions (e.g., “mimic style exactly” vs. “remove stylistic markers”) and record effects on CoTness and ASR.
3. **Cross‑architecture generalisation**: Apply role probes to models with different architectures (decoder‑only vs. encoder‑decoder) and to both instruction‑tuned and base models; test whether the style‑over‑tag pattern holds uniformly.
