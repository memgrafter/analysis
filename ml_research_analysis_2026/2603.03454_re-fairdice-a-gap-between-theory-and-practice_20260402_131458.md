# [Re] FairDICE: A Gap Between Theory And Practice
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03454
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03454
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: FairDICE is theoretically sound but its original continuous implementation contained a broadcasting bug that reduced it to behavior cloning and hid sensitivity to β; after fixing, it scales to high-dimensional objectives but requires careful hyperparameter tuning, contradicting the claimed stability.

## Executive Summary
FairDICE is an offline multi‑objective reinforcement learning algorithm that learns a linear weight vector to promote fair distribution of rewards, building on the OptiDICE framework. This replication study confirms the theoretical soundness of the method but uncovers critical implementation flaws and underspecifications that undermine the original experimental validation.

A broadcasting error in the continuous environment implementation caused the algorithm to ignore the learned weights, effectively reducing FairDICE to pure behavior cloning and masking sensitivity to the regularization parameter β. After correcting the bug and clarifying missing hyperparameters, we verified the core claims in discrete tasks: FairDICE achieves higher Nash Social Welfare and Jain’s fairness than utilitarian baselines. In continuous MuJoCo benchmarks, the method scales to up to 100 objectives and image observations, attaining competitive normalized NSW on D4MORL. However, performance varies strongly with β and requires careful online tuning, contradicting the paper’s assertion of wide stability. The idea remains promising, but the experimental validation needs substantial revision.

## Method Summary
FairDICE extends the OptiDICE offline RL algorithm to multi‑objective settings by introducing a learnable preference weight vector µ. It incorporates a non‑linear fairness utility u_i (e.g., logarithm) via a regularization term added to the DICE loss, encouraging µ to align with the gradient of the fairness objective. The algorithm learns a critic ν(s) to estimate state values, computes stationary distribution correction weights w*(s,a) = max{0, f'^{-1}(e(s,a)/β)} using a divergence f (e.g., Soft‑χ²), and updates the policy π through weighted behavioral cloning with these weights (normalized per batch). All learning is performed purely offline from a static dataset, with no environment interaction.

## Key Results
- A broadcasting bug in the continuous implementation (outer product between w* and log_probs) caused FairDICE to ignore learned weights, reducing it to behavior cloning and obscuring β sensitivity.
- Discrete experiments confirm the theoretical claims: FairDICE attains higher Nash Social Welfare and Jain’s fairness than utilitarian baselines (BC(P), MODT(P), MORvS(P)) on MO‑FourRooms.
- After fixing, continuous MuJoCo benchmarks show FairDICE scales to 100‑objective rewards and image observations, achieving competitive Normalized NSW on D4MORL; however, performance is highly sensitive to β and necessitates careful hyperparameter tuning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Preference vector learning via regularizer
- Claim: FairDICE learns a linear weight vector µ that promotes fair distribution of rewards (e.g., high Nash Social Welfare) while maximizing return.
- Mechanism: A regularization term ∑_i (u_i(k_i*) - µ_i k_i*) is added to the DICE loss, where k_i* = (u_i')^{-1}(µ_i) and u_i is a non-linear transformation (e.g., log). This encourages the Lagrangian multipliers µ to align with the gradient of the non-linear fairness objective.
- Core assumption: The non-linear utility u_i can be expressed as a function of the expected return under the policy; the regularization effectively optimizes the sum of u_i(J_i(π)).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "learning linear weights through a regularizer that promotes fair distribution of rewards"
  - [section] 2.2: Describes the full loss L(ν,µ) including the regularizer term.
- Break condition: If the regularization strength β is too high, the optimizer collapses to imitating the behavior policy; if µ is initialized poorly or the sign of the term is wrong (as in a noted typo), the mechanism fails and reduces to behavior cloning.

### Mechanism 2: DICE-based stationary distribution correction
- Claim: FairDICE uses the DICE framework to estimate state-action visitation weights without requiring online interaction, enabling offline RL.
- Mechanism: Learn a critic ν(s) and define e(s,a) = ∑_i µ_i r_i + γ ν(s') - ν(s). Then compute w*(s,a) = max{0, f'^{-1}(e(s,a)/β)} using a divergence f (e.g., Soft-χ²). These weights approximate the ratio between the stationary distribution of the target policy and the dataset distribution.
- Core assumption: The dataset is sufficiently large and diverse to approximate expectations; the learned ν accurately estimates state values; the choice of f yields valid corrections.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "building on the OptiDICE framework"
  - [section] 2.1: Explains OptiDICE as an offline RL algorithm using stationary distribution correction.
- Break condition: If the dataset lacks coverage of important state-actions, the estimated w* may be degenerate; if the broadcasting error in continuous environments omitted w* from the policy loss, this mechanism becomes inert.

### Mechanism 3: Weighted behavioral cloning
- Claim: The policy is trained via weighted behavior cloning using the corrections w*(s,a), which should yield a policy that optimally balances the fairness regularizer and the data constraints.
- Mechanism: The policy loss is L(π) = -E_{(s,a)~D}[w*(s,a) log π(a|s)]. The weights w* are normalized to have mean 1 per batch. This is analogous to importance sampling but with stationary distribution corrections.
- Core assumption: The dataset distribution contains optimal or near-optimal actions; the weights accurately reflect which actions should be amplified; the policy class can represent the desired optimal policy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] 2.2: Equation (7) and surrounding text.
  - [section] 3.3.1: Describes the loss implementation.
- Break condition: The broadcasting bug multiplies w* (shape [batch,1]) with log_probs (shape [batch]) producing an outer product, effectively setting all weights equal and collapsing to standard BC. This disconnects the policy from the learned ν and µ.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Dataset D of (s, a, r, s') tuples; Critic network ν: MLP mapping state to scalar value; Preference vector µ: trainable vector of length number of objectives; Policy network π: MLP generating action distribution (Gaussian for continuous, categorical for discrete); Loss calculators; Optimizer: Adam with cosine schedule for π, constant for ν/µ.

**Critical path**:
1. Sample minibatch from D.
2. Compute e(s,a,r,s') = ∑_i µ_i r_i + γ ν(s') - ν(s).
3. Compute w*(s,a) = max{0, f'^{-1}(e(s,a)/β)} and normalize to mean 1 per batch.
4. Compute critic loss L(ν,µ) = (1-γ)E[ν(start)] + E[w* e - β f(w*)] + ∑_i (u_i(k_i*) - µ_i k_i*).
5. Update ν and µ via gradient descent.
6. Policy loss L(π) = -E[w* log π(a|s)].
7. Update π via gradient descent.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- β: trade‑off between following the data and optimizing rewards; too high collapses to BC, too low may overfit to dataset outliers.
- α (via u_i): governs fairness type; 0 yields utilitarian, ∞ yields max‑min.
- Model capacity: Larger networks may capture complex value functions but risk overfitting; paper uses 3‑4 layers of 512‑768 units.
- Normalization: reward min‑max ensures non‑negative returns for log; state normalization stabilizes critic training.

**Failure signatures**:
- Broadcasting bug in continuous code: shapes of w* (batch,1) and log_probs (batch) mismatched, producing outer product → policy performance equals BC regardless of β.
- Sign error in regularizer (u_i negated): leads to trivial µ (all zeros) and uniform w*; NSW collapses.
- High variance in NSW across seeds: may indicate insufficient dataset coverage or too large β.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Reproduce discrete MO‑FourRooms results (Sec. 4.1.1) with tabular policy and MOSEK solver, sweeping α∈{0,0.5,1.0,1.25} and β∈[10⁻⁴,10²], evaluate NSW, Utilitarian, Jain’s fairness.
2. Run continuous D4MORL benchmark (Sec. 4.2.2) for fixed β values, using MLP critic and Gaussian policy (3‑4 layers, 512‑768 units), normalize rewards and states, train 10 seeds for 10k steps, 100 evaluation rollouts per seed.
3. Perform hyperparameter sweep over β in continuous tasks (Sec. 4.2.1), confirm sensitivity with Kruskal‑Wallis test across seeds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the FairDICE weight-learning mechanism be integrated into alternative offline RL algorithms (e.g., IQL, CQL) to achieve reduced hyperparameter sensitivity, and does such integration indeed yield stabler performance compared to the original DICE-based approach?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Future work could investigate the application of a similarly learnable linearisation to other offline RL algorithms (e.g. Kostrikov et al., 2021; Kumar et al., 2020) to see if this yields stabler results than with DICE.
- Why unresolved: The current FairDICE implementation exhibits high sensitivity to the regularization parameter β, limiting its applicability in purely offline settings where online hyperparameter tuning is impossible. It is unknown whether the core idea of learning linear weights via a regularizer can be decoupled from the DICE framework to improve robustness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Re-implement the FairDICE regularization loss within other offline RL algorithms, conduct systematic experiments on the same benchmarks (e.g., D4MORL) comparing performance stability across hyperparameter sweeps. Successful resolution would show a consistent reduction in sensitivity (e.g., flatter performance curves) without sacrificing fairness.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What data-driven or theoretical guidelines can be established for selecting the regularization strength β in FairDICE, enabling reliable performance without online tuning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] the corrected algorithm is highly sensitive to the regularisation strength β, with no clear pattern for how to select it across environments — undermining the idea that FairDICE can be applied without hyperparameter tuning (which is necessarily online).
- Why unresolved: β directly controls the trade-off between maximizing the fair non-linear objective and staying close to the behavior policy, but its optimal value appears to vary wildly across tasks. Without a method to set β from offline data alone, FairDICE cannot be confidently deployed in real-world offline settings.
- What evidence would resolve it: A comprehensive empirical study correlating optimal β with measurable dataset properties (e.g., state/action dimensionality, reward correlation, coverage metrics) might yield heuristic rules. Alternatively, a meta-learning approach that predicts β from offline statistics could be developed and validated.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Under what conditions of data bias and coverage can FairDICE successfully learn fair policies, and what are the fundamental limits of its ability to correct for skewed offline datasets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors tested FairDICE on datasets with varying preference weight distributions and on a custom biased environment (MO-GroupFair). They found that while FairDICE can partially mitigate imbalance, it fails when training data is highly biased (e.g., trajectories only from policies strongly favoring a subset of objectives). The paper states: "FairDICE finds (relatively) fair policies when given a mixture of biased and unbiased data, but fails to do so when trained on a highly biased dataset."
- Why unresolved: Real-world offline data is often biased; understanding the boundaries of FairDICE’s robustness is crucial. The current experiments are limited in scope and do not provide a clear characterization of the necessary coverage requirements.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments that parametrically vary the degree and type of bias (e.g., preference concentration, missing objectives, covariate shift) across several environments, measuring fairness metrics. A theoretical analysis deriving sufficient conditions on the data distribution for FairDICE to recover the optimal fair policy would also be decisive.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Does the gradient penalty term on the critic (present in the original code but omitted from the paper) have any meaningful effect on the performance or stability of FairDICE after the policy loss bug is fixed, and what is its intended purpose within the DICE framework?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The public code includes an additional gradient penalty on the critic that was not described. The authors said it was to incentivize smoothness, but its impact was unclear because the policy loss was broken. After fixing, the replication team tested a few values and observed only minor effects, but the question remains open.
- Why unresolved: The term was added based on prior work (Kim et al., 2021; Gulrajani et al., 2017) but its specific role in FairDICE has never been systematically evaluated. It might be crucial for stability when the critic actively shapes the policy, but its effect could also be negligible or even harmful.
- What evidence would resolve it: An ablation study varying the penalty coefficient λ over a wide range, measuring training dynamics (gradient norms, convergence speed), final NSW, and sensitivity to other hyperparameters. If the term is beneficial, we should see improved stability or performance for certain λ; if not, it can be removed to simplify the algorithm.

## Limitations
- Implementation bugs: A broadcasting error in continuous environments and a sign error in the regularizer can cause collapse to behavior cloning if not fixed.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity: β strongly influences performance; the original claim of stability across a wide range was based on the broken implementation. Optimal β varies across tasks and often requires online tuning.
- Generalization: Evaluation on diverse biased datasets is limited; the method’s robustness to data coverage and covariate shift remains unclear.

## Confidence
