# Optimization of Edge Directions and Weights for Mixed Guidance Graphs in Lifelong Multi-Agent Path Finding

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23468
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23468
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Mixed Guidance Graph Optimization (MGGO) jointly optimizes edge directions and weights, achieving up to 2.5× higher throughput than prior GGO methods on benchmark LMAPF problems.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the soft guidance limitation in Guidance Graph Optimization (GGO) for Lifelong Multi-Agent Path Finding (LMAPF), where high edge weights only discourage but do not prevent head-on collisions. It introduces Mixed Guidance Graph Optimization (MGGO), which optimizes both edge directions (hard constraints) and weights (soft guidance). Two MGGO methods are proposed: a two-phase evolutionary approach (MGGO-DS) and a Quality Diversity-based joint optimization (Joint MGGO-PU) using a neural network. Edge Reversal Search (ERS) efficiently enforces strong connectivity. Experiments on warehouse and random maps with RHCR and PIBT planners show MGGO significantly outperforms GGO-DS and human-designed baselines, with Joint MGGO-PU reaching 5.11 ± 0.01 throughput on warehouse-33-36 with RHCR versus 2.07 ± 0.03 for GGO-DS.

## Method Summary
The authors extend GGO to MGGO by adding unidirectional edges, which prohibit opposite-direction traversal and thus prevent head-on collisions. Two optimization pipelines are presented:
1. **Two-phase MGGO-DS**: First, a (1+λ) evolutionary algorithm optimizes edge directions (unweighted graph) with all edges initially bidirected; ERS repairs connectivity. Second, GGO-DS (CMA-ES) optimizes edge weights for the best direction graph.
2. **Joint MGGO-PU**: A CNN-based update model generates both edge directions and weights from precomputed traffic patterns. Quality Diversity (CMA-MAE) optimizes the CNN parameters to maximize throughput while encouraging diversity in unidirectional edge ratios and minimizing ERS repairs (via a regularization term). After generation, ERS ensures strong connectivity.
Both methods evaluate candidate graphs by running LMAPF simulations (RHCR or PIBT) and measuring throughput. ERS identifies strongly connected components via Tarjan’s algorithm and greedily reverses a subset of outgoing edges from source components until the graph becomes strongly connected; bridges are kept bidirected.

## Key Results
- MGGO methods (MGGO-DS and Joint MGGO-PU) achieve significantly higher throughput than GGO-DS and human-designed Directed Crisscross graphs across various maps and planners.
- QD + Joint MGGO-PU achieves 5.11 ± 0.01 throughput on warehouse-33-36 with RHCR, compared to 2.07 ± 0.03 for GGO-DS.
- For PIBT on non-biconnected maps (e.g., random-32-32-20), optimizing edge directions notably reduces rotation actions and improves throughput.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Hard Collision Avoidance via Edge Directions
- **Claim:** Edge directions provide *hard constraints* that prohibit head-on collisions, whereas edge weights offer only *soft guidance* that can be overridden by cost minimization.
- **Mechanism:** In GGO, agents may still traverse high-weight edges if it yields a lower overall path cost, causing head-on collisions. MGGO introduces unidirectional edges, making opposite-direction traversal impossible on those edges, thus strictly preventing such collisions.
- **Core assumption:** The throughput loss from occasional detours on unidirectional edges is outweighed by the gain from avoiding frequent head-on collision resolution.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states high weights only discourage but do not prohibit edge use, causing head-on collisions. Section 1 notes that resolving head-on collisions is challenging and time-consuming. Prior work (GGO) established the soft-guidance limitation.
- **Break condition:** Excessive unidirectional edges may force agents into long detours, especially in non-biconnected graphs where bridges must remain bidirected.

### Mechanism 2: Efficient Strong Connectivity via Edge Reversal Search (ERS)
- **Claim:** ERS efficiently enforces strong connectivity in mixed guidance graphs by greedily reversing edge directions, avoiding the computational cost of optimal MILP-based methods.
- **Mechanism:** ERS uses Tarjan’s algorithm to identify strongly connected components (SCCs). It then reverses a sampled subset (≈ half) of outgoing edges from source SCCs in the condensation DAG, iteratively reducing the number of SCCs until strong connectivity is achieved. Bridge edges are preemptively forced bidirectional.
- **Core assumption:** Reversing a subset of outgoing edges from a source SCC reliably merges SCCs without requiring globally optimal reversals.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.1 details the algorithm and proves that if a source SCC has at least two outgoing edges, at least one reversal will occur. Appendix A.2 shows ERS is orders of magnitude faster than MILP with 100% success rate, though it reverses more edges.
- **Break condition:** If many source SCCs have few outgoing edges, ERS may need many iterations; it does not guarantee minimal reversals.

### Mechanism 3: Joint Optimization via Quality Diversity
- **Claim:** Joint optimization of edge directions and weights via Quality Diversity (QD) with a neural update model improves sample efficiency and generalizability compared to two-phase optimization.
- **Mechanism:** QD (CMA-MAE) optimizes a neural network that generates both edge directions and weights from precomputed traffic patterns. The objective combines throughput (f_res) and a regularization term (Δ) that rewards edge directions requiring fewer ERS repairs, encouraging inherently strongly connected graphs. A diversity measure (ratio of unidirectional edges) explores trade-offs between collision avoidance and detour costs.
- **Core assumption:** Traffic patterns from human-designed directed Crisscross graphs provide sufficient prior knowledge about beneficial directional flows; diversity in unidirectional edge ratios leads to robust solutions across agent densities.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.3 details the update model, objectives, and diversity measure. Section 5.2 ablations show QD-based Joint MGGO-PU outperforms standalone CMA-ES and independent edge-direction representations.
- **Break condition:** Poor choice of diversity measure or insufficient traffic pattern diversity may misguide QD; the approach is computationally heavier per iteration than two-phase methods.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Traffic Pattern Collector -> Optimizer (MGGO-DS: (1+λ) EA; Joint: CMA-MAE) -> Graph Generator (direct encoding for MGGO-DS; CNN for Joint) -> ERS Module -> LMAPF Simulator -> feedback to Optimizer

**Critical path:**  
1. Traffic Patterns are collected (for Joint) via *Nobs* simulations on baseline graphs.  
2. Optimizer samples candidate solutions: MGGO-DS directly encodes edge directions/weights; Joint MGGO-PU generates them via CNN from traffic patterns.  
3. Candidate mixed graph is repaired by ERS to ensure strong connectivity.  
4. LMAPF simulator (RHCR or PIBT) runs multiple repetitions to evaluate throughput.  
5. Objective and diversity scores are computed and returned to optimizer to update parameters/solutions. Iterate until evaluation budget exhausted.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Two-phase MGGO-DS: More interpretable and faster per iteration but risks suboptimal local maxima due to decoupled direction/weight optimization.  
- Joint MGGO-PU: Potentially better joint solutions but higher computational cost per iteration; sensitive to diversity measure design and traffic pattern quality.  
- ERS vs. MILP: ERS is fast and scalable but may reverse unnecessary edges; MILP finds minimal reversals but times out on large graphs.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low throughput despite optimization: Check if ERS reversed most edges (excessive unidirectional), if update model outputs invalid directions (e.g., missing bridges), or if traffic patterns are uninformative.  
- High variance across runs: Indicates unstable optimization; may need more evaluations or adjusted mutation operators (for EA) or archive size (for QD).  
- Poor generalization to unseen agent counts: Suggests diversity measure is inadequate; the ratio of unidirectional edges may not capture all relevant traffic regimes.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Run QD + Joint MGGO-PU and two-phase MGGO-DS on `warehouse-33-36` with RHCR; confirm throughput >5.0 vs. GGO-DS ~2.07 (Table 3, Setup 2).  
2. **ERS validation:** Generate random directed grid graphs (various sizes) and compare ERS vs. MILP (as in Appendix A.2); expect ERS to succeed always but reverse more edges.  
3. **Ablation check:** For Joint MGGO-PU, replace QD with standalone CMA-ES; expect lower throughput (Table 2) showing QD’s benefit.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Sample Efficiency of Joint Optimization:** How can the sample efficiency of jointly optimizing edge directions and weights be improved to consistently outperform weight-only GGO? The paper notes MGGO does not consistently beat all GGO variants due to reduced sample efficiency from increased complexity; future work may use surrogate modeling.
2. **Planner Sensitivity to Edge Directions:** Why do edge directions provide greater throughput benefits for rule-based LMAPF planners (e.g., PIBT) compared to search-based planners (e.g., RHCR)? The interaction between guidance graph topology and planner decision-making is not analyzed.
3. **Optimal Strong Connectivity Repair:** Can we design a strong connectivity repair algorithm that achieves near-optimal edge reversal counts while maintaining ERS’s low overhead? ERS is fast but reverses significantly more edges than an optimal MILP; the trade-off between reversal count and throughput impact is unresolved.
4. **Impact of Traffic Patterns and Architecture:** What is the impact of traffic pattern diversity and neural network architecture choices on QD-based joint MGGO? The paper uses fixed CNN architecture and traffic patterns from only two initial graphs; other designs remain unexplored.

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to two LMAPF algorithms (RHCR, PIBT) and a few benchmark maps; unknown if results generalize to other planners or real-world scenarios.
- Hyperparameters (e.g., EA λ, CMA-ES/CMA-MAE settings) and traffic pattern collection details are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- ERS is a greedy heuristic with no guarantee of minimal edge reversals; its impact on path quality remains under-analyzed.
