# Hierarchical Concept-based Interpretable Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23947
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23947
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: HiCEMs reduce annotation burden while enabling multi-granularity interventions with competitive task accuracy and high concept accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Hierarchical Concept Embedding Models (HiCEMs) and Concept Splitting to overcome key limitations of standard Concept Embedding Models (CEMs). CEMs fail to capture relationships between concepts and require expensive fine-grained annotations. Concept Splitting automatically discovers human-interpretable sub-concepts from high-level concept labels using sparse autoencoders applied to a pretrained CEM's embedding space. HiCEMs explicitly model hierarchies between parent concepts and their sub-concepts via separate embedding pathways and a soft-max aggregation. Evaluations across multiple datasets and a user study show that Concept Splitting yields high-accuracy sub-concepts (test ROC-AUC >0.85). HiCEMs maintain task accuracy within ~2% of CEMs, and multi-granularity interventions on discovered sub-concepts improve performance, with HiCEMs often showing larger gains than CEMs augmented with the same discovered concepts.

## Method Summary
The approach begins by training a standard CEM on high-level concept labels. For each parent concept, Concept Splitting partitions the CEM’s training embeddings into sets where the parent is predicted active versus inactive. Separate sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are trained on each partition; their learned features are thresholded to generate binary sub-concept labels. These labels are matched to an external concept bank (ROC-AUC >0.7) to ensure interpretability. The discovered sub-concepts are then integrated into a HiCEM, which contains dual modules (positive/negative) per parent concept that mix sub-concept embeddings. The parent’s probability is computed as a soft maximum over the relevant module’s sub-concept probabilities, enforcing that sub-concepts imply the parent’s presence. The model is trained with the same CEM objective, preserving task accuracy while enabling fine-grained interventions.

## Key Results
- Discovered sub-concepts achieve high predictive accuracy against a concept bank (mean test ROC-AUC >0.85) and are rated human-interpretable in a user study.
- HiCEMs maintain competitive task accuracy (within ~2% of CEMs) and preserve concept accuracy for both provided and discovered concepts.
- Interventions on discovered sub-concepts improve task accuracy; HiCEMs exhibit larger gains than CEMs with added discovered concepts on datasets like CUB and PseudoKitchens.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Concept Splitting can discover human-interpretable sub-concepts from a pretrained CEM's embedding space without additional annotations.
- Mechanism: For each parent concept, embeddings are split into positive (parent predicted active) and negative sets. A sparse autoencoder (SAE) is trained separately on each set. The SAE’s learned features become binary sub-concept labels via a thresholding procedure.
- Core assumption: The CEM's embedding space encodes finer-grained features that are linearly separable by an SAE under sparsity; these features correspond to human-understandable sub-concepts whose presence correlates with the parent’s state.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "Concept Splitting uses sparse autoencoders on a pretrained CEM's embedding space to automatically discover fine-grained sub-concepts from only high-level concept labels." [section 3] Details partitioning, SAE training, and thresholding. [corpus] Related works explore hierarchical concept discovery but do not validate this specific SAE use.
- Break condition: If the CEM’s embeddings for a parent concept are entangled (not separable into subspaces corresponding to sub-concepts), the SAE may learn uninterpretable features or fail to find meaningful activations.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: HiCEMs explicitly model hierarchical relationships between parent concepts and sub-concepts via separate embedding pathways and probability aggregation.
- Mechanism: For each parent, two modules (positive/negative) take a mixed embedding and produce embeddings weighted by sub-concept probabilities. The parent probability is computed as a soft maximum over sub-concept probabilities from the relevant module (positive when parent active, negative otherwise), ensuring sub-concept existence implies parent presence.
- Core assumption: Real-world concepts form compositional hierarchies where sub-concepts conditionally depend on parent state; modeling this with differentiable modules improves interpretability and intervention efficacy without harming task accuracy.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "HiCEMs explicitly model concept relationships through hierarchical structures." [section 4.1–4.2] Describes dual-module design, mixture, and soft-max aggregation. [corpus] "V-CEM" and "MICA" explore multi-level modeling; HiCEM’s specific hierarchical splitting is novel.
- Break condition: If the domain lacks clear hierarchical sub-concept relationships (e.g., all concepts are primitive and independent), the added complexity may overfit and reduce performance.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Multi-granularity concept interventions (on both parent and discovered sub-concepts) can improve task accuracy in HiCEMs, with sub-concept interventions yielding larger gains due to finer control.
- Mechanism: At test time, an expert corrects concept predictions. Setting a sub-concept’s probability to 1 forces its embedding to dominate the parent’s module output and updates the parent’s probability via the soft maximum, propagating the correction through the task predictor’s linear layer.
- Core assumption: Discovered sub-concepts are both human-interpretable and task-relevant; intervening on them corrects errors that would be masked or misattributed at the parent-only level.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "Interventions on these fine-grained discovered sub-concepts improve task accuracy, with HiCEMs showing larger gains than CEMs with added discovered concepts." [section 4.4] Specifies intervention rules for sub-concepts and parents. [section 5.3] Figure 4 shows task accuracy increasing with number of intervened sub-concepts, especially for HiCEMs vs. CEM+discovered.
- Break condition: If discovered sub-concepts are spurious or not causally related to the task, intervening on them will degrade task accuracy or provide no benefit.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept Embedding Models (CEMs)**: CEMs represent concepts as mixtures of learned positive and negative embeddings, and support test-time interventions by replacing embeddings. They are the baseline that HiCEMs extend.
  - Quick check: How does a CEM compute the embedding for a concept given its predicted probability, and how does this enable test-time interventions?
- **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)**: SAEs learn a sparse dictionary of features from input representations, encouraging interpretability. They are central to Concept Splitting’s discovery of sub-concepts.
  - Quick check: What role does the sparsity constraint play in encouraging SAE features to correspond to human-interpretable concepts?
- **Hierarchical Concept Representation**: Real-world concepts often have hierarchies (e.g., "vehicle" → "car", "bicycle"). Modeling such structure better mimics human reasoning and enables multi-granular control.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Pretrained CEM -> Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) -> Concept Splitting pipeline -> HiCEM backbone ψ -> Top-level embedding generators ϕ⁺, ϕ⁻ -> Sub-concept modules -> Task predictor f

### Critical path
1. Train initial CEM on high-level concepts.
2. For each concept: extract training embeddings, split by CEM’s prediction (active/inactive), train separate SAEs on each split.
3. Convert SAE features to binary sub-concept labels via activation thresholding.
4. Match discovered sub-concepts to external concept bank (ROC-AUC >0.7) for interpretability.
5. Train HiCEM with both parent and discovered sub-concept labels using the hierarchical dual-module architecture.
6. Evaluate task/concept accuracy and perform interventions by sequentially correcting concept predictions.

### Design tradeoffs
- Annotation cost vs. granularity: Concept Splitting reduces need for fine annotations but relies on quality of parent labels and CEM embeddings.
- SAE interpretability vs. computation: Larger dictionaries capture more features but may include noisy ones; BatchTopK sparsity balances reconstruction and interpretability.
- Hierarchical complexity: Dual modules increase parameters and may overfit on small datasets but explicitly model sub-concept dependencies.

### Failure signatures
- Low discovered concept ROC-AUC (<0.7) or poor user study ratings → SAE features not human-aligned.
- HiCEM task accuracy >2% below CEM baseline → hierarchical structure harming predictive performance.
- Interventions on discovered concepts degrade task accuracy → sub-concepts are spurious or misaligned with the task.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can HiCEMs be extended to model concept hierarchies beyond two levels?
- Does offering fine-grained intervention options in concept-based models improve user decision-making, or does it increase cognitive load?
- How reliable is Concept Splitting at discovering semantically meaningful and human-interpretable sub-concepts across different SAE configurations or discovery methods?

## Limitations
- Reproducibility gaps: Missing details on SAE binary thresholding (Bussmann et al.), merging of matched sub-concepts, RandInt intervention implementation, and weighted cross-entropy.
- Embedding separability assumption: Concept Splitting depends on CEM embeddings forming separable subspaces; entanglement could yield uninterpretable SAE features.
- Model complexity risk: HiCEM’s dual-module hierarchy may overfit on small datasets despite regularization.
