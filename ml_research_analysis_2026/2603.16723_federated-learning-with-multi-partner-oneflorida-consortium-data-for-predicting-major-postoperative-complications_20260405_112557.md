# Federated Learning with Multi-Partner OneFlorida+ Consortium Data for Predicting Major Postoperative Complications

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.16723
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16723
*   **Reference count:** 0
*   **Primary result:** The SCAFFOLD federated learning algorithm achieved AUROC scores of 0.73–0.97 across four postoperative complications and five hospitals, matching or exceeding central learning (pooled data) and demonstrating superior generalizability to external sites while preserving data privacy.

## Executive Summary
This study developed and validated privacy-preserving federated learning models to predict four major postoperative complications—in-hospital mortality, unplanned ICU admission, acute kidney injury (AKI), and surgical site infections—using electronic health record data from 358,644 patients across five hospitals in the OneFlorida+ consortium. By comparing the SCAFFOLD algorithm against FedAvg, FedProx, and a central learning benchmark, the research found that SCAFFOLD consistently achieved high discriminatory performance (AUROC 0.73–0.97) and generalizability to unseen hospitals, offering a robust solution for multi-institutional prediction tasks where data cannot be pooled due to privacy constraints.

## Method Summary
The study pooled preprocessed data from five partner hospitals, harmonized into 99 features. Three federated learning algorithms (FedAvg, FedProx, SCAFFOLD) and a central learning model were trained on data from three development sites and internally validated on those same sites. External validation was performed on two held-out hospitals. Model performance was assessed using AUROC and AUPRC, with particular attention to class imbalance. SCAFFOLD's control variate correction was hypothesized to mitigate performance loss from non-IID data distributions across sites.

## Key Results
*   SCAFFOLD achieved AUROC scores ranging from 0.73 to 0.97 across all four outcomes and all five development sites.
*   SCAFFOLD's performance was comparable to or superior to central learning (pooled data) and consistently outperformed FedAvg and FedProx, particularly at sites with heterogeneous data.
*   The federated SCAFFOLD model demonstrated strong generalizability to external, unseen hospitals, often matching or exceeding the performance of the best single-center (local) models when tested on other sites' data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** The SCAFFOLD algorithm mitigates performance degradation in federated learning when data distributions across hospitals are non-Independent and Identically Distributed (non-IID).
*   **Mechanism:** SCAFFOLD introduces a "control variate" correction term during local model updates. This term estimates and compensates for the directional bias introduced by each site's local data drift relative to the global model. Instead of sending raw gradients, clients send corrected updates that are more aligned with the global objective, reducing variance and speeding convergence on heterogeneous data.
*   **Core assumption:** Data heterogeneity (non-IID) across sites is a primary cause of federated learning performance gaps compared to central learning. SCAFFOLD's correction effectively accounts for this heterogeneity.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] States SCAFFOLD "consistently demonstrated comparable or superior performance... showcasing strong generalizability."
    *   [section] Results tables show SCAFFOLD often matches or exceeds Central Learning (CL) AUROC/AUPRC across sites, unlike FedAvg/FedProx which sometimes underperform CL.
*   **Break condition:** If all site data distributions were perfectly homogeneous (IID), SCAFFOLD's correction would be unnecessary, and simpler algorithms like FedAvg would perform similarly.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Federated learning preserves data privacy and security by avoiding raw data aggregation while still leveraging multi-institutional data volume.
*   **Mechanism:** Raw patient data never leaves its local hospital server. Only encrypted model parameter updates (weights/gradients) are transmitted to a central aggregator. The central server computes a weighted average (or corrected average, as in SCAFFOLD) of these updates to improve the global model, which is then redistributed. This process limits exposure to reconstruction attacks from parameter leaks, assuming secure aggregation protocols.
*   **Core assumption:** The privacy risk of sharing model updates is acceptably lower than sharing raw patient-level EHR data, given the study's threat model and compliance requirements (e.g., HIPAA).
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] Explicitly frames federated learning as "privacy-preserving" and contrasts it with central learning which "raises potential privacy concerns."
    *   [section] Methods: "Federated learning addresses these challenges by training the model locally at each center and aggregating the computed models on a central server without exchanging actual data."
*   **Break condition:** If model updates themselves can be reverse-engineered to reconstruct sensitive training data (membership inference or reconstruction attacks succeed), the privacy guarantee is violated.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Training on distributed multi-center data improves model generalizability to unseen sites compared to single-center (local) models.
*   **Mechanism:** The global model learns representations from a broader distribution of patient demographics, clinical practices, and coding patterns. This diversity acts as a regularizer, preventing overfitting to the idiosyncrasies of any single hospital's dataset. Consequently, the model's decision boundaries are more robust when applied to a new hospital's data.
*   **Core assumption:** Differences in patient populations and EHR documentation practices across sites represent meaningful diversity that, if learned, improves out-of-distribution performance.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] Claims federated models have "strong generalizability, with comparable or superior performance... compared to the best local learning model at each site."
    *   [section] Table 4/5: For external validation (new sites), SCAFFOLD's AUROC is consistently at or above the best local model's score (e.g., ICU admission: SCAFFOLD 0.77 vs best local 0.79; AKI: 0.73 vs 0.78; note some are slightly below but within CI overlap). The Discussion explicitly states local models "performed poorly at other sites."
*   **Break condition:** If all sites share nearly identical patient populations and EHR systems (low distribution shift), the benefit of multi-center training over a large single-center dataset would be minimal.

## Foundational Learning
### Non-IID Data in Federated Learning
*   **Why needed here:** The study's data (Table 1) shows significant demographic and outcome prevalence differences across Partners (e.g., Hispanic %, AKI rates). This heterogeneity (non-IID) is the core challenge SCAFFOLD addresses. An engineer must understand this to interpret why FedAvg might fail and why SCAFFOLD is chosen.
*   **Quick check question:** If Partner 3 has 15% ICU admission rate and Partner 4 has 2%, what does this imply for gradient updates during FedAvg, and how might SCAFFOLD's correction help?

### Model Aggregation Algorithms (FedAvg vs. SCAFFOLD)
*   **Why needed here:** The paper's core comparison is between these algorithms. An engineer implementing the system must know that SCAFFOLD requires maintaining and communicating an additional "server update" (control variate) per client per round, increasing communication overhead but improving convergence on skewed data.
*   **Quick check question:** What specific additional state does a client need to store to participate in SCAFFOLD versus FedAvg?

### Evaluation with Class Imbalance (AUROC vs. AUPRC)
*   **Why needed here:** Outcomes like in-hospital mortality (0.1% in Partner 4) are extremely imbalanced. AUROC can be misleadingly high; AUPRC is emphasized as a critical metric for real-world utility. An engineer must compute and report both, understanding that a drop in AUPRC despite stable AUROC can indicate poor precision on the positive (rare) class.
*   **Quick check question:** For Partner 4 in-hospital mortality (prevalence 0.1%), what would a model that always predicts "no complication" score on AUROC and AUPRC, and why?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Local Data Vault → Local Training Node → Secure Aggregator Server → Validation Orchestrator. Hyperparameter Tuning Module runs externally to optimize training parameters.

### Critical path
Data split (train/val/test per site) → Initialize global model → For each round: Server broadcasts global model → Each site: (a) load model, (b) train on local K batches (updating c_i if SCAFFOLD), (c) encrypt and send update → Server: aggregate updates → After final round: Each site evaluates final global model on local test set → Aggregate metrics centrally.

### Design tradeoffs
*   **Communication cost vs. performance:** SCAFFOLD sends larger updates (model weights + c_i), increasing bandwidth needs versus FedAvg. Tradeoff: better convergence on non-IID data.
*   **Local computation vs. privacy:** More local training epochs (E) per round reduces communication but may increase drift from global model. R parameter tuning is critical.
*   **Central validation vs. local evaluation:** The study uses local test sets for fair site-specific evaluation. A central test set is impossible due to privacy. Final generalizability is assessed on separate external sites.

### Failure signatures
*   **High variance in local model performance** (seen in Table 4: Partner 3 model on Partner 6 test has AUROC 0.74 vs 0.87 on its own test) indicates severe local overfitting to site-specific patterns.
*   **SCAFFOLD underperforming Central Learning** (CL AUROC is higher) in Tables 2/3 suggests either: (a) data is actually quite homogeneous (SCAFFOLD overhead without benefit), or (b) implementation error in the SCAFFOLD correction logic.
*   **Poor external AUPRC** (e.g., ICU admission external AUPRC 0.01) despite good AUROC indicates the model fails on the positive (rare) class in new environments—a critical failure for clinical utility.

### First 3 experiments
1.  **Baseline replication:** Implement FedAvg and SCAFFOLD (using NVFlare) on Partner 3's training data. Verify that after 100 rounds, SCAFFOLD's local loss converges faster/more stably than FedAvg on a *simulated* non-IID split (e.g., by patient age group).
2.  **Performance comparison:** Train all three FL algorithms (FedAvg, FedProx, SCAFFOLD) across all development partners (3,4,6). For each, compute internal test AUROC/AUPRC. Confirm Table 2/3 pattern: SCAFFOLD matches or beats CL, and beats FedAvg/FedProx on heterogeneous sites (e.g., Partner 6).
3.  **Generalizability check:** Take the final SCAFFOLD global model and the three best local models (one per development site). Evaluate all four on the *external* test set (Partners 1&2 combined). Verify SCAFFOLD's AUROC is not significantly worse than the best local model (from Partner 3, which had most balanced data) and that local models from Partners 4/6 fail catastrophically (low AUROC).

## Limitations
*   The study’s privacy claim is unverified—no reconstruction or membership inference attacks were tested.
*   External validation used only two sites from the same consortium, limiting confidence in broader generalizability.
*   SCAFFOLD’s role in mitigating non‑IID effects is not isolated; an ablation removing the control variate is missing.

## Confidence
*   Mechanism 1 (SCAFFOLD handles non-IID): **Medium**
*   Mechanism 2 (Privacy preservation): **Low**
*   Mechanism 3 (Generalizability): **High**

## Next Checks
1.  **Non‑IID ablation:** Simulate skewed data partitions and compare SCAFFOLD with a variant that removes the control variate updates to quantify the correction’s specific impact.
2.  **Privacy audit:** Apply state‑of‑the‑art membership inference and gradient inversion attacks to the exchanged model updates to measure potential leakage.
3.  **Broader external validation:** Test the final federated model on an independent hospital network outside the OneFlorida consortium (e.g., different US region) to evaluate robustness to distribution shift.
