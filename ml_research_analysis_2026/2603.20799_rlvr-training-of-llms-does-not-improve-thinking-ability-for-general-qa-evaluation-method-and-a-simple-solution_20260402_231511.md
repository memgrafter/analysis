# RLVR Training of LLMs Does Not Improve Thinking Ability for General QA: Evaluation Method and a Simple Solution

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv**: 2603.20799 | **URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20799 | **Refs**: 20  
- **Result**: START improves thinking win rate to 68.15%; direct RLVR fails to enhance thinking in GQA.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) can improve thinking in general QA. Cross‑generation evaluation reveals a weak coupling between thinking and answering, causing “thinking stagnation” under standard RL. To address this, the authors propose Separated Thinking And Response Training (START), which decouples training via gradient masking: Phase I updates only the thinking process, Phase II performs joint RL. On ExpertQA and UltraFeedback, START achieves a 68.15% win rate for thinking alone and a 59.24% win rate for the final model, significantly outperforming vanilla RL.

## Method Summary
Cross‑generation evaluation swaps thinking traces and answers across model sizes to isolate their contributions. START employs two‑phase RL: Phase I masks gradients on answer tokens so the reward signal shapes only thinking; Phase II proceeds with standard joint training. Experiments use Qwen3 variants, GRPO/DAPO, and hyperparameters from the paper’s tables on ExpertQA and UltraFeedback.

## Key Results
- Thinking efficacy is far lower in GQA than in verifiable tasks.
- Vanilla RLVR yields answer improvement without thinking progress (“thinking stagnation”).
- START: 68.15% win rate for thinking alone, 59.24% for full model; Phase I reward gain +0.0046.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
1. **Loose coupling**: Cross‑gen shows larger gains from better answer models than from better thinking, allowing RL to exploit answer shortcuts.
2. **Gradient masking**: Zeroing gradients on answer tokens forces reward updates to affect only thinking, compelling reasoning refinement.
3. **Two‑stage**: Phase I builds robust thinking; Phase II fine‑tunes answer extraction without degrading thinking.

## Foundational Learning
- **RLVR**: Outcome‑based RL for LLMs; used on verifiable tasks.  
  *Quick check*: How might reward sparsity in ExpertQA vs. MATH affect thinking evolution?
- **Thinking‑Answer Decoupling**: Cross‑generation swaps T/A to measure independent contributions.  
  *Quick check*: If a better thinking trace helps a strong answerer more than a weak one, what does that imply about coupling?
- **Gradient Masking**: Zeroing gradients on answer tokens to force thinking updates.  
  *Quick check*: How does masking change the optimization dynamics?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Prompt → Thinking (T) → Answer (A) → Response → Reward → Advantage → Policy (mask on A in Phase I).  
- **Critical path**: 1) Generate (T, A). 2) Compute reward. 3) Compute advantage. 4) Apply mask (Phase I). 5) Optimizer step. 6) After Phase I, remove mask and continue.  
- **Design tradeoffs**: Adds extra phase; masking may temporarily hurt answers; works with GRPO/DAPO; sensitive to KL/lr.  
- **Failure signatures**: Phase I no T_post+A_pre gain; Phase II thinking win rate drops; reward stagnates early.  
- **First 3 experiments**: 1) Cross‑gen reasoning vs. GQA coupling comparison. 2) Vanilla RL stagnation analysis via T_post+A_pre. 3) START vs. baselines (vanilla, GRPO‑MA).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What task properties cause loose coupling in GQA, and can they be manipulated to improve thinking?
2. Why does GRPO‑MA’s multi‑answer strategy fail to match START’s thinking improvements?
3. Are the increased meta‑context patterns causally responsible for better thinking or just correlated?
4. How robust is START across architectures, scales, and non‑GQA domains?

## Limitations
- Unspecified token split between thinking and answer.
- Undocumented ArmoRM input format.
- Ambiguous token alignment in cross‑generation across model sizes.
- Sparse GRPO‑MA baseline details.

## Confidence
- Thinking stagnation in GQA: **Medium**
- START effectiveness: **Medium**
- Cross‑generation decoupling insight: **Medium**
