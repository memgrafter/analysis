# TildeOpen LLM: Leveraging Curriculum Learning to Achieve Equitable Language Representation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.08182
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08182
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Trains a 30B-parameter model on 2T tokens to achieve state-of-the-art performance across 34 European languages, with up to 13.8% lower perplexity for Baltic languages and up to ten times fewer linguistic errors than Gemma 2.

## Executive Summary
TildeOpen LLM addresses the chronic underperformance of low-resource European languages in large language models caused by data imbalances. It employs a three-phase curriculum learning schedule that alternates between uniform and natural data distributions, iterative tokenizer training to ensure language-equivalent tokenization, and moderate upsampling of low-resource data. Despite a limited 2T token training budget, the model surpasses similar open-weight systems in generation and comprehension, particularly for Baltic, Finno-Ugric, and Slavic languages, while maintaining strong performance on high-resource languages.

## Method Summary
No reproduction notes are provided. Based on the paper, TildeOpen LLM trains a 30B-parameter dense transformer using a multi-stage strategy: (1) a curriculum schedule with initial uniform sampling (7.5% of tokens), intermediate natural distribution (67.5%), and final uniform sampling (25%); (2) upsampling low-resource language data up to 2.5×; and (3) an iteratively balanced tokenizer trained on a carefully composed byte-level corpus to minimize tokenization disparity across languages. The model processes 34 European languages from sources like MADLAD-400 and HPLT, with extensive filtering and a custom curriculum sampler orchestrating the mix.

## Key Results
- Achieves up to 13.8% lower per-character perplexity for Baltic languages compared to prior models.
- Produces up to ten times fewer linguistic errors than Gemma 2 in human evaluations.
- Averages 0.834 COMET score in translation, outperforming EuroLLM's 0.825.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Alternating between uniform and natural data distributions during training (curriculum learning) improves model performance on low-resource languages without sacrificing high-resource language capability.
- **Mechanism:** The training schedule is split into three phases: an initial phase (7.5% of tokens) with uniform language sampling, an intermediate phase (67.5%) with the natural skewed distribution, and a final phase (25%) returning to uniform sampling. This provides balanced exposure to all languages at the start and end, while maximizing the use of abundant high-resource data in the middle.
- **Core assumption:** Early uniform exposure establishes a baseline representation for all languages, preventing the model from being dominated by high-resource languages from the first update. The final uniform phase reinforces this balance before convergence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...a curriculum-based training schedule that alternates between uniform and natural language distributions."
  - [section 4.3] Describes the three-phase schedule and its rationale: "uniform language exposure during the initial phase... and final phase... while allowing more natural data distribution during the intermediate phase."
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers like *Code-Switching Curriculum Learning for Multilingual Transfer in LLMs* explore similar scheduling ideas but are not cited as direct validation.
- **Break condition:** If the uniform phases are too short or the natural phase too long, low-resource language performance would degrade, as the model would spend insufficient time learning from their limited upsampled data.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Iterative tokenizer training to achieve language-equivalent tokenization (similar tokens per sentence) reduces computational inefficiency and improves learning signal for low-resource languages.
- **Mechanism:** The tokenizer is trained on a meticulously balanced byte-level dataset (3B bytes for focus languages, 1.48B for others). Proportions are adjusted iteratively until parallel translations (FLORES 200) yield similar token counts across focus languages. This prevents low-resource languages from being fragmented into many tokens.
- **Core assumption:** Tokenization inefficiency (high tokens per sentence) artificially inflates the cost and reduces the effective learning signal for low-resource languages, creating a hidden resource penalty beyond raw data quantity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We achieve tokenization equity for focus languages by iteratively adjusting the data proportions during tokenizer training..."
  - [section 2] Details the iterative process and Figure 1 showing achieved tokenization equity compared to other LLMs.
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; this is a novel contribution of the paper.
- **Break condition:** If token counts remain highly unequal after adjustment, the model will still face a representation bias, as each "token" in a low-resource language carries less semantic content, requiring more model capacity to learn the same meaning.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Moderate upsampling (up to 2.5x) of low-resource language data, combined with the curriculum, mitigates raw data scarcity without causing severe overfitting or catastrophic interference.
- **Mechanism:** Low-resource language data is repeated more frequently within the training stream (Table 1 shows upsampling factors). This increases the number of optimization steps where the model sees examples from these languages, counteracting their low natural prevalence. The curriculum's uniform phases ensure this upsampled data is presented in a balanced context.
- **Core assumption:** There is a "sweet spot" for upsampling: enough to overcome data scarcity but not so high that the model memorizes limited patterns or disrupts the learning of high-resource languages.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.3] "We apply upsampling to under-represented languages up to 2.5 times."
  - [table 1] Shows the unique tokens and total tokens after upsampling, demonstrating the change in distribution.
  - [section 5.2] Memorization evaluation shows low verbatim reproduction overall, suggesting upsampling did not cause rampant memorization, though code and parallel data showed higher similarity due to structure.
- **Break condition:** If upsampling factors are too high (e.g., >5x), the model may overfit to the limited variety of low-resource data, harming generalization. If too low (<1.5x), the curriculum's uniform phases may not provide enough signal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Curriculum Learning for Data Imbalance**
  - *Why needed here:* Standard training on a natural skewed distribution would lead to a model that is expert in high-resource languages and mediocre in low-resource ones. A curriculum manually shapes the data presentation order to enforce equitable learning.
  - *Quick check question:* What would likely happen if the model were trained only on the natural distribution (no curriculum phases)?
- **Tokenization Efficiency as a Model Bias**
  - *Why needed here:* Per-token computational cost is constant, but per-sentence semantic content varies by language due to tokenization. This creates an implicit resource gap that must be corrected at the tokenizer design stage, not just during model training.
  - *Quick check question:* How does a high "tokens per sentence" ratio for a language affect its effective training budget?
- **Upsampling vs. Epochs Trade-off**
  - *Why needed here:* With fixed total tokens (2T), upsampling low-resource data means those languages are seen for more epochs, while high-resource languages are seen for fewer. This is a deliberate trade-off in a compute-constrained regime.
  - *Quick check question:* If you double the upsampling factor for a language without changing total tokens, what happens to the number of *unique* examples the model sees from that language?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Tokenizer -> Data Pipeline -> Curriculum Sampler -> Model -> Evaluation Harness

**Critical path:** Tokenizer design (fixed) → Data filtering & upsampling (static ratios) → Curriculum schedule definition (hyperparameter) → Model training (orchestrated by sampler) → Evaluation (per-language metrics)

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Upsampling factor:* Higher helps low-resource but reduces diversity of high-resource seen tokens and may cause overfitting. Fixed at 2.5x based on prior work (Muennighoff et al.).
- *Curriculum phase lengths:* Longer uniform phases improve low-resource but reduce total "natural" high-resource exposure. Chosen as 7.5% / 67.5% / 25% (150B / 1.35T / 500B tokens) as a heuristic.
- *Tokenizer vocabulary size:* Larger vocab reduces tokens per sentence but increases embedding matrix size and potential sparsity. Chose 131k as a compromise.

**Failure signatures:**
1. **Per-language perplexity disparity:** If Baltic/Finno-Ugric/Slavic perplexity remains high relative to Germanic/Romance, curriculum/upsampling insufficient.
2. **Tokenization inequality check:** Boxplot (Fig 1) shows focus languages with vastly different tokens/sentence → tokenizer training failed equity goal.
3. **Memorization spikes:** High ChrF++ (>45) or low edit distance on natural language docs (not code/parallel) → deduplication/upsampling caused verbatim learning.
4. **Propaganda leakage:** Qualitative analysis of Russian generations shows state narratives → topic filtering inadequate.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Tokenizer equity validation:** Run inference on FLORES 200devtest for all focus languages. Compute tokens/sentence per language. Target: Interquartile range (IQR) across focus languages < 10%. Failure: Any language > 1.5x median.
2. **Curriculum ablation test:** Train a 1B-parameter small model for 100B tokens with 3 configurations: (a) Full curriculum, (b) No curriculum (natural distribution only), (c) Constant uniform. Evaluate per-language perplexity on a held-out mix. Expected: (a) lowest variance, (b) high-resource favored.
3. **Upsampling sensitivity scan:** For a subset of 4 low-resource languages, train small models with upsampling factors {1.0x, 1.5x, 2.5x, 4.0x} under the same curriculum. Measure perplexity drop vs. factor. Expected: Diminishing returns after 2.5x, potential uptick from overfitting at 4.0x.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **What are the culturally and linguistically specific manifestations of social bias, toxicity, and political orientation in generative outputs for the low-resource European languages (e.g., Baltic, Finno-Ugric, Slavic) targeted by TildeOpen, and how prevalent are they?**
  - *Basis:* Explicitly stated in conclusions and bias evaluations subsection.
  - *Why unresolved:* No systematic safety checks due to lack of benchmarks for these languages.
  - *What evidence would resolve:* Development and application of new, culturally grounded evaluation suites for these specific languages.
- **To what extent does the aggressive filtering of Russian-language data for propaganda removal, while necessary, excise valid societal discourse, cultural references, or linguistic diversity essential for modeling other languages in contact zones (e.g., Ukrainian, Latvian, Estonian)?**
  - *Basis:* Inferred from filtering description and acknowledged trade-off.
  - *Why unresolved:* No analysis on removed content or impact on neighboring languages.
  - *What evidence would resolve:* Comparative study with different filter strictness on languages with Russian diaspora, analyzed by native speakers.
- **Can the proposed curriculum learning schedule (alternating uniform and natural distributions) be formally decoupled from the effects of simple upsampling to determine its unique contribution to balancing performance across languages?**
  - *Basis:* Inferred from confounding of upsampling and curriculum.
  - *Why unresolved:* Ablation impossible due to computational constraints; effect not isolated.
  - *What evidence would resolve:* Controlled experiment with three conditions: upsampling only, curriculum only, full method.
- **Does the 2T token training budget represent a hard limit for achieving equitable performance, or could a slightly larger dataset yield significantly better results, particularly for parametric knowledge tasks?**
  - *Basis:* Inferred from emphasis on efficiency and comparison to larger models.
  - *Why unresolved:* Optimal data scale unknown; choice of 2T likely pragmatic.
  - *What evidence would resolve:* Scaling law experiment with varying token counts (1T–4T) on same curriculum and data mixing.
- **How does pre-training with equitable language representation affect the efficiency and effectiveness of subsequent instruction-tuning for specific downstream tasks?**
  - *Basis:* Explicitly stated in conclusions as future work.
  - *Why unresolved:* Only base model and single translation task evaluated; no comparison of different pre-training equity levels.
  - *What evidence would resolve:* Ablation study where multiple base models (imbalanced, uniform, balanced) are instruction-tuned on same downstream tasks and compared.

## Limitations
- Curriculum necessity unproven: The three-phase schedule's specific benefit vs. simpler constant uniform mixing is not ablated; gains could stem from upsampling/tokenization alone.
- Tokenizer equity scope: Equity is only validated on FLORES-200 parallel sentences; genre/domain diversity might reintroduce imbalance.
- Compute-constrained trade-offs: Fixed 2T tokens force upsampling/epoch trade-offs. Results may not scale to larger token budgets.

## Next Checks
1. **Curriculum ablation:** Train comparable models with full curriculum, constant uniform, and pure natural distribution. Measure per-language perplexity variance and average gains for low-resource sets.

3. **Upsampling frontier:** For 4–6 lowest-resource languages, run factorial design varying upsampling (1.0x–4.0x) and curriculum phase lengths. Identify joint configuration that minimizes perplexity without degrading high-resource performance.
