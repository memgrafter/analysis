# Hardware Efficient Approximate Convolution with Tunable Error Tolerance for CNNs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10100
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10100
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** Reduces MAC operations by 88.42% (ReLU) and 74.87% (Tanh) with zero accuracy loss on LeNet-5/MNIST; estimated power savings 35.2% and 29.96% respectively.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the inefficiency of traditional hard sparsity (skipping exact zeros) in CNN inference on edge devices, especially problematic in deeper layers and with smooth activations like Tanh that produce few zeros. It introduces a "soft sparsity" paradigm: a hardware-efficient Most Significant Bit (MSB) proxy that allows skipping of negligible non-zero multiplications without explicitly computing their products. This mechanism is integrated into the RI5CY core as a custom RISC-V instruction (`conv_approx()`) with a dedicated 5-stage FSM accelerator. On LeNet-5 with MNIST, it achieves up to 88.42% MAC reduction for ReLU and 74.87% for Tanh while maintaining test accuracy, representing a 5x improvement over conventional zero-skipping. The authors estimate power reductions of 35.2% and 29.96% respectively by clock-gating inactive multipliers, though the savings are sub-linear due to persistent memory access overhead.

## Method Summary
The method replaces exact convolution with an approximate version that dynamically prunes multiplications deemed negligible. For each product \(P = a \cdot b\) in a convolution window, the algorithm computes the sum of the MSB positions of the operands (i.e., \(\text{MSB}(a) + \text{MSB}(b)\)) as a proxy for the logarithm of the product magnitude. It identifies the maximum such sum in the window and only computes products where the difference from that maximum is below a tunable threshold \(T\). All other products are omitted, and their contribution is assumed negligible. This decision logic is implemented as a custom RISC-V instruction (`opcode 0x77`) that triggers a 5-stage FSM (IDLE, GET_DATA, STAGE_1, STAGE_2, STAGE_3, DONE) in the processor’s execute stage. The FSM handles a fixed 4x4 input window and a 3x3 kernel; kernel MSB positions are precomputed and stored. Multipliers for pruned operations are clock-gated to save dynamic power. No network retraining is required; the approximation is applied during inference only, with threshold \(T\) set per activation function (e.g., 0.3 for ReLU, 0.2 for Tanh) to preserve accuracy.

## Key Results
- **MAC reduction:** 88.42% (ReLU) and 74.87% (Tanh) on LeNet-5/MNIST with zero accuracy loss.
- **Power reduction estimate:** 35.2% (ReLU) and 29.96% (Tanh) via multiplier clock-gating.
- **5× improvement over zero-skipping:** Soft sparsity yields significantly higher pruning than hard sparsity, especially for Tanh.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: MSB-Based Product Pruning
- **Claim:** The system can skip non-zero multiplications by comparing the approximate magnitude of their products using only the sum of the Most Significant Bit (MSB) positions of the operands, without performing the actual multiplication.
- **Mechanism:** For two candidate products \(P_1 = a \cdot b\) and \(P_2 = c \cdot d\), compute \(\Delta = (\text{MSB}(a) + \text{MSB}(b)) - (\text{MSB}(c) + \text{MSB}(d))\). If \(\Delta \geq T\) (a tunable threshold), \(P_2\) is deemed negligible relative to \(P_1\) and omitted from the sum. This leverages the property that \(\text{MSB}(x) \approx \lfloor \log_2(x) \rfloor\), so the comparison reflects relative product magnitudes.
- **Core assumption:** The MSB position is a sufficiently accurate hardware-efficient proxy for logarithmic magnitude to make safe pruning decisions for CNN convolution sums.
- **Evidence:** Abstract: "utilizes a hardware efficient Most Significant Bit (MSB) proxy to skip negligible non-zero multiplications without explicit product calculation." Section III: "The position of the MSB serves as a simple, hardware-friendly proxy for the logarithmic magnitude of a number." Formal condition \(\Delta \geq T\).
- **Break condition:** If the MSB sum difference \(\Delta\) is less than threshold \(T\), both products are computed. The mechanism fails if the MSB approximation leads to an unacceptable error in the convolution sum (e.g., if a larger-magnitude product is incorrectly pruned).

### Mechanism 2: Custom RISC-V Instruction and FSM
- **Claim:** The MSB-based pruning logic is integrated as a custom RISC-V instruction (`conv_approx()`) with a dedicated 5-stage FSM hardware accelerator, enabling low-overhead dynamic approximation during CNN inference.
- **Mechanism:** A custom instruction (opcode `0x77`) is decoded in the RISC-V processor’s execute stage. A co-located accelerator block, synchronized via FSM states (`IDLE`, `GET_DATA`, `STAGE_1` [MSB extraction], `STAGE_2` [pruning & selective multiplication], `STAGE_3` [accumulation], `DONE`), performs the approximation on 4x4 input windows against stored 3x3 kernel MSBs. The result is written back via the standard pipeline.
- **Core assumption:** The control and dataflow overhead of this custom unit is outweighed by the reduction in active multiplier usage, and it can be seamlessly integrated into a standard RISC‑V pipeline without major stalls.
- **Evidence:** Section IV.A: "Integrated as a custom instruction within a 32-bit RISC‑V processor" using the RI5CY core. Section IV.C: Detailed FSM states and operation: "STAGE_1(MSB Analysis)... STAGE_2(Pruning and Multiplication)... product is suppressed" if condition fails.
- **Break condition:** FSM failure (e.g., state machine hang), data hazard if `conv_approx()` is called before data is ready, or if the 4x4/3x3 fixed window design cannot be tiled efficiently for larger layers without software overhead.

### Mechanism 3: Power Savings via Clock-Gating
- **Claim:** Skipping multiplications via this "soft sparsity" method reduces dynamic power primarily by clock-gating inactive multipliers, though total power savings are sub-linear due to persistent memory access costs.
- **Mechanism:** For each pruned multiplication, the corresponding multiplier hardware is clock-gated. The paper estimates power reduction as a fraction of total inference power, assuming MAC operations contribute ~40% (citing prior work), so power saving ≈ 0.4 × (MAC reduction %).
- **Core assumption:** Clock-gating multipliers is the dominant power‑saving mechanism, and memory access power remains largely unaffected by reduced MAC count.
- **Evidence:** Abstract: "By clock-gating inactive multipliers, we estimate power reductions of 35.2% (ReLU) and 29.96% (Tanh)... sub-linear due to memory access." Section II: Cites prior work: "reducing MAC operations by 100% will result in power reduction by about 25‑50% if corresponding memory access is not optimized."
- **Break condition:** If memory access patterns change significantly (e.g., more random accesses due to irregular pruning), memory power could increase, negating savings. Also, clock‑gating overhead (enable/disable latency) may erode benefits at very fine‑grained pruning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Binary number representation and MSB extraction:** Needed to understand how the MSB position approximates the logarithm of a number and how the algorithm handles signed integers (two’s complement). Quick check: For 32‑bit integer `0x00A00000` (1,048,576), the MSB position (0‑indexed from LSB) is 20 (since \(2^{20}=1,048,576\)). For `-0x00A00000` in two’s complement (0xFF600000), the MSB position is 31 (sign bit). The algorithm likely uses absolute magnitudes for comparison. This concept is foundational because the MSB proxy replaces full multiplication with a simple bit‑position lookup, enabling hardware‑efficient magnitude estimation.
- **CNN convolution and feature map sparsity:** Needed to grasp why soft sparsity is necessary—traditional zero‑skipping fails for deeper layers and smooth activations (e.g., Tanh) that yield few exact zeros but many small‑magnitude values. Quick check: In ReLU‑activated layers, the percentage of exact zeros typically increases with depth due to cumulative nonlinear effects. Tanh outputs are dense (non‑zero) and smooth, so zero‑skipping yields little benefit; soft sparsity targets small‑magnitude non‑zeros. Understanding these sparsity patterns is crucial to appreciate the potential gains from magnitude‑based pruning.
- **Custom instruction integration in RISC‑V:** Needed to comprehend how a new opcode and accelerator are added to the pipeline without disrupting normal operation. Quick check: In a typical 5‑stage RISC‑V pipeline (IF, ID, EX, MEM, WB), a custom arithmetic instruction executes in the EX stage. The FSM accelerator must interface with pipeline control signals (e.g., `stall`, `ready`) to avoid data hazards and ensure correct timing. This background explains how the `conv_approx()` instruction can be invoked with minimal software changes and how the FSM integrates with the processor’s control logic.
- **Approximate computing:** The paper’s method is an instance of approximate computing, where intentional inaccuracies are tolerated to improve energy efficiency. This paradigm justifies the design’s trade‑off between minor errors and significant power savings. Quick check: Approximate hardware techniques (e.g., simplified multipliers, voltage scaling) introduce bounded errors; the proposed soft sparsity selectively omits products only when their contribution is below a threshold, thus controlling the overall deviation from the exact result.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Software layer calls `conv_approx()` (assembly with opcode `0x77`). The instruction is decoded in the ID stage, routing operands (rs1, rs2) to the custom accelerator. The accelerator FSM (5 stages) orchestrates: GET_DATA fetches a 4x4 input window from memory; STAGE_1 extracts MSBs for all 16 input values and computes their maximum; STAGE_2 compares each operand pair’s MSB sum against the threshold, gate multipliers accordingly, and selectively computes products; STAGE_3 accumulates results into an output value; DONE writes the result back to the rd register. The pipeline then proceeds to WB. Kernel MSBs are pre‑stored in a small on‑chip memory. Multiplier array is clock‑gated based on STAGE‑2 decisions.

**Critical path:**  
Longest combinational delay likely spans: (1) parallel MSB extraction for 16 inputs via priority encoders → (2) reduction tree to find `MSB_max` → (3) for each of the 16 products, compute `MSB_input + MSB_weight` and compare to `MSB_max - T` → (4) generate multiplier enable signals. This path determines the FSM clock period; the design must meet timing at the target clock frequency.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Threshold \(T\):** The threshold controls the trade‑off between computational savings and accuracy preservation. A lower \(T\) retains more multiplications, minimizing error accumulation but also reducing MAC reduction; a higher \(T\) prunes more aggressively, potentially harming accuracy. The thresholds used in the paper (0.3 for ReLU, 0.2 for Tanh) were chosen empirically to preserve baseline accuracy on LeNet‑5, but a systematic or per‑layer tuning could further optimize the efficiency‑accuracy balance.
- **Window size:** The fixed 4x4 input window and 3x3 kernel simplify the hardware and reduce control complexity. However, for larger feature maps the convolution must be tiled into overlapping windows in software, which introduces loop overhead and may cause underutilization of the accelerator at layer boundaries (where fewer than a full window of data is available). This tiling overhead could affect overall performance and energy efficiency.
- **Area vs. power:** The custom accelerator adds significant silicon area (~108% overhead compared to the base RI5CY core) due to the FSM, MSB extractors, and additional control logic. Clock‑gating the multiplier array reduces dynamic power proportionally to the fraction of pruned operations, but the static power of the added logic and the constant memory subsystem power (which does not scale with MAC reduction) limit the net energy savings. The reported power reductions (35.2% for ReLU, 29.96% for Tanh) reflect this sub‑linear scaling.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Accuracy drop:** Significant decrease in test accuracy (e.g., >1%) at high \(T\) indicates threshold misconfiguration or layer‑specific sensitivity.
- **Performance loss:** If the FSM cannot supply data to multipliers quickly enough (e.g., due to memory latency in GET_DATA), the achieved speedup may fall short of the MAC reduction potential.
- **Silicon bugs:** Incorrect MSB extraction for negative numbers (two’s complement) or FSM deadlock would produce systematically erroneous outputs.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Functional validation:** Run `conv_approx()` on a known 4x4/3x3 test case (e.g., all‑ones) across thresholds \(T=0\) to maximum. Verify that skipped products exactly follow the condition \((\text{MSB}_{\text{max}} - (\text{MSB}(a)+\text{MSB}(b)) < T\) and that the final sum equals the exact convolution minus pruned terms.
2. **LeNet‑5 end‑to‑end:** Integrate `conv_approx()` into a C simulation of LeNet-5 (or the provided `LeNet‑Accel` model). Sweep threshold \(T\) and record: (a) total MACs per inference, (b) test accuracy on MNIST, (c) per‑layer pruning statistics. Confirm the reported MAC reductions (88.42% ReLU, 74.87% Tanh) at thresholds that preserve baseline accuracy.
3. **Hardware characterization:** Synthesize the FSM + multiplier array in 65 nm (as in Table II). Measure critical path delay, total dynamic power at various activity factors (simulating different \(T\)), and area overhead. Validate that power scales roughly linearly with active multiplier count and that clock‑gating enable/disable timing meets setup constraints.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper explicitly or implicitly leaves several questions unresolved:

1. **Generalization to modern deep CNNs:** Does the approach maintain accuracy and computational gains when applied to deeper architectures (e.g., ResNet‑50) on complex datasets (e.g., ImageNet)? The evaluation is limited to LeNet-5 on MNIST.
2. **Compatibility with other activation functions:** How does the MSB‑based approximation perform with activations like Swish, GELU, or Leaky ReLU, and with quantized/integer‑only networks? Only ReLU and Tanh are tested.
3. **Automatic threshold selection:** Can the tunable threshold \(T\) be automatically or dynamically optimized per layer or per input to maximize energy efficiency under a fixed accuracy constraint? The paper uses manually tuned global thresholds.
4. **Real hardware power impact:** What is the actual power/energy impact when the custom unit is integrated into a full accelerator, considering memory access costs and control overhead? The paper provides only an estimate based on third‑party energy ratios.
5. **Error propagation and robustness:** How does the approximation error propagate through multiple layers and affect classification confidence, calibration, and adversarial robustness? The paper’s error analysis is limited to per‑convolution mean absolute error on MNIST.

## Limitations
- **Signed number handling:** The paper does not clearly specify whether MSB extraction uses absolute magnitude or two’s complement for negative activations, which is critical for Tanh.
- **Threshold tuning methodology:** Thresholds are chosen manually (e.g., \(T=0.3\) for ReLU, \(T=0.2\) for Tanh) without a systematic approach; layer‑specific tuning is unexplored.
- **Memory overhead model:** The power estimate assumes memory access energy remains constant despite reduced MACs; irregular pruning may increase memory traffic and offset savings.
- **Hardware validation:** Only synthesis estimates are provided; no silicon or FPGA measurements are reported, and the FSM’s area/power overheads are not fully characterized.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (MSB proxy):** Medium – Empirical results support validity, but theoretical worst‑case bounds and robustness to atypical distributions are missing.
- **Mechanism 2 (custom instruction):** Medium‑High – FSM design is described in detail, but cycle‑accurate simulation and pipeline stall analysis are absent.
- **Mechanism 3 (power savings):** Low‑Medium – Estimates rely on prior fractions; no direct dynamic power measurements; sub‑linear claim remains unverified in a full system.

## Next Checks
To strengthen the paper’s claims, the following concrete validation steps are recommended:
1. **Sign‑handling test:** Execute `conv_approx()` on synthetic 4×4/3×3 cases containing known negative values; compare against exact sums to determine correct MSB extraction policy (absolute vs. signed magnitude).
2. **Threshold sweep:** Systematically vary \(T\) across all LeNet‑5 layers for ReLU and Tanh, recording accuracy versus MAC reduction; verify that the paper’s fixed thresholds indeed lie on the Pareto frontier.
3. **Power characterization:** Implement the FSM and multiplier array in RTL, synthesize for 65 nm, run gate‑level simulation with realistic activation traces, and measure dynamic power scaling to validate the clock‑gating model and sub‑linear savings claim.
