# Models as Lego Builders: Assembling Malice from Benign Blocks via Semantic Blueprints

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.07590  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07590  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** StructAttack achieves average Attack Success Rates of ~80% on open‑source LVLMs and ~60% on commercial ones, significantly higher than prior methods.

## Executive Summary
Large vision‑language models (LVLMs) are vulnerable to a novel jailbreak attack, StructAttack, that exploits semantic slot filling to reconstruct harmful intents from individually benign components. The attack decomposes a malicious query into a central topic and benign‑appearing slot types (e.g., “Making Process”, “Raw Materials”), embeds them into structured visual diagrams (mind maps, tables, sunburst) with subtle perturbations, and pairs them with a completion instruction. Across models including GPT‑4o, Gemini‑2.5‑Flash, Qwen3‑VL‑Flash, and open‑source LVLMs, StructAttack attains ~80% ASR on open‑source and ~60% ASR on commercial models, outperforming prior attacks. It operates with a single victim query and remains effective under basic system‑prompt defenses (47.2% ASR), demonstrating both effectiveness and efficiency.

## Method Summary
StructAttack comprises two modules: Semantic Slot Decomposition (SSD) and Visual‑Structural Injection (VSI). SSD uses a decomposer LLM (Deepseek‑Chat) to split a malicious query into a central topic and malicious slot types that are individually harmless but collectively encode the harmful intent; a distractor LLM adds irrelevant slots to dilute malicious density. VSI renders these slot types into a structured visual prompt (mind map, table, or sunburst) via Matplotlib, applies random but subtle layout perturbations (e.g., angle jitter), and combines the perturbed image with a fixed completion instruction. The multimodal input is sent to the victim LVLM in a single query, and the response is evaluated using automated safety classifiers (LLaMA‑Guard‑3‑8B, GPT‑4o‑mini).

## Key Results
- StructAttack achieves average ASR of ~80% on open‑source LVLMs and ~60% on commercial ones.
- Outperforms prior jailbreak methods (FigStep, HADES, SI‑Attack, JOOD).
- Maintains robustness with a single query and under system‑prompt defenses (47.2% ASR).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Semantic Slot Filling Vulnerability
LVLMs exhibit a vulnerability where they fill benign‑appearing slot types with harmful content when those slots are part of a structured query. The attack decomposes a malicious instruction into a central topic and slot types that are individually harmless (e.g., “Making Process”, “Raw Materials”) but together encode the harmful intent. The model’s reasoning reassembles these slots when prompted to fill them, bypassing safety checks that focus on surface‑level intent. Evidence: textual SSF attacks already yield 54% ASR on GPT‑4o; visual SSF further increases ASR. Break condition: decomposition fails to preserve the malicious intent or the LVLM recognizes the global harmfulness during reasoning.

### Mechanism 2: Structured Visual Prompts Boost Effectiveness
Embedding decomposed slot types into visual structures (mind maps, tables, sunburst) significantly increases attack success compared to textual prompts. Visual structures provide a scaffold that the LVLM interprets as a coherent diagram, prompting it to complete missing slot values. This evades text‑based safety filters that might detect malicious keywords. Evidence: All three visual structures achieve ~60% ASR on commercial LVLMs and ~80% on open‑source ones, surpassing prior attacks. Break condition: robust visual safety filters or visual parsing failure due to excessive perturbation.

### Mechanism 3: Distractor Slots Dilute Malicious Density
Adding distractor slots (benign, topic‑related but irrelevant) makes the query appear less suspicious and diverts the model’s safety attention. The model’s slot‑level checks are distracted by the presence of benign content. Evidence: Adding 2 distractor slots increases jailbroken samples from 41 to 48 on GPT‑4o. Break condition: distractor slots are too obviously irrelevant or the model employs holistic context understanding.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map (plain text):**  
SSD module (Decomposer LLM + Distractor LLM) → VSI module (Visual rendering via Matplotlib) → Attack execution (image + instruction → victim LVLM) → Evaluation (response → safety classifier)

**Critical path:**  
1. Input malicious query Q to Decomposer (role‑playing prompt) → central topic T and malicious slots.  
2. Input T to Distractor → distractor slots.  
3. Combine all slots into structured template; render with Matplotlib.  
4. Apply perturbation (e.g., position jitter, rotation).  
5. Form multimodal input (perturbed image I′ + completion instruction).  
6. Query victim LVLM with the multimodal input.  
7. Evaluate response for jailbreak success (using LLaMA‑Guard‑3‑8B or GPT‑4o‑mini).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Visual structure choice (Mind Map, Table, Sunburst): all similarly effective; choose based on naturalness for the topic.  
- Number of distractor slots: 2 is empirically optimal; more may cause clutter or over‑dilution.  
- Perturbation level: keep subtle (e.g., ±45° angle jitter) to preserve structure while evading detection; excessive may cause parsing errors.  
- Decomposer LLM: Deepseek‑Chat used; other strong LLMs may work but could affect slot quality.  
- Instruction phrasing: fixed instruction used; variations not explored.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Semantic drift: Decomposed slots fail to fully capture malicious intent.  
- High‑level rejection: LVLM identifies sensitive topic and refuses.  
- Visual parsing failure: LVLM misinterprets diagram due to complexity or perturbation.  
- Distractor irrelevance: Distractor slots too obviously off‑topic, drawing attention.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce textual SSF: Use JSON template with slot types from Decomposer; test on GPT‑4o with completion instruction; expect ASR ~54%.  
2. Visual structure comparison: For a set of queries, generate Mind Map, Table, Sunburst; measure ASR differences; expect similar performance.  
3. Distractor slot ablation: Test with 0, 1, 2 distractor slots on queries; expect highest ASR with 2 distractor slots.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does the choice of auxiliary LLM for semantic slot decomposition affect StructAttack’s success rate?  
2. Can a purely textual slot‑filling attack (no visual structure) achieve comparable performance to StructAttack?  
3. What internal mechanisms (e.g., attention, parsing) lead LVLMs to fill harmful slot values despite benign slot types?  
4. How can LVLMs be defended against semantic slot‑filling attacks beyond simple system prompts?

## Limitations
- Unverified availability of Advbench‑M and SafeBench datasets and the exact codebase (github.com/Yef23/StructAttack) hinders full reproducibility.  
- Generalizability is uncertain; only six LVLMs were evaluated, so transferability to newer models remains untested.  
- Optimal distractor design (fixed at 2) may not generalize across all malicious query categories.

## Confidence
- **High:** Core mechanism claims (semantic slot filling vulnerability and visual prompt boost) are strongly supported by internal experiments (ASR gaps, ablations).  
- **Medium:** Transferability to broader LVLM families; evidence limited to a small model set.  
- **Low:** Universal applicability of the vulnerability; safety alignment variations could nullify the attack on some models.

## Next Checks
1. Cross‑model replication: Test StructAttack on at least two unseen state‑of‑the‑art LVLMs (e.g., Llama‑3.2‑Vision, Claude‑3.5‑Sonnet) using the paper’s prompts and templates.  
2. Decomposition quality audit: Manually inspect 50 random Decomposer outputs to verify slot types remain individually benign yet collectively reconstruct malicious intent.  
3. Defense robustness probe: Implement a simple visual safety filter (e.g., CLIP‑based harmfulness scorer on generated images) and re‑run attacks to test resilience.
