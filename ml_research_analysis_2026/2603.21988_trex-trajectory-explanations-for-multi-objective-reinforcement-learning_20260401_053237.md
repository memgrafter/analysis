# TREX: Trajectory Explanations for Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21988
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21988
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** TREX quantifies the impact of behavioral segments on multi-objective trade-offs by clustering expert trajectories, training complementary policies without each cluster, and computing a Reward Attribution Score (RAS), enabling causal interpretation of how sub-trajectories influence returns under different user preferences.

## Executive Summary
TREX is a post-hoc explainability framework for Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) that addresses the lack of methods to interpret how specific behavioral segments contribute to trade-offs among competing objectives. It segments expert trajectories into semantically coherent clusters, then isolates each cluster’s influence by training a complementary policy (trained without that cluster’s data) and measuring the deviation in per-objective returns (∆Ri). The resulting Reward Attribution Score (RAS) quantifies each segment’s causal impact. Validated on MO-HalfCheetah, MO-Ant, and MO-Swimmer, TREX identifies influential behaviors—such as ‘small hops’ in HalfCheetah that conserve energy at the cost of speed—and provides actionable insights for preference tuning and policy debugging. The approach bridges the gap between black-box MORL policies and human-interpretable explanations of objective interactions.

## Method Summary
TREX processes a set of expert demonstrations for a learned MORL policy. First, it encodes state-action sequences into fixed-length vectors and clusters them (e.g., using K-means) to identify recurring behavioral segments. For each cluster, it trains a complementary policy using all demonstration data *except* trajectories belonging to that cluster. The impact of the removed segment is quantified by comparing the per-objective returns of the complementary policy to those of the original policy across a range of user preferences, yielding ∆Ri values. These are aggregated into a scalar Reward Attribution Score (RAS) that ranks clusters by their influence on trade-offs. The RAS enables identification of which behavioral segments predominantly affect which objectives, offering a causal explanation link between demonstrated behavior and multi-objective performance.

## Key Results
- TREX successfully clustered trajectories into semantically meaningful segments (e.g., ‘running’, ‘small hops’, ‘large hops’ in MO-HalfCheetah) and computed RAS values that aligned with intuitive causal relationships.
- In MO-HalfCheetah under balanced preferences, removing the ‘small hops’ cluster increased energy conservation returns but decreased speed returns, yielding a high RAS and confirming its dual impact on the trade-off.
- The method generalizes across three continuous control benchmarks, demonstrating that impact attribution is consistent across different MORL problems and preference weightings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
TREX’s effectiveness stems from its ablation-based causal reasoning: by systematically removing a behavioral cluster and retraining, it isolates that cluster’s contribution to the policy’s multi-objective performance. Clustering transforms long, unstructured trajectories into discrete behavioral motifs, making the analysis tractable. The use of relative reward deviations (∆Ri) across preferences captures how each segment shifts the Pareto frontier. The work is grounded in imitation learning (to generate the original policy), trajectory segmentation, and complementary policy training to perform a form of counterfactual inference.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Expert Trajectories -> Trajectory Encoder -> Clustering Algorithm (e.g., K-means) -> Behavioral Clusters -> For Each Cluster: Split Data (Exclude Cluster) -> Train Complementary Policy -> Evaluate Across Preferences (Compare to Original Policy) -> Compute ∆Ri per Objective -> Aggregate to RAS -> Rank Clusters

**Critical Path:**  
1. Collect expert demonstrations for a trained MORL policy.  
2. Encode and cluster trajectories into behavioral segments.  
3. For each cluster, train a complementary policy without that cluster’s data.  
4. Evaluate both original and complementary policies over a grid of user preferences.  
5. Compute per-objective return deviations (∆Ri) and aggregate into RAS for ranking.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- **Granularity vs. Computational Cost:** Finer clusters yield more precise explanations but require training O(N) complementary policies (linear overhead). Coarser clusters reduce cost but may conflate distinct behaviors.  
- **Clustering Quality vs. Semantic Interpretability:** Algorithmic metrics (e.g., silhouette score) may not align with human-understandable behaviors; requires qualitative validation.  
- **Policy Distillation Fidelity:** Using an imitated policy as the expert surrogate introduces potential distribution shift; a perfect clone is ideal but often unrealistic.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Clusters lack semantic coherence (e.g., mixing ‘running’ and ‘turning’).  
- Complementary policies fail to train or collapse to random behavior, indicating the excluded cluster was too critical or data was insufficient.  
- RAS values show little variation across clusters, suggesting the clustering did not capture behaviorally distinct segments.  
- ∆Ri patterns are inconsistent across random seeds, indicating high variance in the ablation effect.

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. **Clustering Sanity Check:** Run TREX on a simple MORL environment (e.g., MO-Swimmer) with ground-truth behavioral phases. Manually label clusters and check if they correspond to known behaviors (e.g., ‘glide’, ‘kick’).  
2. **Complementary Policy Training Stability:** For a chosen cluster, train 5 complementary policies with different random seeds. Measure variance in their returns and RAS contributions to ensure results are not noise-driven.  
3. **RAS Sensitivity to Clustering:** Re-run TREX on MO-HalfCheetah using two clustering algorithms (K-means vs. DBSCAN). Compare the resulting RAS rankings and highlight discrepancies, investigating whether one set aligns better with domain knowledge.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the fidelity of the original policy as a surrogate for the expert be improved to ensure explanations accurately reflect the expert’s behavior across all user preferences?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors identify that the preference-level analysis relies on the original policy to mimic the expert, but observed disparities in returns suggest the mimicking process may introduce behavioral shifts. They explicitly state that future work could focus on refining this distillation process.
- **Why unresolved:** The current imitation approach may not perfectly capture the expert’s decision logic, potentially compromising the causal interpretation of attribution scores derived from complementary policies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct comparative experiments using advanced imitation learning or behavioral cloning methods to train the original policy, measure policy divergence (e.g., via KL divergence) and assess whether the resulting attribution scores become more consistent and correlated with expert returns.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the computational cost of TREX be reduced while maintaining granular explanations, for example by avoiding the need to train a separate complementary policy for each identified cluster?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly note that the post-hoc analysis requires training a new complementary policy per cluster, resulting in linear computational overhead. Even when using simpler models, the analysis remains computationally intensive.
- **Why unresolved:** The current approach does not scale well to a large number of clusters or long trajectories, limiting applicability to complex environments or real-time scenarios.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop and evaluate alternative attribution methods that approximate the effect of removing a cluster without full retraining, such as influence function estimation or data reweighting. Empirically compare the new method’s runtime and the similarity of its attribution scores to the original TREX results on benchmark tasks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Which trajectory encoding and clustering techniques produce the most semantically meaningful and stable behavioral clusters for TREX across different MORL environments and user preferences?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly state that the framework is sensitive to the choice of trajectory encoding and clustering, illustrate clusters from K-means, and suggest exploring other clustering algorithms or known initial cluster centers.
- **Why unresolved:** Suboptimal encoding or clustering may group together dissimilar behaviors or split coherent ones, leading to misleading attribution scores and less interpretable explanations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform a systematic study comparing various encoders (e.g., LSTM, VQ-VAE) and clustering algorithms (e.g., DBSCAN, hierarchical) across multiple MORL domains. Use quantitative metrics (silhouette score, cluster purity) and qualitative human evaluation to determine which combinations yield clusters that are both distinct and semantically coherent, and then verify whether the resulting attribution analyses remain consistent.

## Limitations
- **Ecological Validity:** Evaluated only on three standard continuous control locomotion environments; not tested on discrete action spaces, partial observability, or real-world tasks.
- **Explanation Validation:** Relies on qualitative visual inspection and intuitive RAS patterns; lacks statistical significance testing or comparison to alternative causal inference methods.
- **Implementation Gap:** No released code or detailed hyperparameters; reproduction depends on reconstructing the pipeline from the paper description alone, hindering independent verification.

- **Claim:** Results are generalizable to MO-RL problems. **Confidence: Low** (Evaluation on only three similar benchmarks; no testing on diverse domains).
