# Enhance the Safety in Reinforcement Learning by ADRC Lagrangian Methods

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.18142
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18142
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: ADRC Lagrangian methods reduce safety violations by up to 74%, violation magnitudes by 89%, and average costs by 67% while preserving reward performance across multiple RL algorithms.

## Executive Summary
Safe reinforcement learning (RL) often employs Lagrangian methods to enforce cost constraints, but these suffer from oscillations and safety violations due to parameter sensitivity and control lag. This paper introduces ADRC Lagrangian methods, which apply Active Disturbance Rejection Control to dynamically adjust the Lagrange multiplier. The approach uses a reduced-order Extended State Observer to estimate and cancel unknown disturbances and a critically damped reference trajectory to smoothly track the cost threshold. Classical and PID Lagrangian updates become special cases within this unified framework, and theoretical analysis shows ADRC achieves lower disturbance estimation error and reduced phase lag relative to integral control, with a provable lower bound on the observer gain for stability.

Comprehensive experiments on OmniSafe benchmarks with PPO, TRPO, DDPG, and TD3 demonstrate consistent safety improvements: up to 74% fewer violations, 89% smaller violation magnitudes, and 67% lower average costs, all without sacrificing reward performance. The method is robust to hyperparameter choices and can be plugged into any Lagrangian-based Safe RL algorithm, offering a practical and universally applicable enhancement.

## Method Summary
At its core, ADRC replaces the traditional multiplier update—typically an integral controller on the cost error—with an observer‑based control law. The multiplier λ_t is computed as:

\[
λ_t = (k_{ap} + ω_o k_{ad})(x_1 - r) + (k_{ad} + ω_o)(x_2 - \dot{r}) + ω_o k_{ap} \int (x_1 - r) dτ - \ddot{r},
\]

where \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are the cumulative cost and its derivative, \(r(t)\) follows a critically damped reference (parameterized by \(c_r\)) converging to the threshold \(d\), \(k_{ap}, k_{ad}\) are proportional and derivative gains, and \(ω_o\) is the observer gain. The total disturbance \(f(t)\) affecting the cost dynamics is estimated in real time by a reduced‑order Extended State Observer. The method includes a stability condition: \(ω_o\) must exceed a lower bound \(ω^*_o\) derived from Lipschitz constants of the unknown dynamics. The ADRC module is lightweight and can be inserted into any Lagrangian RL algorithm; the objective is rescaled to avoid excessively large multipliers. This design actively rejects unmodeled disturbances and avoids the phase lag inherent to integral‑only updates, leading to smoother and safer learning.

## Key Results
- ADRC cuts safety violations by up to 74% and reduces the magnitude of violations by up to 89% compared to classical and PID Lagrangian baselines.
- Average cumulative costs during training drop by up to 67% while final rewards remain competitive.
- The method demonstrates robustness across a wide range of hyperparameter values and generalizes to four popular RL algorithms (PPO, TRPO, DDPG, TD3).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ADRC treats all unknown influences on the cost dynamics—including model mismatch, approximation errors, and external perturbations—as a lumped disturbance. By estimating this disturbance with an Extended State Observer and injecting its negative into the multiplier update, the controller cancels the disturbance’s effect before it can cause constraint violations. The critically damped reference \(r(t)\) prevents overshoot and oscillations, while the combined proportional‑derivative‑integral‑like terms (via the observer) ensure fast error correction without the phase lag of pure integral action. Theoretical guarantees show that if the observer gain \(ω_o\) is chosen above a computable lower bound, the estimation error remains bounded and the closed‑loop system is stable. Consequently, the multiplier adjusts smoothly yet responsively, keeping the cumulative cost close to the threshold and drastically reducing both the frequency and severity of safety breaches.

## Architecture Onboarding
The ADRC‑augmented Safe RL pipeline can be viewed as a composition of the following components:

**Component map**  
Environment -> Cost Accumulator -> ADRC Module (Reference Generator + ESO) -> Multiplier Update -> Policy Gradient Update -> Agent

**Critical path**  
1. The agent interacts with the environment, receiving reward \(r\) and instantaneous cost \(c\).  
2. The cost accumulator updates cumulative cost \(x_1\) and its derivative \(x_2\) (e.g., via finite differences or a differentiator).  
3. The ADRC module: (a) computes the reference \(r(t)\) and its first/second derivatives; (b) runs a reduced‑order ESO to estimate total disturbance \(f(t)\); (c) evaluates the multiplier λ using Eq. (17).  
4. The policy update uses the Lagrangian objective \(L = \mathbb{E}[\text{reward} - λ·\text{cost}]\) (with possible rescaling).  
5. The updated policy is deployed; loop repeats.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Reference speed** \(c_r\): Larger values make \(r(t)\) converge faster to the threshold but risk overshoot; smaller values slow adaptation and may tolerate higher violations.  
- **Gains** \(k_{ap}, k_{ad}\): High proportional gain speeds error correction but amplifies noise; derivative gain adds damping but can be sensitive to measurement noise.  
- **Observer gain** \(ω_o\): Must exceed the theoretical lower bound \(ω^*_o\) to guarantee stability; too high increases noise sensitivity and computational cost.  
- **Update frequency**: The ADRC step is cheap but may need to run at every environment step; policy updates typically occur less frequently, introducing a timescale separation that must be tuned.

**Failure signatures**  
- **Exploding λ or NaNs**: Often a sign that \(ω_o\) is below the stability bound or that disturbance estimates diverge.  
- **Persistent oscillations in cost and λ**: Indicates poorly tuned \(c_r\) or gains, or an overly aggressive reference.  
- **High violation rate despite ADRC**: Suggests the ESO is not capturing the disturbance quickly enough (e.g., \(ω_o\) too low) or the reference is mis‑aligned.  
- **Reward collapse**: An excessively large multiplier (e.g., from lack of rescaling) over‑penalizes the objective, causing the policy to become overly conservative.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Real‑world applicability**: The method is validated only in simulation; its robustness to non‑stationarity, partial observability, hardware latency, and irreversible consequences remains unknown.  
- **Multiple constraints**: Extending ADRC to handle several cost constraints simultaneously is non‑trivial; separate observers could yield conflicting multipliers, and theoretical guarantees are lacking.  
- **Delayed/sparse cost feedback**: The derivation assumes continuous cost and derivative signals; delays may destabilize the ESO loop, calling for modified observers.  
- **Finite‑sample guarantees**: The analysis is asymptotic; no high‑probability, finite‑time bounds on constraint violations are provided, limiting certifiable safety during learning.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The values of \(c_r\), \(k_{ap}\), \(k_{ad}\), and \(ω_o\) are task‑dependent and lack a universal selection rule.  
- **Underspecified stability bound**: The computation of \(ω^*_o\) requires coefficients of a characteristic polynomial that are not explicitly given; estimating Lipschitz constants via finite differences is noisy.  
- **Mapping theory to practice**: The translation from theoretical quantities (trust‑region radius, batch size) to implementation hyperparameters is not detailed, making it hard to apply the analysis for parameter tuning.
