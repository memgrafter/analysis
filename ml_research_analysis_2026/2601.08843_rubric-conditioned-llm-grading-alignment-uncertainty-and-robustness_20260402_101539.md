# Rubric-Conditioned LLM Grading: Alignment, Uncertainty, and Robustness

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.08843
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08843
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** Rubric-conditioned Qwen 2.5-72B yields 76% accuracy on binary SciEntsBank grading but degrades to 57% on 5-way, with a leniency bias; a trust curve deferral improves effective binary accuracy to 81.1% at the cost of reduced coverage.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates rubric-conditioned grading by large language models (LLMs) on the SciEntsBank dataset. Using Qwen 2.5-72B, the authors find strong alignment with expert judgments for binary classification (76% accuracy, Cohen’s κ=0.51) but a substantial drop for finer-grained 5-way grading (57% accuracy, κ=0.34), accompanied by a leniency bias where partially correct answers are often misclassified as correct. To improve reliability, a consensus-based deferral mechanism (“trust curve”) is introduced, which filters low-confidence predictions to boost effective accuracy (e.g., 81.1% for binary at the cost of lower coverage). Robustness tests reveal the model resists prompt-injection attacks (>90% rejection) but is notably sensitive to synonym substitutions, indicating vulnerability to lexical variations. The study highlights both the potential and the limitations of LLMs for nuanced assessment.

## Method Summary
The evaluation employs rubric-conditioned prompting, where each student answer, the corresponding question, and a detailed rubric are fed to the LLM to predict a grade. Performance is measured against expert annotations across 2-way (correct/incorrect) and 5-way (correct, partially correct, irrelevant, etc.) label sets. The trust curve mechanism aggregates multiple model outputs (via sampling or repeated prompts) to estimate prediction confidence; instances below a confidence threshold are deferred for human review. Robustness is probed by applying controlled perturbations to the input text (e.g., synonym swaps, misspellings) and adversarial prompt injections to test the model’s resilience. Experiments are conducted exclusively on the SciEntsBank short-answer dataset using the Qwen 2.5-72B model.

## Key Results
- Grading alignment: 76% accuracy (binary) and 57% (5-way) with Cohen’s κ of 0.51 and 0.34 respectively; model shows leniency bias (e.g., “Partially Correct” often labeled “Correct”).
- Consensus-based trust curve defers low-confidence predictions, raising effective binary accuracy to 81.1% with 80% coverage; similar gains for 3-way and 5-way but with greater coverage loss.
- Robustness: >90% correct rejection of adversarial prompt injections; synonym substitutions cause the largest performance drop among perturbations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Rubric conditioning frames grade prediction as a structured text-to-text task, allowing the LLM to leverage its pre-trained knowledge of educational concepts and linguistic patterns. The model interprets the rubric as a decision schema and produces a categorical label. The trust curve leverages the observation that model consensus across stochastic forward passes correlates with correctness; low consensus indicates uncertainty, so deferring such cases reduces errors. The model’s resilience to prompt injection likely stems from the rubric’s explicit constraints, making it harder to coerce the model into ignoring grading criteria. However, the sensitivity to synonym substitutions suggests the model over-relies on surface lexical features rather than deeper semantic alignment with the rubric.

## Foundational Learning
- **Rubric-based assessment**: Needed to define consistent grading criteria; quick check: Is the rubric comprehensive and unambiguous enough to guide the LLM?
- **In-context learning with LLMs**: Needed to apply the rubric without model retraining; quick check: Does the prompt preserve rubric structure and mapping to labels?
- **Confidence estimation via consensus**: Needed to identify uncertain predictions for deferral; quick check: Does the frequency of a label across multiple samples correlate with correctness?
- **Robustness evaluation**: Needed to ensure the system withstands input variations and adversarial attacks; quick check: How does performance change under controlled perturbations (e.g., synonym swaps)?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Student Answer, Rubric -> LLM (multiple samples) -> Label Distribution -> Consensus -> Confidence Score -> Trust Curve Threshold -> Final Grade / Deferral  

Critical path: The student answer and rubric are fed into the LLM; multiple stochastic forward passes yield a distribution over grade labels; the most frequent label is selected, its proportion determines confidence; if confidence exceeds the threshold, the grade is issued, else the case is deferred.  

Design tradeoffs: Increasing the number of LLM samples improves confidence estimation but raises computational cost; higher confidence thresholds increase accuracy but reduce coverage; prompt design must balance rubric detail against token limits to avoid truncation.  

Failure signatures: (1) Low confidence for borderline answers; (2) Systematic confusion between similar rubric categories (e.g., “Partially Correct” vs. “Irrelevant”); (3) Sharp accuracy drops when input synonyms are altered, indicating over-reliance on surface forms.  

First experiments:  
1. Evaluate alignment (accuracy, Cohen’s κ) across 2-way, 3-way, and 5-way grading schemes.  
2. Sweep confidence thresholds on the trust curve to characterize the accuracy-coverage trade-off.  
3. Apply text perturbations (synonym substitution, misspelling, prompt injection) to test robustness and identify failure modes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific linguistic or rubric features cause the sharp degradation in alignment (from 76% to 57% accuracy) when moving from 2-way to 5-way grading, and can the model's leniency bias be systematically mitigated?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states alignment degrades significantly with finer granularity and identifies a leniency bias (e.g., classifying "Partially Correct" as "Correct").
- **Why unresolved:** The error analysis (Figure 1) describes the bias pattern but does not investigate *why* the model struggles with nuance—e.g., whether it is due to insufficient rubric specification, model capacity for ordinal distinctions, or conflation of semantic categories.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies manipulating rubric descriptiveness (e.g., adding exemplars, clarifying category boundaries) or probing model representations for the 5-way labels to identify which categories are most confusable and why.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is the consensus-based "Trust Curve" deferral mechanism generally applicable across different LLM architectures, question types, and rubric designs, or is its effectiveness contingent on specific properties of Qwen 2.5-72B and the SciEntsBank dataset?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper introduces the trust curve and demonstrates a trade-off for 2-way, 3-way, and 5-way schemes on one dataset/model.
- **Why unresolved:** The mechanism's generalizability is unknown. The paper notes it was tested only on Qwen 2.5-72B with SciEntsBank; it may not hold for other models (e.g., smaller models, different training) or domains where consensus behavior differs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies using multiple LLM families (Llama, GPT) and diverse ASAG benchmarks (different subjects, question formats) to see if the same accuracy-coverage trade-off curve holds and how the optimal threshold varies.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why is the model most sensitive to synonym substitutions among all perturbations, and can this vulnerability be reduced without sacrificing performance on unperturbed inputs?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The robustness results show synonym substitution caused the largest performance drop.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper speculates it may be due to semantic changes or grammatical inconsistency but does not test this hypothesis. It is unclear if the model is over-relying on surface lexical matches vs. deeper semantics, or if the perturbation introduced out-of-distribution noise.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments measuring performance on (a) semantically equivalent synonyms (e.g., "big" -> "large") vs. (b) subtly shifting synonyms (e.g., "hard" -> "firm") and analyzing model attention/activation patterns to see if critical rubric features are lost.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What are the precise failure modes behind the residual ~5-10% of adversarial "null model" attacks that bypass the rubric-conditioned defense, and can prompt-based defenses be strengthened to eliminate these failure cases?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper reports >90% correct rejection of adversarial inputs but identifies two failure modes: hallucination via ambiguity and misinterpretation of persuasion.
- **Why unresolved:** The qualitative analysis provides examples but not a systematic taxonomy or prevalence across attack types. It is unknown whether these are edge cases exploitable at scale or if defenses can be hardened (e.g., by refining prompt instructions or adding detection layers).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large-scale red-teaming with diverse adversarial payloads, coupled with an error taxonomy, to determine which prompt modifications or post-hoc checks (e.g., consistency checks, content filtering) reliably close these gaps.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** To what extent do semantic ambiguities and overlapping definitions in the 5-way rubric itself (e.g., distinguishing "Partially Correct" from "Irrelevant") contribute to low inter-annotator agreement and model misalignment, independent of model capability?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper notes the 5-way labels are discrete categories and the model shows confusion between them (e.g., labeling "Irrelevant" as "Partially Correct"). Future work mentions addressing semantic ambiguities via improved definitions or in-context learning.
- **Why unresolved:** The root cause of category confusion is unclear: it may stem from poorly defined rubric boundaries in the dataset, which would affect both human and model agreement, or from the model's inability to respect ordinal distinctions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-analyzing the SciEntsBank 5-way labels with multiple human annotators to measure inherent ambiguity; then, fine-tuning or prompting the model with clarified definitions and measuring whether alignment improves, isolating rubric quality as a variable.

## Limitations
- Single-model, single-dataset evaluation limits generalizability.
- Leniency bias and 5-way degradation lack mechanistic explanation.
- Deferral mechanism’s practical utility (e.g., human workload reduction) remains unevaluated.

## Confidence
- Binary alignment & prompt-injection robustness: **High**
- 5-way alignment & deferral improvement: **Medium**
- Generalizability to other models/datasets: **Low**
