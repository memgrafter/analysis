# Narrative Fingerprints: Multi-Scale Author Identification via Novelty Curve Dynamics

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2604.01073
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01073
- **Reference count:** 2
- **Primary result:** Novelty curve features yield a multi-scale author fingerprint: scalar dynamics identify 43.3% of authors at the whole‑book level; SAX motifs achieve 30.5× above‑chance attribution at the chapter level; the fingerprint persists within genre clusters (7–25%) and across eras.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether authors leave a measurable stylistic fingerprint in the information‑theoretic novelty dynamics of their texts. Novelty is defined as semantic surprisal between consecutive paragraphs, computed from embedding cosine distances. Analysis across two large corpora (Books3 and PG‑19) reveals a multi‑scale signal: at the whole‑book level, four scalar features (mean novelty, speed, volume, circuitousness) identify 43.3% of authors significantly above chance; at the finer chapter level (using sliding 20‑paragraph windows), SAX motif patterns achieve 30.5× above‑chance attribution, far outperforming scalars at that scale. The two signals are complementary, and the fingerprint is robust within genre clusters (7–25% of authors) and for classical (pre‑1930) authors, suggesting it captures genuine authorial style rather than modern publishing artifacts.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with raw text segmented into paragraphs. Paragraph embeddings are generated using the Nomic Embed Text v1.5 model (768 dimensional). The novelty curve is then formed by computing \(n_i = 1 - \frac{e_i \cdot e_{i+1}}{\|e_i\|\|e_{i+1}\|}\) for consecutive paragraphs. Two parallel feature extraction branches operate: (1) book-level scalars (mean novelty, speed, volume, circuitousness) summarize the entire curve; (2) chapter-level SAX motifs apply Piecewise Aggregate Approximation (PAA) followed by z‑normalization and discretization into a symbolic string, from which k‑gram distributions are extracted in overlapping windows (default: 20 paragraphs). Author fingerprints are evaluated via intra‑author Jensen‑Shannon divergence (JSD) consistency and nearest‑centroid attribution (top‑k accuracy) with permutation‑based significance testing. The method isolates multi‑scale stylistic signals independent of genre and era.

## Key Results
- Four scalar features (mean novelty, speed, volume, circuitousness) identify 43.3% of authors at the whole‑book level, with top‑1 attribution 3.8% (29× chance).
- SAX motif patterns in sliding 20‑paragraph windows achieve top‑1 accuracy 4.1% (30.5× chance) and identify 30.5% of authors above chance.
- The multi‑scale signal persists within unsupervised genre clusters (7–25% of authors significant) and for classical authors (pre‑1930), indicating genuine authorial style beyond confounds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Authors exhibit consistent overall patterns of semantic novelty intensity and variability across their books, captured by scalar features (mean novelty, speed, volume, circuitousness), enabling discrimination at the whole‑book level.
- **Mechanism:** The novelty curve (cosine distance between consecutive paragraph embeddings) is summarized into a few scalars. If an author’s narrative strategy consistently yields, for example, high mean novelty or high accumulated volume, their books will cluster in this low‑dimensional space.
- **Core assumption:** Authors have stable macro‑level narrative strategies that produce distinguishable aggregate novelty statistics across their oeuvre.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "at book level, scalar dynamics (mean novelty, speed, volume, circuitousness) identify 43% of authors significantly above chance"
  - [section]: Experiment 3 reports that scalar dynamics alone yield 43.3% significant authors and top‑1 attribution 3.8% (29× chance) on Books3, far exceeding SAX motifs.
  - [corpus]: Analysis across 759 authors (Books3) and 1,821 authors (PG‑19) provides statistical power to detect these effects.
- **Break condition:** If an author’s works span highly diverse genres or exhibit deliberate stylistic evolution (e.g., Henry James), scalar features may show no clustering or even anti‑clustering (negative effect size).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Authors exhibit consistent local novelty sequencing patterns within short passages (e.g., chapters), captured by SAX motif distributions in sliding windows, enabling discrimination at the chapter level.
- **Mechanism:** Novelty curves are divided into overlapping windows (e.g., 20 paragraphs). Each window’s curve is transformed into a SAX string (via PAA, z‑normalization, discretization). Recurring k‑gram motifs across an author’s windows form a distribution that is more similar within‑author than between‑authors.
- **Core assumption:** Authors have stable micro‑level pacing rhythms (e.g., alternation of high/low novelty, buildup patterns) that recur across chapters, independently of overall book structure.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "at chapter level (sliding 20‑paragraph windows), SAX motif patterns achieve 30.5× above‑chance attribution, far outperforming the scalar features at that scale."
  - [section]: Experiment 4 shows that for W=20, SAX motifs achieve top‑1 accuracy 4.1% (30.5× chance) while scalar slope features only 0.7–0.8%. Charles Dickens exemplifies this: weak book‑level fingerprint (effect=0.45) but strong window‑level (effect=4.04).
  - [corpus]: The Books3 corpus (after filtering for ≥80 paragraphs) provides 752 authors and 6,317 books for sliding window analysis.
- **Break condition:** If an author’s local pacing varies widely across chapters or if the window size is mismatched to the natural pacing unit (e.g., too large or too small), motif distributions may not cluster.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The narrative fingerprint is not solely a confound of genre or historical era; it persists within genre clusters and is similarly strong for classical (pre‑1930) authors, indicating it captures genuine authorial style.
- **Mechanism:** By clustering books data‑drivenly based on SAX profiles (approximating genre) and testing author fingerprints within each cluster, we find non‑zero within‑genre fingerprint rates. Similarly, comparing fingerprint strengths between modern and classical authors shows comparable effect sizes.
- **Core assumption:** Authorial style in novelty dynamics contains a component that is independent of genre constraints and temporal publishing conventions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "The fingerprint persists for 7–25% of authors within genre clusters and is similarly strong for classical (pre‑1930) authors, suggesting it reflects genuine authorial style rather than modern publishing artifacts."
  - [section]: Experiment 6 reports within‑cluster fingerprint rates: overall 13.6%, but 7.6% in the most formulaic (“Flat”) cluster and 25% in the more literary (“Mid Dip”) cluster. Experiment 5 shows classical authors like Twain (effect=2.32, p<0.001) and Austen (1.73, p<0.001) have significant fingerprints.
  - [corpus]: The PG‑19 corpus (pre‑1920) enables cross‑era validation; Books3 enables genre clustering via unsupervised SAX profile clustering.
- **Break condition:** If genre were the sole determinant, within‑genre fingerprint rates would be near zero; while reduced, they are not, suggesting other factors at play. However, the signal may still be partially confounded.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Semantic novelty (information‑theoretic surprisal)  
  - **Why needed here:** The entire method starts by representing each book as a novelty curve, which measures semantic discontinuity between consecutive paragraphs via embedding cosine distance.  
  - **Quick check question:** How is the novelty value between paragraph i and i+1 computed? (Answer: \(n_i = 1 - \frac{e_i \cdot e_{i+1}}{\|e_i\|\|e_{i+1}\|}\))

- **Concept:** SAX (Symbolic Aggregate approXimation)  
  - **Why needed here:** SAX converts continuous novelty curves into discrete symbolic strings so that motif frequencies (k‑grams) can be extracted and compared across books using text mining techniques.  
  - **Quick check question:** After Piecewise Aggregate Approximation (PAA), what two steps does SAX apply before generating the symbolic string? (Answer: z‑normalization, then discretization via Gaussian quantile breakpoints.)

- **Concept:** Multi‑scale analysis in computational stylometry  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper finds that discriminative features operate at different temporal granularities (book‑level scalars vs. chapter‑level motifs), requiring analysis at multiple scales to capture full authorial fingerprint.  
  - **Quick check question:** Which feature type dominates at book level and which at chapter level? (Answer: Scalars dominate at book level; SAX motifs dominate at chapter level.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. Text ingestion & preprocessing: Load raw text, split into paragraphs.
  2. Embedding: Compute sentence‑transformer embeddings (e.g., Nomic Embed Text v1.5) for each paragraph.
  3. Novelty curve generation: Compute cosine distance between consecutive paragraph embeddings.
  4. Feature extraction branch A (scalar): Compute mean novelty, speed, volume, circuitousness, etc.
  5. Feature extraction branch B (SAX motifs): Apply PAA, z‑normalize, discretize to SAX string; extract k‑gram motifs; optionally aggregate over sliding windows.
  6. Evaluation: For each author, compute centroid of feature distributions; measure intra‑author consistency (JSD) vs. null; compute top‑k attribution by nearest centroid.
  7. Statistical testing: Permutation tests for significance; compute effect sizes.

- **Critical path:** Raw text → Paragraphs → Embeddings → Novelty curve → SAX (or scalars) → Motif/feature vector → Author centroids → Attribution/consistency metrics. The most critical steps are correct novelty curve computation and SAX parameter choices (PAA segments, alphabet size, k‑gram length).

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - SAX resolution (PAA segments): Higher resolution captures finer temporal patterns but requires longer books and yields sparser motif vectors. Paper shows monotonic improvement in significance rate up to 64 segments, but with diminishing returns.
  - Window size (for sliding analysis): Smaller windows focus on local rhythms but may be noisy; larger windows approximate book‑level. Paper finds W=20 (≈ chapter) optimal for SAX.
  - Feature combination: Combining high‑dimensional motif features with low‑dimensional scalars can dilute signal due to curse of dimensionality; scalars alone are strongest at book level.
  - Embedding model: Choice of sentence transformer affects novelty curves; paper uses Nomic Embed Text v1.5, trained for long‑context retrieval.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low within‑author JSD consistency across all authors (near null) suggests feature extraction or evaluation error.
  - Top‑1 attribution at chance level (1/N_authors) indicates no discriminative signal; check if scalars yield ~0.1% on Books3 (chance ~0.13%) or if SAX yields ~0.9% at baseline.
  - No improvement with increasing PAA resolution (should increase significance rate and effect size).
  - Combined features outperforming scalar‑only (opposite of paper’s finding) may indicate improper feature scaling or inclusion of irrelevant dimensions.
  - Extreme variance in effect sizes across authors (e.g., many anti‑fingerprints) could be due to genre mismatch or corpus heterogeneity.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline SAX motif analysis:** Use PAA=16, alpha=5, 4‑gram motifs on Books3. Expect ~13.6% significant authors (p<0.05) and top‑1 accuracy ~0.9% (7× chance). Validate against Table 1 and Figure 1.
  2. **Resolution scaling:** Vary PAA segments (16,32,64) with 4‑gram; expect monotonic increase in % significant and mean effect size. Also test k‑gram lengths at PAA=64; expect top‑1 improvement from 1.7% (4‑gram) to 2.4% (5‑gram) and 2.7% (6‑gram), but significance rate drops at 6‑gram. Validate against Table 2 and Figure 2.
  3. **Multi‑feature comparison:** Compare four feature sets: scalars only, SAX only, PAA vectors only, all combined. Expect scalars to dominate at book level (43.3% sig, 3.8% top‑1) and combined to degrade (18.3% sig, 2.3% top‑1). Validate against Table 3 and Figure 3.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can novelty curve features enhance traditional lexical stylometry when combined in an ensemble classifier?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion explicitly proposes "combining novelty fingerprints with lexical stylometry" to improve ensemble classifiers, but this remains untested.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only compares novelty curve features; it does not integrate them with lexical features.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train and test ensemble models that fuse novelty curve features with standard lexical features on authorship attribution benchmarks (e.g., the PAN competition datasets), measuring whether combined performance exceeds state‑of‑the‑art lexical methods alone.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Are novelty curve fingerprints sufficiently robust and discriminative for forensic attribution in disputed authorship cases?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion suggests applying the method to "disputed attribution case studies (Shakespeare apocrypha, anonymous Federalist Papers)" to test its forensic value.
- **Why unresolved:** The study validates the fingerprint on large corpora with known authors under ideal conditions; it has not been stress‑tested on real historical disputes where sample sizes are small, texts are fragmentary, and scholarly consensus is contested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the full pipeline (novelty curve computation, SAX motif extraction, scalar dynamics) to canonical disputed cases, reporting both quantitative attribution scores and qualitative pattern analysis, then compare findings with existing scholarly attributions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do AI‑generated longform texts exhibit novelty curve dynamics that are distinguishable from human author fingerprints?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The implications section identifies "AI‑generated text detection" as a key application, suggesting that synthetic narratives may lack human‑like novelty rhythms.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper analyzes only human‑authored texts from Books3 and PG‑19; no comparison with AI‑generated outputs is provided, leaving the hypothesis speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Generate longform narratives (e.g., chapters or short stories) using state‑of‑the‑art LLMs (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude), compute their novelty curves and SAX motifs, and statistically compare distributions (e.g., JSD, effect sizes) against those of human authors. Look for systematic deviations (e.g., reduced variance, different motif frequencies) that could serve as detectors.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent are observed author fingerprints confounded with genre, and can robust within‑genre signals be isolated using high‑quality genre metadata?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Experiment 6 and the Limitations section state that "genre confounding is real" and that metadata quality is insufficient for fine‑grained separation; they show fingerprint rates drop from 13.6% to 7.6% in "formulaic" clusters but rise to 25% in "literary" clusters.
- **Why unresolved:** Their genre control uses crude clustering on novelty profiles (k‑means) rather than authoritative genre labels; thus, "within‑cluster" results may still conflate sub‑genre and author effects.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re‑analyze the corpora using detailed, standardized genre classifications (e.g., Library of Congress categories, BISAC codes) for each book. For authors who write multiple books within a narrow sub‑genre (e.g., historical romance, cozy mystery), compute fingerprint significance and effect sizes. The signal should persist for truly author‑specific pacing independent of sub‑genre conventions.

## Limitations
- Dataset and preprocessing details (exact Books3/PG‑19 splits, paragraphization rules) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- SAX implementation specifics (normalization scope, breakpoint calculation) are not fully documented, risking methodological drift.
- Genre control relies on unsupervised clustering; true genre labels are unavailable, so within‑genre tests are approximate, and effect sizes are modest (~4% top‑1), making conclusions sensitive to corpus composition.

## Confidence
- **Book‑level scalar fingerprint (43.3% of authors):** High
- **Chapter‑level SAX motif fingerprint (30.5× above‑chance):** High
- **Persistence within genre/era (7–25% within‑cluster):** Medium
