# EpiQAL: Benchmarking Large Language Models in Epidemiological Question Answering for Enhanced Alignment and Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03471
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03471
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLMs achieve near-perfect recall (EM 0.928) on text-grounded questions but suffer significant drops on multi-step inference (EM 0.868 with CoT) and conclusion reconstruction (EM 0.812), indicating distinct reasoning challenges.

## Executive Summary
EpiQAL is the first benchmark designed to evaluate population-level epidemiological reasoning over scientific literature. It comprises three subsets: A (text-grounded recall), B (multi-step inference requiring integration of evidence), and C (conclusion reconstruction with the Discussion section masked). The dataset was constructed through an automated pipeline guided by an expert-curated taxonomy, multi-LLM verification, and difficulty screening. Evaluation of 14 state-of-the-art large language models shows that while recall performance is high (top Exact Match 0.928), inference and reconstruction remain challenging (top EM 0.868 and 0.812, respectively). Chain-of-Thought prompting improves inference but can harm reconstruction, and model scale does not consistently predict success, highlighting the need for specialized reasoning capabilities.

## Method Summary
The benchmark uses a corpus of 500 full-text articles from PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases. Articles are parsed into standard sections (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion). An expert-defined taxonomy (6 classes, 25 topics) guides question intent for subsets A and B. A generation model (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct) produces questions, correct options, and distractors under subset-specific constraints; for B, external knowledge graphs (eKG-DONs, iBKH) are consulted but only during generation. Each option is independently checked by three diverse LLMs (GPT-5-mini, DeepSeek-Reasoner, GLM-4.5-Air) across three samples, yielding nine votes. An auto-accept/reject threshold (≥6/9 accept, ≤4/9 reject) resolves >96% of cases, with borderline instances (2–4/9 accept) sent for human review. Subsets B and C undergo difficulty control using a judging pool; stems failing a threshold (DiffScore < 0.2) are iteratively refined by replacing salient entities with web-derived descriptions. The final dataset maintains high quality while covering a broad range of epidemiological reasoning skills.

## Key Results
- Top model Exact Match scores: EpiQAL-A (recall) 0.928, EpiQAL-B (multi-step inference) 0.868 (with CoT), EpiQAL-C (conclusion reconstruction) 0.812.
- Model rankings vary significantly across subsets, and parameter count does not correlate with performance, indicating that scale alone is insufficient for epidemiological reasoning.
- Chain-of-Thought prompting improves inference (EpiQAL-B) but yields mixed or negative effects on reconstruction (EpiQAL-C); distractor analysis shows reasoning-level traps uniformly deceive on B, while semantic near-misses dominate failures on A.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Text-Grounded Recall Isolation
**Claim**: EpiQAL-A isolates the capability to retrieve and ground answers in explicit textual evidence, measuring foundational fact-recall without requiring inference.
**Mechanism**: It presents questions whose correct answers are verbatim or directly extractable from the source document. Distractors are valid facts from the same passage but incorrect for the specific query (e.g., wrong entity, context, or metric). This forces models to locate precise evidence rather than rely on parametric knowledge or surface pattern matching.
**Core assumption**: Reliable epidemiological reasoning requires a baseline of accurate evidence retrieval before synthesis.
**Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "...three subsets: A (text-grounded recall)..."; [section 3.2] "EpiQAL-A contains retrieval-based questions whose correct options Oc are explicitly stated in the source document D."; [corpus] The dataset construction pipeline (§3.4, §4.2) ensures options are passage-grounded via multi-model verification against the document.
**Break condition**: If models achieve near-perfect scores on EpiQAL-A but fail on B/C, the benchmark successfully separates recall from higher-order reasoning. A failure mode is models succumbing to "semantic near-miss" distractors (§4.4, Table 5), suggesting they match on surface form without verifying precise alignment with the query.

### Mechanism 2: Multi-Step Inference Evaluation
**Claim**: EpiQAL-B evaluates the ability to perform multi-step inference by integrating disparate pieces of evidence from a document with epidemiological principles to reach a conclusion not explicitly stated.
**Mechanism**: Questions require combining at least two separate evidence spans and applying a domain principle (e.g., causal direction, study design interpretation). Distractors embed reasoning-level flaws (e.g., causal reversal, entity misattribution) that appear plausible only if the inference chain is broken. External knowledge graphs are used *only during generation* to inspire inference-oriented questions but are withheld at test time.
**Core assumption**: Multi-step inference is a distinct and challenging capability for LLMs, separable from simple recall.
**Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "...multi-step inference... requiring models to integrate multiple findings to reach a conclusion not stated in any single sentence."; [section 3.2] "EpiQAL-B targets inference that links multiple cues in D with epidemiological knowledge... Correct options Oc express derived implications rather than passage restatements."; [section 4.5] "Multi-step inference is the primary bottleneck... Performance drops most steeply on EpiQAL-B."
**Break condition**: If difficulty control via stem refinement (§3.5, Appendix B.5) does not increase model error rates (§F.1), the mechanism fails to create a meaningful inference gap. High and uniform deception rates across distractor categories (Table 5) indicate the mechanism successfully creates reasoning traps.

### Mechanism 3: Critical Appraisal via Masked Discussion
**Claim**: EpiQAL-C tests "critical appraisal" by requiring models to reconstruct what conclusions are justified by the presented evidence *when author interpretations (Discussion) are masked*.
**Mechanism**: The Discussion section of the paper is removed from the model's input. Correct answers are conclusions from the actual Discussion, but models must determine if they are logically derivable from the Methods and Results alone. Distractors are plausible but unsupported claims (e.g., speculation, external dependencies, causal reversals) that would be tempting if one relied on prior beliefs or implicit assumptions.
**Core assumption**: A key epidemiological skill is distinguishing between what the data directly supports and what is speculative or externally justified.
**Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "conclusion reconstruction with the Discussion section withheld... models must infer study conclusions from methods and results alone."; [section 3.2] "EpiQAL-C evaluates reconstruction of author-stated conclusions when the Discussion section Dd is masked."; [section 4.4] "Speculation/limitation distractors are the most deceptive (21.0%), suggesting models struggle to distinguish untested hypotheses from evidence-supported conclusions."
**Break condition**: If models perform similarly on EpiQAL-C with and without the Discussion section, the masking mechanism fails to create a meaningful information gap. A success signal is lower performance on C vs. A/B and sensitivity to distractor types like "external dependency."

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Epidemiological Reasoning
- *Why needed:* The benchmark targets population-level inference (e.g., transmission dynamics, intervention effects) distinct from clinical patient-level QA. Understanding core epidemiological concepts (incidence, attack rate, confounding) is necessary to parse questions and options.
- *Quick check question:* What is the key difference between "incidence" and "prevalence" in the context of an outbreak investigation?

### Concept: Set-Based Evaluation (F1 & Exact Match)
- *Why needed:* EpiQAL-A allows multiple correct options per question. Evaluation must handle set prediction (precision/recall/F1) and exact set match (EM). Understanding the metric is crucial to interpret results like the F1-EM gap indicating over-selection.
- *Quick check question:* If a model selects 2 correct options and 1 distractor for a question with 2 true answers, what are its F1 and EM scores?

### Concept: Multi-Model Verification & Vote Aggregation
- *Why needed:* Automated QA construction risks factual errors. The pipeline uses a group of diverse LLMs to vote on option validity (§3.4), routing disagreements to human review. This mechanism is central to ensuring benchmark quality at scale.
- *Quick check question:* Why might using models from different "families" (e.g., GPT, DeepSeek, GLM) in the checking group reduce systematic bias compared to using multiple samples from a single model?

### Component Map

### Critical Path
Document -> [Taxonomy?] -> Mg(Q, Oc, Od) -> Checking votes -> {Auto-accept | Human review | Reject} -> [Difficulty control?] -> {Refine stem | Keep} -> Final instance.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Automation vs. Quality**: Multi-model verification resolves >96% automatically, but the remaining 2–4% human review is essential for catching subtle equivalence; reducing review risks noise.
- **Difficulty Control Efficacy**: Stem refinement yields only modest difficulty increases (mean DiffScore +0.01–0.02) and can over-expand, inadvertently providing hints; the θd=0.2 threshold balances effort vs. gain.
- **Knowledge Graph Utility**: For EpiQAL-B, external KGs contributed minimally (5.6% of instances used knowledge) due to the high information density of full-text papers, suggesting KG augmentation may be less useful for richly detailed sources.

### Failure Signatures
- High instance discard rate (>5%) in verification pipeline indicates generation model producing systematically invalid options.
- Uniformly high deception rates across all distractor categories on a subset (like EpiQAL-B) suggest the questions are too hard or models lack the targeted reasoning skill.
- F1-EM gap >0.4 on a model indicates chronic over-selection (picking plausible distractors), common in smaller models on B/C.
- Stem refinement not increasing DiffScore suggests the mechanism is ineffective for that topic or the base instance was already hard.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline Evaluation**: Run zero-shot (noCOT) and zero-shot CoT on all 14 models (§4.3). Confirm performance hierarchy: A >> B > C for most models, and identify outliers (e.g., Mistral-7B on C).
2. **Distractor Error Analysis**: Classify model misselections by distractor category (§4.4, Appendix F.2). Identify if a model is uniformly vulnerable or weak on specific traps (e.g., "Semantic near-miss" on A, "Speculation/limitation" on C).
3. **Stem Refinement Ablation**: For EpiQAL-C, evaluate the difficulty judging pool on the original stems vs. iteratively refined stems (Table 12). Verify that refinement increases difficulty (lower EM) without excessive over-expansion.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** What specific architectural or training improvements enable LLMs to achieve consistent high performance across all three epidemiological reasoning subsets (recall, multi-step inference, conclusion reconstruction), given that current model rankings shift dramatically across subsets and scale alone does not predict success?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors state that model rankings shift across subsets and scale does not predict success, noting outliers like Mistral-7B.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper identifies this as a core finding but does not investigate what those capabilities are (e.g., instruction-tuning quality, architectural choices, pretraining data). Understanding these factors is critical for developing models suited for epidemiological reasoning.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies comparing models with similar scales but different architectures/training; analysis of activation patterns or attention mechanisms during multi-step inference; controlled fine-tuning experiments targeting epidemiological reasoning skills.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Why does Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting uniformly improve performance on multi-step inference (EpiQAL-B) but yield mixed or negative effects on conclusion reconstruction (EpiQAL-C), and how can prompting strategies be optimized for masked-input reasoning?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors report that CoT benefits multi-step inference but yields mixed results elsewhere; e.g., Llama-3.1-8B improves on EpiQAL-C, while GPT-5-mini degrades.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper hypothesizes that CoT may amplify errors when evidence is incomplete but does not test why this occurs. Is it due to hallucination in reasoning chains, over-reliance on parametric knowledge, or misalignment between generated reasoning and the constrained evidence?  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Error analysis comparing CoT traces for correct vs. incorrect answers on EpiQAL-C; controlled experiments varying the verbosity or structure of CoT prompts; measuring faithfulness of generated reasoning to the provided passage body.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can the automated benchmark construction pipeline be scaled to larger, more diverse corpora (e.g., multilingual epidemiological literature, non-tropical diseases) while maintaining the current quality control standards without proportional increases in human review?  
**Basis in paper:** Limitations state that the corpus is solely from PLOS NTD and English, underrepresenting other domains and multilingual settings. The pipeline discards ~2.2–3.6% of instances after human review.  
**Why unresolved:** Scaling to broader domains may increase uncertainty and the fraction needing review, raising costs. The paper does not explore whether verification thresholds can be adaptively set or alternative quality signals could reduce human burden.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the pipeline to a new multilingual or disease-diverse corpus, measure the human review rate, and correlate it with metadata (domain novelty, language, text complexity). Develop and test adaptive verification thresholds based on model disagreement or confidence scores.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How can LLMs be made robust to the specific reasoning-level distractors that uniformly deceive them on multi-step inference (e.g., variable confusion, causal reversal), given that deception rates remain high (~15–20%) even for top models?  
**Basis in paper:** Distractor analysis shows uniform deception rates across categories on EpiQAL-B (15.9–19.2%), with Variable confusion slightly leading; even DeepSeek-Reasoner has >6% deception.  
**Why unresolved:** The distractor design includes subtle flaws (e.g., flipping causal direction, misattributing entities). High deception rates indicate current models fail to consistently validate logical consistency or epidemiological principles. The paper does not analyze why these traps work or how training could address them.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Detailed case studies of model failures on each distractor category; probing whether models rely on spurious correlations or lack explicit knowledge of epidemiological principles; fine-tuning on adversarial examples that target these specific flaws.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** To what extent do knowledge graphs like eKG-DONs and iBKH genuinely augment epidemiological reasoning in LLMs when source documents are information-dense, and how can knowledge grounding be improved to avoid mere corroboration of existing passage evidence?  
**Basis in paper:** Of 478 instances, only 27 (5.6%) incorporated retrieved knowledge; in every case, the external knowledge provided only surface-level information. The authors attribute this to high information density of source documents and coarse granularity of general-purpose KGs.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper does not systematically evaluate how different knowledge representations could contribute to inference. This is key for future work on knowledge-augmented epidemiological QA.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments comparing different knowledge sources (specialized vs. general KGs, extracted relations vs. raw text) on newly constructed items where external knowledge is *necessary* for inference; analysis of model performance on items where knowledge was successfully used vs. not.

## Limitations
- The exact prompts for generation, verification, and evaluation, as well as parameters for stem refinement (web search engine, snippet count, summarization method), are not disclosed, hindering full replication.
- The external knowledge graphs (eKG-DONs, iBKH) used for EpiQAL-B are not released, making it impossible to assess their contribution or reproduce the generation process faithfully.
- Article sampling details (exact list, random seed) and human review guidelines are unreported, which may affect reproducibility of the dataset splits and quality control.
