# Discounted Beta--Bernoulli Reward Estimation for Sample-Efficient Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18444
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18444
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: RLVR with DBB reduces variance and prevents collapse, yielding +3.22 points in-distribution and +12.49 out-of-distribution on Qwen3-1.7B, and +2.42/+6.92 on Qwen3-8B, with negligible overhead.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) suffers from sample inefficiency because small rollout batches yield high-variance point estimates of reward quality, often causing variance collapse in group-relative advantage calculations. This paper recasts RLVR as reward distribution estimation and introduces Discounted Beta–Bernoulli (DBB) estimation. DBB treats each binary reward as a Bernoulli sample and maintains a Beta posterior over the unknown success probability per prompt. By discounting historical observations with a factor $\lambda\in(0,1]$, DBB blends recent data with a weighted historical mean, adapting to the non‑stationary policy‑induced distribution. Although biased, DBB achieves dramatically lower variance and mean squared error than point estimation, and its posterior variance is strictly positive, preventing collapse. Extensive experiments on Qwen3‑1.7B‑Base and Qwen3‑8B‑Base across six in‑distribution and three out‑of‑distribution mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that GRPO with DBB (GRPO‑DBB) consistently outperforms naive GRPO and RePO, with average accuracy gains of 3.22 and 12.49 points (in‑ and out‑of‑distribution) for the 1.7B model, and 2.42 and 6.92 points for the 8B model, while adding no computational or memory cost beyond two scalars per prompt.

## Method Summary
DBB models each binary reward as a Bernoulli sample with unknown success probability $p_\tau$. For every training prompt, a Beta($\alpha,\beta$) posterior over $p_\tau$ is maintained across rollouts. When a new batch of $N$ rollouts is generated, the previous counts are scaled by a discount factor $\lambda$ before incorporating the new successes $S$ and failures $N{-}S$: $\alpha_\tau = \lambda\alpha_{\tau-1} + S$, $\beta_\tau = \lambda\beta_{\tau-1} + (N{-}S)$. This yields a smoothed mean estimate $\hat{p}_{\text{dbb}} = \alpha_\tau/(\alpha_\tau+\beta_\tau)$ and a variance estimate $dVar_{\text{dbb}} = \alpha_\tau\beta_\tau/(\alpha_\tau+\beta_\tau)^2$. In group‑relative policy optimization (GRPO), the advantage for a response is $(X_i - \hat{p}_{\text{dbb}})/\sqrt{dVar_{\text{dbb}}+\varepsilon}$. The discount $\lambda$ trades bias for variance; because $\alpha,\beta>0$ always (initialized at 1), the variance never collapses to zero even if all current rewards are identical. DBB requires storing only two floats per prompt and integrates seamlessly with existing GRPO, Dr.GRPO, or GPPO frameworks.

## Key Results
- On Qwen3-1.7B-Base, GRPO-DBB achieves average Acc@8 gains of **+3.22** points on six in-distribution benchmarks and **+12.49** points on three out-of-distribution benchmarks versus vanilla GRPO.
- On Qwen3-8B-Base, gains are **+2.42** (in-distribution) and **+6.92** (out-of-distribution).
- DBB matches or exceeds RePO while using orders of magnitude less memory, and maintains stable variance without extra computation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### 1. Variance Reduction via Bayesian Shrinkage
DBB reduces estimator variance by shrinking the point estimate toward a historical mean through Bayesian posterior averaging. Each reward is Bernoulli($p_\tau$); a Beta($\alpha,\beta$) posterior is updated additively. A discount factor $\lambda$ multiplies the previous ($\alpha,\beta$) before the update, blending recent data with a weighted historical mean. This shrinkage lowers variance at the cost of a small bias, assuming the reward distribution evolves slowly enough that past observations retain signal.  
*Evidence:* Update rule $\alpha_\tau = \lambda\alpha_{\tau-1} + S_\tau$, $\beta_\tau = \lambda\beta_{\tau-1} + (N-S_\tau)$ and variance expression $\text{Var}(\hat{p}_{\text{dbb}}) = (1-w)^2 p(1-p)/N$ (Sec 3.2).  
*Break condition:* Abrupt shifts in $p_\tau$ make the historical mean misleading, potentially increasing MSE.

### 2. Prevention of Variance Collapse
In GRPO the sample variance of rewards can become zero if all $N$ rollouts yield identical rewards, collapsing the advantage signal. DBB avoids this by deriving variance from the Beta posterior: $dVar_{\text{dbb}} = \alpha\beta/(\alpha+\beta)^2$. Since $\alpha,\beta$ are always positive (initialized at 1 and incremented by counts), this variance is strictly positive even when $S_\tau=0$ or $S_\tau=N$, guaranteeing a non‑zero denominator in advantage calculation and stable training.  
*Evidence:* Section 3.2 states the posterior variance cannot collapse to zero.  
*Break condition:* If $\lambda$ is extremely low, posterior parameters could shrink numerically toward zero in edge cases, though initialization prevents exact zero.

### 3. Bias–Variance Trade‑off for Lower MSE
DBB’s mean squared error decomposes into bias² and variance. The variance term shrinks with $(1-w)^2$ where $w$ depends on $\lambda$ and total historical mass; bias grows with the discrepancy between historical mean and current $p_\tau$. For moderate $N$ (e.g., 8) and a smooth non‑stationary sequence, variance reduction often outweighs the bias, yielding lower MSE than the unbiased point estimator $\hat{p}_{\text{pt}}=S/N$ whose variance is $p(1-p)/N$.  
*Evidence:* Section 3.3 derives MSE formulas; Figure 3 empirically shows lower MSE for $\lambda{=}0.4$ and $N{\le}16$. Ablations (Sec 5.2) find optimal $\lambda$ around 0.5 for the 1.7B model and 0.75 for the 8B model, reflecting differing smoothness.  
*Break condition:* Highly volatile $p_\tau$ or poorly tuned $\lambda$ can cause bias to dominate.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Bayesian Inference (Beta‑Bernoulli Model)**  
  *Why needed:* DBB is built on the Beta‑Bernoulli conjugate prior. Understanding how the Beta parameters act as pseudo‑counts and how the posterior mean provides shrinkage is essential to grasp the variance reduction mechanism.  
  *Quick check:* Starting from Beta(1,1) (uniform), if you observe 3 successes and 5 failures from rollouts, what are the posterior parameters and the estimated reward probability?

- **Concept: Variance Collapse in Group‑Based RL**  
  *Why needed:* The core problem DBB addresses is variance collapse in GRPO/RePO when the sample variance of binary rewards becomes zero with a small batch size, eliminating the advantage signal. Recognizing this failure mode motivates the need for a guaranteed positive variance estimate.  
  *Quick check:* In GRPO with $N{=}8$ rollouts, which reward patterns cause the sample variance $\sigma^2$ to be exactly zero? Why does that halt learning?

- **Concept: Non‑Stationarity in On‑Policy RL**  
  *Why needed:* The policy changes over time, making the underlying reward success probability $p_\tau$ non‑stationary. DBB’s discount factor $\lambda$ is an adaptation to this, gradually forgetting old data. Without this concept, the need for $\lambda{<}1$ is unmotivated.  
  *Quick check:* Why can’t we simply accumulate all historical reward counts forever ($\lambda{=}1.0$) in an on‑policy RL setting where the policy is constantly updated?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Rollout Buffer → Per‑Prompt Posterior Store → DBB Update Module → Advantage Calculator → Policy Optimizer → Checkpoint Manager

**Critical path:**  
`Sample minibatch (prompts)` → `Load per‑prompt posteriors (α,β)` → `Generate N rollouts per prompt` → `Compute binary rewards` → `DBB Update (new α,β)` → `Compute ˆp_dbb & dVar_dbb` → `Calculate advantages` → **`Policy gradient update (U steps)`** → `Store updated (α,β) for next iteration`.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Memory vs. Replay: DBB stores only two scalars per prompt (~100 KB for 17k prompts), drastically less than RePO which stores full trajectories and token probabilities.
- Bias vs. Variance: $\lambda$ controls the trade‑off; high $\lambda$ (near 1) yields low bias but high variance (point‑estimation‑like), while low $\lambda$ gives high bias (over‑shrinkage) but low variance. Optimal $\lambda$ depends on model and data (paper finds 0.5 for 1.7B, 0.75 for 8B).
- Generality: DBB only replaces the reward distribution estimator; it is compatible with any group‑relative advantage method (GRPO, Dr.GRPO, GPPO).

**Failure signatures:**
- Stale estimates: $\lambda$ too high with fast policy changes leads to outdated $\hat{p}_{\text{dbb}}$, slowing learning.
- Over‑shrinkage: $\lambda$ too low makes $\hat{p}_{\text{dbb}}$ dominated by prior/historical data, creating persistent bias that caps performance.
- Numerical instability: forgetting to persist $(\alpha,\beta)$ with checkpoints resets the estimator, causing variance spikes and unstable early steps after restart.
- OOD performance drop: extreme domain shift may render in‑distribution historical mean a poor prior; a lower $\lambda$ (faster forgetting) may be needed.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Lambda sweep on small subset:** Train on 1% of DAPO‑Math‑17k for 1 epoch across $\lambda\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}$. Monitor validation Acc@8 and average MSE (against a held‑out 128‑rollout sample). Expect a bell‑shaped performance/MSE curve vs $\lambda$.
2. **Variance collapse test:** For a prompt where all $N{=}8$ rollouts are correct (or all incorrect), compute $dVar_{\text{dbb}}$ at $\lambda{=}0.5$ and $\lambda{=}1.0$. Verify $dVar_{\text{dbb}}(\lambda{=}0.5)>0$ while the point‑estimate variance is 0, and that the resulting advantages remain non‑zero.
3. **Posterior persistence check:** Run GRPO‑DBB for 10 steps; at step 5, manually reset $(\alpha,\beta)$ to $(1,1)$ for 10% of prompts. Compare the reward curve and validation accuracy of reset vs. untouched prompts; the reset group should exhibit higher variance and lower performance, confirming the value of accumulated history.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1:** How can the discount factor $\lambda$ in DBB be made adaptive to different stages of training or varying non‑stationarity in the reward distribution?  
  - *Basis in paper:* The authors state in the conclusion: “As future work, we plan to design a dynamic discount factor $\lambda$ that adapts across different stages of training.” They also empirically select $\lambda$ for different model scales and note that $\lambda$ should match learning dynamics.  
  - *Why unresolved:* The current approach uses a fixed $\lambda$ chosen via validation, which may not optimally balance bias‑variance as training progresses or across tasks.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement and evaluate adaptive schemes (e.g., based on reward variance, gradient norms, or temporal‑difference errors) and demonstrate improved performance or robustness over fixed $\lambda$ across diverse settings.

- **Open Question 2:** Can the DBB framework be extended beyond binary verifiable rewards to handle continuous or sparse reward signals while maintaining low variance and avoiding collapse?  
  - *Basis in paper:* The method relies on Beta‑Bernoulli conjugacy, which is specific to binary rewards. The paper focuses on verifiable rewards that are binary (correct/incorrect).  
  - *Why unresolved:* Many RL settings have continuous or sparse rewards; extending DBB would require different conjugate priors or approximations, and it is unclear if the variance reduction benefits hold.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Develop a generalized DBB for other distributions (e.g., Gaussian‑inverse‑Gamma for continuous rewards), derive analogous properties (bias, variance), and test on synthetic and real‑world tasks with non‑binary rewards.

- **Open Question 3:** Is it possible to combine the low‑overhead historical reward aggregation of DBB with richer historical information (e.g., token‑level probabilities) to further improve sample efficiency without significant memory cost?  
  - *Basis in paper:* DBB uses only reward statistics, while RePO uses full trajectories but with high memory cost. The paper shows DBB outperforms RePO despite using less history, suggesting potential for synergy.  
  - *Why unresolved:* It is unknown whether incorporating additional historical signals can enhance DBB’s estimator while keeping memory overhead low. The trade‑off between information and efficiency is not explored.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Design a hybrid method that compresses trajectory information into sufficient statistics (e.g., using exponential families) and integrates with DBB updates. Compare against DBB and RePO in terms of performance and memory usage.

- **Open Question 4:** What are the theoretical guarantees of DBB in terms of regret or convergence under non‑stationary reward distributions with unknown transition dynamics and finite rollouts?  
  - *Basis in paper:* The paper provides MSE analysis assuming known $p_{1:\tau}$ and fixed $N$, but in practice $p_\tau$ is unknown and changes. The analysis does not cover online learning regret or convergence rates.  
  - *Why unresolved:* Without theoretical bounds, it is hard to characterize DBB’s performance in worst‑case non‑stationary scenarios or relate $\lambda$ to stability.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Derive regret bounds for DBB in non‑stationary Bernoulli bandits or MDPs, and empirically validate on synthetic non‑stationary environments with abrupt changes.

## Limitations
- Implementation ambiguities: exact clipping bounds interpretation, whether DBB uses posterior mean or group mean for centering, evaluation prompt templates, number of gradient updates per rollout, and timing of posterior updates are not fully specified.
- Sensitivity to $\lambda$: Extreme non‑stationarity or poorly chosen $\lambda$ can cause bias‑dominated errors, hurting performance.
- Binary‑reward restriction: The Beta‑Bernoulli model only applies to binary outcomes; extending to other reward types remains an open problem.
