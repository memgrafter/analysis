# FLUX: Data Worth Training On

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13972
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13972
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** FLUX extracts 25% more usable tokens (50B vs. DCLM’s 40B) from a single Common Crawl dump while achieving higher downstream accuracy (32.14% MMLU, 51.92% nine‑benchmark aggregate) and reducing training compute by 34.4% compared to DCLM.

## Executive Summary
FLUX rethinks web‑scale language model data preprocessing to resolve the long‑standing trade‑off between token retention and corpus quality. Its pipeline introduces efficient Apex parsing, line‑level cleaning instead of document‑level rejection, Bloom Filter deduplication, and a dual‑bin FastText classifier that merges DCLM’s world‑knowledge signal with a new benchmark‑proximate signal (BETR). Trained on FLUX data, a 3B model surpasses prior state‑of‑the‑art (DCLM, FineWeb) on MMLU and a nine‑benchmark aggregate, while requiring significantly less compute to match DCLM’s performance. Preprocessing is also 6.26× more efficient per terabyte. The work demonstrates that careful curation can yield both higher quality and greater scale.

## Method Summary
FLUX processes Common Crawl WARC files through a 13‑stage deterministic pipeline. After ingestion and parsing with the Apex parser (statistical main‑content detection), filters are applied in sequence: URL‑based pre‑filtering (blocklists and substrings), whole‑document language identification (FastText, English confidence ≥ 0.65), document‑level quality filters (Gopher, Nemo, custom), and a novel line‑level cleaning transformer that removes eleven classes of boilerplate lines. Documents losing > 5% of tokens are rejected. The surviving text undergoes global Bloom Filter fuzzy deduplication (BFF) and, finally, dual‑bin FastText classification: documents are accepted if either a general‑quality classifier (ϕ_DCLM) or a benchmark‑aligned classifier (ϕ_BETR) scores above tuned thresholds (τ_DCLM=0.025119, τ_BETR=0.76). An English‑only final corpus is produced for training an OLMo model.

## Key Results
- FLUX extracts 50 B usable tokens from one Common Crawl dump, a 25% increase over DCLM’s 40 B.
- A 3B model trained on FLUX achieves 32.14% MMLU and 51.92% on a nine‑benchmark aggregate, exceeding DCLM (31.98%, 50.48%) and FineWeb (29.88%, 48.83%).
- Reaching DCLM’s performance requires 34.4% less compute with FLUX (8.044e20 vs. 1.227e21 FLOPs); preprocessing efficiency is 6.26× higher per terabyte.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Line‑Level Cleaning Over Document Rejection
FLUX recovers usable signal by excising low‑quality lines (e.g., navigation, social CTAs, timestamps) within documents rather than rejecting entire documents. Only documents where > 5% of tokens are removed are subsequently discarded. This approach preserves high‑quality content that coexists with noise, based on the assumption that web noise is often localized rather than document‑wide. [abstract] states the trade‑off address; [section 3.2] explains the recovery of signal lost to binary rejection. The +25% retention gain and +1.44pp aggregate improvement are consistent with the mechanism, though the ablation isolating line‑level cleaning alone is not provided. If noise becomes document‑wide, line‑level excision adds overhead with minimal benefit.

### Mechanism 2: Dual‑Bin FastText Classification with Benchmark‑Proximate Signal
A logical‑OR combination of two independently calibrated FastText classifiers—ϕ_DCLM (trained on OpenHermes/ELI5) capturing world‑knowledge density and ϕ_BETR (distilled from embedding similarity to downstream benchmarks) capturing reasoning patterns proximate to evaluation—improves the quality‑retention trade‑off. Documents rejected by one bin may be accepted by the other. Thresholds (τ_DCLM=0.025119, τ_BETR=0.76) are recalibrated via downstream model evaluation. [section 3.4] notes that ϕ_DCLM and ϕ_BETR capture non‑perfectly correlated signals; their union yields a more balanced, task‑aligned corpus. [Figure 6] shows the threshold sweep optimizing aggregate score at 530M scale. If benchmark proximality correlates negatively with general utility (e.g., overfitting risk), the BETR bin could harm broad capability.

### Mechanism 3: Parser Selection with Statistical Main‑Content Detection (Apex)
Adopting the Apex parser, which uses statistical main‑content detection (content density, link density, structural depth), yields higher token yield and comparable downstream quality to more conservative parsers like Trafilatura, while being more computationally efficient than Resiliparse for large‑scale runs. Apex evaluates candidate page regions via composite scoring rather than fixed semantic tags, improving extraction from structurally irregular pages. [section 3.1, Table 3] shows Apex produces 279.5 B multilingual tokens in 4.31 h versus Resiliparse’s 279.4 B in 6.63 h and Trafilatura’s 136.7 B in 47.33 h. [Figure 4] shows downstream aggregate at 530M scale: Apex (0.4123) nearly identical to Resiliparse (0.4089) and superior to Trafilatura (0.4330) despite higher token count. If statistical detection fails on non‑standard pages (e.g., single‑page apps), Apex may underperform parsers with more aggressive heuristics.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: Heuristic Filtering Trade‑offs (Recall vs. Precision)**  
- *Why needed:* Understanding why document‑level filters hurt retention is key to appreciating FLUX’s line‑level design; engineers must see filtering as a spectrum, not a binary quality switch.  
- *Quick check question:* If a document contains 30% high‑quality text and 70% boilerplate, what is the outcome under document‑level rejection vs. line‑level excision? How does this affect the diversity of retained topics?

**Concept 2: FastText Supervised Classification for Document Quality**  
- *Why needed:* The dual‑bin system relies on FastText as a lightweight, scalable quality signal; strengths (speed, sub‑linear inference) and limitations (n‑gram features, calibration sensitivity) must be understood.  
- *Quick check question:* Why might a FastText classifier trained on OpenHermes/ELI5 (ϕ_DCLM) miss high‑quality technical documentation not represented in those datasets?

**Concept 3: Parser Architecture Impact on Corpus Scale**  
- *Why needed:* Parser choice sets an upper bound on achievable retention; FLUX’s computational efficiency partly stems from Apex’s lower runtime, enabling larger raw input processing within the same compute budget.  
- *Quick check question:* How might the choice between a “container‑first” (V1/V2) and “statistical main‑content” (Apex) extraction strategy affect token yield from a news article with many embedded widgets/ads?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
WARC Ingestion → HTML Parsing (Resiliparse) → Statistical Main‑Content Extraction (Apex) → URL‑Based Pre‑filtering (UT1 blocklist, substring filters) → Language Identification (FastText lid.176, threshold 0.65) → Document‑Level Quality Filters (Gopher, Nemo, Custom) → Line‑Level Cleaning Transformer (11 heuristics) → Integrity Filter (Word Removal Ratio ≤ 5%) → Bloom Filter Deduplication (BFF, paragraph+document level) → Dual‑Bin FastText Classification (ϕ_DCLM ∨ ϕ_BETR)

**Critical path:**  
Raw WARC files are parsed and filtered sequentially through Stages 1‑13. Deduplication (Stage 9) and classification (Stage 10) can run in parallel but must complete before final corpus assembly. Stage 6 (line‑level cleaning) is a key divergence from prior pipelines; its output quality directly impacts downstream retention.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Line‑level cleaning adds per‑line overhead but recovers documents otherwise entirely lost.
- Dual‑bin classification increases inference time (~60 min vs. 30 min per 100 B tokens) but yields better quality at similar retention.
- Parser choice trades runtime for yield/quality; Apex selected for best yield per compute hour.
- Bloom Filter false‑positive rate tuned to 10⁻³ with 100 B expected n‑grams targeting ~0.5 sparsity; tightening FP increases memory/runtime with minimal gain.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Excessive document rejection after Stage 11 (DocFilters) indicates overly aggressive thresholds; check per‑filter counts.
- Low final retention (<40 B from a single dump) suggests over‑aggressive dual‑bin thresholds or low Apex yield.
- High sparsity (>0.7) in the Bloom filter post‑run indicates undersized expected n‑gram count, causing premature duplicate flagging.
- Degraded MMLU/ARC‑Challenge relative to aggregate may indicate ϕ_BETR threshold mis‑calibration (τ_BETR too low/high).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Parser Yield Benchmark:** Run Apex, Resiliparse, and Trafilatura on 10K WARC files; compare multilingual/English token counts and wall‑clock time to verify Apex’s advantage.
2. **Filtering Ablation:** Implement four configurations (URL+LID, DocFilter, LineClean, FLUX) on a fixed WARC subset. Measure post‑deduplication token counts and train 530M models to 10.6 B tokens; compute aggregate scores to confirm FLUX’s simultaneous retention and quality gain.
3. **Dual‑Bin Threshold Sweep:** On 100 B post‑deduplication tokens, classify across a (τ_DCLM, τ_BETR) grid. Train small models on each output and identify the operating point maximizing aggregate; confirm τ_DCLM=0.025119, τ_BETR=0.76 as optimal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Question 1:** Does FLUX’s design retain its retention‑quality advantage when applied to multilingual pretraining corpora?  
*Basis:* Paper notes evaluation is English‑only.  
*Why unresolved:* Filters and classifiers are tuned for English quality signals; multilingual noise patterns and quality criteria may differ.  
*Resolution:* Construct FLUX‑style pipelines for major language families, train models, and evaluate on cross‑lingual benchmarks.

**Question 2:** How can Bloom Filter‑based fuzzy deduplication be scaled to all 128 Common Crawl snapshots without prohibitive memory costs while maintaining cross‑dump duplicate detection?  
*Basis:* Deduplication is performed across only two snapshots; scaling to the full archive is an open challenge.  
*Why unresolved:* Single‑shard BFF fits two dumps in RAM but scaling would exceed memory; multi‑shard approaches risk missing cross‑shard duplicates.  
*Resolution:* Implement a distributed BFF or alternative (e.g., MinHash LSH) on the full archive and report retention, duplicate recall, and runtime.

**Question 3:** Do FLUX’s data quality improvements persist at model scales beyond 3B parameters?  
*Basis:* All experiments are conducted up to 3B; interaction with larger regimes is unexplored.  
*Why unresolved:* Scaling laws suggest data‑quality effects may not linearly extrapolate; larger models might need different thresholds.  
*Resolution:* Train models at 10B, 30B, and 100B scales on FLUX vs. DCLM/FineWeb with matched compute, evaluating on the nine‑benchmark suite and large‑model‑specific tasks.

**Question 4:** Does FLUX‑curated data yield superior performance on real‑world, non‑benchmark tasks?  
*Basis:* Evaluation is benchmark‑driven and may not capture deployment behavior.  
*Why unresolved:* Practical applications (coding, safety, instruction following) rely on different data characteristics that FLUX’s filters affect unpredictably.  
*Resolution:* Conduct human evaluations or domain‑specific benchmarks (e.g., HumanEval, chatbot arena) on models trained on FLUX vs. prior data.

## Limitations
- Reproduction is blocked by undisclosed parameters: BFF shingle size, FastText score calibration method, Apex parser thresholds, exact benchmark splits, and UT1 blocklist/lexicon contents.
- Break conditions: If web noise is document‑wide, line‑level cleaning yields minimal benefit; if benchmark‑proximal and general knowledge signals are negatively correlated, BETR bin may harm broad capability; Apex may underperform on JavaScript‑rendered pages.
- Efficiency claims depend on specific hardware/software environment and may not transfer directly.

## Confidence
- **Line‑level cleaning advantage:** Medium (retention and quality gains consistent but lack isolated ablation).
- **Dual‑bin FastText benefit:** Medium (threshold sweep supports improvement, but calibration approach and BETR overfitting risk remain unclear).
- **Apex parser efficiency:** High (direct benchmark shows comparable quality with lower compute and higher yield than Trafilatura).
- **Compute efficiency claim:** Medium (depends on unspecified environment; numbers are given but reproducibility is uncertain).
- **Overall performance superiority:** High (multiple independent evaluations on 530M/1B/3B scales show consistent gains).

## Next Checks
1. **Line‑level cleaning ablation:** Disable Stage 12 (LineClean) on a fixed WARC subset, measure token retention after cleaning, and train a 530M model; expect a large drop in aggregate compared to full FLUX.
2. **Dual‑bin calibration reproducibility:** Retrain ϕ_BETR with different random seeds and benchmark subsets; repeat the threshold sweep to confirm stability of optimal (τ_DCLM, τ_BETR) and the magnitude of aggregate improvement.
3. **Parser benchmark replication:** On identical hardware, parse 10K WARC files with Apex, Resiliparse, and Trafilatura; compare multilingual token counts and wall‑clock time to validate Apex’s compute efficiency advantage.
