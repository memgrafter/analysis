# Why Are Linear RNNs More Parallelizable?

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID**: 2603.03612
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03612
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Linear RNNs (LRNNs) belong to PNC^1 (parallelizable with O(log* n) depth overhead), while nonlinear RNNs solve L-complete/P-complete problems, implying fundamental limits to parallelizability.

## Executive Summary

The paper investigates why linear recurrent neural networks (LRNNs) are more parallelizable than their nonlinear counterparts. By forging a rigorous connection between RNN architectures and circuit complexity classes, it reveals that LRNNs can be transformed into low‑depth circuits: specifically, they lie in PNC^1, meaning they can be parallelized with only a logarithmic‑star overhead beyond the depth of log‑depth transformers. In contrast, nonlinear RNNs are shown to capture L‑complete (with log‑precision) or even P‑complete (with polynomial precision) problems. Under widely believed complexity assumptions (e.g., NC ≠ P, L ⊈ NC^1), this implies that nonlinear RNNs cannot be efficiently parallelized to shallow circuits. The analysis further refines the hierarchy within LRNNs: diagonal‑plus‑low‑rank (DPLR) variants (e.g., DeltaNet, RWKV‑7) are PNC^1‑complete, while permutation‑diagonal (PD) variants are NC^1‑complete. Experiments on two synthetic algorithmic tasks—deterministic graph connectivity (L‑complete) and iterated matrix multiplication (PNC^1‑complete)—validate these theoretical distinctions: nonlinear RNNs excel at the graph task and generalize to longer sequences, whereas DPLR LRNNs master the matrix task where Transformers and Mamba fail.

## Method Summary

Models were evaluated on two synthetic tasks designed to probe length generalization. The deterministic graph connectivity task encodes a topologically sorted directed graph; given source and target vertices, the model must predict reachability. The iterated matrix multiplication task feeds token sequences of matrices (3×3 with entries in {‑1,0,1}) and requires predicting a property of the product (modulo a small prime or over integers). Architectures included a Transformer (4 heads), Mamba (d_state = 128 or 64), RWKV‑7/DeltaNet (4 heads), and a two‑layer Nonlinear RNN (hidden size 256, multi‑head recurrent sublayers). All models used AdamW (lr = 3e‑4, weight decay = 1e‑4, batch size = 128, gradient clipping = 1.0) with BCEWithLogitsLoss, trained for up to 60 k steps on sequences of length 1–100. Testing covered in‑distribution lengths (1–100) and out‑of‑distribution lengths (101–200, 201–300). Accuracy on 10 k test samples per length range was the primary metric.

## Key Results

- **Complexity classification**: LRNNs fall into PNC^1 (parallelizable with O(log* n) depth overhead), while nonlinear RNNs solve L‑complete (log precision) or P‑complete (polynomial precision) problems, suggesting inherent limits to parallelization under standard complexity assumptions.
- **Fine‑grained hierarchy**: Diagonal‑plus‑low‑rank (DPLR) LRNNs (e.g., DeltaNet, RWKV‑7) are PNC^1‑complete; permutation‑diagonal (PD) LRNNs are NC^1‑complete.
- **Empirical expressivity gaps**: Nonlinear RNNs nearly perfectly solve the L‑complete graph connectivity task and generalize better to longer sequences; DPLR LRNNs solve the PNC^1‑complete iterated matrix multiplication task, where Transformers and Mamba consistently fail.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The core mechanism is a tight correspondence between the sequential computation of an RNN and the depth of a simulating circuit. For an LRNN, the state after n steps is a linear combination of past inputs weighted by powers of a transition matrix M. If M is diagonal, each dimension evolves independently, yielding an NC^1 computation via parallel prefix. If M is diagonal‑plus‑low‑rank (DPLR), the low‑rank interactions introduce additional logarithmic depth, placing the overall computation in PNC^1 (polylog depth with a polynomial number of processors). In contrast, a nonlinear RNN can encode arbitrary sequential logic. By reducing known L‑complete problems (e.g., deterministic graph connectivity) to the recurrence, the RNN’s computation inherits their circuit complexity: with log‑precision it requires Ω(log² n) depth under the assumption L ⊈ NC^1, and with polynomial precision it can even solve P‑complete problems, implying even greater depth under NC ≠ P. The completeness results for DPLR (PNC^1‑complete) and PD (NC^1‑complete) follow from showing that iterated matrix multiplication (a PNC^1‑complete problem) reduces to DPLR recurrences, while PD recurrences can only express NC^1 computations. Thus, the algebraic structure of the recurrence directly determines parallelizability.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Nonlinear RNN: Input tokens → Token Embedding → [Multi‑head RNN sublayer → Two‑layer ReLU FFN] × 2 → Output projection.  
DPLR LRNN (e.g., DeltaNet, RWKV‑7): Input tokens → Token Embedding → Linear recurrence (hₜ = (D + UVᵀ)hₜ₋₁ + Wxₜ) → (optional elementwise mixing) → Output projection.

**Critical Path**  
The recurrent state update hₜ = f(hₜ₋₁, xₜ) is inherently sequential in the vanilla view. For LRNNs, the linearity allows the entire unrolled computation to be represented as a product of matrices, which can be parallelized (e.g., via parallel prefix). For nonlinear RNNs, the nonlinearity breaks associativity, forcing a circuit depth that matches the problem’s complexity (e.g., Ω(log² n) for L‑complete tasks).

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Expressivity vs Parallelizability*: Nonlinear RNNs can solve L‑complete/P‑complete tasks but are hard to parallelize; LRNNs are highly parallelizable but cannot capture certain nonlinear dependencies.  
- *Memory and Efficiency*: LRNNs often require storing only the current state (O(d²) parameters) and can be trained with linear‑time parallel scans; nonlinear RNNs may need back‑propagation through time and suffer from vanishing/exploding gradients.  
- *Length Generalization*: DPLR LRNNs generalize well on algebraic tasks (e.g., matrix multiplication); nonlinear RNNs show better generalization on graph‑structured reasoning; Transformers without relative position struggle on both.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Nonlinear RNN fails on graph connectivity ⇒ insufficient nonlinear capacity or poor recurrence design.  
- LRNN fails on matrix multiplication ⇒ low‑rank rank too small, missing key interactions, or numerical instability in unbounded arithmetic.  
- Transformer fails on both ⇒ lack of appropriate inductive bias (e.g., relative position, recurrence).

**First Experiments**  
1. Train a Nonlinear RNN on the deterministic graph connectivity task with small graphs (≤10 nodes); expect near‑perfect accuracy. Train a DPLR LRNN on the same task; expect poor performance.  
2. Train a DPLR LRNN on the iterated matrix multiplication (modulo variant); expect >95% accuracy. Train a Transformer baseline; expect failure as sequence length grows.  
3. Measure length generalization: train each model on maximum sequence length 50, evaluate on lengths up to 200, and plot accuracy vs. length to confirm the reported trends (nonlinear RNN degrades gracefully, DPLR holds for matrix task but not for graph).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Is Ω(log² n) depth fundamental for parallelizing log‑precision nonlinear RNNs?**  
   *Basis*: Corollary 4 ties this to whether L‑complete problems admit NC circuits of o(log² n) depth (Allender, 2023).  
   *Why unresolved*: L ⊆ NC² gives an O(log² n) upper bound; lower bounds require L ⊈ NC¹, which remains unproven.  
   *Evidence needed*: Prove L ⊈ NC¹ or construct an L‑complete problem in NC with o(log² n) depth.

2. **What circuit class (TC⁰ vs NC¹ vs PNC¹) contains other LRNN variants (e.g., gated, different low‑rank)?**  
   *Basis*: The paper shows DPLR LRNNs are PNC¹‑complete, PD LRNNs are NC¹‑complete; S4/Mamba lie in TC⁰.  
   *Why unresolved*: The fine‑grained hierarchy for practical architectures is unknown; intermediate classes may better balance expressivity and parallelism.  
   *Evidence needed*: Prove tight upper/lower bounds for specific LRNNs (e.g., xLSTM, gated linear) or show architectural constraints force membership in a known class.

3. **Do finite‑precision/floating‑point effects invalidate the PNC¹ parallelizability guarantees?**  
   *Basis*: Theory assumes exact rational arithmetic; real hardware uses rounding and bounded precision.  
   *Why unresolved*: Numerical errors could alter computational power; asymptotic depth analyses assume ideal gates.  
   *Evidence needed*: Test long‑sequence arithmetic tasks (e.g., iterated matrix multiplication) under varying precisions; extend theory to approximate/noisy circuits.

4. **Does theoretical expressivity (nonlinear RNNs solving L‑complete) translate to real‑world language modeling gains?**  
   *Basis*: Synthetic tasks show nonlinear RNNs better at length‑generalizable reasoning; LRNNs handle algebraic tasks.  
   *Why unresolved*: Language modeling is statistical; worst‑case complexity may not predict average‑case performance.  
   *Evidence needed*: Compare model classes on large benchmarks with length generalization (e.g., needle‑in‑haystack, multi‑hop QA); correlate theoretical class with in‑context learning.

## Limitations

- The Nonlinear RNN baseline’s exact architecture (cell type, multihead implementation) is unspecified, hindering exact reproduction.
- Experiments are limited to small‑scale synthetic tasks; performance on large real‑world datasets remains unknown.
- Theoretical results depend on unproven complexity separations (e.g., NC ≠ P, L ⊈ NC¹), making the impossibility claims conditional.

## Confidence

- Mapping LRNNs to PNC^1: **High**  
- DPLR completeness (PNC^1‑complete) and PD completeness (NC^1‑complete): **High**  
- Nonlinear RNNs solving L‑complete/P‑complete problems: **High**  
- Experimental expressivity gaps: **Medium**  
- Overall claim that LRNNs are fundamentally more parallelizable: **Medium‑High**
