# CxMP: A Linguistic Minimal-Pair Benchmark for Evaluating Constructional Understanding in Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21978
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21978
- Reference count: 18
- Primary result: CxMP reveals that while syntactic competence emerges early, constructional understanding develops gradually and remains limited even in 70B-parameter LLMs.

## Executive Summary
Language models (LMs) are known to acquire syntactic competence, but their ability to interpret meanings conveyed by grammatical forms—constructional understanding—remains under-evaluated. This paper introduces CxMP, a minimal-pair benchmark spanning nine construction types (e.g., let‑alone, ditransitive) to test form–meaning mappings. The dataset isolates semantic contributions by using pseudowords and switched argument positions, ensuring that performance reflects genuine constructional knowledge rather than lexical or positional cues. Evaluations across diverse model families show that syntactic skills (measured by BLiMP) plateau early, whereas constructional understanding improves slowly with scale. Masked language models (MLMs) consistently outperform causal language models (CLMs), and instruction‑tuning benefits argument‑structure constructions but harms non‑standard ones like let‑alone. Even the largest models (70B parameters) exhibit persistent gaps, indicating that integrating form and meaning remains a challenge for current LMs.

## Method Summary
CxMP consists of ~43k minimal-pair evaluation items. Each item includes a constructional example and two diagnostic continuations (plausible vs. implausible). The dataset covers nine grammatical constructions, with items generated using templates and an LLM (gpt‑5‑chat‑latest), then filtered for acceptability and semantic consistency. Four entity types are used (common nouns, female/male names, name+alphabet) and each item has a “switched” variant where argument positions are reversed. Human validation on 896 items showed 96.65% majority accuracy, confirming high quality.

Models are evaluated zero‑shot. For CLMs, the score is the length‑normalized log probability of the full diagnostic sentence; for MLMs, pseudo log‑likelihood is used. For instruction‑tuned and API models (GPT‑5, Llama‑Instruct), binary‑choice prompts are employed. The primary metric is accuracy (proportion of items where the plausible continuation scores higher). Additional analyses include learning trajectories (vs. BLiMP), bias scores (differences between switched/non‑switched and variant A/B), and performance with pseudowords.

## Key Results
- Syntactic competence (BLiMP) plateaus early (~50B tokens), while constructional understanding (CxMP) improves continuously, indicating a developmental dissociation between surface patterns and form–meaning integration.
- MLMs (e.g., RoBERTa) consistently outperform CLMs of comparable size, suggesting bidirectional context aids holistic interpretation of argument structure.
- Instruction‑tuning raises accuracy on argument‑structure constructions (ditransitive, caused‑motion) but lowers it on non‑standard constructions (let‑alone, comparative‑correlative), revealing a trade‑off.
- Even at 70B parameters, models leave substantial gaps, particularly on constructions involving scalar or comparative semantics, showing that constructional understanding remains limited.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Early syntactic competence vs. gradual constructional understanding
Language models develop syntactic competence (surface patterns) earlier than constructional understanding (form–meaning mappings). Models first acquire local statistical regularities (e.g., word order, agreement) sufficient for acceptability judgments. Deeper integration of syntactic form with semantic relations (e.g., causal roles, scalar implications) requires more exposure and architectural capacity to link distant dependencies and abstract schemas. The core assumption is that constructional understanding depends on representing and applying abstract form–meaning pairings, not just local co‑occurrence. Evidence: BLiMP scores plateau early (~50B tokens) while CxMP scores improve continuously across training (section 5.1); models can achieve high BLiMP but near‑chance CxMP. Break condition: high BLiMP + low CxMP indicates absent constructional integration.

### Mechanism 2: Bidirectional context modeling (MLMs) enhances form–meaning integration
MLMs benefit from bidirectional attention, leading to better constructional understanding than CLMs. Masked training forces the model to attend to all positions simultaneously, strengthening associations between constructional slots and semantic roles. CLMs rely more on linear precedence and local cues. The core assumption is that integrating meaning requires holistic understanding of argument structure and relational roles, which full‑context awareness during training supports. Evidence: RoBERTa (MLM) outperforms similarly sized or larger CLMs (GPT‑2, Pythia) on CxMP (Table 2); the abstract states “MLMs generally outperform CLMs.” Break condition: if an MLM fails to match a smaller CLM on CxMP, the benefit may be construction‑specific or data‑limited.

### Mechanism 3: Instruction‑tuning has differential effects
Instruction‑tuning improves performance on argument‑structure constructions but harms non‑standard constructions like let‑alone. Instruction data align models to task formats (e.g., binary choice), reinforcing heuristics for common argument mappings. For constructions with unique scalar/comparative semantics, this alignment may override construction‑specific patterns learned during pretraining. The core assumption is that instruction data emphasize transparent predicate–argument relations, potentially drowning out subtler form–meaning couplings. Evidence: Llama‑3.1 instruction‑tuned variants score higher on ditransitive/caused‑motion but lower on let‑alone and comparative‑correlative (Figure 5); the abstract notes this pattern. Break condition: if instruction tuning uniformly improves or harms all constructions, the effect is not construction‑specific.

## Foundational Learning
- **Construction Grammar**: Treats form–meaning pairings as basic linguistic units. *Why needed*: Without this lens, evaluation reduces to surface acceptability rather than interpretation of meaning conveyed by form. *Quick check*: What does a construction combine that a mere syntactic rule does not? (It integrates syntactic structure with semantic/pragmatic function.)
- **Minimal‑pair control**: Isolates the semantic contribution of a construction by varying only the diagnostic sentence while holding the constructional example constant. *Why needed*: To directly test whether models map form to meaning, not just recall lexical items. *Quick check*: Why is switching N1/N2 positions necessary in CxMP items? (To control for linear position biases and ensure the effect is due to semantic roles.)
- **Pseudoword control**: Replaces real lexical nouns with nonce forms (e.g., wugs) to remove lexical semantics. *Why needed*: To test pure constructional form–meaning mapping, as in prior psycholinguistic work. *Quick check*: What declines when real nouns are replaced with wugs, and what does that imply? (A performance drop suggests the model relies on lexical semantics rather than abstract constructional schemas.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input layer (constructional example + diagnostic sentence pair) → Scoring module (computes length‑normalized log probability for CLMs or pseudo log‑likelihood for MLMs) → Decision unit (compares scores, selects higher‑scoring diagnostic sentence) → Aggregator (averages correct selections across items).

Critical path: Generate and pair sentences → compute token probabilities → normalize by length → compare scores → output accuracy per construction/variant.

Design tradeoffs:
- Pseudowords vs. real nouns: reduces lexical bias but may introduce unnaturalness and hurt small models.
- Fixed templates vs. diversity: templates ensure control but limit ecological validity; LLM‑based generation increases diversity.
- Probability vs. prompting: direct probability scoring is clean but unavailable for chat models; prompting introduces format biases.

Failure signatures:
- High score gaps between switched and non‑switched items indicate reliance on linear position or lexical chunking.
- Performance drop with pseudowords suggests heavy lexical reliance and weak abstraction.
- Variant A/B score disparity indicates shallow cue reliance (e.g., always picking the first named entity).

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline replication: Evaluate BabyLM and Pythia models on CxMP; confirm near‑chance on non‑standard constructions (let‑alone, comparative‑correlative) and above‑chance on argument‑structure ones.
2. Size scaling: Run full Pythia suite (14M–12B parameters); plot accuracy vs. parameters to identify threshold scales for each construction.
3. Objective comparison: Test GPT‑2‑medium (CLM) vs. RoBERTa‑base (MLM) on matched subsets; verify MLM advantage on argument‑structure items.

## Limitations
- The CxMP dataset is not publicly released; recreating it requires unspecified prompts and may introduce uncontrolled lexical/semantic variation when using a different LLM.
- Evaluation protocols for API/chat models (GPT‑5, Llama‑Instruct) are underspecified, risking prompt‑induced biases that could affect reported scores.
- These gaps impede exact replication, especially for the highest‑performing models.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (syntax before construction): High
- Mechanism 2 (MLM vs. CLM advantage): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (instruction‑tuning effect): Medium
