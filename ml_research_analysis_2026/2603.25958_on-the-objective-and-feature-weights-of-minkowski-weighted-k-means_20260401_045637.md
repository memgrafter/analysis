# On the Objective and Feature Weights of Minkowski Weighted k-Means

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25958
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25958
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** The Minkowski weighted k-means objective is reformulated as a power-mean aggregation of within-cluster dispersions, yielding tight bounds and explicit feature weight formulas that depend solely on relative dispersion.

## Executive Summary
The paper provides a theoretical analysis of Minkowski weighted k-means (mwk-means), an algorithm that jointly learns cluster centroids and feature-specific weights using the Minkowski distance. The core contribution is a reformulation of the objective function as a sum of power means (with order \(r = -1/(p-1)\)) of per-feature within-cluster dispersions. This perspective yields tight bounds on the objective and shows that feature weights follow a strict power-law: the ratio of weights for two features equals the ratio of their dispersions raised to \(1/(p-1)\). Consequently, features with higher dispersion receive exponentially suppressed weights. The algorithm’s convergence is proven for \(p > 1\). Empirical experiments on synthetic data confirm the theoretical predictions, including the normalization of objective values to the interval \([0,1]\) and the transition from sparse to uniform weight distributions as \(p\) increases.

## Method Summary
The mwk-means algorithm alternates between three steps: (1) assign each point to the nearest centroid using weighted Minkowski distance; (2) update each cluster’s centroid to the Minkowski center (the minimizer of the sum of \(p\)-powers of deviations per feature); (3) update feature weights per cluster according to the derived condition \(w_{lv} \propto D_{lv}^{-1/(p-1)}\), where \(D_{lv}\) is the \(p\)-th-power dispersion of feature \(v\) in cluster \(l\), followed by normalization. The theoretical analysis expresses the overall objective \(W_p\) as \((1/m^{p-1}) \sum_l M_r(D_{l1},\dots,D_{lm})\), where \(M_r\) is the power mean of order \(r = -1/(p-1)\). This separation clarifies the role of \(p\) and leads to closed-form bounds and weight relations. Reproduction requires generating synthetic data with informative and noisy features, implementing the centroid update via numerical search (e.g., ternary search), and verifying that normalized objectives lie within \([0,1]\) and that weight sparsity varies with \(p\) as predicted.

## Key Results
- The mwk-means objective is a power-mean aggregation of within-cluster dispersions, with the Minkowski exponent \(p\) controlling the aggregation order via \(r = -1/(p-1)\).
- Feature weights satisfy \(w_{lu}/w_{lv} = (D_{lv}/D_{lu})^{1/(p-1)}\); thus, slightly higher dispersion leads to exponentially smaller weight.
- Tight bounds: \(\min_v D_{lv} \leq m^{p-1} W_p / k \leq (\prod_v D_{lv})^{1/m}\), yielding a normalized objective in \([0,1]\).
- Convergence of the alternating minimisation is guaranteed for \(p > 1\).
- As \(p \to 1^+\), weights concentrate on features with minimal dispersion; as \(p\) grows, weights become more uniform.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The mwk-means objective aggregates within-cluster feature dispersions via a power mean, where the Minkowski exponent \(p\) controls the aggregation order.
- **Mechanism:** The objective \(W_p\) is algebraically equivalent to \((1/m^{p-1}) \sum_l M_r(D_{l1},\dots,D_{lm})\) with \(r = -1/(p-1)\). This reformulation separates the role of \(p\) from the data.
- **Core assumption:** Cluster centroids are optimal Minkowski centers (unique for \(p > 1\)), and feature weights are updated according to the derived optimality condition (Eq. 4).
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states the power‑mean aggregation; [section 3.2, Lemma 2] proves the equivalence; neighboring papers on feature‑weighted k‑means provide context but do not dispute this reformulation.
- **Break condition:** The ordering properties of power means and the weight ratio derivation fail for \(p \leq 1\) due to loss of convexity and non‑positive \(r\).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Feature weights depend solely on the relative dispersion of features within a cluster, following a power‑law: \(w_{lu}/w_{lv} = (D_{lv}/D_{lu})^{1/(p-1)}\).
- **Mechanism:** Substituting the optimal weight update (Eq. 4) into the objective and eliminating common terms reveals that the ratio depends only on the ratio of dispersions \(D_{lv}\) and the exponent \(1/(p-1)\).
- **Core assumption:** Dispersion values \(D_{lv}\) are positive and computed relative to the cluster’s Minkowski centroid.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.3, Proposition 2] derives the weight ratio and monotonicity; [Theorems 3 & 4] give explicit suppression bounds; related work on weight stability does not contradict this dependence.
- **Break condition:** If dispersions are equal, weights become uniform. Zero dispersion would break the ratio.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The algorithm converges in a finite number of steps for \(p > 1\), with each iteration strictly decreasing the objective.
- **Mechanism:** Each step (assignment, centroid update, weight update) is a separate minimisation over one block of variables, holding others fixed. Monotonic decrease follows from optimality of each sub‑step and a lower‑bounded objective.
- **Core assumption:** The Minkowski centroid update is well‑defined and unique (strict convexity for \(p>1\)), and the weight update yields the unique solution under normalisation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.1, Theorem 1] formalises convergence; [Proposition 1] establishes centroid uniqueness; neighboring convergence proofs for k‑means variants use similar block‑coordinate descent logic.
- **Break condition:** For \(p \leq 1\), centroid uniqueness fails, breaking the monotonic decrease guarantee.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Power Means (Generalised Means)**
  - Why needed here: The paper’s reformulation (Lemma 2) expresses the objective as a sum of power means of dispersions. Understanding the ordering of power means (as \(r\) decreases, \(M_r\) decreases for positive numbers) is essential to grasp the bounds (Theorem 2).
  - Quick check: For positive numbers, what happens to the power mean \(M_r\) as \(r \to -\infty\)? How does this relate to the mwk‑means objective as \(p \to 1^+\)?
- **Concept: Feature Weighting in Clustering**
  - Why needed here: The algorithm learns feature weights per cluster. Proposition 2 shows weights depend only on relative dispersion, which is central to the weight‑suppression guarantees.
  - Quick check: In mwk‑means, if a feature has uniformly higher dispersion than others in a cluster, what happens to its weight as \(p\) increases? Why?
- **Concept: Minkowski Distance and Centroid**
  - Why needed here: The distance metric defines dispersion and the centroid update. For \(p=2\), it reduces to Euclidean k‑means; for other \(p\), the centroid is the component‑wise minimiser of \(\sum |x - z|^p\).
  - Quick check: For \(p \to \infty\), what is the Minkowski centroid of a set of 1D points? How does this extreme affect the weight distribution (Corollary 3)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Initialise weights uniformly and centroids randomly → Assignment step (weighted Minkowski distance) → Centroid update (Minkowski centre per feature) → Weight update (Eq. 4 + normalisation) → Compute objective → Check convergence → repeat if not converged.
- **Critical path:** Assignments → Centroid Update → Weight Update → Compute Objective. The objective must decrease monotonically each full iteration.
- **Design tradeoffs:** \(p\) acts as a smoothness knob: small \(p\) (≈1) yields sparse weights (feature‑selection‑like), large \(p\) yields uniform weights. The Minkowski centroid update for \(p \neq 2\) requires iterative optimisation (e.g., ternary search), increasing per‑iteration cost. Like k‑means, the method converges only to a local optimum and is sensitive to initialisation.
- **Failure signatures:** Degenerate weights (e.g., some features receiving near‑zero weight) may indicate high dispersion or numerical issues; non‑monotonic objective suggests incorrect centroid or weight updates; stalling (tiny objective changes but fluctuating assignments/weights) may require a stricter tolerance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalisation to other clustering methods:** Can the power‑mean aggregation interpretation be extended to fuzzy c‑means or entropy‑weighted k‑means? The current analysis is specific to mwk‑means.
- **Model selection:** How can the theoretical bounds be used to select the optimal number of clusters \(k\) or exponent \(p\) in practice? The bounds involve unknown dispersions, making practical criteria unclear.
- **Robustness to outliers:** To what extent is the weight‑suppression guarantee robust when dispersions are distorted by outliers? The paper assumes clean data; contamination may invalidate the derived relations.
- **Case \(p=1\):** What are the theoretical properties when \(p=1\) (Manhattan distance)? The weight derivation and centroid uniqueness rely on \(p>1\); the \(p=1\) case may yield different behaviour.

## Limitations
- Synthetic data generation details (e.g., cluster means, noise distribution) are unspecified, requiring assumptions that may affect reproducibility.
- Empirical validation is limited to synthetic data; performance on real‑world datasets and sensitivity to data characteristics remain unexplored.
- Theoretical bounds and convergence guarantees hold only for \(p > 1\) and positive dispersions; behaviour for \(p \leq 1\) is not addressed.
- Numerical stability near \(p=1\) requires careful handling (large exponents in weight updates).
