# Benchmarking Reinforcement Learning via Stochastic Converse Optimality: Generating Systems with Known Optimal Policies

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17631
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17631
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: Constructs RL benchmark environments with provably optimal policies via stochastic converse optimality, enabling absolute algorithmic metrics.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the lack of objective benchmarks in reinforcement learning by developing a framework to generate control environments with certified optimal policies. It extends converse optimality theory to discrete-time stochastic control-affine systems with additive Gaussian noise, deriving necessary and sufficient conditions for a candidate value function and policy to be optimal. Within the quadratic-Gaussian subclass, it obtains an explicit constructive parameterization that allows systematic generation of benchmark families (e.g., n-link robotic arms, nonholonomic vehicles) by varying control strength and randomizing parameters.

The authors validate the framework by confirming Bellman residuals and closed-loop stability. They then evaluate several popular RL algorithms (PPO, A2C, SAC, TD3, DDPG) using a common random numbers protocol that pairs trajectories across algorithms, computing absolute metrics such as optimality gaps and discounted regrets. Results show PPO excels in stationary and complex dynamics, while TD3 and SAC perform better with diverse initial states, disentangling algorithmic effects from protocol variations.

## Method Summary
The method comprises three pillars: (1) Stochastic converse optimality: Starting from a smooth candidate value function \(V^*\) and stage costs, the theorem specifies conditions on the system drift \(f(s)\) and input coupling \(g(s)\) that make \((V^*,\pi^*)\) optimal for a control-affine system with Gaussian noise. This reverses the usual optimal control problem. (2) Quadratic-Gaussian specialization: For quadratic costs and a quadratic \(V^*\), the Bellman equation reduces to an energy identity that can be satisfied by constructing \(f(s)\) via a state‑dependent metric \(H_\gamma(s)\) and an orthogonal field \(S(s)\). This yields a family of valid benchmark instances. (3) Evaluation protocol: Using common random numbers for initial states and noise, the framework computes normalized optimality gaps and regrets, providing oracle-referenced, low‑variance performance estimates.

## Key Results
- Generated benchmark families (ConverseArm for manipulators, NVDEx for vehicles) with analytically certified optimal policies.
- PPO achieved the lowest optimality gaps in stationary and complex dynamics (e.g., NVDEx gap −15.8), while TD3 and SAC performed better under protocols with diverse initial states.
- Absolute metrics revealed substantial optimality gaps (e.g., TD3 median gap −58.5 in serial random), quantifying suboptimality across algorithms.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Claim: The paper establishes a stochastic converse optimality theorem that gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a candidate value function \(V^*\) and policy \(\pi^*\) to be optimal for a constructed discrete‑time stochastic control‑affine system with additive Gaussian noise.  
Mechanism: Given a stage cost \(c(s,a)=q(s)+\rho(a)\), discount factor \(\gamma\), noise covariance \(\Sigma\), input coupling \(g(s)\), and a \(C^2\) candidate value function \(V^*\), there exists a drift \(f(s)\) such that \((V^*,\pi^*)\) is optimal iff there exists a measurable policy \(\pi^*\) satisfying the first‑order optimality condition \(\nabla_a\rho(a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}[g(s)^\top\nabla V^*(f(s)+g(s)a+w)]=0\) and the Bellman identity \(V^*(s)=q(s)+\rho(\pi^*(s))+\gamma\mathbb{E}[V^*(f(s)+g(s)\pi^*(s)+w)]\) for all \(s\). The proof uses Lemma 1 to interchange differentiation and expectation.  
Core assumption: The system is discrete‑time, control‑affine, with i.i.d. additive Gaussian noise; stage costs separable with \(q\) continuous and \(\rho\) strongly convex; \(V^*, f, g\) satisfy local Lipschitz and polynomial‑growth conditions; \(\Sigma\) positive definite.  
Evidence anchors: Abstract and Section IV (Theorem 1, Lemma 1).  
Break condition: Violations such as non‑Gaussian heavy‑tailed noise, non‑additive disturbances, or non‑convex \(\rho\) may invalidate Lemma 1 or the first‑order condition.

### Mechanism 2
Claim: The Quadratic‑Gaussian (QG) specialization yields an explicit constructive parameterization of the converse‑optimal drift via a state‑dependent metric and an orthogonal field, enabling systematic generation of benchmark families with certified optimal policies.  
Mechanism: With quadratic costs \(q(s)=s^\top Q s\), \(\rho(a)=a^\top R a\) and quadratic value \(V^*(s)=s^\top P s + b\), the Bellman equation reduces to the energy identity \(s^\top(P-Q)s = f(s)^\top H_\gamma(s) f(s)\) where \(H_\gamma(s)=\gamma(P^{-1}+\gamma g(s)R^{-1}g(s)^\top)^{-1}\succ0\). Any drift of the form \(f(s)=H_\gamma(s)^{-1/2} S(s)(P-Q)^{1/2}s\), with \(S(s)\) orthogonal (\(S^\top S=I\)), satisfies the identity exactly. The optimal policy is linear in \(f(s)\): \(a^* = -\gamma(R+\gamma g(s)^\top P g(s))^{-1}g(s)^\top P f(s)\). This allows constructing diverse families by choosing \(g(s)\) (e.g., \(\cos\theta\) for arms) and designing \(S(s)\) as products of bounded rotations (e.g., Givens rotations). A homotopy parameter \(p\in(0,1]\) scales \(g\) to vary difficulty.  
Core assumption: Quadratic costs and \(V^*\); zero‑mean Gaussian noise with \(\Sigma\); \(R\succ0\), \(P\succ0\), \(Q\succeq0\); orthogonal field \(S(s)\) well‑defined and bounded.  
Evidence anchors: Section V (QG derivation) and Section VI (benchmark constructions).  
Break condition: Non‑quadratic costs destroy the energy identity; non‑Gaussian noise with unbounded moments may invalidate integrability; loss of orthogonality in \(S(s)\) breaks the identity.

### Mechanism 3
Claim: The Common Random Numbers (CRN) evaluation protocol, paired with absolute metrics (optimality gap and discounted regret), disentangles algorithmic performance from protocol effects and yields reproducible, oracle‑referenced benchmarking.  
Mechanism: For each benchmark fixture (with certified \((a^*,V^*)\)), evaluation uses identical random seeds for initial‑state schedules and noise across all algorithms. This pairs stochastic trajectories, so differences in cumulative reward reflect algorithmic differences rather than sampling variance. The normalized optimality gap \(\text{OptGap}(\pi;s_0)=\frac{V^\pi_r(s_0)-V^*_r(s_0)}{|V^*_r(s_0)|+\varepsilon}\) measures relative suboptimality, while regret \(\text{Regret}_T(\pi;s_0)=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\gamma^k\bigl(r(s_k,a^*_k)-r(s_k,\pi(s_k))\bigl)\) accumulates per‑step differences along the same CRN trajectory. Varying protocols isolates effects of state‑distribution coverage.  
Core assumption: Environment fully deterministic given seeds; optimal policy and value function exactly known and numerically stable.  
Evidence anchors: Section VII (experimental setup, CRN, definitions) and abstract.  
Break condition: Hidden non‑determinism (unseeded calls, GPU nondeterminism) or inaccurate reference policies compromise CRN pairing.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Parameter space Ψ → QG solver → validator (checks SPD of B(s), Bellman residual, closed‑loop boundedness) → fixture exporter (YAML with parameters, CRN seeds, reference evaluators) → CRN evaluation harness (runs RL algorithms with matched seeds) → metrics module (computes OptGap, Regret) → plotting and reporting scripts.

### Critical Path
Sample ψ from Ψ → extract (Q,R,P,γ,Σ,g) → choose orthogonal field S(s) (e.g., Givens rotations) → construct f(s) via H_γ and S → compute a*(s) → validate B(s) SPD on grid → evaluate Bellman residual → simulate closed‑loop trajectories under a* → if valid, export fixture → for each algorithm: train with identical ISS and noise streams → evaluate on test states with CRN → aggregate metrics.

### Design Tradeoffs
Prioritizes rigor and reproducibility over generality. Assumes control‑affine dynamics, additive Gaussian noise, quadratic costs (restricting to linear optimal policies and quadratic value functions). The QG parameterization injects nonlinearity via S(s) while preserving the energy identity exactly. Homotopy on control strength p smoothly varies difficulty. Validation ensures correctness but may reject theoretically valid but numerically fragile instances (e.g., near‑singular B(s)).

### Failure Signatures
Validation failures: (i) B(s) not SPD on sample grid → risk of non‑unique policy; (ii) Bellman residual exceeds threshold → construction error or numerical instability; (iii) closed‑loop trajectories diverge → system unstable under optimal policy. Evaluation failures: algorithms produce NaNs or exceed state bounds; mismatched CRN seeds across runs; reference evaluators inaccurate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Extend converse optimality to non‑Gaussian noise.
- Basis: The authors state that additive i.i.d. Gaussian noise misses heavy‑tailed disturbances, heteroscedasticity, or temporal correlations. Extensions to broader noise remain future work (Sec. IX).
- Why unresolved: The derivation relies on Gaussian properties (finite moments, additivity) for Lemma 1 and the closed‑form energy equation; non‑Gaussian distributions may break these steps.
- What evidence would resolve it: A new converse theorem for a specific non‑Gaussian class (e.g., sub‑Gaussian) with explicit conditions on f, g, plus at least one constructed benchmark validated numerically (Bellman residual, closed‑loop stability).

### Open Question 2
- Question: Generate benchmarks with non‑quadratic costs and nonlinear optimal policies.
- Basis: The paper notes that the QG specialization excludes non‑quadratic objectives, constraints, and nonlinear controllers (Sec. IX).
- Why unresolved: Quadratic costs lead to linear policies via first‑order conditions; non‑quadratic objectives yield nonlinear optimal actions, making it difficult to solve for f, g that realize a prescribed V*.
- What evidence would resolve it: Identify a tractable non‑quadratic cost class (e.g., convex with quadratic growth) that admits an explicit optimal policy; derive converse conditions on f, g; construct an exemplar instance and verify its Bellman equation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Extend the framework beyond control‑affine dynamics.
- Basis: The authors identify the control‑affine structure as a limitation and call for extensions to broader control models (Sec. IX).
- Why unresolved: Control‑affine form separates drift and input matrix, key to the energy equation and linear policy; general nonlinear control dependence f(s,a) complicates the Bellman minimization.
- What evidence would resolve it: Formulate a converse optimality theorem for a broader input structure (e.g., polynomial in a or via a learnable nonlinearity); provide a constructive parameterization for at least one instance and validate it.

## Limitations
- The framework assumes discrete-time control-affine dynamics, additive i.i.d. Gaussian noise, and separable quadratic costs, limiting its applicability to many real-world RL problems.
- The quadratic cost assumption restricts optimal policies to be linear, which may not capture nonlinear optimal controllers required in many applications.
- The CRN evaluation protocol requires perfect determinism; hidden nondeterminism (e.g., GPU operations) or inaccurate reference policies can bias results.
- The constructive parameterization in the QG subclass depends on the existence of a state‑dependent orthogonal field \(S(s)\), which may not be easily designed for arbitrary input couplings \(g(s)\).
- No external independent validation has been reported (reproduction notes: none), and citation signals indicate low community uptake.

## Confidence
- Stochastic converse optimality theorem: High (conditional on assumptions).
- QG constructive parameterization: High (within QG subclass).
- CRN evaluation protocol: Medium (depends on implementation fidelity and strict determinism).
