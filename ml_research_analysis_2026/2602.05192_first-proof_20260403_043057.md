# First Proof

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05192
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05192
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Frontier AI models (GPT‑5.2 Pro, Gemini 3.0 Deep Think) largely fail to produce correct, rigorous proofs for unreleased, research‑level mathematics problems.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel benchmark, “First Proof”, comprising ten research‑level mathematics problems from diverse fields such as algebraic combinatorics, spectral graph theory, and topology. The correct solutions were kept secret and later released. Two frontier AI systems were prompted under two conditions: one encouraging rigorous LaTeX output and one discouraging web search. Human experts evaluated the AI‑generated proofs against the encrypted solutions. The results show that current AI cannot autonomously solve unreleased, research‑level math problems: only Question 9 received a fully correct solution, and Question 10 yielded a correct but more efficient iterative solver than the human proof. All other attempts contained fundamental errors, such as reliance on unproven claims, trivial constructions, or missing critical quantifiers. The benchmark provides a rigorous, reproducible way to track progress in mathematical reasoning for AI.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed ten novel, research‑level mathematics problems with solutions kept confidential. They selected two state‑of‑the‑art AI models (GPT‑5.2 Pro and Gemini 3.0 Deep Think) and designed two prompt variants: one emphasizing rigorous LaTeX formatting and another discouraging web search. Each model was queried once per question (single‑shot) and the outputs were collected. Domain experts then graded each proof against the released human solutions, categorizing them as fully correct, partially correct, or incorrect. The evaluation focused on logical correctness, coverage of all conditions, and the presence of unsupported claims. The reproduction notes identify several unknowns that could affect reproducibility, such as exact model access, grading subjectivity, potential data contamination, and unreported inference parameters.

## Key Results
- **Question 9** produced a fully correct solution, constructing the required algebraic relations via 5×5 minors.
- **Question 10** yielded a correct iterative solver that was more efficient than the human proof.
- **All other questions** exhibited critical failures: reliance on unproven statements, misdirected algorithms (e.g., Metropolis–Hastings for a deterministic problem), omission of universal quantifiers, or incoherent output.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s effectiveness stems from three design choices: (1) using unreleased problems eliminates data contamination; (2) single‑shot generation forces the model to produce a complete proof without iterative refinement; and (3) blinded expert evaluation against pre‑published correct solutions ensures objective assessment of both correctness and rigor. The two prompts isolate the influence of external knowledge (web search) and output format requirements, providing a clearer picture of the model’s intrinsic reasoning capabilities.

## Foundational Learning
Solving the problems requires deep knowledge across several mathematical domains. The following concepts are foundational, each paired with why it is needed and a quick sanity check.

- **Algebraic combinatorics** – Needed for questions involving symmetric functions, Young tableaux, or combinatorial interpretations of algebraic identities. Quick check: Ask the AI to prove a classic identity (e.g., the hook‑length formula) from first principles.
- **Spectral graph theory** – Required for problems dealing with graph Laplacians, eigenvalue bounds, or connectivity. Quick check: Test if the AI can derive a standard result like Cheeger’s inequality.
- **General topology** – Essential for questions about continuity, compactness, or topological invariants. Quick check: Present a basic theorem (e.g., continuous image of compact set is compact) and verify the proof structure.
- **Logical rigor and quantifier handling** – Critical for translating mathematical statements into proofs with correct universal/existential quantification. Quick check: Provide a statement with nested quantifiers and see if the AI respects all dependencies.

## Architecture Onboarding
The AI systems are treated as black‑box proof generators. Onboarding involves configuring the model access, prompt templates, and evaluation pipeline.

**Component map:**  
User request → Prompt formatter → Model API (GPT‑5.2 Pro / Gemini 3.0 Deep Think) → LaTeX output → Human grader.

**Critical path:** Prompt design → single‑shot generation → output validation (LaTeX compilation, completeness) → expert grading.

**Design tradeoffs:** Using two prompts isolates reasoning from external lookup but may penalize models that benefit from web search; single‑shot prevents iterative correction but reflects real‑world one‑attempt scenarios; blinding graders avoids bias but requires pre‑published solutions.

**Failure signatures:** Outputs containing unproved lemmas, missing “for all” conditions, off‑topic algorithms (e.g., statistical methods for deterministic problems), or incomplete LaTeX.

**First three onboarding experiments:**  
1. **Baseline sanity check** – Run a trivial known theorem (e.g., sum of angles in triangle) to ensure the pipeline produces syntactically valid LaTeX proofs.  
2. **Prompt effect isolation** – Compare the two prompts on a medium‑difficulty problem with a known solution to quantify how discouraging web search influences reasoning.  
3. **Token budget calibration** – Vary max‑token limits to find the minimum that allows full proofs without premature truncation.

## Limitations
- Access to the exact model versions (GPT‑5.2 Pro, Gemini 3.0 Deep Think) may be restricted, hindering exact replication.
- Human expert grading is inherently subjective; inter‑rater reliability was not reported.
- Ten problems provide a limited sample, reducing statistical confidence in broad generalizability.
