# SEAHateCheck: Functional Tests for Detecting Hate Speech in Low-Resource Languages of Southeast Asia

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16070
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16070
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** First functional test suite for hate speech in Indonesian, Tagalog, Thai, and Vietnamese (33,381 instances), revealing model weaknesses in slang, implicit hate, and Tagalog performance.

## Executive Summary
SEAHateCheck introduces a culturally attuned functional test suite to address the lack of evaluation resources for hate speech detection in low-resource Southeast Asian languages. Combining human-curated gold templates with LLM-generated silver instances, it targets detection capabilities and protected categories aligned with local legislation. Benchmarking exposes significant model deficiencies, particularly with Tagalog, slang, and implicit hate, while fine-tuning shows mixed improvements. This work provides the first systematic benchmark for these linguistically diverse contexts.

## Method Summary
The test suite was constructed by first defining functional tests and protected categories based on local legislation. Native speakers created 13,579 gold templates for high-quality, culturally valid instances. For scale, 19,802 silver instances were generated using LLMs with few-shot prompting and validated through automated and human checks for quality, naturalness, and alignment. The final suite includes diverse hate speech expressions across Indonesian, Tagalog, Thai, and Vietnamese, designed to probe specific detection challenges like directness, anonymity, and counter-speech.

## Key Results
- Tagalog consistently showed the lowest accuracy across all evaluated models.
- Slang-based functional tests were the hardest, with models failing to handle culturally nuanced expressions.
- Fine-tuning with language-specific data improved overall F1 scores but caused regressions on some languages and functional tests, highlighting domain adaptation challenges.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
SEAHateCheck works by providing a targeted, legislation-aligned evaluation framework that isolates specific hate speech detection capabilities. The hybrid gold-silver approach balances authenticity with scalability, while functional testing diagnoses model weaknesses in culturally nuanced scenarios. This precision reveals gaps that generic benchmarks miss, guiding data collection and model refinement for low-resource languages.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Functional Testing in NLP**  
   - *Why needed:* To systematically assess model performance on specific hate speech phenomena (e.g., implicit hate, slang).  
   - *Quick check:* Does the model correctly classify hate speech in code-mixed or euphemistic contexts?

2. **Low-Resource Language Challenges**  
   - *Why needed:* Limited annotated data hinders model generalization in languages like Tagalog and Thai.  
   - *Quick check:* Can the model achieve reasonable performance with small training sets or via cross-lingual transfer?

3. **Culturally Nuanced Hate Speech**  
   - *Why needed:* Hate expressions are culture-specific; legal definitions may not capture informal slang or local idioms.  
   - *Quick check:* Are region-specific slurs or metaphors accurately identified as hateful?

4. **LLM-Generated Evaluation Data**  
   - *Why needed:* Scaling test suites with human curation is costly; LLMs offer diversity but risk lower authenticity.  
   - *Quick check:* Do LLM-generated instances match natural language use and maintain consistent labeling?

5. **Protected Categories from Legislation**  
   - *Why needed:* Legal frameworks define target groups, but may lag behind evolving social norms.  
   - *Quick check:* Are emerging vulnerable groups (e.g., LGBTQ+ in conservative contexts) adequately represented?

6. **Implicit vs. Explicit Hate Speech**  
   - *Why needed:* Implicit hate is prevalent and harder to detect, requiring contextual understanding.  
   - *Quick check:* Can the model distinguish subtle, indirect hate from neutral or positive statements?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component Map:  
Gold Template Collection -> LLM Prompting -> Quality Validation -> Test Suite Assembly -> Model Evaluation

Critical Path: Native speakers curate gold templates, which seed LLM prompting for silver generation; validated instances are assembled into functional test groups, then used to benchmark models.

Design Tradeoffs: Scale via LLM generation vs. authenticity via human gold; legal category alignment ensures relevance but may miss emerging groups; depth on four languages vs. breadth across SEA.

Failure Signatures: Poor slang performance indicates cultural adaptation gaps; fine-tuning regressions suggest overfitting or domain mismatch; low Tagalog accuracy points to data scarcity or linguistic complexity.

First Experiments:  
1. Compare model performance on gold vs. silver subsets to measure quality impact on benchmark reliability.  
2. Ablate functional test categories to pinpoint weakest detection capabilities (e.g., implicit hate).  
3. Test fine-tuned models against zero-shot baselines to quantify adaptation benefits and identify regressions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1  
- **Question:** How can we automatically generate high-quality, culturally nuanced hate speech test cases (silver) that match the naturalness and validity of human-curated gold templates?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "Silver test cases face several challenges... their lower high-quality rate (0.72 vs. 0.83) and quality scores indicate reduced naturalness and reliability, as LLMs introduce verbosity or errors... inconsistent target group and functional test alignment reduces diagnostic precision." (Section 2.6)  
- **Why unresolved:** Silver test cases are crucial for scaling evaluation to real-world diversity, but current LLM-based generation produces lower-quality, less natural instances that may mislead model assessment and reduce benchmark reliability.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments comparing LLM prompting approaches (few-shot, RLHF), validation pipelines (majority vote, expert adjudication), and quality thresholds; empirical comparison of model performance when trained/evaluated on improved silver sets vs. existing ones.  

### Open Question 2  
- **Question:** How can hate speech benchmarks remain aligned with evolving social norms and protect emerging vulnerable groups that are not yet codified in law?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors acknowledge: "Another major limitation of this study is the reliance on existing laws to identify target groups, which represents a lag in vulnerable groups in society today." (Section 8)  
- **Why unresolved:** Legal definitions are static and often lag behind societal changes, causing benchmarks to miss contemporary forms of hate and under-served communities, potentially leading to biased or incomplete model evaluation.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a dynamic, community-engaged process for updating protected categories (e.g., periodic consultations with NGOs, affected groups, social scientists); measure the impact of adding newly recognized groups on model performance and bias detection; compare results across time.  

### Open Question 3  
- **Question:** How do state-of-the-art hate speech detection models perform on code-switched text (e.g., Taglish, Singlish) in low-resource SEA languages, and what data is needed to train and evaluate them effectively?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note: "Code-switching is prevalent in SEA online discourse... We therefore include code-mixed functional tests as future work." (Section 2.6) They did not include code-mixed tests in the current release.  
- **Why unresolved:** Code-switching is a common evasion tactic and linguistic reality in SEA, but current models are evaluated primarily on monolingual test cases; performance on mixed language remains unknown, hindering deployment in real-world platforms.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Create a code-switched extension of SEAHateCheck with parallel monolingual and mixed instances; evaluate a range of models on this suite to identify failure modes, quantify performance gaps, and determine training data requirements (e.g., augmentation, code-mixed corpora).  

### Open Question 4  
- **Question:** Can functional testing be extended beyond single-turn utterances to multi-turn conversational contexts to capture hate speech that is indirect or emerges over dialogue?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors state: "The structure of the templates, which have up to two sentences, also does not fully reflect the conversational nature of interactions on social media." (Section 8) This suggests a gap in evaluating discourse-level hate.  
- **Why unresolved:** Real online harassment often occurs across multiple messages, making single-sentence tests insufficient for evaluating models deployed in conversational agents or comment threads.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design and validate a multi-turn functional test suite for SEA languages (e.g., sequences of 3–5 turns with hateful intent gradually revealed); assess model robustness compared to single-turn evaluations; analyze whether multi-turn performance correlates with real-world moderation outcomes.

## Limitations
- Silver test instances are LLM-generated; cultural/linguistic authenticity may vary without native speaker validation.
- Focus on four languages may not generalize to other Southeast Asian low-resource languages (e.g., Burmese, Khmer).
- Evaluation primarily uses standard multilingual models; results may not reflect real-world, platform-specific deployments with dynamic slang.

## Confidence
- **Dataset novelty/coverage:** High (first large-scale functional test suite for these languages).
- **Tagalog/slang as hardest cases:** Medium (supported by benchmark results but may be influenced by test design).
- **Fine-tuning regressions:** Medium (observed in experiments, but root causes require deeper error analysis).
- **Implicit hate/counter-speech weaknesses:** Medium (consistent across models, but labeling consistency remains a potential confounder).

## Next Checks
1. Conduct native-speaker validation on a stratified sample of silver instances to quantify cultural/linguistic misalignment.
2. Replicate benchmarks on newer multilingual models (e.g., BLOOM, mT5-XXL) and regional LLMs (e.g., SeaLLMs) to test if architectural improvements mitigate reported gaps.
3. Perform fine-grained error analysis on Tagalog/slang failures to disentangle data scarcity vs. model capacity issues (e.g., via adversarial subset testing).
