# Full end-to-end diagnostic workflow automation of 3D OCT via foundation model-driven AI for retinal diseases
## Quick Facts
*   arXiv ID: 2602.03302
*   Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03302
*   Reference count: 0
*   Primary result: Achieved patient-level diagnosis F1 of 94.39% on internal data and 90.22%-95.24% on external multi-center validation, matching or exceeding expert performance.

## Executive Summary
This work addresses the critical lack of full diagnostic automation for 3D Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) in retinal disease, a gap caused by prior AI models focusing on isolated, single-slice tasks. The authors introduce FOCUS, an end-to-end framework that sequentially performs image quality assessment, abnormality detection, and multi-disease classification. It leverages a fine-tuned vision foundation model for robust slice-level feature extraction and a novel Unified Adaptive Aggregation Classifier (UAAC) to synthesize 3D patient-level diagnoses from 2D slices. Validated on 3,300 internal and 1,345 external patients across four centers and multiple device vendors, FOCUS achieved high F1 scores (quality: 99.01%, abnormality: 97.46%, diagnosis: 94.39%) and demonstrated stable real-world performance (90.22%-95.24%). In a human-machine comparison, FOCUS matched or exceeded expert ophthalmologists in diagnostic accuracy while offering superior efficiency.

## Method Summary
FOCUS implements a sequential pipeline that mirrors the clinical diagnostic process. First, an EfficientNetV2-S model classifies each 2D B-scan from a 3D OCT volume as pass/fail for image quality. Second, high-quality slices are processed by a fine-tuned Vision Foundation Model (VisionFM), which extracts features and makes slice-level predictions for abnormality detection and multi-disease classification. Third, the UAAC aggregates these slice-level outputs into a single patient-level diagnosis by dynamically weighting each slice's contribution based on prediction confidence and uncertainty, using a Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) approach. The system is trained on multi-center, multi-vendor OCT data and evaluated on held-out internal and external test sets, with F1-score as the primary performance metric.

## Key Results
*   FOCUS achieved F1 scores of 99.01% (image quality), 97.46% (abnormality detection), and 94.39% (patient diagnosis) on an internal test set.
*   Real-world external validation across four independent centers yielded stable patient diagnosis F1 scores ranging from 90.22% to 95.24%.
*   In direct comparison with human experts, FOCUS achieved higher F1 scores for abnormality detection (95.47% vs. 90.91%) and patient diagnosis (93.49% vs. 91.35%) with greater workflow efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Sequential Clinical Workflow Mimicry.** The pipeline's fixed order (quality → abnormality → diagnosis) reduces fragmentation by ensuring only valid data proceeds and by training specialized models for discrete, conditionally dependent tasks, mirroring expert practice. Evidence: The architecture is designed to "seamlessly integrate quality control, anomaly triage, and volumetric diagnosis" (section Page 3). Break condition: Errors in early stages (e.g., quality assessment false negatives) propagate downstream, and conditional independence assumptions may not hold if tasks are interlinked.

**Mechanism 2: Fine-Tuned Vision Foundation Model Robustness.** Transfer learning from a large-scale pre-trained VisionFM, fine-tuned on multi-center OCT data, provides robust, generalizable slice-level features that capture subtle pathology across diverse devices and diseases with limited annotations. Evidence: The authors state they "successfully harnessed its robust, multi-disease capabilities" through multi-center, multi-device fine-tuning (section Page 10). Break condition: Generalization fails if pre-training lacks OCT diversity or fine-tuning data is biased/scarce for specific demographics or device types.

**Mechanism 3: Unified Adaptive Aggregation.** The UAAC employs a MIL framework with uncertainty-aware attention mechanisms to dynamically weight slice contributions. It up-weights high-confidence pathological slices and down-weights ambiguous or normal ones, effectively synthesizing a patient diagnosis from sparse 2D evidence. Evidence: "UAAC empowers the model to dynamically calibrate the influence of each B-scan based on its clinical diagnostic contribution and associated prediction uncertainty" (section Page 11). Break condition: If pathological signs are extremely sparse or uncertainty estimates are miscalibrated, critical information may be diluted.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Vision Foundation Models**
*   **Why needed:** To leverage large-scale pre-trained representations that generalize across diverse OCT devices and disease phenotypes, reducing the annotation burden for medical imaging tasks.
*   **Quick check:** What are the key advantages of using a foundation model pre-trained on a large and diverse dataset compared to training a model from scratch on a smaller, disease-specific dataset?

**Concept: Multiple Instance Learning (MIL)**
*   **Why needed:** To aggregate information from a collection of 2D slices (a "bag") into a single patient-level label without requiring slice-level annotations for the patient-level task, and to effectively handle the sparse and interspersed nature of pathological signs in OCT volumes.
*   **Quick check:** In MIL, how does the model handle bags where only a small subset of instances are positive, and why is this suitable for OCT volumes?

**Concept: Uncertainty Quantification**
*   **Why needed:** To provide confidence estimates for predictions, which can be used for adaptive aggregation (e.g., in UAAC) and to flag low-confidence cases for human review, thereby improving reliability and safety in clinical deployment.
*   **Quick check:** Why is it important for an AI system to output uncertainty estimates alongside predictions, and how might these estimates be used in a clinical workflow?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input Handler -> Quality Assessment Module (EfficientNetV2-S) -> Slice-level Feature Extractor (fine-tuned VisionFM) -> Task-specific Heads -> Unified Adaptive Aggregation Classifier (UAAC) -> Decision Logic

**Critical path:**
1.  Load a 3D OCT volume and extract its constituent 2D B-scan slices.
2.  For each slice: perform quality assessment; if passed, use the fine-tuned VisionFM to extract features and generate slice-level abnormality and disease probabilities.
3.  Collect all slice-level outputs (predictions, features, uncertainties) for the volume.
4.  Feed the collection into the UAAC to compute aggregated, patient-level probabilities.
5.  Apply learned thresholds to the UAAC output to produce final binary abnormality and multi-label diagnosis decisions.
6.  Output the final diagnosis along with per-slice and per-volume confidence scores.

**Design tradeoffs:**
*   *Efficiency vs. accuracy:* Processing independent 2D slices with MIL avoids computationally expensive 3D convolutions but may miss volumentric context; UAAC's adaptive weighting aims to compensate.
*   *Modularity vs. end-to-end:* Separate modules (quality, slice-level, aggregation) allow independent optimization and debugging but risk error propagation from earlier stages; a fully joint training approach could mitigate this at the cost of complexity.
*   *Model size:* Larger foundation models offer better generalization but increase inference latency; a smaller model (EfficientNetV2-S) is chosen for the fast quality assessment stage.
*   *Aggregation complexity:* UAAC adds parameters for uncertainty-weighted attention; simpler pooling (e.g., mean) is faster but less robust to sparse pathology or noise.

**Failure signatures:**
*   Quality module false negatives: Rejection of valid, pathology-containing slices leads to information loss and potential missed diagnoses.
*   Foundation model feature collapse: The slice-level embeddings fail to be discriminative for certain rare diseases or specific device artifacts.
*   UAAC weight collapse: The adaptive mechanism fails to differentiate slices, defaulting to uniform averaging and losing its advantage.
*   Data drift: Performance degrades on patient populations, OCT devices, or disease spectrums underrepresented in the multi-center training data.

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Quality Assessment Validation:** Establish a baseline by evaluating the EfficientNetV2-S quality classifier on held-out internal and external test sets, reporting F1, sensitivity, and specificity against expert slice-level annotations.
2.  **Foundation Model Ablation:** Isolate the contribution of the fine-tuned VisionFM by comparing the full slice-level classifier against variants initialized with random weights or only ImageNet pre-training, measuring impact on patient-level diagnosis F1.
3.  **UAAC Ablation:** Quantify the benefit of adaptive aggregation by replacing UAAC with a non-adaptive pooling method (e.g., average or max pooling) on the external test set, and by comparing adaptive weighting against fixed heuristic weighting schemes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How does FOCUS's diagnostic performance generalize to populations of diverse ethnicities and under different disease spectrums beyond the primarily Chinese cohorts used in training and validation?  
*Basis in paper:* [explicit] "the training and validation datasets were mainly collected from Chinese medical centers. This demographic concentration may limit the epidemiological generalizability of FOCUS to populations with different ethnic backgrounds, disease spectra, or clinical workflows."  
*Why unresolved:* The model's robustness is contingent on the diversity of its training data. Performance may degrade when applied to populations with differing genetic predispositions, disease presentations, or co-morbidity profiles not represented in the current multi-center Chinese dataset.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A prospective, multi-national validation study across continents (e.g., Europe, Africa, Americas) involving diverse ethnic groups, utilizing local OCT devices and capturing the native disease prevalence of each region.

**Open Question 2:** What is the true clinical utility and efficiency impact of FOCUS when deployed in a prospective, real-world, low-prevalence population screening setting, as opposed to the high-prevalence hospital environment used for validation?  
*Basis in paper:* [explicit] "The validation datasets were collected from ophthalmic departments in tertiary and general hospitals, where the prevalence of retinal pathologies is significantly higher than in the general population... Prospective studies conducted in diverse live settings are essential to definitively validate the system's performance under natural disease distributions, evaluate its impact on workflow efficiency, and assess user acceptance among clinicians."  
*Why unresolved:* Performance metrics (e.g., F1-score) are inflated in high-prevalence datasets. The system's false positive rate in a screening context (where most scans are normal) and its actual time-saving effect on a clinical team are unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A prospective, time-motion study in a primary care or community screening center, measuring metrics like scans per technician hour, referral accuracy, and clinician time saved per patient, compared to standard of care.

**Open Question 3:** Can the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) as proposed by the authors successfully enhance FOCUS by providing interactive diagnostic dialogue and automatically generating clinically accurate, interpretable radiology-style reports without introducing hallucinations or regulatory risks?  
*Basis in paper:* [explicit] "the current iteration of FOCUS has not yet integrated recent advances in large language models... Future versions could leverage this technology to enable interactive diagnostic dialogues, automate the generation of clinical reports, and provide even more transparent, human-readable justifications... However, it is also important to note that integrating such systems into medical diagnostics would bring significant regulatory challenges... future work must carefully evaluate these regulatory considerations."  
*Why unresolved:* The technical feasibility of safe, accurate report generation from structured AI outputs (quality, abnormality, diagnosis) and the pathway to regulatory approval for an AI+LLM hybrid system as a medical device are unclear.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A development study training and validating an LLM module on a large corpus of paired OCT findings and expert reports, with rigorous evaluation of factual accuracy, hallucination rates, and alignment with a predefined regulatory framework (e.g., FDA/CE guidelines for AI/ML-based software).

**Open Question 4:** What specific privacy-preserving and security infrastructure (e.g., federated learning, homomorphic encryption) is required to deploy FOCUS in a distributed clinical network while maintaining strict compliance with international data protection regulations (e.g., GDPR, HIPAA) and ensuring model integrity against adversarial attacks?  
*Basis in paper:* [explicit] "the transition from a research prototype to a widely deployed clinical tool necessitates rigorous adherence to data security and ethical standards... real-world implementation demands robust privacy-preserving infrastructures... Future iterations of FOCUS must integrate advanced security measures — including end-to-end encryption and strict access control protocols."  
*Why unresolved:* The paper acknowledges this as a critical barrier but does not specify or evaluate any concrete security architecture. The computational overhead, impact on model performance, and practical deployability of such measures in resource-constrained clinical settings are unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A systems engineering paper detailing a deployable FOCUS architecture implementing specific privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs), accompanied by a formal security analysis and a compliance assessment against relevant jurisdictional legal frameworks.

## Limitations
*   Reproduction is blocked by missing critical details: hyperparameters, exact data splits, augmentation strategies, and a full specification of the UAAC's attention mechanism, loss function, and uncertainty integration.
*   The sequential pipeline carries a risk of error propagation; the quality assessment module may incorrectly discard slices containing subtle but important pathology, particularly across unseen device artifacts.
*   Generalization is a concern due to potential data bias in the multi-center training cohort; performance may degrade on underrepresented populations, rare diseases, or novel OCT devices.
