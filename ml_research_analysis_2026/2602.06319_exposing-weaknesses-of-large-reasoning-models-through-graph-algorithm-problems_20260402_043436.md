# Exposing Weaknesses of Large Reasoning Models through Graph Algorithm Problems

## Quick Facts

- arXiv ID: 2602.06319
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06319
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: GrAlgoBench evaluation of 20 LRMs reveals severe accuracy drop on graphs >120 nodes and pervasive over‑thinking.

## Executive Summary

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) are tested on GrAlgoBench, a benchmark of 2,700 graph algorithm problems derived from real-world networks (8–160 nodes). The evaluation uncovers two major weaknesses: accuracy plummets as graph size grows, falling below 50% for graphs exceeding 120 nodes due to execution errors, weak memory, and redundant reasoning; and widespread over‑thinking, where self‑verification steps inflate reasoning traces with little correctness gain. These findings expose fundamental limitations in long‑context reasoning and efficient verification, and establish graph algorithms as a rigorous testbed for advancing LRM reasoning.

## Method Summary

The study constructs GrAlgoBench by sampling subgraphs from five real-world networks (DBLP, Street Network, OpenFlight, Wikipedia, DBpedia) and generating nine tasks across three reasoning taxonomies: Enumeration (e.g., Maximum Clique), Exploration (e.g., Diameter), and Intuition (e.g., Minimum Spanning Tree). Each task is instantiated with 50 problems per six size levels, totaling 2,700 instances. Models are prompted with the problem description and generate up to 32,768 tokens using temperature 0.6, top_p 0.95, min_p 0. For each problem, 8 responses are sampled (or 4 for some closed models). Correctness is assessed via exact match against a ground‑truth answer, expecting the result inside a `\boxed{}` delimiter. Primary metrics are Pass@k and Cons@k. To diagnose weaknesses, an LLM‑as‑judge pipeline segments responses, identifies the first correct answer, classifies error types (Algorithm Selection, Execution, Information, Output Quality), and quantifies over‑thinking through high‑entropy tokens and self‑verification effectiveness.

## Key Results

- LRM accuracy drops sharply with graph size; for graphs >120 nodes, accuracy falls below 50% across models, driven by step‑by‑step execution errors, weak memory, and redundant reasoning.
- Over‑thinking is pervasive: self‑verification occupies 30–40% of reasoning traces but correctly identifies prior errors in <30% of cases; outcome efficiency remains high (>0.88), indicating correct answers appear early yet are followed by ineffective checks.
- A consistent taxonomy gap exists: models perform best on Enumeration tasks, followed by Exploration, and worst on Intuition, even after normalizing for intrinsic difficulty.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) cannot maintain accurate graph representations or stable reasoning traces as input context grows, causing a collapse in accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** Longer inputs force models to process extended structural information, leading to cascading errors: frequent step‑by‑step execution mistakes, forgetting or misrepresenting graph edges/nodes, and injecting redundant reasoning that distracts from core logic.
- **Core assumption:** Graph algorithm solving requires persistent, exact memory of graph topology across many inference steps; exceeding the model’s effective context window memory disrupts this.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "accuracy declines drastically as graph size (and thus input length) grows, dropping below 50% for graphs exceeding 120 nodes… driven by frequent step‑by‑step execution errors, weak memory, and redundant reasoning."
  - [section] DeepSeek-R1 TRIANGLESUM: 100% at 4k tokens → 22% at 64k; Qwen3-32B: 87% (31–50 nodes) → 47% (121–160 nodes).
  - [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., QwenLong-L1, Progressive Thought Encoding) indicate a broader community interest in long‑context reasoning limits; while not directly about graphs, they contextualize the severity of context‑scale degradation.
- **Break condition:** LRMs maintain ≥80% accuracy on ≥150‑node graphs across at least two taxonomies, with error rates (execution, memory) comparable to small‑graph baselines.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** LRMs engage in pervasive over‑thinking because self‑verification steps are both frequent and ineffective, inflating token counts without reliability gains.
- **Mechanism:** High‑entropy tokens (“wait”, “but”, “so”) trigger self‑verification segments that occupy 30–40% of reasoning traces, but these checks correctly identify prior errors in <30% of cases; most merely restate or add noise.
- **Core assumption:** LRMs lack calibration to distinguish productive from redundant verification, so they default to verbose self‑monitoring that wastes computational budget.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "LRMs suffer from an over‑thinking phenomenon, primarily driven by extensive yet largely ineffective self‑verification, which inflates reasoning traces without improving correctness."
  - [section] Outcome efficiency (fraction of tokens up to first correct answer) stays >0.88 on graph tasks, meaning most correct answers appear early; yet self‑verification proportion is high with effectiveness often <30%.
  - [corpus] Corpus includes papers on inefficient reasoning (e.g., “Reasoning Models Reason Well, Until They Don’t”) suggesting over‑thinking is a recognized failure mode.
- **Break condition:** Self‑verification effectiveness exceeds 50% while outcome efficiency remains >0.9 across graph tasks, indicating productive checks.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** LRMs exhibit a systematic taxonomy gap: performance ranks consistently as Enumeration > Exploration > Intuition, even after normalizing for baseline difficulty.
- **Mechanism:** LRMs excel at systematic, deterministic procedures (brute‑force enumeration, search) but stumble on heuristic, global‑optimization reasoning that requires jumping to local decisions without exhaustive exploration.
- **Core assumption:** Current LRM training and inductive biases reward step‑by‑step verification, not the intuitive pattern recognition needed for greedy algorithms.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Both reasoning and non‑reasoning models achieve the best performance on Enumeration tasks, followed by Exploration, and the worst on Intuition."
  - [section] Normalized z‑scores across 32B and 8B models show the same hierarchy; e.g., at Level‑6, Qwen3‑32B Cons@k: Enumeration 0.63, Exploration 0.32, Intuition 0.26.
  - [corpus] Limited direct evidence; neighboring papers focus on efficiency and long‑context but the taxonomy disparity aligns with documented reasoning weaknesses (e.g., “On the Self‑awareness of LRMs”).
- **Break condition:** At challenging scales (Level‑6), Intuition task accuracy is within 10 percentage points of the best taxonomy (Enumeration) across ≥5 strong LRMs.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Graph sampler -> Task instantiator -> Model executor -> Exact‑match verifier -> Error analysis pipeline

**Critical path:**  
1. **Dataset construction:** For each of the 9 tasks, generate 50 problems per 6 size levels → 2,700 instances with real semantics.  
2. **Inference:** For each model, run 8 generations per instance; extract final `\boxed{}` answer.  
3. **Primary metrics:** Compute Pass@k and Cons@k per model/task/size.  
4. **RQ1 analysis:** Plot metrics vs. graph size and token length; aggregate error types across taxonomies.  
5. **RQ2 analysis:** Segment responses; compute outcome efficiency and self‑verification effectiveness; correlate with entropy.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Real‑world graphs provide semantic grounding but introduce uncontrolled topology variability; synthetic graphs would allow cleaner isolation of size effects.  
- Exact‑match evaluation is rigorous but penalizes harmless formatting alternatives; fuzzy matching would require careful normalization rules.  
- Eight generations offer stable estimates but are expensive; some models were evaluated with only 4 due to budget.  
- LLM‑as‑judge for errors scales better than human annotation but inherits judge model biases; the paper mitigated this with manual spot‑checks (3 annotators).

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Size collapse:** >30% accuracy drop between Level‑3 (31–50 nodes) and Level‑6 (121–160 nodes) even for “Easy” tasks (e.g., Max Degree).  
- **Over‑thinking:** Redundancy errors in >35% of incorrect cases; outcome efficiency >0.90 yet Cons@k <50%.  
- **Memory fragility:** Graph memorization errors exceed 20% in Exploration/Intuition tasks and rise sharply with size.  
- **Benchmark sensitivity:** Changing graph serialization format (adjacency list vs. edge list) should not alter the relative performance patterns significantly (Table 6 shows similar trends).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Size‑scaling replication:** Run Qwen3‑32B on all 2,700 problems; reproduce Table 3 with Cons@k declining from ~0.97 (Level‑1) to ~0.47 (Level‑6) on Intuition.  
2. **Token‑length isolation:** On 80‑node graphs, vary node description length to hit 4k/8k/16k/32k/64k tokens; measure accuracy drop on Triangle/DistanceK/MKC; expect steep decline beyond 16k tokens even if graph structure is fixed.  
3. **Over‑thinking quantification:** For QWQ‑32B, compute outcome efficiency and self‑verification effectiveness on all graph tasks; confirm efficiency >0.88 and effectiveness <0.30, reproducing Figure 6.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can we enhance LRMs' ability to retain accurate graph structure information in long‑context reasoning to mitigate severe performance degradation as graph size increases?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper identifies weak memory capability as a primary bottleneck (Section 3.1), demonstrated by high graph memorization error rates (over 21% in Exploration/Intuition) and performance dropping below 50% for graphs >120 nodes.
- **Why unresolved:** The study thoroughly diagnoses the problem but does not investigate underlying mechanisms or propose concrete improvements for memory retention.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Architectural or training interventions that reduce memorization errors by at least 50% on large graphs (e.g., Level‑6), leading to stable or improved pass@k without increasing reasoning trace length.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What modifications to training, decoding, or self‑reflection mechanisms can suppress frequent yet ineffective self‑verification that causes overthinking while preserving or enhancing solution correctness?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper shows overthinking is primarily driven by extensive self‑verification with effectiveness below 30% across tasks (Section 3.2), inflating reasoning traces without improving correctness.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis quantifies the phenomenon but does not explore why verification is largely ineffective or how to design more efficient verification strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Reduced proportion of self‑verification segments (e.g., below 10%) combined with equal or higher pass@k and similar or improved outcome efficiency (>0.9 on graph tasks).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent are the weaknesses of long‑context degradation and overthinking universal across diverse reasoning domains (e.g., logical deduction, code generation, mathematical proof), versus specific to graph algorithm tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper reports moderate correlations between GrAlgoBench and LongBench‑v2 (ρ=0.60) and GPQA (ρ=0.77), but these are based on a limited subset of models and benchmarks (Section 3.3).
- **Why unresolved:** Generalization remains an open assumption; the paper only tests a few models on two external benchmarks, leaving unclear whether graph‑specific structural reasoning or broader LRM deficiencies are responsible.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies using the same error analysis and segmentation methodology across a wide range of non‑graph reasoning tasks, confirming similar patterns of memory‑related errors and ineffective self‑verification.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can we design evaluation protocols that fairly assess intrinsic reasoning ability when models are allowed to use external tools (e.g., code execution) for solving graph algorithm problems?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes that enabling tool usage "no longer directly evaluates the model’s intrinsic reasoning ability" (Section 3.3) and thus avoided it as the primary evaluation setting.
- **Why unresolved:** There is a fundamental tension: tool usage dramatically improves performance but obscures reasoning quality; the paper highlights this issue but offers no methodology to disentangle tool reliance from reasoning.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A validated framework that measures reasoning separately from tool‑calling, such as requiring explicit justification before tool invocation or controlling for tool access, enabling meaningful comparisons between tool‑augmented and pure reasoning modes.

## Limitations

- Prompt templates are not fully specified; minor phrasing changes may alter model behavior and reproducibility.
- LLM‑as‑judge error labeling lacks human agreement statistics, introducing potential bias.
- Real‑world graphs bring uncontrolled topology, making it difficult to isolate size effects from graph complexity.

## Confidence

- Size collapse: High
- Over‑thinking: Medium
- Taxonomy gap: High
