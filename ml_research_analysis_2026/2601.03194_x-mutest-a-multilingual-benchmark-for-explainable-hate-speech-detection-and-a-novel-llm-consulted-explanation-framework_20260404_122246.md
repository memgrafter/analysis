# X-MuTeST: A Multilingual Benchmark for Explainable Hate Speech Detection and A Novel LLM-consulted Explanation Framework

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03194
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03194
- **Reference count:** 13
- **Primary result:** X-MuTeST achieves SOTA accuracy on Telugu (88.81%), Hindi (87.45%) and English (86.04%) with improved explainability.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces X-MuTeST, a novel framework for explainable hate speech detection in under-resourced languages (Hindi, Telugu, English). The core innovation is a two-stage training approach that aligns model attention first with human-annotated rationales, then with model-derived n-gram importance scores, and finally consults LLaMA-3.1 to produce final explanations. The authors also release new benchmark datasets with token-level rationales for 6,004 Hindi, 4,492 Telugu, and 6,334 English samples.  

Experimental results demonstrate state-of-the-art performance, achieving accuracies of 88.81% on Telugu, 87.45% on Hindi, and 86.04% on English, along with improved token-level explainability (Token-F1 up to 0.6231) and better faithfulness (lower Sufficiency). The framework generalizes effectively to existing English and Portuguese benchmarks, highlighting its robustness for multilingual hate speech detection.

## Method Summary
X-MuTeST is a framework for explainable hate speech detection in under-resourced languages (Hindi, Telugu, English). It uses pretrained multilingual transformers (MuRIL for Telugu/English, XLM-R for Hindi) and introduces a two-stage training regimen. In Stage 1 (first 3 epochs), the model's attention is guided by an attention alignment loss that encourages agreement with human-annotated token rationales, weighted by α=0.3. Stage 2 replaces the human rationales with target masks derived from a novel n-gram-based explainability score: for each n-gram (unigram, bigram, trigram), the drop in class probability upon masking is computed; token scores are aggregated across containing n-grams with fixed weights (0.5, 0.3, 0.2). The top‑k tokens from these scores serve as the attention supervision target, with α tuned per language (Telugu/English: 0.6, Hindi: 0.7). At inference, explanations are generated by re‑applying the n‑gram scoring and then merging the resulting tokens with zero‑shot rationales from LLaMA‑3.1 via set union, aiming to combine model‑derived saliency with LLM contextual reasoning.

## Key Results
- Achieves SOTA accuracy on Telugu (88.81%), Hindi (87.45%), and English (86.04%).
- Improves token-level explainability (Token-F1: Telugu 0.6231, Hindi 0.4344, English 0.5125).
- Enhances faithfulness (lower Sufficiency) and generalizes to existing English and Portuguese benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Guiding the model's attention with human-annotated token rationales in Stage 1 improves alignment between model reasoning and human judgment, particularly for capturing culturally nuanced hate indicators in under-resourced languages.
- **Mechanism:** The attention alignment loss (`L_att`) penalizes divergence between the model's token attention scores (computed via dot-product with `[CLS]`) and the binary human rationale labels. This supervision forces the model to assign higher attention weights to tokens identified by native annotators as hate-relevant.
- **Core assumption:** Human rationales reliably capture the sociocultural context of hate speech for a given language, and aligning model attention to these rationales transfers this contextual knowledge.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "leveraging human rationales during training enhances both classification performance and explainability."
  - [section] "In Stage-1, the model’s attention is guided by human rationales... The attention alignment loss is computed using cross-entropy."
  - [corpus] Evidence is indirect; neighboring papers (e.g., "Aligning Attention with Human Rationales...") support the general approach but are not cited by X-MuTeST. Strength: moderate.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if human rationale annotations are noisy, inconsistent, or incomplete. The paper notes "moderate to substantial agreement" (61-80% Kappa) pre-iteration, implying initial label noise could misguide attention. Performance hinges on annotation quality.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The n-gram-based explainability score in Stage 2 provides a model-derived, context-aware attention target that refines focus beyond human rationales, capturing predictive n-grams that may be missed by annotators.
- **Mechanism:** For each n-gram (unigram, bigram, trigram), the logit drop (`∆P`) upon masking measures the n-gram's contribution to the original prediction. Token scores (`E[t]`) aggregate these drops across all containing n-grams, weighted (`w1=0.5, w2=0.3, w3=0.2`). Top-k tokens form the target attention mask for Stage 2.
- **Core assumption:** Predicting a drop in class probability when an n-gram is masked (`f(S) - f(ng)`) is a valid, model-intrinsic measure of that n-gram's importance for the hate speech decision.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "a novel explainability method that computes the difference between the prediction probabilities of the original text and those of unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams."
  - [section] Explicit equations (5)-(9) define the n-gram contribution scoring and normalization.
  - [corpus] Not directly supported by neighbors; this is a novel formulation. Strength: low/novel.
- **Break condition:** Fails if the masking perturbation (`<MASK>`) does not semantically or functionally replace the hate-indicative content, especially in morphologically rich or code-mixed languages (e.g., Telugu). The method assumes linear, additive contributions, which may not hold for complex, compositional hate expressions.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Consulting LLaMA-3.1 and taking the union of its rationales with the X-MuTeST method's output produces final explanations that are more plausible and faithful than either source alone, combining semantic breadth with task-specific saliency.
- **Mechanism:** The final explanation set `E_final = E_X ∪ E_LLM` merges tokens identified by the n-gram-based method (`E_X`) and by zero-shot prompting of LLaMA-3.1 (`E_LLM`). The assumption is that LLM rationales provide high-level, context-aware reasoning that complements the more locally focused n-gram saliency.
- **Core assumption:** LLaMA-3.1's zero-shot rationales have non-empty, complementary overlap with human judgments for these languages, as suggested by its higher token-F1 than other LLMs in the paper's evaluation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Final explanations are computed as the union between LLM explanations and X-MuTeST explanations."
  - [section] "We integrate LLMs, as they have shown promise in semantic and contextual reasoning... LLaMA-3.1 is selected... it demonstrated a higher token-level alignment with human rationales."
  - [corpus] Not cited in neighbors; the "LLM-consulted" fusion is a novel design choice. Strength: low/novel.
- **Break condition:** The union is detrimental if `E_LLM` is highly noisy or misaligned for under-resourced languages. The introduction example shows LLMs missing offensive Hindi terms due to cultural gaps. If `E_LLM` adds mostly false positives, union plausibility (Token-F1) drops. The "agreement score" (Eq. 11) is diagnostic but not a guardrail.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Attention Mechanism in Transformers**  
  - **Why needed here:** The core training objective (`L_att`) directly manipulates the attention weights (`a_i`) of the encoder. Understanding how attention scores are computed (Eq. 1) and how they can be supervised is essential to grasp Stage 1.  
  - **Quick check question:** How does the dot-product attention between token hidden states `h_i` and `[CLS]` state `h_[CLS]` determine a token's importance for classification?

- **Concept: Explainability Metrics (Plausibility & Faithfulness)**  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's claims of improved explainability are validated solely through these metrics (Token-F1, IOU-F1, Comprehensiveness, Sufficiency). An engineer must understand what each metric measures and its limitations (e.g., sufficiency penalizes reliance on the rationale alone).  
  - **Quick check question:** Why is a *lower* Sufficiency score considered better, and what does a high Comprehensiveness score indicate about the rationale's importance?

- **Concept: Challenges of Multilingual & Low-Resource NLP**  
  - **Why needed here:** The entire motivation hinges on the scarcity of rationale-annotated data and cultural context for Hindi/Telugu. Understanding issues like transliteration errors (Telugu from Latin script), morphological complexity, and lack of pretraining data explains why off-the-shelf LLMs fail and why the two-stage, rationale-guided approach is proposed.  
  - **Quick check question:** How might the transliteration step (IndicXlit) for Telugu introduce errors that affect both classification and LLM rationale quality?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  1. **Input & Tokenizer:** Raw text → token IDs (language-specific tokenizer, e.g., for Muril/XLMR).  
  2. **Encoder:** Transformer (Muril for Telugu/English, XLMR for Hindi) → token hidden states `h_i`, `h_[CLS]`.  
  3. **Attention Module:** Computes normalized attention scores `a_i` (Eq. 1) from `h_i` and `h_[CLS]`.  
  4. **Classifier Head:** Linear layer on `h_[CLS]` → logits `P`.  
  5. **Explainability Module (X-MuTeST):** For a given input, runs masking for all n-grams, computes `∆P_ng`, aggregates to `E[t]` (Eq. 8), normalizes → `˜E[t]`.  
  6. **LLM Consult Module:** API call to LLaMA-3.1 with a prompt to extract hateful tokens → `E_LLM`.  
  7. **Loss Functions:** `L_cl` (cross-entropy), `L_att` (cross-entropy between `a_i` and target mask `R_i` or `˜E[t]`).  
  8. **Two-Stage Trainer:** Switches target attention mask (human rationales → `˜E[t]`) and `α` weight between `L_att` and `L_cl` at epoch boundary.  
  9. **Post-Training Explainer:** For a test input, runs trained encoder through X-MuTeST method, calls LLM, computes `E_final = E_X ∪ E_LLM`.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. **Data Prep:** Load human-annotated dataset (token rationales `R_i`). Split train/val/test.  
  2. **Stage 1 Training (Epochs 1-3):** For each batch, compute `a_i`, `L_att` against `R_i` (Eq. 2), `L_cl`. Total loss `L1 = α*L_att + (1-α)*L_cl` with `α=0.3`.  
  3. **Stage 2 Training (Remaining Epochs):** For each batch, run X-MuTeST explainer *in training mode* (or pre-compute?) to get `˜E[t]` as target mask. Compute `L_att` against `˜E[t]`. Use language-specific `α` (Telugu/Eng=0.6, Hindi=0.7). Total loss `L2`.  
  4. **Evaluation:** For test samples, generate `E_X` (final model's X-MuTeST scores), `E_LLM` (LLaMA call). Compute `E_final`. Metrics: classification (Acc, F1) on full text; plausibility (Token-F1, IOU-F1) comparing `E_final` to human `R_i`; faithfulness (Comprehensiveness, Sufficiency) by masking `E_final` or feeding only `E_final`.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **α parameter:** Balances classification vs. explainability guidance. Higher `α` (Stage 2) forces more attention alignment but risks underfitting classification if the target mask (`˜E[t]`) is poor. Language-specific tuning (`α=0.7` for Hindi) suggests dataset-specific optimal bias.  
  - **n-gram weighting (`w1,w2,w3`):** Prioritizes unigrams (0.5). This may underweight contextual phrases where hate is relational (e.g., "bark" in Hindi context). Fixed weights may not generalize.  
  - **LLM choice:** Fixates on LLaMA-3.1 based on its *relative* token-F1 vs. other LLMs. Does not guarantee absolute quality. Using a more capable or instruction-tuned LLM might change `E_LLM` and `E_final` significantly.  
  - **Union (`∪`) vs. Intersection/Weighted Merge:** Simple union assumes both sources are complementary and equally valid. Could amplify noise if both are wrong on different tokens.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Low Inter-Annotator Agreement (Kappa < 0.6):** Indicates subjective or ambiguous hate cues. Stage 1 training with noisy `R_i` will misalign attention. Check annotation logs.  
  - **High Sufficiency Score (>0.5):** Indicates the rationale (`E_final`) alone nearly preserves the prediction, meaning the model is overly reliant on a few tokens, potentially prone to shortcut learning. Diagnose with per-sample analysis.  
  - **Large Performance Gap between `E_X` and `E_LLM` Agreement:** If `Agreement` (Eq. 11) is very low (<0.3), the union may create noisy, incoherent explanations. Inspect `E_X` and `E_LLM` side-by-side.  
  - **Telugu Performance Collapse:** Likely cause: poor transliteration (Latin → Telugu script) corrupting token boundaries or meanings, breaking both n-gram masking and LLM comprehension. Validate transliteration quality.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Replication:** Finetune Muril/XLMR *without any rationale guidance* (just `L_cl`). Evaluate classification and LIME-based explanations. Establishes the performance ceiling without explainability interventions.  
  2. **Ablation: Stage 1 Only:** Train with only human rationales (`α=0.3` fixed, no Stage 2). Measures contribution of human alignment alone. Expect improved plausibility but potential classification drop vs. full X-MuTeST.  
  3. **Ablation: Stage 2 Only:** Train from scratch with only n-gram guided attention (`α` set to Stage 2 values). Tests if the model-derived masks are sufficient without human seed. Expect lower plausibility than full X-MuTeST, as it lacks human cultural grounding.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How effectively does the X-MuTeST framework generalize to under-resourced languages beyond Hindi, Telugu, and English, particularly those with non-Latin scripts or extremely limited training data?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states: "Further investigations could involve expanding the framework to handle more languages."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates on three languages, all with existing datasets and moderate resources. Scalability to truly low-resource or typologically diverse languages remains untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic evaluation on a diverse set of under-resourced languages (e.g., African, Indigenous, or other Indic languages) with varying script systems, morphological complexity, and data availability, measuring performance drop and explainability consistency.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What linguistic or dataset-specific factors explain the empirically tuned difference in optimal α values (0.7 for Hindi vs. 0.6 for Telugu/English) during Stage-2 training, and how should α be set for new languages?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The methodology uses different α values per language based on "empirical tuning" without theoretical justification or analysis of language characteristics.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not investigate why Hindi requires higher weight on attention loss, leaving practitioners without guidance for new languages.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments correlating optimal α with linguistic features (e.g., morphological richness, syntax) or dataset metrics (e.g., average rationale length, hate speech prevalence), possibly developing a predictive model for α based on these features.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Under what circumstances does consulting LLMs (e.g., LLaMA-3.1) in the explanation union provide meaningful improvements in explainability or classification for low-resource languages, given their observed poor classification performance on Telugu and Hindi?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] LLMs show low classification accuracy on Telugu/Hindi (e.g., Llama-3.1: 0.5172 Acc on Hindi) yet are included in the union without analysis of their contribution's conditions.
- **Why unresolved:** The union approach assumes LLM rationales are complementary, but their inaccurate classifications could introduce noise; the trade-off is not studied.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies removing LLM rationales per language, analyzing cases where LLM rationales align with human rationales despite low classification accuracy, and measuring impact on faithfulness and plausibility metrics when LLM performance varies.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive is the X-MuTeST explainability method to the chosen n-gram weights (w1=0.5, w2=0.3, w3=0.2), and are these weights optimal across different languages and hate speech contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The weights are set heuristically with no tuning or justification.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze whether unigrams should be prioritized over longer n-grams, or if weights should vary by language or domain.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Sensitivity analysis varying weights and measuring changes in Token-F1 and faithfulness across languages, possibly learning weights per language or developing a metric to automate weight selection.

## Limitations
- Moderate inter-annotator agreement (Kappa 61-80%) indicates noisy rationales that may misguide Stage 1 training.
- The n-gram masking method assumes additive contributions, which fails for complex, compositional hate expressions in morphologically rich languages like Telugu.
- LLM consultation relies on zero‑shot prompts; token extraction is brittle and may add noise to explanations.

## Confidence
- *Accuracy gains*: High – reported metrics are clear and reproducible.
- *Explainability improvements*: Medium – metrics correlate with human judgment but have known biases; limited human evaluation.
- *LLM fusion benefit*: Low – novel design with no baseline comparison; effect size may be due to chance or prompt engineering.

## Next Checks

3. Conduct sensitivity analysis on α and n‑gram weights, and test alternative masking (e.g., synonym replacement) for Telugu.
