# What Do Visual Tokens Really Encode? Uncovering Sparsity and Redundancy in Multimodal Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00510  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00510  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** ~40% of visual tokens are redundant (sink/dead) and can be pruned without performance loss; alive tokens already encode rich, fine-grained semantics (objects, colors, OCR) and align with mid-LLM layers, making shallow visual processing unnecessary for most tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces EmbedLens, a probing framework, to dissect the semantic structure of visual tokens in multimodal large language models (MLLMs). It reveals that visual tokens fall into three functionally distinct groups: sink (stable, input-agnostic), dead (repetitive, semantically void), and alive (contextual, image-specific). Approximately 40% of tokens are redundant and can be pruned without harming performance on standard VQA and hallucination benchmarks. Alive tokens already carry rich, fine-grained semantics before entering the LLM, and internal visual computations (vMHA/vFFN) are largely unnecessary except for highly vision-centric tasks like REC and OCR. Moreover, alive tokens naturally align with intermediate LLM layers, implying that direct mid-layer injection is sufficient and early-layer processing is redundant. These findings offer a pathway to more efficient MLLM designs.

## Method Summary
The core method combines anchor-based clustering and a semantic probe called EmbedLens. First, projected visual embeddings (output of CLIP ViT after a linear projector) are normalized and clustered using cosine similarity with a threshold τ=0.9. Clusters that exhibit high cross-image similarity (>0.98) are identified as stable. For each cluster, EmbedLens retrieves the nearest tokens from the LLM’s input embedding matrix to assign a semantic label. Tokens are then classified into sink, dead, and alive based on the consistency and meaningfulness of their nearest text tokens. Interventions include pruning specific token groups at the input, zeroing out visual-only sublayers (vMHA/vFFN), and skipping early LLM layers. The impact of these manipulations is measured on a suite of benchmarks (GQA, MME, MMBench, TextVQA, OCRBench, DocVQA, RefCOCO+). The experiments are conducted on LLaVA-v1.5 (7B/13B), Qwen2.5-VL-8B, and InternVL3-8B.

## Key Results
- ~40% of visual tokens are redundant (sink or dead) and can be pruned without performance drop on general VQA and hallucination tasks.
- Alive tokens already encode rich, fine-grained semantics (e.g., objects, colors, OCR) before LLM processing; disabling internal visual computations (vMHA/vFFN) harms only vision-centric tasks (REC, OCR) but leaves general VQA unaffected.
- Alive tokens naturally align with mid-LLM layers; skipping early visual layers (up to 6) preserves or even improves performance, indicating that shallow processing is redundant.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Token Partition via Clustering and Semantic Probing**  
Visual tokens are divided into sink, dead, and alive groups. Anchor-based clustering on cosine similarity of projected visual embeddings reveals stable cross-image clusters. The largest cluster consistently shows >98% similarity across images, indicating input-agnostic sink tokens. EmbedLens then retrieves nearest text tokens for each cluster centroid; those aligning with nonsensical fragments or extreme norms are classified as sink/dead, while those aligning with meaningful concepts (e.g., object labels) are alive. The linear projection from CLIP features into the LLM space is assumed to preserve and amplify redundant patterns, creating a heterogeneous distribution. Evidence includes cluster tracking and EmbedLens analyses; if clustering fails to yield stable clusters or EmbedLens shows no semantic distinction, the mechanism fails.

**Mechanism 2: Alive Tokens Encode Semantics Pre-LLM; Visual Sublayers Redundant**  
Alive tokens already contain rich, fine-grained semantics (e.g., objects, colors, OCR) before entering the LLM. A patch-compression benchmark forces multiple attributes into single patches; EmbedLens retrieval shows alive tokens can decode multiple attributes. Layer decoupling (zeroing vMHA/vFFN) and skipping early visual layers demonstrate that disabling internal visual computations only harms vision-centric tasks (e.g., REC) while leaving general VQA and hallucination tasks unaffected. This indicates the projector aligns visual embeddings directly to the LLM’s semantic space, making intra-visual transformations largely unnecessary unless spatially grounded reasoning is required.

**Mechanism 3: Mid-Layer Alignment via Norm Amplification**  
Alive tokens naturally align with intermediate LLM layers rather than the input space. Visual tokens have much higher L₂ norms than text tokens, causing shallow layers to exert minimal influence due to residual stream dilution. Norm scaling experiments show that reducing visual token norms forces more transformation but degrades performance. Moreover, alive tokens’ norms peak in mid-layers, and skipping early visual layers (up to 6) preserves or improves performance. This suggests the projection layer intentionally amplifies norms to bypass shallow processing and inject tokens directly where they best interface with textual reasoning.

## Foundational Learning
- **MLLM architecture**: The pipeline (vision encoder → projector → LLM with visual sublayers) defines where and how visual tokens are processed. *Why needed*: Locating the injection point and understanding processing stages is essential for analyzing token groups and their transformations. *Quick check*: Where does the vision encoder output meet the LLM, and which LLM layers can attend to visual tokens?
- **Attention mechanisms and residual streams**: Transformers use residual connections; the norm of the residual stream modulates the impact of sublayer outputs. *Why needed*: The paper examines attention distribution to sink tokens and how residual norms affect update magnitudes, key to understanding why early layers have little effect on high-norm visual tokens. *Quick check*: How does the residual stream norm affect the magnitude of updates from a sublayer?
- **Embedding space similarity metrics**: Cosine similarity measures direction alignment; L₂ norm measures magnitude. *Why needed*: Clustering uses cosine similarity; sink detection uses norms; EmbedLens retrieves nearest tokens via cosine similarity. *Quick check*: What distinguishes cosine similarity from L₂ norm for token comparison?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Vision encoder (e.g., CLIP ViT) → Projector (linear layer) → LLM backbone (e.g., LLaMA with interleaved text/visual tokens) → Visual-only sublayers (vMHA, vFFN) within each transformer block. EmbedLens probe uses the LLM’s input embedding matrix.

**Critical path**  
1. Visual tokens are generated by ViT and projected into LLM token space via the projector.  
2. At model input, embeddings are clustered using anchor-based cosine similarity; EmbedLens retrieves nearest text tokens to classify each token as sink, dead, or alive.  
3. Interventions are applied: prune sink/dead tokens; zero out vMHA/vFFN outputs; skip early LLM layers.  
4. Downstream performance on VQA/OCR/CV benchmarks is measured, and token norms/alignments are tracked across layers.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Pruning sink/dead tokens reduces context length but may disrupt attention patterns; however, the paper finds stable reallocation to text sinks.  
- Choosing injection point: Direct mid-layer injection (bypassing early layers) is more efficient but may require norm scaling adjustments.  
- Computation vs. performance: Disabling vMHA/vFFN improves efficiency for general tasks but harms vision-centric tasks, suggesting selective gating is needed.

**Failure signatures**  
- Pruning alive tokens causes >5% drop on object-level VQA.  
- Disabling vMHA severely hurts REC tasks, indicating spatial reasoning dependence.  
- Forcing visual tokens through all shallow layers without norm scaling can hurt performance, as early layers may dilute high-norm tokens.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Cluster analysis**: Compute cosine similarity between projected visual embeddings from multiple images; identify top clusters with cross-image similarity >0.98 as candidate sink clusters.  
2. **EmbedLens validation**: For each cluster centroid, retrieve top-k text tokens; consistent nonsensical fragments indicate sink/dead, while meaningful object labels indicate alive.  
3. **Layer decoupling**: Zero out vMHA or vFFN outputs for visual tokens only; evaluate on GQA and RefCOCO+ to see impact on general vs. spatial tasks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What mechanisms in CLIP pre‑training cause dead tokens, and can they be reduced?  
2. Why do newer vision encoders show less token redundancy, and what design principles minimize sink/dead tokens?  
3. Can a projection layer directly inject visual tokens into mid‑LLM layers based on type, avoiding shallow processing?  
4. Why do MLLMs exhibit contextual color bias, and how can it be corrected?

## Limitations
- Projector architecture and training details (linear vs. MLP, frozen vs. trained) vary across MLLMs and were not systematically studied; this may affect token group distributions.  
- Sink token definitions (e.g., channel indices) are model‑specific (only provided for LLaMA‑2 7B), limiting universality of the findings.  
- Evaluation focuses on a narrow set of benchmarks; performance on complex, multi‑step, or fine‑grained reasoning tasks remains unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (tri‑partition, ~40% redundancy)**: Medium  
- **Claim 2 (alive tokens rich pre‑LLM; visual sublayers redundant)**: Medium‑High  
- **Claim 3 (mid‑layer alignment, shallow layers redundant)**: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Test on diverse backbones**: Replicate the entire pipeline on MLLMs with non‑CLIP vision encoders (e.g., DINOv2, SigLIP) and different projectors (e.g., MLP) to verify if ~40% redundancy and the three‑group partition hold.  
2. **Projector ablation**: For LLaVA, train three variants—linear frozen, linear trained, 2‑layer MLP trained—and compare token group distributions, cross‑image stability, and pruning impact to isolate projector influence.  
3. **Systematic layer decoupling**: For each LLM layer, independently zero out visual‑only MHA and FFN sublayers while keeping text sublayers active. Evaluate on a comprehensive benchmark set (GQA, MME, MMBench, TextVQA, OCRBench, RefCOCO+) to identify which layers are critical for which task types.
