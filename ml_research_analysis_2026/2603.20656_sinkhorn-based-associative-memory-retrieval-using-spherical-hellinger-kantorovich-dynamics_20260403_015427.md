# Sinkhorn Based Associative Memory Retrieval Using Spherical Hellinger Kantorovich Dynamics

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20656
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20656
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Introduces a dense associative memory for empirical measures using a spherical Hellinger‑Kantorovich gradient flow with debiased Sinkhorn divergences, achieving robust retrieval and exponential capacity under a random pattern model.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a novel associative memory framework for empirical measures (weighted point clouds). Both stored patterns and queries are discrete probability measures; retrieval minimizes a log‑sum‑exp energy built from debiased Sinkhorn divergences. The resulting dynamics follow a spherical Hellinger‑Kantorovich gradient flow, which updates support locations via barycentric transport and weights via multiplicative reweighting. Theoretical guarantees include basin invariance, geometric convergence, and an exponential storage capacity in ambient dimension under a random pattern model. Experiments on synthetic Gaussian point‑cloud memories show robust recovery from perturbed queries, significantly outperforming a Euclidean Hopfield‑type baseline.

## Method Summary
The method defines an energy \(E(\xi)\) over the query measure \(\xi\) that aggregates debiased Sinkhorn divergences \(S_\varepsilon\) to each stored pattern, weighted by a Gibbs distribution with inverse temperature \(\beta\). The gradient flow in the spherical Hellinger‑Kantorovich (SHK) geometry yields a deterministic update rule: at each iteration, compute Sinkhorn couplings between the query and each pattern (including self‑coupling) to obtain potentials; then update support points via a barycentric combination of the couplings and update weights multiplicatively using the potentials. The process repeats for a fixed number of steps or until convergence, naturally handling measures with varying weights and support locations.

## Key Results
- Retrieval dynamics are derived as a spherical Hellinger‑Kantorovich gradient flow of a log‑sum‑exp energy built from debiased Sinkhorn divergences.
- Under a random pattern model, the basins of attraction are disjoint with high probability, implying exponential storage capacity in the ambient dimension.
- Empirical evaluations on synthetic Gaussian point‑cloud memories demonstrate robust recovery from perturbations, significantly outperforming a Euclidean Hopfield baseline.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: SHK gradient flow yields deterministic update
**Claim:** The spherical Hellinger‑Kantorovich gradient flow of the LSE energy updates both support locations (via barycentric transport) and weights (via multiplicative reweighting).  
**Mechanism:** The first variation of the energy produces a scalar field \(u_\xi\). In SHK geometry, the flow is \(\partial_t \xi = \nabla\cdot(\xi v) + \xi r\) with velocity \(v = -\nabla u_\xi\) and reaction rate \(r = -u_\xi/\lambda^2\). Discretization gives support updates using barycentric maps from Sinkhorn couplings and weight updates \(a_{\text{new}} \propto a \exp(-\eta/\lambda^2 u_\xi(x))\).  
**Core assumptions:** (A1)–(A): margin separation in \(S_\varepsilon\), local smoothness of \(E\), existence of basin minimizer, local PL inequality; plus \(\min a_m \ge a_{\min} > 0\) and \(\Delta_{\min}\) separation of supports.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract; sections D.1 and D.3; neighbor paper on SHK flows.  
**Break condition:** If (A1) fails (basins overlap) or step size \(\eta\) is too large, causing weights below \(a_{\min}\), support collapse, or exit from admissible manifold.

### Mechanism 2: Discretization to practical algorithm
**Claim:** The continuous SHK flow discretizes to a deterministic retrieval algorithm (Algorithm 1) that alternates support updates via Sinkhorn barycentric projections and weight updates via multiplicative reweighting, both driven by Sinkhorn potentials.  
**Mechanism:** Each iteration: compute Sinkhorn couplings \(\pi_{\xi,X_i}\) and \(\pi_{\xi,\xi}\), obtain potentials \(f_i, g_i\); compute Gibbs weights \(w_i = \exp(-\beta S_\varepsilon)/\sum_j \exp(-\beta S_\varepsilon)\); for each atom \(m\), compute barycentric maps \(T_i(x_m) = \sum_n \pi_i[m,n]/a_m \cdot y_{i,n}\) and \(z_m = \sum_i w_i (f_i[m] - (f_0[m]+g_0[m])/2)\); update \(x_m \leftarrow x_m + \eta(\sum_i w_i T_i(x_m) - T_0(x_m))\) and \(a_m \leftarrow a_m \exp(-\eta/\lambda^2 z_m)\), then renormalize.  
**Core assumptions:** (A1)–(A) hold locally; \(\eta < \eta_{\text{ret},i}\); accurate Sinkhorn solver.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract; section H; neighbor paper on Sinkhorn semigroups.  
**Break condition:** \(\eta\) too large → weights non‑positive or supports violate \(\Delta_{\min}\); Sinkhorn non‑convergence; exponent overflow/underflow.

### Mechanism 3: Exponential capacity under random pattern model
**Claim:** Under a random pattern model, the Sinkhorn basins of attraction are pairwise disjoint with high probability, implying exponential (in \(d\)) storage capacity.  
**Mechanism:** SampAlgo generates patterns with means \(\mu_i = c + R_0/\sqrt{d}\, s_i\) (\(s_i\) random sign vectors) and random weights/supports. With high probability, means are separated by \(d_{\min} = \sqrt{2(1-\gamma)R_0}\) provided \(N \le \lfloor \sqrt{2p} \exp(\gamma^2 d/4) \rfloor\). For small \(\varepsilon\) (\(\varepsilon \log M < (1-\gamma)R_0^2/16\)), Lemma 14 yields a margin \(\Delta = d_{\min}^2/4\) in \(S_\varepsilon\) between any query near pattern \(i\) and any other pattern \(j\), so basins \(B_i(r)\) are disjoint. Hence \(N = O(\exp(\Theta(d)))\).  
**Core assumptions:** \(\Omega\) contains a ball of radius \(R\); each pattern has \(a_{\min}>0\) and \(\Delta_{\min}>0\); \(\varepsilon\) small; random sign model for means.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Theorem 1; Section F; neighbor paper on capacity bounds.  
**Break condition:** \(\varepsilon\) too large; patterns outside the model (e.g., correlated means); \(N\) exceeding the exponential bound.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Sinkhorn divergence and entropic optimal transport**  
*Why needed:* The energy \(E(\xi)\) uses \(S_\varepsilon\) as the dissimilarity between empirical measures. Understanding its definition (debiased via subtracting self‑terms), its metric properties, and computation via dual potentials is essential.  
*Quick check:* What is the formula for the debiased Sinkhorn divergence \(S_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu)\) in terms of entropic OT costs, and why is the debiasing necessary?

**Concept: Spherical Hellinger‑Kantorovich (SHK) geometry**  
*Why needed:* The retrieval dynamics are derived as a gradient flow in SHK geometry, which uniquely couples transport (Wasserstein) and weight rebalancing (spherical Hellinger). The metric determines the gradient form and retraction.  
*Quick check:* For a tangent pair \((r,v)\) at \(\xi\) (\(v\) velocity, \(r\) reaction with \(\int r\,d\xi=0\)), what is the SHK inner product \(\langle (r,v),(r',v')\rangle_{\text{SHK},\xi}\)? How does \(\lambda\) modulate the relative cost of weight changes?

**Concept: Modern Hopfield networks and log‑sum‑exp energy**  
*Why needed:* The overall architecture is a dense associative memory with an LSE energy over stored patterns, creating basins of attraction. This paper extends that idea to measures by replacing Euclidean distances with \(S_\varepsilon\).  
*Quick check:* How does the inverse temperature \(\beta\) influence the energy landscape and the size/quality of retrieval basins? What happens when \(\beta\to\infty\) or \(\beta\to0\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Stored patterns \(\{X_i\}\) & query \(\xi^0\) → Compute Sinkhorn couplings and potentials \((f_i,g_i)\) for each pattern → Compute Gibbs weights \(w_i = \exp(-\beta S_\varepsilon)/\sum_j \exp(-\beta S_\varepsilon)\) → For each atom \(m\): compute barycentric maps \(T_i(x_m)\) and \(z_m\) → Update support \(x_m\) and weight \(a_m\) multiplicatively → Renormalize weights → Repeat for \(K\) iterations → Retrieved \(\xi^K\).

**Critical path (per iteration):**  
1. Sinkhorn solver between \(\xi\) and each \(X_i\) (and self).  
2. Compute \(S_\varepsilon(\xi,X_i)\) and Gibbs weights \(w_i\).  
3. For each atom \(m\), compute barycentric maps \(T_i(x_m)\) using row \(m\) of coupling matrices.  
4. Update \(x_m \leftarrow x_m + \eta(\sum_i w_i T_i(x_m) - T_0(x_m))\).  
5. Compute \(z_m = \sum_i w_i(f_i[m] - \frac{1}{2}(f_0[m]+g_0[m]))\).  
6. Update \(a_m \leftarrow a_m \exp(-\eta/\lambda^2 z_m)\) and renormalize.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- \(\varepsilon\): larger \(\varepsilon\) speeds up Sinkhorn but reduces discriminativity; theory requires \(\varepsilon \log M\) small.  
- \(\eta\): must be \(\le \eta_{\text{ret},i}\) to stay in basin; larger \(\eta\) speeds convergence but risks leaving admissible manifold.  
- \(\lambda\): larger \(\lambda\) downweights weight updates; must match typical scale of \(z_m\) to avoid exponent saturation.  
- Sinkhorn tolerance: looser tolerance reduces cost but may accumulate errors; warm‑starting potentials across iterations helps.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Weights become \(\le a_{\min}\) or negative → \(\eta\) too large or \(z_m\) too extreme.  
- Support points exit \(\Omega\) or violate \(\Delta_{\min}\) separation → transport step too large.  
- Sinkhorn fails to converge within max iterations → adjust \(\varepsilon\) or increase max iterations.  
- Energy increases after update → likely \(\eta\) exceeds smoothness threshold or query outside basin.  
- Numerical overflow/underflow in exponentials → normalize potentials or clip \(z_m\).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Basic retrieval with distinct Gaussians:** Generate stored patterns as uniform weighted samples from well‑separated Gaussian mixtures. Perturb query via small Gaussian jitter on support points. Verify both SinkhornSHK and Euclidean Hopfield recover the correct pattern; check convergence speed/stability (replicates Figure 1).  
2. **Hard case with identical means:** Use stored patterns sharing the same mean but with different covariances, making Euclidean point clouds overlapping. Show Euclidean Hopfield fails while SinkhornSHK succeeds, confirming the advantage of distributional similarity (replicates Figure 2).  
3. **Capacity scaling test:** Randomly generate patterns with increasing dimension \(d\) (e.g., \(d=4,6,8\)) and measure the largest \(N\) before any retrieval failure. Verify \(N\) grows exponentially with \(d\), as predicted by Theorem 1. Keep \(\varepsilon\), \(a_{\min}\), \(\Delta_{\min}\) fixed to meet assumptions.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on strong geometric assumptions (margin separation, local smoothness, existence of basin minimizer) that may not hold for arbitrary real‑world point clouds, potentially leading to overlapping basins or divergence.
- The random pattern model used for capacity analysis oversimplifies realistic data distributions; actual capacity in practice may be lower.
- Implementation aspects (Sinkhorn convergence tolerance, step‑size selection, handling of weights dropping below \(a_{\min}\)) are not fully addressed theoretically and may cause practical failures.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (SHK gradient flow): High – mathematically rigorous, anchored in SHK geometry.
- Mechanism 2 (Discretization to algorithm): Medium – depends on accurate Sinkhorn potentials and step‑size within \(\eta_{\text{ret}}\); no guarantee against numerical drift.
- Mechanism 3 (Exponential capacity): Medium – proved under restrictive random sign model; capacity may be lower in practice.
