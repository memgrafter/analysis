# SAGE-32B: Agentic Reasoning via Iterative Distillation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04237
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04237
- **Reference count:** 33
- **Primary result:** A 32B-parameter model specialized for tool-use loops achieves near-GPT-4 reliability at ~1/7th the operational cost.

## Executive Summary
Long-horizon agentic tasks suffer from error cascades where early mistakes compound into final failure. SAGE-32B addresses this by combining two key innovations: Iterative Distillation (IDA) and an Inverse Reasoning (IR) head. IDA trains on synthetic trajectories with injected failures and teacher critiques, teaching the model to self-correct. The IR head acts as a meta-cognitive verifier, forecasting plan failures before they occur. On benchmark tasks, SAGE-32B outperforms significantly larger models such as GPT-4-Turbo on MATH-500 and Llama-3-70B on AgentBench DB, while operating at a fraction of the cost.

## Method Summary
The core method comprises (1) IDA, which creates a large dataset of failure-augmented trajectories. Teacher models generate critiques that highlight errors and propose corrections, and a student model learns to mimic these critiques and apply them to its own plans. (2) The IR head is a lightweight module trained to predict the success probability of a proposed plan; it is consulted during inference to filter out high-risk actions. This two-stage approach yields a model that can both anticipate and recover from errors in dynamic tool environments.

## Key Results
- 91.8% on MATH-500 (vs. GPT-4-Turbo 72.6%).
- 78.0% on AgentBench DB tasks (vs. Llama-3-70B 69.1%).
- 76% internal recovery rate (vs. Qwen2.5-32B 35%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach works because it explicitly targets error propagation. IDA’s synthetic failures and teacher critiques train the model to recognize common pitfalls and execute corrections. The IR head adds a forward-looking check that prevents high-risk actions from being taken, reducing the chance of unrecoverable errors. Together, these mechanisms break the cascade effect, leading to higher success rates on complex multi-step tasks.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Iterative Distillation (IDA)**
   - *Why needed:* To scalably produce diverse failure trajectories with corrective supervision without costly real-world execution.
   - *Quick check:* Does the training data contain paired failure examples and corresponding teacher corrections?

2. **Inverse Reasoning (IR) Head**
   - *Why needed:* Provides a pre-execution risk assessment, enabling the system to avoid plans likely to fail.
   - *Quick check:* Can the IR head be queried independently to rank candidate plans by predicted success?

3. **Agentic Tool-Use Loops**
   - *Why needed:* The tasks involve repeated interaction with external tools; the model must manage call syntax, state, and error handling.
   - *Quick check:* Are tool calls generated in the correct format and are tool responses properly parsed?

4. **Failure Injection**
   - *Why needed:* Synthetic failures mimic realistic errors, ensuring exposure to a wide range of failure modes during training.
   - *Quick check:* Are injected failures diverse and representative of observed error patterns?

5. **Self-Correction**
   - *Why needed:* Long-horizon tasks inevitably produce early mistakes; the ability to detect and fix them mid-execution is essential.
   - *Quick check:* Does the model modify its plan after receiving an error response from a tool?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** User Input -> Planner -> IR Verifier -> Tool Executor -> Observation -> (if needed) back to Planner.

**Critical path:** (1) Receive user query, (2) generate initial plan, (3) IR head evaluates plan risk, (4) if acceptable, execute first tool call, (5) process observation and replan, (6) repeat until task completion.

**Design tradeoffs:** Adding the IR head increases inference latency but can prevent costly tool misuse; IDA’s synthetic failures may not cover all real-world edge cases; the 32B size balances capability and cost but still requires substantial compute.

**Failure signatures:** (a) High false positives from IR → excessive replanning and slow responses; (b) False negatives → catastrophic plan failure; (c) Recovery loops → repeated failed corrections without progress.

**First experiments:**
1. Correlate IR head risk scores with actual execution outcomes on a validation set.
2. Measure recovery time when known failures are injected, comparing with a no‑IR baseline.
3. Benchmark end‑to‑end cost and latency against GPT‑4‑Turbo on a fixed set of agentic tasks.

## Limitations
- The paper lacks methodological granularity: details of failure injection and IR head architecture are underspecified.
- Benchmark comparisons use internal tasks (e.g., AgentBench DB) without public references, hindering reproducibility.
- The recovery rate metric is undefined, and no ablation isolates the contributions of IDA versus the IR head.
- Operational cost claims lack a transparent breakdown.

## Confidence
- MATH-500 benchmark score: **Medium** confidence (specific but unpublished benchmark).
- Cost advantage (~1/7 GPT-4): **Low** confidence (no cost model provided).
- Recovery rate & self-correction capability: **Low** confidence (metric undefined).
- Generalizability beyond tested tools/tasks: **Low** confidence (no out‑of‑distribution evaluation).
