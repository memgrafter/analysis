# Beyond Perplexity: A Lightweight Benchmark for Knowledge Retention in Supervised Fine-Tuning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03505
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03505
- **Reference count:** 7
- **Primary result:** The Knowledge Retention (KR) Test effectively distinguishes factual retention from stylistic mimicry, achieving 99.56% accuracy for an oracle model while pretrained models perform near random.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the Knowledge Retention (KR) Test, a lightweight benchmark for evaluating factual knowledge retention in supervised fine-tuning (SFT). The test automatically constructs contrastive example pairs from the training corpus, each with a shared context and two syntactically similar continuations—one factually correct and one incorrect. A model’s knowledge retention is measured by comparing conditional likelihoods of the continuations without requiring generative decoding or instruction tuning. Experiments on WikiText-2 with LoRA fine-tuning show that adapters applied to feed-forward network (FFN) layers outperform those on attention layers under equal parameter budgets, and larger base models exhibit higher and more convergent KR scores.

## Method Summary
The KR-Test generates contrastive examples directly from the training corpus. For each selected context, two continuations are created: one that is factually correct (as in the original text) and an altered version that is factually incorrect but syntactically similar. The model’s conditional likelihood for each continuation given the context is computed, and accuracy is measured based on which continuation receives higher likelihood. This approach avoids the ambiguities of perplexity, which can be influenced by stylistic factors, and does not require the model to generate text or be instruction-tuned. The test is applied to models fine-tuned with LoRA, comparing different adapter placements (FFN vs. attention) and different model scales.

## Key Results
- Oracle model (with direct access to source text) achieves 99.56% accuracy on the KR-Test, while standard pretrained models perform near random, confirming the benchmark’s validity and non-triviality.
- Under equal parameter budgets, LoRA adapters applied to feed-forward network (FFN) layers yield higher KR scores than adapters on attention layers, supporting the hypothesis that FFNs encode factual associations.
- Larger base models (8B vs. 1B) demonstrate both higher initial KR scores and greater convergence during fine-tuning, indicating that absolute capacity for factual retention scales with model size.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The KR-Test isolates factual knowledge by using contrastive pairs that differ only in factual correctness while holding syntactic structure constant. By comparing conditional likelihoods, the evaluation directly probes the model’s internal representation of facts without requiring it to produce fluent or stylistically appropriate generations. The test leverages the fact that models fine-tuned on a corpus should assign higher probability to the true continuation if they have retained the factual information. The success of FFN adapters further suggests that factual associations are primarily stored in the feed-forward layers of transformers, while attention mechanisms handle contextual dynamics.

## Foundational Learning
**Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Adapts a pre-trained model to a specific dataset or task. *Why needed:* Pre-trained models may not perform well on downstream tasks without further training. *Quick check:* Fine-tune a small language model on a subset of data; verify loss decreases.

**LORA (Low-Rank Adaptation):** A parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that injects trainable low-rank matrices into model layers, typically freezing original weights. *Why needed:* Reduces memory and compute costs compared to full fine-tuning. *Quick check:* Compare number of trainable parameters and performance against full fine-tuning on a simple task.

**Knowledge Retention vs. Perplexity:** Perplexity measures overall predictive fit but does not distinguish between memorized facts and stylistic patterns. *Why needed:* SFT often aims to teach models specific facts or domain knowledge; perplexity alone may not indicate factual learning. *Quick check:* Compute perplexity on a validation set containing both factual and stylistic variations; see if it correlates with factual accuracy.

**Contrastive Evaluation:** Creating pairs of inputs that differ in a specific attribute (here factual correctness) to probe model internal states. *Why needed:* Allows controlled assessment of a targeted capability without confounding factors. *Quick check:* Build a small set of contrastive pairs manually and test if the model reliably prefers the correct continuation.

**Feed-Forward Networks (FFN) in Transformers:** The feed-forward layers in transformer blocks are hypothesized to store factual and conceptual knowledge. *Why needed:* Understanding where knowledge resides helps optimize fine-tuning. *Quick check:* Probe FFN activations for factual queries (e.g., using CAUSE) and compare to attention layer probes.

**Model Scaling:** Increasing model size generally improves capabilities, including knowledge retention. *Why needed:* To determine if KR scores scale with capacity and whether larger models converge faster. *Quick check:* Evaluate KR scores for models of different sizes (e.g., 1B vs. 8B) on the same fine-tuned data.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Training Corpus -> Contrastive Example Generator -> (Context, Correct, Incorrect) Triples -> Model (with optional LoRA adapters) -> Conditional Likelihood Computation -> Accuracy Score.

**Critical Path:** The evaluation flows from corpus through generation of contrastive pairs, through model inference to compute likelihoods, to aggregation into accuracy. The most computationally intensive step is model inference on the generated pairs.

**Design Tradeoffs:** 
- Automatic contrast generation vs. manual validation: automatic scaling but risks subtle factual errors; manual verification ensures quality but is costly.
- Likelihood comparison vs. free-form generation: likelihood is efficient and controlled; generation can probe broader capabilities but introduces style and decoding randomness.
- Parameter allocation (FFN vs. attention) for LoRA: FFN adapters yield higher KR scores but may trade off other capabilities.

**Failure Signatures:** 
- Near-random accuracy for an oracle model indicates contrastive pairs are not well-formed or the contrast is too subtle.
- No performance difference between FFN and attention adapters would contradict the hypothesis that FFNs encode factual knowledge.
- If larger models do not show higher KR scores, scaling assumptions may not hold for this benchmark.

**First Three Experiments:** 
1. Validate a random sample of automatically generated contrastive pairs to estimate error rate and correlate with KR scores.
2. Replicate the LoRA adapter placement comparison (FFN vs. attention) on a different dataset (e.g., a news corpus) to check generalizability.
3. Run KR-Test on models of varying sizes (e.g., 1B, 3B, 8B) to confirm scaling trends and convergence behavior.

None.

## Limitations
- Scope limited to WikiText-2 and LoRA fine-tuning; not evaluated across diverse domains or alternative fine-tuning methods.
- Automatic generation of contrastive examples may introduce subtle biases or factual inaccuracies that affect KR scores.
- Evaluation relies solely on conditional likelihood; it does not assess the model’s ability to generate factually correct text or perform reasoning.

## Confidence
- KR-Test discriminates factual retention (oracle 99.56% vs. pretrained near random): **High**
- FFN adapters outperform attention adapters under equal parameter budgets: **Medium** (confined to LoRA on a single dataset)
- Larger models achieve higher and more convergent KR scores: **Medium** (observed for 1B vs. 8B on WikiText-2; scaling may differ elsewhere)
