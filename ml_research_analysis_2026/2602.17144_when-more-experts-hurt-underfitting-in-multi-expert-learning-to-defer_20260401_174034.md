# When More Experts Hurt: Underfitting in Multi-Expert Learning to Defer

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17144
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17144
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** PiCCE mitigates inherent underfitting in multi-expert L2D by adaptively selecting a reliable expert using ground-truth correctness, yielding lower system error and higher coverage across multiple datasets.

## Executive Summary
Multi-expert Learning to Defer (L2D) aims to combine a classifier with multiple experts, deferring to an expert when uncertain. This paper uncovers a fundamental flaw: as the number of experts increases, the standard multi-expert L2D surrogate loss causes the classifier to underfit due to an expert aggregation term that flattens the effective label distribution, worsening performance regardless of deferral costs. To solve this, the authors introduce PiCCE, a continuous surrogate loss that uses ground-truth correctness to restrict expert selection to only those experts who are correct on a given instance, effectively reducing the problem to a single-expert-like setting. Theoretically, PiCCE is consistent and recovers both class probabilities and expert accuracies under a mild “information advantage” condition. Empirically, across synthetic and real-world expert datasets (CIFAR-100, ImageNet, MiceBone, Chaoyang), PiCCE consistently achieves lower system error and higher coverage than standard multi-expert CE and OvA surrogates, while preventing the classifier’s accuracy from deteriorating as more experts are added.

## Method Summary
The paper studies the Learning to Defer (L2D) framework where a base classifier can defer to one of J experts. Each input x yields scores θ ∈ ℝ^{K+J} from a neural network g_θ, with the first K scores corresponding to K classes and the next J to the experts. The system prediction is obtained by a deferral link φ(θ): if the highest class score exceeds the highest expert score, the class is predicted; otherwise the expert with the highest score is selected.

Standard multi-expert L2D uses a surrogate loss that aggregates expert signals via a term A(x) = Σ_j Acc_j(x), which scales linearly with J. This flattening of the effective label distribution causes classifier underfitting as J grows.

PiCCE replaces the aggregation with a selection that considers only the subset of experts who are correct on the instance (i.e., m_j = y). The loss is: ℓ_ϕ(θ, y, m) = ϕ(θ, y) + ϕ(θ, argmax_{j∈S} (θ_{K+j}) + K), where S = {j: m_j = y} and ϕ is a symmetric proper loss (e.g., cross-entropy or OvA-log). If S is empty, the second term is zero. This reduction bounds the aggregation term by Pr(∪_j M_j = Y | X=x) ∈ [0,1], preventing flattening.

Training proceeds by minimizing expected PiCCE loss over the training set using standard stochastic optimization (SGD or AdamW depending on dataset). At inference, the deferral rule φ is applied directly.

## Key Results
- **Inherent underfitting:** In multi-expert L2D, the classifier’s accuracy degrades as the number of experts increases, unlike single-expert L2D where underfitting only occurs with non-zero deferral costs.
- **PiCCE’s effectiveness:** PiCCE consistently reduces system error and increases coverage compared to vanilla multi-expert CE and OvA losses across five datasets, while stabilizing classifier accuracy regardless of expert count.
- **Theoretical guarantees:** PiCCE is statistically consistent and recovers true class probabilities and expert accuracies under Condition 1 (a unique optimal expert with measurable advantage).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper identifies three key mechanisms.

1. **Classifier underfitting in multi-expert L2D**  
   **Claim:** The standard multi-expert L2D surrogate loss inherently causes classifier underfitting as the number of experts J increases, degrading overall system performance.  
   **Mechanism:** The loss contains an expert aggregation term A(x) = Σ_j Acc_j(x), which grows linearly with J. This term effectively flattens the dummy label distribution used to train the classifier (Eq. 6), narrowing the margin between the true class and competitors and making the ground truth harder to learn.  
   **Core assumption:** Expert aggregation is an unbounded sum of non-negative expert accuracies.  
   **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 3.1 (flattening narrows margin); empirical trends showing classifier accuracy dropping with more experts.  
   **Break condition:** If the aggregation term could be bounded independently of J (e.g., all experts identical), flattening would not scale with J.

2. **PiCCE reduces the problem to a single-expert selection**  
   **Claim:** PiCCE mitigates underfitting by dynamically restricting the expert aggregation to only correct experts, effectively reducing the multi-expert problem to a single-expert-like formulation.  
   **Mechanism:** For each instance, PiCCE identifies the set S of experts with m_j = y. The loss then selects the most confident expert from S. This replaces the O(J) aggregation A(x) with Pr(∪_j M_j = Y | X=x), which is bounded by 1 (Lemma 4). The resulting dummy distribution remains informative regardless of J.  
   **Core assumption:** Ground-truth label y and all expert predictions m_j are available during training to determine correctness.  
   **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.1 (adaptive selection); Lemma 4 (aggregation compression); abstract.  
   **Break condition:** If no expert is correct on an instance (S = ∅), PiCCE reverts to a pure classifier and loses deferral benefits.

3. **Consistency under an information advantage condition**  
   **Claim:** Under Condition 1 (the optimal expert provides a measurable advantage over adding any other expert to a subset), PiCCE is statistically consistent: it recovers the Bayes-optimal L2D rule and true expert accuracies.  
   **Mechanism:** Using a symmetric proper loss ϕ (e.g., cross-entropy), minimizing PiCCE risk yields a scoring function where: (i) the classifier component matches Bayes-optimal class probabilities; (ii) expert scores are ordered by true expert accuracies; (iii) the top expert score estimates its accuracy (Theorems 3, 6).  
   **Core assumption:** Condition 1 holds (unique optimal expert with advantage); ϕ is proper and symmetric.  
   **Evidence anchors:** Lemma 5 (classifier consistency); Theorem 6 (system consistency and expert recovery).  
   **Break condition:** If Condition 1 fails (e.g., two experts have equal maximal accuracy), the guarantee for consistent expert selection may not hold, though classifier consistency may still hold.

## Foundational Learning

### Surrogate Loss Consistency  
**Why needed:** The target L2D risk with zero-one loss and deferral options is discrete and non-convex. Surrogate losses provide continuous approximations whose minimization asymptotically yields the Bayes-optimal rule. The paper examines which surrogate structures preserve consistency in multi-expert settings, beyond just system error but also for the classifier’s own accuracy.  
**Quick check:** What does it mean for a surrogate loss to be consistent in L2D, and how does the standard multi-expert CE loss fail this for the classifier’s learning—not just for system error?

### Label Smoothing and Underfitting  
**Why needed:** Underfitting in L2D appears as a classifier becoming overconfident in wrong classes. In single-expert L2D with positive deferral cost, the cost induces label smoothing that flattens the target distribution. The paper reveals a distinct source of flattening in multi-expert L2D (expert aggregation), requiring a different solution.  
**Quick check:** Compare the source of label distribution flattening in (a) single-expert L2D with deferral cost c>0 and (b) multi-expert L2D without explicit costs. Why does the standard remedy from (a) fail in (b)?

### Expert Identifiability  
**Why needed:** Multi-expert L2D requires identifying the best expert per instance. The paper highlights an identifiability challenge: the classifier must distinguish experts’ latent accuracies from aggregated signals, which is absent in single-expert L2D. This explains why naive continuous reductions (e.g., picking the most confident expert) fail due to discontinuity.  
**Quick check:** What makes identifying the best expert from a pool fundamentally harder than simply deciding whether to defer to an unknown single expert? How does PiCCE’s use of ground-truth correctness address this?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Input (x, y, m) -> Scoring network g_θ -> Score vector θ ∈ ℝ^{K+J} -> PiCCE loss ℓ_ϕ(θ,y,m) -> Optimizer (SGD/AdamW) updates θ. Deployment: θ -> φ(θ) yields prediction (class or expert).

### Critical path
**Training loop:** For each batch, forward pass computes θ = g_θ(x). Construct set S = {j: m_j = y}. Compute loss ℓ_ϕ = ϕ(θ,y) + ϕ(θ, argmax_{j∈S}(θ_{K+j})+K). Backpropagate and update parameters.  
**Inference:** Given x, compute θ = g_θ(x). Apply φ(θ): if max(θ_{1..K}) > max(θ_{K+1..K+J}), predict class argmax_{y≤K} θ_y; else defer to expert argmax_{j} θ_{K+j}.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Continuity vs. Expert set size:** PiCCE achieves continuity by filtering experts via [m_j = y]. However, if few/no experts are correct, the effective set shrinks, potentially reducing coverage.
- **Loss symmetry requirement:** The base loss ϕ must be symmetric with respect to the expert dimensions (e.g., cross-entropy, OvA-log). Asymmetric losses are not supported.
- **Access to ground truth:** PiCCE requires y and all m_j during training. This is infeasible if human experts are only available at deployment.

### Failure signatures
1. **Classifier accuracy still drops with more experts:** The expert aggregation reduction failed; incorrect experts still influence scores or model collapses to a single expert.
2. **Coverage collapses (high deferral but low accuracy):** The classifier becomes overly deferential because expert scores dominate due to poor scaling or initialization.
3. **Expert selection misaligned:** The argmax_j θ_{K+j} does not match known expert accuracies on validation data, indicating violation of Theorem 6 or non-convergence.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Diagnose underfitting:** Train standard multi-expert CE and PiCCE-CE on synthetic data (e.g., CIFAR-100 with disjoint animal experts). Plot classifier accuracy vs. number of experts J. Expect vanilla to decrease; PiCCE to remain stable.
2. **Validate dummy distribution:** For a fixed instance, compute the effective label distribution under vanilla (via A(x)) and under PiCCE (via Pr(∪ M_j = Y)). Show that PiCCE’s distribution entropy grows less with J.
3. **Test Condition 1 sensitivity:** Simulate a scenario where two experts have nearly identical accuracy but differ on rare classes. Check whether PiCCE still consistently identifies the optimal expert. Degradation would suggest Condition 1 is practically necessary.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Necessary and sufficient consistency conditions for PiCCE**  
   *Basis:* The authors note Condition 1 is only sufficient and call for exploring necessary/sufficient conditions (Sec 5.2).  
   *Why unresolved:* It is unknown if PiCCE remains consistent when Condition 1 fails, or what weaker assumptions suffice.  
   *Resolution evidence:* A full characterization theorem or counterexamples delineating exact failure cases would resolve this.

2. **Theoretical effect of number of experts on underfitting severity**  
   *Basis:* The paper identifies aggregation term A(x)=Σ Acc_j(x) as the cause and shows empirically that more experts worsen underfitting, but no formal bound linking J to performance is provided.  
   *Why unresolved:* Without a quantitative relation (e.g., bound on excess risk as a function of J and expert accuracies), the theoretical extent remains unclear.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Proving a theorem that bounds the classifier’s suboptimality or system risk in terms of J and expert characteristics.

3. **Consistency of PiCCE when the optimal expert is not unique**  
   *Basis:* Condition 1 assumes a unique optimal expert (|Argmax_j Acc_j(x)| = 1); ties are not discussed (Sec 5.2).  
   *Why unresolved:* The consistency proof relies on uniqueness to correctly identify the optimal expert via Argmax. With ties, selection may become arbitrary, potentially breaking consistency or expert accuracy recovery.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Extending analysis to include deterministic tie-breaking strategies or proving consistency under symmetric distributional assumptions for ties.

## Limitations
- Requires ground-truth labels and all expert predictions during training, making it unsuitable for settings where experts (e.g., human annotators) are only available at deployment.
- Consistency relies on Condition 1 (a unique optimal expert with measurable advantage), which may not hold in real-world expert pools with near-ties or complementary strengths.
