# Fairness over Equality: Correcting Social Incentives in Asymmetric Sequential Social Dilemmas

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.15407  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15407  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Fair&Local modifications to IA and SVO normalize rewards, weight social drives by environmental impact, and use decentralized estimates, enabling effective fairness incentives in asymmetric sequential social dilemmas and outperforming baseline methods.

## Executive Summary
Existing fairness-based intrinsic motivation methods (Inequity Aversion, Social Value Orientation) fail in asymmetric sequential social dilemmas because they enforce raw reward equality, which wrongly incentivizes agents to punish cooperators who earn more due to inherent asymmetries. This paper introduces three corrections: normalizing temporally-smoothed rewards by each agent’s historical performance range, applying agent-specific weights based on environmental impact, and localizing social feedback via decentralized estimates to avoid global information requirements. In asymmetric variants of the Coins and Harvest environments, the Fair&Local versions achieve faster convergence to cooperative policies, higher returns, improved peace (less conflict), and balanced sustainability across agent types, while baseline fairness methods exhibit performance gaps and promote defection.

## Method Summary
The proposed Fair&Local approach adapts standard IA and SVO to asymmetric settings via three modifications. First, each agent normalizes its temporally-smoothed reward using its own historical min and max, mapping performance to a [0,1] range relative to its potential. Second, the social drive parameters (α, β, w) are scaled by an agent-specific modifier φᵢ, predetermined based on the agent’s environmental impact (e.g., zap radius). Third, instead of requiring global access to all agents’ smoothed rewards, each agent maintains local estimates of others’ normalized rewards; visible agents are observed directly, while estimates for non-visible agents are updated by copying recent estimates from visible agents (Algorithm 1). Agents are trained with independent DQN using the modified intrinsic rewards. This yields cooperation incentives that are fair across asymmetric agent types without needing centralized information.

## Key Results
- Fair&Local IA and SVO converge faster to cooperative policies than original IA/SVO in asymmetric Coins and Harvest environments.
- They achieve higher average returns and improved peace (fewer harmful actions) while eliminating performance sustainability gaps between agent types.
- Baseline fairness methods exhibit significant disparities in returns and conflict levels, often incentivizing defection against high-reward or high-impact agents.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Normalizing temporally-smoothed rewards by each agent's historical range prevents defection incentives caused by raw reward inequality in asymmetric SSDs.
- **Mechanism:** Each agent computes normalized reward ê̂tⁱ = (eₜⁱ – e_minⁱ)/(e_maxⁱ – e_minⁱ), where eₜⁱ is the smoothed reward (eₜⁱ = γ·λ·eₜ₋₁ⁱ + rₜⁱ). This maps performance to [0,1] relative to the agent's own potential, enabling fair cross-agent comparisons.
- **Core assumption:** An agent's reward range (e_minⁱ, e_maxⁱ) meaningfully captures its achievable outcomes; normalization makes comparisons valid despite differing reward scales.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4.1 detail the normalization formula and state it reduces the unintended incentive to punish cooperative agents with higher rewards and ensures bounded values.
- **Break condition:** If e_maxⁱ ≈ e_minⁱ (no variation), denominator near zero causes instability; requires epsilon or dynamic initialization.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Agent-specific weighting of the social drive (φᵢ) adjusts for differences in environmental impact, ensuring agents with greater influence bear stronger prosocial incentives.
- **Mechanism:** In IA and SVO, global parameters (α, β, w) are replaced with agent-specific ones: αᵢ = φᵢ·α, βᵢ = φᵢ·β, wᵢ = φᵢ·w. φᵢ ≥ 0 is predetermined per agent type (e.g., high-impact agents get larger φᵢ).
- **Core assumption:** An agent's potential impact on the environment (e.g., zap radius, penalty magnitude) is known or estimable and should correlate with its social responsibility.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4.2 introduce φᵢ as "social drive modifier" and show it removes gaps between agent types in asymmetric settings.
- **Break condition:** Incorrect φᵢ values (too high) may over-penalize agents, causing avoidance of beneficial actions; too low may not correct asymmetry.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Localizing social feedback via decentralized estimates eliminates the need for global information, making fairness methods applicable under partial observability while preserving cooperation.
- **Mechanism:** Each agent i maintains estimates ê̂tᵢ,ⱼ of other agents' normalized smoothed rewards. For visible agents, direct observation updates the estimate. For non-visible agents, the estimate is replaced by the most recent estimate from a visible agent (Algorithm 1). These estimates are used in IA/SVO instead of true rewards.
- **Core assumption:** Estimates remain sufficiently accurate; the communication graph (via visibility) allows information propagation across the group over time.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4.3 present Algorithm 1 and state it relaxes the assumption requiring full access to all agents’ rewards.
- **Break condition:** In highly dynamic or disconnected environments, estimates become stale (high average age), leading to ineffective social incentives and possible cooperation breakdown.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Observation oᵢ -> Policy Network (CNN+LSTM) -> Action aᵢ  
Reward rᵢ -> Smoothed Reward eₜⁱ -> Normalization ê̂ₜⁱ -> Reward Modifier  
Visibility -> Local Estimator (ê̂ₜᵢ,ⱼ, τₜᵢ,ⱼ) -> Reward Modifier  
Reward Modifier (IA/SVO with φᵢ) -> Total Reward r_total -> Replay Buffer -> DQN Update -> Policy Network  

Critical path:  
1. Environment step: agent receives (oᵢ, rᵢ).  
2. Update smoothed reward: eₜⁱ ← γ·λ·eₜ₋₁ⁱ + rᵢ.  
3. Update running min/max; compute normalized ê̂ₜⁱ.  
4. Update estimates for visible agents; for non-visible, use Algorithm 1 to pull latest estimate from visible agents.  
5. Compute intrinsic term using IA/SVO formulas with agent-specific weights and local estimates.  
6. Total reward = rᵢ + intrinsic_term; store transition.  
7. Sample from replay buffer, compute DQN loss, update policy.

Design tradeoffs:  
- Normalization requires maintaining e_minᵢ, e_maxᵢ per agent; adds memory but stabilizes comparisons. Alternative: fixed bounds from environment? May not capture actual range.  
- Agent-specific φᵢ must be set a priori; if unknown, could be learned but adds complexity.  
- Local estimates introduce communication overhead (implicit via visibility); may lag in sparse connectivity. Tradeoff: estimate update frequency vs computational cost.

Failure signatures:  
- Persistent low average return or high defection (e.g., zapping in Harvest, mismatch coin collection in Coins) despite Fair&Local: check normalization ranges (e_max – e_min near zero?) or φᵢ values.  
- Large discrepancy in peace metric between agent types (in Harvest): indicates weighting not compensating for asymmetry.  
- High average age of estimates suggests localization is struggling; consider increasing visibility range or communication frequency.

First 3 experiments:  
1. Symmetric baseline: Train Fair&LocalIA/SVO in symmetric Coins and Harvest. Verify they achieve similar or better cooperation metrics compared to original IA/SVO (Fig. 9,10). This validates implementation.  
2. Asymmetric reward scenario: Use Coins with high/low reward agents and Harvest with high/low reward agents. Check that both agent types achieve similar peace and sustainability (no gap), and overall return exceeds baselines (Fig. 3,4).  
3. Asymmetric action scenario: Use Coins with spawn-biased vs standard, and Harvest with wide-zap vs standard. Verify that the method eliminates performance gaps and reduces conflict (e.g., fewer zaps, higher proportion of own coins) compared to IA/SVO baselines (Fig. 5,6).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do other forms of asymmetry—such as differential access to resources or varied learning capabilities—affect the performance of Fair&Local methods?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in Discussion & Future Work: "Future work could explore other forms of asymmetry, such as differential access to resources or varied learning capabilities."
- **Why unresolved:** The current study only addresses asymmetry in reward scales and action effects. Other asymmetry types may interact differently with the proposed normalization, weighting, and localization mechanisms, potentially requiring further adjustments.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend experiments to environments with resource access inequalities (e.g., agents starting with different initial resources) or heterogeneous learning rates, then compare cooperation metrics (return, peace, sustainability) against baselines and the current Fair&Local approach.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the agent-specific social drive modifier φ be learned automatically during training, rather than being manually specified per agent type?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work proposes: "agent-based weights could be learned automatically during training, eliminating the need to specify them beforehand."
- **Why unresolved:** The current implementation uses hand-tuned φ values based on known agent types, limiting generalizability to unseen or more complex asymmetric scenarios where optimal weights are not obvious.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Integrate a learnable parameter (e.g., via gradient descent or meta-learning) to adjust φ per agent from environmental feedback, then evaluate convergence speed, stability, and final cooperation levels in asymmetric environments compared to fixed φ.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the Fair&Local approach maintain effectiveness in larger populations with multiple heterogeneous agent types, beyond the 10-agent Harvest tests?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper claims scalability ("without sacrificing scalability") but only evaluates up to 10 agents. Larger, more diverse groups could strain the decentralized estimation mechanism (Algorithm 1) or normalization assumptions.
- **Why unresolved:** In larger populations, local communication networks may become sparser, leading to outdated or inaccurate estimates of others' rewards. Normalization based on individual reward ranges might also become less meaningful with many overlapping types.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Scale experiments to 50+ agents in Harvest-like settings with several asymmetry types (e.g., mixed high/low-reward and wide/standard-zap agents), measuring cooperation metrics, estimate accuracy (average age), and variance in returns across agent types.

## Limitations
- Exact asymmetric environment parameters (e.g., spawn-biased mechanics, zap radii) are underspecified, requiring inference from text.
- Agent-specific weights φᵢ require prior environmental knowledge; no heuristic for learning φᵢ online is provided.
- Local estimate protocol (Algorithm 1) relies on implicit visibility-based communication; robustness to sparse/dynamic graphs is unclear.
- Normalization assumes historical reward ranges capture true potential; may fail if agents cannot achieve min/max due to policy constraints.

## Confidence
- Normalization prevents defection incentives: Medium (empirically effective in static asymmetries, but range-assumption untested under non-stationarity)
- Agent-specific weighting corrects impact asymmetry: Medium (gaps eliminated in experiments, but φᵢ selection is heuristic, not optimized)
- Localization maintains cooperation with partial observability: Medium-Low (works in tested connectivity, but failure thresholds (estimate staleness) are not mapped)
- Overall outcome (faster convergence, higher returns, balanced sustainability): High (reproducible results across asymmetric setups)

## Next Checks
1. Ablation Study: Systematically remove each Fair&Local component (normalization, φᵢ weighting, localization) in asymmetric Coins/Harvest to quantify isolated contribution to cooperation gains and fairness.
2. Dynamic Asymmetry Test: Introduce time-varying reward scales or action effects (e.g., periodic role swaps) to evaluate whether normalization adapts or causes reward oscillations/instability.
3. Connectivity Stress Test: Gradually reduce agent visibility graph density (random edge removal) and measure correlation between estimate age (τ) and degradation in peace/sustainability metrics to identify critical connectivity thresholds.
