# AI+HW 2035: Shaping the Next Decade
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05225
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05225
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Proposes a 10-year roadmap for AI+HW co-design to achieve 1000× improvement in intelligence per joule through memory-centric designs, cross-layer optimization, and AI-driven EDA.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the unsustainable trajectory of AI hardware co-evolution, where fragmented innovation leads to excessive energy consumption and inefficient systems. It outlines a coordinated 10-year roadmap spanning algorithms, architectures, systems, and sustainability. Central to the vision are memory-centric computing (e.g., compute-in-memory, 3D integration), photonic interconnects, and AI-driven design automation. Key targets include 100× end-to-end energy efficiency within five years, ≥60% sustained cluster utilization, and democratized infrastructure access. The authors call for academia-industry-government collaboration to realize sustainable, trustworthy AI.

## Method Summary
The paper synthesizes trends from 40 references to project AI+HW evolution to 2035. For empirical validation, it presents historical scaling plots (2020–2025) of model size vs. accuracy, extrapolating toward 1000× efficiency gains. Reproduction requires collecting public model data (parameters, benchmark scores), fitting trends on log-log scales, and projecting to 2030. Challenges include inconsistent benchmark protocols and uncertain extrapolation assumptions.

## Key Results
- Memory-centric co-design (compute-in-memory, 3D stacking) reduces data movement energy, the dominant bottleneck in AI workloads.
- Cross-layer co-design across algorithm, hardware, and system layers yields Pareto-optimal trade-offs unattainable by isolated optimizations.
- AI-driven EDA can collapse hardware design cycles from years to months, keeping pace with AI algorithm evolution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Memory-centric co-design (e.g., compute-in-memory, 3D stacking) reduces system energy by minimizing data movement, which is the dominant bottleneck in modern AI workloads.
- **Mechanism:** By colocating computation with memory, the energy cost of off-chip data transfers is drastically reduced. This is enabled by dense 3D integration and analog/mixed-signal compute units that perform operations directly within memory arrays. Algorithmic techniques like sparsity and low-rank representations further reduce memory traffic, making the co-design synergistic.
- **Core assumption:** Data movement energy exceeds arithmetic energy in large AI models, and hardware can be designed to exploit algorithmic sparsity and locality.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "The exponential growth of large AI models... has made data movement the dominant bottleneck, outpacing advances in compute, memory, and interconnect technologies."
  - [section 3.2] "Data movement has become the primary bottleneck... This creates opportunities for learning paradigms that trade exactness for orders-of-magnitude efficiency gains."
  - [corpus] Weak/indirect; neighboring papers discuss electronic-photonic systems and scaling challenges but do not directly evidence memory-centric gains.
- **Break condition:** If algorithmic sparsity patterns are irregular or dynamic such that hardware cannot adapt its dataflow, or if noise/calibration issues in analog compute-in-memory degrade accuracy beyond recovery.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Cross-layer co-design (algorithm↔hardware↔system) achieves Pareto-optimal trade-offs across accuracy, energy, latency, and reliability, which isolated optimizations cannot.
- **Mechanism:** Continuous feedback between layers ensures that hardware constraints (e.g., memory bandwidth, thermal limits) directly shape algorithmic choices (e.g., precision, sparsity), while algorithmic needs (e.g., attention patterns) dictate hardware features (e.g., interconnect topology). System-level optimizations (e.g., cooling, power delivery) close the loop, enabling holistic efficiency gains.
- **Core assumption:** Layers in the computing stack are tightly coupled and cannot be optimized independently for global efficiency.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Addressing this grand challenge requires rethinking the entire computing stack... spanning algorithms, architectures, systems, and sustainability."
  - [section 2.3] "Cross-layer co-design... algorithms must adapt to physical constraints; hardware must evolve to serve learning dynamics; and system software must act as the connective tissue."
  - [section 3.2] "Future AI hardware must be designed through a cross-layer, system-centric lens... exposing data movement, memory locality, and energy cost as first-class primitives."
- **Break condition:** If disciplinary silos persist (e.g., algorithm designers ignore hardware telemetry, hardware teams work without workload exposure), or if evaluation metrics remain layer-specific (e.g., peak FLOPs vs. end-to-end intelligence per joule).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** AI-driven EDA and design automation shorten hardware development cycles, enabling hardware to keep pace with rapidly evolving AI algorithms.
- **Mechanism:** Large and small language models, reinforcement learning agents, and graph neural networks automate design space exploration, RTL generation, verification, and placement/routing. This creates a virtuous cycle where AI systems design the hardware that accelerates future AI, collapsing traditional multi-year design cycles into months.
- **Core assumption:** The complexity of future heterogeneous AI systems exceeds manual design capacity, and AI tools can learn from existing designs to generate optimal, verified hardware.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "AI-driven EDA to become a core part of the hardware workflow, leveraging large and small language models to automate design space exploration, code generation, verification..."
  - [section 3.1] "AI-driven design automation... is essential. Learning-based design tools can explore massive design spaces... creating a virtuous cycle where AI improves hardware."
  - [section 4.3 Q5] "Self-improving pipelines... Models routinely generate and verify kernels... assist across the EDA flow from specification to RTL."
- **Break condition:** If AI-generated designs consistently fail physical verification, exhibit poor performance-watt trade-offs, or lack innovation beyond human-designed baselines; or if training data for AI-design tools is limited to legacy, non-AI-optimized architectures.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Memory Wall / Data Movement Bottleneck
  - **Why needed here:** The paper identifies data movement as the primary energy bottleneck. Understanding why moving data consumes more energy than computing is crucial for evaluating proposed solutions (CIM, 3D stacking).
  - **Quick check question:** In a typical AI accelerator, does more energy go into fetching weights/activations from memory or into matrix multiplication? Answer: Data movement (fetching) dominates.

- **Concept:** Cross-Layer Co-Design
  - **Why needed here:** The entire roadmap hinges on breaking silos. An engineer must internalize that optimizing a single layer (e.g., adding more tensor cores) may not improve system efficiency without matching algorithmic and system software changes.
  - **Quick check question:** Give one example where an algorithmic innovation (e.g., sparse attention) requires a matching hardware feature (e.g., sparse dataflow support). Answer: Sparse attention requires hardware to skip zero-valued computations efficiently.

- **Concept:** Intelligence per Joule as Primary Metric
  - **Why needed here:** The paper redefines success from scaling FLOPs to scaling useful capability per unit energy. This shifts evaluation from peak performance to sustainable, real-world efficiency.
  - **Quick check question:** What does the 1000x target measure? Answer: Improvement in intelligence per joule (meaningful capability per unit energy) for training and inference.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Device/Materials → 3D Integration/Heterogeneous Packaging → Analog/Mixed-Signal & CIM → Photonic Interconnects → Cooling/Power Delivery → Accelerator Architecture → Memory & Storage Hierarchies → Interconnect & Networking → System/Infrastructure → Compiler/Runtime → Programming Abstractions → Algorithms/Models

- **Critical path:** Start with target application workloads and their algorithmic characteristics (e.g., attention sparsity, precision needs) → map to required hardware capabilities (bandwidth, compute density, memory capacity) → select system-level integration strategy (3D stacking, chiplet composition, interconnect topology) → specify compiler/runtime abstractions to expose data locality and enable co-optimization → define evaluation metrics (energy/accuracy/latency trade-offs, utilization, intelligence per joule).

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Energy vs. Accuracy:** Analog/compute-in-memory introduces noise; algorithms must be robust-aware (e.g., noise-aware training). Tradeoff managed via statistical error compensation.
  - **Specialization vs. Flexibility:** Domain-specific accelerators (NPUs) vs. reconfigurable fabrics (CGRAs). Solution: heterogeneous integration with programmable interfaces.
  - **Bandwidth vs. Latency:** Photonic interconnects offer high bandwidth but may have higher latency for small packets; topology must match AI collective communication patterns.
  - **3D Integration Density vs. Thermal/Reliability:** More stacked layers increase yield challenges and thermal resistance; requires co-design with cooling and adaptive voltage/frequency scaling.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **System-level:** Sustained cluster utilization <60%, high silent data corruption (SDC) rates due to voltage scaling/CIM noise.
  - **Architecture:** Memory bandwidth saturation, poor scaling with context length (KV cache bottleneck), thermal throttling in 3D stacks.
  - **Algorithm-Hardware mismatch:** Models designed for high precision (FP16) running on low-precision (INT4) hardware without quantization-aware training → accuracy collapse.
  - **Software stack:** Underutilized heterogeneous resources due to poor compiler/runtime scheduling, high kernel launch overheads.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Benchmark memory-centric architecture with sparse models:** Run a sparse Transformer (e.g., with 80% sparsity) on a simulator/configurable accelerator that exposes data movement energy (e.g., using ITBench or IMC-Bench). Measure energy/accuracy trade-off vs. dense baseline. **Anchor:** [section 3.2] "Memory-centric... enable new algorithmic abstractions... approximate computation and noisy arithmetic can be embraced."
  2. **Test photonic interconnect topology for AI dataflows:** Compare all-to-all collective performance (e.g., for tensor parallelism) under electrical vs. optical interconnects in a simulated multi-chip system. Measure impact on large-model training scalability. **Anchor:** [section 3.2] "Photonic and optoelectronic interconnects provide near distance-independent bandwidth... enabling scale-out AI systems."
  3. **Measure intelligence per joule on hybrid edge-cloud deployment:** Deploy a distilled small language model (SLM) on an edge device vs. a frontier model in cloud for a specific task (e.g., IT automation from ITBench). Compare task success rate per watt, including communication energy if cloud offload is used. **Anchor:** [section 5.5] "1000x improvement... enables fundamental redistribution of AI traffic from cloud to edge."

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can memory-centric and compute-in-memory architectures overcome noise, non-linearity, and scalability issues to achieve orders-of-magnitude reduction in data movement energy for large AI models?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3.2 lists "Memory and Data Movement Wall" as a key challenge and highlights compute-in-memory and 3D integration as opportunities, but notes unresolved barriers like device variability and error compensation.
- **Why unresolved:** Current analog and in-memory approaches suffer from accuracy degradation at scale, limiting their adoption for training and large inference workloads; integrated solutions across algorithm–hardware layers are needed but not demonstrated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** End-to-end systems using memory-centric designs that reduce energy per operation by 10× for models like LLMs, with robust training-aware error tolerance and validated manufacturability at advanced nodes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can AI-driven electronic design automation (EDA) mature to shorten hardware design cycles to months while ensuring performance, power, area, and reliability targets for heterogeneous AI accelerators?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3.3 Q4 directly questions if hardware cycles can match AI innovation pace, and Section 3.2 posits AI-driven EDA as essential but currently lacking full-stack integration and verification guarantees.
- **Why unresolved:** Existing AI-assisted tools handle limited design subtasks but fail at cross-layer optimization, formal verification, and fleet-scale reliability; scaling to complex 3D-integrated systems remains an open research problem.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** AI-generated RTL designs for AI chips that meet PPA targets with ≤3× reduction in design time, pass all verification, and are successfully taped out with post-silicon validation matching predictions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What algorithmic innovations enable domain-specific small models (e.g., <20B parameters) to rival frontier models in capability for edge and physical AI under strict latency and energy constraints?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4.3 Q2 asks if 10–100× smaller models can be equally capable, and Section 5.5 predicts a shift to edge-deployed small models; Section 4.4 mentions trends like modular architectures and distillation.
- **Why unresolved:** Distillation and sparsity offer partial gains, but maintaining reasoning and generalization with drastic parameter reduction for diverse tasks lacks theoretical foundations and hardware-aware training methods.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Small models achieving parity with frontier models on complex benchmarks (e.g., reasoning, coding) while running on edge devices with ≤1% of the energy, validated through standardized efficiency metrics.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can the community define and adopt cross-layer metrics that replace peak FLOPs to drive Pareto-optimal AI+HW co-design across efficiency, robustness, and societal impact?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3.3 Q5 asks how success should be measured, advocating for intelligence per joule and system-level outcomes; Section 5.5 emphasizes multi-metric evaluation beyond accuracy.
- **Why unresolved:** Current benchmarks (e.g., MLPerf) ignore energy, utilization, and robustness, creating misaligned incentives; developing universally accepted, multi-dimensional metrics that capture real-world deployment trade-offs is unresolved.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Industry and academia widely adopting a benchmark suite (e.g., extending ITBench) that reports intelligence per joule, sustained utilization, and robustness scores, with top conferences requiring such metrics for publication.

## Limitations
- No direct evidence that cross-layer co-design can achieve 1000× compound gains; break conditions (noise, irregular sparsity) may limit memory-centric approaches.
- Reproduction impossible without raw data; public benchmarks vary in evaluation, risking inconsistent trend analysis.
- AI-driven EDA and sustained 60% cluster utilization are speculative and face verification and thermal/reliability hurdles.
