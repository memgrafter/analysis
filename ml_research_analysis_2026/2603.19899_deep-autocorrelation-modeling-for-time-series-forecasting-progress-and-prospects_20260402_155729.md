## Foundational Learning
- **Autocorrelation**  
  *Why needed*: Captures the intrinsic temporal dependence of a time series on its own past values, forming the core property that forecasting models must learn.  
  *Quick check*: Compute and inspect the autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) of the series to identify significant lags.
- **Likelihood Estimation**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a probabilistic foundation for forecasting, enabling uncertainty quantification and principled model comparison.  
  *Quick check*: Evaluate the log‑likelihood of a held‑out validation set under the predicted distribution.
- **Shape Alignment**  
  *Why needed*: Directly optimizes the similarity between predicted and actual temporal patterns, useful for deterministic point forecasts.  
  *Quick check*: Measure alignment using metrics such as Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) or Pearson correlation on predictions versus ground truth.
- **Distribution Balancing**  
  *Why needed*: Ensures that the predicted output distribution matches the empirical distribution of the data, mitigating issues like non‑stationarity.  
  *Quick check*: Compare summary statistics (mean, variance, quantiles) of predicted and actual distributions.
- **Conditional Generation**  
  *Why needed*: Generates diverse sample paths conditioned on historical data, essential for scenario analysis and risk assessment.  
  *Quick check*: Draw multiple forecast samples and evaluate diversity using metrics like prediction interval coverage probability.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**  
  Raw Input → Preprocessing → Embedding → [Core Architecture: Non‑Transformer (RNN/CNN) | Transformer] → [Optional Plug‑in Modules] → Objective Head (Likelihood / Shape / Distribution / Conditional) → Output (point forecast or distribution)
- **Critical Path**  
  Autocorrelation modeling flows through the entire pipeline: embedding extracts local temporal patterns, the core architecture captures short‑ and long‑range dependencies, plug‑in modules refine specific structural aspects, and the objective head aligns the model’s behavior with the desired forecasting goal.
- **Design Tradeoffs**  
  - *Non‑Transformers*: Efficient for short sequences but may fail on long‑range dependencies.  
  - *Transformers*: Strong long‑range capture but computationally heavy and data‑hungry.  
  - *Plug‑in modules*: Add targeted improvements (e.g., attention for global context) but increase complexity and overfitting risk.  
  - *Objectives*: Likelihood‑based methods give uncertainty but are slower; shape alignment is simple but lacks probabilistic calibration.
- **Failure Signatures**  
  - Non‑convergence when the chosen architecture cannot represent the underlying autocorrelation structure.  
  - Overfitting when powerful plug‑ins are used on small datasets.  
  - Poor uncertainty calibration when objectives like MSE are applied to highly stochastic series.
- **First Experiments**  
  1. *Architecture Comparison*: Train an LSTM and a Transformer on a benchmark dataset (e.g., electricity demand) using a Gaussian negative log‑likelihood objective; compare forecast accuracy (e.g., MAE) and calibration (e.g., PICP).  
  2. *Plug‑in Ablation*: Add an attention plug‑in to a CNN forecaster and evaluate performance gain on a dataset with known long‑range dependencies, versus a CNN baseline.  
  3. *Objective Impact*: Train a Transformer with three objectives—likelihood (mixture density network), shape alignment (DTW loss), and MSE—and analyze their effects on point error and prediction interval quality.
