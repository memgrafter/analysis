# Modality Collapse as Mismatched Decoding: Information-Theoretic Limits of Multimodal LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23136
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23136
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Multimodal LLMs suffer "modality collapse" because their text-trained decoders cannot access modality-specific information; the information-theoretic bound `L_log·W1` quantifies this degradation and is empirically validated across models and interventions.

## Executive Summary
Multimodal large language models often fail on seemingly simple non‑textual tasks (e.g., object counting) despite correctly processing inputs. This paper frames the phenomenon as *mismatched decoding*: the decoder (a text‑trained LLM) receives embeddings from a non‑text modality, but its scoring rule is optimized for the distribution of text representations. Using information theory, the authors bound the accessible information via Generalized Mutual Information (GMI) and show it degrades with the Wasserstein distance between the two representation distributions and with the decoder’s Lipschitz sensitivity (`L_log`). Empirical studies on five multimodal LLMs confirm the bound’s predictive power, reveal that modality‑specific eigen‑directions in the adapter output actively harm decoding, and demonstrate that text‑aligned encoders (e.g., SigLIP) reduce the distributional shift by discarding such directions upstream. Finally, low‑rank adaptation (LoRA) fine‑tuned with a modality‑specific objective (emotion detection) restores the lost performance, suggesting a practical remedy.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate five multimodal LLMs (Ultravox, Qwen2‑Audio, LLaVA, Prismatic‑D, Prismatic‑S) using standard encoders (Whisper, CLIP, DINOv2, SigLIP) and Vicuna‑style decoders. They extract activations at four hook points (encoder output, adapter output, LLM layer 16, LLM final layer) on datasets covering speech, audio, and vision (LibriSpeech, CREMA‑D, ESC‑50, MS‑COCO). Linear logistic probes are trained on these representations to measure information retention for attributes like speaker identity, emotion, and object count. Principal component analysis of adapter outputs yields eigen‑directions; alignment scores `ρ_k` with the text covariance matrix classify each direction as text‑aligned (TA) or modality‑specific (MS). Causal ablations project out all MS (or TA) modes and assess the change in decoder cross‑entropy loss. The decoder’s Lipschitz constant `L_log` is estimated as the 95th‑th percentile of gradient norms of the log‑probability, while the Wasserstein‑1 distance `W1` between modal and text representations at LLM layer 16 is computed. Finally, Ultravox is fine‑tuned with LoRA on a forced‑choice emotion detection task, and resulting probe accuracies and task performance are reported.

## Key Results
- LoRA fine‑tuning on Ultravox with an emotion objective raises accuracy from 17.3% to 61.8%.
- In the controlled Prismatic study, the text‑aligned SigLIP encoder improves non‑textual tasks by +4.5–8.2%, while the non‑aligned DINOv2 shows minimal gains (+1.3–4.7%).
- Ablating modality‑specific eigen‑directions reduces decoder loss for non‑aligned models (e.g., Prismatic‑D −11.1%), proving these directions actively interfere; the same ablation has negligible effect for aligned models.
- The product `L_log·W1` correlates strongly with collapse severity across all tested models and modalities (e.g., Ultravox audio: 162, high collapse).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Modality collapse occurs because a text‑trained decoder’s scoring rule is only sensitive to text‑aligned directions, rendering information in modality‑specific directions inaccessible.
- **Mechanism**: The fixed decoder `q(y|z,c)` was optimized for text distributions `P_T`. For a non‑text distribution `P_M`, the Generalized Mutual Information (GMI) that the decoder can extract is bounded and degrades with the Wasserstein distance `W1(P_M, P_T)` and the decoder’s Lipschitz sensitivity `L_log`.
- **Core assumptions**: Joint distributions satisfy `P_M(C,Y)=P_T(C,Y)` (same prompts and targets); decoder log‑score is locally Lipschitz; representations lie in a bounded typical‑state region.
- **Evidence anchors**: Theorem 2 gives the bound `|GMI_PM - GMI_PT| ≤ (1+e^{L_log·D})·L_log·W1`. Correlations between `L_log·W1` and observed performance gaps are reported in Table 8.
- **Break condition**: If the decoder were trained on a balanced multimodal objective, its scoring rule would not be text‑shaped; `L_log` for non‑text directions would be small and the bound would not force a large gap.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Modality‑specific eigen‑directions in the adapter output *harm* decoding; removing them improves decoder loss.
- **Mechanism**: Causal ablation projects out MS modes (defined by low alignment `ρ_k < 0.5`) from the adapter output `z`. For non‑aligned encoders, this *decreases* cross‑entropy loss, indicating the decoder is hurt by MS variance. Text‑aligned encoders have few MS modes and show negligible loss change.
- **Core assumptions**: The PCA decomposition correctly separates MS and TA directions; the projection fully erases those directions.
- **Evidence anchors**: Table 2 and §5.4: ablating MS modes reduces loss for Qwen2‑Audio (‑0.1%) and Prismatic‑D (‑11.1%), while ablating matched TA modes yields no improvement or even harm.
- **Break condition**: If the decoder were indifferent to MS directions, ablating them would either not change loss or degrade it (due to lost information). The observed improvement confirms destructive interference.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Text‑aligned encoders (e.g., SigLIP) reduce the Wasserstein distance by aligning informative directions with text, but this is a workaround that discards non‑text information upstream rather than making it accessible.
- **Mechanism**: Contrastive training forces the modal representations to occupy text‑correlated dimensions, shrinking `W1`. However, modality‑specific attributes that do not correlate with text (e.g., object count) are removed by the encoder, so the decoder never faces the mismatch.
- **Core assumptions**: Alignment score `ρ_k = u_k^T Σ^T u_k / λ_k` correctly captures overlap between modal and text covariances; SigLIP’s higher `ρ_0` and fewer MS modes reflect better alignment.
- **Evidence anchors**: Prismatic controlled study: identical architecture/adapter/LLM, only encoder differs. SigLIP improves non‑text tasks (+4.5–8.2%) while DINOv2 stagnates (+1.3–4.7%).
- **Break condition**: If a non‑aligned encoder were combined with a decoder trained to be sensitive to its specific geometry (e.g., via multimodal pretraining), the mismatch would be resolved without discarding information upstream.

## Foundational Learning
- **Mismatched Decoding**: The core problem where a decoder trained on one distribution (`P_T`) is applied to a different input distribution (`P_M`). *Why needed*: It explains why information can be present in the representations but remain unusable. *Quick check*: If you change the input distribution without changing the decoder, what quantity limits the achievable rate of correct predictions?
- **Generalized Mutual Information (GMI)**: The mutual information evaluated with a fixed scoring rule (the decoder) rather than the optimal one. *Why needed*: It quantifies the information actually accessible to the mismatched decoder. *Quick check*: How does GMI differ from standard mutual information when the decoder is sub‑optimal?
- **Wasserstein‑1 Distance (`W1`)**: A metric between probability distributions that aligns with Lipschitz constraints. *Why needed*: The GMI degradation bound scales with `W1` and the decoder’s Lipschitz sensitivity `L_log`. *Quick check*: Why is `W1` used instead of KL divergence here? (Answer: It directly couples with the Lipschitz constant of the decoder’s log‑score.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
1. **Encoder (`E`)**: Pre‑trained non‑text encoder (Whisper, CLIP, DINOv2, SigLIP). Outputs raw modality features.
2. **Adapter (`A`)**: Learned projection (MLP, linear, Q‑Former) mapping encoder output to the LLM embedding space. Introduces modality‑specific variance.
3. **Decoder (`L`)**: Text‑trained LLM (Llama, Vicuna). Its scoring rule `q(y|z,c)` is fixed at inference and shaped by text pretraining.
4. **Hook points**: Encoder output, adapter output, LLM intermediate layer (e.g., layer 16), LLM final layer (post‑LayerNorm). Used to extract representations for probing.

### Critical Path
Input `X` → `E` → `A` → `Z` (LLM input) → `L` → `Ŷ`. Collapse occurs at the `A→L` interface: `Z` follows the modal distribution `P_M`, but `L`’s scoring rule expects the text distribution `P_T`.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Encoder alignment**: Non‑aligned encoders (DINOv2) preserve non‑text information but cause large `W1` and collapse. Aligned encoders (SigLIP) reduce `W1` by discarding non‑text‑correlated information upstream, thus avoiding collapse at the cost of losing that information permanently.
- **Adapter capacity**: Not the bottleneck; even a linear adapter passes modality‑specific variance. The issue lies in the decoder’s sensitivity, not adapter expressivity.
- **Training objective**: Adapter‑only training does not alter the decoder’s text‑shaped `q`. LoRA on the LLM can reshape `q` if trained on a modality‑specific objective.

### Failure Signatures
1. **Probing gap**: High probe accuracy for non‑text attributes at adapter output but low accuracy at LLM final layer.
2. **Mode alignment**: Adapter’s top eigenmodes have low text alignment `ρ_k ≪ 1` for non‑aligned encoders.
3. **Bound validation**: High `L_log·W1` product correlates with severe degradation in non‑text tasks (e.g., speaker ID in Ultravox: `L_log·W1=162`, −39% retention).
4. **Ablation response**: Removing MS modes *improves* decoder loss for non‑aligned models.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Linear probing across layers**: Extract representations at encoder, adapter, LLM‑16, LLM‑final. Train logistic regression probes for each info‑type. Confirm non‑text info survives to LLM‑final but is not used (probe accuracy high, task accuracy low).
2. **Mode alignment & PCA**: Compute covariance of adapter output under `P_M`. For each eigenvector `u_k`, compute alignment `ρ_k = u_k^T Σ_T u_k / λ_k`. Classify MS (`ρ_k<0.5`) vs. TA modes. Validate that non‑aligned encoders have dominant MS modes.
3. **Causal ablation**: At adapter output, project out all MS modes (`z' = z - Σ_{k∈MS} (z^T u_k) u_k`). Measure change in decoder cross‑entropy on a held‑out set. Expect *improvement* for non‑aligned encoders, minimal change for aligned (SigLIP).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does the GMI‑Wasserstein bound accurately predict information loss in multimodal LLMs at larger scales (e.g., 70B parameter models)?
- Can we design a training objective that systematically modifies the decoder’s scoring rule to exploit modality‑specific information without degrading text capabilities?
- Does modality collapse occur in video or 3D multimodal LLMs, and how does the GMI bound apply?
- Can we derive a tighter information‑theoretic bound that accounts for content‑dependent decoder sensitivity (e.g., per‑attribute Lipschitz constants)?

## Limitations
- The exact source of the “text distribution” `Σ_T` used for mode alignment is unspecified; using in‑domain captions vs. a large‑scale corpus may shift classifications.
- Implementation details for multivariate Wasserstein‑1 distance (`W1`) and Lipschitz constant (`L_log`) estimation are not fully described, potentially affecting the bound’s absolute values and ranking.
- The LoRA intervention’s generalizability is unclear; it succeeded for emotion detection on Ultravox but may not transfer to other non‑text tasks or architectures.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (GMI bound degrades with `L_log·W1`)**: High confidence in the theoretical derivation (Theorem 2); medium confidence in real‑world applicability due to the joint distribution assumption `P_M(C,Y)=P_T(C,Y)`.
- **Mechanism 2 (MS directions harm decoding)**: Medium confidence; ablation shows strong causal evidence for non‑aligned encoders but relies on correct MS/TA mode decomposition and assumes linear effects.
- **Mechanism 3 (Alignment as workaround)**: Medium‑Low confidence; the correlation in the Prismatic study is compelling but the claim that alignment discards non‑text info is inferential and alternative explanations exist.

## Next Checks
1. **Controlled `Σ_T` ablation**: Recompute mode alignment using three text sources: (a) The Pile (original text distribution), (b) COCO captions (in‑domain), and (c) random Gaussian baseline. Assess stability of MS/TA classifications and `L_log·W1` ranking.
2. **Per‑mode sensitivity analysis**: For a non‑aligned model (Prismatic‑D), estimate eigenmode‑specific `L_log,k` by perturbing only along each eigenvector `u_k`. Verify that MS modes indeed exhibit higher sensitivity than TA modes.
3. **LoRA scaling test**: Apply the same LoRA emotion‑tuning protocol (rank 16, α 32, CREMA‑D forced‑choice) to a second non‑aligned model (e.g., Qwen2‑Audio). If emotion accuracy improves by less than +10%, the intervention may be model‑specific rather than a general fix for modality collapse.
