# SageBwd: A Trainable Low-bit Attention

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02170
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02170
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** SageBwd enables trainable low-bit attention by quantizing six of seven attention matrix multiplications to INT8 while keeping the softmax gradient (`dP`) in FP16, achieving up to 1.67× speedups over FlashAttention2 on an RTX4090 and matching full-precision pre-training loss when reducing tokens per step.

## Executive Summary
SageBwd introduces a mixed-precision backward pass for attention mechanisms that makes low-bit (INT8) training feasible. It quantizes most intermediate matrices but retains the softmax gradient (`dP`) in FP16 to prevent catastrophic error propagation into the softmax gradient (`dS`), which has a very small magnitude. Key innovations include RMS-based `QK-norm` to stabilize training at large token counts and `K-smoothing` to reduce quantization error. The method demonstrates that by reducing the tokens processed per step (TPS) from 2.1M to 260K, the systematic bias from INT8 quantization can be masked by gradient noise, allowing convergence to a full-precision-like training loss on a 325M parameter Llama model.

## Method Summary
SageBwd modifies the attention backward pass to use INT8 for all matrix multiplications except the computation of `dP`. It applies symmetric per-block quantization to tensors `Q`, `K`, `V`, and intermediate gradients. A `K-smoothing` technique subtracts a block-wise mean from `Q` and a global mean from `K` before the `QK⊤` multiplication, with corresponding bias corrections in the backward pass for `dK` if `Q` is also smoothed. Crucially, `QK-norm` (RMS normalization on `Q` and `K`) is used to constrain their dynamic range, which reduces the quantization step size and stabilizes training. The forward pass computes the softmax in FP16, quantizes the probability matrix `P` per-token, and performs the `PV` multiplication in INT8.

## Key Results
- Reducing tokens per step from 2.1M to 260K allows SageBwd to match the full-precision attention pre-training loss (2.561 vs 2.563 on a 325M Llama model with 78B tokens).
- `K-smoothing` is essential; `Q-smoothing` provides negligible additional benefit.
- SageBwd achieves up to 1.67× speedups over FlashAttention2 on an RTX4090 GPU.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** `QK-norm` stabilizes pre-training by constraining query and key magnitudes, reducing the dynamic range seen by INT8 quantization and mitigating outlier-induced errors.
- **Mechanism:** RMS normalization applied token-wise to `Q` and `K` scales their activations to a controlled range, directly limiting the scale factors used in uniform INT8 quantization. This shrinks the quantization step size, improving fidelity for `QK⊤`. Stable logits prevent softmax saturation and reduce upstream error propagation into `dS`.
- **Core assumption:** Outlier magnitudes in `Q/K` dominate quantization error; normalizing their scale improves effective bit utilization.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "QK-norm is necessary for stable training at large tokens per step"; [section 4.1] "by compressing the dynamic range of Q and K, it reduces the effective quantization step size".
- **Break condition:** If learned `γ` parameters in RMSNorm grow large during pre-training, effective σ<sub>Q,K</sub> may still increase beyond a tolerance, causing quantization error to spike.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The softmax gradient tensor `dS` is the primary bottleneck in the INT8 backward pass due to its intrinsically small magnitude, leading to poor signal-to-noise ratio under fixed absolute quantization error.
- **Mechanism:** `dS = P ∘ (dP − δ1⊤)` has an RMS scaled by ~1/√N (proven in Appendix B). For long sequences (e.g., N=4096), `dS` becomes very small (~1e-7 RMS). INT8 quantization introduces fixed absolute noise; when signal magnitude is tiny, relative error explodes, corrupting `dS` and propagating to `dQ` and `dK`.
- **Core assumption:** Fixed absolute quantization noise is tolerable for large-magnitude tensors but catastrophic for small-magnitude ones like `dS`.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "quantization errors primarily arise from the backward-pass score gradient dS"; [section 4.2] empirical RMS(dS)≈1×10⁻⁷ and Table 2 shows large deviations in `dS` and downstream `dQ/dK`.
- **Break condition:** If sequence length N grows substantially further, `dS` magnitude drops ~1/√N, worsening the signal-to-noise ratio unless quantization step size is adaptively reduced.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Reducing tokens-per-step (TPS) increases gradient stochasticity, which masks the systematic bias from INT8 quantization error along the `dS` path, allowing optimization to converge to a full-precision-like solution.
- **Mechanism:** Smaller TPS (smaller global batch size) yields more frequent updates with higher gradient noise. At large TPS (2.1M), the optimizer sees a less noisy gradient where persistent quantization bias (especially from `dS`) becomes a dominant signal, leading to a suboptimal trajectory. At small TPS (260K), this bias is drowned in stochastic gradient noise, becoming a negligible perturbation.
- **Core assumption:** Quantization error in `dS` introduces a consistent directional bias in gradients; gradient noise of sufficient magnitude can obscure this bias.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "reducing tokens per step from 2.1M to 260K allows matching full-precision attention pre-training loss"; [section 4.3] hypothesis that at smaller TPS, INT8 error acts as a small perturbation relative to gradient noise; [section 5.2] loss curves show gap closure at 260K TPS.
- **Break condition:** If TPS is reduced too far, other large-batch training issues (convergence speed, final quality, hardware utilization) may emerge.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Quantization error propagation in backward passes (chain rule amplification)
  - **Why needed here:** SageBwd's mixed-precision design—keeping `dP` in FP16—directly addresses `dS` vulnerability. Understanding why quantizing `dP` would cause more severe error propagation than other backward MatMuls is key.

- **Concept:** Softmax gradient magnitude dependency on sequence length
  - **Why needed here:** The 1/√N scaling bound explains why `dS` is tiny for long contexts (N=4096), making it the bottleneck and explaining the TPS sensitivity.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input -> [QK-norm] -> [K-smoothing] -> INT8(QK⊤) -> online softmax (FP16 P) -> [per-token quant P] -> INT8(PV) -> Output. Backward: dO -> dP = dOV⊤ (FP16) -> dS = P ∘ (dP − rowsum(dO∘O)) -> [per-block quant dS] -> dQ = INT8(dS·K), dK = INT8(dS⊤·Q) + [bias if Q-smoothed], dV = INT8(P⊤·dO).
- **Critical path:** The forward pass uses block-wise INT8 for `QK⊤` and `PV`, with `P` quantized per-token. The backward pass keeps `dP` in FP16, quantizes `dS` per-block, and computes `dQ`, `dK`, `dV` with INT8 MatMuls. The `QK-norm` and `K-smoothing` preprocessing are applied before the forward `QK⊤`.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - `K-smoothing` vs. `Q-smoothing`: `K-smoothing` is free (bias term vanishes due to row-sum-zero `dS`); `Q-smoothing` requires an extra bias term in `dK`, introducing another noisy path—hence limited benefit.
  - `dP` precision: Keeping `dP` in FP16 avoids error injection into `dS` but costs memory bandwidth; tradeoff is stability vs. speed.
  - Per-block vs. per-token quantization: Coarser quantization (per-block) faster but less accurate; per-token for `P` balances speed/accuracy for the softmax bottleneck.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Loss explosion early in training (within first few hundred steps) when `QK-norm` is disabled at large TPS.
  - Stable but high loss plateau (e.g., >2.6 at 37.5k steps for 325M model) at large TPS even with `QK-norm`—indicates quantization bias dominating.
  - High relative ℓ2 error in `dS` (>0.5) and `dQ/dK` (>0.3) in intermediate tensor checks across multiple layers/heads.
  - Cosine similarity for `dS` near 0 (not 1) when comparing to FP baseline.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ablation: QK-norm at high TPS.** Train with TPS=2.1M, compare runs with/without QK-norm. Expect: without QK-norm → loss divergence within <10k steps; with → stable but suboptimal (gap to FPA).
  2. **Intermediate tensor error profiling.** Extract one layer/head from a trained checkpoint; compute full-precision intermediates and compare quantized-dequantized versions (as in Table 2). Confirm: dS has highest relative error; dQ/dK next; O and dV are accurate.
  3. **TPS sweep with fixed token budget.** Train at 260K, 1M, 2.1M TPS (adjust batch size, fixed tokens total). Expect: loss gap to FPA shrinks as TPS decreases; at 260K TPS, loss curves overlap with FPA within noise. Also verify K-smoothing is non-negotiable at all TPS.

## Limitations
- Theory assumes uniform INT8 and fixed absolute error; may not extend to other formats (e.g., INT4, FP8).
- Evaluation only on 325M Llama (4096 ctx); scalability to larger models/longer contexts is open.
- TPS effect is correlational; causal mechanism unproven.
- QK-norm overhead and downstream task impact (e.g., fine-tuning, inference) not measured.
- Speedup only tested on RTX4090; generalizability to other architectures (A100/H100) and multi-GPU scaling is unclear.

## Confidence
- **QK-norm necessity for large-TPS stability:** High (divergence without it).
- **dS as primary bottleneck:** High (measured tiny RMS/error).
- **TPS reduction masks quantization bias:** Medium (correlation only, no causation test).
- **K-smoothing essential:** High (ablation shows benefit only when combined with dP-FP16).
- **RTX4090 speedup:** High (measured), generalizability to other hardware: Low.
- **Match full-precision loss at 260K TPS:** High (empirical curves overlap).
