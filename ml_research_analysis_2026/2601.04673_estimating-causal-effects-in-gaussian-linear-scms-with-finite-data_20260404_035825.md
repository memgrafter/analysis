# Estimating Causal Effects in Gaussian Linear SCMs with Finite Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04673
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04673
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** Centralized Gaussian Linear SCMs (CGL‑SCMs) retain identifiability of full GL‑SCMs while reducing overparameterization; the EM‑based CGL‑Go algorithm accurately recovers causal effects from finite data, even with latent confounders.

## Executive Summary
Estimating causal effects in Gaussian Linear Structural Causal Models (GL‑SCMs) from finite samples is often hampered by overparameterization, especially when latent confounders are present. This paper introduces Centralized GL‑SCMs (CGL‑SCMs), a subclass where all exogenous variables are standardized (mean 0, variance 1), and proves that they preserve the exact identifiability properties of general GL‑SCMs. The authors develop CGL‑Go, an expectation‑maximization algorithm that operates on vectorized total‑effect and exogenous‑influence matrices, using gradient‑based updates masked by the known causal graph to enforce sparsity. On synthetic frontdoor and napkin graphs with 10 000 samples, CGL‑Go recovers interventional distributions with remarkable fidelity (e.g., for the frontdoor, true \(P(X_3|do(X_2=1)) \sim \mathcal{N}(1.1,1.09)\) vs estimated \(\mathcal{N}(1.1018,1.069)\)), demonstrating that accurate causal inference is feasible from finite data despite latent variables.

## Method Summary
The method replaces a standard GL‑SCM with a CGL‑SCM by standardizing every exogenous variable (including latent confounders \(U\) and noises \(\varepsilon\)) to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). This reparameterization preserves the observational distribution and identifiability of causal effects while drastically reducing the number of free parameters; the remaining unknowns are the direct effect matrix \(T\) (sparse according to the known graph), a bias vector \(\mu\), and a direct exogenous influence matrix \(C\). The causal graph is assumed known and acyclic. CGL‑Go estimates these parameters via EM: the E‑step analytically computes the posterior distribution of latent \(U\) given observed \(X\); the M‑step maximizes the expected complete‑data log‑likelihood using gradient ascent on \(B\) (the total‑effect matrix) and \(C\), with binary masks applied after each update to respect the graph structure. The bias \(\mu\) has a closed‑form update. After convergence, Algorithm 2 extracts the direct adjacency \(T\) from the total effect matrix \(B\) using the graph’s topological order. The resulting SCM can then be used to compute interventional distributions via standard do‑calculus.

## Key Results
- **Frontdoor graph:** Estimated \(P(X_3|do(X_2=1))\) mean = 1.1018, variance = 1.069 vs ground‑truth mean = 1.1, variance = 1.09.
- **Napkin graph:** Estimated \(P(X_4|do(X_3=1))\) mean = 0.3051, variance = 1.1692 vs ground‑truth mean = 0.3, variance = 1.16.
- The algorithm successfully recovers causal effects from 10 000 finite samples despite the presence of latent confounders, confirming the theoretical equivalence between GL‑SCMs and CGL‑SCMs and the practical viability of the EM approach.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Standardization Reduces Overparameterization without Losing Identifiability
- **Claim:** Standardizing all exogenous variables to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) in CGL‑SCMs reduces overparameterization while preserving identical causal effect identifiability properties compared to general GL‑SCMs.
- **Mechanism:** Fixing the mean and variance of every exogenous variable (both confounders \(U\) and non‑confounders \(\varepsilon\)) drastically reduces the number of free parameters. The remaining parameters (direct effect matrices \(T\) and bias \(\mu\)) can still represent any observational distribution achievable by a GL‑SCM, as shown by a reparameterization argument.
- **Core assumption:** The causal graph structure (adjacency) is known and fixed; identifiability depends solely on the graph and observational distribution, not on the specific parameterization of exogenous distributions.
- **Evidence:** Theorem 2.3 proves expressivity equivalence: for any GL‑SCM there exists a CGL‑SCM producing the same \(P(X)\). Theorem 2.4 extends this to identifiability of causal effects.
- **Break condition:** If the true data‑generating process violates linear‑Gaussian assumptions, or if identifiability of a query critically depends on non‑standardized exogenous variances, the centralized representation may fail.

### Mechanism 2: EM‑Based Estimation with Graph Constraints
- **Claim:** The EM‑based algorithm (CGL‑Go) can estimate CGL‑SCM parameters from finite observational data, even with latent confounders, by iteratively inferring latent exogenous variables and updating parameters under graph constraints.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm alternates: (E‑step) computes the posterior distribution of latent \(U\) given \(X\) and current parameters using closed‑form Gaussian formulas; (M‑step) maximizes the expected complete‑data log‑likelihood via gradient ascent on \(B\) and \(C\), with element‑wise masking (\(B_m, C_m\)) to preserve the known causal graph. Bias \(\mu\) is updated via a closed‑form mean.
- **Core assumption:** The provided causal graph is correct and acyclic; the sample is i.i.d. from a linear‑Gaussian process consistent with some CGL‑SCM; EM converges to a local optimum that matches the true \(P(X)\).
- **Evidence:** Algorithm 1 details the masked gradient updates and posterior computations; Section 4 validates on frontdoor and napkin graphs with 10 000 samples, showing near‑perfect recovery (Tables 1, 2).
- **Break condition:** If the causal graph is misspecified, masks enforce incorrect constraints; EM may converge to a poor local optimum or fail with very limited data or highly correlated latents.

### Mechanism 3: Vectorized Representation Enables Direct Edge Recovery
- **Claim:** Representing the SCM in vectorized form using total effect matrix \(B\) and direct exogenous influence matrix \(C\), together with Algorithm 2 to recover direct edge weights \(T\), enables extraction of the full structural equation from the estimated parameters.
- **Mechanism:** The total effect \(B = I + T + T^2 + \dots + T^d\) (finite path series due to acyclicity) captures cumulative linear effects; \(C\) maps each exogenous variable to its direct children. After EM estimates \(B\) and \(C\), Algorithm 2 recursively subtracts indirect path contributions to isolate \(T\) given a topological order.
- **Core assumption:** The graph is acyclic so \(B = (I - T)^{-1}\) exists; the longest path length \(d\) is finite and known; the estimated \(B\) and \(C\) are sufficiently accurate.
- **Evidence:** Section 3.1 defines \(B\) and \(C\) and gives the vectorized structural equation \(X = B^\top \mu + B^\top C^\top U + B^\top \varepsilon\); Appendix D shows recovered \(T\) matrices match the true sparse patterns.
- **Break condition:** Cycles violate acyclicity; significant error in \(B\) (especially in longer paths) may yield negative or spurious \(T\) entries.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text):**  
Observational data \(X\) and known causal graph \(G\) → Parameter initialization (\(B, C, \mu\)) → EM loop (E‑step: infer \(U\); M‑step: gradient update \(B, C\) with masks, closed‑form \(\mu\)) → Direct edge extraction via Algorithm 2 (topological order) → Estimated SCM for causal queries.

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialise \(B \approx I +\) small random \(\times\) mask, \(C\) small random \(\times\) mask, \(\mu\) as sample mean.  
2. Apply masks to \(B\) and \(C\) to enforce sparsity.  
3. Repeat until convergence:  
   - E‑step: For each sample \(x_i\), compute posterior mean and covariance of \(U_i|x_i\) using current \(B, C\).  
   - M‑step: Update \(\mu\) via closed form; perform gradient ascent on expected log‑likelihood for \(B\) and \(C\) (loop samples, compute gradients, apply learning rate and masks).  
4. After EM, run Algorithm 2 with topological order to recover direct adjacency \(T\).  
5. Use estimated SCM (\(T, C, \mu\)) to compute interventional distributions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Mask enforcement: Element‑wise multiplication after each gradient step is simple but may slow convergence; projection onto the constrained set could be used instead.  
- Gradient vs. closed‑form: No closed‑form updates for \(B\) and \(C\) under masking, so gradient ascent is necessary but computationally heavier per iteration.  
- Handling \(\varepsilon\) variance: EM derivation assumes \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\); extending to a full covariance \(\Psi\) is possible but adds complexity.

**Failure signatures:**  
- EM log‑likelihood fails to increase or plateaus prematurely → learning rate too high, poor initialization, or data scaling issues.  
- Estimated \(B\) not lower‑triangular after reordering → mask \(B_m\) inconsistent with topological order or overly aggressive gradient steps.  
- Recovered interventional distributions far from validation set → model mismatch (non‑linearity) or insufficient data; inspect residuals \(X \approx B^\top \mu + B^\top C^\top \hat{U} + B^\top \hat{\varepsilon}\).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate frontdoor graph using provided parameters; run CGL‑Go; compare estimated \(P(X_3|do(X_2=1))\) mean/variance to Table 1 and verify recovered \(T\) sparsity pattern.  
2. Replicate napkin graph; compare estimated \(P(X_4|do(X_3=1))\) to Table 2 and check \(T\) sparsity.  
3. Sample‑size sensitivity for frontdoor: vary \(n \in \{100, 500, 1000, 5000, 10000\}\), compute error in interventional mean/variance, and plot to assess finite‑sample performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** *Can the CGL‑SCM framework be extended to non‑Gaussian noise distributions while preserving identifiability and enabling finite‑sample parameter estimation?*  
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion explicitly states: “Future works can explore more general distributions ...”
- **Why unresolved:** The current theory and EM algorithm rely critically on Gaussian assumptions for analytical tractability and identifiability proofs; extending to other distributions requires re‑deriving identifiability conditions and adapting the estimation procedure.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical extension proving identifiability for a broader class (e.g., exponential family) and a corresponding scalable estimation algorithm, validated on synthetic and real‑world non‑Gaussian data.

**Open Question 2:** *How can we compute valid bounds or confidence intervals for causal effects when the query is not identifiable from observational data and only finite samples are available?*  
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion mentions “finding bounds for non‑identifiable queries from finite data” as a future direction.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper focuses solely on identifiable queries; non‑identifiable queries require partial identification strategies that account for both model uncertainty and finite‑sample variability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A method for deriving sharp interventional bounds under finite samples (e.g., via Bayesian posterior sampling or sensitivity analysis), tested on graphs where standard identification fails.

**Open Question 3:** *How robust is the CGL‑Go algorithm to errors in the assumed causal graph, and can it be adapted to handle graph uncertainty or learn the graph jointly?*  
- **Basis in paper:** The method assumes a known causal graph (Page 3: “we assume that the causal graph is known”), but in practice, causal discovery may provide incorrect graphs.
- **Why unresolved:** The EM algorithm uses mask constraints to enforce the given graph; incorrect edges lead to biased parameter estimates and invalid causal effects; no robustness analysis is provided.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical robustness tests where the input graph is perturbed (e.g., edge additions/removals), measuring degradation in causal effect accuracy, and development of a joint estimation approach that accounts for graph uncertainty.

## Limitations
- Requires a known, acyclic causal graph; graph misspecification or cycles break mask constraints and identifiability.
- EM algorithm lacks convergence guarantees and is sensitive to initialization, learning rate, and convergence thresholds.
- Relies on linear‑Gaussian assumptions; non‑linearities or heavy‑tailed noises may invalidate the equivalence and estimation.
- Experiments are limited to two small synthetic graphs with 10 000 samples; scalability and performance on real‑world data remain untested.

## Confidence
- **Standardization preserves identifiability:** **High** (Theorems 2.3 & 2.4 provide rigorous proof).
- **EM‑based estimation (CGL‑Go):** **Medium** (empirical success on two graphs but no convergence analysis or extensive hyperparameter study).
- **Vectorized representation and T extraction:** **High** (derivation follows directly from acyclicity; Algorithm 2 empirically recovers sparsity patterns).
