# Modeling Score Approximation Errors in Diffusion Models via Forward SPDEs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08579
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08579
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** SIEM, a new evaluation metric derived from a forward SPDE framework, shows high Spearman correlation with FID (0.8898) on CIFAR-10 and LSUN-Bedroom, and retains strong correlation (0.8605) when computed using only the final 10% of diffusion timesteps, enabling efficient model assessment.

## Executive Summary
This work tackles score estimation errors in diffusion models by treating them as a stochastic source driving the forward Fokker-Planck equation, turning it into a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE). From this framework, the authors derive a novel metric, SIEM, which captures the accumulated quadratic variation of the SPDE solution along the sampling trajectory. Empirical evaluations on CIFAR-10 and LSUN-Bedroom demonstrate that SIEM correlates strongly with the widely used FID score (Spearman 0.8898) and moderately with 2-Wasserstein distance (0.5094). Notably, SIEM computed using only the last 10% of timesteps (900–999) still yields high correlation with FID (0.8605), suggesting that generative quality can be efficiently evaluated without running the full reverse process. The paper also offers theoretical insights into why diffusion models might be robust to score errors, invoking geometric stability concepts like displacement convexity and Log‑Sobolev inequalities.

## Method Summary
The method builds on the reverse‑time Fokker‑Planck equation (FPE) for diffusion models. The deterministic discrepancy term arising from imperfect score estimation is replaced by a conservative stochastic noise term of the form $-\nabla\!\cdot(u_t \circ dW_Q)$, where $W_Q$ is a Hilbert‑space‑valued Wiener process and the systematic bias $\mu_t$ is absorbed into an effective drift. This converts the FPE into a forward SPDE. To quantify the overall impact of the stochastic forcing, the SPDE is projected onto a radial test function $\phi(x)$, yielding a one‑dimensional stochastic process. Its Itô representation reveals a martingale part whose squared increments reflect the instantaneous effect of the score error. After estimating the deterministic bias $\mu_{t,\phi}$ via temporal smoothing, SIEM is defined as the $L^2$ norm of the residual increments over time.

For practical computation, at each reverse timestep $t$ a small auxiliary binary classifier is trained to distinguish between noised real data ($v_t$) and generated samples ($u_t$). The classifier’s output provides an estimate of the density ratio $u_t/v_t$, which is used together with the model’s score $s_\theta$ and the analytic $\nabla\log v_t$ to compute a scalar $\xi(t)$. Smoothing $\xi(t)$ over time yields $\mu_{t,\phi}$; SIEM then aggregates the squared deviations $(\xi(t)-\mu_{t,\phi})^2\Delta t$ and takes the square root.

## Key Results
- SIEM achieves a Spearman correlation of **0.8898** with FID on CIFAR‑10 and LSUN‑Bedroom.
- Correlation with 2‑Wasserstein distance is moderate (**0.5094**).
- Using only the **final 10%** of diffusion timesteps (timesteps 900–999) to compute SIEM still yields high correlation with FID (**0.8605**), indicating significant efficiency gains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1 – Stochastic source in the forward FPE.**  
The deterministic drift perturbation in the reverse FPE, $-\nabla\!\cdot(\bar{u}_t [s_\theta - \nabla\log v_t])$, is recast as a conservative stochastic term $-\nabla\!\cdot(u_t \circ dW_Q)$. This models the score error as a sum of a deterministic bias $\mu_t$ and a zero‑mean stochastic process with spatial correlation, preserving mass conservation. The Stratonovich integration preserves geometric structure for analysis, while the Itô interpretation later yields the martingale needed for the quadratic variation. Key evidence: Eq. (7) and the surrounding discussion.

**Mechanism 2 – Geometric robustness via displacement convexity.**  
Under simplifications (e.g., $f_t=0$, $\bar{g}_t=\sqrt{2}$, $\mu_t=0$), the ideal reverse dynamics become a Wasserstein gradient flow minimizing $\mathrm{KL}(u_t\Vert v_t)$. If the target distributions $v_t$ are log‑concave (ensured by diffusion on a compact domain), the energy potential is displacement convex, leading to a Log‑Sobolev inequality. This gives a differential inequality $dE[u_t]/dt \le -2\lambda E[u_t] + \eta$, where $\eta$ bounds the noise power from the stochastic term. The exponential decay term dominates, bounding the energy deviation and explaining empirical robustness. Evidence: Section 5.1, Eq. (10).

**Mechanism 3 – Early‑time volatility as a predictor.**  
SIEM is derived from the quadratic variation of the martingale part after subtracting the estimated bias $\mu_{t,\phi}$. The residuals $( \xi(t) - \mu_{t,\phi} )$ capture the “structural volatility” driven by the remaining stochastic score error. The authors hypothesize that volatility observed early in the reverse trajectory (e.g., the last 10% of timesteps) is indicative of the final distributional deviation, because the restoring forces of the gradient flow quickly dampen later‑stage errors. Empirically, the high correlation of SIEM computed on timesteps 900–999 with FID supports this view. Evidence: Table 2 and the experimental results.

## Foundational Learning
- **Fokker‑Planck Equation (FPE)** – *Why needed:* The entire SPDE framework is a perturbation of the FPE governing probability density evolution in reverse diffusion. Understanding the deterministic drift term is prerequisite to seeing how stochastic forcing changes dynamics. *Quick check:* How does the deterministic perturbation $-\nabla\!\cdot(\bar{u}_t [s_\theta - \nabla\log v_t])$ differ from $-\nabla\!\cdot(u_t \circ dW_Q)$ in terms of mass conservation?
- **Stratonovich vs. Itô Stochastic Integration** – *Why needed:* The paper switches interpretations: Stratonovich preserves geometric structure for stability analysis, while Itô reveals the martingale property required for quadratic variation. *Quick check:* Why is the Itô interpretation essential for defining $[X,X]_t$, and what correction (Wong‑Zakai) is implicitly accounted for when switching to Stratonovich?
- **Displacement Convexity & Log‑Sobolev Inequality** – *Why needed:* These optimal‑transport concepts underpin the theoretical robustness argument. The Log‑Sobolev constant $\lambda$ controls the contraction rate of the KL energy. *Quick check:* Why does log‑concavity of $v_t$ imply displacement convexity of $\mathrm{KL}(u\Vert v_t)$, and how does that lead to $\mathrm{Dissipation} \ge 2\lambda E[u_t]$?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
1. Forward SPDE Core → 2. Quadratic Variation Accumulator → 3. Density Ratio Estimator → 4. SIEM Aggregator

**Critical path**  
Score model $s_\theta$ → generate $u_t$ samples at various $t$ → train classifier per $t$ → estimate $u_t/v_t$ → compute $\xi(t)$ → smooth → obtain SIEM. The per‑timestep classifier training is the heaviest operation.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Classifier complexity vs. bias:* A more powerful classifier improves density‑ratio estimation but risks overfitting; a simpler one is faster but may be inaccurate, especially at early $t$.
- *Test function choice:* The radial Lorentz $\phi$ is chosen for concentration properties; a simpler Gaussian might be less stable in high dimensions.
- *Timestep sampling:* Full SIEM uses all 1000 steps; the 10% claim uses only the final 100 steps, trading information for speed.

**Failure signatures**  
1. Classifier collapse (accuracy ~50%) makes density‑ratio estimation unstable, causing NaN or wild SIEM.
2. SIEM shows no or inverse correlation with FID/W2 – inspect $\xi(t)$ trends and smoothing kernel.
3. Heavy dependence on clamping $u_t/v_t$ indicates estimator failure.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Re‑derive Eq. (13):** On a single checkpoint, generate 10k $u_t$ and $v_t$ samples at $t=500$, train a classifier, and manually compute $\xi(t)$ for a few samples; verify sensitivity to $\varepsilon$.
2. **Validate 10% claim:** For one model over training, compute full SIEM (0–999) and final‑10% SIEM (900–999) at several checkpoints. Plot both against FID; expect parallel trends.
3. **Ablation on test function:** Replace the radial Lorentz $\phi$ with a simple radial Gaussian and recompute SIEM. Does correlation with FID drop significantly?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Eigenbasis of the stochastic error term** $dW_Q$: The framework models score error as a generic stochastic source; identifying the specific eigenbasis would bridge theory and empirical behavior of neural networks.
2. **Precise form and dynamics of the systematic drift** $\mu(t,x)$: The drift represents score estimation bias; without empirical measurement via conditional density estimation, the SPDE model remains partially specified.
3. **Generalization to larger datasets, higher resolutions, and non‑DDPM architectures:** All experiments are on CIFAR‑10 and 32×32 LSUN‑Bedroom with a U‑Net DDPM; results are preliminary. Validation on ImageNet, FFHQ, and with transformers or latent diffusion is needed.
4. **Impact of non‑uniform spatial error structure:** The robustness analysis assumes generic stochastic forcing; if errors concentrate on specific eigenmodes, the contraction rate $\lambda$ may vary. Spectral analysis of error fields and mode‑specific SPDE simulations are required.

## Limitations
- The SPDE decomposition ($\mu_t + dW_Q$) and its white‑noise assumption are not empirically validated; spatial correlations or non‑Gaussian residuals could invalidate the model.
- The theoretical robustness argument relies on displacement convexity and Log‑Sobolev inequalities, which require log‑concave $v_t$ – an unrealistic assumption for real image data.
- The SIEM metric depends on an underspecified auxiliary classifier and an unreported Lorentz $\varepsilon$ parameter, risking instability and hindering reproducibility.

## Confidence
- **High:** Empirical SIEM–FID correlation (0.89) and the effectiveness of using only 10% of timesteps are strongly supported by the reported experiments.
- **Medium:** The SPDE framework is mathematically plausible, and the early‑time volatility predictor shows promise but may be dataset‑specific without broader validation.
- **Low:** The displacement‑convexity/Log‑Sobolev explanation rests on unrealistic log‑concavity assumptions and is therefore speculative.

## Next Checks
1. **Classifier Ablation:** Vary the auxiliary CNN’s depth, width, training set size (1k–100k), and epochs; recompute full and 10% SIEM and report correlation stability with FID.
2. **$\nabla\log v_t$ Consistency:** Replace the analytic conditional score with a Monte‑Carlo approximation and compare SIEM values to check for systematic bias.
3. **$\varepsilon$ & Smoothing Sensitivity:** Grid‑search Lorentz $\varepsilon$ (e.g., $10^{-3}$–$10^{1}$) and Gaussian smoothing $\sigma$ (5–20); evaluate changes in SIEM–FID correlation.
