# Reinforcement Learning with Conditional Expectation Reward

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.10624
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10624
*   **Reference Count:** 8
*   **Primary Result:** CER provides a model-intrinsic, soft reward signal for RLVR, outperforming exact-match (e.g., 48.7% vs 46.5% average pass@1) and matching rule-based verifier performance without external rules.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning for verifier-free reasoning (RLVR) is hindered by the reliance on handcrafted, often brittle, rule-based verifiers or the sparse, binary feedback from exact-match rewards. This paper introduces Conditional Expectation Reward (CER), which uses the policy model itself as an implicit verifier. CER defines the reward as the expected likelihood of generating a reference answer, conditioned on a generated answer. This provides a soft, graded signal that correlates with partial correctness. Theoretical analysis shows CER is value-equivalent to exact-match in expectation and exhibits a self-consistency amplification effect for correct answers. Empirical results on mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that CER consistently surpasses exact-match baselines and achieves parity with rule-based verifiers, enabling effective RLVR in domains lacking explicit verification rules.

## Method Summary
CER computes a reward `ρ(a, a*)` for a generated answer `a` against a reference `a*` by estimating the expectation of the model's own conditional probability `πθ(a*|s', q)` over solutions `s'` drawn from the distribution conditioned on `a` (i.e., `πθ(s'|q, a)`). This intractable conditional expectation is transformed via Bayes' rule into a form using unconditional samples `s_j ~ πθ(·|q)` (Equation 5). Practically, for a batch of `N` sampled `(s_i, a_i)` pairs, a matrix `W` is built where `W_{ij} = πθ(a_i | s_j, q)` and a vector `P` where `P_j = πθ(a* | s_j, q)`. The reward for the i-th sample is `R_i = [D^{-1}W]_{i,*} · P`, where `D` is a diagonal matrix of row sums. This reward is then used in a policy gradient update (like RLOO) to fine-tune the model `πθ`.

## Key Results
*   CER significantly outperforms exact-match rewards on general reasoning datasets (e.g., 48.7% vs 46.5% average pass@1 on WebInstruct with Qwen3-4B-Base).
*   On mathematical reasoning (MATH-7.5K), CER matches the performance of a simple rule-based verifier without using any external code or rules.
*   CER’s reward distribution is soft and graded, providing meaningful signal for partially correct answers where exact-match fails.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Soft, Graded Signal via Model-Intrinsic Consistency
**Claim:** CER provides a soft, graded reward for general reasoning by using the model's own conditional likelihood to assess answer consistency with a reference.
**Mechanism:** For generated answer `a` and reference `a*`, `ρ(a, a*) = E_{s'~πθ(·|q,a)}[πθ(a*|s', q)]`. This measures how likely the model is to produce `a*` when it has already produced `a`. Higher values indicate greater semantic or surface-form consistency. The core assumption is that the model `πθ` is calibrated such that answers semantically closer to `a*` lead to higher `πθ(a*|s', q)`.
**Evidence:** Abstract states it "provides a soft, graded reward signal." Section 2, Definition (Eq. 3) formalizes it. Corpus work (RLPR, DARL, NOVER) shows field's push to eliminate handcrafted verifiers.
**Break condition:** If `πθ` is poorly calibrated or lacks capacity to distinguish correctness degrees, the signal becomes uninformative. Likely for very small or domain-mismatched base models.

### Mechanism 2: Theoretical Value Equivalence to Exact-Match
**Claim:** CER is theoretically value-equivalent to the exact-match objective in expectation, making it a principled soft relaxation.
**Mechanism:** Theorem 2 proves `E_{q,(s,a)}[ρ(a, a*(q))] = E_{q,(s,a)}[I(a=a*(q))]`. The proof expands CER's definition via Bayes' rule and marginalization, collapsing the double expectation into the exact-match indicator's expectation. The empirical estimator's normalization (Eq. 5) preserves this equality in expectation.
**Evidence:** Section 2, Theorem 2 provides the full algebraic proof. Abstract calls it a "smooth relaxation."
**Break condition:** The proof assumes the ability to sum over all possible `(s', a)`. Finite Monte Carlo estimation (small `M`) introduces high variance, breaking *empirical* equivalence in practice.

### Mechanism 3: Self-Consistency Amplification
**Claim:** CER exhibits a self-consistency amplification effect: when `a = a*`, the conditioned posterior `πθ(·|q, a*)` reweights solutions to increase the expected probability of regenerating `a*`.
**Mechanism:** Theorem 1 shows `ρ(a*, a*) = E_{s~πθ(·|q)}[πθ(a*|s, q)^2] / E_{s~πθ(·|q)}[πθ(a*|s, q)]`. By Jensen's inequality, this is ≥ `E_s[πθ(a*|s, q)]`. Conditioning on having generated `a*` concentrates probability mass on solutions `s'` that already assign high likelihood to `a*`, creating positive feedback that reinforces correct-answer pathways.
**Evidence:** Section 2, Theorem 1. Section 3.4 visualizes sparsity patterns in `D^{-1}W` showing alignment. Corpus paper DARL aims to *encourage diversity*, highlighting CER's contrasting reinforcement effect.
**Break condition:** If for a `q`, `πθ(a*|s, q)` is nearly constant across `s` (high model uncertainty), amplification vanishes (`ρ(a*, a*) ≈ E[πθ(a*|s, q)]`).

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Autoregressive Language Modeling**
    *   **Why needed:** CER's definition and estimator depend on token-level probabilities `πθ(a|s, q)`. Understanding the factorization `πθ(s, a|q) = πθ(s|q)πθ(a|s, q)` is essential for computing the matrix `W` and vector `P`.
    *   **Quick check:** Given an autoregressive model, how would you compute the joint probability of a full solution-answer pair `(s, a)` for a question `q`?
*   **Concept: Policy Gradient Methods (REINFORCE, PPO)**
    *   **Why needed:** CER is a reward function used within RLVR. The training loop involves sampling `(s, a)`, computing detached `R`, and updating `θ` via `∇θ logπθ(a,s|q)` weighted by `R`/advantage. Understanding baselines and variance reduction (RLOO) is crucial for stability.
    *   **Quick check:** In a policy gradient update with a learned reward `R`, why must `R` be detached (treated as constant) with respect to `θ` during the gradient step?
*   **Concept: Bayesian Conditioning and Monte Carlo Estimation**
    *   **Why needed:** CER's definition involves `E_{s'~πθ(·|q,a)}[·]`. The derivation (Eq. 3 to Eq. 5) uses Bayes' rule to rewrite `πθ(s'|q,a)` and then approximates the expectation with samples from `πθ(·|q)`. This transformation from a conditional to an unconditional sampling distribution is non-trivial.
    *   **Quick check:** How does Equation (5) transform the intractable conditional expectation `E_{s'~πθ(·|q,a)}[πθ(a*|s', q)]` into a form that uses samples `s_j ~ πθ(·|q)`?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Policy Model `πθ` -> Sampler -> CER Reward Calculator -> Policy Gradient Optimizer (RLOO) -> `πθ` (updated)

**Critical path:**
1.  Sample `N` independent `{(s_i, a_i)}` from `πθ(·|q)` for a batch of questions `q`.
2.  For each unique `a_i` in the batch (using all `s_j` samples), compute `πθ(a_i | s_j, q)` and `πθ(a* | s_j, q)`.
3.  Assemble matrix `W` (`W_{ij} = πθ(a_i | s_j, q)`), vector `P` (`P_j = πθ(a* | s_j, q)`), normalize `W` by row sums to get `D^{-1}W`.
4.  Compute reward vector `R = (D^{-1}W) * P` (summed across `s_j` dimension per `i`), with `R` detached.
5.  Compute policy gradient estimate `(1/N) Σ_i R_i ∇θ logπθ(a_i, s_i | q)` and update `θ`.
*Key efficiency gain:* Reusing the same `s_j` set for all `a_i` avoids `O(N*M)` redundant forward passes.

**Design tradeoffs:**
*   **Sample count `M` (or `N`)**: Higher `M` improves estimate accuracy but increases forward passes quadratically. Paper shows performance/runtime tradeoff (Table 3), using `M=16`.
*   **Answer deduplication:** Identical `a_i` yield identical reward rows. Caching rewards for unique answers reduces `O(N²)` matrix ops to `O(U*M)` where `U` is unique answer count.
*   **Detachment:** Rewards `R` must be detached from the computation graph. Gradients flowing through `R` create unstable dependence of reward on the updated parameters.

**Failure signatures:**
*   **Sparse/Noisy rewards:** If `πθ(a_i | s_j, q)` is very small for most pairs, normalized `D^{-1}W` becomes sparse and dominated by noise, yielding near-zero, uninformative `R_i`.
*   **Poor self-consistency:** If `πθ(a*|s, q)` varies little with `s` (low model confidence/variance), `ρ(a, a*)` is flat, providing weak discrimination and training signal.
*   **Computational blowup:** Without answer deduplication, `O(N²)` computation is prohibitive for larger `N`.

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Validate Theorem 1 (Self-Consistency):** Generate many `(s,a)` pairs. For `a=a*`, compute `ρ(a*,a*)` and compare to baseline `E_s[πθ(a*|s,q)]`. Confirm `ρ` is larger.
2.  **Compare to Exact-Match & Rule-Based:** Train with CER, exact-match, and a simple rule-based verifier on MATH-7.5K. Measure pass@1 on held-out math and general tasks. Expect CER to close gap to rule-based on math and outperform exact-match on general.
3.  **Visualize Reward Distribution:** For a batch of general-domain questions, plot histogram of CER rewards `R_i`. Compare to binary exact-match histogram. Expect CER's spectrum of values for non-matching answers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
*   **Question:** How does the sample size `M` in CER's Monte Carlo estimator affect the bias, variance, and stability of the resulting reward signal during policy optimization?
*   **Basis in paper:** Section 3.3 describes `M` as a trade-off and Figure 2 visualizes its influence on `D⁻¹W`. No theoretical/empirical analysis quantifies estimation error's impact on gradient variance.
*   **Why unresolved:** Paper reports runtime/performance for `M` but not how finite-sample error propagates through policy gradient or affects convergence. `M` selection remains heuristic.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical bounds on estimator error vs. `M`, `N`, model distribution; empirical ablations measuring reward variance and gradient SNR across `M`; learning curves showing convergence stability for different `M`.

### Open Question 2
*   **Question:** To what extent does CER's performance rely on the calibration quality and inherent biases of the policy model serving as the implicit verifier?
*   **Basis in paper:** CER uses `πθ(a∗|s′, q)` as a correctness proxy, assuming meaningful self-assessment. No experiments test miscalibrated or systematically biased models.
*   **Why unresolved:** Evaluations use standard pretrained models. No tests for scenarios where `πθ`'s internal probabilities are unreliable (e.g., fine-tuned on corrupted data). Unclear if CER amplifies model flaws.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments with miscalibrated models (e.g., perturbed likelihoods, incorrect reasoning chains), measuring CER's correlation with external correctness metrics; analyzing how model confidence calibration correlates with CER's reward discrimination power.

### Open Question 3
*   **Question:** How does CER perform on tasks with multiple equally valid but lexically and semantically diverse answer forms, where a single reference answer may not capture the solution space?
*   **Basis in paper:** CER is defined conditioned on a single reference `a∗` (Eq. 3). Evaluations on MATH500, MMLU-Pro typically have canonical references. Paper does not address tasks with multiple gold standards.
*   **Why unresolved:** In free-form reasoning, correct answers vary (e.g., explanatory science answers). CER might penalize valid but dissimilar answers if `πθ(a∗|s′, q)` is low, causing reward sparsity.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluation on datasets with multiple human-verified correct answers per question, computing CER for each and analyzing distribution; comparison with diversity-aware metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) or human eval.

### Open Question 4
*   **Question:** What is the optimal method for combining CER's continuous reward with discrete rule-based verifiers in domains where both are applicable?
*   **Basis in paper:** Section 3.2 introduces Rule+CER as a simple average and shows improved performance. Notes complementary strengths but doesn't explore other strategies.
*   **Why unresolved:** Averaging is ad-hoc; may not be optimal across tasks/scales. No analysis justifies this choice or investigates learned weights, gating, or other fusion techniques.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic comparison of combination functions (weighted sum, product, learned meta-verifier) on hybrid datasets; ablations on when each component contributes most; theoretical analysis of combination's effect on gradient variance/bias.

## Limitations
*   CER's efficacy hinges on the policy model's calibration and self-consistency; poorly calibrated or low-variance models yield uninformative rewards.
*   Computational cost scales quadratically with sample count (`O(N²)`), mitigated only by answer deduplication; accuracy/efficiency trade-offs for large batches remain unclear.
*   The self-consistency amplification mechanism may reduce answer diversity, potentially harming exploration in domains requiring multiple valid reasoning paths.

## Confidence
*   **High:** Theoretical value equivalence (Theorem 2) and self-consistency amplification (Theorem 1) are mathematically sound under stated assumptions.
*   **Medium:** Empirical superiority over exact-match is supported by experiments but may be dataset-specific; rule-based verifier parity is shown on math-focused benchmarks only.
*   **Low:** Generalization to truly "free-form" domains (e.g., creative writing, ambiguous QA) is unproven—current evaluations center on mathematical and structured general reasoning.

## Next Checks
1.  **Calibration Stress Test:** Fine-tune models with controlled calibration degradation (e.g., via temperature scaling or distribution shift) and measure CER reward signal quality (e.g., Kendall’s τ vs. human judgments) and downstream performance.
2.  **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Transfer:** Apply CER-trained models to novel domains absent from training (e.g., legal or biomedical reasoning) to test if model-intrinsic consistency generalizes beyond curated math/general benchmarks.
3.  **Diversity Impact Quantification:** Train with CER vs. exact-match on a dataset with multiple valid answers; measure output diversity (e.g., distinct n-gram entropy) and coverage of solution space to confirm amplification effect’s trade-off.
