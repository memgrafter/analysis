# A Multi-Agent Framework for Medical AI: Leveraging Fine-Tuned GPT, LLaMA, and DeepSeek R1 for Evidence-Based and Bias-Aware Clinical Query Processing

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14158
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14158
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** A multi-agent system using fine-tuned LLaMA, DeepSeek R1, and evidence retrieval achieved 87% accuracy with 0.80 relevance on medical QA, reducing uncertainty (perplexity 4.13) at a mean latency of 36.5 seconds.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the unreliability of single large language models (LLMs) for medical question-answering due to weak verification, insufficient evidence grounding, and poor confidence signaling. The authors develop a modular multi-agent framework that fine-tunes GPT, LLaMA, and DeepSeek R1 on the MedQuAD dataset and orchestrates specialized agents: a Clinical Reasoning Agent (LLaMA) for structured explanations, an Evidence Retrieval Agent querying PubMed, and a Refinement Agent (DeepSeek R1) for clarity and factual consistency. The system integrates uncertainty quantification and bias detection, aiming to produce more reliable, evidence-based, and transparent clinical responses.

## Method Summary
The framework is built in three phases. First, the base models (GPT, LLaMA, DeepSeek R1) are fine-tuned on a MedQuAD-derived dataset (~20k QA pairs from 12 NIH domains) using parameter-efficient methods like LoRA/Unsloth with model-specific tokenization. Second, a multi-agent pipeline orchestrates the process: a query is sent to the Clinical Reasoning Agent (fine-tuned LLaMA) to generate a structured chain-of-thought; the Evidence Retrieval Agent then queries PubMed via E-utilities for relevant articles (max 3); finally, the Refinement Agent (fine-tuned DeepSeek R1) integrates the initial reasoning with retrieved evidence to produce a final answer. Third, post-processing includes uncertainty estimation via Monte Carlo Dropout and perplexity (using OPT-1.3B) and bias detection using lexical checks, sentiment analysis, and LIME/SHAP. High-uncertainty or flagged outputs are routed for optional human expert review. The full system is evaluated on accuracy, relevance, standard NLP metrics (ROUGE, BLEU), uncertainty reduction, and latency.

## Key Results
- The fine-tuned DeepSeek R1 model achieved the highest individual performance among base models (ROUGE-1: 0.536, ROUGE-2: 0.226, BLEU: 0.098).
- The complete multi-agent system attained 87% accuracy and 0.80 mean relevance on the test set.
- Evidence augmentation reduced answer uncertainty, with a mean perplexity of 4.13. The end-to-end mean latency was 36.5 seconds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach improves reliability by decomposing the complex medical QA task into specialized, verifiable sub-processes. The Clinical Reasoning Agent focuses on generating an initial, structured medical rationale. The Evidence Retrieval Agent grounds this rationale in current, authoritative biomedical literature from PubMed, mitigating hallucination. The Refinement Agent synthesizes these inputs for clarity and factual consistency. Separately, uncertainty quantification (via MC Dropout and perplexity) provides a calibrated confidence signal, while bias detection modules flag potential fairness issues. This modular design allows each component to be optimized and validated independently, and the final verification steps (uncertainty/bias checks, optional human review) create multiple layers of quality control absent in monolithic LLM prompting.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi-Agent Orchestration:** Why needed: Single LLMs lack built-in verification; decomposing tasks enables specialized, robust pipelines. Quick check: Map a complex query to discrete agent roles (reason, retrieve, refine) and verify handoff logic.
- **Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (LoRA/Unsloth):** Why needed: Full fine-tuning of large models is prohibitively expensive; LoRA adapts models efficiently on domain-specific data. Quick check: Implement a LoRA adapter on a small model subset and confirm trainable parameters are <1% of total.
- **Uncertainty Quantification (MC Dropout, Perplexity):** Why needed: Clinicians need confidence scores to gauge reliability; uncertainty highlights low-certainty answers for review. Quick check: Generate multiple MC Dropout samples for a fixed input and compute variance; cross-check with a small proxy model's perplexity score.
- **Bias Detection (Lexical, Sentiment, LIME/SHAP):** Why needed: To identify and flag outputs with problematic demographic or stylistic patterns that could lead to unfair clinical suggestions. Quick check: Run a known biased sample (e.g., gendered pronoun swap) through the pipeline and verify a bias flag is raised.
- **Biomedical Evidence Retrieval (PubMed E-utilities):** Why needed: Grounding in peer-reviewed literature is essential for medical accuracy and reduces model hallucination. Quick check: Execute a PubMed API query for a common medical term and confirm top results are from indexed journals.
- **Domain-Specific Tokenization:** Why needed: Medical terminology (e.g., compound words, acronyms) is often poorly handled by general tokenizers, harming performance. Quick check: Tokenize complex terms like "myasthenia gravis" across tokenizers and compare token count/word integrity.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Query -> Clinical Reasoning Agent (Fine-tuned LLaMA) -> Evidence Retrieval Agent (PubMed API) -> Refinement Agent (Fine-tuned DeepSeek R1) -> Uncertainty & Bias Checks -> Final Response (with confidence/bias flags)

**Critical Path:** The sequential flow from reasoning generation, through evidence grounding, to refinement and validation. The Evidence Retrieval Agent is a synchronous blocking call; its success or failure directly determines if the Refinement Agent proceeds with evidence.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Latency vs. Comprehensiveness:* Using three agents and live PubMed queries increases latency (mean 36.5s) but improves answer quality and grounding. For time-critical scenarios, agent pruning (e.g., skip retrieval) or caching could reduce delay.
- *Specialization vs. Complexity:* Separate agents allow independent optimization but add orchestration overhead and failure points (e.g., API downtime, model crashes).
- *Automation vs. Human-in-the-Loop:* Automated uncertainty/bias flags aim to reduce human review burden, but the "optional human expert validation" introduces a manual step whose integration details are unspecified.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- High perplexity/MC Dropout variance indicates model uncertainty, often from ambiguous queries or poor evidence.
- Bias detection flags (lexical/sentiment/LIME) suggest potential fairness issues.
- PubMed API errors, timeouts, or irrelevant retrieved articles break the evidence grounding chain.
- GPU memory overflow specifically during the DeepSeek R1 refinement step due to context length or model size.

**First Three Experiments to Onboard:**  
1. **Agent Isolation Test:** Run each agent (LLaMA reasoning, PubMed retrieval, DeepSeek refinement) individually on 10 sample queries. Verify LLaMA produces structured reasoning, PubMed returns relevant article snippets, and DeepSeek correctly cites evidence in output.
2. **Evidence Retrieval Audit:** For 100 random test queries, manually score the top 3 PubMed results as "relevant" or "irrelevant" to the query's clinical intent. Calculate retrieval precision and correlate with final answer quality scores.
3. **Uncertainty-Bias Correlation:** Generate responses for a set of queries known to contain demographic variables (e.g., "treatment for a 65-year-old Black female"). Check if high uncertainty (perplexity > threshold) correlates with bias flag triggers from lexical/sentiment modules.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** How can systematic fairness evaluation across demographic attributes (e.g., gender, race, socioeconomic status) be implemented for medical AI systems given the scarcity of publicly available, demographically annotated medical datasets?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly acknowledge that their bias detection "lacks systematic fairness evaluation across demographic attributes" and note that "demographically annotated medical datasets remain extremely limited in public availability due to legitimate privacy protections under GDPR and HIPAA." They state this is a "critical gap" and "priority for future work."  
- **Why unresolved:** The fundamental obstacle is data accessibility—clinical datasets with protected attribute annotations require extensive institutional review board approval, controlled access agreements, and ethical oversight, which were beyond this study's scope. Without such data, fairness cannot be measured or mitigated.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting a multi-centre study with appropriate ethical approvals to access and annotate a large-scale clinical dataset (e.g., MIMIC-IV) with demographic variables. This would enable computation of group-level fairness metrics (e.g., demographic parity, equalized odds) across system outputs for different patient populations. Publishing the annotated dataset (with privacy safeguards) would also allow reproducible fairness benchmarking.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** What specific clinical utility metrics beyond standard NLP similarity scores (e.g., ROUGE, BLEU) are necessary and feasible to evaluate a medical AI system's impact on clinical decision-making, and how should they be operationalized?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state that standard NLP metrics "do not fully capture clinical utility dimensions such as diagnostic accuracy, treatment appropriateness, or reasoning quality." They call for "structured clinical evaluation protocols with domain experts" and "clinician-centred evaluation through formal usability studies."  
- **Why unresolved:** Implementing such evaluation requires access to panels of medical experts, structured clinical evaluation frameworks, and controlled study designs with appropriate ethical oversight. This was infeasible within the current study's computational and timeframe constraints, as they note: "implementing these metrics requires access to clinical expert panels... unavailable within this study's timeframe."  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A prospective clinical trial where clinicians use the system for simulated or actual (with supervision) patient cases. Outcomes would include: (1) expert scoring of system outputs for medical accuracy, appropriateness, and reasoning quality; (2) measures of decision time reduction or accuracy improvement; (3) qualitative analysis of workflow integration via ethnographic observation; and (4) comparison of system recommendations against gold-standard guidelines or expert consensus.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** To what extent do differences in tokenization strategies (Byte-Pair Encoding vs. SentencePiece with Unsloth optimisation) directly cause the observed performance disparities in medical terminology handling between GPT, LLaMA, and DeepSeek R1?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper reports DeepSeek R1's superior performance (e.g., ROUGE-1: 0.536 vs. LLaMA's 0.18) and attributes part of this to its tokenisation's ability to "retain the hierarchical structure of medical terminology." However, this correlation is not isolated as a causal factor; other architectural differences (MoE, attention mechanisms) co-vary. The authors do not conduct an ablation study controlling solely for tokenization.  
- **Why unresolved:** The experiment design does not decouple tokenization from other architectural innovations. DeepSeek R1's advantages (Unsloth framework, 4-bit quantisation, MoE) are implemented concurrently with its tokeniser, making it impossible to attribute performance gains uniquely to tokenization based on the presented results.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study where the same model architecture (e.g., LLaMA) is fine-tuned with different tokenisers (GPT's BPE, LLaMA's SentencePiece, DeepSeek's optimised SentencePiece) on identical medical data. Performance on metrics sensitive to terminology (e.g., rare disease recall, acronym expansion accuracy) would isolate the tokenization effect. Complementary analysis of tokenization patterns for complex medical terms across the three tokenisers would provide qualitative evidence.

**Open Question 4**  
- **Question:** How can LIME and SHAP explainability outputs be effectively integrated with clinical knowledge graphs (e.g., UMLS, SNOMED CT) to provide medically meaningful, causal explanations that support appropriate clinician reliance on AI-generated reasoning?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors acknowledge that their LIME/SHAP implementation "lacks full contextualisation for clinical utility" and state that future work should "integrate clinical knowledge graphs to highlight causal influences of specific symptoms, laboratory values, or patient history elements." They note this requires "comprehensive medical ontologies and sophisticated entity linking mechanisms."  
- **Why unresolved:** The integration requires resolving significant technical and resource challenges: (1) licensing agreements for commercial medical ontologies; (2) developing robust entity linking to map free-text model outputs to structured concepts; (3) designing visualisations that translate SHAP values into clinically interpretable causal narratives; and (4) validating with clinicians that such explanations enhance, rather than confuse, decision-making.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A user study with clinicians comparing two explanation interfaces: (a) standard LIME/SHAP highlighting input words/tokens and (b) a version where highlighted terms are mapped to UMLS concepts and presented within a knowledge graph showing causal relations. Outcomes would measure speed and accuracy of clinicians in verifying AI reasoning, trust calibration, and ability to identify potential AI errors.

**Open Question 5**  
- **Question:** What is the practical trade-off between the multi-agent system's latency (mean 36.5 seconds) and clinical utility in real-time healthcare workflows, and how must the architecture be adapted for time-critical vs. non-urgent settings?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper provides detailed latency benchmarks (Table 5) and discusses deployment in resource-constrained environments in section 5.3. It proposes strategies like "asynchronous processing for non-urgent queries" and "deploying only essential agents for time-sensitive scenarios" but admits these are untested hypotheses.  
- **Why unresolved:** The latency measurements are from controlled experiments on a fixed hardware setup (Google Colab A100). The actual impact on clinical workflow—e.g., whether a 36-second delay is acceptable for a primary care query versus an emergency department decision—depends on clinical context, user expectations, and system integration, which have not been studied. The proposed mitigation strategies (agent pruning, caching) are conceptual.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A longitudinal field study in a clinical setting (e.g., a primary care clinic) where the system is integrated into the workflow. Metrics would include: (1) task completion time with/without AI assistance; (2) clinician satisfaction and perceived utility across different query types; (3) proportion of queries where latency causes abandonment or workarounds; and (4) A/B testing of different agent configurations (full vs. streamlined pipeline) to identify optimal balances between speed and comprehensiveness for specific clinical scenarios.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction gaps:** Critical details are missing, including PubMed API query formulation/fallbacks, exact Unsloth/LoRA configurations for DeepSeek R1, the human expert validation interface, and specifics of the bias lexicon/sentiment model.
- **Generalizability:** Evaluation is restricted to the MedQuAD dataset (12 NIH domains). Performance on diverse, real-world clinical queries or evolving medical knowledge is unknown.
- **Bias/Uncertainty Validation:** The bias detection modules and uncertainty calibration lack validation on established medical bias datasets or through clinical expert assessment.

## Confidence
- **Fine-tuning performance (DeepSeek R1 ROUGE/BLEU scores):** Medium. Hyperparameters are provided, but training variance, data splits, and scores for other models are incompletely reported.
- **End-to-end multi-agent system metrics (87% accuracy, 0.80 relevance, 36.5s latency):** Medium. Pipeline architecture is described, but orchestration logic, test set details, baselines, and statistical significance are unclear.
- **Bias detection and uncertainty quantification mechanisms:** Low. Implementation details for lexicons, SHAP/LIME adaptation, and uncertainty model alignment are sparse, with no dedicated validation.

## Next Checks
1. **Full replication package release:** Obtain exact data splits, random seeds, Unsloth version, and LoRA target modules to independently reproduce DeepSeek R1 fine-tuning and verify reported ROUGE-1 of 0.536 with confidence intervals.
2. **Evidence retrieval audit:** Manually evaluate the relevance of top-3 PubMed articles retrieved for 100 random test queries. Correlate retrieval quality (precision@3) with downstream answer correctness and perplexity reduction.
