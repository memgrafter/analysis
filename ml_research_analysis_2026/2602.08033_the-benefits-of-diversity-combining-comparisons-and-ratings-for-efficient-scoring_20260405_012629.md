# The Benefits of Diversity: Combining Comparisons and Ratings for Efficient Scoring

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08033
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08033
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Combining pairwise comparisons and individual ratings via a unified probabilistic model yields significantly higher accuracy for ranking top entities than using either signal alone, especially under active learning with budget constraints.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces SCoRa, a unified probabilistic model that treats ratings as comparisons to a latent threshold within a generalized Bradley–Terry framework. This allows both ratings and direct pairwise comparisons to be incorporated into a single loss function with theoretical guarantees (convexity, monotonicity, robustness). The method is particularly effective when the accurate ranking of top‑ranked entities is critical and an active learning strategy first collects cheap ratings to guide the selection of more expensive comparisons toward high‑scoring items. Empirical results show that mixing signals provides substantial gains even with modest budgets and varying cost ratios.

## Method Summary
SCoRa learns latent scores for entities by combining two data types: pairwise comparisons (entity B preferred over C with outcome r) and individual ratings (entity A receives rating t). Ratings are modeled as comparisons against a shared threshold θ₀ using a generalized Bradley–Terry (GBT) formulation, while entity–entity comparisons use a separate GBT with its own root law. After representing entities via feature vectors **x**, the model optimizes scores via **θ** = **x**ᵀβ and θ₀ by minimizing the negative log‑posterior, which is strongly convex. An active learning protocol first estimates scores from ratings alone, then preferentially samples comparisons among items with high preliminary scores. The approach provides a convex MAP estimator with monotonicity and Lipschitz resilience guarantees, and remains accurate under modest model mismatch between assumed and true data‑generating root laws.

## Key Results
- SCoRa achieves accurate score recovery even when true and assumed root laws differ.
- When top‑entity ranking accuracy is emphasized and active learning guides comparison selection, combining ratings and comparisons outperforms using either signal alone across a wide range of budgets and cost ratios.
- Even a small proportion of the secondary signal (e.g., 10% comparisons) yields measurable improvements in the weighted correlation metric.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Ratings are effectively modeled as comparisons against a shared latent threshold, unifying two data types under a common probabilistic framework.
- **Mechanism:** The SCoRa model introduces a latent variable θ₀ representing a neutral preference threshold. Each rating outcome *t* is treated as a comparison where the rated entity's latent score θₐ is compared to θ₀, using a generalized Bradley–Terry (GBT) formulation with a rating-specific "root law" *g*. This transforms absolute ratings into relative comparisons on the same score scale as entity–entity comparisons, which use a separate root law *f*.
- **Core assumption:** The latent threshold θ₀ exists and can be learned from data; ratings and comparisons share a common underlying score space defined by β (via embeddings **x**).
- **Evidence anchors:** The model explicitly defines the rating likelihood as a GBT comparison between θₐ and θ₀ with root law *g* (section 2.2). Related work (e.g., Ye & Doermann 2014, Perez-Ortiz et al. 2019) uses similar but more restricted approximations; SCoRa generalizes these.
- **Break condition:** If the rating scale is purely ordinal with no meaningful magnitude or if θ₀ is not identifiable due to severe rating sparsity or uninformative prior.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Active learning that uses initial rating data to prioritize comparisons among high‑scoring entities yields superior top‑entity ranking accuracy compared to uniform data collection or comparison‑only strategies, especially under budget constraints.
- **Mechanism:** Budget *b* is split between comparisons (cost *c_c*) and ratings (cost *c_r*). Ratings are cheap and collected first to obtain a rough score estimate **θ̃**. Comparisons are then sampled preferentially from pairs with high **θ̃** values (e.g., probability ∝ exp(**θ̃**_a + **θ̃**_b)). This focuses costly comparisons on the top entity region where ratings alone are least informative due to the shared threshold θ₀ limiting discrimination among high scores.
- **Core assumption:** The application requires accurate ordering of top‑ranked entities more than overall scoring; the initial rating‑based estimate **θ̃** is sufficiently accurate to guide comparison sampling toward top entities.
- **Evidence anchors:** The active learning protocol is described (section 5.1) and empirical results (Figures 2–4) consistently show a performance peak at intermediate *p_c* (mix of both signals) under the top‑weighted CORREXP metric.
- **Break condition:** If the top‑entity region is not more important (equal weighting) or if active learning is not used (random comparison pairs), the benefit of mixing diminishes (section 4 baseline).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The SCoRa MAP estimator's convex negative log‑posterior loss and associated theoretical guarantees (pairwise monotonicity, Lipschitz resilience) ensure stable and interpretable learning even under model mismatch (e.g., mismatched root laws).
- **Mechanism:** The loss function *L*(β, θ₀|**D**) is proven σ⁻²_max‑strongly convex (Proposition 2.2), derived from cumulant generating functions Φ_f, Φ_g with finite exponential moments. Convexity guarantees a unique global optimum. Monotonicity (Proposition 3.4) ensures score differences increase with higher observed comparison/rating outcomes. Lipschitz resilience (Proposition 3.6) bounds estimator sensitivity to data edits by ∥**x**∥₂ and cost parameters, providing robustness to noise or adversarial edits.
- **Core assumption:** Root laws *f* and *g* have finite exponential moments (ensuring convexity); prior variances σ²_β, σ²_₀ are finite and positive.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper dedicates section 3 to proofs: monotonicity (Props 3.4, 3.5), resilience (Prop 3.6), convexity (Prop 2.2). Section 4.2 shows high correlation recovery even when true root laws (uniform) differ from inferred ones (Gaussian).
- **Break condition:** If root laws have heavy tails (infinite exponential moments) violating convexity assumptions, or if prior variances are zero, the guarantees fail. Extreme model mismatch beyond finite moment conditions could degrade performance.

## Foundational Learning

### Generalized Bradley–Terry (GBT) model
- **Why needed here:** SCoRa builds on GBT as its core comparison model; understanding GBT's parameterization via root laws Φ(·) and linear scores **x**ᵀβ is essential to grasp how comparisons generate probabilities.
- **Quick check question:** In the GBT likelihood p(r|β) ∝ f(r) exp(r **x**ᵀ_{bc}β), what role does the root law *f* play, and how does Φ(**x**ᵀ_{bc}β) normalize the distribution?

### Active learning with budget allocation
- **Why needed here:** The key benefit of mixing signals emerges only under active learning that uses cheap ratings to guide expensive comparisons toward top entities; passive collection does not show the same advantage.
- **Quick check question:** Why would preferentially sampling comparisons among entities with high preliminary scores improve top‑entity ranking accuracy more than uniform sampling?

### Convex optimization and strong convexity
- **Why needed here:** The MAP estimator's convex loss ensures unique, efficiently computable solutions and underpins monotonicity/resilience proofs; non‑convex alternatives would lack these guarantees.
- **Quick check question:** How does strong convexity of *L*(β, θ₀) (Proposition 2.2) lead to a bounded sensitivity of the MAP estimate to small changes in the dataset?

### Latent threshold models for multi‑type preferences
- **Why needed here:** SCoRa's core innovation is treating ratings as comparisons to a single latent threshold, enabling unified inference across two distinct preference elicitation methods.
- **Quick check question:** What identifiability conditions must hold for the threshold θ₀ to be learned from both ratings and comparisons?

### Cumulant generating functions in exponential families
- **Why needed here:** The loss terms involve Φ(·) functions that are cumulant generating functions of the root laws; their convexity and smoothness properties directly determine the overall loss convexity.
- **Quick check question:** How does the domain and curvature of Φ(η) relate to the feasible range of the natural parameter η in the GBT model?

### Budgeted experimental design for preference learning
- **Why needed here:** The paper demonstrates that intelligently allocating budget between two data sources can significantly improve accuracy on the metric of interest, illustrating a general principle in cost‑sensitive learning.
- **Quick check question:** How does the optimal proportion of comparisons p_c depend on the cost ratio c_c/c_r and the emphasis on top‑entity accuracy?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: Ratings (A, t) -> Latent threshold θ₀ comparison -> Score differences (x_aᵀβ - θ₀)
Comparisons (b, c, r) -> Score differences (x_bᵀβ - x_cᵀβ)
-> Loss L(β, θ₀) (sum of prior + comparison terms + rating terms)
-> Optimizer (L‑BFGS) -> MAP estimates (β̂, θ₀̂)
-> Predicted scores θ̂ = xᵀβ̂
(Optional: Active learning: θ̃ from ratings‑only -> sampling distribution exp(θ̃_a + θ̃_b) -> new comparisons)

Critical path: Input data ingestion -> embedding lookup -> loss and gradient computation -> L‑BFGS convergence -> output scores. For active learning, iterate: estimate scores from current data -> sample next comparisons -> update dataset -> re‑optimize.

Design tradeoffs:
- Cost ratio c_c/c_r and budget split p_c: Higher p_c increases top discrimination but reduces total data points. Optimal mix depends on importance of top‑entity accuracy.
- Root law choice (uniform vs. Gaussian): Uniform may be more noise‑robust per Fageot et al. (2024), but Gaussian easier to optimize; should match expected rater noise.
- Embedding structure: One‑hot encoding introduces cluster‑level sharing; richer embeddings (e.g., from metadata) can improve efficiency but increase dimensionality and risk of overfitting.
- Prior variance: Smaller σ² increases resilience (Proposition 3.6) but risks overshrinkage; should be set via cross‑validation or held‑out data.
- Active learning strategy: The exp(θ̃_a+θ̃_b) heuristic focuses on top region but may fail if initial ratings are extremely noisy; uncertainty‑based sampling could be more robust.

Failure signatures:
- Numerical overflow in Φ(η) for large |η| (especially with Cauchy‑distributed β†). Use asymptotic approximations or clipping.
- L‑BFGS fails to converge or finds poor solution: check gradient implementation against finite differences; try multiple initializations (zeros, small random).
- Active learning misguides comparison sampling because first‑phase θ̃ is highly inaccurate; diagnose by computing correlation between θ̃ and true scores before sampling.
- Weighted correlation becomes unstable when true scores have extreme values (Cauchy); consider clipping weights or using robust correlation measures.
- Mismatched root laws cause poor recovery: verify that inference root laws match true ones unless intentionally testing mismatch robustness; large discrepancy (e.g., binary vs. continuous) can degrade performance.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Convergence sanity check:** Run SCoRa on synthetic data with uniform root laws (*f*, *g* uniform), identity embedding, equal costs. Plot correlation vs. budget for p_c∈{0,0.5,1}. Expect all to converge to 1, with p_c=1 slightly faster.
2. **Model mismatch resilience:** Repeat Experiment 1 but infer with Gaussian root laws while generating with uniform. Correlation should remain high, confirming practical robustness.
3. **Active learning top‑weight benefit:** Set c_c > c_r (e.g., c_c=8, c_r=1), use heavy‑tailed true scores (Cauchy), and implement active comparison sampling based on ratings‑only θ̃. Sweep p_c and plot CORREXP. Expect peak at intermediate p_c (∼0.1–0.9), demonstrating synergy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: Beyond top‑entity prioritization and active learning, what other scenarios yield performance gains from combining ratings and comparisons?
- Basis: The paper identifies a specific setting (top‑entity metric + active learning) but doesn't explore broader conditions.
- Why unresolved: Limited experimental scope; only tested with synthetic data under fixed cost ratios and distributions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying metrics (e.g., weighted vs. unweighted), cost structures, and data distributions; theoretical analysis of when the combination is beneficial.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How robust is SCoRa to real‑world data complexities like heterogeneous user biases, miscalibrated scales, and missing data?
- Basis: Model mismatch experiments used simple uniform vs. Gaussian root laws, not real‑world noise.
- Why unresolved: Experiments were on synthetic data; real applications have nuanced biases and sparsity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluation on diverse real‑world datasets (e.g., product reviews, peer grading) with known ground truth or controlled biases; stress tests on adversarial edits.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can optimal active learning strategies for SCoRa be derived that jointly optimize budget allocation and query selection across ratings and comparisons?
- Basis: The paper uses a heuristic (ratings first, then comparisons via active learning) without theoretical guarantees.
- Why unresolved: Active learning strategy was ad hoc; no comparison to other strategies or theoretical optimality.
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical formulation of the active learning problem as a sequential decision process; empirical comparisons of various strategies (e.g., Thompson sampling, uncertainty sampling) under different budgets.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees require root laws with finite exponential moments; heavy‑tailed noise may invalidate them.
- Active learning's benefit depends on accurate initial rating‑based estimates; extremely noisy or sparse ratings could misguide comparison sampling.
- The single latent threshold θ₀ assumes all ratings share the same scale; heterogeneous rater biases or context‑dependent scales might break this assumption.
- Embeddings beyond identity/one‑hot are not explored; performance with high‑dimensional or learned features remains unclear.
- The weighted correlation metric strongly emphasizes top entities; the observed mixing benefit may not generalize to uniform weighting or other importance schemes.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (unified threshold model): **High** – Directly derived, with formal definition and empirical support under controlled conditions.
- Mechanism 2 (active learning mixing): **Medium** – Empirical gains shown under specific cost ratios and top‑weighted metrics, but advantage tightly coupled to active learning protocol and rating quality, limiting generality.
- Mechanism 3 (theoretical guarantees): **High** – Rigorous proofs provided and validated in experiments, though they rely on key assumptions (finite moments, proper priors).
- Overall robustness claim: **Medium** – Model mismatch experiments are encouraging but limited to simple uniform vs. Gaussian mismatch; real‑world complexities may challenge assumptions.

## Next Checks
1. **Real‑world robustness test:** Apply SCoRa to diverse datasets (e.g., product reviews, peer grading) with natural noise and missing data, checking whether empirical performance aligns with theoretical guarantees when exponential moment assumptions are likely only approximately satisfied.
2. **Active learning sensitivity ablation:** Compare the proposed exp(θ̃_a+θ̃_b) sampling against random and uncertainty‑based samplings across varying levels of rating noise and sparsity, quantifying how fragile the mixing benefit is to initial estimate quality.
3. **Heterogeneous threshold extension:** Generalize the model to support multiple or hierarchical thresholds (e.g., rater‑specific offsets) and evaluate on simulations with known rater biases to determine whether this increases recovery accuracy while preserving convexity.
