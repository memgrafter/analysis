# Enhancing Geometric Perception in VLMs via Translator-Guided Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22703
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22703
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** GeoDPO improves VLMs' geometric perception (+26.5% in-domain, +8.0% out-of-domain) and downstream reasoning (+39.0%) by using a translator to provide fine-grained rewards.

## Executive Summary
Vision-language models (VLMs) struggle with geometric perception—misidentifying basic diagram elements like points, lines, and circles—which cascades into reasoning failures. Existing benchmarks conflate perception and reasoning, making it hard to diagnose the core issue. This paper introduces GeoPerceive, a novel benchmark built from a canonical domain-specific language (GeoDSL) and an automated data generation pipeline, isolating pure perception tasks. To improve perception, the authors propose GeoDPO, a translator-guided reinforcement learning framework that trains an NL-to-DSL translator on synthetic data, then uses it to compute fine-grained, permutation-invariant DSL-level rewards for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). This avoids pitfalls of supervised fine-tuning (SFT), which suffers from permutation ambiguities and poor generalization. Experiments show GeoDPO yields large gains across multiple VLMs, significantly boosting both perception accuracy and downstream geometric reasoning.

## Method Summary
GeoPerceive is constructed by generating geometric diagrams via a constraint solver and producing corresponding natural language (NL) descriptions using VLMs. Diagrams are represented in GeoDSL, a canonical language capturing primitive elements and their relationships. Perception is evaluated by comparing the model's generated DSL program against the ground truth using a fuzzy matcher. GeoDPO trains a translator to convert NL inputs into DSL programs. During RL fine-tuning, the VLM generates candidate NL responses; the translator converts them to DSL, which is scored against the ground truth DSL to produce a fine-grained reward. This reward signal feeds into DPO, optimizing the VLM's policy directly on NL outputs while avoiding permutation issues and maintaining alignment with the model's pretrained distribution. The synthetic data pipeline and translator ensure scalable, automated reward computation without human annotation.

## Key Results
- GeoDPO yields a **+26.5%** absolute gain on in-domain geometric perception (GeoPerceive test set).
- It achieves a **+8.0%** improvement on out-of-domain perception (human-curated diagrams).
- Downstream reasoning tasks (geometry problem solving) see a **+39.0%** boost.
- The translator reaches **over 90% F1** on element detection in DSL generation.
- Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) offers marginal gains and can degrade out-of-domain performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
GeoDPO succeeds by addressing two critical failure modes of standard fine-tuning. First, it uses a **canonical DSL** to eliminate permutation ambiguities: sequence-level rewards (e.g., exact match) would penalize functionally equivalent but syntactically different programs; fine-grained DSL scoring ensures consistent credit assignment. Second, it keeps training **within the VLM's NL distribution**—the VLM produces NL text, the translator maps it to DSL, and rewards are computed in DSL space. This avoids distribution shift that occurs when training directly on DSL programs (as in SFT), which are out-of-distribution for a pretrained NL model. The preference-based DPO objective further stabilizes learning by contrasting chosen (better-scoring) and rejected (worse-scoring) responses, rather than maximizing token-level likelihood. Together, these design choices yield robust gains that generalize beyond the synthetic training distribution.

## Foundational Learning
- **Geometric perception**: Recognizing and describing diagram elements (points, lines, circles) and their relations from images. *Why needed*: Isolating perception from reasoning is essential to diagnose VLM failures. *Quick check*: Can a model correctly identify all circles and tangential relationships in a simple diagram?
- **Domain-specific language (DSL)**: A formal, canonical language for representing geometric structures. *Why needed*: Provides unambiguous ground truth for evaluation and stable reward computation. *Quick check*: Does the DSL uniquely represent a triangle with vertices A, B, C without ordering ambiguity?
- **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**: A preference-based RL algorithm that optimizes a policy directly from paired chosen/rejected examples. *Why needed*: Avoids unstable RL sampling and enables use of fine-grained rewards. *Quick check*: Would DPO converge if rewards were noisy but pairwise preferences were consistent?
- **Translator-guided reward**: Using a separate model to convert VLM outputs into a structured representation for scoring. *Why needed*: Enables automatic, dense reward without human labeling. *Quick check*: Does translator error correlate with reward noise and hurt policy learning?
- **Permutation invariance**: Property that different orderings of equivalent programs should be treated as equally correct. *Why needed*: Prevents penalizing valid but differently ordered DSL programs. *Quick check*: If a model outputs "point A, point B" vs "point B, point A", should both be correct?
- **Synthetic data generation**: Automatically creating training data via programmatic means. *Why needed*: Scales to large datasets with perfect ground truth. *Quick check*: Do synthetic diagrams cover the diversity of real human-drawn figures?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Synthetic data generator -> GeoPerceive benchmark (diagrams + NL) -> VLM (policy) -> NL responses -> Translator (NL->DSL) -> DSL programs -> Reward scorer (fuzzy match vs GT DSL) -> DPO loss -> Updated VLM.

**Critical path:**
1. Input diagram and NL question to VLM.
2. VLM generates NL answer.
3. Translator converts NL answer to GeoDSL.
4. DSL is scored against ground truth DSL.
5. Reward guides DPO update of VLM.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Synthetic vs real data*: Synthetic data yields perfect labels and control but may lack natural diversity.
- *Fine-grained vs sequence-level reward*: Fine-grained avoids permutation issues but requires a reliable translator.
- *KL regularization in DPO*: Prevents excessive deviation from pretrained distribution but may limit adaptation.

**Failure signatures:**
- Translator errors (low F1) produce noisy rewards, harming learning.
- Out-of-distribution diagrams (e.g., cluttered, non-canonical) cause both translator and VLM to degrade.
- Over-regularization (high KL penalty) caps performance gains.

**First experiments:**
1. Replicate GeoDPO on a different VLM (e.g., LLaVA) and report perception gains with confidence intervals.
2. Ablate the translator: replace with an oracle (perfect translation) to measure upper bound, and with a random translator to test robustness.
3. Test on a human-annotated geometry diagram set (e.g., from textbooks) to evaluate real-world generalization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can GeoDSL be extended to represent and evaluate quantitative geometric constraints (e.g., equal lengths, specific angles, area measures) while preserving its canonical semantics and enabling precise perception scoring?
- **Open Question 2**: What specific mechanisms in GeoDPO—such as fine-grained DSL-level rewards, preference learning, or KL regularization—contribute to its superior generalization under distribution shift compared to supervised fine-tuning, and how do these interact with the canonical design of GeoDSL?
- **Open Question 3**: How does the accuracy and coverage of the synthetic NL descriptions used to train the translator affect the reliability of the reward signal in GeoDPO, and what methods can mitigate reward noise from imperfect translation?
- **Open Question 4**: To what extent does the synthetic data generation pipeline for GeoPerceive capture the diversity of natural geometric diagrams and human descriptions, and how does this limit generalization to real-world or human-annotated geometry problems?

## Limitations
- GeoPerceive's synthetic diagrams may not capture real-world complexity, risking overestimated gains.
- Translator robustness to diverse, noisy diagrams is untested; its >90% F1 likely holds on in-distribution data only.
- Out-of-domain improvements are modest (+8.0%), suggesting limited transfer beyond benchmark distributions.
- The claim that SFT degrades OOD performance needs validation across more models and datasets.

## Confidence
- VLMs exhibit poor geometric perception: **Medium** (benchmark shows failures but coverage may be incomplete).
- GeoPerceive successfully isolates perception from reasoning: **Medium** (DSL reduces ambiguity but some reasoning may still be involved).
- GeoDPO yields significant perception and reasoning gains: **Medium** (results are strong but evaluated on a limited set of VLMs).
- Translator achieves >90% F1 on element detection: **High** (assuming standard held-out synthetic test).
- SFT provides marginal gains and can degrade out-of-domain performance: **Medium** (needs verification across more models/datasets).

## Next Checks
1. **Reproducibility study**: Re-implement GeoDPO and replicate results on GeoPerceive with at least three additional VLMs (different architectures), including statistical significance tests and confidence intervals.
2. **Real-world generalization**: Collect a dataset of authentic geometric diagrams (e.g., from textbooks, online resources) and evaluate both the translator and GeoDPO-trained models to assess robustness beyond synthetic data.
3. **Ablation and sensitivity**: Remove translator-guided rewards (replace with coarse heuristic) to measure contribution; additionally, simulate translator errors (e.g., by injecting noise) to test GeoDPO’s sensitivity to reward quality.
