# Nonparametric Bayesian Optimization for General Rewards

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07411  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07411  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** ∞-GP achieves sublinear cumulative regret for Lipschitz objectives and a broad class of noise, whereas GP-based methods incur linear regret under these settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the infinite Gaussian process (∞-GP), a Bayesian nonparametric surrogate for Bayesian optimization that models general reward distributions beyond classical Gaussian process assumptions. ∞-GP places a prior over reward distributions via a spatial Dirichlet process mixture of GPs, capturing model uncertainty and adapting complexity to the data. Combined with Thompson Sampling and a truncated Gibbs sampler, ∞-GP provides theoretical no‑regret guarantees (o(n) cumulative regret) for Lipschitz objectives and a broad class of noise, while standard GP methods fail to achieve sublinear regret in these scenarios. Empirical evaluations on synthetic non‑stationary and heavy‑tailed functions, portfolio optimization, and hyperparameter tuning confirm state‑of‑the‑art performance.

## Method Summary
The ∞-GP models the reward as a linear term plus a latent function drawn from an infinite mixture of GPs governed by a spatial Dirichlet process. At each iteration, a truncated Gibbs sampler approximates the posterior over latent surfaces, assignments, and hyperparameters. Thompson Sampling then draws a new surface from the posterior predictive mixture and selects the next point by maximizing the sum of the linear term and the sampled surface. The truncation level grows logarithmically, keeping the number of surfaces O(log n) in expectation and the computational cost near that of a single GP. The theoretical analysis links the regret of Thompson Sampling to the total variation distance between the surrogate’s predictive distribution and the true reward distribution; under mild conditions this distance vanishes, yielding sublinear regret.

## Key Results
- **Theoretical:** ∞-GP‑TS achieves o(n) cumulative regret for Lipschitz objectives and a broad class of noise, while GP‑based methods only yield O(n) regret.
- **Computational:** With a truncated Gibbs sampler, ∞‑GP scales with O(n³ log n) time and O(log n) expected memory, comparable to a single GP.
- **Empirical:** ∞‑GP‑TS attains sublinear cumulative regret on non‑stationary and heavy‑tailed synthetic functions, portfolio optimization, and hyperparameter tuning, outperforming GP‑TS, GP‑UCB, and GP‑KG.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** ∞-GP captures "model uncertainty" by placing a prior over the space of reward distributions via a spatial Dirichlet process (DP) mixture of Gaussian processes (GPs), enabling it to represent a broader class of reward models than a single GP.
- **Mechanism:** The ∞-GP constructs a stochastic process ξ where at each input x, the distribution Gₓ is drawn from a spatial DP. This SDP prior is an infinite mixture of GP surfaces ξ⁽ˡ⁾ with stick‑breaking weights wₗ. This structure allows the model to assign observations to different latent GP surfaces, adapting its complexity to the data. Unlike a classical GP, which assumes all data lie on one sample path, this mixture can represent heteroscedastic, multi‑modal, or non‑stationary reward structures.
- **Core assumption:** The true reward distribution can be approximated by a mixture of GPs drawn from the SDP prior. The SDP prior must be sufficiently rich to include the true distribution in its support (Kullback‑Leibler support).
- **Break condition:** If the true reward distribution lies outside the KL support of the SDP prior (violates Assumption 1), the posterior will not contract, and regret may not be sublinear.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A truncated Gibbs sampler makes the computationally infinite ∞‑GP model scalable, increasing complexity only from O(n³) to O(n³ log n) and memory to O(log n).
- **Mechanism:** The posterior inference over latent surfaces ξ⁽ˡ⁾, assignments zᵢ, and hyperparameters is done via Gibbs sampling. To avoid O(n⁴) cost from per‑observation updates, the infinite mixture is truncated to Lₙ surfaces. Crucially, all surfaces share the same kernel matrix inverse, so kriging cost remains O(n³) per surface, but only Lₙ surfaces are tracked. Proposition 2 shows E[Kₙ] ~ ν log(n/ν), so Lₙ can be set to (ν+κ) log n + 1 (Corollary 1) to keep truncation regret sublinear.
- **Core assumption:** The number of realized surfaces Kₙ grows slowly (logarithmically) with n. The truncation level Lₙ is chosen such that the omitted surface mass has exponentially small probability.
- **Break condition:** If the true process requires more than Lₙ surfaces with significant probability, the truncation introduces a constant TVD gap, leading to linear regret.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A new Thompson Sampling (TS) regret analysis framework for general rewards links instantaneous regret to the total variation distance (TVD) between the surrogate's posterior predictive distribution and the true reward distribution.
- **Mechanism:** Standard TS regret bounds rely on posterior variance or information gain, which require RKHS/Gaussian noise assumptions. This work shows that for any surrogate model, the regret of TS is bounded by µₘₐₓ · δ_TV(fₙ(·|X), f⁎(·|X)), where fₙ is the surrogate's joint predictive distribution over the decision space. Since ∞-GP achieves δ_TV → 0 under mild conditions (Theorem 1), this yields o(n) cumulative regret. The proof uses a discretization‑then‑decoupling argument to handle the growing decision space.
- **Core assumption:** The TVD between the surrogate's predictive distribution and the true reward distribution can be controlled and vanishes as n → ∞. This requires the ∞-GP posterior to contract around f⁎(·|x) for inputs visited by the policy (Assumption 1, Lipschitz µ⁎, tail conditions).
- **Break condition:** If the surrogate's posterior fails to contract to the true distribution (e.g., due to model misspecification beyond the SDP prior's capacity, or insufficient exploration), the TVD remains bounded away from zero, leading to linear regret.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Surrogate Model (∞-GP) -> Inference Engine (Truncated Gibbs) -> Acquisition Policy (TS) -> Data Update  

**Critical path:**  
Data Hₙ → Gibbs Sampler (Step 1) → Sample ˆΘ, ˆξ_table, ˆz → Sample new surface (Step 2) → Form sample path ˆξ⁽ᶻⁿ⁺¹⁾ → TS selection (Step 3) → argmaxₓ xᵀ ˆβ + ˆξ⁽ᶻⁿ⁺¹⁾(x) → Query xₙ₊₁ → Update Hₙ₊₁  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Truncation level Lₙ vs. Memory/Regret: Larger Lₙ improves model fidelity but increases memory and Gibbs cost. Use Lₙ = (ν+κ) log n + 1 for asymptotic optimality.  
- Gibbs iterations B vs. Wall‑clock time: More iterations improve posterior sample quality but increase cost. Paper uses B=500; fewer may be used with careful initialization.  
- Isotropic vs. Anisotropic kernel: Isotropic uses discrete grid sampling for ϕ (simple but coarse); anisotropic uses MLE on posterior surfaces (more accurate but requires gradient optimization).

**Failure signatures:**  
- Linear cumulative regret: ∞-GP posterior not contracting (violates Assumption 1) or TS not exploring adequately (ζ decay too fast). Check Kₙ growth via wₗ; if Kₙ grows linearly, model is not adapting.  
- Exploding memory/time: Number of non‑negligible surfaces Kₙ growing super‑logarithmically. ν may be too large or data truly requires many surfaces.  
- Gibbs sampler not mixing: Monitor autocorrelation of sampled ˆΘ. Poor mixing degrades TS sample quality. Consider more Gibbs iterations or better initialization.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Noiseless stationary test:** Run on Branin‑Hoo. ∞-GP‑TS should match GP‑TS performance and Kₙ should stabilize to 1.  
2. **Heavy‑tailed noise test:** Use Weibull(shape=0.5)‑noisy Rosenbrock. ∞-GP‑TS should show sublinear regret while GP‑EI/GP‑KG show linear. Verify Kₙ increases slowly.  
3. **Non‑stationary test:** Use trigonometric‑exponential non‑stationary Ackley. ∞-GP‑TS should show sublinear regret. Observe wₗ evolution; a small number of surfaces (~2‑3) should capture non‑stationarity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability via large‑scale GP approximations:** Can sparse variational methods be integrated into ∞‑GP to achieve sub‑cubic scaling while preserving no‑regret guarantees in high‑dimensional spaces?
2. **Alternative acquisition functions:** Can UCB or Expected Improvement be used with ∞‑GP to retain sublinear regret under the general reward assumptions (Lipschitz objective, arbitrary noise)?
3. **Non‑stationary base kernels:** Does ∞‑GP retain its guarantees when the base measure G₀ is a non‑stationary GP (e.g., input‑dependent length‑scales), and can this improve performance on highly non‑stationary rewards?
4. **Approximate inference acceleration:** Can variational inference or parallel MCMC speed up ∞‑GP inference while maintaining the quality of posterior samples needed for no‑regret TS?

## Limitations
- **Theoretical assumptions:** The sublinear regret guarantee depends on unverified conditions (KL support of SDP prior, Lipschitz µ⁎). If the true reward lies outside the prior’s support or µ⁎ is discontinuous, o(n) may not hold.
- **Computational scaling:** The O(n³ log n) complexity may be optimistic if the realized number of surfaces Kₙ grows super‑logarithmically in practice.
- **Baseline comparisons:** Empirical results rely on unspecified configurations for GP baselines (e.g., hyperparameter sampling, kernel warping), making performance attribution difficult.

## Confidence
- **High:** ∞-GP model definition, truncated Gibbs algorithm, and computational complexity analysis are clearly specified and rigorous.
- **Medium:** The TVD‑based regret analysis framework is solid, but the contraction rate itself (Assumption 1) is not proven for ∞‑GP under general rewards; empirical advantage over GP baselines is convincing but depends on baseline implementations.
- **Low:** The claim that ∞‑GP achieves sublinear regret for *any* Lipschitz objective with “a broad class of noise” is conditional on unverified assumptions. Practical scalability may degrade if Kₙ grows faster than logarithmic.

## Next Checks
1. **Test theoretical assumptions:** For the heavy‑tailed and non‑stationary synthetic functions, verify whether the true reward distribution satisfies the KL support condition (Assumption 1) and Lipschitz continuity. Compute TVD between true reward and ∞‑GP posterior predictive at visited points to see if it decays with n.
2. **Measure truncation impact:** Run ∞‑GP‑TS with varying truncation levels (0.5×, 1×, 2× the suggested (ν+κ) log n schedule) on the non‑stationary Ackley function. Plot realized Kₙ and cumulative regret to confirm O(log n) growth and identify the point of diminishing returns.
3. **Baseline replication audit:** Re‑implement GP baselines (GP‑TS, GP‑UCB, GP‑KG) using a consistent fully Bayesian treatment (same priors, MCMC iterations, ζ‑greedy schedule) and rerun portfolio optimization and hyperparameter tuning experiments. This isolates the gain to the ∞-GP model rather than implementation differences.
