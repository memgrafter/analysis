# Collaborative Temporal Feature Generation via Critic-Free Reinforcement Learning for Cross-User Sensor-Based Activity Recognition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16043  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16043  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CTFG achieves cross‑user accuracy of 88.53% on DSADS and 75.22% on PAMAP2, with lower inter‑task variance and faster convergence than critic‑based methods.

## Executive Summary
Cross‑user variability in wearable sensor‑based human activity recognition (HAR) severely hampers model transfer. The proposed CTFG framework treats feature extraction as a collaborative sequential generation process. A Transformer‑based autoregressive policy constructs feature tokens step‑by‑step, conditioned on a temporal summary from an encoder. Optimization leverages Group‑Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a novel critic‑free RL algorithm that normalizes advantages within groups of generated sequences from the same input. A tri‑objective reward guides learning toward class discrimination, cross‑user invariance, and temporal fidelity. Evaluated on DSADS and PAMAP2, CTFG attains state‑of‑the‑art cross‑user accuracy, reduced variance, and robust performance even with long token sequences where prior RL methods collapse.

## Method Summary
CTFG first encodes a raw sensor window with a Transformer encoder to produce a context vector \(h_i\). An autoregressive Transformer decoder then generates a sequence of \(s\) feature tokens \(z_{i,1:s}\), each sampled from a Gaussian distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i,j},\operatorname{diag}(\exp(\log\sigma_{i,j})))\) predicted using causal self‑attention over previous tokens and cross‑attention to \(h_i\). For each input, \(G\) independent sequences are produced. The reward engine computes a tri‑objective reward: \(R_{\text{cls}}\) maximizes inter‑class centroid separation, \(R_{\text{inv}}\) minimizes intra‑user scatter and inter‑user distances within classes, and \(R_{\text{tmp}}\) enforces reconstructibility of \(h_i\) from the token sequence. Group‑relative advantages are obtained by normalizing each sequence’s reward by the mean and standard deviation of its group. The policy loss follows a clipped surrogate objective with a KL penalty, updated via Adam. After training, a deterministic pass (using means only) yields features for a lightweight logistic regression classifier. This end‑to‑end RL approach avoids value‑function bias and directly optimizes the distributional quality of the generated features.

## Key Results
- **SOTA cross‑user accuracy:** 88.53% (DSADS) and 75.22% (PAMAP2), surpassing prior domain generalization and RL methods.
- **Stability and scalability:** Inter‑task variance remains low and performance degrades gracefully with longer token sequences; PPO exhibits collapse beyond 10 tokens.
- **Faster convergence:** GRPO converges in fewer epochs than PPO and maintains stable optimization curves across different user splits.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Autoregressive token‑by‑token feature construction separates user‑invariant temporal dynamics from user‑specific artifacts.
- **Mechanism:** The Transformer decoder generates tokens incrementally. Causal self‑attention creates an implicit curriculum: early tokens capture coarse periodicity (stable across users), while later tokens refine with phase transitions (sensitive to user physiology). This compositional modeling aligns with the multi‑scale structure of human motion.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and Section 3.3.2 describe the coarse‑to‑fine process; Fig. 2 (paper) visualizes token evolution. Static activities (e.g., sitting) lead to low F1, confirming reliance on temporal dynamics.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Group‑relative advantage estimation eliminates value‑function bias that destabilizes cross‑user RL optimization.
- **Mechanism:** For each input, \(G\) sequences are sampled. Advantages are computed as \((R^{(g)} - \bar{R}_G)/(\hat{\sigma}_G + \epsilon)\), normalizing rewards within the group. This removes dependence on absolute reward scale and mitigates distribution shift across leave‑one‑user‑out splits.
- **Evidence:** Abstract highlights self‑calibrating signals; Section 3.4.2 defines Eq. (8). Section 4.4 shows PPO’s inter‑task STD surges (0.212) while GRPO narrows monotonically.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A tri‑objective reward jointly enforces class separation, user‑agnostic clustering, and preservation of temporal content.
- **Mechanism:** \(R = w_{\text{cls}}R_{\text{cls}} + w_{\text{inv}}R_{\text{inv}} + w_{\text{tmp}}R_{\text{tmp}}\). The temporal fidelity term \(R_{\text{tmp}}\) prevents collapse to degenerate features by penalizing loss of information relative to the encoder summary.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and Section 3.5.3 discuss necessity of \(R_{\text{tmp}}\). Ablations imply that removing any component harms accuracy; high‑F1 activities (e.g., cycling) align all three objectives.

## Foundational Learning
- **Domain Generalization (DG)**  
  Why needed: Cross‑user HAR is a DG problem where target users are unseen; physiological and device shifts create out‑of‑distribution test conditions.  
  Quick check: *How does cross‑user DG differ from standard DG in image classification?*  
  Answer: Shifts involve human kinematics and sensor placements rather than scene semantics; temporal dynamics are central.

- **Reinforcement Learning for Policy Optimization**  
  Why needed: Feature quality is a batch‑level property; RL’s delayed reward enables holistic optimization where supervised per‑token losses are ill‑defined.  
  Quick check: *Why isn’t supervised learning (e.g., contrastive loss) used directly?*  
  Answer: Non‑differentiable metrics (e.g., rank‑based separability) cannot back‑propagated per token; RL uses evaluative feedback over full sequences.

- **Transformer Autoregressive Decoding**  
  Why needed: Captures long‑range dependencies and builds representations compositionally, matching the multi‑scale temporal structure of human motion.  
  Quick check: *Why not use a non‑autoregressive generator?*  
  Answer: Autoregressive conditioning creates a coarse‑to‑fine curriculum and provides a natural sequential decision process for RL.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Input → Encoder (TransformerEncoder + sinusoidal PE) → \(h_i\) → Decoder (TransformerDecoder, causal) → (µ, logσ) → Sample token \(z_{i,j}\) → (repeat for s tokens) → Complete token sequence \(z_i\) → Reward Engine (R_cls, R_inv, R_tmp) → Group‑relative Advantages → GRPO update (policy + projection matrix).  
Inference: Encoder → Decoder (deterministic µ) → Flatten \(z_i\) → Logistic Regression classifier.

**Critical path**
1. Encode raw sensor window to context \(h_i\).
2. For each of \(G\) sequences, autoregressively decode \(s\) tokens with sampling.
3. Compute tri‑objective reward for each sequence over the batch.
4. Normalize rewards within each group to obtain advantages.
5. Perform policy gradient update (clipped surrogate + KL penalty) and update projection matrix \(W_{\text{proj}}\).
6. After training, freeze policy and generate single deterministic sequence per input; train logistic regression on these features.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Autoregressive vs. parallel generation: Autoregressive enables coarse‑to‑fine learning but is slower; parallel generation would lose sequential credit assignment.
- GRPO (requires \(G\) samples) vs. PPO: GRPO avoids value‑network bias in heterogeneous settings but increases memory/time; PPO is cheaper per step but prone to critic miscalibration.
- Token count \(s\): Larger \(s\) increases capacity but lengthens the horizon, worsening credit assignment; GRPO scales better to longer sequences (empirically stable beyond \(s=15\)).

**Failure signatures**
- **PPO collapse:** For \(s \ge 15\), PPO accuracy drops sharply (e.g., DSADS: 80.37%→64.46%) with exploding inter‑task STD, indicating value‑function miscalibration.
- **Static activity underperformance:** F1 < 0.70 for sitting, standing, elevator activities due to lack of temporal dynamics that \(R_{\text{tmp}}\) expects.
- **High variance across leave‑one‑group‑out splits:** If inter‑transfer STD > 5% after training, GRPO’s normalization may be ineffective; check group size \(G\).

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Baseline comparison:** Train CTFG vs. empirical risk minimization (ERM) on DSADS; expect >10% gain, confirming benefit of sequential generation over single‑pass feature extraction.
2. **Optimizer ablation:** Replace GRPO with PPO (all else equal) on PAMAP2; anticipate higher inter‑transfer STD and lower accuracy on hardest split (AB→C), demonstrating critic‑free stability.
3. **Token count sweep:** Vary \(s\) from 5 to 20; CTFG’s accuracy should remain stable (∼87–88% on DSADS) while PPO degrades past \(s=10\), verifying scalability of group‑relative advantages.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can the group size \(G\) in GRPO be adaptively determined based on source user diversity to maintain stable performance across heterogeneous populations?  
  *Basis:* Future work mentions adaptive group sizing; current evaluation uses fixed \(G\) and does not study interaction with user diversity.  
  *Needed evidence:* Controlled experiments on synthetic datasets with varying diversity, measuring accuracy vs. \(G\); proposal and validation of an adaptive algorithm (e.g., based on intra‑group reward variance).

- **Open Question 2:** How can the cross‑user invariance reward be modulated per class to resolve discrimination‑invariance tension for kinematically similar activity pairs (e.g., Walking vs. Nordic Walking)?  
  *Basis:* Future work suggests class‑conditional invariance modulation; current static weighting cannot tailor trade‑offs per class.  
  *Needed evidence:* Method to learn class‑specific invariance weights (e.g., meta‑learning); evaluation on datasets with known similar pairs; ablation of global vs. class‑conditional weightings.

- **Open Question 3:** Can the computational overhead of GRPO be reduced without sacrificing stability, perhaps via importance sampling or amortized advantage estimation?  
  *Basis:* Authors propose computational reduction; GRPO’s need to generate \(G\) sequences per input is expensive, especially for long horizons.  
  *Needed evidence:* Efficient variants (e.g., single‑sequence variance estimation); runtime, memory, and accuracy comparison against standard GRPO on DSADS/PAMAP2.

- **Open Question 4:** How does CTFG scale to larger demographically diverse populations and multi‑modal sensor configurations beyond inertial measurements?  
  *Basis:* Future work includes extension to larger populations and multi‑modal setups; current evaluation limited to 6–8 users with homogeneous IMU.  
  *Needed evidence:* Training/testing on a larger dataset (>50 users) covering varied ages, body types, sensor placements; inclusion of multi‑modal data; analysis of invariance across demographic subgroups.

## Limitations
- Missing critical hyperparameters (group size \(G\), token count \(s\), KL coefficient) and precise mathematical definitions of reward components (e.g., \(R_{\text{inv}}\)) block faithful reproduction.
- Architecture ambiguities (feed‑forward dimensions, exact layer counts) and potential reward scale imbalances risk implementation errors.
- Performance degrades on static activities (sitting, standing, elevator), revealing a limitation of the temporal fidelity constraint when genuine dynamics are absent.
