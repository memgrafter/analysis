# Is the Trigger Essential? A Feature-Based Triggerless Backdoor Attack in Vertical Federated Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20593
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20593
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** A triggerless backdoor attack in VFL achieves 2–50× higher attack success rate (e.g., 85.08% mASR on CIFAR-10) than trigger-based baselines while minimally impacting main task accuracy, and remains effective under nine defense strategies.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the necessity of triggers for backdoor attacks in vertical federated learning (VFL). It introduces a novel triggerless attack where a malicious passive party directly manipulates its embeddings during inference to induce misclassification to a target label. The attack proceeds in three phases: (1) inferring labels of all training samples by clustering recorded embeddings with a small auxiliary dataset, (2) generating malicious embeddings by amplifying the target class's cluster center and adding Gaussian perturbation, and (3) executing the backdoor by replacing benign embeddings with malicious ones at inference time. Evaluated across five datasets and multi-party VFL settings, the attack substantially outperforms existing trigger-based methods in success rate while evading both training- and inference-phase defenses. The work demonstrates that the embedding space in VFL alone provides a sufficient attack surface, rendering traditional triggers obsolete.

## Method Summary
The attack requires a malicious passive party that follows the VFL protocol honestly during training but becomes malicious at inference. During training, the attacker records the embeddings output by its local bottom model for all samples. After training, it uses a small auxiliary labeled dataset to compute initial class anchors, then clusters the recorded embeddings to assign a pseudo-label to every training sample. From these pseudo-labels, it calculates the density center (mean embedding) for each class. For the target label, it creates a set of malicious embeddings by scaling the target center by an amplification factor η and adding Gaussian noise. At inference, for each incoming sample, the attacker computes its embedding, infers its pseudo-label via nearest-centroid comparison, and—depending on the attack variant (clean-label or dirty-label)—replaces the embedding with a malicious one before sending it to the active party. No training data is poisoned, and no trigger pattern is implanted.

## Key Results
- The attack achieves a mean Attack Success Rate (mASR) of 85.08% on CIFAR-10, compared to less than 10% for three trigger-based baselines (VILLAIN, BadVFL, BASL).
- It scales effectively to 32 passive parties, maintaining high ASR through an amplification factor that counteracts dilution from benign parties.
- The attack remains effective under nine defense strategies, including norm-based clipping, anomaly detection, and training-phase defenses, while causing negligible degradation to main task accuracy (MTA drop < 1% on most datasets).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The attacker can infer the labels of all training samples by clustering the embeddings recorded during VFL training, using only a small auxiliary labeled dataset.
- **Mechanism:** The attacker records embeddings from its bottom model during training. Post-training, it uses auxiliary data to compute mean embeddings per class, then clusters all recorded embeddings (e.g., via K-means) with these class anchors as guidance, assigning a pseudo-label to each sample. The core assumption is that embeddings from a well-trained VFL bottom model naturally cluster by true label.
- **Break condition:** Poorly trained bottom models (low main task accuracy) produce embeddings that do not separate cleanly by class, leading to erroneous label assignments and subsequent attack failure.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The attacker generates malicious embeddings that reliably induce the target label by scaling the target cluster center and adding Gaussian perturbation.
- **Mechanism:** After label inference, the attacker computes the mean embedding `emb_t` for the target pseudo-label. To ensure the malicious signal dominates when multiple benign embeddings are aggregated, it applies an amplification factor η > 1. It then adds a Gaussian perturbation δ to each amplified embedding to create a diverse set `]Emb`, evading simple norm-based detection while remaining within the target class's decision region.
- **Break condition:** η too small causes the malicious embedding to be diluted by benign ones; η too large or perturbation σ too high pushes embeddings outside the target class region, causing misclassification to wrong labels or detection.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The backdoor is executed during inference by directly replacing the attacker’s benign embeddings with malicious ones, without any training-phase poisoning.
- **Mechanism:** At inference, for each sample, the attacker first infers its pseudo-label using the same clustering-based nearest-centroid method. In a clean-label attack, it replaces the embedding only if the inferred label matches a source label; in a dirty-label attack, it replaces unconditionally. The modified embedding is sent to the active party, whose top model then predicts the target label.
- **Break condition:** If the active party's top model is robust to embedding perturbations (e.g., via normalization, dropout, or inference-time validation) or if malicious embeddings fall outside the target class's region, the attack fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Vertical Federated Learning (VFL)**
  - *Why needed here:* VFL's split architecture (passive parties hold disjoint features, active party holds labels) defines the attack surface: a passive party can manipulate its embeddings before they are sent to the active party.
  - *Quick check question:* In VFL, what data does each party hold, and how do they exchange information during training and inference?

- **Concept: Backdoor Attacks**
  - *Why needed here:* Contrasts traditional trigger-based backdoors (poisoning during training) with the proposed triggerless approach, clarifying the attack goal (targeted misclassification) and the shift from training-time to inference-time manipulation.
  - *Quick check question:* What distinguishes a targeted backdoor attack from an untargeted one, and how are triggers typically used?

- **Concept: Embedding Space and Clustering**
  - *Why needed here:* The attack relies on the property that embeddings from a well-trained classifier cluster by class; this underlies both label inference and poison generation.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might the intermediate representations (embeddings) of a classification model group samples of the same class together?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Label Inference Module -> Poison Generation Module -> Backdoor Execution Module
  Training Phase (Embedding Recording) -> Inference Phase (Label Inference & Poison Execution)

- **Critical path:**
  1.  During VFL training, the malicious passive party records embeddings for all samples (preferably from the final epoch).
  2.  Post-training, using an auxiliary dataset, compute mean embeddings per class to initialize clustering.
  3.  Cluster all recorded embeddings to assign a pseudo-label to every training sample.
  4.  Compute the centroid `emb_t` for the target pseudo-label.
  5.  Generate malicious embeddings: `demb = η * emb_t`; for each, `gemb = demb + δ` where `δ ~ N(0, σ²)`.
  6.  At inference, for each new sample, compute its embedding and infer its pseudo-label by nearest-centroid comparison.
  7.  If clean-label and pseudo-label equals source label, or dirty-label, replace the embedding with a random `gemb`.
  8.  Send the modified embedding to the active party; the top model predicts the target label.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Amplification factor η:* Higher η increases influence but risks detection and over-amplification. Recommended: η slightly above the ratio of benign to malicious parties.
  - *Perturbation:* Adding zero-mean Gaussian noise with σ ≈ 0.5 × std(embeddings) adds diversity while staying near the target cluster. Larger σ degrades cluster coherence.
  - *Auxiliary data:* More data improves label inference, but even one sample per class suffices due to strong bottom model performance.
  - *Security assumption:* Attacker is honest-but-curious during training (no protocol violation) and only malicious during inference. Stealthiness depends on the bottom model's quality, which the attacker shares interest in.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - *Label inference failure:* Embeddings not separable (low main task accuracy) → poor label assignment → low attack success.
  - *Amplification insufficient:* In multi-party VFL, malicious embedding drowned out → low mASR.
  - *Perturbation too large:* Malicious embeddings deviate from target cluster → misclassification to wrong class or detection.
  - *Inference-time defense:* Active party monitors embedding norms (e.g., L2) and clips/removes outliers; effective on simple datasets but less so on complex ones.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  *Overall performance comparison:* Evaluate mASR/rASR against VILLAIN, BadVFL, and BASL on five datasets in multi-party VFL. Results show 2–50× higher ASR with minimal impact on main task accuracy.
  2.  *Impact of number of passive parties:* Vary passive parties from 1 to 32. Attack maintains high ASR due to amplification factor, demonstrating scalability.
  3.  *Impact of auxiliary data:* Vary auxiliary data ratio (0.017%–7.5% of training set). Attack remains effective even with one sample per class, showing robustness to limited auxiliary data.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the sparsity of embeddings in VFL affect the performance of triggerless backdoor attacks?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state in Section V.D: "we aim to investigate how the sparsity of embeddings affect attack performance."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper evaluates attack success under various conditions but does not analyze embedding sparsity, which could impact clustering accuracy and poison generation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What universal patterns exist in how specific embedding dimensions contribute to label representation in VFL models?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors plan to "analyze which dimensions primarily contribute to an embedding’s ability to represent labels and strive to uncover universal patterns."
- **Why unresolved:** The current attack uses overall density centers without assessing per-dimension importance, potentially overlooking optimization opportunities for both attacks and defenses.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can inference-phase defenses be designed to reliably detect triggerless backdoor attacks in VFL, especially for complex datasets where magnitude-based detection fails?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note their proposed inference-phase defense (based on L2 norm) is ineffective on complex datasets and call for "developing defense strategies capable of effectively countering backdoor attacks during the inference phase."
- **Why unresolved:** Existing defenses target the training phase or use simple statistical checks; malicious embeddings in complex datasets have diverse distributions that evade such detection.

## Limitations
- **Unspecified clustering details:** The exact clustering algorithm parameters (initialization, convergence) are not provided, which may affect label inference reproducibility.
- **Hyperparameter guidance only:** Optimal values for key hyperparameters (η, σ) per dataset are not precisely reported; only qualitative guidelines are given.
- **Feature split strategy:** The precise column-wise feature partitioning for multi-party experiments (e.g., number of features per passive party) is not specified, making exact replication of the multi-party setup difficult.

## Confidence
- **Overall triggerless attack achieving high ASR with minimal accuracy loss:** High (comprehensive experiments across five datasets and nine defenses).
- **Label inference module's reliability:** Medium (depends on bottom model quality and underspecified clustering choices).
- **Poison generation module's efficacy:** Medium (optimal η and σ likely dataset-sensitive; not precisely reported).
- **Scalability to many parties:** High (experiments up to 32 parties confirm robustness due to amplification).
- **Evasion of inference-phase defenses:** Medium (effective on simple datasets but noted failure on complex ones; defense development is an open challenge).
