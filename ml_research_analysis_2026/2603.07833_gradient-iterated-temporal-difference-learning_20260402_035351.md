# Gradient Iterated Temporal-Difference Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07833
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07833
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Gradient Iterated TD (Gi-TD) unifies gradient TD stability with iterated speed, matching or exceeding DQN/SAC/CQL on Atari/MuJoCo and outperforming on offline and high update-to-data regimes.

## Executive Summary
Semi-gradient temporal difference methods (e.g., Q‑learning, DQN) are efficient but can diverge, while gradient TD methods (e.g., TDRC) are stable but slower. Iterated TD (i‑TD) accelerates learning by updating a sequence of Q‑functions in parallel, yet remains semi‑gradient and unstable due to moving targets. This paper introduces Gradient Iterated TD (Gi‑TD), which modifies i‑TD by computing gradients through the stochastic targets, directly minimizing the sum of Bellman errors across the sequence. Empirically, Gi‑TD converges on divergence‑prone benchmarks (Star MP) where i‑TD fails and learns faster than TDRC on small MDPs. Across 10 Atari games and 6 MuJoCo tasks, Gi‑TD matches or exceeds semi‑gradient baselines (DQN, SAC, CQL) in normalized IQM AUC—the first gradient TD method to be competitive on Atari. It shows particular gains in offline RL (Gi‑CQL doubles CQL’s AUC) and under high update‑to‑data ratios (e.g., Gi‑DQN outperforms by 130% at UTD=4). Ablations confirm the importance of weight decay (β=1) and linear heads for discrete tasks, with Gi‑TD less sensitive to sequence length K than i‑TD.

## Method Summary
Gi‑TD builds on iterated TD, which maintains a sequence of Q‑functions (Q₁,…,Q_K) updated in parallel using bootstrapped targets. Unlike i‑TD that treats targets as constants, Gi‑TD computes gradients through the stochastic targets, thereby backpropagating the Bellman error across the entire sequence. The loss is L = Σ_{k=1}^K (r + γ·max_a' Q_{k-1}(s',a') - Q_k(s,a))^2, and the gradient of L is computed with respect to all parameters. This approach yields a true gradient of a well‑defined objective (the cumulative Bellman error) while preserving the acceleration benefits of iterated updates. The implementation uses weight decay (β=1) and a linear output head for discrete action spaces, as identified by ablations. For continuous control, the method can be combined with policy‑gradient frameworks (e.g., SAC) or used in offline settings (Gi‑CQL).

## Key Results
- Gi‑TD converges on Star MP (a divergence‑prone benchmark) where i‑TD fails, and learns faster than TDRC on Hall and Triangle MDPs.
- On 10 Atari games and 6 MuJoCo tasks, Gi‑TD matches or exceeds DQN, SAC, and CQL in normalized IQM AUC, marking the first competitive gradient TD result on Atari.
- Significant gains in offline RL (Gi‑CQL doubles CQL AUC) and high update‑to‑data ratios (Gi‑DQN +130% at UTD=4); ablations highlight weight decay (β=1) and linear heads as critical for discrete tasks, with Gi‑TD robust to sequence length K.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Gi‑TD stabilizes i‑TD by treating the bootstrapped targets as differentiable rather than fixed. This allows the gradient to account for how the targets change with the parameters, turning the sum of Bellman errors into a proper loss whose gradient points toward reducing the overall error. The iterated structure provides a multi‑step correction that accelerates convergence compared to single‑step gradient TD. Weight decay (β=1) acts as a damping mechanism to prevent divergence, and linear heads for discrete tasks ease gradient flow, as confirmed by ablations. Consequently, Gi‑TD inherits the convergence guarantees of gradient methods and the speed of iterative updates, achieving stability on divergence‑prone benchmarks and competitive performance on Atari.

## Foundational Learning
- **Temporal Difference (TD) Learning:** Bootstraps from later estimates to learn value functions; essential for model‑free reinforcement learning. *Quick check:* Can TD(0) learn the value of a state given its successor’s value?
- **Semi‑gradient TD:** Applies gradient descent to the TD error while ignoring the dependency of the target on the parameters; simpler but can diverge. *Quick check:* Does Q‑learning converge with linear function approximation off‑policy? (Generally no.)
- **Gradient TD (e.g., TDRC):** Computes the gradient of a loss such as the mean squared Bellman error; guarantees stability under certain conditions. *Quick check:* Does GTD2 converge on off‑policy tasks? (Yes.)
- **Iterated TD (i‑TD):** Maintains a sequence of Q‑functions updated in parallel to reduce lag; speeds up learning but remains semi‑gradient. *Quick check:* Does i‑TD improve sample efficiency over single‑step TD? (Yes, but may still diverge.)
- **Bellman Error:** Difference between current Q and the expected return from the Bellman equation; zero at optimum. *Quick check:* Is the Bellman error minimized at the true optimal Q‑function? (Yes.)
- **Weight Decay (L2 regularization):** Penalizes large weights; stabilizes Gi‑TD training and acts as implicit damping. *Quick check:* Does adding weight decay improve convergence on Star MP? (Yes, per ablations.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
State s → Q‑network (linear output head for discrete tasks) → Q(s,a). For K iterations, targets y_k = r + γ·max_a' Q_{k‑1}(s',a') are computed, and the loss L = Σ_{k=1}^K (y_k − Q_k(s,a))² is backpropagated through all Q_k networks (or a shared network with multiple forward passes). Optimizer updates all parameters with gradient descent, optionally with weight decay (β=1).

**Critical path:**  
1. Forward pass to compute Q(s,a).  
2. Iterate K times: compute next‑state Q via current Q, form target y_k, and accumulate TD error.  
3. Backpropagate total loss through the entire computational graph (including target computations).  
4. Apply optimizer step (e.g., SGD/Adam) with weight decay.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Sequence length K: larger K better approximates the fixed point but increases compute/memory; Gi‑TD is less sensitive to K than i‑TD.  
- Weight decay (β=1): crucial for stability on divergent MDPs but may bias solution; ablations show necessity.  
- Linear heads for discrete tasks: ease gradient flow at cost of representational capacity; required for stable Gi‑TD on Atari.  
- Full gradient vs. semi‑gradient: full gradient improves stability at the expense of additional computational overhead.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Divergence on Star MP if weight decay omitted.  
- Performance drop with deep non‑linear heads on discrete tasks.  
- Instability if K is too small (though less sensitive than i‑TD).  
- Potential issues when applying to deeper networks without architectural adjustments.

**First experiments:**  
1. Verify convergence on Star MP (or Baird’s counterexample) to ensure stability.  
2. Compare sample efficiency against TDRC on small MDPs (Hall, Triangle) to confirm acceleration.  
3. Ablate weight decay and linear head on Atari‑like discrete tasks to quantify their impact.

## Limitations
- Evaluation limited to 10 Atari and 6 MuJoCo tasks; broader generalization unknown.
- Reliance on weight decay (β=1) and linear heads for discrete tasks may limit applicability to deeper networks.
- Offline and high‑UTD gains demonstrated only at specific settings (UTD=4, single offline dataset) and lack computational overhead analysis versus semi‑gradient methods.
