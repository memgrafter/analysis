# High-Probability Bounds for SGD under the Polyak-Lojasiewicz Condition with Markovian Noise
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14514
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14514
- **Reference count:** 35
- **Primary result:** First uniform-in-time high-probability O(1/k) convergence bounds for SGD under the PL condition with Markovian and martingale-difference noise; high-probability rate depends on \(t_{\text{mix}}^2\), expected rate on \(t_{\text{mix}}\).

## Executive Summary
This paper derives uniform-in-time high-probability convergence guarantees for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) when the objective satisfies the Polyak-Lojasiewicz (PL) condition and the gradient noise has both Markovian and martingale-difference components. The key technical innovations are a decomposition of Markovian noise via the Poisson equation into a martingale difference and a bounded residual, and a novel probabilistic induction argument that circumvents the need for almost-sure bounds on iterates. The resulting high-probability bound is \(O(t_{\text{mix}}^2 \log(k/\delta)/(\mu^2 k))\) and the expected bound is \(O(t_{\text{mix}}/(\mu^2 k))\), both matching the optimal \(1/k\) rate. The framework is applied to decentralized linear regression, privacy-amplified subsampling, and online system identification, demonstrating its broad applicability.

## Method Summary
The analysis begins by decomposing the Markovian noise \(g(x_k, Z_k) - \nabla f(x_k)\) using the Poisson equation: define \(V(x,z) = \mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty (g(x,Z_\ell)-\nabla f(x)) \mid Z_0=z\big]\), which satisfies \(g(x,z)-\nabla f(x) = V(x,z) - \int V(x,z') p(dz'|z)\). This splits the noise into a martingale difference \(\tilde{M}_{k+1} = V(x_k,Z_{k+1}) - \int V(x_k,z') p(dz'|Z_k)\) and a residual \(d_k = V(x_k,Z_{k+1}) - V(x_k,Z_k)\). The SGD recursion becomes \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha_k (\nabla f(x_k) + \tilde{M}_{k+1} + M_{k+1} - d_k)\), where \(M_k\) is an existing martingale difference. Under geometric ergodicity of the Markov chain and an ABC noise bound \(\|G_k\|^2 \leq A\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + B(f(x_k)-f^*) + C\), the terms involving \(V\) and \(d_k\) are bounded. To obtain uniform-in-time high-probability bounds, a "good event" \(E_k\) is defined where the suboptimality \(\Delta_k\) is controlled by a deterministic sequence \(\Lambda(k,\delta)\). On \(E_k\), the PL condition and ABC bound yield almost-sure bounds on the martingale increments, enabling Azuma-Hoeffding on a constructed martingale. The stepsize \(\alpha_k = a/(k+K_0)\) is chosen with \(a \geq 2/\mu\) and \(K_0\) large enough to satisfy various lower bounds involving constants, mixing time, and confidence \(\delta\).

## Key Results
- Uniform-in-time high-probability bound: With probability at least \(1-\delta\), for all \(k\), \(\Delta_k \leq O\!\big( t_{\text{mix}}^2 \log(k/\delta) / (\mu^2 k) \big)\).
- Expected suboptimality bound: \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta_k] \leq O\!\big( t_{\text{mix}} / (\mu^2 k) \big)\).
- The rates match the optimal \(1/k\) rate and extend to decentralized linear regression, privacy-amplified subsampling, and online system identification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper's success hinges on three interconnected mechanisms:

**Mechanism 1: Poisson decomposition of Markovian noise.** For each state \(z\) and iterate \(x\), the function \(V(x,z) = \mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty (g(x,Z_\ell)-\nabla f(x)) \mid Z_0=z\big]\) solves the Poisson equation and yields \(g(x,z)-\nabla f(x) = V(x,z) - \int V(x,z') p(dz'|z)\). Substituting gives \(g(x_k,Z_k)-\nabla f(x_k) = \tilde{M}_{k+1} - d_k\), with \(\tilde{M}_{k+1}\) a martingale difference and \(d_k\) a bounded residual. This holds under geometric ergodicity and boundedness of \(g\), isolating Markovian dependence into a term amenable to martingale concentration.

**Mechanism 2: Probabilistic induction for uniform high-probability bounds.** Direct Azuma-Hoeffding requires a priori almost-sure bounds on martingale increments, which are unavailable because iterate-dependent bounds rely on the event being proved. The authors define a "good event" \(E_k\) where \(\Delta_k \leq \Lambda(k,\delta)\). On \(E_k\), PL and ABC imply \(\nabla f(x_k)\) and noise magnitudes are bounded, yielding almost-sure increments. A martingale \(M_{\ell+1}\) (involving noise and \(\mathbf{1}_{E_\ell}\)) is bounded on \(E_\ell\). Azuma-Hoeffding on \(\sum M_{\ell+1}\) and a union bound over \(k\) give the high-probability guarantee. Lemma 4.3 reduces the union to a sum of tail probabilities.

**Mechanism 3: Handling growing noise via the ABC condition.** The ABC bound \(\|G_k\|^2 \leq A\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + B(f(x_k)-f^*) + C\) accommodates noise whose variance scales with gradient norm and suboptimality. Combined with PL, it allows recursive control of \(\Delta_k\) even without uniform noise bounds. The residual \(d_k\) is also bounded using ABC-derived bounds on \(V\). This flexibility covers applications like subsampling for privacy amplification where mini-batch variance grows with local loss.

## Foundational Learning
Several core concepts underpin the analysis:

- **Polyak-Lojasiewicz (PL) Condition:** Ensures \(\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \geq 2\mu(f(x)-f^*)\). Crucial for the \(O(1/k)\) rate; without PL, only sublinear rates are typical.  
  *Quick check:* If \(f\) is \(\mu\)-PL and \(L\)-smooth, deterministic GD achieves linear convergence; if \(\mu = L\), the condition becomes strong convexity.

- **Martingale Difference Sequences & Azuma-Hoeffding:** After Poisson decomposition, noise terms are martingale differences. Azuma-Hoeffding gives tail bounds for cumulative sums, but requires (conditional) almost-sure bounds on increments.  
  *Quick check:* Azuma-Hoeffding: For martingale differences \(X_i\) with \(|X_i| \leq c_i\), \(\mathbb{P}(\sum X_i \geq t) \leq \exp(-t^2/(2\sum c_i^2))\). If replaced by conditional variance bound \(\mathbb{E}[X_i^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{i-1}] \leq c_i^2\), one can use martingale Bernstein or Fuk-Nagaev inequalities.

- **Geometric Ergodicity & Mixing Time:** The Markov chain \(\{Z_k\}\) is assumed geometrically ergodic: \(\|p^{(k)}(\cdot|z) - \pi\|_{\text{TV}} \leq 2^{-\lfloor k/t_{\text{mix}}\rfloor}\). This ensures the Poisson solution \(V(x,z)\) is bounded by \(O(t_{\text{mix}})\) and its Lipschitz constant by \(O(t_{\text{mix}} L_g)\).  
  *Quick check:* \(t_{\text{mix}}\) measures how fast the chain forgets its past; if mixing is only polynomial (\(O(k^{-\alpha})\)), the bounds may degrade.

## Architecture Onboarding
Implementing the theoretical framework requires several components:

**Component map:**  
Poisson Solver -> computes/approximates \(V(x,z)\) and its bounds -> Residual Calculator -> computes \(d_k\) -> Event Monitor -> tracks \(\Delta_k\) vs \(\Lambda(k,\delta)\) -> Step Size Controller -> sets \(\alpha_k = a/(k+K_0)\).  
The critical path is: \(x_{k+1} \leftarrow x_k - \alpha_k (\nabla f(x_k) + M_{k+1} + \tilde{M}_{k+1} - d_k)\). The Poisson decomposition must be computed before the gradient step because \(V\) depends on \(x_k\).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- The high-probability bound's \(t_{\text{mix}}^2\) factor vs. expected bound's \(t_{\text{mix}}\): for slow-mixing chains, high-probability guarantees become much looser.
- The ABC constants \(A, B, C\): tighter \(A\) (noise scaling with \(\|\nabla f\|^2\)) is crucial; if \(A\) is large, \(K_0\) must be larger, worsening initial transient.
- The offset \(K_0\) must dominate \(\log(1/\delta)\) and problem constants; for small \(\delta\), \(K_0\) becomes large, leading to slow early convergence. A two-phase schedule (large \(K_0\) initially, then smaller) might be considered.

**Failure signatures:**
- If \(\|V(x_k, Z_{k+1})\|\) grows with \(k\), likely violates ABC or mixing assumptions.
- If the good event \(E_k\) fails early (\(\Delta_k\) exceeds \(\Lambda(k,\delta)\)), either \(\Delta_0\) was underestimated, constants \(\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2\) miscalculated, or noise magnitude exceeds Azuma bound.
- If the residual term \(\sum \alpha_\ell \zeta_{\ell+1,k-1}\langle\nabla f(x_\ell), d_\ell\rangle\) does not decay as \(1/k\), the bounds \(D_1, D_2\) from the proof may be violated.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Poisson decomposition validation:** For a simple finite-state Markov chain (e.g., random walk on 3 nodes) and quadratic \(f\), compute \(V(x,z)\) analytically vs. Monte Carlo. Verify \(g(x,z)-\nabla f(x) \approx V(x,z) - \int V(x,z') p(dz'|z)\).
2. **Event \(E_k\) calibration:** Run SGD on a PL problem (e.g., linear regression with a token algorithm) with known \(\mu, L\). Track \(\Delta_k\) vs \(\Lambda(k,\delta)\) for various \(\delta\). Adjust \(\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2\) if \(\mathbb{P}(\cup \bar{E}_k) > \delta\).
3. **\(t_{\text{mix}}\) scaling test:** Artificially slow mixing (e.g., by making the Markov chain lazy). Verify empirical \(\Delta_k\) scales with \(t_{\text{mix}}^2\) in high-probability regime and \(t_{\text{mix}}\) in expectation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can the high-probability bound's dependence on \(t_{\text{mix}}\) be improved from quadratic to linear?
- Do the results extend to objectives satisfying the weaker Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) condition?
- Can similar uniform-in-time high-probability bounds be derived for asynchronous SGD in decentralized networks?
- Is the assumption of geometric ergodicity and a compact state space necessary, or can the analysis handle slower (e.g., polynomial) mixing or non-compact spaces?
- Can the probabilistic induction technique be applied to other stochastic algorithms (e.g., variance-reduced or accelerated methods) under Markovian noise?

## Limitations
- The high-probability bound's \(t_{\text{mix}}^2\) dependence is a significant penalty for slow-mixing chains, potentially limiting practical use.
- The analysis requires the PL condition, which is stronger than many common assumptions (e.g., convexity only) and may not hold for complex models like deep neural networks.
- Geometric ergodicity and compact state space assumptions are non-trivial to verify for many Markov chains (e.g., from MCMC or decentralized protocols), and chains with slower mixing are not addressed.
- The stepsize offset \(K_0\) must be chosen large enough (often \(O(\log(1/\delta))\)) leading to a slow initial transient and possibly loose early-time bounds.
- The constants in the bounds (\(\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2\), residual bounds) involve multiple assumptions and may be pessimistic in practice.
