# The Power of Decaying Steps: Enhancing Attack Stability and Transferability for Sign-based Optimizers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19096
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19096
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces MDCS, a monotonically decreasing coordinate-wise step-size scheme for sign-based attacks, yielding provable O(1/√T) convergence and large transferability gains (e.g., 99.1% success on ResNet50→Inception-v3) while preserving imperceptible perturbations.

## Executive Summary
Sign-based adversarial attacks like I-FGSM and MI-FGSM often suffer from instability and non-convergence as iteration count increases, limiting their effectiveness in black‑box transfer scenarios. This work recasts these attacks as coordinate‑wise gradient descent, identifying the constant per‑coordinate step‑size as the culprit: near optima, gradient magnitudes shrink, causing step‑sizes to blow up and produce oscillations. The proposed remedy, Monotonically Decreasing Coordinate‑wise Step‑sizes (MDCS), enforces a simple decay rule that automatically scales down steps when momentum is small. Applied to MI‑FGSM (MDCS‑MI), the method attains an optimal O(1/√T) convergence rate for concave objectives. Extensive experiments across image classification and cross‑modal retrieval demonstrate that MDCS dramatically stabilizes attacks and boosts transferability, with MDCS‑OPS achieving 99.1% success on ResNet50→Inception‑v3 and MDCS‑SAAET improving text‑image retrieval R@1 from 54.23% to 60.25% on CLIP‑ViT→ALBEF. The module is model‑agnostic and enhances state‑of‑the‑art attacks with negligible overhead.

## Method Summary
MDCS modifies sign‑based optimizers by introducing a per‑coordinate step‑size variable \(d_t\) that is initialized to 1 and updated each iteration as \(d_{t,i} = \min(1/|m_{t,i}|, d_{t-1,i})\), where \(m_t\) is the momentum accumulator. The adversarial update becomes \(x_{t+1} = \text{Clip}_\epsilon\left( (x_t + \alpha (d_t \odot m_t)) \oslash \sqrt{d_t} \right)\), with \(\odot\) and \(\oslash\) denoting elementwise multiplication and division. This ensures that each coordinate’s effective step‑size never increases, stabilizing the trajectory. The MDCS module can be inserted into existing sign‑based attacks (MI‑FGSM, MEF, OPS) without changing their outer logic, making it a plug‑and‑play improvement. Hyperparameters include total steps \(T\), base step‑size \(\alpha = \gamma \epsilon / T\) (with scaling factor \(\gamma\) typically 2–4), momentum decay \(\beta\) and \(\lambda\) (often 0.999), and a tiny constant to avoid division by zero.

## Key Results
- MDCS‑OPS achieves **99.1%** attack success rate on ResNet50→Inception‑v3 transfer, surpassing prior art.
- MDCS‑SAAET improves text‑image retrieval R@1 from **54.23% to 60.25%** on CLIP‑ViT→ALBEF, demonstrating cross‑modal transferability gains.
- MDCS variants consistently enhance state‑of‑the‑art sign‑based attacks (MI‑FGSM, MEF, OPS) across diverse target models while maintaining imperceptible perturbations (e.g., LPIPS < 0.03).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper’s core insights are threefold:

1. **Constant step‑size causes instability.** Reformulating I‑FGSM/MI‑FGSM as coordinate‑wise gradient descent reveals that the effective per‑coordinate step‑size equals \(\alpha / |\partial J/\partial x_i|\). Near a local optimum, gradient magnitudes shrink, making the step‑size blow up, which leads to overshooting and oscillations. This explains why attack success often degrades with more iterations.

2. **MDCS enforces monotonic decay to stabilize.** The MDCS rule \(d_{t,i} = \min(1/|m_{t,i}|, d_{t-1,i})\) guarantees non‑increasing per‑coordinate step‑sizes. When momentum magnitude is small, \(1/|m_{t,i}|\) becomes large, but the `min` prevents \(d\) from growing; conversely, if momentum is large, \(d\) shrinks further. This automatic scaling prevents excessive steps. The update \(x_{t+1} = \text{Clip}_\epsilon^{D_t^{-1}}(x_t + \alpha D_t m_t)\) (with \(D_t = \text{diag}(d_t)\)) respects the decay.

3. **Optimal convergence rate for concave objectives.** Under assumptions of bounded gradient, bounded domain, and concave loss, MDCS‑MI with appropriate step‑size and momentum schedules (\(\alpha_t = \gamma/\sqrt{t}\), \(\beta_t = \beta \lambda^{t-1}\)) achieves an \(O(1/\sqrt{T})\) convergence rate for the averaged iterate—matching the optimal bound for convex first‑order methods. The proof combines projection properties, concavity (dual gap), and telescoping sums.

Break conditions: Mechanism 1 may not trigger if gradients remain bounded away from zero (e.g., piecewise‑linear objectives). Mechanism 2 could stall progress if some coordinates require larger steps to escape plateaus. Mechanism 3’s guarantee relies on concavity, which is not generally true for deep‑network losses, so the theoretical rate does not directly apply there, though empirical gains are observed.

## Foundational Learning
- **Coordinate‑wise gradient descent:** In sign‑based attacks, the update is effectively a coordinate‑wise descent where each pixel’s step‑size is \(\alpha / |\partial J/\partial x_i|\). *Why needed:* It makes explicit the anomaly that step‑sizes diverge as gradients vanish. *Quick check:* In I‑FGSM, what is the effective step‑size per pixel when writing the update component‑wise?
- **Monotonically decreasing step‑size (MDCS):** A scheme where per‑coordinate step‑sizes are initialized to 1 and updated via \(d_{t,i} = \min(1/|m_{t,i}|, d_{t-1,i})\). *Why needed:* It prevents the blow‑up identified above, stabilizing the attack. *Quick check:* How does the rule guarantee non‑increasing step‑sizes, and why is that helpful near optima?
- **Convergence rate O(1/√T):** The optimal rate for stochastic gradient descent on convex/concave functions. *Why needed:* The paper claims MDCS‑MI attains this rate, situating it theoretically. *Quick check:* Why is O(1/√T) the best possible rate for convex first‑order methods, and what role does iterate averaging play?

## Architecture Onboarding
The MDCS module augments a sign‑based optimizer (e.g., MI‑FGSM) as follows:

**Component map:**  
Clean input -> Gradient computation -> Momentum update (m) -> **MDCS step** (update d = min(1/|m|, d_prev); dir = d ⊙ m) -> Scaled update (u = x + α·dir) -> Rescale (w = u / √d) -> Projection (clip w to ε‑ball) -> adversarial output.

**Critical path:**  
1. Compute gradient ∇J(x_adv).  
2. Update momentum: m ← β·m + ∇J/∥∇J∥₁.  
3. For each coordinate i: d_i ← min(1/(|m_i|+ε_f), d_i_prev).  
4. Form direction: dir = d ⊙ m.  
5. Raw update: u = x_adv + α·dir.  
6. Rescale: w = u / √d.  
7. Project: x_adv ← clip_{[x_clean‑ε, x_clean+ε]}(w).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Scaling factor γ (via α = γε/T) balances transferability and imperceptibility; larger γ increases success but also distortion.  
- Momentum hyperparameters β and decay λ (typically 0.999) affect stability.  
- MDCS adds O(d) memory and computation, negligible in practice.  
- The method is model‑agnostic but may require mild task‑specific tuning (e.g., γ for different budgets).

**Failure signatures:**  
- d_i increases across iterations → MDCS logic inverted or d not initialized to 1.  
- Attack success degrades with more T → likely incorrect projection (e.g., clipping before rescaling).  
- Perturbation exceeds ε → clipping bounds or rescaling order wrong.  
- Numerical NaNs → missing epsilon in division 1/|m|.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Non‑convex convergence:** Does MDCS converge on typical non‑convex deep network losses? The theoretical O(1/√T) guarantee assumes concavity, which rarely holds in practice; empirical gains suggest it may, but a theoretical explanation is lacking.
- **Extension to other norms:** Can MDCS be effectively adapted for L₂‑ or L₁‑bounded attacks? The current mechanism is tailored to L∞ constraints; different constraint geometries may require modifications.
- **Hyperparameter automation:** Can the MDCS hyperparameters (β, λ, γ) be tuned automatically, e.g., via adaptive schedules, rather than manual grid search? This would improve usability across tasks.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** The optimal scaling factor γ and MEF/OPS‑specific parameters are not fully disclosed; grid‑search ranges are given but final values per experiment are unknown, hindering exact replication.
- **Theoretical‑empirical gap:** The optimal convergence rate is proven only for concave objectives; real‑world deep‑network losses are non‑convex, so the theoretical guarantee does not directly justify the observed transferability improvements.
- **Implementation fidelity:** The precise projection order (rescaling before clipping) is critical; a common mistake leads to failure. The paper’s description is clear but non‑trivial to implement correctly, raising reproduction risk.
