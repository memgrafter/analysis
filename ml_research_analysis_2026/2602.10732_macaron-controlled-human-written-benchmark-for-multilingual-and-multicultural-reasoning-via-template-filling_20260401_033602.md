# Macaron: Controlled, Human-Written Benchmark for Multilingual and Multicultural Reasoning via Template-Filling
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10732
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10732
- Reference count: 32
- Primary result: A template-first benchmark achieves near parity between English and local languages for reasoning-oriented models (avg gap -1.1 pp), while open-weight models degrade more (avg gap -7.5 pp) and approach random on True/False tasks.

## Executive Summary
Macaron introduces a controlled benchmark to evaluate multilingual and multicultural reasoning in large language models (LLMs). It addresses limitations of existing benchmarks by using a template-first approach: 100 language-agnostic templates spanning 7 reasoning types and 22 cultural aspects are instantiated by native annotators with culturally grounded content, producing aligned English and local-language multiple-choice (MC) questions. Each MC yields four True/False (T/F) variants, enabling paired evaluation. The dataset comprises 11,862 instances across 20 countries, 20 languages, and 10 scripts. Zero-shot evaluation of 21 LLMs shows reasoning-oriented models lead (79.3% avg) with minimal cross-lingual gaps, while open-weight models lag (55.2% avg) and exhibit shallow response tendencies on T/F tasks.

## Method Summary
Macaron’s core method factorizes reasoning and culture via predefined templates. Native annotators instantiate each template with locally salient content for both English and a primary local language, ensuring scenario alignment. From each base MC question, four aligned T/F statements are derived (True/False × English/local), yielding six evaluation instances per cultural scenario. An LLM-assisted proofreading pass (GPT-5.2-chat) corrects only English spelling/grammar to reduce template-instantiation noise without altering cultural content or cross-lingual alignment. Final validation checks ensure label balance, template coverage, and cultural plausibility. This design enables controlled comparison of language, cultural grounding, and reasoning as separate factors.

## Key Results
- Reasoning-oriented models (e.g., o1, Claude) achieve 79.3% average accuracy with near parity between English and local languages (gap -1.1 percentage points).
- Open-weight models perform worse overall (55.2%) and show larger drops in local languages (avg gap -7.5 pp), with many nearing random chance (~50%) on T/F tasks.
- Mathematical and counting templates are consistently the hardest across models.
- Paired T/F accuracy (both T and F correct per scenario) is substantially lower than per-question T/F accuracy, indicating shallow response tendencies rather than genuine fact verification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Template Factorization
- **Claim:** A template-first design factorizes reasoning type and cultural aspect, enabling controlled cross-language/culture comparisons without confounding.
- **Mechanism:** Predefine 100 language-agnostic templates with explicit slots (e.g., `[COUNTRY]`) and metadata tags for 7 reasoning types and 22 cultural aspects. Native annotators instantiate each with locally salient content, preserving the reasoning skeleton.
- **Core assumption:** The same abstract template maintains reasoning path and difficulty across diverse cultural instantiations; slot constraints and logical structure are portable.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] factorization claim; [section 3.3] template design details; [corpus] weak support from works like MultiMUC (used for translation, not factorial separation).
- **Break condition:** Templates become non-portable if reasoning relies on culturally exclusive knowledge or if slot constraints are violated in some contexts.

### Mechanism 2: Paired True/F Variants
- **Claim:** Systematic derivation of aligned T/F variants from MCQs tests genuine fact verification rather than shallow pattern matching.
- **Mechanism:** For each base MCQ (correct option C), generate two T/F statements: one instantiating the template with C (True) and one with a distractor (False). This yields four T/F items per MCQ (T/F × English/local).
- **Core assumption:** T/F statements are reasoning-equivalent to the parent MCQ; success requires verifying the same cultural premise.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.5] derivation process; [section 5] paired accuracy analysis showing shallow reasoning; [corpus] limited parallel (e.g., MultiNRC lacks paired derivation).
- **Break condition:** Translation of T/F statements introduces semantic shifts or the binary format alters reasoning load (e.g., negation salience), breaking alignment.

### Mechanism 3: English-only LLM Proofreading
- **Claim:** LLM-assisted proofreading restricted to English-only grammatical corrections preserves cross-lingual alignment while eliminating template-instantiation noise in English texts.
- **Mechanism:** After annotation, run GPT-5.2-chat on all English fields (MCQ text, options, English T/F) to correct only spelling/grammar errors; local-language texts remain untouched to avoid cultural distortion.
- **Core assumption:** Surface-level inconsistencies in English are non-reasoning artifacts that unfairly penalize models; fixing them improves reliability without affecting cultural validity.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.6] proofreading rationale; [appendix C.5] prompt forbidding rephrasing; [corpus] no direct precedent for this quality-control step.
- **Break condition:** The proofreading model over-corrects (e.g., changes cultural terms or logical structure), raising the bar for English items or disrupting alignment with local versions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Controlled benchmark design (factorial variables).
  - Why needed: To isolate language, cultural grounding, and reasoning as distinct factors, avoiding confounds in translation-parallel or culture-first benchmarks.
  - Quick check: If a model performs worse on local language than English in Macaron, what are possible causes? How would you distinguish between language brittleness, lack of cultural knowledge, and reasoning errors?

- **Concept:** Template-based data generation.
  - Why needed: To enable scalable, consistent creation of culturally diverse items while maintaining reasoning-type control.
  - Quick check: How does using the same template across 20 cultures help control for reasoning difficulty? What risks arise if an annotator misinterprets a template’s slot constraints?

- **Concept:** Cross-lingual evaluation pitfalls.
  - Why needed: To recognize why translated benchmarks (e.g., XNLI) may retain English-centric assumptions and why culture-first benchmarks may lack reasoning-type consistency.
  - Quick check: What is a concrete example of an English-centric scenario that might survive translation and fail to probe local cultural reasoning?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Template repository -> Annotation pipeline (English instantiation -> local translation) -> Quality control (human cultural review + LLM proofreading on English) -> Dataset aggregator (merges bilingual MC and T/F items, computes splits) -> Evaluation harness (zero-shot prompt formats, paired accuracy calculator).

**Critical path:** Template design -> annotator recruitment & onboarding -> English instantiation -> local translation -> T/F derivation -> LLM proofreading (English only) -> human review -> aggregation -> validation checks (label balance, template coverage). Any break in instantiation quality (e.g., culturally implausible distractors) propagates to final data.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Template reuse vs. cultural nuance: Reusing templates ensures comparability but may force unnatural instantiations in some cultures; annotators can flag non-applicable templates, reducing coverage.
- Automatic proofreading vs. over-correction: Fixing English grammar improves consistency but risks subtly altering meaning if the proofreading prompt is violated; local-language texts are kept pristine to avoid cultural distortion.
- Single primary language per country vs. diversity: One local language simplifies scaling but erases within-country linguistic diversity and dialect variation.

**Failure signatures:**
1. High random-chance accuracy on T/F (~50%) for open-weight models, indicating guessing rather than verification.
2. Large ∆MC (English–local) gaps for certain reasoning types (e.g., Mathematical) and languages (low-resource like Amharic, Yoruba).
3. Low paired T/F accuracy relative to per-question T/F, suggesting models answer each binary question independently without consistent fact verification.
4. Template-specific difficulty extremes: “How many…” templates consistently among hardest, indicating failure on enumeration + constraint satisfaction.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Template portability assay:** For a subset of templates, collect multiple independent instantiations per culture and measure variance in model accuracy across instantiations. High variance suggests non-portability.
2. **Paired T/F vs. MCQ gap analysis:** Compute paired T/F accuracy (both T and F correct per scenario) and compare to MC accuracy. A large gap indicates models can select the right option but fail to verify the fact in binary form.
3. **Reasoning-type ablation:** Evaluate models on subsets tagged with single vs. multiple reasoning types. Hypothesis: multi-hop + mathematical combinations are disproportionately hard, especially in local languages.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can multilingual cultural benchmarks capture within-country diversity (e.g., minority languages, dialects) without sacrificing controlled evaluation?
  - Basis: explicit (Limitations: coverage is coarse; cannot capture within-country cultural diversity, minority languages, or dialect continua).
  - Why unresolved: Manual annotation per dialect is costly; templates may not transfer to all subcultures, risking loss of controlled reasoning alignment.
  - What evidence would resolve: Demonstrate scalable generation of dialectal template instantiations with native validation and measure model performance granularity across communities.

- **Open Question 2:** How can we evaluate multicultural reasoning in open-ended, interactive settings (e.g., dialogue, tool use) that reflect real-world deployment?
  - Basis: explicit (Limitations: task format is controlled; does not reflect open-ended dialogue, interactive reasoning, tool use, or real-world information access).
  - Why unresolved: Interactive evaluations introduce variability that complicates cross-cultural comparison; designing controlled yet realistic scenarios is non-trivial.
  - What evidence would resolve: Introduce an interactive benchmark with standardized scenarios (e.g., role-playing with constraints) and evaluate models on consistency and accuracy across multiple turns.

- **Open Question 3:** Why do open-weight models show larger performance drops on local-language inputs and near-chance accuracy on True/False tasks?
  - Basis: inferred (Results: open-weight models degrade substantially in local languages (Avg. ∆MC =−7.5) and approach random on T/F).
  - Why unresolved: The paper attributes this to "shallow response tendencies" but does not analyze whether it stems from training data imbalance, model capacity, or architecture.
  - What evidence would resolve: Controlled ablations varying training data (multilingual exposure), model size, and decoding strategies to isolate factors affecting cross-lingual robustness and binary verification reliability.

- **Open Question 4:** Why are culture-grounded mathematical and counting templates consistently the hardest, and how can models be improved?
  - Basis: inferred (Results: mathematical reasoning is lowest-accuracy; speculation about "double burden" of locale-specific facts and calculation).
  - Why unresolved: It is unclear whether failures are due to lack of factual knowledge, computational errors, or difficulty integrating culture and math; targeted diagnostics are needed.
  - What evidence would resolve: Decompose performance into fact retrieval and calculation steps via error analysis or by creating fact-only vs. math-only control templates; then propose specialized training (e.g., localized numeric knowledge injection) and measure improvement.

## Limitations
- Template list and exact slot constraints are unpublished, blocking full replication of the template-first design.
- Annotator guidelines (e.g., cultural representativeness thresholds, inter-annotator agreement) are underspecified, limiting assessment of data quality rigor.
- Single primary language per country obscures within-nation linguistic diversity; results may not generalize to dialects or minority languages.
- The LLM proofreading step (English-only) risks unintended semantic shifts if the model over-corrects, potentially misaligning English and local versions.

## Confidence
- Template factorization: **Medium** (design well-described but template portability across cultures remains an assumption not fully validated).
- Paired T/F derivation: **Medium** (evidence from paired accuracy drops supports shallow reasoning, but alignment between MCQ and T/F could be weakened by translation nuances).
- English-only proofreading: **Low** (novel quality-control step with no direct precedent; effectiveness depends on strict prompt adherence, which is unverified).

## Next Checks
1. **Proofreading audit:** Manually sample 100 post-proofread English questions vs. annotator originals to quantify any semantic/cultural changes introduced by GPT-5.2-chat.
2. **Template portability assay:** For 10 high-coverage templates, collect 3+ independent instantiations per culture; measure variance in model accuracy across instantiations to test reasoning stability.
3. **T/F response bias check:** Compute separate accuracy on True vs. False statements per model; significant imbalance (e.g., >60% True correct) confirms guessing tendencies undermining paired T/F validity.
