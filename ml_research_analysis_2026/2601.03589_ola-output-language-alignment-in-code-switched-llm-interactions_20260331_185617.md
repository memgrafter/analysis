# OLA: Output Language Alignment in Code-Switched LLM Interactions

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.03589
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03589
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Multilingual LLMs exhibit a strong, asymmetric bias toward non-English responses in code-switched interactions, but Code-Switching Aware DPO (CS-DPO) with minimal data (~1K examples) substantially reduces misalignment and generalizes to unseen language pairs.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces OLA, a benchmark for evaluating output language alignment in code-switched LLM interactions, focusing on Korean-English mixing where the expected response language is implicit. Evaluation of frontier models reveals a systematic asymmetric bias: pass rates drop sharply when English is the expected output (e.g., 21.7% for Gemini 3 Pro in Simple EN Matrix), while non-English expectations are met more reliably. Chain-of-Thought prompting fails to resolve these errors, indicating weak pragmatic reasoning. However, training with Code-Switching Aware DPO (CS-DPO) on a small, targeted dataset significantly improves alignment, demonstrating that the failure stems primarily from insufficient post-training alignment rather than fundamental modeling limits.

## Method Summary
The OLA benchmark comprises two settings: **Simple** (intra-sentential mixing, matrix vs. embedded language; ~279 Korean-matrix, ~258 English-matrix prompts) and **Complex** (inter-sentential mismatches between instruction and content language; ~570 KO-Instr/EN-Content, ~540 EN-Instr/KO-Content prompts). Evaluation uses fastText language identification with sentence-level majority voting; Complex settings apply a "Decompose-and-Verify" step to isolate task content. Models are assessed on **Response-level Pass Rate** (percentage of responses where the detected primary language matches the expected language), with minor intrusions (e.g., technical terms) allowed. Mitigation employs CS-DPO: preference pairs are constructed from baseline model samples (or explicit-instruction generations) on Simple prompts, where `chosen` responses adhere to the matrix language and `rejected` do not. Training on ~1K pairs with full-parameter DPO (β=0.1) is then evaluated on OLA and cross-lingual (EN-ZH, EN-ID) Simple settings.

## Key Results
- **Asymmetric bias:** All evaluated frontier models show dramatically lower pass rates when English is the expected output (e.g., Gemini 3 Pro: 21.7% in Simple EN Matrix vs. >80% in KO Matrix conditions).
- **CoT failure:** Chain-of-Thought prompting degrades alignment, especially in Simple settings, because flawed language decisions in the reasoning trace are faithfully executed (>92% decision-execution alignment).
- **CS-DPO efficacy:** Training on ~1K Simple intra-sentential examples improves pass rates by up to 16.6 percentage points in unseen EN-ZH settings and generalizes to Complex settings, indicating the alignment gap is trainable.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Surface-Level Heuristic Reliance
**Claim:** Models exhibit a systematic asymmetric bias toward non-English outputs in code-switched interactions, primarily due to reliance on surface-level script heuristics rather than pragmatic inference.
**Mechanism:** When output language is implicit, models default to generating text in the script that is most salient in the prompt (e.g., non-Latin characters) or are influenced by the language of the final word, failing to parse deeper linguistic structure (matrix language) or task semantics (instruction vs. content language).
**Core assumption:** The model's language selection policy is governed by shallow lexical and orthographic patterns rather than a robust, context-aware pragmatic reasoning module.
**Evidence anchors:**
- Abstract: "models exhibit a strong, asymmetric bias toward non-English responses."
- Section 5.1: "pass rates drop sharply when English is the expected output" and Figure 2b.
- Section 6.1: Figures 4 & 5 show strong correlation between output language and (a) proportion of alphabetic/Hangul characters and (b) language of the final word.

### Mechanism 2: CoT Decision-Phase Failure
**Claim:** Chain-of-Thought prompting fails to improve, and often degrades, output language alignment because models lack reliable internal criteria to decide the correct response language during reasoning.
**Mechanism:** The decision phase of CoT itself is flawed; models generate reasoning traces that select the wrong language, and this incorrect decision is almost always faithfully executed. The degradation suggests CoT amplifies existing surface-level biases (e.g., recency) instead of correcting them.
**Core assumption:** Output language selection is not a reasoning step the model can perform reliably; it is a latent preference that must be aligned via training, not inference-time computation.
**Evidence anchors:**
- Abstract: "Chain-of-Thought prompting fails to resolve these errors, indicating weak pragmatic reasoning."
- Section 6.3: Figure 7 shows CoT often reduces pass rates; Table 9 shows >92% decision-execution alignment, meaning errors originate in the language decision within the thought trace.

### Mechanism 3: Insufficient Alignment, Not Fundamental Limits
**Claim:** Output language misalignment primarily stems from insufficient preference alignment, not fundamental modeling limits, as evidenced by substantial improvement from a small, targeted DPO dataset.
**Mechanism:** Code-Switching Aware DPO (CS-DPO) creates a reward signal that directly penalizes responses with the wrong dominant language. Training on ~1K simple intra-sentential examples successfully shapes the model's internal language selection policy, generalizing to more complex and unseen language pairs.
**Core assumption:** The model already possesses the multilingual generation capability (shown by explicit instruction performance); the gap is in learning the implicit *policy* for when to use which language, which can be learned via preference data.
**Evidence anchors:**
- Abstract: "Code-Switching Aware DPO with minimal data ... substantially reduces misalignment, suggesting these failures stem from insufficient alignment rather than fundamental limitations."
- Section 7.2: Table 3 shows gains of up to +16.6 percentage points in unseen EN-ZH settings; improvements generalize to Complex settings despite training only on Simple examples.
- Section 5.1: Oracle condition with explicit instructions yields near-perfect pass rates, confirming capability exists.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Code-switching (intra-sentential vs. inter-sentential)**
  - **Why needed here:** The entire benchmark (OLA) is built on these two patterns. Understanding the distinction is essential to grasp the different pragmatic cues (matrix language vs. instruction/content language) that models must infer.
  - **Quick check:** In the prompt "Could you explain the concept of 애교? Why is it so popular among케이팝아이돌?", which language is the matrix language, and what would a human expect the response language to be?
- **Concept: Matrix Language Frame (MLF) model**
  - **Why needed here:** Governs the expected output in the Simple OLA setting. The paper assumes models should respond in the matrix language, but they fail to identify it correctly.
  - **Quick check:** Given "What is the reason for doing a 워밍업세트 before the main 운동?", identify the embedded language elements and the matrix language.
- **Concept: Pragmatic inference for implicit constraints**
  - **Why needed here:** The core challenge is that users rarely specify output language explicitly. Models must infer it from context, a pragmatic task they currently fail at.
  - **Quick check:** If a user says "Translate this paragraph from the Korean news article: [English paragraph]", what is the implicitly expected output language and why?
- **Concept: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**
  - **Why needed here:** CS-DPO is the primary mitigation strategy. Understanding its objective—to align model output with chosen vs. rejected responses—is key to understanding how the alignment gap is closed.
  - **Quick check:** In the CS-DPO setup, what makes a response "chosen" versus "rejected" in the preference pair?
- **Concept: Response-level pass rate with lenient evaluation**
  - **Why needed here:** The primary metric. Knowing that minor code-switching (e.g., technical terms) is allowed is crucial for interpreting the results and error types.
  - **Quick check:** If a model responds entirely in Korean to an English-matrix prompt but includes the English word "workout", is this a pass or fail under OLA's evaluation? Why?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Synthesis Pipeline -> Human Verification Layer -> Evaluation Harness -> Analysis Modules -> Mitigation Training (CS-DPO)
- **Critical path:** Data Generation -> Human Validation -> Baseline Model Evaluation -> Error Analysis -> Mitigation Experiment (CS-DPO) -> Cross-lingual/Complex Generalization Tests.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Synthetic vs. natural data:* OLA uses synthesized code-switched text for control and scale, but may not capture all naturalistic patterns.
  - *Lenient vs. strict evaluation:* Allowing minor intrusions makes the metric more pragmatic but may under-report severe linguistic instability.
  - *Single-turn focus:* The benchmark is single-turn; multi-turn dialogue dynamics are left for future work.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Asymmetric pass rate: Sharp drop when English is expected vs. high rates for non-English.
  - Mid-response switching/intrusions: Model begins correctly but diverges to another script/language.
  - CoT degradation: Negative Δ pass rate when using CoT, particularly in Simple settings.
  - Post-training amplification: Alignment-tuned models show increased bias toward non-English.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce baseline OLA evaluations on at least two models (e.g., GPT-5.1, Qwen 2.5 Instruct), verifying the asymmetric bias pattern and overall pass rates.
  2. Analyze surface cue dependence: for EN-Matrix prompts, calculate proportion of alphabetic characters and language of final word; correlate with English response generation.
  3. Implement and evaluate CS-DPO: take a 32B base model (e.g., OLMo 3 Base), construct ~1K Simple preference pairs, perform DPO training, and evaluate on Simple and unseen EN-ZH settings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why do multilingual LLMs exhibit a systematic asymmetric bias toward non-English responses in code-switched interactions, even when English is the matrix language or instruction language?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper identifies the bias empirically but does not investigate its root causes, such as training data distribution, architectural biases, or optimization pressures.  
2. **What mechanisms cause character- and sentence-level language intrusions (e.g., spurious Russian or Japanese script) in alignment-tuned models, and why are they more frequent than in base models?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper reports the phenomenon but does not analyze whether intrusions stem from tokenization artifacts, latent multilingual representations, or misalignment in internal routing during generation.  
3. **Why does Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting fail to improve—and often degrade—output language alignment, even though models maintain high consistency between reasoning decisions and final outputs?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper shows CoT does not help but does not explain why models lack reliable internal criteria for language selection.  
4. **How does output language alignment evolve over multi-turn dialogues in code-switched contexts, and do initial misalignments propagate or self-correct?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The study is limited to single-turn interactions; multi-turn dynamics are unexamined despite real-world conversations being sequential.  
5. **What drives annotator disagreements on expected output language in code-switched prompts, and can models be trained to handle such pragmatic ambiguity?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper excludes disagreements from analysis, noting they "reflect the inherent pragmatics of multilingual communication."  
6. **Why do models rely so heavily on surface heuristics (e.g., final word language, script ratio) instead of deeper structural cues like matrix language or instruction-content semantics?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The correlation is demonstrated but not causally explained; it is unclear whether this stems from training data skew, insufficiently structured multilingual representations, or optimization that favors simplicity.  
7. **How does Code-Switching Aware DPO (CS-DPO) generalize across language pairs and complexity levels despite being trained only on ~1K simple intra-sentential Korean–English examples?**  
   *Basis in paper:* CS-DPO "generalizes to more complex settings and unseen language pairs" but the underlying mechanism (e.g., language-agnostic alignment signal vs. common code-switching patterns) is not explained.  
8. **Are current evaluation metrics (response-level pass rate, fastText majority vote) sufficient to capture the quality of code-switched outputs, or do they miss subtle errors like unnatural mixing or translationese?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper uses a lenient evaluation that "does not count minor word- or line-level code-switching as an error," but acknowledges qualitative issues like awkward transliterations.  
9. **What architectural or training modifications could reduce output language misalignment without requiring explicit language instructions or extensive preference data?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The focus is on alignment tuning, but fundamental model design choices might prevent or encourage language confusion; this remains an open design question.  
10. **How do different post-training strategies (SFT, DPO, RLVR) introduce distinct language biases, and can we design alignment methods that balance performance across languages?**  
    *Basis in paper:* Alignment-tuned models show "a pronounced shift toward Korean responses," while RLVR models "partially reverse this trend, showing higher English response rates," but the interplay between objectives and language representation is unclear.

## Limitations
- The **Decompose-and-Verify** evaluation logic for Complex settings is incompletely specified, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **CS-DPO preference pair construction details** (source mix, filtering criteria) are vague, risking reproducibility.
- The **synthetic benchmark** may miss natural code-switching patterns, and the **single-turn design** limits real-world relevance.

## Confidence
- **Asymmetric bias & heuristic reliance:** High – Robust across models, strong correlations with script/final-word cues.
- **CoT decision failure:** Medium – Decision-execution alignment high, but only tested with standard CoT; other prompting may differ.
- **CS-DPO alignment gap fix:** Medium-High – Gains on simple & cross-lingual transfer shown, but scalability and language-family effects unknown.
