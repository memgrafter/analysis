# FlashMoE: Reducing SSD I/O Bottlenecks via ML-Based Cache Replacement for Mixture-of-Experts Inference on Edge Devices

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17063
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17063
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: Improves cache hit rate by up to 51% over LRU/LFU, reduces initial model loading by 4–6.8×, and achieves up to 2.6× end-to-end speedup over Fiddler and DAOP.

## Executive Summary
FlashMoE enables efficient inference of large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models on memory-constrained edge devices by offloading inactive expert weights to SSD storage. It introduces a software design that separates expert and non-expert weights into distinct files, allowing only the small non-expert components (~5–7% of parameters) to be loaded into DRAM at startup. During inference, experts are loaded on-demand per token routing, and a lightweight ML-based cache replacement policy—using a feed-forward network to combine recency and frequency signals—minimizes expensive SSD I/O by maximizing cache hit rate. Asynchronous execution overlaps the ML policy’s compute with SSD reads, ensuring negligible overhead. Evaluated on a desktop with 16 GB RAM and a PCIe 5.0 SSD, FlashMoE significantly reduces initial load latency and end-to-end decoding time compared to prior systems.

## Method Summary
FlashMoE’s core innovation lies in three integrated techniques. First, model weights are decomposed: all non-expert modules (attention, normalization, routing gate) are stored together, while each expert’s weights are saved as separate per-layer files. At launch, only the non-expert file is loaded, drastically cutting initial latency. Second, during prefill and decoding, required experts are fetched from SSD on-demand; a per-layer expert cache with fixed capacity holds recently used experts. Third, a lightweight feed-forward network (FFN) predicts which cached expert will be needed furthest in the future, guiding eviction more effectively than heuristic policies (LRU/LFU). The FFN ingests normalized recency (inverse of steps since last access) and frequency (access count) scores for all experts in a layer, trained offline using Belady oracle labels on routing traces. Finally, the cache policy runs asynchronously: when a cache miss occurs, an SSD read is initiated, and the FFN computes eviction scores during the read’s latency (~3 ms), ensuring the policy adds no wait time.

## Key Results
- Cache hit rate improved by up to 51% over LRU and LFU.
- Initial model loading time reduced by 4–6.8× compared to llama.cpp and prior offloading systems.
- End-to-end decoding speedup of up to 2.6× over Fiddler and DAOP.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Separation & Fine-Grained On-Demand Loading
Separating expert and non-expert weights into distinct files allows only the small non-expert set to be loaded at startup. Experts are subdivided per layer and index, so only the specific experts routed for a given token are fetched from SSD. This exploits MoE sparsity: per token, only a few experts are activated, leaving most weights resident on SSD until needed. The initial load is thus dominated by the non-expert fraction (~5–7%), avoiding premature loading of the full model.

### ML-Guided Cache Eviction
A lightweight FFN per layer combines recency and frequency signals to approximate Belady’s optimal eviction better than static heuristics. Recency alone (LRU) may evict experts that become hot again soon; frequency alone (LFU) may retain experts used in a past burst but no longer relevant. The trained FFN adaptively weights these signals based on patterns in routing history, increasing cache hit rates and reducing SSD reads.

### Asynchronous Overlap
The FFN inference (~158 µs) is fully hidden by the SSD read latency (~3 ms). Upon a cache miss, the SSD read is launched, and the ML policy computes eviction scores in parallel. By the time the new expert loads, the eviction decision is ready, so the replacement operation does not extend the critical path.

## Foundational Learning
- **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Sparse Activation**: In MoE layers, a router selects only a small subset (e.g., top‑k) of experts per token. *Why needed*: This sparsity is fundamental to FlashMoE’s feasibility—it ensures most expert weights can remain offloaded to SSD and only a few are needed at a time. *Quick check*: With 128 experts and top‑2 routing, a batch of 4 tokens can require up to 8 distinct experts per layer (if no overlap), i.e., at most 8/128 ≈ 6.25% of experts.
- **Cache Replacement Policies**: LRU evicts least recently used items; LFU evicts least frequently used; Belady’s algorithm is optimal but requires future knowledge. *Why needed*: Understanding their limitations explains why an ML model can outperform them—LRU suffers from “eviction delay” on cyclic patterns; LFU fails after a burst. *Quick check*: LFU may keep an expert that was heavily used in a past burst but is now obsolete, while a newer, soon-to-be-hot expert gets evicted due to low accumulated frequency.
- **SSD vs. DRAM Access Latency**: DRAM accesses take ~100 ns; SSD reads take ~3 ms, making SSD ~30,000× slower. *Why needed*: The cost of a cache miss is an SSD read, so even modest hit‑rate improvements yield large latency reductions. *Quick check*: If an SSD read costs 3 ms and a DRAM access 100 ns, how many DRAM accesses equal one SSD read? ~30,000, implying a cache policy must avoid misses aggressively.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Model Decomposition Module → Prefill Manager → Decode Scheduler → (ML Cache Policy & Storage I/O Layer)

**Critical path** (per token during decoding): Token input → Router (GPU) → Expert IDs needed? → Cache lookup (VRAM) → Hit? → Compute expert → Output. On miss: async SSD read + ML eviction (overlapped) → wait for load → compute. Longest stall: SSD read (~3 ms).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Cache size per layer**: Larger cache reduces misses but consumes DRAM that could hold other tensors. Equation (1) in Section 3.1 budgets DRAM among cache, KV cache, and activations.
- **ML model complexity**: Deeper FFN may predict better but risks exceeding the SSD I/O window. Paper uses 3‑layer, 128‑hidden (≈113 KB) to fit within ~158 µs.
- **File granularity**: Per‑expert files maximize loading flexibility but increase filesystem overhead (many small files). A coarser bins‑per‑layer approach could reduce overhead but reduce on‑demand precision.

**Failure signatures**:
- **High cache miss rate despite ML policy**: Likely training data mismatch; routing pattern shift. Symptom: decode speed near LRU/LFU baseline.
- **Increased decode latency vs. simpler policy**: ML compute not hidden; SSD too fast or FFN too large. Symptom: `ml_cache` slower than `lru` in benchmarks.
- **Initial load time not reduced**: Non‑expert separation failed; large file still loaded at startup. Symptom: `Model Load` time similar to Fiddler/DAOP in Fig. 7.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Validate decomposition & lazy load**: Run prefill with 1 token; monitor system calls/SSD activity. Expect: only non‑expert file reads; no expert reads until after first routing.
2. **Cache policy sanity check**: Generate synthetic routing trace with repeating pattern (e.g., expert sequence 0,1,2,0,1,2,...). Train FFN; compare hit rate on held‑out segment to LRU/LFU. Expect ML ≥ best heuristic.
3. **Overlap validation**: Instrument decode loop with timestamps: cache miss detected, SSD read issued, ML policy start/end, SSD read complete. Expect ML policy finish (<158 µs) well before SSD read returns (~3 ms).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Edge hardware viability**: How does performance scale on edge devices with slower storage (e.g., eMMC/UFS) versus the PCIe 5.0 SSD used? Slower storage may amplify miss penalties and alter optimal cache sizing.
2. **Cross‑model generalizability**: Does the ML cache policy trained on OLMoE‑1B‑7B and TriviaQA generalize to other MoE architectures (e.g., different expert counts, routing mechanisms) and input domains (code, multilingual)? No cross‑model validation is reported.
3. **Non‑stationary routing adaptation**: Can the static, offline‑trained policy handle evolving routing patterns (e.g., long conversations, fine‑tuned models) without periodic retraining? Performance may degrade under distribution shift.
4. **Compute‑I/O trade‑off on edge**: On resource‑limited edge devices, does the FFN overhead remain negligible relative to I/O savings, especially under varying batch sizes? The evaluation uses desktop‑grade hardware.
5. **Impact of non‑expert ratio**: How does the system perform if non‑expert parameters grow (e.g., deeper attention stacks)? The 4–6.8× loading reduction assumes non‑experts are a small, fixed fraction; larger ratios diminish the advantage.

## Limitations
- **Model file format underspecified**: Exact conventions for saving per‑expert `.pt` files and metadata are not provided, blocking faithful reproduction of the weight separation step.
- **Trace extraction details missing**: Specifics of generating training traces (TriviaQA split, batch size, sequence length) and Belady oracle simulation (e.g., future‑lookahead window) are omitted, risking misaligned policy training.
- **Hardware‑specific tuning**: Results are optimized for a high‑end PCIe 5.0 SSD; generalizability to slower edge storage or different cache sizes is unverified.

## Confidence
- **Separation & on‑demand loading**: High – explicitly described and validated by load‑time reductions vs. full‑load baselines.
- **ML cache policy gains**: Medium – quantitative improvements reported, but dependent on representativeness of training traces.
- **Async overlap hiding overhead**: High – latency measurements and pipeline diagrams confirm overlap; FFN time is dwarfed by SSD read.
