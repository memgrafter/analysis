# Equal Access, Unequal Interaction: A Counterfactual Audit of LLM Fairness
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.02932
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02932
- Reference count: 15
- Primary result: Both GPT-4 and LLaMA-3.1-70B exhibit 0% refusal rates but show statistically significant disparities in interaction quality (hedging, sentiment) across identity cues.

## Executive Summary
This paper audits fairness in large language models beyond the usual focus on refusal rates. Using a counterfactual design, the authors inject identity descriptors (age, gender, nationality) into identical career advice prompts and measure stylistic aspects of the responses. Testing GPT-4 and LLaMA-3.1-70B with standardized output constraints, they find no refusals but systematic differences in hedging, sentiment, and politeness across identities. The results reveal that equal access does not guarantee equal interaction quality, highlighting the need for fine‑grained fairness audits.

## Method Summary
The study builds on 30 base career advice prompts, creating 8 variants each by prefixing identity cues (younger/older, male/female, US‑born/immigrant). Models queried are GPT‑4 (snapshot‑0125‑preview) and LLaMA‑3.1‑70B‑Instruct with temperature 0.2, top_p 0.9, max_tokens 300. Responses are forced into a fixed format (~120–150 words) to control length and content. Refusals are identified via rule‑based patterns. Linguistic metrics include VADER sentiment, hedging/politeness/negative‑tone rates per 100 words (using predefined lexicons), and word count. For each prompt, paired Wilcoxon signed‑rank tests and Cohen’s d compare metrics across the 28 identity pairs; bootstrapped 95% CIs supplement significance.

## Key Results
- Both models show 0% refusal rates across all identity configurations.
- GPT‑4 uses significantly more hedging for younger male users than older female US‑born users (Δ = –0.33, d = –0.50, p = 0.012).
- LLaMA‑3.1‑70B produces lower sentiment for older female immigrants relative to younger male immigrants (Δ = –0.20, d = –0.39, p = 0.047).
- Response lengths are stable across identities, isolating stylistic effects.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The counterfactual design holds all prompt content constant while varying only the identity descriptor, so any systematic change in the model’s output can be attributed to its conditioning on that demographic cue. Enforcing a standardized response format and normalizing metrics per 100 words removes confounds from length or structural variation, cleanly isolating differences in tone, hedging, and politeness. This methodological rigor makes the observed interaction‑level disparities both detectable and interpretable.

## Foundational Learning
- **Counterfactual Auditing**: Needed to isolate the effect of demographic attributes on model behavior. *Quick check*: Can you generate prompt pairs that differ only in the attribute of interest?
- **Lexicon‑based Metric Extraction**: Enables scalable quantification of linguistic phenomena. *Quick check*: Are the lexicons comprehensive and validated for the domain?
- **Paired Non‑parametric Testing**: Accounts for within‑prompt correlation across identity conditions. *Quick check*: Do you know when to use Wilcoxon vs. a t‑test?
- **Output Standardization**: Controls confounds of response length and structure, ensuring metric comparability. *Quick check*: Does fixing the format artificially constrain the model and possibly bias results?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Prompt Generator -> Model API -> Response Parser -> Metric Computation -> Statistical Analyzer
- **Critical path**: The pipeline starts by generating identity‑variant prompts, fetches model responses, parses text and checks format, computes linguistic metrics (VADER, lexicon matches), and finally runs paired statistical tests.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Fixed output format vs. open‑ended responses (controls length but may limit natural style); lexicon‑based metrics vs. manual annotation (scalable but may miss nuance); low temperature to reduce randomness (stability) vs. capturing model variability; using one proprietary and one open model for breadth.
- **Failure signatures**: (1) Refusal rate >0% indicating safety over‑trigger; (2) Response word count CV >10% suggesting format non‑compliance; (3) Poor lexicon coverage (e.g., missing contractions) leading to undercounting; (4) API errors or model version drift.
- **First three experiments**:
  1. **Format compliance check**: Run a pilot (5 prompts × 8 identities) and manually verify adherence to the ~120–150 word structure; compute CV of word counts.
  2. **Lexicon coverage validation**: Two annotators independently apply the lexicons to 50 sampled responses; compute inter‑annotator agreement; revise lexicons if agreement <0.85.
  3. **Descriptive statistics replication**: Mean sentiment and hedging rates per identity are computed and compared to the paper’s Table 2; any mean difference >0.05 triggers audit of prompt reconstruction and API settings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization across domains**: Do the observed disparities hold for other high‑stakes advisory contexts (e.g., finance, health) beyond English career advice?
2. **Human perception alignment**: Do automated metrics correlate with actual user judgments of fairness and trustworthiness? Human‑centered evaluations are needed.
3. **Cross‑cultural and multilingual effects**: How do identity cues interact with linguistic and cultural norms in non‑English settings? Replication across languages (e.g., Mandarin, Arabic, Spanish) is required.
4. **Mitigation strategies**: Can interventions (fine‑tuning, RLHF, prompt engineering) reduce interaction‑level disparities without harming helpfulness? Experimental studies testing alignment techniques are called for.

## Limitations
- The exact lexicons (hedging: 18 terms; politeness: 12; negative tone: 15) and full prompt templates are not detailed in the paper; reconstruction from the repository is necessary, and deviations alter metric counts.
- The enforced output format may constrain natural model expression and possibly influence the measured stylistic differences.
- Results depend on specific model snapshots (GPT‑4 snapshot‑0125‑preview, LLaMA‑3.1‑70B‑Instruct); updates or serving variations could shift effect sizes.

## Confidence
- Refusal rate claims (0% across identities): **High confidence**.
- Disparity claims in hedging and sentiment: **Medium confidence** (modest effect sizes, sensitivity to lexicon/format).
- Causal attribution to identity cues: **Low confidence** (potential unmeasured confounders, e.g., subtle phrasing differences).
