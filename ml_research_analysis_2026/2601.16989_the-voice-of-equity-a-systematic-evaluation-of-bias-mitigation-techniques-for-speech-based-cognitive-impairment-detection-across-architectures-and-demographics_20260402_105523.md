# The Voice of Equity: A Systematic Evaluation of Bias Mitigation Techniques for Speech-Based Cognitive Impairment Detection Across Architectures and Demographics
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.16989
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16989
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Two transformer architectures achieved similar F1 scores (~71%) but exhibited substantial fairness disparities by age (≥80 TPR ~46% vs. 77–83% for younger) and language (Spanish sensitivity 44–48% vs. 57–62% for English), with mitigation effectiveness highly architecture-dependent.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates bias in speech-based cognitive impairment detection using two transformer models—SpeechCARE-AGF and Whisper-LWF-LoRA—on the multilingual NIA PREPARE dataset. Both models achieve strong overall performance (F1 ~71%), but reveal significant fairness gaps: older adults (≥80) and Spanish speakers are consistently under-detected. The paper tests several bias mitigation techniques (frequency reweighting, oversampling, language-specific encoders) and finds that effectiveness varies by model architecture, with frequency reweighting most reliably reducing age-based disparities and a Spanish-specific encoder improving performance for SpeechCARE-AGF. The work underscores that fairness interventions must be tailored to model design and calls for broader validation across datasets and demographic intersections.

## Method Summary
The study uses the NIA PREPARE dataset, a multilingual collection of speech tasks (e.g., sentence reading, picture description) from adults with and without cognitive impairment. Two transformer-based models are evaluated: SpeechCARE-AGF, which uses an adaptive gating fusion module to combine linguistic and acoustic features, and Whisper-LWF-LoRA, which applies Low-Rank Adaptation to a pretrained Whisper encoder. Models are trained with and without bias mitigation techniques—including data-level interventions (oversampling) and loss-level interventions (frequency reweighting)—and evaluated on fairness metrics like true positive rate (TPR) across age and language subgroups. The experimental framework systematically compares baseline and mitigated performance to quantify disparity changes.

## Key Results
- Both architectures achieve comparable overall F1 scores: SpeechCARE-AGF (70.87%), Whisper-LWF-LoRA (71.46%).
- Age disparity: Adults aged ≥80 have substantially lower TPR (~46%) compared to younger groups (77–83%).
- Language disparity: Spanish speakers show reduced sensitivity (44–48%) relative to English speakers (57–62%).
- Mitigation varies by architecture: frequency reweighting most consistently improves age-related TPR (80+ TPR to 59.38% for AGF, 55.14% for Whisper); a Spanish-specific encoder boosts Spanish TPR for AGF but was not tested on Whisper; combined techniques further narrow gaps.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of bias mitigation is fundamentally shaped by architectural design. SpeechCARE-AGF’s adaptive fusion module allows flexible re-weighting of linguistic and acoustic pathways during training, enabling it to respond more robustly to data-driven interventions like oversampling and language-specific encoders. In contrast, Whisper-LWF-LoRA’s fixed fusion strategy, inherited from the pretrained Whisper backbone, limits its adaptability to such interventions; instead, it benefits more from loss-level reweighting. This suggests that models with dynamic feature integration mechanisms may better accommodate fairness adjustments without catastrophic forgetting, while architectures with rigid pretrained representations require more careful, often less effective, fine-tuning for bias mitigation.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  SpeechCARE-AGF: Audio Encoder (wav2vec 2.0) -> Adaptive Gating Fusion (combines wav2vec and textual BERT embeddings) -> Classifier.  
  Whisper-LWF-LoRA: Whisper Encoder (pretrained) -> LoRA Adaptations (low-rank updates) -> Linear Classifier.
- **Critical path**: Raw audio input → feature extraction (spectrogram/embedding) → model forward pass with optional mitigation layer (e.g., sample weights) → impairment logits → subgroup metric computation (TPR by age/language).
- **Design tradeoffs**: Adaptive fusion (AGF) offers flexibility for mitigation but adds complexity; fixed fusion (Whisper) leverages strong pretraining but resists data interventions. LoRA reduces parameter overhead but may constrain adaptation scope.
- **Failure signatures**: Sudden TPR drop for ≥80 group; Spanish TPR remains low despite mitigation; training instability (loss spikes) with oversampling; LoRA rank too low to capture bias-related features.
- **First experiments**:  
  1) Replicate baseline F1 and TPR gaps on PREPARE to confirm dataset split and hyperparameters.  
  2) Apply frequency reweighting to both models, measuring change in age-TPR gap; expect AGF to show larger improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** To what extent do task-type imbalances (e.g., sentence reading vs. picture description) confound language-based fairness disparities in speech-based cognitive impairment detection, and can balanced task sampling mitigate the Spanish performance gap?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors hypothesize that Spanish speakers' lower TPR is "primarily related to task distribution rather than inherent linguistic differences," noting that over 90% of Spanish data came from sentence reading tasks while English used more diverse elicitation methods.
- **Why unresolved:** The study did not control for or stratify analysis by speech task, leaving the hypothesis untested and the confound unquantified.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where Spanish training data includes proportionally balanced speech tasks (e.g., matching picture description rates in English), followed by fairness comparison of language subgroups; or synthetic augmentation to simulate balanced task distributions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Which architectural components (e.g., fusion strategy, pretraining objectives) most critically determine a model's responsiveness to bias mitigation techniques like oversampling or frequency reweighting?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states that "architectural design fundamentally shapes bias patterns and mitigation effectiveness," and notes that SpeechCARE-AGF's adaptive fusion "enables flexible responses to data interventions," while Whisper-LWF-LoRA's fixed fusion showed limited gains from voice conversion.
- **Why unresolved:** Only two architectures were compared, so causal factors remain speculative; the role of specific modules (e.g., gating vs. cross-attention) is inferred but not systematically isolated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies varying fusion mechanisms (adaptive vs. static) and pretraining paradigms (self-supervised vs. supervised) on the same backbone and dataset, measuring changes in mitigation efficacy (e.g., TPR gap reduction) for age/language subgroups.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is frequency-based reweighting robustly effective across diverse clinical speech datasets with varying demographic imbalances, or is its success contingent on specific subgroup–label distributions in the PREPARE dataset?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Frequency reweighting "provided the most consistent improvements across both architectures" and reduced age-related disparities, but evaluation was limited to a single multilingual dataset.
- **Why unresolved:** No external validation on other datasets; the method's generalizability to different clinical populations, recording conditions, or imbalance patterns remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication of the mitigation framework on multiple independent speech-based cognitive impairment datasets (e.g., other challenge corpora or real-world collections), comparing fairness metrics (EO, EO) pre- and post-reweighting to assess consistency.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the imputation of missing education data (35% of participants) affect observed fairness disparities, and would complete education information alter the ranking or effectiveness of bias mitigation techniques?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note education was missing for ~35% of participants and was imputed using Iterative Imputer, acknowledging this as a limitation that could impact fairness estimates.
- **Why unresolved:** No sensitivity analysis was performed to compare imputed versus complete-case results; thus, the stability of education-related fairness conclusions is uncertain.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-run all fairness evaluations using listwise deletion (complete cases only) and alternative imputation strategies (e.g., multiple imputation), then compare subgroup TPR/FPR patterns and mitigation outcomes to assess robustness to missing-data handling.

## Limitations
- Single dataset (NIA PREPARE) used; limited sample sizes for some demographic groups may affect reliability.
- Only two transformer architectures evaluated; findings may not generalize to other model families.
- No statistical significance testing for observed fairness differences; risk of overinterpreting random variation.
- Omitted interactions (e.g., age × language) not examined; intersectional fairness unknown.
- Fairness evaluation focused on TPR; other metrics (FPR, calibration) not reported.

## Confidence

- Age disparity (≥80 TPR ~46% vs. 77–83%): High (consistent across both models).
- Language disparity (Spanish sensitivity 44–48% vs. 57–62%): High (robust across models).
- Mitigation effectiveness varies by architecture: Medium (limited to two architectures; no significance tests).
- Frequency reweighting improves age fairness: Medium (observed improvement but not universal across all metrics).
- Spanish-specific encoder helps Spanish performance: Medium (only tested on AGF; not on Whisper).
