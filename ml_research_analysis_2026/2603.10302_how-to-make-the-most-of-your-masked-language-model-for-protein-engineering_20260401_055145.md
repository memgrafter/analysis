# How to make the most of your masked language model for protein engineering
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10302  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10302  
- **Reference count:** 23  
- **Primary result:** Stochastic beam search with smooth Tchebycheff scalarization achieves up to 100% success rate in antibody engineering campaigns.

## Executive Summary
Protein language models (PLMs) are increasingly used for protein engineering, but optimal strategies for sampling sequences with desired properties remain unclear. This paper introduces a stochastic beam search method for masked language models (MLMs) that efficiently explores sequence neighborhoods by approximating pseudo-perplexity using a single forward pass. The approach incorporates multi-objective guidance (via smooth Tchebycheff scalarization) and post-generation filtering, enabling flexible trade-offs between properties like binding, stability, and humanness. In vitro evaluations on real antibody campaigns show that sampling method choice is as impactful as model selection, with guided stochastic beam search achieving a 100% success rate. The work highlights the power of sequence-level optimization over mutation-centric Gibbs sampling and provides practical recommendations for protein engineering with MLMs.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a stochastic beam search algorithm tailored for masked language models. Given a seed sequence, the method first performs a full forward pass to obtain token-wise conditional distributions for all positions. It then efficiently computes approximate pseudo-log-likelihood (PLL) scores for all one-substitution neighbors using a "wild-type marginal" trick, avoiding expensive re-evaluation. Candidate sequences are perturbed with Gumbel noise to introduce stochasticity and added to a beam. Over multiple expansion steps, the algorithm builds diverse, high-likelihood sequences. To optimize for multiple objectives (e.g., binding affinity, synthesizability), the method scores candidates using smooth Tchebycheff scalarization: standardized scores from the MLM and any black-box oracles are aggregated with user-defined weights. Finally, supervised models can be applied for post-hoc filtering and ranking. This framework enables efficient, flexible protein engineering with MLMs while bridging the gap between model likelihood and functional outcomes.

## Key Results
- Stochastic beam search consistently outperformed Gibbs sampling across both generic (ESM2-650M) and antibody-specific (AbLang2) models.
- Integrating supervised guidance via smooth Tchebycheff scalarization markedly improved synthesizability and binding; one configuration achieved a 100% success rate in vitro.
- The impact of the sampling algorithm was comparable to that of the underlying model, underscoring the importance of search strategy in MLM-based engineering.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Stochastic beam search efficiently explores high-quality sequence neighborhoods by exploiting cheap pseudo-perplexity evaluation for all one-substitution neighbors after a single forward pass.  
**Mechanism:** For a template sequence *x*, an initial O(*L*²) forward pass yields conditional probability distributions *A*<sub>i,:</sub> for all positions *i*. The approximate pseudo-log-likelihood (PLL) for any child sequence *x′* differing by one substitution at *k* is computed as Σ<sub>i≠k</sub> log *A*<sub>i,index(x′<sub>i</sub>)</sub> plus log softmax(*Y*<sub>k,:</sub>)<sub>a</sub>. This "wild-type marginal" approximation avoids O(*L*³) forward passes per child, enabling beam search with O(*BEL*³) total cost for *B* beams and *E* steps.  
**Core assumption:** The error from conditioning position *i≠k* on the template token *x<sub>k</sub>* rather than the child’s token *x′<sub>k</sub>* is acceptable over multiple steps.  
**Evidence:** [abstract] "exploiting the fact that MLMs are remarkably efficient at evaluating the pseudo-perplexity of the entire 1-edit neighborhood of a sequence." [section 2.2] Detailed derivation of O(*L*²) shared computation vs. O(*L*⁴) for exact PLL.  
**Break condition:** Approximation error compounds over many beam steps, especially if the mutation at *k* heavily influences distant positions, causing ranking divergence from true PLL.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Smooth Tchebycheff scalarization (STS) enables flexible multi-objective guidance by aggregating standardized scores from the MLM and any black-box scoring functions, improving success rates without requiring differentiable models.  
**Mechanism:** During beam search, each candidate’s score is computed as log Σ<sub>i</sub> *c*<sub>i</sub> exp(*z̃*<sub>i</sub>), where *z̃*<sub>i</sub> are z-scores of objectives (e.g., negative PLL, predicted success probability) and *c*<sub>i</sub> are weights. This balances trade-offs by aiming to maximize all objectives simultaneously.  
**Core assumption:** Objectives can be standardized (z-scored) relative to the current candidate set and additively combined in log-space to produce a meaningful ranking.  
**Evidence:** [abstract] "smooth Tchebycheff scalarization... achieved a 100% success rate." [section 2.3] STS formulation vs. non-dominated sorting; [section 3.2] STS guidance outperformed NDS and unguided methods in vitro.  
**Break condition:** Objectives are highly conflicting or non-monotonic with respect to the MLM distribution, so aggregated z-scores lead to sequences that satisfy none well. Scoring functions must be reasonably calibrated to the current candidate distribution.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The choice of sampling algorithm (e.g., stochastic beam search vs. Gibbs) has an impact on engineering outcomes comparable to the choice of protein language model itself.  
**Mechanism:** Mutation-centric samplers (Gibbs, denoising) evaluate only partial sequences per step, are computationally heavy, and tend to produce high-likelihood yet dysfunctional sequences. Sequence-centric beam search evaluates full sequences via PLL, directly optimizing model likelihood while allowing flexible external score integration.  
**Core assumption:** The empirical distribution of successful therapeutic antibodies correlates more strongly with full-sequence PLL (or its combination with supervised scores) than with local mutation decisions made by Gibbs.  
**Evidence:** [abstract] "choice of sampling method is at least as impactful as the model used." [section 3.2] In vitro: ESM2-650M Beam > ESM2 Gibbs; AbLang2 Beam > AbLang2 Gibbs; STS Beam + ranking = 100% success. [section 4] Conclusion advises stochastic beam search over Gibbs.  
**Break condition:** Correlation between full-sequence PLL and desired properties breaks down for poorly calibrated models or tasks where beneficial mutations involve non-local interactions not captured by the PLL approximation.

## Foundational Learning
### Masked Language Models (MLMs) for proteins
**Why needed:** Protein sequences lack a natural left-to-right order, making autoregressive models unsuitable; MLMs predict masked residues from bidirectional context, better capturing co-evolutionary constraints.  
**Quick check:** "How does an MLM compute the probability of an amino acid at a position when other positions are masked?"

### Pseudo-log-likelihood (PLL) and pseudo-perplexity
**Why needed:** MLMs do not define a joint probability distribution; PLL averages position-wise conditionals as a surrogate sequence score, enabling sequence-level comparison.  
**Quick check:** "What is the computational cost of exact PLL vs. the wild-type marginal approximation used in beam search?"

### Stochastic beam search with Gumbel noise
**Why needed:** Balances exploitation (high PLL) and exploration (diversity) by adding Gumbel noise to log-probabilities before ranking, avoiding the determinism of argmax decoding.  
**Quick check:** "How does the temperature parameter affect the trade-off between likelihood and diversity in stochastic beam search?"

### Multi-objective optimization via scalarization
**Why needed:** Antibody engineering requires balancing competing properties (binding, stability, humanness); scalarization converts multiple goals into a single ranking.  
**Quick check:** "What is the difference between non-dominated sorting and smooth Tchebycheff scalarization in handling multiple objectives?"

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
MLM forward pass engine -> Neighbor PLL approximator -> Beam search controller -> Scoring function adapters -> Multi-objective aggregator

### Critical path
1. Load seed sequence *x*.
2. Run full forward pass → get token-wise conditionals *A*<sub>i,:</sub>.
3. For each position *k* and amino acid *a*: compute child *x′* (single substitution); set PLL<sub>wt</sub>(*x′*) = Σ<sub>i≠k</sub> log *A*<sub>i,x′<sub>i</sub></sub> + log softmax(*Y*<sub>k,:</sub>)<sub>a</sub>.
4. Perturb log-PLL with Gumbel noise; add to beam if unique.
5. Repeat steps 2–4 for *E* steps per beam item.
6. At termination, for each candidate gather all objective scores, z-standardize, apply smooth Tchebycheff formula, rank.

### Design tradeoffs
- *Beam size B*: Larger *B* increases diversity and unique outputs but raises O(*BEL*³) cost. Guidance may require larger *B* (e.g., 20 vs. 5).
- *Approximation fidelity*: Wild-type marginal is O(*L*³) vs. double-mask O(*L*⁴). Risk of error accumulation; validate task-specifically if needed.
- *Objective weighting*: STS weights reflect priorities; mis-weighting can over-optimize one objective. Calibrate with a validation set if available.

### Failure signatures
- Low success rate despite high model PLL → PLL poorly correlates with function; try alternative models or add supervised ranking.
- Very low intra-seed diversity → beam size too small or temperature too low; increase *B* or τ.
- Generated sequences deviate from seed (high edit distance) → *E* too large or insufficient PLL weighting; reduce *E* or increase *c*<sub>MLM</sub>.
- Guidance causes loss of humanness/stability → check objective balance; include humanness/pI explicitly in STS.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline comparison:** Run unguided Gibbs vs. Beam on 5 seed sequences with *E*=3; measure intra-seed diversity, mean PLL, and in silico oracle success prediction. Expect Beam to have lower diversity but higher PLL.
2. **Guidance calibration:** For a fixed seed set, generate 1000 candidates via Beam. Apply supervised ranking alone vs. STS guidance during generation. Tune STS weights to maximize validation success rate. Expect guided methods to improve success rate but reduce diversity.
3. **Approximation validation:** For a set of child sequences, compare wild-type marginal PLL vs. exact PLL (full masking). Compute rank correlation. If correlation < 0.9, consider using double-mask for final ranking of top candidates (accepting higher cost).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** Why does the choice of sampling method have an impact comparable to model choice in antibody optimization, and what underlying mechanisms drive this effect?  
- **Basis in paper:** The abstract and conclusion state that "choice of sampling method is at least as impactful as the model used" and call for future research into this under-explored area. The paper empirically compares beam search and Gibbs sampling but does not theoretically analyze why one elicits higher-quality sequences.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates empirical superiority of stochastic beam search over Gibbs but does not investigate whether this stems from differences in sequence-space exploration, likelihood ranking, or interaction with model distributional modes. The reasons for Gibbs' tendency to produce unlikely sequences remain unclear.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Detailed analysis comparing the distribution of generated sequences from different samplers (e.g., via likelihood histograms, mutational patterns, or model confidence scores) along with ablation studies on beam search components (e.g., Gumbel noise, temperature) to isolate key factors driving performance.

**Open Question 2:** What are the optimal multi-objective optimization (MOO) strategies for integrating supervised scoring functions with MLM pseudo-perplexity, and how do different scalarization methods (e.g., smooth Tchebycheff vs. NDS) affect trade-offs between success rate and other developability metrics?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper reports that smooth Tchebycheff scalarization achieved a 100% success rate and eliminated weak binders, but notes that guidance can lead to undesirable side effects like reduced humanness. It suggests STS as an alternative but does not fully characterize its trade-offs.  
- **Why unresolved:** While STS outperformed non-dominated sorting (NDS) in one experiment, the paper does not systematically compare these or other MOO methods across multiple objectives (e.g., synthesizability, binding, thermostability, humanness). The relationship between objective weights, guidance strength, and unintended consequences (e.g., bias toward specific mutations) is not explored.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled study varying weights and guidance methods across a suite of developability objectives, measuring not only success rates but also diversity, germline bias, and isoelectric point distributions. Theoretical analysis comparing STS and NDS in the context of MLM pseudo-perplexity landscapes could clarify their behaviors.

**Open Question 3:** Why does ESM-2 (trained on generic proteins) perform as well as or better than antibody-specific models like AbLang2 in antibody engineering, and what properties of the training data or model architecture explain this generalization?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper highlights that ESM2-650M, despite being trained on generic protein sequences, "is still very effective for antibody optimization" and ranks among the top performers. This is presented as surprising without explanation.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not investigate whether ESM-2's strong performance is due to its larger size, broader training data capturing general biophysical principles, or specific architectural features. It also does not test whether antibody-specific models underperform because their training data is biased or limited.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analyses of model representations (e.g., probing for antibody-specific motifs, attention patterns) and controlled experiments where models are trained on varied datasets (e.g., generic vs. antibody-only) with matched architectures to isolate the effect of data. Evaluating model predictions on antibody-specific benchmarks vs. general protein stability tasks could also clarify transferability.

**Open Question 4:** What are the precise failure modes of Gibbs sampling in MLM-based protein optimization, and how do they differ from beam search at the level of sequence generation dynamics?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper states that Gibbs sampling "tends to propose sequences which are closer to the seed sequence" and empirically "tends to elicit unlikely, dysfunctional sequences," while beam search excels. However, it does not dissect whether Gibbs's issues arise from myopic decisions, lack of diversity, or poor handling of the MLM's conditional distributions.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper contrasts overall outcomes but does not analyze the step-wise generation process. For example, it is unclear if Gibbs gets trapped in local modes, fails to accumulate beneficial mutations, or produces sequences with inconsistent residue compatibilities due to single-position resampling.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Tracking the trajectories of Gibbs vs. beam search during generation (e.g., likelihood evolution, mutation accumulation patterns) on held-out test sets. Simulating Gibbs with oracle access to exact PLLs could determine if limitations are algorithmic or due to approximations like the wild-type marginal. Studying the correlation between Gibbs-sampled sequences and model confidence (e.g., entropy per position) might reveal specific decision pitfalls.

**Open Question 5:** How can supervised guidance be designed to avoid degrading developability metrics like humanness or germline bias while still improving success rates, and what is the root cause of these trade-offs?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes that guidance methods (NDS/STS) "produced less human antibodies" and that "using a single guidance model can in principle be ameliorated by guidance with a humanness metric." It cautions about "care when using supervised guidance" but does not investigate mitigation strategies or explain why guidance causes these effects.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper combines a supervised success predictor with the MLM but does not analyze whether the predictor is biased, whether the multi-objective formulation inadequately balances competing goals, or whether the beam search procedure itself amplifies certain biases. The interplay between guidance strength, objective weighting, and metric correlations is unstudied.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments where guidance incorporates multiple objectives explicitly (e.g., including a humanness score in the STS scalarization) to see if trade-offs can be mitigated. Analyzing the training data of the supervised model for biases, and studying the generated sequences' mutation spectra relative to seed positions (e.g., CDR vs. framework) to see if guidance overrides the MLM's natural preferences in harmful ways.

## Limitations
- Approximation error of wild-type marginal PLL over multi-step beam search is not fully validated; potential ranking divergence from true PLL.
- Robustness of smooth Tchebycheff scalarization to objective conflicts and dataset shift remains unclear.
- In vitro evaluation is limited to small seed sets; the reported 100% success rate may not generalize.
- Computational cost analysis assumes ideal shared computation; memory constraints for long sequences are not addressed.

## Confidence
- Stochastic beam search efficiency: Medium  
- STS multi-objective guidance: Medium  
- Sampling method impact comparable to model choice: Medium
