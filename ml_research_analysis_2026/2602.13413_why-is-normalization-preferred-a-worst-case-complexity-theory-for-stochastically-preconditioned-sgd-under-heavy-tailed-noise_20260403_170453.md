# Why is Normalization Preferred? A Worst-Case Complexity Theory for Stochastically Preconditioned SGD under Heavy-Tailed Noise

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13413
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13413
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Normalization in stochastically preconditioned SGD guarantees convergence to a first-order stationary point under heavy-tailed noise, while step clipping can fail due to statistical dependence between the preconditioner and gradient estimates.

## Executive Summary
This paper develops a worst-case complexity theory for stochastically preconditioned SGD (SPSGD) under heavy-tailed gradient noise, comparing two common step transformations: normalization and clipping. It proves that normalization yields optimal convergence rates—O(T^{-(p-1)/(3p-2)}) when problem parameters are known and O(T^{-(p-1)/(2p)}) when unknown—matching those of normalized SGD. In contrast, step clipping may not converge in the worst case because of statistical dependence between the stochastic preconditioner and the gradient noise. The analysis introduces a novel vector-valued Burkholder-type inequality for bounding heavy-tailed martingales, which also simplifies the treatment of momentum. These results theoretically explain the empirical superiority of normalization in adaptive optimizers such as Adam, RMSProp, and Shampoo.

## Method Summary
The paper studies SPSGD, where updates take the form x_{k+1} = x_k - η D_k m_k, with D_k a stochastic symmetric positive definite (SPD) preconditioner and m_k a (possibly momentum-based) gradient estimate. Under a p-th moment bounded condition (p-BCM) on the gradient noise—allowing heavy tails—the method applies either normalization (dividing by ∥D_k m_k∥) or clipping (thresholding at τ) to the preconditioned step. The analysis shows that normalization effectively decouples the step direction from the magnitude induced by D_k, preventing the statistical dependence between D_k and the noise from biasing the update size. For clipping, however, the dependence introduces an irreducible error term that grows with the clipping threshold τ, leading to possible non-convergence. A key technical contribution is a vector-valued Burkholder inequality that bounds the p-th moment of sums of mean-zero martingale differences, enabling unified analysis of momentum under heavy tails and yielding the stated rates. This framework unifies many adaptive optimizers (Adam, RMSProp, Shampoo) and provides a theoretical justification for preferring normalization over clipping.

## Key Results
- Normalization in SPSGD guarantees convergence to a first-order stationary point at rates O(T^{-(p-1)/(3p-2)}) with known parameters and O(T^{-(p-1)/(2p)}) with unknown parameters, matching optimal normalized SGD rates.
- Step clipping can fail to converge in the worst case due to statistical dependence between the stochastic preconditioner D_k and gradient noise.
- A novel vector-valued Burkholder-type inequality is introduced, enabling the analysis of momentum under heavy-tailed noise; it may be of independent interest.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Normalization decouples update direction from preconditioner magnitude
Normalization forces the preconditioned step D_k m_k to have a fixed unit norm, thereby decoupling the direction from the magnitude induced by the stochastic preconditioner. This prevents the statistical dependence between D_k and the gradient noise δ_k from affecting the step size. The expected descent reduces to a term proportional to ∥∇f(x_k)∥ plus an error that sums to O(σ) per iteration, under the core assumptions that D_k is SPD with uniformly bounded condition number κ_D and the gradient noise has finite p-th moment. The bound ∑ E[∥∇f(x_k)∥] ≤ √κ_D (f(x_1)-f*/η + 2∑ E[∥ε_k∥] + η L T/2) captures the effect of κ_D. The break condition: if κ_D grows unbounded, the √κ_D factor degrades guarantees; if D_k is singular, normalization may be undefined.

### Mechanism 2: Clipping fails due to uncorrectable error from dependence
Step clipping retains magnitude information when ∥D_k g_k∥ < τ. The error term ∇f(x_k)^T E_k[D_k ϵ_u_k] captures covariance between D_k and centered noise. Under heavy-tailed noise (p<2), the optimal threshold τ scales as T^{p/(3p-2)}, making the error term O(η τ^{2-p} σ^p) diverge as T→∞ because (2-p)>0, while the descent term is O(η τ^{-2p+2} σ^{2p}). The net per-iteration progress can become positive, preventing convergence. The core assumption is that D_k and g_k are statistically dependent (as in adaptive methods) and p∈(1,2). Evidence: Lemma C.3 shows the error term; Example 5.5 constructs a D_k correlated with sign(g_k) achieving ∇f(x_k)^T E_k[D_k ϵ_u_k] = O(σ^2). The break condition: if D_k and g_k are independent or p=2 (sub-Gaussian), the error can be controlled.

### Mechanism 3: Vector-valued Burkholder inequality unifies heavy-tailed analysis
The novel inequality states that for mean-zero martingale differences X_k with finite p-th moment (1<p≤2), E[∥∑_{k=0}^{T-1} X_k∥^p] ≤ 2^{2-p} ∑_{k=0}^{T-1} E[∥X_k∥^p]. It is used to bound E[∥∑ θ^{k-t} δ_t∥] in Lemma 4.10, avoiding separate treatments for p=2 and p<2. This yields the O(T^{-(p-1)/(2p)}) rate in the parameter-unknown case. The core assumption is finite p-th moment. The inequality may not hold for p>2 and the constant 2^{2-p} may not be tight.

## Architecture Onboarding
The main components of SPSGD are:
- Preconditioner D_k (SPD, possibly stochastic, e.g., diagonal from Adam's v_k, Kronecker from Shampoo)
- Momentum m_k (optional)
- Step transformation: normalization (÷∥D_k m_k∥) or clipping (min(1, τ/∥D_k m_k∥))
- Noise δ_k = g_k - ∇f(x_k) with finite p-th moment.

Component map: D_k and m_k → raw step s_k = D_k m_k → transformation (normalization/clipping) → final step → update x_{k+1}=x_k - η·final_step.

Critical path for new optimizer design: First ensure D_k does not cause unbounded condition number κ_D. Prefer normalization over clipping unless strong independence guarantees exist. If using clipping, the threshold τ must grow as T^{p/(3p-2)}, but this leads to divergence due to an error term O(η τ^{2-p} σ^p) under dependence.

Design tradeoffs:
- Normalization: robust to any D_k up to an O(√κ_D) factor; no need to tune τ; per-iteration norm fixed.
- Clipping: requires τ → ∞ for optimal rate, breaking convergence under dependence; even fixed τ may fail; analysis brittle to dependence structure.
- Momentum: helps normalization achieve optimal rates with single sample; does not fix clipping's fundamental issue.

Failure signatures:
1. Diverging sum of expected gradient norms ∑ E[∥∇f(x_k)∥] not sublinear.
2. Error term proportional to η τ^{2-p} σ^p dominates descent when τ large.
3. Training instability when D_k correlates with gradient sign/magnitude (e.g., AdaGrad with small denominator).

First 3 experiments:
1. Construct synthetic D_k that flips based on sign(g_k) (Example 5.5). Compare normalized SPSGD (converges) vs. clipped SPSGD (diverges) for p=1.5.
2. Run normalized SPSGD with momentum on a nonconvex quadratic with heavy-tailed noise; empirically verify the O(T^{-(p-1)/(2p)}) rate in the parameter-unknown setting (η=1/√T, θ=1-1/√T).
3. Simulate martingale ∑ θ^{k-t} δ_t; numerically verify that its p-th moment is bounded by 2^{2-p} times the sum of individual p-th moments, checking tightness of the Burkholder inequality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **When does step clipping succeed in practice?** The worst-case analysis shows clipping can fail due to statistical dependence, yet empirical studies often report good performance. Characterizing instance-dependent or average-case behavior under realistic distributions could reveal when clipping succeeds.
2. **Extending to nonlinear preconditioning.** Current analysis assumes linear SPD preconditioner D_k; however, modern optimizers introduce nonlinear transformations (e.g., bias correction in Adam, nonlinear updates in Shampoo). Extending the framework to such settings would better guide optimizer design.
3. **Block-structured or layer-wise preconditioners.** The unified theory treats D_k as any SPD matrix. It is unclear whether the worst-case failure of clipping generalises to block-structured (e.g., Kronecker, K-FAC) or layer-wise preconditioners, and what convergence guarantees normalization enjoys.
4. **Tighter rates for specific optimizers.** For Adam, RMSProp, Shampoo, can we derive improved convergence rates under heavy-tailed noise by exploiting their algorithmic structures beyond the worst-case bounds? Proving such improvements requires algorithm-specific analyses.

## Limitations
- **Condition number dependence:** Normalization's rate includes a √κ_D factor, degrading guarantees if κ_D grows large.
- **Noise model:** The p-BCM assumption excludes arbitrarily heavy tails or biased noise observed in practice.
- **Worst-case nature:** Clipping's failure hinges on adversarial correlation; empirical prevalence unclear.
