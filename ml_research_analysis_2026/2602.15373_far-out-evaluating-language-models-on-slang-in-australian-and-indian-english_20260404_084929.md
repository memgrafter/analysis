# Far Out: Evaluating Language Models on Slang in Australian and Indian English

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.15373
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15373
- Reference count: 18
- Primary result: Models achieve higher accuracy on discriminative slang selection (TWS, 0.49) than generative prediction (TWP, 0.02–0.04), with better performance on Indian English slang and on naturalistic web-sourced data.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the first systematic evaluation of language models' ability to understand region-specific slang in Australian and Indian English. Using two datasets—one collected from Urban Dictionary (WEB, 377 examples) and one synthetically generated via Gemini (GEN, 1,492 scenarios)—the authors test seven models on three tasks: target word prediction (TWP), guided TWP (TWP*), and target word selection (TWS). Key findings reveal a stark discriminative–generative asymmetry: models achieve an average accuracy of 0.49 on TWS but only 0.02–0.04 on TWP tasks. Performance is higher on the naturalistic WEB dataset than on synthetic GEN, and models consistently outperform on Indian English slang, with the largest variety effect in TWS (accuracy +0.10 for en-IN). Error analysis shows that models often capture the semantic gist but substitute generic terms instead of the correct slang, indicating a gap between recognition and production of culturally embedded language.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates pre-trained language models without any fine-tuning. Two parallel corpora per variety are constructed: WEB consists of web-sourced slang entries from Urban Dictionary, each including a definition and usage example; GEN comprises scenarios generated by prompting Google Gemini Pro 2.5 to create four unique contextual examples per slang phrase. For evaluation, the slang phrase is masked in each context. Three tasks are defined: (1) TWP: models generate the masked phrase in an open-ended fill-in-the-blank; (2) TWP*: identical to TWP but with an explicit instruction to use Australian or Indian English slang; (3) TWS: models select the correct slang from a set of four options (one correct, three distractors from the same variety). Seven models are assessed: encoder-based (BERT-base, RoBERTa-large, XLM-R-large) using masked language modeling, and decoder-based (Granite-4.0-1B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Olmo-2-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-4B-Instruct) using cloze-style prompts. For TWP/TWP*, generation uses temperature=0.8; for TWS, multiple-choice selection is performed. Primary metrics are exact match accuracy and semantic similarity (cosine similarity between predicted and reference embeddings).

## Key Results
- Discriminative–generative gap: TWS accuracy (0.49) far exceeds TWP (0.02–0.04), showing models recognize slang better than they generate it.
- Domain effect: Models perform better on WEB than GEN, with similarity gains of 0.03–0.05, suggesting reliance on surface patterns or contamination.
- Variety advantage: Indian English slang is easier than Australian English, with TWS accuracy 0.10 higher for en-IN; error analysis reveals frequent generic substitutions despite semantic understanding.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Discriminative–Generative Asymmetry
**Claim:** Current language models are better at recognizing region-specific slang than generating it.
**Mechanism:** In discriminative tasks (TWS), the model only needs to match context to a provided set of slang options, leveraging stored lexical representations. In generative tasks (TWP), the model must produce the exact slang term from the full vocabulary, a low-frequency event prone to substitution by more common generic equivalents.
**Core assumption:** Slang understanding in LLMs is predominantly a matter of lexical recall rather than creative construction of culturally embedded expressions.
**Evidence:** The paper reports a large gap: TWS average accuracy 0.49 versus TWP 0.02–0.04. The authors note that models struggle to generate slang but can recognize it when choices are limited.
**Break condition:** If future models achieve comparable generative accuracy (TWP) to discriminative accuracy (TWS), this asymmetry would be resolved.

### Mechanism 2: Domain Effect ( WEB > GEN )
**Claim:** Performance degradation on synthetically generated data (GEN) compared to naturalistic web data (WEB) suggests models rely on superficial textual patterns or suffer from data contamination, rather than robustly understanding slang's contextual usage.
**Mechanism:** WEB examples, sourced from Urban Dictionary, contain authentic, albeit online, usage patterns that may have been encountered during pre-training (data contamination). GEN scenarios, created by an LLM (Gemini), introduce novel contextual frames and possibly different syntactic structures, exposing a lack of true semantic-pragmatic grounding. The model's performance drops because its representations are tuned to the statistical surface forms of WEB, not the underlying slang concept.
**Core assumption:** Higher WEB performance is partially an artifact of memorization or familiarity with web-derived text distributions, not superior understanding.
**Evidence:** Models consistently perform better on WEB than on GEN across tasks, including TWS. GEN has lower perplexity (higher fluency) but is harder for models, indicating the domain shift impacts performance.
**Break condition:** If performance on GEN converges to WEB after techniques like instruction tuning on synthetic data, the gap was due to distribution mismatch that can be aligned, not a fundamental limitation.

### Mechanism 3: Variety Advantage (en-IN > en-AU)
**Claim:** The consistent performance advantage on Indian English (en-IN) slang over Australian English (en-AU) likely reflects disparities in the representation of these varieties in the pre-training corpora, not intrinsic model capability differences.
**Mechanism:** en-IN, as a high-population, digitally prolific variety, may be over-represented in web-crawled pre-training data relative to en-AU. This leads to denser, more robust lexical representations for en-IN slang. The larger variety effect in TWS (accuracy +0.10) suggests that when forced to discriminate, the signal from training data prevalence becomes decisive.
**Core assumption:** Pre-training data volume and diversity for en-IN > en-AU.
**Evidence:** en-IN tasks outperform en-AU across all models and datasets, with TWS showing the largest disparity. The neighbor paper on sentiment classification benchmarks for English varieties implies en-AU is a known low-resource variety.
**Break condition:** If a model explicitly trained on balanced en-AU data (e.g., via continued pre-training) eliminates the performance gap, the mechanism is confirmed as a data representation issue.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Discriminative vs. Generative Task Design in Evaluation
**Why needed here:** The paper's core finding hinges on the stark difference between TWS (select correct slang from options) and TWP (generate the slang). Understanding this distinction is key to interpreting model capability—is the model knowledgeable or just good at recognition?
**Quick check:** Why might a model get 80% accuracy on a multiple-choice slang question but fail to generate the same slang in a sentence? What does this say about the nature of the knowledge being tested?

### Concept: Dataset Contamination and Synthetic Evaluation
**Why needed here:** The performance difference between WEB (web-sourced) and GEN (LLM-generated) datasets raises the specter of contamination. An engineer must understand that a model's high score on a dataset resembling its training distribution doesn't guarantee generalization.
**Quick check:** If a model performs better on examples from Urban Dictionary (WEB) than on LLM-generated scenarios (GEN) for the same slang, what are two plausible explanations? How would you design an experiment to distinguish between them?

### Concept: Language Varieties as Distinct Low-Resource Dialects
**Why needed here:** The paper treats en-AU and en-IN not as accents but as varieties with distinct vocabulary (slang). An engineer must grasp that "Indian English" is not "broken English" but a legitimate, rule-governed variety with its own lexicon. The en-IN vs. en-AU performance gap is a case study in intra-lingual low-resource challenges.
**Quick check:** What does it mean for a slang term like "prepone" to be part of the Indian English lexicon? How might this differ from an Australian English slang term like "smoko" in terms of standardization and digital footprint?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data layer (WEB/GEN) → Task formulation layer (masking, distractor sampling for TWS) → Model execution layer (encoder/decoder inference) → Metric layer (exact match, semantic similarity)

### Critical Path
Dataset creation (WEB collection/validation → GEN generation) → Instance generation (mask slang, create options) → Model inference (run all models on all tasks/datasets) → Aggregation & analysis (compute metrics, statistical comparisons, qualitative error coding)

### Design Tradeoffs
- **WEB vs. GEN:** Naturalistic WEB has authenticity/contamination risk; synthetic GEN offers controlled, diverse scenarios but may diverge from real usage.
- **TWP vs. TWS:** TWS tests recognition, TWP tests generation. TWS scores are high but may not reflect generative capability needed in open-ended applications.
- **Model selection:** Mix of encoder/decoder, small/large. Tradeoff: broader architectural coverage vs. depth of analysis per model. 8-bit quantization enabled local runs but may slightly affect output distribution.
- **Quantization:** 8-bit inference improves efficiency but introduces minor distribution shifts that could affect low-probability slang generation.

### Failure Signatures
- **High similarity / Low accuracy (in TWP):** Model captures semantic gist but substitutes generic term (e.g., "behemoth" for "absolute unit"). This is the dominant error.
- **Variety-specific drop (en-AU > en-IN):** Systematic underperformance on Australian slang, indicating data scarcity in pre-training.
- **Domain gap (GEN < WEB):** Performance collapse on generated scenarios, indicating reliance on spurious correlations in natural web text.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
To what extent do the observed performance disparities between Australian and Indian English slang generalize to other non-standard English varieties, such as African American Vernacular English or Nigerian English?
*Basis in paper:* The limitation section explicitly states that the study's focus on only en-AU and en-IN means observations about model scaling and performance gaps may not generalize to other non-standard varieties.
*Why unresolved:* The study evaluates only two specific English varieties, leaving unknown whether the patterns (e.g., better performance on en-IN) hold for varieties with different historical and sociolinguistic contexts.
*What evidence would resolve it:* A replication of the evaluation framework across a diverse set of English varieties, controlling for factors like speaker population and digital representation, to test the consistency of the variety effect.

### Open Question 2
What underlying factors in pre-training data or model architecture cause models to perform significantly better on Indian English slang compared to Australian English slang?
*Basis in paper:* Results show a consistent accuracy increase of 0.10 in TWS when moving from en-AU to en-IN, with the authors speculating this may be "due to greater representation or stylistic overlap in pre-training data."
*Why unresolved:* The paper does not investigate the specific content of training corpora or model representations to confirm why en-IN slang is more accessible; the explanation remains an untested hypothesis.
*What evidence would resolve it:* Quantitative analysis of slang term frequency in training corpora for both varieties, or probing studies to measure how models encode variety-specific lexical knowledge.

### Open Question 3
Why do language models exhibit a fundamental asymmetry where discriminative selection of slang (TWS) is effective but generative prediction of slang (TWP) fails, despite both tasks requiring contextual understanding?
*Basis in paper:* A key finding is that average accuracy jumps from ~0.03–0.04 in TWP/TWP* to 0.49 in TWS, highlighting a gap between recognition and generation of slang.
*Why unresolved:* The paper demonstrates the asymmetry but does not explore whether this stems from decoding constraints, vocabulary limitations, training objectives, or a lack of explicit slang representations.
*What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments isolating factors like output space (closed vs. open vocabulary), prompting strategies, and model architecture (encoder vs. decoder) to pinpoint the cause of the generative bottleneck.

### Open Question 4
What specific characteristics of synthetically generated scenarios (GEN) make them more challenging for models than web-sourced examples (WEB), despite GEN having lower perplexity (i.e., higher linguistic fluency)?
*Basis in paper:* Results show a performance drop on GEN vs. WEB across tasks (e.g., TWP accuracy 0.01 vs. 0.04), and the paper notes WEB examples have higher perplexity (less fluent) but are easier for models, suggesting contamination.
*Why unresolved:* While data contamination is proposed, the paper does not disentangle whether the difficulty arises from synthetic scenarios lacking authentic pragmatic cues, differing topic distributions, or reduced surface form variation.
*What evidence would resolve it:* Fine-grained error analysis comparing model failures on GEN and WEB, combined with controlled dataset manipulations (e.g., mixing sources, varying scenario diversity) to isolate the challenging features of GEN.

## Limitations
- The WEB and GEN datasets are not publicly released, and native speaker validation guidelines are unknown, preventing exact replication.
- Potential data contamination from Urban Dictionary (WEB) may inflate scores, while synthetic GEN may not reflect authentic usage distributions.
- Key causal claims (e.g., en-IN advantage due to pre-training data volume) are untested and rely on assumptions.

## Confidence
- Discriminative–generative asymmetry: High
- Domain effect (WEB > GEN): Medium
- Variety advantage (en-IN over en-AU): Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Fine-tuning gap reduction:** Continue pre-training on a balanced mixture of en-AU and en-IN slang contexts (both WEB and GEN). If TWP accuracy approaches TWS after targeted exposure, the asymmetry reflects training-data deficiency rather than an inherent architectural limit.
2. **Contamination-controlled test:** Construct a test set using slang terms coined after the model’s training cutoff (or artificially invented) and generate naturalistic usage examples via human writers. Compare performance on these truly unseen items vs. the original WEB. A diminished or absent domain gap would confirm that contamination inflated WEB results.
3. **Frequency–performance correlation:** Estimate relative frequencies of individual slang terms in large-scale web corpora (e.g., Common Crawl snapshots within the training window). Correlate these frequencies with model accuracy on both varieties. A strong positive correlation would substantiate the data-representation hypothesis for the en-IN advantage.
