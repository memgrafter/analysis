# Persistent Entropy as a Detector of Phase Transitions
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.09058
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09058
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: A model-independent theorem shows that persistent entropy asymptotically separates two phases when their limiting persistence diagrams differ by a macroscopic bar, and a finite-time detection method based on PE stabilization estimates critical parameters for real-world systems.

## Executive Summary
The paper establishes persistent entropy (PE) as a rigorous tool for phase transition detection. Its theoretical contribution is a model‑independent theorem proving an asymptotically non‑vanishing gap in PE between two phases if random persistence diagrams converge in probability to distinct limits and one phase contains a bar with lifetime bounded away from zero while the other does not. To handle finite data, the authors define a topological transition time \(t^*(\lambda)\) based on stabilization of PE in a sliding window, and estimate the critical parameter \(\lambda_c\) from the probability of stabilization within an observation horizon. Experiments on the Kuramoto synchronization transition, the Vicsek flocking transition, and neural network training across datasets of varying complexity and architectures confirm the theory: PE stabilizes and its variability collapses when a limiting low‑complexity topology emerges. Neural‑net results further reveal that convergence depends on dataset complexity and architectural inductive bias.

## Method Summary
The method builds on persistent homology: for a given control parameter \(\lambda\) and time \(t\), the system state is embedded as a point cloud (or distance matrix), a filtration (e.g., Vietoris–Rips) is constructed, and persistence diagrams are extracted (typically H0 for models, H1 for neural nets). Persistent entropy, the Shannon entropy of the normalized lifetimes of the bars, is computed. To detect a phase transition, PE is tracked over time or training epochs. A sliding window of length \(W\) and tolerance \(\varepsilon\) defines the *topological transition time* \(t^*(\lambda)\) as the earliest time when PE becomes stable (maximum deviation within the window ≤ \(\varepsilon\)). For each \(\lambda\), the probability \(p(\lambda) = \Pr(t^*(\lambda) \le T_{\max})\) is estimated over realizations; the critical parameter \(\hat{\lambda}_c\) is identified where \(p(\lambda)\) crosses a threshold (e.g., 0.8). The theoretical foundation is Theorem 1, which guarantees that if the limiting random persistence diagrams on the two sides of \(\lambda_c\) concentrate to different diagrams with a macroscopic lifetime difference, then the PE values separate asymptotically, provided diagrams lie in a subclass with controlled small‑scale structure where PE is continuous.

## Key Results
- Theorem 1: Under convergence in probability of random persistence diagrams to distinct limits and a macroscopic bar in only one phase, PE exhibits an asymptotically non‑vanishing gap between phases.
- Finite‑time detection: The stabilization‑based estimator recovers \(\lambda_c\) reliably for the Kuramoto model, Vicsek model, and neural network training.
- Experimental validation: PE stabilizes and variance collapses across realizations once the system enters an ordered phase (e.g., synchronization, flocking, or low‑complexity weight topology). Neural‑net experiments show that PE decreases monotonically with training loss for simple datasets (DIGITS) but not for CIFAR‑10 with MLP, highlighting the role of inductive bias.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Asymptotic separation arises because convergence in probability of the random persistence diagrams \(D_N(\lambda)\) to deterministic limits \(D^\pm\) on either side of \(\lambda_c\) transfers to PE values via continuity of PE on a subclass with controlled small‑scale structure. If one phase contains a bar with lifetime at least \(\delta>0\) while all bars in the other have lifetimes ≤ \(\epsilon\) with \(\epsilon \ll \delta\), the PE values differ by at least \(\Delta>0\) asymptotically.

### Mechanism 2
Finite‑time detection relies on the heuristic that PE stabilization on a sliding window indicates convergence of the underlying persistence diagram to its limiting form. The probability \(p(\lambda)\) that stabilization occurs within a fixed horizon \(T_{\max}\) therefore becomes a binary indicator of whether the ordered phase is dynamically accessible at that \(\lambda\).

### Mechanism 3
Continuity of PE on diagram classes with bounded total persistence and negligible arbitrarily short bars is essential; without this, diagram concentration might not yield a consistent PE gap. The theorem explicitly assumes diagrams lie in such a subclass (e.g., after applying a lifetime truncation threshold \(\tau\)).

## Foundational Learning
- **Persistent Homology**  
  *Why needed*: Provides persistence diagrams that encode multi‑scale topological features of the data; the entire method depends on these diagrams.  
  *Quick check*: What does a point in a persistence diagram represent, and what does its lifespan indicate?

- **Convergence in Probability for Random Diagrams**  
  *Why needed*: Formalizes the thermodynamic limit: as system size \(N\to\infty\), random diagrams concentrate around deterministic limits, enabling a clean separation between phases.  
  *Quick check*: Why is convergence in probability (rather than almost sure convergence) used for random diagrams in this framework?

- **Continuity of Functionals on Diagram Spaces**  
  *Why needed*: Guarantees that small perturbations in diagrams (e.g., from finite samples) lead to small changes in PE; without it, the asymptotic gap could vanish.  
  *Quick check*: Why isn’t persistent entropy uniformly continuous on the full space of persistence diagrams under the bottleneck distance?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Raw system states or network weights → distance matrix → Vietoris–Rips filtration → persistence diagram (H0 or H1) → lifetime extraction → normalized lifetime distribution → persistent entropy → time series of PE → sliding‑window stability check → topological transition time \(t^*\) → probability \(p(\lambda)\) → \(\hat{\lambda}_c\).
- **Critical path**: Data acquisition → persistent homology computation → PE calculation → stabilization detection → aggregation across realizations → critical parameter estimation.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *Filtration*: Vietoris–Rips is standard but noisy; α‑complexes may suit spatial data better.  
  - *Homology degree*: H0 captures connectivity; H1 captures loops – choice must match expected topological signature.  
  - *Lifetime truncation*: Removing short bars (\(\tau\)) improves PE continuity but may discard meaningful small features.  
  - *Sliding window*: window length \(W\) and tolerance \(\varepsilon\) balance sensitivity vs. false positives.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - PE never stabilizes within \(T_{\max}\) (no limiting diagram).  
  - High variance across realizations (dominant stochastic effects).  
  - Non‑monotonic PE vs. loss (multiple phases or inadequate inductive bias).
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. *Kuramoto model*: Vary coupling \(K\), build synchrony‑based distance matrix, compute H0 diagrams and PE, estimate \(\hat{K}_c\) via \(p(K)\).  
  2. *Vicsek model*: Vary noise \(\eta\), compute distance from orientation differences, obtain H0 diagrams and PE, estimate \(\hat{\eta}_c\).  
  3. *Neural networks*: Train MLP/CNN on DIGITS, MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST, CIFAR‑10; extract weight point clouds from hidden layers, compute H1 diagrams, track PE across datasets and architectures, and relate to training loss.

## Limitations
- Experimental parameters are underspecified (distance metrics, Vicsek settings, neural‑net hyperparameters, filtration bounds, truncation \(\tau\), window \(W,\varepsilon\)), hindering replication.
- Theoretical assumptions (convergence in probability, existence of a continuity subclass) may not hold for finite‑size data or real‑world noise, risking failure of the asymptotic gap.
- Reproducibility is limited: small number of realizations (5–10) and no public code make independent verification difficult.

## Confidence
- Asymptotic PE separation (Theorem 1): **Medium** – proof is rigorous but depends on strong, non‑trivial assumptions.
- Finite‑time detection via stabilization: **Medium** – heuristic lacks theoretical guarantee; performance sensitive to \(W,\varepsilon,\tau\).
- Experimental validation (Kuramoto, Vicsek, neural nets): **Low** – missing critical details and limited runs prevent robust replication.
