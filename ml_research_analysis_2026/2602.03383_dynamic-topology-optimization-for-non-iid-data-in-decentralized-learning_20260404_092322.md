# Dynamic Topology Optimization for Non-IID Data in Decentralized Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03383  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03383  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Morph dynamically selects dissimilar peers for incoming connections, achieving 68.9% test accuracy on CIFAR-10 (1.12× over baselines) and within 0.4 percentage points of the fully-connected upper bound.

## Executive Summary
Morph is a decentralized learning algorithm designed to combat poor model accuracy caused by non-IID data when using static communication topologies. It enables nodes to periodically optimise their incoming neighbours based on local model dissimilarity (cosine similarity) while maintaining a fixed in-degree. Peer discovery uses limited gossip to estimate similarities with unknown nodes, and a college‑admission‑style negotiation governs outgoing connections, respecting an out‑degree cap. Experiments on CIFAR‑10 and FEMNIST show significant improvements in accuracy, faster convergence, reduced inter‑node variance, and near‑zero isolated nodes compared to epidemic and static baselines—all without global coordination.

## Method Summary
Morph operates in synchronous rounds. Every Δr = 5 rounds, each node updates its neighbour selection. It first updates a similarity cache: direct cosine similarity with current incoming peers, and indirect estimates via gossip (each node exchanges a list of similarity values with its outgoing peers; unknown peer similarities are approximated by averaging at most five recent transitive reports). The node then builds a candidate set of known peers. It selects k peers with highest dissimilarity using softmax sampling over (1 – similarity) with a large temperature (β = 500) to introduce stochasticity. To maintain connectivity, it also adds r random peers (e.g., r = 1) uniformly from all node IDs. The resulting set V is the set of peers it requests to receive from. Nodes send these requests concurrently. In the same update round, each node gathers the requests it received from others and selects up to k outgoing peers based on highest dissimilarity (accepting replacements if over capacity). After a local SGD step, it sends its updated model to its outgoing peers and waits for models from those of its requested peers that accepted it. The received models, together with the node’s own half‑update, are uniformly averaged to produce the new local model. Between updates, the same incoming and outgoing sets are reused, and gossip continues to propagate similarity information.

## Key Results
- **Accuracy on CIFAR‑10**: With 100 nodes and Dirichlet α = 0.1, Morph reaches 68.9% test accuracy—1.12 × higher than state‑of‑the‑art baselines and only 0.4 percentage points below the fully‑connected upper bound.
- **Accuracy on FEMNIST**: Morph achieves 1.08 × higher accuracy than Epidemic Learning.
- **Robustness**: Morph reduces inter‑node variance, accelerates convergence, and keeps isolated nodes near zero compared to epidemic and static approaches.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Morph combats non‑IID data by continuously reshaping the communication graph to increase model diversity at each node. The dissimilarity‑driven selection ensures that nodes receive updates from peers with complementary information, mitigating the bias introduced by local data distributions. The college‑admission negotiation respects a fixed in‑degree (preventing overload) and an out‑degree cap (avoiding polarisation), while the inclusion of random peers guarantees that the graph remains connected despite the greedy selection. Gossip‑based similarity estimation enables nodes to discover and evaluate distant peers without global coordination. This combination yields a well‑connected, diverse topology that approximates the fully connected setting’s benefits at a fraction of the communication cost.

## Foundational Learning
- **Gossip protocols**: Lightweight, rumour‑spreading mechanisms for disseminating information across decentralized networks. *Why needed*: To estimate dissimilarities with unknown peers without central coordination. *Quick check*: Verify that similarity estimates converge to true values given indirect reports.
- **Consensus averaging (D‑PSGD)**: The core update rule where nodes average their model with neighbours’ after local SGD. *Why needed*: Ensures all models converge to a common optimum under proper mixing weights. *Quick check*: Simulate with static doubly‑stochastic weights; check agreement with centralised training on IID data.
- **Non‑IID data challenges**: In federated/decentralised settings, local data distributions diverge, causing model divergence and reduced accuracy. *Why needed*: Morph’s motivation. *Quick check*: Train D‑PSGD on Dirichlet‑partitioned CIFAR‑10; observe variance and accuracy drop vs. IID.
- **Cosine similarity for model comparison**: Measures angular alignment between parameter vectors; used to quantify model dissimilarity. *Why needed*: Guides diverse neighbour selection. *Quick check*: Compute pairwise cosine similarity between models trained on disparate datasets; expect near‑zero or negative on average.
- **Topology control in decentralised learning**: Adjusting communication links to improve convergence under constraints. *Why needed*: Static topologies fail under non‑IID data. *Quick check*: Compare D‑PSGD on random 3‑regular vs. fully‑connected graph with non‑IID data.
- **Softmax sampling with temperature**: Converts dissimilarity scores into a probability distribution; temperature β = 500 makes selection nearly greedy but retains stochasticity. *Why needed*: Allows occasional exploration of moderately dissimilar peers, avoiding brittle matching. *Quick check*: Ablate β values; observe accuracy and graph connectivity.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
`LocalSGD` -> `SimilarityCalc` -> `UpdateWantedSenders` -> `ExchangeRequests` -> `NegotiateOutgoing` -> `ModelExchange` -> `Aggregate`  
`GossipIngress` feeds `SimilarityCache` (ongoing)

**Critical path** (during an update round)  
1. Compute/estimate similarities for all known peers.  
2. Select V (incoming request targets) via softmax + random.  
3. Broadcast requests to V.  
4. Collect incoming requests; compute outgoing set O (top‑k dissimilar).  
5. Perform local SGD.  
6. Send model to O.  
7. Receive models from accepted requesters; set those peers as new incoming connections.  
8. Aggregate models; update local model.  
9. Propagate similarity lists (gossip) with each sent model.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Fixed in‑degree vs. connectivity**: Strict in‑degree caps prevent overload but risk isolation; mitigated by random peer additions.  
- **Gossip bandwidth**: Exchanging full similarity lists could be costly; limiting to top‑5 reports per unknown peer reduces overhead.  
- **Deterministic vs. stochastic selection**: Pure greedy choice may lead to fragmentation; softmax with high temperature introduces enough randomness to keep the graph connected.  
- **Update frequency Δr**: Larger Δr reduces negotiation overhead but may slow adaptation to changing data distributions; smaller Δr increases churn.

**Failure signatures**  
- Surge in isolated nodes (in‑degree = 0) indicates request saturation or poor random sampling.  
- Increasing inter‑node variance suggests topology fragmentation or stale similarity estimates.  
- Stagnant accuracy despite many rounds often points to a disconnected graph or overly aggressive random peers diluting diversity.  
- High communication overhead from gossip can be detected by monitoring message sizes; exceeding bandwidth limits requires increasing the top‑K or cache size.

**First three experiments to run**  
1. **Baseline replication**: Run Morph on CIFAR‑10 (100 nodes, α=0.1) with k=3, Δr=5, β=500, r=1. Measure final test accuracy and compare to reported 68.9%.  
2. **Connectivity ablation**: Vary r ∈ {0,1,2} while keeping k=3. After training, count isolated nodes (in‑degree = 0). Expect r≥1 to achieve near‑zero isolation (per Fig. 2).  
3. **Parameter sensitivity**: Sweep β ∈ {1,10,500} and Δr ∈ {1,5,20} on a smaller 20‑node setup to assess impact on convergence speed and final accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question**: How can Morph be extended to incorporate additional node‑level metrics such as latency, data diversity, or learning progress without compromising its decentralized operation or communication efficiency?  
   **Basis**: The conclusion states: "Future work may incorporate additional node‑level metrics, such as latency, data diversity, or learning progress, to enhance scalability, fairness, and adaptability in large, dynamic networks."  
   **Why unresolved**: Morph currently uses only model dissimilarity via cosine similarity for peer selection. Integrating new metrics requires mechanisms to combine them with existing signals, potentially increasing computational and communication overhead, and may destabilise the balance between diversity and connectivity that Morph achieves.  
   **Resolving evidence**: Systematic experiments on diverse datasets and network conditions, where metrics like latency or data diversity are explicitly modelled and integrated into Morph’s selection process, demonstrating improvements in scalability (accuracy vs. network size), fairness (reduced inter‑node variance), or adaptability to dynamic changes, with quantified overhead costs.

2. **Question**: What adaptive or theoretical approach can automatically configure Morph’s key parameters—the softmax temperature β and the similarity evaluation interval Δr—to ensure optimal performance across varying network scales and data heterogeneity levels?  
   **Basis**: The paper acknowledges that Δr “depends on system characteristics such as the number of nodes and dataset scale, and thus should be tuned per deployment,” and shows that β significantly impacts convergence, with lower values (e.g., β=1) outperforming the default β=500 in experiments.  
   **Why unresolved**: Morph requires manual tuning of β and Δr based on empirical ablation, lacking general guidelines or self‑adjusting mechanisms. This hinders plug‑and‑play deployment in real‑world decentralised systems where network size and data distributions are unknown or changing.  
   **Resolving evidence**: A derived relationship between β/Δr and factors like node count or non‑IID severity (via convergence analysis), or an online adaptation algorithm that monitors metrics like similarity variance or convergence rate to adjust parameters dynamically, validated across multiple datasets and scales to match or exceed manually tuned performance.

3. **Question**: Under what conditions does the use of cosine similarity between model parameters fail to capture relevant data heterogeneity, leading to suboptimal peer selection in Morph?  
   **Basis**: Morph assumes that cosine similarity per layer reflects data distribution differences for neighbour selection, but the paper does not empirically correlate this metric with actual data distributions (e.g., class imbalances) or test its validity across training stages and model architectures.  
   **Why unresolved**: In deep learning, model parameters may not linearly correspond to data space, especially with over‑parameterisation or during early/late training phases. If cosine similarity becomes uncorrelated with data dissimilarity, Morph’s diversity‑driven selection could misguide information flow, harming convergence.  
   **Resolving evidence**: Studies measuring both cosine similarity and direct data distribution metrics (e.g., KL divergence between local datasets) during training on various non‑IID settings, identifying thresholds or scenarios where the correlation breaks down, and correlating these with Morph’s accuracy and stability to define operational boundaries.

4. **Question**: How robust is Morph to large‑scale network dynamics, such as high node churn or Byzantine failures, in maintaining connectivity, low isolation rates, and high accuracy?  
   **Basis**: Experiments are confined to 50‑100 nodes; connectivity simulations for up to 2000 nodes are shown but without learning evaluation. The method assumes stable nodes and reliable gossip, with no consideration for malicious peers or frequent joins/leaves.  
   **Why unresolved**: Morph’s gossip‑based discovery and fixed in‑degree strategy may suffer from increased overhead or isolation in large, churning networks. Byzantine nodes could manipulate similarity reports or connection requests, fragmenting the graph or skewing peer selection; yet the paper does not address such adversarial dynamics.  
   **Resolving evidence**: Large‑scale simulations (e.g., 1000+ nodes) incorporating realistic churn models (random failure/join rates) and Byzantine attack models (e.g., false similarity reports), measuring Morph’s resilience via metrics like isolated node percentage, graph connectivity over time, and accuracy degradation compared to baseline decentralised methods.

## Limitations
- Gossip protocol details are underspecified: cache size (5), eviction policy, and ranking of propagated reports remain ambiguous, affecting similarity estimation accuracy.  
- The connection‑negotiation handshake is described as a one‑shot process, but the “college admission” algorithm implies an iterative deferred acceptance to guarantee fixed in‑degree. Our simplification may lead to temporary mismatches and variable in‑degrees.  
- Random peer selection assumes nodes know all peer IDs upfront; in a truly decentralised discovery process, this may not hold, potentially hindering connectivity in large networks.
