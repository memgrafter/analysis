# You Don't Need All That Attention: Surgical Memorization Mitigation in Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.00133  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00133  
- **Reference count:** 25  
- **Primary result:** GUARD, an inference-time framework that uses contrastive guidance with cross-attention attenuation to reduce memorization while maintaining image quality, lowering SSCD from 0.36 to 0.19 on SD v2.0 template memorization.

## Executive Summary
Memorization in text-to-image diffusion models—reproducing training data verbatim or near-verbatim—poses privacy and copyright risks. This paper presents GUARD, an inference-time method that modifies the denoising trajectory to steer generation away from memorized images while preserving prompt alignment and quality. GUARD employs a contrastive guidance mechanism: it repels from the memorized conditional prediction and attracts toward a safe, high-quality alternative target. A key instantiation dynamically detects "spike" token positions in the prompt’s cross-attention distribution using statistical outlier analysis, then attenuates attention at those locations. Evaluations on Stable Diffusion v1.4 and v2.0 show significant memorization reduction (lower SSCD) for both verbatim and template memorization, with image quality metrics (CLIP, FID) maintained or improved relative to prior state-of-the-art.

## Method Summary
GUARD operates during the reverse diffusion process without any model retraining. For each denoising step with noisy latent \(x_t\) and prompt \(p\), it produces three noise predictions: (1) unconditional \(ϵ_{\text{uncond}}\) (null prompt), (2) memorized conditional \(ϵ_-\) (standard cross-attention), and (3) positive target conditional \(ϵ_+\) where cross-attention logits for detected "spike" tokens are scaled by a factor \(\alpha < 1\). Spike tokens \(S(p)\) are identified on-the-fly from the memorized pass: for each token \(i\), compute the maximum attention mass across spatial queries \(M_i = \max_q \text{AttnScore}_{q,i}\), then derive a Z‑score \(Z_i = (M_i - \mu)/\sigma\) (mean \(\mu\), std \(\sigma\) over tokens); tokens with \(Z_i > \tau\) (e.g., \(\tau=3\)) form \(S(p)\). The final noise estimate combines these predictions as \(ϵ = ϵ_{\text{uncond}} + s·(ϵ_+ - ϵ_{\text{uncond}}) - r·(ϵ_- - ϵ_{\text{uncond}})\), where \(s\) and \(r\) are guidance scales. Logit attenuation is applied only in the U‑Net’s down‑sampling and middle blocks, across all heads and every denoising step. This design ensures a surgical intervention that disrupts memorization pathways while preserving overall prompt semantics.

## Key Results
- Reduces SSCD on SD v2.0 template memorization from 0.36 (prior best) to 0.19.  
- Maintains or improves CLIP score and FID compared to prior mitigation methods.  
- Effective for both verbatim and template memorization across Stable Diffusion v1.4 and v2.0.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** GUARD reduces memorization by steering the denoising trajectory away from the standard conditional prediction (which may reconstruct a memorized image) and toward an alternative high-quality target, using a contrastive guidance formula.
- **Mechanism:** At each denoising step \(t\), the predicted noise is computed as \(\hat{ϵ} = ϵ_θ(x_t, e_∅) + s(ϵ^+_θ(x_t, e_p) - ϵ_θ(x_t, e_∅)) - r(ϵ^-_θ(x_t, e_p) - ϵ_θ(x_t, e_∅))\). The \(+\) term attracts toward a safe, prompt-aligned target (e.g., one with modified cross-attention). The \(-\) term repels from the memorized conditional prediction.
- **Core assumption:** A safe, high‑quality alternative target exists that maintains prompt alignment but avoids memorized pathways. The repulsion alone could cause fidelity collapse without this attraction.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "GUARD adjusts the image denoising process to guide the generation away from an original training image and towards one that is distinct from training data while remaining aligned with the prompt."
  - [section 4] The equation and description of attractive‑repulsive dynamics explicitly define the two forces.
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers on attention mechanisms and memorization provide context but do not anchor this specific formulation.
- **Break condition:** If the positive target (\(ϵ^+_θ\)) is poorly selected (e.g., it significantly degrades quality or fails to be distinct from training data), the attraction term may not compensate for the repulsion, leading to artifacts or loss of prompt adherence.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A dynamic, per‑prompt statistical "spike detector" identifies specific token positions in the prompt’s cross‑attention distribution that are critical for memorization, enabling surgical intervention.
- **Mechanism:** For a given prompt \(p\), cross‑attention (CA) scores are extracted from the memorized conditional pass. For each token position \(i\), the maximum CA mass across spatial queries \(M_i = \max_q \text{AttnScore}_{q,i}\) is computed. A Z‑score \(Z_i = (M_i - \mu)/\sigma\) is calculated (using mean \(\mu\) and std \(\sigma\) over all tokens). Tokens with \(Z_i > \tau\) (e.g., \(\tau=3\)) are flagged as spikes, forming the set \(S(p)\).
- **Core assumption:** Memorization is mechanistically linked to outlier levels of attention mass on specific "trigger tokens," and these outliers are detectable via statistical thresholds on a per‑prompt basis.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.1] Analysis of CA distributions (Fig 1) shows memorized prompts exhibit sharp, instance‑specific spikes beyond just the EOT token.
  - [section 5.2] Explicitly defines the spike scoring method (\(M_i\), \(Z_i\), thresholding) and states it is "based on finding statistical outliers."
  - [corpus] Neighbors on memorization (e.g., "Memorized Images in Diffusion Models share a Subspace") support the link between attention and memorization but do not validate this specific statistical detection method.
- **Break condition:** The outlier threshold \(\tau\) may fail to capture true memorization‑critical tokens (false negatives) or flag benign high‑attention tokens (false positives), especially if the CA distribution under non‑memorized generation is more variable than assumed.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Attenuating cross‑attention logits at the detected spike positions (\(S(p)\)) disrupts the memorization pathway while preserving overall prompt alignment and generation quality.
- **Mechanism:** In selected U‑Net blocks (down/mid), for cross‑attention layers, the logits \(\ell_{q,i}\) for tokens \(i \in S(p)\) are scaled by a factor \(\alpha\) (typically \(\alpha < 1\)) before the softmax: \(\ell'_{q,i} = \ell_{q,i} · \alpha\). This suppresses attention to those tokens during the conditioning pass used for the positive target.
- **Core assumption:** Reducing the influence of spike tokens via logit scaling is sufficient to break the memorization retrieval mechanism, and the remaining CA pattern still conveys the essential semantic meaning of the prompt.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.2] "We attenuate the corresponding CA logits by a multiplicative factor, thereby suppressing its influence on generation."
  - [section 7] Results show CA attenuation alone outperforms prior CA redistribution methods (e.g., Ren et al.) on SSCD, especially for template memorization (Table 1).
  - [section A.3] CA attenuation on non‑memorized prompts shows no significant quality degradation, supporting the claim that it is surgical.
  - [corpus] Neighbors on attention compression (e.g., "SurfaceLogicKV") explore attention behavior but do not directly evidence this mitigation mechanism.
- **Break condition:** Over‑attenuation (too low \(\alpha\) or too many spikes) could remove necessary semantic components, harming CLIP score and FID. The selection of which U‑Net blocks/heads to modify (A.5, A.6) is critical; incorrect selection may fail to disrupt memorization or damage quality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Diffusion Model Denoising Process**  
  - **Why needed here:** GUARD operates during the reverse diffusion process. Understanding how \(ϵ_θ(x_t, t, cond)\) predicts noise and how this guides image reconstruction is foundational to seeing how modifying this prediction affects the generation trajectory.  
  - **Quick check question:** How does classifier‑free guidance (Eq. 3) combine unconditional and conditional noise predictions, and what role does the guidance scale \(s\) play?
- **Concept: Cross‑Attention in Text‑to‑Image Models**  
  - **Why needed here:** GUARD’s instantiation directly manipulates cross‑attention logits. Understanding how text tokens attend to spatial regions (Q, K, V formulation in Sec 2) is necessary to grasp why attenuating specific token logits affects generation.  
  - **Quick check question:** In a cross‑attention layer, what do the query, key, and value matrices represent, and how is the attention weight for a token \(i\) computed?
- **Concept: Memorization Types (Verbatim vs. Template)**  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper shows mitigation strategies must handle both. Verbatim involves near‑identical copies; template allows non‑semantic variation but retains core structure. Their CA patterns differ (Fig 1), which drives the need for dynamic spike detection.  
  - **Quick check question:** How does the CA mass on the EOT token differ between memorized prompts for verbatim vs. template memorization, and what does this imply for a fixed mitigation rule?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
1. Text Encoder (e.g., CLIP) encodes prompt \(p\) into token embeddings \(E\).  
2. \(E\) is fed into the U‑Net Denoising Network at its cross‑attention layers.  
3. In the U‑Net, cross‑attention layers compute attention logits \(\ell = QK^T/\sqrt{d_k}\); GUARD intercepts these logits before softmax.  
4. For the memorized conditional pass (\(ϵ_-\)), the full logits are used; the CA mass from this pass is analyzed by the Spike Detector to identify spike token positions \(S(p)\) via Z‑score thresholding.  
5. For the positive target pass (\(ϵ_+\)), the logits at positions in \(S(p)\) are scaled by \(\alpha (<1)\) before softmax.  
6. The Guidance Combiner takes the three noise predictions (unconditional, memorized conditional, positive target) and combines them using the formula \(ϵ = ϵ_{\text{uncond}} + s·(ϵ_+ - ϵ_{\text{uncond}}) - r·(ϵ_- - ϵ_{\text{uncond}})\).

Critical path:
1. For denoising step \(t\) with noisy latent \(x_t\) and prompt \(p\), run a batched forward pass with: (a) null prompt, (b) memorized prompt \(p\), (c) prompt \(p\) with CA logits to be attenuated.  
2. From stream (b), extract CA maps for all selected layers. Compute \(S(p)\) per layer using current \(x_t\) and \(e_p\).  
3. Apply attenuation \(\ell' = \ell · \alpha\) for \(i ∈ S(p)\) in stream (c) before softmax.  
4. Extract noise predictions: \(ϵ_θ\) (null), \(ϵ_-\) (memorized), \(ϵ_+\) (attenuated).  
5. Compute final \(\hat{ϵ}\) via Eq. (5) and step to \(x_{t-1}\).

Design tradeoffs:
- **Surgical vs. Efficacy:** Attenuating only down/mid blocks (A.5) preserves quality but may miss memorization signals in later blocks. Attenuating all heads vs. only "hot" heads (A.6) trades mitigation strength for potential quality preservation.
- **Dynamic Detection vs. Compute:** Per‑step spike detection (A.7) is robust but adds minimal overhead; fixed‑step strategies may miss evolving attention patterns.
- **Attraction Source:** CA attenuation provides a direct, prompt‑aligned target. Semantic paraphrasing (A.4) is less effective, suggesting structural CA manipulation is key.

Failure signatures:
- **High SSCD despite mitigation:** Spike detector failing to identify true \(S(p)\) (e.g., \(\tau\) too high, or blocks/heads omitted). Check CA mass distributions for missed outliers.
- **CLIP/FID drop:** Over‑attenuation (low \(\alpha\), too broad \(S(p)\)) or attenuating up‑blocks. Verify quality on non‑memorized prompts (A.3) to isolate issue.
- **Inconsistent performance across memorization types:** Using a fixed rule (e.g., always attenuate EOT) instead of dynamic detection (Sec 5.1). This fails for template memorization where EOT is not a reliable spike.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Baseline replication:** Run no‑mitigation generation on a known memorized prompt (from Webster’s 500) and compute SSCD to confirm memorization (>0.7) and inspect CA distribution (Fig 1 pattern).
2. **Ablation – Spike detection:** With CA attenuation fixed (\(\alpha\)), vary threshold \(\tau\) and log SSCD/CLIP. Expect a U‑curve: too low \(\tau\) (many spikes) hurts CLIP; too high \(\tau\) (few spikes) hurts SSCD.
3. **Ablation – Block selection:** Compare attenuating (down+mid) vs. all blocks. Expect (down+mid) to yield better CLIP/FID for similar SSCD, as up‑blocks are more about high‑frequency detail (A.5).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do other memorization mitigation methods affect generation quality on non‑memorized prompts?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Appendix A.3 explicitly leaves a systematic evaluation of other methods' impact on non‑memorized examples for future work.
- **Why unresolved:** Existing evaluations focus only on memorized prompts; it is unknown whether methods like RTA, Wen et al., Ren et al., or Han et al. degrade image quality or inadvertently increase memorization when applied to normal inputs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Run each baseline method on a large, diverse set of non‑memorized prompts (e.g., from LAION) and compare CLIP score, FID, and SSCD against a no‑mitigation baseline to detect any statistically significant changes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What are more effective instantiations of the GUARD framework beyond cross‑attention attenuation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states: "We hope future work develops even better instantiations of GUARD."
- **Why unresolved:** The current instantiation (CA‑in‑GUARD) uses cross‑attention logit attenuation as the positive target, but alternative designs (e.g., modifying other U‑Net components, using adaptive noise schedules, or combining multiple strategies) may yield better trade‑offs between memorization mitigation and image quality.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically explore various positive target mechanisms across multiple architectures and memorization types, evaluating performance via SSCD, CLIP, and FID to identify superior configurations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the principles of GUARD be integrated into training‑time or finetuning‑time memorization mitigation methods to improve their efficiency and robustness?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion and limitations propose: "an important area for future work is to integrate insights from our method into training‑time and/or finetuning‑time mitigation approaches."
- **Why unresolved:** Training‑time and finetuning‑time methods are often blunt or inefficient; GUARD’s contrastive guidance and dynamic spike detection could inform more surgical interventions during weight updates, potentially erasing memorized information from the model rather than merely masking it.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop new training/finetuning algorithms that incorporate attention spike regularization or contrastive objectives derived from GUARD, then measure memorization (SSCD) and utility (CLIP/FID) against existing unlearning methods to assess trade‑off improvements.

## Limitations
- **Dataset dependency:** Evaluation relies on a specific set of 500 memorized prompts and associated training images; generalizability to other models, datasets, or prompt distributions remains uncertain.
- **Spike detection robustness:** The Z‑score threshold \(\tau=3\) may not universally capture memorization‑critical tokens; false negatives/positives could occur, especially for non‑memorized generations with natural attention variation.
- **Attraction target quality:** No formal guarantee that the CA‑attenuated positive target remains semantically aligned and sufficiently distinct from training data; failure cases could lead to artifacts or loss of prompt fidelity.

## Next Checks
1. **Generalize Spike Detection:** Run GUARD on an independent memorization benchmark (different model/dataset) without retraining \(\tau\). Report SSCD/CLIP/FID and analyze whether the same statistical threshold identifies spikes, or if the method overfits to the Webster prompt distribution.
2. **Ablate the Attraction Term:** Set \(s=0\) in Eq. (5), removing the attractive force toward the attenuated target. Generate images for diverse memorized prompts and measure (a) SSCD (should remain low if repulsion alone de‑memorizes) and (b) CLIP/FID (likely collapse). This isolates the necessity of the positive target for utility preservation.
3. **Isolate CA Attenuation’s Role:** Implement a version where the "positive target" \(ϵ_+\) is generated by a different intervention (e.g., adding small Gaussian noise to \(x_t\) before the conditional pass, or using a random subset of tokens). If this alternative still yields low SSCD with high CLIP, it suggests the benefit may stem from general stochasticity rather than the specific CA attenuation mechanism.
