# Talk Freely, Execute Strictly: Schema-Gated Agentic AI for Flexible and Reproducible Scientific Workflows

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06394  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06394  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** The paper defines execution determinism (ED) and conversational flexibility (CF), scores 20 systems, finds an empirical Pareto front, and proposes schema-gated orchestration to decouple the trade-off.

## Executive Summary
Scientific workflows powered by LLM agents face a tension: conversational natural language enables flexible exploration but often sacrifices reproducibility. Through interviews with 18 domain experts, the study establishes execution determinism (ED) and conversational flexibility (CF) as dual, competing requirements. Reviewing 20 existing systems across five architectural groups reveals no system achieves high scores on both axes—an empirical Pareto front. However, systems that enforce mandatory schema validation on every tool invocation occupy a "schema-gated" zone (ED/CF ≥3.5). The authors propose separating conversational authority (LLM-driven intent interpretation) from execution authority (validated, versioned invocation objects) via a schema gate. This architecture aims to preserve natural language freedom while guaranteeing deterministic, replayable execution, with provenance captured by construction.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted a two-phase study. First, they interviewed 18 industrial R&D experts to derive requirements for ED and CF. Second, they systematically evaluated 20 agentic workflow systems using a multi-model LLM scoring protocol: for each system, five independent scoring sessions were run per model family (ChatGPT 5.2, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro) using a detailed prompt that queried documentation via web search. Scores (1–5) on ED and CF were aggregated, and inter-rater reliability was computed (Krippendorff’s α=0.80 for ED, 0.98 for CF). Systems were plotted on an ED/CF scatter, the Pareto front was identified, and architectural patterns were analyzed to explain the front. The schema-gated pattern was distilled from top-scoring systems and formalized into reference architecture principles.

## Key Results
- Expert interviews identified two non-negotiable requirements: execution determinism (ED) and conversational flexibility (CF).
- Multi-model scoring of 20 systems shows no system simultaneously achieves high ED and high CF—an empirical Pareto front exists.
- Systems enforcing mandatory schema validation on every tool invocation (IDs 8, 9) occupy a "schema-gated" zone (ED/CF ≥3.5), demonstrating that decoupling conversational and execution authority can break the trade-off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Separating conversational authority from execution authority via a mandatory schema gate decouples the ED/CF trade-off.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM freely converses to interpret intent, but execution occurs only after the proposed action resolves to a validated, versioned invocation object. This preserves CF during planning while guaranteeing ED at the execution gate.
- **Core assumption:** Execution determinism requires the final executable artifact to be a validated, replayable specification independent of conversational variability.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "schema-gated orchestration... separating conversational from execution authority via validated, versioned invocation objects"; Section 3.5: IDs 8 and 9 (schema-gated) score highest on both axes.
- **Break condition:** Insufficient registry coverage (missing tools/workflows) leads to execution refusal, forcing manual workarounds and reducing effective CF.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The empirical Pareto front arises because existing architectures couple conversational and execution authority.
- **Mechanism:** Systems with direct LLM execution authority (generative, tool-augmented) lack pre-execution validation (low ED). Systems with strict validation (workflow-centric) require explicit artifacts, limiting NL-driven exploration (low CF). The front is structural under this coupling.
- **Core assumption:** ED/CF properties stem from system architecture (validation scope and NL control), not from language models themselves.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2: "no reviewed system achieves both high flexibility and high determinism"; Sections 3.3–3.4 describe Paradigm I (generative/tool-augmented) and Paradigm II (workflow-centric) limitations.
- **Break condition:** Emergence of a system natively supporting free-form NL reasoning and workflow-level schema validation without manual bridging would shift the front.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Cross-step workflow-level schema validation (beyond tool-level) is necessary for scientific workflow determinism.
- **Mechanism:** Tool-level validation checks individual calls but misses cross-step mismatches (e.g., training on non-existent columns). Workflow-level validation enforces graph acyclicity, inter-step type compatibility, and parameter resolution before any computation runs.
- **Core assumption:** Scientific workflows involve multi-step dependencies; determinism requires validating the entire DAG.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4: "scientific workflows typically chain multiple steps with cross-step dependencies"; Section 5.3.2: workflow schema enforces acyclicity and type compatibility.
- **Break condition:** If all workflows are simple linear chains without data dependencies, workflow-level gating adds unnecessary overhead—contrary to interviewed domains.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Execution Authority**  
  *Why needed here:* The analysis hinges on identifying which component decides the final executable artifact. Misidentification leads to incorrect ED scoring.  
  *Quick check:* For a system, what is the last machine-consumable specification (script, invocation, config)? Who produces it?

- **Concept: Validation-Scope Spectrum**  
  *Why needed here:* The five architectural groups are defined by *where* validation occurs (none, tool-level, workflow-level). Understanding this spectrum is key to mapping the ED/CF trade-off.  
  *Quick check:* Does validation happen pre-execution? At what granularity (call, workflow, none)? Mandatory or advisory?

- **Concept: Provenance as a Structural By-Product**  
  *Why needed here:* Schema-gated systems capture provenance by construction (each run is a validated, versioned object), not as an afterthought. This is a boundary property.  
  *Quick check:* Is the execution record an explicit, queryable artifact (run ID, parameter snapshot) or an ad-hoc log?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  User -> Conversational Layer (LLM Orchestrator) -> Schema-Gated Gate -> Validated Registry -> Execution Engine -> Shared Context -> User

- **Critical path:**  
  User NL query -> LLM proposes `search_workflows` (validated platform action) -> Registry returns candidates -> User selects -> LLM proposes `get_parameters` -> Schema-loaded -> Dialogue fills parameters -> **Gate validates full invocation** -> Engine executes -> Results return to context -> Next turn.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Coverage vs. Determinism:** Registry must be curated and complete; gaps cause refusals, hurting effective CF. Mitigation: tiered registries, LLM-assisted schema drafting.  
  - **Clarification friction vs. Safety:** Mandatory pre-execution validation surfaces missing/invalid fields as conversational prompts, slowing high-parameter workflows. Mitigation: pre-populate from metadata, carry forward prior values.  
  - **Schema maintenance vs. Evolvability:** Schemas must evolve with tools, requiring versioning, backward compatibility policies, and automated testing.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Gate rejection:** Repeated "I need parameter X" or "value Y is invalid" without execution—signals schema-coverage gap or poor parameter completion.  
  - **Registry stagnation:** Frequent "workflow/tool not found" responses—indicates insufficient domain coverage.  
  - **Provenance gaps:** Execution records missing parameter snapshots or workflow version—misconfigured engine or schema evolution without backward compatibility.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Multi-model scoring replication:** Use the paper’s prompt template (§C) on 5 additional systems to confirm rubric reliability across models and domains. Measure Krippendorff’s α.  
  2. **Schema-gated prototype:** Implement the reference architecture for a narrow domain (e.g., materials property prediction). Measure: (a) % of user queries resolving to a validated invocation without human workaround, (b) average clarification turns per execution, (c) provenance completeness score.  
  3. **Coverage stress test:** Systematically remove tools/workflows from the registry and measure task-completion rate drop vs. baseline. Identify "coverage cliffs" where small removals cause large performance falls.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Generalizability of requirements:** Are ED and CF, derived from a convenience sample of industrial R&D stakeholders, generalizable to the broader scientific community, including generative/ad hoc tooling users?
2. **Registry governance model:** What governance model (centralized, community-extensible, tiered) optimally balances stability, rapid capability addition, and trustworthy coverage?
3. **Empirical validation of decoupling:** Does a deployed schema-gated system achieve simultaneous high ED and high CF, or do conversational friction, coverage gaps, and maintenance costs erode the benefits?
4. **LLM scoring protocol validity:** Can the multi-model LLM scoring protocol be reliably generalized as an alternative to human expert panels for architectural assessment?
5. **Cross-domain effectiveness:** Does the schema-gated pattern maintain high ED/CF across scientific domains beyond materials science (e.g., bioinformatics, food science)?

## Limitations
- Exact system versions/configurations scored are unspecified; reproduction may drift from original conditions.
- Scoring relies on LLM interpretation of documentation, not runtime behavior; actual ED/CF may differ.
- Schema-gated threshold (≥3.5) is data-driven but not theoretically justified; sensitivity to scoring nuances unknown.
