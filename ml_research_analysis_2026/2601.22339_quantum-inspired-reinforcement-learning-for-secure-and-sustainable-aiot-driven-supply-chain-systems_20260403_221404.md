# Quantum-Inspired Reinforcement Learning for Secure and Sustainable AIoT-Driven Supply Chain Systems

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2601.22339  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22339  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Achieved a peak episode reward of 20.51 using 3 spins and learning rate 10⁻⁴, outperforming PPO, DDQN, and model-based baselines (GRAPE, MPC).

## Executive Summary

The paper tackles the multi‑objective optimization of AIoT‑driven supply chains, aiming to simultaneously enhance security, reduce carbon emissions, and improve operational efficiency. It introduces a quantum‑inspired reinforcement learning framework that represents the supply chain as a controllable spin‑chain (XY Hamiltonian), enabling the RL agent to manipulate entangled degrees of freedom that capture complex interdependencies. By combining an ensemble of DQN and PPO agents and using a sliding‑window normalized multi‑objective reward, the method achieves superior performance and robustness in simulation, demonstrating a promising direction for secure and sustainable supply chain management.

## Method Summary

The proposed system models the supply chain state as the wavefunction of an N‑spin XY chain whose local control fields are modulated by real‑time AIoT signals (inventory levels, security alerts, CO₂ metrics). The environment (`SecuredGreenSCSEnv`) evolves the quantum state under a time‑dependent Hamiltonian, adds sensor noise, and computes three raw reward components: fidelity to a target state, a security score, and a carbon penalty. These components are normalized over a sliding window to maintain comparable scales, then combined with coefficients (1.0, 1.0, 0.5) to form the final reward. An ensemble agent merges a Double‑DQN (with experience replay) and a PPO agent (with clipped policy updates); the ensemble weight is adapted based on each agent’s recent cumulative reward. Training follows standard RL loops for 1000 episodes, with the best performance observed for N=3 spins, learning rate 10⁻⁴, and a discount factor 0.95.

## Key Results

- **Peak reward:** 20.51 (N=3, LR=10⁻⁴) versus 20.20 for PPO, 19.60 for DDQN, and lower scores for GRAPE/MPC.  
- **Robust convergence:** The ensemble method shows stable learning curves and graceful degradation under quantum noise (bit‑flip, depolarizing) compared to baselines.  
- **Optimal coefficients:** Ablation confirms (α₁, α₂, α₃) = (1.0, 1.0, 0.5) best balances fidelity, security, and carbon reduction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Quantum Spin‑Chain Analogy

**Claim:** The quantum spin‑chain analogy (XY Hamiltonian) effectively encodes complex, interdependent supply chain dynamics into a unified state representation.  
**Mechanism:** The system’s quantum state |ψ(t)⟩ evolves under the Hamiltonian H_dyn(t), where local control fields B_n(t) are modulated by AIoT sensor inputs (inventory levels, security alerts, carbon metrics). This maps classical supply chain variables to quantum degrees of freedom, allowing the RL agent to manipulate a coupled system that implicitly captures non‑linear interactions via quantum entanglement and Hamiltonian dynamics.  
**Core assumption:** The spin‑chain’s controllability (Lemma 1) and approximate reachability under noise (Lemma 2) ensure that the encoded supply chain states are navigable by the learned policy, and that the quantum analogy preserves essential operational constraints better than decoupled classical models.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section III‑B defines the XY Hamiltonian (Eq. 1) and state space; the abstract states the coupling; corpus shows moderate relevance (avg FMR 0.407).  
**Break condition:** If the mapping between supply chain metrics and quantum state parameters becomes non‑injective or loses critical operational semantics (e.g., security thresholds cannot be uniquely encoded in spin configurations), the analogy fails to represent the true decision manifold.

### Window‑Normalized Multi‑Objective Reward

**Claim:** Window‑normalized multi‑objective reward stabilizes learning by preventing any single objective from dominating due to scale disparities.  
**Mechanism:** At each timestep, raw components F, R_sec, C_co2 are min‑max normalized over a sliding window W to [0,1]. The reward r_t = α₁·bF_t + α₂·bR_sec_t - α₃·bC_co2_t then forms a commensurate trade‑off. This adaptive scaling ensures gradient updates remain balanced across objectives throughout training, even as absolute metric ranges shift.  
**Core assumption:** Sliding‑window statistics provide a reliable local estimate of operational baselines, so normalization does not distort the relative importance of objectives during an episode.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section III‑C gives explicit equations (45) and the chosen coefficient set from ablation (Table III); the abstract mentions window normalization; corpus lacks direct evidence, indicating novelty.  
**Break condition:** If the sliding window W is too short, normalization becomes volatile; if too long, it fails to adapt to non‑stationary conditions (e.g., sudden demand spikes), causing misaligned incentives.

### Ensemble RL (DQN + PPO)

**Claim:** Ensemble RL (DQN + PPO) yields robust convergence in noisy, multi‑objective settings by combining stable off‑policy value estimation with on‑policy policy gradient exploration.  
**Mechanism:** The ensemble policy π_θ(a|s) = ω_dqn·π_DQN + (1‑ω_dqn)·π_PPO merges DQN’s experience replay and target network stability with PPO’s clipped updates and trust region. Theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1‑3) assert that under bounded rewards and discounts, the convex combination inherits convergence to an ϵ‑optimal solution, while empirically the ensemble outperforms single agents under noise.  
**Core assumption:** The two sub‑policies learn complementary aspects of the MDP: DQN caches high‑value actions from replay, while PPO adapts smoothly to policy changes; their weighted mix mitigates individual failure modes (e.g., DQN’s overestimation, PPO’s sample inefficiency).  
**Evidence anchors:** Section III‑B presents Theorems 1‑3; Section IV‑B shows comparative results (Table II); corpus suggests ensemble RL is under‑explored in this context (low FMR).  
**Break condition:** If environment dynamics induce highly non‑stationary optimal policies where DQN’s off‑policy corrections diverge from on‑policy trends, or if PPO’s clipping excessively restricts updates, the ensemble may fail to converge or become unstable.

## Foundational Learning

### Quantum Controllability (Lie Algebra Rank Condition)

**Why needed here:** Justifies that the N‑spin chain can be driven to any target state (including those encoding supply chain goals) via available control fields B_n(t). Without this, the RL agent might face unreachable states.  
**Quick check:** Given the XY Hamiltonian with local z‑controls, what condition on the generated Lie algebra ensures full controllability of an N‑spin system?

### Markov Decision Process (MDP) Formulation for Physical Systems

**Why needed here:** To apply RL, the quantum‑AIoT system must be cast as an MDP with bounded rewards, finite horizon, and Markovian transitions (state includes |ψ⟩, security, CO₂). This bridges quantum dynamics to standard RL theory.  
**Quick check:** Why is including both quantum state components and classical AIoT metrics in the state vector necessary for the MDP to capture the true supply chain objectives?

### Multi‑Objective Reward Normalization

**Why needed here:** Raw fidelity (squared overlap in [0,1]), security score, and CO₂ penalty likely have different scales and variances. Normalization prevents gradient dominance by any one term, enabling balanced learning.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map

SecuredGreenSCSEnv (environment) -> DQN Agent (Double DQN) & PPO Agent -> Ensemble Agent (weighted combination) -> Reward Normalizer (sliding‑window min‑max scaling)

### Critical path

State s_t (quantum wavefunction components + security + CO₂) → Agent selects control action a_t (B_n adjustments) → Environment evolves |ψ⟩ via H_dyn (including noise) and updates security/CO₂ metrics → Computes raw fidelity, security, carbon → Normalizer scales components → Forms reward r_t → Stores transition in replay buffer → DQN update (Double Q‑learning) and/or PPO update (clipped surrogate) → Ensemble weight ω_dqn updated based on recent cumulative rewards → Next step.

### Design tradeoffs

- **Reward coefficients (α₁, α₂, α₃):** Ablation (Table III) shows (1.0, 1.0, 0.5) optimal. Higher α₃ (carbon penalty) reduces overall reward; imbalanced α₂ hurts security‑performance trade‑off.  
- **Number of spins N:** N=3 gives peak reward (20.51). Larger N increases control complexity and noise sensitivity (Fig. 4, Table IV). Choose N based on hardware limits and desired expressivity.  
- **Learning rate:** LR=10⁻⁴ yields stable convergence; higher LRs cause oscillations (Fig. 3). Tune via sweep.

### Failure signatures

- **Divergence at high N:** Reward collapse when N>4, indicating controllability bottleneck despite Lemma 1 (practical noise accumulation).  
- **Noise sensitivity:** Steep reward drop with increasing noise probability p (Fig. 7). If degradation is abrupt, ensemble weighting may need dynamic adjustment.  
- **Reward component imbalance:** If bF_t dominates (α₁ too high), policy ignores security/CO₂; check normalized component trajectories during training.

### First 3 experiments

1. **Learning rate sweep:** Run LRs {5e‑3, 1e‑3, 5e‑4, 1e‑4, 5e‑5, 1e‑5}. Identify LR with smoothest convergence and highest late‑episode average; expected best at 1e‑4.  
2. **Ablation over N:** Test N={2,3,4,5,6}. Record best and mean moving‑average reward; expected peak at N=3, decline for larger N.  
3. **Noise robustness check:** Inject bit‑flip, depolarizing, phase‑flip channels at p={0.0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2}. Compare average reward degradation against baselines; proposed method should degrade gracefully, maintaining higher median reward.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Hardware implementation:** The paper has not yet tested the framework on real AIoT or quantum‑analog hardware. Simulation may miss latency, device heterogeneity, and physical quantum noise characteristics, raising doubts about real‑world performance.  
2. **Noise calibration:** Current noise models are simple memoryless channels. Real systems exhibit dropout, jitter, and bursty congestion, requiring calibration of channel parameters to actual device error statistics.  
3. **Correlated and non‑Markovian noise:** Theoretical guarantees assume bounded, effectively Markovian disturbances. Memory effects could break controllability lemmas and destabilize ensemble convergence, an issue left unaddressed.  
4. **Scalability to large networks:** The multi‑objective reward design may become overly complex when scaling to large, interdependent supply‑chain topologies. The N‑ablation already shows performance decay for N>3, suggesting limited scalability.

## Limitations

- **Omitted implementation details:** Ensemble weighting rule, sliding‑window size, and PPO hyperparameters are unspecified, hindering exact replication.  
- **Weak quantum‑to‑classical justification:** The analogy between spin states and supply‑chain metrics lacks validation on real data; simulation may be oversimplified.  
- **Theoretical assumptions may not hold:** Bounded rewards, Markovian transitions, and noise models could be violated in practice, affecting guarantee validity.
