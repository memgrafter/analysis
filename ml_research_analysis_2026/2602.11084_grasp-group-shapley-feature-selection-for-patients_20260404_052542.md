# GRASP: group-Shapley feature selection for patients

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11084
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11084
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** GRASP produces smaller, more stable, and less redundant feature sets while maintaining or improving predictive accuracy for medical prediction tasks.

## Executive Summary
GRASP addresses unstable and redundant feature selection in medical prediction by combining SHAP-based feature attribution with group L21 regularization. It first derives group-level importance scores from SHAP values computed on a validation set using a pre-trained XGBoost model. These scores are transformed into penalty weights for a group L21-regularized logistic regression, which is solved via proximal-gradient optimization. On NHANES and UKB datasets, GRASP selected the fewest features (average 23 vs. 34–59 for baselines), achieved the highest selection stability (Adjusted Stability Measure 0.593), and lowest redundancy (VIF 2.942). Predictive performance was comparable or superior across multiple classifiers, with better calibration and clinically aligned risk stratification.

## Method Summary
GRASP operates in two stages. In Stage 1, an XGBoost model is trained on a training fold; SHAP values are computed on a held-out validation fold. For each predefined feature group, the mean absolute SHAP value is calculated to obtain a group importance score \(s_g\). These scores are then transformed via \(\omega_g = \exp(-s_g / \tau_0) / \sum_h \exp(-s_h / \tau_0)\) (with \(\tau_0=1\)) to yield normalized penalty weights. In Stage 2, a group L21-regularized logistic regression is solved: \(\min_\beta L(\beta) + \lambda \sum_g \omega_g \|\beta_g\|_2\). The optimization uses a proximal-gradient method: a gradient step on the smooth logistic loss followed by a group-wise soft-thresholding (proximal operator) and Armijo backtracking to determine step size. Groups with final Euclidean norm \(\|\beta_g\|_2 > 0\) are selected; the final feature set is the union of features in these groups.

## Key Results
- **Feature set size:** Average 23 features, significantly smaller than baselines (34–59).
- **Stability:** Highest Adjusted Stability Measure (ASM) at 0.593.
- **Redundancy:** Lowest average Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) at 2.942.
- **Predictive performance:** Comparable or superior accuracy (MCC ~0.44 for logistic regression), better calibration, and risk stratification; for lactate dehydrogenase, derived cutoff aligned best with clinical literature.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** GRASP achieves stable and interpretable feature selection by deriving group importance scores from SHAP values computed on a hold-out set using a pre-trained tree model (XGBoost).
- **Mechanism:** Feature-level SHAP values (\(|\text{SHAP}_{ij}|\)) are averaged within pre-defined clinical or biological feature groups (\(s_g\)). These group scores are then non-linearly transformed into penalty weights (\(\omega_g\)) for the L21 regularizer, such that more important groups receive a smaller penalty multiplier.
- **Core assumption:** SHAP values from a high-performing tree model on a validation fold provide a robust, model-agnostic estimate of intrinsic feature contribution to the prediction task, which can be reliably aggregated at the group level.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "distills group level importance scores from a pretrained tree model via SHAP"
  - [section 2.2] Explicit equations for aggregating SHAP into group scores (Eq. 2) and transforming them into penalty weights (Eq. 3).
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers on "Group Shapley" (e.g., arxiv:2501.03041) validate the conceptual utility of group-level Shapley attribution.
- **Break condition:** If the pre-trained tree model is poorly calibrated or overfits the training data, the derived SHAP values may not generalize, leading to misleading group importance scores and suboptimal feature selection.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The group L21 penalty enforces structured sparsity—entire feature groups are selected or discarded together—reducing redundancy and improving selection stability compared to element-wise LASSO.
- **Mechanism:** The regularizer \(\Omega = \sum_g \omega_g \|\beta_g\|_2\) applies the same penalty to all coefficients within a group. The proximal operator (Eq. 8) either shrinks the entire group subvector \(\beta_g\) proportionally or sets it to zero, based on its Euclidean norm relative to the penalty weight \(\tau \omega_g\).
- **Core assumption:** Features within a meaningful clinical or biological group are expected to exhibit multicollinearity or shared mechanistic relevance. Selecting them as a unit prevents the "ping-pong" effect where unstable selection chooses one proxy over another.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "enforces structured sparsity through group L21 regularized logistic regression"
  - [section 2.3] Defines the objective \(J(\beta) = L(\beta) + \lambda \sum \omega_g \|\beta_g\|_2\).
  - [section 3.2] Reports lower average VIF (2.942) and higher Adjusted Stability Measure (0.593) for GRASP vs. LASSO/SHAP, directly linking group sparsity to reduced redundancy and improved stability.
- **Break condition:** If the initial feature grouping is arbitrary or clinically meaningless, the group L21 penalty may force inclusion of irrelevant features as a block or exclude a relevant group because one weak member dilutes the group's overall importance score.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Proximal-gradient optimization with Armijo backtracking line-search provides a computationally efficient and theoretically sound method to solve the non-smooth, constrained optimization problem posed by the group L21 penalty.
- **Mechanism:** At each iteration, a gradient step is taken on the smooth cross-entropy loss (\(\nabla L\)), followed by application of the group-wise proximal operator (\(\text{prox}_{t_k \lambda \Omega}\)), which analytically solves the subproblem for each group \(\nu_g\) (Eq. 8). The step size \(t_k\) is adaptively chosen via backtracking to satisfy the descent condition (Eq. 9), ensuring convergence.
- **Core assumption:** The loss function \(L(\beta)\) (cross-entropy) is smooth with Lipschitz continuous gradient, and the group L21 penalty \(\Omega(\beta)\) is non-smooth but convex. This structure guarantees convergence of the proximal-gradient scheme.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [section 2.4] Details the full algorithm: gradient descent step (Eq. 6), proximal step with closed-form solution (Eq. 8), and Armijo backtracking condition (Eq. 9).
  - [corpus] The theoretical foundation (e.g., citing Armijo 1966, Vershynin 2012) is standard in convex optimization literature.
- **Break condition:** If the global regularization parameter \(\lambda\) is set incorrectly (too high or too low) via the heuristic (\(\lambda = \text{sd}(E)\)), the proximal steps may produce all-zero coefficients (over-regularization) or fail to induce sufficient sparsity (under-regularization), regardless of the solver's correctness.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values.**  
  **Why needed here:** Provides a theoretically grounded, model-agnostic measure of per-feature contribution to predictions. GRASP uses the absolute magnitude averaged over a validation set as a proxy for overall feature importance, which informs the group penalty weights.  
  **Quick check question:** If two features are highly correlated and one has a consistently higher mean |SHAP| value, how would GRASP's group aggregation (Eq. 2) treat their shared group's importance score?

- **Concept: Group L21 regularization (structured sparsity).**  
  **Why needed here:** Promotes selection/deselection of entire pre-defined feature groups. This directly addresses the paper's goal of reducing redundancy (multicollinearity) and improving stability, as correlated features within a group are either all in or all out.  
  **Quick check question:** Contrast the effect of an L1 penalty (which can select one feature from a correlated pair) with a group L21 penalty (which tends to select both or neither) on the stability of a final feature set across resampled datasets.

- **Concept: Proximal-gradient algorithm.**  
  **Why needed here:** The objective combines a smooth loss (logistic regression cross-entropy) with a non-smooth regularizer (sum of group norms). The proximal-gradient method decouples these: a gradient step on the smooth part, then a proximal step that handles the non-smooth part exactly for each group.  
  **Quick check question:** What is the specific closed-form solution of the proximal operator for the L2 norm (prox<sub>τ||·||2</sub>), and why does it enable "soft" group-wise thresholding?

- **Concept: Feature grouping strategy.**  
  **Why needed here:** The efficacy of group L21 depends entirely on the semantic coherence of the groups. In GRASP, groups are not learned but must be pre-defined (the paper mentions "clinical or biological" groups). The grouping strategy is a core, non-algorithmic design choice.  
  **Quick check question:** What would be a valid way to form groups for the NHANES/UKB features, and what would be an invalid way that would likely diminish GRASP's performance?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Input (X_train, y_train, X_val, y_val, groups G) → Train XGBoost on (X_train, y_train) → Compute SHAP values on X_val → Aggregate per-group mean |SHAP| → Transform to penalty weights ω_g → Initialize β_0 → Proximal-gradient loop: compute gradient ∇L(β), take gradient step β^t = β^{t-1} - t_k ∇L, apply group-wise proximal operator → check convergence → Output selected groups { g : ||β_g||_2 > 0 } and final β.  

- **Critical path:** The pipeline depends on sequential steps: data splitting, XGBoost training, SHAP computation, group aggregation, weight transformation, proximal-gradient optimization, and thresholding. Failure at any step (e.g., poor XGBoost model, incorrect grouping) propagates to the final selection.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Stability vs. computational cost: Two-step process (XGBoost + optimization) is more expensive than LASSO but aims for more stable selections. SHAP computation scales poorly for very high-dimensional data.  
  - Group coherence vs. predictive granularity: Strong, coherent groups yield interpretable selections but may miss features that don't fit neatly; overly broad groups dilute penalty selectivity.  
  - SHAP as importance oracle: Transferring importance from a complex model to a linear model assumes the ranking is stable and transferable across model classes.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High redundancy in output (high VIF): Likely due to poorly defined groups that fail to capture collinearity or λ set too low.  
  - Extremely small or empty feature set: λ too high or ω_g inverted (important groups over-penalized).  
  - Non-converging proximal-gradient loop: Step size or Armijo parameters inappropriate, or loss ill-conditioned.  
  - Poor predictive performance vs. baselines: Misleading SHAP values (e.g., from overfitted XGBoost) or misaligned grouping.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Replication of NHANES results:** Implement GRASP as described and verify that on NHANES (with 76 overlapping features) it achieves mean feature count ~23, ASM >0.5, VIF <~3, and comparable classification accuracy (MCC ~0.44 for LR).  
  2. **Ablation – SHAP weighting:** Replace SHAP-derived ω_g with uniform weights (ω_g = 1/G) and measure changes in feature set size, stability (ASM), and redundancy (VIF). Expect degradation, confirming the benefit of SHAP weighting.  
  3. **Stress test on arbitrary grouping:** Randomly shuffle the 76 features into 10–20 groups of similar size, then run GRASP. Compare metrics against the paper's presumed clinical grouping; expect a drop in stability and increase in redundancy, highlighting the importance of coherent initial grouping.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** How does GRASP's computational efficiency scale with much higher-dimensional feature spaces (e.g., thousands to tens of thousands of features), and can its optimization be accelerated for such scenarios?  
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states: "Future studies could improve efficiency on high-dimensional datasets."  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper evaluates GRASP on a fixed set of 76–86 overlapping features between NHANES and UKB. Its proximal-gradient optimization and SHAP computation steps were not stress-tested on genuinely high-dimensional data (p >> n), which is common in omics or imaging.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmark experiments on datasets with thousands of features (e.g., genome-wide association studies, high-resolution medical images) reporting runtime, memory usage, and convergence behavior of GRASP versus baselines.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** To what extent does GRASP's performance depend on the initial grouping of features, and can a data-driven grouping strategy (e.g., based on correlation or domain ontologies) improve its stability or clinical relevance compared to the manually defined groups used in the paper?  
- **Basis in paper:** The method assumes predefined disjoint groups (e.g., laboratory tests, survey items) but does not explore sensitivity to group definitions. The paper states the group strategy "allows highly correlated features to enter or leave together," implying group structure is a key design choice left unexamined.  
- **Why unresolved:** Group definitions are a critical upstream decision that could drastically alter selected features. The paper used domain-informed groups but provided no ablation on grouping schemes (random, correlation-based, hierarchical).  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A sensitivity analysis comparing GRASP's feature sets and stability metrics across multiple grouping strategies (manual, clustering-based, single-feature groups) on the same datasets.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** Does GRASP's integration of SHAP values from a complex tree model (XGBoost) into a linear logistic regression penalty introduce subtle biases or instabilities, and how does this two-stage separation of attribution and selection compare to an end-to-end approach?  
- **Basis in paper:** The method explicitly decouples SHAP computation (on a validation fold of a pretrained XGBoost model) from the group L21 logistic regression. The paper states this is to "unify" prediction and interpretability but does not analyze the propagation of SHAP variance or the impact of the tree model's hyperparameters into the final selection.  
- **Why unresolved:** SHAP values themselves can be noisy or unstable, especially with correlated features. The paper uses SHAP as a fixed importance oracle; it does not assess whether different tree models or SHAP explanation settings yield different group importances and thus different final feature sets.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An analysis varying the tree model (e.g., Random Forest, different XGBoost parameters) and SHAP settings (e.g., `approximate` vs. `exact`), measuring resulting changes in GRASP's selected feature sets and stability.

**Open Question 4**  
- **Question:** For which specific clinical domains or prediction tasks (beyond all-cause mortality) does GRASP's group-based, SHAP-weighted regularization provide the greatest advantage in deriving clinically parsimonious and interpretable models?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper demonstrates GRASP on mortality prediction in NHANES/UKB and highlights LDH cutoff alignment. However, it does not generalize findings to other outcomes (e.g., disease diagnosis, treatment response) or data types (e.g., time-series, imaging). The claim of "clinically meaningful insights" is supported by a single biomarker example.  
- **Why unresolved:** Medical prediction tasks vary widely in feature correlation structures, signal sparsity, and clinical interpretability needs. GRASP's design (group sparsity + SHAP weighting) may be particularly suited for some tasks but not others without validation.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Application of GRASP to diverse medical datasets (e.g., cancer subtyping, sepsis prediction) with expert clinician evaluation of the selected features' clinical plausibility and utility compared to other methods.

## Limitations
- **Undefined feature grouping:** The exact partition of features into groups is not described, blocking reproduction and assessment of group coherence.
- **Missing solver details:** Proximal-gradient hyperparameters (step size, backtracking, tolerance) are omitted, risking non-reproducible results.
- **SHAP oracle unvalidated:** No evidence that SHAP values from a single XGBoost model are stable or transferable across models or datasets.
