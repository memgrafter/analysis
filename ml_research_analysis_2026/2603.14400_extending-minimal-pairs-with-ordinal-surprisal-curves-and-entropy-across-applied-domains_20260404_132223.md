# Extending Minimal Pairs with Ordinal Surprisal Curves and Entropy Across Applied Domains

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14400  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14400  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** This work extends the minimal‑pairs paradigm to ordinal‑scaled classification by measuring the surprisal (negative log probability) that language models assign to each point on a rating scale, producing interpretable surprisal curves and entropy‑based uncertainty metrics that scale with model size.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a novel, generation‑free evaluation framework for language models on ordinal classification and scoring tasks. Instead of prompting the model to generate an answer, it measures the surprisal that the model assigns to each possible single‑token completion representing a point on an ordinal scale (e.g., “1” through “9”). The token with the minimum surprisal is taken as the model’s preferred rating, while the entropy of the renormalized distribution over the scale tokens quantifies the model’s uncertainty. Applied across four diverse domains—SETS entity scoring, causal statement identification, figurative language detection, and deductive coding—the approach yields clear surprisal curves with minima near expected scores and successfully flags genuinely ambiguous items. Performance improves predictably with model size (e.g., 14B‑parameter models achieve MAE as low as 1.43 on SETS, 89% directional accuracy on causal statements, and 95% discrimination on figurative language). The method is computationally efficient, avoids pitfalls of text generation, and provides an information‑theoretic lens into model judgments.

## Method Summary
For each evaluation item, a prompt is constructed that ends immediately before a single‑token slot representing a rating on a predefined ordinal scale. The model is run in a single forward pass to extract the logits at that position for the tokens corresponding to each scale point (e.g., the tokens “ 1”, “ 2”, … “ 9”, including necessary leading spaces). These logits are converted to probabilities via a softmax restricted to the set of target tokens, thereby renormalizing the mass on the scale while discarding irrelevant tokens. Surprisal for each scale position is computed as –log P(scale token | context). The argmin of surprisal yields the model’s implicit rating. Entropy of the restricted distribution is calculated as –Σ P log P and serves as an uncertainty measure. No training is involved; the approach relies solely on the model’s native probability outputs. The framework allows systematic variation of context (none, minimal, full), scale granularity (5‑point or 9‑point), and anchor wording, enabling fine‑grained analysis of model behavior.

## Key Results
- Surprisal curves are consistently interpretable across all four domains, with clear minima aligning with human‑expected ratings.
- Entropy reliably distinguishes items that are genuinely ambiguous (e.g., correlational statements) from clear‑cut cases.
- Performance scales strongly with model size: 14B‑parameter models achieve MAE 1.43–1.45 on SETS, directional accuracy up to 89% on causal ordinal tasks, and figurative discrimination up to 95%.
- Instruction‑tuned models often underperform their base counterparts on raw surprisal metrics, indicating that alignment training can distort the probability landscape for single‑token rating tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper explains the efficacy of the ordinal surprisal approach through three core mechanisms:

**Mechanism 1: Direct probability‑based rating inference**  
*Claim:* Ordinal surprisal curves can reliably identify a model’s preferred classification or score on a rating scale without text generation.  
*How:* Instead of prompting for a generated answer, the framework measures the negative log probability (surprisal) that the model assigns to each possible single‑token completion representing a point on the ordinal scale (e.g., “1”, “2”, “3”). The token with the minimum surprisal is taken as the model’s implicit “preferred” rating, leveraging the model’s internal probability distribution directly.  
*Core assumption:* The model’s probability distribution over scale tokens, conditioned on the task context, reflects a coherent internal judgment about the correct or most appropriate rating; the argmin of surprisal corresponds to the model’s most “natural” response.  
*Break condition:* This fails if the model’s token probabilities are dominated by surface‑form biases (e.g., a prior preference for “5”) or if the true preferred answer is not among the candidate tokens.

**Mechanism 2: Entropy as an uncertainty signal**  
*Claim:* The entropy of the renormalized probability distribution over the predefined ordinal scale tokens provides a domain‑agnostic, model‑intrinsic measure of uncertainty or ambiguity for a given item.  
*How:* After extracting logits for all target scale tokens, probabilities are renormalized over this restricted set to form a valid distribution P_A. The Shannon entropy H(A|c) = –Σ P_A(a_i|c) log P_A(a_i|c) is computed. High entropy indicates an even spread of mass (high uncertainty/ambiguity); low entropy indicates a peaked distribution (strong preference).  
*Core assumption:* The spread of probability mass over the forced‑choice set of scale tokens reflects the model’s subjective uncertainty about the correct rating for that specific item, independent of absolute probability magnitudes.  
*Break condition:* Entropy is confounded if the restricted set does not contain the model’s true preferred output, or if high entropy arises from model error rather than genuine ambiguity.

**Mechanism 3: Scaling and instruction‑tuning effects**  
*Claim:* Performance on ordinal surprisal‑based classification scales predictably with increased model scale (parameter count), but instruction tuning can non‑monotonically affect results.  
*How:* Larger models develop richer internal representations and sharper probability distributions, leading to lower errors. However, instruction tuning (SFT, RLHF) can reshape the base model’s probability landscape, sometimes flattening curves or introducing biases that harm surprisal‑based metrics relative to the base model.  
*Evidence:* Experiments show a clear relationship between model size and accuracy (e.g., 14B variants achieve lowest MAE). The 14B base model often outperforms its instruction‑tuned counterpart.  
*Break condition:* The positive scaling trend may plateau or reverse for extremely small models or for tasks where instruction tuning optimizes for chat‑style outputs that conflict with single‑token numeric scale completions.

## Foundational Learning
### Information‑Theoretic Surprisal & Entropy
- **Why needed here:** The framework relies on measuring surprisal to find the most natural completion and entropy to quantify uncertainty over scale options. Without these, the core interpretation of curves and ambiguity detection is impossible.
- **Quick check question:** If a model assigns P("5")=0.8 and P("1")=0.1 on a 1–5 scale, what are the surprisal values and the entropy? Which rating is preferred? Is the model more or less certain than if probabilities were [0.5, 0.5]?

### Tokenization Sensitivity & Surface Form Competition
- **Why needed here:** Surprisal is computed on specific tokens; the same semantic rating can be tokenized differently (e.g., “5” vs. “ 5”). Surface biases can affect the probability distribution and thus the argmin and entropy.
- **Quick check question:** Why must you use a leading space for “ 5” instead of “5” in most model tokenizers? What hazard does this introduce when comparing surprisal across different scale formats (e.g., “Yes”/“No” vs. “1”/“0”)?

### Forced‑Choice Renormalization
- **Why needed here:** The model’s full vocabulary distributes mass over thousands of tokens. To obtain a meaningful distribution over only the scale tokens, we must exponentiate the logits for those tokens and renormalize. This preserves the argmin while discarding irrelevant mass.
- **Quick check question:** Given raw logits for tokens “1”=2.0, “2”=1.5, “3”=0.5, and “ apple”=5.0, what are the renormalized probabilities for A={1,2,3}? Does the argmin change after renormalization?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Task & Scale Definer → Prompt Constructor → Logit Extractor → Probability Normalizer → Surprisal & Entropy Calculator → Curve & Metric Analyzer  

**Critical path:** The sequence above must be followed in order. The most fragile links are the Logit Extractor and Probability Normalizer, which require correct token identification for scale points (including exact formatting like leading spaces) and proper renormalization over the restricted token set.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Scale granularity:* Finer scales (e.g., 9‑point) capture nuance but risk multi‑token numbers and noisier distributions; 5‑point scales are safer for single‑digit tokens.  
- *Anchor wording:* Bipolar (e.g., 1=non‑causal, 5=causal) vs. unipolar (1=low, 5=high) affects model priors and must be consistent for comparison.  
- *Context provision:* Varying context (none, minimal, full) can disambiguate but may also narrow probability mass and reduce discriminability for already clear items.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Flat or inverted surprisal curves (no clear minimum or universal preference for “1”) indicate the model fails to understand the task or the scale tokens.  
- Context‑insensitivity (identical curves across context levels) suggests the model ignores contextual clues.  
- Entropy‑accuracy dissociation: low entropy (high confidence) paired with incorrect ratings indicates poor calibration.  
- Formatting sensitivity: results changing dramatically between “ True”/“True” or “1”/“ 1” reveal surface‑form competition dominating.

**First 3 experiments (sanity checks):**  
1. **Binary Causal Classification** – Use minimal context with True/False completions to validate the core mechanism on a clear‑cut binary task.  
2. **SETS Homonym Scoring** – Use moderate context with 1–9 numeric scale across social/ecological/tech dimensions to test ordinal scaling and context sensitivity.  
3. **Figurative Language Paired Discrimination** – Use minimal/full context with 1–5 metaphor intensity scale to test discrimination between figurative and literal minimal pairs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Factual knowledge evaluation** – Can surprisal reliably assess factual retention compared to standard QA benchmarks? The current work focuses on classification/scoring tasks and does not test on factual recall datasets.  
2. **Entropy as calibrated ambiguity indicator** – Does entropy correlate with genuine human‑rated ambiguity across domains? The paper provides suggestive evidence but lacks systematic validation with human difficulty judgments.  
3. **Comparison to prompting‑based classification** – How does the surprisal approach fare against zero‑shot/chain‑of‑thought prompting in accuracy, robustness to prompt variation, and computational efficiency? No head‑to‑head experiments are reported.  
4. **Optimal ordinal scale design** – What choices (number of points, anchor wording, bipolar/unipolar framing) maximize discriminability and calibration? Only 5‑ and 9‑point scales with limited framings were tested.  
5. **Surprisal vs. generation rationalization** – Does avoiding text generation reduce post‑hoc rationalizations and better reflect a model’s underlying knowledge compared to CoT prompting? This is speculative and requires direct comparison of surprisal minima with generated answers.

## Limitations
- **Tokenization sensitivity:** different token representations for the same ordinal value (e.g., “1” vs. “ 1”) can bias surprisal and the argmin decision.  
- **Single‑token constraint:** the approach assumes each scale point is a single token, restricting scales to digits or single‑word anchors and limiting richer ordinal formats.  
- **Entropy confounders:** entropy may reflect model error or missing candidate answers rather than genuine ambiguity, especially when the correct rating is not in the restricted set.

## Confidence
- **Ordinal surprisal replaces generation** – Confidence: *Medium* (strong empirical support but vulnerable to tokenization biases and single‑token limits).  
- **Entropy as an ambiguity measure** – Confidence: *Low‑Medium* (partial validation; confounded by calibration and candidate‑set completeness).  
- **Performance scales with model size; instruction tuning effects** – Confidence: *High* (consistent trends across Qwen2.5 variants and multiple tasks).

## Next Checks
1. **Tokenization robustness test** – For a stratified item sample, recompute surprisal using alternate scale tokens (e.g., “1”, “ 1”, “one”) and report argmin/entropy variance.  
2. **Human‑ambiguity correlation** – Collect human ratings of item ambiguity (e.g., inter‑annotator agreement) and correlate with model entropy across domains to validate the uncertainty signal.  
3. **Out‑of‑domain transfer** – Apply the method to a novel ordinal task (e.g., sentiment intensity scoring on product reviews) with a fresh scale and model, verifying curve interpretability and error metrics against human scores.
