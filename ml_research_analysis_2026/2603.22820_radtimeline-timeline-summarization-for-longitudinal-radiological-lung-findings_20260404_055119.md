# RadTimeline: Timeline Summarization for Longitudinal Radiological Lung Findings

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22820  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22820  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** RadTimeline’s 3-step LLM pipeline produces lung finding timelines with grouping performance (CoNLL F1 84) comparable to human annotators (82); extraction ROUGE-L reaches 67 with GPT-4o.

## Executive Summary
Manual tracking of lung findings across longitudinal radiology reports is time-consuming and error-prone. RadTimeline addresses this with a structured timeline format (exams as columns, temporally related findings grouped in rows) using a 3-step LLM pipeline: extract findings from each report, generate group names representing longitudinal entities, and assign findings to those groups. The authors introduce the RadTimeline dataset—10 patients with hand-corrected timelines—for evaluation. Results show that intermediate group name generation is critical; the best configuration (GPT-4o with multiple prompting) achieves grouping performance (CoNLL F1 84) on par with human annotators (82), though with high recall and some irrelevant findings. Finding extraction yields ROUGE-L 67 for GPT-4o.

## Method Summary
RadTimeline’s pipeline transforms free-text radiology reports into a two-dimensional timeline. First, a finding extractor isolates atomic lung observations (size, location, trend) from each report using an LLM and filters absent findings via RadGraph. Second, a group name generator consumes the full longitudinal context (all reports or extracted findings) to produce descriptive labels for clusters of related findings (e.g., “right upper lobe ground glass nodule”). Third, a group assigner maps each finding to a group name either through LLM prompting (single or multiple, zero-shot or few-shot) or embedding similarity (E5-Mistral with task-specific instructions). Finally, the timeline composer arranges groups as rows and exams as columns. The intermediate name generation step decouples the clustering problem, proving essential for performance.

## Key Results
- Intermediate group name generation is critical for effective grouping; ablations show that generating names with full report context reduces unnecessary names and improves downstream assignment.
- Best pipeline (GPT-4o, multiple prompting) achieves CoNLL F1 84 for grouping, comparable to human inter-annotator agreement (82).
- Finding extraction with GPT-4o yields ROUGE-L 67; however, irrelevant findings remain an issue, especially for Llama 3.1 (40% unnecessary extractions).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Extracting atomic lung findings from individual reports provides the foundational factual units for the timeline.
- **Mechanism:** An LLM is prompted per-report to output a Python list of finding strings, focusing on clinically relevant details (size, location, trend). This isolates facts at the source-document level, enabling later cross-report aggregation.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can reliably distinguish lung-related observations from other report content and normalize key descriptors into atomic, comparable units.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "extract findings" as first step in the 3-step pipeline.
  - [section 4.1] Detailed prompt design, use of RadGraph for filtering absent findings, and evaluation via ROUGE-L (Table 3).
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers (e.g., ReXGroundingCT) also focus on extracting findings from free-text, but RadTimeline's evaluation of extraction quality is self-contained.
- **Break condition:** LLM hallucinates non-lung findings or fails to capture critical details (e.g., "stable", "subsolid"), leading to missing or incorrect cells. High irrelevant findings rate in Llama 3.1 (40% unnecessary) shows this failure mode.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Generating interpretable group names *before* assignment creates a semantic scaffold that guides consistent clustering of temporally related findings.
- **Mechanism:** Given longitudinal contexts (full reports or finding list), an LLM produces a list of specific group names (e.g., "right upper lobe ground glass nodule"). These names act as target labels for subsequent assignment, avoiding expensive pairwise comparisons.
- **Core assumption:** A human-interpretable label space, derived from the full patient history, captures the distinct longitudinal trajectories present in the data.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "group name generation as an intermediate step is critical for effective finding grouping."
  - [section 4.2] Comparison of full vs. finding-only contexts; Table 4 shows finding-only context increases unnecessary group names (46% for GPT-4o), suggesting full context helps generate more precise names.
  - [section 5.3.1] Oracle group names (human-curated) boost embedder-based assignment, proving name quality is a bottleneck.
  - [corpus] Moderate support: DTELS (neighbor) deals with timeline granularity, but RadTimeline's *intermediate name generation* is a novel solution to the grouping problem.
- **Break condition:** Generated group names are too generic ("nodules"), duplicated, or irrelevant, causing findings to be misassigned or orphaned ("Other"). Table 4 shows Llama 3.1 has 60% unnecessary names with full context.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Assigning findings to generated group names—via LLM prompting or embedding similarity—realizes the structured timeline by mapping atomic facts to trajectory rows.
- **Mechanism:** For each finding, the system selects the best-matching group name. LLM prompting (Single/Multiple) uses contextual reasoning; embedding-based methods compute cosine similarity between finding and name embeddings (E5-Mistral).
- **Core assumption:** The semantic similarity between a finding description and a group name (as measured by LLM or embedder) reliably indicates they belong to the same longitudinal entity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Final step "assign findings to groups."
  - [section 4.3] Detailed prompting strategies (Single/Multiple, ZS/FS, context usage) and embedder with task-specific instructions.
  - [section 5.3] Table 5: GPT-4o Multiple ZS reaches 84 CoNLL F1 with its own generated names, comparable to human inter-annotator agreement (82). Embedder with task-specific instruction reaches 84 with oracle names.
  - [section 5.3.2] Table 6: Full context during assignment helps for some annotator gold standards, suggesting cross-report context aids disambiguation.
  - [corpus] Strong support: Multiple neighbors (e.g., Tracking Cancer Through Text, Reconstructing Sepsis Trajectories) tackle longitudinal extraction/grouping, validating the need for such assignment mechanisms.
- **Break condition:** Ambiguous findings or poor group names lead to incorrect assignments. Table 5 shows embedder-based methods lag behind LLM prompting with automatic names (65-69 vs. 84), indicating current embedder-task alignment is insufficient.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Structured Summarization (timeline format)
  - **Why needed here:** The task requires organizing facts into a 2D table (time vs. finding trajectory) for easy comparison and fact-checking, moving beyond unstructured narrative summaries.

  - **Why needed here:** Grouping findings across reports is a CDC-like task: identifying mentions (findings) of the same conceptual entity (e.g., a specific nodule) across documents (reports).

  - **Why needed here:** Using group name generation as an explicit intermediate step decouples the clustering problem into label generation and assignment, which the paper shows is critical for performance.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might generating group names first improve final grouping performance compared to direct clustering or pairwise coreference?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Finding Extractor -> Group Name Generator -> Group Assigner -> Timeline Composer  
  (Group Assigner has two variants: LLM Assigner and Embedding Assigner)
- **Critical path:** Finding Extractor (Step 1) produces per-report finding lists; Group Name Generator (Step 2) consumes all findings (or full reports) to produce group names; Group Assigner (Step 3) maps each finding to a group; Timeline Composer arranges the assignments into the final table.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **LLM vs. Embedder for Assignment:** LLMs are more accurate (84 vs. 69 CoNLL F1 with auto names) but computationally expensive. Embedders are efficient but sensitive to group name quality and require careful instruction tuning.
  - **Full vs. Finding-Only Context in Step 2:** Full report context reduces unnecessary group names (Table 4: GPT-4o Unnec. 37% vs. 46%) but increases cost. Finding-only context is cheaper but noisier.
  - **Single vs. Multiple Prompting in Step 3:** Multiple (tag-injection) is cheaper (one LLM call) and enforces consistency across findings but relies on correct tag parsing. Single is flexible but costly and may be inconsistent.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High "unnecessary" findings in Step 1 output → poor extraction (Table 3: Llama 3.1 40% unnec.).
  - High "duplicate" or "unnecessary" group names in Step 2 → poor name generation (Table 4: Llama 3.1 60% unnec.).
  - Low CoNLL F1 in Step 3 with high-quality gold findings → poor assignment logic. If performance drops with full context (Table 6: Llama Single ZS 67→56), suggests LLM struggles with long contexts.
  - Embedder performance gap with auto vs. oracle names (Table 5: 65→84) → indicates task-specific instruction or name quality is insufficient.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce Finding Extraction:** Run Step 1 with both Llama 3.1 and GPT-4o on RadTimeline reports. Compute report-level and finding-level ROUGE-L, missing, and unnecessary rates (as in Table 3). Verify GPT-4o's lower unnecessary rate.
  2. **Ablate Group Name Generation Context:** For GPT-4o in Step 2, compare full-context vs. finding-only context. Measure impact on group name quality (ROUGE-L, miss, unnec., dup. in Table 4 style) and downstream assignment CoNLL F1 (Table 7 style).
  3. **Compare Assignment Strategies:** With a fixed Step 1 & 2 output (e.g., GPT-4o full-context names), run all Step 3 variants (Single/Multiple, ZS/FS, ± context) and embedder methods. Compute CoNLL F1 (Table 5) to validate "group name generation is critical" and that Multiple ZS with GPT-4o is best.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the RadTimeline framework be generalized to other types of longitudinal clinical data beyond lung radiology findings, such as symptom progression or medication histories?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states that "this work can be extended to a broader clinical longitudinal records to capture temporal nuances in clinical narratives, such as for symptom progression, medicine history, and treatment trajectories."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates the timeline format on lung findings from chest imaging reports; generalizability to other longitudinal clinical data types remains untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the RadTimeline pipeline to diverse longitudinal EHR data (e.g., progress notes, lab results) and evaluate timeline quality through clinician assessment and task-based utility studies.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does the performance of the RadTimeline pipeline scale with larger and more diverse patient cohorts, given the current evaluation set contains only 10 patients?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The limitations section notes "the evaluation dataset contains only 10 patients, due to the costs associated with clinical excerpts and the cognitive effort required," raising concerns about statistical generalizability.
- **Why unresolved:** Small sample size limits confidence in performance stability across varied patient populations, disease severities, and report writing styles.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Curate a larger, multi-center RadTimeline dataset with diverse cases and remeasure system performance, including variance analysis across patient subgroups.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the current automatic evaluation metrics (CoNLL F1 for grouping, ROUGE-L for extraction) adequately capture the clinical utility and factual accuracy needed for real-world deployment?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper uses ROUGE-based alignment for finding extraction and CoNLL F1 for grouping, but acknowledges that "a more fine-grained analysis is needed to assess quality of the alignments" and that human evaluation showed issues like irrelevant findings.
- **Why unresolved:** Automatic metrics may not reflect clinical correctness, readability, or the precision required for decision support, as high recall can coexist with irrelevant outputs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a clinician-facing study where timelines (auto-generated vs. gold) are assessed for factual correctness, usability in patient review, and impact on clinical reasoning tasks.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What linguistic and clinical properties make intermediate group names most effective for enabling accurate grouping and supporting clinician interpretation in the timeline format?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The results highlight that "group name quality is critical for effective grouping," and the paper compares generated names against gold references but does not analyze what makes names clinically useful.
- **Why unresolved:** The pipeline treats group name generation as an intermediate step, but the paper does not investigate which attributes (e.g., specificity, inclusion of anatomical/demographic qualifiers, conciseness) optimize downstream grouping and human readability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform an ablation study manipulating group name characteristics (e.g., length, keyword inclusion) and measure impacts on grouping performance and clinician ratings of name clarity.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How do other HIPAA-compliant large language models compare to Llama 3.1 and GPT-4o on the RadTimeline task, and could specialized models (e.g., clinical fine-tuned LLMs) improve performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The limitations state that "due to data privacy concerns, our choice of proprietary LLMs is limited to those with HIPAA-compliant APIs," and only two models were evaluated.
- **Why unresolved:** The performance of other available HIPAA-compliant models (e.g., Google Med-PaLM, Amazon Careful) is unknown, and domain-specific fine-tuning might address observed issues like irrelevant findings.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmark additional HIPAA-compliant LLMs on RadTimeline and fine-tune open-source models on radiology data to measure gains in extraction, grouping, and reduction of off-topic content.

## Limitations
- **Dataset scale:** Evaluation on only 10 patients limits statistical power and generalizability across diverse lung conditions and reporting styles.
- **Model dependency:** Pipeline performance varies significantly across LLMs; e.g., Llama 3.1 produces high rates of irrelevant findings (40%) and group names (60%).
- **Metric gaps:** ROUGE-L and CoNLL F1 may not fully capture clinical accuracy or utility; irrelevant findings with high recall suggest precision issues.
