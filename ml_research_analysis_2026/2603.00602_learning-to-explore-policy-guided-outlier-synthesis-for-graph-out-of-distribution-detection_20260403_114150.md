# Learning to Explore: Policy-Guided Outlier Synthesis for Graph Out-of-Distribution Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00602
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00602
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** PGOS yields state-of-the-art OOD detection on 12 of 25 graph benchmarks, with average ranks 1.9 (OOD) and 2.1 (anomaly), and large AUC improvements on key datasets; ablation shows RL policy is critical (11.2% drop).

## Executive Summary
Unsupervised graph-level out-of-distribution (OOD) detection suffers from weak decision boundaries when trained solely on in-distribution (ID) data. PGOS (Policy-Guided Outlier Synthesis) addresses this by learning to generate high-quality pseudo-OOD graphs that regularize the detector. It first employs prototypical contrastive learning to structure the latent space into well-separated clusters, creating navigable low-density inter-cluster voids. A reinforcement learning agent then explores these regions using a composite reward (repulsion from cluster interiors, a hard boundary constraint, and spatially-aware entropy regularization) to synthesize informative pseudo-OOD embeddings. These are decoded into graphs and used alongside ID data to train the final OOD detector via outlier exposure. Across 25 benchmarks, PGOS achieves state-of-the-art performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of learned, policy-guided outlier synthesis.

## Method Summary
PGOS comprises three sequential modules. First, the **PGCL module** maps graphs to embeddings via a GCN encoder and learns K trainable prototypes using a debiased contrastive loss, a prototypical consistency loss, and an inter-prototype separation loss, alongside a graph reconstruction objective. This creates a latent space with compact, well-separated clusters. Second, an **RL sampler** (Soft Actor-Critic) is trained in this space: the state is a latent coordinate, actions are displacements, and the reward encourages movement into low-density inter-cluster regions while a hard projection keeps samples within a hypersphere around the global centroid. A spatially-aware entropy target drives thorough exploration near cluster boundaries. Third, a standard **OOD detector** (e.g., GOOD-D) is trained on the original ID graphs plus the decoded pseudo-OOD graphs from the RL policy, using an ID loss and a boundary-aware regularization term that penalizes pseudo-OOD samples classified as ID. The pipeline is end-to-end in the sense of sequential training but not joint.

## Key Results
- SOTA on 12/25 graph OOD detection benchmarks; average rank 1.9 for OOD detection and 2.1 for anomaly detection.
- Substantial AUC gains: +2.2% on PTC-MR/MUTAG and +6.1% on Tox21/SIDER.
- Ablation: Removing the RL policy (replacing with Gaussian sampling) drops performance by 11.2% AUC on average, confirming the policy’s essential role.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Structured Latent Space via Prototypical Contrastive Learning
- **Claim:** Creating explicit, learnable prototypes organizes the latent space into well-segmented clusters, producing navigable low-density regions between them that can be targeted for outlier synthesis.
- **Mechanism:** Prototypical contrastive learning (PGCL) optimizes a graph encoder to cluster embeddings around K trainable prototypes. It combines three losses: (1) *Debiased Contrastive Loss* pulls together augmented views of the same graph while using prototype assignments to avoid false negatives; (2) *Prototypical Consistency Loss* ensures different augmentations of a graph are assigned to the same prototype; (3) *Inter-Prototype Separation Loss* pushes prototypes apart. This yields compact, well-separated clusters. A reconstruction loss further regularizes the latent space.
- **Core assumption:** The ID data can be meaningfully partitioned into K semantic prototypes, and the space between these prototypes correlates with OOD regions.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper states that PGCL “transforms previously unstructured regions into a navigable space” and that “this creates the low-density regions required for our sampling strategy.” Neighboring approaches (e.g., Non-Parametric Outlier Synthesis, HamOS) rely on fixed heuristics, highlighting the novelty of explicit prototypes.
- **Break condition:** If K is too small, clusters are too broad and inter-cluster voids are poorly defined; if too large, clusters fragment and prototypes may not capture true semantics, making navigation unreliable.

### Mechanism 2: Adaptive Outlier Synthesis via Reinforcement Learning
- **Claim:** An RL agent with a tailored reward, hard boundary constraint, and spatially-aware entropy regularization learns an adaptive policy to synthesize pseudo-OOD graphs in low-density inter-cluster regions, outperforming static heuristics.
- **Mechanism:** The sampler is modeled as an MDP where states are latent coordinates and actions are displacements. (1) *Repulsion Reward* penalizes entering a cluster’s interior (within δ_i of its centroid), encouraging exploration in voids. (2) *Hard Boundary Constraint* projects states outside a hypersphere (centered at the global centroid with radius R_max) back onto the sphere, keeping exploration focused on the data manifold. (3) *Spatially‑Aware Entropy Regularization* dynamically adjusts a target entropy for the policy (via SAC’s auto‑tuning) that peaks near cluster boundaries (at distance ≈ average radius ¯r), promoting thorough exploration of informative boundary zones.
- **Core assumption:** The most informative pseudo‑outliers for regularizing an OOD detector lie in low‑density regions just outside the boundary of ID clusters, not in arbitrary faraway or isotropic noise.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper notes that the agent “navigates low‑density regions… sampling representations that most effectively refine the OOD decision boundary.” The composite design explicitly addresses limitations of fixed heuristics used in prior work.
- **Break condition:** Mis‑setting the safety margin δ_i could either prevent the agent from avoiding cluster interiors or trap it in irrelevant voids; mis‑calibrating the entropy target’s peak location ¯r might lead to too conservative or too diffuse exploration.

### Mechanism 3: Outlier Exposure with Policy‑Generated Pseudo‑OOD Graphs
- **Claim:** Training the OOD detector on both ID graphs and the RL‑synthesized pseudo‑OOD graphs explicitly regularizes the decision boundary, forcing the model to learn more robust ID‑OOD separation than ID‑only training.
- **Mechanism:** The detector (e.g., GOOD‑D) is trained on the combined set of original ID graphs and the decoded pseudo‑OOD graphs. The loss standardly optimizes on ID data (L_ID) and adds a *boundary‑aware regularization term* (L_reg) on pseudo‑OOD scores s_G′. L_reg penalizes pseudo‑outliers that receive high ID confidence, pushing the detector to assign higher OOD scores to these boundary‑regularizing samples. The strength is controlled by hyperparameter β.
- **Core assumption:** The pseudo‑outliers generated by the RL policy in the structured latent space, once decoded, preserve semantics that realistically approximate true OOD graphs and thereby define a useful decision boundary when used for training.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper describes integrating pseudo‑outliers “into the training process… jointly optimizing a standard OOD detection loss on in‑distribution data and a boundary‑aware regularization term.” Outlier Exposure is a known paradigm; applying a learned synthesis policy to graph‑level detection is novel.
- **Break condition:** Poor decoder fidelity (high reconstruction loss) may yield unrealistic graphs that mislead the detector. An inappropriate β could cause overfitting to the synthetic distribution or negligible regularization.

## Foundational Learning
**Prototypical Contrastive Learning**  
Why needed here: Replaces standard contrastive learning by introducing discrete, learnable cluster centers that create a structured latent space with explicit inter-cluster voids for the RL agent to navigate. Standard contrastive only pulls positives and pushes negatives without clear centroids, leaving low‑density regions unstructured.  
Quick check: How does imposing a prototype‑based clustering objective differ from using a standard contrastive loss like InfoNCE in terms of the resulting latent space geometry?

**Policy‑Guided Exploration via Reinforcement Learning**  
Why needed here: Moves beyond fixed, non‑adaptive heuristics (e.g., sampling midway between clusters) by learning a mapping from state (location in latent space) to action (movement) that is optimized specifically for discovering informative outlier regions, as defined by the composite reward/constraints/entropy.  
Quick check: What are the three key components that guide the RL agent’s policy in PGOS, and what gap does each fill compared to a naive density‑based sampler?

**Outlier Exposure (OE) for OOD Detection**  
Why needed here: Provides the overarching training paradigm where auxiliary OOD samples (here, synthesized) are used during training to explicitly teach the detector what is “not ID,” addressing the inherent limitation of ID‑only training where the model has no positive examples of OOD data.  
Quick check: Why does training only on ID data lead to “weak decision boundaries” in unsupervised graph OOD detection, and how does OE change the learning objective?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
PGCL Module (graph encoder + K trainable prototypes + graph decoder) → creates structured latent space  
RL Sampler (SAC agent with actor-critic) → navigates latent space to generate pseudo‑OOD embeddings  
Decoder → transforms embeddings into pseudo‑OOD graphs  
OOD Detector (e.g., GOOD‑D) → trained on ID graphs + decoded pseudo‑OOD graphs with regularization

**Critical path:**  
1. Train PGCL module on ID graphs to obtain a well‑structured latent space (frozen thereafter).  
2. Train RL agent in this space using the defined reward, boundary constraint, and entropy regularization.  
3. Generate a set of pseudo‑OOD latent vectors via the trained policy, decode them into graphs.  
4. Train the final OOD detector on the combined dataset (original ID + pseudo‑OOD) with the outlier‑exposure loss.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Prototype count K*: Higher K yields more clusters and finer structure but risks over‑segmentation; lower K simplifies but may merge distinct semantics.  
- *RL exploration vs. relevance*: The hard boundary constraint keeps samples on‑manifold but may limit discovery of truly novel OOD regions far from ID support.  
- *Decoder fidelity*: A more powerful decoder could generate more realistic graphs but may introduce training instability or mode collapse.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Poor PGCL clustering**: t‑SNE of embeddings shows overlapping or indistinct clusters → RL lacks navigable structure.  
- **RL agent stays in clusters**: Trajectories remain in cluster interiors; reward or boundary constraint misconfigured.  
- **Pseudo‑OODs overlap ID**: t‑SNE of decoded graphs shows no separation; policy failed or decoding destroyed semantics.  
- **Ablation insensitivity**: Removing RL (using Gaussian sampling) does not significantly degrade AUC → policy ineffective.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Validate PGCL structure**: Train PGCL and visualize embeddings via t‑SNE; confirm K distinct, compact clusters with visible inter‑cluster gaps.  
2. **Audit RL exploration**: Execute the trained policy from random starts; plot trajectories to ensure they predominantly occupy inter‑cluster regions and respect the boundary constraint.  
3. **Ablation check**: Compare full PGOS against a variant that replaces RL with Gaussian sampling at cluster midpoints (PGOS‑RL). Significant AUC drop confirms RL’s necessity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the number of prototypes \(K\) in PGCL be determined automatically rather than through manual hyperparameter tuning?
- **Basis in paper:** Sensitivity analysis in Figure 5 shows large performance variation with \(K\), yet \(K\) is treated as a fixed hyperparameter without an adaptive selection mechanism.
- **Why unresolved:** Manual tuning of \(K\) is dataset‑specific and may not generalize to new domains where the inherent cluster structure is unknown, potentially leading to suboptimal latent space structuring and outlier synthesis.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An automatic \(K\) selection method (e.g., based on clustering validity indices or a learnable prototype count) evaluated across all 25 benchmarks, demonstrating consistent performance without manual intervention.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the repulsion‑based reward function in the RL agent fail to capture informative OOD regions that lie near high‑density ID clusters but are semantically distinct?
- **Basis in paper:** The reward design (Equation 8) explicitly focuses on low‑density inter‑cluster voids, assuming OOD samples reside there.
- **Why unresolved:** Some OOD graphs may share high‑density latent regions with ID data yet differ semantically (e.g., similar graphs from shifted domains), which the current reward might not incentivize the agent to explore, limiting boundary regularization.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Latent space visualizations on datasets with known cluster overlaps (e.g., ENZYMES/PROTEIN in Table 1) showing sampled points, combined with reward modifications (e.g., density‑gradient or adversarial terms) and their impact on detection AUC in such cases.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the PGOS framework, reliant on graph‑specific prototypical contrastive learning and decoding, be effectively transferred to non‑graph modalities (e.g., images or text) without fundamental redesign?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion explicitly lists “extending our paradigm to other data modalities” as future work, implying the current formulation is tailored to graphs.
- **Why unresolved:** The PGCL module and graph decoder (using GCNs and adjacency reconstruction) exploit graph structural inductive biases, and it is unclear if the same latent space structuring and synthesis principles generalize to Euclidean or sequential data.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Adaptation of PGOS to image or text data by replacing graph‑specific components with convolutional or transformer‑based encoders/decoders, followed by rigorous evaluation on standard OOD detection benchmarks (e.g., CIFAR‑10 vs. SVHN) to test portability.

## Limitations
- Prototype count \(K\) is critical but not auto‑tuned; a poor choice yields poor latent structure.  
- RL reward hyperparameters (\(\delta_i\), \(R_{\text{max}}\), \(\bar{r}\)) require careful calibration; mis‑sets cause ineffective exploration.  
- Decoder fidelity impacts pseudo‑OOD realism; lack of quantitative validation.

## Confidence
**Confidence in Mechanisms:**
- Mechanism 1 (PGCL structuring): *Medium* – Conceptual evidence; inter‑prototype density not directly measured.
- Mechanism 2 (RL policy): *High* – Ablation removing RL drops AUC by 11.2% on average; strong empirical support.
- Mechanism 3 (Outlier exposure): *High* – Built on OE; consistent gains across benchmarks confirm.
