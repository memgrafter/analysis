# Model Evolution Under Zeroth-Order Optimization: A Neural Tangent Kernel Perspective
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.21169
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21169
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Introduces the Neural Zeroth-order Kernel (NZK) to analyze zeroth-order optimization; proves expected NZK is time‑invariant for linear models, yields closed‑form evolution, extends to linearized neural networks, and demonstrates acceleration by reusing a single random vector.

## Executive Summary
Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization approximates gradients using function evaluations at randomly perturbed parameters, making it suitable for problems where gradients are unavailable or expensive. However, the training dynamics of ZO are less transparent than those of first-order methods, which are well-characterized by the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) in the infinite-width limit. This paper introduces the Neural Zeroth-order Kernel (NZK) to analyze ZO updates from a function-space perspective. For linear models under squared loss, the authors prove that the expected NZK is time‑invariant and depends only on the first and second moments of the perturbation distribution, enabling a closed‑form expression for the evolution of model predictions. The analysis extends to linearized neural networks, showing that ZO updates perform kernel gradient descent with a constant NZK. These insights lead to a simple acceleration technique: reuse a single random vector for both perturbations, which scales up the effective kernel and speeds up convergence without extra memory. Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR‑10, and Tiny ImageNet validate the theoretical findings and confirm that sharing the random vector yields faster convergence.

## Method Summary
The method centers on defining the Neural Zeroth‑order Kernel (NZK), which captures the functional effect of a ZO update. Given a model \(f_\theta\), a typical ZO update uses two random directions \(\zeta\) and \(z\) and step size \(\epsilon\) to estimate the gradient as \(G_t = \frac{f(\theta_t+\epsilon\zeta)-f(\theta_t+\epsilon z)}{\epsilon \|\zeta-z\|^2}(\zeta - z)\). The corresponding change in the model’s prediction at input \(x\) can be expressed as a linear combination of NZK columns, where the NZK entry \(K_{\zeta,z}(x_i,x_j)\) is the inner product of the finite‑difference feature vectors. For a linear model \(f_\theta(x)=\theta^\top x\), the NZK simplifies to a trilinear form involving \(x_i, x_j, \zeta, z\). Taking expectations over independent Gaussian perturbations yields a constant expected NZK that equals the standard inner product (i.e., the NTK) under zero‑mean unit‑variance assumptions. This constancy allows the expected prediction update to be written as \(\mathbb{E}[f_{\theta_{t+1}}(x_i)-f_{\theta_t}(x_i)] = -\frac{\eta}{N} \bar{K}_{\zeta,z}(f_{\theta_t}(x_i)-y_i)\), i.e., gradient descent in function space with step size \(\eta\bar{K}\). The same structure holds for linearized neural networks by replacing \(x\) with the neural tangent feature vector \(\nabla_\theta f_\theta(x)\). Moreover, setting \(\zeta = z\) (a single shared random vector) modifies the expected NZK by a factor \((d+2)\sigma_z^4\) for Gaussian perturbations, effectively increasing the step size and accelerating convergence. The authors empirically approximate the expected NZK by averaging over large batches and test the predictions on synthetic and real datasets, confirming the theoretical advantages of the shared‑vector approach.

## Key Results
- Introduces the Neural Zeroth‑order Kernel (NZK) to analyze zeroth-order optimization in function space.
- Proves that for linear models under squared loss, the expected NZK is time‑invariant, leading to closed‑form dynamics; extends the result to linearized neural networks, showing ZO updates are equivalent to kernel gradient descent with the NZK.
- Demonstrates that reusing a single random vector (\(\zeta = z\)) scales the expected NZK by \((d+2)\sigma_z^4\), accelerating convergence; validates theory and acceleration on MNIST, CIFAR‑10, and Tiny ImageNet.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The expected Neural Zeroth‑order Kernel (NZK) is time‑invariant and explicitly depends on the first and second moments of random perturbation directions.
- **Mechanism:** For a linear model, each NZK entry is a trilinear form: \(K_{\zeta,z}(x_i,x_j) = \zeta^\top x_i x_j^\top z z^\top \zeta\). Taking expectations over independent Gaussian \(\zeta \sim N(\mu_\zeta \mathbf{1}, \sigma_\zeta^2 I)\) and \(z \sim N(\mu_z \mathbf{1}, \sigma_z^2 I)\) yields a constant matrix whose entries are linear combinations of \(\langle x_i, x_j\rangle\) and \(\langle x_i, \mathbf{1}\rangle \langle \mathbf{1}, x_j\rangle\). Under standard zero‑mean unit‑variance assumptions this reduces to the inner product, equaling the NTK. This invariance permits closed‑form dynamics under squared loss.
- **Core assumption:** The model is linear; the loss is squared; the random directions are independent Gaussian with fixed first/second moments; the expected NZK is defined by averaging over ζ and z (or large batch).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "proves the expected NZK is time‑invariant and explicitly depends on the first and second moments of random perturbation directions"
  - [section] Theorem 1 statement and proof (Eqs. 45–54) derive the constant expected kernel.
  - [corpus] No direct citations; corpus contains foundational NTK works (e.g., arXiv:2509.24991) that motivate the kernel viewpoint but do not address ZO.
- **Break condition:** If the distributions of ζ or z change over time, or if they lack finite second moments, the expected NZK may become time‑varying. The linearity assumption is also broken for nonlinear networks beyond the linearized regime.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Zeroth‑order updates can be interpreted as kernel gradient descent with respect to the NZK, unifying ZO and FO dynamics in function space.
- **Mechanism:** Starting from the parameter update (Eq. 1) and applying the chain rule with the finite‑difference estimator (Eq. 3), the evolution of the model value \(f_\theta(x)\) is expressed (Eq. 4) as a sum of loss‑difference terms multiplied by the NZK columns. For squared loss, after simplification (Eq. 20) the expected update becomes \(\mathbb{E}[f_{\theta_{t+1}}(x_i) - f_{\theta_t}(x_i)] = -\frac{\eta}{N} \bar{K}_{\zeta,z}\,[f_{\theta_t}(x_i)-f^*(x_i)]\), i.e., gradient descent in function space under the constant expected NZK. The same structure holds for linearized neural networks (Eq. 34) with a different but also constant kernel.
- **Core assumption:** The model is either linear or linearized; the loss is differentiable (for the squared loss simplification); the expectation is taken over ζ and z.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Interpreting ZO updates as kernel gradient descent via NZK provides a novel perspective"
  - [section] Eq. (21)–(23) for linear models; Eq. (34) for linearized networks show the closed‑form kernel descent.
  - [corpus] The corpus includes papers on NTK (e.g., arXiv:2512.08264) that treat FO updates as kernel gradient descent; this work adapts that perspective to ZO.
- **Break condition:** The derivation of closed‑form dynamics relies on the expected NZK being constant. Finite batch sizes introduce stochasticity that deviates from the closed form. For deep nonlinear networks without linearization, the kernel may evolve with time, breaking the simple descent picture.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Using a single shared random vector (setting \(\zeta = z\)) scales the expected NZK by \((d+2)\sigma_z^4\) for Gaussian perturbations, accelerating convergence without extra memory.
- **Mechanism:** When \(\zeta = z\), the NZK entry becomes \(K_{z,z}(x_i,x_j) = (\mathbb{V}[z_i^2] + d\,\mathbb{E}^2[z_i^2])\,\langle x_i, x_j\rangle\). For \(z_i \sim N(0,\sigma_z^2)\), \(\mathbb{E}[z_i^2]=\sigma_z^2\), \(\mathbb{V}[z_i^2]=2\sigma_z^4\), giving factor \(2\sigma_z^4 + d\sigma_z^4 = (d+2)\sigma_z^4\). This scaling accelerates the effective step size in the functional update \(\Delta f \propto -\eta K_{\zeta,z}\), leading to faster loss decay. Corollary 2 formalizes this and the experiments confirm the speedup.
- **Core assumption:** ζ and z are drawn from the same distribution with independent coordinates; the distribution has finite second and fourth moments; the model is linear (or linearized) under squared loss.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "demonstrate acceleration when using a single shared random vector"
  - [section] Corollary 2 (Eqs. 55–66) derives the scaling; Figure 1(b) shows the empirical speedup.
  - [corpus] The acceleration strategy is novel; corpus does not address this specific reuse technique.
- **Break condition:** The scaling factor depends on dimension \(d\); in very high dimensions the factor could cause instability if not paired with an appropriate learning rate \(\eta\). Also, the variance of the gradient estimator may grow with \(\sigma_z^4\), so too large \(\sigma_z\) could increase noise.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Zeroth‑order optimization
  - **Why needed here:** The entire paper studies ZO, which approximates gradients via forward‑pass finite differences. Without understanding Eq. (1), the nature of the gradient estimate and its bias‑variance trade‑off is unclear.
  - **Quick check question:** Write the expression for the ZO gradient estimator \(G_t\) and identify the role of the random direction \(z\) and step size \(\epsilon\).

- **Concept:** Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
  - **Why needed here:** NTK provides the benchmark for FO dynamics in wide networks. The NZK is defined analogously, and the paper contrasts their properties (e.g., constancy vs. stochasticity). Knowing NTK clarifies why FO is "well‑characterized" and what the paper aims to replicate for ZO.
  - **Quick check question:** What does the NTK matrix \(K(x_i,x_j)\) represent in the infinite‑width limit of a neural network trained with gradient descent?

- **Concept:** Closed‑form kernel regression dynamics
  - **Why needed here:** The theoretical contribution yields explicit formulas like Eq. (7) and Eq. (12) for the evolution of model predictions. These come from treating the expected kernel as constant and solving linear difference equations, which is standard in kernel methods. Recognizing this pattern is key to interpreting the results.
  - **Quick check question:** Under squared loss and constant kernel \(\bar{K}\), what is the matrix update rule for the vector of predictions \(\mathbf{f}_t\) in terms of \(\mathbf{f}^*\) and the initialization?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - **NZK definition:** Kernel matrix entries given by inner products of finite‑difference feature vectors (Eq. 5).
  - **Theoretical backbone:** Theorem 1 (expected NZK invariance for linear models), Corollary 2 (identical ζ,z scaling), extension to linearized neural networks (Eq. 11–12).
  - **Empirical validation:** Synthetic student‑teacher experiments; MNIST classification with linear model; CIFAR‑10/Tiny ImageNet with linearized networks; loss curves and kernel visualizations.
  - **Implementation detail:** Use of large batch averaging to approximate expectations; choice of learning rate \(\eta\), perturbation scale \(\epsilon\), and \(\sigma_z\).

- **Critical path:**
  1. Recognize that ZO obscures dynamics because gradients are estimated with random directions.
  2. Define NZK to capture the functional effect of ZO updates.
  3. Prove expected NZK is constant (Theorem 1) for linear models.
  4. Derive closed‑form evolution under squared loss (Eq. 7).
  5. Extend to linearized networks by replacing inputs with neural tangent random features (Eq. 12).
  6. Identify acceleration opportunity by sharing the random vector (Corollary 2).
  7. Validate on synthetic and real data; compare FO, independent ZO, and shared‑vector ZO.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Memory vs. convergence:** Independent ζ and z require two random vectors per iteration (higher memory). Sharing \(\zeta = z\) saves memory and increases the effective kernel scaling, speeding convergence.
  - **Variance vs. step size:** Larger \(\sigma_z\) boosts acceleration but also increases gradient estimation variance (the variance of a ZO estimator scales with \(\sigma_z^2/\epsilon^2\)). Tuning \(\sigma_z\) and \(\epsilon\) is essential.
  - **Model fidelity:** The analysis assumes linear or linearized models; for finite‑width nonlinear networks the kernel may evolve, so the theory is only an approximation. The experiments use linearized networks to approximate wide networks.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Stochastic noise dominating:** If batch size is too small or \(\epsilon\) too large, the actual NZK will vary strongly across steps, deviating from the constant‑kernel assumption and causing oscillations.
  - **Non‑Gaussian heavy tails:** If \(\zeta\) or \(z\) have infinite fourth moments, the scaling factor in Corollary 2 may be undefined or sensitive, breaking the theoretical guarantee.
  - **Dimensionality explosion:** In very high dimensions, the factor \((d+2)\sigma_z^4\) may become huge, leading to instability unless \(\eta\) is reduced accordingly.
  - **Finite‑width nonlinearity:** For a fully trained finite‑width network, the NTK is not exactly constant; similarly, the NZK may drift, invalidating the closed‑form predictions.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **2‑D linear teacher‑student:** Visualize model trajectories (Fig. 5) and loss curves (Fig. 1) for FO, ZO with independent \(\zeta,z\) (varying \(\sigma_z\)), and ZO with shared \(\zeta=z\). This isolates the effect of the scaling factor.
  2. **MNIST linear classifier:** Train a linear model on downsampled MNIST (8×8) comparing loss progress (Fig. 15) and NZK matrices (Fig. 16) for FO, parametric ZO (independent), and kernel ZO (shared). Validates acceleration on a real dataset.
  3. **CIFAR‑10/Tiny ImageNet with linearized neural network:** Use a three‑layer network linearized around initialization (Eq. 10) to train on downsampled CIFAR‑10/Tiny ImageNet. Compare loss curves (Fig. 2) and NZK visualizations (Fig. 18). Shows that the theory extends to higher‑dimensional data and more features.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the Neural Zeroth-order Kernel (NZK) framework be extended to analyze the training dynamics of non-linearized deep neural networks under zeroth-order optimization?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred. The paper derives NZK for linear models and linearized neural networks, but acknowledges that practical neural networks are finite-width and non-linear, relying on the linearized approximation as a simplifying assumption.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical invariance and closed-form dynamics depend on the linearity property from the Neural Tangent Kernel regime, which may not hold for deeper or wider networks without linearization. The extension to non-linear networks requires addressing interactions between layers and non-linear activations that break the linear relationship.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis extending NZK to non-linear networks, possibly via mean-field approximations or conditions under which linearity approximately holds, coupled with empirical validation on deeper architectures beyond linearized models.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does the choice of loss function affect the invariance property of the expected NZK and the closed‑form evolution dynamics?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred. The paper's theoretical results, including Theorem 1 and Equation 7, are derived specifically for squared loss. Experiments use cross‑entropy for classification, but no theoretical treatment is provided for other losses.
- **Why unresolved:** The derivation leverages the specific gradient structure of squared loss, where the loss gradient simplifies to a linear function of predictions. For non‑smooth or non‑convex losses like cross‑entropy or hinge loss, the relationship between ZO gradient estimates and model updates may differ, potentially breaking invariance or altering dynamics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Generalizing the NZK derivation to other common loss functions, proving invariance conditions if they exist, and comparing empirical dynamics across losses to identify discrepancies or validate extensions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the theoretical justification for the acceleration observed when using a shared random vector (ζ=z) in zeroth‑order optimization, particularly for linearized neural networks?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred. Corollary 2 proves that for linear models with ζ=z, the expected NZK scales by (d+2)σ^4_z, implying acceleration. Experiments on linearized neural networks show acceleration with identical ζ and z, but the theory for such networks assumes separate ζ and z with zero mean and does not derive the scaling effect.
- **Why unresolved:** The proof for linear models relies on the explicit form of NZK for linear functions. For linearized neural networks, NZK involves neural tangent features (Equation 33), and the impact of ζ=z on this kernel is not analyzed theoretically, leaving the acceleration mechanism unexplained in this context.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Deriving the expected NZK for linearized neural networks with ζ=z, showing scaling similar to linear models, and establishing convergence rate improvements under this condition, possibly through eigenvalue analysis of the kernel matrix.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How robust is the time‑invariance of the expected NZK to deviations from the Gaussian perturbation distribution assumed in the theory?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred. Theorem 1 assumes z and ζ are Gaussian with specified first and second moments. Corollary 2 explores other distributions (Laplace, Student's t) for linear models but only by matching moments to achieve similar scaling; the invariance property itself is not generally proven for non‑Gaussian cases.
- **Why unresolved:** The derivation of expected NZK uses Gaussian properties, such as independence and specific moment calculations. For non‑Gaussian distributions, the cross‑moments and kernel form may differ, potentially causing NZK to vary with time or depend on higher‑order statistics not accounted for.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyzing NZK under various perturbation distributions (e.g., uniform, heavy‑tailed) to determine conditions for invariance, such as symmetry or moment constraints, and conducting experiments to measure time‑variation of empirical NZK.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the NZK perspective be leveraged to design novel zeroth‑order optimization algorithms that outperform existing methods in practical deep learning scenarios?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicit. The conclusion states that findings "pave the way for linking ZO dynamics to recent research analyzing neural networks from a functional perspective," implying algorithmic advancements. The paper introduces NZK and demonstrates acceleration via shared random vectors but does not propose new ZO algorithms beyond this simple modification.
- **Why unresolved:** While NZK characterizes evolution as kernel gradient descent, translating this into practical algorithms—such as adaptive step sizes, preconditioning, or leveraging kernel structure for efficiency—remains unexplored. Existing ZO methods (e.g., one‑point or two‑point estimators) may not fully exploit NZK properties.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing ZO algorithms inspired by NZK (e.g., using NZK estimates to guide perturbations or learning rates), evaluating them on deep learning tasks (e.g., large‑scale vision or language models), and comparing convergence and memory efficiency against state‑of‑the‑art ZO methods.

## Limitations
- Theoretical results are restricted to linear or linearized models; the behavior for finite‑width nonlinear networks remains an open question.
- The analysis assumes Gaussian perturbations and squared loss; other perturbation distributions or loss functions are not theoretically supported.
- Experiments use downsampled datasets and linearized networks, so the practical impact on large‑scale deep learning is not fully demonstrated.

## Confidence
- Expected NZK time‑invariance (linear models): High
- ZO as kernel gradient descent (linear/linearized): Medium‑High for linear, Medium for linearized
- Acceleration via shared random vector: High (theoretically) but Medium for general deep networks
