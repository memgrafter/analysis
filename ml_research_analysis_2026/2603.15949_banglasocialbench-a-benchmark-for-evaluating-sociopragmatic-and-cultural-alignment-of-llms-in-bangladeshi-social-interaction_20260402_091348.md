# BanglaSocialBench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Sociopragmatic and Cultural Alignment of LLMs in Bangladeshi Social Interaction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15949  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15949  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** LLMs exhibit consistent cultural misalignment in Bangladeshi social interactions, notably defaulting to overly formal address terms and showing asymmetric cross-religious substitution, with accuracy ranging from 31.88% to 92.46% across domains.

## Executive Summary
BanglaSocialBench is the first benchmark designed to evaluate large language models (LLMs) on sociopragmatic and cultural alignment in Bangladeshi social contexts. It moves beyond factual recall to test context-dependent language use across three domains: Bangla address terms, kinship reasoning, and social customs. Using 1,719 native-speaker-authored instances, the authors evaluated 12 state-of-the-art LLMs in a zero-shot setting. Results reveal systematic failures: models frequently default to the formal pronoun "apni" even when informal alternatives are appropriate, struggle with ambiguity (assigning near-certain probabilities to single options), and display significant asymmetric substitution of religious terms (e.g., using Muslim terms in Hindu contexts). These findings highlight substantial gaps in cultural reasoning and the need for better alignment with localized sociopragmatic norms.

## Method Summary
The benchmark was constructed by native Bengali speakers to capture authentic social scenarios. Instances are divided into three domains: (1) Bangla Address Terms, requiring selection of appropriate pronouns/nouns based on relative age and relationship; (2) Kinship Reasoning, involving identification of familial ties across religious traditions; and (3) Social Customs, testing knowledge of culturally specific practices. Twelve LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, Llama 2) were evaluated zero-shot by prompting with a scenario and measuring accuracy, output probability distributions, and error patterns. Statistical tests (e.g., asymmetry analysis) were applied to quantify biases.

## Key Results
- Models exhibit a strong bias toward the formal address term "apni," even in informal or downward-hierarchy contexts, and allocate near-certain probability mass to a single option where multiple acceptable answers exist.
- Cross-religious substitution is significantly asymmetric in 11 of 12 models (p<0.05), with Muslim terms incorrectly substituted into Hindu contexts more often than vice versa.
- Overall accuracy varies widely by domain and model (31.88%–92.46%), with errors clustering in informal settings and scenarios involving lower-status individuals, indicating a fundamental gap in handling nuanced social hierarchies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
BanglaSocialBench effectively isolates sociopragmatic decisions that require implicit cultural knowledge. The benchmark’s strength lies in its focus on context-dependent choices where factual recall alone is insufficient—models must infer relational dynamics, religious sensitivities, and situational formality. The observed failures likely stem from LLMs’ reliance on majority patterns in training data, which overrepresent formal registers and may conflate religious groups. The zero-shot setup further exposes the lack of robust internal representations for Bangladeshi social norms, as models cannot rely on task-specific fine-tuning or examples.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Sociopragmatic competence** – Ability to select language appropriate to social context. *Why needed*: LLMs must avoid pragmatic failures in real-world interactions. *Quick check*: Given a scenario with a younger sibling, does the model use informal address?
2. **Cultural alignment** – Matching outputs to culturally specific expectations. *Why needed*: Misalignment can cause offense or reduce utility. *Quick check*: Query about a Hindu ritual; does the response use correct terminology?
3. **Address term systems** – Complex Bengali pronominal/nominal hierarchies (e.g., apni vs. tumi). *Why needed*: Incorrect use signals disrespect. *Quick check*: Present a context with an elder stranger; is "apni" selected?
4. **Kinship reasoning** – Understanding family relationships that differ across religions (e.g., terms for uncle/mother’s brother). *Why needed*: Kinship terms carry cultural weight. *Quick check*: Given a family tree, can the model identify the correct term for a specific relative?
5. **Social customs knowledge** – Awareness of rituals, festivals, and etiquette. *Why needed*: Generates culturally grounded responses. *Quick check*: Ask about a common custom (e.g., Pohela Boishakh greeting); is the description accurate?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input prompt (social scenario description) → LLM (decoder-only transformer) → Output (predicted term/answer)  
**Critical path:** The prompt activates the model’s latent cultural knowledge; autoregressive decoding generates tokens until a termination condition (e.g., end of answer) is met.  
**Design tradeoffs:** Zero-shot evaluation tests inherent knowledge but is sensitive to prompt phrasing; a large, diverse instance set improves coverage but may introduce annotator bias.  
**Failure signatures:** (1) Over-formality: default to "apni" regardless of context; (2) Asymmetric substitution: preferential use of Muslim terms in Hindu contexts; (3) High maxP in ambiguous cases: near-certain prediction when multiple answers are acceptable.  
**First experiments:**  
1. Systematically vary prompt templates (e.g., add role instructions like "You are a Bengali speaker") to test sensitivity.  
2. Introduce few-shot examples with explanations to measure whether performance improves, indicating knowledge gaps vs. reasoning failures.  
3. Compare models fine-tuned on Bengali text vs. multilingual models to isolate the effect of language-specific exposure.

## Limitations
- **Data provenance & cultural authenticity:** Annotator demographics (region, dialect, age, religion) are unreported, raising questions about representativeness of Bangladesh’s diversity.
- **Evaluation setup:** Zero-shot prompting details (templates, instructions) are insufficiently documented, leaving ambiguity whether failures stem from knowledge or task framing.
- **Benchmark scope & realism:** The three domains are critical but not exhaustive; it is unclear if errors generalize to dynamic, multi-turn conversations or broader pragmatic inference.
- **Human baseline absent:** No native-speaker performance ceiling is provided, making it hard to gauge the true difficulty and model–human gap.

## Confidence
- **High:** Performance disparities across domains/models are well-supported by accuracy ranges and statistical tests (e.g., p-values for asymmetry).
- **Medium:** Default to overly formal address and low probability allocation are descriptively convincing but inferential regarding causality.
- **Medium:** Asymmetric cross-religious substitution is statistically significant in 11/12 models, but mechanisms and generalizability beyond tested terms require replication.

## Next Checks
1. **Audit data collection & annotation:** Re-analyze the 1,719 instances for annotator demographics, inter-annotator agreement per subcategory, and handling of ambiguous cases; assess representation of cultural variations (urban vs. rural, dialectal differences).
2. **Reproduce evaluation with controlled prompts:** Systematically vary prompt phrasing (e.g., explicit role specification, inclusion of examples) to determine whether performance changes dramatically, isolating knowledge gaps from instruction-following issues.
3. **Establish human performance ceiling:** Recruit a diverse cohort of native Bangladeshi speakers (stratified by age, religion, region) to solve the benchmark, quantifying inherent difficulty and setting a realistic target for LLM alignment.
