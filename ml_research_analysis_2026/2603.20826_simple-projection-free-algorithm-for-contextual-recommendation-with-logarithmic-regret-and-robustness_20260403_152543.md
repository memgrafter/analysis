# Simple Projection-Free Algorithm for Contextual Recommendation with Logarithmic Regret and Robustness

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20826
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20826
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: A projection-free algorithm for contextual recommendation achieving O(d log T) regret and robustness to suboptimal user actions by exploiting scale invariance.

## Executive Summary
Contextual recommendation learns from user actions (not explicit rewards) to minimize regret against an unknown utility. State-of-the-art ONS incurs O(d log T) regret but requires computationally expensive Mahalanobis projections each round. This paper introduces CoRectron, a simple projection-free algorithm that exploits the scale invariance inherent in recommendation: the recommended action depends only on the direction of the utility predictor, not its norm. CoRectron uses a second-order perceptron update with cumulative residuals and a preconditioner, achieving the same O(d log T) regret while naturally handling suboptimal actions with a bound scaling with the square root of cumulative suboptimality. Its per-round time is O(d^2), avoiding the O(d^ω) cost of projections, and it extends to Hilbert spaces (e.g., kernels) with similar benefits. Experiments confirm lower runtime and better regret than ONS, with stable hyperparameters.

## Method Summary
CoRectron maintains a preconditioner matrix \(A_t = \lambda I + \sum_{i=1}^t g_i g_i^\top\) and cumulative residual \(\zeta_t = \sum_{i=1}^t g_i\), where \(g_i = \hat{x}_i - x_i\) is the difference between recommended and observed actions. Each round: compute \(\hat{w}_t = -A_{t-1}^{-1} \zeta_{t-1}\), then recommend \(\hat{x}_t = \arg\max_{x \in X_t} \langle \hat{w}_t, x \rangle\) via a linear optimization oracle. After observing the user's action \(x_t\), update \(g_t = \hat{x}_t - x_t\), and increment \(A_t\) and \(\zeta_t\). No projection step is required; the update is "improper" as \(\hat{w}_t\) can have unbounded norm. The algorithm extends to kernelized settings by replacing inner products with a kernel and maintaining the Gram matrix over past contexts. Regularization \(\lambda > 0\) ensures invertibility; theoretically \(\lambda = \Theta(B^2 d)\) under boundedness assumption.

## Key Results
- Theorem 1: Under optimal user actions and boundedness (Assumption 2), CoRectron achieves regret \(R_T(u) = O(d \log T)\).
- Theorem 2: When user actions can be suboptimal, regret scales as \(O\left( B H_T + \|u\| \sqrt{\lambda H_T} + \sqrt{2B \Delta_T(u) H_T} \right)\), where \(H_T = \log\det(I + \lambda^{-1} K_T)\) and \(\Delta_T(u)\) is cumulative suboptimality.
- Empirical: CoRectron runs faster than ONS and often achieves lower regret, with hyperparameter stability across several orders of magnitude.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: CoRectron eliminates Mahalanobis projections by exploiting the scale invariance (improperness) inherent to contextual recommendation.
- **Mechanism**: The recommendation rule \(\hat{x}_t = \arg\max_{x\in X_t} \langle \hat{w}_t, x \rangle\) depends only on the direction of \(\hat{w}_t\), not its norm. Thus the learner need not constrain \(\hat{w}_t\) to a bounded set. CoRectron updates \(\hat{w}_t = -A_{t-1}^{-1} \zeta_{t-1}\) without projection, where \(A_t = \lambda I + \sum_{i=1}^t g_i g_i^\top\) and \(\zeta_t = \sum_{i=1}^t g_i\). This resembles a second-order perceptron but without a bounded domain.
- **Core assumption**: User actions are (approximately) optimal for an unknown utility \(u\); the scale \(\|u\|\) is not identifiable from action-only feedback.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "exploiting improperness... removes the Mahalanobis projection step"; [section 1.1] "positive rescaling of the utility predictor leaves the recommendation unchanged"; [section 3.1] "this algorithm is projection-free."
- **Break condition**: If optimality fails severely or feasible sets are such that direction alone does not determine recommendations, the improper update may misbehave. Unbounded diameter of \(X_t\) could cause large residuals and numerical instability unless \(\lambda\) is tuned appropriately.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The cumulative residual \(\zeta_t\) and preconditioner \(A_t\) together yield an \(O(d \log T)\) regret bound via a potential argument.
- **Mechanism**: The prediction \(\hat{w}_t = -A_{t-1}^{-1} \zeta_{t-1}\) and residual \(g_t = \hat{x}_t - x_t\) update \(A_t\) and \(\zeta_t\). Regret \(R_T(u) = -\langle u, \zeta_T \rangle\). Optimality of \(\hat{x}_t\) for \(\hat{w}_t\) implies the sign condition \(\langle g_t, A_{t-1}^{-1} \zeta_{t-1} \rangle \le 0\), which controls the potential \(\Phi_t = \|\zeta_t\|_{A_t^{-1}}^2\): \(\Phi_T \le \sum_{t=1}^T \langle g_t, A_t^{-1} g_t \rangle\). The elliptical potential lemma bounds this sum by \(\log\det(I + \lambda^{-1} K_T)\) with \(K_T = (\langle g_i, g_j \rangle)_{i,j=1}^T\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(R_T(u) \le \|u\|_{A_T} \sqrt{\log\det(\cdots)}\). Under optimal feedback and boundedness, \(\|u\|_{A_T}^2 \le \lambda \|u\|^2 + B R_T(u)\), leading to \(R_T(u) = O(d \log T)\).
- **Core assumption**: Losses are linear \(\ell_t(w) = \langle w, g_t \rangle\). Assumption 2 bounds \(|\langle u, x - x' \rangle| \le B\), ensuring residuals \(r_t \in [-B, B]\). Invertibility of \(A_t\) follows from \(A_t \succeq \lambda I\).
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 3.2] Theorem 1; [section 3.2.1] Lemma 1 (sign condition); [appendix B] proof of elliptical potential lemma.
- **Break condition**: Violating boundedness (Assumption 2) can decouple \(\|u\|_{A_T}\) from regret. If \(\|g_t\|\) is large, \(K_T\) may grow faster than \(T\), making \(\log\det(I+\lambda^{-1}K_T)\) super‑logarithmic, leading to linear regret.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The same algorithm naturally handles suboptimal feedback actions, achieving a regret bound that scales with \(\sqrt{\Delta_T(u)}\), where \(\Delta_T(u)=\sum_{t=1}^T (\max_{x\in X_t} \langle u, x \rangle - \langle u, x_t \rangle)\).
- **Mechanism**: When \(x_t\) is not optimal, \(r_t = \langle u, x_t - \hat{x}_t \rangle\) may be negative. The sign condition and elliptical potential inequality still hold because they rely only on the optimality of \(\hat{x}_t\) for \(\hat{w}_t\). The bound \(\|u\|_{A_T}^2 = \lambda \|u\|^2 + \sum_t r_t^2\) remains. Lemma 4 gives \(\sum_t r_t^2 \le B R_T(u) + 2B \Delta_T(u)\). Combining with the template bound \(R_T(u) \le \|u\|_{A_T} \sqrt{H_T}\) (with \(H_T = \log\det(I+\lambda^{-1}K_T)\)) yields
  \(R_T(u) \le B H_T + \|u\|\sqrt{\lambda H_T} + \sqrt{2B \Delta_T(u) H_T}\).
- **Core assumption**: Boundedness (Assumption 2) still holds and \(\delta_t(u)\) defined as above. Algorithm unchanged.
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 4] Theorem 2; Lemma 4 (stated in Section 4).
- **Break condition**: If \(\Delta_T(u)\) grows linearly (\(\Omega(T)\)), the bound becomes \(O(\sqrt{T})\) (losing logarithmic dependence). The bound also requires a known \(B\); underestimating \(B\) may invalidate the inequality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Online Convex Optimization (OCO)
  - *Why needed*: The paper frames the problem as OCO with linear losses \(\ell_t(w)=\langle w, g_t \rangle\). Understanding OCO (FTRL, strong convexity, second-order methods) is essential to grasp the O(d log T) rate and differences from first-order methods like OGD.
  - *Quick check*: What regret does OGD achieve on general convex losses, and how does adding a second-order pre‑conditioner improve the rate when the losses are exp‑concave?
- **Concept**: Improper Learning and Scale Invariance
  - *Why needed*: CoRectron’s core design ignores the magnitude of the predictor vector, a property specific to contextual recommendation. Without recognizing that the recommendation rule is scale‑invariant, one would incorrectly insist on bounded domains and miss the projection‑free advantage.
  - *Quick check*: If the user’s utility \(u\) is replaced by \(c u\) (\(c>0\)), how do the optimal actions \(x_t^*=\arg\max_{x\in X_t} \langle u, x \rangle\) change? What does this imply about the identifiability of \(\|u\|\) from action‑only feedback?
- **Concept**: Elliptical Potential and Gram Matrix Determinant Bounds
  - *Why needed*: The O(d log T) bound originates from the inequality \(\sum_t \langle g_t, A_t^{-1} g_t \rangle \le \log\det(I + \lambda^{-1} K_T)\). This “elliptical potential” lemma links cumulative second‑order updates to the volume of the ellipsoid defined by \(A_t\). It explains the logarithmic rate and extends to kernelized settings via effective dimension.
  - *Quick check*: Suppose \(\|g_t\| \le G\) and \(K_T \preceq G^2 T I\). Show that \(\log\det(I + \lambda^{-1}K_T) \le d\log(1 + G^2 T/\lambda)\). Why does this imply O(d log T)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input (feasible set \(X_t\)) → State (\(A_t\), \(\zeta_t\)) → Compute \(\hat{w}_t = -A_{t-1}^{-1} \zeta_{t-1}\) → Linear optimization oracle → Recommended action \(\hat{x}_t\) → Observe user action \(x_t\) → Compute residual \(g_t = \hat{x}_t - x_t\) → Update \(A_t = A_{t‑1} + g_t g_t^\top\), \(\zeta_t = \zeta_{t‑1} + g_t\) → Repeat.
- **Critical path** (per round): 1) Solve linear system \(A_{t-1} \hat{w}_t = -\zeta_{t-1}\); 2) Call linear optimization oracle to get \(\hat{x}_t\); 3) Receive \(x_t\); 4) Compute \(g_t\); 5) Update \(A_t\) and \(\zeta_t\). Steps 1 and 2 dominate runtime (O(d^2) finite‑dimensional; O(t^2) kernelized).
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *\(\lambda\)*: Smaller \(\lambda\) yields larger steps but risks ill‑conditioning. Theory suggests \(\lambda = \Theta(B^2 d)\); in practice sweep orders of magnitude. For kernels, \(\lambda\) also governs effective dimension.
  - *Kernelization*: Naïve kernel version stores \(K_t \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times t}\) and updates in O(t^2). For \(T \gg 10^3\), consider budgeted dictionaries, random features, or sketching.
  - *Memory*: Storing full \(A_t\) costs O(d^2); storing \(K_t\) costs O(t^2). Both become prohibitive quickly. Low‑rank factorizations or fixed‑budget approaches mitigate this.
  - *Boundedness*: Algorithm does not project but Assumption 2 requires \(\|g_t\|\) implicitly bounded by the diameter of \(X_t\) and \(B\). If base action sets are not normalized, scale \(\lambda\) relative to an estimate of \(B\).
- **Failure signatures**:
  - *Spiking regret*: Large residuals \(g_t\) indicate unbounded action sets or poor \(\lambda\). Check \(\|g_t\|\) and clip or rescale contexts.
  - *Ill‑conditioning*: High condition number of \(A_{t-1}\) leads to noisy \(\hat{w}_t\). Increase \(\lambda\) or use a numerically stable linear solver with diagonal padding.
  - *Kernel runtime blowup*: Per‑round cost O(t^2) slows for t>10^4. Monitor t and switch to an approximate kernel (e.g., Nyström) beyond a budget.
  - *Memory exhaustion*: Full matrix storage will crash for large d or T. Adopt streaming algorithms that maintain a fixed‑size dictionary.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. *Baseline reproduction*: Finite‑dimensional synthetic problem (d=10, unit ball, T=1000). Compare CoRectron, ONS, OGD. CoRectron should exhibit flat (O(log T)) regret, while OGD grows like √T. CoRectron runtime lower due to no projections.
  2. *Hyperparameter stability*: Sweep \(\lambda/\bar{\lambda}\) (\(\bar{\lambda}=B^2 d\)) across {10^{-3}, 10^{-2}, ..., 10^3}. CoRectron’s final regret remains stable across \(\lambda\), while ONS/OGD degrade when regularization is too weak.
  3. *Kernel scalability*: CoRectron‑K with RBF kernel on a 2‑D nonlinear dataset (circle classification), T=2000. Compare against KONS. CoRectron‑K’s per‑round time O(t^2) beats KONS’s Mahalanobis projection cost, and total runtime advantage widens with T.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can CoRectron be adapted to use approximate linear‑optimization or kernel sketching without sacrificing logarithmic regret?
- **Basis in paper**: "our guarantees still rely on access to an exact linear‑optimization oracle over each feasible set. In addition, the exact kernelized implementation still incurs O(t^2) per‑round cost, and thus further approximation or compression is needed for truly large‑scale or very long‑horizon settings."
- **Why unresolved**: The analysis hinges on exact solutions; approximations could destabilize the second‑order update and break the log‑determinant potential bound.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A theoretical analysis of error propagation in approximate updates, or a practical implementation with controlled approximations showing empirical logarithmic regret.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can sketching or embedding techniques reduce the per‑round cost of kernelized CoRectron while preserving logarithmic regret?
- **Basis in paper**: "Similar acceleration techniques may also be useful for our method, and exploring this is an interesting direction for future work."
- **Why unresolved**: Sketching introduces bias that may increase the effective Gram matrix norm, potentially ruining the log‑determinant bound.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Integrate random features or Nyström approximation, derive a regret bound that accounts for sketching error, and demonstrate speedups experimentally.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Why does empirical regret decrease when user actions become more suboptimal, contrary to the worst‑case bound involving cumulative suboptimality?
- **Basis in paper**: "Bridging this gap—developing a theoretical guarantee that explains the empirical decrease in regret as the user action becomes more suboptimal—is an interesting open problem."
- **Why unresolved**: Theorem 2 suggests regret grows with cumulative suboptimality, but experiments show the opposite trend.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A refined analysis exploiting structure of suboptimality (e.g., stochastic perturbations) to yield a bound that captures the negative correlation.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What is the empirical trade‑off between CoRectron and the MetaGrad‑based ONS approach under suboptimal feedback?
- **Basis in paper**: "We leave an experimental comparison with the MetaGrad‑based method to future work."
- **Why unresolved**: MetaGrad adapts to unknown suboptimality via many ONS learners, which may be slower; CoRectron is simpler but may not adapt as well.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implement MetaGrad with appropriate hyperparameter tuning and compare directly with CoRectron on the same suboptimality models, measuring both regret and runtime.

## Limitations
- Assumption 2 (bounded \(\langle u, x-x' \rangle\)) and a known bound \(B\) are required; \(\lambda\) is theoretically \(\Theta(B^2 d)\) but may need careful tuning.
- Scale invariance relies on recommendation depending only on predictor direction; if feasible sets have unbounded norm, residuals \(g_t\) can become large, risking numerical instability.
- The kernel extension retains O(t^2) time and memory, making it impractical for \(T > 10^4\) without budgeted approximations; the paper does not evaluate such strategies.
- Real‑world feedback may include significant noise or strategic misreports, violating the “near‑optimal action” premise underlying the suboptimality‑robust bound.

## Confidence
- **O(d log T) regret under optimal feedback**: High (proof follows standard elliptical potential arguments, assumptions clearly stated).
- **Regret bound scaling with √Δ_T when actions are suboptimal**: Medium (derivation correct but tightness not fully validated; depends on known B).
- **Projection‑free advantage in runtime**: Medium (avoids Mahalanobis projections but solving linear systems may have comparable overhead; speedup, especially kernelized, needs realistic implementation tests).
