# Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning for Large-Scale Tote Allocation in Human-Robot Collaborative Fulfillment Centers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.24182
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24182
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: Formulated tote allocation as large-scale MORL with competing KPIs and constraints; solved via zero-sum Lagrangian game. Learned policies achieved ETPH 20.52 while satisfying all constraints (positive slacks), outperforming random and unconstrained baselines.

## Executive Summary
We address the challenge of allocating totes to stations in a human-robot collaborative fulfillment center by modeling it as a constrained multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) problem. The method frames the interaction between a policy learner and a Lagrange multiplier regulator as a zero-sum game. The learner best-responds to fixed multipliers using DQN with scalarized rewards, while the regulator updates multipliers via no‑regret online gradient descent. Theoretically, this process yields a time‑averaged policy distribution that respects all constraints. Empirically, on a realistic warehouse simulator, the approach discovers individual stationary policies that simultaneously satisfy all operational constraints and achieve substantial throughput (Empty Totes Per Hour), surpassing both random and unconstrained policies that violate constraints.

## Method Summary
The warehouse is modeled as an episodic MDP, where each state aggregates tote‑slot features (e.g., number of large totes, ETPH, station queue lengths) and actions assign totes as source/destination to human or robot stations (or ignore). The objective is to maximize ETPH while keeping four KPIs—fraction of large totes, source/destination balance, manual station capacity, and robot station capacity—below thresholds αi. The algorithm proceeds in rounds. For a fixed Lagrange multiplier vector λ, the learner solves a scalarized single‑objective RL subproblem via DQN, obtaining a deterministic policy as its best response. The regulator then evaluates this policy’s average constraint violations and updates λ by projected online gradient descent. After T rounds, the time‑averaged multiplier ¯λ is computed; a post‑processing step selects a stored iterate with low Lagrangian under ¯λ to extract a single deployable policy. All training uses an event‑driven simulator that mimics ten days of warehouse operations per episode.

## Key Results
- Achieved ETPH 20.52 with all constraints satisfied: Nlarge slack 362.62, S/D slack 10.81, manual capacity slack 83.21, robot capacity slack 258.23.
- Outperformed a random policy (ETPH 9.19) and an unconstrained high‑ETPH policy (61.81) that suffered severe violations (e.g., manual capacity −563.23).
- Individual feasible policies emerged frequently during training, enabling deployment of a single stationary policy despite theoretical guarantees applying only to the time‑averaged mixture.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Zero‑sum Lagrangian game yields time‑averaged feasibility.**  
Formulating the constrained MORL as a zero‑sum game converts it into iterative best‑response (learner) vs. no‑regret (regulator) dynamics. The learner best‑responds to fixed λ by maximizing the scalarized Lagrangian; the regulator updates λ via online gradient descent to minimize it. Theorem 3.1 guarantees that the time‑averaged strategies (¯D, ¯λ) form an approximate minimax equilibrium, ensuring constraints are satisfied in expectation. Evidence: abstract, Section 3, Theorem 3.1.

**Mechanism 2: DQN computes best‑response for each λ.**  
For a given λ, the scalarized reward rλ = r₀ + Σ λᵢ(αᵢ/H − rᵢ) is constructed. DQN then finds a policy that maximizes expected cumulative rλ, serving as the learner’s move Dₜ. Single‑objective DQN training demonstrates steadily increasing returns, validating its ability to optimize scalarized rewards. Evidence: Section 4, Section 5.1.

**Mechanism 3: Single feasible policies extracted from iterates.**  
Although theory guarantees feasibility only for the time‑averaged mixture, the repeated game often produces individual policies that satisfy all constraints. Appendix A reformulates constraints using positive parts and shows that if the mixture is near‑minimax and the Jensen gap is small, some iterate must have a Lagrangian value close to the game’s optimum. Empirically, many stored policies are feasible; a representative one yields positive slacks across all constraints. Evidence: abstract observation, Section 5.3 (Figure 8, Table 1), Appendix A.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
MDP Simulator → Learner (DQN) → Policy (Dₜ) → Policy Evaluator → Constraint violations (gₜ) → Regulator (OGD) → λₜ → (back to Learner for next round)  
Stored policies {Dₜ} and time‑averaged multipliers ¯λ → Extraction Module → Deployable deterministic policy

**Critical path:**  
1. **Initialization** – Define MDP parameters, thresholds αᵢ, simulator, λ⁰ ∈ Λ, η, constant C, and number of rounds T.  
2. **Iteration (t = 1 … T)**  
   a. **Learner** – Train DQN on rλᵗ⁻¹ for ∼30 episodes; output deterministic policy πₜ and distribution Dₜ.  
   b. **Evaluation** – Estimate average constraint accumulations E[Vᵢ(πₜ)] over many episodes; compute gₜ = (E[Vᵢ] − αᵢ).  
   c. **Regulator** – Update λₜ = ΠΛ(λₜ⁻¹ − η gₜ) with L1‑ball projection.  
   d. (Optional) Track time‑averages.  
3. **Post‑processing** – Compute ¯λ = (1/T) Σ λₜ; select Dₜ⋆ with smallest Lagrangian bL(Dₜ, ¯λ); extract deterministic policy.  
4. **Deployment** – Use the extracted policy for real‑time tote allocation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **RL algorithm:** DQN suits discrete actions but may suffer approximation errors; policy‑gradient methods (e.g., PPO, SAC) could be considered for continuous actions.  
- **Thresholds αᵢ:** Tight thresholds force throughput trade‑offs; loose thresholds may trivialize constraints. Slack tuning affects feasibility of single policies.  
- **Regulator step size η:** Large η induces oscillations; small η slows adaptation. Must balance with T.  
- **Rounds T:** More rounds improve average convergence but increase computational cost.  
- **Projection bound C:** Too small limits penalty strength; too large may yield unrealistic multipliers.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Individual policies consistently violate constraints despite feasible ¯D → high Jensen gap; check λₜ oscillations and Dₜ performance spread.  
- Multipliers diverge or repeatedly hit Λ boundary → thresholds too tight or η mis‑set.  
- DQN fails to improve scalarized reward → poor hyperparameters, insufficient capacity, or simulator mismatch.  
- Simulation‑reality gap → learned policy degrades when deployed due to unmodeled dynamics.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Single‑objective DQN sanity check** – Train DQN on r₀ (ETPH) only; verify steady improvement and final ETPH > 50 (matching Figure 3).  
2. **Repeated‑game dynamics** – Run Algorithm 1 with T≈50–100; plot λₜ trajectories and individual constraint values; observe average feasibility trend and oscillations.  
3. **Feasible‑policy extraction** – After training, evaluate all stored Dₜ under ¯λ; compute fraction of individually feasible policies (as in Figure 8); pick policy with best Lagrangian and compare its metrics to Table 1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Alternative MDP formulations and abstractions** – The conclusion suggests exploring other MDP designs (e.g., hierarchical, continuous actions, richer states) to improve scalability and realism. This remains open because only one formulation was tested. Resolution requires controlled experiments comparing alternative designs on the same simulator, measuring computational and operational metrics, alongside theoretical analysis of abstraction error.
2. **Initializing learner with single‑objective policies** – Warm‑starting the DQN from policies optimized on each individual objective may accelerate convergence and stability. This is unexplored; an ablation study comparing random vs. warm‑start initialization, measuring convergence speed and final performance, would provide evidence.
3. **Incorporating strategic interactions and incentives between human and robot agents** – The current model treats agents as static and identical. Introducing strategic decision‑making and incentive schemes could enhance complementarity. This requires extending the MORL framework to a multi‑agent setting with distinct capabilities and possibly private objectives, evaluating via simulation with human behavior models or small real deployments.
4. **Frequent feasibility of single policies** – While theory ensures only time‑averaged feasibility, the paper observes many individual policies satisfying all constraints. The reason is unexplained. Theoretical analysis proving conditions (e.g., strong feasibility, bounded duality gap) that force pointwise feasibility, combined with empirical studies varying constraint tightness and number of objectives, would resolve this.

## Limitations
- Simulator dynamics (state transitions, reward calculations) are incompletely specified, hindering exact replication.
- Constraint thresholds αᵢ values are undisclosed, creating ambiguity about the feasible region.
- DQN and regulator hyperparameters (network architecture, learning rates, step size η, etc.) are omitted, affecting convergence behavior and reproducibility.
