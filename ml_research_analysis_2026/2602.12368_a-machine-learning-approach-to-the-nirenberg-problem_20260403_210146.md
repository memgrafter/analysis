# A Machine Learning Approach to the Nirenberg Problem

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12368  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12368  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** The Nirenberg Neural Network (NNN) directly learns the conformal factor for a prescribed curvature on the sphere, achieving very low PDE residuals (10⁻⁷–10⁻¹⁰) for realizable cases and higher losses for unrealizable ones, providing an empirical classifier.

## Executive Summary
The Nirenberg problem asks whether a given smooth function can be realized as the Gaussian curvature of a metric conformal to the round sphere. This paper introduces the Nirenberg Neural Network (NNN), a mesh-free physics-informed neural network that learns the conformal factor by minimizing a loss function enforcing the curvature PDE. The NNN leverages automatic differentiation to compute the Laplace-Beltrami operator exactly on the sphere, avoiding discretization errors. For curvature functions known to be realizable, the network attains very low losses (10⁻⁷–10⁻¹⁰); for unrealizable prescribers, losses remain orders of magnitude higher, enabling an empirical distinction. Results are validated through Gauss-Bonnet consistency checks and by fitting spherical harmonic expansions to the learned conformal factor, yielding interpretable analytic expressions. The work demonstrates that neural solvers can serve as quantitative computational tools for geometric analysis.

## Method Summary
The approach is a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) that directly parameterises the conformal factor \(u\) as a function of 3D Cartesian coordinates on the sphere \(S^2\). Input points are uniformly sampled on \(S^2\) and partitioned into two stereographic chart patches (north and south) without overlap. The network architecture consists of an optional Random Fourier Features (RFF) encoder, followed by six fully-connected residual blocks with SiLU activations (width 64), and a final linear output layer producing \(u(x,y,z)\). The Laplace-Beltrami operator \(\Delta_{g_0}u\) is computed via automatic differentiation (TensorFlow/JAX) using the formula for the round metric \(g_0\) in patch coordinates. The loss is a normalized mean squared error between the predicted curvature \(R_g = e^{-2u}(2 - 2\Delta_{g_0}u)\) and the prescribed target \(2K\), scaled by \(N = \int_{S^2} (2K)^2 dA_{g_0}\). Training uses the Adam optimizer for 150 epochs with a learning rate schedule (cosine annealing or exponential decay). The trained model is evaluated via final loss, Gauss-Bonnet integral \(\int K_g e^{2u} dA_{g_0}\), visualization of \(u\) and the metric component \(g_{00}\), and optional fitting of a finite spherical harmonic expansion to \(u\) for interpretability.

## Key Results
- For prescribed curvatures known to be realizable (e.g., round metric, low-degree spherical harmonics), the NNN achieves very low losses between \(10^{-7}\) and \(10^{-10}\).
- For prescribed curvatures with known obstructions (e.g., violating Kazdan-Warner identities), the loss remains orders of magnitude higher, providing an empirical classifier of solvability.
- Gauss-Bonnet integrals confirm geometric consistency, and spherical harmonic fits yield coefficients close to analytic ground truth for low-degree spectral pair prescribers, offering interpretability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Automatic differentiation for exact Laplace-Beltrami computation
The NNN uses autograd to compute first and second derivatives of \(u\) with respect to the input coordinates. By applying the chain rule symbolically over the computational graph, it obtains an exact (up to floating-point) expression for \(\Delta_{g_0}u\) in the stereographic chart coordinates. This mesh‑free approach avoids finite‑difference discretization errors and respects the geometry of the sphere. Evidence: The abstract highlights the mesh‑free PINN; Section 3.2 explicitly states the use of autograd for exact derivatives; the low PDE residuals and Gauss‑Bonnet checks across numerous tests validate the accuracy. Break condition: If the learned \(u\) is not smooth enough, autograd gradients may become unstable, leading to divergence or poor convergence, particularly for high‑frequency \(K\).

### Mechanism 2: Loss magnitude as an empirical solvability classifier
The training loss \(L = \mathbb{E}[(R_g - 2K)^2 / N]\) serves as a proxy for whether a solution exists. If a conformal factor \(u\) satisfying the Nirenberg PDE exists, the network can drive \(L\) to near numerical precision (≈\(10^{-7}\)–\(10^{-10}\)). If no solution exists due to geometric obstructions (e.g., Gauss‑Bonnet, Kazdan‑Warner), \(L\) remains significantly higher regardless of hyperparameters. Evidence: The abstract reports very low losses for realizable prescribers and higher losses for unrealizable ones; Figure 5 shows Gauss‑Bonnet errors correlate with loss. Break condition: For borderline or extremely oscillatory prescribers, the network might lack expressive capacity, leading to false‑positive high loss despite realisability.

### Mechanism 3: Spherical harmonic expansion for interpretable recovery
To make the learned \(u\) analytically accessible, a finite spherical harmonic expansion is fitted to the network output. The ansatz \(\tilde{u} = \sum_{\ell \le L} \sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} c_{\ell,m} / (\ell(\ell+1)) \cdot Y_{\ell,m}\) is optimised by supervised learning on samples of \(u_{\text{NN}}\). Once coefficients \(\{c_{\ell,m}\}\) are obtained, the induced curvature \(\tilde{R}\) can be computed via the “Spectral Pairs” formula, allowing direct comparison to \(K\). Evidence: The abstract mentions fitting harmonic expansions; Section 4.4 and Table 6 show that for spectral pair prescribers the fitted coefficients match the ground truth, confirming the mechanism. Break condition: If the true \(u\) requires high‑order modes beyond the truncation order \(L\), the expansion becomes biased, and \(\tilde{R}\) may deviate from \(K\) even if the PNN loss is low.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Uniform point sampler -> Stereographic chart split -> Input network (optional RFF) -> Residual backbone (6× SiLU) -> Output \(u(x,y,z)\) -> Autograd computes \(\Delta_{g_0}u\) -> Loss \(L\) computes \(R_g\) vs \(2K\) -> Optimizer updates weights -> Diagnostics (Gauss‑Bonnet integral, harmonic fitting, visualizations).

### Critical path
1. Sample points uniformly on \(S^2\) and assign to north/south patches.
2. Build NNN with chosen RFF configuration, residual blocks, and output layer.
3. For each batch, compute \(u\) and use autograd to obtain \(\Delta_{g_0}u\) in patch coordinates.
4. Form \(R_g = e^{-2u}(2 - 2\Delta_{g_0}u)\), compute normalized MSE loss.
5. Train with Adam (≈150 epochs) while logging loss and Gauss‑Bonnet error.
6. After training, evaluate final loss, Gauss‑Bonnet consistency, fit spherical harmonics, and visualise.

### Design tradeoffs
- Mesh‑free vs. expressivity: Avoiding a grid removes discretization but demands the network learn global smoothness; RFFs expand frequency support at the cost of extra parameters.
- Input representation: Raw Cartesian coordinates avoid chart stitching complexities but require the network to implicitly respect \(\|x\|=1\); the paper finds it works without explicit constraint.
- Loss weighting: Normalization by \(N\) balances scales but may obscure absolute error; Gauss‑Bonnet provides a hard check.
- RFF bandwidth: Fixed at \(\sigma=1\) for unit sphere; too low loses high frequencies, too high over‑smooths.

### Failure signatures
- Loss stagnates above \(10^{-3}\) for known solvable \(K\) → network too small, no RFFs, or poor LR.
- Gauss‑Bonnet error huge (> \(10^{-6}\) relative) even with moderate loss → autograd instability or metric blow‑up; check \(g_{00}\) plots.
- Exploding \(u\) during training → optimizer instability; reduce LR or add gradient clipping.
- For known unsolvable \(K\), loss might dip moderately but Gauss‑Bonnet error remains orders of magnitude larger than for solvables.
- Harmonic fitting yields large \(\tilde{R}\) MSE despite low \(u\)‑MSE → expansion order \(L\) too low for spectral content.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Round metric test (\(K \equiv 1\)):** Expect loss \(\sim10^{-7}\), \(u \approx 0\), Gauss‑Bonnet error \(\sim10^{-9}\). Validates autograd and loss.
2. **Known solvable non‑constant (\(K = Y_{2,0}\)):** Expect loss \(\sim10^{-7}–10^{-10}\), Gauss‑Bonnet error < \(10^{-6}\), fitted harmonic matches known \(c_{2,0}\).
3. **Known unsolvable (\(K = 2 + x\)):** Expect loss > \(10^{-1}\) (orders higher than case 2), Gauss‑Bonnet error huge (\(\sim10^{-1}–10^{0}\)). Confirms classifier behaviour.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can the empirical loss threshold be formally linked to known obstructions like Kazdan‑Warner or degree‑theoretic criteria?
- How to systematically address the non‑uniqueness of solutions due to the action of \(\text{Conf}(S^2)\) (Möbius transformations) within the PINN framework?
- To what extent can the Nirenberg Neural Network approach be generalized to higher‑dimensional manifolds (e.g., \(S^n\)) or other geometric PDEs such as Yamabe or \(\sigma_k\)-curvature?
- Can equivariant neural architectures (respecting rotational or conformal symmetries) improve sample efficiency, reduce loss variance, and enhance interpretability?
- What architectural modifications (depth, width, RFFs) are necessary to accurately represent highly oscillatory prescribers (e.g., spherical harmonics with \(\ell > 3\)), and can these achieve losses suitable for computer‑assisted proofs?

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter choices (network size, RFF count, learning schedule) are not fixed and significantly affect performance, especially for challenging \(K\).
- The Möbius gauge freedom is not quotiented, so learned \(u\) is not unique, complicating comparison with analytic solutions.
- Expressivity may be insufficient for high‑frequency prescribers (\(\ell > 3\)) without larger architectures, risking false negatives.

## Confidence
- Automatic differentiation computes the Laplace‑Beltrami operator exactly: **High** (validated by low PDE residuals and Gauss‑Bonnet checks).
- Loss magnitude distinguishes realizable vs. unrealizable prescribers: **Medium** (works for tested cases but unproven for borderline or high‑frequency \(K\)).
- Harmonic fitting yields interpretable conformal factors: **Medium** (accurate for low‑degree spectral pairs; truncation errors for higher \(\ell\)).

## Next Checks
- Systematic capacity sweep: increase network size and RFF bandwidth for high‑degree spherical harmonics (\(\ell = 5,7,9\)) to quantify expressivity thresholds and adjust loss criteria.
- Gauge robustness test: apply random Möbius transformations to known solutions; verify NNN still attains low loss and that recovered \(u\) transforms consistently, informing gauge‑invariant loss design.
- Benchmark against finite‑element solvers: solve a diverse set of prescribers (including obstructions) with standard numerical methods and compare conformal factors, curvature residuals, and Gauss‑Bonnet integrals.
