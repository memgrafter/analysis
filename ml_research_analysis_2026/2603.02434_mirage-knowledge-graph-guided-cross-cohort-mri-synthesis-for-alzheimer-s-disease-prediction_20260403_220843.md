# MIRAGE: Knowledge Graph-Guided Cross-Cohort MRI Synthesis for Alzheimer's Disease Prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02434
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02434
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Improves AD classification balanced accuracy to 0.70 (13% gain over unimodal EHR baseline) on ADNI data.

## Executive Summary
MIRAGE tackles the problem of missing MRI scans for Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis by transferring knowledge from cohorts with available MRI to those with only electronic health records (EHR). It employs a biomedical knowledge graph and graph attention networks to propagate embeddings from MRI-rich patients to EHR-only patients, using a frozen 3D U-Net decoder as an anatomical regularizer to ensure biologically plausible latent representations without synthesizing full 3D volumes at inference. On the ADNI dataset, MIRAGE achieves a balanced accuracy of 0.70 for AD classification using a simple MLP classifier—a 13% improvement over the best unimodal EHR baseline—while human evaluation confirms high anatomical realism in the generated surrogate representations.

The framework reformulates the missing-MRI problem as latent distillation rather than image synthesis, which mitigates the clinical risks of hallucinating detailed anatomy and reduces computational overhead. By distilling a compact 1D diagnostic‑surrogate representation, MIRAGE enables safe and efficient AD prediction from EHR alone, as evidenced by comprehensive ablations that demonstrate the necessity of the knowledge graph, adapter alignment, and autoencoder prior.

## Method Summary
MIRAGE consists of several components: a pre‑trained 3D U-Net autoencoder trained on MRI‑rich patients (Ptr) to extract latent representations z_i and hierarchical skip connections; a biomedical knowledge graph (iBKH) that links patients to standardized medical concepts using SapBERT embeddings; a three‑layer graph attention network (GAT) for embedding propagation; an adapter network with a whitening‑coloring (WC) transform to align GAT outputs to the autoencoder’s latent space; and a lightweight classifier (MLP or CNN). The GAT is trained in two stages: first via supervised regression to predict z_i for Ptr patients, then fine‑tuned with decoder‑consistency losses (MSE and SSIM) that encourage decoded outputs to match real MRI. For EHR‑only patients (Pte), the GAT propagates embeddings from connected concept nodes; these are adapted via the adapter and combined with skip features from the top‑K=1 most similar Ptr patient (by cosine similarity). During training, a frozen decoder reconstructs a synthetic MRI from the adapted latent and aggregated skips, providing reconstruction losses (L1, SSIM, total variation) that regularize the latent to be anatomically plausible. At inference, the decoder and skip features are discarded; only the distilled latent vector is fed to the classifier.

The training data includes paired 3D MRI and 88‑dimensional EHR features for the MRI‑rich cohort, while the EHR‑only cohort provides only EHR (its MRI is masked). The knowledge graph is constructed by connecting patient nodes to concept nodes from iBKH based on semantic similarity of their SapBERT embeddings; exact thresholds are unspecified. After pipeline training, the final performance is evaluated by training a separate 3D CNN on synthetic MRIs generated for the EHR‑only cohort and measuring balanced accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, and specificity on held‑out ADNI subjects. Ablation studies (removing KG, adapter, or autoencoder) show significant performance drops, validating the contribution of each design choice.

## Key Results
- Achieves balanced accuracy of **0.70** for AD classification using a simple MLP on distilled latents, a **13%** relative improvement over the best unimodal EHR baseline (0.62) on ADNI.
- Maintains high anatomical realism in the synthesized surrogate representations, as assessed by human experts.
- Ablations reveal critical roles: removing the KG drops performance to 0.54, removing the adapter to 0.58, and removing the autoencoder to 0.61, confirming the importance of all three components.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A Biomedical Knowledge Graph (KG) propagates structural priors from MRI‑rich to EHR‑only patients, addressing EHR sparsity/heterogeneity by mapping disparate clinical variables into a unified semantic latent space.
- **Mechanism:** Patient nodes are linked to standardized medical concepts (diseases, symptoms) via SapBERT embeddings. A Graph Attention Network (GAT) then performs message passing, where an EHR‑only patient’s initial zero embedding aggregates weighted features from connected concepts and, transitively, from MRI‑rich patients sharing those concepts. This infers a latent embedding approximating what an MRI‑derived representation would be.
- **Core assumption:** The KG’s ontological structure provides a stable semantic scaffold such that clinical similarity (via shared concept nodes) correlates with neuroanatomical similarity, allowing knowledge transfer.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "leverages a Biomedical Knowledge Graph (KG) and Graph Attention Networks to map heterogeneous EHR variables into a unified embedding space that can be propagated from cohorts with real MRIs to cohorts without them."
  - [section 2, "KG‑Guided Embedding Propagation"]: Details the two‑stage GAT training (supervised regression then decoder‑consistency) to ensure compatibility with the MRI latent manifold.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors (e.g., "Multimodal MRI Accurately Identifies Amyloid Status…") focus on direct MRI analysis, not KG‑based cross‑modality transfer. Evidence for *this specific mechanism* is primarily internal; corpus shows active research on AD multimodal integration but not this KG‑propagation approach. **Assumption:** The cited iBKH KG and SapBERT linking are effective for this task, as validated by their in‑house ADNI experiments.
- **Break condition:** If the KG lacks sufficient, high‑quality connectivity between EHR‑only and MRI‑rich patients for a given clinical concept (e.g., rare symptom codes), message passing fails, yielding an uninformative latent vector (evidenced by the w/o KG ablation dropping BAcc to 0.54).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A frozen, pre‑trained 3D U‑Net decoder acts as a differentiable anatomical constraint, forcing 1D latent vectors from the GAT to encode biologically plausible, macro‑level pathology semantics without requiring full voxel synthesis at inference.
- **Mechanism:** The adapter‑mapped latent vector (plus top‑K aggregated skip features from clinically similar MRI patients) is passed through the frozen decoder. Reconstruction losses (MSE, SSIM, TV) on the *synthetic* 3D volume create a gradient that refines the 1D latent vector during training, ensuring it contains information sufficient to activate the decoder’s spatial filters for plausible anatomy. At inference, the heavy decoder is discarded; only the distilled 1D vector is used.
- **Core assumption:** A pre‑trained autoencoder’s decoder latent space is structured such that semantically meaningful anatomical variations (e.g., atrophy) are linearly separable and can be modulated by a 1D condition vector, while high‑frequency skip features supply generic anatomical context.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "a frozen pre‑trained 3D U‑Net decoder strictly as an auxiliary regularization engine… forcing 1D latent representations to encode biologically plausible, macro‑level pathological semantics."
  - [section 2.3, "Adapter and Decoder Alignment"]: Describes the WC transformation and top‑K skip feature aggregation (K=1) to supply spatial priors, with reconstruction losses applied during joint adapter/classifier training.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors like "Multi‑modal MRI‑Based… with Transformer‑based Image Synthesis" use image synthesis for downstream tasks, but do not employ a frozen decoder as a pure regularizer. **Assumption:** This decoupling of representation learning from full synthesis is novel and effective, as shown by the w/o AE (BAcc collapse to 0.61) and w/o Adapter (drop to 0.58) ablations.
- **Break condition:** If the frozen decoder’s latent manifold is poorly aligned with the GAT’s output distribution (e.g., due to severe domain shift between ADNI training MRIs and a target hospital’s scanner), reconstruction losses become uninformative or cause training instability, failing to distill useful features.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Reformulating missing‑MRI as latent distillation (not image synthesis) circumvents the ill‑posed, high‑risk generation of detailed 3D anatomy from sparse EHR, achieving efficient and clinically safe AD prediction.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of generating a full 3D MRI volume for an EHR‑only patient at inference (which would be blurry, hallucinated, and computationally heavy), MIRAGE generates only a compact 1D "diagnostic‑surrogate" embedding. This embedding is directly fed to a lightweight classifier (e.g., MLP). The expensive 3D decoder is used *only* during training as a regularizer.
- **Core assumption:** The downstream AD classification task depends on a lower‑dimensional, pathology‑focused representation of brain structure (e.g., encoded atrophy patterns) rather than on pixel‑perfect reconstruction of a full MRI volume. The frozen decoder’s gradients are sufficient to imbue the 1D vector with this information.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "By exclusively utilizing this distilled 'diagnostic‑surrogate' representation during inference, MIRAGE completely bypasses computationally expensive 3D voxel reconstruction."
  - [section 1]: Explicitly states synthesizing de novo 3D scans from tabular EHR is "technically challenging and poses severe clinical risks." The solution is "anatomy‑guided latent distillation."
  - [experiments, Table 1]: The best result (MLP+Syn MRI BAcc=0.70) uses the 1D latent vector from the adapter (post‑decoder distillation) as input to an MLP, *not* the synthetic 3D volume itself. The 3D CNN baseline uses *actual* synthesized images, performing worse (0.68).
- **Break condition:** If the clinical task requires explicit volumetric measurements (e.g., exact hippocampal volume) that are not captured in the 1D distilled latent, performance would degrade compared to a model with access to a high‑fidelity synthesized image. The human evaluation (Table 3) suggests anatomical realism is maintained, but this mechanism’s success is task‑specific to classification.

## Foundational Learning

### Biomedical Knowledge Graphs (KGs)
- **Why needed here:** To resolve EHR schema heterogeneity and sparsity by grounding disparate patient records (labs, diagnoses, medications) in a shared, structured ontology (iBKH), enabling meaningful similarity propagation.
- **Quick check question:** If two patients have completely different EHR coding systems (e.g., ICD‑10 vs. local lab codes), what property of a KG allows the model to infer they might represent similar clinical states?

### Graph Attention Networks (GATs)
- **Why needed here:** To perform differentiable, attention‑weighted message passing on the heterogeneous KG, allowing the model to learn which concept nodes (diseases, symptoms) are most relevant for inferring a missing MRI‑based representation for a given patient.
- **Quick check question:** In the two‑stage GAT training, why is the decoder‑consistency fine‑tuning (Loss 4) necessary after the supervised regression (Loss 3)?

### Autoencoder Latent Manifolds as Priors
- **Why needed here:** The pre‑trained 3D U‑Net autoencoder defines a compressed, structured space where valid brain anatomies reside. Using its frozen decoder as a regularizer forces the GAT’s output to lie on this manifold, preventing biologically implausible generations.
- **Quick check question:** Why is a pre‑trained autoencoder’s decoder used instead of training a new generator from scratch conditioned on EHR?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Pre‑train: 3D U‑Net autoencoder (encoder+decoder) on Ptr MRI.  
Pipeline: Ptr & Pte EHR → KG construction via SapBERT linking → 3‑layer GAT propagation → Adapter (WC transform + MLP) → Top‑K=1 skip feature retrieval from Ptr → (training only: frozen decoder with reconstruction losses) → Classifier (MLP/CNN).

### Critical Path
EHR (Pte) → KG Linking → GAT Propagation (u_j) → Adapter Alignment (tilde{z}_j) → Classifier. The 3D decoder path is a side branch for regularization only.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **K=1 Skip Aggregation vs. K>1:** Paper uses K=1 for efficiency. Larger K could provide more robust spatial priors but risks diluting patient‑specific pathology with averaging.
- **Frozen Decoder vs. Fine‑tuning:** Freezing prevents catastrophic forgetting of population anatomy but may limit adaptability to cohort‑specific scanner artifacts. Fine‑tuning would break the "structural prior" assumption.
- **1D Surrogate vs. 3D Synthesis:** Distillation sacrifices potential volumetric biomarker extraction for efficiency and safety. Tradeoff is explicit interpretability of synthetic scans.

### Failure Signatures
- **w/o KG ablation (BAcc 0.54):** Model fails to map heterogeneous EHR; likely outputs near‑zero or random latents.
- **w/o Adapter ablation (BAcc 0.58):** GAT output not aligned to decoder manifold; reconstruction loss ineffective, latents fail to modulate decoder meaningfully.
- **w/o AE ablation (BAcc 0.61):** Replacing pre‑trained decoder with random init/MLP generator destroys anatomical constraint; latent space unregulated.
- **High synthetic MRI SSIM but low classifier BAcc:** Decoder produces generic, cohort‑average brains that pass structural metrics but lack patient‑specific pathology for diagnosis.

### First 3 Experiments (Engineer's Checklist)
1. **AE Convergence Check:** Train 3D U‑Net autoencoder on Ptr MRI. Verify reconstruction loss (MSE) plateaus and qualitative samples show clear anatomy (ventricles, hippocampi).
2. **GAT Propagation Sanity:** After GAT Stage 1 & 2, compute cosine similarity between u_j (for Pte) and its own z_i (if available in a validation fold) vs. random patients. Should show higher self‑similarity.
3. **Adapter Alignment Visualization:** After WC transform, project u_j and tilde{z}_j (t‑SNE/UMAP). tilde{z}_j should cluster more coherently with training z_i by diagnosis (CN/AD), showing alignment to the AE’s latent disease manifold.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** How does MIRAGE generalize to external, real‑world EHR‑only cohorts with different data collection protocols and patient demographics beyond the ADNI dataset?  
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "the study is limited by a lack of evaluation on external cohorts beyond ADNI… future work will validate the framework on independent real‑world EHR‑only cohorts to assess generalization and robustness across clinical settings."  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper’s strong performance is demonstrated only on a single dataset (ADNI). Generalization to diverse healthcare systems with varying EHR schemas, imaging protocols, and population characteristics is critical for real‑world deployment but remains untested.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi‑center external validation study using ID‑only patient data from at least two other major healthcare institutions or databases (e.g., UK Biobank, MIMIC‑IV), reporting balanced accuracy and anatomical fidelity metrics compared to the ADNI internal validation.

2. **Question:** To what extent does the performance of MIRAGE depend on the specific structure, coverage, and quality of the underlying Biomedical Knowledge Graph (e.g., iBKH)?  
   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The framework’s core innovation is KG‑guided propagation. The KG is treated as a fixed, monolithic resource ("we use iBKH"), and the ablation "w/o KG" uses a simpler direct mapping. This suggests sensitivity to KG design but does not analyze it.  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper asserts that the KG links heterogeneous EHR variables but does not investigate how factors like concept density, relation types, or noise in the KG affect the quality of the propagated embeddings and final AD classification.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study varying the KG (e.g., using a different ontology like SNOMED CT vs. a more specialized neurodegeneration KG) or systematically removing/perturbing edge types and concept nodes, measuring the resulting change in BAcc and reconstruction SSIM.

3. **Question:** Can the anatomy‑guided latent distillation approach of MIRAGE be effectively extended to other 3D medical imaging modalities (e.g., CT, PET) and multi‑class disease classification tasks beyond binary AD vs. CN?  
   **Why unresolved:** The method is developed and evaluated solely for 1.5T structural T1‑weighted MRI and a binary AD classification task. The claim that it synthesizes "biologically plausible, macro‑level pathological semantics" is validated only for AD biomarkers (hippocampal atrophy, ventricular enlargement). Its ability to capture modality‑specific and disease‑specific nuanced pathologies is unknown.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Re‑implementation and testing of the MIRAGE pipeline (or its core principles) on a multi‑class dataset (e.g., distinguishing CN, MCI, AD) with a different modality (e.g., FDG‑PET), reporting per‑class AUC/BAcc and conducting radiologist review for disease‑specific anatomical features.

4. **Question:** What is the minimal necessary conditioning on the cohort‑aggregated skip features (K=1 neighbors) to maintain diagnostic utility, and does this lead to anonymization or privacy leakage risks?  
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "For efficiency, we use K=1 to supply spatial priors." The case study shows anatomically realistic outputs. However, the paper states the synthetic MRI is used "without paired EHR–MRI training data" but note the training patients provide skip features.  
   **Why unresolved:** Using spatial priors from a single nearest neighbor (K=1) risks propagating that patient’s unique anatomical idiosyncrasies rather than general pathology. This could compromise patient privacy (if synthetic scans are too similar to real ones) or reduce generalizability (if the K=1 neighbor is an outlier). The trade‑off between anatomical realism and population‑level pathology encoding is not analyzed.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic study varying K (1, 3, 5, 10, 20, all) measuring (a) downstream AD classification performance, (b) pairwise anatomical dissimilarity (e.g., SSIM) between synthetic scans and all real training scans, and (c) a privacy audit (e.g., member inference attack success rate) to quantify uniqueness.

5. **Question:** How does the computational cost and inference latency of MIRAGE (which still invokes a frozen 3D U‑Net decoder during training with skip feature retrieval) compare to a truly lightweight model that directly maps graph‑propagated embeddings to a classifier, and is the 13% gain worth this overhead?  
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "MIRAGE completely bypasses computationally expensive 3D voxel reconstruction" at *inference*. However, training involves complex steps: GAT propagation, WC transformation, adapter training, and crucially, generating synthetic volumes via the frozen decoder to compute reconstruction losses (L_recon).  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper emphasizes efficient inference but provides no analysis of training time, memory footprint, or the cost of the top‑K skip feature retrieval and decoder forward passes during training. The 13% gain is relative to unimodal EHR baselines; the cost‑benefit versus a simpler model that directly uses graph embeddings for classification is unquantified.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed computational benchmarking section reporting training time (GPU hours), peak memory usage, and per‑patient inference latency for MIRAGE vs. (a) the unimodal EHR MLP baseline, and (b) a variant where the adapter and classifier are trained without the decoder reconstruction loss (L_recon = 0). This would isolate the cost of the anatomical regularization.

## Limitations
- **KG linking mechanism unspecified:** The threshold for patient‑concept edges and the exact mapping from heterogeneous EHR fields to KG nodes are not described, which could lead to noisy propagation for rare or poorly coded concepts.
- **Missing hyperparameters and architectural details:** Crucial parameters (e.g., AE depth, GAT dimensions, learning rates, loss weights, adapter architecture) are omitted, preventing exact replication and making sensitivity analysis difficult.
- **Single‑dataset evaluation:** All experiments are conducted on ADNI; generalization to external hospitals, different scanners, and other neurodegenerative diseases remains untested.

## Confidence
- **KG propagation effectiveness:** Medium (ablation shows gain but KG construction is a black box and no external validation).
- **Frozen decoder as anatomical prior:** Medium (ablation supports its role, but alignment of GAT outputs to the decoder manifold is not directly verified).
- **Latent distillation vs. full synthesis:** High for classification (direct evidence from experiments); Medium for clinical safety (human evaluation limited to a small sample).

## Next Checks
1. **KG sensitivity analysis:** Vary patient‑concept similarity thresholds (e.g., 0.5–0.9) and measure balanced accuracy drop; visualize edge connectivity for AD‑relevant concepts to assess robustness.
2. **Latent space alignment verification:** After adapter training, project GAT embeddings (u_j) and adapted latents (tilde{z}_j) with UMAP. Confirm that tilde{z}_j clusters by diagnosis (CN/AD) alongside AE latents (z_i), indicating successful alignment.
3. **Skip aggregation (K) ablation:** Test K=1 against K=3,5,10; evaluate downstream AD classification performance, anatomical diversity via pairwise SSIM between synthetic and real scans, and privacy via a member inference attack to determine if higher K mitigates privacy risks.
