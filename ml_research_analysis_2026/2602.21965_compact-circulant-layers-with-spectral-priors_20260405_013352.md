# Compact Circulant Layers with Spectral Priors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21965  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21965  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Introduces spectral circulant/BCCB layers parameterized by FFT coefficients, enabling exact Lipschitz bounds, a Gaussian process prior, and Hermitian-aware variational inference; achieves strong accuracy/calibration with 7‑72× fewer parameters and improves Vision Transformer efficiency.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes spectral circulant and block‑circulant circulant‑with‑boundary (BCCB) layers that are parameterized by their real FFT half‑spectrum coefficients. This representation drastically reduces the number of free parameters (from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d)\) for circulants) while preserving the full linear operator. The authors place a discrete spectral Gaussian process prior on the coefficients, encouraging smoothness and stationarity, and develop a Hermitian‑aware structured variational posterior for Bayesian inference. Exact Lipschitz constants are obtained in closed form via the maximum magnitude of the frequency response (1‑D) or maximum singular value across frequencies (2‑D). The method is evaluated in three regimes: small variational Bayesian neural networks, Bayesian heads over frozen features, and deterministic Vision Transformers. Across all settings, spectral layers deliver substantial parameter savings, high in‑distribution accuracy, well‑calibrated uncertainties, improved OOD detection, and tighter robustness certificates compared to dense baselines.

## Method Summary
Spectral layers implement the operation \(y = \text{IRFFT}( h_{\text{half}} \odot \text{RFFT}(x) )\) for 1‑D signals; for 2‑D multi‑channel data they compute \(\text{RFFT2}(X)\) per channel, perform frequency mixing via a trainable tensor \(K_{\text{half}}\) (output channels × input channels × frequency grid), then apply \(\text{IRFFT2}\). The half‑spectrum \(h_{\text{half}}\) contains all non‑redundant real FFT coefficients; its size is \(\lfloor d/2 \rfloor + 1\) for length‑\(d\) signals, ensuring Hermitian symmetry for real outputs. A spectral Gaussian process prior assigns independent (proper complex) Gaussians to each frequency bin with variance \(S(k) = \sigma_0^2 / (1 + \rho(k)\alpha)\), where \(\rho(k)\) is the normalized frequency radius, thus favoring low‑frequency content. For Bayesian inference, the variational posterior is defined in the real coordinate space obtained by a linear map \(T\) that unpacks the free parameters of the half‑spectrum. It follows a low‑rank‑plus‑diagonal Gaussian distribution \(q(a) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, U \text{diag}(\lambda^2) U^\top + \text{diag}(\sigma^2) + \epsilon I)\), from which samples are drawn and transformed back via \(T\) to obtain \(h_{\text{half}}\). The KL divergence with the diagonal prior admits a closed‑form expression. Lipschitz constants are computed exactly: for a single 1‑D spectral layer \(d_{\text{Lip}} = \max_k |\hat{h}(k)|\); for 2‑D, it is \(\max_{u,v} \sigma_{\text{max}}(K(u,v))\). The approach is applied (i) as end‑to‑end Bayesian networks on MNIST/Fashion‑MNIST, (ii) as compact Bayesian heads on frozen CIFAR‑10 ResNet features, and (iii) as drop‑in replacements for square linear layers in a small Vision Transformer, where all \(d_{\text{model}} \to d_{\text{model}}\) projections become spectral circulant layers.

## Key Results
- Parameter efficiency: 7‑72× fewer parameters than dense baselines (e.g., 8.6k vs 622k) while achieving 0.92 accuracy on MNIST→Fashion‑MNIST and AUROC 0.81 for OOD detection.
- Bayesian heads on frozen CIFAR‑10 features match the accuracy of a Dense‑32 head (0.971) with ~3× fewer weights and provide tighter Lipschitz certificates (e.g., certified radius larger by factor >2).
- In Vision Transformers, spectral projections improve accuracy (CIFAR‑10: 0.716 vs 0.685) and lower NLL, while reducing total parameters by 1.4×; they also exhibit flatter curvature in attention projections.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Spectral circulant layers exploit the Fourier diagonalization of circulant matrices: matrix multiplication becomes an elementwise product in the frequency domain. By parameterizing only the non‑redundant half‑spectrum, the number of free parameters is linear in the signal length, yet the spatial filter remains fully expressive. The spectral GP prior, with variance decreasing with frequency, imposes a smoothness inductive bias that acts as an effective regularizer, improving generalization and calibration. The Lipschitz constant is obtained exactly because the operator norm of a circulant matrix equals the maximum magnitude of its eigenvalues (the frequency response); for 2‑D block‑circulants, it is the maximum singular value over all frequency matrices. The Hermitian‑aware variational posterior respects the symmetry constraints, allowing efficient Bayesian inference with a low‑rank structure that captures correlations in the real coordinate space. Together, these properties yield networks that are compact, uncertainty‑aware, and equipped with geometric robustness certificates.

## Foundational Learning
- **Circulant matrices**: Each row is a circular shift of the first; diagonalized by the Fourier transform. Enables \(O(n \log n)\) matrix‑vector products and reduces parameters from \(O(n^2)\) to \(O(n)\). *Quick check:* Generate a random vector \(h\), compute circulant \(C\) via IFFT of \(h\); verify \(Cx = \text{IRFFT}(h \odot \text{RFFT}(x))\).
- **RFFT half‑spectrum**: The real FFT of a real‑valued signal yields a complex spectrum with Hermitian symmetry; only the first \(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor+1\) entries are independent. *Why needed:* Reduces effective parameter count and guarantees real outputs. *Quick check:* Compare lengths of RFFT vs full FFT; check \(X[-k] = \overline{X[k]}\).
- **Hermitian symmetry**: For a real signal, its Fourier coefficients satisfy \(X(-k) = \overline{X(k)}\). *Why needed:* Ensures the inverse FFT of a half‑spectrum yields a real spatial filter. *Quick check:* Construct a half‑spectrum, compute IFFT, assert all imaginary parts are near zero.
- **Spectral Gaussian process prior**: Place independent Gaussians on frequency coefficients with variance \(S(k)\). Encodes smoothness/stationarity in the spatial domain. *Why needed:* Acts as a structured prior that favors low‑frequency content, improving uncertainty and calibration. *Quick check:* Sample many filters; compute empirical spatial covariance; should be stationary and decay with lag.
- **Lipschitz constant of circulant layers**: For 1‑D, \(\|C\|_2 = \max_k |\hat{h}(k)|\); for 2‑D, \(\|K\|_2 = \max_{u,v} \sigma_{\text{max}}(K(u,v))\). *Why needed:* Provides a certified robustness radius (e.g., for adversarial examples). *Quick check:* For random \(h\), compute norm via power iteration on \(C\) and compare with \(\max |\hat{h}|\).
- **Variational inference with structured posterior**: The posterior is a low‑rank‑plus‑diagonal Gaussian in the real coordinates after applying \(T\). *Why needed:* Balances flexibility and computational cost while respecting Hermitian constraints; yields closed‑form KL. *Quick check:* Draw samples from the posterior, transform to spatial filter, verify Hermitian symmetry and that ELBO decreases during training.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input → RFFT (1D) / RFFT2 (2D, per channel) → Elementwise multiplication with trainable half‑spectrum \(h_{\text{half}}\) (1D) or frequency mixing tensor \(K_{\text{half}}\) (2D) → IRFFT / IRFFT2 → (optional bias) → Non‑linearity (e.g., tanh) → Dense classifier.  
  In Vision Transformers: each square linear layer (\(d_{\text{model}} \to d_{\text{model}}\)) is replaced by a spectral circulant layer; patch embedding and final head remain dense.
- **Critical path:** Data flows from spatial domain into frequency domain (via RFFT), is modulated by learned frequency coefficients, then transformed back. All trainable parameters reside in the half‑spectrum; the FFT/IFFT are fixed, efficient, and differentiable.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  • *Band‑limiting* (e.g., radial mask) reduces parameters further but may limit expressivity; tradeoff between compactness and model capacity.  
  • *Prior slope* \(\alpha\) controls smoothness: larger \(\alpha\) yields flatter priors (more high‑frequency content) and may hurt calibration.  
  • *Variational rank* \(r\) trades posterior expressivity for computational cost; higher \(r\) captures more correlations but increases KL complexity.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. **Invalid Hermitian reconstruction** – output of IRFFT is complex or non‑real.  
  2. **Poor SVI convergence** – ELBO oscillates or diverges; likely due to wrong prior hyperparameters, learning rate, or insufficient MC samples.  
  3. **Mismatched parameter counts** – total parameters differ significantly from reported values (e.g., ViT dense ~1.2M vs spectral ~0.8M).  
  4. **OOD entropy collapse** – ID and OOD predictive entropy distributions overlap (AUROC ≈ 0.5), indicating overconfidence or ineffective prior.  
  5. **Lipschitz miscomputation** – computed bound does not match max‑magnitude formula; often due to taking max over full spectrum instead of half‑plane.  
  6. **Encoder mismatch in frozen CIFAR experiment** – feature dimension not 2048, e.g., using ResNet‑18 with 512‑D output.
- **First experiments (critical validation steps):**  
  1. Implement the core spectral layer (1D and 2D) and assert that outputs are real and parameter counts match half‑spectrum size plus biases.  
  2. Implement the spectral prior and Hermitian‑aware SVI for a single layer; sample from the prior and verify stationarity of the resulting spatial filters.  
  3. Replicate the MNIST→Fashion‑MNIST Bayesian experiment: train a small spectral BCCB network with SVI, and confirm in‑distribution accuracy ~0.92 and OOD AUROC ~0.81.

## Limitations
- The exact mapping \(T\) from effective real coordinates to the complex half‑spectrum is described conceptually but lacks implementation details (index ordering, handling of DC/Nyquist bins).  
- Prior hyperparameters \(\sigma_0^2\) and \(\alpha\) are not reported; their values likely affect the KL term and overall performance.  
- Vision Transformer architecture details (number of layers, attention heads, MLP width) are missing; matching the reported parameter counts requires reverse‑engineering.  
- Frozen CIFAR‑10 encoder specifics (ResNet variant, pretraining source, pooling layer) are unclear, leading to potential feature dimension mismatches.
