# GMP: A Benchmark for Content Moderation under Co-occurring Violations and Dynamic Rules

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01724  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01724  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Models exhibit a coverage deficit (Micro-F1 0.79 vs Macro-F1 0.54) and alignment inertia (Precision drops from 0.72 to 0.51 under dynamic rules).

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses a critical gap in evaluating AI content moderation: real-world scenarios often involve multiple co-occurring violations and dynamically changing policies, which static single-label benchmarks do not capture. To bridge this gap, the authors introduce the Generalized Moderation Policy (GMP) Benchmark, a dual-task evaluation suite. Task A measures multi-label classification of all violations in a single post, while Task B assesses zero-shot adaptation to novel, context-specific moderation rules that may conflict with the model's inherent safety alignment. Evaluations on over 20 large language models reveal two systemic failures: a coverage deficit where models miss long-tail, co-occurring harms (large gap between Micro-F1 and Macro-F1), and alignment inertia where models struggle to override safety priors when rules permit harmful content, leading to significant performance drops in dynamic contexts. Ablation studies indicate that disabling Chain-of-Thought reasoning improves both accuracy and efficiency, and mid-sized models offer a favorable cost-performance trade-off for deployment.

## Method Summary
The GMP Benchmark comprises two complementary tasks designed to mimic realistic moderation demands. Task A is a multi-label classification challenge where each post may contain several violation types simultaneously; models must identify all applicable labels, thereby testing coverage of co-occurring harms. Task B introduces zero-shot adaptation: models are provided with a novel moderation rule (e.g., a platform-specific allowance for certain language in informal contexts) and must apply it to posts without additional training, even when the rule conflicts with the model's pretrained safety objectives. The benchmark dataset was constructed to include a diverse set of violations and their intersections, as well as rule variations that require contextual flexibility. Evaluation metrics include precision, recall, and F1 at both micro and macro levels, allowing the detection of label imbalance effects. The authors test a broad set of LLMs, including both API-based and open-weight models, to assess generalization across architectures and scales.

## Key Results
- **Coverage deficit**: Models reliably flag frequent violations but miss rare, co-occurring harms, reflected in a large gap between Micro-F1 (0.79) and Macro-F1 (0.54) scores.
- **Alignment inertia**: When dynamic rules permit otherwise harmful content, model precision drops significantly (e.g., from 0.72 to 0.51 in informal esports chat), showing difficulty overriding safety priors.
- **Ablation and scaling**: Disabling Chain-of-Thought reasoning improves both accuracy and efficiency; mid-sized models achieve the best cost-performance trade-off compared to larger or smaller counterparts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The observed coverage deficit stems from the long-tail distribution of co-occurring violation pairs in training data; models are biased toward frequent single-violation patterns and lack explicit supervision for rare intersections. Multi-label prediction further compounds this by requiring models to jointly consider multiple labels, which is under-supported by standard cross-entropy losses. Alignment inertia arises because safety alignment during pretraining instills strong priors to refuse any potentially harmful content, making it difficult for models to conditionally allow certain content based on nuanced rule specifications, especially in few-shot or zero-shot settings. The improvement from disabling Chain-of-Thought reasoning suggests that explicit step-by-step prompting can lead models to over-apply generic safety heuristics or get distracted by intermediate reasoning, degrading task-specific accuracy and increasing latency. Mid-sized models strike a balance: they are large enough to capture complex patterns yet not so over-parameterized that they overfit to spurious safety correlations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi-label classification**  
  *Why needed*: Real-world posts often contain multiple harms; single-label systems fail to capture intersections.  
  *Quick check*: Given a post, does the model output all relevant violation tags?

- **Zero-shot rule adaptation**  
  *Why needed*: Moderation policies evolve; models must adjust without retraining.  
  *Quick check*: Provide a new rule in natural language; does the model correctly apply it to test cases?

- **Safety alignment inertia**  
  *Why needed*: Over-aligned models resist contextual allowances, limiting flexibility.  
  *Quick check*: Does the model still flag content that is explicitly permitted by a supplied rule?

- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning**  
  *Why needed*: CoT can improve interpretability but may hinder efficiency and adherence to specific rules.  
  *Quick check*: Compare performance with and without CoT prompts on GMP tasks.

- **Label imbalance**  
  *Why needed*: Rare co-occurring violations are underrepresented, leading to poor recall on minority classes.  
  *Quick check*: Examine micro vs macro F1 scores; large gap indicates imbalance impact.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
User Post + Dynamic Rule → Prompt Builder → LLM Inference → Output Parser → Metric Calculator

**Critical path**:  
1. Construct prompt that includes the post and the current moderation rule(s).  
2. Send prompt to LLM and obtain raw response.  
3. Parse response into structured labels (e.g., list of violations).  
4. Compare against ground truth to compute evaluation metrics.  
Any failure in prompt construction or parsing breaks the pipeline.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Prompt length vs rule detail*: Including full rule text consumes context window but ensures clarity.  
- *Static vs dynamic few-shot examples*: Adding examples can improve rule interpretation but may bias toward specific rule styles.  
- *Direct classification vs CoT*: CoT may enhance interpretability but increases latency and can dilute task focus.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Model refusal to answer under dynamic rules (safety override).  
- Inconsistent label sets (e.g., missing some violations or returning unrelated tags).  
- Parsing errors due to non-standard output formats.

**Three first experiments**:  
1. Baseline Task A: Evaluate multi-label performance without any rule adaptation.  
2. Task B zero-shot: Introduce novel rules that conflict with safety priors; measure precision/recall shifts.  
3. CoT ablation: Repeat Task A/B with and without explicit "think step by step" prompting to quantify impact on accuracy and latency.

## Limitations
- Synthetic/curated data may not reflect real-world moderation complexity (slang, cultural nuance).  
- Predefined co-occurring violations risk missing novel intersecting harms.  
- Zero-shot dynamic rule evaluation oversimplifies iterative policy changes with human oversight.
