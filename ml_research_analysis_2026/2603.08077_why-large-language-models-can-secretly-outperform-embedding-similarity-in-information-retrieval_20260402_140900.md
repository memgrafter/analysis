# Why Large Language Models can Secretly Outperform Embedding Similarity in Information Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08077
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08077
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** LLM-based relevance judgment systems with reasoning can identify relevance missed by embedding-based retrievers and human annotators, but this advantage is invisible in standard benchmarks due to annotation limitations.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether Large Language Model-based Relevance Judgment Systems (LLM-RJS) with Chain-of-Thought reasoning can overcome a fundamental limitation of Neural Embedding Retrieval Systems (NERS). NERS operationalizes relevance as vector-space similarity, which fails to identify documents that are relevant but textually dissimilar to a query. Using the TREC-DL 2019 dataset, the authors find that while top LLM-RJS models perform on par with state-of-the-art NERS on standard ranking metrics (NDCG), a deeper analysis reveals that reasoning LLMs systematically assign higher relevance scores to certain passages. Many of these passages, rated perfectly relevant by the LLM, were scored as irrelevant by human annotators; manual inspection suggests the LLM was correct and the human labels were erroneous due to the same "short-sighted" reliance on surface similarity. The core conclusion is that LLM-RJS with reasoning may actually outperform NERS and human judgment on a subset of queries, but this superiority is masked because the evaluation benchmarks themselves suffer from the very limitation the LLM overcomes.

## Method Summary
The study uses the TREC-DL 2019 test set, comprising query-passage-human score triples (scores 0–3). **Experiment 1** compares ranking performance: systems score all pairs, passages are ranked per query, and NDCG metrics compare these rankings against the human-annotated ideal ranking. Models evaluated include NERS (Gemini Embeddings, Nomic Embed), BM25, and multiple LLM-RJS (GPT-5.1, Sonnet 4.5, etc.) with and without reasoning. Only models with low failure rates (non-integer outputs) are included, using the intersection of successful scoring pairs for fair comparison. **Experiment 2** focuses on direct score distribution: GPT-5.1 scores are compared to human annotations via average scores and confusion matrices, with a specific analysis of cases where the LLM rated a passage 3 but humans rated it 0. The average NERS similarity for these "false positive" LLM ratings is compared to similarity for true positives (human=3 & LLM=3).

## Key Results
- Top LLM-RJS models (e.g., GPT-5.1) achieve mean NDCG@10 ~0.72, statistically on par with state-of-the-art NERS (Gemini Embeddings), showing no significant improvement in direct ranking benchmarks.
- The reasoning LLM-RJS assigns systematically higher direct relevance scores (average 1.23) than humans (0.79) and the non-reasoning LLM (1.03).
- In 94 cases where reasoning GPT-5.1 rated a passage perfectly relevant (3) but humans rated it irrelevant (0), 89 passages were demonstrably relevant upon manual inspection, indicating annotation errors. For these cases, average NERS similarity was lower (~0.44) than for true positives (~0.50), consistent with the "short-sightedness" hypothesis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: NERS Short-Sightedness
Neural Embedding Retrieval Systems (NERS) conflate relevance with textual/vector similarity. By mapping queries and documents into dense spaces where relevance is operationalized as geometric proximity (e.g., cosine similarity), NERS fails when a relevant document uses different terminology or requires inferential steps not captured by surface form. Evidence: The paper's abstract and section 2 frame this as a "necessary limitation." Correlative evidence shows NERS similarity scores are lower for passages the LLM identifies as relevant but humans miss (~0.44 vs. ~0.50 for true positives), suggesting those passages are less textually proximate.

### Mechanism 2: Reasoning LLM-RJS Overcomes Short-Sightedness
LLM-RJS with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning performs implicit multi-step inference to judge relevance beyond surface similarity. The model decomposes queries, interprets document content in context, and infers answers not explicitly lexical matches. Evidence: Section 5 states reasoning "allows the model to identify relevance that is not obvious." The empirical signature is the distribution shift: reasoning models give more perfect scores (3) than humans or non-reasoning models. For 94 "false positive" passages (LLM=3, human=0), manual review found ~89% genuinely relevant, suggesting the LLM's judgments were correct and the human labels were short-sighted.

### Mechanism 3: Annotation Short-Sightedness Masks LLM Advantage
Standard IR benchmarks like TREC-DL 2019 use human annotations that suffer from the same short-sightedness as NERS. Annotators, under time or instruction constraints, default to surface similarity when assigning scores. Consequently, a ranking metric like NDCG, which aligns LLM-RJS output with these annotations, does not reward the LLM's correct but non-similar judgments—treating them as errors. The LLM's latent superiority is invisible because the evaluation target (human labels) is flawed. Evidence: The paper's abstract calls human annotations "false-positives" due to short-sightedness. Section 5 explicitly states that human annotations "suffer from similar short-sightedness as the NERS systems, explaining why the LLM-RJS do not reach a noticeable improvement" in ranking experiments.

**Synthesis:** Together, these mechanisms explain why LLM-RJS with reasoning can identify relevance missed by NERS and human annotators, but this superiority is masked in standard benchmarks because the evaluation targets (human annotations) suffer from the same short-sightedness that the LLM overcomes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Neural Embedding Retrieval Systems (NERS)**  
  *Why needed:* Understand the baseline technology critiqued in the paper—how it operationalizes relevance as vector similarity and why that is insufficient for non-obvious relevance.  
  *Quick check:* How would a NERS system rank a passage "Plants convert light energy into chemical energy" for the query "What is photosynthesis?" if its training corpus strongly associates photosynthesis with terms like "chlorophyll"?

- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning in LLMs**  
  *Why needed:* The paper's central hypothesis depends on reasoning as the key differentiator for LLM-RJS; CoT is the mechanism by which an LLM might perform deeper linguistic processing.  
  *Quick check:* What intermediate steps might a CoT-enabled LLM generate when judging relevance for a query "How do I fix a leaky faucet?" and a passage about replacing a washer?

- **IR Evaluation Metrics & Annotation Limitations**  
  *Why needed:* The paper's null result in Experiment 1 and its interpretation hinge on understanding that metrics like NDCG are only as good as the ground-truth labels; the critique is of the evaluation paradigm, not just model performance.  
  *Quick check:* If a system correctly identifies 10 passages as relevant (score 3) that humans scored 0, but all 10 are actually relevant upon expert re-review, how would its NDCG@10 compare to a system that only retrieves passages humans scored 2 or 3?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Query & Document Corpus -> NERS Engine (compute embeddings, similarity) / LLM-RJS Engine (prompt LLM for 0–3 score) -> Score Generation -> Ranking Aggregator -> Evaluation Module (NDCG vs. human scores) -> Analysis Subsystem (error analysis, e.g., LLM vs. human score discrepancies)
- **Critical path:** Input (query + document pairs) -> Scoring (similarity for NERS, verbalized judgment for LLM-RJS) -> Ranking per query -> Metric computation against human annotations -> Analysis of score distributions and disagreements.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  *Cost & Latency:* NERS: pre-embed documents once → cheap, fast retrieval. LLM-RJS: infer per pair → ~10x more expensive and slower (per paper).  
  *Generalization:* LLM-RJS is zero-shot and may identify "deep" relevance; NERS is bound by embedding training distribution.  
  *Scalability vs. Depth:* NERS scales for large corpora; LLM-RJS currently requires re-ranking or subset retrieval (CoT re-ranking proposed as hybrid).
- **Failure signatures:**  
  *Experiment 1 (Ranking):* NERS/LLM-RJS perform similarly on NDCG → expected empirical failure but argued to be an evaluation failure.  
  *Experiment 2 (Direct Scores):* Reasoning LLM shows systematic distribution shift (higher means, more 3s vs. human 0s) → stated mechanism's signature.  
  *Operational:* LLM prompt non-compliance (extra text output) causing parsing failures (e.g., Llama3.2:3B, Sonnet 4.5).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate Experiment 1: Implement NERS (e.g., nomic-embed-text) and multiple LLM-RJS (e.g., GPT-5.1, Llama3.1:8B) on TREC-DL 2019; compute NDCG@10; expect top LLM-RJS ~on par with NERS (mean ~0.72).
  2. Replicate Experiment 2: Use GPT-5.1 with reasoning_effort "none" vs. "medium"; compare direct score distributions (mean, confusion matrix) against human; expect reasoning model yields higher average and more 0->3 disagreements.
  3. Validate "Annotation Short-sightedness": Manually inspect the 94 cases where reasoning LLM gave 3 and human gave 0; verify paper's claim that ~89/94 are genuinely relevant but missed.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Can LLM-RJS with reasoning outperform human annotations when re-evaluating existing relevance judgments with provided reasoning chains?**  
  *Basis:* Future work suggests but does not definitively show LLM-RJS can outperform humans. A controlled study where experts reassess pairs, comparing original labels to LLM ratings augmented with reasoning chains, is needed to adjudicate accuracy.  
  *Why unresolved:* Current evaluation relies on potentially flawed human-annotated benchmarks, preventing clear demonstration of LLM advantages because the ground truth may be incorrect.  
  *Resolving evidence:* Expert re-annotation study with reasoning chain support.

- **How should IR evaluation benchmarks be redesigned to avoid short-sighted annotations and properly assess reasoning-based retrieval systems?**  
  *Basis:* Standard benchmarks like TREC-DL 2019 use human labels that suffer from short-sightedness, creating a circular evaluation that disadvantages LLM-RJS regardless of actual performance. The paper calls for re-specifying IR subtasks and evaluation for LLM-RJS.  
  *Why unresolved:* No existing benchmark explicitly includes non-similar but relevant cases or evaluates reasoning depth; annotations are typically produced under constraints that encourage surface similarity.  
  *Resolving evidence:* New benchmark with guidelines emphasizing inferred relevance, inclusion of known "deep relevance" cases, and evaluation across multiple paradigms.

- **To what extent do human annotation limitations (short-sightedness) generalize across different IR datasets and domains beyond TREC-DL 2019?**  
  *Basis:* The authors acknowledge short-sightedness is not universal and might be mitigated with expert annotators, better instructions, and more time, but they only evidence it in TREC-DL 2019.  
  *Why unresolved:* The study's evidence is limited to a single passage retrieval dataset; prevalence in other tasks (document retrieval, web search) or domains (biomedical, legal) is unknown.  
  *Resolving evidence:* Replication analysis across diverse benchmarks (MS MARCO, BEIR, domain-specific collections) with expert adjudication of LLM-human disagreements.

## Limitations
- Small manual validation sample (94 passages) may not generalize to broader patterns of annotation error.
- Evidence is confined to a single dataset (TREC-DL 2019); generalizability across IR tasks and domains is unestablished.
- Critical implementation details are unspecified: exact GPT-5.1 model identifier, reasoning_effort parameter semantics, and embedding extraction procedures for Nomic Embed.
- Human annotation reliability (e.g., inter-annotator agreement) is not reported, weakening the claim that human labels are systematically short-sighted.
- The proposed hybrid CoT re-ranking is discussed but not empirically evaluated, leaving the practical path to improvement speculative.

## Confidence
- **NERS short-sightedness is a fundamental limitation:** Medium (theoretically plausible, supported by correlative evidence and examples, but not proven causal).
- **Reasoning LLM-RJS can identify relevance missed by NERS and humans:** Medium (distribution shift and manual validation of 94 cases support this, but validation is small and selective).
- **Standard IR benchmarks mask LLM-RJS gains due to annotation short-sightedness:** Medium (the paper's interpretation of the null result hinges on the unverified claim that human annotations are themselves short-sighted for the disputed cases).
- **LLM-RJS with reasoning is cost-prohibitive for first-stage retrieval:** High (explicitly stated; per-pair inference is orders of magnitude slower/more expensive than embedding pre-computation).

## Next Checks
1. **Re-annotation study:** Randomly sample query-document pairs where reasoning LLM-RJS and NERS strongly disagree (e.g., LLM=3, NERS similarity low). Have expert annotators re-label using guidelines that explicitly require considering inferred relevance and provided LLM reasoning chains; measure agreement shift and adjudicate true labels.
2. **Cross-dataset replication:** Repeat the core analysis (Experiment 2 and manual validation) on other IR benchmarks (e.g., MS MARCO, BEIR, a domain-specific collection like PubMed). Quantify the frequency of LLM-human disagreements and the proportion where LLM appears correct upon expert review.
3. **Reasoning-effort ablation:** For a fixed LLM (e.g., GPT-5.1), vary reasoning depth (e.g., "none," "low," "medium," "high") and measure impact on: (a) disagreement rate with NERS similarity rankings, (b) distribution of direct scores relative to humans, and (c) proportion of LLM-perfect/human-zero cases deemed relevant on review. This isolates the effect of reasoning intensity.
