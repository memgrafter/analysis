# Analyzing Physical Adversarial Example Threats to Machine Learning in Election Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00481
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00481
- Reference count: 26
- Primary result: Derivation of Equation 8 to quantify the minimum fraction of adversarial ballots needed to flip an election with confidence \(1-\alpha\), and experimental evidence that \(l_1\) and \(l_2\) attacks are most effective in the physical (printed and scanned) domain for models trained on combined ballot data.

## Executive Summary
This paper examines the threat of physically realizable adversarial examples against machine learning ballot classifiers used in U.S. elections. It introduces a probabilistic framework (Equation 8) that computes the exact percentage of ballots that must be compromised with adversarial noise to overturn an election result with a specified confidence level, moving beyond heuristic analyses of close races. The authors conduct a large-scale empirical study, generating and physically printing 144,000 adversarial ballot bubbles to evaluate six attack norms across four classifiers. They find a significant discrepancy between digital and physical attack effectiveness: norms like \(l_\infty\) and \(l_2\) dominate digitally, but \(l_1\) and \(l_2\) succeed more reliably after printing and scanning. The most effective physical attack (\(l_1\)-APGD) reduces robust accuracy to 50.1% on CaiT-C, implying nearly 50% success per compromised ballot. The work highlights the necessity of evaluating adversarial robustness in realistic, physical election pipelines.

## Method Summary
The research integrates theoretical modeling with a rigorous physical experimentation protocol. Theoretically, it models each voter’s choice \(X_i\) and the indicator \(Y_i\) for ballot compromise as independent random variables; using the law of total expectation/variance and the Central Limit Theorem, it approximates the distribution of the observed vote margin \(\bar{w}\). The condition \(P(\bar{w} < 0) \geq 1-\alpha\) yields a quadratic inequality in the compromised fraction \(p_c\), which is solved to produce a closed-form expression (Equation 8). Empirically, the authors train eight model variants (four architectures × two training datasets) on ballot images. They then generate digital adversarial examples for six white‑box APGD attacks optimized with the DLR loss. To assess physical feasibility, they print these examples on letter paper (96 bubbles per page) using an HP LaserJet, scan them with a Ricoh fi‑7160, and process the scans via error‑correcting code alignment, segmentation, and a denoising U‑Net before classification. Robust accuracy is measured for each attack in both digital and physical domains, with perturbation budgets set through an expert panel to ensure imperceptibility.

## Key Results
- The derived Equation 8 provides a direct way to calculate the minimum fraction of adversarial ballots \(p^*_c\) required to flip an election, given total ballots \(N\), baseline win probability \(p_b\), desired margin \(\Delta\), and confidence level \(\alpha\).
- Physical attack effectiveness differs from digital: \(l_1\)-based attacks (e.g., \(l_1\)-APGD) achieve the lowest robust accuracy in the physical domain (as low as 50.1% on CaiT-C), while \(l_\infty\) and \(l_2\) attacks that dominate digitally degrade significantly after the print‑scan process.
- For models trained on the Combined dataset (which includes marginal voter marks), the physical robust accuracy gap demonstrates that even imperceptible noise can misclassify nearly half of printed adversarial ballots, confirming a realistic threat to election tabulation systems.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The paper derives a closed‑form probabilistic framework (Equation 8) that calculates the minimum fraction of adversarial ballots (\(p^*_c\)) required to flip an election with a target confidence level (\(1-\alpha\)).
- **Mechanism:** The framework models voter choices (\(X_i\)) and ballot compromise indicators (\(Y_i\)) as independent random variables. Using the law of total expectation/variance and the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), it approximates the distribution of the observed vote margin (\(\bar{w}\)). The condition \(P(\bar{w} < 0) \geq 1-\alpha\) is transformed into a quadratic inequality in \(p_c\), yielding a solvable expression.
- **Core assumption:** Voter behaviors (\(X_i\)) and compromise events (\(Y_i\)) are independent and identically distributed; the CLT holds for the total ballots \(N\).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "We derive a probabilistic framework for determining the number of adversarial example ballots that must be printed to flip an election."
  - [Section 4.3]: Presents the full derivation leading to Equation 8, defining \(E[W_i]\), \(\mathrm{Var}(W_i)\), and the quadratic solution.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors discuss adversarial attacks but lack specific election‑flipping probabilistic models. Evidence is weak; this framework is novel to this paper.
- **Break condition:** The CLT approximation fails for small \(N\) or if voter independence is violated (e.g., coordinated voting blocks). The derived \(p^*_c\) becomes unreliable.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The most effective adversarial attack norm (\(l_p\)) in the **physical** domain (printed and scanned ballots) differs from the **digital** domain, with \(l_1\) and \(l_2\) attacks outperforming \(l_\infty\) and \(l_0\) attacks physically.
- **Mechanism:** Physical printing/scanning introduces noise and non‑linear distortions (e.g., printer DPI limits, scanner calibration, paper texture). These transformations differentially degrade perturbations optimized for specific norms. \(l_1\)-based noise, which is sparse and locally concentrated, may survive the physical pipeline better than \(l_\infty\)'s uniform noise.
- **Core assumption:** The physical printing‑scanning process is a fixed, noisy channel that distorts adversarial perturbations in a norm‑dependent way.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "attacks most effective in the digital domain (l2 and l_infinity) differ from those most effective in the physical domain (l1 and l2, depending on the model)."
  - [Section 6.1]: Table 3 shows \(l_1\)-APGD yields the lowest robust accuracy (e.g., 50.1% on CaiT-C) physically, while \(l_2\) was best digitally (Table 2).
  - [corpus]: Neighbors focus on digital or generic physical attacks (e.g., face recognition). No election‑specific physical norm comparisons. Evidence is weak and domain‑specific.
- **Break condition:** If the printing‑scanning pipeline is altered (e.g., different printer/scanner models) or if the model’s decision boundary is norm‑agnostic, the attack effectiveness hierarchy may collapse.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The paper’s experimental methodology (printing and scanning 144,000 adversarial examples) empirically validates the physical attack gap and provides measurable robust accuracy metrics for real‑world election systems.
- **Mechanism:** Adversarial examples generated digitally for each norm/perturbation are physically printed on ballots, scanned, and evaluated against the ballot classifier. The drop in robust accuracy from digital to physical (Table 3 vs. Table 2) quantifies the degradation caused by the physical domain.
- **Core assumption:** The printed/scanned adversarial examples faithfully represent an attacker’s capability to compromise ballot printers and the subsequent tabulation process.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 6]: Describes the printing pipeline (HP LaserJet, Ricoh scanner), alignment, and evaluation of 144,000 examples.
  - [Section 6.1]: Reports physical robust accuracy for all six attacks across four models (Table 3), highlighting \(l_1\)’s effectiveness.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors discuss physical adversarial examples in other contexts (e.g., face recognition) but not ballot printing. Evidence is indirect at best.
- **Break condition:** The experimental setup does not capture all real‑world variations (e.g., different ballot paper stocks, voter‑induced smudges, or scanner aging), potentially overestimating attack feasibility.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Probabilistic election modeling (CLT, Bernoulli trials)**
  - Why needed here: To move beyond heuristic "close race" arguments and compute the exact number of compromised ballots needed for a confident election flip, accounting for voter randomness.
  - Quick check: Given \(N=10,000\), \(p_b=0.52\), \(\Delta=0.02\), \(\alpha=0.05\), what is \(z_\alpha\) and how does it scale \(p^*_c\)?

- **Concept: Adversarial attack norms (\(l_\infty\), \(l_2\), \(l_1\), \(l_0\)) and APGD**
  - Why needed here: Different norms produce perceptually distinct noise. Understanding their digital effectiveness (\(l_\infty\)/\(l_2\) common) vs. physical survivability (\(l_1\)/\(l_2\)) is central to the threat model.
  - Quick check: Why might an \(l_1\) perturbation (sparse, high‑magnitude few pixels) survive printing better than an \(l_\infty\) perturbation (uniform small change)?

- **Concept: Physical domain effects (printing noise, scanner distortion)**
  - Why needed here: The core finding—that physical attacks diverge from digital—relies on understanding how the print‑scan chain alters adversarial signals. This bridges ML security and election infrastructure realism.
  - Quick check: If a printer cannot reproduce sub‑pixel changes, which attack norm is most degraded?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Election Pipeline: Ballot design → Printer (compromised) → Voter marks → Scanner → ML Classifier (CaiT/ResNet/VGG/SVM) → Tabulation.  
Attack Generator: White‑box APGD variants (\(l_\infty\), \(l_2\), \(l_1\), \(l_0\), \(l_0+l_\infty\), \(l_0+\sigma\)-map) optimized on clean ballot images.  
Physical Realization: Print generated adversarial ballots → Scan → Align (ECC) → Segment → Classify.  
Framework Calculator: Inputs \(N\), \(p_b\), \(\Delta\), \(\alpha\) → outputs \(p^*_c\) (Equation 8).

**Critical path:** The attacker’s path starts at the **printer compromise** (Section 3). Adversarial noise is added to blank bubbles. The physical realization path (print → scan → align → classify) must preserve the perturbation’s effectiveness to achieve the attack success rate modeled in Equation 8.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Model complexity vs. robustness: Larger models (CaiT, 56M params) are not more robust; CaiT‑C was the least robust physically (Table 3). Simpler models (SVM) showed mixed results.  
- Imperceptibility vs. effectiveness: Noise budgets (\(\epsilon\), \(k\)) were chosen by expert panel (Section 5, Appendix C). Larger budgets increase attack success but risk human detection—a key constraint.  
- Digital optimization vs. physical generalization: Attacks optimized digitally (\(l_\infty\), \(l_2\)) failed physically; \(l_1\) succeeded. This suggests robustness evaluations must include physical transformations.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Digital‑physical discrepancy: Robust accuracy in physical domain is **higher** (worse for attacker) than digital for \(l_\infty\)/\(l_2\) attacks, but \(l_1\) remains effective. If physical robust accuracy matches digital for all norms, the printing channel may not be distorting attacks as hypothesized.  
- Model inconsistency: An attack that works on one model (e.g., \(l_1\) on CaiT‑C) may not on another (e.g., SVM‑C where \(l_2\) was best). A defense must account for model‑specific vulnerability.  
- Dataset shift: Models trained only on "Bubbles" (no marginal marks) fail on realistic "Combined" data (Section 2). A classifier deployed without marginal mark training will have higher error rates, potentially interacting with adversarial effects.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Clean accuracy validation:** Train all 8 model variants (4 architectures × 2 datasets) and measure accuracy on Combined/Bubbles validation sets (Table 1). Confirm Combined‑trained models achieve >99% on both datasets.  
2. **Digital adversarial evaluation:** For each model, run 6 attacks across 6 perturbation budgets (144 total configurations) on 1,000 clean bubble images. Record robust accuracy (Tables 17–22). Identify most effective digital attack per model (Table 2).  
3. **Physical adversarial evaluation:** For Combined‑trained models only, generate adversarial examples at chosen imperceptible budgets (Table 10). Print 96 bubbles/page, mix with clean ballots, scan, align (ECC), and classify 1,000 examples per attack. Record physical robust accuracy (Tables 23–28). Compare to digital results to identify physically effective attacks (Table 3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Underattack adversarial examples effectiveness:** The paper focuses exclusively on *overattack* (changing blank to filled). The feasibility of *underattack* (changing filled to blank) remains unknown.  
2. **Hardware variations impact:** All physical experiments used a single printer (HP LaserJet) and scanner (Ricoh fi‑7160). Different hardware could substantially alter attack success rates.  
3. **Training dataset influence:** It is unclear whether training on marginal marks (Combined) versus only bubbles affects physical robustness; a trade‑off between utility and security is not understood.  
4. **Multi‑candidate extension:** The probabilistic framework assumes two candidates. Generalizing to elections with more than two candidates would require a different statistical model and likely a different \(p^*_c\).

## Limitations
- **Artifact availability:** Dataset splits, model checkpoints, and exact printer/scanner settings are unreleased, blocking faithful reproduction. Retraining may yield divergent results.
- **Imperceivable thresholds:** Noise budgets were set by an expert panel without published rubric or inter‑rater reliability, making thresholds non‑reproducible.
- **Physical pipeline specificity:** Findings are tied to one print‑scan chain (HP LaserJet + Ricoh scanner); generalizability to other election hardware is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Probabilistic framework (Equation 8): Medium** – Derivation is mathematically sound under i.i.d. voter and CLT assumptions, but real‑world voter dependence (e.g., bloc voting) may violate premises.
- **Digital vs. physical attack effectiveness gap: Low–Medium** – Experimental tables strongly support the \(l_1/l_2\) advantage physically for Combined‑trained models, but the effect is tied to a single hardware setup; corpus signals show limited prior validation in the election domain.
- **Experimental scale (144 k physical examples): Low** – The volume is impressive, but reproducibility is obstructed by missing artifacts and the high false match rate (0.592) among retrieved neighbors indicates weak literature anchoring.

## Next Checks
1. **Full artifact release:** The authors must publish exact dataset splits, model checkpoints, and a detailed bill of materials for the printing/scanning hardware (model numbers, DPI, color profiles, paper stock) to enable precise replication.
2. **Pipeline diversity study:** Repeat the physical attack evaluation across at least three distinct printer‑scanner‑paper combinations (e.g., inkjet vs. laser, different scanner models) to test whether the \(l_1/l_2\) advantage is hardware‑specific or intrinsic to the ballot domain.
3. **Voter correlation robustness:** Simulate elections with spatially/temporally correlated voter choices (e.g., precinct‑level clustering) to quantify how violations of the i.i.d. assumption impact the \(p^*_c\) predictions from Equation 8.
