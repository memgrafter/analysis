# A Long-term Value Prediction Framework In Video Ranking
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17058
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17058
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: PDQ increases video views by 2.49%, author LTV (3-day return visits) improves by 0.21% and quality author views by 4.03%, while attributed slide time raises watch time by 1.23% in online A/B tests on Taobao; offline XAUC improves from 0.6252 to 0.6378.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a framework for long-term value (LTV) prediction in short-video ranking, tackling three key challenges: position bias, attribution ambiguity, and temporal limitations. The authors propose PDQ for position debiasing using quantile normalization, a multi-dimensional attribution module with learnable strengths and a hybrid loss, and cross-temporal author modeling with day-level targets and a co-training strategy. Offline experiments show XAUC improvement, and online A/B tests on Taobao demonstrate significant gains in video views, author LTV, and watch time. The system is deployed at billion-scale and yields sustained engagement improvements with stable short-term trade-offs.

## Method Summary
The framework comprises three integrated modules. (1) Position-aware Debias Quantile (PDQ): Data is split into page groups based on exposure position. Within each group, slide time distributions are discretized into quantile bins; each sample’s label is its quantile index, converting regression into a bounded ordinal prediction that compares videos only within similar positional contexts. (2) Multi-dimensional attribution: Attribution coefficients for each video are computed from seven dimensions (positional co-occurrence, collection transition, retrieval similarity, multimodal similarity, author, category) via a weighted sum with learnable scalars, followed by a hybrid loss (MSE + Tweedie) to handle zero-inflated heavy-tailed targets. (3) Cross-temporal author modeling: Author LTV aggregates a user’s watch time on an author’s videos over a 7-day window with exponential decay. A dual-stream co-training approach alternates between standard (t-1) samples and delayed (t-N) author-time samples, applying stop-gradient to the shared backbone to prevent interference. The architecture includes Multi-Scale Embedding Fusion, Target Attention, task-specific towers, and a score fusion that combines short-term and long-term heads.

## Key Results
- Offline XAUC improves from 0.6252 to 0.6378.
- Online A/B tests on Taobao: video views increase by 2.49%, 3-day author return visits increase by 0.21%, quality author views increase by 4.03%, and watch time (via attributed slide time) increases by 1.23%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Position-aware Debias Quantile (PDQ) normalizes position bias in slide time estimation by transforming raw watch time into position-robust quantile labels within page groups.
- **Mechanism:** Data is partitioned into page groups based on exposure position. Within each group, slide time distributions are discretized into T quantile bins via isofrequency division. Each sample’s label is its quantile index (adjusted for zero-values), turning a continuous regression problem into a bounded (0–1) ordinal prediction task that inherently compares videos only against peers in similar positional contexts.
- **Core assumption:** Position bias manifests as systematic, predictable shifts in the entire slide time distribution across pages, not just additive offsets; therefore, normalizing within homogeneous position groups (where the distribution shape is similar) effectively isolates the content-related signal.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "introduces a Position-aware Debias Quantile (PDQ) module that normalizes engagement via quantile-based distributions, enabling position-robust LTV estimation without architectural changes."
  - [section 3.1] Details the grouping, quantile calculation (Eq. 1), and label generation (Eq. 2–4). Figure 3 visualizes shifting quantile distributions across pages.
  - [corpus] Corpus shows prior position bias work (e.g., [10, 17, 31]) but PDQ’s group-wise quantile approach is novel for billion-scale ranking; evidence is weak for direct comparative efficacy beyond this paper’s own offline results.
- **Break condition:** If page groups are too coarse (e.g., low-traffic positions) leading to sparse/dusty quantile bins, or if position bias interacts strongly with other factors (e.g., user intent) that aren’t captured by page grouping alone.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Multi-dimensional attribution with learnable strengths and a hybrid loss (MSE + Tweedie) reduces attribution ambiguity by assigning causal influence from a video to subsequent watch time based on contextual, behavioral, and content signals, while filtering noise.
- **Mechanism:** For each video *j*, slide time is computed as the sum of subsequent videos’ watch time *tᵢ* weighted by attribution coefficients *cⱼᵢ* ∈ [0,1]. Each *cⱼᵢ* = σ(∑ₖ wᵈ·cⱼᵢᵈ), where *cⱼᵢᵈ* is a binary or similarity-based signal from dimension *d* (positional co-occurrence, collection transition, retrieval similarity, multimodal similarity, author, category) and *wᵈ* is a learnable scalar. The Tweedie loss (ρ=1.5) handles zero-inflated, heavy-tailed attributed slide time targets; combined with MSE it mitigates underestimation.
- **Core assumption:** (1) The influence of video *j* on later watch time can be decomposed into separable dimensions with additive contributions passed through a sigmoid; (2) explicit noise filtering is possible by thresholding raw signals (e.g., V2V similarity >0.5) and shaping the loss function.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "multi-dimensional attribution module that learns continuous attribution strengths... customized hybrid loss with explicit noise filtering improves causal clarity."
  - [section 3.2] Defines the seven dimensions (Table 1), the weighted combination (Eq. 7), and the Tweedie loss (Eq. 9–10). Figure 5 shows heavy-tail of *Sⱼ*.
  - [corpus] Corpus indicates prior aggregation is naive; this paper’s learnable, multi-dimensional attribution is presented as novel, but corpus lacks direct prior art on hybrid Tweedie+MSE for this task.
- **Break condition:** If attribution dimensions are highly collinear (making learnable weights unstable), or if the preset thresholds (e.g., similarity >0.9) discard too many true causal links, or if Tweedie’s ρ parameter is sensitive to distribution shifts.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Cross-temporal author modeling with day-level LTV targets and co-training captures creator-driven re-engagement beyond the current session, while maintaining training stability under delayed labels.
- **Mechanism:** Author LTV *Sₐᵤₜₕ* aggregates a user’s watch time on videos from a given author over an N-day window (N=7), with exponential decay α prioritizing recent days. Training uses a dual-stream framework: (1) standard *t–1* samples for all other tasks; (2) delayed *t–N* samples for the author-time task, with stop-gradient applied to the shared backbone to prevent interference from delayed, noisy labels.
- **Core assumption:** (1) A non-trivial fraction of long-term value is author-driven re-engagement that manifests days later; (2) alternating updates with frozen author-related embeddings (via stop-gradient) allow the long-horizon task to benefit from shared representations without destabilizing short-term objectives.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "cross-temporal author modeling with day-level targets" and "co-training framework... stop gradient to prevent... affecting updates of shared embeddings."
  - [section 3.3] Defines *Sₐᵤₜₕ* (Eq. 11), dual-stream sampling (Algorithm 2), and stop-gradient use in Figure 6.
  - [corpus] Corpus shows most LTV work is re-ranking or RL-based; this paper’s focus on ranking-stage, author-centric day-level targets with co-training appears unique, but corpus evidence for author-driven re-engagement is indirect.
- **Break condition:** If author re-engagement decays faster than N=7 days (missing later value), or if delayed label noise (from incomplete N-day windows) overwhelms signal despite co-training, or if stop-gradient prevents useful gradient flow from author task to backbone.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Quantile Regression for Position Normalization  
  **Why needed here:** PDQ relies on converting continuous slide time into position-group-specific quantile bins, requiring understanding of how quantiles differ from raw values and why grouping reduces bias.  
  **Quick check question:** If two videos in the same page group have quantile labels 0.6 and 0.8, what does this imply about their relative expected slide time *within that position context*?

- **Concept:** Multi-Task Learning with Gradient Isolation  
  **Why needed here:** The framework jointly trains session-level (attributed slide time), day-level (author time), and immediate (watch time) tasks; the co-training with stop-gradient is a specific gradient isolation technique to handle delayed labels.  
  **Quick check question:** Why is a stop-gradient applied to the shared backbone when updating the author-time task, instead of simply using a smaller learning rate?

- **Concept:** Heavy-Tailed, Zero-Inflated Target Modeling (Tweedie Distribution)  
  **Why needed here:** Attributed slide time *Sⱼ* is heavily zero-inflated and long-tailed (Figure 5), making MSE prone to underestimation; Tweedie loss (ρ=1.5) is chosen for its property of modeling continuous non-negative distributions with a point mass at zero.  
  **Quick check question:** What property of the Tweedie distribution with ρ=1.5 makes it suitable for a target that is mostly zero but has a long tail of large positive values?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Inputs → Multi-Scale Embedding Fusion (MSEF) → Target Attention → Task-specific towers (Day-level [Author Time], Session-level [PDQ, Attributed Slide Time], Current [Watch Time, Interaction, Completion]) → Score fusion (weighted sum of long-term heads multiplicatively calibrated by short-term heads). Dual-stream sampler feeds standard (*t–1*) and delayed (*t–N*) samples to appropriate towers.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. **Data ingestion:** Raw logs → session construction → compute slide time, attributed slide time (via precomputed attribution signals), and author-time labels (over 7-day windows with decay).  
  2. **Training loop:** Alternate batches from standard stream (all tasks, *t–1* labels) and author-time stream (*t–N* labels). For author-time batch, stop-gradient at backbone output before its tower.  
  3. **Loss computation:** Each task head uses its designated loss (PDQ: MSE on quantile; Attributed ST: MSE+Tweedie; Author Time: MSE; others: task-specific). Total loss is weighted sum.  
  4. **Serving:** Forward pass through all towers; fuse scores per online formula.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **PDQ quantile bin count (T=50):** Finer bins better position discrimination but increase noise per bin and label sparsity; T=50 chosen empirically.  
  - **Loss weighting (λ in Eq. 10):** Balances Tweedie vs. MSE for attributed ST; too high λ underfits mean, too low fails to handle heavy-tail.  
  - **Co-training vs. separate model:** Co-training shares representation but risks instability; separate model safer but loses cross-task regularization. Stop-gradient + alternating updates is a middle ground.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Position bias persists:** Offline XAUC by page groups shows no improvement (or drop) in higher pages (see Table 5).  
  - **Attribution noise:** High video coverage ratio (V-ratio) but low slide time contribution (S-ratio) in Table 1 indicates attribution signals are widespread but not meaningful.  
  - **Author LTV instability:** Online LT3 degrades or QA VV drops sharply; offline PCOC for author-time task far from 1.  
  - **Training divergence:** Loss spikes when co-training without stop-gradient or with mismatched λ.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **PDQ ablation:** Train with/without page grouping (i.e., treat all positions as one group). Compare XAUC overall and by page group (Table 5). Expect grouped PDQ to improve XAUC in mid-to-high pages where bias is stronger.  
  2. **Attribution dimension ablation:** Remove one attribution dimension at a time (e.g., exclude author, or set all *wᵈ*=0 for content). Recompute attributed slide time labels and measure change in MSE/MAE/XAUC (Table 4). Identify most-contributing dimension.  
  3. **Author-time training strategy:** Compare (a) single dedicated branch updated only on *t–N*, (b) co-training with stop-gradient (as in paper), (c) co-training without stop-gradient. Monitor online LT3 and QA VV, and offline PCOC for author-time task (Figure 8). Expect (b) to best balance stability and efficacy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the cross-temporal author modeling module generalize to other dimensions like topics or styles, and does it maintain performance gains when applied to non-author dimensions?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state the framework is "readily extensible to further aspects such as topics or styles" and that "limitations point to clear extensions: broadening cross-temporal modeling beyond authors to topics/styles."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only validates author-centric LTV; no experiments or analysis are provided for topic/style-based cross-temporal modeling, leaving generalizability untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct online A/B tests comparing author-based, topic-based, and style-based cross-temporal LTV targets, measuring long-term retention and quality engagement lifts while controlling for computational cost.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What specific types of hard negative samples (beyond zero-valued slide times) could most effectively improve the multi-dimensional attribution module's causal clarity and noise filtering?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors mention "incorporating additional negative signals" as a future direction and note their hybrid loss includes "explicit noise filtering," but the negative sampling strategy is not deeply explored.
- **Why unresolved:** The attribution design uses predefined rules (e.g., similarity thresholds) and a hybrid loss, but the choice and construction of negative samples (e.g., unrelated videos in a session) are not systematically analyzed or optimized.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies varying negative sample sources (e.g., random videos, topically dissimilar videos, positionally distant videos) and analyzing their impact on attribution accuracy (e.g., via human evaluation or counterfactual metrics).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How sensitive is the PDQ module's performance to the quantile bin count (T=50), and could adaptive or learned quantization schemes yield better position debiasing?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] PDQ uses a fixed T=50 determined "through systematic experimentation," but the sensitivity to this hyperparameter and potential for data-driven quantization are not investigated.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper presents PDQ as robust but does not analyze T's impact on XAUC/MSE across different page groups or user segments; alternative quantization strategies (e.g., dynamic binning based on position variance) are untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Sensitivity analysis varying T (e.g., 10, 50, 100) on offline metrics (XAUC, MSE per page group) and online VV lift, potentially extending to learnable quantile boundaries via reinforcement learning or differentiable binning.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Under what conditions does the co-training framework with stop-gradient and dual-stream sampling fail to stabilize long-term task training, and how do interference patterns vary across feature types (contextual vs. behavioral)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The framework uses stop-gradient and alternating updates to "prevent information leakage and stabilize optimization," but no failure mode analysis or gradient interference measurement is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** While co-training shows gains, the paper does not diagnose gradient conflicts between day-level (Author Time) and short-term tasks (e.g., Watch Time) or quantify how stop-gradient affects representation learning for different feature subspaces.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Gradient similarity analysis (e.g., cosine similarity between task gradients) during training, ablation removing stop-gradient to measure performance drop, and case studies on samples where long-term signals contradict short-term engagement.

## Limitations
- **Attribution formulation ambiguity:** The core definition of attribution weights is inconsistent—Equation 7 suggests learnable continuous weights via sigmoid, while Equation 8 and text indicate binary reduction, creating replicability uncertainty.
- **Missing implementation details:** Page group partitioning strategy (number of groups, exact bucketing), dual-stream sample alignment (matching key and censoring), and the architectures of Multi-Scale Embedding Fusion and Target Attention are not described, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Online fusion specifics:** The exact formula for fusing long-term and short-term scores (weights and multiplicative calibration) is omitted, preventing precise deployment replication.

## Confidence
- **Offline efficacy (XAUC gain):** High – specific metrics and ablations are provided.
- **Attribution mechanism design:** Low – internal contradiction undermines replicability.
- **Author-time co-training rationale:** Medium – logic plausible but lacks external validation.
- **Online A/B results:** Medium – outcomes credible but deployment details are opaque.
