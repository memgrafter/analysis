# Bootstrapping Embeddings for Low Resource Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01732
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01732
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** XL-LoRA, a cross-lingual LoRA-based synthetic data generation method, achieves up to ~8 Spearman point gains on semantic textual similarity (STS) and significant improvements in retrieval recall@10, without requiring any parallel data in the target language.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the scarcity of supervised triplet data for training effective sentence embeddings in low-resource languages. It explores three synthetic data generation strategies using the Gemma 3 27B LLM: in-context prompting (SynCSE), adapter composition (AdaMergeX), and a novel cross-lingual approach (XL-LoRA). Experiments on STS and retrieval benchmarks (MTEB, MIRACL, Belebele) reveal that SynCSE underperforms a simple cross-lingual transfer baseline, while both AdaMergeX and XL-LoRA consistently and significantly improve over all non-synthetic baselines. XL-LoRA emerges as the top performer, yielding gains of up to ~8 Spearman points on STS tasks and notable retrieval boosts. Crucially, XL-LoRA does not require parallel data in the target language for generator training, offering a practical path for many low-resource settings.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with an LLM (Gemma 3 27B) that generates synthetic (anchor, positive, hard negative) triplets for a target low-resource language. Three generation strategies are compared: **SynCSE** uses frozen in-context prompting with English examples; **AdaMergeX** composes three LoRA adapters (English task, source language, target language) to combine task knowledge with target fluency; **XL-LoRA** instruction-tunes a single LoRA adapter on a cross-lingual dataset where the premise is in some source language (not necessarily the final target) and the hypothesis is in English, then at inference generates only the positive and negative in English while keeping the anchor in the target language. The synthetic triplets are used to fine-tune a multilingual sentence encoder (XLM-R or mBERT) via a SimCSE contrastive loss. The resulting embeddings are evaluated on semantic textual similarity (STS) and retrieval tasks (MTEB, MIRACL, Belebele).

## Key Results
- SynCSE (in‑context prompting) underperforms a strong cross‑lingual transfer baseline, largely because the LLM struggles to produce fluent, semantically appropriate target‑language sentences.
- Both AdaMergeX (adapter composition) and XL-LoRA yield consistent and statistically significant improvements over all non‑synthetic baselines.
- XL-LoRA achieves the highest performance overall, with gains of up to ~8 Spearman points on STS and notable increases in recall@10 on retrieval benchmarks; it uniquely requires no parallel data in the target language for generator training.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** In‑context prompting (SynCSE) underperforms because the LLM generator lacks sufficient competency in the target low‑resource language, leading to ungrammatical outputs and poor semantic alignment.
- **Mechanism:** The generator (Gemma 3 27B) is prompted with English examples to produce target‑language triplets. Without explicit linguistic adaptation, it defaults to code‑switching, generates non‑fluent sentences, and fails to match the pragmatic tone of anchors.
- **Core assumption:** A model's ability to generate coherent, semantically appropriate sentences in a language correlates with its internal linguistic competency for that language.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Page 6] "The model often struggled to generate fluent and coherent sentences in the target language. Frequently mixing in words from other languages..."
  - [Page 6] "Contextual and pragmatic mismatch: The generated hard negatives often failed to be contextually or pragmatically aligned with the anchor sentence."
  - [Page 6, Figure 5] High lexical overlap between anchor and hard negative compared to anchor and positive.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Adapter composition (AdaMergeX) improves over prompting by explicitly separating the model's linguistic competency (language adapters) from its task‑specific knowledge (triplet generation adapters), but its gains are limited by the lexical diversity of the generated positives/negatives.
- **Mechanism:** Three LoRA adapters are trained: a task adapter on English NLI, a source language adapter, and a target language adapter. Composition (task + (target_language - source_language)) aims to combine English triplet‑generation skill with target‑language fluency. However, the resulting triplet pairs show higher anchor‑positive lexical overlap than human annotations, leading to weaker alignment in the final embedding space.
- **Core assumption:** LoRA adapter composition can modularly transfer capabilities, but the quality of the generated data (lexical diversity) directly impacts the contrastive learning objective's effectiveness.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Page 7] "Alignment and uniformity analysis... reveals that embeddings produced by adapter composition exhibit weaker alignment than those from cross‑lingual and prompting methods... because adapter composition relies on anchor–positive pairs with higher lexical overlap."
  - [Page 7] "While adapter composition demonstrates a strong potential, its weaker alignment highlights clear opportunities for further refinement."
  - [Page 6] "we found that task adaptation worked extremely well... the performance of the composed adapters was more variable."

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** XL‑LoRA achieves the highest and most consistent gains by circumventing the generator's target‑language output weakness: anchors remain in the low‑resource language, but positives/negatives are generated in English, leveraging the LLM's superior English proficiency and internal cross‑lingual representation alignment.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM is instruction‑tuned on a small, high‑quality parallel dataset (XNLI human translations + synthetic English data) where the premise is in a source language (not necessarily the final target) and the hypothesis (positive/negative) is in English. At deployment, given a low‑resource language anchor, the tuned model generates the positive and negative *in English*. The final encoder is trained on (target_anchor, English_positive, English_negative) triplets. This exploits the LLM's English bias and middle‑layer cross‑lingual concept spaces.
- **Core assumption:** Contrastive learning can align a target‑language anchor with semantically equivalent English hypotheses, and the encoder (multilingual like XLM‑R) can learn to map the target anchor close to its English semantic equivalent.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Abstract] "XL‑LoRA achieves the highest performance... Crucially, XL‑LoRA requires no parallel data in the target language for generator training."
  - [Page 4] "LLMs display internal cross‑lingual alignment... LLMs have a strong bias towards outputting English... masking true capabilities... XL‑LoRA... generates English positives and negatives while only the anchor remains in the target."
  - [Pages 7‑8] "XL‑LoRA generally exhibits improved uniformity across languages... It maintains strong alignment compared to both adapter composition and unsupervised methods."
  - [Page 8] "XL‑LoRA is highly sensitive to training data quality: when trained on XNLI machine‑translated data... produces positives which exhibit notable semantic mismatches... replacing machine‑translated data with XNLI human‑translated data augmented by synthesised examples resolves this issue."

## Foundational Learning
### Contrastive Learning (SimCSE Objective)
**Why needed:** The entire pipeline optimizes an embedding encoder using a supervised contrastive loss. Understanding the role of (anchor, positive, hard negative) triplets, the temperature parameter, and the distinction between unsupervised (dropout positive) and supervised (human/LLM‑generated positive/negative) formulations is essential to interpret results.  
**Quick check:** How would the loss function (Equation 1) change if the hard negative for an anchor were lexically very similar but semantically different, versus a lexically different but semantically similar sentence?

### Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning (LoRA) & Adapter Composition
**Why needed:** AdaMergeX and XL‑LoRA both rely on Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to efficiently tune the LLM generator. Adapter composition is a non‑trivial arithmetic merge of multiple LoRA modules. Understanding rank, scaling (`lora_alpha`), and how adapter weights are summed/subtracted is key to grasping the methodology.  
**Quick check:** In the AdaMergeX formula `T_target = T_src + λ(L_target - L_src)`, what is the intended purpose of subtracting the source language adapter `L_src` from the target language adapter `L_target` before adding to the task adapter `T_src`?

### Cross‑Lingual Transfer in Multilingual Models
**Why needed:** The cross‑lingual baseline and XL‑LoRA both depend on the premise that multilingual encoders (XLM‑R, mBERT) and LLMs (Gemma) develop shared, language‑agnostic semantic spaces. The success of XL‑LoRA assumes that an encoder can align a Turkish anchor with an English positive generated by the LLM.  
**Quick check:** What property of a multilingual model's embedding space enables zero‑shot transfer of an embedding model fine‑tuned on English NLI to, say, Telugu?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Anchor Sentences → [LLM Generator (with adapters)] → Synthetic Triplets → SimCSE Fine‑Tuning → Encoder Model → Evaluation

### Critical Path
Anchor Sentences → [LLM Generator (with adapters)] → Synthetic Triplets → SimCSE Fine‑Tuning → Encoder Model → Evaluation

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Data Quality vs. Availability:** XL‑LoRA needs high‑quality human translations for generator tuning (limited to ~14 languages). AdaMergeX needs target‑language monolingual data for the language adapter, which is more widely available but may be noisier (e.g., Aya collections).
- **Compute vs. Generality:** In‑context prompting (SynCSE) is cheapest but worst. Adapter methods add modest LoRA training cost but yield large gains. XL‑LoRA's generator training is fixed‑cost (10k examples) and scales to any target language without re‑tuning.
- **Triplet Semantics:** XL‑LoRA generates semantically sound *English* positives/negatives, which may be more reliable than target‑language generations from a weak multilingual generator. The tradeoff is that the encoder must learn a cross‑lingual mapping.

### Failure Signatures
1. **SynCSE/Target‑Language Generation Failure:** Inspect synthetic triplets for code‑switching, grammatical errors, and nonsensical negatives (Figure 4, Page 6). High lexical overlap with simple negation (Page 6, Figure 5).
2. **AdaMergeX Failure:** Weak alignment in final embedding space (high `ℓ_align` in Figure 7). May manifest as retrieval tasks where positives are not consistently closer to anchors than negatives.
3. **XL‑LoRA Failure:** Semantic mismatch between target anchor and generated English positive/negative. This occurs if the generator's tuning data is machine‑translated (Table 12, A.6). Check by validating a sample of English hypotheses against the non‑English premise via human or high‑quality MT.
4. **Universal Backbone Failure:** If the base multilingual encoder (XLM‑R/mBERT) has poor initial cross‑lingual alignment for the language pair (anchor_English), all synthetic methods will struggle. Verify with a simple word translation probe.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline Sanity & Triplet Inspection:** For one high‑resource language (e.g., Hindi) and one low‑resource language (e.g., Telugu), run the full pipeline with the cross‑lingual baseline and one synthetic method (e.g., XL‑LoRA). Manually review 50 generated triplets per language. Quantify: % with grammatical errors, % with semantic mismatch (positive not entailing anchor, negative not contradicting).
2. **Adapter Ablation on Generator:** For AdaMergeX, ablate the language adapter composition. Train and compare: (a) English task adapter only (no composition), (b) naive sum (`T_src + L_tgt`), (c) full AdaMergeX (`T_src + λ(L_tgt - L_src)`). Evaluate resulting triplets' lexical diversity (Dice coefficient anchor‑positive) and downstream STS on one language.
3. **XL‑LoRA Data Quality Sensitivity:** Train XL‑LoRA generator adapters on (a) XNLI human translations only, (b) XNLI human + 10k synthetic English data (as in paper), (c) pure machine‑translated parallel data. Generate triplets for a held‑out low‑resource language (e.g., Hausa) and evaluate encoder STS performance. Expect (c) to fail catastrophically, confirming sensitivity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How do synthetic data generation methods generalize across diverse language families and typological features (e.g., morphology, word order, script)?
- What performance improvements can be expected from scaling up the LLM generator beyond 100B parameters?
- Can decoder‑only or instruction‑tuned LLMs serve as effective backbones for embedding models trained on synthetic triplet data?
- Which specific aspects of training data quality (e.g., translation accuracy, semantic equivalence) most critically determine XL‑LoRA’s success?

## Limitations
- **Generator language competency:** SynCSE and AdaMergeX rely on LLM fluency in target languages, which is weak for many low‑resource languages, risking ungrammatical/semantically poor outputs.
- **Data quality sensitivity:** XL‑LoRA requires high‑quality human translations for generator tuning; such datasets are limited to ~14 languages, restricting applicability.
- **Backbone dependency:** All methods assume strong initial cross‑lingual alignment in the encoder (e.g., XLM‑R); this may fail for typologically distant languages, undermining transfer.

## Confidence
- **SynCSE underperformance:** Medium confidence. Evidence of code‑switching and lexical issues is strong, but causality (incompetence vs. prompting) not fully proven.
- **AdaMergeX gains limited by lexical diversity:** Medium confidence. Alignment metrics suggest correlation, but a controlled test isolating lexical diversity is missing.
- **XL‑LoRA superiority & zero target data:** High for observed gains; Medium for cross‑lingual transfer assumption due to sensitivity to generator training data quality.

## Next Checks
1. **Semantic validity of XL‑LoRA triplets:** Manually assess entailment/contradiction in a sample of generated (anchor, English pos/neg) across diverse low‑resource languages to quantify cross‑lingual semantic accuracy.
2. **Adapter ablation on generator:** Compare variants: (a) `T_src + L_tgt`, (b) `T_src + λ(L_tgt − L_src)`, (c) `T_src` only. Measure downstream STS and lexical overlap to isolate the effect of subtracting `L_src`.
3. **XL‑LoRA generator tuning with machine‑translated data:** Train adapters on machine‑translated XNLI for languages without human translations, then evaluate final embeddings. Expected failure would confirm data quality brittleness.
