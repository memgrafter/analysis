# When Verification Hurts: Asymmetric Effects of Multi-Agent Feedback in Logic Proof Tutoring

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27076
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27076
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** Adding a verification Judge agent improves learning gain when upstream Tutor feedback is error-prone (<70% rule accuracy) but degrades performance when Tutor feedback is already reliable (>85%).

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates multi-agent LLM feedback for propositional logic proof tutoring using a knowledge-graph-grounded benchmark of 516 student proof states. Three pipelines—Tutor (sees only optimal step symbol), Teacher (sees full derivation), and Judge (verifies and may override Tutor feedback)—produce scaffolded feedback that avoids answer revelation. The core finding is an asymmetric verification effect: the Judge improves post-feedback accuracy by 10–14 percentage points for weak Tutors (rule accuracy <70%) but harms it by 4–6 points for strong Tutors (>85%). All pipelines fail on states exceeding Step Complexity 4–5, revealing a universal reasoning bottleneck. Automated accuracy metrics overestimate quality by not penalizing failures like answer revelation or unjustified correctness.

## Method Summary
The study simulates tutoring interactions with a Student Agent that generates initial proof steps, then revises only if errors are flagged. Three feedback pipelines—Tutor (partial solution access), Teacher (full derivation access), and Judge (verification layer over Tutor)—produce scaffolded feedback. A Knowledge Graph (KG) encodes all valid proof transitions for each problem, enabling fine-grained evaluation: steps are classified as Optimal, Invalid, or Unjustified, and metrics like Tutor Rule Accuracy and Unique Improvement Count are computed. Step Complexity (weighted operator count) segments results and identifies a ceiling beyond which all pipelines fail. Comparisons across multiple LLMs and pipelines isolate the effects of verification, information access, and problem complexity.

## Key Results
- Verification (Judge) yields asymmetric benefits: +10–14pp learning gain for Tutors with rule accuracy <70%, but −4–6pp for Tutors with >85% accuracy.
- Unique Improvement Count (states where Judge succeeds but Teacher fails) is 84–103 for weak models versus 17–23 for strong models.
- No model or pipeline reliably succeeds on proof states with Step Complexity >4–5, indicating a shared reasoning ceiling.
- Automated accuracy metrics overestimate true pedagogical quality by ignoring unpenalized failures such as answer revelation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Asymmetric Verification Benefit
The Judge agent improves outcomes when upstream Tutor feedback is error-prone by overriding incorrect feedback (e.g., "echo effects" where the Tutor validates a student's wrong rule). It does so by referencing the full derivation context (rule, parents) to correct errors. However, when Tutor feedback is already accurate, the Judge's override imposes overly abstract derivational constraints that strip away the Tutor's specific scaffolding, reducing actionability. Tutor Rule Accuracy serves as a proxy for upstream reliability. Evidence: Section 6.3 shows Judge gains of +10–14pp for weak models (low Rule Acc.) versus losses of ~−5pp for strong models (high Rule Acc.); Unique Improvement Count is much higher for weak models. Break condition: Tutor Rule Accuracy >85% triggers over-specification that outweighs correction benefits.

### Mechanism 2: Universal Complexity Ceiling
All feedback pipelines fail on proof states exceeding a Step Complexity threshold (~4–5), indicating a fundamental reasoning bottleneck independent of feedback architecture. Step Complexity (weighted sum of logical operators with nesting penalty) captures intrinsic symbolic difficulty. Beyond the threshold, required forward-chaining inference becomes intractable for current LLMs regardless of feedback richness; the KG shows fewer viable paths and longer derivations, overwhelming step prediction. Evidence: Abstract notes universal failure beyond complexity 4–5; Table 7 shows performance degradation and rising "feedback hurts" instances with mean complexity. Break condition: Step Complexity > ~4.5 leads to unreliable optimal step generation even for Teacher (full access) and Judge pipelines.

### Mechanism 3: Capability-Dependent Information Access
The extent of solution information access (Tutor's partial vs. Teacher's full) interacts with model baseline reasoning capability to shape feedback quality. Full derivation access (Teacher) enables precise diagnosis but risks premature answer revelation. Its benefit is conditional: strong models (high Pre accuracy) leverage extra context to improve on harder instances without over-revealing, while weak models cannot effectively use the context and suffer more from answer leakage. Evidence: Abstract motivates adaptive, difficulty-aware routing; Section 6.2 shows Teacher × Complexity interaction significant mainly for strong models (Gemini, DeepSeek); human evaluation shows Teacher scores highest on Answer Revelation. Break condition: For weak models (Pre < ~45%), Teacher increases Answer Revelation without Correctness gain; for strong models, benefit plateaus at high complexity due to the universal ceiling.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Knowledge Graph-Grounded Evaluation
**Why needed:** Traditional binary correctness masks intermediate reasoning failures (e.g., correct statement via wrong rule). The KG encodes all valid inference paths with annotated edges (rule, parents), enabling classification of steps as Optimal (shortest path), Invalid (no KG edge), or Unjustified (correct symbol, wrong justification). This reveals pedagogical issues like partial correctness validation.
**Quick check:** If a student predicts `C` via Modus Ponens from `(A→C)` and `A`, but the KG edge requires Hypothetical Syllogism from `(A→B)` and `(B→C)`, the step is `Unjustified`—correct symbol, wrong justification—indicating a flawed reasoning process that feedback must address.

### Concept: Scaffolding Theory (Vygotsky, Wood et al.)
**Why needed:** Tutoring feedback must balance guidance and productive struggle. The paper operationalizes this via constraints: no answer revelation, Socratic questioning, acknowledging correct reasoning first. Violations (premature revelation, over-specification) directly cause measured performance degradation.
**Quick check:** If feedback states "The correct next step is `¬(I∧Q)` using Modus Tollens on `(I∧Q)→D` and `¬D`", it violates scaffolding by revealing the answer and rule explicitly, likely causing student anchoring without reasoning.

### Concept: Step Complexity Metric
**Why needed:** Provides a model-independent difficulty predictor. Computed as weighted sum of logical operators in the derived statement (e.g., `w(¬)=1, w(∧)=2, w(∨)=1.5, w(→)=2`) plus nesting depth penalty. Used to segment results (low/mid/high complexity) and identify the universal ceiling, guiding adaptive routing.
**Quick check:** Compare `c(P)` vs. `c(P∨Q)` vs. `c(P→(Q∧R))`. `c(P→(Q∧R))` is most complex due to implication (weight 2) and nested conjunction (weight 2 + penalty); such nested implications require deeper forward-chaining and are harder for LLMs to derive.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Student Agent -> (Tutor Agent / Teacher Agent / Judge Agent) -> Knowledge Graph (evaluation). Tutor Agent receives optimal step symbol only; Teacher Agent receives optimal step + full derivation; Judge Agent receives full derivation + Tutor feedback and may override. Knowledge Graph supplies valid transitions, computes Step Complexity, and classifies predictions.

### Critical path
1. For each of 516 proof states, Student generates initial step (2–3 candidates, selects one).
2. Student response + state sent to Tutor pipeline (or Teacher pipeline separately).
3. For Judge pipeline: Student response, Tutor feedback, and full derivation sent to Judge.
4. Judge outputs final scaffolded feedback (may enhance or override Tutor).
5. Student revises step only if feedback flags an error.
6. Revised step evaluated against KG for Post accuracy, Optimal/Invalid/Unjustified.
7. Metrics computed: Δ accuracy, Tutor Rule Accuracy, Unique Improvement Count, Step Complexity.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Tutor vs. Teacher:** Tutor (minimal access) reduces answer leakage risk but may produce vague feedback (Echo Effect). Teacher (full access) enables precise diagnosis but increases Premature Revelation.
- **Adding Judge:** Improves reliability for weak Tutors by correcting errors but introduces Verification Over-Specification for strong Tutors, reducing Actionability; increases latency and LLM calls.
- **Complexity handling:** No pipeline works beyond Step Complexity ~4.5; requires escalation (human tutor) or hint decomposition for harder states.

### Failure signatures
- **Echo Effect (Tutor):** Feedback validates student's incorrect rule instead of correcting. Detected by low Mistake Identification in human eval.
- **Partial Correctness Validation:** Student's step is correct symbolically but via wrong rule/parents; Tutor validates anyway. KG catches as Unjustified; automated accuracy misses it.
- **Premature Revelation (Teacher):** Feedback explicitly/implicitly states correct next step/rule. Human eval Answer Revelation high; student revision shows anchoring.
- **Verification Over-Specification (Judge):** Judge override makes feedback too abstract/constraining for already-correct Tutor feedback. Manifests as performance drop for strong models and lower Actionability.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Validate asymmetric effect on a subset (N=50).** Run all pipelines for 1–2 weak and 1–2 strong models. Expect: Judge > Teacher for weak Tutor Rule Acc. <70%; Judge < Teacher for strong Tutor Rule Acc. >85%.
2. **Verify complexity ceiling.** Plot Post accuracy binned by Step Complexity for all pipelines. Expect sharp drop after complexity 4.5 regardless of pipeline. Compute mean complexity of "feedback hurts" instances to correlate with ceiling.
3. **Measure Unique Improvement Count (UIC).** For each model, count states where Judge succeeds (Optimal revised step) and Teacher fails. Expect high UIC (80+) for weak models, low UIC (<25) for strong models. Correlate UIC with average complexity of those states—should be higher complexity for weak models.

## Limitations
- **Corpus evidence weak:** Neighbor papers provide context on logic tutoring and LLM feedback but do not directly support the paper’s novel asymmetric effect, universal ceiling, or capability-dependent claims.
- **Student simulation validity:** The Student agent’s revision behavior (“only if errors identified”) simplifies human learning; real students may persist with incorrect reasoning or respond differently to feedback styles.
- **Metric overestimation risk:** Automated accuracy fails to penalize answer revelation or unjustified correctness, potentially inflating perceived pipeline performance.

## Confidence
- **Asymmetric verification effect:** Medium (strong internal evidence from controlled comparisons, but lacks external corpus validation; relies on Tutor Rule Accuracy proxy).
- **Universal complexity ceiling:** High (robust, consistent failure across all models and pipelines beyond complexity ~4.5, tightly coupled with KG-derived metric).
- **Capability-dependent information access:** Medium-Low (interaction effects observed but nuanced; causality inferred from correlation; no ablation of *how* strong models leverage full derivation).

## Next Checks
1. **Test asymmetric effect on out-of-sample models.** Replicate Tutor/Teacher/Judge comparison with 2–3 new LLMs (e.g., Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 70B). Confirm the ~70%/~85% accuracy thresholds for Judge benefit vs. harm.
2. **Probe complexity ceiling with alternative metrics.** Re-analyze failure points using a different complexity measure (e.g., proof depth, branching factor, symbol count). Check if the >4.5 ceiling persists.
3. **Isolate Teacher pipeline failure for weak models.** Conduct human evaluation (n=30 proof states) comparing Teacher feedback for weak vs. strong models on Answer Revelation and Actionability; supplement with LLM-as-judge scoring of feedback specificity.
