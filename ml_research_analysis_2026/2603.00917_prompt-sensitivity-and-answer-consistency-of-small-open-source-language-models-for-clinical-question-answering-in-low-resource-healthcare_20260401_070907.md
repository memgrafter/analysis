# Prompt Sensitivity and Answer Consistency of Small Open-Source Language Models for Clinical Question Answering in Low-Resource Healthcare

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00917
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00917
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** Answer consistency and accuracy are independent; high consistency does not imply correctness, as shown by Gemma 2's "reliable incorrectness."

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates five small open-source language models (2B–7B parameters) on clinical question answering under varying prompt styles, simulating low-resource healthcare deployment. Models were tested on three medical QA datasets (MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA) using five prompt formulations. Key findings reveal a critical decoupling: Gemma 2 achieved the highest answer consistency (0.845–0.888) but the lowest accuracy (33.0–43.5%), producing perfectly consistent yet incorrect answers on 38.5% of MedQA questions—a dangerous "reliable incorrectness" failure mode. Llama 3.2 balanced accuracy (49.0–65.0%) and consistency best. Roleplay prompts systematically degraded accuracy across all models, and domain-pretrained Meditron-7B failed instruction-following completely (99% UNKNOWN on PubMedQA). The results demonstrate that safe clinical AI requires joint evaluation of consistency, accuracy, and instruction adherence, not accuracy alone.

## Method Summary
The study sampled 200 questions per dataset (MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA) using a fixed seed. Five semantically equivalent prompt styles (original, formal, simplified, roleplay, direct) were generated for each question, yielding 15,000 total prompts. Five off-the-shelf small models (Phi-3 Mini, Llama 3.2, Gemma 2, Mistral 7B, Meditron-7B) were queried via Ollama on a consumer CPU with temperature 0 and a 10-token limit. Responses were parsed with regular expressions to extract single-letter (A–D) or categorical (yes/no/maybe) answers; failures were marked UNKNOWN. Metrics computed per question included consistency (proportion of prompt styles agreeing on the majority answer) and overall accuracy (majority answer vs. ground truth). Statistical comparisons used Wilcoxon and McNemar tests.

## Key Results
- Consistency and accuracy are independent: Gemma 2 shows highest consistency (0.845–0.888) but lowest accuracy (33.0–43.5%), yielding "reliable incorrectness."
- Roleplay prompts reduce accuracy across all models; Phi-3 Mini declines 21.5 percentage points on MedQA.
- Meditron-7B, despite domain-specific pretraining, exhibits near-complete instruction-following failure on PubMedQA (99.0% UNKNOWN rate).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Knowledge distillation stabilizes teacher model's errors
- **Claim:** Knowledge distillation from a larger teacher can yield high consistency coupled with low accuracy in small clinical models.
- **Mechanism:** The student inherits the teacher's response patterns and confidence calibration rather than learning task-specific reasoning. If the teacher has systematic biases (e.g., favoring certain answer positions), the student replicates these errors consistently across prompt variations.
- **Core assumption:** The distillation process preserves the teacher’s error structure without correcting for task-specific ground truth.
- **Evidence anchors:** Gemma 2's high consistency and low accuracy; paper notes Gemma 2 uses KD from a larger teacher, which may instill stable but miscalibrated tendencies.

### Mechanism 2: Roleplay persona framing interferes with small-model reasoning
- **Claim:** Roleplay-style prompts introduce persona-based task interference that degrades accuracy in small instruction-tuned models.
- **Mechanism:** Persona framing forces the model to simulate a clinical identity while solving a structured QA task. For small models with limited capacity, this dual requirement diverts attention from core reasoning, activating conversational patterns instead of exam-style logic.
- **Core assumption:** Small models lack sufficient representational capacity to handle persona instructions without harming task performance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Systematic roleplay accuracy drop across all models; paper discussion suggests dual requirement dilutes attention from core reasoning.

### Mechanism 3: Domain pretraining is orthogonal to instruction following
- **Claim:** Domain pretraining alone is insufficient for structured clinical QA; instruction tuning is an orthogonal prerequisite for producing constrained outputs.
- **Mechanism:** Continued pretraining on medical corpora builds domain knowledge but does not train the model to follow structured output constraints (e.g., single-letter answers). Without instruction tuning, the model defaults to open-ended next-token prediction, failing to produce valid responses in structured formats.
- **Core assumption:** Instruction tuning and domain pretraining target disjoint capabilities—knowledge vs. output formatting compliance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Meditron-7B's 99% UNKNOWN rate on PubMedQA; paper explicitly states domain knowledge and task usability are orthogonal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Prompt sensitivity**
  - Why needed: The core phenomenon studied—output variation across semantically equivalent prompts—must be distinguished from accuracy. Understanding that phrasing can change answers is prerequisite to interpreting consistency scores.
  - **Quick check:** If a model gives different answers to "What causes chest pain?" and "What is the most likely cause of chest pain in a 45-year-old smoker?", what failure mode is demonstrated?
- **Concept: Consistency vs. accuracy independence**
  - Why needed: The paper’s central finding is that these metrics are uncorrelated. Misunderstanding this leads to assuming stable outputs imply correct ones.
  - **Quick check:** A model has consistency 1.0 (same answer every time) but accuracy 30%. What does this tell you about its clinical suitability?
- **Concept: Instruction tuning**
  - Why needed: Explains why Meditron fails and why other models succeed in producing structured responses. It is a distinct training phase from domain pretraining.
  - **Quick check:** Why would a model trained only on PubMed articles struggle to answer a multiple-choice question with "A, B, C, D"?

## Architecture Onboarding
Datasets -> Models -> Prompt Engine -> Inference -> Metrics

### Critical Path
1. Sample 200 questions per dataset using fixed seed.
2. Generate 5 prompt variations per question.
3. Query each model via Ollama API with deterministic settings.
4. Extract answer token or label UNKNOWN.
5. Compute per-question consistency and majority answer.
6. Aggregate metrics across models/datasets; run statistical tests.

### Design Tradeoffs
- CPU-only inference ensures low-resource realism but limits model size/throughput.
- No fine-tuning evaluates base model behavior but underestimates potential with medical adaptation.
- Fixed 10-token limit prevents verbose answers but may cause truncation artifacts.
- 5 prompt styles balance coverage vs. computational cost; more styles could reveal finer sensitivity patterns.

### Failure Signatures
- Reliable incorrectness: Consistency ~1.0 with low accuracy (Gemma 2 pattern).
- Roleplay degradation: Accuracy drop >15pp in Roleplay vs. Direct prompts.
- Instruction-following collapse: UNKNOWN rate >20% on structured tasks (Meditron, Phi-3 Mini).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What underlying architectural or training factors cause consistency and accuracy to be independent in small clinical LMs, such that a model (e.g., Gemma 2) can achieve high consistency but low accuracy?
2. Can roleplay-style prompting be made safe for clinical QA through specific model architectures or fine-tuning, or must it be categorically avoided?
3. Does combining domain-specific pretraining (like Meditron) with targeted instruction tuning on structured clinical QA tasks yield models that surpass general instruction-tuned models in both accuracy and consistency?
4. Do prompt sensitivity and reliability findings generalize to open-ended, multi-turn clinical dialogues and real-world physician queries, or are they confined to the constrained multiple-choice/yes-no formats of the studied benchmarks?
5. What specific characteristics of the instruction-tuning process (e.g., diversity of training prompts, use of RLHF vs. SFT, chat template design) most strongly predict low UNKNOWN rates and stable consistency in small LMs for clinical QA?

## Limitations
- Exact prompt texts and selected 200 question IDs per dataset are not published; reproducibility relies on external repository access.
- Evaluation uses 200 questions per dataset, which may not capture full benchmark diversity.
- CPU-only inference with Ollama may limit throughput and introduce nondeterminism; no GPU baseline.
- Only five prompt styles were tested; broader semantic variations are unexplored.
- Models were evaluated off-the-shelf, without medical fine-tuning; results may differ after domain adaptation.

## Confidence
- **Reliable incorrectness (Gemma2):** High confidence. Strong cross-dataset consistency-accuracy dissociation.
- **Roleplay accuracy drop:** Medium confidence. Effect is consistent but magnitude may vary with prompt nuance.
- **Domain pretraining vs instruction tuning:** High confidence. Meditron's 99% UNKNOWN on PubMedQA is stark.
- **Consistency-accuracy independence:** High confidence. Correlation near zero across all 15 model-dataset combinations.
