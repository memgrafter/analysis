# Online Causal Kalman Filtering for Stable and Effective Policy Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10609
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10609
- **Reference count:** 38
- **Primary result:** KPO-clipped achieves state-of-the-art average accuracy (avg@16) on five of six math‑reasoning benchmarks (AIME’24, AIME’25, AMC’23, MATH500, Olympiad) and best pass@16 on three, outperforming GRPO, GMPO, and GSPO. It stabilizes token‑level importance sampling via causal Kalman filtering, increasing token run‑length from ~1.6 to >119 and reducing switching frequency from 0.43 to 0.01.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning for large language models suffers from high‑variance token‑level importance sampling (IS) ratios, which destabilize policy optimization. Existing approaches either collapse to a single sequence‑level ratio or treat tokens independently, ignoring temporal coherence. This paper identifies that local off‑policy deviation is structurally inconsistent at the token level, causing noise spikes and frequent switching. The authors propose Online Causal Kalman Filtering for Policy Optimization (KPO), which models the desired token‑wise IS ratio as a latent state evolving smoothly across tokens. A Kalman filter is applied autoregressively (using only past tokens) to smooth the noisy observed log‑IS ratios while preserving local coherent structure and suppressing spikes. Evaluated on six math‑reasoning benchmarks, KPO‑clipped sets new records on most metrics, exhibits stable training (increasing rewards, maintained entropy, stable policy loss), and dramatically improves token coherence metrics. The method effectively transforms token dynamics into long, stable segments, enabling more effective policy updates.

## Method Summary
KPO integrates a causal Kalman filter into group‑relative policy optimization (GRPO). For each response, raw token‑level log‑IS ratios are computed as \( z_t = \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot) - \log \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid \cdot) \). The latent log‑IS ratio \(\rho_t\) is assumed to follow a random walk: \(\rho_t = \rho_{t-1} + \eta_t\) with process noise \(\eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q)\). The observation model is \(z_t = \rho_t + \varepsilon_t\) with \(\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, V)\). A scalar Kalman filter runs sequentially over tokens: predict \(\hat{\rho}_{t|t-1} = \hat{\rho}_{t-1|t-1}\), compute innovation \(\delta_t = z_t - \hat{\rho}_{t-1|t-1}\), update gain \(K_t\), and obtain posterior \(\hat{\rho}_{t|t}\). The smoothed ratio \(\tilde{r}_t = \exp(\hat{\rho}_{t|t})\) replaces the raw ratio in a PPO‑style clipped objective (KPO‑clipped) or an unclipped variant. Key hyperparameters are \(Q\) (process noise) and \(V\) (observation noise); optimal values are \(Q=10^{-6}\), \(V=1\). The algorithm adds only the sequential filtering step between advantage computation and loss calculation.

## Key Results
- **Benchmark performance:** KPO‑clipped achieves the best avg@16 on five of six math‑reasoning benchmarks (AIME’24, AIME’25, AMC’23, MATH500, Olympiad) and best pass@16 on three (AIME’24, AMC’23, Olympiad), beating GRPO, GMPO, and GSPO.
- **Training stability:** KPO attains higher final rewards, maintains policy entropy (avoids collapse), and exhibits a stable policy gradient loss, whereas GRPO diverges or collapses.
- **Token‑level impact:** Average run‑length of coherent token segments jumps from ~1.6 to >119; switching frequency plummets from 0.43 to 0.01; local variance of log‑IS ratios drops to ~\(10^{-5}\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Latent State‑Space Smoothing
KPO stabilizes token‑level IS ratios by treating the desired log‑IS ratio as a latent state that evolves smoothly across token positions. The observed noisy log‑ratio \(z_t\) is modeled as a noisy measurement of an underlying \(\rho_t\), which follows a random walk \(\rho_t = \rho_{t-1} + \eta_t\) with process noise \(Q\). The Kalman filter recursively predicts \(\hat{\rho}_{t|t-1}\) and updates it with the innovation \(\delta_t = z_t - \hat{\rho}_{t-1|t-1}\), weighted by the Kalman gain \(K_t\), yielding a smoothed estimate \(\hat{\rho}_{t|t}\). The core assumption is that the true off‑policy deviation signal is a low‑frequency process, while fluctuations in \(z_t\) are high‑frequency noise. Evidence includes the formal state‑space equations and the three‑step filtering procedure. A break condition occurs if the latent state changes abruptly (e.g., at semantic boundaries) faster than \(Q\) permits; the filter then lags, introducing bias.

### Mechanism 2: Causal Autoregressive Filtering
KPO’s design is fully causal: the prediction step uses only the previous posterior, and the update uses only the current observation. This ensures compatibility with autoregressive generation and prevents lookahead bias. The assumption is that token‑wise off‑policy deviation depends only on past tokens. This is evidenced by the authors’ explicit statement that the filter updates “based on the states of past tokens, regardless of future tokens.” A limitation arises if future semantic content heavily influences deviation (e.g., a late‑correcting reasoning step); the causal filter cannot incorporate that future signal, potentially smoothing over necessary adjustments.

### Mechanism 3: Structure‑Aware Integration into Policy Objective
By feeding Kalman‑filtered ratios into a PPO‑style clipped objective, KPO preserves local heterogeneity in off‑policy degrees while suppressing destructive noise spikes. The filtered ratio \(\tilde{r}_t = \exp(\hat{\rho}_{t|t})\) replaces the raw ratio in the GRPO surrogate. Because the filtered log‑ratios are locally coherent (long run‑lengths, low switching), adjacent tokens receive similar weights, preventing the gradient from being dominated by isolated high‑variance tokens. Clipping (in KPO‑clipped) further caps extreme coherent deviations. The assumption is that erratic fluctuations represent noise that distorts gradients, while slower coherent variations reflect true off‑policy differences. Empirical evidence shows token run‑lengths increased >100×, switching frequency dropped by two orders of magnitude, and local variance reduced to \(10^{-5}\). Training dynamics also show stable reward increase and maintained entropy. A break condition is that if observed variance is actually meaningful signal (e.g., due to MoE routing disparities), aggressive smoothing with very small \(Q/V\) could over‑suppress variation and bias gradient estimates.

## Foundational Learning
- **Token‑level Importance Sampling (IS) ratio**  
  *Why needed:* Corrects distribution shift in off‑policy policy gradient updates by reweighting log‑probability gradients from the behavior policy to the updated policy.  
  *Quick check:* If a token has an IS ratio > 1 in an off‑policy update, what does this imply about the updated policy’s probability relative to the behavior policy for that token?

- **Kalman Filter as Optimal Linear Estimator**  
  *Why needed:* Provides an online, causal, minimum‑variance estimator for a latent state given noisy observations, balancing trust in the dynamic model (process noise \(Q\)) vs. measurements (noise \(V\)).  
  *Quick check:* In the scalar Kalman filter, what happens to the Kalman gain \(K_t\) if the observation noise \(V\) is very large relative to the predicted variance \(P_{t|t-1}\)?

- **Local vs. Global Smoothing in Sequence Modeling**  
  *Why needed:* The paper contrasts KPO’s local coherence preservation with sequence‑level methods’ global smoothing; understanding this trade‑off is key to why KPO outperforms GSPO/GMPO on complex reasoning.  
  *Quick check:* What is a potential downside of using a fixed sequence‑level IS ratio for all tokens in a long chain‑of‑thought?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text):**  
Sampling & Rollout → Reward Computation → Log‑Ratio Extraction → Kalman Filter Engine → Objective Computation → Policy Update

**Critical path:** Sampling → Advantage calculation → Log‑ratio extraction → Causal Kalman filtering over tokens → Loss aggregation → Policy update.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Q/V tuning:* Smaller values yield strong smoothing, higher reward and stability but risk lag on true shifts; larger values track fluctuations better but re‑introduce noise and instability.
- *Clipping integration:* KPO‑clipped adds PPO‑style clipping thresholds (\(\epsilon^-\), \(\epsilon^+\)), providing an extra stabilization layer but requiring additional hyperparameter tuning.
- *Autoregressive overhead:* The Kalman filter processes tokens sequentially per response, adding compute proportional to sequence length; it cannot be fully parallelized like attention.

**Failure signatures:**
- Reward plateau or decay mid‑training (e.g., for \(Q/V = 10^{-2}\)) indicates insufficient smoothing (\(Q\) too large) or filter divergence.
- Near‑zero entropy collapse (like GRPO) suggests ratio noise still dominates, possibly \(Q\) too large or \(V\) too small.
- Extremely low clip fraction alongside poor reward may indicate over‑smoothing (\(Q\) too small) causing all ratios to cluster near 1, removing learning signal.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. *Synthetic sanity check:* Run KPO on a tiny dataset with a known piecewise‑constant true \(\rho_t\). Verify the filtered \(\hat{\rho}_{t|t}\) tracks latent segments with minimal delay and variance reduction versus raw \(z_t\). Vary \(Q/V\) to observe the lag‑vs‑noise trade‑off.
2. *Token dynamics validation:* On a small held‑out set, compute pre/post‑Kalman metrics (run‑length, switch frequency, local variance). Confirm KPO increases run‑lengths by >10× and reduces switch frequency to <0.05.
3. *Integration test:* Compare KPO‑clipped against GRPO on a MATH500 subset for 50 training steps. Monitor reward mean, entropy, and policy loss; expect KPO to show smoother reward increase and non‑collapsing entropy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Parallelization:** How can the Kalman filtering procedure be parallelized to enable efficient large‑scale training without sacrificing its autoregressive smoothing properties? *Basis:* Authors state in conclusion that designing parallelized Kalman filtering is an interesting future direction. *Why unresolved:* Autoregressive nature limits throughput and scaling to longer sequences. *What evidence would resolve:* Propose a parallelizable variant (e.g., block‑wise or approximate filtering) and demonstrate comparable performance with measurable speedup.
- **Generalization to other tasks:** Does KPO generalize beyond math‑reasoning benchmarks (e.g., to code generation, instruction following, or safety alignment)? *Basis:* Evaluation was restricted to six math benchmarks; other common RL for LLMs tasks were not tested. *Why unresolved:* Success on math tasks may not transfer to domains with different reward structures, response lengths, or semantic patterns. *What evidence would resolve:* Extensive experiments on diverse tasks (HumanEval, MT‑Bench, safety scenarios) comparing KPO to baselines and reporting stability metrics.
- **Adaptive noise parameters:** Can the process/observation noise parameters \(Q\) and \(V\) be automatically adapted during training based on evolving IS ratio statistics? *Basis:* Parameter analysis shows different \(Q/V\) values significantly affect reward and stability; the best setting was manually chosen. *Why unresolved:* Manual tuning may be brittle across models, tasks, or training stages. *What evidence would resolve:* Develop an online method (e.g., maximum likelihood, EM, or heuristic) to estimate \(Q\) and \(V\); compare performance and robustness against best manual settings.
- **Context‑enhanced filtering:** Would incorporating richer contextual information (e.g., token embeddings, positional encodings) into the state‑space model improve preservation of semantically meaningful local structure while smoothing noise? *Basis:* Current KPO uses a scalar random‑walk on the log‑IS ratio, ignoring semantic context. *Why unresolved:* Semantic shifts might correspond to changes in off‑policy degree that are not purely numeric; modeling context could better distinguish true changes from noise. *What evidence would resolve:* Extend KPO to a multivariate or nonlinear state‑space model where observations/states depend on token embeddings; evaluate improvements in token coherence and final task performance.
- **Theoretical guarantees:** What theoretical properties (e.g., bounds on variance reduction, convergence guarantees) can be established for KPO relative to raw token‑level or sequence‑level IS ratios? *Basis:* The paper provides empirical evidence but lacks formal analysis. *Why unresolved:* Without theory it is unclear whether KPO is optimal or how it interacts with policy optimization; this may limit further rigorous development. *What evidence would resolve:* Derive analytical results (e.g., Kalman filter minimizes mean‑squared error under assumptions) and connect to policy gradient variance reduction; possibly prove improved sample complexity.

## Limitations
- **Reward computation details** (answer extraction, normalization) are underspecified, hindering exact reproducibility.
- **Training schedule** (number of steps/epochs) is not stated; optimal checkpoint selection remains ambiguous.
- **Domain coverage** is limited to math‑reasoning tasks; transfer to code generation, dialogue, or safety alignment is unproven.
- **Autoregressive overhead** from the sequential Kalman filter may impede scaling to very long responses or large models.

## Confidence
- Latent state‑space smoothing effectively reduces token‑level variance: **High**
- Causal autoregressive filtering is theoretically sound and compatible with autoregressive generation: **Medium**
- KPO yields stable training dynamics and higher rewards vs. GRPO/GMPO/GSPO on math tasks: **Medium**
- Over‑smoothing risk with very small \(Q/V\) could suppress meaningful variation: **Low**
