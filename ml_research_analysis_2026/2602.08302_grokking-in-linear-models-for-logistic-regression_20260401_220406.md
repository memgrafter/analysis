# Grokking in Linear Models for Logistic Regression

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08302  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08302  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Grokking (delayed generalization) in linear logistic regression arises from a learnable bias’s slow dynamics under gradient descent, occurs only under test distribution shifts (concentrated near the margin or adversarial), and scales exponentially with support vector imbalance.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates grokking—the phenomenon where test accuracy improves long after training accuracy reaches near-perfect levels—in the simplest setting: linear logistic regression on linearly separable data. The key finding is that a learnable bias, even when the true separator has zero bias, induces a three-phase learning process due to the implicit bias of gradient descent: (1) rapid alignment of the weight direction to the maximum-margin separator, (2) a period where the bias drifts away from zero due to asymmetric support vectors (causing temporary test accuracy drops), and (3) slow recovery of the bias as the hyperplane shifts back, leading to delayed generalization. Crucially, grokking appears only when test data are either concentrated near the decision margin or adversarially perturbed; standard i.i.d. test数据 from the same distribution do not exhibit it. Theoretically, the grokking time is characterized as exponential in the imbalance measure δ among support vectors. This demonstrates that grokking does not require neural network depth or representation learning—it can emerge in linear models through bias dynamics alone.

## Method Summary
The authors train a linear classifier \( f(x) = w^T x + b \) using logistic loss and (full-batch) gradient descent on synthetic linearly separable datasets. Training data are generated with a fixed small margin γ, while test sets come in three types: (i) standard i.i.d. from the same distribution, (ii) concentrated near the margin (controlled by a concentration parameter α > 1), and (iii) adversarially perturbed via PGD. They record training/test accuracies, weight norm \( \|w\| \), and bias \( b \) over many steps (up to \( 10^7 \)). Theoretically, they analyze the late-time dynamics where the weight direction has converged to the maximum-margin separator and only support vectors affect the bias gradient. This yields a differential equation for \( b(t) \) whose solution shows non-monotonic behavior when support vectors are imbalanced (δ ≠ 1). They validate the theory by planting support vectors with varying imbalance and measuring grokking delays.

## Key Results
- Grokking is exhibited in linear logistic regression and is driven solely by bias dynamics under the implicit bias of gradient descent; no neural networks or representation learning are needed.
- The phenomenon is conditional: it occurs only when test accuracy is sensitive to the offset \( |b|/\|w\| \), i.e., for test data concentrated near the margin or adversarially perturbed, but not for i.i.d. test data matching the training distribution.
- The grokking time \( T_{\text{te}}(\epsilon) \) scales as \( \exp(|\log \delta| / (1 + 2\epsilon(2\alpha-1))) \), where δ captures support vector asymmetry and α controls test concentration.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Implicit bias aligns weight direction early
- **Claim:** Gradient descent on linearly separable logistic regression quickly aligns the weight vector with the maximum-margin separator, completing Phase 1 before bias dynamics dominate.
- **Mechanism:** The implicit bias (Soudry et al.) causes the weight direction to converge to the max‑margin solution while its norm grows logarithmically. After a transition time \( t_0 \), the weight direction is fixed; subsequent training changes only the bias, moving the hyperplane parallel to itself.
- **Core assumption:** Data are linearly separable with positive margin γ; constant small learning rate; logistic loss (exponential tail); finite support; support vectors span the dataset.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Theorem 4.5 (rephrased from Soudry et al., 2024): \( w(t) = \hat{w} \log t + \rho(t) \), with \( \hat{w} \) the max‑margin direction.  
  - Section 4.1: weight direction convergence.  
  - Neighbors like "Grokking at the Edge of Linear Separability" study linear settings but do not derive bias‑driven phases.
- **Break condition:** If data are not strictly separable (e.g., label noise, γ=0) or gradient descent is constrained (e.g., weight normalization), the implicit bias may not yield a max‑margin direction, preventing Phase 1 as described.

### Mechanism 2: Asymmetric support vectors drive bias drift
- **Claim:** The bias term evolves according to a slow dynamics governed solely by the support vectors; asymmetry in their numbers (or distribution) between classes pushes \( b \) away from zero and then slowly back, creating the unlearning and generalization phases.
- **Mechanism:** After weight direction stabilizes (t ≥ t₀), only support vectors contribute to the bias gradient:  
  \( \frac{db}{dt} = \frac{1}{t} (A_S^+ e^{-b} - A_S^- e^{b}) \),  
  where \( A_S^\pm = \sum_{i \in S_\pm} e^{\mp (\tilde{w}^\top x_i)} \). The imbalance \( \delta = \sqrt{A_S^- / A_S^+} \) sets equilibrium \( b_\infty = -\log \delta \). The solution \( b(t) \) (Equation 11) is non‑monotonic when δ ≠ 1: \( b \) drifts then recovers on a timescale \( O(\sqrt{A_S^+ A_S^-} \log t) \).
- **Core assumption:** Support vectors are a finite set; their exponential inner products with the max‑margin direction are finite; the late‑time approximation (only support vectors matter) holds; the bias is learnable.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “the bias undergoes a slower, support‑vector‑driven evolution that governs delayed generalization.”  
  - Section 4.2: Theorem 4.5 derives the bias differential equation.  
  - Section 4.4: “only support vectors contribute … the imbalance δ … determines equilibrium \( b_\infty = -\log \delta \).”  
  - Neighbor work finds that support vector imbalance induces grokking, consistent with this mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If support vectors are perfectly balanced (δ = 1), then \( db/dt \propto e^{-b} - e^{b} \) and \( b \) converges quickly to 0 without drift. Also, if the bias is fixed (not learnable), Phases 2 and 3 disappear.

### Mechanism 3: Test distribution sensitivity converts bias drift into delayed generalization
- **Claim:** Grokking (delayed generalization) appears only when test accuracy is sensitive to the offset \( |b|/\|w\| \), i.e., when test points are concentrated near the margin or adversarially shifted; standard i.i.d. test data do not exhibit grokking because they are robust to small hyperplane shifts.
- **Mechanism:** Phase 1 yields near‑perfect training accuracy quickly due to growing \( \|w\| \approx \log t \). In Phases 2–3, the hyperplane moves parallel to itself as \( b(t) \) drifts and recovers, while \( \|w\| \) continues to grow. For test distributions with many points close to the margin (small γ_test) or adversarial points pushed to the margin, classification error is roughly proportional to \( |b|/\|w\| \) (and margin). Since \( |b|/\|w\| \propto 1/\log t \) in the convergence regime, test accuracy improves only slowly, creating a training‑test gap—grokking. For a standard test distribution with margin matching training, test accuracy remains high even when \( b \) is non‑zero, so no delay occurs.
- **Core assumption:** Test distribution differs from training: it is concentrated near the decision boundary (α > 1) or adversarially shifted; the problem satisfies Assumption 3.1 (separable with margin).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “Grokking … occurs only when test data are either concentrated near the margin or adversarially perturbed … not when test data follow the same distribution.”  
  - Section 5.2: compares standard vs concentrated test sets; grokking only in the latter.  
  - Section 4.6: grokking time formula \( T_{\text{te}}(\epsilon) = \exp(|\log \delta|/(1+2\epsilon(2\alpha-1))) \) shows dependence on δ and α.  
  - Related work links grokking to adversarial robustness.
- **Break condition:** If the test distribution is identical to the training distribution (i.i.d., same margin), test accuracy does not strongly depend on \( |b|/\|w\| \) and grokking vanishes. Alternatively, if α = 1 (test margin equals training margin), the sensitivity makes the delay negligible.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Implicit bias of gradient descent on separable data
- **Why needed here:** The whole three‑phase analysis depends on the fact that gradient descent on logistic loss with linearly separable data implicitly regularizes toward the maximum‑margin direction. Without this, the weight evolution would not decouple from bias as assumed.
- **Quick check:** “What does the implicit bias imply about the limit direction of w(t) as t → ∞ for a separable dataset?”

### Concept: Support vectors in linear classification
- **Why needed here:** In late training, only support vectors affect the bias gradient; their number and distribution (through \( A_S^\pm \)) determine whether asymmetry exists and thus whether grokking occurs.
- **Quick check:** “Given a max‑margin separator, which training points are support vectors? How would you compute \( A_S^+ \) from a dataset?”

### Concept: Grokking metric definition
- **Why needed here:** To rigorously claim delayed generalization, one must define grokking time \( T_{\text{gr}}(\epsilon) = T_{\text{te}}(\epsilon) - T_{\text{tr}}(\epsilon) \). Without such a metric, the phenomenon could be subjective.
- **Quick check:** “In Definition 3.4, how are \( T_{\text{tr}}(\epsilon) \) and \( T_{\text{te}}(\epsilon) \) defined? What does a large \( T_{\text{gr}}(\epsilon) \) indicate?”

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Data generator (synthetic linearly separable points with controllable margin, class balance, and support vector placement) → Linear model (\( f(x)=w^T x + b \)) → Logistic loss (\( \sum \log(1+\exp(-y \cdot f(x))) \)) → Gradient descent optimizer (fixed learning rate η) → Logger/Tracker (periodically records train/test accuracy, w, b, norms) → Analysis tools (compute max‑margin separator, identify support vectors, measure δ, plot phases).

### Critical path
1. Generate training dataset D and test sets T_std (i.i.d.), T_conc (concentrated, α>1), and/or T_adv (PGD adversarial).
2. Initialize w = 0, b = 0.
3. For t = 0 to T_max:  
   a. Compute loss and gradients.  
   b. Update w ← w - η ∇_w L, b ← b - η ∇_b L.  
   c. Every K steps (e.g., 100): evaluate train accuracy, test accuracies on each set; optionally store w, b, ||w||, |b|/||w||.
4. Plot accuracy vs epoch, b(t), |b|/||w||; identify Phase 1 (quick alignment), Phase 2 (bias drift), Phase 3 (bias recovery).
5. Compute grokking time T_gr = T_te(ϵ) - T_tr(ϵ) from accuracy curves.

### Design tradeoffs
- Learning rate: Must be small constant (paper uses η = 10⁻²) to preserve implicit bias; larger η may accelerate but distort max‑margin convergence.
- Dimensionality: Low d (e.g., 2) makes support vector effects visually clear; high d (e.g., 1024) tests scaling but reduces signal (A_S^± become tiny). Choose based on goal.
- Support vector planting: To control δ, fix a subset of points as support vectors (e.g., closest to separator). Trade‑off: more controlled but less natural; requires prior knowledge of max‑margin direction.
- Logging frequency: Too frequent slows training; too coarse may miss transitions. K = 100 is a reasonable default.

### Failure signatures
- Weight direction does not converge to w_true: Check cos(w(t), w_true) < 0.9 or decreases. Could indicate non‑separable data or wrong learning rate.
- Bias evolves monotonically (no drift up then down): either support vectors are balanced (δ≈1) or bias update is incorrect (e.g., gradient clipping).
- No grokking on concentrated test: either α too small (test points not sufficiently near margin) or training not long enough (need >10⁷ steps for d=2). Verify test set margin distribution.
- Numerical instability: ||w|| grows as log t; after many steps it may exceed float32 range. Use float64 and monitor overflow.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline (i.i.d. test):** Train on dataset with margin γ = 10⁻³, evaluate on i.i.d. test set. Expect training and test accuracies to rise in lockstep, T_te ≈ T_tr. Confirms grokking is not universal.
2. **Concentrated test:** Train on same dataset, evaluate on concentrated test set (α=10). Expect training accuracy to reach ~1 within ~10³ epochs, while test accuracy lags dramatically, catching up only after ~10⁶ epochs. Plot b(t) and |b|/||w|| to see non‑monotonic distance.
3. **Imbalanced support vectors:** Vary the number of support vectors per class while keeping total points fixed (e.g., S⁺:350, S⁻:350 vs S⁺:500, S⁻:200). Measure grokking time T_gr from accuracy curves and compare against predicted exp(|log δ|). Expect higher imbalance (δ far from 1) to increase T_gr, validating Theorem 4.6.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the grokking time scale with the support vector imbalance in datasets with many support vectors, and can the theoretical bound involving δ be generalized beyond the current asymptotic analysis?
- **Basis in paper:** Theorem 4.6 characterizes grokking time as exponential in |log δ|, where δ depends on sums over support vectors. However, experiments in high dimensions show reduced phase distinctions due to smaller A_S^+ and A_S^-, suggesting the bound may not capture finite‑sample or high‑dimensional effects.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis assumes idealized late‑time dynamics where only support vectors matter, but in practice, with numerous or high‑dimensional support vectors, their contributions may average out, altering grokking time unpredictably.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the theory to incorporate concentration inequalities for support vector sums, or experiments systematically varying the number and distribution of support vectors while measuring grokking delays.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What precise distributional properties of the test data induce grokking, and can we derive a general condition that predicts grokking emergence for any test distribution shift?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper shows grokking occurs for test data concentrated near the margin or adversarially perturbed, but not for standard test data matching training distribution (Abstract, Section 5). However, no unified criterion is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** The explanation ties grokking to test sensitivity via parameter α, but this is specific to the planted concentrated distribution. A general theory linking test distribution geometry (e.g., density near decision boundary) to grokking is lacking.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Defining a metric like “margin sensitivity” based on test data distribution and proving that values above a threshold guarantee grokking under the model, validated across diverse synthetic and real‑world shifts.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the three‑phase learning dynamics and associated grokking persist under stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with mini‑batches, and how does gradient noise affect the bias evolution and grokking time?
- **Basis in paper:** The analysis uses full‑batch gradient descent, assuming deterministic dynamics. Real‑world training often uses SGD, which introduces noise that might alter implicit bias and phase transitions.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical framework relies on exact gradient flow; noise could accelerate convergence or change the support vector dominance, potentially suppressing or enhancing grokking, as hinted by connections to lazy vs. rich dynamics in related work.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis of SGD noise effects on the bias differential equation, or controlled experiments comparing GD and SGD with varying batch sizes and noise levels on the same linear setup.

## Limitations
- **Support vector set ambiguity:** The theoretical δ depends on the precise set of support vectors. In practice, with finite data and numerical margins, identifying this set is nontrivial, leading to uncertainty in measuring δ and comparing to theory.
- **Onset of late‑time dynamics (t₀):** The three‑phase model assumes a sharp transition to a regime where only support vectors influence the bias. Empirically, this transition is gradual, making it difficult to pinpoint t₀ and possibly blurring phase boundaries.
- **Assumption sensitivity:** The analysis relies on perfect separability, logistic loss, constant learning rate, and continuous‑time GD. Real‑world settings often include label noise, alternative losses, or adaptive optimizers, which may disrupt the mechanism.

## Confidence
- **Three‑phase bias‑driven learning:** High  
- **Test distribution sensitivity as grokking trigger:** Medium–High  
- **Exponential scaling of grokking time with |log δ|:** High (qualitative), Medium (quantitative)
