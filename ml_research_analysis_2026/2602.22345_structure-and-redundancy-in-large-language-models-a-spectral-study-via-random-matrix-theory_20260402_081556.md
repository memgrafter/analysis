# Structure and Redundancy in Large Language Models: A Spectral Study via Random Matrix Theory

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22345
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22345
- Reference count: 11
- Primary result: EigenTrack detects hallucinations with AUROC 0.842–0.894 on LLaMa models; RMT‑KD compresses models up to 80% (BERT‑base) while maintaining or improving accuracy, yielding 3× throughput gains.

## Executive Summary
This thesis tackles unreliability (hallucinations, out‑of‑distribution failures) and inefficiency in large language models using spectral analysis grounded in Random Matrix Theory (RMT). Two contributions are presented: (1) **EigenTrack**, a lightweight recurrent classifier that monitors the temporal dynamics of activation spectra (e.g., entropy, divergence from the Marchenko–Pastur bulk) to detect hallucinations early with high AUROC (0.842–0.894) on LLaMa models, outperforming baselines. (2) **RMT‑KD**, a compression framework that projects activations onto subspaces defined by outlier eigenvalues beyond the noise bulk, followed by self‑distillation; it reduces parameters by up to 80% (BERT‑base) while preserving or slightly improving accuracy, and delivers 3× throughput gains with lower power and memory. Both methods are interpretable, hardware‑friendly, and require no retraining of the base model (only fine‑tuning for RMT‑KD).

## Method Summary
**EigenTrack** operates by sliding a temporal window over hidden activations from selected transformer layers. For each window, it computes the covariance matrix, extracts its eigenvalues, and derives spectral descriptors (entropy, top‑eigenvalue mass, eigengaps, KL divergence to the Marchenko–Pastur law). These descriptors form a time series fed to a lightweight recurrent classifier (RNN/GRU/LSTM) that outputs a per‑token hallucination/OOD risk score.

**RMT‑KD** compresses a trained model layer‑by‑layer. For a given layer, it first collects calibration activations to estimate the Marchenko–Pastur bulk edge (λ⁺). Eigenvectors corresponding to eigenvalues above λ⁺ define a “causal subspace.” The layer’s weight matrix is projected onto this lower‑dimensional subspace, reducing the number of parameters. Self‑distillation is then applied: the projected model (student) is fine‑tuned to match the logits of the original (teacher) while continuing task training, stabilizing performance across layers.

## Key Results
- EigenTrack achieves AUROC 0.842–0.894 for hallucination detection on LLaMa models, outperforming existing detectors.
- RMT‑KD reduces parameters by up to 80% (BERT‑base) while maintaining or slightly improving accuracy on downstream tasks.
- Compression yields 3× throughput gains with lower power and memory consumption.
- Both methods are interpretable (spectral features) and hardware‑friendly (dense projections, no retraining).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Spectral separation via the Marchenko–Pastur (MP) law distinguishes structured, task‑relevant signal from isotropic noise in activation covariances.
- **Mechanism:** For a high‑dimensional activation matrix, the eigenvalue spectrum of its covariance matrix is expected to follow the MP law under isotropic noise assumptions. Outlier eigenvalues that detach from the MP bulk (per the Baik–Ben Arous–Péché transition) indicate low‑rank signal subspaces carrying causal, task‑specific information. The bulk represents redundant or noise‑dominated variability.
- **Core assumption:** Hidden activations of trained models contain a mix of structured signal (aligned with the task) and unstructured noise, and their covariance eigenvalue distribution reflects this mixture in a way that RMT can characterize.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “…eigenvalue dynamics of hidden activations…separate structured, causal representations from noise‑dominated variability.”
  - [section 2] “…the MP bulk is a calibrated reference for noise‑like activations…outliers indicate structured, task‑relevant directions.”
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., “Spectral Geometry for Deep Learning…”) use similar RMT frameworks, but direct empirical validation for this specific separation in LLMs is primarily from the thesis’s own analysis (Figure 6, eigenvector projections).
- **Break condition:** If activations are inherently low‑rank due to architectural constraints (e.g., extreme bottlenecking) rather than learned sparsity, the MP bulk may not form, making outlier detection unreliable. Also, severe covariate shift could distort the entire spectrum, breaking the signal/noise dichotomy.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Hallucinations and OOD failures induce a characteristic “spectral drift” toward noise‑like activation statistics over time, detectable via temporal modeling of spectral descriptors.
- **Mechanism:** During generation, factual/in‑distribution reasoning maintains a degree of spectral concentration (large eigengaps, low entropy) as representations remain structured. Hallucination/OOD states cause representations to destabilize, increasing entropy, reducing eigengaps, and lowering divergence from the MP baseline—indicating a collapse toward isotropic noise. A lightweight recurrent classifier learns these temporal trajectory patterns to issue early warnings.
- **Core assumption:** The evolution of internal representation geometry, not just final output logits, contains precursors to failure states. This drift is systematic enough to be separable from stable generation paths.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “…EigenTrack monitors temporal dynamics of activation spectra…achieving AUROC 0.842–0.894 for hallucination detection.”
  - [section 3] “Figure 2: hallucinated sequences tend to stay closer to the MP‑like regime, while factual sequences diverge toward a more structured spectra.”
  - [section 3, ablation] “Performance rises sharply from chance within the first few tokens…cues emerge early.”
- **Break condition:** If a model hallucinates in a highly structured way (e.g., repeating a memorized but false pattern) or if OOD data is itself highly structured (e.g., a new domain with strong internal correlations), the spectral drift signal may be attenuated or reversed, reducing detector sensitivity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Projecting activations onto the subspace spanned by outlier eigenvectors (beyond the MP bulk) preserves task‑relevant information while discarding redundant/noisy directions, enabling aggressive compression without retraining the base model.
- **Mechanism:** RMT‑KD iteratively identifies the upper edge of the MP bulk for a layer’s activation covariance. Eigenvectors corresponding to eigenvalues above this edge define a “causal subspace.” The layer’s weight matrix is projected to this lower‑dimensional subspace. Self‑distillation (using the pre‑projection model as teacher) then fine‑tunes the reduced student model to recover any lost accuracy, stabilizing the process across layers.
- **Core assumption:** Outlier eigenvectors consistently capture directions essential for the task across the dataset, and the remaining bulk directions are either redundant or actively detrimental (noise). The projection is dense, so computational gains come purely from reduced dimensionality.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “RMT‑KD compresses models by projecting activations onto subspaces defined by outlier eigenvalues beyond the noise bulk…reduces parameters by up to 80%…while maintaining or slightly improving accuracy.”
  - [section 4] “By interpreting outlier eigenvalues…as carriers of task‑relevant information, RMT‑KD progressively projects networks onto lower‑dimensional subspaces.”
  - [section 4, results] Figure 7a shows accuracy maintained or improved despite large parameter reductions; Figure 7b shows corresponding throughput gains.
- **Break condition:** If the task relies on highly distributed representations without clear spectral outliers (i.e., signal is spread across many small eigenvalues within the bulk), this projection would discard critical information. Also, if the MP bulk edge is misestimated (e.g., due to non‑i.i.d. activations or small calibration set), either too much signal or too much noise could be retained.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Random Matrix Theory (RMT) fundamentals
- **Why needed here:** Provides the statistical null model (Marchenko–Pastur law) for what a “noise‑only” eigenvalue spectrum looks like in high dimensions. This is the baseline against which “signal” (outliers) is defined. Without this, spectral analysis lacks a principled threshold.
- **Quick check question:** Given a covariance matrix from high‑dimensional i.i.d. Gaussian data with aspect ratio p/n → c, what is the expected support of its eigenvalues according to the MP law?

### Concept: Spiked covariance model & BBP transition
- **Why needed here:** Formalizes how low‑rank signal (“spikes”) manifests as outlier eigenvalues that detach from the MP bulk when strong enough. This directly justifies identifying “causal subspaces” for compression (RMT‑KD) and recognizing structured vs. noise‑dominated states (EigenTrack).
- **Quick check question:** According to the BBP transition, what condition on spike strength θ causes a sample eigenvalue to become an outlier (detach from the bulk) for a given noise variance σ² and aspect ratio c?

### Concept: Spectral entropy and eigengap as descriptors
- **Why needed here:** These are the concrete, computable features extracted from the covariance spectrum to feed into EigenTrack’s recurrent detector. Entropy measures dispersion (noise‑like vs. concentrated), eigengap measures the strength of dominant directions. They translate abstract RMT concepts into actionable signals.
- **Quick check question:** If a representation’s activation covariance has one very large eigenvalue and all others near zero, what would you expect for its spectral entropy and leading eigengap relative to a spectrum following the MP law?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
EigenTrack pipeline: Spectral Analyzer -> Temporal Classifier  
RMT‑KD pipeline: RMT‑KD Projection Module -> Self‑Distillation Stabilizer

### Critical path
**For EigenTrack**:  
(1) Select monitor layers (e.g., every 4th transformer block).  
(2) At each generation step, stack last *N* activation vectors from monitored layers → window matrix.  
(3) Compute covariance + eigenvalues → descriptor vector.  
(4) Feed descriptor history to recurrent head → get risk score.  
(5) Threshold score for early stopping/intervention.  

**For RMT‑KD**:  
(1) Train base model to target accuracy.  
(2) For each layer (from input to output):  
 (a) Collect calibration activations → covariance → eigenvalues.  
 (b) Fit MP law (estimate σ², λ⁺).  
 (c) Identify outlier eigenvectors → construct projection matrix.  
 (d) Apply projection to layer’s weight matrix.  
 (e) Self‑distill (teacher = pre‑projection model) for *K* steps.  
 (f) Repeat for next layer.

### Design tradeoffs
- **EigenTrack:** Sliding window length *N*: shorter windows give finer‑grained, higher‑latency detection; longer windows smooth signals, reduce latency but may miss early cues. Monitored layer density: more layers increase accuracy but linearly increase overhead.
- **RMT‑KD:** MP bulk edge quantile: higher quantile (more aggressive projection) yields greater compression but higher accuracy risk. Self‑distillation epochs *K*: more steps stabilize accuracy but add computational cost during compression. Layer‑wise reduction order: reducing early layers first may limit later layer capacity more.

### Failure signatures
- **EigenTrack:**  
  (1) Descriptor time series show no consistent separation between hallucination/factual trajectories (AUROC ≈ 0.5).  
  (2) MP divergence consistently near zero for all sequences, indicating failure to capture any structure.  
  (3) Recurrent head fails to converge during training (loss plateaus).
- **RMT‑KD:**  
  (1) Post‑projection accuracy drops catastrophically (>5%) even after distillation, indicating over‑aggressive projection (bulk edge set too high) or that signal resides in bulk.  
  (2) No throughput gain despite parameter reduction, often due to memory bandwidth bottlenecks from non‑contiguous memory access (if projection not implemented as single dense matmul).  
  (3) Distillation loss diverges, suggesting teacher/student gap too large after projection.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate MP bulk estimation:** On a pretrained BERT‑base, extract activations from a middle layer on a calibration set, compute covariance eigenvalues, and visually verify the MP bulk fit (histogram vs. MP density). Adjust variance estimator if fit is poor.
2. **EigenTrack sanity check:** On a small LLM (e.g., LLaMa 1B), generate synthetic “hallucination” sequences (e.g., by prompt manipulation) and factual sequences. Compute spectral descriptors for each token. Plot temporal trajectories (entropy, KL to MP) to visually confirm the expected drift pattern before training the recurrent head.
3. **RMT‑KD single‑layer pilot:** Apply the RMT‑KD pipeline to only the first transformer feed‑forward network (FFN) layer of BERT‑tiny. Measure accuracy change on a GLUE task (e.g., SST) after projection and after self‑distillation. This isolates the effect of one projection step and validates the self‑distillation recovery mechanism before attempting iterative layer reduction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How do EigenTrack and RMT‑KD generalize to other model architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder, multimodal) and modalities beyond text?
- What are the precise conditions under which the MP bulk assumption breaks down, and how can we detect or correct for such violations in practice?
- For EigenTrack, does spectral drift reliably precede all types of hallucinations, including those that are highly structured or memorized?
- For RMT‑KD, when does the task‑relevant signal reside within the MP bulk rather than in outlier eigenvectors, and how can we adapt the compression strategy accordingly?
- Can the theoretical guarantees of the spiked covariance model be extended to the non‑i.i.d. activation distributions typical of deep networks?

## Limitations
- Scope limited to LLaMa (EigenTrack) and BERT (RMT‑KD); generalizability to other architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder, multimodal) is unknown.
- MP bulk estimation assumes activation covariances follow the Marchenko–Pastur law; real data may violate i.i.d. assumptions, producing mis‑estimated noise edges.
- Break conditions (low‑rank bottlenecks for RMT‑KD, highly structured hallucinations for EigenTrack) remain unvalidated and could collapse performance under distribution shift.
- Minimal external citations; the field is nascent, so independent replications are scarce.

## Confidence
- Spectral separation (outliers = signal): **Medium**
- EigenTrack temporal drift detection: **Medium‑High**
- RMT‑KD compression via outlier projection: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑architecture replication:** Run the full pipeline (spectral analysis, EigenTrack, RMT‑KD) on three diverse architectures (e.g., GPT‑Neo, T5, ViT) to test universality of the RMT framework.
2. **Stress‑test OOD/hallucination patterns:** Generate OOD inputs with tunable internal correlation (random noise vs. structured domain shifts) and measure EigenTrack’s AUROC; include memorized falsehoods to probe the “structured hallucination” break condition.
3. **Projection threshold sweep:** For RMT‑KD, vary the MP bulk quantile threshold (e.g., 0.90–0.99) on a held‑out set, plot accuracy/throughput trade‑offs, and confirm that the default threshold lies near the Pareto frontier while linking accuracy drops to loss of signal.
