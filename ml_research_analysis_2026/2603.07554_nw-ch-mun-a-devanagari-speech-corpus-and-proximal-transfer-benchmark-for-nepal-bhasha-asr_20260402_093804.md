# Nwāchá Munā: A Devanagari Speech Corpus and Proximal Transfer Benchmark for Nepal Bhasha ASR

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07554
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07554
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Proximal transfer from Nepali yields 17.59% CER with data augmentation, matching Whisper-Small's 17.88% on the first Nepal Bhasha ASR benchmark.

## Executive Summary
Nepal Bhasha (Newari) is an endangered language with no existing automatic speech recognition (ASR) system. This work addresses its digital marginalization by releasing *Nwāchá Muná*, a 5.39‑hour Devanagari‑script speech corpus with 5,727 utterances from 18 native speakers. The authors establish the first script‑preserving ASR benchmark for the language and evaluate whether proximal transfer from a related, higher‑resource language (Nepali) can match the performance of massive multilingual pretraining (OpenAI’s Whisper). Using a Nepali‑pretrained Conformer (NepConformer) fine‑tuned on the tiny Newari set, they show that straightforward data augmentation reduces character error rate (CER) from 52.54% (zero‑shot) to 17.59%—statistically indistinguishable from Whisper‑Small (17.88%) despite having far fewer parameters. Additional experiments indicate that freezing the encoder and fine‑tuning only the decoder yields 18.77% CER, suggesting the encoder already captures transferable acoustic knowledge. In contrast, semi‑supervised pseudo‑labeling from out‑of‑domain broadcast audio worsens performance (19.83% CER). The study demonstrates that for ultra‑low‑resource ASR, leveraging linguistic proximity within a language cluster offers a computationally efficient alternative to training or fine‑tuning massive multilingual models.

## Method Summary
The core data is the *Nwāchá Muná* corpus: 5.39 hours of read speech in Nepal Bhasha, recorded at 16 kHz mono, with transcripts in native Devanagari script. The corpus is split into training (4.31 h, 4,575 utterances), validation (0.54 h, 576), and test (0.54 h, 576). Two source models are used: (1) *NepConformer*—a Conformer‑CTC model pretrained on Nepali (18 encoder layers, 256‑dim, 128‑token BPE)—and (2) *Whisper‑Small* (244M parameters). For NepConformer, the authors evaluate several fine‑tuning strategies on the small training set: (a) full model fine‑tuning, (b) decoder‑only fine‑tuning (encoder frozen), (c) data augmentation (offline speed perturbation, volume randomization, noise injection to create a 5× larger set; plus online time‑stretch, pitch‑shift, and Gaussian noise during training), and (d) semi‑supervised learning where pseudo‑labels for 9.33 hours of unlabeled broadcast audio (filtered by confidence >70%) are concatenated with the labeled set. Whisper‑Small is fine‑tuned with and without augmentation, forced to use the Nepali language token. Shallow fusion with a 5‑gram KenLM (trained on all Newari text) is also explored. All models are evaluated on the fixed test set using character error rate (CER) as the primary metric.

## Key Results
- Zero‑shot NepConformer: 52.54% CER.
- With static/dynamic data augmentation, NepConformer (full fine‑tune): 17.59% CER.
- Fine‑tuned Whisper‑Small: 17.88% CER (with augmentation); the small model matches despite having ~16× more parameters.
- Decoder‑only fine‑tuning (encoder frozen): 18.77% CER—only a 1.2% absolute increase, indicating the encoder already provides strong acoustic representations.
- Shallow fusion with KenLM: slight CER increase (not reported exactly, but noted as non‑beneficial for CER).
- Semi‑supervised pseudo‑labeling: worsened CER to 19.83%, likely due to acoustic/linguistic mismatch between broadcast audio and the read‑speech corpus.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Proximal transfer succeeds because Nepal Bhasha and Nepali are linguistically close: they share the Devanagari script, have overlapping phonology, and belong to the same South Asian language area. Pre‑training on Nepali gives the Conformer encoder a robust initialization for acoustic modeling that transfers effectively to the related language. Data augmentation (speed changes, noise, pitch shifts) artificially expands the limited 4.31‑hour training set, teaching the model to be invariant to common acoustic variations and thereby reducing overfitting. The decoder‑only result shows that the encoder already encodes most of the necessary acoustic–phonetic information; the decoder only needs to adapt to the specific output token space and language idiosyncrasies. Whisper‑Small, despite massive multilingual pretraining, requires more computation but achieves similar accuracy. Semi‑supervised pseudo‑labeling fails because the unlabeled broadcast audio differs significantly from the clean, read speech in the training corpus—both in acoustic conditions (noise, spontaneity) and in vocabulary (media jargon vs. literary/conversational text)—so pseudo‑labels introduce distributional shift that harms the model.

## Foundational Learning
- **Proximal cross‑lingual transfer**  
  *Why needed:* Overcomes extreme data scarcity by borrowing representations from a related language.  
  *Quick check:* Measure zero‑shot CER of a related‑language model; a moderate CER (<40%) indicates transfer is feasible.

- **Data augmentation for low‑resource ASR**  
  *Why needed:* Increases effective dataset size and improves model robustness to acoustic variability.  
  *Quick check:* Compare validation CER with/without augmentation; a consistently lower validation CER confirms benefit.

- **Semi‑supervised learning with pseudo‑labeling**  
  *Why needed:* Leverages abundant unlabeled speech to potentially boost performance.  
  *Quick check:* After pseudo‑labeling, compute confidence distribution and OOV rate against gold text; high OOV or low confidence suggests domain mismatch.

- **Shallow fusion with an external language model**  
  *Why needed:* Integrates pure text knowledge to resolve homophones and improve language modeling.  
  *Quick check:* Tune the LM weight (λ) and penalty (β) on a validation set; if validation CER decreases, fusion is helpful.

- **CTC‑based sequence modeling**  
  *Why needed:* Enables monotonic alignment and efficient training without attention mechanisms, suitable for limited data.  
  *Quick check:* Monitor training convergence; CTC typically converges faster than attention‑based seq2seq on small datasets.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Raw audio (16 kHz mono) → Log‑Mel filterbank (80 bands) → Conformer Encoder (12 blocks: self‑attention + feed‑forward + convolution) → Linear projection → CTC softmax (128 BPE tokens) → Token decoding (greedy/beam).  
For augmentation: Raw audio → Time‑stretch / Pitch‑shift / Gaussian noise → Feature extraction.

**Critical path**  
The acoustic signal flows from raw waveform through handcrafted features into the Conformer encoder, which generates a high‑level representation. The CTC head produces per‑frame token probabilities, and greedy or beam search yields the final transcript. During training, CTC loss compares the predicted sequence to the reference token sequence.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Conformer vs. Transformer*: Conformer incorporates convolutions to capture local speech patterns, improving efficiency for acoustic modeling, at the cost of slightly increased model complexity.
- *CTC vs. attention*: CTC assumes conditional independence and monotonic alignment, simplifying training and enabling parallelization, but may struggle with long‑range dependencies.
- *Full fine‑tuning vs. decoder‑only*: Fine‑tuning all parameters adapts the encoder to the new language but risks overfitting and requires more compute; freezing the encoder reduces trainable parameters dramatically and relies on the quality of pre‑training, leading to only a small CER increase.
- *Static vs. dynamic augmentation*: Static expansion (e.g., speed perturbation) multiplates storage and loading time; dynamic augmentation adds CPU overhead during training but offers infinite variety.

**Failure signatures**  
- *Overfitting*: Training CER → 0% while validation CER rises sharply.
- *Augmentation too aggressive*: Model fails to converge; check by visualizing augmented spectrograms—distortions should remain within plausible ranges.
- *Pseudo‑label domain shift*: High‑confidence pseudo‑labels still cause validation CER to increase; verify by measuring OOV rate of pseudo‑texts against in‑domain vocabulary.
- *Shallow fusion degradation*: Adding KenLM increases CER; likely due to limited or mismatched text corpus (e.g., including Nepali text). Retrain LM on only in‑domain Newari text and tune hyperparameters on validation set.
- *Encoder freezing overly restrictive*: Decoder‑only CER gap >2% absolute indicates encoder may need adaptation; consider unfreezing some top encoder layers.

**First three experiments**  
1. Zero‑shot evaluation of pretrained NepConformer on the Newari test set to quantify the initial transfer gap.
2. Full model fine‑tuning on the base 4.31‑hour training set (no augmentation) to establish the supervised baseline.
3. Full model fine‑tuning with the described data augmentation (5× static expansion plus dynamic transforms) and measure CER improvement over Experiment 2.

## Limitations
- The *Nwāchá Muná* corpus is claimed to be released but no public URL/DOI is provided, blocking independent verification and follow‑up work.
- The exact augmentation pipeline applied to Whisper‑Small is unspecified; it is unclear whether it matches the dynamic transforms used for NepConformer, which complicates the parameter‑efficiency comparison.
- Semi‑supervised pseudo‑labeling used broadcast audio that is acoustically and linguistically mismatched with the read‑speech corpus; the resulting performance drop is likely specific to this domain shift and may not generalize.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Proximal transfer from Nepali (augmented NepConformer) matches Whisper‑Small performance. **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Claim:** Encoder fine‑tuning alone (decoder‑only) yields competitive CER (18.77% vs. 17.59%), indicating encoder sufficiency. **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Claim:** Semi‑supervised pseudo‑labeling with broadcast audio degrades performance (19.83% CER). **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Claim:** The corpus and benchmark establish the first script‑preserving ASR resource for Nepal Bhasha. **Confidence:** Low (due to lack of public access verification).
