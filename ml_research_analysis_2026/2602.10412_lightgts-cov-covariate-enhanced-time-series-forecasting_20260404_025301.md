# LightGTS-Cov: Covariate-Enhanced Time Series Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10412
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10412
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** Extends the LightGTS backbone with a small residual fusion plug-in to incorporate both past and future-known covariates, achieving state-of-the-art forecasting accuracy on public benchmarks and real-world deployments.

## Executive Summary
LightGTS-Cov addresses the ineffective integration of exogenous covariates into time series foundation models, a critical need for applications like energy forecasting. It builds on the lightweight LightGTS backbone by adding a tiny post-decoder MLP plug-in (~0.1M parameters) that performs a two-stage residual fusion: first aligning and fusing past covariates with decoded target tokens, then refining them with future-known covariates. This design preserves the backbone’s period-aware decoding pipeline while enabling covariate conditioning. Evaluations on EPF and Energy benchmarks, as well as two industrial deployments (PV power and electricity price forecasting), show consistent improvements over the LightGTS baseline and competitive performance among covariate-aware models. The approach demonstrates that efficient, backbone-preserving covariate integration can yield practical gains in accuracy and operational stability.

## Method Summary
LightGTS-Cov freezes (or lightly fine-tunes) a pretrained LightGTS backbone and introduces a lightweight fusion module. Past covariates are tokenized using the same period-aware scheme as the target, producing representations \(Z_{\text{past}}\) aligned token-wise with the backbone’s target decoder output \(Z_{\text{target}}\) (shape \([C, D, O]\)). Future-known covariates are linearly embedded from patches to a horizon-aligned tensor \(f\) (shape \([M_f, D, O]\)). The fusion proceeds in two MLP stages per token index \(j\): (1) concatenate \(Z_{\text{target}}[:,:,j]\) and \(Z_{\text{past}}[:,:,j]\), flatten, apply LayerNorm and an MLP to get \(h_j\); (2) concatenate \(h_j\) with all \(f[m,:,j]\), apply LayerNorm and another MLP to get residual token \(r_j\). Residual tokens are tiled across target variables to form \(R_{\text{tile}}\) (shape \([C, D, O]\)). The final forecast is \(\hat{Y} = \text{Head}(Z_{\text{target}}) + \text{Head}(R_{\text{tile}})\), where the shared head projects both to the data space. Only the plug-in parameters (and optionally the head) are trained, minimizing memory and compute overhead.

## Key Results
- Outperforms the vanilla LightGTS baseline on all public benchmarks (EPF and Energy).
- Achieves best or second-best performance among covariate-aware models, with or without future-known covariates.
- Delivers superior accuracy and stable operational performance in two real industrial deployments (PV power and electricity price forecasting).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Residual fusion preserves backbone knowledge.** The additive composition \(\text{Head}(Z_{\text{target}}) + \text{Head}(R_{\text{tile}})\) allows the backbone’s original forecast to dominate when covariates are uninformative, while enabling covariate-guided adjustments when beneficial. The small residual branch does not alter the pretrained representations, ensuring training stability and avoiding catastrophic forgetting. Evidence: Equation 11 defines the residual formulation and the paper reports consistent improvements over the baseline. A break condition arises if the backbone’s representations are poorly suited to the target domain; the residual branch alone lacks capacity to fully compensate.

**Token-aligned processing ensures temporal consistency.** Past covariates are tokenized identically to the target, yielding \(Z_{\text{past}}\) with token index \(j\) aligned to \(Z_{\text{target}}[: ,: ,j]\). Future covariates are embedded to \(f\) with the same token count \(O\), each token corresponding to a forecast horizon segment. This alignment permits token-wise concatenation and fusion without temporal misalignment. Evidence: Sections 3.4 and 3.5 detail the tokenization and alignment; shape mismatches would be immediately apparent. The approach may fail if covariates have different sampling rates that cannot be cleanly tokenized to the same \(O\).

**MLP-based cross-variate fusion within each token.** For token \(j\), representations from target, past, and future covariates are concatenated along the variable dimension and flattened; a lightweight MLP then models interactions among variables at that time segment. This design captures useful cross-variate conditioning without the parameter overhead of attention. Evidence: Section 3.5 motivates that each token covers the same temporal segment across variables, making cross-variable interactions beneficial. However, an MLP cannot capture cross-token dependencies; if long-range variable interactions are critical, a more powerful mechanism might be required.

## Foundational Learning
- **Residual connections:** The entire LightGTS-Cov design relies on residual addition to inject covariate corrections while preserving the backbone. *Quick check:* If the covariate branch outputs zero, the model reduces exactly to vanilla LightGTS, confirming the backbone’s standalone capability.
- **Time series tokenization/period-aware patching:** Understanding tokenization is essential to grasp alignment and the meaning of \(O\). *Quick check:* How does token length \(O\) relate to forecast horizon \(F\) and cycle length \(P\)? Typically \(O = \lceil F/P \rceil\) under period-aware patching; alignment across variables ensures each token \(j\) covers the same time segment.
- **Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT):** LightGTS-Cov freezes the backbone and only trains small added modules, which is key for deployment efficiency and stability. *Quick check:* What are the trade-offs of freezing versus full fine-tuning? Freezing preserves pretrained knowledge and reduces compute, but may limit adaptation if the backbone is suboptimal for the target task.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Pretrained LightGTS backbone → \(Z_{\text{target}}\) (target decoder).  
Past covariates → tokenization (same as target) → backbone → \(Z_{\text{past}}\).  
Future covariates → patching → linear embedding \(W_f\) → \(f\).  
Then per token \(j\): MLP\(_{\text{past}}\)(flatten(\(Z_{\text{target}}[:,:,j]\), \(Z_{\text{past}}[:,:,j]\)) → \(h_j\); MLP\(_{\text{fut}}\)(concat(\(h_j\), all \(f[m,:,j]\)) → \(r_j\).  
Stack \(r_j\) → \(R\) → tile to \(R_{\text{tile}}\) → \(\hat{Y} = \text{Head}(Z_{\text{target}}) + \text{Head}(R_{\text{tile}})\).

Critical path:  
1. Backbone forward on target → \(Z_{\text{target}}\) \([C, D, O]\).  
2. Backbone forward on past covariates → \(Z_{\text{past}}\) \([M_p, D, O]\).  
3. Future covariates → \(f\) \([M_f, D, O]\).  
4. For each \(j=1..O\): flatten \(Z_{\text{target}}[:,:,j]\) and \(Z_{\text{past}}[:,:,j]\) → \(u_1\); LayerNorm → MLP\(_{\text{past}}\) → \(h_j\); concat \(h_j\) with \(f[:,j]\) → \(u_2\); LayerNorm → MLP\(_{\text{fut}}\) → \(r_j\).  
5. Stack \(r_j\) → \(R\) \([O, D]\), tile across \(C\) → \(R_{\text{tile}}\) \([C, D, O]\).  
6. Apply shared head to \(Z_{\text{target}}\) and \(R_{\text{tile}}\), sum → \(\hat{Y}\).

Design tradeoffs:  
- MLP vs attention: MLP chosen for low parameter count and stability, but may underfit complex cross-token variable interactions that attention could capture.  
- Decoder-side vs encoder-side injection: Adding after decoding avoids modifying backbone internals, but may miss earlier representation enrichment.  
- Shared vs separate heads: Shared head enforces compatibility; separate heads could allow specialization but increase parameters and risk misalignment.

Failure signatures:  
- Dimension mismatch: \(O\) must match across \(Z_{\text{target}}\), \(Z_{\text{past}}\), and \(f\); otherwise concatenation fails.  
- Covariate noise amplification: No gating mechanism to suppress erroneous future covariates; residual branch may degrade forecasts.  
- Training instability: If backbone is unfrozen without careful learning rate scheduling, pretrained knowledge may be overwritten.

First 3 experiments:  
1. Backbone sanity: Pass dummy target through LightGTS; verify \(Z_{\text{target}}\) shape \([C, D, O]\) matches expectations (e.g., \(O = \lceil F/P \rceil\)).  
2. Covariate pathway check: Process past and future covariates separately; confirm shapes \([M_p, D, O]\) and \([M_f, D, O]\) and that token indices align temporally.  
3. Fusion verification: For a single token, manually compute \(h_j\) and \(r_j\); ensure no dimension errors in concatenations and MLP inputs. Run full forward on a small batch; compare output shape to ground truth \([C, F]\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Transferability to other TSFMs:** The plug-in is claimed backbone-agnostic, but validation only exists for LightGTS; compatibility with diverse architectures remains untested.  
- **Robustness to noisy/missing future covariates:** Real-world future covariates (e.g., weather forecasts) are often uncertain or incomplete; the current model lacks mechanisms to handle such imperfections.  
- **Integration of operational constraints:** Forecasts ignore physical or economic constraints (e.g., capacity limits), potentially leading to infeasible decisions; embedding such constraints could improve practical utility.

## Limitations
- Backbone hyperparameters (model dimension \(D\), number of layers, heads) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.  
- Lack of ablations for key design choices (e.g., residual vs. non‑residual fusion, two‑stage ordering) prevents causal attribution of improvements.  
- No evaluation of robustness to noisy, missing, or misaligned covariates, despite their prevalence in real deployments.

## Confidence
- Residual fusion preserves backbone: **Medium** (baseline improvements reported, but no direct ablation).  
- Token-aligned processing: **High** (alignment is explicit and shapes are consistent).  
- MLP-based cross-variate fusion: **Low** (plausible but not compared against alternatives like attention).

## Next Checks
- Ablate residual design: Train without residual addition (use only covariate branch output) to isolate its impact on accuracy and stability.  
- Alignment stress test: Deliberately shift past covariate tokens by one position and quantify performance degradation versus correct alignment.  
- Replace MLP fusion with lightweight attention: Compare performance, parameter count, and training dynamics to test if MLP capacity is sufficient.
