# Empirical Comparison of Encoder-Based Language Models and Feature-Based Supervised Machine Learning Approaches to Automated Scoring of Long Essays

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2601.02659
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02659
*   **Reference Count:** 0
*   **Primary Result:** A feature-based voting ensemble using handcrafted linguistic features from full essays (no truncation) achieved a state-of-the-art QWK of 0.822 on the held-out test set, significantly outperforming individual truncated transformer models.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates automated essay scoring (AES) for long texts that exceed the 512-token context limit of standard encoder-based language models like BERT. It compares the performance of individual transformer models, ensembles combining their embeddings, and feature-based ensembles using handcrafted linguistic features extracted from the complete essay. Using a dataset of 17,307 essays scored 1–6, the research finds that truncation harms accuracy. While individual models peaked at a QWK of 0.790 (DeBERTaV3), the highest score of 0.822 was achieved by a voting ensemble (XGBoost, LightGBM, Neural Network) trained on full-essay linguistic features, demonstrating the superiority of explicit, full-text feature engineering for this task.

## Method Summary
The study used the ASAP AES 2.0 dataset (17,307 essays, scores 1–6, lengths up to 1,656 words), split 80/10/10 for train/validation/test. Baseline models fine-tuned individual pre-trained transformers (BERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERT, DeBERTa) with essays truncated to 512 tokens. Alternative approaches included: (1) ensemble-of-embeddings models that concatenated embeddings from one or more transformers and fed them to a gradient-boosted classifier (XGBoost/LightGBM) or MLP; and (2) a feature-based voting ensemble where separate XGBoost, LightGBM, and neural network models were trained on a comprehensive set of handcrafted linguistic features (syntactic complexity, lexical richness, discourse markers, etc.) extracted from the *full, untruncated* essay text. All models were evaluated using Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK).

## Key Results
*   Individual transformer models, limited by 512-token truncation, achieved modest best performance (DeBERTaV3: QWK 0.790).
*   An ensemble combining embeddings from multiple transformers with gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM) improved performance (best: QWK 0.812).
*   The highest performance was attained by a feature-based voting ensemble using handcrafted features from the full essay (QWK 0.822 on test set, 0.840 on Kaggle hidden test).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Truncation Discards Critical Information
*   **Claim:** Truncating long essays to 512 tokens impairs scoring accuracy by removing linguistically significant content, particularly from conclusions and late-stage argumentation.
*   **Mechanism:** Fixed-context transformers cannot process beyond their window, so exceeding texts are cut off. This discards information predictive of holistic scores related to coherence, development, and conclusion strength.
*   **Core assumption:** The full text contains global discourse features and final argumentative points crucial for scoring that are absent in the initial segment.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states truncation is expected to impair accuracy; page 6 notes truncation is insufficient for long essays and biases toward shorter texts.

### Mechanism 2: Ensemble-of-Embeddings Captures Complementary Signals
*   **Claim:** Combining embeddings from diverse pre-trained transformers (BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa) via a gradient-boosted ensemble mitigates individual model weaknesses and yields a more robust representation.
*   **Mechanism:** Different models learn distinct contextual representations. A boosting algorithm learns to weight and combine these heterogeneous embeddings, creating a meta-representation more predictive than any single source.
*   **Core assumption:** Predictive information for scoring is distributed and orthogonal enough across model embeddings that combination yields super-additive gains.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and page 10 describe the ensemble-of-embeddings approach; Table 2 shows Model 3 (DeBERTa + XGBoost/LightGBM) at QWK 0.812 vs. single DeBERTa at 0.790.

### Mechanism 3: Full-Text Handcrafted Features Outperform Truncated Embeddings
*   **Claim:** A feature-based ensemble using linguistically motivated features extracted from the *entire* essay outperforms models relying on truncated contextual embeddings.
*   **Mechanism:** Explicit features (syntax, lexicon, discourse) computed over full text bypass token limits and directly operationalize rubric constructs (e.g., organization). A voting ensemble leverages these structured signals.
*   **Core assumption:** Holistic scores are more directly predicted by a constellation of explicit linguistic measures calculable on full text than by an implicit, truncated context window.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Abstract cites the feature-based ensemble's QWK 0.822; page 5 lists feature types (syntactic complexity, lexical richness, etc.); page 11 details full-essay extraction; Table 2 shows Model 5's top QWK.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK)
    *   **Why needed:** Primary evaluation metric; measures agreement between predicted and human scores while weighting ordinal disagreements (e.g., 2 vs. 6 penalized more than 5 vs. 6), matching the ordered score scale.
    *   **Quick check:** Why is QWK generally preferred over simple accuracy for evaluating automated essay scorers?
*   **Concept:** Encoder-Based Language Model Tokenization & Context Windows
    *   **Why needed:** Core problem is the 512-token limit; understanding fixed-length inputs, wordpiece tokenization, and positional embeddings explains truncation and its consequences.
    *   **Quick check:** What happens to the 513th token and beyond in a BERT model when processing a 600-token essay?
*   **Concept:** Gradient Boosting for Tabular Data (XGBoost, LightGBM)
    *   **Why needed:** These classifiers powered the winning ensembles, suited for high-dimensional, heterogeneous feature spaces from embeddings or handcrafted features.
    *   **Quick check:** In the context of this paper, what is the key difference in input data format between training a transformer (like BERT) versus training XGBoost?

## Architecture Onboarding
1.  Data Ingestion -> (A) [Truncate -> SLM -> Embedding] OR (B) [Full-text -> Feature Extraction] -> Combine/Select Features -> Ensemble Classifier -> Score Prediction. Path (B) is critical for outperformance on long essays.
*   **Critical path:** Raw text diverges into two preprocessing branches (truncated embedding extraction vs. full-text feature extraction), which then feed into separate ensemble model heads before final scoring.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   Truncation (computationally cheap) vs. Full-Text Feature Extraction (preserves content, computationally heavier).
    *   Embedding Complexity (opaque, dense representations) vs. Feature Interpretability (sparse, rubric-aligned, requires domain knowledge).
    *   Single Model (simpler, faster) vs. Ensemble (higher accuracy, more complex tuning).
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Sudden QWK drop on longer essays indicates truncation harm.
    *   High validation variance suggests feature set or ensemble instability.
    *   Predictions clustered around mid-scores (e.g., 3-4) may signal poor discrimination of score extremes.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  Train individual SLMs (BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa) with 512-token truncation to establish single-model baseline performance.
    2.  Implement ensemble-of-embeddings models: extract embeddings from one or more SLMs and train a gradient boosting classifier (XGBoost/LightGBM) on them; compare QWK to baselines.
    3.  Implement the feature-based voting ensemble: extract the full suite of handcrafted linguistic features from untruncated essays; train separate XGBoost, LightGBM, and neural network models; combine via majority voting.

## Limitations
*   The precise list and formulas for the handcrafted linguistic features used in the top ensemble are not specified, hindering exact replication.
*   The architecture, hyperparameters, and voting mechanism for the neural network component of the feature-based ensemble are not detailed.
*   It is unclear whether embeddings for the ensemble-of-embeddings models were generated from frozen or fine-tuned base transformers, and the exact pooling method (CLS vs. mean) is unspecified.
