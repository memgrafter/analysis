# Tagarela - A Portuguese speech dataset from podcasts
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15326
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15326
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: TAGARELA corpus: 8,972 hours of Portuguese speech from podcasts, with a 2,800-hour clean subset for TTS; fine-tuned Parakeet v2 achieves 15.18% WER on ASR test set.

## Executive Summary
TAGARELA addresses the lack of large-scale, high-quality Portuguese speech datasets by curating an 8,972-hour corpus from raw podcast audio. The authors developed a multi-stage pipeline including audio standardization, speaker diarization, overlapping speech removal, a two-stage bootstrap transcription with hallucination filtering, denoising, and dialect labeling. The resulting dataset supports both ASR (full set) and TTS (2,800-hour clean subset). Evaluations show strong performance: a fine-tuned Parakeet v2 model reaches 15.18% WER and 7.09% CER on the ASR test set, while TTS models like Orpheus-TTS and Chatterbox achieve MOS scores above 4.15, demonstrating the dataset's quality and utility for Portuguese speech technology.

## Method Summary
The dataset construction begins with the "Cem Mil Podcasts" collection (~76,000 hours). Audio is standardized to 16 kHz mono FLAC and segmented into 5–20 second clips using silence detection. Speaker diarization via pyannote.audio separates speakers; overlapping speech is detected with a Wav2Vec2-XLS-R classifier and removed to ensure single-speaker segments. A bootstrap transcription strategy generates pseudo-labels: a small (~1,000 hour) subset is transcribed using a commercial ASR (ElevenLabs Scribe v1) to fine-tune Whisper large-v3 and train a second Wav2Vec2-XLS-R model. Segments where the two models agree (low WER/CER) are retained, filtering potential hallucinations. A repurposed Vocos neural vocoder denoises the audio. Finally, speaker clustering (RedimNet + HDBSCAN) and dialect classification (fine-tuned wav2vec-base) assign metadata. The full 8,972-hour set is used for ASR; a further cleaned 2,800-hour subset serves TTS.

## Key Results
- Dataset scale: 8,972 hours total (ASR), with a 2,800-hour clean subset for TTS, covering both Brazilian and European Portuguese.
- ASR performance: Fine-tuned Parakeet v2 achieves 15.18% WER and 7.09% CER on the TAGARELA test set.
- TTS naturalness: Orpheus-TTS obtains 4.155 MOS and 0.095 WER; Chatterbox achieves 4.176 MOS on the clean subset.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Three core mechanisms explain the pipeline's effectiveness:
- **Bootstrap transcription with hallucination filtering**: A high-fidelity commercial ASR seed fine-tunes Whisper, while a second Wav2Vec2-XLS-R model trained on the same seed provides an independent transcription. Segments with high agreement between the two model outputs are kept, effectively filtering Whisper hallucinations. This leverages model consensus as a proxy for accuracy, enabling scalable pseudo-labeling without proportionate human cost.
- **Overlapping speech removal**: A custom Wav2Vec2-XLS-R classifier detects segments with multiple talkers. Removing such segments is essential for creating a clean single-speaker subset suitable for TTS training, as overlapping speech confounds speaker modeling and prosody learning.
- **Neural vocoder denoising**: A Vocos vocoder, repurposed as a denoiser, suppresses background noise and artifacts common in podcasts. Cleaner audio improves feature extraction for ASR and provides a higher-quality target for TTS synthesis.

Each mechanism is supported by pipeline design (evidence anchors in the paper) and validated indirectly by the final performance metrics. Break conditions include non-representative seed data, low recall in overlap detection, and over-aggressive denoising that harms speech fidelity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Speaker Diarization  
  Why needed here: Podcasts are multi-speaker; diarization separates audio by speaker identity, enabling single-speaker segment extraction for TTS and preventing speaker label confusion in ASR.  
  Quick check question: What is a key failure mode of clustering-based diarization on long-form, multi-host podcast conversations, and how might it propagate errors to downstream TTS?

- **Concept**: Bootstrap Learning with Pseudo-Labeling  
  Why needed here: Manual transcription of ~9,000 hours is infeasible; pseudo-labeling using a small seed set scales transcription cost-effectively.  
  Quick check question: Why is a two-model agreement filter (Whisper vs. Wav2Vec2) employed instead of relying on confidence scores from a single pseudo-labeling model?

- **Concept**: Self-Supervised Speech Representation Learning (wav2vec2/XLS-R)  
  Why needed here: These robust pre-trained models serve as backbones for overlap detection, dialect classification, and fine-tuning in the bootstrap process, reducing the need for large labeled datasets.  
  Quick check question: How does cross-lingual pre-training (XLS-R) specifically benefit the processing of a low-resource language like Portuguese in this pipeline?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Raw podcast audio → Standardization (format conversion, silence segmentation) → Diarization (pyannote.audio) → Overlap detection (Wav2Vec2-XLS-R classifier) → Bootstrap transcription (ElevenLabs seed → fine-tuned Whisper & second Wav2Vec2-XLS-R → agreement filter) → Denoising (Vocos vocoder) → Speaker clustering (RedimNet + HDBSCAN) & dialect labeling → Final datasets (8,972h ASR set, 2,800h clean TTS subset).
- **Critical path**: The transcription bootstrap loop (seed → Whisper fine-tune → pseudo-labeling → agreement filter) is pivotal; its quality depends on the representativeness of the seed and the agreement threshold. Overlap detection acts as a hard filter that directly reduces the pool for the clean TTS subset.
- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - Scalability vs. label fidelity: Pseudo-labeling scales but introduces noise; the agreement filter trades recall for precision.
  - Cleanliness vs. diversity: Strict overlap/denoising yields a clean TTS set but may exclude acoustically challenging samples, potentially limiting robustness.
  - Reproducibility vs. performance: The denoiser uses a private dataset for higher quality, while a public-only alternative is provided for full reproducibility.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - ASR: Unusually high WER on TAGARELA test set suggests faulty bootstrap (seed misaligned or threshold mis-set).
  - TTS: Voice mixing, glitches, or unstable prosody indicate diarization/overlap detection failures letting multi-speaker segments into the clean set.
  - Metadata: Excessive unique speaker labels per podcast points to misconfigured clustering (over-clustering).
- **First 3 experiments** (validation steps before full processing):
  1. Compute diarization error rate (DER) and overlap detector precision/recall on a small manually annotated subset; target DER < 15%, overlap recall > 80%.
  2. On the seed set, measure WER between commercial ground truth and the two pseudo-label models; set agreement threshold to maximize F1 on a held-out seed portion.
  3. After denoising, run TorchAudio-Squim metrics (STOI, PESQ, SI-SDR) on a random sample of the clean subset; enforce minimum median thresholds (e.g., STOI > 0.95).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1**: How can text-audio alignment in TAGARELA be systematically improved to reduce errors for TTS training? The paper acknowledges room for refinement in alignment, as misalignments from bootstrap labeling may degrade TTS performance.
- **Question 2**: How effective is the repurposed Vocos denoiser at removing podcast-specific artifacts without introducing speech distortion that harms TTS quality? The denoiser's contribution to MOS scores is not isolated; an ablation is needed.
- **Question 3**: What is the accuracy of the dialect classifier (Brazilian vs. European Portuguese) on the actual TAGARELA distribution, and how do potential labeling errors impact model training? The classifier was fine-tuned on balanced external datasets, but its performance on the imbalanced podcast data is unknown.
- **Question 4**: What is the optimal agreement threshold between Whisper and Wav2Vec2-XLS-R models to maximize transcript quality without excessive data discard? The specific threshold is not disclosed, and its tuning is critical for balancing label noise and data quantity.

## Limitations
- Bootstrap reproducibility depends on undisclosed Whisper fine-tuning hyperparameters and the proprietary ElevenLabs seed, preventing independent verification of initial label quality.
- Denoising details are opaque: the Vocos vocoder's training data and modifications are private, hindering assessment of potential timbre distortion or over-smoothing.
- Speaker clustering uses HDBSCAN with unspecified parameters, risking speaker label inconsistencies (over/under-clustering) that could affect TTS models.
- Dialect balance and classifier accuracy on the dataset’s natural distribution are not reported, particularly for European Portuguese which is underrepresented.

## Confidence
- **High confidence**: The existence of a large (~9k hour) multi-stage pipeline and final dataset statistics; clear description of component choices (pyannote, Whisper, Wav2Vec2-XLS-R).
- **Medium confidence**: The two-model agreement filter effectively removes Whisper hallucinations; supported by final AS performance but lacking ablation.
- **Low confidence**: That denoising improves TTS naturalness; MOS results are strong but no direct ablation isolates the denoiser's contribution, and potential artifacts remain unquantified.

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a pseudo-label fidelity audit: manually transcribe a stratified random sample (e.g., 100 hours) from the final ASR set, compute WER against pseudo-labels, and correlate with agreement scores to validate filtering.
2. Test overlap detector recall on a manually annotated subset with known overlapping speech; low recall would indicate significant multi-speaker leakage into the TTS clean set.
3. Perform a denoising impact ablation: train identical TTS models on the clean subset with and without the denoising step, comparing MOS, objective metrics (e.g., speaker similarity via ECAPA-TDNN), and artifact prevalence.
