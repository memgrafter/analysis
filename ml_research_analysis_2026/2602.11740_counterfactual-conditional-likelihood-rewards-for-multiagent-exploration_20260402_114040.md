# Counterfactual Conditional Likelihood Rewards for Multiagent Exploration

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11740
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11740
- Reference count: 5
- Primary result: Counterfactual Conditional Likelihood (CCL) rewards enable efficient coordinated exploration in sparse-reward cooperative multiagent systems, significantly outperforming local entropy maximization and succeeding in tightly coupled tasks where alternatives fail.

## Executive Summary
Cooperative multiagent reinforcement learning with sparse team rewards suffers from redundant individual exploration that neglects coordination. This paper introduces Counterfactual Conditional Likelihood (CCL) rewards, which assign each agent an intrinsic reward equal to the change in conditional log-likelihood of its observation when replaced by its previous observation, conditioned on teammates' current observations. The method uses fixed random local encoders and k-nearest neighbor density estimation in the concatenated joint embedding space, thereby isolating each agent's unique contribution to joint exploration without learning a joint model. Experiments in continuous multi-rover and particle environments show that CCL substantially improves team reward and coordinated behavior compared to local entropy maximization (OEM). CCL discovers complementary strategies even in highly sparse tasks with tight coupling factors (5–6), where OEM fails. Combining CCL with OEM via mixture rewards accelerates convergence and raises peak performance in several domains, though CCL alone remains most robust under the tightest coordination requirements. Across results, CCL consistently reduces redundant exploration and speeds learning in sparse-reward multiagent coordination.

## Method Summary
The approach integrates CCL into a Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) framework, using MAPPO as the base algorithm. Each agent processes its observations through a frozen random encoder (a small MLP) to obtain a low-dimensional embedding. During training, the joint embedding is constructed by concatenating all agents' embeddings. For each agent, a counterfactual joint embedding is created by substituting the agent's current embedding with its embedding from the previous timestep. Both the actual and counterfactual joint embeddings are compared using k‑nearest neighbor density estimation within an episodic memory of past joint embeddings from the same episode. Specifically, a shared radius is computed as the maximum Chebyshev distance to the k‑th neighbor of either point in the joint space. Then, within that radius, the number of memories falling into the agent’s own embedding subspace is counted for both actual and counterfactual points. The difference in digamma‑transformed counts gives a raw conditional log‑likelihood change, which is shaped via a Softplus function and clipped to a maximum of 5.0. The resulting per‑agent intrinsic reward is combined with the sparse team reward for policy updates. Optionally, a mixture with per‑agent Local Entropy Maximization (OEM) rewards is used, with mixing weight α; in rover tasks the intrinsic reward may also be scaled by a saliency value (POI detection). The random encoders avoid instability from learning joint embeddings under non‑stationarity, and k‑NN provides a stationary, model‑free density estimator.

## Key Results
- CCL achieves significantly higher team reward and more coordinated behavior than OEM across multi‑rover and particle environments.
- CCL succeeds in extremely sparse, tightly coupled tasks (e.g., single‑POI rover with coupling factor 5–6) where OEM yields no learning.
- Mixture of CCL and OEM yields faster convergence and higher peak performance in several domains, though CCL alone remains most robust under the tightest coordination.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** CCL rewards promote coordinated exploration by quantifying each agent's unique informational contribution to the joint observation.
- **Mechanism:** For each agent, the method compares the log‑likelihood of its actual observation to a counterfactual (previous timestep) observation, conditioned on teammates' current observations. This difference is estimated via k‑NN density in a joint embedding space formed by concatenating random local encodings. A higher reward indicates the agent's current observation is more informative for the team's joint state than its past state.
- **Core assumption:** An agent's unique contribution to coordinated exploration is reflected in how much its *current* observation reduces uncertainty about the *joint* observation relative to its *past* observation, holding teammates fixed.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "CCL isolates each agent's unique contribution to joint exploration." [section 3.2] "The key idea... is to exploit joint observations effectively... by substituting an individual agent’s embedding with a counterfactual one, we can estimate how that agent’s behavior influences the likelihood of the overall joint observation." [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers focus on entropic or influence‑based exploration, not counterfactual likelihood. *Assumption: The counterfactual conditioning frame is novel and not directly validated by cited corpus.*
- **Break condition:** If agents cannot affect the joint likelihood (e.g., in fully observable or deterministic settings where observations are perfectly predictable from teammates), the reward signal vanishes, providing no incentive.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Combining CCL with local entropy maximization (mixture rewards) unifies decentralized diversity and coordinated joint exploration, accelerating early learning.
- **Mechanism:** Local OEM rewards (Eq. 2) encourage each agent to visit novel states independently. CCL rewards encourage states that are *jointly* informative. Their weighted sum (Eq. 6) provides dense, multi‑granular intrinsic feedback.
- **Core assumption:** The state space contains regions where local novelty alone is insufficient to discover coordination (requiring CCL) and regions where local diversity is a useful precursor (requiring OEM). The mixture covers both.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Combining CCL with OEM via mixture rewards yields faster convergence and higher peak performance in several domains." [section 3.3] "The intuition is that local entropy encourages each agent to diversify its own trajectory, while centralized joint exploration promotes coordinated exploration; combining the two reduces redundant search." [section 5] "Mixture rewards achieve faster convergence and higher peak performance in some settings."
- **Break condition:** In tasks with extremely tight coupling (single‑POI rover with high coupling factor), the benefit of OEM diminishes, as local diversity can mislead agents away from the narrow coordination corridor.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Using fixed random encoders and k‑NN density estimation makes joint exploration feasible in non‑stationary, high‑dimensional Dec‑POMDPs.
- **Mechanism:** Random encoders avoid training a joint embedding network, which would be destabilized by non‑stationarity. k‑NN estimation in the concatenated embedding space provides a consistent, stationary density estimator that does not require fitting a parametric model to a shifting distribution.
- **Core assumption:** Random projections preserve sufficient local structure (neighborhood relationships) to make k‑NN density estimates meaningful for the reward, even though the underlying joint distribution shifts.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2] "We embed each agent’s observations using local random encoders, thereby avoiding to learn intractable joint embeddings... To estimate the likelihoods... we use k‑nn based local density approximations." [section 4] "The encoder is a three‑layer MLP... fixed random encoder ϕ used for CCL reward computation." [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., MIMEx, Safe Multiagent Coordination) also use random encoders for intrinsic motivation, providing indirect support for this design pattern.
- **Break condition:** If the observation space is too high‑dimensional or complex, random projections may not preserve the topological structure needed for meaningful density comparisons; the k‑NN estimate becomes noisy and the reward signal unreliable.

## Foundational Learning
### Dec‑POMDP (Decentralized Partial Observable MDP)
- **Why needed here:** Defines the problem class: cooperative agents with private observations, joint actions, and sparse global reward. All credit assignment and exploration challenges stem from this structure.
- **Quick check question:** Given a Dec‑POMDP, what is the difference between a local observation `o_i^t` and a joint observation `o^t`? How does partial observability complicate joint exploration?

### Intrinsic Motivation / Task‑Agnostic Rewards
- **Why needed here:** The core innovation is a new intrinsic reward (CCL) to solve sparse external reward problems. Understanding novelty, entropy maximization, and prediction‑error based intrinsic rewards is prerequisite.
- **Quick check question:** Why might maximizing local observation entropy lead to redundant exploration in a multiagent setting?

### Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE)
- **Why needed here:** CCL computation requires access to all agents' current observations during training (to form `o_{−i}^t` and the joint embedding). Execution remains decentralized. This paradigm is fundamental to the algorithm's design.
- **Quick check question:** In CTDE, what information is available to the policy during execution vs. during training? Why is this separation necessary for CCL?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Policy Layer (MAPPO with shared LSTM actors, centralized critic) → Observation Processor (fixed random encoder ϕ per agent) → Joint Builder (concatenate embeddings) → Counterfactual Generator (replace current embedding with previous) → Density Estimator (k‑NN in joint Chebyshev space, shared‑radius calculation, neighbor counts in agent’s subspace) → Reward Calculator (digamma difference, Softplus shaping) → Mixture Scheduler (combine with OEM if α>0) → Saliency Modulator (rover tasks only, multiply by POI detection value) → Intrinsic reward added to PPO advantage.

**Critical path:**  
1. Collect rollout: each agent observes `o_i^t`, encodes to `z_i^t`.  
2. Centralized training step: build `z^t` for each timestep in batch.  
3. For each agent i and timestep t: build `˜z^(i)^t` (counterfactual), query memory `M` (past `z^t` from same episode) for k‑NN radii and counts.  
4. Compute `r_i^CCL(t)`. Combine with `r_Local_OEM(i,t)` if mixing.  
5. Add (possibly saliency‑modulated) intrinsic reward to PPO advantage computation. Update actor and critic.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Random vs. Learned Encoders: Random avoids non‑stationarity collapse but may lose semantic efficiency; learned could adapt but become unstable.  
- k‑NN vs. Parametric Density: k‑NN is simple and stationary but noisy in high dimensions; parametric (e.g., normalizing flow) could be smoother but must be trained on non‑stationary data.  
- CCL vs. OEM mixture: Adding OEM helps with local coverage but can dilute the coordinated exploration signal. α must be tuned per domain; paper found α=0.5 best for denser tasks, α=0.1 for hardest sparse ones.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Reward Collapse:** `r_CCL` becomes consistently near zero or negative after shaping, indicating agents' actions no longer affect joint likelihood (possibly due to overfitting to a single strategy or failed exploration).  
- **Redundant Heatmaps:** In trajectory visualizations, agents cluster in the same regions despite CCL, suggesting the counterfactual distinction is lost (maybe k too large/small, or encoder too weak).  
- **Mixture Degradation:** Adding OEM (α>0) causes performance drop in tight‑coupling tasks, indicating local entropy incentives conflict with required coordination.  
- **Adversarial Domain Failure:** In particle environments, if CCL agents lose to the adversary despite higher intrinsic reward, the coordinated exploration may not align with the true team objective.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Multi‑Rover, 2 POIs, Coupling Factor 3‑5: Baseline test comparing CCL, OEM, Mixture learning curves (Figure 2). Expect CCL > OEM; Mixture fastest early convergence.  
2. Multi‑Rover, 1 POI, Coupling Factor 3‑6: Stress test for tight coordination. Expect OEM fails (flat zero reward), CCL succeeds, Mixture benefit minimal (Figure 4).  
3. Particle Environments (Predator‑Prey, Keep Away, Physical Deception): Generalization test with adversary. Expect CCL > OEM across all (Figure 5). No saliency modulation, so intrinsic reward is pure CCL/OEM.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How does CCL's performance scale with increasing team size?** The estimator accuracy may degrade as team size grows, but experiments only went up to 10 agents. Larger team dynamics remain unexplored.
2. **What task characteristics determine the optimal balance between CCL and local entropy rewards?** Mixture weight α showed sensitivity to task structure (e.g., α=0.5 vs 0.1) without a clear principle; an adaptive scheduling rule is needed.
3. **Can replacing fixed random encoders with learned joint observation embeddings improve CCL performance without compromising stability?** The trade‑off between representational quality and training stability is unexamined.

## Limitations
- Unpublished environment specifications (multi‑rover dynamics, particle observations) hinder exact replication.
- Ambiguity in CCL’s neighbor count procedure (marginal vs conditional) affects theoretical validity and implementation.
- Mixing with OEM requires careful per‑domain tuning of α; no automatic method provided.

## Confidence
- CCL beats OEM: **High** (consistent gains across 5 tasks)
- Mixture benefits: **Medium** (faster in some domains, fails in tight coupling, α‑sensitive)
- Random + k‑NN feasibility: **Medium** (empirically works but not compared to learned alternatives; may degrade in higher dimensions)
