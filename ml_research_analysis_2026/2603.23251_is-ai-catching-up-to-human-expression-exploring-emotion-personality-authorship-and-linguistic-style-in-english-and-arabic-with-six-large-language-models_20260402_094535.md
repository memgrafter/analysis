# Is AI Catching Up to Human Expression? Exploring Emotion, Personality, Authorship, and Linguistic Style in English and Arabic with Six Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.23251  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23251  
- **Reference count**: 5  
- **Primary result**: Six LLMs produce text that is easily distinguishable from human writing (F1 > 0.95) but classifiers rely on superficial cues – paraphrasing sharply degrades detection; a generalization gap exists between human and AI data; synthetic data augmentation notably improves Arabic personality classification; AI text is more readable and shows inflated authenticity scores.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates six large language models (GPT-4o, Gemini, Claude, Llama, DeepSeek, and Mistral) on their ability to mimic human emotional expression in English and personality traits in Arabic. Using machine classifiers (BERT for English, AraBERT for Arabic), the authors assess whether AI-generated text can be distinguished from human-authored text, examine generalization across data sources, and explore linguistic differences via LIWC and readability metrics. Key findings include near-perfect initial classification performance that collapses after paraphrasing, a significant generalization gap (trained on one domain, fails on the other), and substantial gains from adding synthetic data for Arabic personality tasks. Linguistic analysis reveals that AI outputs are systematically more readable and exhibit unnaturally high authenticity scores. The work highlights fundamental mismatches between human and AI expression and points to synthetic data as a valuable resource for under‑resourced languages.

## Method Summary
The authors design two main tasks: (1) binary classification of AI‑generated vs. human‑authored text, and (2) emotion classification (English) or personality trait classification (Arabic) with a focus on cross‑domain generalization. Human English data are sourced from four public emotion datasets (Ironic Corpus, Sarcasm Dataset, Text Emotion, Emotions Dataset); human Arabic personality data come from the MSAPersonality corpus (Big Five traits in Modern Standard Arabic). AI‑generated data are produced by prompting each of the six LLMs to write samples for each emotion (1,000 samples) or personality trait (100 samples), using median token constraints and temperature = 1.0. All texts are cleaned (URLs, hashtags, mentions removed; tokens shorter than three characters filtered). Classifiers based on BERT (English) and AraBERT (Arabic) are fine‑tuned with AdamW (learning rate 2e‑5, 5 epochs, batch sizes 32/16) under three training regimes: human‑only, AI‑only, and combined (human + AI), evaluated via 5‑fold cross‑validation on fixed test sets. Primary metrics are macro‑averaged F1, accuracy, precision, and recall. English outputs additionally undergo LIWC analysis (Tone, Authentic, Clout, Analytic) and readability assessment (Flesch, Gunning Fog, ARI, Dale‑Chall). A paraphrasing test using QuillBot (English only) probes the robustness of AI detection.

## Key Results
- AI‑generated text is highly distinguishable from human text (F1 > 0.95), but paraphrasing causes a sharp drop in recall, indicating classifiers rely on superficial stylistic cues.
- A clear generalization gap exists: classifiers trained on human data perform poorly on AI‑generated text and vice versa, particularly for English emotion; conversely, augmenting Arabic personality training with AI‑generated data significantly improves performance on human‑authored text.
- Linguistic analysis shows AI text is consistently more readable and scores near‑maximum on LIWC’s Authenticity dimension, while GPT‑4o and Gemini exhibit superior affective coherence and LLaMA underperforms.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The strong initial classification results stem from systematic stylistic differences between human and AI writing: LLMs tend to produce simpler sentence structures, higher readability, and an over‑representation of self‑referential language that inflates authenticity scores. These surface‑level features act as reliable cues for detection. The generalization gap arises because the feature distributions shift between human‑authored and AI‑generated corpora; a model trained on one domain fails to capture the patterns of the other. Combining both domains during training enables the classifier to learn invariant representations, which is especially beneficial for Arabic where limited human data make the synthetic complement highly valuable. The performance drop after paraphrasing confirms that many cues are brittle and easily altered, whereas deeper semantic or pragmatic markers remain underexplored. Model‑specific differences in affective coherence likely reflect variations in training objectives, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), and inherent architectural choices.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text)**:  
Human datasets (English emotions, Arabic personality) & AI‑generated datasets (per model) → Preprocessing (cleaning, token filtering) → Feature extraction (BERT/AraBERT embeddings) → Fine‑tuning (AdamW, LR = 2e‑5, epochs = 5, batch 32/16) with 5‑fold CV under three regimes (human‑only, AI‑only, combined) → Performance evaluation (macro‑F1, accuracy, precision, recall) on fixed test sets → Linguistic characterization (LIWC for English, readability metrics) → Paraphrasing robustness test (English only).

**Critical path**: The sequence that must succeed for the study: (1) generate AI samples with controlled prompts, (2) fine‑tune classifiers for each regime, (3) evaluate on held‑out human and AI test sets, (4) compute linguistic metrics.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Fixed test sets guarantee comparability across training regimes but may not represent the full data distribution.  
- 5‑fold CV mitigates small‑sample issues (especially Arabic) yet introduces variance across folds; results are not averaged over multiple random splits.  
- Small Arabic sample size (100 per trait) risks overfitting; smaller batch size partially addresses memory but not statistical stability.  
- Use of external tools (QuillBot for paraphrasing, LIWC) introduces configurability that is not fully documented, affecting reproducibility.  
- Macro‑averaged F1 ensures balanced class consideration but can hide per‑class degradation.

**Failure signatures**:  
- High variance in F1 across CV folds indicates unstable training, likely due to limited data.  
- Large performance gap between training and test sets suggests overfitting or domain mismatch.  
- Dramatic recall drop after paraphrasing confirms reliance on superficial cues.  
- Uniformly inflated authenticity scores across all LLMs point to a systematic bias in generation.  
- Unexpectedly high Tone values for negative emotions (e.g., anger) may indicate dictionary misalignment or prompt effects.

**Three first experiments**:  
1. Train BERT on human‑only English emotion data, evaluate on human test set (baseline classification).  
2. Train BERT on AI‑only English emotion data, evaluate on AI test set (pure AI detection).  
3. Train BERT on combined human + AI data, evaluate on both human and AI test sets to quantify generalization improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Why does training classifiers on human‑authored emotional text in English fail to generalize to AI‑generated emotional text, while synthetic data augmentation improves Arabic personality classification performance?  
- **Basis**: The authors report a “significant generalization gap” in English emotion classification where “classifiers trained on human data perform poorly on AI‑generated texts” (Abstract, 4.2). Conversely, for Arabic personality classification, “augmenting training with AI‑generated data enhances performance” on human‑authored Arabic texts (Abstract, 4.2).  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper presents this cross‑linguistic discrepancy but does not investigate its root causes. The difference could stem from (a) the distinct nature of the tasks (emotion vs. personality), (b) varying degrees of linguistic complexity and resource scarcity between English and Arabic, or (c) fundamental differences in how LLMs encode affective states versus personality traits. The study design confounds language with task type, preventing isolation of the cause.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A controlled study that evaluates both emotion classification *and* personality classification within the *same* two languages (e.g., English and Arabic). This would decouple the effects of linguistic context from the expressive trait being modeled. Ablation studies comparing dataset size, dialectal variation, and morphological richness would clarify whether resource limitations or structural linguistic properties drive the observed pattern.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: To what extent can paraphrasing successfully obscure AI authorship in Arabic text, and what specific stylistic fingerprints remain robust to paraphrasing?  
- **Basis**: The paraphrasing experiment was conducted “only for English due to the limited size of the Arabic dataset and the lack of reliable paraphrasing tools for Arabic” (3.2.1). The results for English show a “sharp drop” in AI detection recall after paraphrasing (4.1), indicating reliance on superficial cues.  
- **Why unresolved**: The absence of a paraphrasing experiment for Arabic leaves a critical gap in understanding the robustness of AI detection methods for a key under‑resourced language. It is unknown whether the stylistic signatures detected in Arabic (e.g., via AraBERT) are equally superficial or encode deeper, more persistent patterns.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: First, develop or identify a high‑quality paraphrasing tool for Modern Standard Arabic. Then, replicate the English paraphrasing pipeline (using QuillBot or a suitable Arabic alternative) on the Arabic LLM‑generated personality dataset. Measuring the degradation in classifier F1‑score (and per‑class recall/precision) would quantify the durability of Arabic‑specific AI fingerprints. Complementary analysis (e.g., comparing LIWC‑style features pre/post‑paraphrase) could identify which cues survive.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What specific linguistic or psycholinguistic features (beyond readability) explain the inflated “Authenticity” scores in AI‑generated English text across emotion categories?  
- **Basis**: The LIWC analysis shows AI text exhibited “inflated authenticity scores… compared to human writing” across emotions (Abstract, 4.4). Table 14 shows many AI outputs scoring near 100 on “Authentic” (e.g., GPT‑4o on anger: 92.11; DeepSeek on disgust: 89.77), while human scores vary widely.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper reports this pattern but does not drill down into *which* LIWC sub‑features (e.g., first‑person pronouns, negation, certain function words) drive the high Authenticity dimension. “Authenticity” in LIWC is a composite metric; the specific lexical or syntactic choices that make AI text seem artificially “genuine” or “self‑reflective” are not identified. Without this, we cannot diagnose if the inflation stems from formulaic overuse of personal disclosure, avoidance of hedging, or other mechanisms.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Perform a fine‑grained ablation analysis on the LIWC output. Regress the “Authentic” score against all constituent LIWC categories (e.g., “I”, “we”, “articles”, “cognitive words”) for both human and AI texts. Identify which features are most strongly and uniquely associated with the AI‑authored samples’ inflated scores. This would reveal whether the effect is driven by a few exaggerated categories (e.g., excessive first‑person) or a diffuse shift across many features.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Are the observed differences in emotional tone (Tone LIWC dimension) between human and AI text (e.g., GPT‑4o assigning high positive Tone to negative emotions like anger) an artifact of the models’ training objectives or a limitation of the LIWC dictionary’s applicability to synthetic text?  
- **Basis**: The analysis notes that in negative emotions (anger, fear, sadness), “human texts consistently scored near zero, reflecting an appropriately negative emotional tone. In contrast, GPT‑4o and DeepSeek produced unexpectedly high Tone values” (4.4). This suggests a misalignment in affective valence signaling.  
- **Why unresolved**: It is unclear whether this misalignment is (a) a genuine failure of the LLM to produce affectively appropriate language, (b) a mismatch between the LIWC dictionary’s design for human text and the stylistic patterns of LLMs, or (c) a consequence of the prompting strategy (e.g., avoiding negative keywords). The paper does not probe whether the high Tone scores correspond to genuinely positive lexical choices or if the dictionary misinterprets neutral/formal LLM language.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct a qualitative error analysis. Manually inspect a sample of AI‑generated texts from emotions like “anger” that receive high Tone scores. Code the actual emotional valence of the words and phrases used. Simultaneously, audit the LIWC dictionary entries that contribute to the Tone score for these samples to see if LLM‑preferred synonyms or constructions are missing or misclassified. A human annotation study comparing perceived emotional valence vs. LIWC Tone would also validate the metric’s reliability on synthetic text.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: Do the simplified readability scores of AI‑generated text (e.g., lower Gunning Fog, Dale‑Chall) reflect a deliberate design choice in LLM training (e.g., preference for clarity/fluency) or an emergent property of their token prediction objective on web‑scale data?  
- **Basis**: The results show AI text is “consistently more readable” with “simpler vocabulary and shorter sentence structures” than human text across all models (4.4, Table 15). The authors note this suggests LLMs “prioritize accessibility and fluency at the expense of… complexity.” However, the *reason* for this systematic simplification is not explored.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper describes the *what* but not the *why*. Is the simplicity a byproduct of LLMs optimizing for next‑token prediction on predominantly simple, high‑frequency web text? Is it an implicit bias in RLHF or preference tuning towards helpful/inoffensive responses? Or is it a necessary trade‑off for maintaining coherence in longer generations? The study’s correlational design cannot distinguish these generative mechanisms.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Compare readability metrics of LLM outputs when prompted for different audiences (e.g., “write for a 5th grader” vs. “write for a PhD committee”). If simplicity is tunable, it may be a controllable surface feature. If it persists across prompts, it likely reflects a deeper training‑data bias. Additionally, analyzing the pretraining data’s readability distribution (e.g., via Common Crawl sampling) could correlate data source complexity with model output complexity. Controlled fine‑tuning experiments on corpora with varying readability levels could test causality.

## Limitations
- Missing generation parameters (e.g., top_p, max_tokens) beyond temperature = 1.0; defaults may vary across providers and affect output distribution.  
- Ambiguity in the exact AraBERT variant used risks tokenizer mismatches and non‑reproducible pretraining details.  
- Small Arabic sample size (100 per trait) introduces high variance; statistical significance not assessed.  
- Paraphrasing test limited to English and dependent on QuillBot’s undocumented settings, restricting generalizability.
