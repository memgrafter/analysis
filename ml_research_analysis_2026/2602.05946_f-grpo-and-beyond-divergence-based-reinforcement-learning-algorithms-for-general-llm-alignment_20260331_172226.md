# f-GRPO and Beyond: Divergence-Based Reinforcement Learning Algorithms for General LLM Alignment

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05946
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05946
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: The paper unifies preference alignment (PA) and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) under an f-divergence estimation framework, with f-GRPO achieving up to 98.5 Relative Overall score on OpenRS (vs. GRPO 90.5) and hybrid f-HAL achieving up to 66.1 Total Score on safety alignment (vs. GRPO 38.2).

## Executive Summary
Current LLM alignment methods fall into two distinct camps: preference alignment (PA) that uses chosen/rejected pairs, and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) that uses scalar rewards. This paper presents a unified perspective: alignment corresponds to minimizing an f-divergence between an aligned (high-reward/preferred) response distribution and an unaligned (low-reward/rejected) distribution. For RLVR, they derive f-GRPO, an on-policy algorithm that uses group-reward importance sampling to estimate the divergence, and f-HAL, a hybrid objective that combines f-GRPO with off-policy preference alignment. Theoretical results guarantee alignment consistency and monotonic reward improvement under weak assumptions about reward-density correspondence. Empirically, f-GRPO variants consistently beat GRPO on math reasoning benchmarks, while f-HAL (λ=0.5) mitigates reward hacking in safety alignment, outperforming DPO, KTO, BCO and on-policy baselines. The divergence estimation framework thus emerges as a universal principle for LLM alignment across both regimes.

## Method Summary
The core idea is to formulate alignment as minimizing an f-divergence \( D_f(P^+||P^-) \) between aligned (\(P^+\)) and unaligned (\(P^-\)) response distributions. For preference alignment, \(P^+\) and \(P^-\) are directly given by chosen and rejected pairs. For RLVR, these densities are constructed from the verifiable reward \(r(x,y)\) under a monotonicity assumption: \(c^+(r)\) is non-decreasing and \(c^-(r)\) non-increasing in reward. Using the variational representation of f-divergences, the authors derive practical algorithms. f-GRPO is an on-policy method that, for each prompt, samples \(G\) responses from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta^{old}}\), computes advantages \(a_i\) (standardized by group mean/std), estimates importance weights \(\hat{w}_i^\pm\) via a softmax over \(\pm r_i - \log \pi_{\theta^{old}}(y_i|x)\) truncated by sign of \(a_i\), and minimizes a loss (Eq. 15) that combines contributions from above- and below-average reward responses using a canonical link function \(g\). f-HAL hybridizes f-GRPO with an off-policy preference loss (FDO) via a mixture coefficient \(\lambda\): \(L_{\text{f-HAL}} = \lambda L_{\text{FDO}} + (1-\lambda) L_{\text{f-GRPO}}\). Training proceeds via fixed-point iterations: set old policy, sample, compute advantages and importance weights, evaluate losses, update policy with AdamW and a KL penalty \(\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta || \pi_{\text{ref}})\).

## Key Results
- **Math reasoning**: f-GRPO variants achieve Relative Overall scores up to 98.5 on the challenging OpenRS benchmark (vs. GRPO 90.5) and consistently outperform GRPO across LIMR, GSM8K, MATH500, AMC23, AIME24/25 for Qwen2.5-Math models.
- **Safety alignment**: Hybrid f-HAL with \(\lambda=0.5\) reduces attack success rate (ASR) and improves robustness, yielding Total scores up to 66.1 compared to GRPO’s 38.2, and surpasses DPO, KTO, BCO and pure on-policy methods.
- **Generalization**: The divergence framework successfully bridges PA and RLVR, enabling a single algorithmic family to handle both settings and demonstrating that f-divergences provide a universal lens for alignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Divergence Estimation as a Unifying Framework
- **Claim**: Alignment (both PA and RLVR) minimizes an f-divergence between aligned and unaligned response distributions.
- **Mechanism**: For PA, prior work shows DPO, KTO, BCO correspond to specific f-divergences. For RLVR, artificial densities \(c^+(r)\) and \(c^-(r)\) are built from reward under Assumption 4.2 (non-decreasing/non-increasing in \(r\)). Expectations over these densities are estimated via importance sampling from \(\pi_{\theta^{old}}\) using group-reward softmax normalization, yielding an on-policy divergence estimator (f-GRPO). Hybrid f-HAL interpolates between off-policy PA divergence and on-policy RLVR divergence.
- **Core assumption**: Weak reward-density correspondence (Assumption 4.2) – densities of aligned/unaligned responses are non‑decreasing/non‑decreasing in reward. Necessary to define artificial distributions.
- **Break condition**: Fails if verifiable reward \(r(x,y)\) is not monotonically related to alignment quality (e.g., reward hacking), causing artificial densities to misrepresent true aligned/unaligned distributions.

### Mechanism 2: f-GRPO as an On-Policy Divergence Estimator
- **Claim**: f-GRPO directly minimizes an f-divergence between reward‑aligned and unaligned distributions, yielding theoretical guarantees of monotonic improvement and alignment consistency, especially with canonical link functions \(g\) satisfying \(g^{-1}(f'_\infty)=\infty\).
- **Mechanism**: Starting from the variational representation of f-divergences, the expected divergence is expressed in terms of \(T = g(r_\theta)\). Importance sampling from \(\pi_{\theta^{old}}\) approximates expectations. For a group of \(G\) samples per prompt, compute standardized advantages \(a_i\) and truncated importance weights \(\hat{w}_i^\pm\) that emphasize above‑average (aligned) and below‑average (unaligned) rewards. The loss \(L_{\text{f‑GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_x \sum_i -\frac{a_i}{(1+\beta^{-1})G} \psi(r_{\theta,i}, a_i)\) (Eq. 15) is minimized, where \(\psi\) uses \(\hat{w}^\pm\) and \(g\). The fixed‑point iteration yields a policy that reweights responses by \(\exp(\beta^{-1} g^{-1}(f'_\infty) \mathbf{1}_{D^{-}=0})\), concentrating on above‑average reward responses. This leads to strict average reward improvement per iteration when \(g^{-1}(f'_\infty)=\infty\) and eventual convergence to the maximal‑reward policy if reachable.
- **Core assumption**: Assumption 4.2 plus canonical link \(g\) (Table 1) ensuring \(g^{-1}(f'_\infty)=\infty\) for strict improvement.
- **Break condition**: If \(g^{-1}(f'_\infty) < \infty\) (e.g., Total Variation), strict improvement fails; small group size \(G\) increases variance; large policy drift or heavy‑tailed rewards can destabilize importance sampling.

### Mechanism 3: f-HAL Hybrid Objective to Mitigate Reward Hacking
- **Claim**: f-HAL combines on‑policy RL (f‑GRPO) with off‑policy preference alignment (FDO) via \(\lambda\)-weighting, using both verifiable rewards and supervised preference data to mitigate reward hacking while maintaining performance.
- **Mechanism**: \(L_{\text{f‑HAL}} = \lambda L_{\text{FDO}} + (1-\lambda) L_{\text{f‑GRPO}}\). The mixture estimates a divergence between mixture measures \(M^+ = \lambda D^+ + (1-\lambda) c^+(x) D^+(r,\theta^{\text{old}})\) and similarly \(M^-\). The off‑policy term anchors the policy to high‑quality preference data, preventing collapse to reward‑hacked solutions. Alignment consistency (Eq. 21) shows the optimal policy upweights responses with higher mixture‑aligned density.
- **Core assumption**: Preference data accurately reflects intended alignment and rewards are not entirely misaligned; \(\lambda\) balances exploration and supervision.
- **Break condition**: If preference data is low‑quality or mismatched with rewards; if \(\lambda\) is too high (offline bias) or too low (insufficient mitigation); small group size makes on‑policy component noisy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: f-divergences and variational representation  
  **Why needed**: The entire framework represents alignment as minimizing an f-divergence and uses its variational form to derive algorithms (Lemma A.3). Understanding convex conjugates is essential.  
  **Quick check**: "Given an f-divergence \(D_f(P||Q)\) and its convex conjugate \(f^*\), what is the variational representation (supremum over functions \(T\)) for \(D_f(P||Q)\)?"

- **Concept**: On-policy policy gradient methods (e.g., GRPO/PPO)  
  **Why needed**: f-GRPO extends the standard GRPO objective by incorporating f-divergence terms. Knowing GRPO's advantage estimation and clipping explains the modifications.  
  **Quick check**: "In GRPO, how is the advantage \(A_{\pi_\theta}(x,y)\) computed from a group of samples? What is the purpose of clipping the probability ratio?"

- **Concept**: Importance sampling and off-policy correction  
  **Why needed**: f-GRPO uses importance sampling to estimate expectations over the (intractable) aligned/unaligned distributions using samples from \(\pi_{\theta^{old}}\). Derivation of truncated weights and softmax normalization (Eq. 12) relies on these techniques.  
  **Quick check**: "Why does f-GRPO use truncated importance weights \(\hat{w}_i^\pm = \hat{p}_\pm(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta^{old}}(y_i|x) \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\pm a_i>0}\)? What trade-off does this introduce?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**  
  Policy \(\pi_\theta\) (trainable) and reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (frozen) -> Reward source (verifiable \(r(x,y)\) or learned \(r_\phi\)) -> f-divergence module (stores \(f\), \(f^*\), link \(g\), computes \(\psi\)) -> Advantage estimator -> Importance weight calculator -> Loss aggregator (combines \(L_{\text{f‑GRPO}}\) and \(L_{\text{FDO}}\) with \(\lambda\)) -> Optimizer (AdamW, LR schedule, gradient accumulation).

- **Critical path**  
  1. Sampling: For each minibatch of prompts \(x_b\), generate \(G\) responses \(\{y_{b,i}\}\) from current policy \(\pi_{\theta^{\text{old}}}\).  
  2. Reward computation: Obtain rewards \(r_{b,i} = r(x_b, y_{b,i})\).  
  3. Advantage: For each prompt group, compute \(\mu_b = \text{mean}_i r_{b,i}\), \(\sigma_b = \text{std}_i r_{b,i}\), then \(a_{b,i} = (r_{b,i} - \mu_b)/\sigma_b\).  
  4. Implicit reward: Compute \(r_{\theta,b,i} = \beta \ln(\pi_\theta(y_{b,i}|x_b) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{b,i}|x_b))\).  
  5. Importance weights: For each group, compute \(s_i^+ = r_{b,i} - \ln \pi_{\theta^{old}}(y_{b,i}|x_b)\), \(s_i^- = -r_{b,i} - \ln \pi_{\theta^{old}}(y_{b,i}|x_b)\). Apply softmax to get \(\hat{p}^\pm\). Then \(\hat{w}_{b,i}^\pm = \hat{p}_{b,i}^\pm / \pi_{\theta^{old}}(y_{b,i}|x_b) \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\pm a_{b,i}>0}\).  
  6. On-policy loss: For each sample, compute \(\psi(r_{\theta,b,i}, a_{b,i})\) using Eq. 14. Then \(L_{\text{f‑GRPO}} = -\frac{1}{B G} \sum_{b,i} \frac{a_{b,i}}{1+\beta^{-1}} \psi\).  
  7. Off-policy loss (if any): Sample supervised pairs \((x, y_w, y_l)\) or \((x, y, \text{label})\). Compute \(L_{\text{FDO}}\) using \(g\) and \(f^*\).  
  8. Hybrid loss: \(L_{\text{total}} = \lambda L_{\text{FDO}} + (1-\lambda) L_{\text{f‑GRPO}}\).  
  9. Backpropagation and parameter update: \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \cdot \nabla L_{\text{total}}\) with gradient clipping.

- **Design tradeoffs**  
  - Choice of f-divergence: Different \(f\) (KL, JS, TV) yield different regularization; canonical links (Table 1) guarantee theoretical properties but may require tuning.  
  - \(\lambda\) in f-HAL: Balances on-policy exploration vs. off-policy supervision; \(\lambda=0.5\) often works best for safety, but optimal value may vary with model scale and task.  
  - Group size \(G\): Larger \(G\) reduces variance but increases memory/computation; typical \(G=4\) for small models.  
  - Truncation indicator \(\mathbf{1}_{\pm a_i>0}\): Reduces variance by ignoring low-advantage samples but introduces bias; alternative weighting schemes possible.  
  - KL coefficient \(\beta\): Balances alignment with staying close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\); typical \(\beta=0.1\).

- **Failure signatures**  
  - Sudden drop in reward/alignment metrics: Instability from high variance (small \(G\)) or learning rate issues; check gradient norms.  
  - Reward hacking (on-policy \(\lambda=0\)): Model exploits reward model; monitor reward vs. ASR on adversarial prompts; check output length/format.  
  - Divergence collapse (NaN): Numerical issues from extreme importance weights; use gradient clipping and stable log probs.  
  - Poor convergence: Verify implicit reward scaling; if all \(r_\theta\) very large, softmax saturates; adjust \(\beta\) or initialization.

- **First 3 experiments**  
  1. Unit test of f-GRPO gradient: On a tiny synthetic dataset (2 prompts, 2 responses each with known rewards), manually compute loss and gradient for a small network to confirm that increasing probability of above‑average reward responses decreases loss.  
  2. Reproduce GRPO baseline: Run GRPO on a simple RLVR task (e.g., Qwen2.5‑Math‑1.5B on GSM8K) with same hyperparameters (Table 7) to establish baseline and validate pipeline.  
  3. Test f-HAL components separately: Run pure FDO (\(\lambda=1\)) and pure f‑GRPO (\(\lambda=0\)) on a safety alignment dataset (e.g., compliance–refusal), then combine with \(\lambda=0.5\) and measure robustness (ASR, separation) to confirm hybrid improvement; also plot loss curves.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How robust are f‑GRPO and f‑HAL to violations of the weak reward‑density correspondence assumption (Assumption 4.2) in real‑world RLVR settings where reward signals may not monotonically reflect alignment quality?  
  *Basis*: Theoretical guarantees rely on Assumption 4.2; paper does not test sensitivity when reward and alignment are decoupled (e.g., reward hacking). *Resolution*: Controlled experiments with synthetic/modified benchmarks that vary monotonicity, measuring alignment metrics and reward improvement under f‑GRPO/f‑HAL vs. baselines.

- **Open Question 2**: For f‑divergences where the canonical link does not satisfy \(g^{-1}(f'_\infty) = \infty\) (e.g., Total Variation), what are the practical trade‑offs in alignment performance and convergence, and can new divergences be designed to better balance theoretical guarantees with empirical effectiveness?  
  *Basis*: Theorem 4.3 requires \(g^{-1}(f'_\infty)=\infty\) for strict improvement; paper notes some divergences have finite mixture scalers but does not analyze why non‑canonical choices still perform reasonably. *Resolution*: Extensive evaluation across tasks correlating performance with the \(g^{-1}(f'_\infty)\) property; develop novel f‑divergences tailored for alignment.

- **Open Question 3**: How should the hybrid weight \(\lambda\) in f-HAL be optimally set for different alignment regimes, model scales, and task types, and is there a theoretical principle (e.g., based on data distribution or reward model accuracy) to determine \(\lambda\) without exhaustive tuning?  
  *Basis*: f-HAL interpolates between FDO (\(\lambda=1\)) and f‑GRPO (\(\lambda=0\)); \(\lambda=0.5\) used in safety experiments but no method for setting it is proposed. *Resolution*: Ablation studies over \(\lambda\) for various model sizes and tasks, analyzing correlation with factors like reward model error; theoretical derivation of \(\lambda\) as a function of distribution divergences.

- **Open Question 4**: What are the computational efficiency and scalability limits of f‑GRPO's importance sampling with softmax corrections as model size and sampling budget \(G\) increase, and can approximations be made without sacrificing the theoretical benefits?  
  *Basis*: f‑GRPO uses group‑wise softmax normalization (Eq. 12); paper uses \(G=4\) but no analysis of complexity or scalability for larger models. *Resolution*: Scaling experiments with larger models (e.g., Llama‑3 70B) and varying \(G\), measuring time, memory, performance; investigate approximations (e.g., subset of samples, kernel methods).

## Limitations
- The theoretical guarantees (alignment consistency, monotonic improvement) rely on Assumption 4.2 (weak reward‑density correspondence), which may not hold under reward hacking or noisy rewards, potentially breaking the theory.
- Details of the safety reward model (dataset, training procedure) are not fully specified, hindering reproducibility of the preference alignment results.
- Evaluation metrics for robustness (e.g., DB separation, ASR on SALAD) have implementation ambiguities (PCA, attack sampling) that could lead to score discrepancies.
- Hyperparameters such as group size \(G\) and mixture weight \(\lambda\) are sparsely explored; sensitivity to these may affect generalizability across model scales and tasks.

## Confidence
- **Divergence unification framework**: Medium (solid theory but relies on assumptions that may not always hold)
- **f‑GRPO monotonic improvement**: Medium (guarantees require canonical link and valid density correspondence; empirical support needs independent verification)
- **f‑GRPO outperforms GRPO on math tasks**: Medium‑high (strong numbers but limited to specific benchmarks and model scales; replication needed)
- **f‑HAL mitigates reward hacking**: Medium (hybrid approach plausible but depends on preference data quality and λ tuning)
- **Generalization across tasks/scales**: Low (only 1.5B/7B models evaluated; larger models may behave differently)

## Next Checks
1. **Implementation unit test**: On a tiny synthetic dataset (two prompts, two responses with known rewards), manually compute the f‑GRPO loss and gradient. Verify that increasing the probability of above‑average‑reward responses strictly decreases the loss, confirming correct coding of Eq. 15 and importance weights.
2. **Assumption stress test**: Construct a controlled RLVR task where reward is anti‑correlated with true alignment (e.g., a hacked reward model). Run f‑GRPO; it should fail to improve alignment or degrade it, validating the necessity of Assumption 4.2.
3. **λ sweep on safety benchmarks**: Systematically vary \(\lambda \in \{0.0, 0.1, \dots, 1.0\}\) for f‑HAL on both small and large models, measuring ASR and utility. This identifies whether \(\lambda=0.5\) is universally optimal or task‑dependent, and quantifies robustness of the hybrid approach.
