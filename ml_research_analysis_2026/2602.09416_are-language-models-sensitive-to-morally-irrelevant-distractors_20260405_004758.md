# Are Language Models Sensitive to Morally Irrelevant Distractors?
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09416
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09416
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Morally irrelevant textual and visual distractors significantly shift LLM moral judgments, with negative affect decreasing moral action probability by up to 30% in MORALCHOICE and increasing disapproving verdicts by up to 9.5% in r/AITA.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) exhibit situationist moral biases—sensitivity to irrelevant contextual cues—similar to humans. Using a curated set of 60 affective distractors (textual narratives and images), the authors inject them into two established moral benchmarks: MORALCHOICE (action selection) and r/AITA (verdict prediction). Across multiple models, negative distractors reliably reduce the probability of moral actions and increase critical verdicts, while visual distractors show comparable effects. The findings challenge assumptions of stable moral preferences in LLMs and underscore the need for evaluation frameworks that account for contextual robustness.

## Method Summary
The authors curate 60 morally irrelevant emotional distractors (20 positive, 20 neutral, 20 negative) from the IDEST text and OASIS image datasets, adapting them to second-person narratives. These are prepended to prompts in two benchmarks: MORALCHOICE (forced-choice between two actions, with low/high ambiguity scenarios) and r/AITA (multi-label verdict prediction with reasoning). Models are queried under distractor and no-distractor conditions. For MORALCHOICE, output tokens are constrained, and marginal moral action probabilities (MMAP) are computed. For r/AITA, responses are parsed to extract verdicts and moral foundation scores via an off-the-shelf classifier. Shifts from baseline are aggregated, and statistical tests (paired tests, chi-square) assess significance.

## Key Results
- Negative textual distractors decreased moral action probability (MMAP) by up to 30% in low-ambiguity MORALCHOICE scenarios.
- Negative distractors increased the proportion of "Everyone Sucks Here" (ESH) verdicts by up to 9.5% in r/AITA.
- Effects were statistically significant (p<0.05) across most models; visual distractors produced similar magnitude shifts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not empirically establish a causal mechanism but speculates that LLMs may overweight negative affective cues due to training data imbalances (e.g., narratives where negative experiences excuse immoral actions). No internal model probing is conducted to validate this hypothesis or to distinguish between affective priming, instruction-following failures, or other pathways.

## Architecture Onboarding
Distractor Curation -> Benchmark Injection -> Model Inference -> Output Parsing -> Statistical Analysis
- **Critical path**: Prepare distractor-augmented prompts → run model inference (greedy or low-temperature) → extract probabilities/verdicts → aggregate per scenario/rule → test for significant shifts.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Token restriction (MORALCHOICE) ensures parseability but may constrain natural responses; off-the-shelf classifier (r/AITA) scales analysis but adds dependency.
- **Failure signatures**: Low compliance rates (refusals, malformed outputs); baseline distributions diverging from published values; visual distractor failures due to unsupported image formats.
- **First experiments**: (1) Reproduce baseline MMAP and verdict distributions; (2) Measure average MMAP shift per distractor valence; (3) Replicate visual distractor effect on Gemma-3-4B-IT.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: To what extent do morally irrelevant distractor effects generalize across model families, scales, and training objectives?
- Basis: [inferred] The paper's model ablations reveal inconsistent trends (e.g., Gemma-270M shows opposite effect) but are limited to four families and few sizes due to compute constraints.
- Why unresolved: It remains unknown whether distractor sensitivity is a universal property of LLMs or varies systematically with architecture, parameter count, or post-training.
- What evidence: Systematic evaluation of 15+ model families (including base, instruct, reasoning variants) spanning 100M to 100B parameters on both benchmarks; statistical modeling of distractor shift vs. model characteristics.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does sequential exposure to moral distractors in multi-turn interactions influence the cumulative stability and direction of LLM moral judgments?
- Basis: [inferred] The paper tests only single-turn distractor injection; real-world deployments involve extended dialogues where context may accumulate or habituate.
- Why unresolved: Interaction effects between distractor valence and conversational history are unstudied; could lead to non-linear drift or desensitization.
- What evidence: Longitudinal experiments simulating 5–10 turn conversations with controlled distractor introduction (persistent vs. varied); track per-turn changes in MMAP/verdict distributions and test for monotonicity.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can targeted alignment interventions (e.g., reasoning prompts, adversarial training) reliably mitigate distractor-induced biases without degrading helpfulness?
- Basis: [explicit] The paper shows reasoning partially reduces effects in low-ambiguity scenarios and calls for more robust alignment practices.
- Why unresolved: No comparative analysis of alignment methods under distractor stress; potential trade-offs with model utility are unexplored.
- What evidence: Fine-tune or prompt models with various alignment techniques using distractor-augmented datasets; measure mitigation (e.g., % MMAP shift reduction) and concurrent drop in task performance (e.g., helpfulness rating) across benchmarks.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Do distractor effects generalize across languages and cultural moral frameworks, or are they confined to English-centric, Western contexts?
- Basis: [inferred] Both benchmarks are English-only and reflect predominantly Western norms; moral psychology suggests cross-cultural variation in situationist biases.
- Why unresolved: Cultural differences in emotional processing and moral norms may moderate sensitivity; unknown if findings replicate globally.
- What evidence: Replicate experiments with professionally translated distractors and culturally specific moral dilemmas (e.g., from non-Western r/AITA analogues); compare effect sizes and statistical significance across language/culture cohorts.

### Open Question 5
- Question: What internal model mechanisms explain why negative distractors exert stronger influence on moral outputs than positive ones?
- Basis: [explicit] The paper speculates training data imbalances (e.g., negative experiences used to excuse immorality) but provides no empirical evidence.
- Why unresolved: No probing of representations or causal pathways; the hypothesized data-driven explanation remains untested.
- What evidence: Causal mediation analysis or activation patching to identify if negative affect pathways disproportionately suppress moral rule activations; correlate distractor valence with training data occurrence frequencies of excuse patterns.

## Limitations
- Distractor moral irrelevance is based on automated filtering from IDEST/OASIS without post-hoc human validation; subtle moral content may remain.
- Visual distractor results are limited to Gemma-3-4B-IT due to multimodal constraints; OASIS image selection criteria are insufficiently specified for exact replication.
- Prompt formatting and classifier (Zangari et al., 2025) details are approximate, risking implementation drift and affecting comparability.

## Confidence
- Existence of textual distractor effect: **High** (consistent p<0.05 across models, clear dataset specifications)
- Magnitude and generalizability of effect: **Medium** (unknown if holds for unseen scenarios or other benchmarks)
- Visual distractor effect parity: **Low** (only one multimodal model tested, image sourcing ambiguous)
- Proposed mechanism explanation: **Low** (speculative, no empirical validation)
