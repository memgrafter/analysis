# RelayGen: Intra-Generation Model Switching for Efficient Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06454
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06454
- **Reference count:** 18
- **Primary result:** RelayGen achieves up to 2.2× end-to-end speedup on math/science benchmarks while maintaining accuracy within 2% of the large model, using no training and fully composable design.

## Executive Summary
RelayGen introduces a novel approach to reduce the computational cost of large reasoning models (LRMs) by dynamically switching between a large and a small model during generation. Instead of relying on per-token routers or static allocation, it identifies discourse-level cues (e.g., "thus", "alternatively") that signal a transition to a lower-difficulty segment. During inference, the large model generates until it emits such a cue, at which point the small model takes over for the ensuing sentence or segment, resuming the large model only after the segment ends. This segment-level switching avoids the overhead of fine-grained routing and leverages the fact that many parts of a reasoning trace are easier to produce. Evaluated on AIME 2025, MATH500, and GPQA-Diamond, RelayGen attains pass@1 scores within 2% of the large model (e.g., 68.3% vs 70.0% on AIME with Qwen3-32B/1.7B) while substantially cutting latency. When combined with speculative decoding, it yields a 2.2× speedup, outperforming token-level (R2R) and step-level (Speculative Thinking) baselines. The method requires no additional training and is composable with existing decoding strategies.

## Method Summary
RelayGen operates in two phases. In an offline calibration phase, a held-out set of reasoning problems is used to compute token probability margins (the difference between the top-1 and top-2 token probabilities) for each generated token. Token sequences are then segmented by sentence boundaries, and the average margin after each sentence is analyzed. Discourse-level cues that frequently precede sentences with unusually high margins (i.e., lower uncertainty) are selected into a cue set. During online inference, the large model starts generating the solution. The generation controller monitors the output token-by-token; when the most recently generated token matches a cue from the set, the large model is paused, and the small model resumes generation from the same KV cache, continuing until a sentence boundary is detected. At that point, the controller may switch back to the large model, and the cycle repeats. The final answer segment (typically a short concluding phrase) is always generated by the small model, conditioned on the full reasoning KV cache. This coarse-grained, segment-level switching eliminates the need for per-token routing decisions and leverages the stability of the answer stage under model handoff. The whole pipeline does not require any training and can be combined with speculative decoding for further speedups.

## Key Results
- On AIME 2025, RelayGen (using Qwen3-32B as large and Qwen3-1.7B as small) achieves a pass@1 of 68.3% compared to 70.0% for the large model alone, while reducing average latency.
- Across MATH500 and GPQA-Diamond, RelayGen similarly stays within a 2% absolute accuracy drop relative to the large model.
- With speculative decoding, RelayGen delivers up to 2.2× end-to-end speedup over the large model baseline, surpassing prior token-level (R2R) and step-level (Speculative Thinking) approaches in the accuracy-efficiency trade-off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Segment-Level Switching Captures Difficulty Variation
RelayGen relies on the insight that reasoning traces exhibit intra-generation difficulty variation that can be aligned with model capacity at a coarse granularity. An offline calibration phase profiles token probability margins on a held-out set, identifying discourse-level cues (e.g., "thus", "alternatively") that are systematically followed by sentences with higher margins, indicating lower uncertainty. At runtime, the large model generates until it emits one of these cues; then the small model takes over for the subsequent segment (typically a sentence), resuming the large model after the segment ends. This segment-level control assumes that most of the reasoning steps after a cue are less demanding, so a smaller model suffices without jeopardizing overall correctness. Fine-grained per-token routers are avoided, reducing overhead and complexity. Evidence includes analyses showing post-sentence margin variation across cues (Section 3.2) and the cue selection procedure (Section 4.2). The mechanism fails if cues do not consistently precede easier segments for a target distribution, or if the small model’s capacity is insufficient even for these segments, causing cascading errors.

### Mechanism 2: Stable Answer Stage Under KV Cache Handoff
The final answer generation is highly robust to being delegated to the small model, provided the preceding reasoning trace’s KV cache is preserved. After the reasoning stage (delimited by a special marker or natural conclusion), the answer is usually a short, well-specified output (e.g., a number or choice) that depends primarily on the context already built. By transferring the KV cache from the large model to the small model, the small model can continue without recomputation and without needing high capacity to interpret the problem. This handoff is safe because the answer stage is less sensitive to minor variations in reasoning style and more dependent on the content of the reasoning trace. The mechanism is supported by experiments showing that small-model final answers (with KV cache) yield similar accuracy to full large-model generation. Potential break conditions include cases where the answer requires intricate reasoning beyond the small model’s capabilities even with context, or if the KV cache dimensions mismatch prevents direct transfer.

## Foundational Learning
- **Discourse-level cues in reasoning**
  - *Why needed:* They provide natural boundaries where the difficulty of the next segment likely changes, enabling dynamic capacity allocation without disrupting logical flow.
  - *Quick check:* Given a step-by-step solution, label at least two tokens that commonly introduce a new sub-argument or conclusion (e.g., "therefore", "however").

- **Token probability margin as difficulty proxy**
  - *Why needed:* Quantifies model uncertainty on a per-token basis; higher margins indicate easier next tokens and thus potentially easier subsequent sentences.
  - *Quick check:* For a probability distribution `[0.6, 0.3, 0.1]`, compute the margin (0.6-0.3=0.3) and interpret whether the next token is likely easy.

- **Segment-level dynamic model switching**
  - *Why needed:* Balances efficiency and correctness by aligning model size with local difficulty, while avoiding the high overhead of token-level routing.
  - *Quick check:* Compare the theoretical average switching overhead per token if switching every token versus every 10 tokens; estimate the saved compute.

- **KV cache preservation across model handoff**
  - *Why needed:* Maintains the attention state built by the large model, allowing the small model to seamlessly continue without recomputation and with full context.
  - *Quick check:* Explain how the KV cache from a transformer layer can be reused by a smaller model of the same architecture (e.g., same number of heads but smaller head dimension).

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map (arrow notation)
Offline: CalibrationSet → TokenMarginProfiler → CueSet  
Online: Input → LargeModel → CueMatcher → Switch → SmallModel → SegmentDetector → (loop back to LargeModel or end) → FinalAnswer (SmallModel)

### Critical Path
1. Offline calibration (one-time): compute token margins on a held-out set, select cue set.
2. For each new problem:
   a. Large model generates tokens until a cue from the set appears.
   b. Switch to small model (KV cache transferred), generate until a sentence boundary.
   c. Optionally switch back to large model and repeat from step a.
   d. When a special answer delimiter or problem end is detected, generate the final answer entirely with the small model.
The total latency is dominated by the large model steps, while switching overhead (including KV transfer and small model warm-up) is minimal.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Switching granularity:** Finer-grained (e.g., phrase-level) switching could offload more compute but increases routing overhead and risk of error propagation; RelayGen chooses sentence-level as a sweet spot.
- **Cue set size:** A larger cue set yields more offloading opportunities but may include false positives (cues that do not precede easy segments), harming accuracy.
- **Model size ratio:** A smaller offload model reduces latency per token but may have lower accuracy on delegated segments; the chosen pair (e.g., 32B → 1.7B) balances this.
- **Calibration effort:** More calibration data yields a more reliable cue set but incurs upfront cost; the method’s performance may suffer if the calibration domain mismatches deployment.

### Failure Signatures
- **Cascading errors:** The small model produces an incorrect intermediate step that propagates to the final answer, often without immediate detection.
- **Missed cues:** The large model continues generating in an easily delegable segment because the expected cue did not appear (e.g., paraphrased), leading to unnecessary compute use.
- **False cue triggers:** An unexpected token matching a cue appears in a difficult segment, causing premature handoff and likely small-model failure.
- **Boundary detection failures:** The segment detector fails to identify the end of a segment, causing the small model to run too long or the large model to be recalled prematurely.

### First Experiments
1. **Baseline replication:** Run RelayGen on AIME 2025, MATH500, and GPQA-Diamond with the same model pairs (Qwen3-32B/1.7B) to confirm the reported pass@1 and latency improvements.
2. **Ablation of cue selection:** Replace the learned cue set with random sentence-terminating tokens or a manually curated set (e.g., {"thus", "therefore", "alternatively"}) to quantify the impact on accuracy and offloading ratio.
3. **Sensitivity to offload model scale:** Test different small model sizes (e.g., 7B, 1.7B, 0.5B) while keeping the large model fixed, measuring the trade-off between speedup and accuracy drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly include an open questions section, but the following questions are implied by its limitations and scope:

- **Domain generalization:** Do the identified discourse cues and segment-level switching strategy transfer to non-math reasoning tasks (e.g., logical deduction, creative writing) and non-English languages?
- **Calibration robustness:** How sensitive is the cue set to the size and domain of the calibration set? Can the calibration overhead be reduced without sacrificing accuracy?
- **Error propagation analysis:** Under what conditions do errors in small-model delegated segments cascade to final answers, and can such errors be detected or corrected during generation?

## Limitations
- Evaluation is confined to math/science benchmarks; it is unclear if the identified discourse cues generalize to other reasoning domains (e.g., logical deduction, creative writing) or non-English languages.
- The cue set is derived from a specific calibration set; performance may degrade under domain shift or if the small model fails on a delegated segment despite a cue, leading to error propagation that is not analyzed.
- The offline calibration overhead (data size, compute) and its sensitivity to calibration set characteristics are not quantified.
