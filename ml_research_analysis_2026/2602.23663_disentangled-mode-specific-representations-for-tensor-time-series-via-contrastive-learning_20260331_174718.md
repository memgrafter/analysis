# Disentangled Mode-Specific Representations for Tensor Time Series via Contrastive Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23663
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23663
- **Reference count:** 39
- **Primary result:** Achieves state-of-the-art classification accuracies (Daily: 0.726, Realdisp: 0.766) and consistently reduces forecasting errors (e.g., US#1 MSE 0.752 vs. CoST 0.900) by disentangling mode-specific and mode-invariant features via tensor slicing and contrastive learning.

## Executive Summary
MoST introduces a framework for representation learning on multi-mode tensor time series (TTS) by explicitly disentangling features specific to each non-temporal mode (e.g., location, query) from mode-invariant temporal patterns. It uses tensor slicing to decompose the TTS into independent multivariate time series per mode, processes each slice with a shared causal convolutional encoder, and aggregates slice representations. A contrastive loss with two components—instance loss (ensuring consistency within a mode across augmentations) and mode loss (enforcing invariance across modes at the same timestamp)—learns both types of features. Without fine-tuning, frozen MoST representations fed to linear probes achieve superior performance on classification and forecasting benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The method processes a window of a 3rd-order TTS $\mathcal{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_2 \times w}$ by slicing along non-temporal modes: mode-1 slices $\{\mathcal{X}_{:,j}\}_{j=1}^{d_2}$ (each $\in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times w}$) and mode-2 slices $\{\mathcal{X}_{i,:}\}_{i=1}^{d_1}$ (each $\in \mathbb{R}^{d_2 \times w}$). Each slice is encoded independently by a shared feature encoder: linear projection, additive temporal embedding, stack of $L+1$ causal convolution blocks with exponentially increasing kernel sizes, and temporal average pooling. Mode-specific representations $\mathbf{V}^{(d_1)}, \mathbf{V}^{(d_2)}$ are obtained by pooling over all slices of that mode (average or max). These are concatenated into $\mathbf{V}$. For pretraining, two random crops of the same TTS window create augmented views. The instance loss $\mathcal{L}_I$ treats overlapping-timestamp representations from the same mode in the two views as positives and other samples’ representations as negatives. The mode loss $\mathcal{L}_M$ treats representations from different modes at the same timestamp in the same sample as positives and cross-mode representations from other samples as negatives. Total loss $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_I + \alpha(\mathcal{L}_M^{(d_1)} + \mathcal{L}_M^{(d_2)})$ is optimized end-to-end. Downstream tasks use frozen encoders to extract $\mathbf{V}$, followed by a linear classifier/regressor.

## Key Results
- **Classification:** MoST achieves 0.726 accuracy on Daily and 0.766 on Realdisp, outperforming all self-supervised (CoST, TS2Vec, TS-TCC, ATD) and end-to-end (Informer, LaST) baselines.
- **Forecasting:** Across nine datasets (US#1–4, World#1–3, KnowAir, NYC-CB) and multiple horizons, MoST consistently yields lower MSE and MAE. For example, on US#1 with a 2‑week forecast, MSE is 0.752 vs. 0.900 for CoST.
- **Ablation:** Both tensor slicing and both loss components (instance, mode) are essential; removing any significantly degrades performance in classification and forecasting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Tensor slicing for intra‑mode focus
The tensor slicing operation decomposes the TTS into independent slices per non‑temporal mode, reducing structural complexity and allowing the encoder to specialize in intra‑mode dependencies without cross‑mode interference. Sliced representations are aggregated to form mode‑specific features. This assumes intra‑mode dependencies dominate predictive signal, with cross‑mode interactions captured indirectly by mode‑invariant features. Ablation studies show slicing markedly outperforms random or cross‑mode slicing strategies. However, if cross‑mode interactions are strongly predictive and not recoverable from mode‑invariant features, slicing may lose critical information.

### Mechanism 2: Contrastive disentanglement via instance and mode losses
The contrastive framework uses two complementary objectives. The instance loss (within‑mode, across augmentations) encourages consistency of mode‑specific features under random cropping. The mode loss (across‑mode, same timestamp) encourages invariance to mode changes, capturing temporal patterns common across modes. This dual design assumes the defined positive pairs (overlapping timestamps within a mode; same timestamp across modes) correspond to true latent factors. Ablation confirms both losses contribute: instance loss more critical for forecasting, mode loss for classification. If positive pair definitions misalign with underlying factors (e.g., random cropping destroys mode‑specific patterns), representations may collapse or become less expressive.

### Mechanism 3: Hierarchical causal convolutional encoder
A stack of $L+1$ causal convolution blocks with kernel sizes $2^k$ aggregates multi‑scale temporal information without future leakage, enabling capture of long‑range dependencies. Average pooling across scales yields a robust temporal representation. This design assumes temporal patterns are locally stationary and can be modeled by fixed‑size kernels. Ablation shows replacing this encoder dramatically hurts performance (MSE +14.1%, Acc −28.1%). Limitations include a fixed receptive field; very long‑range or non‑stationary dependencies may exceed capacity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Tensor Time Series:** A multi‑way array with one mode as time and others representing distinct domains (e.g., location, query). *Why needed:* MoST’s slicing and disentanglement are defined on this structure. *Quick check:* For a TTS with modes (Location, Query, Time), a mode‑1 slice is a matrix (Location × Time) for a fixed query; a mode‑2 slice is (Query × Time) for a fixed location.
- **Contrastive Learning (InfoNCE):** A framework that pulls representations of positive pairs together and pushes negatives apart in embedding space. *Why needed:* MoST’s instance and mode losses are InfoNCE‑style objectives. *Quick check:* In instance loss, positives are same sample’s overlapping‑timestamp representations from two crops; negatives are other samples’ representations at the same timestamp.
- **Disentangled Representations:** Latent factors separated into independent, semantically meaningful subspaces. *Why needed:* MoST aims to separate mode‑specific (intra‑mode) from mode‑invariant (temporal) features. *Quick check:* Mode‑specific: patterns unique to a location (e.g., regional trends); mode‑invariant: shared weekly cycles across all locations.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input TTS window -> Tensor Slicing (mode‑1 and mode‑2 slice sets) -> Slice Feature Encoder (shared across slices and modes) -> Aggregator (pool over slices per mode) -> Concatenation $\mathbf{V} = [\mathbf{V}^{(d_1)}; \mathbf{V}^{(d_2)}]$ -> Contrastive Loss Computation ($\mathcal{L}_I$, $\mathcal{L}_M$).
**Critical path:** Forward: slicing → independent per‑slice encoding → mode aggregation → loss calculation. Backward: gradients from loss update all encoder parameters jointly.
**Design tradeoffs:** 
- Slicing reduces parameters and focuses on intra‑mode patterns but may miss cross‑mode interactions not captured by mode‑invariant features.
- Aggregator pooling: average (robust to noise) vs. max (highlights salient features); choice dataset‑dependent.
- Loss weight $\alpha$ balances instance vs. mode loss; too high may force invariance at expense of mode‑specificity.
- Causal conv depth $L$: larger $L$ increases receptive field but risks overfitting.
**Failure signatures:** 
- Poor disentanglement: t‑SNE of $\mathbf{V}^{(d_1)}$ and $\mathbf{V}^{(d_2)}$ shows mixed clusters (as in TS2Vec/CoST).
- Ablation failures: Removing tensor slicing (using random/CI/CD) degrades both classification and forecasting.
- Loss component removal: Removing instance loss hurts forecasting more; removing mode loss hurts classification more.
- End‑to‑end supervised training (MSE loss) performs poorly vs. contrastive pretraining.
**First 3 experiments:** 
1. Classification on Daily and Realdisp datasets, comparing against self‑supervised and end‑to‑end baselines.
2. Forecasting on nine TTS datasets (US#1–4, World#1–3, KnowAir, NYC‑CB) across multiple horizons.
3. Ablation study on tensor slicing strategies, temporal embedding removal, causal encoder replacement, and loss component removal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How well does MoST generalize to tensors with more than three modes and to diverse TTS domains beyond those evaluated?
- Do the disentangled representations actually capture distinct intra‑mode dependencies in real‑world TTS, or does the contrastive objective produce representations that are not semantically interpretable?
- How are cross‑mode interactions (between different non‑temporal modes) handled by MoST, and could explicit modeling of such interactions improve performance?
- What is the sensitivity of MoST to the loss weight $\alpha$, and can we develop a principled method to set it per dataset/task?

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter & implementation gaps:** Critical details (learning rate, batch size, loss temperature, window size per dataset, pooling strategy per task, exact temporal embedding design) are unspecified, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **External validation weakness:** Efficacy is supported primarily by internal ablations; low average FMR (0.538) and zero citations indicate limited independent validation of disentanglement claims in the broader literature.
- **Core assumption risks:** Assumes intra‑mode dependencies dominate and that positive pairs in contrastive loss correctly correspond to latent factors; if invalid, representations may be suboptimal.

## Confidence
- **Tensor Slicing (Mechanism 1):** Medium confidence. Strong internal ablation evidence, but reliance on indirect capture of cross‑mode interactions lacks external/theoretical validation.
- **Contrastive Disentanglement (Mechanism 2):** Medium‑low confidence. Ablation shows both losses contribute, but specific positive/negative pairing logic is not justified against alternatives; weak corpus support for the exact formulation.
- **Causal Encoder (Mechanism 3):** High confidence. Use of causal convolutions for long‑range modeling is standard; strong ablation result validates its necessity. Hierarchical kernel design is plausible but less precedented.

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter sensitivity sweep:** Vary $\alpha$, temperature, and window size $w$ on a key dataset (e.g., US#1). Measure impact on disentanglement quality (t‑SNE cluster separation metrics) and downstream MSE/Accuracy to identify stable regimes.
2. **Cross‑mode interaction stress test:** Construct synthetic or curated TTS where a specific cross‑mode interaction (e.g., Location × Query product) is the sole predictor. Evaluate if MoST’s mode‑invariant features recover this signal or if performance degrades compared to a non‑sliced joint model.
3. **Positive pair sanity check:** For instance loss, test alternative augmentations (time warping, masking). For mode loss, use hard negatives (samples with dissimilar temporal dynamics). Degradation would challenge the paper’s positive pair assumptions.
