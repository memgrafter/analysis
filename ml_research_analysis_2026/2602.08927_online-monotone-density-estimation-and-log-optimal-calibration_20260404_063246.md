# Online monotone density estimation and log-optimal calibration

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08927
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08927
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Both OG and EA achieve \(O(n^{1/3})\) expected cumulative excess KL-risk; EA further attains \(O(\sqrt{n \log n})\) pathwise regret relative to the best offline monotone estimator under minimal spacing conditions, enabling asymptotically log-optimal p-to-e calibration.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles online monotone density estimation: given a stream of observations from an unknown monotone density on \([0,1]\), an estimator must be produced sequentially while preserving monotonicity. Two algorithms are proposed: the Online Grenander (OG) estimator, which recomputes the constrained maximum likelihood monotone density (the Grenander estimator) at each step using all past data; and the Expert Aggregation (EA) estimator, which employs exponential weighting over a finite class of monotone histogram experts. In the stochastic well-specified setting, both achieve an expected cumulative log-likelihood gap (excess KL‑risk) of order \(O(n^{1/3})\) relative to the true monotone density. Moreover, EA obtains a pathwise regret bound of \(O(\sqrt{n \log n})\) compared to the best offline monotone estimator chosen in hindsight, under minimal spacing conditions on the data. As an application, constructing log‑optimal p‑to‑e calibrators for sequential hypothesis testing is reformulated as online monotone density estimation; applying OG or EA yields empirically adaptive calibrators that are asymptotically log‑optimal. Simulations show that EA typically achieves smaller finite‑sample regret and adapts more quickly to distribution shifts than OG.

## Method Summary
**Online Grenander (OG):** For each time \(t \ge 2\), OG computes the constrained Grenander estimator—the maximum likelihood monotone density over the class \(\mathcal{D}_{a,b}\) (densities bounded between \(a\) and \(b\) and non‑increasing)—based on the observations \(X_1, \dots, X_{t-1}\). At \(t=1\) it outputs the uniform density.

**Expert Aggregation (EA):** EA uses a pre‑specified finite set \(\mathcal{E}\) of monotone densities (histogram experts) constructed by discretizing \([0,1]\) into bins and assigning non‑increasing heights. At each step \(t\), the output \(\hat{f}_t\) is the exponentially weighted mixture of experts, with weights proportional to their accumulated likelihood up to time \(t-1\). After observing \(X_t\), each weight is multiplied by the expert’s density at \(X_t\), and weights are renormalized. The expert class is designed so that the offline Grenander estimator can be approximated arbitrarily well by some expert, with distortion that grows sublinearly in \(n\) when parameters are chosen appropriately (e.g., number of bins \(k \asymp \sqrt{n / \log n}\)).

**Calibration application:** In sequential testing, under the alternative, p‑values are i.i.d. from a distribution with monotone density \(q\). The log‑optimal p‑to‑e calibrator (maximizing expected log e‑value) is the KL‑projection of \(q\) onto the monotone density class. By treating the p‑values as observations and applying OG or EA to estimate this projection, one obtains an online calibrator. The resulting e‑process wealth ratio converges almost surely to the optimum, yielding asymptotic log‑optimality.

## Key Results
- **OG risk:** In the stochastic well‑specified setting, the expected cumulative excess KL‑risk of OG is bounded by \(C n^{1/3}\) for some constant \(C\) (Theorem 2.2).
- **EA regret:** For any sequence lying in the good set \(S_n(\beta,\gamma)\) (which holds with high probability under i.i.d. sampling from a monotone density), EA incurs pathwise regret at most \(C' \sqrt{n \log n}\) relative to the best offline monotone estimator in hindsight (Theorem 2.3). This implies the same \(O(n^{1/3})\) excess KL‑risk in the stochastic setting.
- **Calibration optimality:** The online estimators adapted to p‑values produce e‑processes whose normalized log‑wealth converges almost surely to that of the log‑optimal calibrator (Theorem 3.1). Simulations confirm EA’s superior finite‑sample adaptivity, especially after distribution shifts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: OG recomputation
OG outputs at each \(t\) the constrained Grenander estimator based on \(X_1,\dots,X_{t-1}\). The offline constrained Grenander estimator from \(m\) samples achieves an excess KL‑risk of order \(O(m^{-2/3})\) under the assumption that the true density is monotone and bounded away from 0 and \(\infty\) (Lemma 2.1). Summing over \(t\) gives \(\sum_{t=1}^n O((t-1)^{-2/3}) = O(n^{1/3})\), which yields the claimed cumulative risk bound (Theorem 2.2). The mechanism relies directly on the offline optimality of the Grenander estimator and does not require any discounting of past data.

### Mechanism 2: Expert aggregation
EA employs exponential weighting over a carefully constructed finite class \(\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}\) of monotone histogram experts. The class includes histograms with at most \(k\) bins, breakpoints on a regular grid of spacing \(\Delta_b = n^{-(\beta+1)}\), and log‑heights separated by at least \(V/k\) (where \(V = \log(b/a)\)). This design ensures that the offline Grenander estimator can be approximated by some expert with total cumulative loss distortion bounded by \(O(n/k + n\Delta_b + k)\) (Lemmas 2.5 and 2.6). Exponential weighting then guarantees that the predictor’s cumulative log‑loss is at most that of the best expert plus the log‑size of the class. Choosing \(k \asymp \sqrt{n / \log n}\) and a suitable \(\beta > 0\) balances the terms, yielding a pathwise regret of \(O(\sqrt{n \log n})\) (Theorem 2.3). The analysis assumes the observed sequence lies in the good set \(S_n(\beta,\gamma)\), i.e., points are well‑separated (minimum spacing \(n^{-\beta}\)) and away from the right boundary 1 (no point closer than \(n^{-\gamma}\) to 1, with \(\gamma < \beta-1/2\)). Under i.i.d. sampling from a monotone density, this holds with high probability for appropriate \(\beta,\gamma\) (Lemma B.2).

### Mechanism 3: Reduction to log‑optimal calibration
Log‑optimal p‑to‑e calibration is equivalent to estimating the monotone density of p‑values under the alternative. The optimal calibrator at each time, projecting the true density onto monotone densities, coincides with the density estimate produced by the online monotone density estimator. By substituting OG or EA, the cumulative log‑wealth difference relative to the optimum converges to zero almost surely (Theorem 3.1), establishing asymptotic log‑optimality.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
- **OG:** \(X_1,\dots,X_{t-1} \rightarrow\) constrained isotonic regression (Grenander) \(\rightarrow \hat{f}_t\)  
- **EA:** Expert set \(\mathcal{E}\) with weights \(w_{t-1} \rightarrow\) weighted mixture \(\hat{f}_t\); then \(X_t\) used to update \(w_{t-1}(f) \leftarrow w_{t-1}(f) f(X_t)\) and renormalize to obtain \(w_t\)

**Critical path:**
1. For OG: At each \(t \ge 2\), solve a constrained isotonic regression problem to find the Grenander MLE from \(X_1,\dots,X_{t-1}\). This can be implemented via pooled adjacent violators (PAVA) with bound constraints or a convex solver (e.g., CVXPY).  
2. For EA: Precompute a finite expert set (or approximate it). Initialize uniform weights. At each step, compute the current mixture by summing weighted expert densities; then update weights multiplicatively after observing \(X_t\). Use log‑weights for numerical stability.  
3. For calibration: Apply OG or EA to p‑values to obtain monotone density estimates \(h_t\); e‑values are \(h_t(P_t)\); the e‑process is the product \(M_t = \prod_{s=1}^t h_s(P_s)\).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- OG: Simpler to implement but computationally heavy: each step requires solving an MLE from scratch, leading to \(O(t)\) per step and \(O(n^2)\) total time. It does not discount older data, so it may adapt slowly to distribution shifts.
- EA: Better adaptivity and often lower regret, especially after changes. However, the nominal expert class is combinatorially large; practical approximations (coarser grids, limited bins) are needed. The regret bound depends on spacing conditions; heavily clustered data may degrade performance.
- Parameter choices: Bounds \(a,b\) must contain the true density; conservative choices increase robustness but may slow convergence. For EA, parameters \(k\) (max bins), \(\beta\) (grid fineness), and \(\gamma\) (boundary margin) affect the trade‑off between approximation error and computational cost; theory suggests \(k \sim \sqrt{n/\log n}\) and \(\beta>2\).

**Failure signatures:**
- OG: The constrained MLE solver fails to produce a valid monotone density (e.g., negative values, violation of monotonicity) due to numerical issues; also, if the true density is not monotone, the estimator is inconsistent.
- EA: The precomputed expert set does not include a good approximation of the truth (grid too coarse). Weight updates may suffer from underflow/overflow when many experts become very unlikely; using log‑weights is essential. The spacing condition (2.6) may be violated if data cluster excessively, leading to larger regret.
- Both: Under extreme misspecification (e.g., true density far from monotone), the algorithms cannot achieve small regret and may exhibit linear cumulative loss.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Well‑specified density estimation:** Generate \(n=1000\) i.i.d. samples from a known monotone density (e.g., linear \(q(u)=5/4 - u/2\) on \([0,1]\)). Run OG and EA (with appropriate parameters) and compute the cumulative excess KL‑risk relative to the true density. Compare to the offline Grenander. Expect EA to have smaller regret, especially in later steps.
2. **Distribution shift:** Change the monotone density at time \(\tau=200\) (e.g., switch from linear with slope 1 to a flatter slope). Repeat the experiment and observe that EA adapts more quickly, showing a significant reduction in cumulative regret after the change, while OG lags.
3. **Calibration performance:** Under the alternative, generate p‑values from a monotone density \(q\) (e.g., \(q(p)=2(1-p)\)). Use OG and EA as calibrators to produce e‑values and form the e‑process \(M_n = \prod_{t=1}^n h_t(P_t)\). Compute \((1/n)\log M_n\) and compare to the theoretical optimum \(\int_0^1 q(u) \log q(u)\,du\). Verify convergence to the optimum as \(n\) grows. Also, under the null (\(P_t \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\)), check that the e‑process remains a valid e‑process (e.g., \(\mathbb{P}(\sup_n M_n \ge 1/\alpha) \le \alpha\)).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do the online monotone density estimators (OG and EA) perform under non‑iid alternatives, and do they maintain asymptotic log‑optimality for p‑to‑e calibration in such settings?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state in the Discussion: “A promising direction for future work is to analyze the behavior of these empirically adaptive calibrators under non‑iid alternatives, which would further enhance their applicability in practical sequential testing settings.”
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical guarantees are derived under the assumption that observations are i.i.d. from a monotone density. Under dependence or non‑stationarity, the validity and optimality of the calibrators remain unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop risk and regret bounds for classes of dependent data (e.g., mixing processes, Markov chains) and prove asymptotic log‑optimality under appropriate alternatives. Complement with simulations under realistic non‑iid scenarios (e.g., concept drift, dependent p‑values).

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the minimax optimal pathwise regret rate for online monotone density estimation in the adversarial setting?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper establishes an \(O(\sqrt{n \log n})\) regret bound for EA (Theorem 2.3) but does not provide a lower bound. The optimality of this rate is therefore unknown.
- **Why unresolved:** Without a matching lower bound, it is unclear whether the \(O(\sqrt{n \log n})\) rate is the best achievable or if improved algorithms exist.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Prove a minimax lower bound (e.g., via reduction to online learning or entropy arguments) that matches the upper bound up to constants, or construct an algorithm with a strictly smaller worst‑case regret.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are the spacing conditions (the “good set” \(S_n(\beta,\gamma)\)) necessary for the \(O(\sqrt{n \log n})\) pathwise regret bound of EA, or can they be relaxed?
- **Basis in paper:** Theorem 2.3 explicitly assumes the observed sequence lies in \(S_n(\beta,\gamma)\), which enforces minimum separation \(n^{-\beta}\) and a margin from the boundary 1. The necessity of these conditions is not addressed.
- **Why unresolved:** Sequences with arbitrarily close points or points near 1 are excluded; such sequences could potentially degrade performance, but it is unknown whether the conditions are fundamental.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Either prove the regret bound without these conditions (e.g., via a different discretization or martingale techniques) or construct an adversarial sequence outside \(S_n(\beta,\gamma)\) that forces any monotone density estimator to suffer at least \(\Omega(\sqrt{n \log n})\) regret, demonstrating that the conditions are necessary.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why does EA empirically achieve smaller finite‑sample regret and adapt more quickly to distribution shifts compared to OG?
- **Basis in paper:** Simulations consistently show EA outperforming OG in both well‑specified and mis‑specified settings, particularly after a change point. The asymptotic analysis, however, yields the same \(O(n^{1/3})\) rate for both in the stochastic case and does not address finite‑sample differences or adaptation speed.
- **Why unresolved:** The reason for EA’s practical advantages is not explained by the current theory, making it difficult to predict when such advantages will occur.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive non‑asymptotic high‑probability bounds that expose differences in constants or dependence on data distribution (e.g., spacing, density variation). Analyze dynamic regret under drift or change‑point models to formally quantify adaptation speed.

## Limitations
- Theoretical risk and regret bounds rely on strong assumptions: the true density must be monotone and strictly bounded away from 0 and \(\infty\); for EA, the observed sequence must satisfy the good‑set spacing condition. Violations may degrade the rates or invalidate the guarantees.
- The paper does not specify crucial details for replication, such as the exact construction of the expert set used in simulations, precise choices of parameters (\(a,b\)), tie‑handling procedures, or the number of replications. Our reproduction must make ad‑hoc approximations that may not match the reported results.
- Computational complexity: OG requires recomputing the constrained MLE at every step, leading to quadratic time; EA’s nominal expert class is combinatorially huge, so practical implementations rely on heuristics not covered by the theory, which could affect performance and guarantees.

## Confidence
- **OG \(O(n^{1/3})\) excess KL‑risk:** High – the bound directly follows from the well‑known offline Grenander risk (Lemma 2.1) and a summation.
- **EA \(O(\sqrt{n \log n})\) pathwise regret:** Medium – the proof in Section 2.2 appears sound but depends on the feasibility of the expert approximation and the good‑set assumption; the bound is not known to be tight.
- **Calibration asymptotic log‑optimality:** Medium – reduces to consistency of the density estimators (Theorem 3.1) under the stated assumptions; however, the reduction to real‑world p‑value streams may introduce complications.
