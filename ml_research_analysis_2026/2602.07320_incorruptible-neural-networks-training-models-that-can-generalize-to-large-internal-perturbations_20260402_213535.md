# Incorruptible Neural Networks: Training Models that can Generalize to Large Internal Perturbations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07320  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07320  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Dynamic perturbation schedules (quadratic ramping) dramatically improve robustness of neural networks to large internal weight noise; RWP dominates under high‑noise RRAM, SAM under low‑noise SONOS.

## Executive Summary
Training neural networks robust to random internal weight perturbations is essential for analog hardware deployments, where device noise can corrupt model parameters. This paper investigates two robustness‑enhancing methods: Sharpness‑Aware Minimization (SAM) and Random Weight Perturbation (RWP), and introduces dynamic schedules that increase perturbation strength during training. Theoretical analysis using PAC‑Bayes bounds shows that over‑regularizing with a training noise variance larger than the test variance ($\sigma_{\text{train}} > \sigma_{\text{test}}$) yields tighter generalization guarantees. Empirically, SAM outperforms RWP for small perturbations but suffers from a flatness‑induced vanishing gradient effect at large radii, causing accuracy to collapse. RWP is more stable under large noise. Quadratic perturbation schedules mitigate the vanishing gradient problem and boost performance for both methods. Analog hardware simulations (RRAM and SONOS) confirm the trends: RWP excels in high‑noise RRAM, while SAM leads in low‑noise SONOS, with dynamic schedules providing further gains.

## Method Summary
Experiments use ResNet‑18 on CIFAR‑100, Tiny‑ImageNet, and ImageNet‑100 with standard SGD (cosine LR decay, weight decay $5{\times}10^{-4}$, momentum $0.9$, label smoothing $0.1$).  
- **SAM**: For each minibatch, a perturbation $\varepsilon = \rho \cdot \nabla L / \|\nabla L\|$ is added to weights; the loss at $w+\varepsilon$ is backpropagated.  
- **RWP**: Gaussian noise $\varepsilon \sim \calN(0, \max(|w|)^2 \sigma_{\text{train}}^2 I)$ is sampled per minibatch and added before loss evaluation.  

Both methods are evaluated under constant perturbation strengths and dynamic schedules (linear or quadratic) that multiply the nominal strength by a factor $s(t)$ ramping from $0$ to $1$ over $T^*$ iterations. Test accuracy is reported under applied Gaussian weight noise ($\sigma_{\text{test}}$). Early stopping uses the maximum perturbed validation accuracy. Analog hardware noise is simulated with CrossSim for RRAM and SONOS devices.

## Key Results
- Over‑regularization ($\sigma_{\text{train}} > \sigma_{\text{test}}$) yields tighter generalization bounds and improves noisy test accuracy, especially for RWP at large $\sigma_{\text{test}}$.
- SAM achieves higher accuracy than RWP for small perturbations (e.g., $79.12\%{\pm}0.28$ at $\sigma_{\text{test}}=0.02$ on CIFAR‑100) but degrades sharply at larger $\rho$ ($53.61\%{\pm}2.61$ at $\sigma_{\text{test}}=0.07$) due to vanishing gradients.
- Quadratic schedules mitigate vanishing gradients and boost robustness: RWP reaches $65.27\%{\pm}2.05$ and SAM $60.24\%{\pm}1.98$ at $\sigma_{\text{test}}=0.07$ on CIFAR‑100. In analog sim, RWP outperforms SAM for high‑noise RRAM ($73.96\%$ vs $68.42\%$), while SAM leads for low‑noise SONOS ($80.28\%$ vs $78.69\%$).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
PAC‑Bayes bounds link expected sharpness (average loss difference between original and perturbed weights) to generalization under perturbations. Over‑regularization ($\sigma_{\text{train}} > \sigma_{\text{test}}$) tightens the bound because the posterior variance term benefits from larger training perturbations. Empirically, RWP’s random noise explores the loss landscape uniformly, encouraging flat minima robust across a wide perturbation range. SAM’s adversarial perturbations target the steepest ascent direction; for small $\rho$ this finds flat regions, but for large $\rho$ it pushes weights into overly flat areas where gradients vanish, harming optimization. Dynamic schedules gradually increase perturbation strength, allowing the model to first converge to a good basin before exploring wider perturbations, thus balancing stability and ultimate robustness.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Sharpness‑Aware Minimization (SAM)**  
   *Why needed*: Explicitly minimizes worst‑case loss within a neighborhood, promoting flat minima correlated with generalization and robustness.  
   *Quick check*: Does SAM improve test accuracy under small Gaussian weight noise compared to SGD? Yes.
2. **Random Weight Perturbation (RWP)**  
   *Why needed*: Trains the network to operate under random weight corruptions, mimicking analog hardware device noise.  
   *Quick check*: Does injecting Gaussian noise during training improve robustness to the same noise at test time? Yes.
3. **Over‑regularization ($\sigma_{\text{train}} > \sigma_{\text{test}}$)**  
   *Why needed*: Using larger training noise than expected test noise yields tighter PAC‑Bayes generalization bounds for robustness.  
   *Quick check*: For fixed $\sigma_{\text{test}}$, does increasing $\sigma_{\text{train}}$ beyond $\sigma_{\text{test}}$ eventually improve noisy test accuracy? Yes, up to a point.
4. **Dynamic Perturbation Schedules**  
   *Why needed*: Gradually increase perturbation strength to avoid early optimization issues (e.g., vanishing gradients) while still achieving high robustness.  
   *Quick check*: Does a quadratic ramp ($s(t) = (t/T^*)^2$) yield higher final noisy test accuracy than constant training with the same maximum strength? Yes.
5. **Analog Hardware Noise Simulation (CrossSim)**  
   *Why needed*: Validates whether trends observed with synthetic Gaussian noise transfer to realistic non‑ideal device behaviors (RRAM, SONOS).  
   *Quick check*: Do RWP vs SAM performance patterns hold in CrossSim simulations? Partially (RWP better for RRAM, SAM for SONOS).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Input Data → DataLoader (augmentations) → Model (ResNet‑18) → Loss (CrossEntropy) → Perturbation Module (RWP: add Gaussian; SAM: compute adversarial $\varepsilon$) → Gradient → Parameter Update (SGD) → Evaluation (with $\sigma_{\text{test}}$)

**Critical Path**  
Per iteration: load minibatch → augment → forward → compute loss → generate perturbation (schedule‑modulated) → compute perturbed loss → backpropagate gradient → optimizer step. Validation/test: apply fixed $\sigma_{\text{test}}$ noise to weights and measure accuracy.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- SAM vs RWP: SAM effective for small perturbations but vulnerable to vanishing gradients at large $\rho$; RWP stable under large noise but may underperform for small noise.  
- Constant vs dynamic schedules: constant large perturbations risk early optimization failure; dynamic ramping introduces hyperparameters (ramp length, max strength) but yields better robustness.  
- Robustness vs clean accuracy: over‑regularization and large perturbations often degrade noiseless accuracy, creating a Pareto trade‑off.  
- Hyperparameter sensitivity: performance heavily depends on $\rho$, $\sigma_{\text{max}}$, and schedule; careful tuning required.

**Failure Signatures**  
- SAM with large constant $\rho$: gradient norm plummets near zero within first epochs and stays low, causing stagnant loss and random‑guess accuracy.  
- RWP with excessive $\sigma_{\text{train}}$: noisy training loss becomes volatile or diverges; final model generalizes poorly.  
- Mis‑configured schedule (e.g., too slow ramp): final perturbation strength not reached, yielding suboptimal robustness.  
- Over‑regularization without tuning: clean accuracy drops significantly (>5% absolute); noisy accuracy may not compensate.

**First Experiments**  
1. Baseline: train ResNet‑18 on CIFAR‑100 with standard SGD (no perturbations) to confirm $\approx79.3\%$ noiseless accuracy.  
2. RWP schedule comparison: constant $\sigma_{\text{train}}=0.05$ vs quadratic schedule ramping to $\sigma_{\text{max}}=0.1$ over 100 epochs; evaluate noisy accuracy at $\sigma_{\text{test}}=0.07$ to verify dynamic schedule benefit.  
3. SAM gradient monitoring: train with constant $\rho=0.5$, log average gradient norm per epoch; expect early decline. Repeat with quadratic schedule (ramp $\rho$ to $0.5$) and check if gradient norm remains healthy and final accuracy increases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. For what classes of test‑time noise distributions (beyond isotropic Gaussian) does training with over‑regularized noise ($\sigma_{\text{train}} > \sigma_{\text{test}}$) fail to improve generalization robustness compared to matching the test distribution?  
2. What precise loss landscape properties (e.g., curvature, Hessian spectrum) determine the threshold where SAM’s performance degrades relative to RWP for large perturbation magnitudes, and can these properties be efficiently estimated during training to select the optimal method without exhaustive hyperparameter search?  
3. Can dynamic perturbation schedules be automated or adapted online during training to optimally balance optimization stability and noise robustness, eliminating the need for manual schedule design and hyperparameter tuning?  
4. How well do the findings on synthetic Gaussian weight noise transfer to actual analog hardware with complex, non‑stationary noise profiles (e.g., device drift, cycle‑to‑cycle variation, programming errors), and can training methods be made inherently robust to such hardware‑specific non‑idealities?  
5. Is there a fundamental, quantifiable trade‑off between achieving low unperturbed training loss and high perturbed test robustness that is inherent to the optimization process, and can this trade‑off be theoretically characterized to guide objective design?

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameter grids for dynamic schedules (optimal $\rho_{\text{max}}$, $\sigma_{\text{max}}$, $T^*$) are not fully disclosed, hindering precise replication and sensitivity analysis.  
- CrossSim analog simulation configuration (device models, array dimensions, peripheral circuitry) remains unspecified, making hardware validation difficult.  
- It is unclear whether the validation noise strength ($\sigma_{\text{val}}$) exactly matches the test noise ($\sigma_{\text{test}}$) for early stopping; this assumption could affect reported results.
