# Revisiting Label Inference Attacks in Vertical Federated Learning: Why They Are Vulnerable and How to Defend
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18680
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18680
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Advancing the VFL cut layer forward by three layers reduces label inference attack accuracy by up to 70% while improving main task accuracy by up to 2.5%, with zero computational overhead.

## Executive Summary
This paper re-evaluates the vulnerability of vertical federated learning (VFL) to embedding-based label inference attacks (LIAs). It challenges the fundamental assumption that well-trained bottom (passive party) models inherently learn to represent output labels. Through theoretical analysis using mutual information and the data processing inequality, the authors prove that label representation increases monotonically with layer depth, meaning shallow bottom models primarily extract features while the top (active party) model handles label mapping. Empirical task reassignment experiments, which deliberately break the natural alignment between input features and output labels, cause LIA performance to collapse dramatically while leaving main task accuracy intact. This demonstrates that LIA success relies on incidental feature-label alignment, not learned label representation. The proposed defense—advancing the cut layer to make bottom models shallower—exploits this mechanism. By reducing the mutual information between bottom model embeddings and the true labels, this zero-overhead change severely degrades LIA accuracy, often while simultaneously improving main task performance due to the strengthened top model.

## Method Summary
The research combines theoretical proof with extensive empirical validation across five image datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, CINIC-10, SVHN, GTSRB). Theoretically, VFL is modeled as a series of lumped Markov chains, and the data processing inequality is applied to prove that mutual information between any intermediate layer's output and the label increases with layer depth. Empirically, two main experiments are conducted: (1) **Task Reassignment:** Original classification tasks are replaced with new tasks (e.g., predicting digit parity instead of identity on MNIST) that disrupt the natural statistical alignment between raw features and labels while preserving the training protocol. LIA performance (cluster-based and model completion attacks) is measured before and after. (2) **Cut Layer Advancement Defense:** For a fixed total model depth, the boundary between passive (bottom) and active (top) models is moved forward by 1–3 layers. The mutual information of the new bottom model output with the label is measured, and both LIA accuracy and main task accuracy (MTA) are evaluated. The defense's synergy with other techniques like differential privacy and gradient clipping is also tested.

## Key Results
- The mutual information between a layer's output and the label increases monotonically with depth in VFL networks, confirming that label mapping is concentrated in deeper layers and the top model.
- Task reassignment—which breaks natural feature-label alignment—causes LIA accuracy to drop sharply (often to near random), while main task accuracy remains largely unaffected across all five datasets.
- Advancing the cut layer forward by three layers reduces LIA attack accuracy by up to 70% and frequently *improves* main task accuracy by up to 2.5%, demonstrating a zero-overhead, utility-enhancing defense.
- The cut-layer defense synergizes with existing defenses (e.g., DP, gradient clipping), providing compounded protection without additional computational cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*Claim:* In VFL, the mutual information between a layer's output and the labels increases monotonically with layer depth.
*Mechanism:* The feedforward process is modeled as a lumped Markov chain (Xᵢ → Tᵢ₁ → ... → Tᵢₙ → S₁ → ... → Sₘ → Y). The data processing inequality (DPI) guarantees I(Xᵢ;Y) ≤ I(Tᵢ₁;Y) ≤ ... ≤ I(Tᵢₙ;Y) ≤ I(S₁;Y) ≤ ... ≤ I(Sₘ;Y). Shallow bottom layers propagate primarily feature information (low MI with Y), while deeper layers and the top model progressively encode label-relevant information.
*Break condition:* Violations may occur if the training process is non-ergodic or gradients exhibit extreme pathological dependence (e.g., explosion/vanishing), breaking the Markov property.

### Mechanism 2
*Claim:* The success of existing embedding-based LIAs is not due to bottom models intrinsically learning label mappings, but due to a natural, dataset-dependent alignment between feature distributions and label distributions.
*Mechanism:* When features and labels are naturally aligned (e.g., MNIST digit images), even a feature-focused bottom model produces embeddings that are coincidentally clustered by label, enabling LIA. Task reassignment (e.g., re-labeling MNIST by digit parity) deliberately breaks this alignment while preserving VFL training dynamics. The resulting collapse in LIA accuracy demonstrates that attack efficacy depends on this external alignment, not on learned label representation.
*Break condition:* If a bottom model is explicitly regularized (e.g., via MI maximization) to learn label representations, it might develop robust label-mapping capability even without natural feature-label alignment, potentially restoring LIA effectiveness.

### Mechanism 3
*Claim:* Advancing the cut layer (increasing the proportion of the top model) is a zero-overhead defense because it reduces the label representation capability (MI with labels) of the embeddings sent by passive parties.
*Mechanism:* Moving the cut layer forward reduces the number of layers in the bottom model. Per Mechanism 1, shallower bottom models have lower mutual information with labels. The active party's top model (which handles label mapping) is deepened, compensating for any utility loss and often improving MTA due to its stronger label representation capacity ("model compensation").
*Break condition:* If the bottom model is already very shallow, further advancement may excessively limit its feature extraction capacity, harming the main task. The trade-off with increased vulnerability to feature inference attacks from the active party remains unquantified.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Mutual Information (MI)**
  *Why needed:* Core metric quantifying the "label representation capability" of intermediate layer outputs. The paper's theoretical and empirical arguments hinge on measuring I(Layer Output; Label).
  *Quick check:* Given I(X;Y)=0, X and Y are statistically independent. A higher I(T;Y) for a layer output T means T retains more information about Y, making label inference from T more feasible.

- **Concept: Markov Chain & Data Processing Inequality (DPI)**
  *Why needed:* The theoretical proof (Section III.C) models the feedforward pass as a Markov chain. DPI (I(A;B) ≥ I(f(A);B) for deterministic f) establishes that deeper layers cannot have less information about the label than shallower ones.
  *Quick check:* In the chain X → A → B → Y, which inequality is guaranteed by DPI? (c) Both (a) I(X;Y) ≤ I(A;Y) and (b) I(A;Y) ≤ I(B;Y).

- **Concept: Cut Layer in VFL**
  *Why needed:* It defines the boundary between bottom (passive) and top (active) models. The defense mechanism directly manipulates its position. Understanding what information (embeddings) crosses this boundary is critical.
  *Quick check:* In aggVFL, passive parties transmit local model embeddings (zᵢ) to the active party at the cut layer. During backpropagation, the active party sends gradient vectors ∇zkᵢ L (gradients w.r.t. received embeddings) to each passive party.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Passive Party (features Xᵢ, bottom model fᵢ) -> Cut Layer (embedding zᵢ) -> Active Party (aggregation agg, top model g) -> Prediction ŷ. Backward: Active sends ∇zk L to Passives; Passives compute ∇θ L and update.

- **Critical path:**
  1. Sample alignment (global sample IDs).
  2. Forward: Each Passive computes zᵢ = fᵢ(Xᵢ) truncated at cut layer. Active aggregates z = agg({zᵢ}), computes ŷ = g(z), loss L(ŷ, y).
  3. Backward: Active computes ∇φL, ∇zkᵢL, sends ∇zkᵢL to Passives. Passives compute ∇θᵢL = ∇zkᵢL · J(zᵢ; θᵢ) and update θᵢ.
  4. Iterate to convergence.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Cut Layer Depth:** Deeper fᵢ (cut later) may extract more features but increases MI(zᵢ;Y), raising LIA risk. Shallower fᵢ (cut earlier) reduces LIA risk and may improve MTA via a stronger top model, but risks underpowered feature extraction.
  - **Aggregation Method:** Concatenation is common; simpler aggregation may reduce leakage but harm utility.
  - **Model Symmetry:** Paper assumes symmetric passive architectures; heterogeneous bottom models would alter MI dynamics.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Task Reassignment Failure:** If LIA accuracy remains high after task reassignment, the bottom model may have learned robust label features, contradicting the paper's claim. Check MI(fᵢ(Xᵢ); Y_new).
  - **MTA Drop on Cut Advance:** If moving the cut layer forward causes significant MTA degradation, the top model's "compensation" is insufficient—the bottom model was already too shallow.
  - **No MI Gradient:** If MI between fᵢ outputs and Y does not increase with depth, the Markov chain assumption may be violated (e.g., due to skip connections).

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce MI vs. Layer Depth:** For your model/dataset, compute MI between each layer's output and labels (using an estimator like [36]). Verify monotonic increase and the MI decrease at the new cut layer after advancement.
  2. **Task Reassignment LIA Test:** Retrain VFL on new tasks (e.g., MNIST even/odd, CIFAR-10 coarse classes). Compare lift-adjusted LIA accuracy between original and new tasks; expect a significant drop.
  3. **Cut Layer Advancement Defense:** For a baseline 2-party VFL, systematically move the cut layer 1, 2, 3 layers earlier (keeping total depth constant). Record LIA attack accuracy and MTA; expect attack accuracy to drop and MTA to potentially rise.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How effective are gradient-based label inference attacks in multi-party VFL beyond early training phases?** The paper only briefly reports low accuracy for gradient-based LIAs without analyzing dynamics across epochs or party counts.
- **What is the optimal cut layer position that maximizes defense against LIAs while minimizing vulnerability to feature leakage attacks?** The trade-off between LIA defense and feature leakage risk is noted but not quantified.
- **Do the observed mutual information trends and "model compensation" hold for modern architectures like transformers or graph neural networks in VFL?** Experiments are limited to MLP, CNN, AlexNet, ResNet, and LeNet; generalizability to state-of-the-art architectures is unknown.

## Limitations
- Exact model architectures (layer counts, widths) per dataset are not specified, hindering faithful replication.
- Mutual information estimation details (e.g., binning method, sample size) from cited source [36] are not provided.
- Precise class remappings for the three new tasks per dataset (Appendix A) are missing.
- Theoretical analysis assumes a lumped Markov chain; skip connections, batch normalization, or pathological gradients could violate monotonic MI increase.
- Focus is on embedding-based LIAs; the defense might shift vulnerability to feature inference attacks from the active party.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (MI increases with layer depth):** High for theoretical proof (DPI); Medium for empirical generality due to potential architecture-specific factors (skip connections).
- **Mechanism 2 (LIA success depends on feature-label alignment):** High – consistent LIA collapse across five datasets after task reassignment, with stable MTA.
- **Mechanism 3 (Cut-layer advancement as zero-overhead defense):** Medium-High – strong empirical results (LIA ↓ ≤70%, MTA ↑ ≤2.5%), but may fail if bottom model becomes too shallow or for highly heterogeneous architectures.
