# Widespread Gender and Pronoun Bias in Moral Judgments Across LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13636  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13636  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** LLMs show consistent, statistically significant biases in moral fairness judgments: favoring non-binary subjects over women over men, and favoring third-person over first-person over second-person, with plural subjects reducing fairness perception across nine models.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how grammatical person, number, and gender markers bias large language models' binary fairness judgments on morally ambiguous scenarios. Using a counterfactual dataset of 14,850 sentences derived from 550 ETHICS Justice examples, the authors evaluate nine models from six families. Results reveal robust, model-agnostic patterns:singular and third-person sentences are more often judged fair, second-person constructions are penalized, and a clear gender hierarchy emerges where non-binary subjects are consistently favored and male subjects disfavored. These biases persist across model architectures and scales, suggesting deep-seated distributional and alignment effects in training data.

## Method Summary
The methodology centers on a carefully constructed counterfactual evaluation framework. Starting from 550 balanced "fair"/"unfair" sentences from the ETHICS Justice subset, the authors generate 27 semantic variants per base sentence by systematically varying grammatical person (1st, 2nd, 3rd), number (singular, plural), and explicit gender/group markers (e.g., "as a man," "as a group of women and non-binary people"). This process combines automated rewrite generation (using LLaMA 3.3), manual auditing, and disambiguation fixes to ensure grammatical correctness and semantic equivalence. Nine models are then queried in a zero-shot, deterministic (temperature=0.0) setting with a constrained prompt requiring a single-digit "0" or "1" output. Variants of the same base sentence are batched together to avoid sequential context bias. Primary metrics are per-variant accuracy and Statistical Parity Difference (SPD) between variant groups, with significance assessed via two-proportion z-tests.

## Key Results
- A consistent **gender bias gradient** emerges: non-binary subjects are judged fairer than women, who are judged fairer than men (NB > W > M) across all models, with effect sizes up to ~20% SPD.
- A clear **person/number hierarchy** is observed: third-person > first-person > second-person, and singular > plural. Second-person plural male subjects face the strongest fairness penalty (SPD down ~22.9% in Grok 4).
- All main effects are **statistically significant** (p < 0.05) across models, with high inter-model correlation in SPD rankings, indicating a shared underlying bias pattern.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Non-binary Favoring via Distributional Bias
- **Claim:** Non-binary subject markers trigger systematically higher "fair" classifications due to distributional bias in pre-training data.
- **Mechanism:** Training corpora overrepresent non-binary references in contexts discussing equality, inclusion, or social justice. During moral judgment tasks, co-occurrence patterns between "non-binary" and fairness-related language activate more positive evaluation pathways in the model's internal representations.
- **Break condition:** If counterfactual variants with non-binary markers show no fairness advantage when tested on models trained on corpora where non-binary references are randomly distributed (not correlated with justice discourse).

### Mechanism 2: Second-Person Penalty via Alignment Heuristics
- **Claim:** Second-person pronouns incur a fairness penalty due to alignment training that associates "you" with prescriptive or confrontational contexts.
- **Mechanism:** In instruction-tuned and safety-aligned models, second-person constructions ("you should/should not") are frequently used in toxic, corrective, or rule-enforcement contexts during RLHF. Models learn a heuristic that directly addressing the subject signals potential harm, leading to stricter moral judgments.
- **Break condition:** If ablation of RLHF/safety fine-tuning eliminates the SPD(2*→3*) penalty while preserving other effects, indicating alignment-specific origin.

### Mechanism 3: Singular Advantage via Narrative Salience
- **Claim:** Singular subjects are judged fairer than plurals due to a salience bias toward individual moral agents in training narratives.
- **Mechanism:** Moral exemplars and dyadic scenarios (common in pre-training) typically describe singular actors ("he/she/I") rather than group actions. This leads to a base-rate effect where models more readily map singular subjects onto familiar moral templates, increasing consistency with learned "fair" patterns.
- **Break condition:** If training on a balanced corpus of individual vs. group moral scenarios reverses the effect (plurals judged fairer), showing dependency on base-rate distribution.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Counterfactual bias evaluation**  
  *Why needed:* To isolate the effect of demographic/linguistic markers from content, requiring generation of semantically equivalent sentence variants.  
  *Quick check:* How would you design a variant set to test whether an effect is due to the marker itself versus residual semantic changes?
- **Concept: Statistical Parity Difference (SPD)**  
  *Why needed:* Quantifies fairness disparity between groups as the difference in positive outcome rates; used to measure bias across 27 variant groups.  
  *Quick check:* What does SPD(V₁→V₂) = 0.2 imply about the model's treatment of the two variant groups?
- **Concept: Prompt engineering for controlled inference**  
  *Why needed:* Ensures models output only binary labels without explanation, minimizing format-related variance and focusing judgment on sentence content.  
  *Quick check:* Why is temperature=0.0 used, and what risk does a non-zero temperature introduce in this bias measurement?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Base sentence selection (ETHICS Justice, 550 items) → Variant generation (LLaMA 3.3 rewrite → manual audit → disambiguation → marker injection) → Inference engine (API calls to 9 models with fixed system/user prompts, temperature=0.0, batching by base) → Post-processing (label extraction, error rate, SPD calculation, significance testing).
- **Critical path:** Prepare full 14,850-sentence counterfactual dataset → Batch inference on all 9 models → Compute per-variant accuracy → Aggregate by person/number/gender groups → Calculate SPD pairwise → Test statistical significance.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Automated vs. manual variant generation (speed vs. correctness); wildcard grouping (*S*, *P*) aggregates power but masks within-group heterogeneity; temperature=0.0 ensures determinism but may not reflect typical use.
- **Failure signatures:** High error rates on "unfair" base labels (model softening); model-specific anomalies (e.g., DeepSeek 1.5B near-zero gender SPD); low inter-model Spearman correlation; ungrammatical variants breaking counterfactual assumption.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate base accuracy on original 550 sentences for all models.  
  2. Compute SPD(3S-NB → 3S-M) for GPT-4o and LLaMA 70B; expect significant negative values.  
  3. Swap gender markers (e.g., "non-binary" → "person") and measure SPD(*NB→*N); should collapse toward zero.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization beyond ETHICS:** Do bias patterns hold for other moral foundations (harm, autonomy) or different topic distributions? The study uses only the Justice split; broader coverage is needed.
- **Cross-linguistic validity:** How do biases manifest in languages with different grammatical systems (gendered vs. genderless, pronoun-drop)? The dataset is English-only.
- **Causal mechanisms:** What specific training data properties (co-occurrence stats, alignment data) cause the favoring of non-binary subjects and penalization of second-person? The paper conjectures but does not test.
- **Prompt dependency:** Are biases robust to different prompt formats or output constraints (e.g., chain-of-thought)? The fixed prompt may interact with linguistic variants.
- **Model factors:** Which architectural or alignment choices (scale, SFT, RLHF) explain variance in bias strength and exceptions (e.g., LLaMA 70B's opposite number effect)?

## Limitations
- The full 550-sentence dataset and precise disambiguation rules are not public, hindering exact replication.
- Grok 4 Fast Reasoning is inaccessible, preventing direct model comparison.
- Inference parameters (beyond temperature) are unspecified, risking output variability.
- Mechanisms remain speculative; no ablation evidence links observed biases to specific training stages.

## Confidence
- **Gender bias gradient (NB > W > M):** High – consistent across nine models with statistical significance, though dataset dependency.
- **Person/number hierarchy (3rd > 1st > 2nd; singular > plural):** Medium – robust empirical pattern, but mechanisms not definitively isolated.
- **Proposed mechanisms (distributional, alignment, salience):** Low – speculative; no causal tests provided.
