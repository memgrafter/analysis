# A Contrastive Variational AutoEncoder for NSCLC Survival Prediction with Missing Modalities

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.17402
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17402
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: MCVAE achieves mean C-index of 0.651±0.026 on TCGA-LUAD and 0.575±0.033 on TCGA-LUSC, outperforming MUSE and M3Care (which report ~0.580 and ~0.540 respectively), and maintains robust performance under simulated missingness up to 90%.

## Executive Summary
Accurate survival prediction for non‑small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients often requires integrating multimodal data (histopathology, transcriptomics, DNA methylation, clinical). However, real‑world datasets suffer from missing modalities, which many existing methods cannot handle robustly. The authors propose the Multimodal Contrastive Variational AutoEncoder (MCVAE), a model that explicitly accounts for missing data through variational uncertainty modeling, adaptive fusion with learnable gating, and a multi‑task objective combining Cox survival loss, reconstruction, and cross‑modal contrastive learning, all trained with stochastic modality masking. Evaluated on TCGA‑LUAD (n=475) and LUSC (n=446), MCVAE significantly improves concordance index over state‑of‑the‑art approaches (MUSE, M3Care) and remains stable even when up to 90% of non‑clinical modalities are missing. Notably, the study also finds that adding modalities does not universally enhance performance over clinical‑only models, underscoring the need for careful multimodal integration.

## Method Summary
MCVAE employs a separate variational encoder for each modality (clinical, transcriptomics, DNA methylation, histopathology). Each encoder outputs parameters (μ, σ²) of a Gaussian distribution; sampling via reparameterization (z = μ + σ⊙ε) yields a latent embedding that captures both signal and uncertainty (higher σ indicates lower confidence). Missing modalities are represented by a zero vector. The modality‑specific embeddings are fused through an adaptive bottleneck: learnable scalar gates γ_k, passed through a sigmoid, produce weights σ(γ_k) that scale the embeddings; the weighted sum is normalized by the number of available modalities C and transformed by a two‑layer residual feed‑forward network to produce the joint representation z_fused. A survival head (linear layer) predicts hazard from z_fused. Separate decoders reconstruct each observed modality (excluding clinical) to preserve information, while an InfoNCE contrastive loss aligns representations of the same patient across different modalities and separates those of different patients.

The model is trained with a composite loss comprising Cox partial log‑likelihood (L_task), Mean‑Squared‑Error reconstruction losses (L_recon) for available modalities, KL divergences (L_KL) to a standard normal prior for each modality (each weighted by a learnable parameter), and the contrastive loss (L_contrast). Losses are balanced using uncertainty weighting (learnable scaling factors). KL regularization is annealed from 0 to a maximum β to avoid posterior collapse. Critically, stochastic modality dropout is applied: during training each non‑clinical modality is independently masked with probability p_drop (e.g., 0.3), simulating incomplete data and encouraging robustness. Optimization uses AdamW with early stopping on validation C‑index; results are averaged over 5‑fold cross‑validation and multiple random seeds.

## Key Results
- MCVAE achieves mean C‑index of 0.651±0.026 on LUAD and 0.575±0.033 on LUSC, outperforming MUSE and M3Care (which obtain scores around 0.580 and 0.540 respectively).
- Robustness: Under simulated missingness up to 90%, MCVAE maintains stable or even improved performance (e.g., on LUAD at 90% missingness, C‑index 0.673±0.029).
- Surprising finding: Adding modalities (especially histopathology) often degrades performance compared to using clinical features alone, indicating that multimodal integration is not universally beneficial.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Variational Encoding for Uncertainty Quantification
- **Claim**: Modality-specific variational encoders model uncertainty in each data source’s representation, allowing the model to handle missing modalities by representing confidence.
- **Mechanism**: Each encoder outputs mean µ and variance σ² vectors; sampling via reparameterization z = µ + σ ⊙ ε creates latent variables where higher σ indicates lower confidence. Missing modalities are represented as zero vectors.
- **Core assumption**: Posterior variance reliably reflects modality reliability; missing modalities contribute no information through zero embeddings.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "modality-specific variational encoders to model uncertainty"  
  - [section III.B.1] "Variational Encoding with Uncertainty Quantification: For each available modality k, we learn a latent representation z(k) using a variational encoder... larger posterior variances indicate lower confidence"
- **Break condition**: If variance estimates are miscalibrated (e.g., due to poor encoder training), the fusion may overweight unreliable modalities or underweight available ones, especially under severe missingness. Assumption: KL divergence regularization prevents posterior collapse but may over‑regularize if β annealing is misconfigured.

### Mechanism 2: Adaptive Fusion with Availability‑Aware Gating
- **Claim**: The fusion bottleneck with learned gating normalizes contributions from present modalities, adapting to arbitrary missingness without imputation.
- **Mechanism**: Gating parameters γ_k scaled by σ(·) produce modality weights; available embeddings are aggregated, normalized by the count of available modalities C, and transformed via a fusion network h(·) to produce z_fused.
- **Core assumption**: Learnable gates capture relative modality importance, and normalization ensures consistent scaling regardless of how many modalities are present.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "adaptive fusion bottleneck with learned gating to weight available modalities"  
  - [section III.B.2] "Adaptive Fusion with Availability‑Aware Gating: Modalities for patient i are aggregated... where γ_k are learnable modality‑specific gate parameters that capture the relative importance"
- **Break condition**: If gating weights saturate (e.g., all near 0 or 1) due to improper initialization or loss imbalance, the fusion may ignore informative modalities or overemphasize noise. Assumption: The fusion network h(·) must be sufficiently expressive; shallow networks may limit adaptive capacity.

### Mechanism 3: Multi‑Task Objective with Cross‑Modal Contrastive Loss
- **Claim**: The composite loss (survival, reconstruction, KL, contrastive) enforces cross‑modal alignment and regularizes representations, maintaining robustness under missingness.
- **Mechanism**: L_task (Cox partial likelihood) drives survival prediction; L_recon reconstructs observed modalities to preserve information; L_KL regularizes latent space; L_contrast (InfoNCE) pulls same‑patient embeddings across modalities together and pushes different patients apart.
- **Core assumption**: Contrastive loss aligns modalities even when some are missing during training, and reconstruction acts as a consistency regularizer without imputing missing data.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "multi‑task objective combining Cox survival loss, reconstruction, and cross‑modal contrastive loss"  
  - [section III.B.3] "Multi‑Task Learning Objective: MCV AE is trained as a multi‑task learning objective with four loss components... Contrastive Cross‑Modal Alignment… encourages representations from different modalities of the same patient to be similar"
- **Break condition**: If loss weighting (via uncertainty coefficients σ_i) is unstable, one component (e.g., contrastive) may dominate, degrading survival accuracy. Assumption: Stochastic masking must cover diverse missingness patterns; inadequate masking may not generalize to extreme missingness.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  1. Modality‑specific variational encoders (one per modality: clinical, transcriptomics, WSI, methylation).  
  2. Fusion module: gating mechanism (γ_k), normalization by available count, fusion network h(·).  
  3. Decoders per modality for reconstruction (clinical excluded).  
  4. Survival head (function f_θ) for hazard prediction.  
  5. Loss components: Cox loss, reconstruction MSE, KL divergence, InfoNCE contrastive.  
  6. Stochastic modality dropout (retain clinical always).

- **Critical path**:  
  - Training: Input batch → apply modality dropout (Eq. 11) → encode available modalities to (µ, σ) → sample z(k) → fuse to z_fused (Eq. 4) → compute L_task, L_recon, L_KL, L_contrast → weight via uncertainty (Eq. 10) → backpropagate.  
  - Inference: Input patient data → encode available modalities → fuse to z_fused → predict survival via f_θ(z_fused).

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Variational uncertainty modeling adds computational overhead and requires β‑annealing to avoid posterior collapse, but improves robustness.  
  - Contrastive loss enhances cross‑modal alignment but increases batch size needs and risks gradient conflict with survival loss.  
  - Reconstruction loss regularizes without imputing missing data, avoiding imputation bias, but provides no estimates for missing modalities.  
  - Gating mechanism adapts to missingness but introduces extra parameters; improper weighting may lead to modality collapse.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Performance plateaus or degrades with >90% missingness, as all models approach unimodal clinical baseline (Fig. 4).  
  - Modality collapse: WSI integration often reduces C‑index (Tables I & II), indicating conflicting gradients or weak signal.  
  - High variance across CV folds for MUSE and M3Care (Fig. 2), suggesting instability in graph‑based or imputation approaches.  
  - If contrastive loss weighting is too high, survival C‑index drops due to misaligned optimization objectives.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Survival Prediction**: Compare MCVAE vs. MUSE and M3Care on TCGA‑LUAD/LUSC using 5‑fold CV; MCVAE achieves higher mean C‑index with lower variance.  
  2. **Modality Combination Analysis**: Ablate all modality subsets including clinical; find that adding modalities (especially WSI) often degrades performance vs. unimodal clinical.  
  3. **Modality Dropout Analysis**: Train with p_drop ∈ {0.0,0.1,…,0.9}; MCVAE improves with higher dropout on LUAD (e.g., 0.673±0.029 at p_drop=0.9 vs. 0.658±0.036), indicating robustness from stochastic masking.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does MCVAE generalize to other cancer types and prospective clinical datasets?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "Validation across diverse cancer types, and, critically, prospective clinical datasets, is essential for studying clinical applicability."
- **Why unresolved**: The study only evaluates TCGA‑LUAD and TCGA‑LUSC, which may have specific missingness patterns and biological characteristics not representative of broader oncology populations.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Testing MCVAE on multiple external cancer cohorts (e.g., from different institutions or countries) and in a prospective clinical trial setting would demonstrate generalizability and clinical utility.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Would systematic unimodal benchmarking alter the conclusion that multimodal integration is not universally beneficial?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "Our evaluation compared MCV AE with two methods… and excluded unimodal baselines for histopathology, transcriptomics, and methylation… A broader comparison against additional state‑of‑the‑art multimodal methods and systematic unimodal benchmarking would provide a more comprehensive evaluation."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper shows unimodal clinical features often outperform multimodal combinations, but without unimodal benchmarks for WSI, transcriptomics, and methylation individually, it is unclear if those modalities have weak signals or if the fusion mechanism suppresses them.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Evaluating unimodal foundation models for each modality (e.g., UNI for histopathology) on the same survival task, with identical train‑test splits, would quantify each modality’s standalone predictive power.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How do extreme dropout rates during training affect the latent space structure and uncertainty calibration?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "The benefit of extreme dropout rates deserves deeper investigation. Analyzing learned representations, gate weights, and cross‑modal patterns can clarify how extensive masking and reconstruction promote robust feature learning."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper shows performance improvements at high dropout (e.g., 90%) for LUAD, but the mechanisms—whether the model learns truly modality‑agnostic features or over‑relies on clinical anchors—are not analyzed.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Performing representational similarity analysis (e.g., CCA, Procrustes) between latents from models trained with varying dropout rates, and measuring uncertainty calibration (e.g., reliability diagrams) for predictions under missingness.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Would integrating a probabilistic imputation component improve robustness under severe missingness compared to reconstruction‑only regularization?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "The reconstruction component of MCV AE… does not actually impute missing modalities… Future work could explore controlled imputation strategies that quantify uncertainty in generated modalities."
- **Why unresolved**: MCVAE avoids imputation to prevent bias, but this limits its ability to estimate missing data, which could be clinically valuable. It is unclear whether a hybrid approach (reconstruction + uncertainty‑aware imputation) would maintain robustness while providing interpretable missing‑data estimates.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Augmenting MCVAE with a modality generation head (e.g., conditional VAE) and comparing survival prediction, imputation accuracy (on held‑out modalities), and uncertainty estimates against the original MCVAE under varying missingness rates.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: Can probing with cross‑modal attention mechanisms reveal clinically interpretable cross‑modal relationships in MCVAE?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "We consider applying probing strategies via cross‑attention transformers, generalized to multiple modalities."
- **Why unresolved**: The contrastive loss aligns modalities but does not expose which features from each modality contribute to survival predictions, limiting clinical trust and biological insight.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Adding a cross‑attention layer between modality embeddings before fusion, then analyzing attention weights (e.g., linking attention patterns to known biomarkers or histopathology regions) and correlating them with survival subgroups.

## Limitations
- Reproduction is impeded by missing architectural details (e.g., gating parameter shapes, fusion network width, contrastive temperature, KL annealing schedule, uncertainty weighting optimization) and incomplete preprocessing specifications (exact pathway gene sets, methylation feature algorithms, WSI patch sampling).
- Evaluation is limited to TCGA cohorts (LUAD/LUSC) with moderate sample sizes (~500 per cancer); no external validation raises concerns about overfitting and generalizability.
- The finding that multimodal integration often underperforms unimodal clinical features challenges the assumed universal benefit of multimodal data and requires further investigation into when and how to integrate additional modalities.
