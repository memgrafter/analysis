# An Empirical Study on Preference Tuning Generalization and Diversity Under Domain Shift
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05882
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05882
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** The choice of adaptation strategy (e.g., target SFT, pseudo-labeling) matters more than the preference tuning objective itself for generalization under domain shift; pseudo-labeling achieves high target win rates but collapses diversity, while online RL (GRPO) preserves diversity at a slight performance cost.

## Executive Summary
This paper systematically studies how different preference tuning methods (DPO, KTO, ORPO, PPO, GRPO) behave when the target domain differs from the source preference data. Across summarization and QA tasks, it finds that the adaptation strategy—specifically whether and how the model is exposed to target-domain data before or during alignment—has a larger impact on target performance than the choice of alignment objective. Pseudo-labeling, which uses a strong teacher model to generate synthetic preferred responses for the target domain, dramatically improves target win rates (up to 83.37%) but causes severe diversity collapse, reducing semantic variety to near zero. In contrast, online RL methods like GRPO maintain output diversity and show stable cross-domain performance. For QA helpfulness, domain shift effects are minimal. A core trade-off emerges between reliability (high target win rate) and diversity, with mixed-domain SFT offering a balanced compromise. The study also highlights data efficiency (10% of pseudo-labeled data suffices) and the critical role of adaptation order (target-first SFT aids transfer).

## Method Summary
The study evaluates five preference objectives (DPO, KTO, ORPO, PPO, GRPO) under domain shift between a source preference dataset and a target-domain unlabeled corpus. Base models (Llama-3.1-8B, OLMo-3-7B) undergo adaptation via various SFT strategies (source-only, target-only, mixed, pseudo-labeling) before preference optimization. For pseudo-labeling, a teacher model (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) generates multiple responses per target prompt; these are paired with the dataset's original response to form synthetic preference pairs. Online methods (PPO, GRPO) first train a reward model on these pairs, then perform on-policy optimization on target prompts with KL penalties. Evaluation uses GPT-5-nano as a judge to compute win rates against target references and measures diversity via syntactic (EAD), semantic (SBERT cosine), and logical (NLI) metrics, sampling 16 outputs per test prompt at temperature 1.0. All fine-tuning uses LoRA with standardized hyperparameters (rank 16, learning rates 1e-5 for SFT, 1e-6 for preference tuning).

## Key Results
- Adaptation strategy (SFT configuration, pseudo-labeling) dominates the alignment objective in determining cross-domain performance.
- Pseudo-labeling achieves very high target win rates (up to 83.37%) but collapses semantic diversity to near zero (SBERT ~0.06–0.07).
- Online RL, especially GRPO, preserves output diversity and exhibits minimal generalization gap compared to PPO.
- For QA helpfulness, domain shift effects are small; summarization shows large gaps without proper adaptation.
- A trade-off exists: pseudo-labeling maximizes target reliability but sacrifices output variety; mixed-domain SFT balances both.
- Data-efficient adaptation: using only 10% of pseudo-labeled target data yields comparable performance to full data.
- Adaptation order is critical: target-first SFT significantly improves transfer compared to source-first then target.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Pseudo-labeling reduces domain shift by transferring target-domain stylistic priors from a teacher model but causes severe diversity collapse due to low-entropy teacher outputs.
**Mechanism:** The teacher model generates responses that capture target-domain style (e.g., formal news summaries). These are treated as *chosen* responses in synthetic preference pairs, with the original target responses as *rejected*. Training on this data aligns the student to the teacher's patterns, boosting target win rates. However, teacher outputs are often deterministic and templated, leading the student to overfit to a narrow set of patterns, collapsing semantic diversity.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.1–5.2 report high target win rates with pseudo-labeling alongside diversity collapse (SBERT ~0.06–0.07).

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Online RL (especially GRPO) preserves diversity and cross-domain stability via group-relative advantage estimation.
**Mechanism:** Online methods sample multiple outputs per prompt and compute advantages relative to group statistics (mean/std for GRPO) or a learned value function (PPO). This group-relative baseline normalizes rewards, reducing the incentive to exploit a single high-reward pattern. The inherent stochasticity of sampling and the relative advantages encourage exploration, maintaining a broader output distribution. GRPO's design uses smaller KL penalties than PPO, avoiding over-specialization and yielding better stability across domains.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 5.1 shows GRPO maintains source win rate with small generalization gap; Section 5.2 notes higher semantic diversity for PPO/GRPO.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Adaptation strategy matters more than the alignment objective because target-domain SFT initializes domain-specific representations that preference tuning then refines.
**Mechanism:** Performing SFT on target-domain data before preference optimization grounds the model in the target distribution (e.g., formal style, structure). This creates robust feature representations that are later tuned via preferences. Without this grounding, preference tuning on source data overfits to source-specific cues and fails to transfer. The order is critical: target-first SFT yields a stable foundation; source-first then target SFT causes forgetting of source competence.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 5.1 identifies SFT as key for summarization adaptation; Section 6.2 shows target-first SFT improves target win rate (56.40% vs. 35.22%).

## Foundational Learning
- **Preference Optimization Objectives (DPO, KTO, ORPO, PPO, GRPO)**  
  *Why needed:* To understand how offline (static dataset) vs. online (on-policy) alignment methods behave under domain shift, particularly regarding exploration and stability.  
  *Quick check:* What is the key difference between DPO (offline, static pairs) and GRPO (online, group-relative advantages) in terms of sampling and diversity preservation?

- **Pseudo-labeling for Domain Adaptation**  
  *Why needed:* It is the primary strategy that drastically reduces the domain gap but introduces diversity collapse; understanding its mechanism is crucial for safe application.  
  *Quick check:* Why does using a teacher model’s outputs as *chosen* responses in synthetic pairs lead to reduced semantic diversity in the student?

- **Diversity Metrics (EAD, SBERT, NLI)**  
  *Why needed:* To quantify the diversity-generalization trade-off and detect mode collapse beyond lexical variation.  
  *Quick check:* How does semantic diversity measured by SBERT cosine similarity differ from syntactic distinctness captured by EAD?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Data inputs (source preference pairs, target corpus) -> Optional teacher model (generates synthetic choices) -> SFT module (adapts base model) -> Alignment module (applies preference objective) -> Evaluation module (computes win rates and diversity)

Critical path:
1. Load source preference dataset and target corpus; split source into train/test.
2. If pseudo-labeling: for each target prompt, sample 3 teacher responses (temp 0.7); pair each with the dataset's chosen response as rejected to form synthetic preferences.
3. Perform SFT on chosen adaptation data (e.g., D_T, D_S+T, D_synth_T) using LoRA.
4. Use the SFT model as policy (and reference) for alignment.
5. For offline objectives: train on source or synthetic preferences (e.g., DPO). For online: train regression reward model on pseudo pairs, then run PPO/GRPO on target prompts using RM reward and KL penalty.
6. Evaluate final policy on source and target test sets: generate outputs, compute win rates via LLM judge, and diversity metrics (EAD, SBERT, NLI) over 500 prompts with 16 samples each.

Design tradeoffs:
- Pseudo-labeling vs. Mixed SFT: Pseudo-labeling yields higher target win rates (up to 83.37%) but collapses diversity (SBERT ~0.07); Mixed SFT (D_S+T) balances both (target win ~57%, SBERT ~0.30).
- Online vs. Offline: Online RL (GRPO) preserves diversity and stability but requires on-policy sampling; offline (DPO) is efficient but overfits to static data.
- Teacher model size: Larger teachers produce higher-quality synthetic data, but small subsets (10%) yield comparable performance, enabling efficiency.
- Adaptation order: Target-first SFT improves transfer; source-first then target SFT degrades source competence.

Failure signatures:
- High generalization gap: |Source win – Target win| > 15% in summarization indicates poor transfer.
- Diversity collapse: Semantic diversity (SBERT) < 0.1, especially with pseudo-labeling.
- Training instability: PPO KL divergence spikes, loss oscillations, or GRPO advantage miscalculation.
- Style mismatch: Outputs retain source-domain style (e.g., informal summaries) on target evaluation.
- Judge bias: LLM judge may favor certain structures, distorting win rates.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline SFT on source only: Train SFT on D_S, evaluate on D_S and D_T. Expect high source win, low target win, moderate diversity. Confirms domain gap.
2. Pseudo-labeling minimal: Generate synthetic preferences for 10% of D_T using teacher, train SFT+DPO on that. Expect target win rate >80%, semantic diversity ~0.07. Demonstrates trade-off.
3. GRPO on pseudo-labeled data: Same as Exp 2 but use GRPO instead of DPO. Expect target win rate slightly lower (~80%) but semantic diversity >0.20. Shows diversity preservation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How can preference tuning objectives be designed to optimize for output distributional diversity rather than scalar win rates, thereby avoiding mode collapse while maintaining strong target-domain performance?
  *Basis:* Explicit in conclusion; current objectives optimize scalar rewards and collapse diversity under deterministic supervision.
  *Why unresolved:* No existing method in the study preserves both high win rates and diversity; novel objectives needed.

- **Question 2:** How does noise in instruction tuning and preference labels affect the robustness of preference optimization under domain shift?
  *Basis:* Explicitly mentioned as future work (Alajrami et al., 2025).
  *Why unresolved:* The study uses clean preferences and high-quality teacher outputs; real-world noise could alter trade-offs and gaps.

- **Question 3:** What is the underlying mechanism by which pseudo-labeling causes severe diversity collapse? Is it due to the teacher model's low-entropy distribution, the deterministic synthetic preference pairs, or inherent sensitivity of offline objectives to distribution mismatch?
  *Basis:* Inferred; pseudo-labeling reduces diversity to near-zero across offline objectives, but cause not isolated.
  *Why unresolved:* No ablations separating teacher diversity, synthetic pair construction, or objective interaction.

- **Question 4:** What intrinsic properties of tasks determine their sensitivity to domain shift in preference tuning? Why does QA helpfulness show minimal gaps while summarization exhibits large gaps?
  *Basis:* Inferred; QA gaps near zero, summarization gaps >20% in many configs.
  *Why unresolved:* Only two tasks studied; no analysis of what makes a task domain-robust.

## Limitations
- Dataset splits and preprocessing details are not specified, possibly affecting absolute win rates.
- Reward model architecture, training epochs, and loss weighting for online RL (PPO/GRPO) are under-reported.
- Access to the teacher (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) and judge (GPT-5-nano) models may be restricted, forcing substitutes that could alter results.
- QA pseudo-label construction (which response serves as rejected) is unclear.
- Potential data leakage from base model pretraining could reduce measured domain gap.

## Confidence
**Adaptation Strategy Dominance:** Medium – Strong empirical matrix but external validity to other tasks/models untested.
**Pseudo-Labeling Trade-off:** High for the trade-off observation (clear data); Medium for the mechanistic explanation (plausible but not causally verified).
**Online RL (GRPO) Diversity Preservation:** Medium – Positive results but sparse implementation details; performance may be sensitive to hyperparameters.
**Minimal QA Domain Shift:** Low – Based on limited datasets and one metric; broader evidence needed.

## Next Checks
1. **Teacher diversity replication:** Generate pseudo-labels from multiple teachers (different models/temperatures) and train students; measure if diversity collapse correlates with teacher entropy.
2. **GRPO hyperparameter sweep:** Systematically vary group size (4, 8, 16) and KL penalty (0.01, 0.1, 1.0) to test robustness of diversity preservation and stability.
3. **LLM judge calibration:** Collect human preference labels on a representative sample; compute agreement with GPT-5-nano and, if correlation is low, adjust win-rate estimates to confirm effects persist.
