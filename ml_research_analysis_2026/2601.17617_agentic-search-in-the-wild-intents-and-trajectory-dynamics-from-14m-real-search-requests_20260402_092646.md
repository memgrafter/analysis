# Agentic Search in the Wild: Intents and Trajectory Dynamics from 14M+ Real Search Requests

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17617  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17617  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Analysis of 14.44M search sessions reveals intent-driven query reformulation patterns and that 54% of new query terms are traceable to previously retrieved evidence.

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a large-scale empirical analysis of LLM-powered search agents operating in a real-world sandbox (DeepResearchGym). By processing 14.44M search requests across 3.97M sessions, the authors characterize how agents structure multi-step information-seeking tasks. Using LLM-based annotation, they classify session intents (Declarative, Procedural, Reasoning) and step-wise query reformulation trajectories (Specialization, Generalization, Exploration, Repetition). A novel metric, Context-driven Term Adoption Rate (CTAR), quantifies evidence reuse by measuring lexical overlap between new query terms and prior retrieved context. The study finds most multi-turn sessions are short (≤10 steps) and fast-paced (89% of inter-step intervals <1 minute), with behavior varying systematically by intent—Declarative sessions increasingly repeat queries over time, while Reasoning sessions sustain broader exploration. Over half of all new query terms adopt language from the accumulated evidence context, including from steps beyond the immediate predecessor.

## Method Summary
The analysis uses anonymized logs from DeepResearchGym, spanning 14.44M search requests. After filtering malformed entries and testing traffic, sessions are constructed per IP using a neural continuity classifier (a 3-layer MLP on Qwen3 embeddings) with a 0.5 score threshold and a 10-minute hard cutoff. Multi-turn sessions (2–10 steps) are annotated with intents, and adjacent step pairs are labeled with trajectory types using an LLM-as-judge (`gpt-5-nano`). To compute CTAR, the authors replay logged queries via the DRGym API to retrieve documents, tokenize queries and evidence (lowercase, stopword removal), and measure the fraction of new terms in a query that appear in the prior evidence context (either from the last step or all previous steps). Auxiliary metrics (session length, intervals, similarity measures) support the analysis.

## Key Results
- Over 90% of multi-turn sessions contain at most ten steps; 89% of inter-step intervals are under one minute.
- Intent drives trajectory patterns: Declarative sessions show increasing repetition over time, while Reasoning sessions maintain higher exploration.
- Agents frequently reuse evidence: 54% of new query terms are traceable to the aggregated prior context, with Specialization trajectories showing ~78% CTAR and Repetition ~20%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not explicitly investigate causal mechanisms but demonstrates that LLM-based search agents exhibit structured, intent-dependent behaviors in multi-step tasks. The high CTAR suggests agents iteratively incorporate retrieved evidence into subsequent queries, enabling progressive refinement. Short, frequent steps indicate agents operate in an interactive, near-real-time mode. The intent-specific patterns imply the LLM’s internal planning or reasoning strategy adapts to the perceived goal type—e.g., Declarative tasks may converge on a specific fact, leading to repetition, while Reasoning tasks require broader exploration.

## Foundational Learning
- **LLM-as-judge annotation:** Scalable labeling of intents and trajectories using powerful LLMs. *Why needed:* Manual annotation of millions of sessions is infeasible. *Quick check:* Compare agreement with a different model (e.g., `gemini-3-flash-preview`) on a sample.
- **Neural sessionization:** Uses a trained continuity classifier to group queries into sessions. *Why needed:* IP-based grouping is unreliable; temporal cutoffs alone split coherent tasks. *Quick check:* Validate that session length distribution matches Figure 3 (90% ≤10 steps).
- **Context-driven Term Adoption Rate (CTAR):** Measures lexical adoption of evidence terms in new queries. *Why needed:* Quantifies evidence reuse beyond simple result overlap. *Quick check:* Recompute CTAR on a held-out sample; verify overall ~54% and trajectory-specific curves (Figure 8).
- **Intent taxonomy (Declarative/Procedural/Reasoning):** Categorizes overarching session goals. *Why needed:* Reveals how task type shapes agent behavior. *Quick check:* Sample annotated sessions to confirm intent labels align with query content.
- **Trajectory labels (Specialization/Generalization/Exploration/Repetition):** Characterizes query reformulation between steps. *Why needed:* Maps dynamic search strategies. *Quick check:* Inspect step pairs with high/low CTAR for expected patterns (e.g., Specialization → high CTAR).
- **Evidence reconstruction via API replay:** Retrieves documents for logged queries to recover context. *Why needed:* CTAR requires the actual retrieved evidence; logs alone lack document content. *Quick check:* Ensure replay results match logged `num_of_docs` and document IDs where available.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Logs → Preprocessing → Sessionizer → LLM Annotator → Evidence Replay → CTAR Calculator → Analysis

**Critical path:** Raw logs are cleaned and segmented into sessions via the continuity classifier. Sessions are labeled with intents; step pairs get trajectory labels. Queries are replayed to fetch evidence, enabling CTAR computation per step. Auxiliary metrics are derived from log fields (timestamps, `num_of_docs`).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- LLM-as-judge vs. human annotation: Scalability gained at the cost of label consistency (model version/temperature sensitivity).  
- Sessionization threshold (0.5) and temporal cutoff (10 min): Simple heuristics risk mis-splitting or merging coherent tasks.  
- Lexical CTAR: Computationally cheap but misses semantic term adoption (e.g., synonyms).

**Failure signatures:**  
- Session length distribution diverges from Figure 3 (e.g., many sessions >50 steps).  
- Aggregate CTAR deviates strongly from ~54% (e.g., <40% or >70%).  
- Intent or trajectory label distributions differ significantly from Table 5 without clear reason (e.g., >50% Reasoning).

**First three experiments:**  
1. Reproduce session length and inter-step interval statistics (Figure 3) after adjusting sessionization parameters.  
2. Compute CTAR on a stratified sample of 1,000 step transitions and compare trajectory-wise values to Table 6/Figure 8.  
3. Measure inter-annotator agreement between two LLM judges on 500 annotated sessions/step pairs; report Cohen’s κ and distribution similarity.

## Limitations
- LLM-as-judge uses unreleased models (`gpt-5-nano`, `gemini-3-flash-preview`) and settings; label reliability depends on model version and prompt sensitivity.
- Evidence reconstruction requires exact match with the original DRGym retrieval backend; unknown corpus versions or model updates may bias CTAR.
- Sessionization relies on an arbitrary threshold (0.5) and 10-minute cutoff; mis-grouping is possible, affecting all downstream metrics.
- Findings are specific to DeepResearchGym’s sandbox environment and may not generalize to commercial search engines or different agent architectures.
