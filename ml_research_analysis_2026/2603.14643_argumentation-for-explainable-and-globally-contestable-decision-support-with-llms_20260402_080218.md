# Argumentation for Explainable and Globally Contestable Decision Support with LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14643
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14643
- **Reference count:** 13
- **Primary result:** Competitive accuracy (0.8818 label match rate, 0.9771 NDCG) with 2–9× fewer inference tokens; global contestation improves label match rate by 7.75% in a case study.

## Executive Summary
ArgEval addresses the opacity and unpredictability of large language models (LLMs) in high-stakes decision domains by constructing general decision ontologies and corresponding argumentation frameworks (QBAFs) from policy documents. These frameworks are instantiated per case to produce explainable recommendations, while enabling *global contestability*: modifications to the shared QBAFs or parameter schema improve future decisions. Evaluated on glioblastoma treatment recommendation, ArgEval achieves strong performance with significantly lower token usage than instance-specific baselines. A case study demonstrates that contesting a single sample’s QBAF and parameter schema yields a 7.75% absolute improvement in label match rate across the dataset, proving the efficacy of the global contestation mechanism.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with ingestion of clinical guidelines, which are semantically chunked. An LLM then iteratively constructs a decision ontology (entities, hierarchy, provenance) from the chunks. For each leaf treatment entity, a general QBAF is mined: a bipolar argument tree with natural-language conditions, base scores, and formalized conditions (JSON Schema). This process simultaneously builds a global parameter schema describing the patient features referenced in conditions. At inference time, a case vignette is parsed into structured parameters via the schema. Each general QBAF is instantiated by removing arguments whose conditions fail against the extracted parameters; the remaining graph is evaluated using the DF‑QuAD gradual semantics, where the root strength is the recommendation score. Contestation allows users to edit the general QBAFs (e.g., argument weights) or the parameter schema; these changes persist and affect all future instantiations that apply to the modified conditions.

## Key Results
- Best variant: **0.8818** label match rate (LMR), **0.9771** NDCG.
- **2–9×** fewer inference tokens than instance‑specific baselines (ArgLLMs-O).
- Contesting a single sample’s QBAF and parameter schema improves overall LMR by **7.75%** (0.8009 → 0.8784), demonstrating global contestability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Faithful Explanations via QBAF Instantiation
ArgEval produces explanations that are directly readable from the instantiated QBAF: arguments whose conditions are satisfied survive pruning and their attack/support relations determine the score via a deterministic semantics. The core assumption is that the condition formalization and parameter extraction are accurate enough to correctly prune irrelevant arguments. Evidence: the abstract and Section 4.2 claim that instantiated QBAFs serve as faithful explanations, and Section 5.3 (Figure 4) visually shows how the graph explains a recommendation. Break condition: errors in `extract_params` or `eval_cond` would retain inappropriate arguments or remove relevant ones, misaligning the explanation with clinical reality and corrupting the score.

### Mechanism 2: Global Contestability
Because the QBAFs and parameter schema are shared across all cases, user‑driven modifications (e.g., adjusting a base score or refining a parameter definition) propagate to future inferences that satisfy the affected arguments’ conditions. This assumes that an observed error stems from a general rule that also impacts unseen similar cases. Evidence: the abstract and Section 5.3 report a 7.75% LMR gain after contesting one case by changing general QBAF scores and schema entries. Break condition: if the error is truly case‑specific (e.g., a rare parameter combination not captured in the schema), modifying general artifacts may not help and could harm other cases.

### Mechanism 3: Computational Efficiency via Separation of Concerns
The expensive LLM mining of arguments and base scores is performed once per decision option during the “general task processing” phase, yielding compact general QBAFs. At inference time, only lightweight parameter extraction and deterministic instantiation/semantics evaluation are needed. This contrasts with baselines like ArgLLMs‑O, which re‑mine a full QBAF for every new case. Evidence: the abstract and Section 5.2 (Table 3) show ArgEval’s inference token counts (~1 M) are 2–9× lower than the baselines (~80–96 M). Break condition: if the number of inference cases is very small, the upfront cost may not be amortized; also, if instantiation requires LLM calls (e.g., for complex condition checking), token savings diminish.

## Foundational Learning
- **Quantitative Bipolar Argumentation Framework (QBAF):** A graph of arguments (A) with attack (R⁻) and support (R⁺) relations, each argument has a base score (τ) that is refined via gradual semantics into a final strength (σ). *Why needed:* It is the core representational and inferential structure that turns a structured debate into a numerical recommendation. *Quick check:* What is the difference between a base score (τ) and a final strength (σ), and why is the latter needed?
- **Decision Ontology & Parameter Schema:** The ontology defines decision options (treatments) and their hierarchy; the schema defines patient parameters (e.g., age, KPS) that argument conditions reference. *Why needed:* They provide a systematic, generalizable bridge from free‑text guidelines and cases to formal reasoning. *Quick check:* How does the parameter schema enable extraction of structured parameters from a natural‑language vignette?
- **Gradual Semantics (DF‑QuAD):** A discontinuity‑free semantics (Rago et al., 2016) that computes final argument strengths from base scores and attack/support relations. *Why needed:* It is the deterministic inference engine that aggregates pros and cons into a recommendation score. *Quick check:* If an argument has stronger attackers than supporters, does its final strength increase or decrease relative to its base score, and by what principle?
- **Global vs. Local Contestability:** Global contestability modifies shared general artifacts (QBAFs, schema), affecting all future applicable cases; local contestability alters only a single instance’s output. *Why needed:* ArgEval’s key innovation is to let users correct systemic flaws that propagate broadly, rather than patching individual errors. *Quick check:* If you change a base score in a general QBAF, which case recommendations are affected? How is this different from changing an argument in a case‑specific QBAF?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Policy Docs → Ontology Constructor → General QBAF Miner & Parameter Schema → Case Parameter Extractor → QBAF Instantiator → Semantics Engine → Recommendation  
Contestation Interface loops back to modify General QBAFs or Parameter Schema.

**Critical path:**  
Guidelines are chunked → ontology mined → for each treatment, a general QBAF (arguments with conditions) and base scores are generated, while a global parameter schema is built → for a new case, parameters are extracted → the general QBAF is instantiated by pruning arguments whose conditions fail → DF‑QuAD computes root strength → score is the recommendation. Contestation edits the stored general QBAFs or schema, affecting later instantiations.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *QBAF depth:* Depth‑2 captures nuanced support chains but increases construction complexity and risk of spurious arguments.  
- *Root score estimation:* Estimating root scores can lead to overconfident outputs that are hard to override; fixing root to 0.5 makes the outcome entirely dependent on attackers/supporters.  
- *Argument scheme inclusion:* Providing a scheme (e.g., Table 2) guides the LLM toward domain‑relevant arguments but may restrict exploration of valid but uncovered points.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Ontology bloat/noise:** Irrelevant entities (e.g., misclassified drug names) increase cost without benefit. Check entity frequency across source documents.  
- **Condition‑formalization mismatch:** Natural‑language condition χᴺ does not map cleanly to formal χ (JSON Schema), causing `eval_cond` failures. Validate a sample of formal conditions against their source.  
- **Instantiation over‑/under‑pruning:** Inaccurate parameter extraction or faulty condition logic keeps wrong arguments or discards valid ones. Manually spot‑check instantiated QBAFs against vignettes.  
- **Semantics saturation:** DF‑QuAD may produce unintuitive scores if attack/support cycles exist (though trees are acyclic). Monitor for root scores pinned at extremes (0.0/1.0) or exactly 0.5 too frequently.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline performance comparison:** Run ArgEval variants (varying depth, root estimation, scheme) vs. Base LLM and ArgLLMs‑O on the 360‑case glioblastoma dataset. Compute LMR and NDCG.  
2. **Token efficiency analysis:** Compare total inference I/O tokens across methods. ArgEval should show 2–9× reduction.  
3. **Contestation impact case study:** Select a poorly performing case; identify flawed arguments/schema via QBAF inspection; apply minimal contestation (adjust scores, refine parameter descriptions); re‑evaluate on the full dataset to quantify global performance lift (e.g., +7.75% LMR).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do end‑users (e.g., clinicians) actually utilize ArgEval’s explainability and global contestability features in practice, and does this improve trust, understanding, or decision quality compared to opaque LLM baselines? The paper notes the need for future user studies.
2. To what extent can the performance improvements from global contestation observed in the glioblastoma case study be generalized to other decision domains and to contestations initiated by different users or on different initial model variants? The paper suggests application in other domains but lacks empirical generalization.
3. How robust and maintainable are the automatically constructed general QBAFs and decision ontologies when the underlying policy documents are large, incomplete, contradictory, or outdated? The evaluation uses refined, high‑quality guidelines; real‑world policy landscapes are messier.
4. What are the optimal strategies and limits for parameter schema evolution during contestation? Specifically, how much can the parameter schema be safely extended or refined before it loses generality or introduces spurious correlations? The paper shows a minimal successful fix but does not systematize the trade‑off.
5. Can the ArgEval paradigm be efficiently scaled to decision spaces with hundreds or thousands of options, and does the “global” nature of contestation remain computationally and cognitively manageable at that scale? The evaluation uses a pruned ontology of 9 treatments; scaling properties are unknown.

## Limitations
- Exact LLM prompts for ontology/QBAF mining are undisclosed, risking replication variance.
- Evaluation uses synthetic patient vignettes; real clinical texts may contain more noise and ambiguity.
- General QBAFs built from only four guidelines; coverage of real‑world treatment nuances may be limited.
- Global contestability assumes errors stem from generalizable rules; case‑specific idiosyncrasies may not benefit.
- Token efficiency gains depend on amortizing upfront construction over many inference cases; small‑scale use may not offset the cost.

## Confidence
- **Explainability via faithful QBAF instantiation:** Medium (relies on accurate parameter extraction and condition formalisation, not fully validated on real cases).
- **Global contestability:** Medium (single‑case improvement demonstrated, but generalisation to diverse errors and domains is uncertain).
- **Computational efficiency:** High (architecture clearly reduces per‑case LLM calls; token counts are reported).
- **Overall performance (LMR/NDCG):** High (empirical results on test set, though domain‑specific).

## Next Checks
