# On the Reliability Limits of LLM-Based Multi-Agent Planning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.26993
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26993
- Reference count: 11
- Primary result: Finite acyclic LLM multi-agent planning systems without new exogenous signals are always dominated by a centralized Bayes decision maker; adding novel external signals can boost accuracy (e.g., from 24.3% to 82.7%), while longer relay chains without new signals degrade performance (90.7% to 22.5% on MMLU).

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes a decision-theoretic foundation for understanding reliability limits in LLM-based multi-agent planning. The authors model such systems as finite acyclic delegated decision networks and prove that, when no new exogenous information is introduced at intermediate stages, the final decision quality cannot exceed that of a centralized Bayes observer with access to the same total information. The work further characterizes communication loss under proper scoring rules as an expected posterior distortion, which for logarithmic loss equals conditional mutual information. Empirical experiments on the MMLU dataset and a synthetic knowledge task demonstrate that increasing relay depth (from 2 to 5 agents) significantly reduces accuracy, whereas incorporating genuinely novel tool outputs yields large improvements. Structured message formats (probability vectors) mitigate information loss compared to natural language summaries.

## Method Summary
The experimental evaluation compares nine architectural variants: a centralized single-agent baseline; serial relays of 2, 3, and 5 agents with free-text messages; a 3‑agent relay using normalized probability vectors; single‑agent with Wikipedia lookup; multi‑agent with two specialists (Wikipedia and Semantic Scholar) plus an aggregator; a duplicate‑Wikipedia condition; and a synthetic task with a tool providing access to unknown entities. Two OpenAI models were used: *gpt‑4.1‑mini* (token log‑probabilities extracted to form posteriors) and *o4‑mini* (final answer only). The dataset comprised 200 balanced MMLU multiple‑choice questions and a synthetic knowledge base of 200 fictional entities. Primary metrics were accuracy and KL divergence between the centralized posterior and the terminal posterior as a proxy for information distortion. The results confirm the theoretical predictions: relay chains without new signals degrade performance, while adding informative tools improves it; structured relays suffer less degradation.

## Key Results
- Relay chain length negatively impacts accuracy: from ~90.7% (2 agents) down to ~22.5% (5 agents) on MMLU.
- Access to novel external information via tools dramatically boosts accuracy on synthetic tasks, from 24.3% (no tool) to 82.7% (with tool).
- Structured posterior relays (numeric class probabilities) preserve accuracy far better than free‑text message passing, demonstrating the importance of communication format.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** If LLM multi‑agent planning stages do not introduce new exogenous signals (i.e., all information originates from shared evidence B), then the system’s terminal decision quality is never better than that of a centralized Bayes decision maker that directly observes B and all privately generated randomness.
- **Mechanism:** The acyclic graph ensures the final action A is a measurable function of the exogenous signals E_exo (common B and any private Z_v) plus independent ancilla ξ. By Lemma 2, any such function (a randomized garbling of E_exo) cannot reduce Bayes risk relative to acting directly on E_exo. Thus V(E_exo; ℓ) ≤ E[ℓ(A, Y)].
- **Core assumption:** The network is finite and directed acyclic; all randomness ξ is conditionally independent of Y given E_exo; loss ℓ bounded (or integrable); spaces are standard Borel for measurability.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "without new exogenous signals, any delegated network is decision‑theoretically dominated by a centralized Bayes decision maker with access to the same information."
  - [section] Proposition 6: "Consider any finite directed acyclic delegated network ... then for every bounded loss ℓ, E[ℓ(A, Y)] ≥ V(E_exo; ℓ)."
  - [corpus] The corpus reveals many multi‑agent systems focusing on coordination and tool use, but none directly challenge the domination result; they often implicitly assume new signals via tools or human review.
- **Break condition:** The claim fails if the graph contains cycles (allowing feedback loops that could refine information) or if some stage introduces a genuinely new exogenous signal Z_v that carries information about Y not already in B. Also if the centralized observer cannot access the same private signals (e.g., dynamic environment), the comparison changes.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Under a proper scoring rule s, the loss penalty from communication (i.e., the difference between central Bayes value V(H; s) and value after communication V(M; s)) equals the expected divergence D_s(π_H, π_M), where π_H = P(Y|H) and π_M = P(Y|M). For log loss this is I(Y;H|M); for Brier score it is E[∥π_H − π_M∥²].
- **Mechanism:** Proper scoring rules s have the property that the Bayes‑optimal action is to report the true posterior. Then V(H;s)=E[s(π_H,Y)], V(M;s)=E[s(π_M,Y)]. Using Lemma 3 (given H,M we have P(Y|H,M)=π_H and π_M = E[π_H|M]), the difference becomes E[∑_y π_H(y)(s(π_M,y)−s(π_H,y))] = E[D_s(π_H, π_M)].
- **Core assumption:** Y finite; s proper (strictly proper for zero iff π_H=π_M); M generated from H via a channel independent of Y given H; regular conditional distributions exist.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Under proper scoring rules, the gap between the centralized Bayes value and the value after communication admits an expected posterior divergence representation, which reduces to conditional mutual information under logarithmic loss and to expected squared posterior error under the Brier score."
  - [section] Theorem 8 states exactly: V(M;s) − V(H; s) = E[D_s(π_H, π_M)] and identifies special cases.
  - [corpus] The corpus contains papers on proper scoring rules and information‑theoretic measures, but not specifically their application to multi‑agent communication loss; the connection is novel.
- **Break condition:** The identity relies on s being proper; a non‑proper scoring rule does not have posterior as Bayes action, so the formula changes. Also if the communication channel explicitly depends on Y beyond H (e.g., an encoder that looks at Y), Lemma 3 fails and the posterior equality does not hold.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Adding an external verifier signal W at the terminal stage improves the Bayes value for some decision problems if and only if the joint experiment (M, W) is not Blackwell‑dominated by M (i.e., W provides information about Y that is not already reducible from M). Otherwise, if W is conditionally redundant given M, the added signal never improves any decision.
- **Mechanism:** If W is Blackwell‑redundant (i.e., (M,W) is generated from M via a channel independent of Y), then any (M,W)-measurable decision rule is also M‑measurable, so V((M,W);ℓ) = V(M;ℓ) for all ℓ. Conversely, if not Blackwell‑dominated, Blackwell’s theorem guarantees the existence of a decision problem (finite action set, bounded loss) where the larger information set yields strictly lower risk.
- **Core assumption:** Blackwell’s comparison theorem applies (standard Borel spaces, finite action sets suffice); loss bounded; W observed at terminal stage.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "adding truly novel external signals (tool access to unknown entities) boosts accuracy from 24.3% to 82.7%."
  - [section] Corollary 9: "If an additional verifier signal W is available ... then for every bounded loss ℓ, V((M,W);ℓ) ≤ V(M;ℓ), with strict inequality for some bounded loss whenever (M,W) is not Blackwell‑dominated by M."
  - [corpus] Corpus includes papers on tool‑augmented agents (e.g., "Agentless", "OSWorld") which implicitly rely on adding new information; the theory clarifies when such additions are beneficial vs. redundant.
- **Break condition:** The Blackwell condition is necessary and sufficient. If W is a deterministic function of M or follows a channel independent of Y after M, then no improvement is possible for any loss. Additionally, if the decision maker cannot properly integrate W with M (e.g., due to interface limitations), the theoretical gain may not be realized in practice.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Bayes decision theory
- **Why needed here:** The paper’s framework is built around Bayes risk V(H;ℓ) as the measure of reliability. Understanding how optimal actions are derived from posteriors under loss ℓ is essential to grasp Lemma 1, Proposition 6, and Theorem 8.
- **Quick check question:** Given Y ∈ {0,1} with prior P(Y=1)=0.6. After observing H, the posterior is P(Y=1|H)=0.8. What action (0 or 1) minimizes expected squared error loss? What about if the loss is weighted asymmetric ℓ(1,0)=10, ℓ(0,1)=1?

### Concept: Blackwell’s order of experiments
- **Why needed here:** The paper uses Blackwell’s theorem repeatedly: to show that centralized observation is at least as informative as any garbling delegation (Prop 6), to characterize when added signals are redundant (Cor 9), and to frame the common‑evidence design as an experiment choice (Thm 7). Without Blackwell’s theorem we cannot formally compare information structures.
- **Quick check question:** Consider two signals S and T. S is generated by first sampling T, then adding independent noise N ∼ Uniform(0,1) (independent of Y). Which one is more informative for predicting Y? Explain using Blackwell’s criterion.

### Concept: Proper scoring rules and posterior calibration
- **Why needed here:** Theorem 8 hinges on proper scoring rules to express communication loss as posterior distortion. Proper scoring rules link the decision problem of reporting probabilities to information‑theoretic divergences. They also justify why the Bayes action is the true posterior.
- **Quick check question:** Show that for log loss ℓ(a,y) = −log a(y), the expected loss of a fixed report q is E[s(q,Y)|H] = −∑_y π_H(y) log q(y). Prove that this is minimized uniquely at q=π_H.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- Nodes = LLM agents/modules (planner, worker, critic, reviewer). Each node v takes incoming messages T_{u→v} and possibly private signal Z_v (tool output) and produces an action or message.
- Common signal B = shared context (user query, retrieved documents). All nodes ultimately derive from B unless they have private Z_v.
- Private exogenous signals Z_v = new information arriving at stage v (e.g., tool call results). If absent, the system is in the common‑evidence regime.
- Edges (messages) = communication links, modeled as T_{u→v} = σ(U_u, ξ). In practice: prompts, summaries, JSON objects. These can distort information.
- Terminal communication state M = the final workspace/message observed by the output stage. This is the information state on which the final decision is based; its format/content are determined by the upstream DAG and any communication budget (e.g., token limit).
- Escalation (△) = human review option at final stage, incurring conditional loss R_h(H).

### Critical path
1. Formalize architecture as a DAG. Identify nodes, edges, exogenous signals (B, any Z_v), and ancilla ξ. Ensure acyclicity.
2. Compute terminal action distribution. Given the DAG and exogenous signals, the final answer A is a measurable function of (E_exo, ξ). This establishes that without new signals, A is a garbling of E_exo.
3. Compare to centralized Bayes. By Lemma 2, any such garbling cannot reduce Bayes risk: E[ℓ(A,Y)] ≥ V(E_exo; ℓ). Thus the centralized observer that sees E_exo directly is optimal.
4. Specialize to common‑evidence (all Z_v empty). Then M is a stochastic encoder of B with cost C(G) = log|M|. Theorem 7 shows design reduces to choosing Q(M|B) under a budget.
5. Quantify communication loss. Under proper s, the gap V(M;s)−V(B;s) = E[D_s(π_B, π_M)] (Theorem 8). For log loss, = I(Y;B|M). For serial relays, loss adds across stages.
6. Evaluate added signals. Using Blackwell (Cor 9): a new signal W helps if (M,W) is not Blackwell‑reducible to M. In practice: tool outputs that contain independent information about Y.
7. Design review policy. Optimal escalation threshold: automate if R_a(H) ≤ R_h(H), escalate otherwise (Theorem 10). This depends on the posterior risk at M.

### Design tradeoffs
- Depth vs. information preservation: Adding more relay stages (without new signals) tends to increase posterior distortion (additively under log loss) and degrade accuracy, as in Figure 1. Longer chains are generally harmful unless each stage preserves posterior (e.g., structured relay).
- Interface format: Free‑form prose relays cause larger distortion than structured numeric/value relays (Figure 3). The communication budget (e.g., token limit) should allocate capacity to preserve decision‑relevant statistics, not to produce verbose summaries.
- Tool diversity vs. redundancy: Multiple tools that provide independent information about Y can improve performance (e.g., Wikipedia + Scholar). Duplicate tools (two Wikipedia searches) yield no gain (Table 2: D vs. D_same). Cost of tool calls must be weighed against potential Blackwell improvement.
- Human review cost: Escalation incurs loss R_h(H). A well‑designed system should keep posterior risk low enough that escalation is rarely needed. Alternatively, if review is cheap and accurate, a higher risk threshold may improve overall reliability.

### Failure signatures
- Degrading accuracy with more agents: When increasing relay length (without new signals) consistently lowers accuracy (as in B2, B, B5), it signals that the communication format is too lossy and the system suffers from information distortion.
- No gain from additional tools: If adding a second tool (even a different‑seeming one) does not improve over a single tool, likely the new tool’s output is conditionally redundant given the first tool’s output and B (check by estimating mutual information between tool outputs and Y).
- High posterior distortion but stable accuracy? If KL(π_A∥π_final) is large but accuracy drop is small, maybe the loss function is insensitive (e.g., if the model was already wrong with high confidence, distortion may not change the argmax). But generally a strong correlation should hold for proper losses.
- Static escalation triggers: Using a fixed number of stages to trigger human review (e.g., after 3 rounds) rather than a dynamic threshold based on uncertainty may cause unnecessary escalations or missed errors.

### First 3 experiments
1. Relay chain degradation: Run a controlled experiment with a fixed dataset and a single LLM. Baseline: single agent with direct question. Then implement relay chains of 2, 3, 5 agents where each stage receives only the previous stage’s message (no original question). Measure accuracy drop. Expect monotonic decline. Also compute posterior distortion (KL between initial model distribution and final relayed distribution) per question; expect positive correlation with accuracy drop.
2. Interface format comparison: Keep depth = 3 agents. Compare three communication formats: (a) natural language summary, (b) JSON object with key‑value pairs, (c) numeric posterior vector (class probabilities). Measure accuracy and posterior distortion. Expect (c) to perform best due to minimal distortion; (b) intermediate; (a) worst.
3. Redundant vs. independent tools: Single agent with optional tool calls. Condition A: no tool. Condition B: tool T1 (e.g., Wikipedia). Condition C: tools T1+T2 (e.g., Wikipedia + Google search). Condition D: two instances of T1 (duplicate). Measure accuracy. Expect B > A, C > B (if T2 adds independent info), and D ≈ B (duplicate adds no new information). Compute mutual information between tool outputs and Y to confirm redundancy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we design optimal communication interfaces (i.e., the stochastic encoder Q(m|B)) for a given finite communication budget in common‑evidence multi‑agent systems, beyond the reduction to constrained experiment design?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows that optimizing common‑evidence graphs under a communication budget reduces to choosing a budget‑constrained stochastic experiment on the shared signal (Theorem 7), but does not provide a scalable algorithm for this optimization in high‑dimensional LLM contexts.
- Why unresolved: The reduction is theoretical; solving for the optimal encoder Q in practice for complex tasks (e.g., MMLU) with rich shared context B is computationally intractable without further approximations.
- What evidence would resolve it: A practical algorithm (e.g., using reinforcement learning or variational methods) that finds near‑optimal message distributions Q under a given budget and loss function ℓ, validated on controlled multi‑stage LLM tasks showing improved reliability over heuristic message formats (e.g., prose vs. structured posteriors).

### Open Question 2
- Question: What operational criteria define a "genuinely new exogenous signal" that can improve the Bayes envelope of a delegated network, and how can we measure the degree of informational redundancy between a new tool's output and the existing shared evidence?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Corollary 9 states that added signals help only if they are not Blackwell‑redundant, but the paper does not provide a practical measure for redundancy beyond the abstract Blackwell comparison.
- Why unresolved: Blackwell dominance is theoretically clear but empirically difficult to test with LLM‑generated signals and high‑dimensional contexts. Real‑world tool outputs (e.g., web search) may partially overlap with parametric knowledge.
- What evidence would resolve it: An empirical measure (e.g., based on conditional mutual information I(W;Y|M) or a proxy from model output distributions) that predicts when tool access will yield significant gains, demonstrated on a suite of tasks with varying degrees of overlap between tool‑retrieved information and model parametric knowledge.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How should we design empirical evaluations of multi‑agent LLM architectures to cleanly separate the effect of information structure change from pure organizational effects (i.e., the number or roles of agents)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Remark 3 argues that many reported multi‑agent gains may reflect added information rather than improved delegation, and calls for matched comparisons of exogenous signals and terminal state capacity.
- Why unresolved: Current LLM agent benchmarks often confound multiple design dimensions (e.g., adding a tool also adds an agent role). There is no standard protocol for holding information structure constant while varying only the delegation structure.
- What evidence would resolve it: A benchmark suite or evaluation protocol where information acquisition (shared/common evidence and private signals) and terminal communication budget are explicitly controlled and reported, along with a taxonomy of architecture changes that isolates "pure delegation" effects. Validation would show that some architecture changes improve performance only when they also increase information, not otherwise.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Can the theoretical optimal selective review rule (Theorem 10) be implemented in practice with LLM‑based systems, and how does the use of approximate posteriors (from model output distributions) affect its performance compared to the ideal Bayes risk threshold?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper characterizes optimal review as a threshold on the automated posterior risk Ra(H), but in practice H is the terminal communication state M, and the posterior π_H is not directly accessible; it must be estimated (e.g., from model log‑probabilities as in the experiments). The effect of estimation error on the review decision is not analyzed.
- Why unresolved: LLMs provide imperfect and potentially mis calibrated probability estimates. The theory assumes perfect knowledge of the conditional loss and posterior risk. Implementing a threshold requires reliable estimates of Ra(H) from the possibly garbled message M.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with LLMs on controlled tasks where the true posterior is known (e.g., synthetic distributions), comparing the performance of review policies using thresholded model‑derived risk estimates against the optimal Bayes rule. Analysis of calibration and robustness of the threshold to posterior estimation error.

### Open Question 5
- Question: To what extent do the theoretical limits (domination by a centralized Bayes decision maker) hold when the delegated network uses continuous or unbounded message spaces (e.g., free‑form language of varying length), rather than finite alphabets as assumed in the communication budget log|M|?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The model and budget assume finite message spaces (Theorem 7 uses log|M|). LLM communication is typically via natural language, which is theoretically unbounded. The reduction to a finite experiment may require discretization or truncation, potentially losing generality.
- Why unresolved: The proofs rely on finite |M| to define the budget. Continuous or countably infinite message spaces introduce issues of measurability and might allow different forms of compression that are not captured by the finite alphabet model.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extension of the theoretical framework to measurable spaces with continuous messages (e.g., via rate‑distortion theory with a distortion measure on messages) and empirical comparison of relay chains using natural language versus quantized/structured messages to see if the same reliability limits apply.

## Limitations
- Theoretical results rely on idealized assumptions (finite acyclic graphs, exogenous signal independence, bounded losses, standard Borel spaces). Real LLM systems may violate these, e.g., via implicit feedback loops or continuous communication.
- Experimental validation uses proprietary models with undisclosed sampling parameters and incomplete documentation of data splits and tool interfaces, hampering full reproducibility.
- Findings are based on limited tasks (MMLU, a synthetic knowledge base) and small‑scale agent setups (up to 5 stages). Generalization to complex planning domains or larger networks is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Theoretical domination result (Proposition 6): **High**
- Communication loss representation via proper scoring rules (Theorem 8): **High**
- Empirical relay degradation and tool benefits: **Medium**
