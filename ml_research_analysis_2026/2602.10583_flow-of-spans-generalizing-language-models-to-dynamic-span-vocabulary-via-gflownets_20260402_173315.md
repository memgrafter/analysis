# Flow of Spans: Generalizing Language Models to Dynamic Span-Vocabulary via GFlowNets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10583  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10583  
- **Reference count**: 36  
- **Primary result**: FoSS improves MAUVE scores by up to 12.5% over Transformers and 5.51% over state-of-the-art CoG on open-ended text generation, and achieves average 3.5% gains on knowledge-intensive QA tasks.  

## Executive Summary
Standard language models generate token-by-token from a fixed vocabulary, imposing a tree structure that cannot exploit the directed acyclic graph (DAG) nature of span composition. We introduce Flow of Spans (FoSS), a generative flow network (GFlowNet) framework that dynamically constructs a span-level vocabulary from retrieved documents, explicitly modeling a DAG state space where the same output can be reached via multiple distinct segmentation paths. FoSS employs a hybrid online-offline training regime with a subtrajectory balance objective and a reward model that combines a language model fluency score with a preference model alignment score. On open-ended generation, FoSS yields up to 12.5% relative MAUVE improvement over Transformers and 5.51% over CoG, while also achieving 3.5% average gains on several knowledge-intensive QA benchmarks. Ablations confirm the necessity of the DAG structure and both reward components. Scaling experiments further show consistent gains from larger models, more data, and richer retrieval corpora.  

## Method Summary
FoSS defines a state as a partial sequence of spans; actions are either variable-length spans (2–8 tokens) drawn from a dynamic vocabulary or single tokens from a fixed set. The dynamic vocabulary is derived from documents retrieved for the current prefix using DPR+FAISS and from spans extracted during offline training. The policy network scores each candidate action via a dot product between a prefix encoder (GPT-2 based) and a span encoder (BERT based). Training follows a GFlowNet objective: sample trajectories proportional to a reward function. The reward combines an LM fluency term (causal GPT-2 fine-tuned on the corpus) and a preference model term (Bradley–Terry discriminator) to balance quality and diversity. Training uses a hybrid regime: an initial epoch on offline trajectories constructed by a DAG-inducing probabilistic segmentation algorithm, followed by mixing online policy samples (80%) with offline replay (20%). The loss is the subtrajectory balance (SubTB) over all valid partial sentence pairs within each trajectory. At inference, given a prefix, FoSS retrieves documents, encodes candidate spans, and greedily or nucleus-samples actions until a terminal token is produced. For QA tasks, the likelihood of an option is computed by summing probabilities over all valid span decompositions using dynamic programming.  

## Key Results
- FoSS achieves up to **12.5%** relative improvement in MAUVE over standard Transformers and **5.51%** over the previous state-of-the-art CoG on open-ended text generation.  
- On knowledge-intensive QA (TruthfulQA, OpenBookQA, ARC‑Challenge, MedMCQA, Med‑USMLE), FoSS obtains an average **3.5%** gain in accuracy over baselines.  
- Ablation experiments demonstrate that removing the DAG structure (forcing a tree), the preference model, or the language model component each significantly degrades performance, underscoring the necessity of all three design choices.  
- Scaling experiments show consistent performance improvements as model size, training data quantity, and retrieval corpus richness increase.  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: DAG‑structured state space
**Claim**: FoSS generalizes language generation by explicitly modeling a DAG‑structured state space, where identical text can be reached via multiple distinct span‑composition paths.  
**Mechanism**: Instead of a fixed token vocabulary and tree structure, FoSS constructs a dynamic span vocabulary from retrieved documents. Each state is a partial sequence; actions are variable‑length spans. Different segmentations of the same text (e.g., “AB”→“CDEFGH” vs. “AB”→“CD”→“EFGH”) yield the same final string, causing the state graph to have multiple trajectories converging to the same node, thus forming a DAG. This DAG covers a broader region of the state space with fewer training examples than a tree, enabling more efficient exploration and better generalization.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract emphasizes DAG modeling and exploration of diverse compositional paths; Section 1 illustrates with examples; neighboring works on dynamic vocabularies and GFlowNet generalization provide contextual support.  
**Break condition**: If the segmentation algorithm produces strictly non‑overlapping spans per document (each token belongs to exactly one span in all trajectories), the graph collapses to a tree and the DAG advantage vanishes (validated by the w/o‑DAG ablation in Table 4).  

### Mechanism 2: Hybrid online‑offline training with SubTB
**Claim**: A hybrid online‑offline training regime with a subtrajectory balance objective enables stable and efficient learning over the combinatorially large DAG.  
**Mechanism**: GFlowNets require sampling trajectories in proportion to reward. FoSS first pre‑fine‑tunes the policy on high‑quality offline trajectories derived from the corpus using the DAG‑inducing segmentation. Then it mixes online samples from the current policy with offline replay of high‑reward trajectories (Bernoulli(0.2)). The subtrajectory balance loss is applied to every valid partial sentence pair within a trajectory, providing dense gradient signals and stabilizing training.  
**Evidence anchors**: Section 3.2 describes the hybrid approach and SubTB objective; Algorithm 1 specifies the mixing scheme; related work on replay buffers and pre‑training for GFlowNets supports this design.  
**Break condition**: If offline trajectories are low‑quality or not diverse, the policy initialization may be poor and subsequent online sampling may not recover good performance. The paper states the initial fine‑tuning is crucial, though a direct ablation is not shown.  

### Mechanism 3: Reward model combining LM fluency and PM alignment
**Claim**: A reward combining a language model (LM) fluency score and a preference model (PM) alignment score yields generations that are both high‑quality (fluent, coherent) and human‑like (diverse, non‑generic).  
**Mechanism**: The reward is \( R(s_n) = \exp(\alpha \log p_{LM}(s_n|c) + (1-\alpha) \log p_{PM}(s_n)) \). The LM component (causal GPT‑2 fine‑tuned on the corpus) encourages grammaticality and contextual relevance. The PM (Bradley‑Terry discriminator fine‑tuned on human vs. policy outputs) counteracts the LM’s tendency toward generic high‑likelihood outputs. The linear combination in log‑space balances fluency and diversity.  
**Evidence anchors**: Section 3.4 presents the equation and rationale; Section 4.5 ablations removing either component reduce MAUVE, confirming necessity and complementarity.  
**Break condition**: If the PM cannot distinguish human from model text or if α is extreme (near 0 or 1), the balance tips too far toward diversity (risking incoherence) or fluency (risking repetitiveness), leading to performance drops (Table 4).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (plain text):
1. Retriever (DPR + FAISS): Given a prefix, retrieves top‑k related documents from the corpus.  
2. Span Segmenter (offline, Algorithm 2): Processes each training document with probabilistic early‑stopping to produce multiple segmentation trajectories, populating the replay buffer.  
3. Prefix Encoder: Transformer (GPT‑2 initialized) with causal attention; encodes current state (prefix + previously generated spans) into a vector \( h_i \).  
4. Span Encoder: Bi‑directional Transformer (BERT‑base fine‑tuned) over retrieved documents; for each candidate span (substring length 2–8), computes start/end embeddings via MLPs and concatenates them into \( v_a \).  
5. Policy Network: Computes \( P_F(a|s_i) \propto \exp(h_i^T v_a) \) over all candidate spans, single tokens (fixed vocabulary \( V \)), and terminal action \( \top \).  
6. Reward Model: Comprises a language model \( p_{LM}(s_n|c) \) and a preference model \( p_{PM}(s_n) \); combined as \( R(s_n) = \exp(\alpha \log p_{LM} + (1-\alpha) \log p_{PM}) \).  
7. GFlowNet Training Loop: Samples trajectories (online from policy or offline from replay buffer), computes subtrajectory balance loss \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{SubTB}} \) on all valid partial sentence pairs, and updates the policy parameters.  

**Critical path**:  
1. Pre‑processing: Build phrase index from corpus; run probabilistic segmentation (Algorithm 2) on all training documents to generate trajectories and fill the replay buffer \( B \).  
2. Reward Model Training: Fine‑tune GPT‑2 on the training corpus for \( p_{LM} \). Train \( p_{PM} \) via Bradley–Terry using human references and negatives sampled from an initial policy (e.g., fine‑tuned GPT‑2 without GFlowNets).  
3. Policy Pre‑fine‑tuning: First training epoch uses only offline trajectories from \( B \) to initialize \( P_F \).  
4. Main Training: Epoch 2+ mixes online sampling (80%) and offline replay (20%). For each batch, construct a dynamic vocabulary: fixed token vocab + phrases present in the batch + top‑128 retrieved spans. Compute \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{SubTB}} \) and update the policy.  
5. Inference: Given a test prefix, retrieve top‑1024 documents, extract candidate spans, and run the policy (greedy or nucleus with \( p=0.95 \)) to sample spans until the terminal action is drawn. For QA, compute option likelihood by summing probabilities over all valid span decompositions via dynamic programming.  

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Efficiency vs. Diversity*: Single retrieval per prefix reduces latency but limits diversity; FoSS mitigates via DAG exploration, yet the single‑retrieval bottleneck remains a limitation.  
- *DAG Complexity vs. Training Stability*: A richer DAG (more overlapping paths) increases exploration potential but also introduces higher variance in GFlowNet updates. The hybrid training regime and subtrajectory objective are designed to maintain stability; ablations show that removing either the DAG structure or the hybrid regime harms performance.  
- *Reward Component Weighting*: The mixing coefficient α must be tuned to balance LM fluency and preference diversity. Poor choices (extremes or mismatched PM) can cause reward hacking, either producing incoherent text (low α) or repetitive outputs (high α).  

**Failure signatures**:  
- **Poor retrieval quality**: Irrelevant documents introduce noise into the dynamic vocabulary, leading to low MAUVE scores.  
- **Replay buffer degeneracy**: If offline segmentation thresholds are too narrow, trajectories lack diversity and the DAG collapses toward a tree, causing a sharp drop in performance (e.g., MAUVE ~29 in w/o‑DAG ablation).  
- **Reward model collapse**: An ineffective preference model fails to steer away from the LM’s generic outputs, resulting in a policy that reverts to LM‑only behavior and lower MAUVE.  
- **Training instability**: Random online sampling without offline initialization may not produce enough high‑reward trajectories; monitor average trajectory reward to detect this.  

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Validate DAG Formation**: Run the offline segmentation algorithm on 100 training documents. Build the state‑action graph from the resulting trajectories and compute the fraction of non‑initial nodes with indegree > 1. A value significantly above 0 confirms a DAG.  
2. **Component Ablation (Mini)**: Train three small models on a subset of data for one epoch: (a) full FoSS, (b) FoSS without preference model, (c) FoSS without language model. Evaluate MAUVE on a held‑out set. Expect the full model to outperform both ablations.  
3. **Retrieval Sensitivity**: Fix all other hyperparameters and vary the number of retrieved documents k (128, 512, 1024). Measure MAUVE and inference latency. MAUVE should increase with k up to a point, while latency grows linearly.  

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can FoSS improve diversity by increasing retrieval frequency (e.g., retrieving at multiple generation steps) without incurring prohibitive latency overhead?  
- How do the early‑stopping thresholds in the DAG‑inducing probabilistic span segmentation algorithm affect the resulting DAG’s structure (branching factor, path overlap) and, consequently, generation quality?  
- Would alternative GFlowNet training objectives—detailed balance or flow matching—offer better training stability or generation quality compared to the subtrajectory balance objective used in FoSS?  

## Limitations
- Segmentation hyperparameters (early‑stopping probabilities, minimum/maximum phrase lengths) are not reported, yet they crucially determine DAG density and thus generalization.  
- Preference model training details (negative sampling strategy, Bradley‑Terry margin, centering weight) and the reward mixing coefficient α are unspecified, hindering precise replication.  
- The implementation of the backward policy for enumerating suffixes in the DAG (required for flow matching detailed balance) is computationally unclear and may become a bottleneck for larger state spaces.  

## Confidence
- **DAG generalization claim**: High – Table 4 (w/o DAG) shows a large MAUVE drop, confirming necessity.  
- **Hybrid training claim**: Medium – The paper asserts the initial offline fine‑tuning is crucial but provides no direct ablation; its effectiveness likely depends on offline data quality, which is not quantified.  
- **Reward model claim**: High – Ablations removing either LM or PM components both reduce MAUVE, validating their complementary roles.
