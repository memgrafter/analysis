# ESG-Bench: Benchmarking Long-Context ESG Reports for Hallucination Mitigation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.13154
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13154
- **Reference Count:** 18
- **Primary Result:** A 4-step Chain-of-Thought (CoT) approach achieves up to 96.00% balanced accuracy on ESG-Bench, substantially reducing both additive and omissive hallucinations compared to standard prompting and direct fine-tuning, with transfer gains to HaluEval and BioASQ.

## Executive Summary
ESG-Bench introduces a benchmark for evaluating and mitigating hallucinations in LLM analysis of lengthy ESG reports. It provides human-annotated QA pairs with fine-grained hallucination labels and proposes a three-stage method: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), task-specific CoT prompting at inference, and CoT-annotated fine-tuning. The 4-step CoT template forces explicit evidence retrieval and answerability judgment, leading to state-of-the-art performance in balancing accurate extraction (WA) and conservative abstention (WoA). Results demonstrate that structured reasoning internalized via fine-tuning effectively reduces hallucinations and transfers to general QA benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The core method uses a three-stage training pipeline on the ESG-Bench dataset, which contains long-context passages from ESG reports (average 2,604 tokens, up to 46,562), questions, and human labels (correct, hallucination, incomplete, answer not found). First, SFT establishes basic grounding. Second, a 4-step CoT template ("Identify topic → Search report → Determine answerability → Generate answer") is applied at inference to enforce evidence verification. Third, CoT fine-tuning trains the model on examples annotated with intermediate rationales (topic, report summary, answerability), using GPT-4o’s binary groundedness judgment as a scalable proxy supervision signal for the answerable label. Models (LLaMA, Gemma, Mistral) are evaluated on WA/WoA accuracy, balanced accuracy, and F1.

## Key Results
- The 4-step CoT fine-tuning dramatically reduces hallucinations, achieving up to 96.00% balanced accuracy on ESG-Bench (LLaMA) and high F1 scores.
- It significantly improves WoA (abstention) capability, addressing omissive hallucinations better than standard SFT or 2-step CoT.
- Performance gains transfer to general hallucination and QA benchmarks (HaluEval, BioASQ), demonstrating robustness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Task-specific Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting reduces hallucinations by forcing the model to explicitly verify evidence before answering. The 4-step CoT template structurally separates evidence retrieval from conclusion, reducing reliance on parametric knowledge that may conflict with the source document. The core assumption is that LLMs can follow structured templates to perform explicit evidence search when guided by intermediate steps. Evidence includes the abstract's description of evidence grounding and the detailed 4-step template in the "Strategies" section. The mechanism fails if the model cannot retrieve relevant passages due to context length limits or if intermediate steps introduce new hallucinations.

### Mechanism 2
Progressive training—from supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to CoT fine-tuning—internalizes structured reasoning, making hallucination mitigation more robust than prompting alone. SFT initializes factual grounding; CoT prompting introduces deliberate evidence checking; CoT fine-tuning entrenches this multi-step process into the model's weights, improving consistency across answerable and unanswerable cases. The core assumption is that learning from explicit CoT rationales improves factual accuracy more than learning only from final answers. Evidence: abstract statements on CoT-annotated fine-tuning outperforming alternatives, and Table 2 showing consistent gains from SFT → CoT(2) → CoT(4). Break condition: overfitting on the CoT template structure, causing mechanically correct but contextually irrelevant reasoning chains.

### Mechanism 3
Using GPT-4o's post-hoc "groundedness judgment" (yes/no) as a proxy supervision signal enables scalable annotation without additional human labeling. Instead of labeling every training example's intermediate steps, GPT-4o's self-assessment ("can the answer be found in the background?") provides a binary label to train models to predict groundedness. The core assumption is that GPT-4o's judgment is a reliable proxy for human labels, validated by agreement. Evidence: abstract claim, confusion matrices showing 80.4% human agreement and 83.7% internal consistency. Break condition: misalignment with human judgment for certain ESG sub-domains or complex multi-modal evidence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on QA Data**  
  *Why needed:* Baseline method to teach the model to extract answers from provided context. Phase 1 uses SFT to establish initial grounding before introducing explicit reasoning.  
  *Quick check:* What is the key limitation of standard SFT that later methods aim to overcome? *(Answer: It does not make the reasoning process transparent or explicitly teach the model to assess answerability.)*

- **Concept: Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**  
  *Why needed:* Core technique to decompose the QA task into intermediate steps that encourage evidence checking. The paper designs task-specific CoT templates for long ESG documents.  
  *Quick check:* How does the paper's 4-step CoT differ from standard zero-shot CoT in its approach to hallucination mitigation? *(Answer: It explicitly includes a "search the report" step and a binary "answerable" judgment, forcing retrieval and verification.)*

- **Concept: Hallucination Taxonomy (Additive vs. Omissive)**  
  *Why needed:* The evaluation framework distinguishes between generating unsupported information (additive) and failing to answer when evidence exists (omissive). This dual focus shapes training and metrics (WA vs. WoA).  
  *Quick check:* Why is measuring both WA and WoA accuracy critical for ESG applications? *(Answer: ESG analysis requires both accurate extraction and conservative abstention when data is missing to avoid misleading stakeholders.)*

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Collection → Human Annotation → CoT Rationale Annotation → Model Training (SFT → CoT-FT with proxy supervision) → Evaluation

**Critical path:**  
1. Collect ESG reports and generate questions.  
2. Annotate answers and hallucination labels (human).  
3. Annotate subset with CoT rationales (topic, summary, answerable).  
4. Train: SFT on (context, question, answer); then CoT-FT on (context, question, CoT steps) using GPT-4o proxy for answerable label.  
5. Evaluate on ESG-Bench and transfer benchmarks (HaluEval, BioASQ).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **CoT Step Count:** 4-step vs. 2-step. More steps improve WA/WoA balance but increase inference cost and risk of errors in intermediate steps (e.g., incorrect topic extraction).  
- **Proxy vs. Human Labels:** Using GPT-4o's judgments scales training but introduces potential bias from the proxy model's own hallucinations; mitigated by validating agreement (~80%).  
- **Dataset Scope:** ESG-Bench focuses on text/table/pdf reports, not multimodal graphics, limiting generalizability to fully multi-modal ESG documents.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low WoA accuracy: model hallucinates answers when no answer exists, indicating failure to internalize abstention.  
- Large WA-WoA gap: model is either over-confident or overly conservative, showing imbalance.  
- Degraded transfer performance: gains on ESG-Bench do not appear on HaluEval/BioASQ, suggesting overfitting to ESG-specific patterns.  
- CoT step inconsistency: intermediate steps (e.g., report summary) contain unsupported details, propagating errors.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Establishment:** Fine-tune each model (LLaMA, Gemma, Mistral) with standard SFT on ESG-Bench (no CoT). Evaluate WA/WoA accuracy to quantify initial hallucination levels.  
2. **CoT Prompting Ablation:** Apply 2-step and 4-step CoT prompts at inference to the SFT model without additional fine-tuning. Measure improvement in WoA accuracy and F1.  
3. **CoT Fine-Tuning:** Fine-tune the model on ESG-Bench examples with CoT rationales (using proxy supervision for answerable label). Compare 4-step CoT fine-tuned model against experiment 1 (SFT) and 2 (prompted CoT) to isolate the effect of internalized reasoning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does framing ESG report analysis strictly as a single-turn QA task with verifiability constraints fully capture the complexity of real-world ESG assessment needs, such as comparative analysis across reports or synthesis of qualitative narratives?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly frames the problem as "ESG report analysis as a QA task with verifiability constraints" and builds ESG-Bench as a QA dataset, noting this enables "systematic evaluation of LLMs' ability to extract and reason over ESG content." However, it also acknowledges ESG reporting involves "qualitative data" and "multi-modal processing," suggesting broader analytical demands beyond extractive QA.  
- **Why unresolved:** The benchmark and methods are designed for single-document, evidence-grounded QA. They do not evaluate performance on tasks requiring integration across multiple reports, temporal trend analysis, or summarization of unstructured narratives—common in ESG auditing and investment analysis.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing complementary benchmarks or augmenting ESG-Bench with tasks like cross-report comparison, longitudinal analysis, or multi-document synthesis, then evaluating whether CoT-based hallucination mitigation generalizes to these more complex ESG workflows.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How scalable and consistent is the human annotation process for hallucination labels in ESG-Bench when applied to a larger diversity of ESG reports across more industries, languages, and regulatory frameworks?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper reports inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s Kappa) ranging from 68.89% to 86.67% across three annotator groups, with variation attributed to annotator expertise. The dataset construction relied on a limited pool of PhD-level annotators familiar with GRI standards for 94 reports. This suggests potential scalability challenges in terms of cost, annotator training, and consistency when expanding to more diverse or niche ESG disclosures.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze how annotation consistency might vary with report complexity, industry-specific jargon, or non-English reports. The current setup may not reflect the variability encountered in global ESG reporting practices.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting a larger-scale annotation study with a more diverse annotator pool (including industry practitioners) across a wider sample of reports, measuring agreement stratified by industry, report length, and conceptual complexity. Analyzing the effect of annotator background on hallucination labels would clarify robustness.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does the 4-step CoT’s superiority over the 2-step CoT and other methods stem from the specific sequence of reasoning steps (topic extraction → report search → answerability → answer) versus simply increasing the number of intermediate reasoning steps?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper introduces two CoT templates: a 2-step ("determine answerable → answer") and a 4-step ("identify topic → search report → determine answerable → answer"). Results show the 4-step CoT fine-tuning consistently achieves the highest balanced accuracy and F1 scores across models and datasets. However, the design is not ablated—the 4-step structure is treated as a whole.  
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether each added step contributes uniquely to grounding (e.g., explicit topic anchoring might be crucial for long-context retrieval) or whether any multi-step structured reasoning would yield similar gains. This limits theoretical understanding of what reasoning patterns best mitigate hallucinations in long documents.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study systematically removing or reordering steps in the 4-step CoT (e.g., removing topic extraction, reversing order) during fine-tuning and evaluation. Comparing performance would isolate the contribution of each step’s semantic function.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the CoT-based hallucination mitigation approach be effectively extended to the multimodal elements commonly found in ESG reports (e.g., charts, tables, images) without sacrificing the gains observed on textual contexts?  
- **Basis in paper:** The introduction identifies "multi-modal processing" as a key challenge for LLMs analyzing ESG reports, noting they "typically feature a mix of text, tables and graphics." However, ESG-Bench and all experiments focus exclusively on textual contexts (QA pairs grounded in text passages), and the CoT prompting strategy is designed for textual evidence search and reasoning.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not address whether the CoT framework—which instructs models to "search the report for sentences or paragraphs"—can be adapted to reason over non-textual modalities. Hallucinations may manifest differently when interpreting visual data, and the evidence grounding mechanism may need redesign.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending ESG-Bench to include multimodal QA pairs where answers require interpreting tables or charts. Developing a multimodal CoT template (e.g., steps for describing visual elements, extracting tabular data) and evaluating hallucination rates compared to text-only baselines.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does the use of GPT-4o’s binary groundedness judgments ("yes/no" on answer support) as proxy supervision for fine-tuning introduce subtle biases or artifacts that limit the generalizability of the resulting models to truly unseen or adversarially crafted ESG queries?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper validates using GPT-4o’s self-assessment ("Given the background, question, and answer, can the answer be found?") as a proxy for human hallucination labels, showing high agreement (~80%). This proxy signal is then used to generate CoT rationales for fine-tuning smaller models. However, the paper acknowledges this is a proxy and does not analyze potential biases in GPT-4o’s own judgments (e.g., overconfidence, stylistic preferences).  
- **Why unresolved:** If GPT-4o’s groundedness judgments systematically diverge from human intuitions in edge cases (e.g., nuanced ESG claims, implicit evidence), models fine-tuned on this proxy may learn a distorted notion of "groundedness." This risks poor performance on queries where GPT-4o’s heuristic fails, especially under distribution shift.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting an error analysis comparing cases where GPT-4o’s judgment disagrees with human annotators in ESG-Bench. Fine-tuning models separately on human-labeled vs. GPT-4o-proxy-labeled data and evaluating on a held-out set with challenging, ambiguous, or adversarial ESG queries to measure divergence in hallucination patterns.

## Limitations
- **U1:** The source of the four-step CoT’s "report summary" (human annotation vs. GPT-4o generation) is unreported, blocking faithful reconstruction of CoT training data.
- **U2:** Long-context handling details are missing; test reports reach 46k tokens while base models natively support ≤8k tokens, so truncation/chunking strategy is unknown and may affect evidence availability.
- **U3:** Train/test split sampling from the larger corpus is not described, making exact WA/WoA balance and reproducibility of the 2,807/300 split uncertain.

## Confidence
- **M1** (Task-specific CoT prompting forces explicit evidence verification): Medium. Supported by ablation gains but assumes attention can be steered; break condition if context length prevents retrieval.
- **M2** (Progressive training internalizes structured reasoning): High. Strong evidence from consistent SFT → CoT fine-tuning improvements across models and both WA/WoA metrics.
- **M3** (GPT-4o groundedness judgments as proxy supervision): Medium. 80.4% human agreement is moderate; risk of systematic bias in specific ESG sub-domains.
