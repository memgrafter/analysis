# ARROW: Augmented Replay for RObust World models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11395
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11395
- **Reference count:** 35
- **Primary result:** ARROW reduces forgetting by over 6x on Atari and achieves near-zero maximum forgetting in two-cycle training.

## Executive Summary
Continual reinforcement learning (CRL) suffers from catastrophic forgetting, often exacerbated by the memory demands of large replay buffers. ARROW introduces a model-based CRL algorithm that augments DreamerV3 with an efficient replay mechanism. It combines a short-term FIFO buffer with a long-term global distribution-matching buffer to preserve task diversity without excessive memory growth. Trained on Atari and Procgen CoinRun benchmarks, ARROW reduces forgetting by up to 6× compared to DreamerV3 and achieves superior worst-case accuracy, demonstrating a better stability-plasticity trade-off.

## Method Summary
ARROW extends the DreamerV3 world model framework with an augmented replay buffer. The buffer maintains two components: a short-term FIFO buffer for recent experiences and a long-term buffer that stores a globally representative subset of all past data via distribution matching (e.g., using Maximum Mean Discrepancy). During world model training, samples are drawn from both buffers, ensuring the model retains knowledge of previous tasks while remaining adaptable to new ones. The policy is then updated using imagined rollouts generated by the world model, decoupling policy learning from the need for fresh environment interactions. This design allows ARROW to combat catastrophic forgetting efficiently in memory-constrained settings.

## Key Results
- On Atari (no shared structure), ARROW reduces forgetting from 1.217 (DreamerV3) to 0.197 and maximum forgetting in two-cycle training from 0.735 to 0.012.
- It achieves the highest worst-case accuracy (WC-ACC) across all tested settings.
- On Procgen CoinRun (shared structure), ARROW attains the best WC-ACC and exhibits negative maximum forgetting (-0.089) in two-cycle training, indicating retention and recovery upon task revisit.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ARROW mitigates catastrophic forgetting by ensuring the world model observes a balanced and diverse distribution of past and present experiences. The short-term FIFO buffer captures recent task-specific data, maintaining plasticity, while the long-term distribution-matching buffer preserves a global snapshot of all encountered tasks, providing stability. This dual mechanism prevents the world model from overwriting previously learned dynamics with new data, which is the root cause of forgetting in standard replay. Moreover, training the policy on imagined rollouts from the robust world model allows the policy to adapt without directly risking performance on past tasks. The result is a superior stability-plasticity trade-off, evidenced by reduced forgetting and higher worst-case accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Environment -> Policy -> Augmented Replay Buffer (FIFO short-term + Global long-term) -> World Model -> Policy (update via imagination)

**Critical path:**  
1. Policy acts in environment, generating transitions (s, a, r, s').  
2. Transitions are added to the short-term FIFO buffer; distribution matching selects a subset to add to the long-term buffer.  
3. World model is trained on minibatches sampled from both buffers.  
4. Policy (actor-critic) is updated using imagined rollouts produced by the world model.  
5. Repeat for each new task.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Buffer size split: Larger long-term improves stability but may reduce plasticity and increase memory/computation overhead.  
- Distribution matching frequency: More frequent updates keep the long-term buffer representative but add computational cost.  
- Sampling strategy: How to combine buffers (uniform, weighted) affects learning dynamics.  
- Forgetting vs. sample efficiency: Over-representation of old data may slow adaptation to new tasks.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Forgetting spike after task switch → long-term buffer not adequately representing previous tasks.  
- World model loss divergence → buffer corruption or distribution mismatch.  
- Policy collapse on old tasks → world model has forgotten old dynamics.  
- Unbounded buffer growth → distribution matching adding too many samples.

**Exactly 3 first experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Comparison:** Train ARROW and DreamerV3 on a fixed Atari sequence (e.g., Atari-100) and compute forgetting and WC-ACC to verify the reported reduction in forgetting.  
2. **Ablation of Long-term Buffer:** Disable the long-term component (use only FIFO) and measure forgetting; expect performance similar to DreamerV3 if the long-term buffer is key.  
3. **Buffer Ratio Sensitivity:** Run ARROW with varying splits (e.g., 25% short-term /75% long-term, 75%/25%) and evaluate forgetting and WC-ACC to understand the trade-off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can strategic, memory-efficient replay to World Models maintain robust continual learning performance while matching the sample efficiency of standard replay buffers?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] This is the central question posed in section 1.3: "Can strategic, memory-efficient replay to World Models enable robust continual learning while retaining the sample efficiency of existing approaches?"
- **Why unresolved:** While ARROW shows improved stability, the paper reports mixed results on sample efficiency compared to DreamerV3—superior in some Atari tasks and orderings but inferior in CoinRun default order and two-cycle settings. The trade-off between memory efficiency and sample efficiency needs systematic investigation across more domains and buffer sizes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comprehensive empirical study varying buffer sizes, task distributions, and environment complexities, measuring both stability metrics (forgetting) and sample efficiency (frames to threshold) across multiple CL benchmarks, to establish conditions under which memory-efficient replay achieves Pareto optimality.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the optimal strategy for allocating memory between short-term and long-term replay buffers in ARROW, and should this allocation be static or adaptive to task characteristics?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in section 6: "A natural extension of this idea would be to test different splits or dynamically allocate memory based on task characteristics." They currently use a fixed 50/50 split.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not explore how varying the proportion of short-term vs. long-term buffer capacity affects performance. Different task sequences (e.g., with vs. without shared structure) might benefit from different splits, and an adaptive scheme could potentially improve upon the fixed allocation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments evaluating ARROW with various fixed splits (e.g., 25/75, 75/25) and potentially adaptive mechanisms (e.g., based on task similarity detection or performance trends) on both Atari and Procgen benchmarks, measuring stability-plasticity metrics and recovery in two-cycle settings.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the augmented replay mechanism in ARROW generalize to continuous control and robotic domains, or is it specific to discrete action spaces and pixel observations?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly state in section 6: "Extending ARROW to continuous control or robotics domains like MuJoCo (Todorov et al., 2012) could further validate the generalization capabilities of our approach."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates on Atari (discrete actions) and Procgen CoinRun (discrete actions). Continuous control domains present different challenges (e.g., higher-dimensional action spaces, different observation types), and it's unclear if the distribution-matching replay strategy would be equally effective.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementing ARROW on standard continuous control benchmarks like MuJoCo or DeepMind Control Suite in continual learning settings (e.g., sequences of related tasks), comparing performance against model-based baselines like DreamerV3 with standard replay, and analyzing whether the stability benefits observed in discrete domains transfer.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why is ARROW's performance so sensitive to task ordering, and can we develop methods to make continual learning algorithms more robust to arbitrary task sequences?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper notes task order effects: in two-cycle training, ARROW shows exceptional recovery but this depends on order; Frostbite shows unusually high performance in DreamerV3 per Appendix G of a referenced paper, and ARROW's performance varies between default and reversed orders (e.g., sample efficiency in CoinRun: DreamerV3 faster in default, similar in reversed). The authors implemented two random sequences to quantify ordering effects but don't deeply investigate causes.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper doesn't analyze why certain orderings (like Frostbite in DreamerV3) lead to outlier performance. Understanding the interaction between task characteristics (e.g., visual complexity, reward scale) and their position in the sequence is crucial for designing robust CL algorithms but remains unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A large-scale study across many random task permutations on Atari and CoinRun, correlating task features (e.g., visual variance, reward magnitude, dynamics complexity) with performance impact when placed in different sequence positions, potentially identifying principles for ordering-invariant continual learning or methods to normalize for ordering effects.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the augmented replay buffer in ARROW be effectively combined with other continual learning techniques (e.g., parameter regularization like EWC, architectural methods like P&C, or behavior cloning like CLEAR) to further reduce forgetting?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors mention in section 6: "Our methods can be used in conjunction with prior state-of-the-art approaches to combating catastrophic forgetting such as EWC and P&C (...), and CLEAR (...)."
- **Why unresolved:** While the authors suggest this combination, no experiments are presented. ARROW addresses replay efficiency at the data level, while EWC/P&C operate on parameters and CLEAR uses model-free replay with behavior cloning. Synergies or conflicts might exist (e.g., EWC might constrain learning of new tasks that ARROW's World Model needs to adapt), and the combined efficacy is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementing hybrid algorithms (e.g., ARROW+EWC, ARROW+CLEAR) and evaluating on the same continual learning benchmarks, measuring whether forgetting is further reduced compared to ARROW alone, and analyzing computational and memory overheads to assess practical viability.

## Limitations
- **Benchmark Scope:** Evaluation is restricted to Atari and Procgen CoinRun; generalization to other domains (e.g., continuous control, robotics) is unknown.
- **Comparison Suite:** Limited comparisons (primarily DreamerV3); lacking evaluation against other dedicated CRL algorithms.
- **Ablation Depth:** No analysis of buffer ratio sensitivity, computational overhead, or impact on world model fidelity vs. policy performance.
