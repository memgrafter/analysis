# Cross-Preference Learning for Sentence-Level and Context-Aware Machine Translation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25183  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25183  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CPL enables a single model to adaptively use context, achieving consistent COMET improvements (average +1.10/+1.12, +0.84/+0.87, +0.60/+0.64 over strongest baselines) and robustness to noisy context without architectural changes.

## Executive Summary
Context-aware machine translation (MT) does not consistently outperform sentence-level MT because the benefit of context varies across sentences. Existing methods lack explicit mechanisms to decide when to use context. This paper introduces Cross-Preference Learning (CPL), a preference-based training framework that integrates intra-condition (within each translation mode) and cross-condition (between sentence-level and context-aware outputs) preferences. CPL allows a single model to learn adaptive context usage—leveraging it when helpful and ignoring it otherwise—without any architectural modifications. Evaluated on six translation directions with Qwen3-4B/8B and Llama-3-8B, CPL achieves substantial and consistent COMET gains over both sentence-level and context-aware baselines, and remains robust when context is noisy (e.g., random sentences), nearly matching pure sentence-level performance.

## Method Summary
CPL constructs preference pairs from multiple translation candidates per input to train a single model for both sentence-level (`p(y|x)`) and context-aware (`p(y|x,c)`) translation. For each condition, two candidates are sampled and scored (using COMET and document-level COMET) to form intra-condition pairs `(y+, y-)`. For cross-condition learning, the best among all four candidates (two per condition) is identified; pairs are formed between this winner and outputs from the losing condition, explicitly teaching the model which input condition (with or without context) is preferable. The combined loss (`L_CPL = L_intra + L_cross`) updates LoRA parameters of a base LLM. Training requires precomputing these preference pairs offline from a validation set using a fixed COMET scorer; at inference, the same model is used for both conditions by simply including or omitting context.

## Key Results
- Consistent COMET improvements across six translation directions (En→De/Es/Fr/It/Nl/Ru): average gains of +1.10/+1.12, +0.84/+0.87, and +0.60/+0.64 over the strongest sentence-level/context-aware baselines for Qwen3-4B/8B and Llama-3-8B respectively.
- Robustness to noisy context: when context is replaced with random sentences, context-aware CPL’s performance drops to nearly identical levels as sentence-level translation, demonstrating effective learned inhibition.
- Enhanced translation quality as measured by auxiliary metrics: improved coherence (SimCSE), faithfulness (ALTI+), and fluency (GPT-based) compared to baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Intra‑condition preference learning
Intra‑condition preferences stabilize and improve translation quality within each mode (sentence‑level or context‑aware). For each condition, two candidates are sampled and scored by COMET; the higher‑scoring candidate is preferred. The Contrastive Preference Optimization (CPO) loss encourages the model to assign higher probability to the better candidate while maintaining likelihood of the preferred output. This refines the model’s output distribution for that specific input condition, ensuring both modes can generate high‑quality translations in isolation. Core assumption: COMET scores reliably rank translation quality. Break condition: if COMET is misaligned with human judgment, the learned preferences become mis‑specified, degrading that condition’s performance.

### Mechanism 2: Cross‑condition preference learning
Cross‑condition preferences force the model to align outputs across conditions, enabling it to learn a shared representation that adaptively exploits context when beneficial. For a source sentence with context, the best translation among four candidates (two per condition) is identified as the winner. The condition that produced this winner is deemed “winning.” Two cross‑pairs are formed: (winner, winner’s counterpart from the losing condition) and (winner, loser from the losing condition). The Cross‑CPO loss compares these pairs with differing inputs (with vs. without context), explicitly teaching the model to trust the winning condition for that sentence type. Core assumption: the condition producing the highest‑scoring translation for a sentence is optimal for similar sentences. Break condition: if the “winning condition” label is noisy due to metric error, the cross‑signal becomes conflicting, hindering development of a coherent conditional strategy.

### Mechanism 3: Unified objective and robustness
The unified CPL objective (`L_CPL = L_intra + L_cross`) creates a single parameterization that inherits strengths from both conditions while mitigating individual failure modes. `L_intra` ensures each condition can produce quality outputs alone; `L_cross` injects signal about which condition is better per instance. Together, they shape internal representations such that context information is selectively integrated—potentially gated or weighted—based on learned patterns, rather than blindly used. This yields robustness: when context is unhelpful or noisy, the model effectively defaults to sentence‑level behavior. Core assumption: the two loss components are complementary and non‑conflicting. Break condition: contradictory gradients between `L_intra` and `L_cross` (e.g., a candidate wins cross‑condition but is low‑reward intra‑condition) could destabilize training or force suboptimal compromises.

  *Why needed:* CPL extends Contrastive Preference Optimization to multiple conditions; understanding single‑condition CPO is prerequisite.  
  *Quick check:* What is the purpose of the `-log pθ(y+|x)` term in the CPO loss (Eq. 1), and how does it differ from a pure contrastive loss?

- **Multi‑Condition Modeling with Shared Parameters**  
  *Why needed:* CPL uses one model for two input configurations (`x` vs. `(x,c)`); engineer must understand gradient interactions.  
  *Quick check:* How are candidates `{y1_s, y2_s}` and `{y1_c, y2_c}` generated, and what ensures they are comparable across conditions?

- **Automatic Metric Scoring for Preference Pair Construction**  
  *Why needed:* All preferences derive from COMET/d‑COMET scores; metric quality directly impacts training signal.  
  *Quick check:* Why use the *average* of sentence‑level and document‑level COMET for intra‑condition preferences, and what might be the risk?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Candidate Generator -> Scorer -> Preference Pair Constructor -> Loss Computer -> Trainer

**Critical path:**  
1. Prepare document‑aligned parallel data; truncate preceding context to 256 tokens.  
2. Offline: For each training instance, generate four translation candidates (two per condition) using base LLM; score all with COMET; filter to build preference sets (`Ps`, `Pc`, `Pcr`).  
3. Training: Sample batches of intra‑ and cross‑condition pairs; compute combined loss `L_CPL`; update LoRA parameters.  
4. Inference: Use same model for both conditions by toggling context in prompt.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Computational cost vs. signal quality: More candidates and better metrics improve preferences but increase offline cost.  
- Metric dependency: Performance tightly coupled to COMET quality; biases may be learned.  
- Loss balance: Default 1:1 weight for intra vs. cross; tuning may help but adds hyperparameters.  
- Noise handling: Cross‑condition pairs vulnerable to COMET errors on individual sentences.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Drop in one condition’s performance indicates imbalance in learned preferences.  
- Lack of robustness on random‑context test suggests cross‑loss failed to teach conditional inhibition.  
- Degradation on new domains if training preference distribution doesn’t match test.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Ablate cross‑loss (train with only `L_intra`). Expect: reduced gains and poorer robustness to noisy context.  
2. Random context robustness test: evaluate context‑aware output with random context; expect performance to drop to sentence‑level baseline.  
3. Metric sensitivity: train using BLEU instead of COMET for pair construction; expect significant performance drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Metric biases in preference construction** – How do COMET’s inherent biases propagate into CPL’s learned preferences and net translation quality? Unresolved because paper does not analyze metric‑specific distortions; requires human‑labeled pairs or multi‑metric comparison.
2. **Computational overhead reduction** – Can candidate sampling/scoring costs be reduced without sacrificing gains? Unresolved due to lack of ablation on sampling strategies.
3. **Long‑context discourse handling** – How does CPL perform with >256‑token contexts and complex discourse phenomena (e.g., multi‑sentence coreference)? Unresolved as experiments used fixed 256‑token window on standard datasets.
4. **Generalization across languages/models** – Is CPL effective for non‑European languages and encoder‑decoder architectures? Unresolved due to limited language pairs and decoder‑only LLMs tested.

## Limitations
- **Metric dependency:** All preference pairs rely on COMET; misalignment with human preferences could mis‑specify training signal.
- **Candidate sampling opacity:** Details like temperature, top‑p, and number of candidates are not fully described, hindering exact replication.
- **Generalization gap:** Evaluation restricted to news commentary; performance in other genres or with very long documents is unknown.

## Confidence
- CPL improves translation quality (consistent COMET gains): **High**  
- Intra‑condition preferences stabilize quality: **Medium**  
- Cross‑condition preferences enable adaptive context use: **Medium**  
- Robustness to noisy context: **High**
