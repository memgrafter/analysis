# Routing Sensitivity Without Controllability: A Diagnostic Study of Fairness in MoE Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27141
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27141
- **Reference count:** 8
- **Primary result:** Demographic routing sensitivity in MoE models does not guarantee fairness control; only OLMoE achieves robust bias reduction but at a significant utility cost, and the improvement does not transfer to generated text.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a diagnostic study of fairness in Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) language models through the FARE framework. FARE profiles experts that are sensitive to demographic content and then adaptively reweights their routing distributions, aiming to reduce stereotype preferences without altering model weights. The authors apply FARE to five diverse MoE architectures (OLMoE, Mixtral, DeepSeekMoE, Qwen1.5‑MoE, Qwen3). The results reveal three distinct controllability regimes: three models (Mixtral, Qwen1.5, Qwen3) exhibit no significant bias reduction; DeepSeekMoE shows a suggestive but non‑robust effect; and only OLMoE achieves a robust, statistically significant decrease in CrowS‑Pairs bias (−4.4%p, p<0.001). However, this success comes at a substantial utility cost (−6.3%p TruthfulQA) and does not translate into reduced bias in generated text, as measured by three independent protocols. Expert masking experiments on OLMoE further demonstrate that fairness‑sensitive experts are collectively critical for core knowledge—masking the top‑10 sensitive experts reproduces the full utility loss. The study concludes that demographic routing sensitivity, while necessary, is insufficient for effective stereotype control, and that improvements in log‑likelihood bias metrics do not guarantee better behavior in open‑ended generation.

## Method Summary
The FARE pipeline operates as a post‑hoc intervention. First, for each MoE layer, the authors extract routing logs (pre‑softmax logits, probabilities, expert masks) from both neutral and demographic prompts. From these logs they compute an expert fairness sensitivity score φ(e,l) using the Fairness Sensitivity Profiling (FSP) method, which aggregates three measurements: Activation Rate Difference (ARD), Jensen‑Shannon Divergence (JSD), and Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI). Next, Architecture‑Aware Layer Selection (AALS) probes each layer’s fairness‑efficiency ratio R(l) = Δbias / |ΔPPL| using a fixed probe λ=1.0 on a validation set and selects layers with R(l) above the 75th percentile for intervention. The Adaptive Routing Reweighting (ARR) then modifies the router logits of the selected layers as z' = z − λ·φ_l, where λ is tuned via grid search to minimize |CrowS‑Pairs(λ) − 0.5| subject to a maximum allowed increase in perplexity (β ≤ 1.0). The final intervention is evaluated on test sets using bias metrics (CrowS‑Pairs, StereoSet, BBQ), utility metrics (TruthfulQA, perplexity), and generation protocols (BOLD toxicity, StereoSet generation, stereotype completion). This approach avoids weight fine‑tuning and directly manipulates the routing bottleneck.

## Key Results
- Three MoE architectures (Mixtral, Qwen1.5, Qwen3) show no statistically significant stereotype reduction via routing perturbation.
- DeepSeekMoE shows a non‑robust trend (CrowS‑Pairs −2.0%p, pBH=0.17).
- OLMoE achieves robust bias reduction (−4.4%p, p<0.001) but incurs substantial utility cost (−6.3%p TruthfulQA), with no transfer to generation metrics; masking experiments reveal that fairness‑sensitive experts are collectively knowledge‑critical.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Demographic content modulates routing distributions across multiple experts, creating a measurable "fairness sensitivity" signal via the FSP profile.
- **Mechanism:** FARE quantifies routing shifts using three metrics: Activation Rate Difference (ARD), Jensen‑Shannon Divergence (JSD), and Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI). These are normalized per layer and aggregated (weights: ARD=1.0, JSD=0.5, PMI=0.3) into an expert‑layer sensitivity score φ(e,l). This score identifies experts that change activation probabilities when processing demographic vs. neutral prompts. The approach assumes these shifts are semantically meaningful rather than tokenization artifacts, supported by analysis on a length‑matched subset.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract ("demographic prompts produce systematic routing shifts"); Section 3.3 defines the metrics; indirect support from neighboring work on MoE routing (average FMR 0.394).
- **Break condition:** If φ(e,l) scores do not correlate with bias metrics like CrowS‑Pairs across architectures, the profiling fails to isolate bias‑relevant experts.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Perturbation breadth matters; targeting a sufficient number of experts is necessary for bias reduction, but the threshold is architecture‑specific.
- **Mechanism:** ARR applies a penalty based on φ(l). Synthetic ablation on OLMoE (Table 4) shows that targeting very few experts (top‑5) yields negligible bias reduction, while broader targeting (top‑50+) yields substantial shifts. This pattern does not generalize (e.g., Mixtral, Qwen3 show null effects even at maximum breadth).
- **Core assumption:** Bias representations are distributed across a non‑trivial subset of experts; isolating a tiny "bias module" is not feasible.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.2 ("Perturbation breadth is critical: targeting 5 experts yields −0.3%p, while 50+ experts produce −4.7%p"); Table 4 comparative results; neighbor papers on routing sparsity provide indirect context.
- **Break condition:** If increasing target breadth consistently fails to produce bias reduction in a given architecture, that architecture’s routing mechanism may not encode bias in a manipulable distributed form.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Group‑level bias–knowledge entanglement explains the utility cost: fairness‑sensitive experts collectively encode knowledge‑critical functions, so disrupting them degrades general capability.
- **Mechanism:** Expert masking on OLMoE (Table 2) shows that masking the top‑10 φ experts reproduces FARE’s full utility cost (−6.3%p TQA), while masking the bottom‑10 causes minimal harm (−0.3%p). This indicates that the set of experts most sensitive to demographics is also indispensable for core knowledge tasks.
- **Core assumption:** Utility cost from routing perturbation stems primarily from silencing knowledge‑critical experts, not from generic disruption.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.2 ("masking the top‑10 φ experts reproduces FARE’s full utility cost"); abstract ("bias and core knowledge are deeply entangled within expert groups"); cross‑domain evidence from Fair‑MoE (Wang et al. 2025b) suggests entanglement is a broader concern.
- **Break condition:** If masking the top‑φ experts does *not* cause disproportionate utility loss compared to random or bottom‑φ masking, then entanglement is not the primary cost driver.

## Foundational Learning
### Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) routing specialization
- **Why needed here:** Understanding that MoE models use a learned router to dynamically activate parameter‑efficient expert subsets per token is fundamental. FARE intervenes at this discrete routing bottleneck, unlike dense‑model methods.
- **Quick check question:** "If you modify the router's logits before top‑k selection, what happens to the activated expert set and the final output projection?"

### Stereotype evaluation via minimal‑pair log‑likelihood (e.g., CrowS‑Pairs)
- **Why needed here:** FARE optimizes against CrowS‑Pairs preference, which compares model probabilities for stereotypical vs. anti‑stereotypical sentence pairs. This metric is known to sometimes diverge from generated text behavior, so understanding its limitations is crucial.
- **Quick check question:** "Why might a model show improved CrowS‑Pairs scores (reduced stereotype preference) but still generate stereotypical text?"

### Statistical significance testing with multiple‑comparison correction (p<sub>BH</sub>)
- **Why needed here:** The paper stresses robust vs. non‑robust results (e.g., DeepSeek p<sub>BH</sub>=0.17). Uncorrected p‑values can be misleading when testing multiple models/benchmarks.
- **Quick check question:** "If you test 5 independent hypotheses at α=0.05, what is the family‑wise error rate? How does the Benjamini‑Hochberg procedure differ?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Router:** Gating module (linear layer + softmax) producing expert selection probabilities per token.
  2. **Experts:** Feed‑forward networks (often identical) that process the token if selected.
  3. **Top‑k selection:** Discretizes router output; only k experts active per token/layer.
  4. **Shared experts (optional):** Always‑active experts (e.g., DeepSeekMoE, Qwen1.5) that act as a disruption buffer.
  5. **FSP Profiler:** Computes φ(e,l) from routing logs using ARD, JSD, PMI.
  6. **AALS Layer Selector:** Probes each layer's fairness‑efficiency ratio R(l) to pick intervention layers.
  7. **ARR Perturbation:** Applies `z' = z - λ·φ_l` to router logits of selected layers; shared experts remain unchanged.
- **Critical path:** (Prompt Input) → Router Logits (extracted) → FSP φ‑profile offline → AALS selects layers → ARR modifies logits at those layers → New Top‑k → Expert outputs → Dense projection → Next layer/router.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Soft vs. Hard reweighting:** ARR's soft penalty allows partial activation, causing less perplexity spike than expert masking but may require broader λ tuning.
  - **Layer selection:** AALS (data‑driven) vs. fixed middle‑layers (heuristic). AALS finds layer‑specific peaks (e.g., DeepSeekMoE layer 1).
  - **FSP metric composition:** ARD alone is strongest but composite (ARD+JSD+PMI) improves stability.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. **Null regime:** λ tuning yields no significant ΔCrowS even at high λ (Mixtral, Qwen1.5, Qwen3). Likely causes: too few experts, dispersed sensitivity, reverse‑layer sensitivity.
  2. **Likelihood–generation disconnect:** Significant CrowS reduction but null generation metrics (BOLD, StereoSet gen). Indicates intervention affects token probabilities but not final text output.
  3. **High utility cost:** Robust CrowS reduction accompanied by large TQA/perplexity drop (OLMoE). Suggests strong entanglement.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Routing extraction replication:** Feed 100 neutral/demographic prompt pairs through the model with hooks to log pre‑softmax logits and selected expert masks for all MoE layers. Verify that demographic prompts produce higher JSD between routing distributions than neutral vs. neutral pairs.
  2. **FSP profile sanity check:** Compute φ(e,l) on a held‑out slice. Confirm that top‑φ experts at a high‑R(l) layer show visibly different activation heatmaps for demographic vs. neutral prompts.
  3. **ARR sweep:** For a single AALS‑selected layer, sweep λ from 0 to λ_max. Plot ΔCrowS vs. ΔTQA (or ΔPPL) to observe the bias–utility curve and locate the Pareto frontier. Expect non‑monotonic or flat trends in null architectures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can hybrid interventions overcome the bias-utility trade-off?** The paper's conclusion calls for combining routing with decoding/representation interventions, but no such hybrid approaches have been explored. Evidence: hybrid strategies are explicitly suggested as future work.
2. **Are the three architectural design conditions (shared buffers, perturbation breadth thresholds, generation evaluation) generalizable beyond the studied models?** The conditions are post‑hoc hypotheses derived from five architectures only; broader validation across diverse MoE families is needed. Evidence: implications state this need.
3. **Is bias-knowledge entanglement a universal property of MoE routing?** The entanglement finding relies solely on OLMoE; other models might not exhibit the same pattern. Evidence: limitations note group‑level masking evidence only from OLMoE.
4. **Why do log‑likelihood preference improvements from routing perturbation fail to transfer to decoded generation?** The mechanism is unclear; hypotheses involve MoE’s shared dense layers but lack empirical confirmation. Evidence: Section 5.4 reports robust likelihood shifts but null effects across three generation protocols.

## Limitations
- Restricted to five MoE architectures, limiting generalizability of the three controllability regimes.
- Exact demographic prompt templates, vocabulary, and validation/test splits are undisclosed, hindering precise replication.
- Evaluation relies heavily on log‑likelihood bias metrics (e.g., CrowS‑Pairs), which may not reflect human perceptions or actual generation outcomes; the robust likelihood‑generation disconnect suggests a fundamental limitation.

## Confidence
- **Controllability regimes (sensitivity ≠ controllability):** Medium
- **FARE bias-reduction efficacy:** Low
- **Entanglement of fairness‑sensitive experts with core knowledge:** Medium
- **Likelihood‑generation disconnect:** High (OLMoE)

## Next Checks
1. **Exact replication with original prompts:** Acquire the authors’ full demographic prompt set and splits, recompute φ(e,l) profiles, and rerun ARR to verify the reported regimes and OLMoE’s bias reduction.
2. **Cross‑architecture generalization:** Apply the FARE pipeline to at least three additional MoE models (e.g., Google’s GLaM, Meta’s MoE variants) to test whether the three controllability regimes and entanglement patterns hold broadly.
3. **Focused entanglement analysis on OLMoE:** Ablate perturbed expert subsets (top‑k, random) and evaluate impacts on a diverse knowledge suite (TruthfulQA, MMLU, HellaSwag) to identify which knowledge domains are most critical and whether selective routing can alleviate the utility cost.
