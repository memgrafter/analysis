# Implicit neural representations for larval zebrafish brain microscopy: a reproducible benchmark on the MapZebrain atlas
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26811
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26811
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** Explicit spectral (Fourier) and multiscale (Haar) positional encodings preserve high-frequency neuroanatomical detail (sharp boundaries, thin neurites) significantly better than smooth-biased alternatives (SIREN, Grid) under a deterministic column-wise hold-out benchmark on the MapZebrain atlas.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes a reproducible benchmark for implicit neural representations (INRs) on the challenging task of compressing high-resolution larval zebrafish brain microscopy from the MapZebrain atlas. The core problem is preserving fine neuroanatomical structures like sharp region borders and thin neuronal processes. The authors implement a strict, seed-controlled evaluation protocol across 950 grayscale images, using a deterministic 40% column-wise hold-out to test spatial generalization. Four INR variants are compared: SIREN, Fourier features, Haar wavelet encoding, and a multi-resolution grid. Results show Fourier and Haar encodings achieve the highest macro-averaged PSNR (~26 dB), best SSIM (0.689), and lowest edge error (0.183), demonstrating superior fidelity for critical boundaries. SIREN performs poorly on most metrics due to spectral bias but remains competitive on area-weighted micro averages in heterogeneous datasets. The work highlights the importance of frequency-structured priors for high-fidelity neuroanatomical reconstruction and provides a unified framework for future comparison.

## Method Summary
The benchmark uses 950 grayscale microscopy images from the MapZebrain atlas (227 curated "regions" slices, 723 "all-in-one" files). Each image undergoes per-image normalization to [0,1] using the 1st and 99th percentiles computed from a random 10% sample of pixels *only* in the future training columns—a crucial step to avoid data leakage. A deterministic column-wise hold-out mask along the X-axis (40% of columns reserved for testing) is generated per image; in the "all-in-one" regime, this mask is derived deterministically from the file path hash combined with a global seed. All four INR models share a common small MLP backbone (width 192, depth 4) after their specific positional encoding layers. Models are trained for 10 epochs using AdamW (lr=1e-3, wd=1e-6) with Smooth-L1 loss (β=0.01), sampling ~10% of coordinates exclusively from the training columns. Evaluation is performed on the full grid of held-out test columns, reporting macro-averaged and area-weighted micro-averaged PSNR, SSIM (11x11 Gaussian), and edge-focused MAE (mean absolute error on the top 10% Sobel edge pixels).

## Key Results
- Fourier features and Haar encoding achieve the highest macro-averaged PSNR on held-out columns (approximately 26 dB), significantly outperforming the multi-resolution Grid (≈24 dB) and SIREN (≈14 dB).
- Fourier features also achieve the best SSIM (0.689) and the lowest edge-focused MAE (0.183), confirming superior preservation of sharp neuropil boundaries and thin processes.
- SIREN's smooth inductive bias leads to strong attenuation of high-frequency detail, but it remains competitive on area-weighted micro averages, particularly in the heterogeneous "all-in-one" dataset regime.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Explicit Frequency & Multiscale Priors Capture Detail
- **Claim:** Explicit spectral (Fourier) and multiscale (Haar) positional encodings better preserve high-frequency neuroanatomical detail (sharp boundaries, thin neurites) than smooth-biased alternatives (SIREN, Grid) because they directly inject frequency-structured priors into the coordinate mapping.
- **Mechanism:** Fourier features map coordinates into a high-dimensional sinusoidal basis, explicitly representing a wide spectrum of frequencies and mitigating the innate low-frequency preference (spectral bias) of standard MLPs. Haar encoding provides piecewise-constant basis functions at multiple scales, offering a discrete multiresolution prior that captures localized edges and textures. Both avoid the "spectral bias" where networks with smooth activations or dense grid lookups converge to smoother solutions.
- **Core assumption:** The larval zebrafish microscopy data contains critical signal content at high spatial frequencies (sub-micron neurites, sharp region borders), and an effective INR must represent these frequencies accurately under spatial generalization.
- **Evidence anchors:** Paper abstract states explicit encodings better capture high-frequency detail; Section 5 shows Fourier/Haar best preserve neuropil layering and sharp borders while SIREN attenuates high-frequency detail.

### Mechanism 2: Column-Wise Hold-Out Probes Anisotropic Generalization
- **Claim:** The deterministic 40% column-wise hold-out along the X-axis effectively probes an INR's ability to generalize across contiguous spatial gaps—a critical failure mode for mosaic and sparse-view microscopy—rather than testing pixel-wise memorization.
- **Mechanism:** Holding out entire vertical columns tests interpolation across anisotropic, structured missing data. Models with smooth inductive biases (SIREN, Grid) can overfit to local training patterns and fail to interpolate coherently across the gap, causing blurring. Encodings with broader frequency support (Fourier) or multiscale continuity (Haar) maintain structural coherence.
- **Core assumption:** Neuroanatomical structures are spatially contiguous and anisotropic; generalization across linear gaps is a realistic proxy for registration errors in stitched mosaics or incomplete acquisitions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions spatial generalization is tested with column-wise hold-out; Section 3.4 describes the deterministic split; Section 6 notes this emphasizes interpolation across anisotropic gaps.

### Mechanism 3: Per-Image Normalization Stabilizes Training but Skews Metrics
- **Claim:** The per-image (1,99) percentile normalization stabilizes training by suppressing near-constant background regions, but it artificially inflates PSNR metrics for low-variance atlas slices, necessitating robust statistics alongside macro averages.
- **Mechanism:** Percentile clipping rescales each image's dynamic range based on its empirical distribution, preventing bright structures from dominating the loss and aiding convergence on dim regions. However, for images where the non-outlier range is narrow, normalization yields near-constant target fields, leading to artificially perfect reconstruction (≥100 dB PSNR) that misrepresents performance on biologically textured data.
- **Core assumption:** The microscopy data exhibits heavy-tailed intensity distributions with large dark regions; normalization must be locally adaptive but distorts global metric averages.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.4 specifies the normalization; Appendix Tables 6,7,9 show PSNR outliers (≥100 dB) only in the "regions" regime corresponding to nearly constant fields, causing macro means to substantially exceed medians.

## Foundational Learning

  - **Why needed:** INRs represent a signal as a continuous function mapping spatial coordinates to intensity values via a neural network, enabling super-resolution, compression, and spatial generalization beyond discrete pixel grids.

- **Concept: Spectral Bias**
  - **Why needed:** The paper's central comparison hinges on how different encodings handle spectral bias—the tendency of MLPs with smooth activations to fit low-frequency components first, attenuating high-frequency details like neurites.

- **Concept: Evaluation Metrics for Spatial Generalization**

## Architecture Onboarding
Input (x,y) in [0,1]² -> Positional Encoding Layer (one of: SIREN, Fourier, Haar, Grid) -> Shared MLP (e.g., [192,4]) -> Output Intensity

**Critical path:**
1.  Generate deterministic column-wise hold-out mask (40% of X columns) for each image using specified seeding.
2.  Compute per-image (1,99) percentiles **only** from pixels in the training columns; normalize entire image to [0,1].
3.  For each INR variant, sample ~10% of coordinates from training columns; train for 10 epochs with AdamW and Smooth-L1 loss.
4.  After training, query the model on the full [0,1]² grid; evaluate metrics solely on the held-out test columns.
5.  Aggregate macro (per-image mean) and micro (area-weighted pooled) statistics across 950 images.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Fourier vs. Haar:** Fourier yields smoother transitions but may blur finest details; Haar preserves sharp edges but risks blocking artifacts at basis boundaries. Choose Haar for absolute edge fidelity (e.g., registration), Fourier for balanced detail.
- **SIREN:** Minimal parameters, fast convergence, but poor high-frequency capture. Only suitable if smooth background modeling suffices.
- **Grid:** Efficient but shows axis-aligned blurring under column hold-out; performance sensitive to hash table size and resolution schedule.

**Failure signatures:**
- **SIREN:** Globally smooth reconstructions; missing punctate neurite texture; high edge MAE relative to SSIM.
- **Grid:** Vertical banding aligned with training columns; severe blur in held-out zones; low PSNR, especially in "all-in-one" regime.
- **Haar:** Soft, block-like seams at Haar basis transitions; structured oscillations in MAE, particularly across region boundaries.
- **All models:** Catastrophic drop in test-column PSNR if percentiles are computed using *all* pixels (data leakage).

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Sanity Check:** Run all four INR types on one high-contrast atlas slice. Verify Haar/Fourier visibly recover thin processes better than SIREN/Grid; check test-column PSNR ranges (SIREN ~14-18 dB, Fourier >25 dB).
2.  **Hold-out Sensitivity:** For one image, vary held-out column fraction α (0.1, 0.4, 0.7). Confirm Fourier/Haar degrade more gracefully than Grid/SIREN as α increases, especially for long thin processes crossing the gap.
3.  **Normalization Integrity:** Intentionally compute percentiles using *all* pixels (including test columns). Observe inflated PSNR across all models, proving the necessity of the restricted percentile estimation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does longer training and hyperparameter tuning change the relative performance of grid encoders versus Fourier/Haar? The benchmark used a short fixed training budget; Grid's potential with more resources remains unknown.
- Can cross-image priors (e.g., transfer learning) improve INR performance and cross-specimen generalization? The paper trains independently per image, ignoring shared anatomical features.
- Do soft wavelet variants or overlapping windows mitigate Haar's block boundary artifacts while preserving edge fidelity? The paper suggests this but provides no empirical validation.

## Limitations
- The exact deterministic mask generation for "all-in-one" images (specific hash function) and full specifications of the Grid encoder (hash table size, resolution schedule) are not detailed, hindering exact replication.
- The biological relevance of the edge-focused MAE metric (top 10% Sobel gradients) as a proxy for neuropil boundary fidelity is assumed but not validated against expert annotations.
- The reported dual role of (1,99) normalization—stabilizing training and inflating macro PSNR—is a post-hoc observation from outlier analysis, not a formally tested hypothesis.

## Confidence
- **High:** The described benchmark protocol (seed control, column hold-out, per-image normalization) is explicitly defined and reproducible in principle.
- **Medium:** The performance hierarchy (Fourier/Haar > Grid > SIREN) is strongly supported by internal experiments but may be sensitive to hyperparameters and dataset characteristics.
- **Low:** The purported metric inflation effect of per-image normalization is an insightful post-hoc observation, not a formally controlled experiment.
