# Test-Time Scaling Makes Overtraining Compute-Optimal

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01411
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01411
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Jointly optimizing pretraining and inference compute under a fixed end‑to‑end budget shifts the optimal pretraining regime toward smaller models trained on more tokens (over‑training), significantly outperforming Chinchilla‑optimal models when test‑time scaling via repeated sampling is used.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the mismatch between pretraining scaling laws (e.g., Chinchilla) and modern test‑time scaling via repeated sampling, where inference cost grows with model size and sample count. Existing laws do not jointly optimize pretraining and inference compute. It introduces Train‑to‑Test (T2) scaling laws that jointly optimize model size \(N\), training tokens \(D\), and inference samples \(k\) under a total compute budget \(6ND + 2Nk\). Two complementary modeling approaches are proposed: (1) extending the Chinchilla loss with a power‑law term in \(k\) to minimize \(-\log(\text{pass}@k)\), and (2) directly modeling \(\text{pass}@k\) by treating per‑sample accuracies as a Beta distribution whose parameters depend on the Chinchilla loss. Across eight tasks, T2 forecasts consistently indicate that accounting for inference cost radically shifts optimal pretraining into the overtraining regime—models are substantially smaller and trained on more tokens than Chinchilla recommends. Validation with newly trained checkpoints confirms these predictions: overtrained models significantly outperform Chinchilla‑optimal ones when using repeated sampling under the same total compute. The overtraining optimum persists after post‑training (fine‑tuning/SFT), demonstrating that pretraining decisions must change if test‑time scaling is anticipated.

## Method Summary
The method builds on Chinchilla’s scaling law for the negative log‑likelihood (NLL) \(L(N,D)\) and extends it to incorporate test‑time compute. For a given end‑to‑end compute budget split into training \(C_{\text{train}} \approx 6ND\) and inference \(C_{\text{inf}} \approx 2Nk\) FLOPs, the number of inference samples \(k\) is tied to the model size: \(k = C_{\text{inf}}/(2N)\). Two complementary models predict end‑to‑end performance. Approach 1 augments the loss with a power‑law term in \(k\): \(bL(N,D,k) = L(N,D) + G/k^\gamma\), where the coefficients are fitted to \(-\log(\text{pass}@k)\) measured across a suite of pretrained checkpoints. Approach 2 bypasses the loss and directly models the distribution of per‑sample accuracies across tasks as a Beta distribution, with mean and concentration functions of \(L(N,D)\). The expected \(\text{pass}@k\) is then computed in closed form. Model hyperparameters are fitted via L‑BFGS‑B with many random restarts using data from models pretrained on RefinedWeb and evaluated on eight tasks, with sampling parameters (temperature, nucleus) standardised but unspecified. Given a total budget, the isoFLOP frontier is explored by varying \(N\) and setting \(D\) and \(k\) accordingly. The optimal \(N\) (and thus \(D\) and \(k\)) is the one minimizing \(bL\) or maximizing expected \(\text{pass}@k\). This joint optimisation yields a new compute‑optimal scaling law that contrasts with the classic Chinchilla optimum (obtained by fixing \(k=1\)).

## Key Results
- Test‑time scaling (repeated sampling) fundamentally changes the compute‑optimal pretraining regime: under a fixed total compute budget, models should be overtrained—smaller in size (lower \(N\)) and trained on more tokens (higher \(D\))—than predicted by Chinchilla. The optimal \(D/N\) ratio increases by an order of magnitude.
- Across eight tasks, the Train‑to‑Test (T2) scaling laws predict this overtraining shift consistently using either loss‑based or Beta‑based models. The loss‑based approach achieves low extrapolation error (2.8% relative error), while the Beta approach yields slightly higher error (8.4%).
- Newly trained overtrained checkpoints, chosen according to T2 forecasts, significantly outperform the best Chinchilla‑optimal checkpoints when evaluated with repeated sampling under the same total compute. The advantage remains after post‑training (fine‑tuning or SFT), albeit somewhat reduced.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Loss extension with power‑law in \(k\)**  
Adding \(G/k^\gamma\) to the Chinchilla loss captures the diminishing returns of repeated samples. Under the inference budget \(C_{\text{inf}} = 2Nk\), substituting \(k = C_{\text{inf}}/(2N)\) yields \(bL(N,D) = L(N,D) + G (2N/C_{\text{inf}})^\gamma\). This term penalises large \(N\), favouring smaller models with more training tokens. The power‑law is supported by analytical results on pass@k scaling and by empirical fits. It may break for extreme \(k\) or if inference cost per sample deviates from linearity in \(N\).

**Mechanism 2: Beta‑distributed per‑sample accuracy**  
Instead of modeling loss, this approach models the distribution of single‑sample accuracies across tasks as a Beta distribution. Its mean \(\mu\) and concentration \(\nu\) are smooth functions of \(L(N,D)\) (e.g., \(\mu = \sigma(\theta_1(L-\theta_0))\), \(\nu = \exp(\theta_3+\theta_4 L)\)). The expected \(\text{pass}@k\) is then \(1 - B(a,b+k)/B(a,b)\) with \(a = \mu\nu\), \(b = (1-\mu)\nu\). This captures both average accuracy and variability across tasks, avoiding over‑optimistic naive averaging. The Beta assumption is plausible and validated by prior work, but may fail for non‑Beta (e.g., multimodal) accuracy distributions or poor sigmoid fits.

**Mechanism 3: Joint optimisation shifts optimum**  
Chinchilla finds \(N,D\) minimising \(L(N,D)\) under \(6ND = C_{\text{train}}\). Adding the inference constraint \(2Nk = C_{\text{inf}}\) and substituting \(k = C_{\text{inf}}/(2N)\) introduces an \(N\)-dependent term into the objective. For Approach 1, this term \(G (2N/C_{\text{inf}})^\gamma\) increases with \(N\), making large \(N\) more costly. Optimising under the same total compute (or separate budgets) therefore raises the optimal \(D/N\) ratio—by an order of magnitude in experiments—because smaller models yield cheaper inference, allowing more samples \(k\) within the same inference budget. The shift persists as long as inference cost scales linearly with \(N\) and \(k\) and \(\text{pass}@k\) is sub‑linear in \(k\).

## Foundational Learning

- **Chinchilla Scaling Laws**  
  *Why needed:* Establishes the baseline compute‑optimal trade‑off between model size \(N\) and training tokens \(D\).  
  *Quick check:* According to Chinchilla, if you double the pretraining compute \(C_{\text{train}}\), how should \(N\) and \(D\) scale?

- **Test‑Time Scaling & \(\text{pass}@k\)**  
  *Why needed:* T2 treats repeated sampling as a core component of deployment; \(\text{pass}@k\) is the key metric.  
  *Quick check:* Given a single‑sample success probability \(p\), what is \(\text{pass}@k\) for \(k\) attempts?

- **Beta Distribution and Regression**  
  *Why needed:* Approach 2 models the distribution of per‑sample accuracies with a Beta; understanding its parameters is key.  
  *Quick check:* In a \(\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\), what is the mean and how does total concentration \(\nu = \alpha+\beta\) affect variance?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**  
Scaling Data Collector → Chinchilla Loss Fitter → Inference Module (Power‑law or Beta Regression) → Cost Integrator (k = C_inf/(2N)) → IsoFLOP Optimizer → Validator

**Critical Path:**
1. Define training and inference budgets \(C_{\text{train}}, C_{\text{inf}}\).
2. Gather scaling data: for each model, record \((N, D, L(N,D), \text{pass}@k \text{ for various } k)\).
3. Fit the Chinchilla loss \(L(N,D)\) if not already known.
4. Fit the inference extension: either the power‑law parameters \((G,\gamma)\) (Approach 1) or the Beta regression parameters \(\theta_0..\theta_4\) (Approach 2) using the full dataset \((N,D,k,-\log\text{pass}@k)\) or \((N,D,p)\).
5. For a grid of model sizes \(N\), set \(D = C_{\text{train}}/(6N)\) and \(k = C_{\text{inf}}/(2N)\).
6. Evaluate the combined model (loss or expected accuracy) at each point.
7. Select the optimal \(N^*\) that minimizes loss or maximizes expected accuracy.
8. Train a new model exactly at \((N^*, D^*)\) and measure actual \(\text{pass}@k\) under the allocated \(k = C_{\text{inf}}/(2N^*)\).
9. If prediction error exceeds a threshold, expand the data with additional \(D/N\) ratios and repeat.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Approach 1 vs. 2:** Approach 1 uses only NLL data, is simpler, and shows lower extrapolation error (2.8% vs 8.4%). Approach 2 gives direct \(\text{pass}@k\) forecasts but requires multi‑\(k\) data and may overestimate if the Beta assumption is poor.
- **Inference cost model:** Assuming 2 FLOPs per sample per parameter yields \(C_{\text{inf}} = 2Nk\). Batching, KV‑caching, or different architectures change the constant but not the qualitative shift; if cost per sample deviates strongly from linear in \(N\), the relationship \(k \propto 1/N\) must be adjusted.
- **Budget split:** The paper uses separate budgets: \(6ND \le C_{\text{train}}\) and \(2Nk \le C_{\text{inf}}\). A combined total could be considered but yields a different trade‑off.
- **Global vs. task‑specific parameters:** exponents (\(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\)) and Beta linking functions are fit globally across tasks; task‑specific parameters could improve fit but require more data.

**Failure Signatures:**
- Optimal \(D/N\) ratio lies far outside the range of the training data (e.g., >100 while data only covers up to 50).
- IsoFLOP curve after inference correction becomes non‑monotonic, indicating model misspecification.
- Approach 2: sigmoid linking NLL to Beta mean yields \(R^2 < 0.8\) or fails to saturate at low NLL.
- Consistent >10% relative error on held‑out overtrained checkpoints.
- Fitted Beta parameters \(\alpha,\beta\) are negative or exceed 1000, suggesting poor fit.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. Replicate isoFLOP curves for a specific \(C_{\text{inf}}\) (e.g., \(2\times10^9\) FLOPs) and verify that the optimal \(D/N\) ratio is much larger than 20 (the typical Chinchilla optimum).
2. Train a model at the T2‑predicted optimal overtrained checkpoint and demonstrate a significant improvement in \(\text{pass}@k\) over the best Chinchilla‑optimal checkpoint for the same total compute.
3. Apply fine‑tuning or SFT to both the overtrained and Chinchilla‑optimal models and confirm that the overtrained advantage persists post‑training.

## Limitations
- Data and sampling details are incomplete: the exact source of the Porian checkpoints, pass@k sampling procedure (temperature, nucleus, number of samples), and whether NLL is computed teacher‑forced or on generations are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Inference budget value inconsistency (e.g., 140B vs \(2\times10^9\)) and the simplified cost model (\(C_{\text{inf}} = 2Nk\)) may affect quantitative predictions, though the qualitative overtraining shift is robust.
- Modeling assumptions may fail: the power‑law in \(k\) could break for very small or very large \(k\); the Beta distribution might not capture multimodal accuracy variations across tasks; the sigmoid linking may misfit.

## Confidence
- **Overtraining shift**: High (empirically validated across 8 tasks with new checkpoints).
- **Scaling law accuracy**:
  - Loss‑based model: Medium (low 2.8% error but power‑law may break at extremes).
  - Beta‑based model: Medium‑Low (higher 8.4% error, strong distributional assumptions).
- **Persistence after post‑training**: Medium (validated only for fine‑tuning/SFT setups tested).

## Next Checks
- Expand the \(D/N\) range in the pretraining suite (e.g., train models with \(D/N \in \{5, 50, 200\}\)) to directly measure the isoFLOP curve and confirm the optimum lies within the observed range.
- Refine the inference cost model to account for KV‑cache reuse, batching, and any architecture‑specific factors, then recompute T2 optima to test robustness of the overtraining trend.
- Validate the Beta fit by performing Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests on per‑question accuracies for held‑out tasks; if rejected, explore mixture or non‑parametric alternatives.
