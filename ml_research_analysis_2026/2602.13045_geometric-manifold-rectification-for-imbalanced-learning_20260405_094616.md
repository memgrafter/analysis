# Geometric Manifold Rectification for Imbalanced Learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.13045
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13045
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: GMR is a classifier-agnostic undersampling preprocessing step that achieves the best overall average rank (4.22) against 18 resampling baselines across 27 benchmark datasets and ranks first for 6 out of 7 evaluated classifiers.

## Executive Summary
Geometric Manifold Rectification (GMR) addresses binary imbalanced classification by focusing on the geometric structure of the training data to clean ambiguous and noisy sample boundaries. It acts as a preprocessing undersampling technique that removes uncertain majority-class instances while conservatively protecting minority-class samples. Evaluated extensively on 27 datasets with 7 diverse classifiers, GMR consistently improves minority-class performance (AUPRC) and demonstrates strong generalizability, including competitive gains when integrated with deep generative models like TabDDPM on large-scale data.

## Method Summary
GMR is a two-phase preprocessing method. First, it performs geometric confidence estimation: for each training sample, it identifies its k=15 nearest neighbors and computes inverse-distance weighted votes to estimate class probabilities and a predicted label. The distance metric is adaptively chosen—Euclidean for feature dimensions ≤100, cosine for >100—to mitigate the curse of dimensionality. Second, asymmetric cleaning rules are applied. Majority-class samples are removed if their predicted label is incorrect or their confidence in their true class is below α=0.3. Minority-class samples are considered for removal only under stricter conditions (predicted label is 0 and majority-class confidence > β=0.7), and total minority removals are capped at γ·|D_min| (γ=0.1). This conservative approach prevents the typical over-cleaning of already scarce minority instances.

## Key Results
- Achieved the best overall average rank (4.22) compared to 18 resampling baseline methods across 27 benchmark datasets.
- ranked first for 6 out of 7 tested classifiers (e.g., Decision Tree, KNN, Logistic Regression).
- Demonstrated competitive AUPRC improvements on large-scale datasets when combined with the TabDDPM deep model, confirming its effectiveness as a general preprocessing step.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Imbalanced datasets often suffer from overlapping class manifolds and noisy samples, particularly in the majority class, which can bias classifiers toward the majority. GMR’s core mechanism is to identify and remove these ambiguous majority samples that lie near or across the decision boundary, as estimated by local k-neighborhood geometry. By using inverse-distance weighting, it gives more influence to closer neighbors, leading to a more reliable confidence estimate. The asymmetric rules are critical: they aggressively clean the majority to clarify the boundary but impose a strict cap on minority removal to prevent the loss of crucial minority information, which is already limited. This selective cleaning reduces model bias toward the majority without exacerbating the data scarcity for the minority class.

## Foundational Learning
- **Imbalanced Classification**: The problem where one class significantly outnumbers the other, leading to biased models. *Why needed*: GMR’s entire design targets this specific scenario. *Quick check*: Verify dataset imbalance ratio and baseline classifier’s minority recall.
- **Geometric Confidence Estimation**: Using local neighborhood structure (kNN with inverse-distance weighting) to estimate sample-level class probabilities. *Why needed*: Provides a data-driven measure of sample ambiguity without requiring a full model fit. *Quick check*: For a sample, inspect the weighted class distribution of its nearest neighbors.
- **Asymmetric Cleaning**: Applying different removal criteria to majority vs. minority classes. *Why needed*: Prevents the common pitfall of undersampling that dangerously deletes informative minority samples. *Quick check*: Ensure minority removal count is capped and never exceeds γ·|D_min|.
- **Adaptive Metric Selection**: Switching between Euclidean and cosine distance based on feature dimensionality. *Why needed*: Euclidean distance becomes less discriminative in high dimensions; cosine is more robust. *Quick check*: For a dataset with >100 features, confirm cosine metric is used.
- **Boundary Denoising**: The concept of removing samples that are likely mislabeled or lie in overlapping regions. *Why needed*: Such samples confuse the learning algorithm and degrade decision boundary quality. *Quick check*: Compare classifier decision boundaries on cleaned vs. original data.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**: Raw Training Data -> Metric Selection -> k-Nearest Neighbors Search -> Inverse-Distance Weighted Voting -> Confidence & Label Prediction -> Asymmetric Cleaning Rules -> Cleaned Training Set
- **Critical Path**: The asymmetric cleaning rules are the decision core. The path from confidence estimation (majority/minority confidence scores) directly feeds into the boolean removal flags for each sample.
- **Design Tradeoffs**: 
  - Aggressiveness (α, β, γ) vs. Safety: Lower α or higher β increases majority removal but risks over-cleaning; γ protects minority samples but limits potential benefit.
  - Undersampling-only: GMR only removes data, never synthesizes. This avoids generating synthetic noise but cannot increase minority representation beyond original.
  - Metric heuristic: The simple >100 feature threshold for cosine is not theoretically derived but practical; misapplication hurts performance.
- **Failure Signatures**: 
  - Minority removal count exceeds cap.
  - All or most weights from kNN become nearly equal (indicating metric failure in high dimensions).
  - No samples are removed (α/β too conservative or k too large).
- **First 3 Experiments**:
  1. Run GMR on a known binary imbalanced dataset (e.g., `abalone`). Log the number of majority and minority samples removed; verify minority removals ≤ γ·|D_min|.
  2. For a high-dimensional dataset (>100 features), force Euclidean metric and compare AUPRC to using cosine. Expect significant degradation with Euclidean.
  3. Vary α from 0.1 to 0.5 on a medium-imbalance dataset. Plot AUPRC vs. α to find the optimal operating point; expect a peak before performance drops from over-cleaning.

## Limitations
- The exact data sourcing, preprocessing (missing values, categorical encoding), and feature scaling protocols are unspecified, which can significantly affect distance-based neighbor calculations.
- The full list and characteristics (dimensionality, size, imbalance ratio) of the 27 benchmark datasets are not provided, making it impossible to fully verify the adaptive metric trigger condition or generalize findings.
- Potential discrepancies in software environments (e.g., scikit-learn version) could lead to subtle differences in nearest neighbor retrieval and distance computations, affecting reproducibility.
