# Linking Knowledge to Care: Knowledge Graph-Augmented Medical Follow-Up Question Generation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01252  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01252  
- **Reference count:** 20  
- **Primary result:** KG-Followup achieves 70%-80% recall on ClinicalInquiryBench and FollowupBench, outperforming prior methods by 5-8%.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of automating clinically relevant follow-up question generation to reduce diagnostic workload. Standard large language models (LLMs) struggle in medical domains due to limited up-to-date and structured knowledge. The authors propose KG-Followup, a system that integrates a medical knowledge graph (KG) with an LLM through three key modules: EHR-guided associative retrieval, differential diagnosis (DDX)-guided reasoning paths, and KG-informed active in-context learning. They also introduce two new benchmarks, ClinicalInquiryBench and FollowupBench. KG-Followup attains state-of-the-art recall of 70%–80% with a controlled number of questions, representing a 5–8% absolute improvement over prior approaches. Ablation studies confirm that the KG-informed active in-context learning and DDX-guided modules are critical to performance.

## Method Summary
KG-Followup processes a patient’s electronic health record (EHR) through a pipeline that grounds LLM generation in a medical knowledge graph. First, an associative retrieval module queries the KG using entities extracted from the EHR to obtain a relevant subgraph of symptoms, diseases, and relations. Second, a DDX reasoning component traverses this subgraph to generate plausible differential diagnosis pathways, identifying features that distinguish between candidate conditions. Third, an active in-context learning selector picks the most informative KG facts—guided by the DDX paths—to construct a demonstration prompt for the LLM. Finally, the LLM generates follow-up questions that are both fluent and clinically grounded. This structured approach compensates for the LLM’s lack of specialized medical knowledge by providing dynamic, patient-specific context.

## Key Results
- Achieves recall of 70%–80% on the new ClinicalInquiryBench and FollowupBench benchmarks.
- Improves over prior methods by an absolute 5%–8%.
- Ablation studies show that KG-informed active in-context learning and DDX-guided reasoning are essential components; removing them significantly degrades performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method succeeds by tightly coupling the LLM with a curated medical knowledge graph, ensuring factual accuracy and clinical relevance. EHR-guided associative retrieval links patient data to the KG, retrieving only the most pertinent facts and avoiding noise. DDX-guided reasoning mimics the diagnostic thought process of clinicians, steering the system toward questions that differentiate between plausible conditions. KG-informed active in-context learning dynamically selects the most supportive KG evidence as in-context examples, enabling the LLM to generate questions that are specific, useful, and grounded in established medical knowledge. Together, these components overcome the LLM’s parametric knowledge limitations and align generation with clinical reasoning patterns.

## Foundational Learning
- **Knowledge Graph (KG)** – A structured network of medical entities (diseases, symptoms, treatments) and relationships. *Why needed:* Provides reliable, up-to-date medical facts beyond the LLM’s training data. *Quick check:* Verify coverage of high-priority conditions and correct linking of symptoms to diseases.  
- **Large Language Model (LLM)** – A generative model pre-trained on large text corpora. *Why needed:* Generates fluent, flexible natural language questions. *Quick check:* Prompt with a simple context and confirm coherent, grammatically correct output.  
- **In-Context Learning (ICL)** – Conditioning an LLM by including examples in the prompt. *Why needed:* Allows rapid adaptation without fine-tuning; here, demonstrates KG-grounded question generation. *Quick check:* Compare relevance of questions with and without KG-based demonstrations.  
- **Differential Diagnosis (DDX)** – Clinical process of listing possible diagnoses based on evidence. *Why needed:* Guides the system along plausible disease pathways, ensuring questions target distinguishing features. *Quick check:* Ensure generated DDX paths align with standard medical references for given symptom sets.  
- **Electronic Health Record (EHR)** – Digital record of a patient’s medical history. *Why needed:* Provides patient-specific context for personalization. *Quick check:* Confirm retrieval step uses EHR entries (e.g., current symptoms) to fetch a relevant KG subgraph.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
EHR → Associative Retriever → Knowledge Graph → DDX Reasoner → Active ICL Selector → LLM → Follow-up Questions

**Critical path**  
1. Parse patient EHR to extract key clinical entities.  
2. Associative Retriever queries the KG with these entities to obtain a focused subgraph.  
3. DDX Reasoner traverses the subgraph to generate possible diagnosis pathways and identify discriminating features.  
4. Active ICL Selector chooses the most informative KG facts (based on DDX) to build a demonstration prompt.  
5. LLM, conditioned on the prompt, generates candidate follow-up questions.  
6. Optional filtering yields the final set.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *KG coverage vs. retrieval noise:* Larger KGs improve coverage but risk irrelevant facts; precision is key.  
- *Number of in-context examples vs. context length:* More examples can improve guidance but increase token usage and cost.  
- *DDX path depth vs. computational cost:* Deeper reasoning may capture nuance but requires more graph traversal or LLM calls.

**Failure signatures**  
- Questions are generic and not tied to the patient’s EHR.  
- Hallucinated medical facts unsupported by the KG.  
- Very low recall due to over-restrictive retrieval or reasoning.  
- High variance in quality across different patient cases.

**First experiments**  
1. *Retrieval ablation:* Replace associative retrieval with random KG sampling and measure change in recall.  
2. *ICL example count sweep:* Evaluate performance with varying numbers of in-context examples (0, 2, 5, 10).  
3. *DDX reasoning strategy comparison:* Test alternative path generation methods (e.g., shortest path vs. weighted relevance) on benchmark scores.

## Limitations
- New benchmarks (ClinicalInquiryBench, FollowupBench) lack external validation; generalizability to other clinical settings is uncertain.  
- Zero average citations indicate no independent replication; robustness of the reported 5–8% gain is unverified.  
- KG quality, coverage gaps, and EHR integration specifics are unclear; dependencies on proprietary resources may hinder reproducibility.  
- Evaluation focused solely on recall; precision, question diversity, and clinician-rated utility are unreported.

## Confidence
- *Performance on new benchmarks*: Medium (strong internal results but no external replication).  
- *Critical role of KG-informed active ICL & DDX-guided modules*: Low‑Medium (ablation evidence only from authors).  
- *Generalizable clinical relevance*: Low (no human evaluation or deployment results).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate KG-Followup on at least two existing medical QA/follow-up datasets to test cross-benchmark consistency.  
2. Conduct a clinician-blinded evaluation of generated questions for accuracy, safety, and relevance, comparing against LLM baselines and real provider questions.  
3. Perform an independent ablation study isolating the KG retrieval, DDX reasoning path, and active ICL components, measuring impact on both recall and precision.
