# Agentic Trust Coordination for Federated Learning through Adaptive Thresholding and Autonomous Decision Making in Sustainable and Resilient Industrial Networks

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.25334
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25334
- **Reference count:** 17
- **Primary result:** The proposed Agentic Trust Control Loop (ATCL) is expected to improve omission accuracy, reduce trust score volatility, and enhance global model stability under attack or noise in industrial federated learning, while maintaining low computational overhead and requiring no client-side modifications.

## Executive Summary
Federated Learning (FL) in industrial networks is vulnerable to unreliable or adversarial client behavior, where static or reactive trust adaptation mechanisms fail under dynamic conditions. This paper introduces the Agentic Trust Control Loop (ATCL), a server-side supervisory mechanism that observes multi-dimensional trust and system signals, infers high-level operating states (Normal, Degraded, Stabilising), and autonomously adjusts trust parameters such as omission thresholds and exponential moving average smoothing. Built upon prior ATSSSF results, ATCL aims to provide context-aware, stable trust management without altering client implementations, targeting improved resilience and convergence in sustainable industrial deployments.

## Method Summary
The Minimum Viable Reproduction Plan involves three staged implementations. First, establish a synchronous FL baseline (e.g., using the Flower framework) with heterogeneous clients, varied data distributions, and optional attack injection. Second, implement the ATSSSF mechanism: compute multi-criteria trust scores via entropy-TOPSIS, apply fixed-parameter EMA smoothing, and use a static omission threshold to exclude low-trust clients before aggregation. Third, superimpose the ATCL supervisory loop: after each round, aggregate client-level signals (gradient similarity, volatility, consistency) and system-level trends (global loss, trust dispersion); infer the current operating state via lightweight, rule-based reasoning; and conditionally adapt the omission threshold and EMA factor or manage temporary client exclusion/reinstatement. Key unknowns include the precise state inference thresholds/temporal windows and the exact adaptation functions for trust parameters.

## Key Results
- Improves omission accuracy under dynamic attack or noise conditions.
- Reduces trust score volatility compared to fixed-threshold or reactive baselines.
- Enhances global model stability (convergence reliability and final performance) during degraded and stabilising phases.
- Maintains low computational overhead with server-side-only deployment.
- Requires no modifications to client implementations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** ATCL improves stability by replacing static thresholding with continuous multi-signal observation of client behavior. The server aggregates per-round signals (gradient similarity, volatility, participation consistency) and system-level trends (global loss, trust dispersion) into a structured agent state, moving beyond single-metric triggers. The core assumption is that a composite view of behavioral and system indicators better captures underlying client reliability than isolated statistical deviations. Evidence anchors include the abstract's description of observing "trust related and system level signals" and Section IV's listing of statistical and model-level indicators. A break condition exists: if any primary signal stream becomes systematically corrupted (e.g., adversarial manipulation of loss reporting), the composite state inference may become misleading.

**Mechanism 2:** ATCL reduces reactive oscillations by inferring high-level operating states (Normal/Degraded/Stabilising) from signal trends rather than instantaneous thresholds. A lightweight reasoning layer interprets joint temporal evolution of trust scores, volatility, and global loss to classify the training phase, enabling deliberate intervention only when sustained patterns are detected. The core assumption is that distinguishing transient noise from persistent instability is possible with minimal, interpretable rules over aggregated multi-round signals. Evidence includes Section II's framing of trust adaptation as a control problem and Section IV's mention of detecting "sustained deviations." Break condition: in highly volatile environments where signal patterns do not clearly separate into the three predefined states, the reasoning layer may incorrectly label phases, leading to inappropriate adjustments.

**Mechanism 3:** ATCL enhances omission accuracy and trust score stability by making parameter adjustments (omission threshold θ, EMA factor α) conditional on the inferred system state, rather than reactive metric breaches. When the state is "Degraded", ATCL tightens θ and increases smoothing (α); when "Stabilising", it gradually relaxes them and reinstates excluded clients. The core assumption is that context-aware, gradual parameter steering yields more stable trust evolution and accurate client exclusion than fixed or locally adaptive rules. Evidence includes the abstract's statement on autonomous parameter adjustment and Algorithm 1's explicit state-to-action mappings. Break condition: if state inference lags behind rapid system changes (e.g., sudden attack cessation), conditional adjustments may be mis-timed, causing prolonged unnecessary exclusion or premature client reintroduction.

- **Trust scoring in FL (e.g., ATSSSF):** ATCL directly extends the ATSSSF paradigm; knowing its entropy-TOPSIS ranking and EMA smoothing baseline is required to appreciate the "agentic" shift. *Quick check:* What are two limitations of fixed omission thresholds and constant EMA smoothing in dynamic industrial FL settings?
- **Agentic control loops (Perception-Reasoning-Action):** The core innovation is framing trust adaptation as a control problem; this concept explains the separation of signal observation, state inference, and intervention. *Quick check:* How does an agentic control loop differ from a simple reactive rule (e.g., "if trust < 0.5, then omit") in terms of decision timing and context?

## Architecture Onboarding
Signal Monitor -> Reasoning Engine -> Trust Parameter Controller -> Trust-Aware Aggregator

The critical path runs: Client updates → Signal Monitor → Reasoning Engine → Parameter Controller → Trust-Aware Aggregator → Global model. This cycle must complete within a single server aggregation round to avoid training stalls.

Design tradeoffs include: (1) Lightweight deterministic rules vs. expressive ML models—chosen for low overhead and interpretability but sacrifices subtle detection nuance; (2) Server-side only vs. client cooperation—eases deployment but limits telemetry richness; (3) Three coarse states vs. continuous regime—simplifies implementation but may oversimplify gradual transitions.

Failure signatures: (1) High volatility in trust scores despite ATCL suggests state inference lag or poor parameter mapping; (2) Persistent "Degraded" state with no recovery indicates over-sensitive reasoning rules; (3) Global model accuracy collapse after client reinstatement suggests a falsely inferred "Stabilising" state.

First 3 experiments: (1) Replay attack simulation: inject periodic malicious clients; compare ATCL state timelines and omission accuracy against fixed-threshold and adaptive ATSSSF baselines; (2) Transient noise stress test: introduce temporary sensor failures/connectivity drops; measure trust score volatility and global model convergence lag; (3) Overhead characterization: profile per-round CPU/memory usage of Signal Monitor + Reasoning Engine under varying client counts (100 vs. 1000) to confirm "lightweight" claim.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How robust is the ATCL's state inference logic to highly heterogeneous client behaviour patterns and simultaneous multi-type attacks (e.g., combined data poisoning and communication denial)? *Basis:* The methodology describes a "lightweight reasoning layer" using rule-based logic over aggregated signals, aiming to "distinguish transient noise from persistent instability," but the mechanism for handling overlapping failure modes is not detailed. *Why unresolved:* State inference is a key advancement but concrete algorithms, thresholds, and temporal models are unspecified. *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments with varied combined attack models measuring precision/recall of state classification against ground-truth system states.

**Open Question 2:** What are the computational and convergence trade-offs when scaling the ATCL's reasoning component from its current "lightweight, rule-based" design toward more sophisticated, possibly learning-based, inference for the longer-term SOFI vision? *Basis:* Section VI outlines a future direction toward "Self Organising Federated Intelligence (SOFI)" with multiple coordinated agentic modules; Section IV states current reasoning is deliberately non-LLM-based and operates on a low-dimensional state. *Why unresolved:* No analysis of how increased reasoning complexity impacts server overhead, decision latency, or convergence stability. *What evidence would resolve it:* Benchmarking current rule-based ATCL against prototypes with incremental reasoning complexity, measuring per-round processing time, memory, and impact on accuracy/convergence speed.

**Open Question 3:** How does the ATCL perform in non-stationary industrial environments characterised by frequent, unpredictable shifts in data distribution (concept drift) across client cohorts, not just malicious behaviour? *Basis:* Section III lists "data drift" as a benign factor affecting client behaviour; expected results focus on handling "unreliable or adversarial clients." The agent's state detection is framed around "instability" and "recovery," but the distinction between attacks and legitimate non-stationarity is not discussed. *Why unresolved:* Trust mechanisms may inadvertently penalise clients undergoing legitimate operational changes; context-aware intervention needs validation under benign systemic drift. *What evidence would resolve it:* Simulations with controlled synthetic concept drift (e.g., rotating dominant features), monitoring whether "Degraded" state triggers and exclusion actions correlate more with drift or attack, and measuring resulting global model performance degradation.

**Open Question 4:** Can the ATCL's control actions be formalised into a provably stable control theory model, and what are the theoretical bounds on convergence under its supervision? *Basis:* The paper frames ATCL as a "control problem" and an "observation–analysis–decision–action cycle," but Algorithm 1 and text describe heuristic state-based conditionals with no mention of Lyapunov stability, convergence guarantees, or formal models of trust dynamics. *Why unresolved:* Claiming a "control problem" perspective implies a need for theoretical foundations to ensure decisions do not induce oscillations or divergence; current description is phenomenological. *What evidence would resolve it:* Development of a dynamical system model representing trust score evolution under ATCL actions, proving stability conditions (e.g., boundedness, convergence to a set-point) for the control policy, validated by empirical stability tests across wide parameter ranges.

## Limitations
- Precise state inference logic (thresholds, temporal windows) is unspecified, blocking exact replication.
- Exact adaptation functions for omission threshold θ and EMA factor α are not detailed.
- Weak direct empirical validation exists for the integrated agentic loop; claims rely heavily on conceptual extension of prior ATSSSF work.
