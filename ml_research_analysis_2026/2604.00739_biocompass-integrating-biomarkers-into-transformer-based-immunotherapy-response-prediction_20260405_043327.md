# BioCOMPASS: Integrating Biomarkers into Transformer-Based Immunotherapy Response Prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00739
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00739
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** BioCOMPASS outperforms COMPASS on leave-group-out evaluations (e.g., LOCO accuracy 70.00% vs 63.10%, ROC-AUC 73.58% vs 70.99%) via biomarker-aligned training without requiring biomarker inputs at inference.

## Executive Summary
BioCOMPASS extends the COMPASS transformer for immunotherapy response prediction by introducing training-only components that align the model’s intermediate biological concepts with external biomarkers and treatment information. This alignment, enforced through pathway consistency, concept alignment, auxiliary tasks, and treatment gating losses, improves generalization across unseen cohorts, cancer types, and treatments. Evaluations using leave-one-cohort-out, leave-one-cancer-type-out, and leave-one-treatment-out strategies on eight immunotherapy cohorts demonstrate consistent performance gains over the base COMPASS model, validating the strategy of integrating domain knowledge via latent space regularization.

## Method Summary
The method builds on COMPASS, which uses a transformer encoder and a concept bottleneck layer to produce 44 interpretable biological concept scores from gene expression. BioCOMPASS adds four training-only components: (1) a pathway consistency loss that predicts external CTLA-4/PD-1 pathway activity scores from encoder embeddings; (2) a concept alignment loss that minimizes the distance between projected concept scores and external cell-type abundance biomarkers; (3) auxiliary prediction heads for TIDE, IPRES, and immune phenotypes from the concept layer; and (4) a treatment gating mechanism that scales the concept vector based on the immunotherapy target (PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4, or combination). During fine-tuning, the COMPASS encoder is frozen while the bottleneck, classifier, and new components are trained with a weighted sum of response and auxiliary losses. At inference, only gene expression and treatment type are required.

## Key Results
- BioCOMPASS achieves higher accuracy (70.00% vs 63.10%), ROC-AUC (73.58% vs 70.99%), and F1-score (54.01% vs 46.65%) than COMPASS in the leave-one-cohort-out setting.
- Ablation studies show that removing treatment gating or pathway consistency loss causes the largest performance drops, indicating their critical role in generalization.
- Improvements are consistent across leave-one-cancer-type-out and leave-one-treatment-out evaluations, demonstrating robust cross-cohort and cross-treatment generalizability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Treatment Gating
- **Claim:** Treatment gating improves generalization by making the model’s biological concepts conditional on the immunotherapy target.
- **Mechanism:** A gating network embeds the treatment type and produces weight vectors that element-wise scale the 44 COMPASS concepts, adaptively emphasizing concepts relevant to a specific treatment mechanism.
- **Core assumption:** The relationship between tumor biology and response is modulated by the immunotherapy’s molecular target; thus, concept representations should be treatment-aware.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 2.2 describe the gating formulation; ablation shows performance drop when gating is removed (Table 3).
- **Break condition:** If treatment type does not modulate relevant biological concepts, gating would not improve over a treatment-agnostic model.

### Mechanism 2: Pathway Consistency Loss
- **Claim:** Pathway consistency loss encourages the encoder to learn representations that capture known biological pathway activity, reducing cohort-specific noise.
- **Mechanism:** An auxiliary head predicts 42 external CTLA-4/PD-1 pathway scores from encoder embeddings; minimizing the MSE between predictions and targets regularizes the latent space.
- **Core assumption:** External pathway scores are valid ground-truth measures of pathway activation; representations capturing these generalize better.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 2.2 state the loss improves generalizability; ablation supports its contribution (Table 3).
- **Break condition:** If external pathway scores are noisy or irrelevant, minimizing this loss could introduce bias or hurt performance.

### Mechanism 3: Concept Alignment Loss
- **Claim:** Concept alignment validates the model’s internal biological concepts against external cell-type biomarker scores, preventing conceptual drift.
- **Mechanism:** A projection layer maps both the 44 COMPASS concepts and 43 external cell-type abundance scores to a shared latent dimension; their L2 distance is minimized.
- **Core assumption:** External cell-type abundance scores accurately proxy the concepts the model learns; aligning them ensures measurability.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 2.2 describe aligning concepts with biomarker scores; ablation shows a smaller but positive contribution (Table 3).
- **Break condition:** If external biomarkers are inaccurate or measure distinct phenomena, alignment could force misaligned representations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Self-supervised pre-training on pan-cancer data**
  - *Why needed:* COMPASS is pre-trained on TCGA gene expression via triplet loss, providing robust initial representations that capture pan-cancer biological variation. This prevents learning cohort-specific noise during fine-tuning on small, heterogeneous immunotherapy cohorts.
  - *Quick check:* Why might pre-training on a large, diverse dataset like TCGA improve performance on small, distinct immunotherapy cohorts?
- **Concept: Concept bottleneck layers**
  - *Why needed:* The COMPASS bottleneck produces 44 interpretable biological concept scores. BioCOMPASS adds losses that align these *latent* concepts with external biomarkers, injecting domain knowledge without altering input requirements.
  - *Quick check:* What is the functional difference between feeding biomarker data as input versus aligning it with a model’s intermediate concepts?
- **Concept: Multi-task auxiliary learning**
  - *Why needed:* Predicting TIDE, IPRES, and immune phenotypes from the concept layer acts as a regularizer, encouraging concepts to capture information useful for multiple clinically relevant biomarkers.
  - *Quick check:* How does auxiliary prediction of established biomarkers differ from directly aligning concepts with those biomarkers?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Gene expression (TPM) -> COMPASS encoder (transformer, frozen) -> Pathway consistency head (auxiliary) -> COMPASS concept bottleneck (44 concepts) -> Concept alignment projection (auxiliary) -> (Optional: Auxiliary heads for TIDE/IPRES/phenotype) -> Treatment gating (MLP on treatment embedding) -> Classifier -> Response prediction.
- **Critical path:** Input gene expression passes through the frozen encoder to generate embeddings. These feed the pathway consistency branch and the concept bottleneck. Concepts are aligned with biomarkers via a projection and also sent to auxiliary task heads. The treatment gate scales the concepts, which are then classified.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Biomarker dependency:* Requires high-quality external biomarker and pathway scores during training but not inference, trading data availability for model flexibility.
  - *Component complexity:* Adds four loss branches and a gating module, increasing training compute and hyperparameter sensitivity.
  - *Interpretability vs. performance:* Concept alignment aims to improve generalization but may constrain the model if biomarkers are imperfect.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Poor LOCO/LOCTO/LOTO performance relative to COMPASS despite biomarker training.
  - Ablation shows largest drop when removing treatment gating or pathway consistency.
  - Training instability: auxiliary losses dominating or conflicting with main response loss.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce COMPASS baseline on the 8 cohorts using provided pretrained weights, confirming similar LOCO performance (~63% accuracy).
  2. Incrementally add BioCOMPASS components: pathway consistency, then concept alignment, then auxiliary tasks, finally treatment gating—measure LOCO change after each to isolate contributions.
  3. Full comparison: Train BioCOMPASS with all components and compare to COMPASS across LOCO, LOCTO, and LOTO settings; validate consistency across seeds and cohort splits.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What specific complementary clinical information and domain knowledge should be integrated next?** The authors suggest biomedical text mining or biological knowledge graphs as promising but do not investigate which types yield the greatest marginal benefit or might harm performance.
2. **What is the practical trade-off and minimum data requirement for the "alignment during training only" strategy?** How does performance scale with the availability of external biomarker data during fine-tuning, and what is the impact in settings where such biomarkers are scarce?
3. **What is the underlying reason why treatment gating and pathway consistency have the largest impact on generalizability?** The ablation shows their removal causes the largest drops, but the causal mechanism (e.g., preventing spurious correlations vs. enforcing fundamental biological priors) is not explored.
4. **To what extent are improvements driven by the specific architectural additions versus the increase in model capacity?** No control matches BioCOMPASS’s parameter count with task-irrelevant additions, so the benefit of biologically guided components versus mere capacity increase is unclear.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter specifics (loss weights, learning rates, epochs) are not fully disclosed; reproduction may be sensitive.
- Exact definitions and sources of the 43 cell-type abundances, 42 pathway features, and auxiliary biomarkers are ambiguous.
- Treatment mapping to the four gating categories (PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4, combo) is unclear, especially for combination therapies.
- Quality of external biomarker scores is not assessed; noise or misalignment could limit benefits.
- Partial fine-tuning (freezing the encoder) might restrict adaptation; full fine-tuning effects are unknown.

## Confidence
- **Overall generalization improvement (BioCOMPASS outperforms COMPASS):** Medium – Solid evaluation design and consistent results, but reliant on unreproduced implementation details.
- **Critical role of treatment gating and pathway consistency:** Medium – Ablation evidence supports, but causality is conditional on correct implementation and hyperparameters.
- **Contribution of concept alignment:** Low-Medium – Benefit is modest and highly dependent on accuracy and relevance of external cell-type biomarkers, which are not fully validated.
