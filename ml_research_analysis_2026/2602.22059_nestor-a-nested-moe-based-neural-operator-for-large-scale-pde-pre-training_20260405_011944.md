# NESTOR: A Nested MOE-based Neural Operator for Large-Scale PDE Pre-Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22059  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22059  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Pre‑trained on twelve PDE datasets, NESTOR achieves state‑of‑the‑art relative L2 error on 6 of 12 datasets (pre‑training) and on 9 of 12 after fine‑tuning; on a downstream 2D turbulence task it improves prediction accuracy by 47.3% while activating only 16.67% of its 83 M parameters.

## Executive Summary
NESTOR addresses a key limitation of existing neural operators: their inability to fully capture the heterogeneity and complex spatiotemporal dependencies across diverse partial differential equation (PDE) systems when scaling to large pre‑training corpora. The authors propose a nested Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) framework that integrates two levels of sparse activation. An image‑level MoE selects experts based on global image statistics to handle different PDE families, while a token‑level Sub‑MoE processes local spatial tokens to capture fine‑grained physical correlations. This design allows a single model to specialize substructures for widely varying dynamics while maintaining computational efficiency. Pre‑trained on twelve public PDE datasets, NESTOR sets new records on a majority of them, and fine‑tuning yields substantial gains on a challenging 2D turbulence benchmark. With 83 million total parameters, the model activates only about 16.7% during inference, demonstrating that large‑capacity pre‑training can be made efficient through clever sparsity.

## Method Summary
NESTOR’s architecture begins with a spatio‑temporal encoder that splits input frames into non‑overlapping patches, adds positional encodings, and compresses the time dimension via a weighted sum. The core consists of consecutive Nested MoE blocks. Each block contains an image‑level MoE router: it globally averages the feature map, passes it through a linear layer to compute scores for seven experts (six non‑shared FlashAttention modules and one shared AFNO). The top‑two non‑shared experts are activated alongside the always‑on shared expert. The output of this image‑level expert is then fed into a token‑level Sub‑MoE within the feed‑forward network of each FlashAttention expert. For every token independently, a linear gate scores seven MLP experts (six non‑shared plus one shared); the top‑two non‑shared and the shared MLP are selected. Training minimizes a combined loss: relative L2 error of the next‑frame prediction plus auxiliary load‑balancing terms for both routing levels, which encourage uniform expert utilization. Pre‑training samples batches from the twelve datasets with balanced weights, uses Adam with a cyclic learning rate (peak 1e‑3, 200‑epoch warm‑up) for 1000 epochs, and runs on eight RTX 4090 GPUs. The total parameter count is 83 M, but per‑forward activation drops to ~13.9 M (16.67%) due to top‑2 routing at both levels.

## Key Results
- Pre‑training: achieves SOTA relative L2 error on 6 of 12 PDE datasets.
- Fine‑tuning: improves to SOTA on 9 of 12 datasets.
- Downstream task: on 2D turbulence, fine‑tuned NESTOR reduces prediction error by 47.3%.
- Efficiency: 83 M total parameters; ~16.7% activated per inference.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Global PDE‑Type Specialization via Image‑Level MoE**  
The image‑level MoE computes a global representation (average pooling) of the input feature map, then routes to two non‑shared FlashAttention experts plus a shared AFNO expert. This allows the model to assign different PDE families (e.g., Navier‑Stokes vs. Shallow Water) to dedicated expert networks. The core assumption is that distinct PDE equations produce characteristic macroscopic statistical patterns; specialized experts can learn tailored transformations. Evidence includes the contrast with MoE‑POT, which lacks explicit image‑level routing and shows inferior cross‑dataset performance. Break condition: if routing probabilities collapse to uniform or the same expert combination is chosen for nearly all inputs, the mechanism degenerates into a single‑network baseline, losing heterogeneity handling.

**Mechanism 2: Local Correlation Modeling via Token‑Level Sub‑MoE**  
Within each FlashAttention expert’s feed‑forward network, a second MoE router operates on each spatial token independently, selecting two non‑shared MLP experts and a shared MLP. This addresses the fact that a single PDE solution often contains regions with different local dynamics (vortices, shocks, boundaries). Token‑level sparsity lets different locations be processed by different experts, capturing multi‑scale phenomena efficiently. Evidence: Ablation (Table 4) shows that removing Sub‑MoE increases average L2 error by 0.0024; spatial heatmaps (Section 5.6) reveal region‑specific expert activation. Break condition: if token‑level routing always selects the same expert across tokens (e.g., due to poor initialization), the module reduces to a plain MLP and fails to model local correlations.

**Mechanism 3: Efficient Scaling via Hierarchical Sparsity**  
Both MoE levels use top‑2 routing. At the image level, only 3 experts (2 non‑shared + 1 shared) out of 7 are active; inside each activated FlashAttention, the token‑level Sub‑MoE activates 3 MLP experts per token out of 7. This cascaded sparsity drastically reduces engaged parameters per inference. The large pool of experts (12 non‑shared + 2 shared) provides high capacity and diversity, but any input only requires a small subset. Evidence: the reported 16.67% activation ratio (13.9 M of 83 M) is far lower than baselines (e.g., MoE‑POT‑T at 56.67%). Load‑balancing losses prevent expert collapse and maintain sparsity. Break condition: if routing degenerates (e.g., one non‑shared expert dominates >90% of selections), effective capacity shrinks and the model may underfit or suffer load imbalance, negating efficiency gains.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Spatio‑Temporal Encoder → Nested MoE Block (Image‑level Router → Image‑level Experts (AFNO shared + 6× FlashAttention non‑shared) → Token‑level Sub‑MoE Router → Token‑level Experts (MLP shared + 6× MLP non‑shared)) → Head → Loss

**Critical path:**  
Input → Patch Embedding → Temporal Compression → [Image‑level Router → (Shared AFNO + 2× FlashAttention) → FlashAttention output → Token‑level Sub‑MoE Router → (Shared MLP + 2× MLP)] × L → Output Head → Loss

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Expert count vs. Optimization:* More experts increase capacity but make routing and load balancing harder; experiments showed 6 non‑shared experts optimal, 12 degraded performance.  
- *Shared vs. Non‑shared:* Shared experts provide a stable backbone; non‑shared enable specialization. Too many non‑shared can destabilize training.  
- *Activation sparsity vs. Representational richness:* Lower activation ratio improves efficiency but risks underfitting if top‑k too small or experts poorly initialized.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Load imbalance: one expert (>50% activation) indicates routing collapse; monitor per‑expert utilization.  
- Poor cross‑dataset transfer: pre‑training L2RE much higher than fine‑tuned suggests lack of generalizable features; check routing entropy and dataset sampling.  
- High variance across runs: MoE sensitive to routing initialization; if results vary significantly, adjust loss weights or learning rate.  
- Out‑of‑memory (OOM) despite low activation: intermediate tensors store all expert outputs; implement sequential computation or validate token count.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Pre‑training baseline reproduction:** Train from scratch on the 12‑dataset mix, verify SOTA on ≥6 datasets and activated ratio ~16.67%.  
2. **Sub‑MoE ablation:** Remove token‑level Sub‑MoE (replace with standard MLP), retrain identically; expect average L2RE increase ~0.0024.  
3. **Scaling non‑shared experts:** Train variants with 2, 4, 6, 12 non‑shared experts; verify optimal performance at 6, confirming the inverted‑U pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Optimal expert counts and activation ratios:** The paper notes future work is needed; limited scaling experiments (only non‑shared varied) leave interactions with shared counts, top‑k, and depth unexplored. Resolution: systematic grid searches across hyperparameters on multiple PDE benchmarks.
- **Adaptability of hierarchical routing to different PDE dependency structures:** The fixed image‑token split assumes global vs. local separation, but not validated for PDEs with varying dependencies (e.g., diffusion‑ vs. wave‑dominated). Resolution: test on PDEs with quantified dependency patterns, analyze routing probabilities and activation maps per type.
- **Generalization to out‑of‑distribution PDEs:** Pre‑training used mostly 2D datasets; transfer to 3D, new equation families, or complex geometries is unverified. Resolution: perform zero‑shot and fine‑tuning evaluations on 3D Navier‑Stokes, novel nonlinearities, and irregular domains.
- **Load‑balancing loss design for nested MoE:** Standard losses are used without adaptation; coefficients are arbitrary; ablation shows minimal improvement. Resolution: quantify expert uniformity under different weights, design nested‑aware losses (e.g., cross‑level constraints), and correlate with performance.

## Limitations
- Critical hyperparameters (patch size, FlashAttention kernel details, AFNO internal dimensions, load‑balancing coefficients α/β, sampling weights) are omitted, hindering exact replication.
- Routing stability and expert collapse risks are not analyzed; no random‑seed variance is reported, raising reproducibility concerns.
- Generalization beyond the tested 2D datasets (e.g., to 3D, novel PDE families, complex geometries) remains unverified.

## Confidence
- Image‑level MoE captures global PDE‑type features: **Medium** confidence.  
- Token‑level Sub‑MoE captures local physical correlations: **High** confidence.  
- Nested MoE achieves efficient scaling via sparse activation: **Medium** confidence.
