# On the Dynamics & Transferability of Latent Generalization during Memorization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19865  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19865  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Latent generalization peaks early and can be transferred via linear weight editing, except for ResNet-18.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the phenomenon of latent generalization—generalization inherent in internal representations—during memorization, where models are trained with randomly corrupted labels. The authors track latent generalization using the MASC probe, a quadratic classifier based on class-conditional subspaces, and introduce a linear analog, VeLPIC, that uses class-wise principal vectors. They find that latent generalization (as measured by MASC) typically peaks early in training alongside model test accuracy and later declines less steeply. VeLPIC often surpasses MASC, especially under high corruption. Replacing the model’s pre-softmax weights with VeLPIC class vectors (weight editing) immediately improves test accuracy for most architectures, but not for ResNet-18. Interventions applied mid‑training yield mixed improvements, suggesting that the transferability of latent generalization is architecture‑dependent and tied to the linear decodability of the last layer’s representations.

## Method Summary
Models (MLP, CNN, AlexNet, ResNet‑18) are trained on several datasets (MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST, CIFAR‑10, Tiny ImageNet) with randomly corrupted training labels. Model checkpoints are saved at regular epochs. For each checkpoint, activations from selected layers are extracted on the corrupted training set. The MASC probe constructs a class‑conditional subspace for each class via PCA on augmented (original plus negative) activations, retaining components explaining 99% of the variance; test samples are classified by the largest squared projection onto these subspaces. The VeLPIC probe computes a single top principal component per class from the same augmented activations and aligns its sign with the mean of the original class activations; classification is by maximum dot product. A logistic regression baseline is also trained. To transfer latent generalization, the model’s final linear weight matrix is replaced by the transpose of the matrix of VeLPIC vectors (bias zeroed), and the model is evaluated on the true test set either immediately or after continued training.

## Key Results
- Latent generalization (MASC) typically peaks early alongside model test accuracy and later declines less steeply.
- VeLPIC often surpasses MASC (especially under high corruption) but underperforms for early layers of ResNet‑18.
- Weight editing via VeLPIC immediately improves test accuracy in most models, except ResNet‑18; mid‑training interventions yield mixed improvements.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1 – Early Phase of Training Generates Generalizable Representations  
- **Claim:** Latent generalization (probe accuracy) and model test accuracy rise together during early training, suggesting that the feature representations learned initially are beneficial for both the model’s decisions and the geometry of internal activations.  
- **Mechanism:** Gradient descent initially moves weights toward solutions that fit the true underlying data patterns. Because label corruption is partial and the model has not yet overfitted to noisy labels, these early features remain aligned with the true class structure. This alignment is simultaneously reflected in the model’s outputs and in the class-conditional geometry captured by probes.  
- **Core assumption:** Corruption is less than 100% and training is stopped before memorization fully takes hold.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] “latent generalization (MASC) typically peaks early alongside model test accuracy”; [Section 4] Figure 1 shows co‑rise for MLP, CNN, AlexNet, and (with nuance) ResNet‑18.  
- **Break condition:** At 100% corruption the early peak is less pronounced; with continued training the model overfits to noise and both model and probe accuracy may decay.

### Mechanism 2 – Quadratic Subspace Projection in MASC Extracts Robust Class Geometry  
- **Claim:** MASC’s quadratic decision rule (maximizing squared projection onto class‑conditional subspaces) is particularly effective at uncovering latent generalization from high‑dimensional representations because it captures the spread of each class in a noise‑robust manner.  
- **Mechanism:** For each class, MASC builds a subspace via PCA on corrupted training activations (augmented with negatives to enforce origin). For a test sample it projects onto each class subspace and selects the class whose subspace yields the smallest angle (largest squared cosine). Squaring the projection emphasizes how well the sample lies within the class subspace, effectively measuring alignment with the principal modes of that class.  
- **Core assumption:** Class‑conditional representations occupy distinct low‑dimensional subspaces even in the presence of label noise.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [Section 5] proves MASC is quadratic; [Section 6.1] shows MASC outperforms linear probes in layers with high ambient dimensionality (e.g., ResNet‑18 L0/L1), suggesting quadratic geometry matters where linearity struggles.  
- **Break condition:** If class subspaces heavily overlap (e.g., due to extensive corruption or poor representations), the quadratic measure may not provide a discriminative advantage and could even overfit.

### Mechanism 3 – Linear Last‑Layer Transfer Can Repair Memorization  
- **Claim:** Replacing the model’s pre‑softmax weights with the class vectors obtained from the VeLPIC probe immediately improves test accuracy, thereby transferring the latent generalization present in the last hidden layer to the full model.  
- **Mechanism:** VeLPIC computes a single principal component per class from corrupted training activations, sign‑aligned with the class mean. Using these vectors as rows of the pre‑softmax weight matrix (and zero bias) forces the model to perform exactly the same linear classification that VeLPIC does on the last layer. This substitution overrides the corrupted‑label‑learned decision boundary with one that generalizes better on true labels.  
- **Core assumption:** The earlier layers already produce representations linearly decodable for the true task; only the final classifier has been degraded by memorization.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] “VeLPIC often surpasses MASC… Weight editing via VeLPIC immediately improves test accuracy in most models, except ResNet‑18”; [Section 8] details the procedure and Figure 7; [Section 9] explores dynamics after mid‑training intervention.  
- **Break condition:** For ResNet‑18 (and likely other deep residual networks) the intervention fails or worsens accuracy, indicating that latent generalization in those cases may not be linearly decodable from the last layer or that earlier layers also require adjustment.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Memorization under label noise**  
  Why needed? The paper studies models trained with randomly corrupted labels; understanding this regime is essential to interpret why latent generalization can exist despite poor model accuracy.  
  Quick check: What is memorization in deep nets, and how does it differ from generalizing on true labels?

- **Concept: Representation probing**  
  Why needed? The core analysis relies on probes (MASC, VeLPIC) that operate on internal activations; distinguishing linear vs. quadratic probes is central to the paper’s arguments.  
  Quick check: How does a linear probe differ from a quadratic probe like MASC, and what does each imply about the geometry of a layer’s representations?

- **Concept: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for class‑conditional modeling**  
  Why needed? Both MASC and VeLPIC depend on PCA to extract class-specific directions from training data representations; the construction details (augmentation, variance retained) affect probe behavior.  
  Quick check: Why is data augmentation (adding negatives) used before PCA in MASC, and how does VeLPIC resolve the sign ambiguity of the principal component?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map (arrow‑style):  
Data (MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST, CIFAR‑10, Tiny ImageNet) -> Corrupted Labels (uniform random with probability p) -> Model (MLP/CNN/AlexNet/ResNet‑18) -> Checkpoint (epoch k) -> Layer Activations (pre/post ReLU) -> Probe (MASC: class subspaces via PCA; VeLPIC: top PC vectors) -> Test Accuracy. Transfer: VeLPIC vectors (from last layer) -> Replace pre‑softmax weights (W = Vᵀ, bias = 0) -> Model -> Test Accuracy (immediate/after training).

Critical path:
1. Train model with corrupted data using chosen optimizer/hyperparameters; save checkpoints at specified epochs.
2. For each checkpoint, extract activations of the corrupted training set from selected layers.
3. Compute MASC subspaces (PCA on augmented data, retain 99% variance) and VeLPIC vectors (top PC with sign alignment).
4. Evaluate both probes on the test set to obtain accuracies.
5. For weight transfer, construct the final weight matrix from VeLPIC vectors (transpose) and zero the bias; evaluate the model on the test set.
6. Optionally, edit weights at a chosen epoch and continue training to study dynamics.

Design tradeoffs:
- Subspace dimensionality: MASC uses multiple PCs (captures richer geometry but may include noise); VeLPIC uses single PC (simpler, often stronger under high corruption).
- Layer choice: best‑performing layer varies; high ambient dimensionality often correlates with higher probe accuracy, but ResNet‑18 early layers favor MASC.
- Corruption degree: high corruption (>60%) tends to favor VeLPIC relative to MASC; low corruption may show the opposite.
- Intervention timing: early editing yields large immediate gains; later editing yields smaller or negative gains, especially in residual networks.

Failure signatures:
- Weight editing harms accuracy (e.g., ResNet‑18, low corruption) → latent generalization may not be linearly decodable or earlier layers need modification.
- VeLPIC accuracy consistently below model accuracy → probe fails to extract useful information; representations may already be optimal for corrupted objective.
- MASC accuracy below VeLPIC in later layers → quadratic extension adds no benefit; linear might suffice.
- Probe accuracy at random initialization far above chance but far below early training → latent generalization emerges during training.

First 3 experiments:
1. Reproduce dynamics: Train MLP on MNIST with 40% corruption, checkpoint every 10 epochs. Compute MASC and VeLPIC for every layer at each checkpoint and plot vs. model test accuracy. Expect early peak followed by divergence.
2. Compare probe types: Train CNN on Fashion‑MNIST with 0%, 20%, 80% corruption. At the best‑performing layer compute MASC, VeLPIC, and logistic‑regression probe accuracies. Verify logistic regression underperforms and VeLPIC often beats MASC at high corruption.
3. Weight editing transfer: Train ResNet‑18 on CIFAR‑10 with 60% corruption. At epoch 40, replace pre‑softmax weights with VeLPIC vectors from the last layer and continue training to 100 epochs. Record test accuracy immediately after replacement and at the end. Expect immediate gain but possibly subsequent decay; compare to baseline without edit.
