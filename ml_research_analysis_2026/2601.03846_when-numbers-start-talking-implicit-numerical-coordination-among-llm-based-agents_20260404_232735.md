# When Numbers Start Talking: Implicit Numerical Coordination Among LLM-Based Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03846  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03846  
- **Reference count:** 9  
- **Primary result:** LLM agents spontaneously generate low-entropy numerical sequences as covert signals; such structured communication selectively reduces cooperation in Stag Hunt and Prisoner’s Dilemma for mixed personalities, but is less effective than natural language.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores whether LLM-based agents can implicitly coordinate through numerical signals in strategic two-player games, without explicit language. Using GPT-4o across the Prisoner’s Dilemma, Snowdrift, Stag Hunt, and Harmony games, the authors compare several communication regimes: natural language, no communication, covert decimal/hexadecimal numbers, and random baselines. They find that when instructed to communicate via numbers, agents produce low-entropy, structured sequences—evidencing endogenous signaling. However, the behavioral impact of these covert signals is game-dependent: they reduce cooperation in games with strategic uncertainty (Stag Hunt, Prisoner’s Dilemma) when agents have mixed personalities, but have little effect in Harmony or Snowdrift. In repeated interactions, decimal channels become highly concentrated (a single symbol >80% in Harmony), while hexadecimal remains dispersed. Correlation with natural-language dynamics is moderate (ρ≈0.48). The study concludes that structured numerical covert communication emerges without semantic guidance, yet its strategic benefits are limited compared to explicit language.

## Method Summary
The experimental framework, FAIRGAME, runs 50 one-shot and 20 repeated (10 rounds each) two-player interactions. Agents are assigned either cooperative (C) or selfish (S) personalities, yielding three pairings: (C,C), (C,S), (S,S). For each game, six communication conditions are tested: no communication (None); natural language (NL); covert decimal numbers (C(D)); covert hexadecimal numbers (C(H)); agent-generated random decimal/hex numbers (LR(D)/(H)); and externally injected random decimal/hex numbers (R(D)/(H)). In covert conditions, agents must output exactly ten numbers per round; random-output conditions ask for numbers without communicative intent; injected random numbers are supplied independently. The LLM used is GPT-4o. Primary metrics are Shannon entropy of the pooled numerical sequences (lower = more structured signaling), cooperation rate (binary action), and Pearson correlation of cooperation trajectories between NL and numerical channels. Results show that covert conditions produce entropies around 0.4–0.9, significantly lower than random baselines (>0.95). Cooperation effects are selective, and decimal codes concentrate heavily in repeated games.

## Key Results
- **Structured covert signalling:** Covert decimal/hex sequences have Shannon entropy 0.4–0.9, far below the >0.95 of random baselines, demonstrating endogenous low‑entropy signalling.
- **Selective cooperation disruption:** For mixed (C,S) pairs in one‑shot games, covert communication reduces cooperation in Stag Hunt and Prisoner’s Dilemma, but not in Harmony or Snowdrift.
- **Channel‑dependent concentration:** In repeated Harmony, decimal covert signals become extremely concentrated (top symbol >80%), while hexadecimal signals remain use a broader set (top symbol ≤12.5%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Spontaneous Low‑Entropy Signalling
- **Claim:** LLM agents can spontaneously generate structured, low‑entropy numerical sequences as covert signals when explicitly instructed to communicate via numbers, distinguishing intentional signaling from random output.
- **Mechanism:** The instruction to “communicate by outputting exactly ten numbers” creates a dedicated channel. Agents, driven by game‑theoretic incentives, select numerical tokens that correlate with strategic choices, reducing Shannon entropy relative to both agent‑generated random numbers (LR) and externally injected random baselines (R). The entropy drop reflects a non‑uniform, incentive‑constrained distribution.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM policy, when prompted for communication, maps strategic context to token selection in a way influenced by the payoff structure, leading to predictable clustering.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “covert communication produces significantly lower entropy… indicating endogenous signalling.” [section 3.1] Table 2 shows consistently lower S, M, R² for C(D) and C(H) versus LR and R across all games. The decimal variant C(D) is particularly low.
- **Break condition:** If entropy for C(D)/C(H) does not fall significantly below LR/R (per statistical tests), the claim fails.

### Mechanism 2: Context‑Dependent Cooperation Effects
- **Claim:** The behavioral impact of covert numerical signals is context‑dependent, primarily reducing cooperation in games with strategic uncertainty (Stag Hunt, Prisoner’s Dilemma) for heterogeneously paired agents, but having minimal effect in games with dominant strategies (Harmony) or pure anti‑coordination (Snowdrift).
- **Mechanism:** Signals have no predefined meaning; agents must infer correlation with cooperation/defection from payoff feedback. In games where equilibrium selection is risky (SH, PD), the opaque signal introduces ambiguity, especially when personalities differ (C,S), disrupting natural alignment. In Harmony (dominant cooperation) and Snowdrift (anti‑coordination), core incentives dominate, so the signal has little effect.
- **Core assumption:** Heterogeneous personalities lead to divergent interpretations of the emergent code, causing friction in coordination‑critical games.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “covert signals affect cooperation selectively: they reduce cooperation in Stag Hunt and Prisoner’s Dilemma for mixed personalities in one‑shot games, but effects are weaker or absent in Harmony and Snowdrift.” [section 3.2] Fig 1a shows lowered cooperation under C(D) and NL vs. None/R for (C,S) in SH and PD; H and SD show minimal variation. Table 3 shows concentrated symbol use in SH and PD.
- **Break condition:** If cooperation under C(D) for (C,S) in SH and PD is statistically indistinguishable from the ‘None’ baseline, the selective disruptive effect is falsified.

### Mechanism 3: Channel‑Capacity Shapes Symbol Concentration
- **Claim:** The representational constraints of the communication channel (decimal vs. hexadecimal) fundamentally shape the emergent signaling structure: the smaller decimal alphabet induces strong concentration (low entropy, high symbol dominance), while the larger hexadecimal alphabet sustains a flatter, more dispersed distribution.
- **Mechanism:** With only 10 decimal tokens (0–9), agents quickly converge on a small subset that reliably maps to strategic choices, leading to high symbol frequency (e.g., one symbol >80% in H repeated). The 16‑symbol hexadecimal space provides more bandwidth, allowing a larger set of symbols to be used with less competition, preventing any single symbol from dominating even in repeated interactions.
- **Core assumption:** Alphabet size is a primary driver of signaling convention formation, independent of game‑specific payoffs.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “in repeated games, natural language maintains higher cooperation than numerical channels, while decimal covert signals become highly concentrated (e.g., one symbol >80% in Harmony).” [section 3.3] Table 4 shows C(D) top symbol frequencies dramatically higher (e.g., 83.4% in H) than C(H) top frequencies (max 12.5% in H). The text states “repeated interaction amplifies the structural differences.”
- **Break condition:** If symbol frequency distributions for C(D) and C(H) show similar concentration (e.g., top symbol difference <20%) in repeated games for the same game, the channel constraint explanation is insufficient.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Game‑Theoretic Equilibrium Selection
- **Why needed:** To understand *why* communication effects vary by game (PD vs. SH vs. H). The paper’s interpretation relies on knowing PD has a dominant defection equilibrium, SH has a risk‑dominant vs. payoff‑dominant tension, and H has dominant cooperation.
- **Quick check:** In the Stag Hunt game (payoffs: (C,C)=4,4; (D,C)=0,3; (C,D)=3,0; (D,D)=2,2), what are the two equilibria and why is coordination challenging?

### Concept: Shannon Entropy as a Measure of Signal Structure
- **Why needed:** The core metric for detecting covert signaling is normalized entropy (S, M, R²). Understanding that low entropy indicates a non‑uniform, predictable distribution (structured signal) versus high entropy (random noise) is critical for interpreting Table 2.
- **Quick check:** If a communication channel has 10 possible symbols and one is used 80% of the time, what is the approximate Shannon entropy relative to a uniform distribution?

### Concept: Prompt Engineering as a Control for Agent Behavior
- **Why needed:** The entire experimental design manipulates outcomes via prompt templates (“communicate” vs. “output without communicative purpose”). Grasping how subtle prompt changes define the communication regime is essential for replicating the study.
- **Quick check:** How does the prompt for condition C(D) differ from LR(D) in inducing intentionality, and why is this distinction methodologically crucial?

### Design tradeoffs
- **Decimal vs. Hexadecimal:** Decimal enables strong concentration and potentially clearer payoff anchoring but risks over‑interpretation of specific numbers. Hexadecimal resists concentration, suggesting signaling is less about specific numeric values and more about patterned variation, but makes human interpretation harder.
- **One‑shot vs. Repeated:** One‑shot tests pure signaling emergence without reputation. Repeated games allow conventions to solidify (seen in lower entropy in C(D) repeated) but introduce trajectory analysis complexity.
- **Natural Language Baseline:** NL is the gold standard for coordination but is semantically rich and hard to decompose. Numerical channels are sparse and opaque, providing a cleaner but less potent signal.

### Failure signatures
- **No signaling emergence:** C(D)/C(H) entropy values statistically indistinguishable from LR/R conditions (Table 2).
- **No behavioral selectivity:** Cooperation under C(D) changes uniformly across all games/personalities, contrary to the predicted pattern (Fig 1).
- **Channel effect collapse:** No significant difference in symbol concentration or cooperation between C(D) and C(H).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate entropy ordering:** For Prisoner’s Dilemma one‑shot, run all 7 communication conditions. Verify mean S(C(D)) < S(LR(D)) < S(R(D)) with statistical significance (as in Table 2).
2. **Replicate selective cooperation effect:** For the (C,S) personality pairing, run Stag Hunt and Prisoner’s Dilemma one‑shot. Verify C(D) and NL cooperation is significantly lower than ‘None’ baseline, while Harmony and Snowdrift show no such drop (using Fig 1a data).
3. **Replicate channel‑concentration effect:** For Harmony repeated games, run C(D) and C(H). Verify the top symbol frequency for C(D) exceeds 70% while for C(H) it remains below 15% (as in Table 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do LLM agents select, combine, and interpret numerical symbols within covert communication to coordinate actions?
- **Basis:** [explicit] “it is unclear how these values are selected, combined, or interpreted within the agents’ decision processes” (p.5–6).
- **Why unresolved:** The study only analyses the statistical structure (entropy, symbol frequencies) of the messages, not the internal reasoning or mapping between numbers and action choices; thus the mechanism of meaning‑making remains opaque.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Elicit LLMs’ reasoning alongside numerical outputs, apply mechanistic interpretability to trace how numbers influence policy decisions, or run controlled experiments that manipulate hypothetical semantic mappings to test interpretation hypotheses.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Do covert communication phenomena (structured signalling, impact on cooperation) generalize to other LLM architectures and prompt languages beyond GPT-4o and English?
- **Basis:** [explicit] Conclusion states: “In future work, we will extend the study to additional prompt languages and models with stable strategic behaviour.” Acknowledges limitation to GPT-4o and English.
- **Why unresolved:** Other LLMs (e.g., Claude, Mistral) were excluded due to unstable strategic behaviours; it is unknown whether they would exhibit similar covert signalling. Cultural/linguistic differences in prompts might also affect emergence.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate experiments with a range of LLMs selected for stable game‑theoretic behaviour, and with prompts in various languages (e.g., Chinese, Arabic). Compare entropy patterns and cooperation outcomes across models and languages.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can fine‑tuning or other training interventions be used to strengthen, reshape, or improve the interpretability of covert numerical signalling?
- **Basis:** [explicit] Authors state they will “explore fine‑tuning strategies to test whether covert numerical signalling can be strengthened, reshaped, or made more interpretable.”
- **Why unresolved:** Unknown whether the spontaneous formation of low‑entropy codes can be guided or optimised via learning objectives. Also whether agents can be trained to produce signals that are more semantically transparent to human analysts remains open.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply fine‑tuning with rewards that encourage signal consistency (e.g., mutual information between messages and actions) or clarity (e.g., human decodability). Analyse resulting signal distributions, coordination success, and human interpretability.

## Limitations
- **Unspecified implementation details:** Exact prompt wording, FAIRGAME protocol (parsing rules, API parameters), GPT‑4o version, and statistical testing methods are not provided, hindering precise replication.
- **Limited model and game scope:** Experiments use only GPT‑4o and four canonical two‑player games, so generalizability to other LLMs and strategic contexts is uncertain.
- **Sensitivity to design choices:** Results may depend on unreported factors such as number of repetitions, temperature settings, or personality induction phrasing.
