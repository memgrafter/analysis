# Distill and Align Decomposition for Enhanced Claim Verification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21857
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21857
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The 8B decomposer achieves 71.75% macro-F1 over six evaluation settings, outperforming prompt-based methods (+1.99 vs VeriScore, +6.24 vs FActScore) and prior RL (+5.84 vs DyDecomp).

## Executive Summary
Claim verification via the decompose‑then‑verify pipeline often suffers from a mismatch between the quality of generated subclaims and the capabilities of the verifier. This paper introduces Distill and Align Decomposition (DAD), a framework that reframes decomposition as a sequential reasoning task, warm‑starts the decomposer via supervised fine‑tuning on teacher‑distilled exemplars, and optimizes it with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using a multi‑objective reward. The resulting 8B decomposer attains 71.75% macro‑F1 across six evaluation settings, significantly improving over prompt‑based and prior RL methods, while producing a balanced number of subclaims per sentence (8.14 on average). Human evaluations confirm high verifiability, coherence, clarity, completeness, and uniqueness, demonstrating that smaller models can achieve state‑of‑the‑art verification when decomposition is properly aligned with the verifier.

Beyond achieving superior verification accuracy, DAD demonstrates that aligning decomposition with the verifier via reinforcement learning and careful reward design can unlock the potential of smaller models, reducing reliance on massive proprietary systems. The balanced granularity and human‑validated quality of the generated subclaims also suggest that the framework produces outputs that are both machine‑friendly and human‑interpretable, which is crucial for real‑world fact‑checking applications.

## Method Summary
DAD comprises three stages. First, decomposition is reframed as a single‑call sequential reasoning task with four explicit steps: claim detection, decontextualization, relationship identification, and claim extraction. This structured prompt enforces intermediate reasoning and reduces error propagation. Second, the student decomposer (Llama‑3.1‑8B‑Instruct) is warm‑started via supervised fine‑tuning on 13.7k synthetic examples generated by a teacher model (Llama‑405B) using the same prompt. Third, the model is fine‑tuned with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using a multi‑objective reward that combines format compliance, a dense verifier signal (Brier score from Bespoke‑MiniCheck‑7B), and a checklist‑based quality assessment from an LLM judge (Llama‑70B). Training employs LoRA adapters (SFT: r=32, α=64, 1 epoch, LR 2e‑4; RL: r=16, α=32, 600 steps, LR 2e‑6) and a group size of 8 completions per prompt. The verifier remains fixed; only the decomposer policy is optimized. The final system produces subclaims that are both verifier‑aligned and balanced in granularity, leading to improved verification accuracy.

## Key Results
- 71.75% macro‑F1 over six evaluation settings, surpassing prompt‑based (+1.99 vs VeriScore, +6.24 vs FActScore) and prior RL (+5.84 vs DyDecomp).
- Balanced subclaim count: 8.14 per sentence, avoiding the extremes of over‑decomposition (22.92) and under‑decomposition (1.66) seen in other methods.
- Human evaluation indicates high scores on verifiability, coherence, clarity, completeness, and uniqueness, enabling smaller models to reach state‑of‑the‑art verification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Sequential Reasoning Improves Subclaim Quality
- **Claim:** Framing decomposition as a sequential reasoning task (claim detection → decontextualization → relationship identification → claim extraction) within a single model call improves subclaim quality by enforcing explicit intermediate reasoning.
- **Mechanism:** The structured prompt forces the model to articulate each step, reducing error propagation. For example, resolving references (decontextualization) before splitting claims prevents incoherent or ambiguous subclaims.
- **Core assumption:** Intermediate reasoning steps align with human‑like decomposition and are learnable via SFT/RL.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "reframes decomposition as a sequential reasoning task"; [section 3.1] Four‑step process described; "enforces explicit reasoning about verifiability, decontextualization, relationships, and claim boundaries".
- **Break condition:** If any step fails (e.g., incorrect relationship identification), subsequent steps degrade, yielding unfaithful or unverifiable subclaims. Appendix Table 9 shows step‑wise contributions.

### Mechanism 2: Teacher‑Distilled SFT Provides High‑Quality Warm‑Start
- **Claim:** Supervised fine‑tuning on teacher‑distilled exemplars provides a high‑quality initialization for RL, avoiding cold‑start problems and teaching format adherence.
- **Mechanism:** A large teacher model (Llama‑405B) generates synthetic decompositions using the same sequential prompt. The student (Llama‑8B) matches these via token‑level loss, acquiring baseline skills before RL alignment.
- **Core assumption:** Teacher outputs are high‑quality and diverse enough to bootstrap the student policy.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "warm‑starts the policy via supervised fine‑tuning on teacher‑distilled exemplars"; [section 3.2] "distill decomposition exemplars from a larger teacher policy πT".
- **Break condition:** Teacher model biases or noise in synthetic data propagate to student, limiting ceiling. Paper acknowledges "synthetic exemplars from a single teacher model" as a limitation (Section 9).

### Mechanism 3: GRPO with Multi‑Objective Reward Aligns Decomposition to Verifier
- **Claim:** Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a multi‑objective reward (format + verifier Brier score + checklist) jointly optimizes decomposition for verifier alignment and quality desiderata without trade‑offs.
- **Mechanism:** GRPO uses group‑relative advantages for stable RL updates. The dense Brier reward provides finer‑grained verifier feedback than sparse accuracy. The checklist reward (LLM‑as‑judge) enforces quality criteria (verifiability, decontextualization, etc.) independent of the verifier’s latent preferences.
- **Core assumption:** The three reward components are sufficiently aligned and combinable via equal weighting.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "optimizes with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using a multi‑objective reward combining format compliance, a dense verifier signal (Brier score), and a checklist‑based quality assessment"; [section 3.3] Reward definitions; Table 3 ablation shows full model (all three rewards) performs best.
- **Break condition:** Conflicting reward signals could destabilize training or bias toward one objective (e.g., verifier‑only RL leads to under‑decomposition per Table 3).

- Why needed: DAD uses GRPO (an RL algorithm) with a multi‑objective reward; grasping policy gradient methods, advantage estimation, and reward shaping is key.

**Concept: LLM‑as‑a‑Judge for quality assessment**
- Why needed: The checklist reward relies on a judge model (Llama‑70B) to score subclaims against quality criteria; this is a form of automated, scalable evaluation.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Teacher model (Llama‑405B) generates synthetic decompositions for SFT; Student decomposer (Llama‑8B) is the policy; Verifier (Bespoke‑MiniCheck‑7B) provides dense Brier reward during RL; Judge (Llama‑70B) provides checklist reward; Retrieval module (Wikipedia/Google Search) supplies evidence to the verifier; GRPO trainer optimizes the student.
- **Critical path:** Data generation (teacher SFT data) → SFT warm‑up → RL with GRPO (multi‑objective reward). The verifier is fixed; only the decomposer policy is trained.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  1. Sequential reasoning prompt vs. latency: Single model call is efficient but may constrain granularity compared to iterative/cascade methods.
  2. Multi‑objective reward vs. tuning complexity: Equal weighting simplifies design but may not suit all datasets; paper shows ablation in Table 3.
  3. Teacher‑distilled SFT vs. diversity: Low‑cost warm‑start but risks narrow distribution; alternative could be diverse human annotations.
  4. LLM‑as‑a‑judge vs. cost/biases: Scalable checklist but inherits judge model’s flaws; human eval used for validation only.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. Poor format compliance (missing tags, invalid list) → parsing failures downstream.
  2. Over/under‑decomposition (extreme subclaim count) → likely from reward mis‑specification (e.g., verifier‑only RL).
  3. Low human‑evaluated quality (verifiability, coherence) despite high automated metrics → checklist reward insufficient or judge bias.
  4. Performance collapse on out‑of‑domain data (e.g., BINGCHAT) → overfitting to training distribution (Wikipedia/PerplexityAI).
  5. Retrieval failures cause verifier errors even with good decompositions; e.g., missing evidence for time‑sensitive claims.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ablation of reward components** (Table 3): Train with only format+verifier (sparse/dense) vs. adding checklist; observe subclaim count and F1 on PerplexityAI/FELM.
  2. **Generalization test with non‑aligned verifier** (Table 2): Pair trained DAD decomposer with ClearCheck‑8B vs. base Llama‑8B; assess if improvements transfer.
  3. **Reasoning trace analysis** (Appendix Table 9): Manually inspect intermediate steps (claim detection, decontextualization, relationships) for correctness on sampled inputs; identify which step contributes most to final subclaim quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization across verifiers and knowledge sources:** The paper only evaluates DAD with Bespoke‑MiniCheck‑7B and Wikipedia/Google Search, leaving unknown whether the decomposition policy transfers to other verifiers or knowledge sources. Resolving this requires systematic evaluation across multiple verifier architectures and knowledge sources, reporting verification accuracy, subclaim statistics, and failure cases.
2. **Impact of single‑teacher distillation:** The authors note reliance on synthetic exemplars from a single teacher (Llama‑405B) may limit diversity and introduce bias. Evidence needed: compare students trained on multiple teachers (e.g., ensemble distillation) measuring diversity and downstream performance.
3. **Reliability of LLM‑as‑a‑Judge reward:** The checklist reward depends on Llama‑3.3‑70B judgments; biases or inconsistencies may affect training. Resolution: ablations replacing or removing the checklist reward, testing different judges, or using reward models trained on human annotations.
4. **Adaptation to non‑English and specialized domains:** The approach was tested only on English Wikipedia/Google Search; multilingual and domain‑specific verification remain open. Evidence: evaluation on multilingual fact‑checking benchmarks (e.g., X‑FACT) and domain‑specific datasets (e.g., biomedical claims).
5. **Fixed sentence‑level segmentation:** Sentence boundaries may not capture multi‑sentence factual relationships; adaptive segmentation could improve verification. Evidence: implement and compare segmentation strategies on datasets with multi‑sentence claims.

## Limitations
- Implementation details (retrieval, prompts, SFT data generation, GRPO) are incompletely specified, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Reliance on a single teacher model (Llama‑405B) risks bias and limits decomposition diversity.
- LLM‑as‑judge for checklist reward may introduce biases and cause training instability.

## Confidence

- SFT warm‑start effectiveness: Medium
- GRPO multi‑objective reward optimization: High
- Overall DAD achieves SOTA verification: High
