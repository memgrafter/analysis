# Stabilizing Policy Optimization via Logits Convexity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.00963
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00963
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: LCO reduces gradient norms, prevents collapse, and consistently outperforms PPO, GRPO, DAPO; LCO-KLD achieves 73.20% Pass@1 on MATH500 (Qwen-3-4B) vs PPO's 67.80%.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning (RL) for aligning large language models (LLMs) suffers from instability compared to supervised fine-tuning (SFT). This work identifies logits convexity in the SFT loss as a key stabilizing property missing in standard policy gradient methods like PPO. The authors propose Logits Convex Optimization (LCO), which reformulates RL as supervised regression toward optimal logit targets derived from advantage signals. LCO objectives (MSE, log-cosh, KL) are proven logits-convex, yielding self-stabilizing gradient bounds. Empirically, across multiple model families (Qwen, Llama, Mistral) and benchmarks (MATH500, QA-Feedback, AlpacaEval), LCO reduces gradient volatility, prevents training collapse, and consistently improves performance over PPO and recent variants.

## Method Summary
LCO transforms the RL objective into a convex loss over model logits. The core idea is to define an optimal target distribution \( \pi^* \) whose logits are \( z^* = z_{\text{old}} + A/\beta \), where \( z_{\text{old}} \) are the behavior policy logits, \( A \) is the advantage, and \( \beta \) is a temperature. The current policy is then trained to match this target using a convex divergence: mean squared error (LCO-MSE), log-cosh (LCO-LCH), or forward KL divergence (LCO-KLD). These losses are convex in the logits, ensuring stable gradient descent. The approach can leverage dense advantage estimates (e.g., from a DPO-trained reward model) or sparse estimates (only for sampled actions). Training proceeds by sampling prompts, generating responses, estimating advantages, computing the LCO loss between current and target logits, and updating the policy via gradient descent. A single epoch of SFT on high-quality demonstrations is typically applied beforehand.

## Key Results
- LCO-KLD achieves **73.20% Pass@1** on MATH500 with Qwen-3-4B, outperforming PPO's 67.80% and GRPO/DAPO.
- LCO methods show **stable training dynamics**: gradient norms decrease smoothly and policy entropy remains healthy, whereas PPO exhibits spikes and collapse.
- Across Qwen, Llama, and Mistral models on reasoning and instruction-following benchmarks, LCO variants consistently match or exceed PPO, GRPO, and DAPO, with particular gains when using dense advantage estimates and higher sample efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Logits Convexity in SFT vs Non-convex PPO
- **Claim**: The convexity of the SFT loss with respect to model logits yields stable, steadily decreasing gradient norms, whereas the non‑convex PPO objective produces volatile gradients that can cause training collapse.
- **Mechanism**: In SFT, the Hessian of the negative log‑likelihood is positive semi‑definite (diag(π) − ππᵀ), guaranteeing logits convexity (Lemma 4.5). This ensures the parameter‑space gradient points toward the optimum (Proposition 4.4). In PPO, the clipping mechanism introduces non‑convexity (Equation 40), violating gradient directionality and leading to spikes, especially for negative‑advantage samples (Figure 4).
- **Core assumption**: The network operates in a local linear regime where the first‑order Taylor expansion of logits w.r.t. parameters holds (Assumption 4.2), which is reasonable for over‑parameterized LLMs.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "SFT loss exhibits logits convexity... PPO lacks this stabilizing property."
  - [section 4.3] "Lemma 4.5. The SFT loss function LSFT is logits convex... PPO is not logits convex."
  - [corpus] The retrieved related work (e.g., "Stabilizing Policy Gradients for Sample‑Efficient Reinforcement Learning in LLM Reasoning") focuses on clipping, KL penalties, or variance reduction; none identify logits‑level convexity as the root cause of instability.
- **Break condition**: The proof of convexity relies on the softmax decomposition; if the policy uses a non‑softmax output or advantage estimates become extremely noisy, the Hessian may lose positive semi‑definiteness and gradients could destabilize.

### Mechanism 2: LCO Objectives Emulate Logits Convexity
- **Claim**: By aligning the current policy logits with optimal targets derived from the RL objective, LCO objectives (MSE, log‑cosh, KL) are logits convex, thereby inheriting SFT‑like stable gradients while still solving the RL problem.
- **Mechanism**: LCO reformulates RL as supervised regression toward **z**∗ = **z**ₒₗ𝒹 + **A**/β. The MSE loss is strongly convex (H = 2/|V| I), log‑cosh is locally strongly convex (diag(sech²) > 0), and forward KL inherits the SFT Hessian (diag(π) − ππᵀ). All are convex (Lemma 4.6). Their gradient norms are bounded by functions of the loss (Proposition 4.7), providing a self‑stabilizing property.
- **Core assumption**: The optimal logits target **z**∗ is correctly estimated from advantage signals, and the policy capacity is sufficient to represent π∗.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.1] Definitions of LCO‑MSE, LCO‑LCH, LCO‑KLD.
  - [section 4.3] "Lemma 4.6. The LCO loss functions ... are all logits convex at each time step."
  - [corpus] No prior method in the corpus constructs a convex logits‑level loss; existing heuristics (clipping, KL) do not guarantee convexity.
- **Break condition**: Severely biased advantages or violation of the zero‑mean normalization for **A** can mis‑specify **z**∗, pushing the policy toward a suboptimal solution. An excessively large β flattens gradients and may stall learning.

### Mechanism 3: Self‑Stabilizing Gradient Magnitude Bounds
- **Claim**: LCO objectives have gradient norms that are upper‑bounded by monotonic functions of the current loss, causing updates to naturally diminish as training converges, preventing sudden spikes that cause collapse.
- **Mechanism**: For LCO‑MSE, ∥∇θL∥ ≤ (2σ_max/|V|)√(|V|L) (Proposition 4.7). For LCO‑LCH, bound involves (1 − exp(−2L)); for LCO‑KLD, bound is σ_max√(2L). These bounds depend on L, which decreases over time (Figure 5), guaranteeing smooth decay of gradient magnitude.
- **Core assumption**: The Jacobian ∇θ**z**θ (singular value σ_max) is approximately constant locally (Assumption 4.2).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.3] "Proposition 4.7 (Gradient Norm Upper Bounds)" presents the inequalities.
  - [section 6.2] Figure 6 shows LCO‑KLD gradient norm decays while PPO spikes.
  - [corpus] Existing stabilization techniques impose hard bounds (e.g., clipping) but lack a theoretical guarantee that gradient magnitude shrinks with the loss; LCO’s bound is a novel theoretical contribution.
- **Break condition**: If the Jacobian’s σ_max varies widely due to high non‑linearity, the bounds become loose, and gradient norms could still spike when the loss temporarily rises.

## Foundational Learning
- **Logits Convexity**: The loss Hessian with respect to logits is positive semi‑definite, ensuring gradient descent converges to a global minimum without spurious stationary points.  
  *Why needed*: The core theoretical guarantee of LCO hinges on convexity in logit space, which contrasts with non‑convex PPO.  
  *Quick check*: Given a loss L(z) over logits z, how does positive semi‑definiteness of its Hessian guarantee that gradient descent avoids spurious stationary points in parameter space?

- **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**: A widely used on‑policy RL algorithm that stabilizes training via clipping of the probability ratio.  
  *Why needed*: PPO is the primary baseline whose instability we analyze; its clipping mechanism and its effect on gradients must be understood.  
  *Quick check*: What role does the clipping mechanism in PPO play, and why does it fail to preserve logits convexity?

- **KL Divergence in RLHF**: Used to penalize deviation from a reference policy; forward KL matches modes while reverse KL encourages coverage.  
  *Why needed*: LCO‑KLD uses forward KL between optimal and current policies; KL penalties are central to RLHF.  
  *Quick check*: How does minimizing forward KL differ from reverse KL in terms of mode covering vs. mode seeking, and why might forward KL be preferred for stable alignment?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Base policy model -> Reward model -> Advantage estimator -> Optimal logits generator -> LCO loss module -> Optimizer

- **Critical path**:  
  a) Sample prompts, generate responses with current policy, compute advantages via reward model.  
  b) Detach old logits (z_old).  
  c) Compute optimal logits z* = z_old + A/β.  
  d) Compute LCO loss between current policy logits and z* (or derived π*).  
  e) Backpropagate and update policy parameters.  
  f) Periodically refresh old policy for next iteration.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  *Advantage density*: Dense estimators (e.g., DPO‑based) give per‑token signals, improving performance (Table 5) but require a good reward model. Sparse estimators only update sampled actions, reducing computational cost.  
  *Loss choice*: LCO‑MSE is strongly convex but sensitive to outlier advantages; LCO‑LCH is more robust via log‑cosh; LCO‑KLD aligns distributions and preserves exploration but needs careful advantage normalization to avoid degeneracy.  
  *Hyperparameters*: β scales advantage magnitude; too small risks under‑regularization, too large flattens gradients. Learning rate should respect the local linear regime (Assumption 4.2).

- **Failure signatures**:  
  *Early gradient explosion*: Often from noisy advantages or too large β; monitor gradient norms (should decay as in Figure 5).  
  *Policy collapse*: Probabilities concentrate on one token if KL to old policy is too weak; track entropy (Figure 6 shows entropy drop in PPO vs. stability in LCO).  
  *Divergence from reference*: If the reverse KL (old→new) isn’t controlled, the assumption of fixed π_old for z* breaks, causing mismatch.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Gradient norm comparison**: Train SFT, PPO, LCO‑KLD on a small dataset; plot gradient norms. Expect SFT/LCO decreasing, PPO spiking (Figure 6).  
  2. **Ablation on advantage estimator**: Compare LCO‑MSE with dense vs. sparse advantages on MATH500; dense should yield higher pass@1 (Table 5).  
  3. **Loss variant evaluation**: Test LCO‑MSE, LCO‑LCH, LCO‑KLD on a reasoning benchmark (e.g., MATH500 small) with fixed seeds; measure Pass@1 and gradient variance. Expect LCO‑KLD to be most robust, LCO‑LCH most sample‑efficient.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Do GRPO, DAPO, and GSPO have logits convex losses?  
*Basis*: The paper only proves PPO's lack of logits convexity; newer stable algorithms like GRPO, DAPO, and GSPO are used as baselines but their convexity is not analyzed.  
*Why unresolved*: Determining whether logits convexity is necessary for stability or if other mechanisms can compensate would clarify the fundamental cause of RL instability and guide future algorithm design.  
*Evidence*: Derive the Hessian of each algorithm's loss with respect to logits; check for positive semi‑definiteness; correlate convexity with empirical gradient norm stability during training.

### Open Question 2
How does sparse advantage estimation (setting advantage to zero for non‑sampled actions) affect LCO's theoretical convergence guarantees and practical stability?  
*Basis*: The theoretical analysis (Propositions 4.8, 4.9) assumes access to dense advantages for all actions, but the paper also proposes a sparse strategy and experiments with it without theoretical examination.  
*Why unresolved*: Sparse estimation is more realistic but may weaken the monotonic gradient decay property or alter the optimization landscape, impacting the claimed benefits of logits convexity.  
*Evidence*: Develop convergence bounds under sparse advantage models; run controlled experiments varying the sparsity of advantage information and measuring convergence speed, gradient norm trajectories, and final performance.

### Open Question 3
To what extent does the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) linearization assumption (Assumption 4.2) hold during actual RL training of LLMs, and how do violations impact the logits convexity stability guarantees?  
*Basis*: The gradient directionality guarantee (Proposition 4.4) and convergence rates rely on a first-order Taylor expansion, which assumes small parameter displacements. This assumption may not hold early in training when policies are far from optimal.  
*Why unresolved*: If linearization fails, the guarantee that gradients point toward optimal parameters could break down, potentially leading to instability even with convex losses. Understanding this robustness is key for applying the theory to practice.  
*Evidence*: Empirically measure the error in the linear approximation across training; deliberately use larger learning rates to induce larger updates and observe whether gradient directionality and norm bounds degrade; extend the theory to account for higher‑order terms.

### Open Question 4
When advantages are not normalized, does choosing the shift C = -mean(A) in the optimal logits target z* = z_old + A/β + C improve convergence of LCO-MSE and LCO-LCH compared to using C=0?  
*Basis*: The paper shows that under advantage normalization (mean zero), C=0 minimizes the convergence bound. However, in practice, advantages may not be perfectly normalized, and the impact of an adaptive C is unexplored.  
*Why unresolved*: The convergence bounds depend on ||A+C||²; an optimal C could tighten the bound and accelerate convergence without extra computational cost.  
*Evidence*: Implement LCO with adaptive centering (C = -mean(A)) and compare against C=0 on training dynamics (gradient norm decay, convergence speed) and final accuracy across multiple benchmarks and advantage estimation methods.

## Limitations
- **Advantage estimation sensitivity**: LCO's stability relies on accurate, low‑variance advantages. Noisy reward model outputs could mis‑specify z* and destabilize training.
- **Jacobian assumption robustness**: Theoretical gradient bounds assume constant singular values (σ_max) in the local linear regime. Highly nonlinear LLM updates may violate this, making bounds loose.
- **Scalability**: Experimental validation is limited to small models (≤4B parameters). Stability of logits convexity in larger billion‑parameter LLMs remains unverified.

## Confidence
- **Logits convexity in SFT**: High (mathematically proven via Hessian PSD).
- **LCO convexity & gradient bounds**: Medium (proofs correct but hinge on local linearity assumption; empirical validation only on small models).
- **Empirical superiority over PPO**: Medium‑Low (promising but narrow benchmark coverage; limited independent verification).
