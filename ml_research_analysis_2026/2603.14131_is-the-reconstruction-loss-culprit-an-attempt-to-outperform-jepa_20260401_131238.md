# Is the reconstruction loss culprit? An attempt to outperform JEPA

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.14131  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14131  
- **Reference count**: 0  
- **Primary result**: Gated predictive autoencoders, which explicitly select predictable components for prediction while reconstructing all features, maintain high linear probe accuracy across all noise levels and match or outperform JEPA; standard autoencoders collapse under high noise.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates why reconstruction-based autoencoders fail on a controlled linear dynamical testbed where predictive models like JEPA succeed. It diagnoses the failure to bottleneck-induced variance-seeking behavior and proposes a gated predictive autoencoder that over-parameterizes the latent space and uses a trainable gate to select the most predictable features for the prediction loss while reconstructing all observations. This design yields robust recovery of the true latent state across a wide range of noise intensities, matching or exceeding JEPA performance. The results highlight the importance of focusing predictive learning on stable signal components rather than high-variance distractors.

## Method Summary
The experimental testbed is the "TV-series" environment: a 4‑dimensional latent state `s_t` evolves via a stable linear dynamics (`s_{t+1} = A s_t + w_t`), a 4‑dimensional distractor `d_t` follows an AR(1) process, and noisy observations `x_t` (20‑D) are generated as `x_t = C s_t + D(σ d_t) + e_t`. The scalar σ controls distractor strength. Models compared include JEPA (predictive learning with student‑teacher EMA), standard autoencoders (AE/VAE), predictive autoencoders (PredAE), and the proposed gated predictive autoencoder. The gated model uses an encoder to produce M > 4 latent features (with normalized weights). A trainable gate selects the top‑4 features based on learned logits; these are fed to a predictor that forecasts the next representation (from a target encoder). The decoder reconstructs `x_t` from all M features. Training combines a reconstruction loss (MSE) and a prediction loss (MSE) with weight λ. Representation quality is evaluated by training a linear probe to predict `s_t` from latents and measuring R² on held‑out data.

## Key Results
- JEPA remains robust to increasing distractor noise, while standard autoencoders (AE/VAE) suffer severe degradation in linear probe R² (collapse).
- PCA baselines reveal that predictable signal components occupy higher principal components under high noise, causing variance‑seeking autoencoders to discard them.
- The gated predictive autoencoder, with over‑complete latents and a learned gate, maintains high R² across all noise levels, matching or surpassing JEPA.
- Ablations (random gate, no gate, using all features for prediction) show that both gating and over‑parameterization are essential for the observed robustness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Training with a predictive loss in representation space (as in JEPA) biases the model to capture the predictable latent dynamics, which remain aligned with the true state even under high noise.  
- **Mechanism**: The predictor maps the current representation to the next‑time‑step representation (from a target encoder). Minimizing prediction error forces the representation to contain information that is temporally predictable. Since the true latent state follows a stable linear dynamics, its components are predictable; distractor noise, even if structured, may not align with this predictability, so the representation converges to the signal subspace.  
- **Core assumption**: The true latent state is the only part of the observations that is predictably related across time under the given dynamics; distractor noise does not share the same predictive relationship.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - JEPA robust to noise; Predictive Encoder (prediction‑only) also robust.  
  - Related work on JEPA denoising supports this bias.  
- **Break condition**: If dynamics are highly stochastic or the distractor process itself is predictable from the signal, the predictive objective may also capture noise, reducing alignment with the true state.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Autoencoders with a fixed‑capacity bottleneck allocate latent dimensions to the highest‑variance directions in the observations, which under high noise correspond to distractor dynamics, causing collapse in alignment with the true state.  
- **Mechanism**: Reconstruction loss (MSE) encourages capturing the principal components of the observation distribution. When distractor noise dominates variance (high σ), the top principal components correspond to noise. With a fixed latent dimension (4), the encoder discards lower‑variance signal components, losing alignment with the true state.  
- **Core assumption**: Distractor noise has higher variance than the signal when σ is large, and its principal components are orthogonal to the signal subspace. A linear probe can recover the state only if the latent spans the signal subspace.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - First four principal components perform roughly as VAE latents; second four components outperform JEPA and show much more stability for large noise levels.  
  - Autoencoder failures strongly influenced by bottleneck/component‑selection effects; confirmed by PCA baselines.  
- **Break condition**: If the true signal has higher variance than noise, autoencoders might succeed; or if the latent dimension is large enough to include both signal and noise, but then the representation may not be compact.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: An over‑complete latent space with a trainable gate that selects a predictable subset for the prediction loss allows reconstruction of all observations (including noise) while focusing predictive learning on the signal, outperforming both standard autoencoders and JEPA.  
- **Mechanism**: The encoder produces M > 4 latent features. All features are used by the decoder for reconstruction, capturing both signal and noise. A gate (learned logits) selects the top‑4 features that minimize the prediction loss; only these feed the predictor. The prediction loss trains the selected features to be predictable of the next state (from a target encoder). The remaining features handle noise for reconstruction. Weight normalization prevents collapse.  
- **Core assumption**: The signal components are among the M latent features and can be identified by their predictability. The gate can learn to select them via gradient descent.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Gated autoencoders outperform all other kinds of AE and JEPA on this data.  
  - The model matches or outperforms JEPA across the entire noise range.  
- **Break condition**: If the signal components are not the most predictable (e.g., noise has predictable patterns), the gate may select wrong features. Also, if M is too small to contain both signal and noise, the model fails.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**:  
Input → Encoder (online) → z_t (M‑dim). From z_t, Gate selects top‑4 indices I; selected features z_t[I] → Predictor → predicted z_{t+1}. Target Encoder (EMA copy of online Encoder) produces z_{t+1} as prediction target. Decoder uses the full z_t to reconstruct x_t. Encoder weights are normalized to unit norm.

**Critical path (training loop)**:
1. For each batch, compute online encoder z_t and target encoder z_{t+1} (via EMA).
2. Apply gate to z_t to get selection indices I (same for all timesteps in the batch).
3. Predict next representation: ŷ_{t+1} = predictor(z_t[I]).
4. Compute prediction loss: L_pred = MSE(ŷ_{t+1}, z_{t+1}[I]).
5. Compute reconstruction loss: L_rec = MSE(x_t, decoder(z_t)).
6. Total loss = L_rec + λ * L_pred.
7. Backpropagate to update online encoder, decoder, predictor, and gate logits.
8. Update target encoder weights as EMA of online encoder.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **M (over‑parameterization)**: Larger M increases capacity to separate signal/noise but raises compute and data needs. Too small may omit signal components.
- **Gate selection**: Hard top‑k (straight‑through) is simple but may cause gradient instability; soft selection (e.g., Gumbel‑Softmax) is smoother but may yield diffuse weights.
- **Weight normalization**: Prevents collapse by fixing feature norm, but may limit adaptive scaling; separate gain parameters might help.
- **EMA decay**: Higher decay stabilizes targets but slows adaptation; typical values 0.99–0.999.
- **Loss weighting λ**: Balances reconstruction and prediction; should be tuned on validation.

**Failure signatures**:
- Gate selects non‑predictive features: Monitor correlation of selected features with true state; if low, increase λ or adjust gate regularization.
- Encoder collapse: Latent features have near‑zero variance; check weight norms and prediction loss scale.
- Overfitting to noise: If M is too large and gate ineffective, selected features may still correlate with noise; evaluate R² on held‑out noise levels.
- Training instability: Loss spikes or NaNs; try lower learning rate or softer gating.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline replication**: Train gated model on TV‑series testbed across σ, compare R² (linear probe) against JEPA, VAE, and PCA baselines; reproduce Fig. 5.
2. **Gate ablation**: Replace learned gate with random top‑4 selection or use all M features for prediction; measure performance drop to validate gate necessity.
3. **Component analysis**: After training, compute each latent feature’s R² with true state; verify that selected features are the highest‑scoring. Vary M to study capacity effects.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Combining reconstruction and prediction in complex settings**: How can reconstruction and prediction objectives be combined effectively in nonlinear, high‑dimensional environments? The paper only validates on a linear testbed; it’s unclear how findings transfer to realistic data distributions where reconstruction may interact unpredictably with prediction.
2. **Scaling to nonlinear, high‑dimensional data**: Can gated predictive autoencoders scale to nonlinear observation models and high‑dimensional inputs while maintaining stable performance across noise levels? The current evaluation is limited to linear low‑dimensional data; nonlinearities could cause collapse or training instability, and the linear gate may become insufficient.
3. **Necessity of EMA in JEPA**: Is the student‑teacher architecture with exponential moving average (EMA) necessary for JEPA‑style predictive learning, or can simpler predictive objectives suffice? The “Predictive Encoder” variant sometimes matched JEPA without the teacher, but the necessity of EMA remains unsettled.
4. **Better evaluation metrics for world models**: What evaluation metrics beyond linear probe accuracy best capture a representation’s suitability for long‑horizon planning and control? Linear probe R² is a weak proxy; metrics like dynamics consistency, rollout error, and planning success may be more indicative.

## Limitations
- **Generalization**: Experiments solely on linear‑Gaussian dynamics; real‑world data often involve nonlinearities and complex, structured noise, so it is uncertain whether the conclusions hold broadly.
- **Architectural sensitivity**: The gated model relies on several heuristic choices (hard vs. soft gate, overcompleteness factor M, weight normalization, loss weight λ) whose individual impacts were not systematically ablated.
- **Hyperparameter ambiguity**: Key training details (EMA decay, learning rate schedule, number of epochs, batch size) are not fully specified, which may affect reproducibility and performance.
