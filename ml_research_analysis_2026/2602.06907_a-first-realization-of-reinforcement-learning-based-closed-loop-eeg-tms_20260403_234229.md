# A first realization of reinforcement learning-based closed-loop EEG-TMS

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.06907  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06907  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A reinforcement learning agent learned to target mu-rhythm phases associated with high or low corticospinal excitability, yielding immediate MEP increases and differential SMA-M1 connectivity plasticity.

## Executive Summary
This study presents the first closed-loop real-time EEG-TMS system using reinforcement learning (RL) to automatically identify an individual’s mu-rhythm phase that maximizes or minimizes corticospinal excitability. In 25 healthy participants, the Deep Q-Network agent learned to select phase bins that immediately increased paired-pulse MEP amplitudes in the “INCREASE” condition compared to baseline—an effect absent with random phases. Thirty minutes after training, a uniform long-term potentiation-like increase in MEP was observed across all conditions, but differential changes in resting-state functional connectivity (imaginary coherence) between supplementary motor area (SMA) and primary motor cortex (M1) were found: only the INCREASE condition showed a significant SMA-M1 connectivity increase. These results demonstrate the feasibility of user-independent, state-adaptive neuromodulation.

## Method Summary
The experimental protocol combined high-density EEG, real-time phase estimation via the Brain Oscillation State Sensor (BOSS), and a Deep Q-Network (DQN) agent controlling two TMS coils (SMA conditioning, M1 test). The agent selected one of eight discrete mu-rhythm phase bins for each trial; BOSS triggered the TMS pair at the predicted phase. The reward was the measured ppMEP amplitude relative to a baseline-derived target (1.5× for INCREASE, 0.7× for DECREASE). Over 400 training trials, the agent updated its policy to favor bins yielding higher rewards. Outcomes included immediate post-training ppMEP at the learned optimal phase, and long-term changes (30 min) in ppMEP and resting-state SMA-M1 functional connectivity. Sessions with low mu-rhythm signal-to-noise ratio (<4 dB) were excluded.

## Key Results
- Immediate post-training: ppMEP was significantly higher in the INCREASE condition when targeting the RL-learned optimal phase versus baseline (p = 0.009); no such effect for random phases.  
- Long-term (30 min): a uniform ppMEP increase across conditions (pooled p = 0.030), indicating non-specific potentiation.  
- Differential connectivity: only INCREASE showed a significant increase in SMA-M1 imaginary coherence (p = 0.011); DECREASE did not.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: RL identifies phase–excitability association
- **Claim:** The DQN agent learns an individual’s mu-rhythm phase bins that yield high or low corticospinal excitability by associating phase selection with immediate ppMEP reward.  
- **Mechanism:** At each trial the agent picks a phase bin; BOSS triggers the SMA-M1 TMS pair at that phase; ppMEP amplitude provides the reward; the agent updates Q‑values over 400 trials to increase the probability of reward‑maximizing bins.  
- **Evidence:** The agent’s optimal phase selection frequency increased across learning epochs; the distribution of learned phases differed between INCREASE (ascending mu phase in 66% of sessions) and DECREASE (descending phase in 64%) conditions (p = 0.017).  
- **Break condition:** Mu‑rhythm SNR < 4 dB prevents reliable phase estimation and destabilizes learning.

### Mechanism 2: High‑excitability phase pairing induces LTP‑like plasticity
- **Claim:** Repeated SMA‑M1 paired stimulation at the RL‑identified high‑excitability phase leads to specific strengthening of the SMA‑M1 connection, manifesting as increased functional connectivity.  
- **Mechanism:** The 6 ms SMA‑M1 inter‑stimulus interval follows Hebbian spike‑timing‑dependent plasticity: when SMA stimulation precedes M1 during a state of high M1 excitability (learned phase), cortico‑cortical synapses from SMA to M1 are potentiated.  
- **Evidence:** Only the INCREASE condition showed a significant increase in SMA‑M1 imaginary coherence at Post30 (p = 0.011).  
- **Break condition:** If the learned phase does not correspond to genuine M1 excitability peaks, differential connectivity effects fail to emerge.

### Mechanism 3: Non‑specific LTP from high‑dose paired stimulation
- **Claim:** The 400 paired‑pulse trials themselves induce a uniform, long‑term potentiation‑like increase in corticospinal excitability regardless of phase.  
- **Mechanism:** The sheer number of paired associative stimuli drives homeostatic or heterosynaptic plasticity in M1, leading to sustained heightened responsiveness.  
- **Evidence:** Pooled ppMEP across all conditions increased at Post30 (p = 0.030).  
- **Break condition:** Insufficient total pulses or inappropriate inter‑stimulus interval would not produce this uniform effect.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Reinforcement Learning (Deep Q‑Networks)
- **Why needed:** Enables the system to learn a subject‑specific policy for phase selection from scalar rewards without a predefined model.  
- **Quick check:** How does ε‑greedy exploration balance trying new phase bins versus exploiting currently best‑performing bins?

### Concept: EEG‑TMS Coupling & Mu‑Rhythm Phase
- **Why needed:** The mu‑rhythm phase serves as the state variable that modulates corticospinal excitability; its oscillatory nature provides a temporal framework for state‑adaptive stimulation.  
- **Quick check:** Why use mu‑rhythm phase rather than power or other frequencies for targeting excitability?

### Concept: Corticospinal Excitability & Paired‑Pulse TMS
- **Why needed:** Paired‑pulse MEP (ppMEP) reflects cortico‑cortical connectivity and acts as both reward signal and outcome measure.  
- **Quick check:** How does a 6 ms SMA‑to‑M1 inter‑stimulus interval facilitate Hebbian plasticity?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
High‑density EEG → Real‑time Phase Estimator (BOSS) → RL Agent (DQN) → TMS Stimulators (SMA & M1) → EMG & MEP Acquisition → Master Control PC (with Neuronavigation support).

**Critical path (one trial):**  
EEG stream → mu‑band filtering → BOSS phase prediction → wait until predicted phase → trigger SMA coil → trigger M1 coil (6 ms later) → record EMG → compute ppMEP → calculate reward → update RL agent.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Discrete (8) phase bins simplify the action space but discard continuous phase information.  
- Agent memory (LSTM) was restricted during testing to avoid overfitting given limited data.  
- Reward shaping uses baseline multiples; sensitive to baseline stability.  
- SNR threshold (4 dB) balances phase reliability against data quantity.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Agent fails to learn: optimal‑phase selection frequency remains near random (~12.5% per bin).  
- No differential effect: Post_optimal ppMEP shows no improvement, and no difference between INCREASE and DECREASE.  
- Uniform LTP swamps signal: similar ppMEP increase at Post30 across conditions, erasing condition‑specificity.  
- High intra‑individual phase variability: learned optimal phase differs across sessions for the same subject.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Open‑loop phase‑MEP mapping: Deliver single M1 pulses at all 8 mu‑phase bins (many trials/bin) offline to verify subject‑specific coupling.  
2. RL with cached rewards: Use pre‑recorded MEP data; agent selects bins and receives average reward from cache to test algorithm convergence without real‑time constraints.  
3. Closed‑loop with short training and full probe: Train for only 100 trials, then probe all 8 bins equally to evaluate specificity of the learned association.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Is the differential long‑term functional connectivity change caused by stimulation of the SMA‑M1 network, or would effective M1‑only stimulation suffice?  
2. Do the observed changes in corticospinal excitability and functional connectivity translate into measurable behavioral motor improvements?  
3. Would deploying the RL agent with its full learned policy during testing reveal multiple viable phase options and potentially enhance outcomes?  
4. Can incorporating a broader set of EEG features (e.g., mu power, phase‑power coupling, network connectivity) improve the accuracy and robustness of individualized closed‑loop TMS?  
5. To what extent is adaptive closed‑loop superior to traditional open‑loop stimulation in inducing long‑term plasticity and therapeutic effects?

## Limitations
- Proprietary BOSS phase‑estimation algorithm is not fully described, hindering replication.  
- Critical DQN hyperparameters and network architecture (Table S1) are missing.  
- High exclusion rate (~30% of sessions) due to low mu‑SNR (<4 dB) reduces generalizability.

## Confidence
- Immediate effect (INCREASE ppMEP ↑): **Medium**  
- Differential plasticity (SMA‑M1 FC ↑ only in INCREASE): **Medium**  
- Feasibility of RL‑based closed‑loop: **Medium‑High**

## Next Checks
1. Quantify real‑time phase estimation accuracy of the BOSS device (circular error < 10° vs. offline Hilbert transform).  
2. Disclose full DQN hyperparameters and network architecture; perform sensitivity analysis to assess robustness.  
3. Identify baseline metrics (mu‑SNR, phase‑MEP coupling strength) that predict learning success to refine inclusion criteria and power calculations for future studies.
