# Harmonic Dataset Distillation for Time Series Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.03760
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03760
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: HDT achieves state-of-the-art MSE across multiple backbones, scales with synthetic data size, maintains robust cross-architecture performance, and enables 80× faster fine-tuning of Moirai with only 2.5% MSE degradation.

## Executive Summary
Time series forecasting (TSF) often suffers from high computational costs due to massive datasets. Conventional dataset distillation (DD) methods, which synthesize small surrogate datasets, face architectural overfitting and limited scalability because they operate on local windows. This paper introduces Harmonic Dataset Distillation (HDT), a novel DD approach for TSF that decomposes sequences into sinusoidal harmonics via Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and aligns the most significant frequency components (Harmonic Matching). By performing updates in the frequency domain, HDT modifies the entire synthetic sequence globally, preserving long-range dependencies. Combined with gradient matching, HDT produces compact, model-agnostic distilled datasets. Extensive experiments on multiple TSF benchmarks demonstrate HDT’s superiority: it reduces MSE compared to prior methods (e.g., ETTh1: 0.430 vs. 0.492 with DLinear), scales effectively with synthetic data size, generalizes robustly across architectures, and approaches full-data performance even on large datasets like CA (46.6 vs. 44.25). Moreover, HDT enables 80× faster fine-tuning of the Moirai foundation model with only a 2.5% MSE increase.

## Method Summary
HDT formulates dataset distillation as a bi-level optimization problem. Given an original training set **X**, the goal is to synthesize a small dataset **S** that, when used to train a backbone model, yields performance comparable to training on **X**. Instead of distilling window-by-window (local updates), HDT represents **S** in the frequency domain: it computes the FFT of **S** to obtain coefficients **F_S**. For each outer-loop iteration, a random subsequence **X_sub** of equal length **M** is sampled from **X** and transformed to **F_X**. The top‑k harmonic frequencies (by amplitude) are selected from **F_X**, and both **F_X** and **F_S** are masked to retain only these harmonics. A harmonic loss **L_harm** (L1 norm of amplitude differences) encourages the synthetic data to match the original’s dominant periodic structure. The masked spectra are inverted to the time domain to obtain **X_H** and **S_H**, which are then used in a gradient‑matching loss **L_grad**. Specifically, a backbone model is trained for a few steps on **X_H** and **S_H**, and the normalized gradients with respect to the model parameters are compared. The total loss **L_total = L_grad + λ L_harm** is backpropagated to update **F_S**. After convergence, the final **S** is recovered via inverse FFT. This frequency‑domain global update mechanism allows a single synthetic sequence to capture long‑range dependencies across the entire window, overcoming the scalability limits of window‑based DD. The process is model‑agnostic because harmonic matching is intrinsic to the data, while gradient matching adapts to the backbone’s learning dynamics.

## Key Results
- **SOTA MSE**: On ETTh1, HDT achieves 0.430 MSE with DLinear, surpassing CondTSF (0.492) and other baselines.
- **Scalability**: Performance improves consistently as the synthetic size **M** increases, unlike window‑based methods that plateau.
- **Cross‑architecture robustness**: Distilled datasets generalise well across different backbones (DLinear, iTransformer, xPatch) with minimal MSE increase.
- **Near full‑data performance**: On the large CA dataset, HDT (46.6) approaches full‑data results (44.25) with a fraction of the data.
- **Efficient fine‑tuning**: For the Moirai foundation model, HDT enables 80× faster fine‑tuning while incurring only 2.5% MSE degradation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Harmonic Matching Preserves Global Periodic Structure
HDT decomposes both original and synthetic subsequences into frequency components via FFT and selects the top‑k frequencies (harmonics) from the original. The synthetic spectrum is updated to minimise the L1‑norm distance between the amplitudes of these selected harmonics. This forces the synthetic data’s dominant periodic patterns to align with those of the original, capturing the core, model‑agnostic periodic information sufficient for effective forecasting. The approach assumes that the most significant harmonics contain essential global temporal dependencies. If the original series has a flat power spectrum (noise‑dominated), this mechanism may capture noise rather than signal, limiting its effectiveness.

### Mechanism 2: Frequency‑Domain Global Updates Preserve Long‑Range Dependencies
Each harmonic basis function (a sinusoid) spans the entire sequence length **M**. Updating its coefficients in **F_S** changes the synthetic time series **S** everywhere, not just within a local window. In contrast, conventional window‑based DD updates individual data points within randomly sampled windows, ignoring context outside those windows. HDT’s global update mechanism therefore maintains the integrity of long‑range dependencies such as seasonality and trends, which is critical for TSF. This mechanism hinges on **M** being large enough to represent the longest meaningful period in the data; otherwise, the global influence is restricted by the window length.

### Mechanism 3: Combining Harmonic Matching and Gradient Matching Yields Model‑Agnostic Datasets
HDT simultaneously minimises harmonic loss (data‑centric periodic structure) and gradient matching loss (model‑centric training dynamics). Harmonic matching acts as a regulariser that anchors the synthetic data to fundamental periodic properties of the original series, independent of any specific architecture. Gradient matching ensures that training on **S** induces similar parameter updates as training on the original data. The combination prevents the synthetic dataset from overfitting to the inductive biases of the backbone used during distillation, thereby improving cross‑architecture generalisation. A potential conflict arises if the backbone’s learning dynamics are misaligned with the harmonic structure (e.g., a model that poorly captures periodicities); then the dual objectives might yield suboptimal **S**.

## Foundational Learning

### Fourier Transform & Spectral Analysis
**Why needed**: HDT operates in the frequency domain, representing a time series as a sum of sinusoids (harmonics). Understanding FFT, inverse FFT, amplitude spectra, and how masking frequencies affects time‑domain reconstruction is essential.
**Quick check**: If you zero out all but the top 5 amplitude frequencies in an FFT and apply iFFT, what primary property of the original signal is most likely preserved?  
*Answer: The dominant periodic components (e.g., main seasonalities or trends) are retained, while high‑frequency noise is suppressed.*

### Dataset Distillation (Bi‑level Optimization & Surrogate Objectives)
**Why needed**: HDT is an instantiation of dataset distillation; it aims to synthesize a small surrogate dataset that trains a model to similar performance as the full dataset. The bi‑level nature (inner loop: train on surrogate; outer loop: evaluate on original) is computationally expensive, hence gradient matching is used as a surrogate to approximate the outer‑loop loss.
**Quick check**: Why is directly optimizing \(\min_{S} L(T(\theta,S), X_{\text{test}})\) prohibitive, and what does gradient matching approximate?  
*Answer: It requires nested optimisation (training a model for each candidate S), which is orders of magnitude more expensive than distilling. Gradient matching approximates the outer‑loop loss by aligning the gradients of the model trained on the original and surrogate data, avoiding full training cycles.*

### Time Series Forecasting & Window‑based Processing
**Why needed**: The paper’s motivation and comparisons rely on standard TSF practice: fixed‑length lookback/window inputs and forecasting horizons. Understanding how sliding windows generate many training samples from a long series and why window‑based DD disregards global context is key to appreciating HDT’s design.
**Quick check**: Given a series of length 20 000 and a lookback window of 96, how many training samples are typically generated, and what assumption does each sample make?  
*Answer: Approximately 20 000 – 96 samples, each assuming the window is a representative, independent segment of the series, ignoring longer‑range dependencies beyond the window.*

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input (X) -> Initialize random S (length M) -> FFT(S) -> F_S  
Outer loop (until convergence):  
 Sample X_sub (length M) from X  
 FFT(X_sub) -> F_X  
 Select top‑k harmonics from F_X -> indices H  
 Mask F_X and F_S with H -> ÕF_X, ÕF_S  
 Compute L_harm = || |ÕF_X| - |ÕF_S| ||_p  
 iFFT(ÕF_X) -> X_H, iFFT(ÕF_S) -> S_H  
 Compute L_grad: train backbone on X_H and S_H for i and j steps, then normalized gradient difference  
 L_total = L_grad + λ L_harm  
 Update F_S via gradient descent on L_total  
Output S = iFFT(F_S)

**Critical path**: The key innovation is the harmonic alignment step: after sampling a subsequence, both original and synthetic are transformed to the frequency domain, the top‑k harmonics from the original are identified, and the synthetic’s amplitudes for those harmonics are forced to match. This global update (via frequency coefficients) modifies the entire synthetic sequence at once, preserving long‑range dependencies that window‑based updates miss.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **k (number of harmonics)**: Too few omits meaningful periodicities; too many includes noise or dataset‑specific artefacts, harming generalisation. Optimal *k* depends on the spectral characteristics of the data.
- **λ (regularisation weight)**: Balances adherence to original harmonic structure (L_harm) and fidelity to the backbone’s learning dynamics (L_grad). A high λ forces strong harmonic alignment but may ignore model‑specific details; too low reverts to standard gradient matching.
- **M (synthetic size)**: Larger M allows representation of longer‑period harmonics, improving scalability. However, computation increases with *M*, and there may be diminishing returns if the original data’s dominant periods are already captured.

**Failure signatures**:
1. **Poor cross‑architecture generalisation**: Large MSE increase when evaluating a distilled S (from backbone A) on backbone B suggests insufficient harmonic regularisation (λ too low) or mis‑aligned harmonic selection.
2. **Scalability plateau**: If MSE does not improve as M grows, the global update mechanism is not leveraging the extra capacity—possibly because top‑k selection favours low frequencies and ignores longer periods that require larger M, or because the harmonic matching loss is not properly scaled with M.
3. **Synthetic data distortion**: Visualisations of S show unrealistic, flat, or backbone‑specific patterns. This indicates instability in the inner‑loop gradient matching or an imbalance between L_harm and L_grad.
4. **Dominance of harmonic loss**: L_harm decreases but L_grad remains high, resulting in poor downstream performance. Tuning λ is needed to balance the two objectives.

**First three experiments** (to validate claims):
1. **Global vs local ablation**: Compare HDT against a variant that performs gradient matching on window‑based updates but still operates in the frequency domain (i.e., apply FFT/iFFT without harmonic selection). Isolates the benefit of global harmonic alignment.
2. **Harmonic selection analysis**: Sweep *k* (e.g., M/4, M/8, M/2) across datasets and measure both MSE and cross‑architecture ΔMSE to determine sensitivity and optimal ranges.
3. **Cross‑architecture stress test**: Distill S using one backbone (e.g., DLinear), then evaluate on at least two other architectures (iTransformer, xPatch). Report ΔMSE; HDT should show a notably smaller increase than baselines, confirming reduced architectural overfitting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Periodicity‑agnostic performance** – Does HDT work on time series lacking strong periodic components? The method relies on extracting and aligning dominant harmonics. Evaluation on synthetic aperiodic or low‑periodicity series is needed to assess generalisability.
2. **Theoretical assumptions and real‑world validity** – Theorem 1 assumes autoregressive processes and smooth power spectral densities. Many real series exhibit non‑stationarity, trends, or abrupt changes that may violate these assumptions. Testing the bound tightness and correlating assumption violations with performance gaps would clarify robustness.
3. **Extension beyond forecasting** – Can the HDT framework be adapted to other time series tasks (e.g., classification, anomaly detection) where harmonic structure might be less relevant? A task‑specific loss (e.g., feature matching) combined with harmonic matching would need empirical validation.
4. **Compression limits (M vs N)** – The relationship between synthetic length **M** and original length **N** is not fully characterised. How small can **M** be relative to **N** before performance degrades significantly? A systematic study varying both **M** and **N** would reveal practical compression ratios.
5. **Automatic harmonic selection** – The optimal number of harmonics *k* varies across datasets and is currently set via grid search. Developing an automatic *k* selection criterion (e.g., based on spectral entropy or cumulative amplitude threshold) would improve usability and robustness.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter ambiguity**: The paper does not fully specify key training details (inner‑loop steps *i,j*, exact per‑channel vs. global harmonic selection, bounds on initial *S*), hindering reproducibility.
- **Unverified break condition**: The proposed failure mode for non‑periodic data is plausible but lacks empirical testing on aperiodic benchmarks.
- **Scalability scope**: Evidence for scaling with *M* is limited to \(M \leq 768\); performance on much longer synthetic sequences or high‑frequency data remains unknown.

## Confidence
- **Harmonic Matching**: Medium – depends on top‑\(k\) capturing meaningful periodicities; sensitivity to \(k\) not fully explored.
- **Global frequency‑domain updates**: High – algorithmic clarity and strong empirical scalability results support this mechanism.
- **Model‑agnostic combination (L_harm + L_grad)**: Medium – cross‑architecture gains are demonstrated, but the interplay between \(\lambda\) and potential objective conflicts needs deeper analysis.

## Next Checks
1. **Global vs local ablation**: Implement a control that performs gradient matching after globally transforming the entire sequence with FFT/iFFT (no harmonic selection) to confirm that the harmonic alignment component, not merely frequency‑domain operation, drives the observed gains.
2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity sweep**: Perform a systematic search over \(k \in \{M/2, M/4, M/8\}\) and \(\lambda \in [10^{-1}, 10^{-5}]\) on multiple datasets, measuring both within‑architecture MSE and cross‑architecture \(\Delta\)MSE to identify robust settings and trade‑offs.
3. **Cross‑architecture stress test**: For a distilled dataset \(S\) produced with DLinear, evaluate on iTransformer, xPatch, and PatchTST; report the relative MSE increase.
