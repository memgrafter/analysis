# Discovering Decoupled Functional Modules in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.17823
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17823
- **Reference count**: 21
- **Primary result**: The ULCMOD framework with IterD algorithm discovers semantically coherent, hierarchically organized functional modules in LLMs, substantially outperforming clustering baselines and enabling more accurate prediction of ground-truth categories.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the problem of discovering decoupled functional modules—neuron sets that co-activate on specific, unknown topics—within Large Language Models without supervision. It proposes the Unsupervised LLM Cross-layer MOdule Discovery (ULCMOD) framework, which formalizes module discovery as a dual partitioning optimization problem. The objective function `L(F)` maximizes intra-module activation density and balance across modules, solved efficiently by the Iterative Decoupling (IterD) algorithm. Evaluations on Qwen2.5 models show IterD significantly outperforms five clustering baselines on `L(F)`. The discovered modules serve as more informative features for predicting sample categories and exhibit semantic coherence, hierarchical organization, and a spatially neighboring arrangement of related functions, offering a novel tool for LLM interpretability.

## Method Summary
ULCMOD frames module discovery as jointly partitioning neurons and samples into `K` modules to maximize an objective `L(F)`. `L(F)` combines **intra-module activation density** (neurons in a module should co-activate strongly on the module's samples) and **balance** (modules should have similar numbers of neurons and samples). The IterD algorithm optimizes `L(F)` via iterative greedy swaps: it alternates between assigning neurons to modules (given sample assignments) and assigning samples to modules (given neuron assignments), each step choosing swaps that improve `L(F)`. This avoids combinatorial search and scales to large models. The exclusivity constraint (each neuron and sample belongs to exactly one module) simplifies the dual problem but may not capture polysemanticity.

## Key Results
- IterD substantially outperforms five clustering baselines (K-means, spectral, etc.) on the optimization objective `L(F)` across three Qwen2.5 model sizes (e.g., for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with K=10, `L(F)=64.6` vs. 43.9 for the next best baseline).
- Discovered modules yield higher accuracy and macro-F1 scores in logistic regression probes predicting ground-truth sample categories (e.g., 0.7536/0.7527 for K=10), demonstrating their predictive utility.
- Qualitative analysis reveals modules exhibit semantic coherence (topics like "Programming", "Law"), hierarchical organization (e.g., "Programming" splits into finer sub-functions at higher K), and a spatial arrangement where functionally related modules are neighboring in the neuron index space.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The `L(F)` objective simultaneously encourages **coherence** (via density: neurons that fire together on the same samples are grouped) and **coverage** (via balance: modules are similarly sized, preventing degenerate solutions like one large module). Density captures functional specialization: neurons co-activating on a shared set of samples likely contribute to a common function. Balance ensures modules represent distinct, non-overlapping functional aspects, avoiding redundancy. IterD's greedy swaps efficiently explore the discrete partitioning space, converging to high-quality assignments that reflect the underlying functional organization without supervision. The exclusivity constraint initially establishes a clear semantic foundation per module, though it may oversimplify polysemantic neurons.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Functional Modularity**: LLMs might decompose capabilities into specialized neuron groups; needed to interpret model internals. Quick check: Do neurons consistently activate for specific functions across inputs?
2. **Activation-Based Analysis**: Using activation statistics to infer function; needed as a direct, scalable interpretability signal. Quick check: Can activation patterns reveal semantic topics without labeled data?
3. **Dual Partitioning**: Jointly assigning neurons and samples; needed because modules are defined by their sample coverage. Quick check: Does alternating optimization between neurons and samples improve module quality over one-sided clustering?
4. **Clustering with Balance**: Traditional clustering ignores module size uniformity; balance promotes diverse, non-trivial modules. Quick check: Does enforcing balance lead to more semantically distinct modules?
5. **Polysemanticity**: Single neurons often participate in multiple functions; exclusivity may force arbitrary splits. Quick check: What fraction of neurons activate across multiple semantic categories?
6. **Layer-wise Computation**: Functions may be distributed across layers; ULCMOD analyzes each layer independently. Quick check: Do module semantics differ between early (feature extraction) and late (task-specific) layers?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input Samples -> Compute Layer Activations -> IterD Partitioning (Neurons ↔ Samples) -> Output Modules  
Critical path: IterD loop—(1) initialize random neuron/sample assignments, (2) for each neuron, compute `L(F)` change if reassigned to each module; move if improvement, (3) repeat for samples, (4) iterate until convergence.  
Design tradeoffs: Exclusivity constraint simplifies optimization but may miss polysemantic neurons; balance term ensures diverse modules but could force artificial splits in imbalanced data.  
Failure signatures: Low `L(F)` score, modules with incoherent sample topics (e.g., mixed domains), highly unbalanced module sizes, poor predictive performance on probing tasks.  
First three experiments: (1) Vary `K` to test scalability and semantic meaningfulness of modules; (2) Apply ULCMOD to different layer ranges (early, middle, late) to map spatial functional organization; (3) Relax exclusivity via soft assignments (e.g., fuzzy c-means) and compare module coherence against exclusive IterD.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does relaxing the one-to-one neuron/sample assignment constraint to allow overlapping membership affect the quality and interpretability of discovered modules?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors explicitly restrict each neuron and sample to exactly one module in Sec 3.1 to simplify the dual partitioning problem, noting this helps establish a semantic foundation but may not capture polysemanticity.
- **Why unresolved**: Neurons are known to participate in multiple functions; forcing exclusivity might arbitrarily split multifunctional neurons or obscure overlapping functional roles, limiting the modules' fidelity to actual LLM organization.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop a variant of IterD with soft or multi-membership assignments (e.g., fuzzy clustering), then compare module coherence (via I(F)), semantic meaningfulness, and spatial arrangement against the exclusive version.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: To what extent do the discovered decoupled modules generalize across different LLM architectures (e.g., decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder) and training regimes (e.g., base vs. instruction-tuned)?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Experiments are conducted solely on three sizes of the Qwen2.5-Instruct model family; no evaluation on other architectures (e.g., Llama, GPT, T5) or training paradigms.
- **Why unresolved**: Modular organization might be architecture-specific or influenced by tuning objectives; without cross-architectural validation, the findings may not reflect universal properties of LLMs.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Apply ULCMOD to a diverse set of models (e.g., Llama 3, GPT-2, T5) with comparable data; analyze consistency in module semantics, hierarchical patterns, and spatial locality to determine universal versus model-specific modularity.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Do the identified modules have a causal role in generating their associated functions, such that targeted interventions on module activations can selectively enhance or impair specific capabilities?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The framework relies on correlation (activation density and linear classifier performance) without any causal intervention studies (e.g., ablation, steering).
- **Why unresolved**: Correlational evidence does not establish whether modules are necessary or sufficient for functions; causal validation is critical for interpretability, model editing, and safety applications.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct intervention experiments: (1) ablate (zero out) activations of a module and measure performance drop on related tasks; (2) steer (amplify) module activations and observe improved or degraded outputs; compare effects across modules.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What underlying principles (e.g., semantic granularity, training data distribution, layer-wise computation) govern the observed hierarchical organization where modules split into finer sub-functions at higher K?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Sec 4.2 describes hierarchical splits (e.g., Programming → Code Analysis & Debugging, Software & System Programming) but offers no explanation for why or how this hierarchy emerges.
- **Why unresolved**: Understanding the drivers of hierarchy could reveal how LLMs compose complex functions from simpler primitives and whether this reflects human-defined taxonomies or emergent learning dynamics.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Track module evolution across training checkpoints to see when and how splits occur; correlate split patterns with dataset task frequency or human-curated functional taxonomies; analyze if splits align with specific layer ranges or computational motifs.

## Limitations
- **Generalization scope**: Results are shown only on Qwen2.5 variants; unknown if findings hold for non-Qwen architectures (e.g., GPT, LLaMA) or non-Instruct-tuned models.
- **Evaluation robustness**: Predictive accuracy relies on logistic regression; alternative probing classifiers (e.g., neural networks, human evaluation) may yield different insights into module utility.
- **Qualitative interpretation**: Semantic coherence and hierarchical claims are based on manual inspection; lacks statistical validation (e.g., inter-rater reliability, automated topic coherence metrics).
