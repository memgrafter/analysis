# Gradient-based Optimisation of Modulation Effects

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04867
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04867
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** Achieves error-to-signal ratios (ESR) as low as -12.39 dB for the Boss BF-2 flanger (no feedback) and -10.27 dB for the Marshall SV-1 chorus, with perceptual tests indicating outputs are indistinguishable from analog references; however, performance degrades for effects with feedback (e.g., BF-2 with feedback, ESR -4.53 dB) and long delay times remain challenging.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a differentiable digital signal processing (DDSP) framework for emulating analog modulation effects (phasers, flangers, chorus) with low computational cost and zero latency at inference. The model is trained in the time-frequency domain using a loss function that emphasizes low frequencies via triangular kernel inputs or pre-emphasis filters. This weighting smoothes the loss landscape, enabling gradient-based optimization of delay times. At inference, the learned parameters are transferred to a purely time-domain implementation that processes audio sample-by-sample without look-ahead. Experiments on hardware pedals show high fidelity, with ESR below -10 dB for several effects, and perceptual tests confirm near-perfect mimicry. The method fails to fully capture feedback effects and struggles with long delay times, highlighting future work directions.

## Method Summary
The approach uses a frame-based training regime where audio is split into non-overlapping frames of length N (e.g., 1024–4096 samples). For each frame, a learnable low-frequency oscillator (LFO) is generated from a look-up table and MLP, producing a control value c_m. This controls a frequency response h_m that combines a linear-phase delay (for flanger/chorus) or a cascade of all-pass filters (for phaser) with two state-variable biquad (SVF) filters. The per-frame response h_m is applied to the frame’s STFT coefficients, and the output is inverted back to time domain. The training loss is a normalized frequency-domain L2 distance, optionally weighted by a triangular low-frequency kernel to avoid local minima. After training, the LFO and filter parameters are extracted; the LFO is resampled to the original sample rate, and the system is reimplemented as a causal time-domain filter graph (linear delay line, all-pass network, or feedback comb with SVFs) that processes audio sequentially with zero look-ahead.

## Key Results
- The model achieves ESR of -12.39 dB for the Boss BF-2 flanger (no feedback) and -10.27 dB for the Marshall SV-1 chorus, with MUSHRA tests indicating outputs are perceptually indistinguishable from the analog pedals.
- For the Boss BF-2 flanger with feedback (BF-2-B), performance drops significantly to ESR -4.53 dB, and spectrograms reveal the model underestimates feedback, producing broader spectral peaks.
- The method faces challenges for effects with very long delay times, and the optimal frame size and input weighting depend on the effect type (e.g., low-frequency weighting helps flangers but can harm phasers).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Low-Frequency Loss Weighting for Stable Delay Optimization
- **Claim:** Weighting the loss at low frequencies smooths the loss landscape for delay estimation, allowing gradient descent to find the global minimum even with spectrally flat inputs.
- **Mechanism:** The gradient of the frequency-domain loss with respect to delay depends on the input power spectrum |X(k)|². For flat inputs, many sharp local minima appear. By using a triangular kernel (or pre-emphasis filter) that downweights high frequencies, the convex region around the true delay widens, facilitating convergence.
- **Core assumption:** The target analog effect can be approximated by a time-varying linear delay, and optimizing a frequency-domain loss yields accurate parameters.
- **Evidence:** The paper provides analytical plots (Figs. 1–2) showing the loss surface with and without triangular weighting, and toy experiments confirm reliable recovery of delay only with low-frequency emphasis.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails for effects with feedback, where the system becomes highly nonlinear, or for very long delays where frame boundary issues dominate.

### Mechanism 2: Training in Time-Frequency Domain, Inference in Time Domain
- **Claim:** Splitting the training into frame-based frequency-domain processing and then transferring to a causal time-domain implementation at inference yields zero-latency operation.
- **Mechanism:** During training, audio is divided into non-overlapping frames. A per-frame frequency response h_m is predicted and applied in the STFT domain, assuming stationarity within the frame. After training, the learned parameters (LFO waveform, filter coefficients) are used to construct a time-domain filter graph (e.g., delay line, all-pass network) that processes samples sequentially with no look-ahead.
- **Core assumption:** The frame-wise stationary approximation is sufficient to learn the overall time-varying behavior, and the time-domain implementation can faithfully reproduce the frequency-domain prediction.
- **Evidence:** The abstract and Section III-A explicitly describe this train/infer split, and experiments show the time-domain model matches the frequency-domain training performance while achieving zero latency.
- **Break condition:** If the learned parameters lead to unstable time-domain filters (e.g., from poor SVF initialization) or if LFO resampling introduces noticeable discontinuities, the approach may degrade.

### Mechanism 3: Multi-Channel Model for Complex Effects
- **Claim:** Using a multi-channel architecture (C > 1) with shared parameters but independent signal paths enables emulation of effects with both dry and wet signals (e.g., chorus) and internal feedback loops.
- **Mechanism:** The model output is a sum over C parallel channels. Each channel can represent a distinct component: an unprocessed path, a modulated wet path, or a filter in a feedback configuration. The feedback placement (inside or outside an SVF filter) is controlled by a switch, offering two topologies (config i/ii).
- **Core assumption:** The analog effect’s output can be expressed as a linear combination of a small number of parameterized time-varying filter paths.
- **Evidence:** For the Marshall SV-1 chorus, increasing channels from C=1 to C=2 improved ESR, especially at moderate depth settings. For the Small Stone phaser, different feedback configurations yielded perceptual differences, supporting the need for flexible topology.
- **Break condition:** For highly nonlinear feedback (e.g., BF-2-B with pedal distortion), the linear multi-channel model is insufficient, leading to poor fidelity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Frequency-domain gradient descent for time-varying systems**  
  *Why needed:* Optimizing delay or all-pass parameters requires comparing predicted and target signals. Using an STFT-based loss provides a differentiable, sparse gradient and avoids time-domain aliasing for non-integer delays.  
  *Quick check:* Why does a spectrally flat input cause many local minima in delay optimization, and how does a triangular kernel input reshape the loss surface?

- **Differentiable parametrization of stable IIR filters (SVF/FLAMO)**  
  *Why needed:* Gradient-based training of IIR filters can easily produce unstable coefficients. The paper uses state-variable filters with constrained parameters (via tanh and log(1+exp) activations) to guarantee stability throughout training.  
  *Quick check:* What is the purpose of the tanh and log(1+exp) activations in Eq. 17, and how do they enforce stability?

- **Trade-off between time and frequency resolution in frame-based processing**  
  *Why needed:* Frame size N determines the stationarity assumption. A short N gives better time resolution for fast LFOs but poorer frequency resolution for accurate filter shaping; a long N improves frequency accuracy but may smear fast modulations.  
  *Quick check:* For a flanger with LFO period 50 ms and delay modulation 5 ms at Fs=44.1 kHz, what risks arise if N=1024?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input audio (length L) -> Split into M non-overlapping frames of length N -> Compute STFT (X_m) -> LFO generator (LUT + MLP) produces c_m per frame -> Compute per-frame frequency response h_m (comb delay or APF cascade plus two SVF biquads) -> Multiply h_m with X_m -> Inverse STFT to get time-domain output Ŷ -> (At inference) Convert learned LFO and filter params to time-domain filter graph (delay line, APF network, or comb with optional feedback) -> Process audio sample-by-sample with zero latency.

**Critical path:** The LFO generation is pivotal; its smoothness and accuracy directly affect modulation quality. After training, the mapping from discrete LFO values c_m to a continuous waveform (via frequency estimation and cubic resampling) must preserve the learned modulation depth and rate.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Frame size N: Larger N improves frequency resolution but reduces time resolution; may cause boundary aliasing if N' ≥ N−D. Paper tests N∈{1024,2048,4096}.  
- Input signal: Triangular low-pass kernel (with N'=N/2) aids delay learning for flangers/chorus, but full-band chirp works better for phasers, especially with feedback.  
- Feedback placement: Config i (SVF outside comb) is simpler; config ii (SVF inside feedback loop) improves low-frequency accuracy for phasers but shows higher training variance.  
- Number of channels C: More channels help capture complex wet/dry mixes (e.g., SV-1 at Depth=0.5) but yield diminishing returns; overparameterization may lead to non-identifiability.

**Failure signatures:**  
- ESR near 0 dB with full-band input on flanger indicates delay optimization failed; learned LFO likely near-zero. Remedy: use triangular kernel input or pre-emphasis.  
- Poor performance on feedback effects (e.g., BF-2-B) shows as overly broad spectral combs; the linear feedback model underestimates resonance.  
- Training instability (NaNs) often stems from poor SVF initialization; the prescribed initialization (f' ~ N(-π,1), R' ~ N(0,1), mixing coefficients uniform) is crucial.  
- LFO jitter or discontinuities in phaser training suggest inadequate loss smoothing; pre-emphasis can help.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Toy Problem (Sec. IV): Train the model against a known target (ground truth parameters) with varying N and input signals (triangular, chirp ± pre-emphasis). Confirms that triangular input or pre-emphasis is necessary for reliable delay recovery; full-band chirp fails.  
2. BF-2 Flanger (Sec. V): Apply to real Boss BF-2 pedal recordings (without and with feedback). Compare triangle vs. chirp inputs. Finds triangle yields ESR -12.39 dB for no-feedback case but fails for feedback (ESR -4.53 dB), highlighting linear model limitation.  
3. SV-1 Chorus (Sec. VI): Test multi-channel (C=1,2,3,4) with triangle input on Marshall SV-1 chorus. Determines C≥2 required for Depth=0.5, and that the "Wave" control affects required capacity. Demonstrates model scaling with control complexity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**How can the model's feedback mechanism be improved to accurately emulate analog modulation effects with strong feedback (e.g., Boss BF-2 flanger at maximum resonance)?**  
The current approach uses a simplified linear comb inside the feedback loop, which underestimates feedback, yielding poor ESR (-4.53 dB). More physically accurate feedback (including nonlinearities or additional filtering) might help, but initial experiments were unsuccessful. Resolving this requires designing and testing alternative feedback topologies.

### Open Question 2
**Why does the multi-channel model sometimes significantly improve performance (e.g., for Marshall SV-1 chorus at Depth=0.5) while for other settings (Depth=1) a single channel suffices?**  
The role of additional channels is unclear: they may model distinct signal paths (dry/wet) or provide redundancy. The paper notes non-identifiability of parameters, suggesting the loss does not uniquely constrain solutions. A systematic ablation and regularization study is needed to understand channel contributions.

### Open Question 3
**What is the relationship between training frame size N and the maximum reliably learnable delay time D, and does N'=N/2 optimally balance time-aliasing mitigation and loss surface smoothness for long delays?**  
The paper identifies long delays as a challenge and uses N'=N/2 as a trade-off without quantitative exploration beyond ~13 ms. For longer delays, the optimal choice of N and N' may differ, potentially affecting zero-latency inference design.

### Open Question 4
**Why does low-frequency weighting dramatically improve convergence for linear delay (flanger/chorus) but not consistently for nonlinear phase shifts (phaser), and can this be formalized into a general principle for choosing loss weighting based on effect type?**  
The paper shows that for the phaser, low-frequency weighting can introduce new local minima, contrary to the flanger case. A theoretical analysis linking phase nonlinearity to loss curvature is missing, as are systematic experiments across varying all-pass pole locations.

## Limitations
- The model struggles with effects that have strong feedback (e.g., Boss BF-2 with resonance), where the linear multi-channel architecture underestimates feedback, leading to ESR as high as -4.53 dB.
- The multi-channel architecture (C>1) is not well understood; its integration, scaling, and potential overparameterization remain unclear.
- Details of LFO estimation (peak picking, resampling) are underspecified, which may cause reproduction inconsistencies.

## Confidence
- **Low-frequency loss weighting enabling stable delay optimization:** High confidence (toy experiments and loss landscape analysis strongly support).
- **Zero-latency inference via train/infer split:** Medium confidence (concept is clear, but potential instability or LFO resampling artifacts are not fully validated).
- **Multi-channel modeling for complex effects:** Low confidence (empirical improvements observed, but architectural rationale and scaling limits are not justified).

## Next Checks
1. **Feedback modeling:** Replicate BF-2-B results; systematically vary feedback placement (config i vs. ii) and measure ESR and spectral peak widths to confirm the linear feedback limitation.
2. **Multi-channel ablation:** Train SV-1 with C=1,2,3,4 while controlling for wet/dry mix; inspect individual channel outputs to determine whether channels model distinct paths or redundancy.
3. **LFO estimation robustness:** Implement the described peak-picking and cubic resampling; on the toy problem, compare estimated LFO frequency/phase against ground truth to quantify jitter and harmonic leakage.
