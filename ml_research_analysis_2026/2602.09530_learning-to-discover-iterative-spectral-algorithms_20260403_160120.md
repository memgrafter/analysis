# Learning to Discover Iterative Spectral Algorithms
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09530
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09530
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** AutoSpec automates discovery of iterative spectral algorithms, delivering up to ~10× fewer iterations on real-world linear systems and eigenproblems versus classical methods.

## Executive Summary
AutoSpec is a framework that automatically discovers iterative spectral algorithms for large-scale numerical linear algebra tasks. It uses a neural network to map coarse spectral probes—a few approximate eigenvalues and residual norms from a cheap warm-start eigensolver—to coefficients of an executable polynomial recurrence. The recurrence is applied matrix-free via short matvecs. The system is trained offline on small synthetic diagonal matrices with task-specific losses (e.g., minimizing residual reduction for linear systems, maximizing eigenvalue gap for eigenproblems). Despite training on small, synthetic data, the learned algorithms generalize to large real-world sparse matrices (SuiteSparse), achieving orders-of-magnitude improvements in iteration counts for solving linear systems and computing eigenvalues, and outperforming classical polynomial methods like Chebyshev and Neumann series.

## Method Summary
AutoSpec formulates iterative spectral algorithms as a unified state-transition recurrence parameterized by scalar coefficients. This recurrence subsumes classical methods (e.g., Chebyshev, damped Richardson) and is fully differentiable, enabling end-to-end training. Given a new operator, a cheap warm-start eigensolver extracts a spectral probe. A neural engine processes this probe and outputs recurrence coefficients for a fixed degree. The recurrence is then executed matrix-free. Training uses self-supervision on synthetic diagonal matrices: the neural engine is optimized to minimize a task-specific loss (e.g., log-ratio of iteration counts relative to a baseline) across a diverse spectrum of synthetic matrices. The dimension-agnostic nature of the spectral probe input allows the model to transfer from small synthetic problems (`n ≤ 10^3`) to much larger real matrices (`n ~ 10^6`).

## Key Results
- Eigenproblem iterations reduced by up to ~5× (e.g., `thermal2`: 11 vs 45 for power method).
- Linear system CG iterations reduced by up to ~10× (e.g., `thermal1`: 173 vs 1851 for unpreconditioned CG).
- Preconditioner condition number dropped from 3.2e5 to 5.0e2; matrix inverse square root approximation error improved by ~10× vs Neumann series.
- Learned polynomials often exhibit near-minimax, Chebyshev-like behavior; ablation confirms importance of residual features in probes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** AutoSpec adapts to operator-specific spectral structure using coarse spectral probes.
- **Mechanism:** A neural engine maps a spectral probe (a few coarse eigenvalue estimates with residual norms) to coefficients of an executable polynomial recurrence. The recurrence is applied matrix-free via short matvecs, and the polynomial shape is task-adapted via training.
- **Core assumption:** Coarse spectral statistics contain sufficient information to guide construction of an effective spectral transform for the target task.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] models learn under low computational budgets and adapt using coarse spectral information; [section 3.1] describes the three-step procedure (probe extraction, coefficient prediction, execution).
- **Break condition:** If the spectral probe misses critical spectral features (e.g., due to slow eigenvector convergence), the learned recurrence may produce a poorly conditioned transform or fail to target the relevant region.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The unified state-transition parameterization subsumes classical iterative spectral algorithms and enables differentiable end-to-end training.
- **Mechanism:** The recurrence structure (Eq. 4) modularly combines basis generation and linear combination via scalar coefficients. This covers affine three-term recurrences (Chebyshev, damped Richardson) and basis-plus-expansion schemes. Differentiability allows gradients of a task-defined loss to flow through the recurrence and update the neural engine.
- **Core assumption:** The chosen linear block structure is expressive enough for near-optimal polynomials for the tasks, and the degree is sufficient.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] unified parameterization spanning broad class; induced polynomials exhibit near-minimax behavior; [section 3.2] Propositions 3.1 and 3.2 recover classical templates.
- **Break condition:** For operators with highly non-polynomial amenable spectra (e.g., sharp interior transitions), polynomial recurrences may lack expressivity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Self-supervised training on synthetic spectra with task-specific objectives yields algorithms that generalize to large real-world operators.
- **Mechanism:** The neural engine is trained offline on small synthetic diagonal matrices, minimizing a loss derived from the log-ratio of a baseline metric versus the learned method’s metric. Transfer occurs because the input is dimension-agnostic spectral statistics.
- **Core assumption:** The synthetic training distribution captures relevant spectral heterogeneity of real-world operators (e.g., condition number ranges, decay profiles).
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] efficient training on small synthetic problems with transfer; [section 4.3] model learns size-independent mapping enabling extrapolation.
- **Break condition:** If real spectra lie outside the training manifold (e.g., novel decay patterns or extreme condition numbers), generalization may fail.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Spectral probing and coarse eigenvalue estimation**
  - *Why needed:* AutoSpec’s premise is that a cheap, approximate spectrum can inform construction of a high-quality polynomial transform.
  - *Quick check:* Given only top 10 Ritz values and residuals from 20 Lanczos steps, could you design a degree-20 polynomial that significantly accelerates CG for an SPD matrix with condition number 10^5? Why or why not?
- **Concept: Polynomial preconditioning and three-term recurrences**
  - *Why needed:* The executable recurrence must be matrix-free and low-communication; polynomial filters via short recurrences are ideal.
  - *Quick check:* How does the affine three-term recurrence (C_{k+1} = γ_k I + η_k C_{k-1} + α_k C_k + β_k X C_k) relate to the standard Chebyshev recurrence? Which coefficients recover Chebyshev polynomials on [a,b]?
- **Concept: Self-supervised task loss for algorithm discovery**
  - *Why needed:* AutoSpec optimizes directly for the downstream metric (e.g., eigenvalue gap improvement) rather than imitating existing algorithms.
  - *Quick check:* Why is using `log(iterations_baseline) / log(iterations_model)` as a loss component preferable to simply minimizing `iterations_model`? What does the logarithmic scaling achieve?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Spectral Probe Extractor -> Neural Network Engine -> Recurrence Executor
- **Critical path:** Probe extraction (cheap eigensolver warm-start) → embedding → coefficient generation (per recurrence step) → recurrence execution (matrix-free) → task metric evaluation (during training) or downstream use.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Recurrence expressivity vs. matrix-free efficiency: linear block structure ensures at most one matvec per step but limits to polynomial transforms.
  - Probe quality vs. extraction cost: more subspace iterations yield better probes but increase upfront cost; embedding layer trained for robustness but performance degrades below a probe quality threshold.
  - Training data synthesis vs. generalization: synthetic spectra must cover relevant spectral regimes; overly narrow synthesis risks poor transfer.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Degenerate coefficients leading to growth instead of damping on unwanted spectrum.
  - Numerical instability at high polynomial degrees without state normalization.
  - Poor probe alignment: probe’s spectral window misses target region (e.g., gap boundary for eigenvalues).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Backbone pre-training:** Train neural engine on synthetic diagonal matrices with accurate spectra; verify first k layers produce polynomial improving task metric over baseline.
  2. **Embedding fine-tuning:** Freeze backbone, train embedding on coarse spectral probes (varying Lanczos iterations); test robustness to probe quality.
  3. **Real-world transfer:** Deploy trained engine on SuiteSparse matrices; extract probes with fixed budget, run recurrence, compare convergence vs. baselines (power/Richardson).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can AutoSpec effectively utilize higher-order polynomial recurrences and nonlinear transition maps to enhance its algorithmic expressivity and performance for NLA tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** Conclusion notes AutoSpec can support richer update classes beyond linear recurrences, including higher-order polynomial recurrences and nonlinear transition maps (Appendix A.1).
- **Why unresolved:** Experiments exclusively use linear recurrences; higher-order/nonlinear structures not implemented or evaluated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop neural engines with higher-order/nonlinear state transitions, train on synthetic data, benchmark on real-world matrices, compare against linear-recurrence AutoSpec and classical methods.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can AutoSpec be extended to learn rational spectral transforms, and would these outperform polynomial methods for tasks with sharp spectral variations?
- **Basis in paper:** Appendix A.1 proposes rational models by replacing linear shifts with resolvents, noting advantages for functions with sharp spectral behavior.
- **Why unresolved:** Only polynomial recurrences implemented and tested; rational extensions remain theoretical.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement differentiable rational recurrence modules, train for matrix-function tasks, demonstrate improved accuracy/iteration counts vs. polynomial baselines and rational approximation methods (e.g., AAA).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does AutoSpec remain effective for non-diagonalizable operators (with Jordan blocks), or does its reliance on eigenvalue-based probes and polynomial recurrences break down?
- **Basis in paper:** Framework assumes diagonalizable matrices; all experiments use symmetric positive definite (or squared indefinite) operators. Non-diagonalizable cases not considered.
- **Why unresolved:** Many engineering operators are non-diagonalizable; general applicability unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Generate synthetic matrices with controllable Jordan structure, apply trained AutoSpec engines, measure convergence/stability, compare with algorithms for non-normal operators (e.g., Schur-based methods).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What precise spectral properties ensure successful transfer from small synthetic training operators to large real-world matrices, and what spectral mismatches cause failure?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.3 claims size-independent generalization via dimension-agnostic spectral summaries but gives no formal criteria. Test matrices limited and may share similar spectra with training data.
- **Why unresolved:** Without characterization, deployment risks failure on operators with spectra far from synthetic training distribution.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically vary spectral features (condition number, decay rate, clustering) in synthetic training sets, test on diverse real matrices, correlate performance with spectral distance metrics; perhaps derive generalization bounds.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can AutoSpec-generated preconditioners compete with state-of-the-art iterative solvers (e.g., GMRES with ILU, MINRES, algebraic multigrid) across a broad benchmark?
- **Basis in paper:** Comparisons limited to power, Richardson, Neumann, and occasionally Chebyshev polynomials. Modern solvers not evaluated; focus on polynomial recurrences may exclude more effective preconditioning strategies.
- **Why unresolved:** Paper reports “orders-of-magnitude” gains over basic baselines, but relevance to cutting-edge practice unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Integrate AutoSpec as plug-in preconditioner in solver frameworks (e.g., PETSc, Trilinos), run head-to-head trials on SuiteSparse/Matrix Market problems, report iteration counts, time-to-solution, robustness against advanced methods.

## Limitations
- Embedding architecture dimensions (e.g., `l0`, `k0`) not fully specified; must be inferred, complicating reproduction.
- Synthetic training distribution may not cover extreme real spectra (κ > 1e5, very slow decay), risking generalization failures.
- Polynomial-only recurrences lack expressivity for non-polynomial optimal filters (e.g., rational functions), potentially limiting performance on some tasks.
- Probe quality threshold for reliable adaptation unknown; performance degrades below unspecified warm-start iteration count.
- State normalization details (e.g., clipping thresholds) not fully disclosed; could affect stability at high degrees.

## Confidence
- **Adaptive probing:** Medium-High. Ablation validates residual features and robustness, but warm-start budget not precisely quantified.
- **Unified recurrence:** High. Mathematical recovery of classical methods; differentiable training proven.
- **Synthetic-to-real transfer:** Medium. Strong empirical results, but synthetic distribution might be insufficiently broad for all real-world spectra.
