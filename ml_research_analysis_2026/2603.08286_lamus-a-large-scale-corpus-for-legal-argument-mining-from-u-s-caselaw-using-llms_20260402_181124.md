# LAMUS: A Large-Scale Corpus for Legal Argument Mining from U.S. Caselaw using LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08286
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08286
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Introduced LAMUS, a 2.9M-sentence legal argument corpus built via LLM-human pipeline, enabling 85.32% classification accuracy with a fine-tuned LLaMA-3-8B model.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality annotated datasets for U.S. legal argument mining by introducing LAMUS. The corpus comprises 2.9 million sentence-level annotations from U.S. Supreme Court and Texas criminal cases, constructed through a pipeline that first uses an LLM for automatic labeling, followed by targeted human verification. Key results show that human verification corrected 19.4% of initial LLM annotations, achieving a high inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ=0.85). For the argument classification task, a fine-tuned LLaMA-3-8B model reached 85.32% accuracy, while the best prompted result was 75.89%. The work provides a substantial resource and demonstrates a scalable methodology for legal NLP dataset creation.

## Method Summary
The core methodology is a two-stage annotation pipeline. First, an unspecified LLM is used to automatically label sentences in legal opinions with argument-related tags (e.g., Premise, Conclusion). Second, human experts verify a subset of these annotations, correcting errors and providing a high-confidence gold standard. This verified subset is used to assess the LLM's initial error rate and to train and evaluate supervised models. The final LAMUS corpus combines the LLM's large-scale output with human corrections, balancing scale and quality. Models are then evaluated both via fine-tuning (supervised learning) and prompting (in-context learning) on the corpus.

## Key Results
- LAMUS corpus contains 2.9 million sentence-level annotations from U.S. Supreme Court and Texas criminal cases.
- Human verification corrected 19.4% of the initial LLM-generated annotations.
- Verification subset achieved high inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.85).
- Fine-tuned LLaMA-3-8B model achieved 85.32% accuracy on the classification task.
- Best chain-of-thought prompting result was 75.89%; few-shot prompting consistently degraded performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach works by strategically combining the scale of LLM automation with the accuracy of human expertise. LLMs provide a cost-effective way to generate initial annotations across millions of sentences, capturing broad patterns in legal text. Human verification then acts as a quality control filter, correcting systematic LLM errors (19.4% correction rate) and establishing a reliable gold standard. This hybrid pipeline mitigates the prohibitive expense of full human annotation while producing a corpus large enough for effective model training. The high verification agreement (κ=0.85) indicates the task is well-defined and the human-in-the-loop process successfully standardized labels.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Raw U.S. Caselaw -> LLM Annotation (unspecified model) -> Human Verification (legal experts) -> LAMUS Corpus (2.9M sentences) -> Model Development (Fine-tuning LLaMA-3-8B / Prompting experiments) -> Evaluation Metrics (Accuracy, Cohen's κ)

**Critical Path:** The irreversible sequence runs from raw case text through LLM labeling to human correction. The quality of the final LAMUS corpus is bounded by the initial LLM's capabilities and the thoroughness of the human verification sample. Model performance is then contingent on the curated corpus.

**Design Tradeoffs:** The primary tradeoff is between annotation scale and precision. Relying solely on LLMs risks systemic bias and errors; full human annotation is prohibitively expensive for 2.9M sentences. The chosen middle ground accepts a 19.4% initial error rate, correcting it selectively. Another tradeoff is between fine-tuning (higher performance, 85.32%) and prompting (more flexible, 75.89% max), with few-shot learning underperforming, suggesting the task distribution is not trivially captured by in-context learning.

**Failure Signatures:** 1) A high and unexplained error correction rate (19.4%) indicates the LLM's base knowledge or prompts are misaligned with the legal argument mining task. 2) The significant performance gap between fine-tuned and prompted models suggests the task requires learning nuanced patterns not easily invoked via prompts. 3) Degradation with few-shot examples signals potential prompt misinterpretation or that the provided examples conflict with the model's pre-trained legal knowledge.

**First Experiments:** 1) **Annotation Pipeline Replication:** Run the described LLM annotation + human verification protocol on a small, held-out set of Texas civil cases (not in original corpus) to measure out-of-domain consistency and correction rates. 2) **Baseline Model Sanity Check:** Train a simple logistic regression model on TF-IDF features of a 1k-sentence subset of LAMUS to establish a non-LLM performance baseline and confirm label separability. 3) **Error Mode Analysis:** Manually inspect 100 sentences from the verification set where corrections were made, categorizing errors (e.g., misclassifying rhetorical questions as arguments, missing implicit conclusions) to identify systematic LLM failure patterns.

## Limitations
*   **LLM Annotation Bias:** The nature and systemic bias of the initial LLM's errors are not analyzed; its pre-training likely shaped the final annotation distribution in unexamined ways.
*   **Dataset Scope and Generalizability:** The corpus is limited to U.S. Supreme Court and Texas criminal opinions; its applicability to other jurisdictions or document types (e.g., contracts, statutes) is unknown.
*   **Performance Discrepancy:** The large gap between fine-tuned (85.32%) and prompted (75.89%) performance, along with few-shot degradation, is noted but not definitively explained—root causes could include task complexity, prompt engineering limits, or residual dataset noise.

## Confidence
*   **High:** The existence and basic quality of the 2.9M-sentence LAMUS corpus, evidenced by the high inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ=0.85) on the human verification sample.
*   **Medium:** The overall efficacy of the proposed LLM-assisted annotation *pipeline* for this specific domain and scale, given the reported error correction rate but incomplete error analysis and an unspecified base LLM.
*   **Low:** The broader claim that the resource and findings provide a "scalable foundation" for *general* legal NLP, due to the narrow domain focus (Supreme Court/Texas criminal) and unverified performance on other models or legal tasks.

## Next Checks
1.  **Pipeline Replication on Out-of-Domain Text:** Execute the exact annotation pipeline (same LLM, prompts, verification protocol) on a held-out set from a different jurisdiction (e.g., federal district court opinions) to measure drop in annotation consistency and error correction rate.
2.  **Architecture Generalization Test:** Evaluate the fine-tuned LLaMA-3-8B model's performance on a newer, competing LLM architecture (e.g., a Mistral or Gemma-based model) using the same train/test split to determine if the 85.32% accuracy is model-specific or intrinsic to the LAMUS dataset.
3.  **Error Mode Analysis:** Perform a detailed qualitative analysis of the remaining errors in the post-correction validation set to categorize failure modes (e.g., ambiguous legal phrasing, nuanced argument structures, LLM hallucination patterns) and correlate error types with specific case topics or opinion authors.
