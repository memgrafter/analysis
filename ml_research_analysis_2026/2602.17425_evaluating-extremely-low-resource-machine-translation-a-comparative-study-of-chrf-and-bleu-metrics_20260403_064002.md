# Evaluating Extremely Low-Resource Machine Translation: A Comparative Study of ChrF++ and BLEU Metrics

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17425

## Executive Summary
Evaluating machine translation for extremely low-resource languages (ELRLs) is fraught with challenges, as conventional single-metric assessments can be easily misled by specific failure modes such as hallucinations, source copying, and diacritic errors. This study investigates the complementary information provided by BLEU (token‑level n‑gram precision) and ChrF++ (character‑level n‑gram F‑score) on translations generated by large language models (Aya‑101, Airavata) and a neural machine translation model (mT5‑Large) across three Indic ELRLs: Magahi, Bhojpuri, and Chhattisgarhi.

The analysis reveals systematic and often large score disparities between BLEU and ChrF++. Distinct divergence patterns correlate with particular error types: a stable ChrF++ paired with a sharp BLEU drop signals hallucinations; an increase in ChrF++ accompanied by a BLEU decrease suggests source copying; and a substantial BLEU rise without a commensurate ChrF++ change indicates improved lexical accuracy and diacritic handling. The findings underscore that BLEU and ChrF++ capture complementary aspects of translation quality, and their combined use yields a more reliable and interpretable evaluation in ELRL settings.

## Method Summary
The study conducts a comparative evaluation using BLEU and ChrF++ on machine translation outputs. Models include the large language models Aya‑101 and Airavata, and the neural model mT5‑Large. Experiments cover three extremely low‑resource Indic languages: Magahi, Bhojpuri, and Chhattisgarhi. The core analysis focuses on patterns of agreement and divergence between the two metrics. No detailed reproduction instructions (e.g., data splits, tokenization settings, or code) are provided.

## Key Results
- BLEU and ChrF++ often exhibit large score disparities across ELRL translations.
- Stable ChrF++ with a significant BLEU drop indicates hallucinations.
- Increased ChrF++ with decreased BLEU suggests source copying; a significant BLEU rise with minor ChrF++ change reflects improved lexical accuracy and diacritic handling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
BLEU measures n‑gram precision at the token level, rewarding exact word matches and thus lexical accuracy. ChrF++ computes character n‑gram F‑score, capturing morphological similarities and being robust to word‑level variations. In ELRL scenarios, models often produce errors like hallucinations (irrelevant content) or source copying (verbatim reproduction). Such errors affect token‑level and character‑level overlap differently. For example, hallucinations may introduce many function words that maintain character n‑gram overlap, keeping ChrF++ stable while BLEU drops due to fewer matching content words. Source copying yields high character overlap with the source but low lexical precision against the reference, causing ChrF++ to increase while BLEU decreases. Diacritic inaccuracies can lower BLEU (if words differ) but have limited impact on character n‑grams. Therefore, the interplay between the two metrics acts as a diagnostic signal for these artifacts, making the combined evaluation more robust than either alone.

## Foundational Learning
- **BLEU**  
  *Why needed*: The de facto standard for MT evaluation, based on modified n‑gram precision; essential for benchmarking.  
  *Quick check*: BLEU increases when more exact word n‑grams from hypothesis match the reference.

- **ChrF++**  
  *Why needed*: Character‑level metric that accounts for morphological similarity and performs better on morphologically rich or low‑resource languages.  
  *Quick check*: ChrF++ increases when character n‑grams of hypothesis match those of the reference, even if words differ.

- **Extremely Low‑Resource Languages (ELRLs)**  
  *Why needed*: Languages with very limited parallel training data; MT systems struggle, and evaluation metrics must account for sparse vocabulary and morphological complexity.  
  *Quick check*: Verify resource size (e.g., <100K parallel sentences) to qualify as ELRL.

- **Hallucination**  
  *Why needed*: A critical error where the model generates content unrelated to the source, which can inflate surface‑based metrics.  
  *Quick check*: Compare hypothesis to source; presence of unrelated phrases indicates hallucination.

- **Source Copying**  
  *Why needed*: Model may simply reproduce source text instead of translating, inflating surface similarity but failing to convey meaning.  
  *Quick check*: High overlap between source sentence and hypothesis suggests copying.

- **Diacritic Variation**  
  *Why needed*: Diacritics affect meaning in many languages; metric sensitivity to them distinguishes nuanced correctness.  
  *Quick check*: Observe whether missing/incorrect diacritics affect BLEU more than ChrF++.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Source Text -> Translation Models (Aya‑101, Airavata, mT5‑Large) -> Generated Translations -> Metric Computation (BLEU, ChrF++) -> Score Comparison -> Divergence Analysis

**Critical Path**  
The essential workflow: (1) generate translations using each model; (2) compute BLEU and ChrF++ scores against references; (3) identify cases where scores diverge beyond a threshold; (4) classify divergence patterns to hypothesize error types; (5) validate patterns through qualitative inspection.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- BLEU is token‑based and requires proper tokenization; sensitive to vocabulary mismatch.  
- ChrF++ operates at character level, avoiding tokenization issues but can be inflated by surface overlap even when translation is poor.  
- Using multiple models (LLMs vs NMT) allows comparison of architecture tendencies but increases computational cost.

**Failure Signatures**  
- High ChrF++ + low BLEU → source copying or over‑generation.  
- Low ChrF++ + stable BLEU → hallucination (generated content with common function words).  
- BLEU increases significantly while ChrF++ remains stable → improvements in lexical accuracy and diacritic usage without overall character distribution change.

**First Experiments**  
1. Compute BLEU and ChrF++ for each model‑language pair to establish baseline scores.  
2. For each translation, compute absolute difference between normalized BLEU and ChrF++; filter for differences exceeding a threshold (e.g., 0.2) to flag potential issues.  
3. Manually review a sample of flagged translations to assign error categories (hallucination, copying, diacritic issues) and verify the hypothesized signatures.

## Limitations
- Correlation between divergence patterns and error artifacts is not causal; pattern mapping may not be exclusive.  
- The study covers only three Indic languages; results may not generalize to other language families or scripts.  
- Lack of human evaluation leaves untested whether metric discrepancies correspond to perceived translation quality differences.
