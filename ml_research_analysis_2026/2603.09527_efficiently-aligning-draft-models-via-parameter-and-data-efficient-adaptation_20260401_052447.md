# Efficiently Aligning Draft Models via Parameter- and Data-Efficient Adaptation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09527
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09527
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** EDA restores speculative decoding performance after target fine-tuning, achieving higher average acceptance length (τ) and speedup with only 60.8% of full fine-tuning cost on GSM8K.

## Executive Summary
Speculative decoding accelerates large language model inference by having a draft model propose tokens that are verified by a target model, preserving the output distribution while reducing latency. However, when the target model is fine-tuned for a specific domain, its next-token distribution diverges from that of the pre-trained draft, leading to lower acceptance rates and reduced speedup. This paper introduces Efficient Draft Adaptation (EDA), a framework that efficiently realigns the draft to the fine-tuned target. EDA comprises three key innovations: (1) a decoupled draft architecture with frozen shared and trainable private experts, (2) a data regeneration strategy that leverages the target model to generate domain-specific training data, and (3) a sample selection mechanism that prioritizes high-value samples based on representation shift. Across math, code, and medical domains, EDA significantly improves average acceptance length and decoding speedup compared to full fine-tuning or LoRA baselines. Notably, on GSM8K with Qwen2.5-Math-7B, EDA achieves τ=4.79 versus 4.37 for full fine-tuning, with only 60.8% of the training cost.

## Method Summary
EDA proceeds in three stages. First, the draft model's feed-forward network in each Transformer block is replaced by a two-expert gated module: a frozen shared expert initialized from a pre-trained draft, and a trainable private expert plus gating parameters. Only the private expert and gating are updated during adaptation. Second, the fine-tuned target model generates completions for a set of domain-specific prompts, producing a self-generated dataset D_self that better reflects the target's conditional token distribution. Third, a sample selection pipeline identifies a high-value subset D_compact from D_self by computing a Mahalanobis distance of answer-token hidden representations (PCA-reduced) from a general reference distribution, selecting samples with the largest tail quantile. The draft is then trained on D_compact to predict the target's next tokens. At inference, the gated draft combines the shared and private expert outputs to propose tokens for speculative decoding with the target.

## Key Results
- On GSM8K (Qwen2.5-Math-7B), EDA achieves an average acceptance length of 4.79, surpassing full fine-tuning's 4.37 while using only 60.8% of the training cost.
- Across math, code, and medical domains, EDA consistently outperforms full retraining and LoRA baselines under both greedy and stochastic decoding settings.
- The combination of self-generated data and representation-based selection yields significant gains in draft-target alignment, as evidenced by higher acceptance rates and decoding speedups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Decoupling the draft model into shared and private components allows parameter-efficient adaptation by reusing stable, transferable knowledge (the shared expert) while updating only a lightweight, target-specific module (the private expert).
- **Mechanism:** The FFN in each Transformer block is replaced with a two-expert gated module. The shared expert `E_s` is pre-initialized from a draft model trained on general data and frozen during adaptation. The private expert `E_p` and routing weights are trained on target-domain data. During inference, the gate dynamically combines outputs: `˜h_t = g_s * E_s(h_t) + g_p * E_p(h_t)`.
- **Core assumption:** The output distributions of pre-fine-tuned and fine-tuned target models share a substantial, reusable overlap (`shared output distribution`), while differences (`target-specific output distribution`) can be captured by a small, adaptable component.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “decoupled architecture that utilizes shared and private components to model the shared and target-specific output distributions separately, enabling parameter-efficient adaptation by updating only the lightweight private component.”
  - [section 4.1] “We define the overlapping portion... as the shared output distribution... Thus, we decouple the draft model into a shared expert and a private expert... the shared expert remains fixed, while only the lightweight private expert is updated.”
  - [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., PARD, TIDE) explore incremental or parallel draft adaptation, supporting the premise that full retraining is costly and reuse is beneficial, but do not directly evidence the shared-private split.
- **Break condition:** If the fine-tuning induces a distributional shift so severe that the pre-trained shared expert’s knowledge becomes detrimental or misaligned (e.g., negative transfer), freezing it could harm performance rather than help.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Regenerating training data using the fine-tuned target model (self-generation) aligns the draft model’s training objective with the actual tokens it must predict during speculative decoding, mitigating the train-test mismatch.
- **Mechanism:** For domain-specific prompts `x_i`, the target model generates completions `y_i ~ P_θt(·|x_i)`. The draft model is then trained on `D_self = {(x_i, y_i)}` to minimize cross-entropy against the target’s next-token predictions, rather than on static, possibly mismatched reference data.
- **Core assumption:** Standard datasets used for SFT (or public surrogates) do not reflect the conditional token distribution the draft will encounter during verification, and target self-generation provides a closer approximation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “data regeneration strategy that utilizes the fine-tuned target model to regenerate training data, thereby improving the alignment between training and speculative decoding, leading to higher average acceptance length.”
  - [section 4.2] “the draft model is optimized to predict the next token in the provided dataset... during speculative decoding, it needs to predict the target model’s next token, which may differ... To address this, we use a domain-specific self-generation strategy... produce the training set generated by the target model itself.”
  - [corpus] Weak. Neighbors discuss alignment (e.g., Direct Alignment, Reward-Shifted Speculative Sampling) but not specifically data regeneration via self-generation.
- **Break condition:** If the target model’s self-generated data contains systematic errors, hallucinations, or out-of-distribution artifacts not present in true test conditions, training on this synthetic data could degrade generalization.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Prioritizing training samples where the target model’s answer-token representations deviate most from a general reference distribution (via PCA-reduced Mahalanobis score) focuses the private expert’s limited capacity on high-value adaptation data, improving efficiency.
- **Mechanism:** For each self-generated sample, answer-token hidden states from the target model are PCA-reduced. Their Mahalanobis distance `d_t(x)` from a mean/covariance derived from a general dataset (`D_general`) is computed. The sample’s score `s(x)` is the high-quantile (ρ) of these token-level distances. Top-K samples by `s(x)` form the compact adaptation set.
- **Core assumption:** A sample’s value for adapting the private expert is proportional to the degree its target-model representation shifts away from general (pre-fine-tuned) patterns, and this shift concentrates in critical answer segments.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “sample selection mechanism that prioritizes high-value data for efficient adaptation.”
  - [section 4.3] “specialization often manifests as structured shifts in the representation space... we introduce a training-free sample value metric... based solely on the hidden states... Mahalanobis score... to quantify the deviation... aggregate with a tail-sensitive statistic.”
  - [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., ML-SpecQD, ParallelSpec) address draft efficiency via quantization or parallelism, not data selection; evidence for this specific metric is paper-internal.
- **Break condition:** If the target’s domain-specific behavior is not well-captured by linear shifts in the PCA-reduced space (e.g., involves complex, non-linear transformations), the Mahalanobis score may fail to identify truly valuable samples.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Speculative Decoding**
  - *Why needed here:* EDA’s entire premise relies on speculative decoding’s acceptance mechanism; understanding `τ` (average acceptance length) as the key performance metric is essential to grasp why draft-target alignment matters.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the relationship between the draft model’s token distribution and the final output distribution during speculative decoding? (Answer: The final distribution is exactly preserved via accept-reject sampling, regardless of draft errors, but speedup depends on consecutive acceptances.)
- **Concept: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)**
  - *Why needed here:* EDA adapts only a small subset of parameters (private expert + routing); understanding LoRA/adapter motivations (avoid catastrophic forgetting, reduce cost) explains why updating all parameters is unnecessary.
  - *Quick check question:* In LoRA, what is the mathematical formulation for the low-rank update, and why does it reduce trainable parameters? (Answer: `W = W_0 + ΔW`, where `ΔW = A * B`, `A∈ℝ^{d×r}`, `B∈ℝ^{r×k}`, `r << d,k`; only `A,B` are trained.)
- **Concept: Output Distribution Shift in Fine-Tuning**
  - *Why needed here:* The core problem is that SFT changes the target model’s next-token distribution, breaking alignment with a pre-trained draft. Recognizing that fine-tuning induces *distributional* (not just task) changes is key.
  - *Quick check question:* If a base model and its SFT variant are evaluated on the same test set, what changes in their output logits? (Answer: The conditional probability distribution `P(xt|x<t)` shifts, often sharpening or redirecting mass toward domain-specific tokens.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Domain prompts -> Data Regeneration Pipeline (Target Model) -> D_self -> Sample Selection Pipeline -> D_compact; Base Draft (with frozen E_s and trainable E_p+Gating) trained on D_compact -> Adapted Draft. At inference: hidden state h_t -> Gating Network -> g_s*E_s(h_t) + g_p*E_p(h_t) -> draft token.
- **Critical path:** Initialization (load pre-trained draft weights into E_s) → Self-generation (target produces D_self) → Selection (score D_self, get D_compact) → Adaptation (train E_p, w_s, w_p on D_compact with frozen E_s) → Inference (gated draft+target for speculative decoding).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Parameter Capacity vs. Efficiency:* Smaller `E_p` reduces cost but may underfit strong domain shifts. The gating mechanism offers dynamic capacity but adds routing parameters.
  - *Data Selection Bias:* Aggressive top-K selection risks discarding samples with subtle but important patterns; ρ and K are budget-dependent hyperparameters.
  - *Self-generation Cost:* Generating `D_self` requires full target model passes, but this is a one-time offline cost amortized over adaptation.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - *Low `τ` despite adaptation:* Indicates persistent misalignment—check if `E_s` is corrupted (wrong initialization) or if `D_compact` misses critical patterns.
  - *High variance across domains:* Suggests private expert capacity insufficient for certain domains, or PCA/Mahalanobis metrics failing for non-linear shifts.
  - *Degraded quality on general prompts:* If `E_s` was too large a shift from general data, freezing it may hurt base capabilities; monitor performance on mixed-domain tests.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ablation of Shared-Private Split:** Compare full fine-tuning (all params) vs. EDA (only private) vs. freezing all but LM head. Hypothesis: EDA should match full-FT `τ` with far fewer params, confirming shared knowledge reuse.
  2. **Data Selection Validation:** On a fixed small budget (e.g., 5k samples), compare (a) EDA’s PCA-Mahalanobis selection, (b) random, (c) reverse-PCA. Measure `τ` and convergence speed. Expect our selection to reach higher `τ` faster.
  3. **Self-generation vs. Original SFT Data:** Train two EDA variants on (a) original SFT dataset, (b) target self-generated dataset. Hypothesis: self-generation yields higher `τ` due to reduced train-decoding mismatch, especially under stochastic sampling (`T=1`).

## Limitations
- Shared expert freezing may cause negative transfer under severe distribution shifts.
- Self-generated data could propagate target model errors, harming draft generalization.
- PCA-based selection assumes linear representation shifts, missing non-linear specializations.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (shared-private decoupling): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (self-generation alignment): High
- Mechanism 3 (representation-shift selection): Medium

## Next Checks
1. **KL divergence analysis:** Measure token-level KL between target and draft distributions on a held-out set, with draft using (a) full fine-tuning, (b) EDA’s frozen shared expert. Expect the shared expert to maintain lower KL on overlapping tokens, validating the core premise.
2. **Gold-standard data control:** For one domain, train EDA using human-written completions instead of self-generated ones. If τ drops significantly, it confirms that self-generation introduces target-specific artifacts; else it suggests robustness.
3. **Selection metric ablation:** Compare EDA’s Mahalanobis selection against random and reverse-score baselines at equal sample budgets. Track τ and training loss curves. A consistent advantage for the proposed metric would strengthen Mechanism 3; comparable performance would indicate the selection heuristics are non-essential.
