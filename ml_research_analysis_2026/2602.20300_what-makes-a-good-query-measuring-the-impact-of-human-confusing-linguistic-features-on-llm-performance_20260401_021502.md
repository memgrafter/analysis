# What Makes a Good Query? Measuring the Impact of Human-Confusing Linguistic Features on LLM Performance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20300
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20300
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Linguistic features that challenge human comprehension (e.g., lack of specificity, clause complexity) increase LLM hallucination risk, while answerability and intention grounding reduce it.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) sometimes generate false or ungrounded information, known as hallucination. This paper explores whether linguistic properties of user queries can predict such behavior. Analyzing over 360,000 queries from 13 question-answering datasets, the authors define 17 binary features capturing ambiguity, complexity, and grounding. For each query, they produce up to six paraphrases, ask GPT-4o to answer, and evaluate each response with a hybrid metric (LLM judge, fuzzy match, BLEU) to label it as Safe, Borderline, or Risky. Using an ordinal logistic regression with dataset and scenario controls, they find that lack of specificity (odds ratio 2.38), clause complexity (1.76), negation, and anaphora raise the odds of a Risky label, while answerability (OR 0.33) and intention grounding lower it. The relative impact of features is stable across extractive, multiple-choice, and abstractive settings, though absolute risk differs. The results enable automated query risk triage and suggest targeted rewriting to mitigate hallucinations.

## Method Summary
The study builds a pipeline on 369,837 real-world queries from 13 QA benchmarks. First, 17 binary linguistic features are extracted via GPT-4o prompts (e.g., does the query contain anaphora?), and four numeric metrics (token count, clause count, dependency depth, parse height) are computed with spaCy and tiktoken. To estimate hallucination propensity, the system generates up to six semantic paraphrases per query (GPT-4o, temperature 1.0) and retains those with a similarity score ≥0.85 to the original. Each paraphrase is answered by GPT-4o using a uniform task prompt, then scored by a hybrid metric: 60% LLM judge binary decision, 30% fuzzy string match (rapidfuzz), and 10% BLEU-1. Paraphrases scoring >0.5 are marked as hallucinated. The number of hallucinated paraphrases per query determines a triage label: Safe (0), Borderline (1–3), Risky (4–6). Finally, an ordinal logistic regression (proportional odds) with dataset and scenario as fixed effects is fit to the labeled queries, yielding coefficients and odds ratios that quantify each feature’s effect on the odds of higher risk.

## Key Results
- **Ambiguity markers increase risk:** Lack of specificity (OR=2.38), clause complexity (OR=1.76), negation, and anaphora all significantly raise the odds of a Risky label.
- **Grounding decreases risk:** Answerability (OR=0.33) and intention grounding strongly reduce hallucination odds.
- **Cross‑scenario stability:** Relative feature effects are consistent across extractive, multiple‑choice, and abstractive QA, though baseline risk levels differ.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The observed correlations stem from how linguistic challenges affect an LLM’s generation process.

- **Ambiguity expands the hypothesis space.** When queries are underspecified, the model must consider many possible interpretations; lacking explicit constraints, it tends to “fill in” plausible details, producing hallucinations. Evidence: lack of specificity shows the highest positive coefficient (β=0.868, OR=2.382).
- **Grounding narrows the output space.** Queries that are answerable and have a clear operational intention (e.g., summarize, extract) give the model a concrete target, reducing speculation. Evidence: answerability has the largest negative coefficient (β=-1.106, OR=0.331), and intention grounding is also negative (β=-0.168).
- **Syntactic complexity taxes parsing.** Multi‑clause queries increase the cognitive load on the model’s ability to track dependencies; misinterpretations cascade into erroneous generation. Evidence: clause complexity has β=0.568 (OR=1.764) and clusters with other ambiguity markers in the ECDF analysis.

If models were fine‑tuned to request clarification for ambiguous prompts (break condition 1), or if architectures were improved to handle long‑range dependencies (break condition 3), these correlations might weaken.

## Foundational Learning
Several conceptual tools underpin the analysis:

**Ordinal Logistic Regression**  
*Why needed:* Models the ordered outcome (Safe < Borderline < Risky) while providing interpretable odds ratios for each feature.  
*Quick check:* What does an odds ratio of 2.38 for “Lack of Specificity” imply about the odds of being in a higher risk category? (Answer: The odds are 2.38 times higher.)

**Propensity Score Overlap Diagnostics**  
*Why needed:* The study uses propensity scores to assess whether comparisons between groups (e.g., feature present vs. absent) have common support, which is essential for unbiased causal estimates from observational data.  
*Quick check:* Why is common support critical for estimating an average treatment effect? (Answer: Without overlap, treated and control units are not comparable, leading to extrapolation bias.)

**Hybrid Hallucination Metric**  
*Why needed:* No single metric captures semantic correctness, surface similarity, and lexical overlap perfectly; a weighted combination balances these aspects to approximate human judgment.  
*Quick check:* Why did the authors weigh the LLM‑judge at 60% rather than relying solely on it? (Answer: To mitigate judge bias and incorporate lexical similarity signals.)

## Architecture Onboarding
The pipeline consists of five main components:

**Component map:**  
Feature Extractor -> Paraphrase Generator -> Hallucination Scorer -> Outcome Aggregator -> Ordinal Risk Model

**Critical path:**  
For a new query: (1) extract 17 binary (LLM‑based) and 4 numeric (spaCy/tiktoken) features; (2) generate up to six paraphrases meeting similarity ≥0.85; (3) score each paraphrase with the hybrid metric; (4) count hallucinated paraphrases and assign risk label; (5) apply the fitted ordinal regression coefficients (with scenario fixed effects) to compute predicted risk.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Hybrid metric weights* (0.6, 0.3, 0.1) trade off semantic accuracy against surface similarity; they were tuned on a held‑out human set not released.  
- *Fixed effects* control for dataset/scenario confounders but limit generalizability to unseen scenarios.  
- *LLM‑based feature extraction* introduces measurement error; the authors assume attenuation rather than sign flips.  
- *Paraphrase similarity threshold* (≥0.85) may be too strict, causing scarcity and potential bias.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High error rates in binary feature extraction (malformed JSON, inconsistent labels) per feature.  
- Many queries yielding fewer than six qualifying paraphrases, skewing risk counts.  
- Hybrid metric drift if fuzzy/BLEU implementations or judge model change.  
- Applying the regression to non‑GPT‑4o models without refitting produces biased odds ratios.  
- Poor propensity overlap for grounded features (Answerability, Intention Grounding) undermines causal interpretation.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Feature extraction validation:** Manually annotate 100 random queries per feature; compute Cohen’s κ with LLM detector; recalibrate prompts if κ < 0.8.  
2. **Hybrid metric calibration:** Compare triage labels on a 500‑query held‑out set with human hallucination judgments; adjust metric weights via grid search to maximize F1.  
3. **Generalizability stress test:** Run the fitted ordinal model on outputs from Llama‑3.1‑405B and Claude‑3.5‑Sonnet (same query sample); record changes in coefficients and predicted P(Risky) distributions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What query rewriting strategies most effectively reduce hallucination risk for high‑risk linguistic features?  
2. To what extent are the observed feature‑risk associations causal, or are they confounded by unmeasured query properties like topic complexity?  
3. Do these linguistic risk features generalize to non‑English languages and to LLMs beyond GPT‑4o?  
4. How do interactions between multiple linguistic features (e.g., lack of specificity combined with anaphora) influence hallucination risk beyond additive effects?  
5. How does the incorporation of visual or multimodal context modify the relationship between linguistic features and hallucination risk?

## Limitations
- Measurement validity: reliance on unreleased prompt templates, reference mappings, and hyperparameter choices (e.g., paraphrase similarity threshold, hybrid weights) may bias feature and risk estimates.
- Generalizability limited: the ordinal regression is fitted exclusively on GPT‑4o; fixed effects reduce transportability; poor overlap for grounded features weakens causal claims; external validity to other models or domains remains unverified.
- Observational design: without experimental manipulation, causality cannot be established; confounders may drive both feature presence and hallucination.

## Confidence
- **Linguistic features predict hallucination risk:** High  
- **Mechanism (ambiguity → speculation):** Medium  
- **Causal effect of answerability/grounding:** Low  
- **Cross‑scenario consistency of feature effects:** Medium
