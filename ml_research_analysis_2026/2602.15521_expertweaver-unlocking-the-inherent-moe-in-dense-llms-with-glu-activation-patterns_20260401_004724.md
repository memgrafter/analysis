# ExpertWeaver: Unlocking the Inherent MoE in Dense LLMs with GLU Activation Patterns

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15521
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15521
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** Training-free conversion of dense GLU LLMs to MoE via activation analysis, improving zero-shot performance by 3.1–5.6% at 25% sparsity and retaining 87.6% of dense capability with only 25% of MLP parameters active.

## Executive Summary
ExpertWeaver addresses a key inefficiency in converting dense LLMs to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures: existing methods disrupt the model’s intrinsic activation patterns, leading to suboptimal expert construction. By leveraging the Gated Linear Unit (GLU) mechanism—which already produces data-dependent sparsity—the method uncovers a latent MoE structure within dense models. Using a multi-task calibration set, it analyzes fine-grained neuron activations to partition neurons into universally-activated "shared experts" and task-specialized "routed experts" via layer-aware configurations and balanced clustering. All steps are training-free. At 25% sparsity, ExpertWeaver improves average zero-shot performance (e.g., MMLU, ARC) by 3.1–5.6% over strong baselines. For downcycling, it initializes a model with 3.5B active parameters that, after 200B tokens of continued pretraining, achieves a 63.5 average score—outperforming larger MoE baselines trained on more data and retaining 87.6% of its dense parent’s capability while activating only a quarter of MLP parameters.

## Method Summary
ExpertWeaver is a three-stage, training-free pipeline. First, it records per-neuron gate activation magnitudes (Swish(xW_gate)) across a diverse multi-task calibration set (Flan-v2 subset) to build an activation profile matrix per layer. Second, it computes the Coefficient of Variation (CV) for each neuron’s activation vector across tasks; neurons with CV > τ are deemed specialized. The layer’s specialization ratio r_ℓ determines the shared expert allocation α_ℓ via a linear mapping (α_ℓ = α_max − (α_max − α_min) × r_ℓ), setting the number of shared experts N_se,ℓ and routed experts N_re,ℓ for that layer. Third, it constructs experts: shared experts are formed by concatenating the top-activated neurons (by mean absolute activation); routed experts are created by balanced K-Means clustering of the remaining specialized neurons. Router weights are derived by averaging the original gate_proj vectors within each routed cluster. The dense MLP is replaced with this MoE layer. For dynamic pruning (e.g., 25% sparsity), the shared expert is always active, and the router selects k − N_se,ℓ routed experts per token without weighted sum. For downcycling, the router is later fine-tuned with a softmax and load-balancing loss during continued pretraining.

## Key Results
- At 25% sparsity, ExpertWeaver improves average zero-shot performance by 3.1–5.6% over baselines like Wanda and SparseGPT (e.g., 67.0 vs. 65.0 on Qwen2.5-7B).
- Downcycling to 3.5B active parameters yields a 63.5 average score after 200B tokens, surpassing OLMoE-1B-7B* (+4.6) despite less training data.
- Retains 87.6% of dense parent capability while activating only 25% of MLP parameters, demonstrating high parameter efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: GLU Gate as Sparsity Signal
**Claim:** The SwiGLU gate activation magnitudes provide a natural, data-dependent sparsity signal that directly informs structural expert partitioning.
**Mechanism:** For each neuron, the mean absolute value of its gate activation (Swish(xW_gate)) over calibration samples indicates its general relevance: high values mean frequently important, low values mean rarely used. This creates a token-level sparsity pattern at the neuron level.
**Core assumption:** Pretrained GLU gate activations are meaningful indicators of a neuron’s functional role across the model’s knowledge space.
**Evidence anchors:** 
- The AbsTopk-GLU experiment (keeping top-k neurons by absolute gate activation) significantly outperforms unstructured pruning (Table 1).
- Figure 1 visualizes that neurons with high gate magnitudes form coherent functional blocks.
**Break condition:** If gate activation magnitudes show no correlation with neuron importance (e.g., via ablation studies), the foundational signal fails.

### Mechanism 2: Neuron Specialization via CV
**Claim:** Neurons naturally separate into universally-activated "shared" and task-specialized "routed" classes, detectable via multi-task activation pattern analysis.
**Mechanism:** Compute the Coefficient of Variation (CV = std/mean) for each neuron’s activation vector across calibration tasks. Low CV (consistent activation) indicates universal neurons; high CV (selective activation) indicates specialized neurons. Clustering (e.g., K-Means) groups co-activated specialized neurons into routed experts.
**Core assumption:** Functional specialization is reflected in the variance of activations across a representative task distribution; co-activated neurons contribute to a common sub-function.
**Evidence anchors:** 
- Figure 1 shows a subset of neurons activating uniformly (shared) and others forming block-diagonal similarity patterns between related tasks (routed clusters).
- Partitioning via CV yields coherent expert groups as validated by downstream performance.
**Break condition:** If a multi-task calibration set fails to reveal distinct, reproducible neuron clusters (high intra-cluster activation similarity, low inter-cluster similarity), the clustering premise fails.

### Mechanism 3: Layer-Aware Configuration
**Claim:** The ratio of universal to specialized neurons varies systematically across transformer layers, requiring a layer-aware expert allocation strategy.
**Mechanism:** For each layer ℓ, compute the specialization ratio r_ℓ as the fraction of neurons with CV > τ. Map r_ℓ to a shared expert neuron ratio α_ℓ via α_ℓ = α_max − (α_max − α_min) × r_ℓ. Then set N_se,ℓ = round(α_ℓ × d_ffn / d_expert) and N_re,ℓ = N_e − N_se,ℓ. Shallow/deep layers (low r_ℓ) get larger shared experts; middle layers (high r_ℓ) get more, smaller routed experts.
**Core assumption:** Layers have distinct functional roles (e.g., early layers process generic features, middle layers build abstractions), reflected in the distribution of neuron specialization.
**Evidence anchors:** 
- Figure 2 shows a U-shaped CV pattern across layers (low CV at boundaries, higher/varied in middle).
- Figure 7 (Appendix) visualizes the resulting layer-wise expert counts, aligning with the U-shape.
**Break condition:** If CV distribution is statistically uniform across all layers, a layer-agnostic configuration would perform equally well, making this mechanism’s complexity unnecessary.

## Foundational Learning
### Gated Linear Unit (GLU) Architecture
**Why needed:** ExpertWeaver’s entire signal comes from the SwiGLU gate (Swish(xW_gate)), which produces per-token modulation scores. Understanding that these scores are data-dependent and dynamically scale the up-projection output is essential to grasp why their statistics are meaningful for expert partitioning.
**Quick check:** In y = (Swish(xW_gate) ⊙ (xW_up)) W_down, what does the element-wise product with Swish(xW_gate) represent, and why is it data-dependent? (Answer: It gates the up-projection’s output, making each neuron’s contribution conditional on the input; the gate values change with x.)

### Activation Pattern Analysis & Coefficient of Variation (CV)
**Why needed:** CV is the core metric for distinguishing universal (low CV) from specialized (high CV) neurons based on activation consistency across tasks.
**Quick check:** Neuron A has mean activation 1.0 and std 0.1 across 100 samples; Neuron B has mean 0.5 and std 0.3. Which is more likely “universal” per ExpertWeaver? What is a limitation of CV? (Answer: A (CV=0.1) is more universal. Limitation: CV can be high for low-mean neurons even if absolute variance is small, potentially misclassifying them.)

### Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Routing & Sparse Activation
**Why needed:** ExpertWeaver constructs a static MoE from dynamic pruning; understanding standard MoE (router → top-k experts → weighted sum) clarifies how ExpertWeaver differs (binary routing, concatenated weights, no weighted sum).
**Quick check:** In a standard MoE layer, what are the roles of the router and the top-k gating function? How does ExpertWeaver’s dynamic structural pruning mode (Eq. 12) differ? (Answer: Router computes logits; top-k selects experts; weighted sum combines. ExpertWeaver uses the router to select experts but then uses concatenated weights from the original dense neurons, effectively pruning the rest.)

### Structured Pruning vs. Dynamic Routing
**Why needed:** ExpertWeaver bridges structured pruning (permanently removing neurons) and MoE routing (token-level dynamic selection) via a training-free construction that groups neurons into always-on shared and selectively-activated routed experts.
**Quick check:** What’s the key difference between pruning a neuron permanently vs. routing it conditionally? (Answer: Pruning removes the neuron entirely; routing includes it only for certain tokens. ExpertWeaver makes static groups that are dynamically selected as wholes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Calibration Data → Activation Recording (per-layer gate matrices A) → CV Calculation → Layer Configuration (r_ℓ, α_ℓ, N_se,ℓ, N_re,ℓ) → Shared Expert Selection (top-activated neurons) → Routed Expert Clustering (balanced K-Means on remaining) → Router Construction (average gate vectors) → MoE Layer Replacement.

### Critical Path
1. Run forward passes on Flan-v2 calibration set, capturing pre-Swish gate activations xW_gate for all d_ffn neurons per layer to build matrix A.
2. Compute CV for each neuron’s activation vector across tasks; obtain layer specialization ratio r_ℓ (fraction with CV > τ).
3. Map r_ℓ to shared expert ratio α_ℓ; compute N_se,ℓ and N_re,ℓ given d_expert = d_ffn / N_e.
4. Select top N_se,ℓ × d_expert neurons by mean absolute activation for shared expert; concatenate their gate/up/down weights.
5. Cluster remaining specialized neurons into N_re,ℓ clusters of size d_expert using balanced K-Means on activation vectors; concatenate weights per cluster for routed experts.
6. Build router by averaging original gate_proj vectors within each routed cluster.
7. Replace each dense MLP with the constructed MoE layer (1 shared + N_re,ℓ routed experts).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Shared expert ratio (α_ℓ):** Higher preserves universal knowledge but reduces capacity for specialization; layer-aware mapping attempts to balance per layer.
- **Expert granularity (d_expert):** Smaller experts enable finer specialization but increase router size/computation and risk overfitting clusters; paper finds ~64 optimal.
- **Calibration set diversity:** Must cover the task space to reveal true specialization; too narrow a set misrepresents neuron functions.
- **Specialization threshold (τ):** Directly controls r_ℓ; affects which neurons are deemed specialized and thus layer configuration.

### Failure Signatures
1. **Poor cluster separation:** Heatmaps of routed expert activation patterns (like Figure 8) show high overlap between clusters, indicating clustering failed to find coherent functional groups.
2. **Layer misconfiguration:** Extreme N_se,ℓ values (e.g., 0 or N_e) for many layers suggest flawed CV calculation or layer mapping; specialized layers may be given too many shared neurons or vice versa.
3. **Router degeneracy:** Router logits (x @ W_router) are nearly uniform across experts, leading to random routing, poor load balance, and low expert utilization during continued pretraining.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Reproduce AbsTopk-GLU Baseline:** Implement the simple top-k by absolute gate activation on a dense GLU model (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B). Validate on MMLU 5-shot that it outperforms unstructured pruning (Wanda, SparseGPT) at the same sparsity level, confirming the gate signal’s utility.
2. **Visualize Layer CV Distribution:** For a model like Qwen2.5-7B, compute CVs across all layers using the Flan-v2 calibration set. Plot (like Figure 2). Verify the U-shaped pattern (low CV in boundary layers, higher/varied in middle). If absent, the layer-aware mechanism may not generalize.
3. **Validate Clustering Coherence:** After balanced K-Means for a middle layer, compute intra-cluster vs. inter-cluster variance of activation vectors. Expect significantly lower intra-cluster variance (p<0.01). Alternatively, manually inspect which calibration tasks most strongly activate each routed expert cluster to see if semantic grouping emerges.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Can ExpertWeaver be applied to architectures without GLU activations?** The method explicitly uses GLU gating signals; applicability to ReLU/GELU models (e.g., GPT-3) is unknown.
- **Is the coefficient of variation (CV) the most effective metric for quantifying neuron specialization?** Alternative importance measures (gradient norms, activation magnitude) might improve partitioning but are untested.
- **Can ExpertWeaver eliminate the need for a multi-task calibration dataset?** Dependence on Flan-v2 limits practicality; a calibration-free variant (e.g., using synthetic prompts) would be more generally applicable.

## Limitations
- **Calibration set ambiguity:** Exact sampling, prompt formatting, and tokenization for the Flan-v2 subset are unspecified, risking activation distribution shifts and reproducibility issues.
- **Clustering implementation details:** Balanced K-Means specifics (iterations, convergence, distance metric) are omitted, potentially affecting expert coherence and stability.
- **Generalization scope:** The layer-aware configuration mapping (α_ℓ) is derived from Qwen2.5-7B; its validity for other architectures (varying depth/width) is untested.
- **Downcycling hyperparameters:** Continued pretraining learning rate schedule, optimizer settings, and SFT dataset mixtures are incompletely documented, hindering exact replication of reported scores.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (GLU gate as sparsity signal):** High. Strong internal evidence (AbsTopk-GLU ablations) and theoretical alignment with GLU’s data-dependent gating.
- **Mechanism 2 (Neuron specialization via CV):** Medium. Supported by visualizations and CV-based partitioning, but depends critically on calibration set diversity; corpus analogies are indirect.
- **Mechanism 3 (Layer-aware configuration):** Low-Medium. Relies on observed U-shaped CV pattern; no independent verification. May not generalize beyond models with similar layer function distribution.

Recompute layer CV distributions and verify if the U-shape persists and if average zero-shot performance at 25% sparsity drops by less than 2%.

Run a brief continued pretraining (10B tokens) and monitor validation loss to identify a consistent optimal range or necessity for model-specific tuning.
3. **Clustering Coherence & Router Diagnostics:** After clustering, compute: (a) intra-cluster vs. inter-cluster variance of activation vectors; (b) router logit entropy on calibration data; (c) expert load balance (CV of token counts per routed expert) during continued pretraining. Success metrics: intra-cluster variance significantly lower than inter-cluster (p<0.01), router entropy <3.0 nats, load-balance loss <0.1 after 50B tokens.
