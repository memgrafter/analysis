# Beyond Calibration: Confounding Pathology Limits Foundation Model Specificity in Abdominal Trauma CT

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10359  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10359  
- **Reference count**: 28  
- **Primary result**: Foundation models achieve comparable AUC to task-specific models for traumatic bowel injury detection but suffer a 50–51 percentage point specificity drop when negative cases include confounding solid organ injuries, whereas task-specific models drop only 12–41 pp, demonstrating that negative-class heterogeneity—not prevalence miscalibration—drives specificity failures.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates foundation models (MedCLIP zero-shot, RadDINO linear probe) against task-specific architectures for detecting traumatic bowel injury in abdominal CT scans under compound distribution shift (class imbalance and appearance heterogeneity). Using a multi-institutional dataset (training: 3,147 patients, 2.3% prevalence; enriched test: 100 patients, 42% prevalence), foundation models matched task-specific discrimination (AUC 0.64–0.68 vs 0.58–0.64) without task-specific training. However, stratified analysis revealed a catastrophic specificity loss for foundation models (50–51 pp drop) when negative cases contained confounding solid organ injuries, while task-specific models were more robust (12–41 pp drop). The findings indicate that foundation models' specificity failures for rare pathologies stem primarily from negative-class heterogeneity—specifically, an inability to discriminate the target pathology from other injuries—rather than from prevalence-related calibration issues alone.

## Method Summary
The study leveraged the RSNA Abdominal Traumatic Injury CT (RATIC) dataset, with a training set (3,147 patients, 2.3% bowel injury prevalence) and an enriched test set (100 patients, 42% prevalence). A no-pathology subgroup (50 patients) and a solid-organ-injury-only subgroup (58 patients) were isolated for stratified analysis. All inputs underwent soft-tissue windowing (width 400, level 50 HU). Models evaluated: MedCLIP (zero-shot, 5-prompt ensemble, slice-level image-text similarity, patient-level max aggregation), RadDINO (linear probe on frozen ViT-B/14 features, max-pooled CLS tokens, standardized, logistic regression with L2 and class weighting), and three task-specific models (CNN: EfficientNet-B3 + BiLSTM with focal loss; Transformer: Swin-Tiny + LSTM; Team Oxygen: ensemble of CoaT-Lite, EfficientNetV2, and RNNs). Primary metric: AUC; secondary metrics included sensitivity, specificity, F1, PPV, NPV. Operating points were derived via Youden’s J for task-specific models and a fixed threshold (0.8) for MedCLIP.

## Key Results
- Foundation models (MedCLIP AUC 0.64, RadDINO AUC 0.66) achieved discrimination on par with task-specific models (AUC 0.58–0.64) without any task-specific training.
- When negative cases included confounding solid organ injuries, foundation model specificity plummeted 50–51 percentage points: MedCLIP from 84.0% to 32.8%; RadDINO from 84.4% to 33.3%. Task-specific models showed smaller reductions (12–41 pp).
- The specificity deficit demonstrates that negative-class heterogeneity—organ confusion—is the primary driver of foundation model failures for rare pathologies, not prevalence miscalibration.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Foundation models are pre-trained on vast, diverse datasets to learn generic visual representations. While this grants them strong abnormality detection capabilities, they lack organ-specific discrimination: they respond to any tissue disruption or fluid collection, whether from bowel injury or solid organ damage. In contrast, task-specific models, trained on labeled examples of both bowel injury and other pathologies, learn to distinguish the target sign from confounders. The compound shift—rare target combined with heterogeneous negatives—exposes this representational gap: foundation models classify many false positives when the negative set contains other injuries, collapsing specificity. Task-specific models, despite lower overall discrimination, are more robust to negative-class heterogeneity because their training explicitly includes confounding cases.

## Foundational Learning
**Foundation model**: A large model pre-trained on broad, often multimodal data (e.g., images and text) to acquire general-purpose representations. *Why needed*: Enables transfer to new tasks without extensive labeled data. *Quick check*: Probe on multiple diverse downstream tasks; reasonable performance across tasks indicates generality.

**Zero-shot classification**: Using a foundation model with textual prompts to perform a new task without any additional training. *Why needed*: Rapid deployment for novel tasks. *Quick check*: Vary prompt wording and ensure predictions are stable and semantically meaningful.

**Linear probe**: Training a linear classifier (e.g., logistic regression) on frozen features from a pre-trained model. *Why needed*: Efficient adaptation with minimal data and compute. *Quick check*: Achieve above-chance AUC on a held-out set; if performance is near random, features may be task-incompatible.

**Negative-class heterogeneity**: The negative class comprises multiple distinct subpopulations (e.g., healthy, other pathologies). *Why needed*: Affects model specificity in medical imaging, especially for rare targets. *Quick check*: Stratify test set by negative subgroups and compare specificity; large drops indicate vulnerability to heterogeneity.

**Organ confusion**: A model's tendency to misattribute findings from one organ to the target pathology of another. *Why needed*: Critical for precise diagnosis in multi-organ imaging. *Quick check*: On false positive cases, annotate the actual pathology present; high rate of confounding injuries indicates organ confusion.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
*MedCLIP*: CT slices → ViT image encoder → slice embeddings; Text prompts → text encoder → prompt embeddings; Cosine similarity (slice, prompt) → slice-prompt scores; Max over slices per prompt → patient-prompt scores; Majority vote (≥3/5 > 0.8) → prediction.  
*RadDINO*: CT slices → frozen ViT → CLS tokens; Max over slices → patient embedding; Standardization (train mean/std) → standardized embedding; Logistic regression (L2, class-weighted) → probability; Youden’s J threshold → prediction.

**Critical Path**  
- *MedCLIP*: Alignment between image and text embeddings must be accurate; majority vote threshold and number of prompts are critical.  
- *RadDINO*: Quality of frozen features and hyperparameters of the linear classifier (regularization, class weighting) are critical.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *MedCLIP*: Zero-shot avoids task-specific data but is highly sensitive to prompt design and pre-training data coverage. Multiple prompts increase robustness but add complexity. Max pooling assumes the injury appears in at least one slice; sensitive to slice selection.  
- *RadDINO*: Linear probe is parameter-efficient but may underfit if features are not linearly separable. Frozen backbone prevents adaptation to the abdominal CT domain. Standardization scope (global vs. per-slice) trades off simplicity for capturing slice-level statistics.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Both models show high false positive rates when the negative set contains solid organ injuries (organ confusion).  
- MedCLIP performance varies with prompt wording; poor prompts yield low AUC.  
- RadDINO performance degrades with mismatched normalization or inappropriate regularization.  
- Both are sensitive to input preprocessing (windowing, resizing) and aggregation method.

**First Experiments**  
1. **MedCLIP prompt and aggregation validation**: Reproduce the 5-prompt ensemble with majority vote (threshold 0.8) and max aggregation; compare AUC/specificity to reported numbers. Then, ablate using a single best prompt to quantify ensemble benefit.  
2. **RadDINO standardization and aggregation validation**: Reproduce max-pooled CLS embeddings, global standardization, and class-weighted logistic regression with Youden’s J threshold. Then, ablate by switching to mean pooling and omitting standardization.  
3. **Stratified specificity confirmation**: For both models, compute specificity on the no-pathology and solid-organ-injury-only subgroups; verify the reported 50–51 pp drop for foundation models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What specific adaptation strategies (e.g., adapter modules, mixture-of-experts, organ-specific fine-tuning) most effectively mitigate organ confusion in foundation models for rare pathology detection? The conclusion suggests parameter-efficient approaches but does not evaluate them.
2. Do the internal feature representations of foundation models fail to disentangle target pathology (bowel injury) from confounding pathologies (solid organ injury) in the embedding space? The paper infers organ confusion from output behavior but does not probe latent representations.
3. How does foundation model performance (especially specificity) degrade under true clinical prevalence (~2–5%) versus the enriched test set prevalence (42%) when confounding solid organ injuries are present? The enriched test set may alter calibration and trade-offs.
4. Is the progressive reduction in susceptibility to negative-class heterogeneity observed with task-specific models due to the quantity of labeled training data, the architectural inductive biases, or their combination? The comparison across heterogeneous model families cannot isolate the causal factor.

## Limitations
- Small test cohorts (enriched n=100, no-pathology n=50) limit statistical power and generalizability.
- Critical implementation details are missing: exact MedCLIP prompt texts, RadDINO feature standardization scope and logistic regression hyperparameters, Team Oxygen ensemble composition, and slice sampling strategies.
- Potential data leakage between the training set and the stratified negative subgroups is unaddressed; subgroup integrity needs verification.

## Confidence
- **Claim**: Foundation models match task-specific AUC without training. **Confidence**: Medium (results depend on undisclosed details; reproducibility uncertain).
- **Claim**: Foundation models show a larger specificity drop than task-specific models when confounding solid organ injuries are present. **Confidence**: High (large effect size, clear subgroup definitions).
- **Claim**: Negative-class heterogeneity, not prevalence miscalibration, drives specificity failures. **Confidence**: Medium (interpretation plausible but not exclusive; further ablations needed).
