# Using predictive multiplicity to measure individual performance within the AI Act

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11944  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11944  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Predictive multiplicity metrics (conflict ratios and δ‑ambiguity) enable compliance with the EU AI Act’s individual‑level reporting requirement; dataset multiplicity via bootstrapping yields accurate estimates with modest model counts (~500).  

## Executive Summary
The EU AI Act mandates that high‑risk AI providers report performance not only at the dataset level but also for specific persons. This paper identifies predictive multiplicity—the phenomenon where multiple models with similar overall accuracy produce conflicting predictions for the same individual—as a core compliance issue. The authors define conflict ratios (fraction of models disagreeing on an individual’s prediction) and δ‑ambiguity (the proportion of individuals with conflict ratio above a threshold δ). To compute these, they construct an ad‑hoc Rashomon set by combining parametric variations (hyperparameters, random seeds) and dataset multiplicity (bootstrapping). Experiments on synthetic data, the ACS Employment dataset (1.7M records), and COMPAS show that dataset multiplicity is essential for accurate conflict ratio recovery and that 500 models suffice to approximate the full Rashomon set within 0.05 δ‑ambiguity. The approach offers a practical tool for providers to meet the Act’s transparency and accuracy obligations.  

## Method Summary
The method proceeds in six steps: (1) Train a baseline model \(g_0\) to achieve maximal accuracy on the training data; (2) define an accuracy threshold \(\varepsilon\) and generate a diverse set of models within the same model class by varying hyperparameters (parametric multiplicity) and training on bootstrapped samples of the data (dataset multiplicity); (3) retain only models whose accuracy satisfies \(\text{acc}(g) \ge \text{acc}(g_0) - \varepsilon\) to form the Rashomon set \(R\); (4) for each individual \(x\) in a test or deployment set, compute the conflict ratio \(c_R(x)\) as the fraction of models in \(R\) that predict the minority label; (5) choose a threshold \(\delta\) and compute \(\delta\)-ambiguity \(A_\delta(R)\) as the proportion of individuals with \(c_R(x) > \delta\); (6) report \(A_\delta(R)\) and optionally the per‑individual \(c_R(x)\) to deployers. The ad‑hoc approximation is validated against exhaustive enumeration (TreeFarms) for decision trees, showing that even 500 models yield \(\delta\)-ambiguity within 0.05 of the ground truth.  

## Key Results
- Dataset multiplicity (bootstrapping) is necessary to recover true conflict ratios; ignoring it underestimates \(\delta\)-ambiguity.  
- With both parametric and dataset multiplicity, \(\delta\)-ambiguity estimates stabilize with around 500 models, even for large datasets (e.g., 1.7 M ACS records).  
- The ad‑hoc method approximates exhaustive Rashomon set enumeration (TreeFarms) closely, enabling practical and scalable compliance assessment.  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Legal relevance of per‑individual performance.** Articles 11(1) and 13(3) of the EU AI Act require reporting accuracy for specific persons, implying evaluation at the individual level. Predictive multiplicity directly addresses this by quantifying disagreement among equally accurate models for each person, highlighting unreliability. The core assumption is that the Act’s wording mandates individual‑level assessment; if later interpreted as subgroup analysis, this motivation weakens.  

**Mechanism 2: Necessity of dataset multiplicity.** The ad‑hoc Rashomon set combines parametric variations (e.g., hyperparameters, random seeds) with dataset multiplicity (bootstrapping). Varying the training data captures uncertainty from data collection, a major source of multiplicity. Without dataset multiplicity, the Rashomon set becomes overly homogeneous, leading to underestimation of conflict ratios. Evidence from synthetic and large‑scale experiments shows that including dataset multiplicity yields conflict ratios that match ground truth and that bootstrapping alone provides a good approximation.  

**Mechanism 3: Practicality of modest model counts.** For realistic model classes and datasets, a relatively small number of diverse models (~500) suffices to compute \(\delta\)-ambiguity accurately. The combination of parametric and dataset variations covers the dominant sources of disagreement; additional models yield diminishing returns. Validation against exhaustive enumeration (TreeFarms) on COMPAS data shows that 500‑model ad‑hoc sets achieve \(\delta\)-ambiguity within 0.05 of the ground truth, confirming the approach’s practicality for compliance.  

## Foundational Learning
**Rashomon Set**  
- *Why needed:* It defines the set of models with nearly optimal accuracy, forming the basis for measuring predictive multiplicity. Without this set, we cannot assess disagreement among plausible models.  
- *Quick check:* What is the Rashomon set, and what does it represent in machine learning model selection?  

**Dataset Multiplicity (Bootstrapping)**  
- *Why needed:* It introduces variation in training data to reflect uncertainty in the data‑generating process. This is crucial for generating a diverse Rashomon set; otherwise, only parametric changes yield similar models and underestimate conflict.  
- *Quick check:* How does bootstrapping help approximate the Rashomon set, and why is it important compared to only varying model hyperparameters?  

**δ‑Ambiguity**  
- *Why needed:* It aggregates individual conflict ratios into a single dataset‑level metric that regulators can use, capturing the extent of unreliable predictions.  
- *Quick check:* Given a set of models and a dataset, how would you compute the \(\delta\)-ambiguity for a chosen threshold \(\delta\)?  

## Architecture Onboarding  
**Component map**  
Input (dataset D, model class H, accuracy threshold ε) → Train baseline model g₀ → Sample diverse models (vary hyperparameters & bootstrap training data) → Filter by accuracy (acc(g) ≥ acc(g₀)−ε) → Compute conflict ratio for each individual → Aggregate δ‑ambiguity → Output metrics  

**Critical path**  
(a) Determine ε and baseline accuracy; (b) generate diverse models; (c) evaluate models on each individual; (d) compute conflict ratios; (e) compute δ‑ambiguity. Steps (b) and (c) can be parallelized.  

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Number of models: more models improve estimation but increase computational cost.  
- Dataset multiplicity strategy: bootstrapping is cheap but may not fully capture distribution shifts; generating new datasets is more expressive but costly.  
- ε selection: smaller ε yields a stricter Rashomon set, potentially lowering ambiguity but risking too few models; larger ε increases ambiguity estimates but may include models with noticeably worse accuracy.  
- Representativeness: the model set must span plausible variations; otherwise conflict ratios are biased low.  

**Failure signatures**  
- Overly homogeneous Rashomon set (only parametric variations) → underestimated δ‑ambiguity; detected by low prediction variance across models.  
- Inconsistent ε (too large) → inflation of artificial ambiguity.  
- Data leakage (using same test set for baseline and conflict computation) → biased estimates; require separate data.  
- Non‑stationarity (deployment data differs from training) → conflict ratios may not generalise.  

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Synthetic data experiment:** Ground truth conflict ratios known; compared four levels of dataset multiplicity (none, bootstrapping with few/many models, full new datasets). Result: dataset multiplicity is necessary; bootstrapping already approximates well.  
2. **ACS Employment experiment:** Large real‑world dataset; compared bootstrapping, feature subsampling, feature noise, and full dataset multiplicity. Findings: bootstrapping yields similar δ‑ambiguity to full dataset multiplicity with fewer models; ignoring dataset multiplicity underestimates ambiguity.  
3. **TreeFarms comparison:** On COMPAS, compared ad‑hoc approach (varying size) with exhaustive enumeration (TreeFarms). Result: ad‑hoc sets of 500 models achieve δ‑ambiguity within 0.05 of the exhaustive ground truth.  

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. For which model classes beyond decision trees does the ad‑hoc Rashomon set approximation reliably estimate conflict ratios and δ‑ambiguity?  
2. How should the δ parameter in δ‑ambiguity be set in practice to balance detection of ambiguous individuals with actionable thresholds for deployer intervention under the AI Act?  
3. What concrete standards and documentation formats should regulators adopt to mandate the reporting of conflict ratios and δ‑ambiguity under the AI Act’s transparency and accuracy requirements?  
4. What are the computational limits and optimization strategies for applying the ad‑hoc approach to very large datasets (e.g., billions of records) or complex models in real‑world AI deployments?  
5. How does predictive multiplicity, as quantified by conflict ratios, correlate with robustness to distribution shifts or adversarial attacks, and should these be jointly evaluated for AI Act compliance?  

## Limitations
- The assumption that the AI Act requires per‑individual performance metrics is not legally settled; it may be interpreted as subgroup analysis, reducing the urgency of multiplicity measurement.  
- Results are demonstrated on decision trees and small neural networks; applicability to large deep models remains uncertain.  
- Bootstrapped datasets may not fully capture distribution shifts in deployment, potentially under‑estimating conflict ratios.  

## Confidence
- AI Act requires individual reporting: **Medium** (textual support but no legal precedent)  
- Dataset multiplicity is essential: **High** (consistent empirical evidence)  
- ~500 models suffice for δ‑ambiguity: **Medium** (validated on simple models; unknown for complex ones)
