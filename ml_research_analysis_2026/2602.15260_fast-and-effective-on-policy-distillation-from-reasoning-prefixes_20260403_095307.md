# Fast and Effective On-policy Distillation from Reasoning Prefixes
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.15260
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15260
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: On-policy prefix distillation reduces training FLOP by 2–47× while matching or approaching full on-policy distillation accuracy on math and out-of-domain benchmarks.
## Executive Summary
This paper introduces on-policy prefix distillation (prefix OPD), a method that dramatically lowers the computational cost of training small reasoning models. By analyzing token-level reverse-KL losses, the authors discover that most learning signal lies in the early prefix of generated reasoning traces. They therefore truncate student rollouts after a fixed prefix length and apply the distillation loss only on those tokens, optionally using a linear schedule that gradually increases the prefix. Experiments with Qwen3 students on AI-for-Math (MATH500, AIME-24/25) and out-of-domain benchmarks (GPQA, MMLU-Pro) show that with moderate prefix lengths (1024–2048 tokens) prefix OPD achieves accuracy nearly identical to full on-policy distillation while using less than 10% of the FLOP. A prefix schedule narrows the performance gap further, reaching near-full OPD results with under 50% FLOP. The approach makes training of efficient reasoning models far more practical.
## Method Summary
Prefix OPD modifies standard on-policy distillation: during training, the student generates completions up to a fixed prefix length \(L_{\text{train}}\); the reverse-KL loss (student vs. teacher log-probabilities) is computed only on these prefix tokens, after which sampling stops. This avoids the cost of generating and scoring long tails where the learning signal is weak. A linear prefix schedule can be used: start at \(L_{\text{train}}=1\) and add \(\Delta L=256\) after each update, gradually exposing the student to longer rollouts. Training follows the Verl framework with AdamW (lr=5e-5), batch size 512 (4 samples per prompt), BF16 precision. Inputs are formatted by appending a "" token after the question to align with teacher behavior. FLOP is measured using Verl's counter; evaluation uses temperature=1.0, top_p=1.0, and maximum 16,384 tokens. The teacher is Qwen3-8B (instruct); students are Qwen3-1.7B‑Base or 8B‑Base.
## Key Results
- Prefix OPD with \(L_{\text{train}}\in\{1024,2048\}\) attains accuracy within ∼1% of full OPD on MATH500 and AIME while consuming <10% FLOP.
- A linear prefix schedule (growing to ∼15k tokens) narrows the gap to full OPD, using <50% FLOP.
- Short teacher prefixes (2048–8192 tokens) alone can steer an untrained student to correct solutions, confirming the steering effect.
- For the smallest student (1.7B), very short prefixes hurt out‑of‑domain performance; prefix scheduling mitigates this.
## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Token-level training signals in on‑policy distillation are concentrated in the early prefix of the generated output.
- **Mechanism:** The reverse‑KL loss is significantly larger (on average) during the first several hundred to thousand tokens and then decays. This indicates the student’s primary learning challenge lies in high‑level planning steps that occur early in the reasoning chain; later token predictions are easier and contribute less to the loss.
- **Core assumption:** The magnitude of the token‑level loss is a good proxy for the importance of that token to the final answer correctness.
- **Evidence anchors:** abstract; section 2.2 Figure 2; nearby corpus (weak).
- **Break condition:** If loss were uniformly or tail‑heavy, prefix distillation would discard most learning signal and underperform full OPD.
### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A short teacher‑generated prefix can steer the student to complete the reasoning correctly, even without additional training.
- **Mechanism:** The teacher prefix establishes a correct initialization of the reasoning state; the student builds on this foundation, avoiding early compounding errors.
- **Core assumption:** The student has capacity to continue from a correct prefix; formulating the right approach is the hardest part.
- **Evidence anchors:** abstract; section 2.3 Figure 3; section 4.4 case study.
- **Break condition:** If students could not continue even with long teacher prefixes, prefix‑based methods would fail; data show consistent improvements.
### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Supervising only the first \(L_{\text{train}}\) tokens—especially with a linear schedule—preserves nearly full OPD performance while cutting FLOP by 2–47×.
- **Mechanism:** The prefix carries most signal; truncating avoids expensive sampling/scoring of the uninformative tail. The schedule gradually increases \(L_{\text{train}}\), allowing the student to benefit from longer‑horizon feedback as it improves.
- **Core assumption:** Knowledge from the prefix generalizes to the tail; additional tail supervision yields diminishing returns.
- **Evidence anchors:** abstract; section 4.2 Tables 1–2; section 5.1 Figure 4.
- **Break condition:** If tail tokens contained critical signal, performance would degrade sharply for small \(L_{\text{train}}\); empirical curves show plateauing benefits, confirming diminishing returns.
## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** On‑policy vs. off‑policy distillation  
  *Why needed:* The paper modifies on‑policy distillation; understanding exposure bias and why on‑policy updates reduce train‑test mismatch is key.  
  *Quick check:* What is the difference between sampling trajectories from the current student (on‑policy) and using a fixed teacher dataset (off‑policy) in knowledge distillation?
- **Concept:** Reverse KL divergence for token‑level alignment  
  *Why needed:* The loss uses reverse KL to match student token distribution to teacher’s; its mode‑seeking nature explains why it’s chosen over forward KL and reduces reward hacking.  
  *Quick check:* Why might reverse KL be preferred to forward KL in on‑policy distillation for arithmetic reasoning tasks?
- **Concept:** Curriculum/prefix scheduling as progressive training  
  *Why needed:* The prefix schedule gradually increases supervised length; framing it as a curriculum clarifies why it improves robustness for smaller students.  
  *Quick check:* How does gradually increasing the training prefix length act as a form of curriculum learning?
## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Student policy network (Qwen3‑Base) → Rollout sampler → Token‑level log‑prob calculator → Reverse‑KL loss module → Prefix scheduler (optional) → Training loop (Verl); Teacher policy network (Qwen3‑8B) feeds log‑probs.
  Path: Student generates prefixes; teacher scores them; loss backpropagates to student; schedule updates \(L_{\text{train}}\).
- **Critical path:** For each update step:  
  1. Sample K rollouts per prompt from student, stopping at \(L_{\text{train}}\).  
  2. For each token \(t \le L_{\text{train}}\), compute \(\log \pi_s\) and \(\log \pi_T\) for next token.  
  3. Aggregate reverse‑KL loss (sum over tokens and rollouts).  
  4. Back‑propagate and update student parameters.  
  5. If using schedule, increment \(L_{\text{train}}\) by \(\Delta L\).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Prefix length: shorter \(L_{\text{train}}\) reduces FLOP linearly but may sacrifice accuracy; 1024–2048 tokens sweet spot.  
  - On‑policy memory: prefix truncation limits memory vs. full OPD, but off‑policy prefix reuse risks distribution mismatch.  
  - Reverse KL vs. forward KL: reverse KL stable and mode‑seeking; forward KL encourages diversity but can degrade reasoning.  
  - Schedule vs. fixed: scheduling mitigates tail undertraining (critical for small models) with modest extra cost.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Early saturation: validation accuracy plateaus or drops when \(L_{\text{train}}\) grows beyond a size, indicating tail tokens are uninformative.  
  - Tail degeneration: With very short prefixes on small students (1.7B), generations beyond prefix become incoherent or repetitive.  
  - OOD collapse: For \(L_{\text{train}}=256\) on 1.7B student, GPQA/MMLU‑Pro scores drop below base model.  
  - Training instability: large learning rates (≥2e‑4) cause rapid collapse; optimal LR ≈5e‑5.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Token‑level loss distribution: Compute average reverse‑KL loss across positions on OpenThoughts3 to confirm early concentration.  
  2. Teacher‑prefix probing: Feed varying‑length teacher prefixes to untrained students and measure continuation accuracy, showing prefix steerability.  
  3. Prefix OPD evaluation: Train with fixed \(L_{\text{train}}\) (256–2048) and with prefix scheduling; compare against SeqKD and full OPD on benchmarks, recording FLOP.
## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can on‑policy prefix distillation generalize to other long‑form generation tasks beyond reasoning (e.g., summarization, story generation)?  
  *Basis:* Limitations: “It remains unknown whether prefix distillation can be more generally adopted in other long‑form generation scenarios…”  
  *Why unresolved:* Only math reasoning evaluated; task‑specific characteristics may limit applicability.
- How can we design adaptive prefix‑scheduling strategies that dynamically adjust prefix length to optimize compute‑performance trade‑offs?  
  *Basis:* Section 4.2: “It is worth exploring more adaptive schedulers (e.g., curriculum based on validation performance or KL/entropy signals) in future work.”  
  *Why unresolved:* Only a simple linear schedule tested; adaptive strategies could yield better efficiency.
- Why does prefix distillation hurt tail performance for small models, and how can this be mitigated while retaining compute savings?  
  *Basis:* Finding 3: “Learning the prefix benefits the tail for a student model with sufficient parameters, but can hurt the tail given a small model size.” Mechanism unexplained; practical issue for small deployments.
- Can off‑policy data or forward KL divergence be integrated with prefix distillation without sacrificing the generalization benefits of on‑policy learning?  
  *Basis:* Section 5.3 title and experiments show off‑policy reverse KL gives only mild gains, forward KL hurts. Overcoming this trade‑off would improve scalability.
## Limitations
- Prompt format and teacher variant ambiguities: The exact template and whether “Qwen3‑8B” is base or instruct are unspecified, risking implementation mismatch.
- Missing importance sampling details: The cited internal documentation for correcting off‑policy bias is unavailable; gradients may be biased if omitted.
- Dataset and FLOP counter specifics: No seed/exact subset of OpenThoughts3 (30.7k) provided; Verl’s FLOP counter implementation is not fully described, hindering exact cost comparisons.
