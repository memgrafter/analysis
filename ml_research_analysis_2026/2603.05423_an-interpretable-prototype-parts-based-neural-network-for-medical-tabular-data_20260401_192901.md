# An interpretable prototype parts-based neural network for medical tabular data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05423
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05423
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: MEDIC achieves competitive classification (g‑mean: 0.6889 Cirrhosis, 1.0000 CKD, 0.7367 Diabetes) while providing interpretable discretization and prototype-based explanations.

## Executive Summary
MEDIC is a novel interpretable neural network designed for medical tabular data, addressing the interpretability gap in healthcare machine learning. It combines differentiable discretization of continuous features, sparse patching masks to form clinically meaningful feature subsets (parts), and prototype learning anchored in real patient records. The model undergoes a three-stage training process: fuzzy binning with end-to-end optimization, transition to hard symbolic binning and mask binarization, and finally replacement of synthetic prototypes with actual training examples. On benchmarks, MEDIC matches the performance of strong baselines like XGBoost, with g‑mean scores of 0.6889 (Cirrhosis), 1.0000 (CKD), and 0.7367 (Diabetes). Case studies reveal that learned bin intervals align with clinical thresholds and prototype parts capture diagnostically relevant feature combinations, enabling transparent, case‑based reasoning. This work demonstrates a path toward high‑performance yet inherently interpretable models in medicine.

## Method Summary
MEDIC processes medical tabular data through a pipeline that begins with a differentiable fuzzy binning layer. For each continuous feature, learnable bin centers and a shared bandwidth produce soft assignments which are later converted to hard, contiguous intervals for symbolic interpretability. Sparse binary masks multiply the binarized input to extract p feature subsets (“parts”). Each part is embedded by a shared shallow MLP. The model maintains n prototype vectors in the embedding space; the input’s parts are compared to all prototypes via L2 distance, and the maximum distance per prototype is taken to form a feature vector for classification.

Training proceeds in three stages. Stage 1 optimizes all parameters (including bin centers and prototypes) end‑to‑end with cross‑entropy loss, plus L1 regularization on masks for sparsity and a diversity term to spread prototypes apart. In Stage 2, fuzzy binning switches to hard binning (nearest center) and masks are binarized (thresholded). Stage 3 then replaces each synthetic prototype with the embedding of the nearest real training part, freezes these anchors, and fine‑tunes only the classification head. Hyperparameters (e.g., number of prototypes, embedding dimension, regularization strengths) are tuned via Tree‑Parzen Estimator within 5‑fold cross‑validation.

## Key Results
- Competitive predictive performance: g‑mean scores of 0.6889 on Cirrhosis, 1.0000 on CKD, and 0.7367 on Diabetes, comparable to XGBoost and other baseline models.
- Learned discretization intervals align with established medical thresholds (e.g., albumin ~3.5 g/dL), providing symbolic, interpretable ranges.
- Prototype parts reflect diagnostically relevant feature combinations (e.g., elevated bilirubin and hepatomegaly), and explanations are grounded in real patient records.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Differentiable Discretization via Fuzzy Binning
- **Claim:** MEDIC uses a fuzzy binning layer to transform continuous medical features into interpretable discrete bins during training, then switches to hard binning for symbolic inference.
- **Mechanism:** For each continuous input value *x*, a set of *K* learnable bin centers *μₖ* and shared bandwidth *σ* define Gaussian kernel similarities. Soft assignments via normalized exponentials (Eq. 1–2) yield a differentiable, probabilistic bin membership vector. Post‑training, hard binning assigns *x* to the nearest center via argmin (Eq. 3), producing a one‑hot representation with contiguous intervals (Eq. 4) that map to clinical ranges.
- **Core assumption:** Meaningful clinical thresholds (e.g., normal/abnormal lab cutoffs) can be discovered from data through gradient‑based optimization of bin centers.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "employs differentiable discretization of continuous features"
  - [section 3.1] Full derivation of fuzzy/hard binning, including Gaussian kernel and interval computation.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., ProtoEHR, ProtoNAM) use prototypes but not discretization; MEDIC’s approach is novel for tabular medical data.
- **Break condition:** Learned bin intervals do not align with established clinical reference ranges (e.g., albumin threshold ≠ ~3.5 g/dL) or switching to hard binning degrades accuracy >5%.

### Mechanism 2: Sparse Patching Masks for Feature Subset Extraction
- **Claim:** Trainable patching masks *M* ∈ ℝ<sup>p×d′</sup> select sparse, clinically coherent feature subsets ("parts") from the discretized input vector.
- **Mechanism:** The binary discretized input is multiplied element‑wise with each mask row, yielding *p* sparse part vectors. L1 regularization on *M* (Eq. 5) drives many mask entries to zero, so each part activates only a few features. This encourages the model to focus on small, interpretable feature conjunctions (e.g., "high glucose ∧ elevated BMI").
- **Core assumption:** Diagnostic reasoning in medicine often relies on a limited combination of key features rather than all available measurements.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "sparse patching masks to create clinically meaningful feature subsets"
  - [section 3.2] Masks defined via Hadamard product; parts as "meaningful combinations of clinical indicators."
  - [section 3.4] ℒ<sub>sparsity</sub> explicitly regularizes mask density.
- **Break condition:** Average activated features per mask >10 (non‑sparse) or domain expert review finds selected feature combinations clinically implausible.

### Mechanism 3: Prototype Learning with Real-Data Anchoring
- **Claim:** MEDIC learns *n* prototype vectors in embedding space, compares input parts to them via L2 distance, and finally replaces synthetic prototypes with embeddings from actual patient records for case‑based explanations.
- **Mechanism:** Each part embedding from the shared MLP is compared to all prototypes, producing a *p×n* distance matrix. Max‑pooling over parts selects the most similar part per prototype. During Stage 3 training, the closest real training part to each prototype is copied into the prototype memory, freezing them while fine‑tuning the classifier.
- **Core assumption:** Similarity to representative past cases (prototypes) is a valid and interpretable basis for medical decision support.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "learns prototypes from real patient records" and "case‑based reasoning"
  - [section 3.2] Prototype comparison and max‑pooling operation.
  - [section 4.2] Case study: prototypes like "Bilirubin∈[0.79,3.43) ∧ Hepatomegaly=0" align with clinical knowledge.
- **Break condition:** Prototypes do not cluster coherent patient subgroups (low intra‑prototype similarity) or replaced real parts are outliers (e.g., extreme feature values not representative).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Differentiable programming**
  - *Why needed here:* The fuzzy binning layer must be trainable via backpropagation; hard binning is non‑differentiable (argmin). Fuzzy binning’s soft assignments provide gradient flow.
  - *Quick check question:* "Why can’t we use hard binning from the start, and how does the Gaussian kernel in fuzzy binning enable optimization?"
- **Concept: Regularization for interpretability**
  - *Why needed here:* Without ℒ<sub>sparsity</sub>, patching masks become dense; without ℒ<sub>diversity</sub>, prototypes collapse into redundant clusters. Both are necessary for human‑understandable parts and diverse explanations.
  - *Quick check question:* "What happens if λ<sub>diversity</sub> = 0? How does the diversity penalty (Eq. 6) mathematically encourage prototype spread?"
- **Concept: Prototype anchoring**
  - *Why needed here:* Synthetic prototypes (randomly initialized) may not correspond to real patient patterns. Replacing them with actual data parts (Stage 3) grounds explanations in real cases, increasing trust.
  - *Quick check question:* "Why freeze prototypes during Stage 3 fine‑tuning? What risk does further training on prototypes pose?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Input layer:** Raw continuous/categorical features.
  2. **Binning module:** Fuzzy (Stage 1) or hard (Stage 2–3) discretization per continuous feature; one‑hot for categoricals.
  3. **Concatenation:** All binarized features into *d′*-dim vector.
  4. **Patching masks:** Matrix *M* (size *p×d′*) sparsifies input into *p* parts via Hadamard product.
  5. **Shared feature extractor:** Shallow MLP (ReLU) embeds each part into *h*-dim vector.
  6. **Prototype memory:** *n* learnable *h*-dim vectors.
  7. **Distance & pooling:** L2 distances between each part embedding and all prototypes; max‑pool over parts → *n*-dim distance vector.
  8. **Classification head:** Linear layer maps distances to class probabilities.
- **Critical path:** Binning → Masking → MLP embedding → L2 distance computation → Max‑pooling → Linear classifier. Discretization and masking are the interpretability‑critical stages; prototype replacement is post‑hoc (Stage 3).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Fuzzy vs. hard binning:** Fuzzy enables end‑to‑end training but final model uses hard bins for crisp, symbolic intervals.
  - **Sparsity λ:** Higher λ yields fewer active features per part (more interpretable) but may reduce predictive capacity. Target: 5–7 features/part.
  - **Prototype count:** More prototypes increase model capacity but risk redundancy. Paper shows self‑regularization (Table 3): max allowed > unique found, indicating reuse.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Binning: Learned intervals include non‑physiologic bounds (e.g., negative for copper) or overlap excessively with standard clinical ranges.
  - Masking: Average mask density >0.3 (many active features) after Stage 2.
  - Prototypes: Low diversity (pairwise distances near zero) or prototype parts not found in any training instance after Stage 3.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Binning sanity check:** After Stage 1, plot bin centers for key features (e.g., albumin, bilirubin). Verify they move toward clinically plausible thresholds (e.g., albumin ~3.5 g/dL).
  2. **Sparsity tuning:** Run Stage 2 with varying λ<sub>sparsity</sub>. Measure average activated features per mask. Select λ where mean ≤7 without >2% g‑mean drop vs. unregularized.
  3. **Prototype anchoring validation:** After Stage 3, for each prototype, retrieve the nearest training part. Manually inspect 5 random prototypes: do the feature‑value combinations co‑occur in real patients and make clinical sense?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can domain‑specific prior knowledge be incorporated into MEDIC's prototype learning process to further align the learned representations with established medical understanding?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the Conclusions (Section 5): "First, incorporating domain‑specific prior knowledge into prototype learning could further align representations with medical understanding."
- **Why unresolved:** The current MEDIC learns prototypes purely from data without leveraging existing medical knowledge, which could limit clinical validity and acceptance. Integrating prior knowledge is challenging due to the need to encode it in a differentiable manner within the prototype learning framework.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A study where MEDIC is extended to incorporate prior knowledge (e.g., via constraints on prototypes, informed initialization, or regularization from medical ontologies) and evaluated both in terms of predictive performance and expert assessment of prototype plausibility. Comparative experiments with and without such integration would quantify benefits.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can MEDIC be adapted to support dynamic prototype updates in response to symptom drift caused by evolving diseases or treatments?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the Conclusions: "Second, dynamic prototype adaptation would enable the model to update representations in response to symptom drift caused by evolving diseases or treatments [27]."
- **Why unresolved:** MEDIC prototypes are currently learned from a static training set; in real‑world clinical settings, disease presentations and treatment effects change over time (concept drift). The model lacks a mechanism to incrementally update prototypes without full retraining, which could degrade performance and explanation relevance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of an online or continual learning variant of MEDIC that can update prototypes from streaming data, followed by empirical evaluation on temporal medical datasets showing maintained performance and stable interpretability over time.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do physicians perceive the utility and trustworthiness of MEDIC's case‑based explanations compared to other XAI methods, and how can the explanations be optimized for clinical decision‑making?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the Conclusions: "Finally, rigorous user studies with physicians would provide insights into how this approach affects clinical decision‑making and how explanations could be optimized for maximum utility."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates technical interpretability but lacks empirical evidence of how medical professionals actually interact with and value the explanations. Without user evaluation, it is unclear whether the prototype‑based reasoning aligns with clinical workflows and improves decision confidence.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting controlled user studies with physicians, presenting MEDIC's predictions and explanations alongside other XAI techniques (like SHAP) across realistic clinical scenarios, measuring outcomes such as understanding, trust, agreement, and decision time.

## Limitations
- Omitted hyperparameter specifics (λ_sparsity, λ_diversity) and shared MLP architecture hinder faithful reproduction and independent validation.
- Interpretability claims rely on anecdotal case studies; no quantitative evaluation (e.g., expert review, fidelity metrics) or standardized interpretability benchmarks are provided.
- Risks include prototype collapse, overfitting (especially on the small Cirrhosis cohort), and failure of fuzzy binning to discover clinically valid thresholds if training data are biased; generalizability to external populations remains unverified.
