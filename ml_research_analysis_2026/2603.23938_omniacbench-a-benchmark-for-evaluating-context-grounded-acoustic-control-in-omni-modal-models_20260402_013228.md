# OmniACBench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Context-Grounded Acoustic Control in Omni-Modal Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23938
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23938
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduced OmniACBench; all eight evaluated models performed poorly relative to reference scores on context-grounded acoustic control tasks.

## Executive Summary
OmniACBench is a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the ability of omni-modal models to generate speech with acoustic features (e.g., rate, emotion) that are contextually grounded in both a spoken instruction and an accompanying image. The benchmark comprises 3,559 carefully constructed instances, each requiring a model to read a neutral text script aloud while matching six target acoustic features to cues from the multimodal context. Evaluation of eight state-of-the-art models reveals uniformly poor performance across both measurable (e.g., speech rate, pronunciation) and abstract (e.g., emotion, timbre) features, with all models falling far short of reference scores. Analysis identifies three primary failure modes: weak direct acoustic control, failed implicit inference from context, and failed multimodal grounding, highlighting the need for improved context integration in future omni-modal architectures.

## Method Summary
The benchmark was built using a three-stage pipeline. First, six acoustic features were selected based on their multimodal groundability (i.e., whether they can be visually cued) and evaluation diversity. Second, tri-modal instances were generated: a neutral text script via an LLM, a paraphrased spoken instruction specifying the target acoustic feature, and an image generated via meta-prompting to provide relevant visual cues. Third, rigorous quality control was applied, including LLM/human filtering for semantic preservation and text neutrality, checks for paraphrase diversity, speech fidelity (WER/CER/STOI), and image diversity (CLIP/LPIPS). The evaluation protocol uses eight omni-modal models that accept text, image, and speech inputs and generate speech outputs. Metrics include Word Error Rate (WER) for semantic fidelity, and feature-specific measures: ΔWPM for speech rate, Phoneme Error Rate (PER) for pronunciation, Voiced Frame Ratio (VFR@0.3) for phonation, and classification accuracy (via WavLM-based evaluators) for emotion, global accent, and timbre. Controlled ablations (e.g., Oracle, All-to-T, S-to-T, I-to-T) and linear probing analyses further diagnose failure modes.

## Key Results
- Semantic fidelity (WER) ranged 1.2–5.96% (reference 0.05%).
- Measurable features: speech rate (ΔWPM) −2.82 to 1.04, pronunciation PER 5.46–21.97%, phonation VFR@0.3 ≤1.69% (reference 96.78%).
- Abstract features: emotion accuracy 14.57–21.7% (random 16.7%), global accent 28.71–40.0% (33.3%), timbre 24.33–25.34% (25%).
- All models performed poorly; analysis revealed the main bottleneck is multimodal context integration, identifying three failure modes: weak direct control, failed implicit inference, and failed multimodal grounding.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Weak Direct Control
**Claim:** Models lack reliable direct acoustic control over certain attributes (e.g., timbre, phonation), failing to realize explicitly specified target values even without contextual inference.
**Mechanism:** The speech generation subsystem (TTS decoder or integrated token generator) does not receive or cannot act upon precise, low-level acoustic conditioning signals derived from the spoken instruction. The acoustic parameter space is not directly manipulable via the model's internal representation.
**Core assumption:** Acoustic control requires explicit, disentangled conditioning on target feature values at the generation stage.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "...identify three common failure modes—weak direct control, failed implicit inference, and failed multimodal grounding..."
- [section 5.2] "For some features, performance remains at chance level even under Oracle conditions... Timbre is the clearest example: all models remain near random across all conditions."
- [corpus] *Weak corpus evidence for this specific mechanism; neighboring papers (e.g., MGM-Omni) discuss decoupled "brain-mouth" designs but do not directly evidence direct control failure.*

### Mechanism 2: Failed Implicit Inference
**Claim:** Models fail at implicit acoustic inference—deducing the intended target acoustic value from multimodal context (image + spoken instruction) rather than from an explicit directive.
**Mechanism:** The multimodal context integration module (LLM backbone or Thinker) cannot reliably map visual scene semantics and spoken instruction semantics to a discrete, controlled acoustic target value. The inference from "situation in the given image" to "speech rate: fast" is not robustly learned.
**Core assumption:** Acoustic intent is implicit in the scene and must be inferred before control can be applied.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "...main bottleneck lies not in processing individual modalities, but in integrating multimodal context for faithful speech generation."
- [section 5.2] "A second failure mode occurs when models can execute acoustic control once the target value is explicitly provided, but fail when it must be inferred from context... Speech Rate of Qwen3-Omni 30B... improves substantially when given explicit instructions, yet remains close to random when the target acoustic value must be inferred from context."
- [corpus] *Corpus shows related work on multi-hop reasoning (OMHBench) and future forecasting (FutureOmni), suggesting implicit inference is a broader challenge, but not specific to acoustic grounding.*

### Mechanism 3: Failed Multimodal Grounding
**Claim:** Models fail at multimodal acoustic grounding—linking the acoustically inferred intent to the actual acoustic realization in speech generation, especially when cues are distributed across vision and speech.
**Mechanism:** There is a representational disconnect or information loss between the point where multimodal context is processed (e.g., LLM hidden states) and the speech generation module (Talker/TTS decoder). The acoustic intent does not survive the transition to the acoustic parameter space.
**Core assumption:** Successful contextual acoustic control requires a tight, decodable coupling between multimodal context representations and the speech generation latent space.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "...main bottleneck lies not in processing individual modalities, but in integrating multimodal context..."
- [section 5.3] "MiniCPM-o 4.5 retains context-relevant acoustic information throughout the LLM backbone and into the TTS decoder, whereas Qwen3-Omni 30B drops from high decodability in the Thinker to chance in the Talker."
- [section 5.2] "MiniCPM-o 4.5 exhibits this pattern for Speech Rate. Its performance remains relatively strong in the All-to-T condition... but drops in S-to-T and I-to-T."

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Multimodal Feature Groundability
  - *Why needed here:* The benchmark design (Section 3.1) requires selecting acoustic features that can be visually grounded. An engineer must assess whether a new target acoustic property (e.g., "breathiness") has natural, unambiguous visual correlates in images to create valid benchmark instances.
  - *Quick check question:* Can you list three image keywords for "speech rate: fast" that do not rely on text in the image and are culturally neutral?

- **Concept:** Controlled Synthetic Data Pipeline
  - *Why needed here:* OmniACBench is built via a deterministic, staged pipeline (Section 3.2). Understanding each stage (text script generation, instruction paraphrasing, image keyword expansion, image generation, TTS) and its quality filters (Section 3.3) is crucial for reproducibility and extending the benchmark.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the purpose of the "Text Neutrality" filter, and what artifact would it remove?

- **Concept:** Task-Specific Evaluator Training
  - *Why needed here:* For abstract features (Emotion, Global Accent, Timbre), off-the-shelf classifiers are inadequate (Appendix C). An engineer must train a reliable, label-space-matched evaluator (e.g., WavLM-based classifier) to score model outputs on the benchmark's specific taxonomy.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is an evaluator trained on GLOBE/EDACC data necessary for evaluating "Global Accent: Australia" instead of using a pre-trained accent classifier?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
1. **Input Encoders:** Separate processors for text script (LLM tokenizer), spoken instruction (audio encoder → semantic features), and image (vision encoder).
2. **Multimodal Fusion/LLM Backbone ("Thinker"):** Integrates three modalities into a shared representation. Responsible for semantic task understanding and acoustic intent inference.
3. **Acoustic Conditioning Interface:** The pathway that conveys the inferred/intended acoustic target (e.g., "emotion: angry") from the backbone to the speech generator. This could be explicit tokens, hidden state injection, or cross-attention.
4. **Speech Generator ("Talker"/TTS Decoder):** Autoregressive or flow-based module that synthesizes waveform conditioned on the text script *and* the acoustic conditioning signal. Must preserve semantic fidelity (WER) while modifying acoustic parameters.
5. **Task Controller:** The spoken instruction itself, which specifies *which* acoustic dimension to control (e.g., "use a phonation style...").

**Critical path:** `Spoken Instruction (Audio) + Image (Vision) + Text Script (Text)` → **Multimodal Fusion/LLM** → `Acoustic Target Inference` → **Acoustic Conditioning Interface** → `Speech Generator (Conditioned on Text + Acoustic Target)` → `Output Speech`.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **End-to-end vs. Cascaded:** End-to-end (MiniCPM-o) may preserve context better but is harder to train. Cascaded (ASR→LLM→TTS) risks losing intent at interfaces.
- **Explicit vs. Implicit Conditioning:** Using discrete acoustic control tokens (explicit) is more interpretable but limits granularity. Relying on the LLM's continuous latent space (implicit) is flexible but less controllable.
- **Single vs. Multi-Feature Control:** OmniACBench isolates single features. Extending to joint control (e.g., fast + angry) increases complexity exponentially and risks feature entanglement.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Signature 1 (Weak Direct Control):** Oracle condition performance near random for timbre/phonation. *Diagnosis:* Speech generator cannot manipulate the target acoustic parameter.
- **Signature 2 (Failed Implicit Inference):** Large gap between All-to-T and Oracle. *Diagnosis:* LLM backbone fails to map context (text description of image) to acoustic target.
- **Signature 3 (Failed Multimodal Grounding):** Performance drops from All-to-T to S-to-T or I-to-T. *Diagnosis:* Information loss when moving from textual context to actual speech/vision modalities; fusion module weak.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Isolate Speech Generation Fidelity:** Feed *only the text script* (no instruction, no image) to the model. Measure WER. Confirms baseline ability to read aloud.
2. **Test Explicit Control Capability (Oracle):** Replace the spoken instruction and image with a text instruction that explicitly states the target value (e.g., "Speak as fast as possible"). Measures maximum achievable control assuming perfect context inference.
3. **Probe Context Decodability:** Train a linear probe on the LLM backbone's hidden states (per layer) to predict the true target acoustic value from the multimodal input. A sharp drop in accuracy before the speech generator indicates where context information is lost (as in Section 5.3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can omni-modal models be improved to jointly control multiple acoustic features (e.g., fast speech rate and angry emotion) in a single generation, given that current models struggle even with single-feature control?
- **Basis in paper:** The Limitations section states: "speech generation may involve joint control of multiple acoustic features (e.g., a fast speech rate and angry emotion), whereas each instance in OmniACBench evaluates only a single target acoustic feature."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows that all models perform poorly on single-feature control across measurable and abstract features; compositional control would be even more complex without first addressing single-feature reliability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending OmniACBench with multi-feature instances and evaluating whether model performance scales linearly, degrades gracefully, or exhibits catastrophic interference would clarify the gap and motivate targeted architectural or training interventions.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How can non-speech audio cues (e.g., environmental sounds, background music) be integrated as contextual grounding for acoustic control, given that OmniACBench only uses spoken instructions as audio input?
- **Basis in paper:** The Limitations section notes: "OmniACBench focuses on spoken instructions as the audio input, excluding non-speech signals such as environmental sounds or background music that may also provide contextual cues for acoustic delivery."
- **Why unresolved:** Real-world scenarios often involve ambient audio that influences appropriate speech delivery (e.g., loud environment → raised voice); the current benchmark’s design omits this modality combination, leaving unknown how models handle such context.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Constructing an extended benchmark with non-speech audio inputs (e.g., crowd noise, music) and measuring model ability to infer and realize corresponding acoustic adjustments (e.g., volume, prosody) would reveal whether models can generalise beyond spoken instructions.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What architectural modifications enable better retention of multimodal context information up to the speech generation stage, given that models like MiniCPM-o preserve decodable context while others like Qwen3-Omni do not?
- **Basis in paper:** The context-flow analysis (Section 5.3) shows MiniCPM-o retains context-relevant acoustic information into the TTS decoder, whereas Qwen3-Omni loses it in the Talker; the paper suggests this points to "architectural advantage" but does not isolate the cause.
- **Why unresolved:** The probing compares two specific architectures but does not ablate specific design choices (e.g., hidden-state integration vs. decoupled Thinker–Talker); thus, the key factors driving better context retention remain unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled architectural experiments that vary connectivity (e.g., cross-attention, fusion modules) between multimodal encoders and speech decoders, measuring context decodability and final speech-generation performance, would identify which design patterns support effective context grounding.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Why is timbre control particularly unsuccessful across all models (near random accuracy), while other abstract features like emotion show some above-chance performance?
- **Basis in paper:** Table 2 shows timbre accuracy (24.33–25.34%) is at random baseline (25%) for all models, whereas emotion accuracy (14.57–21.7%) is slightly above random (16.7%) for some.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies timbre as a failure type (lack of direct control) but does not investigate why timbre is harder than emotion; possible reasons include insufficient training data for age/gender attributes, weaker visual grounding in images, or intrinsic difficulty of controlling voice characteristics via text-to-speech models.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Error analysis on timbre instances to identify systematic confusions (e.g., adult vs. elderly), testing whether visual cues are discriminative enough for humans/models, and probing whether TTS backends can condition on age/gender when explicitly instructed would pinpoint the bottleneck.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** Why do models fail at implicit acoustic inference from multimodal context (Failure Type II), even when they can execute explicit control (Oracle condition)?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 5.2 defines Failure Type II: "models can execute acoustic control once the target value is explicitly provided, but fail when it must be inferred from context," observed in Speech Rate for Qwen models and Global Accent/Phonation for MiniCPM-o.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates the phenomenon but does not diagnose whether the failure lies in multimodal fusion (e.g., aggregating visual and spoken instructions), in the inference process (e.g., lacking a mapping from context to acoustic values), or in training data lacking such implicit cues.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training analysis to check whether models see sufficient examples where acoustic targets are implicit; probing intermediate representations to see if visual and spoken-instruction features are properly aligned; and adding synthetic data where context explicitly implies the target to see if performance improves would isolate the root cause.

## Limitations
- **Data Pipeline Specifics:** Exact generation parameters, model versions, and filtering thresholds for the commercial LLMs/image generators/TTS models used to create OmniACBench are undisclosed, blocking perfect reproduction of the benchmark's data distribution and quality.
- **Probing Analysis Replicability:** Critical details for the linear probing experiments (exact layer definitions, full hyperparameters, split strategies) are incomplete, limiting the ability to validate the reported "information loss" signatures across models.
- **Evaluator Training:** While the abstract feature evaluators are specified, their exact training code, data splits, and final checkpoint performance are not public. This introduces variance in validating the reported accuracy scores for emotion, accent, and timbre.

## Confidence
- **Weak Direct Control (Timbre/Phonation):** High Confidence. The evidence is direct: Oracle condition scores for these features are consistently at/near chance (Section 5.2). The break condition is clearly defined and met.
- **Failed Implicit Inference:** Medium Confidence. Strong evidence from the Oracle vs. All-to-T performance gaps (Section 5.2). Confidence is reduced by the unknown of whether the bottleneck is in inference or in the conditioning interface transfer.
- **Failed Multimodal Grounding (S/I-to-T drop):** Medium Confidence. Supported by comparative performance drops (e.g., MiniCPM-o in Section 5.3) and the probing analysis showing decodability loss. Reduced by the unknown of whether the drop occurs in fusion or conditioning.
- **Benchmark Construction Validity:** High Confidence. The pipeline stages, quality filters (WER/CER/STOI, CLIP/LPIPS), and groundability criteria are explicitly detailed. The main risk is the undisclosed commercial model choices affecting subtle nuances.

## Next Checks
1. **Oracle Timbre/Phonation Control Test:** Feed the explicit text instruction (e.g., "speak with a dark, resonant timbre") directly to the speech generation module of target models (e.g., via a separate control token or prefix). Confirmation: Performance must rise far above random (~25% / ~1.69%) to disprove a "direct control" bottleneck.
2. **Text-Only Context Inference Probe:** Replicate the All-to-T condition but with a textual description of the image instead of the image itself. A minimal performance drop from All-to-T to this new condition would isolate failure to visual processing/integration, confirming the multimodal grounding signature.
3. **Acoustic Target Representation Analysis:** For a model like MiniCPM-o, extract the intermediate acoustic conditioning vector (from the Thinker output) during generation. Train a lightweight classifier to predict the true target acoustic value from this vector. High accuracy would confirm successful inference, placing the failure in the Talker's ability to use that signal.
