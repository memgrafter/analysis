# Framing Effects in Independent-Agent Large Language Models: A Cross-Family Behavioral Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.19282
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19282
- **Reference count:** 4
- **Primary result:** Linguistic framing significantly shifts LLM decisions in independent-agent threshold voting tasks, with framing effect magnitude (ΔP) up to 0.90 for Claude and only Grok showing non-significant effect (ΔP=0.18, p=0.070).

## Executive Summary
This study examines how prompt framing influences decisions in isolated large language model agents performing a threshold voting task with individual-group conflict. Two logically equivalent but differently framed prompts were tested across multiple LLM families. Results show that surface linguistic cues can override logical equivalence, shifting choice distributions and often increasing preference for risk-averse options. The framing effect magnitude (ΔP) was substantial and statistically significant for most families (e.g., ΔP = 0.90 for Claude, 0.89 for Llama; p < 0.001), with only Grok showing minimal sensitivity (ΔP = 0.18, p = 0.070). The findings indicate a tendency toward instrumental rationality over cooperative rationality in independent-agent settings.

## Method Summary
The study implemented an independent-agent protocol where each model instance executed single-turn calls with fresh sessions, no memory, and user-only prompts. Models from eight families (Claude, DeepSeek, GLM, GPT, Gemini, Grok, Llama, Qwen) were presented with two scenarios framing a threshold public goods dilemma: Scenario A emphasized individual survival certainty, while Scenario B emphasized collective survival conditionality. Each model performed 5 trials per scenario. Raw outputs were classified as Option A, Option B, or non-compliant (Category C). Family-level counts were aggregated to compute the probability of choosing B (P(B)) in each scenario and the framing effect ΔP = P(B)_B − P(B)_A. Statistical significance was assessed via Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. The design isolates the impact of linguistic framing by ensuring no inter-agent communication or contextual carryover.

## Key Results
- Framing effects were large and statistically significant (p < 0.001) for seven of eight families, with ΔP ranging from 0.65 to 0.90.
- The risk-averse Option A was chosen more frequently under the individually framed scenario (A), indicating sensitivity to narrative orientation.
- Grok was the only family without a statistically significant framing effect (ΔP = 0.18, p = 0.070).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Surface-level linguistic framing in a prompt can override the logical equivalence of the described decision problem, shifting LLM choice distributions.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs process the entire prompt as a holistic textual pattern rather than decomposing it into a formal logical structure. The narrative orientation (e.g., emphasis on individual survival vs. collective survival) activates different priors from training data (e.g., risk-averse stories, cooperative narratives), biasing the final token prediction toward one option.
- **Core assumption:** The model's next-token prediction objective, trained on human text containing embedded cognitive and narrative biases, causes it to associate certain phrasing patterns with preferred outcomes.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "surface linguistic cues can even override logically equivalent formulations."
  - [section 3.2] The two scenarios are formally verified as logically equivalent but differ in narrative framing.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Syntactic Framing Fragility" and "More or Less Wrong" corroborate that LLM judgments are sensitive to prompt phrasing, though this paper's specific task is novel.
- **Break condition:** If models consistently choose the same option when logical equivalence is explicitly stated and reinforced, or when the framing is removed entirely, the mechanism would be disproven.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The independent-agent (non-communicating) setting amplifies framing effects because each model instance must rely solely on its isolated interpretation of the prompt without corrective signals from other agents.
- **Mechanism:** Without communication, there is no opportunity for agents to align beliefs, negotiate meaning, or correct for individual biases. The decision becomes a pure reflection of the single prompt's framing as processed by the model's internal priors, magnifying any inherent sensitivity.
- **Core assumption:** In interactive multi-agent settings, communication can mitigate individual framing biases by allowing consensus-building or information pooling.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "In many real-world applications, large language models (LLMs) operate as independent agents without interaction, thereby limiting coordination."
  - [section 3.3] Describes the isolation protocol (memory isolation, single-turn, fresh session) designed to remove coordination possibilities.
  - [corpus] "When Wording Steers the Evaluation" discusses framing in LLM judges, but this paper's isolation constraint is a distinct, contributing factor.
- **Break condition:** If framing effects are equally strong or stronger in interactive multi-agent settings where communication is allowed, the amplification hypothesis for isolation would be falsified.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The magnitude of framing sensitivity (ΔP) is heterogeneous across LLM families, likely due to differences in alignment strategy (e.g., RLHF), training data composition, or architectural priors.
- **Mechanism:** Different fine-tuning objectives (e.g., helpfulness vs. safety) and base model corpora shape how strongly a model weights certain narrative frames. For instance, models heavily tuned for cooperative, helpful dialogue might be more swayed by "collective survival" framing.
- **Core assumption:** Family-level behavioral differences observed here are attributable to training and alignment variations rather than random variance, as all models face the same logical task.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [section 4.3] Table 2 shows ΔP ranging from 0.18 (Grok) to 0.90 (Claude), a substantial spread.
  - [section 5] "The heterogeneity in ΔP across LLM families underscored the need for family-specific behavioral profiling."
  - [corpus] "Magic Words or Methodical Work?" finds annotation results depend on model choice, supporting heterogeneity across families.
- **Break condition:** If all families, regardless of size or alignment method, show uniform ΔP values under this task, the inference about training/alignment causes would be wrong; the variation might then stem from uncontrolled prompt quirks or stochasticity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Framing Effects (Behavioral Economics)**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire study tests if a classic human cognitive bias—where logically equivalent choices are framed differently (gain vs. loss, individual vs. collective)—manifests in LLMs. Understanding prospect theory's core idea (evaluation relative to a reference point) is essential to interpret Scenario A (certain individual survival) vs. Scenario B (conditional collective survival).
  - *Quick check question:* In the classic "Asian disease" problem, why do more people choose a sure gain (200 saved) than a probabilistic gain (1/3 chance 600 saved), even when the outcomes are equivalent?

- **Concept: Independent-Agent Multi-Agent Systems**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper defines a specific deployment scenario (no communication, shared state) that contrasts with most multi-agent LLM research. This isolation is the key experimental manipulation to test framing amplification.
  - *Quick check question:* How would the expected outcome differ if the same voting task were run with agents that could exchange messages after seeing the prompt?

- **Concept: Threshold Public Goods & Collective-Risk Social Dilemmas**
  - *Why needed here:* The task structure is a formalization of these game theory concepts. The conflict between individual risk-aversion (choosing A guarantees survival if others choose A) and collective cooperation (choosing B risks death but enables all-survive if enough choose B) creates the dilemma where framing can tip the balance.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the key strategic tension in a game where a public good is provided only if at least θ% of players contribute, and individual contributors pay a cost?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Prompt Engine -> Isolation Enforcer -> Model Pool -> Sampling Controller -> Response Classifier -> Aggregator -> Stat Test Module

### Critical Path
For each (Family, Scenario) pair: Iterate all models in family → for N=5 trials: send isolated prompt → record raw output → classify → aggregate family counts → compute P(B) and ΔP → run statistical test.

### Design Tradeoffs
- N=5 trials per model: Minimizes cost/latency but limits statistical power, especially for families with few models.
- Family-level aggregation: Hides individual model variation to compare families, but masks potential outlier models within a family.
- Strict user-only prompt: Ensures framing is the only variable, but may not reflect real-world deployments where system prompts exist.

### Failure Signatures
- High Category C (>15%) in a family: indicates models are misunderstanding the task or refusing, invalidating choice shift interpretation.
- Non-significant Chi-square (p>0.05) with large |ΔP|: small N may cause false negatives; trust effect size but report caution.
- Consistent "B" responses in Scenario A across families: suggests framing failed or task logic was misunderstood.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replication sanity check:** Run the full protocol on one family (e.g., GPT) with N=10. Verify ΔP sign and approximate magnitude matches paper (expect ~0.65). Check for Category C explosions.
2. **Isolation validation:** For a single model, run 5 trials with *shared session* (allow history). Compare P(B) to isolated condition. Expect weaker framing effect if memory allows "reasoning" that overrides initial framing.
3. **Framing strength gradient:** Create a third scenario with an even more extreme risk-averse frame (e.g., "certain death if you choose B"). Test one family. Expect P(A) to approach 100% if framing mechanism is monotonic.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Does the independent-agent setting amplify framing effects compared to interactive multi-agent deployments?
  - *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The independent-agent amplification hypothesis (H1) is proposed but not directly tested against interactive conditions; future work calls for extending to interactive simulations.
  - *Why unresolved:* The study only measured framing effects in isolation, so the comparative amplification remains speculative without empirical contrast.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct controlled experiments with both independent and interactive agent groups using identical tasks and framings, statistically comparing framing effect magnitudes (Δ𝑝).

- **Question:** What factors account for the heterogeneity in framing sensitivity across LLM families?
  - *Basis in paper:* [inferred] Results show substantial variation in Δ𝑃 (e.g., Claude 0.90, Grok 0.18), but the observational design cannot identify causal mechanisms behind these differences.
  - *Why unresolved:* No analysis links framing effects to specific model attributes like alignment strategies, training data, or architecture.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Correlate Δ𝑃 with model metadata (e.g., RLHF usage, scale) or perform ablation studies manipulating alignment approaches.

- **Question:** Can structured coordination protocols mitigate framing-induced biases in independent-agent LLMs?
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] Future work explicitly proposes "investigating ways to combine framing cues with structured coordination protocols."
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper demonstrates persistent framing effects without communication; it is unknown whether protocols like shared prompts or ex-ante commitments can reduce bias.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement coordination mechanisms (e.g., embedded shared goals) in the threshold voting task and measure changes in choice distributions and Δ𝑃.

- **Question:** How generalizable are these framing effects to other decision contexts and linguistic framings?
  - *Basis in paper:* [inferred] Authors note that "results depend on the specific linguistic framings chosen" and the task is a stylized survival-based construct.
  - *Why unresolved:* Only one task with two framings was tested, limiting external validity to other scenarios.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate the study across multiple tasks (e.g., varied public goods dilemmas) and diverse framings to assess consistency of framing effect magnitudes.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction unknowns:** Exact prompt wording, precise model identifiers per family, and implementation details for "memory isolation" (API parameters) are unspecified; Category C classification rules are ambiguous.
- **Statistical power:** N=5 trials per model may yield low power; non-significant p‑values with large ΔP could be false negatives.
- **External validity:** Findings are restricted to single‑turn, user‑only prompts; effects may differ with system prompts or multi‑turn dialogue.
