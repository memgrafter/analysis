# CADO: From Imitation to Cost Minimization for Heatmap-based Solvers in Combinatorial Optimization
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08210
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08210
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: CADO reduces the optimality gap on TSP-1k to 0.88% (from 1.86%) and achieves a 2.78% drop on MIS-ER without expensive post-processing like 2-opt.

## Executive Summary
Combinatorial optimization (CO) solvers that generate heatmaps via diffusion models are typically trained with supervised learning to imitate optimal solutions. However, this leads to Decoder-Blindness and Cost-Blindness: minimizing imitation loss does not guarantee low cost after decoding the heatmap. CADO introduces a reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning framework that directly optimizes the true post-decoded solution cost. It reformulates the diffusion denoising process as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), applies REINFORCE with a novel Label-Centered Reward that uses ground-truth costs as unbiased baselines, and employs Hybrid Fine-Tuning (combining LoRA and selective full parameter updates) for stable adaptation. Across TSP (100–10,000 nodes) and MIS benchmarks, CADO achieves state-of-the-art results: on TSP-1k, the optimality gap drops to 0.88% from 1.86% (DIFUSCO); on MIS-ER, it attains a 2.78% drop versus 18.53% (DIFUSCO) and 9.52% (FastT2T). Even without expensive search heuristics like 2-opt, CADO outperforms fully equipped baselines. It also generalizes to real-world TSPLIB instances and remains robust under low-quality training data, underscoring the importance of objective alignment for heatmap-based CO.

## Method Summary
CADO starts from a pre-trained supervised diffusion model (e.g., DIFUSCO) that predicts heatmaps for CO problems. Instead of training on imitation loss, CADO wraps the diffusion sampling loop in an MDP: each denoising step is an action, and the final decoded solution’s cost serves as the sole reward (negative cost). Policy gradients are estimated with REINFORCE. To reduce variance, the reward can be centered by subtracting the ground-truth solution cost (Label-Centered Reward), yielding an unbiased estimator. For efficient fine-tuning, CADO applies Hybrid Fine-Tuning: low-rank adaptations (LoRA) are added to the input layer and the first 11 GNN layers, while the final GNN layer and output head are fully fine-tuned. Training uses a batch size of 64, learning rate 1e-5, and a limited number of denoising steps (e.g., 20 for CADO-L). This approach aligns the training objective directly with the true cost while preserving pre-trained features and ensuring stability.

## Key Results
- **TSP**: On TSP-1k, CADO reduces the optimality gap to 0.88% (from 1.86% by DIFUSCO) and scales to 10k nodes with a gap of 1.52%. Even without 2-opt, CADO-L beats baselines that use such heuristics.
- **MIS**: On MIS-ER (800 nodes), CADO achieves a 2.78% drop, dramatically lower than DIFUSCO’s 18.53% and FastT2T’s 9.52%. On SATLIB, it also substantially improves.
- **Generalization & robustness**: CADO generalizes to real-world TSPLIB instances and remains effective when trained on suboptimal labels (e.g., LKH tours yield 1.36% drop on TSP-1k), demonstrating the robustness of the Label-Centered Reward.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CADO addresses the Decoder-Blindness and Cost-Blindness problems through three interconnected mechanisms.

First, it formulates the diffusion denoising process as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The state comprises the noisy heatmap and instance representation, actions correspond to denoised heatmaps at each timestep, transitions follow the fixed diffusion forward process, and the reward is defined only at the terminal state (t=0) as the negative post-decoded solution cost. This explicit MDP framing ensures that the policy gradient signal directly targets the true cost, bypassing the non-differentiable decoder and eliminating the imitation-cost gap.

Second, to stabilize RL training without sacrificing unbiasedness, CADO introduces Label-Centered Reward (LCR). Instead of using the ground-truth solution as an imitation target, LCR subtracts the instance-specific optimal (or best-known) solution cost from the incurred cost, yielding a reward of R = -(c_g(f_g(x0)) - b_D(g)). This instance-specific baseline cancels out in the policy gradient, providing an unbiased estimator for expected cost while significantly reducing variance. LCR remains effective even when the baseline is suboptimal, as demonstrated with heuristic labels.

Third, CADO employs Hybrid Fine-Tuning: LoRA (low-rank adaptation) is applied to the input layer and the first 11 GNN layers, while the final GNN layer and output head undergo full fine-tuning. This combination preserves the robust features learned during pre-training, reduces memory overhead, and still grants sufficient expressive power to the most cost-sensitive layers. Ablations confirm that Hybrid-FT converges faster and more stably than full fine-tuning, LoRA alone, or selective full tuning of intermediate layers.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: The system comprises: Problem Instance Encoder (anisotropic GNN backbone) → Diffusion Process Controller (manages timestep t and sinusoidal positional features) → Heatmap Prediction Head (node/edge classification/regression) → Decoder f_g (e.g., greedy nearest neighbor for TSP, greedy for MIS) → Cost Calculator c_g (tour length or negative independent set size) → RL Fine-Tuning Module (REINFORCE, reward computation, Hybrid-FT updates). The pre-trained supervised model includes the encoder and prediction head; the RL module wraps the sampling loop and updates parameters via policy gradients.

**Critical path**: Starting from a pre-trained checkpoint, for each episode: (1) sample pure noise x_T; (2) for t = T down to 1: predict x_0 from x_t using the model; compute posterior q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0) (Eq. 16) and sample x_{t-1}; (3) after reaching t=0, decode solution from x_0 via f_g; (4) compute cost c_g; (5) compute reward R (either Standard Reward or LCR); (6) accumulate policy gradient; (7) update model parameters using Hybrid-FT (LoRA on early layers, full FT on last layers). Training uses batch size 64, learning rate 1e-5, and typically 10–20 denoising steps.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Hybrid-FT vs. Full-FT*: Full fine-tuning risks catastrophic forgetting and instability, while LoRA alone may lack capacity to adapt the final layers sufficiently to the cost objective.
- *Standard Reward (SR) vs. Label-Centered Reward (LCR)*: SR is label-free and robust to poor baselines but yields higher gradient variance; LCR leverages ground-truth costs to reduce variance while staying unbiased, even when baselines are suboptimal.
- *Number of denoising steps*: More steps (e.g., T=50) allow finer refinement but lengthen RL trajectories; CADO-L uses fewer steps (T=20) for efficiency, relying on a learned prior.

**Failure signatures**:
- *Training instability*: Exploding policy gradient norms or rapidly increasing SL loss alongside worsening drop indicate divergence; remedy by lowering the learning rate or increasing reward normalization batch size.
- *Decoder mismatch*: Degenerate or over-smoothed heatmaps after RL fine-tuning suggest the decoder used during training differs from that at test time; ensure consistency.
- *Poor generalization*: Significant performance drop on out-of-distribution instances (e.g., TSPLIB) suggests overfitting; mitigate by increasing RL episode diversity.
- *Memory/compute bottlenecks*: RL fine-tuning may become prohibitively expensive for very large instances; monitoring required.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. *Pre-training validation*: Verify the baseline SL model’s heatmap quality by decoding multiple samples per instance and measuring average drop relative to optimal.
2. *SL vs. RL objective alignment*: Run a few RL epochs, plot both SL loss and drop versus epoch to confirm that increasing RL training reduces drop even if SL loss rises. Compare SR and LCR.
3. *Hybrid-FT ablation*: On a small-scale task (TSP-100), compare Full-FT, LoRA-FT, Selective-FT, and Hybrid-FT in terms of training stability (loss curves) and final optimality gap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**1. Theoretical conditions for Decoder- and Cost-Blindness**  
The paper empirically demonstrates that supervised learning heatmap solvers suffer from a gap between imitation loss and final cost, but it does not provide a general theoretical characterization. Without such conditions, it is unclear for which combinatorial problems RL fine-tuning is necessary. Resolving this would require deriving analytical relationships between the solution space topology, cost function properties, and decoder behavior, and validating them across a diverse set of CO tasks.

**2. Scalability to extremely large-scale problems**  
Experiments cover up to TSP-10k and MIS-ER with 800 nodes. The computational and memory demands of diffusion models and RL fine-tuning at scales of millions of nodes remain unknown. Evidence needed: benchmarks on progressively larger instances (e.g., TSP-50k, 100k) measuring training time, memory usage, solution quality, and convergence stability.

**3. Robustness of Label-Centered Reward with poor labels**  
LCR assumes ground-truth baselines are feasible and independent of the policy. In practice, labels may be highly suboptimal, noisy, or drawn from a mismatched distribution. The paper shows LCR works with LKH tours but only over a limited quality range. To identify failure thresholds, systematic corruption experiments (e.g., random edge flips, biased heuristics, cross-distribution labels) should be performed, comparing LCR against Standard Reward.

## Limitations
- The MDP formulation uses a terminal reward only; potential benefits of intermediate rewards are unexplored.
- Label-Centered Reward requires instance-specific baseline costs; performance may degrade if labels are missing or extremely poor.
- Hybrid Fine-Tuning’s layer selection is tailored to DIFUSCO’s GNN; its effectiveness for other architectures (e.g., transformers) is unverified.

## Next Checks
1. **Decoder robustness test**: After CADO training, evaluate the learned heatmaps using multiple decoding strategies (greedy, beam search, 2-opt) to confirm that improvements are intrinsic to the heatmaps and not decoder-specific.
2. **Cross-architecture study**: Apply CADO’s RL fine-tuning and LCR to a transformer-based heatmap solver (e.g., T2T) to assess whether Hybrid-FT and LCR generalize beyond GNNs.
3. **Label quality stress test**: Intentionally degrade the baseline costs used in LCR (e.g., add noise, use heuristic solutions) across a range of degradation levels to measure performance sensitivity and pinpoint failure points of LCR.
