# Where Relevance Emerges: A Layer-Wise Study of Internal Attention for Zero-Shot Re-Ranking
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22591  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22591  
- Reference count: 28  
- Primary result: Discovered a universal bell-curve distribution of relevance signals across transformer layers; Selective-ICR (using only peak-centered layers) reduces inference latency by 30%-50% without loss in effectiveness; on reasoning-intensive BRIGHT, a 0.6B model outperforms RankGPT-4 and an 8B model matches a 14B reinforcement-learned reranker.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how internal attention signals in decoder-only transformers can be harnessed for zero-shot document re-ranking (In-Context Re-ranking, ICR). Through a systematic layer-wise analysis, the authors reveal that relevance signals follow a consistent bell-curve pattern: middle layers capture deep semantic relevance most effectively, while early and late layers contribute less or even noise. Building on this insight, they propose Selective-ICR, which restricts attention aggregation to a small window of layers centered on the empirically identified peak. This strategy reduces inference latency by 30-50% compared to using all layers, while maintaining or improving ranking quality on the BEIR benchmark. On the reasoning-focused BRIGHT benchmark, isolating the optimal layer (Oracle Peak-ICR) unlocks substantial latent capability: a 0.6B model surpasses state-of-the-art generative rerankers like RankGPT-4, and an 8B model matches a 14B reinforcement-learned reranker.

## Method Summary
The base ICR method concatenates a query and candidate documents into a single prompt processed by a decoder-only LLM. Two forward passes are performed: one with the actual query and one with a content-free “N/A” query to calibrate away inherent model biases. For each layer, attention weights from query tokens to document tokens are extracted, summed per document, and then calibrated by subtracting the “N/A” scores. The final document score aggregates these values across selected layers, and documents are ranked accordingly. Selective-ICR modifies this pipeline by limiting aggregation to a fixed-size interval (e.g., 4 layers) centered on the layer that achieved peak performance in a pilot study (e.g., on TREC‑DL). This early termination of the forward pass reduces computational cost dramatically while retaining the high-signal layers.

## Key Results
- **Bell-curve distribution**: Across multiple models (Llama, Mistral, Qwen) on BEIR, relevance signals peak in middle layers; early layers focus on syntax, late layers on next‑token prediction.
- **Selective-ICR efficiency**: On BEIR, a 4‑layer window cuts latency by 30‑50% and matches or exceeds all‑layer ICR in nDCG@10 for all tested models.
- **Reasoning‑intensive BRIGHT**: Oracle Peak-ICR (per‑dataset best layer) yields large gains; e.g., Llama 3.1 8B improves nDCG@10 from 0.1750 (All‑ICR) to 0.2092, surpassing the 14B Rank‑R1 (0.2050) and RankGPT‑4 (0.1700). A tiny 0.6B model achieves 0.1858, exceeding RankGPT‑4.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Non‑Uniform Distribution of Relevance Signals
- **Claim**: Relevance signals in decoder‑only transformers for document re‑ranking are distributed non‑uniformly across layers, forming a consistent “bell‑curve” with peak effectiveness in intermediate layers.
- **Mechanism**: Early layers primarily encode surface‑level syntactic and lexical features, while intermediate layers develop deeper semantic representations necessary for assessing query‑document relevance. Final layers are often specialized for next‑token prediction, introducing noise for the re‑ranking task. Information pertinent to semantic relevance thus propagates most effectively through the model’s “core” layers.
- **Core assumption**: The internal attention patterns over the full context (query + documents) reflect the model’s assessment of document relevance, and this assessment evolves in a predictable manner as representations deepen.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “identify a universal ‘bell‑curve’ distribution of relevance signals across transformer layers”; [section 2.2] “the ranking signal exhibits a ‘bell‑curve’ trend… middle layers near the center capture deep semantic relevance more effectively”; [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., “ReAttn”, “Contrastive Retrieval Heads”) also study layer‑wise effectiveness, supporting non‑uniform contributions, though the specific bell‑curve pattern is a novel contribution of this paper.
- **Break condition**: The pattern breaks for tasks with extreme syntactic/structural demands (e.g., the “Pony” programming language retrieval task in BRIGHT), where relevance signals shift toward earlier layers designed for formal syntax parsing.

### Mechanism 2: Selective Aggregation Mitigates Signal Dilution
- **Claim**: Selective aggregation of only the peak‑centered layers (Selective‑ICR) improves ranking effectiveness and reduces latency by mitigating “signal dilution” from non‑informative boundary layers.
- **Mechanism**: By truncating the forward pass to a small window (e.g., 4 layers) centered on the empirically identified peak‑performance layer, the method removes contributions from early (lexical noise) and late (task‑specific output signals) layers. This concentrates the relevance signal while cutting the linear compute cost associated with executing all transformer layers.
- **Core assumption**: A compact, high‑signal layer interval can robustly capture the core semantic relevance representation without needing the full model depth. The peak layer’s neighborhood provides stability against minor dataset shifts.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “Selective‑ICR strategy that reduces inference latency by 30%–50% without compromising effectiveness”; [section 2.3] “Center‑Biased Interval Selection… preserves peak‑centered relevance signals while mitigating dilution from low‑informative boundary layers”; [corpus] Evidence is indirect; adjacent work (e.g., “ReAttn”) focuses on re‑weighting attention *within* all layers, not truncating layers. The citation for the original ICR [1] provides the baseline against which dilution is measured.
- **Break condition**: For very small models (e.g., Qwen3 0.6B), the optimal interval may not generalize across diverse datasets, as the signal distribution becomes highly task‑dependent and a static window fails to capture shifting peaks.

### Mechanism 3: Optimal Layer Isolation Reveals Latent Reasoning Capability
- **Claim**: Isolating the optimal layer for internal attention scoring (Oracle Peak‑ICR) reveals substantial latent capability, allowing a small zero‑shot model to match or surpass much larger or reinforcement‑learning‑trained generative rerankers on reasoning‑intensive tasks.
- **Mechanism**: The peak intermediate layers encode a rich, compressed representation of query‑document semantic alignment that is particularly effective for tasks requiring multi‑step reasoning. Generative methods (requiring text decoding) and full‑layer aggregation (suffering from dilution) fail to access this high‑fidelity signal efficiently. Directly extracting attention from the optimal layer provides a concentrated, noise‑free relevance score.
- **Core assumption**: The quality of the internal attention signal for complex reasoning is fundamentally bounded by the model’s representational capacity in its most informative layers, not by its total parameter count or explicit output fine‑tuning.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “a zero‑shot 8B model matches the performance of 14B reinforcement‑learned re‑rankers… even a 0.6B model outperforms… RankGPT‑4”; [section 4.3] “Llama 3.1 8B improves from 0.1750 to 0.2092… achieving the highest overall average and surpasses competitive reasoning‑focused baselines, including the largest 14B Rank‑R1 model”; [corpus] The BRIGHT benchmark [16] is cited as the reasoning‑intensive testbed; competitive baselines like Rank‑R1 and RankGPT‑4 are explicitly compared against.
- **Break condition**: The advantage diminishes or reverses if the optimal layer cannot be identified (e.g., using a non‑adaptive window on a model where peaks vary wildly per task) or if the task’s reasoning is so syntax‑bound that the optimal signal lies in early layers (as with “Pony”).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Transformer Attention as a Relevance Signal**  
  *Why needed here*: In‑Context Re‑ranking (ICR) is predicated on the idea that attention weights between query and document tokens indicate the model’s focus and thus relevance. Understanding that attention is a distribution (sums to 1 per query token) and the need for calibration (via a content‑free “N/A” query) is fundamental.  
  *Quick check question*: Why is the “N/A” calibration pass necessary, and what specific bias does it remove?
- **Concept: Layer‑wise Representation Analysis**  
  *Why needed here*: The paper’s core discovery—the bell‑curve—relies on decomposing model behavior by layer. One must understand that transformer layers sequentially transform representations, and their functional roles (syntax vs. semantics vs. output) can differ.  
  *Quick check question*: What is the hypothesized role of the model’s middle layers versus its final layers in the context of a re‑ranking task?
- **Concept: Ranking Frameworks (Listwise vs. Setwise)**  
  *Why needed here*: The paper compares ICR’s efficiency within different iterative ranking algorithms. Listwise processes all candidates at once; Setwise breaks ranking into comparisons. This context defines where ICR’s O(1) advantage is practical.  
  *Quick check question*: Why does the paper find Internal Attention less suitable for Setwise (e.g., bubblesort) despite its O(1) theoretical complexity?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input Processor -> Model Executor -> Scoring Engine -> Ranker  
**Critical path**:  
1. **Pilot Study**: Run layer‑wise ablation on a small TREC‑DL set → identify peak layer `l_peak` → define interval `I` using Center‑Biased constraint.  
2. **Validation**: Apply `Selective‑ICR` with fixed `I` across full BEIR → confirm effectiveness/efficiency gains.  
3. **Stress Test**: Evaluate `Oracle Peak‑ICR` (per‑dataset best layer) on BRIGHT → quantify latent potential.  
**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **Window Size (`w`)**: Larger `w` (more layers) increases robustness to peak shifts but adds latency and potential noise. Paper uses `w=4` (peak + 3 neighbors) for BEIR, `w=6` for decoupling study.  
- **Calibration**: Dual‑pass (“N/A” query) is crucial for effectiveness but doubles forward pass cost. Single‑pass (“w/o Calibration”) is faster but performs substantially worse.  
- **Interval Selection**: Static interval (from TREC‑DL) is efficient but may not generalize to all tasks (fails for 0.6B on BEIR). Dynamic, dataset‑specific peak selection (Oracle) is optimal but requires a validation set.  
**Failure signatures**:  
- **Signal Dilution**: All‑layer aggregation underperforms a single peak layer, especially in deeper models (observed in BEIR for Qwen3 4B/8B, BRIGHT).  
- **Context Limit Sensitivity**: Performance drops in sliding‑window listwise as global context is lost, exposing ICR’s reliance on full‑set attention.  
- **Small Model Fragility**: Static Selective‑ICR interval harms tiny models (0.6B) on diverse BEIR, indicating their signal distribution is less stable.  
- **Reasoning Task Mismatch**: All‑ICR underperforms BM25 on BRIGHT for larger Qwen3 models due to severe dilution; only Oracle Peak‑ICR unlocks potential.  
**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Layer‑wise nDCG Profiling**: On a pilot set (TREC‑DL), compute nDCG@10 for every individual layer of target models. Plot to verify the bell‑curve and identify `l_peak` for each architecture.  
2. **Selective‑ICR vs. All‑ICR Efficiency‑Effectiveness**: On BEIR, for each model, run with `All‑ICR`, `Selective‑ICR` (using `I` from Exp 1), and `Peak‑ICR` (`{l_peak}`). Measure (a) nDCG@10 average, (b) forward pass latency reduction.  
3. **Calibration Ablation**: Run `Selective‑ICR` with and without the “N/A” calibration pass on a subset (e.g., Covid, NFCorpus) to quantify the effectiveness cost of removing the dual‑pass overhead.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1  
- **Question**: Can alternative layer‑selection strategies (e.g., symmetric windows, non‑consecutive layers, or adaptive top‑*n* aggregation) outperform the proposed Center‑Biased Interval Selection in terms of robustness and cross‑dataset generalizability, especially for smaller models?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The conclusion section explicitly lists future work directions including “symmetric windows, non‑consecutive layer selection based on relevance thresholds, or adaptive top‑*n* aggregation.”  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper only evaluates a fixed window of 4 layers anchored at a peak, noting that the smallest model (Qwen3 0.6B) showed performance decline with this static interval on BEIR, suggesting the approach may not generalize optimally.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A systematic comparison of multiple selection strategies across diverse model scales (especially <1B parameters) and domains, measuring both effectiveness (nDCG@10) and robustness (variance across datasets).

### Open Question 2  
- **Question**: How can the computational overhead of the dual‑pass calibration in ICR be eliminated or significantly reduced without compromising the quality of the relevance signals, particularly for setwise ranking frameworks?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The ablation study in Table 3 (Selective‑ICR & w/o Calibration) shows a 50.7% latency reduction for heapsort but with noticeable performance drops. The text identifies the dual‑pass requirement as “the primary computational bottleneck.”  
- **Why unresolved**: The calibration step (query vs. “N/A” prompt) remains essential for mitigating intrinsic model bias, but its cost is prohibitive for iterative setwise comparisons. No alternative is proposed.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development and evaluation of a single‑pass calibration method (e.g., learned bias parameters, synthetic null prompts, or architectural modifications) that preserves effectiveness while matching the speed of the uncalibrated variant.

### Open Question 3  
- **Question**: Why does the universal “bell‑curve” distribution of relevance signals deviate in specific reasoning‑intensive tasks (e.g., the Pony domain) or for ultra‑lightweight models, and what architectural or data factors explain these deviations?  
- **Basis in paper**: [exerted] While the paper states the bell‑curve is “consistent” across BEIR and BRIGHT, Figures 4 and 5 show Pony shifting to early layers, and Section 4.4 notes Qwen3 0.6B’s suboptimal Selective‑ICR performance. The explanation offered is speculative (“structural and syntactic parsing” for Pony; “signal dilution” for small models).  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not provide a mechanistic analysis (e.g., attention head contributions, training data distribution, or model depth effects) to explain why the signal distribution changes.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An ablation study isolating factors like model depth, attention head types (e.g., QRHeads), or task syntax/semantic complexity, correlating these with shifts in the peak layer position.

### Open Question 4  
- **Question**: To what extent can a unified framework that jointly optimizes layer selection and attention head selection (e.g., integrating Query‑Focused Retrieval Heads) further improve ICR’s effectiveness and efficiency, particularly for reasoning‑intensive retrieval?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The future work section cites recent work on “Query‑Focused Retrieval Heads (QRHeads)” and suggests “a unified framework that jointly selects informative layers and attention heads may…fully exploit internal attention signals.”  
- **Why unresolved**: The current Selective‑ICR strategy operates only at the layer level and does not consider head‑level variation, which prior work suggests contains additional signal. The interaction between layer and head selection remains unexplored.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implementing and evaluating a framework that ranks heads by relevance signal quality (e.g., via attention entropy or query‑document affinity) and aggregates only top heads within the selected layers, testing it on BRIGHT and other benchmarks.

## Limitations
- The paper does not disclose exact prompt templates for the “N/A” calibration or generation baselines, precise tokenization/truncation rules, nor full hyperparameters for Setwise/Listwise experiments, hindering full reproduction.
- Static Selective‑ICR interval selection may fail for very small models or highly syntax‑bound tasks (e.g., Pony retrieval), limiting generalizability.
- Dual‑pass calibration doubles inference cost; its cost‑effectiveness tradeoff remains a bottleneck, especially for setwise ranking.
