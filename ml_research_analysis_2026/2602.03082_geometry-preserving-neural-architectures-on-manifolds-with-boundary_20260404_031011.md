# Geometry-Preserving Neural Architectures on Manifolds with Boundary

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03082
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03082
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introduces a unified framework for geometry‑preserving neural architectures that guarantee constraint satisfaction by construction via interleaved geometric updates.

## Executive Summary
Neural networks often violate hard geometric constraints, such as predictions that must lie on a manifold with or without boundary. This paper proposes a unified framework based on Intermediate Augmented Architectures (IAA) and Final Augmented Architectures (FAA). IAA interleaves standard layers with geometric updates—either explicit projections onto the constraint set or intrinsic exponential‑map updates—ensuring all intermediate representations satisfy the constraint. FAA applies a geometric transformation only at the output, offering a cheaper alternative. When analytic projections are unavailable, they are learned via flow matching, inspired by diffusion models. Theoretical universal approximation guarantees are provided for both IAA and FAA under prox‑regularity assumptions. Empirical evaluations on spheres, rotation groups, a disk, and a protein dataset show that projected IAA achieves very low test loss and near‑zero distance to the manifold, effectively eliminating constraint violations. FAA often matches IAA’s accuracy with lower computational cost, while learned flow projections approximate analytic ones near the manifold but degrade with increased noise.

## Method Summary
The core method modifies standard neural networks by inserting geometric update steps. In IAA, after each standard layer (e.g., linear + activation), the activations are mapped onto the constraint manifold using either an orthogonal projection (if available) or an exponential map derived from the manifold’s Riemannian geometry. This ensures that all hidden states remain feasible. FAA omits these intermediate updates and only applies the geometric map to the final output, reducing computational overhead. For manifolds where analytic projections are intractable, the authors learn a projection operator via flow matching: a neural network is trained to generate a velocity field that, when integrated, reproduces the projection dynamics. This approach leverages diffusion model principles to approximate the projection without requiring explicit formulas.

## Key Results
- Universal approximation theorems for IAA (under bounded vector fields and uniform prox‑regularity) and FAA (under additional reach condition) guarantee that the architectures can represent any continuous function into the manifold.
- Empirically, projected IAA achieves near‑zero constraint violations (e.g., mean distance ~8e‑7 on SO(3)) and low test loss (e.g., MSE ~2.22e‑1 on SO(3)), outperforming exponential updates and standard unconstrained networks.
- Learned flow‑matching projections converge to analytic ones at an O(√t) rate near the manifold but exhibit increased distance‑to‑manifold as noise grows, indicating sensitivity to distributional shift.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism relies on enforcing feasibility at the level of activations (IAA) or output (FAA). By applying a projection or exponential map, the network is forced to produce values that lie exactly on the constraint manifold. This geometric update acts as a hard constraint that cannot be violated by gradient‑based optimization, because the projection is idempotent and the exponential map is a diffeomorphism onto the manifold. The universal approximation proofs show that, under suitable geometric conditions (prox‑regularity) and with bounded vector fields, the interleaved architecture can approximate any continuous target function arbitrarily well. For FAA, the proof requires that the unconstrained network’s outputs stay within a tubular neighborhood of the manifold, ensuring that the final geometric map can correct them. Thus, constraint satisfaction is guaranteed by construction.

## Foundational Learning
- **Manifold**: A topological space that locally resembles Euclidean space; needed because the constraint set is often a manifold (e.g., sphere, rotation group).  
  *Why needed*: The entire framework assumes predictions must lie on a manifold with or without boundary.  
  *Quick check*: What are the defining properties of a smooth manifold?
- **Projection onto a manifold**: The mapping that sends a point to the closest point on the manifold (in some metric); used to enforce constraints by correcting off‑manifold activations.  
  *Why needed*: Provides an explicit way to map any point back onto the constraint set.  
  *Quick check*: How do you compute the orthogonal projection onto the unit sphere Sⁿ?
- **Exponential map**: A map from the tangent bundle to the manifold defined via geodesics; provides an intrinsic way to move along the manifold without leaving it.  
  *Why needed*: Offers a coordinate‑free alternative to projection that respects the manifold’s geometry.  
  *Quick check*: What is the exponential map on the circle S¹?
- **Prox‑regularity**: A condition on a set that ensures the projection operator is single‑valued and Lipschitz near the set; crucial for the universal approximation proof.  
  *Why needed*: Guarantees well‑behaved geometric updates and stable optimization.  
  *Quick check*: Does a convex set satisfy prox‑regularity?
- **Flow matching**: A method to learn a vector field whose flow approximates a target transport (e.g., projection); used when analytic projection is unavailable.  
  *Why needed*: Allows the architecture to incorporate geometric constraints even when no closed‑form projection exists.  
  *Quick check*: What loss function is typically minimized in flow matching?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
- IAA: `Input -> [Standard Layer -> Geometric Update (Projection/Exponential)] * L -> Output`  
- FAA: `Input -> [Standard Layer] * L -> Geometric Update -> Output`

**Critical path**  
- IAA: The geometric update after each layer is essential; omitting it breaks constraint enforcement.  
- FAA: The final geometric update is the sole constraint enforcement point; preceding layers must produce outputs that remain within a reach tube of the manifold.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- IAA vs FAA: IAA provides stronger intermediate guarantees but incurs higher computational cost due to repeated geometric operations; FAA is cheaper but may produce intermediate off‑manifold states and requires the unconstrained network to stay within a tubular neighborhood.  
- Projection vs Exponential: Projection (e.g., orthogonal) is explicit and often computationally heavier but guarantees minimal distance; exponential map is intrinsic and may be cheaper but can accumulate discretization errors and may not be a true projection.

**Failure signatures**  
- IAA: Intermediate activations drift off the manifold (detected by monitoring distance to manifold at each layer).  
- FAA: Final projection causes large jumps or distortions, indicating the unconstrained network’s output is far from the manifold; may also see poor accuracy if the network cannot learn to keep outputs near the manifold.  
- Learned flow projections: Constraint violations persist after integration, or the learned velocity field produces non‑manifold points even after many steps.

**First experiments**  
1. **Baseline comparison on S²**: Implement IAA with orthogonal projection and FAA with projection; train on a simple regression task; verify that IAA maintains near‑zero distance at all layers while FAA only at output; compare test MSE.  
2. **Flow matching vs analytic projection**: On a manifold where analytic projection is known (e.g., sphere), train a flow‑matching model to approximate the projection; evaluate distance‑to‑manifold after projection under varying levels of input noise; check convergence rate O(√t).  
3. **Manifold with boundary test**: Apply IAA and FAA to the disk (2D manifold with boundary); use projection onto the disk (clipping); verify that predictions respect the boundary and compare accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Why do flow‑matching learned projections degrade relative to analytic projections, even though Theorem 2.5 guarantees O(√t) convergence near the manifold?
- **Basis**: Inferred. Empirical results (Figure 2, Table 1) show flow IAA/FAA have higher distance‑to‑manifold than analytic‑projection counterparts, contrary to theoretical expectation.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not investigate whether the learned velocity field vθ is inaccurate or whether intermediate states drift far beyond the small‑time regime. No diagnostic of distances or vθ error is provided.
- **What evidence would resolve**: Measure the distribution of hidden state distances from M in flow IAA/FAA; evaluate vθ’s projection error across distances; train with samples farther from M or longer integration horizons.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Under what conditions does FAA achieve accuracy comparable to IAA, given that FAA only applies the geometric map at output while IAA enforces it at every layer?
- **Basis**: Inferred. The paper provides separate universal approximation theorems: IAA (Theorem 2.2) requires vector fields bounded and M uniformly prox‑regular, while FAA (Theorem 2.3) additionally requires fθ(Ω) to lie within the reach of M. Empirically, FAA often matches IAA (Table 1) but may fail if fθ outputs leave the tubular neighborhood.
- **Why unresolved**: No theoretical comparison linking the two bounds; the influence of the unconstrained network’s range is not studied.
- **What evidence would resolve**: Derive an error bound for FAA that explicitly depends on the maximum deviation of fθ from M; empirically monitor fθ’s outputs during training; vary network capacity and regularize to keep outputs near M.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Why does projection‑based IAA (orthogonal projection) achieve lower test error and smaller constraint violations than exponential IAA (Riemannian exponential) on SO(3) and sphere, despite exponential maps being intrinsic and avoiding explicit projections?
- **Basis**: Inferred. Table 1 shows Proj IAA outperforms Exp IAA on both datasets (e.g., SO(3): 2.22e‑1 vs 3.10e‑1 MSE; sphere: 6.29e‑3 vs 8.86e‑3). The paper mentions this but offers no explanation.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not analyze the geometric or numerical differences between projection and exponential updates; the choice of update rule may depend on curvature or loss function but this is not examined.
- **What evidence would resolve**: Compare truncation errors of projected vs. exponential Euler schemes for approximating a target vector field; conduct experiments on manifolds with varying curvature; measure alignment between update direction and gradient of the task loss.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: For exponential FAA, what topological obstructions limit uniform approximation of continuous F:M→M, and can they be circumvented (e.g., by using multiple basepoints or learned basepoints)?
- **Basis**: Inferred. Section 2.2 states that if F is a homeomorphism, the requirement expp ◦ f = F forces f to be a topological embedding, which is not always possible (e.g., S²). A uniform‑norm approximation result is not provided; only an L² theorem is given.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not characterize the closure of {expp ◦ f} in the uniform topology, nor does it explore whether the use of multiple basepoints could enlarge the expressive class.
- **What evidence would resolve**: For specific manifolds (like S²), construct homeomorphisms that cannot be uniformly approximated by any expp ◦ f; experimentally test exponential FAA with a set of basepoints or with a trainable basepoint on tasks requiring such maps.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees depend on prox‑regularity, a condition that may exclude many manifolds of practical interest (e.g., non‑smooth or stratified spaces).
- Empirical validation is restricted to low‑dimensional manifolds (S², SO(3), a disk) and a single protein dataset; performance on higher‑dimensional or more complex geometries remains unknown.
- Computational overhead of frequent geometric updates is not thoroughly analyzed; learned flow‑matching projections degrade as noise grows, potentially limiting robustness.

## Confidence
- Constraint satisfaction by construction: **High**
- Universal approximation under prox‑regularity: **Medium**
- Empirical superiority (low loss, near‑zero violation): **Medium**
- Learned flow projections approximate analytic ones near the manifold: **Medium**
