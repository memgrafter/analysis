# Sample Efficient Generative Molecular Optimization with Joint Self-Improvement
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10984
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10984
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** JSI achieves state-of-the-art hit ratios in molecular optimization (offline: up to 76.94% on JAK2; online: up to 84.91% on 5HT1B) under 3000-evaluation budgets.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Joint Self-Improvement (JSI), a method for sample-efficient generative molecular optimization. JSI jointly trains a generative model and a surrogate predictor with shared parameters to align molecule generation with regions where the predictor is reliable. At inference, a self-improving sampling scheme uses predictor-derived advantages to bias generation toward high-scoring candidates without policy gradient retraining. Evaluations on docking score optimization under strict evaluation budgets show JSI significantly outperforms prior methods in both offline and online settings.

## Method Summary
JSI uses a Hyformer backbone that shares parameters between an autoregressive generative head (pθ(x)) and a predictive head (pθ(y|x)). The model is trained via a joint likelihood loss on (x, y) pairs—either from an offline dataset or online evaluations—balancing generative and predictive objectives. During inference, a self-improving sampler draws a beam of K sequences, computes global advantages (predictor score minus mean), and perturbs next-token logits to favor prefixes leading to higher scores. This avoids high-variance policy gradient updates and mitigates distribution shift between generated molecules and the surrogate.

## Key Results
- **Offline:** JSI achieves hit ratios up to 76.94% on JAK2 (vs. 33.19% for RaM).
- **Online:** JSI achieves hit ratios up to 84.91% on 5HT1B (vs. 68.19% for SATURN).
- **Ablations:** Removing either joint modeling or self-improving sampling causes near-zero performance, demonstrating both components are essential.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Joint Parameterization Mitigates Distribution Shift
By forcing the generative and predictive models to share backbone parameters θ, the generator is regularized to stay within regions where the predictor pθ(y|x) is reliable. The joint loss Lλ(θ) trains both tasks simultaneously, preventing the generator from exploring out-of-distribution areas that would decouple it from the predictor’s knowledge. **Break condition:** If training data is extremely sparse or biased, the generator may still drift OOD during iterative sampling.

### Mechanism 2: Self-Improving Sampling with Global Advantages
At inference, JSI computes global advantages Advθ(x) = pθ(y|x) − μ over K sampled sequences and deterministically perturbs next-token logits. This locally tilts the generative distribution toward prefixes leading to higher predicted scores, implementing "inference-time policy improvement" without high-variance policy gradient updates. **Break condition:** If the predictor is miscalibrated or overconfident, advantage estimates mislead the sampler to poor regions.

### Mechanism 3: Likelihood-Based Training Supersedes Policy Gradients
Instead of using RLFT’s high-variance Monte Carlo gradient estimator, JSI optimizes the standard negative log-likelihood of the joint distribution. This provides stable gradients from supervised (x, y) pairs, avoiding the credit assignment problem and sparse-reward issues of policy gradient methods. **Break condition:** If target molecules are extremely rare and unseen during training, likelihood maximization may not provide a strong enough signal to discover novel high-scoring regions.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Likelihood-Based Training of Generative Models
- **Why needed:** JSI replaces RL policy gradients with maximum likelihood on the joint distribution for stable, low-variance updates.
- **Quick check:** How does minimizing NLL on (x, y) pairs differ from maximizing a reward-weighted likelihood in RL, in terms of gradient variance?

### Concept: Advantage Estimation for Sequence Models
- **Why needed:** The self-improving sampler relies on computing global advantages over full sequences to perturb generation.
- **Quick check:** Why use a global advantage (computed over full sequences) rather than token-level advantages for practical implementation?

### Concept: Joint Modeling of Generation and Prediction
- **Why needed:** Shared parameters enforce alignment between the generator and surrogate, mitigating distribution shift.
- **Quick check:** What are the risks of multi-task training with loss weight λ, and how might one balance generative vs. predictive performance?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Hyformer Backbone -> Generative Head (autoregressive), Predictive Head (regression); Sampling Module (Stochastic Beam Search with Gumbel-Top-k); Training Loop (joint loss, iterative self-improving sampling).

Critical path:
1. Pretrain Hyformer on ZINC250K.
2. Joint fine-tune on offline dataset OR initialize online loop with pretrained model.
3. Run N rounds of self-improving sampling (Alg.1) with beam K and step σ to produce candidates.
4. (Online only) Evaluate candidates with oracle, add to dataset, return to step 2.

Design tradeoffs:
- Beam width K: Larger K improves advantage fidelity but increases compute linearly (K * T).
- Step size σ: Controls exploration vs. exploitation; too small yields little improvement, too large destabilizes generation.
- Loss weight λ: Balances predictive and generative performance; improper weighting harms either task.

Failure signatures:
- Low Hit Ratio: Predictor inaccuracy (check correlation between predicted and true scores).
- Low diversity (IntDiv1): σ too small or K too small causing mode collapse.
- Slow sampling: K or N rounds too high, or inefficient trie implementation for logit updates.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Ablation: W/O joint modeling** – Fine-tune separate predictor and generator; expect significant performance drop.
2. **Ablation: W/O self-improving sampling** – Use Best-of-N rejection sampling; expect lower Hit Ratio and higher sampling time.
3. **Hyperparameter grid search** – Systematically vary K, σ, N rounds on a held-out target to identify robust operating ranges.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Improving the Predictive Component
- **Basis:** Limitations state that surrogate inaccuracy may limit optimization performance.
- **Why unresolved:** No specific techniques for enhancing surrogate robustness within the joint framework are proposed or validated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic comparison of various surrogate training approaches (e.g., adversarial training, uncertainty regularization) on the same joint architecture, measuring hit ratio and docking score improvements on difficult targets.

### Open Question 2: Incorporating Uncertainty Estimates
- **Basis:** Limitations note that incorporating uncertainty estimates into sampling could improve robustness and exploration.
- **Why unresolved:** Current sampling uses deterministic advantages from point predictions, ignoring predictive uncertainty.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend the sampling algorithm to use uncertainty (e.g., via Bayesian neural networks or Monte Carlo dropout) and evaluate performance on offline benchmarks, focusing on hit ratio, diversity, and convergence.

### Open Question 3: Quantifying Distribution Shift Mitigation
- **Basis:** The claim that joint modeling alleviates distribution shift is supported only indirectly via overall performance gains.
- **Why unresolved:** No direct empirical quantification of shift or surrogate OOD behavior is provided.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compute distributional similarity metrics (e.g., MMD) between generated molecules and offline dataset, and measure surrogate prediction error on held-out OOD samples, comparing joint and decoupled models.

## Limitations
- **Docking protocol opacity:** Exact software, protein structures, and preparation steps for computing docking scores are unspecified, hindering faithful reproduction of offline datasets and online oracle evaluations.
- **Hit Ratio threshold ambiguity:** The score threshold defining "known actives" is not provided; assumptions may alter reported performance.
- **Missing hyperparameter/architectural details:** Specifics such as attention heads, dropout rates, and online batch sizes/rounds are absent, potentially affecting reproducibility and comparison validity.

## Confidence
- **Joint modeling mitigates distribution shift:** Medium (supported by ablations but may fail under predictor miscalibration or sparse data).
- **Self-improving sampling drives high Hit Ratios:** High (strong empirical evidence; ablations show near-zero performance without it).
- **Likelihood-based training supersedes RL:** Medium (comparative results convincing but may be task-specific).

## Next Checks
1. **Score reproducibility audit:** Recompute docking scores for a random subset (e.g., 200 molecules) using a standard tool (AutoDock Vina) with the same protein structures to quantify deviation from paper’s values.
2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity profiling:** Systematically vary beam width K and step size σ on a held-out target to map their impact on Hit Ratio, diversity (IntDiv1), and sampling time.
3. **Predictor calibration check:** Train an independent regression model on the offline dataset and compute Pearson/Spearman correlation between predicted and true scores on held-out generator output; if correlation is low (<0.7), re-run JSI with a calibrated predictor.
