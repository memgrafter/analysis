# Semantic Novelty Trajectories in 80,000 Books: A Cross-Corpus Embedding Analysis
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01791  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01791  
- **Reference count:** 6  
- **Primary result:** Modern books (1990–2010) exhibit 10% higher mean semantic novelty and 67% higher trajectory circuitousness than pre-1920 books; convergent narrative curves are 2.3× more common in older literature, with poetry showing the largest structural shift.

## Executive Summary
This study tests Schmidhuber’s compression progress theory of interestingness by analyzing semantic novelty trajectories across 81,526 English-language books. Using paragraph-level embeddings from the `all-mpnet-base-v2` model, it computes a running-centroid novelty score for each paragraph and summarizes each book’s novelty sequence with geometric descriptors (speed, volume, circuitousness). Comparing two large corpora—pre-1920 (PG19) and modern (Books3, 1990–2010)—reveals that modern books have higher average novelty and more oscillatory (circuitous) trajectories. Clustering trajectories into eight narrative-shape archetypes shows significant shifts in archetype prevalence between eras. Notably, semantic novelty does not correlate with reader quality ratings.

## Method Summary
The method operationalizes novelty via `1 - cosine(e_i, centroid_{1:i-1})`, where `e_i` is a paragraph embedding and the centroid is the mean of all prior embeddings. Paragraphs are extracted via double-newline segmentation and encoded using `all-mpnet-base-v2`. For each book, a novelty sequence is generated and summarized by: (1) trajectory descriptors (speed, volume, circuitousness), (2) curve classification (divergent/convergent/flat) based on linear trend slope, and (3) a fixed-length Piecewise Aggregate Approximation (PAA‑16) for clustering. K‑means (k=8) is applied separately to each corpus’s PAA‑16 vectors to identify common narrative shapes. All analyses are aggregated at the corpus level and by genre.

## Key Results
- Mean novelty is 10% higher in modern books (0.503) vs. pre-1920 (0.459).
- Modern books show 67% higher circuitousness (oscillatory exploration) than older books.
- Convergent (novelty-declining) narrative curves are 2.3× more common in pre-1920 literature.
- Poetry exhibits a nearly 600% increase in circuitousness in the modern corpus.
- Semantic novelty shows no correlation with reader ratings (r = -0.002).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
The running-centroid novelty measure operationalizes Schmidhuber’s compression progress theory by quantifying the semantic deviation of each paragraph from the accumulated context. For each paragraph after the first, its embedding is compared via cosine similarity to the centroid of all preceding paragraph embeddings. A lower similarity (higher `1 - cosine`) indicates greater novelty, i.e., the new paragraph challenges the current semantic model more, implying potential compression progress if the model adapts. Core assumption: sentence embeddings capture semantic meaning such that cosine similarity reflects semantic relatedness, and the running centroid adequately represents the reader’s accumulated context. Evidence: the novelty distributions differ meaningfully between eras (Figure 1).

### Mechanism 2
Trajectory descriptors (speed, volume, circuitousness) reduce a high‑dimensional novelty sequence to interpretable geometric summaries that enable cross‑corpus comparison of narrative exploration patterns. Speed (mean absolute first difference) measures how quickly novelty changes; volume (standard deviation) measures overall spread; circuitousness (cumulative path length / net displacement) quantifies oscillation vs. monotonicity. These summarize key aspects of the trajectory’s shape. Core assumption: a few summary statistics sufficiently capture the dimensions of narrative shape relevant for large‑scale comparative analysis. Evidence: modern books show +67% higher circuitousness (Table 1).

### Mechanism 3
Clustering PAA‑16 trajectory representations identifies recurring narrative‑shape archetypes, and shifts in their prevalence between corpora reveal diachronic changes in literary style. Each novelty sequence is resampled to 16 equal‑length segments (PAA‑16), yielding a fixed‑dimensional vector that smooths local fluctuations while preserving global shape. K‑means groups books into archetypes (e.g., “Flat”, “Gradual Rise”). Comparing cluster distributions across corpora highlights structural evolution. Core assumption: PAA‑16 retains enough shape information for meaningful clustering, and a fixed `k=8` captures the major archetypes. Evidence: the Flat archetype drops from 26.1% to 15.1% and Gradual Rise rises from 14.3% to 24.7% (Table 4).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Sentence embeddings and cosine similarity  
  *Why needed:* The novelty measure depends on embeddings that map paragraphs to a semantic vector space; cosine similarity quantifies their relatedness.  
  *Quick check:* If embeddings are L2‑normalized, cosine similarity reduces to a dot product, which is computationally efficient.
- **Concept:** Running centroid as incremental context model  
  *Why needed:* The centroid of all previous embeddings represents the accumulated semantic context against which each new paragraph is judged.  
  *Quick check:* The incremental update formula is `centroid_i = ((i-1)*centroid_{i-1} + e_i) / i`, with O(d) time per paragraph (d = embedding dimension).
- **Concept:** Piecewise Aggregate Approximation (PAA) for time‑series reduction  
  *Why needed:* PAA converts variable‑length novelty sequences into a fixed‑size representation (16 values) suitable for clustering while preserving overall shape.  
  *Quick check:* PAA‑16 works by dividing the sequence into 16 equal‑length windows and averaging the novelty values within each window; the number 16 signifies the number of segments used.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Paragraph segmenter -> Embedding encoder (all-mpnet-base-v2) -> Novelty calculator (running centroid) -> Trajectory analyzer (TADs, curve classification, PAA‑16/SAX) -> Clustering module (k-means, k=8) -> Exploration toolkit (Streamlit dashboards)
- **Critical path:**  
  Load text -> Segment paragraphs -> Batch embed -> Compute novelty sequence -> Derive TADs & curve label -> Compute PAA‑16 -> (Clustering) -> Aggregate statistics / visualizations.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Embedding model: `all-mpnet-base-v2` offers quality but slower inference; alternatives trade accuracy for speed.  
  - Segmentation: Double‑newline works for prose but may create inconsistent units for poetry/drama.  
  - PAA length & k: 16 segments and 8 clusters were chosen empirically; different values could yield different archetypes.  
  - Curve classification: Threshold ε for slope is heuristic; adjusts sensitivity to trends.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Novelty scores all near 0 or 1 → embedding or segmentation failure.  
  - Extremely high circuitousness (e.g., > 1000) may indicate noisy embeddings or pathological segmentation.  
  - Clusters with high intra‑cluster distance → PAA‑16 may be too low‑dimensional or k inappropriate.  
  - Unexpected genre patterns (e.g., fiction more convergent) → possible embedding bias.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Single‑book sanity check:** Process a well‑known book (e.g., *Pride and Prejudice*), plot its novelty trajectory; expect non‑trivial oscillations and a plausible curve type.  
  2. **Corpus‑level validation:** On a small random subset (e.g., 100 books per corpus), compute mean novelty and circuitousness; confirm modern books show higher novelty and circuitousness (≈ +10% and +67% respectively).  
  3. **Clustering pilot:** Cluster PAA‑16 vectors from a mixed sample of PG19 and Books3 books; visualize cluster centroids to verify distinct archetypes (Flat, Gradual Rise, etc.) and check that cluster assignments roughly align with visual inspection.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How do semantic novelty trajectories evolve on a decade-by-decade basis between 1800 and 2010, and what historical or cultural factors correlate with observed shifts?  
*Basis:* The study’s coarse era-level comparison and temporal gaps prevent fine-grained analysis.  
*Why unresolved:* Corpora have sparse pre-1800 coverage (PG19) and a large modern gap (Books3 only 1990–2010). Genre and author variations over time remain unexamined.

### Open Question 2
What is the distribution of the eight narrative-shape archetypes when all 81,526 books are clustered jointly, and how does this unified clustering alter interpretations of diachronic shifts?  
*Basis:* Trajectory clusters were computed independently per corpus, limiting direct archetype matching.  
*Why unresolved:* Separate K‑means clusterings prevent direct comparison; labels were assigned by visual inspection, risking inconsistency.

### Open Question 3
To what extent do individual authors exhibit consistent, identifiable semantic novelty trajectory signatures across their works, and how do these correlate with author-level metadata (genre, period, reputation)?  
*Basis:* Author-level trajectory fingerprinting is proposed as a future avenue.  
*Why unresolved:* Analysis aggregates at the book level; author identities are not linked across works, and no control for author-specific patterns exists.

### Open Question 4
How does the integration of semantic novelty with sentiment and topic trajectories yield a multidimensional account of narrative shape, and which dimensions best predict reader engagement or critical reception?  
*Basis:* The author calls for integration with sentiment and topic trajectories for a multidimensional account.  
*Why unresolved:* The study isolates novelty as the sole dimension; interactions with affective and thematic dynamics are unexplored, and the null novelty–rating correlation suggests other dimensions may matter more.

## Limitations
- **Unknown threshold ε** for narrative curve classification; reported prevalence ratios depend on this unreported heuristic.
- **Books3 corpus access**; the original dataset is not publicly redistributable, so substitutions may introduce selection bias.
- **Clustering reproducibility**; k-means seed, initialization method, and exact PAA‑16/K choice are unspecified, affecting archetype distribution.
- **Embedding model bias**; `all-mpnet-base-v2` may under-represent archaic semantics, potentially inflating the pre-1920 novelty gap.

## Confidence
- **Mean novelty difference (+10% modern):** Medium. Relies on embedding semantic stability across centuries; plausible but vulnerable to model bias.
- **Circuitousness shift (+67% modern):** Medium. Geometric metrics are well-defined but sensitive to segmentation and embedding noise.
- **Poetry structural shift (+600% circuitousness):** Low. Extreme ratio suggests possible genre-specific segmentation artifacts.
- **No correlation with ratings:** High. Simple correlation is straightforward to recompute; null result likely robust.

## Next Checks
1. **Embedding bias test:** Recompute novelty using a historically tuned encoder (e.g., `roberta-base` fine-tuned on Early English) on a balanced sample; measure change in era gap.
2. **Corpus substitution audit:** Replace Books3 with a public modern equivalent (e.g., Gutenberg 2000–2020), match genre distributions, and re-run primary metrics to test robustness.
3. **ε sensitivity sweep:** Vary curve-classification threshold across a plausible range (e.g., slope ±0.001–0.01) and report stability of "convergent more common pre-1920" ratio.
