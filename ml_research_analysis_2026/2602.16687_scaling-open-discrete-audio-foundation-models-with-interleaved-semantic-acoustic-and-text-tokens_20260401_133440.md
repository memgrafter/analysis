# Scaling Open Discrete Audio Foundation Models with Interleaved Semantic, Acoustic, and Text Tokens

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16687  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16687  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: SODA establishes the first discrete audio foundation model with interleaved tokens, yielding validated training recipes and scaling laws, and achieving competitive cross-modal performance across semantic, acoustic, text, and translation tasks.

## Executive Summary
SODA is a native discrete audio foundation model that overcomes limitations of existing text-first or semantic-only audio models. It systematically investigates data mixtures, token composition, and text ratios, establishing a validated training recipe. Key innovations include interleaving semantic, acoustic, and text tokens at the utterance level, enabling a single autoregressive model to perform both audio generation and cross-modal tasks (ASR, TTS) without architectural modifications. The paper derives the first scaling laws for discrete audio models via IsoFLOP analysis, revealing that optimal training data scales faster than model size. SODA models from 135M to 4B parameters, trained on up to 500B tokens, achieve competitive results across semantic, acoustic, text, and cross-modal benchmarks. Cold-start initialization outperforms warm-start for audio skills, and the model's flexible backbone supports voice‑preserving speech‑to‑speech translation via fine‑tuning. This work provides a foundation for scaling discrete audio models.

## Method Summary
The training pipeline converts raw audio into discrete tokens using the Mimi neural codec (8 codebooks, 12.5 Hz, 100 tokens/sec). Text is tokenized with a standard BPE tokenizer (e.g., Llama3/Marin). Each utterance is represented by interleaving audio tokens—semantic from the first codebook, acoustic from the remaining seven—with transcript tokens, in either text‑first or audio‑first order, and surrounded by special boundary tokens. Sequences are packed to a fixed length of 4096 tokens. A decoder‑only Transformer (Qwen3 architecture) is trained via next‑token prediction on this mixed data, supplemented with a small proportion (5%) of high‑quality text‑only data to enhance linguistic knowledge. Training uses AdamW with a warmup‑stable‑decay schedule, gradient clipping, and z‑loss. Models ranging from 135M to 4B parameters are trained on up to 500B tokens. Evaluation includes validation negative log‑likelihood (NLL) on LibriSpeech (audio+text) and Paloma (text), and downstream zero/few‑shot tasks: automatic speech recognition (ASR), text‑to‑speech (TTS), semantic speech (sBLIMP, sWUGGY), acoustic speech (Salmon), and text benchmarks (tBLIMP, tWUGGY, HellaSwag).

## Key Results
- Derived first scaling laws for discrete audio models: optimal training tokens scale as \(D^* \propto C^{0.579}\), faster than model size \(N^* \propto C^{0.367}\).
- SODA models (135M–4B) achieve competitive performance across semantic, acoustic, text, and cross‑modal tasks; validation loss (NLL) reliably predicts downstream results.
- Cold‑start initialization yields superior audio skills (e.g., lower ASR WER) and avoids instability seen with warm‑start from a text‑pretrained LLM.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Interleaved tokens enable multimodal capabilities
**Claim**: Interleaving semantic, acoustic, and text tokens at the utterance level enables a single autoregressive model to acquire both audio generation and cross‑modal capabilities without architectural modifications.  
**Mechanism**: During pre‑training, each audio utterance is encoded with the Mimi codec into semantic (first codebook) and acoustic (remaining 7 codebooks) tokens. These are interleaved with the transcript text tokens in either text‑first or audio‑first order. The transformer predicts the next token across this mixed sequence, learning to map between modalities within a unified language modeling objective.  
**Evidence**: Table 1 shows that the S+A+T configuration achieves non‑zero cross‑modal metrics (ASR 18.3% WER, TTS 27.1% WER) while S+A only fails to perform these tasks. Neighbor analysis indicates most related audio models are either text‑first (e.g., SALMONN, Qwen‑Omni) or semantic‑only (e.g., TWIST, SpiritLM); SODA’s native interleaved acoustic+text approach is rarely represented, highlighting its novelty.

### Mechanism 2: Small text mixture improves text knowledge
**Claim**: Adding a small proportion (5%) of high‑quality text‑only data to the speech training mixture improves textual knowledge and reasoning while preserving audio performance.  
**Mechanism**: Text‑only documents (e.g., Nemotron‑CC) supplement the speech data by providing clean, well‑structured linguistic examples. The model sees both modalities and learns to allocate capacity to textual tasks. Because speech dominates (95%), audio capabilities remain largely unaffected.  
**Evidence**: Figure 2 shows that adding up to 5% text drastically reduces Paloma text NLL while LibriSpeech audio NLL stays at baseline; beyond 5% audio NLL degrades.

### Mechanism 3: Cold‑start avoids interference
**Claim**: Cold‑start training (random initialization) yields more stable optimization and stronger audio skills compared to warm‑start from a text‑pretrained LLM, despite warm‑start’s advantage on pure text tasks.  
**Mechanism**: Text‑pretrained weights carry strong biases for purely textual next‑token prediction. When exposed to interleaved audio tokens, the model struggles to repurpose these weights, leading to loss spikes and interference that harm audio→text mapping (e.g., ASR). Cold‑start avoids these biases, allowing balanced joint learning and smoother loss curves.  
**Evidence**: Figure 11 shows cold‑start achieves lower ASR WER at all checkpoints and avoids the large loss spike observed in warm‑start (Figure 10). Table 8 confirms final performance gaps.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Discrete Audio Tokenization with Neural Codecs**  
- Why needed: The entire framework relies on converting continuous audio into discrete tokens. Neural codecs like Mimi compress raw audio into semantic and acoustic tokens, enabling the use of transformer language models.  
- Quick check: What is the role of the first codebook in Mimi compared to the later codebooks? (First captures coarse semantic content; later ones capture fine acoustic details.)

**Concept: Autoregressive Next‑Token Prediction**  
- Why needed: All training and generation use next‑token prediction over interleaved sequences. This standard LLM objective is extended to multimodal data; understanding it for text clarifies how the model learns cross‑modal tasks without explicit alignment.  
- Quick check: In autoregressive training, if the input sequence is "A B C", what is the target for the step where input is "A B"? ("C".)

**Concept: Scaling Laws and IsoFLOP Analysis**  
- Why needed: The paper establishes the first scaling laws for discrete audio models, showing data scales faster than model size (\(D^* \propto C^{0.579}\)). This informs compute allocation when scaling and explains differences to text LLM scaling.  
- Quick check: If you double the compute budget \(C\), how does the optimal number of training tokens \(D^*\) change according to the paper's exponent? (Multiply by \(2^{0.579} \approx 1.49\), a ~49% increase.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Raw audio + transcripts -> VAD splitting -> Mimi codec (audio tokens) + text tokenizer -> Interleaving (with special tokens) -> Packed sequences (4096 tokens) -> Transformer (decoder‑only, Qwen3‑style) -> Next‑token prediction loss -> AdamW update. Evaluation: NLL on LibriSpeech/Paloma; downstream ASR, TTS, sBLIMP, sWUGGY, Salmon, tBLIMP, tWUGGY, HellaSwag.

### Critical Path
1. Load sample: audio file + transcript.  
2. Apply VAD to split into chunks/utterances.  
3. Encode each audio chunk with Mimi → token sequence (flattened codebooks).  
4. Tokenize transcript with text tokenizer.  
5. Interleave: choose format (audio‑first or text‑first); insert boundary special tokens; pack sequences to length 4096 without padding.  
6. Forward pass: transformer predicts next token; compute cross‑entropy loss on all non‑special tokens.  
7. Backward pass; update weights; repeat.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Token composition: S‑only maximizes semantic accuracy but loses acoustic detail and cross‑modal ability; S+A improves acoustic but slightly reduces semantic; S+A+T unlocks cross‑modal at cost of a small drop in S+A acoustic scores.  
- Text ratio: low text (<5%) preserves audio performance; higher text improves text benchmarks but harms audio NLL.  
- Initialization: cold‑start gives stable audio skills but lags in text knowledge; warm‑start gives jumpstart in text but introduces instability and ASR degradation.  
- Over‑training: training smaller models far beyond \(D^*\) reduces loss at expense of efficiency; larger models approach compute‑optimal frontier.  
- Token rate: 100 tokens/sec yields long sequences, demanding larger context and more data; reduces information density per token.

### Failure Signatures
- Loss spikes: sudden increases in training loss, especially with warm‑start.  
- Cross‑modal regression: ASR WER plateaus or increases despite decreasing NLL.  
- Acoustic saturation: Salmon accuracy stalls around 70% across scales.  
- Text knowledge gap: Cold‑start models show large gap to warm‑start on tWUGGY/HellaSwag even after full training.  
- Data mismatch: Using unpunctuated transcripts (e.g., MLS) leads to poor ASR/TTS.  
- Codec quality: Poor audio reconstruction (high MCD or DNSMOS) indicates Mimi underfitting.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Token composition validation**: Train 150M models for 10B tokens on Yodas speech only using S‑only, S+A, and S+A+T token sets. Evaluate sBLIMP/sWUGGY (semantic), Salmon (acoustic), and ASR/TTS (cross‑modal). Expect S+A+T to be the only one achieving non‑zero cross‑modal scores.  
2. **Text ratio sweep**: With a fixed 150M model and 10B tokens, vary the percentage of Nemotron text (0% to 50%) mixed with Yodas speech. Monitor NLL on LibriSpeech (audio) and Paloma (text). Identify the turning point where audio NLL begins to rise; expected ~5%.  
3. **IsoFLOP scaling law replication**: Choose a compute budget \(C\) (e.g., \(3×10^{19}\) FLOPs). Train model‑size and data pairs satisfying \(6ND = C\) (within ±10% tolerance). At least 6 points spanning \(N\) from ~200M to 2B. Compute validation NLL for each. Fit quadratic \(L\) vs \(\log N\) and \(L\) vs \(\log D\) to find optimal \(N^*, D^*\). Verify exponents roughly follow \(N^* \propto C^{0.367}\) and \(D^* \propto C^{0.579}\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Impact of tokenizer and architecture choices**: How do alternative tokenizers (different neural codecs), token sampling rates, RVQ‑weighted loss functions, and architectural modifications affect training efficiency and downstream performance? The study fixed Mimi, rate, loss, and decoder‑only architecture; these variables remain unexplored.
- **Validity of scaling laws at larger scales**: Do the derived exponents (\(N^* \propto C^{0.367}, D^* \propto C^{0.579}\)) hold beyond the studied regime (\(>3×10^{20}\) FLOPs)? IsoFLOP curves flattened at larger scales and predictions failed for models up to \(1.3×10^{22}\) FLOPs, suggesting potential regime shifts.
- **Semantic–acoustic trade‑off mitigation**: What modifications can reduce the trade‑off when using interleaved semantic+acoustic+text tokens? Adding acoustic tokens improves Salmon but hurts sBLIMP; the cause and mitigation strategies are unclear.
- **Hybrid training for combined advantages**: Can cold‑start pre‑training followed by text‑enriched fine‑tuning combine cold‑start’s audio stability with warm‑start’s text knowledge? This hybrid approach was proposed but not tested.
- **Emergent audio capabilities**: Under what scaling conditions might discrete audio models exhibit emergent zero‑shot task generalization analogous to LLMs? SODA models up to 4B parameters and 500B tokens showed gradual improvements, not sudden emergence; larger scales or different evaluations might reveal such abilities.

## Limitations
- **Codec bottleneck**: Mimi’s 100 tokens/sec and 8‑codebook design trades resolution for efficiency; impact of lower‑bitrate codecs on scaling and cross‑modal alignment is unknown.
- **Scaling law generalizability**: IsoFLOP analysis covered only \(3×10^{18}\)–\(3×10^{20}\) FLOPs; exponents may shift at larger budgets or with different data/codec distributions.
- **Warm‑start mitigation**: Cold‑start superiority was shown only against naive warm‑start (full LLM initialization). Intermediate schemes (partial warm‑start, gradual modality injection) could close the audio–text capability gap but remain unexplored.

## Confidence
- Interleaved token composition enables multimodal emergence: **High** (based on Table 1 and ablations).
- Adding up to 5% text improves text NLL without audio loss: **Medium** (Figure 2; potential confounder in text quality).
- Cold‑start initialization outperforms warm‑start for audio skills: **Medium‑High** (Figures 10‑11, Table 8).

## Next Checks
1. **Codec variation test**: Train identical 150M SODA models with Mimi at 4, 6, and 8 codebooks (same token rate). Compare validation NLL, Salmon acoustic accuracy, and ASR/TTS WER to isolate codec capacity.
2. **Scaling law extrapolation**: Train at least 3 models >10B parameters with compute budgets predicted by \(D^* \propto C^{0.579}\). Fit new scaling curves; test if exponents hold or saturate at larger scales.
3. **Warm‑start stabilization**: Implement a gradual modality‑introduction schedule (e.g., 50% text tokens for first 10% of steps, then ramp to 5%). Compare final ASR WER and text benchmarks against both cold‑start and full warm‑start baselines.
