# Learning Optimal Individualized Decision Rules with Conditional Demographic Parity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05226
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05226
- **Reference count:** 8
- **Primary result:** Proposed DP-IDR and CDP-IDR achieve lower unfairness (UF ~0.03–0.05 vs. 0.13 for Fair CATE) and higher policy value (PV ~1.88 vs. 1.43) in simulations; real-data analysis (Oregon Health Insurance Experiment) confirms gains (unfairness 0.017 vs. 0.047, PV 972.9 vs. 707.9).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of learning individualized decision rules (IDRs) from potentially biased data while ensuring fairness with respect to a sensitive attribute. Existing fairness constraints, such as imposing demographic parity on the conditional average treatment effect (CATE), are often overly restrictive and lead to substantial utility loss. The authors instead incorporate demographic parity (DP) or conditional demographic parity (CDP) directly into the IDR optimization objective. Theoretically, they prove that the optimal constrained IDR can be expressed as a perturbation of the unconstrained optimal rule (which simply takes the sign of the CATE). This perturbation is determined by a fairness term that solves a scalar equation within each subgroup defined by a legitimate feature \(L\). The reduction to per‑subgroup root finding yields a computationally efficient algorithm. Additionally, \(\epsilon\)-constrained variants allow practitioners to trade off fairness violations against policy value. Convergence rates for both the policy value and the fairness constraint are established. Empirical evaluations on four synthetic scenarios and the Oregon Health Insurance Experiment demonstrate that the proposed DP-IDR and CDP-IDR methods achieve significantly lower unfairness while improving policy value compared to state-of-the-art fair IDR approaches such as Fair CATE.

## Method Summary
The estimation procedure comprises four steps. First, the conditional average treatment effect \(\delta_R(Z) = \mathbb{E}[R|Z,A=1] - \mathbb{E}[R|Z,A=-1]\) is estimated using two separate deep neural networks trained on the treated (\(A=1\)) and control (\(A=-1\)) subsamples. Second, the conditional probabilities \(\pi(s|l) = \mathbb{P}(S=s|L=l)\) are estimated empirically within each stratum of the legitimate feature \(L\), and the fairness coefficients \(\psi_l(S) = I(S=1)/\pi(1|l) - I(S=0)/\pi(0|l)\) are constructed. Third, for each subgroup \(l\), the fairness multiplier \(\omega_l\) is obtained by solving the equation \(\hat{G}_l(\omega) = \pm\epsilon\) (or \(0\) for exact fairness), where \(\hat{G}_l(\omega)\) is a smoothed analogue of the fairness constraint: \(\hat{G}_l(\omega) = n^{-1}\sum_i \Phi\big((\hat{\delta}_R(Z_i) - \omega \hat{\psi}_l(S_i))/h\big) \hat{\psi}_l(S_i) I(L_i=l)\). The smoothing with a normal CDF bandwidth \(h\) handles the non‑smooth indicator; the root is found via bisection over a bounded interval \([-K,K]\). Fourth, the final decision rule is formed as \(\hat{D}(Z) = 2I\big\{\hat{\delta}_R(Z) - \sum_l I(L=l)\hat{\omega}_l \hat{\psi}_l(S) > 0\big\} - 1\). Theoretical guarantees (Consistency, convergence rates) are provided under Conditions C1–C5 (e.g., boundedness, smoothness) and the paper specifies how network architecture should scale with sample size (Theorem 2) and how to choose \(h\) (Theorem 3) to achieve the stated rates. The \(\epsilon\)-constrained version simply adjusts the target in the root‑finding step.

## Key Results
- **Simulations:** DP-IDR and CDP-IDR yield lower unfairness (UF ~0.03–0.05) and higher policy value (PV ~1.88) than Fair CATE (UF 0.13, PV 1.43) in Case 1, with similar improvements across other scenarios.
- **Real data (Oregon Health Insurance Experiment):** CDP-IDR reduces unfairness to 0.017 and increases PV to 972.9, substantially outperforming Fair CATE (unfairness 0.047, PV 707.9).
- **\(\epsilon\)-constrained variants:** Allow a controllable fairness‑utility trade‑off: tightening \(\epsilon\) reduces unfairness but also lowers policy value.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Optimal IDR as a Perturbed Unconstrained Rule
- **Claim:** The optimal IDR under DP or CDP constraints is obtained by adding a fairness‑aware perturbation to the unconstrained optimal IDR.
- **Mechanism:** The constrained maximization of \(V(D)\) with a CDP constraint is tackled via a Lagrangian. After rearrangement (Section 3.1), the solution satisfies \(I(f(Z)>0) = I\{\delta_R(Z) - \omega_l I(L=l)\psi_l(S) > 0\}\) for each subgroup \(l\), where \(\psi_l(S) = \frac{I(S=1)}{\pi(1|l)} - \frac{I(S=0)}{\pi(0|l)}\). The optimal IDR becomes \(D^*(Z)=2I\{\delta_R(Z) - \sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}} I(L=l)\,\omega_l^*\psi_l(S) > 0\}-1\), with \(\omega_l^*\) solving a scalar equation. The term \(\omega_l^*\psi_l(S)\) is the fairness‑driven perturbation.
- **Core assumption:** The CDP constraint can be written as a linear functional of \(I(f>0)\) (Eq. 4), and the Lagrangian dual has zero duality gap (strong duality holds). Moreover, \(G_l(\omega) = \mathbb{E}[I\{\delta_R-\omega\psi_l>0\} I(L=l)\psi_l(S)]\) is continuous and strictly monotonic, guaranteeing a unique root.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “We show that the theoretically optimal IDRs under DP and CDP constraints can be obtained by applying perturbations to the unconstrained optimal IDRs.”; [section] Section 3.1, Equation (7) and the ensuing derivation leading to \(D^*_{\text{cdp}}(Z)\).
- **Break condition:** If \(G_l(\omega)\) fails to be strictly decreasing (e.g., \(\psi_l\) degenerate or distribution of \(\delta_R\) pathological), the root may be non‑unique or lie outside the searched interval, yielding an infeasible \(\omega_l^*\) and thus an invalid IDR.

### Mechanism 2: Reduction to Scalar Root‑Finding
- **Claim:** Computing the fairness perturbation reduces to solving a one‑dimensional root‑finding problem per subgroup efficiently.
- **Mechanism:** Substituting the optimal decision rule given \(\omega\) into the constraint yields \(G_l(\omega)=0\) (or \(=\pm\epsilon\) for \(\epsilon\)-CDP). Lemma 1 shows \(G_l\) is continuous and non‑increasing; therefore the equation has at most one solution. The authors propose a bisection algorithm over a bounded interval \([-K, K]\), turning the high‑dimensional constrained optimization into a set of scalar problems.
- **Core assumption:** Conditions (C1)–(C5) ensure that \(\psi_l(S)\) is well‑defined, the expectations exist, and \(G_l\) is continuous and monotonic. The bisection interval \([-K,K]\) is chosen large enough to contain the root.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “enabling a computationally efficient solution … solving a scalar equation per subgroup.”; [section] Lemma 1 and Theorem 1 formalize the monotonicity and existence of \(\omega_l^*\); Section 3.2 describes the bisection method.
- **Break condition:** If \(G_l(\omega)\) is constant over a range (e.g., \(\delta_R\) independent of \(\psi_l\) within a subgroup), the equation may have infinitely many roots or none, causing ambiguity in \(\omega_l^*\). Numerical issues may also arise if \(K\) is insufficient.

### Mechanism 3: Smooth Surrogate for Stable Estimation
- **Claim:** The discontinuous indicator in the constraint is replaced by a smooth surrogate to enable stable estimation and asymptotically valid fairness control.
- **Mechanism:** The true constraint involves \(I(f(Z)>0)\), which is non‑smooth. In the estimation procedure (Section 4, Step 3), this is approximated by \(\Phi\big((\hat{\delta}_R(Z)-\omega\hat{\psi}_l(S))/h\big)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF and \(h\) a bandwidth. This yields a smooth empirical analogue \(\hat{G}_l(\omega)\). As \(h\to0\) and \(n\to\infty\), Theorem 3 shows the estimated IDR satisfies the CDP constraint up to a higher‑order term, allowing controlled fairness violations.
- **Core assumption:** The true conditional distribution of \(\delta_R\) given \((S,L)\) is sufficiently smooth (Condition C5) to approximate the indicator uniformly. The bandwidth \(h\) decays at an appropriate rate relative to sample size (\(h \asymp n^{-\beta/(6(d+2\beta))}\)).
- **Evidence:** [section] Section 4, Step 3 explicitly defines the smooth approximation; Theorem 3 provides the convergence rate for the fairness violation.
- **Break condition:** A bandwidth \(h\) that is too large introduces bias, causing the final constraint violation to exceed the target even asymptotically. Conversely, \(h\) too small yields high variance and possible numerical instability in the bisection because \(\hat{G}_l\) becomes nearly discrete.

## Foundational Learning
### Individualized Decision Rules (IDRs)
- **Why needed:** IDRs are the core objects the paper seeks to learn; they map individual covariates \(Z\) to treatment actions, allowing personalized decisions.
- **Quick check:** How is the unconstrained optimal IDR related to the conditional average treatment effect \(\delta_R(Z)\)? (Answer: It is \(\operatorname{sign}(\delta_R(Z))\).)

### Demographic Parity (DP) & Conditional Demographic Parity (CDP)
- **Why needed:** These are the fairness criteria imposed on the IDR. DP requires treatment assignment independent of the sensitive attribute \(S\); CDP relaxes this to independence conditional on a legitimate feature \(L\).
- **Quick check:** What is the key difference between DP and CDP, and why might CDP be preferable in some applications? (Answer: CDP allows heterogeneity across legally acceptable groups \(L\) while blocking unfair influence of \(S\).)

### Lagrangian Duality and Perturbation Method
- **Why needed:** The paper converts the constrained optimization into an unconstrained form with a tunable perturbation parameter \(\omega_l\); this makes the problem tractable and yields a closed‑form expression for the optimal rule.
- **Quick check:** In Equation (7), why does the solution take the form \(I\{\delta_R - \omega I(L=l)\psi_l(S) > 0\}\)? (Answer: Because the Lagrangian combines the original objective and a linear constraint in \(I(f>0)\), and the sign threshold preserves optimality.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Outcome models (\(F_1, F_0\)) → CATE estimator (\(\hat{\delta}_R\)) → Group propensity estimator (\(\hat{\pi}(s|l)\)) → Fairness coefficients (\(\hat{\psi}_l(S)\)) → Constraint smoother (\(\hat{G}_l(\omega)\)) → Root finder (bisection) → Final IDR (\(\hat{D}\))

### Critical path
1. Train two deep neural networks on treated and control subsamples to obtain \(\hat{\delta}_R(Z)\).
2. Estimate \(\hat{\pi}(s|l)\) from the training data.
3. For each subgroup \(l\), solve for \(\hat{\omega}_l\) by finding the root of \(\hat{G}_l(\omega) = \pm\epsilon\) (or 0) via bisection.
4. Combine \(\hat{\omega}_l\) into the decision function \(\hat{D}(Z) = 2I\{\hat{\delta}_R(Z) - \sum_l I(L=l)\hat{\omega}_l\hat{\psi}_l(S) > 0\} - 1\).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Epsilon (\(\epsilon\)):** Smaller \(\epsilon\) tightens fairness but reduces policy value because a larger perturbation \(|\omega_l|\) is required.
- **Bandwidth (\(h\)):** Smaller \(h\) reduces approximation bias but increases variance; the rate \(h \asymp n^{-\beta/(6(d+2\beta))}\) balances the two.
- **Neural network size:** Larger depth/width improves \(\hat{\delta}_R\) accuracy but risks overfitting; Theorem 2 prescribes scaling with sample size.
- **Subgroup sizes \(N_l\):** Very small \(N_l\) cause noisy \(\hat{\pi}(s|l)\); pooling or regularization may be needed.
- **Bisection interval \(K\):** Must be wide enough to contain the true \(\omega_l^*\); too small truncates the search, too large wastes computation.

### Failure signatures
- **Unstable policy value** across cross‑validation folds → likely overfitting or poor network capacity.
- **Fairness violation** above \(\epsilon\) after training → bandwidth \(h\) too large or inaccurate \(\hat{\pi}(s|l)\).
- **Bisection non‑convergence** → \(\hat{G}_l\) not strictly monotonic (flat region) due to extreme imbalance or large \(h\).
- **Discrepancy between training and test PF/CUF** → distribution shift or poor surrogate approximation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to non‑binary sensitive attributes:** While the current formulation assumes binary \(S\), extending DP/CDP‑IDR to categorical or continuous \(S\) would broaden applicability. The binary assumption simplifies the constraint to a single equation per subgroup; for non‑binary \(S\) the constraint becomes multivariate, potentially requiring multiple multipliers and destroying the simple closed‑form solution. Theoretical properties (existence, uniqueness, computational efficiency) are unclear.
2. **Incorporating value fairness:** The framework can be extended to other constraints like *value fairness* (equal expected benefit across subgroups). The proposed constraint involves \(\delta_R\) and couples the decision rule with the outcome model, which may jeopardize monotonicity and smoothness needed for the scalar root‑finding approach. Existence, uniqueness, and estimation stability remain unresolved.
3. **Integration with outcome‑weighted learning (OWL) or A‑learning:** The authors note that fairness‑constrained optimization could be formulated within OWL or A‑learning frameworks, potentially offering robustness or efficiency. However, translating DP/CDP constraints into these frameworks may not yield a simple closed‑form solution, and the statistical properties of such integrated estimators are unknown.
4. **Principled selection of the legitimate feature \(L\):** The choice of \(L\) for CDP is given but influences fairness and utility. No guidance is provided on how to select \(L\) in practice; an inappropriate \(L\) (e.g., a proxy for \(S\)) may fail to eliminate discrimination or cause unnecessary value loss. Robustness to misspecified \(L\) and a data‑driven selection method are open problems.

## Limitations
- The theoretical derivation relies on strong duality and strict monotonicity of \(G_l(\omega)\); degenerate cases (e.g., \(\psi_l\) degenerate or irregular \(\delta_R\) distribution) may invalidate the perturbation solution.
- Practical implementation lacks concrete guidance for choosing the smoothing bandwidth \(h\) and the bisection interval bound \(K\); inappropriate choices can lead to fairness violations or root‑finding failures.
- Empirical validation is limited to a single real dataset (Oregon Health Insurance Experiment) and a few simulation scenarios, restricting generalizability.

1. **Hyperparameter sweep:** Systematically vary the bandwidth \(h\) and bisection interval bound \(K\) to assess stability of policy value and unfairness in simulation; derive robust default ranges.
2. **Alternative CATE estimators:** Replace the deep neural networks with random forests or linear models to test sensitivity to outcome model misspecification and evaluate robustness.
3. **Subgroup stress test:** Simulate extreme imbalance in \(S|L\) and very small subgroup sizes to evaluate the stability of \(\hat{\pi}(s|l)\) and the bisection procedure; monitor failure rates and propose mitigations.
