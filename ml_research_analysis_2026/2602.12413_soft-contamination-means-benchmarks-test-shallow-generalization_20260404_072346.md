# Soft Contamination Means Benchmarks Test Shallow Generalization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12413
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12413
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Semantic duplicates of benchmark items in LLM training corpora inflate benchmark scores by enabling shallow generalization; even ~5% contamination can cause ~15% performance gains.

## Executive Summary
The paper reveals that "soft contamination" — the presence of semantically equivalent (but not string-identical) duplicates of benchmark items in training data — is widespread in large language model corpora. Through embedding-based similarity search on the Olmo-3 training corpus, the authors find that a majority of tasks from major coding and reasoning benchmarks have semantic duplicates in the training data. Finetuning experiments demonstrate that exposure to these duplicates improves performance not only on the duplicated items but also on genuinely held-out items from the same benchmarks, a phenomenon they term "shallow generalization." This indicates that benchmark progress may overestimate true reasoning capability, as models are effectively trained on the benchmark distribution rather than generalizing out-of-distribution. Even ecologically plausible contamination levels (~5% of finetuning data) yield significant score inflation (~15% on MuSR), suggesting that recent improvements are confounded by data accumulation and leakage.

## Method Summary
The study combines large-scale embedding-based duplicate detection with controlled finetuning experiments. First, they embed a 1% stratified sample of the Olmo-3 pretraining corpus (Dolma and Dolmino) and all instruction tuning data (Dolci), along with benchmark items from CodeForces, MBPP, MuSR, and ZebraLogic. Using cosine similarity, they retrieve the top-100 most similar corpus chunks per benchmark item and annotate these pairs to estimate exact and semantic duplicate rates. To validate the causal effect of soft contamination, they generate synthetic semantic duplicates for three benchmarks (MuSR, ZebraLogic, MBPP) via LLM-driven transformations (paraphrasing, category substitution, etc.). They then finetune Olmo-3 Instruct on these duplicates (or on mixtures containing a small proportion of duplicates) and evaluate on held-out halves of the benchmarks, comparing performance to controls trained on non-duplicate but cosine-similar data. The key metric is the relative improvement in benchmark accuracy attributable to the presence of semantic duplicates.

## Key Results
- Semantic duplicates exist for 78% of CodeForces problems and 100% of MBPP tasks in the Olmo-3 training corpus; exact duplicates are found for 50% of ZebraLogic tasks.
- Finetuning on semantic duplicates yields ~20% gains on genuinely unseen items from the same benchmark (e.g., MuSR), confirming shallow generalization.
- Training on high-cosine-similarity but non-duplicate data fails to produce such gains, highlighting that semantic equivalence is essential.
- Mixing just 5% semantic duplicates into a clean finetuning set (~500 out of 10k samples) inflates MuSR scores by ~15%, demonstrating that even modest soft contamination substantially biases benchmark results.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Shallow Generalization via In-Distribution Exposure
Training on semantic duplicates of benchmark items improves performance on genuinely held-out items from the same benchmark because the duplicates share the underlying reasoning structure and answer space. The model learns the benchmark's problem manifold rather than building broadly applicable reasoning skills. Evidence: Finetuning on exact or semantic duplicates of MuSR, ZebraLogic, and MBPP yields ~20% gains on unseen halves of the same benchmark (section 4.3). Break condition: If duplicates lack true semantic equivalence, the boost does not occur.

### Mechanism 2: Benchmark Score Inflation from Corpus Scale
As LLM training corpora grow, they inevitably contain many semantically equivalent fragments to major benchmarks, even after exact-match deduplication. This soft contamination acts as distributed training on the benchmark distribution, making high scores partly an artifact of data leakage rather than robust OOD reasoning. Evidence: Ecological valid finetuning with only 5% semantic duplicates yields ~15% gain on MuSR (section 4.4). Break condition: If soft contamination rates were negligible (<0.1%), benchmark scores would remain reliable.

### Mechanism 3: Semantic Equivalence vs. Superficial Similarity
Finetuning on data that is merely topically similar (high cosine similarity) but not semantically equivalent fails to improve benchmark performance. This contrast shows that the model requires exposure to the precise reasoning pattern and answer constraints, not just surface resemblance. Evidence: Across three benchmarks, "cosine similar" finetuning gives negligible gains whereas duplicate finetuning gives large gains (Table 2, Figure 2). Break condition: If a perfect semantic-equivalence detector were applied to the top-k similar items, all selected items would be duplicates and the contrast would vanish.

## Foundational Learning
- **Data Contamination (Exact vs Semantic):** Exact contamination refers to string-identical copies; semantic contamination refers to paraphrased or transformed versions that preserve the core reasoning task. The paper relies on distinguishing these to show that even after exact deduplication, semantic duplicates remain pervasive. Quick check: Give an example of a semantic duplicate of a coding problem that would not be caught by n‑gram filtering.
- **Shallow vs. Genuine Generalization:** Shallow generalization is improvement on benchmark items that share the same distribution as the training data (even if not identical strings), whereas genuine generalization requires out-of-distribution reasoning. The paper argues that many benchmark scores reflect the former. Quick check: Why is improvement on "unseen" benchmark items after training on duplicates still considered "shallow"?
- **Embedding-Based Similarity Search:** Using high-dimensional embeddings (e.g., from llama-embed-nemotron-8b) and cosine similarity to approximate semantic equivalence, because traditional n‑gram overlap cannot detect paraphrased duplicates. Quick check: Why can't traditional n‑gram overlap reliably detect semantic duplicates, and how does cosine similarity help?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Olmo-3 training corpora (Dolma, Dolmino, Dolci) -> Embedding model (llama-embed-nemotron-8b) -> Cosine similarity computation -> Top-k retrieval (e.g., top-100) -> Annotation (Gemini 3 Flash) -> Duplicate classifiers (Qwen-based) -> Synthetic duplicate generation (Claude Opus) -> Finetuning (LoRA on Olmo3 Instruct) -> Evaluation (benchmark pass rates on seen/unseen splits).

**Critical path:**  
1. Sample 1% of pretraining data (stratified) and embed all instruction tuning data; embed benchmark items.  
2. Compute cosine similarities between every benchmark item and corpus chunks; retrieve top-k matches.  
3. Annotate a subset of matches to identify exact/semantic duplicates; train lightweight classifiers to scale prevalence estimates.  
4. Generate synthetic semantic duplicates for selected benchmarks using controlled transformations.  
5. Run finetuning experiments: (a) on duplicates vs controls (cosine-similar non-duplicates); (b) on ecologically valid mixtures (e.g., 5% duplicates in 10k clean samples).  
6. Evaluate models on full benchmarks, comparing "seen" (duplicated) and "unseen" halves and related benchmarks.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Sampling vs. full corpus: Embedding all training data is infeasible; 1% stratified sampling introduces uncertainty but enables scale.  
- Similarity threshold: No universal cosine cutoff; they used top-100 or top-0.1% then annotated, balancing precision/recall.  
- Annotation cost: Manual/LLM annotation is expensive; they later trained lightweight classifiers to scale.  
- Ecological contamination level: Chose 5% based on observed duplicate density; may not generalize to all corpora.  
- Teacher model choice: Different teacher models (Opus 4.5 vs GPT-4.1-mini) affect baseline and gains; introduces variability.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Noisy finetuning results: e.g., Qwen3 ecological experiment showed unexpected gains in "clean" condition, possibly due to leakage or instability.  
- Annotation errors: LLM-based labeling may misclassify borderline cases; residual noise remains even with classifiers.  
- Benchmark saturation: High baseline scores limit headroom to observe contamination-driven improvements.  
- Embedding bias: The embedding model may not capture semantic equivalence equally across domains (e.g., logic puzzles vs code), causing false negatives.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Contamination prevalence:** Quantify exact/semantic duplicate rates across Olmo3's training stages using embedding search + annotation.  
2. **Duplication-driven shallow generalization:** Finetune on exact/semantic duplicates of half of each benchmark and measure gains on the held-out half; compare to finetuning on cosine-similar but non-duplicate data.  
3. **Ecologically valid contamination:** Inject 5% semantic duplicates into a clean 10k-sample finetuning set and measure overall benchmark improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Prevalence in closed-source models:** How prevalent is soft contamination in large proprietary LLMs with inaccessible training corpora? The current study only examined the open-data Olmo3 model; frontier models use much larger corpora, making direct measurement impossible with current methods.
2. **Scaling relationship:** What is the scaling relationship between the proportion of soft contamination in training data and the magnitude of benchmark performance gains? Only a single contamination level (5%) was tested ecologically; the curve across doses remains unknown.
3. **Robust benchmark design:** Can we design benchmarks that are inherently robust to soft contamination while still measuring meaningful generalization? Unclear what principles would prevent accidental semantic duplication in large-scale data without sacrificing ecological validity.
4. **Transfer scope:** Does soft contamination induce narrow, benchmark-specific improvements, or does it transfer to broader reasoning capabilities across related domains? The paper found limited cross-benchmark transfer, but the scope of "related" benchmarks was narrow; comprehensive evaluation across a diverse suite is needed.

## Limitations
- Sampling uncertainty: The 1% pretraining sample may not fully represent the corpus, affecting duplicate rate estimates.
- Dependency on specific models: Results rely on Olmo-3, a single model family; scaling to larger architectures or different training regimes is unknown.
- Annotation and classifier noise: Duplicate detection depends on LLM annotation and subsequent classifiers, which may have false positives/negatives.
- Ecological contamination level: Only 5% was tested; sensitivity to other rates and other benchmark types remains unexplored.
- Teacher model accessibility: Inability to reproduce exact CoT traces due to use of proprietary models (Opus 4.5, GPT-4.1-mini).

## Confidence
- Shallow generalization exists: *Medium* (supported on 3 benchmarks but only one model family).
- Soft contamination inflates scores: *Medium* (ecological gains shown, but real-world scaling may differ).
- Semantic duplicates are necessary, not just topical similarity: *High* (clear control experiment contrast).
