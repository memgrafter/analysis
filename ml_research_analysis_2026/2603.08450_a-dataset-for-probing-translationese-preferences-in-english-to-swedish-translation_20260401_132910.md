# A Dataset for Probing Translationese Preferences in English-to-Swedish Translation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.08450
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08450
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Models show a strong bias toward translationese; without source context, human idiomatic alternatives are preferred only 30–58% of the time, and performance drops when the English source is provided.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the prevalence of translationese—source-language interference—in multilingual large language models (LLMs) translating English to Swedish. To this end, the authors construct a dataset of 600 minimal pairs contrasting machine translations (from OPUS-MT and GPT-5) with human-written idiomatic Swedish alternatives, annotated with fine-grained error tags. By probing a range of smaller multilingual LLMs, they find that models consistently favor literal, translationese-like phrasing. Notably, the inclusion of the English source sentence in the prompt exacerbates this bias, leading to even lower preference for idiomatic human alternatives. Additional context (5–10 preceding sentences) yields only marginal improvements. Error analysis reveals that models overprefer direct translations (DIR) and lexical preference issues (PR) while avoiding clear grammatical errors (GR) or missing words (SAK). Scandinavian-adapted models (ai.se-LLaMA) and larger models exhibit reduced bias, suggesting that scale and domain-specific pretraining can mitigate translationese tendencies.

## Method Summary
The evaluation employs a minimal-pair probing framework using a newly released dataset (`liu-nlp/translationese-opensubtitles`). Each instance comprises an English source sentence, its preceding context (0–10 sentences), a machine-translated Swedish sentence (OPUS-MT or GPT-5), and a human-crafted idiomatic alternative. The authors compute the length-normalized mean log probability (MeanLP) of each Swedish variant given a prompt. Two prompt conditions are tested: (1) no translation context, where the model scores the Swedish sentence alone; (2) with translation context, where the model is instructed to translate the English source (optionally including preceding English sentences). The model is considered to prefer the variant with higher MeanLP. Primary metrics are accuracy (percentage of pairs where the human alternative is preferred) and ΔLP (the relative difference in MeanLP between human and machine translations). No model training is performed; a variety of base and instruction-tuned multilingual LLMs (up to 12B parameters) are evaluated.

## Key Results
- Without source context, models prefer the human idiomatic alternative in only 30–58% of cases.
- Adding the English source sentence as context reduces human preference, indicating source exposure drives literal translation bias.
- Providing 5–10 preceding sentences slightly improves outcomes but does not eliminate the bias; error analysis shows strong preference for direct translations (DIR) and lexical preference issues (PR), while grammatical errors (GR) and missing words (SAK) are avoided.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The presence of the English source sentence in the prompt biases models toward producing literal, translationese output.
- **Mechanism**: When the source text is provided, the model's decoding process may overemphasize alignment with the source tokens, increasing the probability of translations that preserve source structure (even if unnatural in the target language). This stems from training on parallel corpora where the model learns to map source to target; at inference, heavy attention to the source can lead to literalism.
- **Core assumption**: Models are optimized for translation accuracy (preserving meaning) at the cost of target-language fluency, especially when the source is visible.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: "exposure to the English source sentence biases them toward literal translations"; Section 5: "Adding translation context could help the model interpret how an expression is intended, but it may also bias it toward preferring the more literal machine-translated sentence. when no translation context is provided, results are substantially better: accuracy is higher than even in the best translation setups."
- **Break condition**: If models are trained to ignore the source in favor of target-language priors (e.g., via monolingual fine-tuning or decoding constraints), this bias may weaken.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Models have learned to reproduce translationese because their training data contains a large proportion of translated text, leading them to treat translationese as the target language norm.
- **Mechanism**: A significant portion of web-crawled corpora consists of translations. Language models see target-language text that is itself translationese, so the learned distribution deviates from native idiomatic language. The model thus generates output that matches this skewed distribution.
- **Core assumption**: The training corpora for LLMs include substantial amounts of translationese, which shapes the model's internal representation of the target language.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: "large portions of modern web-crawled corpora that constitute the training data of LLMs are translations"; Section 2: "for many languages, it is common to use translated datasets especially for LLM evaluation and instruction tuning"; Section 6: Even without source context, models prefer translationese in 30–58% of cases, suggesting an inherent bias.
- **Break condition**: If training data is filtered to exclude non-native translations, or if models are explicitly trained on native target-language corpora, this bias could be reduced.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Models lack sufficient exposure to idiomatic target-language expressions in context, causing them to default to literal translations or unnatural word choices.
- **Mechanism**: Idiomatic language often requires fine-grained, contextual knowledge that may not be captured by standard parallel corpora or training objectives. Models may learn surface-level mappings but fail to internalize when to use idiomatic alternatives, especially for domain-specific or emotional expressions.
- **Core assumption**: Idiomatic proficiency requires abundant exposure to natural, context-rich target-language text.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 4: Error analysis shows models overprefer direct translations (DIR) and lexical preference issues (PR), but avoid clear grammatical errors (GR) and missing words (SAK); Section 5: Human alternatives are longer and have higher type-token ratio, indicating richer, more idiomatic language; Section 6: "including preceding context in translation prompt guides models towards the human alternative for some samples, but the overall preference toward translationese phrasing remains strong."
- **Break condition**: Increased model scale and Scandinavian adaptation (ai.se-LLaMA) show reduced bias, suggesting that more data and domain-specific training can help; further targeted training on idiomatic expressions may break this pattern.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Translationese**
  - *Why needed*: The paper investigates models' preferences for translationese vs idiomatic alternatives; understanding that translationese refers to source-language interference in translations is fundamental.
  - *Quick check question*: "What are common features of translationese in Swedish translations from English (e.g., article use, noun phrase structure)?"

- **Concept: Minimal pair probing**
  - *Why needed*: The experimental design uses minimal pairs (translationese vs human alternative) to probe model preferences; this methodology controls for content while isolating linguistic differences.
  - *Quick check question*: "How does comparing log-likelihoods of two semantically similar sentences reveal a model's intrinsic biases?"

- **Concept: Error typology (e.g., MQM)**
  - *Why needed*: The dataset annotates errors with fine-grained tags (DIR, PR, SEM, etc.); understanding error categories helps interpret which types of idiomatic failures models struggle with.
  - *Quick check question*: "What is the difference between an error cause (like direct translation) and an error effect (like loss of meaning) in translation evaluation?"

## Architecture Onboarding
Dataset (minimal pairs with source, context, machine/human translations) -> Prompt Templates (no-context: Swedish sentence only; with-context: English source ± context) -> Model Inference (compute log probabilities for both Swedish variants) -> Comparison (MeanLP, human preference) -> Metrics (Accuracy, ΔLP)

### Critical path
1. Dataset construction: select source sentences, generate machine translations (OPUS-MT, GPT-5), write human idiomatic alternatives, annotate with error tags and extract preceding context.
2. Prompt definition: create text prompts for the two scoring conditions (Figure 2).
3. Model inference: for each model, tokenize each Swedish variant under each prompt, sum token log-likelihoods, normalize by token count to obtain MeanLP.
4. Preference decision: compare MeanLP for human vs machine variant per pair.
5. Metrics aggregation: compute overall accuracy and average ΔLP per model/condition; stratify by error tags and context length.

### Design tradeoffs
- Minimal pairs control for content but may not capture full translation variability; pairs are curated for clear distinction.
- Length-normalization mitigates bias toward longer sentences but may still have edge cases.
- Custom error tags capture nuances of idiomatic issues but differ from standard MQM, limiting comparability.
- Small dataset (600 pairs) allows detailed annotation but limits statistical power and domain diversity.
- Model selection focuses on smaller models due to compute constraints; results may not generalize to much larger models.

### Failure signatures
- Models prefer translationese >50% even without source (high baseline bias).
- Adding source context does not improve human preference and often worsens it.
- Error tag analysis shows high model preference for DIR/PR but low for GR/SAK; if models also struggle with GR/SAK, it indicates deeper issues.
- Scandinavian-adapted models show no improvement over base in some settings, contradicting the adaptation hypothesis.

### First 3 experiments
1. Replicate the no-context vs with-context preference experiment on a different language pair (e.g., English-to-German) to test generalizability of source bias.
2. Fine-tune a base model on native Swedish text (e.g., Swedish Wikipedia) and evaluate whether translationese preference decreases using the same probing tasks.
3. Extend the dataset to other domains (e.g., news, formal documents) to assess if translationese bias varies with genre; incorporate domain-specific error tags.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does the translationese bias vary across different textual domains (e.g., news, literature, technical manuals) beyond the conversational OpenSubtitles domain?
2. To what extent can the custom error-tagging scheme be standardized or aligned with existing frameworks like MQM to enable consistent, large-scale analysis of translationese phenomena?
3. Do the latest generation of large multilingual LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5, Llama 3.1 405B) exhibit a reduced translationese bias compared to the smaller models tested, and does their scale mitigate the counterproductive effect of source context?
4. What is the precise causal mechanism by which providing the English source sentence increases a model's preference for translationese phrasing?
5. How do specific adaptation techniques (e.g., continued pre-training on Scandinavian text, RLHF for naturalness) change the internal representations of translationese vs. idiomatic language, and can these be used to "de-bias" models?

## Limitations
- Findings are limited to English→Swedish and smaller LLMs (≤12B parameters); generalizability to other language pairs, larger models, or different domains remains open.
- The custom error tagging scheme is not fully aligned with standard metrics like MQM, hindering broad comparability.
- The evaluation uses length normalization and minimal pairs, which may not capture all aspects of translation quality or real-world translation scenarios.

## Confidence
- Source context drives literalism: Medium (strong evidence within the experimental setup, but causal mechanism not fully isolated).
- Training data skew toward translationese: High (supported by corpus statistics and prior literature, though direct attribution to the models' training data is not proven in the study).
- Models struggle with idiomatic nuance: Medium (error patterns are clear, but it's unclear whether this stems from data sparsity, model scale, or architectural constraints).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the no-context/with-context experiment on a distantly related language pair (e.g., English→German) using the same minimal-pair protocol to test cross-linguistic generality.
2. Fine-tune a base model (e.g., LLaMA-3-8B) on a large corpus of native Swedish text (e.g., Swedish Wikipedia + literature), then re-run the probing task to isolate training-data effects.
3. Extend the dataset to a formal domain (e.g., news or legal texts) and re-annotate using a standardized metric like MQM; evaluate whether domain shift alters the translationese bias pattern.
