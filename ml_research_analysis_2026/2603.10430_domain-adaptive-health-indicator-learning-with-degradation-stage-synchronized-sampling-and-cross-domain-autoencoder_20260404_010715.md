# Domain-Adaptive Health Indicator Learning with Degradation-Stage Synchronized Sampling and Cross-Domain Autoencoder

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10430  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10430  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** The framework achieves an average 24.1% improvement in Comprehensive Index (CI) over state-of-the-art methods.

## Executive Summary
Domain-adaptive health indicator (HI) learning is critical for machinery prognostics under varying operating conditions but faces two major challenges: (1) random mini-batch sampling often mixes source and target samples from different degradation stages, leading to misleading domain discrepancy losses; (2) small‑kernel 1D‑CNNs, commonly used for vibration signals, have insufficient receptive fields to capture long‑range temporal dependencies inherent in degradation processes. This paper introduces a unified framework that addresses both issues. It proposes Degradation Stage Synchronized Batch Sampling (DSSBS) to align source‑target mini‑batches by degradation stage using kernel change‑point detection, and a Cross‑domain Alignment Fusion Large Autoencoder (CAFLAE) that leverages large‑kernel multi‑scale convolutions and cross‑attention for robust domain‑invariant feature extraction.

Extensive validation on a Korean defense system dataset (KWS) and the XJTU‑SY bearing dataset demonstrates the framework’s effectiveness, achieving an average 24.1% improvement in the Comprehensive Index (CI) compared to state‑of‑the‑art methods. Ablation studies confirm that DSSBS stabilizes training and improves domain alignment, while CAFLAE’s large‑kernel design and cross‑attention mechanism significantly enhance long‑horizon feature learning. The work provides a promising direction for reliable health monitoring across domain shifts.

## Method Summary
The proposed method integrates two synergistic components: Degradation Stage Synchronized Batch Sampling (DSSBS) and the Cross‑domain Alignment Fusion Large Autoencoder (CAFLAE). DSSBS first computes channel‑wise Root Mean Square (RMS) features over sliding windows of the run‑to‑failure (RtF) signals. For the target domain, it applies kernel change‑point detection to identify change points and partition the sequence into degradation stages; the same number of stages is imposed on the source domain. During training, mini‑batches are constructed by sampling equal numbers of source and target windows from the corresponding matched stage index. CAFLAE processes each window through a domain‑specific RevIN normalization layer, patch embedding, and a Parallel Modern multi‑scale Temporal Convolution (PMTC) block featuring large kernels (13, 23, 31). The encoder then employs bidirectional cross‑attention (with domain‑specific query/key/value projections) to fuse features from both domains before global average pooling produces the HI. A symmetric decoder reconstructs the input signal for auxiliary supervision. The model is trained with three losses: a monotonicity constraint (SCF), a maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) on encoder outputs for domain alignment, and a reconstruction loss; their relative weights are dynamically balanced using Dynamic Weighted Averaging (DWA).

## Key Results
- Average 24.1% improvement in Comprehensive Index (CI) over state‑of‑the‑art methods on the Korean Weapon System (KWS) and XJTU‑SY bearing datasets.
- Ablation studies validate that DSSBS reduces MMD loss variance and improves CI by aligning degradation stages, while CAFLAE’s large kernels yield a broader effective receptive field (ERF) and superior feature extraction.
- Removing the cross‑attention fusion module leads to a CI drop of up to 30.8% under large domain shifts (e.g., B2‑4), underscoring the importance of explicit domain feature interaction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Degradation Stage Synchronized Batch Sampling (DSSBS)
**Claim:** Random mini‑batch sampling mixes source and target samples from different degradation stages, causing misleading domain discrepancy losses.  
**Mechanism:** DSSBS uses kernel change‑point detection (KCP) on channel‑wise RMS features to segment each domain’s RtF signal into degradation stages. It then forces mini‑batch sampling to pair source and target windows from the same detected stage index, aligning the distribution of degradation phases during MMD loss computation.  
**Core assumption:** Degradation progress is monotonically consistent within a stage across domains; MMD on matched‑stage features provides a valid alignment signal.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract identifies stage misalignment; Section 2.1 details KCP and synchronous sampling; Section 5.1 ablation shows DSSBS reduces MMD variance and boosts CI.

### Mechanism 2: Large‑Kernel Multi‑Scale Temporal Convolution in CAFLAE
**Claim:** Small‑kernel 1D‑CNNs have limited effective receptive field (ERF), failing to capture long‑range temporal dependencies in degradation signals.  
**Mechanism:** CAFLAE’s encoder replaces small kernels with a Parallel Modern multi‑scale Temporal Convolution (PMTC) block that applies depthwise separable convolutions with large kernels (13, 23, 31). These large kernels expand the ERF within a single layer, enabling aggregation over long time horizons.  
**Core assumption:** Long‑horizon patterns are critical for characterizing degradation; sufficiently large kernels can capture them without excessive depth.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 2.2.1 describes PMTC; Section 3.5.3 ERF analysis shows CAFLAE achieves broader, smoother temporal sensitivity versus small‑kernel baselines.

### Mechanism 3: Cross‑Attention for Domain Feature Fusion
**Claim:** Standard weight‑sharing autoencoders may not fully exploit complementary domain‑specific information during alignment.  
**Mechanism:** After the shared PMTC encoder, CAFLAE computes domain‑specific query/key/value projections and applies bidirectional cross‑attention (source→target and target→source). This allows each domain’s features to attend to the other’s representations, fusing domain‑invariant and domain‑specific cues before HI construction.  
**Core assumption:** Cross‑attention directly relates source and target feature maps, facilitating aligned representations.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 2.2.1 defines cross‑attention equations; Section 5.4 ablation shows CI drop (e.g., 30.8% on B2‑4) when removing cross‑attention.

## Foundational Learning
**Domain Adaptation via Distribution Alignment**  
*Why needed:* To mitigate the domain shift between source (labeled) and target (unlabeled) operating conditions, the framework aligns features via MMD loss on encoder outputs.  
*Quick check:* How does MMD measure distribution discrepancy between source and target embeddings, and why might aligning raw inputs be insufficient?

**Change‑Point Detection for Degradation Segmentation**  
*Why needed:* DSSBS requires accurate segmentation of degradation trajectories into stages; kernel‑based change‑point detection (KCP) identifies statistically homogeneous segments from RMS features.  
*Quick check:* What homogeneity cost J(a,b) does KCP minimize, and how does the penalty term prevent over‑segmentation?

**Multi‑Scale Temporal Convolution**  
*Why needed:* Long‑range temporal dependencies in degradation signals are critical; PMTC combines multiple large kernels to expand the effective receptive field without excessive depth.  
*Quick check:* Why employ depthwise separable convolutions (DWConv+PWConv) in each branch, and how does concatenating multi‑scale outputs benefit representation?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input vibration window → (for DSSBS) channel‑wise RMS → KCP → stage sets → synchronized batch sampler → RevIN (domain‑specific) → Patch embedding → PMTC encoder (DWConv branches + ConvFFN) → Domain‑specific Q/K/V → Bidirectional cross‑attention → Concatenation → Global average pooling → HI head; Decoder: HI → PMTC decoder (transposed DWConv) → Inverse RevIN → Reconstruction.

**Critical path:**
1. Compute RMS over sliding windows for both domains; apply KCP on target to obtain M target stages; impose same M on source to get source stages.
2. For each mini‑batch: choose a stage index m, sample equal numbers of windows from source stage m and target stage m.
3. Process sampled windows through CAFLAE encoder: RevIN → patch embedding → PMTC → cross‑attention fusion.
4. Fuse features, apply GAP, pass through feed‑forward network to output HI.
5. Decode HI via PMTC decoder and inverse RevIN; compute reconstruction loss.
6. Compute SCF (monotonicity), MMD (on PMTC outputs), and reconstruction losses; weight them via DWA and backpropagate.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *PMTC kernel size:* Larger kernels increase receptive field but raise computational cost; multi‑scale mitigates but kernel set must be tuned.
- *Stage count M̂:* Fixed from target to avoid negative transfer; if source degradation rate differs, fixed M may cause misalignment.
- *Cross‑attention:* Adds parameters for Q/K/V projections; limited data may lead to overfitting to domain‑specific noise.

**Failure signatures:**
- *MMD instability:* Without DSSBS, MMD loss oscillates; t‑SNE shows separated source‑target clusters; CI suffers.
- *Inadequate long‑range modeling:* CAFLAE with small kernels yields localized ERF; HI becomes noisy/non‑monotonic; reconstruction degrades on long patterns.
- *Cross‑attention breakdown:* Removing cross‑attention drops CI markedly under large domain shifts, indicating poor fusion of complementary features.
- *HI scaling ambiguity:* The paper’s HI visualization suggests [0, 1] normalization, but exact scaling (post‑processing or training target) is unclear, potentially affecting robustness metrics.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **DSSBS ablation:** Train with and without DSSBS on KWS RC1‑2; monitor MMD loss variance and CI over epochs; expect lower variance and higher CI with DSSBS.
2. **ERF analysis:** Compute gradient‑based effective receptive field for CAFLAE and a small‑kernel baseline (e.g., DCAE) on a normalized batch; visualize and quantify sensitivity width to confirm CAFLAE’s broader temporal coverage.
3. **Cross‑attention ablation:** Disable the cross‑attention module, keep all other components fixed, and retrain on all dataset pairs; compare HI trajectories and CI, especially for high‑shift conditions like B2‑4; anticipate significant CI drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Degradation feature choice:** How does using RMS versus integrated time‑frequency features affect DSSBS stage segmentation accuracy and robustness? (Basis: paper uses RMS but suggests richer features in conclusion.)
2. **Optimal PMTC kernel configuration:** What mixture of kernel sizes (e.g., small + large) best balances short‑term transient capture and long‑term dependency modeling? (Basis: paper notes uniform large kernels may miss short‑term patterns.)
3. **Alternative cross‑domain fusion:** Can other domain adaptation techniques (adversarial, contrastive) surpass cross‑attention for HI generalization? (Basis: cross‑attention was not compared to other paradigms.)
4. **Robustness to continuous degradation:** How does DSSBS perform when degradation stages are gradual or overlapping, violating discrete change‑point assumptions? (Basis: experiments used abrupt failure transitions; continuous degradation not tested.)
5. **Generalization across machinery types:** To what extent does the framework transfer to other industrial assets (gears, pumps, engines) and highly variable environments? (Basis: conclusion calls for further research on diverse equipment.)

## Limitations
- Incomplete reporting of key hyperparameters (e.g., patch length, stride, dropout rates, expansion ratio in ConvFFN, number of attention heads, DWA temperature).
- Insufficient description of the Kernel Change‑Point algorithm’s implementation details (e.g., dynamic programming procedure, penalty integration, handling of edge cases).
- Ambiguity in health indicator scaling and the mapping between life‑cycle index and window‑level predictions for the monotonicity constraint.

## Confidence
- **DSSBS stage synchronization:** Medium confidence (mechanism plausible and ablation supportive, but no direct causal evidence linking synchronized sampling to MMD stabilization).
- **Large‑kernel temporal modeling:** Medium‑High confidence (PMTC design and ERF analysis clearly demonstrate improved receptive field; kernel sizes may still require dataset tuning).
- **Cross‑attention domain fusion:** Medium confidence (ablation shows benefit, especially under large shifts, but lacks comparison to alternative fusion methods).
