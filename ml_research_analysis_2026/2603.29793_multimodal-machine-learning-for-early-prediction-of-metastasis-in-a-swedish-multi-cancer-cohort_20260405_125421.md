# Multimodal Machine Learning for Early Prediction of Metastasis in a Swedish Multi-Cancer Cohort

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.29793  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29793  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Intermediate fusion achieved the highest F1 scores (breast 0.845, colon 0.786, lung 0.819, prostate 0.845); a text-only model was best for lung cancer (F1 0.829). Deep learning outperformed traditional classifiers, and modality importance varied by cancer type.

## Executive Summary
This study developed multimodal machine learning models to predict metastasis onset one month ahead using six months of routine electronic health record (EHR) data from four Swedish cancer cohorts (breast, colon, lung, prostate). The models integrated demographics, laboratory exams, medication records, and clinical notes. Six fusion strategies were compared, with intermediate fusion delivering the best performance across all cohorts except lung, where a text-only model (SweDeClinBERT) slightly outperformed it. Explainability analysis via SHAP revealed that the most predictive modality differed by cancer type: text for lung and colon, static features for breast, and medications for prostate. The work highlights the value of multimodal integration and deep learning for early metastasis prediction, while also noting limitations due to dataset size and the need for external validation.

## Method Summary
The authors used the proprietary SU-ADE corpus from Karolinska University Hospital, selecting patients with at least seven months of EHR history and defining a six-month observation window followed by a one-month prediction horizon for metastasis (ICD-10 C77–C79). Modalities were preprocessed separately: static data (demographics, comorbidities) were one-hot encoded; laboratory and medication data were aggregated into monthly time series; clinical notes were tokenized with the Swedish clinical BERT variant SweDeClinBERT. Unimodal classifiers included traditional models (KNN, logistic regression, gradient boosting) and deep learning architectures (MLP for static data, GRU-based RNNs for time series, Transformer+GRU for text). Fusion strategies comprised early fusion (flattened tabular data without text), late fusion (weighted average of unimodal predictions), and intermediate fusion (concatenation of latent vectors from each modality’s encoder, followed by a joint classifier). Models were trained in two stages (freeze encoders then fine-tune) and selected via nested cross-validation optimizing AUPRC. Explainability used a multimodal SHAP adaptation: features from different modalities and time steps were serialized into a single vector with special delimiters (∞ between modalities, −∞ between time steps) and analyzed with KernelExplainer.

## Key Results
- Intermediate fusion achieved the highest F1 scores: breast 0.845, colon 0.786, lung 0.819, prostate 0.845.
- For lung cancer, the text-only SweDeClinBERT model slightly outperformed intermediate fusion (F1 0.829 vs. 0.819).
- Deep learning classifiers consistently outperformed traditional machine learning models across all cohorts and fusion strategies.
- SHAP analysis showed modality importance was cancer-type specific: text most influential for lung and colon, static features for breast, and medications for prostate.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Intermediate fusion (IF) delivers superior predictive performance by learning both modality-specific representations and cross-modal interactions.
- **Mechanism:** IF employs separate deep learning encoders for each modality (Static, Labs, Medications, Text) to extract latent features. These latent vectors are concatenated and passed through a joint classifier, preserving intra-modality temporal or sequential patterns while explicitly modeling their combined influence on the final prediction.
- **Core assumption:** Predictive signals for metastasis are distributed non-redundantly across heterogeneous data modalities, and their joint interaction contains information not captured by either single modalities or simpler fusion strategies.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states "Intermediate fusion achieved the highest F1 scores... demonstrating strong predictive performance." The results section confirms "Intermediate fusion consistently delivered the best results... IF achieved the best AUPRC and F1 scores in the breast, colon, and prostate cohorts."
- **Break condition:** Performance would degrade if one modality overwhelmingly dominates predictive information or if modalities are extremely sparse/noisy, making the concatenated latent space uninformative.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Deep learning (DL) classifiers consistently outperform traditional models due to their capacity for automatic hierarchical feature extraction from complex, high-dimensional EHR data.
- **Mechanism:** DL architectures (MLP for static data, RNN/GRU for time series, Transformer+GRU for text) learn layered representations directly from raw or minimally preprocessed inputs. This allows them to capture nonlinear relationships, temporal dependencies, and contextual semantics without manual feature engineering.
- **Core assumption:** The underlying patterns indicating imminent metastasis involve complex, nonlinear interactions across features and time that are not efficiently captured by linear or shallow models.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes "Deep learning classifiers consistently outperformed traditional models." The results section reports "In all cohorts, deep learning models consistently outperformed classical models and time series models, both in all single modalities and EF."
- **Break condition:** The advantage would diminish if training data is extremely limited (as in the colon cohort) or if the true decision boundary is inherently simple/linear.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The relative predictive importance of clinical data modalities is cancer-type specific, reflecting differing biological and care pathways.
- **Mechanism:** SHAP analysis on the IF models quantifies each modality's contribution to predictions. Global averages reveal cohort-level modality salience, while local analysis shows patient-level variation. The model learns to weight modalities differently because metastasis manifests through distinct clinical trajectories per cancer type.
- **Core assumption:** The EHR signatures preceding a metastasis code differ across cancers; each cancer type has a characteristic progression pattern that leaves traces in different parts of the record.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states "SHAP analysis revealed that the relative importance of data modalities varied significantly across cancer types, with text being most influential for lung and colon, static features for breast, and medications for prostate." The explainability section reports "Globally... Text was the most relevant modality for colon and lung cancer, Static for breast cancer, and Meds for prostate cancer."
- **Break condition:** If the metastasis definition were highly inconsistent across cancers or if care pathways were standardized regardless of cancer type, modality importance would homogenize.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Multimodal Fusion Strategies (Early, Late, Intermediate)  
  *Why needed here:* The paper’s core innovation is integrating structured (static, labs, meds) and unstructured (text) EHR data. Understanding the trade-offs between EF (flattens time-series, losing temporal structure), LF (preserves modality-specific models but limits cross-modal interactions), and IF (balanced but complex) is essential to interpret why IF won.  
  *Quick check:* Why might early fusion degrade performance for time-series modalities like laboratory results?

- **Concept:** SHAP for Multimodal, Time-Series Data  
  *Why needed here:* Standard SHAP assumes tabular data. The paper’s “serialization” technique (using ∞ and −∞ as delimiters) adapts SHAP to their fused multimodal architecture, enabling explainability across heterogeneous inputs.  
  *Quick check:* How does inserting special values (∞, −∞) into the serialized feature vector allow SHAP to treat time steps or tokens as distinct yet grouped features?

- **Concept:** Evaluation under Class Imbalance & Small Samples  
  *Why needed here:* Cohorts are imbalanced (e.g., prostate 27% positive) and colon is small (n=387). The paper uses AUPRC (not just AUROC) and nested cross-validation to obtain robust estimates, preventing misleadingly high accuracy from majority-class bias.  
  *Quick check:* Why is AUPRC a more meaningful metric than AUROC for the prostate cohort with 27% positive prevalence?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Preprocessing Pipeline -> Unimodal Encoders (Static: MLP; Labs/Medications: RNN with GRU; Text: SweDeClinBERT + GRU) -> Fusion Layer (Intermediate Fusion: concatenation + dense layers; alternatives: Late Fusion: weighted average; Early Fusion: flattened tabular) -> Training Loop (Stage 1: freeze encoders, train fusion; Stage 2: fine-tune end-to-end; hyperparameter search via 5-fold CV) -> Explainability Module (serialization with ∞/−∞ delimiters, SHAP KernelExplainer, aggregation by modality)

**Critical path:**  
1. Validate cohort generation logic (7-month window, metastasis label from C77-C79).  
2. Implement and benchmark each unimodal encoder on a validation set to select the best per modality (as per Appendix Table 3).  
3. Assemble the fusion strategy (IF recommended) using the selected encoders.  
4. Run SHAP serialization and perturbation experiments to validate faithfulness (Appendix: Analysis of SHAP faithfulness).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- IF vs. Complexity: IF gives best performance but requires maintaining three separate encoder architectures and a two-stage training process. LF/EF are simpler but sacrifice cross-modal learning or temporal fidelity.  
- Text inclusion: Text boosts performance (especially lung/colon) but introduces NLP pipeline complexity and potential for “shortcut learning” on metastasis mentions (mitigated by censoring analysis).  
- Evaluation strategy: TRIPOD 2a (single 80-20 split) is efficient but can be unstable on small cohorts (colon). Nested CV provides more stable estimates at computational cost.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Colon cohort performance (F1 0.786) is notably lower; if replication sees a similar drop, check sample size effects and potential data leakage in preprocessing.  
- Medications-only RNN shows high sensitivity but very low specificity (e.g., prostate: Spec 0.277, Sens 0.977) → model may be over-relying on prescription patterns common in end-of-life care.  
- SHAP faithfulness plots (Fig 6): If “High to Low” and “Low to High” curves are nearly identical (as in breast), it suggests no single feature dominates; if “Low to High” rises initially (as in lung), it indicates strong reliance on a few top features.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce unimodal baselines: For each cancer cohort, train the four unimodal classifiers (Static-MLP, Labs-RNN, Meds-RNN, Text-SweDeClinBERT) as per Table 2. Verify DL outperforms traditional on at least 2/4 cohorts.  
2. Build and compare fusion strategies: Using the best unimodal encoders from Experiment 1, construct EF (w/o text), LF (weighted avg), and IF models. Confirm IF achieves highest AUPRC/F1 on breast, colon, prostate; check if Text-only beats IF on lung.  
3. Run SHAP serialization & censoring: Serialize a validation patient’s data, compute SHAP values, and verify modality contributions roughly match Fig 2. Then, censor metastasis terms in text and re-evaluate IF model; expect minimal performance drop for breast/lung, some drop for colon (as in Table 2, IF vs IF(censored)).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to external cohorts and prospective settings:** The study is retrospective and single-institution; validation in diverse populations and real-time deployment is needed to assess translational potential.  
2. **Optimal history window duration:** The study fixed a seven-month inclusion criterion; alternative cutoffs might affect performance and generalizability.  
3. **Cross-cohort transfer learning for rare cancers:** The authors propose leveraging data from common cancers to improve predictions for rarer malignancies with limited samples (e.g., sarcoma, CNS tumors).  
4. **Impact of temporal disease progression and stage stratification:** Models aggregated six months of data without considering stage evolution; incorporating time-to-event and staging could enhance robustness.

## Limitations
- The SU-ADE corpus and the SweDeClinBERT model are not publicly available, hindering independent verification and replication.  
- The colon cancer cohort is relatively small (n=387), raising concerns about overfitting and the reliability of performance estimates.  
- The multimodal SHAP approach relies on a custom serialization trick with limited validation; its faithfulness is only supported by a single perturbation experiment.

## Confidence
- Intermediate fusion superiority: **Medium** (consistent 5‑fold CV results but no external test).  
- Deep learning > traditional classifiers: **Medium** (advantage holds across cohorts, yet traditional methods may be under‑tuned; small colon sample could exaggerate DL gains).  
- Cancer‑type specific modality importance: **Low** (SHAP values for high‑dimensional temporal‑textual data are approximate and sensitive to encoding choices; rankings lack robust statistical testing).

## Next Checks
1. **External temporal validation:** Apply the best intermediate fusion models to a later time slice from Karolinska (if accessible) or to another Swedish EHR collection with identical inclusion criteria; report AUPRC shift to gauge generalizability.  
2. **Leakage audit beyond censoring:** Systematically remove all sentences containing any cancer‑progression keywords (e.g., “recurrence”, “progression”, “stage IV”) and re‑evaluate; also train on notes where metastasis terms are masked to isolate non‑leakage predictive signals.  
3. **SHAP robustness to background choice:** Recompute SHAP values using multiple background datasets (random subset, mean‑imputed vector, and shuffled modalities); assess stability of modality rankings via correlation. Significant variation would invalidate the claim of stable importance.
