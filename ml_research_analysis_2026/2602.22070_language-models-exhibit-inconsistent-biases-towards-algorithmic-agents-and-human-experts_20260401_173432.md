# Language Models Exhibit Inconsistent Biases Towards Algorithmic Agents and Human Experts

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.22070  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22070  
- **Reference count:** 39  
- **Primary result:** LLMs show algorithm aversion in stated trust ratings but algorithm appreciation in betting decisions, revealing inconsistent biases across experimental formats.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models (LLMs) inherit the human tendency known as *algorithm aversion*—the distrust of algorithmic advice even when it outperforms human experts. The authors test eight LLMs using two experimental formats: (1) stated preferences, where models directly rate trust in human experts versus algorithms across 27 tasks; and (2) revealed preferences, where models are shown performance data and asked to place a bet on the better predictor. The results reveal a striking discrepancy: in stated preferences, LLMs consistently rate human experts higher, replicating human algorithm aversion. In revealed preferences, they disproportionately choose algorithms, even when the algorithm is objectively weaker. This stated–revealed gap suggests that LLMs encode inconsistent biases toward algorithmic agents and human experts, raising concerns about their reliability in delegation decisions and highlighting the sensitivity of their outputs to task framing.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct two zero‑shot prompting studies with eight LLMs from four families (OpenAI GPT, Anthropic Claude, Meta Llama). **Study 1** presents 27 task scenarios (e.g., air traffic control, disease diagnosis) and asks the model to assign a trust rating from 1 to 100 for a human expert and an algorithm (order randomized). The trust gap (human rating minus algorithm rating) measures algorithm aversion. **Study 2** uses six binary‑classification tasks (e.g., recidivism, heart disease). For each task, the model receives 10 examples of predictions from two agents along with true outcomes. One agent is strong (90% accuracy) and the other weak (50%), with the agent type (human/algorithm) counterbalanced. The model is instructed to bet $100 on one agent; its choice reveals its delegation preference. Performance data are presented in‑context, and the model must aggregate it to identify the stronger predictor. The two studies together test whether LLMs’ biases are stable across direct evaluation and incentivized decision‑making.

## Key Results
- **Stated aversion:** All eight LLMs gave human experts significantly higher trust ratings than algorithms (mean gaps 5.14–30.68, all *p* < 0.001).
- **Revealed appreciation:** LLMs disproportionately selected algorithms in the betting task, with selection rates up to 69.9% even when the algorithm was weaker.
- **Inconsistency:** Every model (except two) showed opposite biases between studies; the median relative risk of choosing the strong predictor for algorithms versus humans was 2.62, indicating a reversal from stated aversion to revealed appreciation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1  
- **Claim:** LLMs encode a human‑like bias to *state* distrust toward algorithms because their training data contains widespread textual expressions of algorithm aversion from human‑authored content (e.g., surveys, opinion pieces, social commentary).  
- **Mechanism:** During pretraining, the model internalizes statistical correlations between concepts like “algorithm” or “AI” and negative sentiment/trust descriptors when paired with decision‑making contexts. This latent association is activated by direct query prompts that ask for trust ratings, causing the model to reproduce a human‑like aversion pattern.  
- **Core assumption:** The training data sufficiently captures and amplifies documented human algorithm aversion (from economics/psychology literature) to become a retrievable textual pattern for the model.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “When prompted to rate the trustworthiness of human experts and algorithms across diverse tasks, LLMs give higher ratings to the human expert, which correlates with prior results from human respondents.”  
  - [section 4.1] “Stated Aversion... all trust gaps are significantly positive (*p* < 0.001)... five out of eight models have significant positive trust gaps in *all* tasks.”  
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers discuss LLMs simulating human biases (e.g., “LLM Agents Display Human Biases”), but do not pinpoint training data as the specific mechanism for algorithm aversion.  
- **Break condition:** If newer training datasets systematically exclude or reverse narratives around algorithm trust (e.g., more pro‑AI content), the stated aversion could diminish or reverse, as hinted by the 2026 replication showing reduced or reversed gaps.

### Mechanism 2  
- **Claim:** LLMs reveal a preference for algorithms in *delegation* decisions because they overgeneralize the “algorithm = accurate” heuristic from in‑context demonstrations of performance data, failing to properly weigh the countervailing evidence of a stronger human expert.  
- **Mechanism:** In Study 2’s betting prompt, the model engages in pattern matching on the presented performance snippets. The algorithmic agent is often framed as a consistent, numerical predictor (“algorithm”), which may be associated in the model’s training with precision and lack of error. The human expert’s errors or variability may be overweighted relative to overall accuracy, triggering a heuristic to defer to the more “mechanical” source.  
- **Core assumption:** The model’s in‑context learning prioritizes surface‑level framing cues (“algorithm” as technical/consistent) over holistic performance aggregation when making the bet.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “when shown the performance of a human expert and an algorithm and asked to place an incentivized bet between the two, LLMs disproportionately choose the algorithm, even when it performs demonstrably worse.”  
  - [section 4.2] “LLMs exhibit revealed distrust towards humans... claude‑3‑haiku and llama‑3‑8b are the most algorithm‑appreciative, with the latter... always picking the algorithm in the recidivism task.”  
  - [section 4.2] “gpt‑3.5‑turbo, both llama‑3 models, and both claude‑3 models were significantly more likely to choose strong algorithm in *all* of the tasks than the strong human.”  
- **Break condition:** If prompts explicitly force the model to compute accuracy metrics (e.g., “count correct predictions”) or if the human’s performance superiority is made more salient (e.g., larger margin), the algorithm appreciation may decrease. The 2026 results show improved performance, suggesting better reasoning can mitigate this.

### Mechanism 3  
- **Claim:** The inconsistency between stated and revealed preferences arises because the two experimental contexts (direct trust query vs. performance‑based bet) activate distinct, non‑reconciled knowledge pathways in the LLM, leading to format‑dependent bias expression rather than a stable internal attitude.  
- **Mechanism:** “Stated preference” prompts tap into the model’s socio‑linguistic knowledge about trust norms (where human expertise is valorized). “Revealed preference” prompts tap into its pattern‑matching and decision‑heuristic knowledge from technical/quantitative contexts (where algorithms are often presented as superior). These are separate “memories” without a unifying belief state, so the output depends on the task presentation.  
- **Core assumption:** LLMs do not possess a coherent, cross‑context “attitude” toward algorithms; their responses are contextually assembled from relevant training fragments.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “These discrepant results suggest that LLMs may encode inconsistent biases towards humans and algorithms.”  
  - [section 4.3] “We find that RRsr > 1 for all LLMs... indicating that they *state* algorithm aversion... but *reveal* algorithm appreciation.”  
  - [section 5] “their suboptimal choices are also *internally inconsistent* with their stated trust attitudes... the stated‑revealed distinction is rarely made in evaluation frameworks for LLMs.”  
- **Break condition:** If future fine‑tuning or prompting explicitly forces the model to reconcile the two contexts (e.g., “Given your trust ratings and this performance data, who would you bet on?”), consistency might improve. The 2026 results show reduced discrepancy, possibly due to improved reasoning or alignment.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Algorithm Aversion (Behavioral Economics)  
- **Why needed here:** The entire study is framed as testing if LLMs inherit this well‑documented human bias. Understanding the original phenomenon (people distrust algorithms even when they outperform humans) is prerequisite to interpreting the LLM results as a simulation or manifestation of it.  
- **Quick check question:** *What are two key reasons from Dietvorst et al. (2015) why humans might avoid algorithmic advice after seeing it err, and how could those reasons map to an LLM’s “behavior”?*

### Concept: Stated vs. Revealed Preferences  
- **Why needed here:** The paper’s core design contrasts these two measurement methods. Misunderstanding this distinction would lead to conflating the two studies’ results. It’s fundamental to the RQ3 and the paper’s main conclusion about inconsistency.  
- **Quick check question:** *In human economics, why might someone say they trust a human advisor more (stated preference) but actually choose an algorithm when given performance data (revealed preference)? How is this operationalized in Studies 1 and 2?*

### Concept: In‑Context Learning (ICL) in LLMs  
- **Why needed here:** Study 2 relies entirely on the model’s ability to learn from the 10 demonstration examples to infer which agent is stronger. The mechanism for algorithm appreciation likely resides in how the model processes and reasons over this in‑context information.  
- **Quick check question:** *What are two potential failure modes of ICL that could cause a model to choose a worse‑performing agent (the weak algorithm) when shown a stronger human?*

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
1. **Task & Agent Schema:** Hardcoded list of 27 tasks (Study 1) and 6 tasks (Study 2) with predefined “human expert” and “algorithm” roles.  
2. **Prompt Generator:** Produces two prompt templates: one for direct trust ratings (Study 1), one for betting with performance examples (Study 2). Handles randomization of agent order and task sequencing.  
3. **Model Interface Layer:** Wrapper for API calls (OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta via HuggingFace). Manages parameters (temperature=0.3, top‑p=0.99), enforces JSON response format, handles retries.  
4. **Response Parser:** Extracts numeric trust ratings (Study 1) or choice string (“Algorithm”/“Human”) (Study 2) from model JSON output. Flags “neutral” responses.  
5. **Analysis Engine:** Computes trust gaps (Study 1), choice probabilities (Study 2), relative risks (RR$_{ah}$), fits mixed‑effects logistic regressions.  
6. **Robustness Module:** Generates alternative prompt framings (LLM agent, expert algorithm, two algorithms) and repeats experiments.

**Critical path:** Task List → Prompt Generator → Model Interface → Response Parser → Analysis Engine → Figures/Tables. Any break in parsing (invalid JSON) or analysis (incorrect gap calculation) invalidates the pipeline.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Within‑subjects vs. Between‑subjects:* Paper uses within‑subjects for LLM‑as‑subject (asks about both agents per trial) to match Study 2 and enable direct comparison. Tradeoff: higher risk of ordering effects (mitigated by randomizing agent order).  
- *Prompt Fidelity vs. Comparability:* They adapted human study prompts but did not make them identical across studies (e.g., betting vs. survey question). Tradeoff: ecological validity for human studies vs. direct model comparison. They acknowledge this limits causal claims about inconsistency.  
- *Model Selection:* Chose “smaller” and “larger” models from 4 families in 2024. Tradeoff: breadth across families vs. depth of newer models. Led to replication crisis in 2026, showing the tradeoff between snapshot findings and longitudinal stability.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. **Uniform Responses:** Model outputs same trust rating or choice for all tasks/conditions. Suggests prompt failed to engage task‑specific reasoning or model is stuck in a default mode.  
2. **High Neutral Rate:** Especially in Study 2, >20% “neutral” or non‑committal responses. Indicates model is uncertain or the betting prompt is ambiguous. Paper notes gpt‑4‑turbo had 94% of neutrals.  
3. **Random Choice in Study 2:** P(choose stronger predictor) ≈ 0.5 for all tasks. Suggests model is not aggregating performance data correctly, failing the ICL requirement.  
4. **Reverse Pattern in Robustness Check:** If changing agent labels (to “A”/“B”) causes systematic preference, it indicates labeling bias, not human/algorithm bias.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate Study 1 on a single model & single task:** Use the exact prompt for “air traffic” task with gpt‑3.5‑turbo, n=10. Verify you get a positive human‑algorithm trust gap. Check JSON parsing.  
2. **Replicate Study 2 on a single model & single task (Strong Human condition):** Use “heart disease” prompt with strong human (90%), weak algorithm (50%). n=10. Verify the model chooses the *algorithm* >50% of the time, demonstrating algorithm appreciation.  
3. **Run both studies on the same model & task (e.g., “recidivism”):** Generate the stated trust gap (Study 1) and revealed choice (Study 2). Calculate RR$_{sr}$ for this one task‑model pair. Expect RR$_{sr}$ >1 (stated aversion, revealed appreciation). This confirms the inconsistency at the micro‑level before aggregating.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** What underlying mechanisms (e.g., training data biases, tool‑use fine‑tuning) cause LLMs to exhibit inconsistent algorithm aversion between stated and revealed preferences?  
- *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The Discussion states, “the underlying mechanisms leading LLMs to obtain these inconsistent behaviors... remain unclear and require future investigation.”  
- *Why unresolved:* The paper documents the discrepancy but does not isolate causal factors like pretraining data distribution or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) objectives.  
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Ablation studies comparing models trained with/without algorithm‑specific data or tool‑use capabilities, measuring bias consistency across experimental formats.

**Open Question 2:** How stable are LLMs’ algorithm‑related biases over successive model generations, and what drives changes in bias direction or magnitude?  
- *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The subsection “Interpreting Static Evaluations of Evolving Models” notes shifts in newer models’ behaviors and calls for “continuous evaluation and benchmarking.”  
- *Why unresolved:* The paper compares only two snapshots (mid‑2024 vs. early‑2026) without systematic tracking; causes (e.g., data updates, architecture changes) remain speculative.  
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Longitudinal studies tracking the same model families across multiple releases with standardized tasks and statistical models linking changes to training data or objective modifications.

**Open Question 3:** In multi‑agent systems where LLMs interact with other algorithmic agents, how do their inconsistent biases affect collaborative decision‑making and system performance?  
- *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The Discussion asks, “If LLMs are inconsistently algorithm‑averse or algorithm‑appreciative... how would their performance be impacted if other agents are revealed to be artificial?”  
- *Why unresolved:* The study only tests single‑LLM delegation between human and algorithm; multi‑agent dynamics (e.g., peer LLMs, hybrid human‑AI teams) are not explored.  
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Experiments simulating multi‑agent environments (e.g., LLMs bargaining, delegating, or debating with agents of varying perceived origins) and measuring outcome quality, robustness, and bias propagation.

**Open Question 4:** Which aspects of task framing and prompt design most reliably elicit consistent bias assessments from LLMs, and can standardized evaluation protocols mitigate format sensitivity?  
- *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The abstract and Discussion emphasize “the sensitivity of LLMs to task presentation formats that should be broadly scrutinized in evaluation robustness.”  
- *Why unresolved:* The paper shows sensitivity (e.g., wording “algorithm” vs. “LLM agent”) but does not develop or test standardized prompts or cross‑format validation frameworks.  
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic prompt‑variation studies across diverse tasks identifying invariant assessment criteria, followed by the creation and adoption of benchmark suites with controlled framing for fair comparisons.

## Limitations
- Exact datasets and synthetic performance generation details for Study 2 are unreported, preventing precise replication.  
- Randomization seeds, trial counts per condition, and agent labeling procedures are unspecified, limiting reproducibility.  
- Mechanistic claims are not directly tested; they rely on indirect evidence from patterns in model outputs.

## Confidence
- **Stated aversion (Study 1):** High – all eight LLMs showed significantly positive trust gaps.  
- **Revealed appreciation (Study 2):** Medium – models favored algorithms but with notable neutral responses and framing sensitivity.  
- **Inconsistency across formats:** Medium – all models exhibited RR$_{sr}$ > 1, though the effect size varied with model age.  
- **Mechanistic accounts:** Low – proposed mechanisms are plausible but lack direct experimental validation.

## Next Checks
1. **Exact replication of Study 2 with fixed dataset:** Use a publicly available dataset for each of the six tasks; generate exactly 10 instances per trial; enforce strong agent 90% accuracy via deterministic label flipping; log all randomization parameters.  
2. **Within‑subject reconciliation trial:** After obtaining a trust rating in Study 1 for a given task, immediately ask the same model (same session) to place a bet on that task’s strong/weak agents. Measure alignment between stated and revealed preferences on a per‑task basis.  
3. **Probe model internals:** Use diagnostic prompts or activation analysis to test whether “human” and “algorithm” labels trigger distinct contextual representations in the two formats, e.g., by measuring attention to label tokens in the betting prompt.
