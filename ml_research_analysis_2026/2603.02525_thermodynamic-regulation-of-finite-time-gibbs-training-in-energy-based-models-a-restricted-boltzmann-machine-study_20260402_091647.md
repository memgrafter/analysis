# Thermodynamic Regulation of Finite-Time Gibbs Training in Energy-Based Models: A Restricted Boltzmann Machine Study

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02525
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02525
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Self-regulated RBM improves AIS effective sample size (310.97 vs 65.23) and test log-likelihood (-684.56 vs -714.29) on MNIST.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses instability in Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM) training caused by fixed-temperature Gibbs sampling, which can lead to asymptotic freezing and parameter drift when effective fields grow relative to temperature. It introduces an endogenous thermodynamic regulation framework where temperature evolves as a dynamical state variable via feedback from flip-rate and Cesàro-averaged free-energy statistics. Under local Lipschitz conditions and two-time-scale separation, the method guarantees local exponential stability of the thermodynamic subsystem and global parameter boundedness with positive ℓ2 regularization. On MNIST, the self-regulated RBM substantially improves sampling efficiency (AIS ESS) and test log-likelihood while maintaining reconstruction quality, confirming that adaptive temperature control stabilizes finite-time Gibbs training without harming generative performance.

## Method Summary
The method replaces fixed-temperature Gibbs sampling with a hybrid temperature regulation scheme. Temperature \(T_t\) is controlled via two coupled state variables: \(\lambda_t\) (log-temperature) updated by flip-rate feedback, and a macroscopic term from the Cesàro-averaged free-energy gap \(\Delta F_t\). Specifically, \(\lambda\) evolves as \(\lambda_{t+1} = \lambda_t - \eta_\lambda (r_t - c_t)\), where \(r_t\) is the observed flip rate and \(c_t\) is a smoothed reference, while \(T_t = e^{\lambda_t} + \kappa|\Delta F_t|\). Parameter updates use Contrastive Divergence (CD-K) with Gibbs chains run at the current \(T_t\). The analysis assumes a two-time-scale separation: thermodynamic updates (\(\eta_\lambda\)) are much faster than parameter updates (\(\eta\)), and relies on local Lipschitz continuity of the expected flip rate in \(\lambda\).

## Key Results
- AIS effective sample size improves dramatically: mean 310.97 (adaptive) vs 65.23 (fixed \(T=1\)).
- Test log-likelihood significantly better: -684.56 vs -714.29.
- Reconstruction error remains comparable: 0.016073 vs 0.016797.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Fixed-temperature Gibbs sampling in RBMs can freeze asymptotically if effective fields grow relative to temperature. As weights increase during training, energy differences amplify, causing flip probabilities to decay exponentially. If any effective-field component diverges (\(\limsup \beta_{\text{field},t} = \infty\)), the average flip rate \(r_t \to 0\), leading to conductance collapse and degenerate negative-phase samples. This is supported by Theorem 1 and Lemma 2, linking effective-field divergence to \(r_t \to 0\) and \(\Phi_t \to 0\). ℓ2 regularization alone can bound parameters but does not guarantee mixing; temperature must also prevent \(\beta_{\text{field}}\) from growing too large.

**Mechanism 2:** The adaptive temperature control law stabilizes the thermodynamic subsystem locally exponentially. Under a frozen-parameter mean-field approximation, the \((\lambda, c)\) subsystem forms a discrete-time nonlinear system. Its Jacobian \(J\) must have spectral radius \(\rho(J) < 1\) for local exponential stability. This condition depends on the flip-rate sensitivity \(s = \partial_\lambda r(\theta^*, \lambda^*)\) and damping factors. The two-time-scale separation (\(\eta \ll \eta_\lambda\)) justifies the frozen approximation. Theorem 5 and Proposition 1 formalize this, showing that with \(\rho(J) < 1\) and bounded noise, \((\lambda_t, c_t)\) remains bounded almost surely. If \(s\) is too large or timescales are not separated, stability fails.

**Mechanism 3:** The hybrid rule combining flip-rate feedback and free-energy gap prevents \(\beta_{\text{field},t}\) from diverging within a forward-invariant set. The flip-rate component maintains \(r_t > 0\), while the \(\kappa|\Delta F_t|\) term adds a macroscopic safeguard against persistent energy imbalance. Together, they keep \(\beta_{\text{norm},t} = \|\theta_t\|/T_t\) bounded. With positive ℓ2 regularization (\(\psi > 0\)), Theorem 4 gives global parameter boundedness. Theorem 7 shows that if \(\liminf R_t > 0\), then \(\beta_{\text{norm},t}\) remains bounded, which the hybrid design ensures. Zero regularization or absence of the macroscopic term can still allow drift.

## Foundational Learning
- **Gibbs sampling in RBMs:** Alternating conditional updates generate model samples for the negative phase. It is needed because RBM training relies on approximating the model distribution via Markov chains. *Quick check:* How does \(p(h_j=1|v)\) change when effective field \(x\) increases at fixed \(T\)?
- **Contrastive Divergence (CD-K):** An estimator using short Gibbs chains to approximate the gradient. It is the core update rule in RBM training. *Quick check:* What happens to CD gradient if Gibbs chain produces no flips?
- **Effective field and inverse temperature:** The quantity \(\beta_{\text{field}} = |x|/T\) governs flip probability suppression. It explains why fixed \(T\) can cause freezing as \(\|W\|\) grows. *Quick check:* If \(\|W_t\|\) grows but \(T_t\) grows proportionally, does \(\beta_{\text{field}}\) necessarily increase?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data minibatch → positive phase (\(v_{\text{data}}, h_{\text{data}}\)) → Gibbs chain at \(T_t\) (K steps) → compute \(r_t\) and \(\Delta F_t\) → update \((\lambda_t, c_t)\) → set \(T_{t+1}\) → CD parameter update using negative phase samples → repeat.

**Critical path:** The temperature state \((\lambda, c)\) evolves per minibatch based on Gibbs chain statistics (\(r_t, \Delta F_t\)). The parameter update uses Gibbs samples at the current \(T_t\). The timescale separation means \(\lambda\) updates much faster than \(\theta\), allowing the thermodynamic subsystem to quasi-equilibrate.

**Design tradeoffs:** \(\eta_\lambda\) (control gain): high causes oscillations, low slows response. \(\alpha\) (smoothing): high reduces noise but slows adaptation to drift. \(\kappa\) (macro coupling): balances micro activity and macro energy consistency. \(K\) (Gibbs steps): longer chains improve sampling but increase compute; short chains risk poor mixing.

**Failure signatures:** \(r_t \to 0\) over epochs → freezing (check \(T_t\), \(\beta_{\text{field}}\)). \(\Delta F_t\) persistently large → energy imbalance (increase \(\kappa\) or adjust \(\eta_\lambda\)). Parameter norms growing linearly → \(\beta_{\text{field}}\) divergence (ensure \(\psi > 0\), check temperature response).

**First 3 experiments:** 1. Compare fixed \(T=1\) vs adaptive on MNIST (AIS ESS, test LL, \(r_t\) trajectory). 2. Vary \(\eta_\lambda\) across orders of magnitude to assess stability and performance. 3. Ablate ℓ2 regularization (\(\psi_W=0\)) to test if adaptive \(T\) alone prevents drift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
While the paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions, its limitations and suggested future work point to several important directions:
- How do the theoretical assumptions (two-time-scale separation, local Lipschitz conditions) hold under common learning-rate schedules that decay over time?
- Can the stability analysis be extended to global convergence guarantees, and how does the algorithm behave near poor local minima?
- What is the impact of noise in the free-energy gap estimation on the stability and performance of the adaptive temperature controller?
- Does the method scale to larger, more complex datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10, SVHN) and deeper energy-based models?
- How sensitive is the method to the hyperparameters \(\eta_\lambda\), \(\alpha\), \(\kappa\), and \(K\), and are there principled ways to set them?

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees require strict two-time-scale separation and local Lipschitz conditions, which may not hold with typical learning-rate schedules.
- Stability analysis is local; global convergence and avoidance of poor minima are not addressed.
- Hybrid rule depends on \(\Delta F_t\) estimation; its statistical noise could destabilize the controller.
- Experiments limited to MNIST and modest models; scalability unproven.
- Additional hyperparameters (\(\eta_\lambda, \alpha, \kappa\)) increase tuning burden.
