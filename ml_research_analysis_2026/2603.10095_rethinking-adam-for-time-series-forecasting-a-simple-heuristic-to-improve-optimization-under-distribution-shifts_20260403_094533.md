# Rethinking Adam for Time Series Forecasting: A Simple Heuristic to Improve Optimization under Distribution Shifts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10095  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10095  
- **Reference count:** 8  
- **Primary result:** TS_Adam reduces MSE by 12.8% and MAE by 5.7% on ETT datasets with MICN, outperforming Adam and generalizing across models and datasets.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies that Adam’s second‑order bias correction (scaling by \(1/(1-\beta_2^t)\)) suppresses the effective learning rate during most of training, limiting the optimizer’s responsiveness to persistent distributional drift in non‑stationary time series forecasting. To address this, they propose TS_Adam, a simple modification that removes the second‑order bias correction, keeping the effective learning rate close to the base rate. On the ETT datasets with the MICN model, TS_Adam achieves an average reduction of 12.8% in MSE and 5.7% in MAE compared to standard Adam. Improvements generalize across multiple models (PatchTST, SegRNN) and datasets (ETT, M4, ECL, Weather), and statistical significance is confirmed via pairwise t‑tests. Theoretically, the modification shifts the dynamic regret trade‑off to prioritize drift suppression over noise suppression in the non‑stationary setting.

## Method Summary
TS_Adam is a drop‑in replacement for Adam. After computing the first‑moment estimate \(m_t\) and the second‑moment estimate \(v_t\), it **skips** the bias correction for \(v_t\) (i.e., does not divide by \(1-\beta_2^t\)). The parameter update uses \(\hat{m}_t = m_t / (1-\beta_1^t)\) (bias‑corrected momentum) and the raw \(v_t\) in the denominator:
\[
\theta \gets \theta - \alpha \cdot \frac{\hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{v_t} + \epsilon}.
\]
All other Adam logic (bias correction for \(m_t\), weight decay if any) remains unchanged. No extra hyperparameters are introduced; the same base learning rate \(\alpha\), \(\beta_1\), \(\beta_2\), and \(\epsilon\) are used as in the standard Adam configuration.

## Key Results
- On ETT datasets with MICN, TS_Adam yields an average **MSE reduction of 12.8%** and **MAE reduction of 5.7%** over Adam.
- Gains generalize to other models (PatchTST, SegRNN) and datasets (ETT, M4, ECL, Weather), with **statistical significance** (pairwise t‑tests).
- TS_Adam also reduces cumulative dynamic regret in synthetic non‑stationary settings, confirming the theoretical advantage.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**1. Bias correction suppresses effective learning rate**  
Adam’s second‑order bias correction scales the per‑step learning rate by \(\eta_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{(1-\beta_2^t)/(1-\beta_1^t)}\). Because \(\beta_2 \approx 0.999\), \(\eta_{\text{eff}} \ll 1\) for most of training. This limits the weight of the drift term \(\delta \sum 1/\eta_t\) in the dynamic regret bound (Theorem 2), causing regret from a shifting objective to accumulate. Break condition: if the problem is stationary (\(\delta \approx 0\)), the bias correction’s noise suppression would outweigh its responsiveness cost.

**2. Removing correction keeps effective learning rate high**  
Without bias‑correcting \(v_t\), the modulation term becomes \(\sqrt{(1-\beta_1^t)/(1-\beta_1^t)} \approx 1\) quickly (since \(\beta_1\) decays faster). Thus \(\eta_{\text{eff}} \approx \alpha\), shifting the optimizer to the “drift‑prioritizing” side of the trade‑off in Theorem 2. This reduces the impact of dynamic variation at the possible cost of a larger noise term. Empirical cumulative regret curves (Fig. 4) show TS_Adam accumulates less total regret, driven by better drift handling despite slightly noisier early updates. Break condition: extremely noisy gradients (heavy‑tailed, high mini‑batch variance) could make the increased noise term dominate.

**3. Generality across models and datasets**  
The modification is model‑agnostic; it alters only the optimizer’s state update. The underlying cause of non‑stationarity (e.g., evolving seasonal means/variances as per Theorem 1’s STL model) creates persistent drift (\(\delta\)) that is common across real‑world forecasting tasks. This explains consistent improvements on MICN, PatchTST, SegRNN and across ETT, M4, ECL, Weather. However, the benefit diminishes on trend‑dominated series with low seasonal contribution, as the authors note.

## Foundational Learning
- **Adaptive Optimizer State Updates**  
  *Why needed:* To understand what TS_Adam changes, one must know Adam’s state: momentum (\(m_t\)) and scaled squared gradient (\(v_t\)) with bias corrections \((1-\beta^t)\). The modification targets only the second‑order correction.  
  *Quick check:* What is the purpose of the \(1/(1-\beta_2^t)\) term in Adam’s denominator during early training steps?

- **Dynamic Regret in Online Learning**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s theoretical justification (Theorem 2) frames optimizer performance under non‑stationarity via dynamic regret, a sum of per‑round losses minus losses of the best *per‑round* comparator. This captures tracking a moving target.  
  *Quick check:* In Eq. (2)’s bound, what does the term \(\delta \sum 1/\eta_t\) represent, and how does \(\eta_{\text{eff}}\) influence it?

- **Time Series Non‑Stationarity & STL Decomposition**  
  *Why needed:* The paper models non‑stationarity via STL (trend, seasonal, residual). Theorem 1 shows how seasonal periodicity (\(S_t\)) and trend accumulation (\(\sum \varepsilon\)) induce time‑dependent mean/variance, justifying persistent drift (\(\delta\)).  
  *Quick check:* According to Theorem 1, why does the variance of \(Y_t\) increase with \(t\) even if innovations (\(\varepsilon_t, R_t\)) are i.i.d.?

- **Bias Correction as Variance Stabilizer**  
  *Why needed:* Adam’s initial \(\eta_{\text{eff}} \ll 1\) stabilizes updates against high variance in \(v_t\) when gradients are noisy or sparse. TS_Adam trades this early stability for long‑term adaptability.  
  *Quick check:* If \(\beta_2\) is very close to 1, what happens to the bias‑corrected variance estimate \(\hat{v}_t\) in the first 1000 steps compared to the raw \(v_t\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
TS_Adam is a direct replacement for `torch.optim.Adam` in the training script, interfacing with the model’s `parameters()` and receiving `loss.backward()` gradients. No changes to model architecture, data loading, or loss function are needed.  
*Plain text representation:* `TrainingScript -> Optimizer(TS_Adam) -> Model`.

**Critical path:**  
1. Instantiate `TS_Adam(model.parameters(), lr=α, betas=(β1, β2))` instead of `Adam`.  
2. In the optimizer’s `step()` method, skip the line `v̂ = v / (1-β2^t)`; use raw `v` directly.  
3. Perform parameter update: `θ -= α * m̂ / (√v + ε)`. All other Adam logic (momentum update, bias correction of `m`) remains identical.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
*Pros:* ~8.3% FLOP reduction per step (no division), better adaptation to drifting distributions, consistent improvements on seasonal/drifting datasets.  
*Cons:* Potentially noisier/less stable early updates; may not help (or could slightly hurt) on very small datasets or near‑stationary series; benefit magnitude correlates with seasonal strength.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Training loss/validation metric curves show high variance or divergence in the first 5–10 epochs, which should stabilize later.  
- No performance gain (or regression) on datasets with low seasonal contribution and high trend (e.g., ETTh2 vs ETTh1 patterns).  
- Improvements vanish when using a very small learning rate (\(\alpha \le 5\text{e}-5\)).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Run MICN on ETT (e.g., ETTh1) with both Adam (default hyperparams: lr=1e‑3, β1=0.9, β2=0.999) and TS_Adam (same hyperparams). Expect ~10% MSE reduction on ETTh1. Verify no extra code needed beyond optimizer swap.  
2. **Generalization check:** Apply TS_Adam to PatchTST on M4‑Hourly. Expect consistent SMAPE/MASE reduction (~5%). Confirms model‑agnostic effect.  
3. **Sensitivity probe:** Sweep β1 ∈ {0.8, 0.9, 0.95} and lr ∈ {1e‑4, 1e‑3} on ETTh1 with MICN. TS_Adam should be robust; best gains at higher lr. If performance collapses at β1=0.95, note potential over‑smoothing of momentum reduces benefit.
