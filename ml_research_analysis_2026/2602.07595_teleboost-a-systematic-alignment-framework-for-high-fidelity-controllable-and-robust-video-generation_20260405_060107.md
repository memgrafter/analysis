# TeleBoost: A Systematic Alignment Framework for High-Fidelity, Controllable, and Robust Video Generation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07595
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07595
- Reference count: 15
- Primary result: TeleBoost outperforms Wan2.2-14B I2V with 71.18% overall win rate (32.71% margin), and gains 24.90% margin in motion quality and 24.13% margin in text alignment.

## Executive Summary
TeleBoost addresses the key challenges of video post‑training—high rollout costs, temporally compounding errors, and uncertain feedback—through a systematic three‑stage pipeline. Stage I Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) establishes a stable, controllable policy by integrating spatial and physics constraints. Stage II Group‑Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with modular extensions (ViPO, BPGO, Self‑Paced, Joint Reward) improves perceptual fidelity and temporal coherence under automatic, noisy rewards. Stage III Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) refines holistic quality using human preferences. Against Wan2.2‑14B I2V, TeleBoost achieves a 71.18% win rate overall with substantial margins in motion quality (24.90%) and text alignment (24.13%), confirming gains in spatial consistency, temporal coherence, and alignment.

## Method Summary
TeleBoost post‑trains a pretrained video diffusion backbone via three stages. SFT fine‑tunes the decoder (encoder frozen) with instruction supervision, spatial‑structure‑aware loss reweighting, and an auxiliary optical‑flow branch for physics, yielding a stable, controllable reference policy. GRPO then optimizes the policy without a value critic by grouping prompts and computing advantages within each group. Key innovations include: ViPO, which uses a frozen visual backbone to produce spatiotemporal advantage maps for localized gradient weighting; BPGO, which applies Bayesian priors to scale group trust and renormalize rewards; Self‑Paced GRPO, which dynamically adjusts the curriculum based on reward statistics; and Joint Reward, which balances multiple objectives via minimum‑norm weighting at the advantage level. Finally, DPO refines the model using human preference pairs—constructed from policy‑on‑policy hard negatives and synthetic temporal perturbations—with a dynamic β schedule to control KL divergence. The framework employs Ray‑based orchestration for parallel rollouts and memory‑efficient DPO training.

## Key Results
- Overall win rate of 71.18% vs. Wan2.2‑14B I2V (32.71% margin).
- Motion quality improvement: 24.90% margin.
- Text alignment improvement: 24.13% margin.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Supervised Fine‑Tuning with Spatial and Physical Constraints
SFT establishes a stable reference policy by integrating structural priors. It combines instruction/control supervision, spatial‑structure–aware loss reweighting to preserve global geometry under camera motion, and physics‑aware motion supervision via an auxiliary optical flow branch. Applied at the decoder level with a frozen backbone, SFT shapes the feasible behavior space early, preventing disruptive failure modes that could overwhelm noisy RL feedback. Over‑regularization or noisy structural signals may limit diversity or misguide optimization.

### Mechanism 2: Group‑Relative RL with Modular Refinements
GRPO with modular extensions addresses video RL challenges such as spatiotemporal credit assignment, ambiguous feedback, reward saturation, and multi‑objective trade‑offs. GRPO uses group‑relative advantages to avoid value critics. ViPO leverages a frozen visual backbone to produce spatiotemporal advantage maps, weighting gradients by local importance. BPGO applies Bayesian priors to scale group trust and renormalize rewards. Self‑Paced GRPO dynamically adjusts the curriculum based on reward statistics. Joint Reward balances multiple objectives via minimum‑norm weighting. These modules collectively enhance stability and sample efficiency, though they introduce computational overhead and potential instability if misconfigured.

### Mechanism 3: Direct Preference Optimization for Holistic Alignment
DPO refines the policy using human preference pairs to capture nuanced qualities like cinematic pacing and aesthetic composition. It optimizes a Bradley‑Terry objective with a KL penalty against the Stage II reference policy. The preference dataset includes policy‑on‑policy hard negatives, synthetic temporal perturbations, and human‑aligned rankings, and uses a dynamic β schedule to balance exploration and stability. This holistic signal corrects distributional shifts from earlier stages, though noisy preferences or poorly tuned KL constraints can lead to reward hacking or collapse.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Group‑relative policy optimization (GRPO)**
  - Why needed here: Stage II relies on GRPO as a critic‑free RL backbone that scales to high‑dimensional video rollouts by using intra‑group score normalization, avoiding unstable value function estimation.
  - Quick check question: Why is GRPO preferred over PPO for video generation post‑training? (Answer: Eliminates need for a value critic, which is hard to train stably under distribution shifts in high‑dimensional video spaces.)
- **Concept: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**
  - Why needed here: DPO provides an offline alignment mechanism that avoids explicit reward modeling and is well‑suited for diffusion‑based video generators, enabling final holistic refinement.
  - Quick check question: How does DPO avoid the need for a separate reward model, and what is its relationship to KL‑regularization? (Answer: Implicitly defines a reward via the preference data and optimizes a KL‑constrained objective directly on policy.)
- **Concept: Spatiotemporal credit assignment**
  - Why needed here: Video defects are localized; without structured feedback (e.g., ViPO), gradient signals are too coarse to correct specific artifacts efficiently.
  - Quick check question: Why can't scalar video‑level rewards effectively guide the model to fix a localized temporal artifact like a hand collapse? (Answer: Scalar rewards lack spatial/temporal resolution, causing credit assignment problems where the model cannot identify which frames or pixels caused the low score.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
Pretrained Backbone -> SFT -> GRPO -> DPO -> Final Model
Components:
- SFT: instruction supervision, spatial‑structure loss reweighting, physics‑aware branch
- GRPO: rollout generator, reward ensemble, ViPO, BPGO, Self‑Paced scheduler, Joint Reward balancer
- DPO: preference dataset, dynamic β loss, reference model
Infrastructure: Ray‑based orchestration, MPS, memory‑efficient DGB
- **Critical path**:
  1. Initialize from pretrained backbone.
  2. Run SFT until convergence on combined objectives (instructions + structure + physics); save reference policy π_ref_SFT.
  3. For GRPO: each iteration samples a prompt group, generates rollouts, computes rewards (VLM, motion, etc.), applies BPGO trust allocation, computes ViPO advantage maps, aggregates with Joint Reward, updates policy with KL constraint, and monitors self‑paced metrics to adjust reward curriculum.
  4. After GRPO convergence, collect preference pairs (policy‑on‑policy, synthetic negatives, human) and train DPO with π_ref_GRPO as reference using a dynamic β schedule.
  5. Deploy the final model.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - SFT: Strong structural priors improve stability but may limit diversity; balance via loss reweighting strength.
  - GRPO: Larger group sizes improve advantage estimation but increase rollout cost; modular extensions add compute overhead but improve sample efficiency.
  - DPO: High‑quality preference data is expensive; synthetic negatives are cheap but may not capture all failure modes. KL constraint β trades off alignment vs. stability.
  - Infrastructure: Co‑locating rewards reduces idle time but requires careful resource partitioning; MPS improves utilization but may cause interference.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - SFT: Poor controllability (ignoring instructions), spatial collapse under camera motion, implausible fluid dynamics. Diagnose via slice metrics on camera‑motion prompts and optical flow consistency.
  - GRPO: Advantage values collapsing to near‑zero (reward saturation), gradient norms exploding, sudden drop in diversity. Check reward distribution statistics and intra‑group variance.
  - DPO: Over‑generation of preferred modes (loss of diversity), increased static artifacts (overfitting to synthetic negatives), KL divergence spikes. Monitor likelihood distributions and human eval on edge cases.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. SFT validation: Evaluate spatial consistency on a held‑out set of camera‑motion prompts; compute object‑level geometric consistency metrics (e.g., shape distortion) and compare to baseline.
  2. GRPO stability: Track per‑step reward mean/variance, advantage norm, and policy KL divergence. Run a small ablation turning off BPGO or Self‑Paced to observe impact on convergence speed and final VBench scores.
  3. DPO data ablation: Compare models trained with only policy‑on‑policy negatives, only synthetic negatives, and combined set; measure via human evaluation on temporal coherence and alignment to identify which data source contributes most to final quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How generalizable are the modular GRPO extensions (ViPO, BPGO, Self‑Paced) across diverse video domains and backbone architectures?
- Does synthetic preference data (temporal perturbations) provide a valid proxy for human preferences, or does it introduce bias and superficial artifacts?
- What is the actual scalability and resource efficiency of the Ray‑based orchestration for longer video sequences?
- How does the strength of spatial‑structure loss reweighting in SFT affect the balance between stability and diversity?
- How robust are the modular components (ViPO, BPGO, Self‑Paced) to reward noise and ambiguous feedback across diverse prompts?

## Limitations
- Generalizability of modular GRPO extensions (ViPO/BPGO/Self‑Paced) across diverse video domains and backbone architectures is uncertain.
- Validity of synthetic preference data (temporal perturbations) for DPO carries risk of reward hacking or superficial artifacts.
- Scalability and resource efficiency claims of the Ray‑based orchestration, especially for longer sequences, require external validation.
