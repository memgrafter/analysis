# Sign-Based Optimizers Are Effective Under Heavy-Tailed Noise
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07425
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07425
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Sign-based optimizers (e.g., Lion, Muon) achieve sharp convergence rates under a heavy-tailed noise model that better captures language model training, offering a theoretical justification for their empirical superiority over AdamW.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the theoretical gap behind the observed superiority of sign-based optimizers (Lion, Muon) over AdamW in large language model (LLM) training. It introduces a generalized heavy-tailed stochastic noise model where the noise magnitude scales with the gradient norm, more accurately reflecting the noise encountered in language modeling. Under this model, the authors provide the first rigorous convergence analysis for SignSGD, Lion, Muon, and Muonlight, deriving sharp rates \( O(T^{-(p-1)/(3p-2)}) \) that match information-theoretic lower bounds. Key technical innovations include new martingale concentration inequalities in ℓ₁ and nuclear norms. LLM pretraining experiments validate the noise model and confirm the practical advantage of sign-based methods, yielding a strong theoretical foundation for their effectiveness.

## Method Summary
The core methodological contribution is the proposal of a heavy-tailed noise model that captures the empirical scaling of gradient noise variance with the norm of the gradient. This model deviates from classical Gaussian assumptions and better represents the stochasticity in LLM training. The authors then analyze sign-based optimizers—algorithms that update parameters using only the sign of the gradient (or a variant combining sign and momentum). They derive convergence guarantees for both convex and nonconvex settings, employing novel martingale concentration inequalities tailored to ℓ₁ (for dense parameters) and nuclear norms (for attention layers). The analysis covers SignSGD, Lion, Muon, and a streamlined variant Muonlight, demonstrating optimal dependence on problem dimension and noise parameters.

## Key Results
- Derived sharp convergence rates \( O(T^{-(p-1)/(3p-2)}) \) under the heavy-tailed noise model, matching lower bounds.
- Provided the first rigorous convergence analysis for SignSGD, Lion, Muon, and Muonlight in this setting.
- LLM pretraining experiments validate the noise model and show a consistent advantage of sign-based methods over AdamW.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The heavy-tailed noise model reveals that gradient outliers are common in LLM training, with noise variance increasing with gradient magnitude. Traditional optimizers like AdamW, which rely on precise gradient magnitudes, are sensitive to such outliers, leading to slower convergence or instability. Sign-based optimizers, by discarding magnitude information and using only the sign of the gradient, inherently resist heavy-tailed fluctuations, making them robust under this noise regime. The theoretical analysis confirms that under the proposed model, sign-based methods achieve near-optimal convergence rates, explaining their empirical robustness. Moreover, the use of ℓ₁ and nuclear norms aligns with the sparse (e.g., embeddings) and low-rank (e.g., attention) structures prevalent in LLM gradients, further sharpening the guarantees.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Heavy-tailed noise model**  
   - *Why needed*: To realistically capture gradient noise in LLM training, where variance scales with gradient norm, unlike Gaussian assumptions.  
   - *Quick check*: Does the noise distribution have tails that decay slower than exponential, and does its variance grow superlinearly with gradient magnitude?

2. **Sign-based optimization**  
   - *Why needed*: Provides robustness to heavy-tailed noise by eliminating sensitivity to gradient magnitude, focusing solely on descent direction.  
   - *Quick check*: Does the optimizer update using only the sign (or quantized version) of the gradient?

3. **Martingale concentration inequalities**  
   - *Why needed*: Crucial for proving convergence under dependent heavy-tailed noise; the paper develops new inequalities in ℓ₁ and nuclear norms.  
   - *Quick check*: Can one bound the deviation of a martingale sum in ℓ₁ norm with high probability under sub-Gaussian or heavy‑tailed assumptions?

4. **ℓ₁ and nuclear norms**  
   - *Why needed*: Align with sparse (dense layers) and low‑rank (attention) structures in LLMs, enabling sharper theoretical bounds.  
   - *Quick check*: For a vector, does ℓ₁ control sparsity? For a matrix, does nuclear norm control both Frobenius and spectral norms?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Heavy‑tailed noise model → Sign‑based optimizer (SignSGD/Lion/Muon/Muonlight) → Convergence analysis (ℓ₁/nuclear norm martingales) → LLM pretraining experiments.
- **Critical path**: The insight that gradient noise magnitude scales with gradient norm leads to the heavy‑tailed model; this necessitates new analytical tools, yielding sharp rates that justify sign‑based updates; finally, real‑world LLM experiments confirm the theory.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Discarding magnitude information loses speed in low‑noise regimes but gains robustness under heavy tails.  
  - Choosing ℓ₁ for dense and nuclear for attention layers tailors analysis to structure but may be overly specific.  
  - Simpler variants (e.g., Muonlight) trade adaptivity for efficiency.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - If noise is light‑tailed (Gaussian), sign‑based methods may underperform AdamW.  
  - Anisotropic or structured noise not captured by isotropic assumptions could degrade theoretical rates.  
  - Extremely large models (>100B) with complex data pipelines might violate the noise scaling hypothesis.
- **3 first experiments**:  
  1. Synthetic heavy‑tailed stochastic optimization to verify the derived convergence rates.  
  2. Pretraining a decoder‑only Transformer (e.g., 1.3B parameters) on a standard corpus (e.g., C4) comparing Lion, Muon, Muonlight, and AdamW.  
  3. Measurement of gradient noise statistics during LLM training to confirm the superlinear scaling of variance with gradient norm.

## Limitations
- The heavy‑tailed noise model assumes gradient norm‑dependent variance, but its fidelity to actual LLM training noise (e.g., from data pipeline asynchronicity, hardware variability) remains unverified outside the paper’s experimental setup.  
- Theoretical rates assume specific norm conditions (ℓ₁/ℓ₂ for dense, nuclear for attention) and isotropic noise; real LLM gradients are highly anisotropic and structured, potentially affecting tightness.  
- Experiments focus on a narrow set of LLM architectures (Transformer‑based) and scales; it is unclear if gains persist for other architectures (e.g., CNNs, RNNs) or extremely large models (>100B params) where optimization dynamics may differ.

## Confidence
- **High**: Theoretical convergence rates for sign‑based methods under the proposed heavy‑tailed model are sharp and match lower bounds.
- **Medium**: Empirical validation on LLM pretraining shows consistent advantage over AdamW, but limited to a few model families and compute regimes.
- **Low**: Generalization of the noise model to all language modeling setups; the paper’s noise characterization may omit confounding factors (e.g., gradient checkpointing, mixture‑of‑experts).

## Next Checks
1. **Scale and diversity experiment**: Pretrain sign‑based (Lion/Muon) vs. AdamW on at least three additional model families (e.g., decoder‑only LLMs of varying depth/width, encoder‑decoder, non‑Transformer architectures) across multiple scales (1B–10B params), measuring final perplexity and training stability.
2. **Noise model validation**: Instrument real LLM training to estimate gradient noise distributions (e.g., via running variance over minibatches) and test if variance scales superlinearly with gradient norm as proposed; compare against simpler Gaussian or heavy‑tailed‑but‑norm‑independent baselines.
3. **Ablation on norm assumptions**: In theory, relax the isotropic noise assumption and derive/adjust rates for empirically observed gradient covariance structures; run synthetic experiments with controlled anisotropic heavy‑tailed noise to see if theoretical gaps emerge.
