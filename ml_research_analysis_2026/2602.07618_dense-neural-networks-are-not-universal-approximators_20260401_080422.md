# Dense Neural Networks are not Universal Approximators

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07618
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07618
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Dense ReLU networks with fixed depth and bounded weight norms cannot universally approximate Lipschitz functions beyond a certain input dimension; their expressivity saturates irrespective of width.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the question of whether dense feedforward ReLU networks with fixed depth and bounded weights can approximate all Lipschitz continuous functions. By reinterpreting such networks as message-passing graph neural networks and applying the weak regularity lemma, the authors prove that any network in this class can be compressed to a fixed-size architecture independent of its width. This compression implies a saturation of expressive power, leading to an explicit threshold on the input dimension beyond which some Lipschitz functions become unapproximable. The theoretical conclusions are supported by experiments on MNIST, where accuracy plateaus for weight-constrained dense networks as width increases, while standard networks continue to improve.

## Method Summary
The core method models a dense ReLU network with bounded weights as a message-passing graph neural network (MPNN) defined on a computational kernel. The weak regularity lemma from graph theory is then applied to this kernel, yielding a compressed network with a hidden size that depends only on depth, weight bound, and desired approximation error, not on the original width. This compressed network approximates the original uniformly over inputs. By comparing the parameter count of this compressed class to a VC-dimension lower bound for approximating all Lipschitz functions, the authors show that for sufficiently large input dimensions, the compressed network size is insufficient, proving non-universality.

## Key Results
- Dense ReLU networks with fixed depth and bounded weight norms are not universal approximators of Lipschitz functions once the input dimension exceeds an explicit threshold.
- The expressive power of such networks saturates: any network can be approximated by one with hidden size \(O\left(\frac{L^2 d_L^2 (2B)^L}{\varepsilon} \cdot 2^{O(1/\varepsilon^2)}\right)\), independent of width.
- Empirical results on MNIST show that weight-clamped dense networks plateau at ~90-91% accuracy for widths ≥64, whereas standard networks reach ~98-99%, confirming the saturation effect.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Expressivity Saturation via Compression
The paper shows that dense networks with bounded weights (strongly dense) can be compressed to a fixed-size approximator. The weak regularity lemma applied to the computational kernel produces a step kernel with a bounded number of intervals, yielding a compressed network whose hidden dimension does not grow with the original width. Thus, even arbitrarily wide dense networks cannot exceed a certain expressive capacity beyond which additional width becomes redundant.

### Mechanism 2: VC-Dimension Lower Bound vs. Upper Bound
A lower bound from VC-dimension theory states that to approximate all Lipschitz functions within \(\varepsilon\), a ReLU network must have at least \(c^{-1/2}(6\varepsilon)^{-d_0/2}\) parameters. This grows exponentially with input dimension \(d_0\). In contrast, the compressed network size from Mechanism 1 is independent of \(d_0\). When \(d_0\) exceeds a threshold, the lower bound surpasses the compressed size, implying that some Lipschitz functions cannot be approximated by any network in the class, regardless of width.

### Mechanism 3: Empirical Saturation in Overparameterized Dense Networks
Experiments with a single hidden layer ReLU network on MNIST, where weights are clamped to enforce the boundedness condition, show that both training and test accuracy plateau at around 90-91% for widths beyond 64. This plateau aligns with the theoretical prediction that width alone cannot increase expressive power beyond the compressed bound. In contrast, standard networks (without weight clamping) continue to improve, indicating that universality is restored when the weight bound is removed.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Computational kernel constructor → B-IR-MPNN module → Regularity compressor → VC-dimension calculator

**Critical path**:
1. Given a network \(\Theta(W,b)\), construct the computational kernel \(K\) and input signal \(f_x\).
2. Apply the weak regularity lemma to \(K\) to obtain a step kernel \(K'\) with hidden dimension \(d = O(1/\varepsilon^2)\).
3. Use Theorem 5 (Lipschitz continuity of the network map) to bound \(\|\Theta - \Theta'\|_\infty\) via the computational cut distance.
4. Compare the parameter count of the compressed network \(\Theta'\) to the VC lower bound from Theorem 8 for the given input dimension \(d_0\) and accuracy \(\varepsilon\).
5. If the compressed size is smaller than the VC lower bound, conclude that the class \(\mathcal{N}_{\text{dense}}(B,L,d_0,d_L)\) cannot approximate all Lipschitz functions.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Weight bound \(B\): Increasing \(B\) increases the compressed hidden size (roughly \(d \propto B^L\)) and may weaken the strong denseness condition; decreasing \(B\) strengthens the saturation bound but risks underfitting.
- Depth \(L\): The saturation threshold on \(d_0\) grows polynomially with \(L\) and exponentially via \(B^{2L}\); deeper networks hit the non-universality regime at smaller \(d_0\).
- Output dimension \(d_L\): Larger \(d_L\) increases the compressed size (quadratically) and raises the critical \(d_0\) threshold.

**Failure signatures**:
- Input dimension \(d_0\) exceeds the bound from Theorem 9, guaranteeing the existence of unapproximable Lipschitz functions regardless of width.
- During training, weight clipping causes accuracy to plateau early as width increases.
- Computationally, the VC lower bound \(c^{-1/2}(6\varepsilon)^{-d_0/2}\) surpasses the maximum possible compressed network size.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Compression sanity check**: Randomly initialize a dense ReLU network, apply the regularity compressor, and verify that the compressed network’s outputs are within \(\varepsilon\) of the original on a fine grid of inputs.
2. **Dimension sweep**: For fixed \(B\), \(L\), \(d_L\), vary \(d_0\) and compute the VC lower bound versus the maximum compressed parameter count; identify the crossover where the lower bound exceeds the compressed size.
3. **Replicate Table 1**: Train dense (with weight clamping) and standard one-hidden-layer ReLU networks on MNIST across increasing widths; plot accuracy curves to observe the plateau in the dense case.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Can the compression framework apply to non-MLP architectures like CNNs or transformers?** The method may generalize to any subclass with a compression guarantee, but extending it to architectures with weight sharing or local connectivity requires new models and proofs.
- **Does sparse connectivity guarantee universality under bounded weights?** The paper suggests sparsity is necessary for true universality, but the expressiveness gap between sparse and dense bounded-weight networks remains unexplored.
- **How tight are the theoretical thresholds, and can the compression be computed efficiently?** The derived bounds are likely loose, and the compression via the weak regularity lemma is non-constructive; improving bounds and designing efficient algorithms are open challenges.

## Limitations
- Theoretical thresholds rely on unspecified constants, making exact saturation dimensions vague.
- Experimental validation is limited to MNIST with a single hidden layer; generalizability to higher-dimensional or deeper tasks is uncertain.
- Weight clamping in experiments is an artificial constraint not commonly used in practice, so the observed saturation may not reflect typical training dynamics.

## Confidence
- Compression Guarantee: High
- VC-Dimension Conflict: Medium
- Empirical Saturation: Low
