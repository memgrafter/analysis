# Reinforcement Learning for Control with Probabilistic Stability Guarantee: A Finite-Sample Approach
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00043
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00043
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Provides a finite-sample probabilistic stability guarantee for reinforcement learning control via a Lyapunov-based theorem and the L-REINFORCE algorithm.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the problem of learning stabilizing control policies for stochastic nonlinear systems using only finite sampled trajectories—a gap between model-free RL and rigorous control theory. It introduces a theoretical framework that converts an infinite-sample Lyapunov stability condition into a single verifiable inequality over a finite set of trajectories, yielding a probabilistic bound that improves with the number and length of trajectories. Based on this, the authors develop L-REINFORCE, a policy gradient method that explicitly optimizes to satisfy the finite-sample condition. On a Cartpole stabilization task, L-REINFORCE succeeds where standard REINFORCE fails, and empirical stability probability increases with data as predicted by the theory, bridging RL and stability analysis.

## Method Summary
The core method consists of a finite-sample stability theorem (Theorem 1) that links satisfaction of a sample-average Lyapunov inequality to an infinite-sample stability guarantee with high probability, under geometric ergodicity (Assumption 3). The left-hand side of the inequality is estimated from M trajectories of length T collected under the current policy π_θ, using a Lyapunov function L_ϕ(s) that must satisfy upper/lower bounds (4). To satisfy the inequality, the authors derive a policy gradient (Theorem 2) that matches REINFORCE with a specific reward shaping: the gradient weights log-policy probabilities by a “return” l(τ,t) computed from the trajectory’s cumulative clipped cost plus terminal Lyapunov value. The L-REINFORCE algorithm alternates between collecting trajectories, evaluating the sample Lyapunov average, updating the policy via this gradient, and updating the Lyapunov network f_ϕ to approximate a value-function target (e.g., TD learning). Hyperparameters like α₃, ε, T, M are chosen to meet the probabilistic bound, and the Lyapunov network is trained concurrently to approximate a valid Lyapunov function.

## Key Results
- Theoretical finite-sample stability guarantee: Theorem 1 provides a probabilistic bound that the infinite-sample condition holds if the finite-sample inequality is satisfied.
- L-REINFORCE algorithm: a model-free policy gradient method that directly optimizes to meet the finite-sample Lyapunov condition.
- Empirical validation on Cartpole: L-REINFORCE stabilizes when REINFORCE fails; empirical stability probability increases with M and T, aligning with theory.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The infinite-sample Lyapunov stability condition (expectation over stationary distribution) is converted into a single verifiable inequality over a finite set of trajectories.
- **Mechanism:** Introduce a finite-time sampling distribution (FSD) as an intermediate between the true stationary distribution and the empirical distribution from M trajectories of length T. Bound the deviation between the expectation under the stationary distribution and under the FSD using Assumption 3 (geometric ergodicity). Then, use concentration inequalities (Hoeffding) to bound the deviation between the empirical average and the expectation under the FSD. Unify these bounds to show that satisfying a single sample-average inequality on finite data implies the infinite-sample condition with high probability.
- **Core assumption:** The MDP is geometrically ergodic under the policy (Assumption 3), meaning the state distribution converges to the stationary distribution at a geometric rate.
- **Evidence anchors:** Lemma 2 (bias from finite T) and Lemma 3 (variance from finite M), unified in Theorem 1. Similar finite-sample analyses exist for operator methods, but the conversion mechanism is paper-specific.
- **Break condition:** If the MDP is not geometrically ergodic, the bound does not hold, and the probability guarantee cannot be derived.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The probability of mean square stability increases monotonically with the number of trajectories (M) and their length (T), converging to 1 as data grows.
- **Mechanism:** The derived probabilistic bound shows the failure probability decreases exponentially with M and polynomially with T (via T^(γ⁻¹)). This follows from combining the bias term (∝ T^(γ⁻¹)) from Lemma 2 and the variance term (∝ 1/√M) from Lemma 3. By choosing ϵ larger than the bias term, the argument ensures the probability of the infinite-sample Lyapunov condition being satisfied is lower-bounded by 1 minus an exponentially decaying term in M.
- **Core assumption:** The Lyapunov function candidate L(s) satisfies the upper and lower bounds (4) with constants α₁, α₂, and trajectories are generated by a fixed policy during data collection.
- **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 1 and its proof, specifically bound (27) and the requirement T ≥ (b₁/ϵ)^(1/(1-γ)). Scaling with M and T is consistent with standard finite-sample theory.
- **Break condition:** If the Lyapunov function does not satisfy the bounding inequalities (4) (i.e., is not a valid Lyapunov candidate even in expectation), the entire mechanism fails as the target expectation is not necessarily negative.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The policy gradient that optimizes the finite-sample Lyapunov condition is equivalent to the REINFORCE gradient with a specific reward shaping, enabling a model-free stabilization algorithm.
- **Mechanism:** Parameterize the policy π_θ(a|s) and the Lyapunov function L_ϕ(s). The gradient of the left-hand side of (26) with respect to θ equals the expected sum of log-policy gradients weighted by a “return”-like term l(τ,t) (38), which is the sum of clipped costs from time t onward plus the terminal Lyapunov value. This is structurally identical to the REINFORCE gradient if one sets c(s) = L(s) and α₃=1, but the theorem allows other α₃ and L. Thus, L-REINFORCE uses this gradient to iteratively satisfy (26).
- **Core assumption:** The policy is differentiable (e.g., softmax over a neural network), and the expectation can be estimated by sampling trajectories from the current policy.
- **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 2 derivation, specifically equation (46) linking the gradient to the sum of log-policies times l(τ,t). REINFORCE is a classic algorithm; its application to explicit Lyapunov condition satisfaction is novel.
- **Break condition:** If the policy parameterization lacks expressiveness to represent a stabilizing policy, or if gradient steps are too large causing policy collapse, the algorithm may not converge to a policy satisfying (26).

## Foundational Learning
#### Lyapunov's Direct Method
**Why needed:** The stability guarantee rests on constructing a scalar function L(s) whose expected one-step change along trajectories is negative (5). This is the classical control-theoretic foundation translated to a sample-based setting.
**Quick check:** Given a candidate L(s), condition (5) implies that the expectation of L(s_t) decreases over time under the stationary distribution, driving it to zero; thus the system is mean square stable.

#### Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)
**Why needed:** The system dynamics are modeled as an MDP (1) with possibly unknown transition probabilities. All probability statements (stationary distribution, sampling distributions) are defined relative to this structure.
**Quick check:** The closed-loop transition probability P^π(s'|s) marginalizes over actions according to policy π, as opposed to the original transition P(s'|s,a) which conditions on the action.

#### Policy Gradient Methods (REINFORCE)
**Why needed:** L-REINFORCE is a policy gradient algorithm that directly optimizes parameters of a stochastic policy to satisfy the finite-sample Lyapunov condition, rather than optimizing a cumulative reward.
**Quick check:** In REINFORCE, the “return” (sum of future costs) provides a scalar signal weighting the log-policy gradient; this return corresponds to l(τ,t) in (38) and indicates the long-term “cost” following a state-action pair.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Collector → Condition Evaluator → Policy & Lyapunov Update Modules → (back to) Data Collector

**Critical path:**
1. Collect M trajectories of length T using current policy π_θ.
2. Compute the sample average ΔL_avg = (1/(MT)) Σ ΔL(s_t) from these trajectories and check if ΔL_avg ≤ -ε.
3. If condition holds for a sufficient number of iterations, stop. Otherwise:
4. For each trajectory, compute l(τ,t) = Σ_{j=t+1}^T c(s_j) + L(s_{T+1}).
5. Compute policy gradient ∇_θ E[ΔL] ≈ (1/T) Σ_t ∇_θ log π_θ(a_t|s_t) l(τ,t) (with optional baseline).
6. Update policy parameters θ ← θ - α ∇_θ E[ΔL]; concurrently, update Lyapunov network ϕ via TD learning to approximate a target value function.
7. Repeat from step 1.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **M and T:** Larger M reduces variance, larger T reduces bias from finite horizon; both increase per-iteration cost. The probability bound shows diminishing returns for very large T.
- **Clip constant c̄:** Larger c̄ gives richer gradient signal in large states but loosens the bound b₁, requiring more data for the same confidence; smaller c̄ tightens the bound but may saturate cost.
- **Network capacity:** Deeper/wider networks increase representational power but also sample complexity and risk of overfitting to finite data.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Persistent violation of (26):** The sample average ΔL_avg remains positive or only slightly negative across iterations.
- **Oscillating policy parameters:** Parameter updates cause cyclic changes without improvement in ΔL_avg.
- **Diverging states during evaluation:** Rollouts under the learned policy show state divergence, possibly due to distribution shift or an invalid Lyapunov function.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Cartpole stabilization baseline:** Compare L-REINFORCE against vanilla REINFORCE on Cartpole; visualize state trajectories to confirm L-REINFORCE stabilizes while REINFORCE fails.
2. **Finite-sample probability validation:** After training, collect many fresh trajectories, estimate empirical stability probability as function of M and T, and compare to theoretical bound surface.
3. **Ablation on α₃ and c̄:** Systematically vary α₃ and clip constant c̄; measure iterations to satisfy (26) and final policy stability; quantify trade-offs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How can the finite-sample stability framework be extended to more sample-efficient RL algorithms (e.g., actor-critic, Q-learning) and other stability notions (e.g., exponential stability, Lyapunov stability)? The paper states this as a future direction in the conclusion, but no theoretical extension or algorithm is provided.

### Open Question 2
Under what conditions can Assumption 3 (constant mixing rate γ) be relaxed or replaced, and how does the probabilistic stability bound degrade if the state distribution convergence is slower than exponential? Assumption 3 is crucial for bounding sampling bias; many practical MDPs may not exhibit uniform exponential mixing, but the sensitivity is unanalyzed.

### Open Question 3
What is a theoretically grounded and automated procedure for selecting hyperparameters (α₃, ε, clip value c) in L-REINFORCE to achieve a desired confidence level δ with minimal sample complexity? The algorithm uses heuristic stopping; no principled method exists to balance conservativeness and sample efficiency.

### Open Question 4
Does the proposed method scale to high-dimensional continuous state spaces, and how does required sample complexity (M, T) grow with state dimension n for fixed confidence? The bound constants may implicitly depend on n or function class complexity; no theoretical scaling or empirical studies beyond low-dimensional systems are provided.

### Open Question 5
Can the Lyapunov function learning be made fully concurrent and mutually stabilizing with policy learning, rather than using a separate value-function-like target? The current decoupled approach risks that the learned Lyapunov function may not remain valid for the latest policy, undermining the guarantee; joint optimization remains open.

## Limitations
- Geometric ergodicity may not hold for many real-world systems, and the framework offers no fallback.
- The training procedure for the Lyapunov network is underspecified; approximation errors could invalidate the required Lyapunov conditions.
- Experiments are limited to a single low-dimensional environment (Cartpole); scalability and performance on more complex tasks are unproven.

## Confidence
- **Conversion theorem (Thm 1):** High – rigorous proof under explicit assumptions.
- **Probabilistic bound (27):** High – analytically derived with empirical support.
- **L-REINFORCE effectiveness:** Medium – demonstrated on Cartpole only; no convergence proof; depends on correct Lyapunov training.

## Next Checks
1. **Bound tightness vs. data:** Systematically vary M ∈ {10,50,100,200} and T ∈ {100,250,500,1000} on Cartpole. After training, evaluate on 500 fresh trajectories to estimate empirical stability probability. Plot empirical surface against theoretical bound (27). Expect both to approach 1 as M,T increase.
2. **Lyapunov training ablation:** Compare three conditions: (a) learned f_ϕ as described, (b) fixed random f_ϕ, (c) hand‑crafted quadratic L(s)=sᵀs. Record iterations to satisfy (26) and assess final policy stability via long rollouts. This isolates the necessity of a valid, trained Lyapunov function.
3. **Non‑ergodic stress test:** Apply L-REINFORCE to an MDP with questionable mixing properties (e.g., deterministic double integrator with sparse rewards or absorbing states). Measure empirical stability probability and compare to bound; significant deviation would highlight sensitivity to Assumption 3.
