# Replaying pre-training data improves fine-tuning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.04964
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04964
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Replaying generic web data during fine-tuning improves target task performance, yielding up to 1.87× data efficiency gains on 150M models, 2.06× on mid‑training setups, and at scale improves agentic web navigation success by 4.5% and Basque QA by 2%.

## Executive Summary
Standard fine‑tuning of language models first pre‑trains on a large generic corpus and then adapts to a limited target dataset, often suffering from overfitting or catastrophic forgetting. This paper shows that simply mixing generic pre‑training data back into the fine‑tuning process (replay) unexpectedly boosts performance on the target task, especially when the target domain was under‑represented in the original pre‑training. Through controlled experiments with 150M‑parameter models and large‑scale evaluations with 8B Llama models, the authors demonstrate substantial gains in data efficiency and downstream metrics. The findings suggest that replay acts as an implicit regularizer, stabilizes optimization, and preserves generic knowledge required for scarce target tasks.

## Method Summary
The work employs a two‑stage training framework. Stage 1 consists of generic pre‑training on a corpus such as C4. Stage 2 fine‑tunes on the limited target dataset. The core modification is a replay fraction ρ: during Stage 2, a fraction of steps use generic data instead of target data, shortening Stage 1 to keep total compute constant. For models using the Warmup‑Stable‑Decay (WSD) learning rate schedule, a mid‑training variant is also explored where both generic and target data are mixed throughout a single stage, with a parameter α controlling the fraction of steps containing target data. Smaller models are evaluated on target validation loss; larger (8B) models are assessed on downstream tasks (web navigation success, Basque QA accuracy). Data efficiency improvements are quantified by fitting a reference scaling law to translate loss differences into equivalent amounts of target data.

## Key Results
- Replay boosts target data efficiency by up to 1.87× for standard fine‑tuning and 2.06× for mid‑training (150M models).
- At 8B scale, replay yields a 4.5% absolute gain in agentic web navigation success rate and a 2% gain in Basque question‑answering accuracy.
- The benefit is most pronounced for target domains that were scarce in pre‑training; when target data was already abundant, replay provides little or no improvement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Implicit Regularization Against Overfitting
**Claim:** Replaying generic data provides implicit regularization that mitigates overfitting to small target datasets.  
**Mechanism:** Exclusive training on limited target data leads to overfitting on noise and idiosyncrasies. Interleaving generic samples keeps updates grounded in the broader data distribution, preventing memorization of finite target samples.  
**Core assumption:** The target dataset is small enough that standard fine‑tuning would overfit without explicit regularization.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 3.1] Overfitting is observed when repeating data past a certain epoch count.  
- [appendix B.2] A linear regression toy model demonstrates that replay reduces overfitting when the number of samples n is less than the dimension d.  
- Nearby literature on replay focuses on forgetting prevention, not overfitting reduction.  
**Break condition:** When the target dataset is sufficiently large that overfitting is not observed even at maximal repetition counts (e.g., very high‑resource domains).

### Mechanism 2: Optimization Stabilization via Reduced Distribution Shift
**Claim:** Replay stabilizes optimization by reducing the distributional shift between pre‑training and fine‑tuning, mitigating early loss spikes.  
**Mechanism:** Standard fine‑tuning causes a sudden distribution shift at Stage 2 onset, triggering optimization instability (loss spike). Replay creates a gentler transition by including familiar generic samples early in Stage 2, allowing the optimizer to maintain stability while adapting to target‑specific features.  
**Core assumption:** The abrupt shift from generic‑only pre‑training to target‑only fine‑tuning causes transient optimization instability that harms final performance.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [appendix B.1] A large loss spike occurs during the first few steps of training; replay reduces its severity.  
- [section 3.2] Replay benefits are largest for domains farthest from generic data (StarCoder filtered from C4) and smallest for most overlapping ones (Flan), consistent with shift magnitude affecting benefit.  
**Break condition:** When the target distribution is highly similar to the generic pre‑training distribution (minimal shift), or when learning‑rate warmup is tuned to absorb the shift.

### Mechanism 3: Preservation of Generic Representations for Scarce Domains
**Claim:** Replay preserves core generic representations that serve as a foundation for learning target tasks, especially when the target was under‑represented in pre‑training.  
**Mechanism:** Target tasks, especially scarce ones, may rely on generic linguistic or reasoning primitives developed during pre‑training. Exclusive target fine‑tuning risks overwriting these representations; replay co‑training maintains them while adapting task‑specific heads or late layers.  
**Core assumption:** The target task performance depends on maintaining generic knowledge structures alongside task‑specific adaptations.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 4.4] Replay is most helpful when Stage 2 data is most dissimilar from Stage 1; when pre‑training already includes 75% of target data, replay is no longer helpful.  
- [appendix F] Replay significantly reduces forgetting of generic loss compared to schedules without replay.  
- [abstract] Replay helps more when there is less target data present in pre‑training.  
**Break condition:** When the target task is completely novel and does not depend on generic knowledge, or when pre‑training already included ample target data.

## Foundational Learning

**Data Efficiency Scaling Laws**  
*Why needed:* Quantifies how much target data a strategy effectively leverages; the paper’s core results (1.87× improvements) rely on fitting a reference scaling law to convert loss differences into data equivalents.  
*Quick check:* If Strategy A matches the loss of Strategy B using D tokens while B needs 2D tokens, what is A’s data‑efficiency improvement over B? (Answer: 2×)

**Warmup‑Stable‑Decay (WSD) Learning Rate Schedule**  
*Why needed:* WSD is critical for mid‑training performance; its sharp decay phase leverages the “river valley” landscape to make rapid final progress, making data ordering more impactful. Without WSD, replay benefits diminish.  
*Quick check:* Why does placing target data at the end of training only help when using WSD (or similar sharp‑decay schedules)? (Answer: The sharp decay allows the model to make large, focused updates when target data is presented, making order matter; a gradual schedule dilutes this effect.)

**Catastrophic Forgetting**  
*Why needed:* Traditional replay prevents forgetting of old knowledge. This paper’s surprising finding inverts the expectation: replay improves target performance rather than just preserving old knowledge. Understanding baseline forgetting contextualizes why replay is expected to hurt target performance but instead helps.  
*Quick check:* In standard catastrophic forgetting, what happens to performance on Task A when you train on Task B? How does this paper’s result differ? (Answer: Performance on Task A degrades; here replay improves both target performance and retains generic knowledge.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Pre‑trained checkpoint -> Data scheduler -> Learning rate scheduler -> Fine‑tuning loop -> Validation suite

**Critical path**  
1. Choose learning rate schedule: Prefer WSD with ~10% cooldown if possible; else cosine.  
2. Set baseline: No replay, all target data at end (α=1, ρ=0). Tune LR and epochs within overfitting limit (~64 rep max).  
3. Sweep replay fraction ρ (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.875). Expect sweet spot; 0 often suboptimal.  
4. If allowed mid‑training: additionally sweep target stage‑2 allocation α (1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125).  
5. Validate on target validation loss; compute data efficiency improvement via reference scaling law.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Compute vs performance: Replay increases total steps (factor 1/(1‑ρ)). Allocate extra compute to replay rather than more target epochs to avoid overfitting.  
- Forgetting vs adaptation: Higher ρ preserves generic knowledge better but may dilute target learning. Tune ρ to minimize target validation loss; forgetting is secondary unless maintaining generic capability is required.  
- Simplicity vs optimality: Two‑stage schedules (α, ρ) beat pure replay but require more tuning; single‑parameter replay often captures most benefit for fine‑tuning‑only scenarios.

**Failure signatures**  
- Overfitting to target: Loss decreases then increases on target validation while training loss continues dropping. Remedy: reduce target epochs or increase replay.  
- Divergence: Large loss spikes persist beyond initial steps. Remedy: lower learning rate or increase warmup steps.  
- No benefit: Replay fraction ρ ≈ 0 yields same/best loss. Likely target already abundant in pre‑training or domains overlap heavily (e.g., Flan vs C4). Check domain similarity.  
- Catastrophic forgetting: Generic validation loss spikes after fine‑tuning. Mitigation: increase ρ or save pre‑annealed checkpoint instead of final.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Reference scaling law fit: Train baseline (no replay, cosine LR) with varying target data amounts (4M, 8M, 16M, 32M, 64M tokens). Fit power law L(D)=aD^b+c to interpolation points.  
2. Baseline fine‑tuning sweep: Fix target data amount (e.g., 4M). Sweep learning rate and repetition count (up to 64) on cosine schedule to find optimal Target‑only baseline, respecting overfitting threshold.  
3. Replay fraction sweep: Using optimal baseline hyperparams, sweep ρ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.875} while conserving total steps. Measure target validation loss improvement and compute data efficiency × relative to baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** Under what conditions does modifying pre‑training (Stage 1) become necessary to maximize replay benefits, compared to only adjusting fine‑tuning (Stage 2)?  
*Basis:* The authors directly ask this in Section 7, showing that Stage 2‑only replay captures only 67.4% and 46.0% of potential gains for FineMath and Flan, while for StarCoder it suffices.  
*Unresolved:* They do not characterize what intrinsic properties (e.g., distributional similarity, data size) determine whether pre‑training modifications are essential, nor their interaction with model scale.

**Open Question 2:** What is the primary mechanism by which replaying generic data during fine‑tuning improves target performance—does it mitigate initial optimization instability, reduce overfitting to small target datasets, or both?  
*Basis:* Appendix B proposes two hypotheses: a loss spike at fine‑tuning start is less pronounced with replay, and a linear regression toy model shows replay reduces overfitting.  
*Unresolved:* The paper presents both explanations but does not disentangle them experimentally; the relative contribution and potential interaction remain unknown.

**Open Question 3:** How do the benefits of data replay generalize when pre‑training involves multiple diverse domains rather than a single generic distribution?  
*Basis:* The limitation in Section 7 notes the assumption of two distributions, whereas real pre‑training is a multi‑task learning problem with much higher diversity.  
*Unresolved:* All controlled experiments use C4 as the sole generic source. Real‑world pre‑training comprises dozens of domains, which may interact differently with replay; some might be more “target‑like” and alter optimal replay fraction.

**Open Question 4:** How well does validation loss on the target domain correlate with downstream task performance across different data schedules and domains, especially for smaller models?  
*Basis:* Section 2.2 acknowledges that validation loss “might not perfectly correlate with downstream metrics” and they only bridge to downstream tasks for 8B models in Section 5.  
*Unresolved:* In the controlled 150M experiments, they rely solely on validation loss; the correlation strength is unknown for those settings, and it is unclear whether replay gains in loss translate to gains in task accuracy for smaller models.

**Open Question 5:** What are the theoretically optimal Warmup‑Stable‑Decay (WSD) schedule parameters (e.g., cooldown duration) for maximizing target performance when data order is optimized, and how do they interact with model scale and domain similarity?  
*Basis:* Section 4.1 and Appendix I highlight the critical role of WSD but only tune the cooldown duration (fixed to 10%).  
*Unresolved:* The paper uses a heuristic 10% cooldown across all domains and scales; optimal decay schedules may differ for very small models, for domains with less target data in Stage 1, or for varying replay fractions. Theoretical guidance for ordered training with WSD is underdeveloped.

## Limitations
- Exact dataset preprocessing, filtering, and tokenization details for key corpora (C4, FineMath, StarCoder, Flan, and 8B replay proxies) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Critical hyperparameters (learning rates, batch sizes, weight decay settings) are not fully reported across experiments, requiring extensive tuning.
- The proposed mechanisms rely on correlational evidence; direct causal validation (e.g., isolating overfitting vs. distribution shift effects) is limited.

## Confidence
- Core claim (replay improves target data efficiency): **High**
- Mechanism 1 (implicit regularization against overfitting): **Medium**
- Mechanism 2 (optimization stabilization via reduced distribution shift): **Medium**
- Mechanism 3 (preservation of generic representations for scarce domains): **Medium‑High**

## Next Checks
1. Compute‑controlled ablation: Match total training compute between replay and no‑replay conditions by adjusting target‑epoch count; verify whether the gain persists beyond pure compute scaling.
2. Representation similarity analysis: Use CKA/SVCCA to compare layer activations of pre‑trained, fine‑tuned (no replay), and replay models; test if replay better preserves generic representations while adapting task‑specific ones.
3. Scarcity boundary test: Select a target domain already abundant in pre‑training (e.g., common web text); confirm that replay provides no benefit, strengthening the claim that gains hinge on pre‑training scarcity.
