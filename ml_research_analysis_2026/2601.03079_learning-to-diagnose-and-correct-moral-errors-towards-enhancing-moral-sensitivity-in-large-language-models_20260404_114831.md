# Learning to Diagnose and Correct Moral Errors: Towards Enhancing Moral Sensitivity in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03079  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03079  
- **Reference count:** 21  
- **Primary result:** Models fine-tuned with pragmatic inference (light-load for explicit toxicity, heavy-load for implicit bias) achieve high correction accuracy across tasks; combined approach reaches up to 94.4% on jailbreak correction.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of improving moral sensitivity in LLMs by moving beyond surface-level alignment. The authors propose training models to diagnose specific moral errors and generate corrections using a pragmatic inference framework distinguished by "pragmatic inference load." Light-load inference handles explicit cues like toxic slurs via direct lexical detection, while heavy-load inference tackles implicit, context-dependent errors like social bias through structured, multi-step reasoning grounded in Moral Foundations Theory. Models are fine-tuned on moral reasoning (MIC) and toxic prompt datasets, then evaluated on separate benchmarks. Results demonstrate cross-task generalization: heavy-load excels at bias correction, light-load reduces toxicity scores, and their combination outperforms baselines on jailbreak correction. Diagnostic outputs further enhance off-the-shelf LLM performance, and intervention experiments confirm that improvements stem from learned moral reasoning links rather than superficial heuristics.

## Method Summary
The method involves fine-tuning base LLMs (e.g., Llama-3.2-1B/3B, Qwen3-4B) on two task-specific datasets using stepwise prompting templates. For light-load inference, models are trained on a processed split of RealToxicityPrompts to identify and remove explicit toxic cues. For heavy-load inference, models are trained on the MIC dataset to produce a reasoning chain: identify actions, consequences, relevant moral foundations (from Moral Foundations Theory), a judgment, and a correction. Training data is generated by prompting DeepSeek with templates (Tables 11–14). Evaluation is conducted on held-out benchmarks: BBQ for social bias, JailbreakBench for jailbreak replies, and a separate RealToxicityPrompts split for toxicity, using metrics like accuracy (bias/jailbreak) and Perspective API toxicity scores. A combined "Light+Heavy" model concatenates outputs from both fine-tuned models for jailbreak tasks.

## Key Results
- Heavy-load inference achieved up to 97.7% accuracy in correcting indirect social biases (Qwen3-4B on nationality bias in BBQ).
- Light-load inference produced the lowest toxicity scores on explicit toxic language across all models.
- The combined Light+Heavy approach achieved up to 94.4% accuracy on jailbreak correction (Qwen3-4B), outperforming individual models and baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Light-Load Inference for Explicit Moral Errors
- **Claim:** Light-load pragmatic inference corrects explicit moral errors (e.g., toxic slurs) by detecting conventionalized negative linguistic cues without complex reasoning.
- **Mechanism:** The model is fine-tuned to identify surface-level, lexically toxic elements (e.g., slurs, profanity) and remove them directly, bypassing deeper contextual analysis.
- **Core assumption:** Explicit moral violations are indexed by stable, context-independent lexical items whose harm is conventionally recognized.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 3.3 state that light-load uses explicit linguistic cues and reduced toxicity scores most effectively. Corpus focus on explicit cue removal.
- **Break condition:** Fails on implicit or context-dependent moral errors where no explicit lexicon signals harm (e.g., subtle social bias).

### Mechanism 2: Heavy-Load Inference for Implicit Moral Errors
- **Claim:** Heavy-load pragmatic inference corrects implicit, context-dependent moral errors (e.g., social bias) by simulating a multi-step reasoning chain linking actions, consequences, and moral foundations.
- **Mechanism:** The model is trained to decompose a reply into (1) actions, (2) consequences, (3) relevant moral foundations (from Moral Foundations Theory), and (4) a judgment, then revise based on violated foundations.
- **Core assumption:** Moral sensitivity requires explicitly reasoning about harm through structured, theory-grounded steps rather than pattern matching.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports high accuracy on indirect biases; section 3.3 details the four steps; section 5.2 intervention experiments confirm reliance on MFT links.
- **Break condition:** Computationally expensive; may overfit to the structure of training data if moral foundation annotations are noisy or limited.

### Mechanism 3: Cross-Task Generalization via Inference Load Alignment
- **Claim:** Cross-task generalization is enabled by framing diverse morality-related tasks through the unified lens of "pragmatic inference load," aligning training and testing conditions via inferred reasoning depth rather than task semantics.
- **Mechanism:** Models fine-tuned on moral reasoning (MIC) or toxic prompts learn inferential processes that transfer to unseen tasks (BBQ bias, JailbreakBench) because both require similar pragmatic steps at matching load levels.
- **Core assumption:** The core challenge in moral sensitivity is the *degree* of pragmatic reasoning required, not the specific domain (toxicity vs. bias).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract frames pragmatic inference load as capturing reasoning degree across tasks; section 4 shows cross-task success; section 5.1 demonstrates diagnostic transfer to off-the-shelf LLMs.
- **Break condition:** Generalization may fail if test tasks require novel combinations of inference load or moral foundation applications not seen during fine-tuning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Pragmatic Inference (Light vs. Heavy Load):**  
  *Why needed:* It is the core architectural variable determining whether the model uses lexical lookup (light) or structured reasoning (heavy).  
  *Quick check:* Given a reply with a subtle stereotype but no slurs, which inference method should activate and why?
- **Moral Foundations Theory (MFT):**  
  *Why needed:* Provides discrete, theory-backed labels (care, fairness, etc.) that structure heavy-load reasoning and link diagnosis to correction.  
  *Quick check:* If a reply violates 'authority' by disrespecting a social role, what might its corrective action look like?
- **Instruction Fine-Tuning with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Style Prompts:**  
  *Why needed:* The training data is generated by prompting an LLM to produce stepwise diagnostic outputs; the fine-tuned model must mimic this format.  
  *Quick check:* Why is the training data created via an off-the-shelf LLM (DeepSeek) instead of human annotation, and what risk does this introduce?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Encoder -> Inference Router (implicit) -> [Light-Load Module or Heavy-Load Module] -> Correction Generator. Training Pipeline (separate) generates supervision data.

**Critical path:**  
1. Preprocess benchmark data into (prompt, reply, diagnosis, correction) quadruples using DeepSeek with task-specific templates (Tables 11–14).  
2. Fine-tune base LLM (Llama-3.2-1B/3B) on light-load (RealToxicityPrompts) and heavy-load (MIC) datasets separately.  
3. For jailbreak task, ensemble outputs from both fine-tuned models (Light+Heavy).  
4. Evaluate on held-out benchmarks (BBQ, JailbreakBench, RealToxicityPrompts) using external metrics (Perspective API, DeepSeek judge).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Specialization vs. Generalization: Separate models per load maximize performance but increase deployment complexity; a single router could reduce overhead but may misclassify load.  
- Data Generation Cost: Using DeepSeek avoids human annotation but risks propagating its heuristic biases into training data.  
- Interpretability vs. Efficiency: Heavy-load provides transparent steps but increases latency; light-load is fast but less interpretable.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Heuristic reliance: Model produces correct revisions without referencing diagnosed cues/foundations (tested via intervention experiments in 5.2).  
- Load misclassification: Light-load applied to implicit bias yields no correction; heavy-load applied to explicit toxicity may overcomplicate and fail to remove slurs.  
- Distribution shift: Performance drop on test tasks with different semantic distributions from fine-tuning data (e.g., novel bias types).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Light-load validation:** Fine-tune on RealToxicityPrompts, evaluate toxicity reduction on held-out toxic prompts using Perspective API. Expect lowest toxicity scores vs. Direct/CoT/Heuristic.  
2. **Heavy-load validation:** Fine-tune on MIC, evaluate bias correction accuracy on BBQ (gender/nationality/disability) using DeepSeek judge. Expect highest accuracy vs. baselines.  
3. **Cross-task mixing:** For jailbreak correction, ensemble light- and heavy-load outputs; evaluate accuracy on JailbreakBench. Expect Light+Heavy to outperform individual load models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the pragmatic inference load framework reliably generalize across multiple benchmarks within the same moral task domain (e.g., various social bias or toxicity datasets beyond those used)?
- **Basis in paper:** The Limitation section states: "In this paper, we apply one benchmark for each of our proposed inference methods due to the limited number of existing off-the-shelf benchmarks."
- **Why unresolved:** Only one benchmark per task was used (BBQ for bias, JailbreakBench for jailbreaks, RealToxicityPrompts for toxicity), so it is unclear whether the light/heavy load distinction consistently predicts performance across different datasets measuring the same moral construct.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct experiments with 2–3 additional benchmarks per task (e.g., StereoSet and CrowS-Pairs for bias; implicit toxicity corpora for direct toxic language) and verify whether performance patterns (light-load better for explicit cues, heavy-load for implicit) hold robustly.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the light-load pragmatic inference method be effectively extended to detect and correct implicit toxic language, which was not evaluated and may require heavier inference?
- **Basis in paper:** The Limitation section explicitly notes: "For the benchmark of toxic speech, we only focus on explicit toxic language without exploration with the implicit toxic language which is more challenging."
- **Why unresolved:** Implicit toxic language (e.g., sarcasm, dog whistles) lacks overtly harmful cues and relies on context, potentially requiring heavy-load rather than light-load reasoning. The method’s capability for such cases remains untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the light-load model to implicit toxicity benchmarks (e.g., implicit hate speech datasets), compare performance against heavy-load inference and baselines, and analyze failure cases to inform design adaptations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do inaccuracies in the off-the-shelf LLM–generated training data (DeepSeek) undermine the moral sensitivity and correctness of the fine-tuned pragmatic inference models?
- **Basis in paper:** The Limitation section states: "we use off-the-shelf LLMs to generate the datasets for training our pragmatic inference models. Although these models achieve strong performance, we cannot guarantee that the training data produced by the off-the-shelf LLMs is entirely accurate."
- **Why unresolved:** Training data for both light-load (RealToxicityPrompts revisions) and heavy-load (MIC dataset) relies on DeepSeek’s outputs; any errors (e.g., incorrect diagnoses, flawed corrections) could propagate to the models, but the paper does not assess data quality or its impact on final performance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform human evaluation on a stratified sample of the generated training data to estimate error rates; then run ablation studies training models on high-quality (human-verified) versus low-quality subsets to quantify effects on diagnostic and correction accuracy.

## Limitations
- **Data Generation Black Box:** Training/evaluation data creation via an unreleased DeepSeek prompt pipeline introduces unverified biases and heuristic artifacts; the anonymous repository is inaccessible, preventing exact replication.
- **Evaluation Opacity:** Reliance on DeepSeek as a judge and the Perspective API (version/key unspecified) risks metric instability; judge model alignment with human moral judgment is unvalidated.
- **Generalization Boundaries:** Cross-task success may reflect dataset overlap or superficial pattern matching rather than true moral reasoning transfer; the "pragmatic inference load" construct lacks independent validation.

## Confidence
- **Light-load mechanism (explicit cue removal):** Medium. Evidence is task-specific (toxicity reduction) but may not generalize beyond lexical heuristics.
- **Heavy-load mechanism (structured MFT reasoning):** Medium. Strong performance on BBQ, but intervention experiments suggest partial reliance on simplified cues rather than full chain reasoning.
- **Cross-task generalization:** Low. Success could stem from shared superficial features in benchmarks rather than a unified inference load principle.

## Next Checks
1. **Data Pipeline Audit:** Recreate the DeepSeek data generation using the cited prompt templates (Tables 11–14) with a fixed model version. Compare semantic diversity and error rates against the paper’s released samples.
2. **Metric Calibration:** Run all evaluations with a stable Perspective API version and a separate human validation set (n=200) for DeepSeek judge agreement on correction quality.
3. **Mechanism Stress Test:** Ablate the heavy-load chain (e.g., randomize moral foundation labels, remove consequence steps) on BBQ to quantify performance drop, isolating the contribution of each reasoning component.
