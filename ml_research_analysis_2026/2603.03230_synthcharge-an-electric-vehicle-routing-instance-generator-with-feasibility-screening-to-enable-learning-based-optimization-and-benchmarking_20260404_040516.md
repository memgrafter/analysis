# SynthCharge: An Electric Vehicle Routing Instance Generator with Feasibility Screening to Enable Learning-Based Optimization and Benchmarking

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03230  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03230  
- **Reference count:** 21  
- **Primary result:** SynthCharge is a parametric EVRPTW instance generator that integrates geometry-conditioned energy scaling and range-aware charging station placement with a two-stage feasibility screen; it produces non-trivially feasible instances (37–47% acceptance) at high speed (<0.01 s for N>10), and all screened instances are solvable by a baseline metaheuristic.

## Executive Summary
Electric Vehicle Routing Problems with Time Windows (EVRPTW) lack standardized, verifiable benchmarks, impeding reproducible evaluation of learning-based solvers. SynthCharge addresses this by generating synthetic EVRPTW instances with controllable parameters: spatial topology (random, clustered, mixed), customer count, time‑window tightness, and energy constraints. Its core innovation is a two‑stage feasibility screening process—fast linear structural checks followed by an exact MILP for very small instances—that filters out obviously unsolvable cases while maintaining generation efficiency. Battery capacity is adaptively scaled to the maximum inter‑customer distance, and charging stations are placed using range‑aware heuristics to avoid trivial infeasibilities. Validation shows the screening strongly correlates with actual solvability; a metaheuristic found feasible routes for 100% of the screened instances. The generator operates efficiently and yields diverse, challenging benchmarks suitable for training and testing learning-based methods.

## Method Summary
SynthCharge constructs an EVRPTW instance in five stages: (1) generate depot and customer coordinates in the unit square according to a chosen spatial family (random uniform, clustered Gaussian, or mixed); (2) compute the maximum pairwise customer distance `d_max` and adaptively set battery capacity `B` by scaling a target range `R* = κ·d_max` to stay within `[R_min, R_max]`; (3) place charging stations via range‑aware rules (e.g., near midpoints of distant customer pairs, along depot‑rays for remote customers) with separation constraints; (4) assign demands (uniformly sampled, rescaled if total exceeds 3×vehicle capacity), service times, and time windows based on a planning horizon `H` and width parameter ϕ; (5) run feasibility screening: Stage 1 checks three linear necessary conditions (energy reachability from depot/station, depot return time, station accessibility), and for `N≤10` Stage 2 solves an exact MILP. Instances passing all applicable stages are output with full metadata; rejected instances are discarded.

## Key Results
- Acceptance rates vary meaningfully (37–47%) across spatial families and time‑window regimes for `N=5–100`, indicating non‑trivial feasibility challenges.
- Generation is fast: averages <0.01 seconds per instance for `N>10` (Stage 1 only) and ~0.06–0.10 seconds for `N≤10` (including MILP).
- A baseline metaheuristic (VNS/TS) found feasible routes for 100% of the generation‑screened instances, confirming that Stage 1 structural screening strongly correlates with actual routing feasibility.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Two‑Stage Feasibility Screening**  
- **Claim:** The screening filters out unsolvable instances efficiently; Stage 1’s linear checks are a strong necessary proxy for global feasibility, validated by Stage 2 MILP and metaheuristic success.  
- **Mechanism:** Stage 1 applies `O(N)` necessary‑condition tests: every customer must be within range `R` of the depot or a station, the depot must be returnable within the horizon, and all customers must be within range of some station. For `N≤10`, Stage 2 solves an exact MILP to certify global feasibility.  
- **Core assumption:** Satisfying Stage 1’s conditions is highly correlated with the existence of a feasible route; exact verification on small instances confirms this.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section III‑F] describes the two stages; [section IV‑C] reports 100% metaheuristic success on Stage 1‑passed instances.  
- **Break condition:** If the metaheuristic (or exact solver for `N>10`) began failing on Stage 1‑passed instances, the correlation would break. No such failures were observed in tests.

**Mechanism 2: Geometry‑Conditioned Adaptive Energy Scaling**  
- **Claim:** Tying battery capacity `B` to the spatial dispersion `d_max` prevents trivial feasibility/infeasibility across layouts and customer counts, maintaining a consistent energy‑constrained regime.  
- **Mechanism:** Compute `d_max` from customer coordinates; set target range `R* = κ·d_max`; clamp effective range `R` to `[R_min, R_max]`; define `B = r·R`. This makes energy availability proportional to the maximum pairwise distance.  
- **Core assumption:** `d_max` is a meaningful summary of the energy challenge posed by a Euclidean layout. Bounding `R` relative to the unit square avoids extremes.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section III‑D] defines adaptive scaling; [section IV‑A] reports varied acceptance rates (37–47%) across spatial families and time‑window widths, showing non‑trivial constraint regimes.  
- **Break condition:** If `d_max` poorly predicts required range (e.g., due to non‑convex layouts or clustered extremes), scaling could yield trivially easy/hard instances. The observed γ variation suggests the mechanism works within the tested unit‑square model.

**Mechanism 3: Range‑Aware Charging Station Placement**  
- **Claim:** Heuristic station placement using thresholds based on range `R` ensures basic spatial reachability, reducing obvious infeasibilities before screening.  
- **Mechanism:** Place stations near midpoints of customer pairs with `d_ij > 0.8R`; for customers far from depot (`d_0i > 0.7R`), place a station at distance `0.6R` along the depot‑ray if no station lies within `0.5R`. Enforce minimum inter‑station (`0.3R`) and station‑customer (`0.04`) separations.  
- **Core assumption:** These geometric rules, tied to `R`, prevent large coverage gaps that would cause immediate energy infeasibility.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section III‑C] gives explicit thresholds; [section IV‑A] shows γ never reaches 100% (max 47.4%), so placement alone does not guarantee feasibility—Stage 1 catches remaining issues.  
- **Break condition:** If spatial topology systematically violates heuristics (e.g., many isolated customers beyond `0.8R` without depot‑proximate extremes), placement might fail, leading to high Stage 1 rejections. The reported γ indicates effectiveness within tested families.

## Foundational Learning
- **Parametric instance generation for benchmarking**  
  **Why needed:** SynthCharge produces distributions of instances with controlled parameters; a learning model’s performance may vary across these distributions.  
  **Quick check:** If you change the spatial family from ‘clustered’ to ‘random’ while keeping `N` and `ϕ` constant, what aspect of the instance distribution changes, and why would a learning model’s performance potentially change?

- **Feasibility screening as a proxy, not a guarantee**  
  **Why needed:** Stage 1 checks are necessary but not sufficient; a learner must not assume all screened instances are trivially solvable. The 100% metaheuristic success is empirical correlation, not proof.  
  **Quick check:** For an instance with `N=50` that passes Stage 1 screening, what specific types of infeasibility could still exist that Stage 1 would miss?

- **Acceptance rate (γ) as a data reliability metric**  
  **Why needed:** γ quantifies the yield of the generator. Low γ indicates a regime that produces mostly infeasible instances, making it a poor training/evaluation distribution.  
  **Quick check:** To generate a challenging but mostly feasible dataset, would you aim for a higher or lower γ? What parameter adjustments might move γ in that direction?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Spatial Topology Module → Charging Infrastructure Module → Parameter Assignment Module → Feasibility Screening Module → Output & Metadata Module  
- **Critical path:** Geometry generation → Charging station placement → Parameter assignment (demands, service times, time windows, battery) → Stage 1 screening → (if `N≤10`) Stage 2 MILP → Output + metadata  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  1. Speed vs. Validation: Stage 1 is `O(N)` but incomplete; Stage 2 is exact but exponential (restricted to `N≤10`).  
  2. Generality vs. Control: Unit‑square Euclidean model enables fast, controlled generation but sacrifices realism (no road networks, time‑dependent travel).  
  3. Adaptive Scaling: Tying `B` to `d_max` maintains consistent difficulty but adds dependency; extreme cluster separation could force `B` to `R_max`, capping difficulty.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Consistently low γ (<20%) across seeds: parameters too strict (tight ϕ, low R, clustered); adjust ϕ up, increase `R_max`, or change spatial family.  
  - Stage 1 passes but metaheuristic fails: breakdown in screening correlation; likely due to subtle routing/temporal conflicts.  
  - Generation hangs/fails: rejection‑sampling loops (customer/station placement) with pathological parameters (e.g., `d_min` too large).  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Regime Yield Test:** For fixed `N` (e.g., 50), generate many instances per `(spatial_family, ϕ)` combination; compute γ and verify trends match Table II.  
  2. **Screening Correlation Check:** For `N=5–10`, solve all Stage 1‑passed instances with the provided MILP and a simple heuristic; confirm 100% feasibility.  
  3. **Parameter Sensitivity Test:** Hold spatial family (R) and ϕ (medium) constant; vary adaptive scaling parameters (κ, `R_min`, `R_max`); observe γ shifts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the exact MILP feasibility verification stage be scaled efficiently beyond `N≤10` to provide stronger guarantees for larger SynthCharge instances?
2. To what extent do the Euclidean unit‑square domain and deterministic travel time assumptions in SynthCharge limit the generalizability of learning‑based EVRPTW solvers trained on its instances to real‑world road networks with non‑Euclidean distances and time‑dependent travel speeds?
3. What is the causal relationship between the generator’s key parameters (e.g., spatial family, time window width ϕ, adaptive energy scaling) and the observed acceptance rate γ, and how does this affect the diversity and difficulty distribution of the generated benchmark suite?
4. Can the range‑aware charging station placement heuristic be generalized to incorporate more realistic charging infrastructure models (e.g., heterogeneous charger types, partial recharging, nonlinear charging curves) while maintaining computational efficiency and feasibility screening reliability?
5. How should benchmarking protocols for learning‑based EVRPTW solvers leverage SynthCharge’s parametric generation to rigorously evaluate omni‑generalization across constraint regimes, and what minimal evaluation suites are needed to reveal sensitivity to distribution shifts?

## Limitations
- Missing critical parameters: cluster center count `k`, planning horizon `H`, midpoint perturbation `δ`, and exact Stage 2 MILP formulation details (charging time modeling) are not provided.
- Screening sufficiency for `N>10` relies on Stage 1 checks alone; while correlated with solvability in tests, this is not an exact guarantee.
- Adaptive scaling robustness depends on `d_max` as a predictor; this may fail for complex geometries beyond unit‑square Euclidean layouts.
