# SurveyLens: A Research Discipline-Aware Benchmark for Automatic Survey Generation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11238
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11238
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Deep Research Agents achieve highest rubric scores (macro 3.41/5) and a trade-off exists between structural outline quality (ASG systems) and content synthesis (Deep Research Agents), with a retrieval-synthesis gap revealed by RAMS/TAMS.

## Executive Summary
Automatic Survey Generation (ASG) systems often produce outputs that do not adhere to discipline-specific academic standards, as current evaluation relies on generic metrics predominantly biased toward Computer Science. This paper introduces SurveyLens, a comprehensive benchmark designed to address this gap. SurveyLens comprises (1) SurveyLens-1k, a dataset of 1,000 human-written surveys spanning 10 academic disciplines, and (2) a dual-lens evaluation framework that combines Discipline-Aware Rubric Evaluation (leveraging LLM-as-a-judge with preference-aligned weights) and Canonical Alignment Evaluation (using RAMS and TAMS metrics). Evaluation of 11 ASG methods reveals that Deep Research Agents consistently achieve the highest rubric scores (macro-average 3.41/5), while a clear trade-off emerges: ASG systems excel in structural outline quality (~4.0) but lag in content synthesis, where Deep Research Agents dominate (>4.0). Canonical alignment metrics uncover a retrieval-synthesis gap: TAMS (~0.3-0.5) is much higher than RAMS (~0.05-0.2), indicating that generated surveys contain high-quality isolated segments but suffer from poor global coherence. These findings demonstrate the importance of discipline-aware evaluation and highlight areas for improvement in ASG systems.

## Method Summary
SurveyLens operationalizes survey evaluation through a Structured Survey Representation (SSR) that decomposes surveys into outline (O), content (C), and references (R). The benchmark includes SurveyLens-1k, a curated collection of 1,000 human-written surveys balanced across 10 disciplines, constructed via Semantic Scholar API, citation-based ranking, and human verification. For evaluation, two complementary lenses are employed. The Discipline-Aware Rubric Evaluation distills universal aspects (e.g., "Synthesis and Integration"), expands them into discipline-specific criteria using high-quality human surveys, merges them, and optimizes aspect weights via the Bradley-Terry model on pairwise preferences from Gemini-3 Pro. The weighted sum of scores from an LLM-as-a-judge (Qwen-30B) yields the final rubric score. The Canonical Alignment Evaluation computes Redundancy-Aware Matching Score (RAMS) via Hungarian matching with a redundancy penalty and Thresholded Average Maximum Similarity (TAMS) to capture local semantic coverage and global synthesis when comparing generated and human SSR entries using embeddings (Qwen-Embedding-8B).

## Key Results
- Deep Research Agents achieve the highest macro-average rubric score (3.41/5), significantly outperforming Vanilla LLMs (2.43) and traditional ASG systems (3.02).
- A clear trade-off exists: ASG systems excel in structural outline quality (e.g., SurveyForge ~4.0) while Deep Research Agents dominate content synthesis (scores >4.0).
- Canonical alignment metrics reveal a retrieval-synthesis gap: TAMS scores (~0.3-0.5) are much higher than RAMS (~0.05-0.2), indicating proficient local segments but poor global coherence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Discipline-aware rubric evaluation aligns ASG outputs with field-specific standards via learned preference weights.
- **Claim:** Discipline-aware rubric evaluation aligns ASG outputs with field-specific standards via learned preference weights.
- **Mechanism:** The system distills universal evaluation aspects (e.g., "Synthesis and Integration") from existing literature, expands them into discipline-specific criteria using high-quality human surveys (SurveyLens-1k), and merges them into consolidated rubrics. It then uses the Bradley-Terry model to optimize aspect weights based on pairwise comparisons of human-written surveys, simulating expert judgment to prioritize discipline-critical criteria.
- **Core assumption:** Expert preferences for survey quality can be approximated by a strong LLM (Gemini-3 Pro) and that these preferences vary systematically across disciplines.
- **Evidence:** Section 3.3.1 details the two-stage rubric generation and Bradley-Terry optimization; Table 7 shows that learned weights improve alignment with human ELO ratings compared to uniform weights.
- **Break condition:** If Bradley-Terry weights fail to improve alignment with human ELO ratings (per Table 7 ablation) or if discipline-specific rubrics do not discriminate between ASG methods across fields (e.g., no performance variation in Table 4).

### Mechanism 2: Canonical alignment metrics disambiguate local semantic recall from global narrative synthesis.
- **Claim:** Canonical alignment metrics (RAMS/TAMS) disentangle local semantic recall from global narrative synthesis, revealing a retrieval-synthesis trade-off.
- **Mechanism:** RAMS uses Hungarian matching with a redundancy penalty to measure precise, non-repetitive coverage of human survey entries. TAMS computes the average maximum similarity per generated entry without matching, capturing the best possible local semantic match. The gap between high TAMS and low RAMS indicates systems retrieve relevant facts (high local similarity) but fail to synthesize them coherently (low unique coverage).
- **Core assumption:** Long-form survey quality requires both accurate information retrieval (local) and non-redundant, logically integrated synthesis (global), which are separable dimensions.
- **Evidence:** Section 3.3.2 formally defines RAMS and TAMS; Section 4.2.2 Finding 5 reports consistently higher TAMS than RAMS, supporting the retrieval-synthesis gap.
- **Break condition:** If RAMS and TAMS are tightly correlated across systems (no gap) or if human evaluations do not penalize redundancy as RAMS does, suggesting the metrics do not capture distinct quality dimensions.

### Mechanism 3: Architectural trade-offs between structural planning (ASG systems) and content fluency (Deep Research Agents) lead to discipline-dependent performance.
- **Claim:** Architectural trade-offs between structural planning (ASG systems) and content fluency (Deep Research Agents) lead to discipline-dependent performance.
- **Mechanism:** ASG systems use specialized pipelines (e.g., outline-first, memory-driven drafting) that enforce academic structural conventions (hierarchical sections, citation formatting), excelling in STEM fields with rigid formats. Deep Research Agents leverage extended context and agentic workflows for fluent, information-dense prose but lack domain-specific structural constraints, leading to generic organization. This creates a skeleton-versus-flesh trade-off.
- **Core assumption:** Disciplinary norms vary in emphasis on structural rigor vs. narrative synthesis (e.g., Engineering values formal precision; Humanities value integrative narratives).
- **Evidence:** Section 4.2.1 Finding 3 shows component analysis: ASG systems (e.g., SurveyForge) score ~4.0 on Outline, Deep Research Agents score >4.0 on Content. Finding 4 shows ASG systems favor STEM; vanilla LLMs better in humanities; Deep Research Agents balanced.
- **Break condition:** If Deep Research Agents match or exceed ASG systems in outline scores without losing content quality, collapsing the trade-off, or if discipline performance patterns reverse (e.g., Deep Research Agents fail in humanities).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Discipline-Aware Evaluation**  
  Why needed: Survey writing conventions differ by field (e.g., evidence hierarchy in Medicine vs. benchmarking in CS); generic metrics like ROUGE cannot capture these norms.  
  Quick check: What are two concrete differences between a high-quality survey in Physics and one in Sociology, based on Table 1 statistics?

- **Concept: Structured Survey Representation (SSR)**  
  Why needed: SSR (S = (O, C, R)) decomposes surveys into outline, content, and references, enabling fine-grained, component-wise evaluation (rubric aspects, RAMS/TAMS matching).  
  Quick check: How would you represent a survey's "reference list" in SSR format, and why is this decomposition necessary for canonical alignment?

- **Concept: Bradley-Terry Model for Preference Alignment**  
  Why needed: Equal weighting of rubric aspects assumes all criteria are equally important across disciplines. Bradley-Terry learns weights from pairwise comparisons to reflect field-specific priorities (e.g., "Substantive Integrity" may weigh more in Biology than in Business).  
  Quick check: Why use pairwise comparisons of human-written surveys instead of asking experts to score each aspect independently?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** SurveyLens-1k Construction → SSR Generator → Rubric Engine → Evaluator

**Critical path:** Input topic → ASG method generates survey → SSR conversion → (a) Rubric scoring per component (O, C, R) with weighted sum → (b) Canonical alignment (RAMS/TAMS) against human SSR → output scores.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Rubric granularity vs scalability: Fine-grained aspects (11 sub-metrics) improve alignment but increase LLM call cost and prompt complexity.
- RAMS strictness vs recall: Hungarian matching prevents redundancy but may penalize valid paraphrasing; TAMS captures semantic coverage but ignores repetition.
- LLM judge consistency: Using a single LLM (Gemini-3 Pro for weight learning, Qwen-30B for scoring) risks model-specific biases; ensemble or multiple judges would increase robustness.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low rubric scores in "Reference" across all systems indicate persistent citation hallucination.
- High TAMS-RAMS gap (>0.3) signals retrieval-synthesis failure.
- Discipline-specific weight vectors failing to improve concordance with human ELO ratings.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline benchmarking:** Run 11 ASG methods on 100 SurveyLens-1k topics, compute rubric macro-scores (Table 4) and component scores (Figures 4-5) to identify paradigm strengths/weaknesses.
2. **Canonical gap analysis:** Calculate RAMS/TAMS per discipline (Table 5) and structural ratios (Table 6) to diagnose retrieval vs. synthesis failures (e.g., "Wall of Text" bloat).
3. **Human alignment validation:** Collect pairwise preferences from domain experts (7 PhDs) for 100 surveys; compute Spearman ρ between rubric scores and ELO ratings; ablate weighting schemes (Table 7) to confirm preference learning utility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can discipline-aware metrics be effectively integrated as optimization objectives to align ASG systems with specific disciplinary norms during training or inference? (The paper introduces evaluation but not optimization.)
2. Why does LLM-based evaluation of content quality show significantly lower alignment with human expert judgments compared to outline and reference evaluation? (Potential issues with LLM judge, rubric design, or subjectivity.)
3. Can canonical alignment metrics (RAMS/TAMS) be extended or supplemented to quantitatively measure narrative coherence and adherence to disciplinary narrative conventions, beyond semantic segment matching? (Current metrics capture coverage but not logical flow.)
4. To what extent does the construction of SurveyLens-1k—particularly its reliance on Semantic Scholar API and citation-based ranking—introduce biases that affect the generalizability of its findings across academic disciplines? (Sample may favor English, high-impact, STEM venues.)

## Limitations
- Hyperparameters λ and τ_c for RAMS/TAMS are undisclosed; their exact values affect absolute scores and gap magnitudes.
- The pairwise preference collection protocol (prompt phrasing, number of pairs, sampling) is missing, hindering weight reproducibility.
- Access to proprietary Deep Research Agents (Gemini Deep Research, Qwen Deep Research) may require commercial subscriptions and involves undocumented, potentially non-deterministic APIs, challenging exact replication.
