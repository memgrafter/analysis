# How does Graph Structure Modulate Membership-Inference Risk for Graph Neural Networks?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17130
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17130
- **Reference count:** 37
- **Primary result:** Graph structure (sampling and edge access) significantly modulates membership inference risk for GNNs; snowball sampling increases risk, providing all edges at inference reduces risk, and the generalization gap is an unreliable proxy.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies how graph structure influences membership inference (MI) risk for node-level tasks in graph neural networks (GNNs). It formalizes MI attacks that consider node-neighborhood tuples and investigates two key factors: (i) training-graph construction via random or snowball sampling, and (ii) inference-time edge access (original train/test edges, all edges, or no edges). Experiments on CORA, CHAMELEON, PUBMED and GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT models reveal that snowball sampling—by favoring high-degree nodes—increases both the generalization gap and membership advantage relative to random sampling. Strikingly, enabling all edges during inference (including inter-train-test edges) reduces the generalization gap and yields the lowest membership advantage, even when the gap is small. Thus, the generalization gap is an incomplete proxy for MI risk. Theoretically, the authors prove that inductive training splits break statistical exchangeability, invalidating standard differential privacy membership advantage bounds for GNNs.

These findings highlight the critical role of graph structural context in privacy risk: the way training nodes are sampled and the edges provided during inference directly affect the distinguishability of member vs. non-member nodes. The results challenge the common practice of using generalization gap as a privacy metric and call for graph-aware privacy auditing frameworks.

## Method Summary
The study adopts an inductive node-split setting where training and test nodes are disjoint and no edges connect them during training. Training subgraphs are generated via either uniform random node sampling or snowball sampling (starting from seeds and iteratively adding neighbors). At inference, three edge access regimes are considered: ORIG (edges wholly within train or test sets, discarding train-test edges), ALLEDGES (the full original graph), and NOGRAPH (no edges, only node features).

Target GNN models—2-layer GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT—are trained on the training subgraph. For any query node under a given inference regime, the model yields posterior class probabilities and the cross-entropy loss given the true label. A supervised attack model (2-layer MLP) is trained to predict membership from the concatenated posterior vector and loss. Membership advantage is defined as the maximum difference between true positive and false positive rates over all thresholds. The entire pipeline is repeated across multiple seeds, datasets, and model configurations to assess the impact of sampling and edge access on MI risk.

## Key Results
- Snowball sampling increases MI risk due to coverage bias, while enabling all edges at inference (ALLEDGES) reduces MI risk and often shrinks the generalization gap, demonstrating that edge access can lower MI risk even when the gap is small.
- The empirical generalization gap is an unreliable proxy for MI risk; edge access and training-graph construction modulate MI risk independently of the gap.
- Inductive training splits (random or snowball) break statistical exchangeability, undermining standard differential privacy membership advantage bounds for GNNs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Snowball sampling increases membership inference (MI) risk by inducing coverage bias that oversamples high-degree nodes, creating a structural distribution shift between training and test nodes.
- **Mechanism:** Starting from seed nodes, snowball sampling iteratively expands by selecting neighbors. This makes high-degree nodes more likely to be included early and repeatedly, while peripheral nodes or distinct communities are under-sampled. The resulting training subgraph has higher average connectivity and a biased degree distribution. This structural bias makes member nodes (from the snowballed subgraph) more distinguishable from non-member nodes (from the less-connected periphery) to an MI attacker using posterior probabilities or loss values.
- **Core assumption:** The attacker can query node predictions with access to neighborhood structure, and model predictions systematically differ between the biased training subgraph and the held-out test set due to the structural shift.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: "snowball's coverage bias often harms generalisation relative to random sampling, while ... increases ... membership advantage"
  - Section V.A: "snowball sampling ... tends to favor high-degree nodes ... the probability of selecting a node in the training set increases with its degree"
  - Neighbor papers focus on MI attacks but do not explicitly study snowball sampling bias; this mechanism is a novel contribution.
- **Break condition:** If snowball sampling is constrained to perfectly match the degree distribution of the full graph (e.g., via re-weighted sampling), the coverage bias and associated MI risk increase should diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Inference-time edge access (specifically, allowing inter-train-test edges) reduces MI risk by aligning the query neighborhood context with the full graph distribution, even when this shrinks the generalization gap.
- **Mechanism:** During inference, the GNN aggregates features from the provided neighborhood. If only original train/test disjoint edges are used (ORIG), the neighborhood for a test node excludes edges to training nodes, creating a "context shift." Using ALLEDGES includes all edges from the full graph, providing a more representative neighborhood for both train and test nodes. This reduces the discrepancy in the aggregation context between members and non-members, making their posterior distributions more similar and thus harder to distinguish for an MI attacker.
- **Core assumption:** The GNN's message-passing operation is sensitive to the structural context of the query neighborhood, and this sensitivity can be exploited by an attacker. Providing a fuller, more realistic neighborhood reduces systematic differences in model outputs.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "enabling inter–train–test edges at inference improves test accuracy, shrinks the train–test gap, and yields the lowest membership advantage"
  - Section V.B shows KL divergence between posteriors changes dramatically when comparing ALLEDGES vs. NOGRAPH inference.
  - Neighbors study MI attacks but typically assume fixed neighborhood access; the role of dynamic edge provision is under-explored.
- **Break condition:** If the GNN architecture is entirely insensitive to edge structure (e.g., a model that ignores the graph and uses only node features), then varying edge access would have no effect on MI risk.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The empirical generalization gap (train-test accuracy difference) is an unreliable proxy for MI risk because edge access and training-graph construction modulate MI risk independently of the gap.
- **Mechanism:** MI risk depends on the distinguishability of model outputs (posteriors, losses) between members and non-members. This distinguishability is directly affected by (i) the neighborhood context at inference (edge access) and (ii) the structural bias in the training graph (sampling). These factors can alter posterior distributions without proportionally changing the aggregate train/test accuracy gap. For example, ALLEDGES may shrink the gap by improving test accuracy but also lower MI risk by homogenizing neighborhood contexts.
- **Core assumption:** An MI attacker uses fine-grained output signals (posterior vectors, loss values) that capture nuances not reflected in scalar accuracy metrics. Changes in these signals can be uncorrelated with changes in overall accuracy.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "generalisation gap ... is an incomplete proxy for MI risk: access to edges dominates—MI can rise or fall independent of gap changes."
  - Section VI.C Figures 5-7 show cases (e.g., PUBMED with GCN, snowball, ALLEDGES) where performance gap is near zero but membership advantage is high, or vice versa.
  - Some neighbor papers hint at gaps between robustness and privacy, but this specific decoupling is a key finding here.
- **Break condition:** If the only attack signal is binary accuracy (an unrealistic MI attack), then generalization gap would perfectly correlate with MI risk. The mechanism breaks when attackers use richer signals.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: GNN Message Passing & Neighborhood Aggregation**  
  *Why needed:* The entire analysis hinges on how a GNN's prediction for a node depends on its L-hop neighborhood. The mechanisms involve how changes in that neighborhood (via sampling or inference access) alter outputs.  
  *Quick check:* If you change the edges incident to a query node but keep its features and label fixed, what layers in a GCN/GraphSAGE/GAT are directly affected, and how might the final class prediction change?

- **Concept: Inductive vs. Transductive Learning in GNNs**  
  *Why needed:* The paper's setting is inductive (train/test nodes disjoint, no train-test edges during training). This contrasts with transductive settings where all nodes are present, which affects statistical exchangeability and DP bounds (Section VII).  
  *Quick check:* In a transductive GNN training, can information from a test node influence the representation of a training node during message passing? How does this differ in the inductive setting?

- **Concept: Membership Inference Attack (MIA) Setup**  
  *Why needed:* To understand the "membership advantage" metric and the attack model's input (posterior + loss). The paper modifies the standard MIA threat model to include graph structure.  
  *Quick check:* In the paper's attack, what two pieces of information are concatenated to form the attack model's input for a node? Why is the loss value included?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Data Pipeline -> GNN Trainer -> Inference Simulator -> Attack Evaluator.
- **Critical path**: Sampling strategy -> Training subgraph structure -> Trained GNN parameters -> Inference neighborhood (edge access) -> Node predictions -> Attack features -> Membership advantage.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Sampling strategy:* Random gives i.i.d.-like node distribution but may yield poorly connected subgraphs; Snowball gives well-connected subgraphs but introduces coverage bias. Tradeoff is between realistic connectivity for GNN training and statistical exchangeability for DP auditing.
  - *Edge access at inference:* ORIG is strict inductive but may give poor test performance; ALLEDGES improves accuracy and can lower MI risk by providing context; NOGRAPH isolates node features but may hurt performance. Tradeoff is between realistic query context and privacy.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - High train-test accuracy gap but low MI advantage (e.g., PUBMED 50% snowball, ALLEDGES, GCN). Indicates edge access homogenized neighborhoods.
  - Low train-test accuracy gap but high MI advantage (same setting). Shows gap is insufficient.
  - Attack success varies dramatically across edge access regimes while accuracy gap changes less.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- What specific edge manipulation techniques (e.g., adding, removing, or altering edges in queries) maximize membership inference advantage against GNNs? The current study uses a static edge context; developing intelligent attacks would require exploring active edge manipulation strategies that differentiate member and non-member queries.
- How can we construct privacy auditing frameworks for differentially private GNNs that remain valid despite the breakdown of statistical exchangeability in common inductive training schemes? Existing auditing techniques assume train/test samples are exchangeable; this fails for GNNs due to neighborhood dependencies. Without valid bounds, practitioners cannot reliably certify DP guarantees.
- Under which combinations of training paradigm (transductive vs. inductive), node sampling strategy (random, snowball, others), and neighborhood scope (L-hop) does statistical exchangeability hold for GNNs? A comprehensive characterization is absent, yet it underpins the applicability of many DP privacy bounds.

## Limitations

- Hyperparameter choices (e.g., GNN layers, hidden size, attack MLP configuration) are not fully specified; reproduction relies on assumed defaults.
- Evaluation is limited to three citation networks (CORA, PUBMED, CHAMELEON) and three standard GNN architectures; generalizability to heterogeneous, temporal, or large-scale graphs remains unknown.
- The membership attack uses a specific supervised MLP on posterior+loss; alternative attack designs (loss-only, shadow models) might yield different trends.
