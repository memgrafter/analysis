# N-gram-like Language Models Predict Reading Time Best

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.09872
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09872
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: The inverse scaling effect—where larger language models show worse correlation with human reading time—is explained by a tight link between a model’s surprisal correlation with reading time and its correlation with bigram/trigram surprisal; lower-order n-grams (unigram, bigram) consistently predict reading time metrics best.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the inverse scaling paradox: as neural language models improve in next-word prediction, their surprisal values become less correlated with human reading time measures from eye-tracking. The authors test the hypothesis that reading time is primarily driven by simple, local sequential statistics (n-grams) rather than complex contextual patterns. Using diverse large corpora and multiple neural model families (Pythia, GPT-2, Gemstone) at various training stages, they compute surprisal and correlate it with four eye-tracking metrics across two reading corpora (Provo, GECO). They find that lower-order n-grams (unigram, bigram) consistently yield higher correlations with reading time than higher-order n-grams. Crucially, across all models and training steps, the extent to which a neural model’s surprisal correlates with reading time is nearly perfectly linearly related to how closely its surprisal aligns with bigram and trigram surprisal (Pearson’s r up to 0.993). This explains the inverse scaling: as models learn higher-order dependencies, their surprisal diverges from the low-order statistics that govern reading difficulty.

## Method Summary
The study analyzes eye-tracking data from the Provo Corpus (2,449 words) and GECO (43,251 words), excluding sentence-initial/terminal words and skipped tokens. Surprisal (negative log probability) is computed for each word using: (1) n-gram models of orders 1–5 trained on six large text corpora (Pile, OpenWebText, C4, Dolma, DCLM, OLMo-Mix) via infini-gram and Stupid Backoff; (2) neural language models from the Pythia suite (14M–12B parameters, multiple checkpoints), Open GPT-2 (124M, 355M, multiple seeds), and Gemstone (50M–2B parameters, checkpoints). For each model checkpoint, Pearson correlation is calculated between its surprisal and each reading time metric (First Fixation Duration, First Pass Duration, Go-Past Duration, Total Duration). Separately, the correlation between the model’s surprisal and corresponding n-gram surprisal (orders 1–3, on the same training corpus) is computed. The key analysis correlates these two sets of correlation values across all models/checkpoints to test whether a model’s fit to reading time tracks its alignment with low-order n-gram statistics.

## Key Results
- Lower-order n-grams (unigram, bigram) consistently produce higher correlations with all reading time metrics than higher-order n-grams (4-gram, 5-gram), across all six training corpora.
- For neural language models, the correlation between model surprisal and reading time follows a peak-and-decline trajectory over training steps; this trajectory is nearly identical to the correlation between model surprisal and bigram/trigram surprisal.
- Across model families, datasets, and metrics, the Pearson correlation between (LM→reading time) and (LM→bigram/trigram) is extremely strong (r ≥ 0.986), explaining the inverse scaling effect.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Local Statistics Drive Reading Time
Reading time is sensitive to simple, local sequential statistics (bigram/trigram) rather than the complex, long-range dependencies captured by advanced neural LMs. Neural models initially learn low-order statistics during training, so their surprisal correlates highly with reading time. As training progresses and models improve at next-word prediction, they capture higher-order patterns, causing their surprisal to diverge from the low-order statistics that actually influence online reading effort. This divergence explains why correlation peaks and then declines as models scale. Evidence: The alignment between LM-reading time correlation and LM-bigram/trigram correlation is near-perfect (r up to 0.993) across training steps; lower-order n-grams consistently outperform higher orders for predicting reading time.

### Mechanism 2: Differential Metric Sensitivity
Different eye-tracking metrics reflect varying sensitivity to n-gram order. Early metrics like First Fixation Duration (~200ms) align more closely with trigram surprisal, while later metrics (First Pass, Go-Past, Total Duration) align more with bigram surprisal. This maps onto temporal processing stages: the earliest ocular decisions may use a slightly richer local context before orthographic identification completes, whereas later integration relies on immediate transitional probabilities. Evidence: For Pythia on Provo, the LM→First Fixation correlation correlates most with trigram surprisal (r=0.993), while LM→First Pass and LM→Go-Past correlate highest with bigram surprisal (r=0.984 each).

### Mechanism 3: Early Processing Bottleneck
The predominance of single, early fixations in natural reading (Provo: 82.5%; GECO: 88.7%) suggests that eye movement decisions are made before deep lexical or syntactic integration. This early stage—governed by rapid orthographic identification—can only utilize shallow, surface-level statistics effectively captured by (n-1)-grams. Deeper predictions from neural LMs’ higher layers require more processing time and thus do not influence the earliest fixation durations. Evidence: The N400 ERP component, which correlates with larger LMs, arises around 300ms post-word onset, later than the initiation of saccade planning; thus, early reading time metrics likely rely on low-level statistical patterns.

## Foundational Learning
- **Surprisal**: Negative log probability of a word given its context; higher surprisal indicates greater unexpectedness and predicts longer reading time. *Why needed*: It is the core linking variable between language model outputs and human processing effort. *Quick check*: If a word has probability 0.1 in context, its surprisal is −log₂(0.1) ≈ 3.32 bits; would you expect longer or shorter reading time than for a word with probability 0.5?
- **N-gram Language Models**: Probabilistic models that predict the next word based on the previous *n*−1 words; order (unigram, bigram, etc.) defines context window. *Why needed*: They represent simple statistical learners whose performance is compared against neural LMs to isolate the role of contextual complexity. *Quick check*: What is the key difference in context used between a bigram (n=2) and trigram (n=3) model?
- **Inverse Scaling Effect**: The phenomenon where larger, more accurate language models show diminishing or negative returns in fit to human reading time data. *Why needed*: It is the central puzzle the paper explains. *Quick check*: Describe the typical correlation trajectory between a transformer LM’s surprisal and reading time as training progresses (use “increases,” “peaks,” “declines”).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Source Corpora (Pile, OpenWebText, etc.) -> N-gram Engine -> N-gram Surprisal; Reading Corpora (Provo, GECO) -> Eye-Tracking Metrics; Both Corpora -> Neural LM Suite -> LM Surprisal; [N-gram Surprisal, LM Surprisal, Eye-Tracking Metrics] -> Analysis Core -> Pearson Correlations -> Alignment Analysis

**Critical path**:
1. **Data Alignment**: Tokenize reading texts with each LM’s tokenizer; handle subword aggregation; align word-level reading time means with token-level surprisal.
2. **Surprisal Extraction**: For each model checkpoint, compute token probabilities; aggregate to word-level surprisal. For n-grams, retrieve word-sequence probabilities via Stupid Backoff.
3. **Correlation Calculation**: For each (model, dataset) pair, compute r(LM_surprisal, RT_metric) and r(LM_surprisal, n-gram_surprisal).
4. **Alignment Analysis**: Across all models/checkpoints, correlate the two sets of r values to quantify how much LM→RT correlation is explained by LM→n-gram correlation.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **N-gram order vs. corpus size**: Lower orders are reliably estimable from smaller data; higher orders require massive corpora but still underperform in predicting reading time.
- **LM scale vs. alignment**: Largest models (12B+) show clearest inverse scaling but are costly; signal is already strong at 1B–2B parameters.
- **Corpus choice for n-grams**: Matching the LM’s training corpus (e.g., Pile for Pythia) ensures fair comparison; domain mismatches introduce noise.

**Failure signatures**:
- **Tokenization mismatch**: Reading words split into multiple tokens vs. word-level n-grams causes misalignment.
- **Unstable n-gram estimates**: High-order n-grams on smaller corpora yield many zero probabilities; requires robust smoothing.
- **Corpus-specific artifacts**: Some corpora (e.g., C4) produce anomalously high n-gram correlations, suggesting domain bias.
- **Pooling errors**: Incorrect aggregation across models/checkpoints can inflate or obscure the alignment correlation.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Replicate n-gram scaling**: Compute correlations for unigram–5-gram surprisal with all reading time metrics across six corpora; confirm lower orders dominate.
2. **Training trajectory alignment**: Using Pythia checkpoints, plot r(LM, RT) and r(LM, bigram/trigram) over training steps; expect near-identical peak-and-decline curves and high cross-correlation.
3. **Cross-model/dataset generalization**: Repeat Exp 2 for Open GPT-2 and Gemstone on both Provo and GECO; confirm strong positive correlation between (LM→RT) and (LM→n-gram) holds universally.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Why do neural language models sometimes show higher correlations with reading time than raw n-grams?** The authors note neural models slightly outperform n-grams at their peak but do not test whether this is due to better smoothing or semantic relatedness.
- **What is the specific relationship between n-gram statistics and the cognitive processes underlying different reading time metrics?** While n-grams predict reading time, it is unclear whether this reflects orthographic identification, lexical access, or integration.
- **Why do reading time metrics correlate with n-gram statistics while neural indices like the N400 correlate with more complex language model predictions?** The paper speculates a dissociation between behavioral (rapid, surface-level) and neural (deeper) processing but does not test it directly.

## Limitations
- Subword aggregation method for LM surprisal (e.g., sum vs. mean of token surprisals) is unspecified and may affect correlations.
- Corpus-specific anomalies (e.g., C4) suggest domain effects could bias n-gram estimates and generalizability.
- The correlational design cannot establish that low-order n-grams causally drive reading time; they may both be influenced by third variables like word frequency.
- Findings are restricted to English; languages with richer morphology might yield different optimal n-gram orders.

## Confidence
- **Lower-order n-grams predict reading time best**: High – robust across six corpora and four eye-tracking metrics.
- **Inverse scaling explained by divergence from bigram/trigram**: High – replicated across three model families and two reading corpora with near-perfect correlations (r ≥ 0.986).
- **Differential sensitivity of eye-movement metrics to n-gram order**: Medium – pattern holds for some models/metrics but is weaker and less consistent in GECO.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-linguistic replication**: Test whether bigram/trigram dominance holds for languages with rich morphology (e.g., Finnish, Hungarian) using available eye-tracking corpora.
2. **Expand model coverage**: Compute surprisal from latest open LMs (e.g., Llama 2, Falcon) across training steps to verify if the inverse scaling and n-gram alignment pattern persists beyond the studied families.
3. **Causal experiment**: Design sentences where bigram/trigram probability conflicts with higher-order statistics; measure early (first fixation) and late (total duration) times to directly test the proposed temporal dissociation.
