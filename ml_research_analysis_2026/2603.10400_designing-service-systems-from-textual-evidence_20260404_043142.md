# Designing Service Systems from Textual Evidence

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10400  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10400  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** PP-LUCB achieves perfect best-arm identification on real support tickets with 90% audit cost reduction.

## Executive Summary
Service system design often relies on textual evidence (e.g., support tickets) and LLM judges to evaluate configurations, but LLMs are biased and human audits are expensive. This paper introduces PP-LUCB, an algorithm for fixed‑confidence best‑arm identification that combines cheap proxy scores from an LLM with selectively audited ground‑truth outcomes. Using prediction‑powered estimation and anytime‑valid confidence sequences, PP-LUCB stops as soon as it can confidently identify the best configuration while dynamically concentrating expensive audits on the most informative regions. Theory guarantees δ‑correctness and near‑optimal cost scaling; empirical results on a real ticket classification task show perfect accuracy with 90% fewer audits, and synthetic experiments confirm 48–50% savings over uniform auditing.

## Method Summary
PP-LUCB addresses the trade‑off between evaluation cost and confidence. Each arm (a service configuration) yields a bounded proxy score \(F\) (LLM output). Optionally, a human audit provides the true outcome \(Y\). The true mean \(\theta_k = \mathbb{E}[Y|k]\) is decomposed as \(\mathbb{E}[F|k] + \mathbb{E}[Y-F|k]\). The first term is estimated from every pull; the second (the residual) is estimated via inverse propensity weighting (IPW) from the selectively audited samples. Audit decisions depend only on observed history (arm, instance features, \(F\)), satisfying a conditional ignorability assumption that ensures IPW unbiasedness.

Anytime‑valid confidence sequences (CS) combine stitched‑boundary half‑widths for the proxy and residual estimators. These CS remain valid under adaptive sampling and optional stopping. The algorithm pulls arms according to a LUCB principle: maintain the current leader (highest \(\hat{\theta}_k\)) and a challenger (highest upper confidence); stop when the leader’s lower bound exceeds the challenger’s upper bound.

Auditing follows a Neyman‑allocation style: the audit probability \(\pi_k(x,f)\) is proportional to the square root of the conditional residual second moment \(g_k(x,f)=\mathbb{E}[(Y-F)^2|k,X=x,F=f]\). Since \(g_k\) is unknown, it is estimated online by binning the \((X,F)\) space. A multiplier \(\lambda\) is tuned so the empirical average audit rate matches a target \(\rho\). Delayed audits are handled by augmenting the residual CS width with a pending‑audit term.

## Key Results
- Real‑world support ticket classification: PP‑LUCB selected the best configuration in all 40 trials with 90% reduction in audit cost.
- Synthetic experiments: Neyman allocation reduces cost by 48–50% compared to uniform auditing while maintaining δ‑correct identification.
- Anytime‑valid confidence sequences maintain correct coverage even under adaptive sampling and delayed audit returns.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Unbiased estimation via IPW under selective auditing  
- **Claim:** The true mean \(\theta_k\) can be estimated unbiasedly despite biased LLM judges and selective audits.  
- **Mechanism:** Decompose \(\theta_k = \mathbb{E}[F|k] + \mathbb{E}[Y-F|k]\). The proxy mean is estimated from all pulls; the residual mean is estimated via IPW using audited samples weighted by \(1/\pi_t\), where \(\pi_t\) is the audit probability at time \(t\).  
- **Core assumption:** Audit decisions depend only on observable history (instance \(X_t\), proxy \(F_t\)), not on the unobserved audit outcome \(Y_t\) (Assumption 3.2).  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “prediction‑powered estimator combining proxy scores with inverse‑propensity‑weighted residual corrections”; [section 4.1] IPW corrects the selection effect.  
- **Break condition:** Mis‑specified or unlogged \(\pi_t\), or audit decisions that depend on unobserved factors correlated with \(Y_t\), lead to residual bias.

### Mechanism 2: Anytime‑valid confidence sequences for adaptive stopping  
- **Claim:** Stitched boundaries provide valid simultaneous coverage for all arms and times, enabling correct early stopping.  
- **Mechanism:** For bounded outcomes, the variance process for the proxy estimator is \(N_k(t)/4\); for the IPW residual, worst‑case range \(M=2/\pi_{\min}\) gives variance \(N_k(t)M^2/4\). Applying the stitched boundary \(\psi(v;\alpha)\) to each half‑width and summing yields a CS that holds uniformly over time.  
- **Core assumption:** Bounded outcomes (\(Y,F \in [0,1]\)) and \(\pi_{\min}>0\) ensure finite variance envelopes.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.2] explicit variance processes and sub‑Gaussian increments; [corpus] Howard et al. time‑uniform methods.  
- **Break condition:** Unbounded outcomes or extremely small \(\pi_{\min}\) make the worst‑case IPW width vacuous, risking premature stopping.

### Mechanism 3: Neyman‑optimal audit allocation  
- **Claim:** Targeting audits to regions with high residual variance \(g_k(x,f)\) minimizes the residual estimator variance for a given average audit rate, thus reducing total cost.  
- **Mechanism:** Under constraint \(\mathbb{E}[\pi_k(X,F)|k]=\rho\), the optimal policy satisfying \(\pi_k(x,f) \propto \sqrt{g_k(x,f)}\) (clipped to \([\pi_{\min},1]\)) minimizes asymptotic variance. Intuitively, spend audits where the gap \(Y-F\) is most variable.  
- **Core assumption:** \(g_k(x,f)\) is bounded away from zero and infinity (Assumption 5.2).  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 5] derivation of Neyman allocation; [section 8.3] empirical cost savings 48–50%.  
- **Break condition:** If \(g_k\) is nearly constant, Neyman gains vanish; if unbounded, clipping distorts optimality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Inverse Propensity Weighting (IPW)** – Corrects selection bias when audits are not randomly sampled. Needed because audit decisions are data‑dependent.  
  *Quick check:* Why can’t we simply average \(Y\) over audited samples to estimate \(\mathbb{E}[Y|k]\)?
- **Anytime‑valid confidence sequences** – Provide uniform coverage across all stopping times, essential for adaptive algorithms that may stop early.  
  *Quick check:* What is the primary difference between a confidence sequence and a fixed‑horizon confidence interval?
- **Fixed‑confidence best‑arm identification** – Pure exploration setting where the goal is to output the best arm with high probability, not to minimize cumulative regret. Leads to \(1/\Delta^2\) sample complexity.  
  *Quick check:* Why does the gap dependence scale as \(1/\Delta^2\) rather than \(1/\Delta\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Arms → Instance sampler → Proxy generator → Audit decision module → IPW estimator → Confidence sequence calculator → Stopping rule (LUCB) → back to Arms  
- **Critical path:** Pull arm \(k_t\) → draw instance \(X_t\), get proxy \(F_t\) → compute audit probability \(\pi_t\) → draw \(A_t \sim \text{Bern}(\pi_t)\) → if \(A_t=1\), obtain audit \(Y_t\) and residual \(R_t = Y_t-F_t\) → update IPW sums and confidence sequences → check stopping condition \(L_{\hat{k}}(t) > \max_{j\neq\hat{k}} U_j(t)\).  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Audit budget vs. stopping time:* Higher target rate \(\rho\) reduces residual uncertainty faster but increases per‑pull cost.  
  - *Neyman allocation needs online \(g_k\) estimation:* Binning \(X,F\) stabilizes estimates; small \(\pi_{\min}\) reduces worst‑case IPW width but may under‑sample informative regions.  
  - *Conservative CS:* Stitched boundaries guarantee validity but are over‑conservative; variance‑adaptive bounds could tighten if additional assumptions hold.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - IPW bias: audit decisions use unobserved information → confidence sequences may not cover.  
  - Audit starvation: \(\pi_{\min}\) too small or coarse binning leaves some strata unaudited → residual estimates remain noisy.  
  - Delayed feedback: forgetting pending‑audit term in residual CS leads to premature stopping.  
- **First 3 experiments for a new engineer:**  
  1. Validate CS coverage on a known Bernoulli instance (e.g., \(\theta_k=0.5\) for all \(k\)); ensure empirical coverage matches \(1-\delta\).  
  2. Compare IPW vs. naive selective averaging under a biased audit policy; verify IPW eliminates asymptotic bias while naive does not.  
  3. Replicate synthetic cost‑savings experiment (K=4, bias b=0.1, noise σ=0.15) comparing Neyman (PP-LUCB) to uniform \(\pi\equiv0.1\); expect ~50% reduction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Multiple reviewers:** How to handle noisy audits from multiple reviewers with potentially conflicting judgments? The paper assumes a single gold‑standard audit; extending to aggregation or hierarchical models is unresolved.
- **Systematic auditor preferences:** Systematic biases (e.g., favoring verbose responses) may invalidate the residual model; understanding and correcting such structure remains open.
- **Relaxing \(\pi_{\min}>0\):** The theoretical requirement of a strictly positive minimum audit probability imposes practical constraints. Can valid inference be maintained with regions of zero or extremely low coverage?

## Limitations
- Incomplete specification: λ update rule, binning strategy, and initialization of the Neyman score are not fully detailed, requiring heuristic choices.
- Real‑LLM experiments use proprietary models/prompts; minor differences may affect reproducibility of the reported 90% cost reduction.
- Theoretical assumptions (bounded outcomes, audit decisions based only on observed history, π_min>0) may be violated in practice, risking breakdown of guarantees.
- Early estimation of residual variance \(g_k\) is noisy; Neyman efficiency may not be realized until later stages.
- Delayed feedback model assumes bounded or known delays; asynchronous or unbounded delays could impair performance.
