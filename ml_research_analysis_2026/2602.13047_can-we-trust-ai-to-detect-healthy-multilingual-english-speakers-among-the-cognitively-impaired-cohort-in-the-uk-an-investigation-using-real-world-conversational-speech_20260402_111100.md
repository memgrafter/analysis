# Can we trust AI to detect healthy multilingual English speakers among the cognitively impaired cohort in the UK? An investigation using real-world conversational speech

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13047
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13047
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** AI classifiers for cognitive decline detection show significant bias against multilingual English speakers, especially in memory, fluency, and reading tasks, with South Yorkshire accent speakers more likely to be misclassified as having dementia.

## Executive Summary
This study examines bias in artificial intelligence systems used to detect cognitive decline from speech, focusing on multilingual English speakers from ethnic minority groups in the United Kingdom. Analysis of 1,395 participants reveals that while automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems do not show significant word error rate (WER) differences between monolingual and multilingual speakers, downstream classifiers and regressors using acoustic and linguistic features exhibit systematic performance disparities against multilingual individuals. The bias is particularly severe for tasks involving memory, fluency, and reading, and for speakers with South Yorkshire accents. Training models on the public DementiaBank dataset, which primarily contains monolingual American English speech, further widens the accuracy gap. The findings suggest that current AI diagnostic tools are not yet trustworthy for multilingual populations and highlight the need for bias-mitigated models.

## Method Summary
The study leveraged a cohort of 1,395 participants, including both monolingual and multilingual English speakers, who performed various speech tasks (e.g., picture description, story recall, reading). Automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems (Whisper, Wav2Vec 2.0, NeMo) were used to transcribe audio, and word error rates (WER) were compared between language groups. Acoustic features (e.g., prosody, voice quality) and linguistic features (e.g., lexical diversity, syntax) were extracted from the transcriptions and audio. These features fed into machine learning models—support vector machines (SVM) for classification and large language models (LLM) for both classification and regression—to predict cognitive diagnoses (healthy control, mild cognitive impairment, dementia) and MMSE scores. The researchers also fine-tuned these models on the public DementiaBank dataset to evaluate the effect of biased training data. Performance metrics (accuracy, F1, RMSE) were computed overall and stratified by monolingual/multilingual status, with statistical tests (e.g., chi-squared, t-tests) to identify significant disparities.

## Key Results
- Off-the-shelf ASR systems show no significant WER bias between monolingual and multilingual speakers (p=0.1386).
- Classifiers and regressors exhibit significant bias against multilingual speakers, particularly in memory, fluency, and reading tasks, with lower accuracy (e.g., 58% vs 70% when trained on DementiaBank).
- Multilingual speakers, especially those with South Yorkshire accents, are more likely to be misclassified as having dementia and to receive higher severity predictions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Cognitive decline affects language production, leading to changes in speech patterns such as reduced fluency, lexical retrieval difficulties, and syntactic simplification. These changes manifest in both acoustic properties (e.g., increased pause frequency, altered prosody) and linguistic features (e.g., lower type-token ratio, more errors). Machine learning models can learn to recognize these patterns from labeled speech data, enabling automated screening. However, multilingualism and regional accents also influence these same features, creating confounding effects that lead to biased predictions.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR)**
   - *Why needed:* Converts raw audio into text, enabling extraction of linguistic features and serving as input to language models.
   - *Quick check:* Compute WER on a held-out set of accented speech; if >30%, consider fine-tuning or alternative ASR.
2. **Acoustic Feature Extraction**
   - *Why needed:* Captures non-lexical cues (e.g., articulation, prosody) that may be affected by cognitive impairment independent of language content.
   - *Quick check:* Extract MFCCs, jitter, shimmer from a sample; verify distributions differ between healthy and impaired groups.
3. **Linguistic Feature Extraction**
   - *Why needed:* Measures aspects of language use (e.g., lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, semantic coherence) that are known to degrade with cognitive decline.
   - *Quick check:* Compute type-token ratio, average sentence length, or use an LLM to score narrative coherence; compare across diagnostic groups.
4. **Bias Auditing**
   - *Why needed:* Ensures that model performance is equitable across demographic subgroups (e.g., monolingual vs. multilingual), preventing discriminatory outcomes.
   - *Quick check:* Stratify evaluation metrics by key demographic variables (language background, accent); perform statistical tests for significant differences.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Audio Recording → ASR (Whisper/Wav2Vec2) → Transcript → Feature Extraction (Acoustic & Linguistic) → Classification/Regression Model (SVM/LLM) → Cognitive Status Output
- **Critical path:** ASR must produce a usable transcript → features must be correctly extracted → model must load and run inference → output must be interpreted.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Off-the-shelf vs. fine-tuned ASR: off-the-shelf is convenient but may not capture domain-specific or accented speech well; fine-tuning improves accuracy but requires labeled audio and compute.
  - Handcrafted features vs. deep features: handcrafted features are interpretable and less data-hungry; deep features (from embeddings) can capture complex patterns but are less transparent and may require large data.
  - Model choice (SVM vs. LLM): SVM is simple, fast, works well with smaller feature sets; LLMs can leverage contextual information but are computationally heavy and may overfit on small datasets.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Disparate error rates across language groups (e.g., higher false positive rate for multilinguals).
  - Extremely high WER for certain accents, leading to poor transcript quality.
  - Inconsistent predictions on paraphrased inputs (lack of robustness).
  - Feature importance indicating reliance on culturally specific words (e.g., proper nouns) rather than cognitive markers.
- **3 first experiments:**
  1. **ASR Bias Assessment:** Transcribe a balanced set of monolingual and multilingual audio samples using the chosen ASR(s) and compute WER per group to detect transcription disparities.
  2. **Baseline Classification/Regression:** Train a simple model (e.g., logistic regression or SVM) on combined acoustic+linguistic features and evaluate overall accuracy and group-wise performance to establish baseline bias level.
  3. **Training Data Impact:** Fine-tune the model on DementiaBank data and re-evaluate on the diverse test set to quantify how biased training data affects multilingual performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific architectural modifications, data augmentation strategies, or training protocols can effectively mitigate bias against multilingual speakers in AI models for cognitive decline detection, while maintaining diagnostic accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly state in the conclusion and future work that they aim to "develop more generalisable, bias-mitigated models" because "current speech-based AI systems demonstrate significant bias against multilingual individuals."
- **Why unresolved:** The study identifies significant bias in classifiers and regressors but does not propose or test concrete mitigation techniques. The bias persists even when multilingual data is included in training, suggesting current standard architectures (e.g., SVM, LLM fine-tuning) are insufficient.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and rigorous evaluation of new model variants (e.g., adversarial debiasing, domain adaptation, culturally-aware feature engineering) on the CognoMemory dataset and ideally new cohorts with cognitively impaired multilinguals. Success would be measured by statistically non-significant performance gaps (e.g., in accuracy, RMSE) between monolingual and multilingual test groups.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why do memory, fluency, and reading tasks exhibit statistically significant classification bias against multilingual speakers when using linguistic features, while acoustic features show no such prompt-specific bias?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Results in Table IV and Figure 3 show significant p-values for these specific tasks only with linguistic features (SVM+Text, LLM+Text), not acoustic features (SVM+Audio). The discussion notes this pattern but does not explain the underlying cause.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates the phenomenon but cannot disentangle whether the bias stems from (a) lexical choices influenced by cultural/linguistic background, (b) ASR errors propagating into linguistic features (despite overall non-significant WER bias), or (c) inherent differences in how cognitive decline manifests linguistically across language backgrounds for those task types.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi-method analysis: (1) error analysis of ASR transcripts for these specific tasks to see if error patterns differ; (2) feature ablation/importance studies (e.g., SHAP values) on the linguistic features for misclassified multilingual samples; (3) controlled experiments where task prompts are culturally adapted.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What precise phonetic, prosodic, or lexical characteristics of the South Yorkshire accent (as spoken by South Asian multilinguals) cause it to be systematically misclassified as indicating more severe cognitive decline (e.g., dementia vs. MCI), as shown in Figure 4?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The abstract and Section V-B explicitly state: "multilinguals, particularly those with South Yorkshire accents, more likely to be misclassified as having dementia" and "more likely to be misclassified as living with a more severe cognitive decline."
- **Why unresolved:** The study identifies the accent-specific effect but does not analyze which specific speech attributes drive it. The finding is based on overall classification results, without acoustic-phonetic inspection or linguistic feature analysis specific to the South Yorkshire accent group.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Detailed acoustic analysis (e.g., formant tracking, vowel space, speaking rate, pause patterns) and lexical analysis of the South Yorkshire multilingual subgroup's speech compared to other groups, correlated with classifier decisions. This could involve training models on just accent-specific features to see if they independently predict the severe misclassification.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do culturally salient lexical differences (e.g., referencing places of origin vs. British public figures, as seen in TF-IDF analysis) directly cause classifier bias, versus being proxies for other confounding factors like accent or socioeconomic status?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The qualitative TF-IDF analysis (Section V-D, Figure 7) shows distinct top keywords for different groups (e.g., "Pakistan/Lahore" for multilingual South Asians, "prime ministers" for HC). The discussion suggests these patterns may relate to bias but does not establish causality.
- **Why unresolved:** TF-IDF highlights correlated words but cannot prove they are decision-driving features for the biased classifiers. The keywords may correlate with accent or other unmeasured variables (e.g., education, recency of migration) that are the true sources of bias. The paper used TF-IDF as a feature but did not perform bias auditing at the feature level.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** (1) Training classifiers on a normalized lexicon that masks culturally specific proper nouns (e.g., replacing all place names/person names with tokens) and measuring change in bias; (2) adversarial training to remove accent-related information from linguistic features while keeping cognitive content; (3) collecting and controlling for additional covariates (e.g., years in UK, education level) in model training.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does the use of off-the-shelf, un-fine-tuned ASR systems (which showed no overall WER bias) contribute to the observed downstream classification and regression bias, or is the bias introduced primarily at the feature extraction and classification stages?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper finds no significant ASR WER bias overall (Section V-A) but significant bias in classifiers using ASR-generated transcripts (Sections V-B, V-C). They deliberately did not fine-tune ASR, so the relationship between ASR performance disparity (which may exist at the task or phoneme level despite overall non-significance) and classifier bias remains unexplored.
- **Why unresolved:** The pipeline uses ASR outputs as inputs to feature extraction. If ASR makes systematic errors on certain multilingual speech (e.g., mis-transcribing key cognitive markers like word-finding pauses or specific vocabulary), these errors could poison downstream features. The non-significant overall WER does not guarantee equal performance on critical error types.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** (1) Compute WER disaggregated by error type (e.g., substitution of content words vs. function words) and correlate with classifier errors; (2) train classifiers on human transcripts (available for 349 participants) and compare bias magnitude to ASR-transcript-based classifiers; (3) fine-tune ASR on multilingual speech and reassess downstream bias.

## Limitations
- Geographic and linguistic generalizability limited to UK cohort, especially South Yorkshire.
- Confounding socioeconomic and educational factors not fully controlled.
- Binary multilingual grouping obscures individual language experience gradients.

## Confidence
- ASR (WER) shows no significant bias: High
- Classification/regression models exhibit significant bias: Medium-High
- Bias pronounced for South Yorkshire accents and specific tasks: Medium
- Training on DementiaBank exacerbates the performance gap: High

## Next Checks
1. **Expand Geographic and Linguistic Diversity:** Replicate the study with comparable datasets from other English-speaking countries (e.g., Canada, Australia, Singapore) featuring different dominant immigrant language groups and regional accents to test the generalizability of the bias pattern.
2. **Disentangle Accent vs. Multilingualism:** Conduct a within-monolingual analysis comparing native speakers with strong regional accents (e.g., Glaswegian, Deep Southern US) to "standard" accent speakers on the same tasks to isolate the contribution of phonetic divergence from L1 interference.
3. **Control for Socioeconomic and Educational Covariates:** Incorporate detailed participant-level data on education level, occupational history, and neighborhood deprivation index into the predictive models as control variables to quantify how much of the variance previously attributed to multilingualism is explained by these confounders.
