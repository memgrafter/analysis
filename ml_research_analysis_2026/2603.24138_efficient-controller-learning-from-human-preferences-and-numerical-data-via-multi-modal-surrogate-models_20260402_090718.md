# Efficient Controller Learning from Human Preferences and Numerical Data Via Multi-Modal Surrogate Models

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.24138
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24138
*   **Reference Count:** 36
*   **Primary Result:** A multi-modal multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization framework that combines low-fidelity simulation data with high-fidelity human preferences to significantly reduce the number of costly human-involved experiments needed to tune control policies.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the inefficiency of tuning control policies using only human pairwise preferences, which are expensive and provide limited information per query. It introduces a Bayesian optimization framework that leverages cheap, low-fidelity numerical data (e.g., from simulation) alongside the expensive, high-fidelity preference queries. The core innovation lies in multi-modal Gaussian process surrogate models—specifically, an intrinsic coregionalization (ICM) model and a hierarchical autoregressive (AR1) model—that integrate these disparate data types. Applied to personalized autonomous driving, the approach demonstrably accelerates convergence to preferred driving styles while minimizing the number of required human feedback sessions, with the AR1 model showing particularly robust and consistent performance.

## Method Summary
The method employs a phased Bayesian optimization loop. Initially, it performs a low-fidelity exploration phase using cheap numerical evaluations and an acquisition function like Integral Predictive Variance (IPV) to broadly map the objective landscape. This phase builds an initial surrogate model. The optimization then transitions to a high-fidelity phase, where it selects pairs of controller parameters to present to a human for pairwise comparison. A multi-modal Gaussian process, trained on all accumulated low-fidelity numerical data and the stream of high-fidelity preference comparisons, models the latent utility function. Acquisition functions like Expected Utility of the Best Option (EUBO) use this model to propose the next most informative comparison. Two surrogate structures are proposed: ICM models fidelities as correlated outputs in a multi-output GP, while AR1 first fits a low-fidelity GP and models the high-fidelity utility as the low-fidelity prediction plus an additive correction term learned from preferences. Posterior inference uses MCMC due to the non-Gaussian probit likelihood from preferences.

## Key Results
*   Both proposed multi-fidelity approaches (ICM and AR1) achieve lower regret faster than standard preferential Bayesian optimization that uses only human preferences.
*   The hierarchical AR1 surrogate model performs more consistently across trials and reaches the regret level of preference-only methods using approximately 20-30 fewer closed-loop experiments.
*   The framework successfully adapts a vehicle's driving style to individual human preferences while substantially reducing the need for costly human-involved experiments.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Combining low-fidelity numerical data with high-fidelity human preferences reduces the number of expensive human-involved experiments needed to optimize controller parameters.
*   **Mechanism:** The low-fidelity data trains an initial surrogate model that provides a coarse estimate of the objective landscape. This model guides cheap exploration, focusing the subsequent high-fidelity queries on promising regions of the parameter space. The information transfer ensures that when human preferences are finally queried, the Bayesian optimization algorithm is already near optima, accelerating convergence.
*   **Core assumption:** The low-fidelity numerical model is correlated with the high-fidelity preference objective; parameter configurations that score well on the approximate metric also tend to be preferred by humans.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   Abstract states the approach "integrates low-fidelity numerical data with high-fidelity human preferences... showing that combining numerical and preference data significantly reduces the need for experiments involving the human decision maker."
    *   Section II explicitly states: "To improve data efficiency, the outputs of the additional information source (3) are assumed to be correlated with the original objective function (2)."
    *   Neighboring works on multi-fidelity BO for controller tuning empirically validate the general effectiveness of leveraging low-fidelity simulations, though the joint combination with preferential feedback is novel.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** The proposed multi-modal Gaussian process structures (ICM and AR1) enable joint modeling of numerical and preferential data within a single surrogate, facilitating information transfer across modalities and fidelities.
*   **Mechanism:** Both models extend multi-fidelity GP frameworks to handle different observation types. ICM treats each fidelity as an output in a multi-output GP with a coregionalization matrix encoding correlations. The AR1 model first fits a low-fidelity GP to numerical data, then adds a correction term (delta) modeled as a preference GP. Both integrate the probit likelihood (for preferences) and Gaussian likelihood (for numerical data) via MCMC.
*   **Core assumption:** The latent functions underlying both fidelities can be represented as Gaussian processes with kernels that capture cross-fidelity correlations. The additive structure in AR1 (high = low + delta) and the linear mixing in ICM are valid approximations. The probit model accurately reflects human decision-making noise.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   Abstract notes the use of GP surrogate models "with both hierarchical, autoregressive and non-hierarchical, coregionalization-based structures, enabling efficient learning from mixed-modality data."
    *   Section IV details both models with formal equations (8) and (9) for the ICM kernel and AR1 recursion.
    *   Foundational work on multi-fidelity GPs and preferential BO exists, but the direct unified combination as done here is not present in neighboring abstracts.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** A phased Bayesian optimization strategy—initial low-fidelity exploration followed by high-fidelity exploitation—maximizes the cost-benefit of multi-fidelity data and accelerates convergence.
*   **Mechanism:** The algorithm first spends a budget of cheap low-fidelity queries, using an exploration-oriented acquisition function (e.g., IPV) to broadly sample the parameter space. Once a sufficient number of low-fidelity points are collected, it switches to the high-fidelity phase, where the multi-modal surrogate proposes pairwise comparisons via an acquisition function like EUBO. This stages human queries to concentrate in promising regions identified by the cheap data.
*   **Core assumption:** The low-fidelity model provides a reasonably accurate ranking or landscape that aligns with the high-fidelity objective, so the initial exploration does not lead the high-fidelity search into irrelevant regions. The switching point is chosen appropriately.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   Abstract notes both multi-fidelity approaches "achieve lower regret faster than standard preferential Bayesian optimization," implying the benefit of phased integration.
    *   Section V describes the protocol: a low-fidelity phase with IPV for 80 episodes, followed by 20 episodes using EI, before high-fidelity queries begin.
    *   Corpus includes papers using phased multi-fidelity BO for controller tuning, independently corroborating the strategy of separating cheap exploration from expensive exploitation.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Gaussian Process (GP) Regression**
    *   *Why needed here:* GPs are the core surrogate models that capture the unknown objective (or latent utility) from both numerical and preferential data. They provide predictive means and variances that drive acquisition functions, enabling data-efficient Bayesian optimization.
    *   **Quick check question:** What are the two main components of a Gaussian process prior (mean and covariance), and how do they combine with data to form a posterior predictive distribution?

*   **Concept: Preferential Bayesian Optimization (PBO) and the probit likelihood**
    *   *Why needed here:* Human feedback arrives as pairwise comparisons, not numeric scores. PBO models a latent utility function and uses a probit likelihood (derived from Gaussian noise on comparisons) to update the GP posterior. This allows the framework to ingest qualitative human judgments.
    *   **Quick check question:** In the standard PBO model, how is the probability that parameter ξᵢ is preferred over ξⱼ expressed in terms of the latent function values g(ξᵢ), g(ξⱼ) and noise variance σₙ²?

*   **Concept: Multi-fidelity modeling (ICM vs AR1)**
    *   *Why needed here:* To combine data of different fidelity (low-fidelity simulation, high-fidelity preferences) and modality (numeric vs. comparison), we need GP structures that relate the sources. Understanding the ICM and AR1 models is key to see how information flows across fidelities.
    *   **Quick check question:** What is the key structural difference between the ICM and AR1 models in how they mathematically relate the low-fidelity function gˡᶠ(ξ) and the high-fidelity function gʰᶠ(ξ)?

*   **Concept: Bayesian Optimization (BO) Loop**
    *   *Why needed here:* The overall framework is an instance of BO, where a surrogate model is used to sequentially decide the next most informative experiment (either a low-fidelity simulation or a high-fidelity human comparison) to minimize the number of expensive queries.
    *   **Quick check question:** What are the three main components of a Bayesian optimization algorithm, and what role does the acquisition function play?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Controlled System (e.g., autonomous vehicle) -> Tunable Controller Parameters (ξ) -> Low-fidelity Simulator (cheap ˆG(ξ)) -> Multi-Modal GP Surrogate (ICM or AR1) -> Acquisition Function (IPV for low-fi, EUBO for high-fi) -> Next Query Selection -> Closed-Loop Experiment (runs controller, generates trajectories) -> Human-in-the-loop (for high-fi, provides pairwise preferences) -> Dataset (numerical scores + comparisons) -> Update Surrogate -> Recommendation (ξ* = argmax posterior mean)

*   **Critical path (high-level sequence):**
    1.  **Initialization:** Gather small random set of low-fidelity data.
    2.  **Low-fidelity phase:** For a fixed number of episodes (e.g., 80), use IPV acquisition to select next ξ, evaluate ˆG(ξ), and update the surrogate (for AR1, this trains a separate low-fi GP; for ICM, updates the joint model).
    3.  **Transition to high-fidelity:** For AR1, fit the low-fi GP on all accumulated low-fi data; for ICM, the model is already joint. Prepare to include preference data.
    4.  **High-fidelity phase:** Use EUBO to propose a pair (ξⁱ, ξʲ). Execute two closed-loop experiments, present trajectories to human, obtain preference. Add comparison to dataset, update multi-modal GP via MCMC.
    5.  **Iterate** step 4 until high-fidelity budget exhausted.
    6.  **Recommendation:** At any point, return ξ maximizing the posterior mean of the high-fidelity latent function.

*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **ICM vs AR1:** ICM offers flexible correlation modeling but needs more data to estimate the coregionalization matrix and can be unstable with limited high-fidelity data (as seen in experiments). AR1's additive hierarchical structure is simpler and more robust but assumes the high-fidelity objective equals the low-fidelity prediction plus a zero-mean residual, which may be restrictive if the low-fi model has systematic bias.
    *   **Phase allocation:** The fixed split (e.g., 80 low-fi episodes) is simple but not adaptive. More low-fi data improves the initial surrogate but may waste budget if the fidelity correlation is weak; too little low-fi data leaves the high-fi search poorly guided.
    *   **Acquisition functions:** IPV is for aggressive low-fi exploration; EI could offer more targeted search. EUBO is specific for pairwise selection in PBO. Alternatives (e.g., qEUBO) might improve sample efficiency but increase computational cost.
    *   **Inference method:** MCMC (NUTS) provides accurate posteriors for preference data but is computationally heavy. Faster approximations (Laplace, variational) risk accuracy, especially with sparse comparisons.

*   **Failure signatures:**
    1.  **Plateauing regret** – Regret curve flattens at a high value despite many iterations. Indicates surrogate is not capturing the true preference objective; likely causes: weak/low fidelity correlation, misspecified kernel, or probit model mismatch.
    2.  **High variance across trials** – Some runs achieve low regret, others remain poor (observed for ICM). Suggests sensitivity to data or hyperparameters; may indicate unstable MCMC or overfitting from a flexible model like ICM.
    3.  **Human fatigue signals** – High-fidelity queries frequently sample clearly suboptimal regions. Means acquisition function is not focusing; likely due to poor surrogate modeling or incorrect exploration-exploitation balance.
    4.  **Computational bottleneck** – MCMC becomes prohibitively slow as comparisons grow, delaying the BO loop. Forces switch to approximations that may degrade performance.

*   **First 3 experiments (to validate the framework):**
    1.  **Baseline comparison vs standard PBO:** Run multi-fidelity (ICM/AR1) and standard preferential BO with equal high-fidelity query budgets. Plot regret vs number of high-fidelity episodes and count total closed-loop runs to demonstrate reduction in human experiments.
    2.  **ICM vs AR1 head‑to‑head:** Under identical conditions (same low-fi data, acquisition functions, random seeds), compare the two multi-modal surrogates. Measure final regret and its variance across 20+ random trials to confirm AR1's lower variance and more consistent performance.
    3.  **Low‑fidelity data sweep:** Vary the number of low-fidelity episodes before switching (e.g., 0, 20, 50, 80, 120) with fixed total cost. Analyze impact on convergence speed and final regret to identify optimal budget split and test robustness of the phased approach.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
*   **Question:** How can we evaluate the performance of multi-modal multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization in real human-in-the-loop experiments when the true objective function underlying human preferences is unknown?
*   **Basis in paper:** The paper states: "Regret plots cannot be generated in realistic settings, where the true objective guiding the preferences is unknown." It notes evaluation relied on a simulated passenger with a known objective.
*   **Why unresolved:** In real applications, ground-truth objectives are unavailable, making it difficult to measure optimization progress, compare algorithms, or guarantee alignment with user preferences.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and validation of alternative evaluation metrics or protocols (e.g., surrogate measures of user satisfaction, long-term user retention, A/B testing) that do not require known objectives, and empirical studies comparing such metrics against regret in controlled simulated settings.

### Open Question 2
*   **Question:** Under what conditions (e.g., amount of high-fidelity data, strength of correlation between fidelities) does the hierarchical AR1 surrogate model outperform the non-hierarchical ICM model when combining numerical and preferential data?
*   **Basis in paper:** The paper observed AR1 achieved more consistent and better performance than ICM in the case study. The authors conjecture this is because AR1's structure aligns better, while ICM's correlation matrix must be inferred from limited data and is sensitive to hyperparameter estimation.
*   **Why unresolved:** The paper lacks a systematic comparison across varying data regimes or a theoretical characterization of when each model is preferable. This limits practitioners' ability to choose the appropriate model for new applications.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Extensive empirical studies varying the size of the high-fidelity dataset, the fidelity of the low-fidelity model, and the true correlation structure; or theoretical analysis deriving conditions on data and correlation that favor one model over the other.

### Open Question 3
*   **Question:** How can we automatically construct or identify low-fidelity numerical information sources that are well-correlated with human preferences for use in multi-modal multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization?
*   **Basis in paper:** The framework assumes a low-fidelity numerical source (e.g., simulation) that approximates the human decision maker's evaluation. The paper uses a heteroscedastic GP trained on human driver data as the low-fidelity model but does not address how to derive such a model for new tasks or ensure its informativeness.
*   **Why unresolved:** In many human-centered control applications, a suitable low-fidelity model of human preferences may not be readily available; its design may require domain expertise and empirical tuning. The framework's success hinges on the quality of this source, but guidelines for its construction are lacking.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Methodologies for learning or adapting low-fidelity preference models from limited human data, metrics to assess the compatibility between low- and high-fidelity sources, and experimental validation across diverse domains showing that automatically constructed low-fidelity models improve BO efficiency.

### Open Question 4
*   **Question:** Can we develop scalable inference methods (e.g., variational or sparse approximations) for multi-modal multi-fidelity Gaussian processes with preference data that maintain sample efficiency while reducing computational cost for larger problems?
*   **Basis in paper:** The paper uses MCMC (NUTS) for posterior inference because the likelihood from preference data is non-Gaussian and the models involve latent functions. This is computationally intensive and may not scale to high-dimensional parameter spaces or large datasets.
*   **Why unresolved:** The paper does not discuss computational complexity or scalability beyond the 5-parameter case. For broader applicability, efficient approximations are needed.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Proposals of approximate inference algorithms (e.g., variational inference with flexible posteriors, inducing point methods) for the specific multi-modal likelihood, along with empirical demonstrations that they achieve comparable sample efficiency to MCMC at lower computational cost and can handle higher dimensions.

## Limitations
*   The method's success critically depends on a positive correlation between low-fidelity numerical metrics and high-fidelity human preferences; a poor or biased low-fidelity model can mislead the search.
*   The probit likelihood assumption for modeling human comparisons may not accurately capture the true, often inconsistent, noise characteristics of human decision-making.
*   Inference relies on computationally expensive MCMC sampling, which may hinder scalability to higher-dimensional parameter spaces or larger datasets.

## Confidence
*   **Reduced human queries through multi-fidelity integration:** Medium — Empirical support from simulations, but conditional on the fidelity correlation assumption.
*   **AR1's more consistent performance compared to ICM:** Medium — Demonstrated in the paper's experiments, but limited to a single domain and a small number of trials.
*   **Personalized adaptation of driving style:** Medium — Validated in one driving simulator scenario; broader generalization to other control tasks or real-world deployment remains untested.
