# Off-Policy Evaluation for Ranking Policies under Deterministic Logging Policies

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.21485
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21485
- Reference count: 26
- Primary result: Click-based Inverse Propensity Score (CIPS) enables unbiased off-policy evaluation under deterministic logging policies and substantially reduces bias and mean squared error compared to standard OPE methods.

## Executive Summary
Existing off-policy evaluation (OPE) methods for ranking rely on importance sampling weights derived from the logging policy’s propensity scores. When the logging policy is deterministic, the propensity for any unselected action is zero, violating the common support assumption and leading to infinite variance or biased estimates. This paper introduces Click-based Inverse Propensity Score (CIPS), a method that replaces the logging policy’s propensity with a click probability model, thereby exploiting the inherent randomness in user clicks.

The authors prove that CIPS is unbiased under two conditions: click‑wise common support and independence of potential rewards—assumptions that are easier to satisfy than those of traditional IPS. Moreover, CIPS remains unbiased when click probabilities are estimated from data, with the bias governed by the accuracy of the estimated click‑probability ratio. An extension, Click‑based Doubly Robust (CDR), adds a control variate to lower variance while preserving the same bias as CIPS. Extensive experiments on synthetic data and the KuaiRec dataset show that CIPS and CDR achieve dramatically lower bias and mean squared error than IPS, IIPS, and RIPS under deterministic logging. The work challenges the long‑standing belief that accurate OPE is impossible in this setting.

## Method Summary
The paper considers the task of estimating the expected number of clicks (or utility) of a target ranking policy π using logs from a deterministic logging policy μ. Standard IPS fails because μ(a|x) is zero for any action a not taken, breaking the common support requirement. CIPS circumvents this by treating the click as the primary stochastic event. It first learns a click model p̂(c|x,a) from the logged data. The target policy’s marginal click probability p_π(c=1|x) (e.g., p(c=1|x,π(x)) for deterministic π) and the logging’s marginal p_μ(c=1|x) = p(c=1|x,μ(x)) are obtained from the click model. The CIPS estimator then averages the observed clicks weighted by the ratio of these marginal probabilities:

\[
\hat{V}_{\text{CIPS}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i \cdot \frac{p_π(c_i=1|x_i)}{p_μ(c_i=1|x_i)}.
\]

When p̂ is used in place of the true probabilities, the estimator’s bias depends on the accuracy of the ratio p̂_π/p̂_μ.

The Click‑based Doubly Robust (CDR) estimator further incorporates a direct estimate \(\hat{p}_π(c=1|x)\) as a control variate:

\[
\hat{V}_{\text{CDR}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ \hat{p}_π(c=1|x_i) + (c_i - \hat{p}_μ(c=1|x_i)) \cdot \frac{\hat{p}_π(c_i=1|x_i)}{\hat{p}_μ(c_i=1|x_i)} \right].
\]

CDR achieves the same bias as CIPS but often with lower variance by leveraging the outcome model.

## Key Results
- **Theoretical guarantees**: CIPS is unbiased under click‑wise common support and independence of potential rewards—conditions that are easier to satisfy than the common support needed for IPS.
- **Robustness to estimation**: Even with an estimated click model, CIPS remains unbiased; the bias is determined by the accuracy of the estimated click‑probability ratio.
- **Empirical superiority**: On synthetic data and the KuaiRec dataset, CIPS and its doubly robust variant CDR significantly lower bias and mean squared error compared to IPS, IIPS, and RIPS when the logging policy is deterministic.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CIPS works because it shifts the source of stochasticity from action selection to the user click. In a deterministic logging regime, the action distribution carries no randomness, making standard IPS impossible. However, the click given the action is random and can be modeled. By expressing the target policy’s expected click as an expectation over the click marginal and then applying importance sampling on that marginal, CIPS obtains a finite importance weight that never requires μ(a|x) for unseen actions. The click model provides estimates of p_μ(c=1|x)=p(c=1|x,μ(x)) for the logged action and p_π(c=1|x) for the target’s action. The ratio of these two probabilities, multiplied by the observed click, yields an unbiased estimate of the target’s marginal click probability. The requirements are click‑wise common support (i.e., the click model assigns positive probability to the observed click under both policies) and independence across impressions. Thus, the inherent randomness in clicks, independent of the deterministic logging, enables accurate OPE.

## Foundational Learning
- **Off‑Policy Evaluation (OPE)**: aims to estimate the performance of a new policy using only historical data from a different logging policy. *Why needed*: Online evaluation is often costly or risky; OPE provides a safe, data‑driven alternative. *Quick check*: Can you derive the IPS estimator and state its key assumption?
- **Importance Sampling (IPS)**: a technique to reweight samples from one distribution to estimate expectations under another. *Why needed*: Forms the basis of many OPE methods, including the traditional approach for ranking. *Quick check*: What is the formula for the IPS estimator, and why does it require common support?
- **Deterministic Logging Policy**: a policy that always selects the same action for a given context (e.g., an editorial ranking). *Why needed*: Common in production systems; breaks IPS because common support fails. *Quick check*: Explain why IPS yields infinite or biased estimates under a deterministic logger.
- **Click Propensity / Click Model**: a model that predicts the probability of a click given an item and its context. *Why needed*: Captures the inherent stochasticity in user behavior; CIPS uses it to replace policy propensities. *Quick check*: How would you estimate a click model from logs, and what features might be important?
- **Doubly Robust (DR) Estimation**: combines an outcome model (e.g., click model) with importance weighting to reduce variance while maintaining bias under partial correctness. *Why needed*: Provides practical variance reduction for OPE; CDR extends this idea to the click‑based setting. *Quick check*: Describe the general DR formula and its bias/variance properties.

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component Map
Logging Policy (μ) → Action a_i for context x_i → User click c_i → Logs (x_i, a_i, c_i) → Click Model p̂(c|x,a) → Compute p̂_μ(x_i)=p̂(c=1|x_i,a_i) and p̂_π(x_i)=∑_a π(a|x_i) p̂(c=1|x_i,a) (or for deterministic π: p̂(c=1|x_i,π(x_i))) → Weight w_i = p̂_π(x_i)/p̂_μ(x_i) → CIPS estimate: V̂ = (1/N)∑ c_i w_i.  
CDR variant adds direct estimate \(\hat{p}_π(x_i)\) and combines with residual term.

#### Critical Path
1. Generate deterministic rankings via μ and record clicks.
2. Train click model p̂ on the collected logs.
3. For each logged impression, compute p̂_μ and p̂_π using the click model and π.
4. Compute CIPS weights and average; for CDR, incorporate the direct p̂_π estimate.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- **Click model accuracy**: CIPS performance depends heavily on the quality of p̂. More flexible models (e.g., deep nets) generalize better but need more data and risk overfitting. Simpler models may bias the estimator but avoid high variance.
- **Variance vs. bias**: CDR reduces variance by using a direct outcome estimate, but if p̂ is misspecified it may introduce a small bias. Weight truncation or regularization can mitigate extreme weights when p̂_μ is tiny.
- **Computational cost**: Training and evaluating a click model adds overhead relative to pure IPS, but this is usually acceptable given the gain in unbiasedness.

#### Failure Signatures
- **Exploding variance**: When p̂_μ(x) is very small for many impressions, weights become huge, leading to high variance. Diagnose via a long‑tailed weight distribution.
- **Poor generalization**: If the click model cannot predict for actions not seen in the logs (e.g., new items), p̂_π becomes inaccurate, causing bias. Check performance on held‑out items.
- **Clustered clicks**: Correlated clicks (e.g., from the same user session) violate the independence assumption, inflating variance and potentially bias. Detect by clustering or time‑series analysis.

#### First Experiments
1. **Toy verification**: Simulate a known click model and deterministic logger; compute true target value analytically. Confirm that CIPS recovers it accurately with the true model and degrades gracefully when the model is perturbed.
2. **IPS failure comparison**: Run IPS (expected to fail) and CIPS on the same deterministic logging data. Observe IPS’s instability and CIPS’s stability, highlighting the advantage of the click‑based approach.
3. **CDR variance check**: Apply CIPS and CDR to a medium‑sized log (e.g., KuaiRec) and compare empirical variance or MSE. Verify that CDR reduces variance without increasing bias.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees of CIPS depend on click‑wise common support and independence of potential rewards; these may be violated if deterministic logging never exposes certain items or if clicks exhibit position bias or serial correlation.
- Empirical validation is limited to one real‑world dataset (KuaiRec) and synthetic simulations; generalizability to other domains (e.g., news, e‑commerce) and diverse user populations remains open.
- The impact of click‑model estimation error in high‑dimensional feature spaces or with limited training data is not thoroughly explored.

## Confidence
- Unbiasedness under stated assumptions: Medium‑High
- Superiority over IPS/IIPS/RIPS: Medium
- CDR’s variance reduction with same bias: Medium
- Feasibility of accurate OPE under deterministic logging: Low‑Medium
