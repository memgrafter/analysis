# Beyond a Single Signal: SPECTREG2, A Unified MultiExpert Anomaly Detector for Unknown Unknowns

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv**: 2603.21160  
- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21160  
- **References**: 33  
- **Result**: SPECTRE-G2 achieves state-of-the-art unknown unknown detection, ranking highest on mean AUROC for 11 of 12 anomaly types and outperforming 12 baselines on multiple metrics.

## Executive Summary
SPECTRE-G2 introduces a unified multi-expert anomaly detector targeting "unknown unknowns"—structural anomalies that violate learned model assumptions. It combines eight complementary signals derived from a dual-backbone neural network ensemble: a spectral-normalised Gaussianization encoder and a geometry-preserving plain MLP. These signals capture density, geometry, uncertainty, discriminative, and causal aspects of the data. After normalisation and calibration using synthetic out-of-distribution points, an adaptive top‑k fusion selects the most informative signals per test set. Extensive evaluation on synthetic, Adult, CIFAR‑10, and Gridworld datasets shows SPECTRE-G2 achieving the highest mean AUROC on 11 of 12 anomaly types and outperforming 12 baselines on AUPR, FPR95, and confident error rate. Ablation studies confirm that while any single signal can be removed with minimal impact, the fusion of multiple signals is essential for robust performance.

## Method Summary
The method trains two neural network backbones in parallel. The first backbone, GaussEnc, consists of an ensemble of five models with spectral normalisation and a Gaussianization loss that forces penultimate features toward a standard Gaussian. This stabilises density estimation and yields the GaussScore signal. The second backbone, PlainNet, is a single MLP without spectral norm, preserving input geometry for distance-based signals like FtMahaP and InMaha. Both backbones produce logits for uncertainty signals (Energy, Entropy, Mutual Information) and a separately trained USD classifier provides discriminative confidence. For tabular data with ≤30 features, a causal signal is added by fitting per-variable MLP regressors and averaging residual magnitudes.

Prior to scoring, a pseudo-out-of-distribution (OOD) set is generated by linearly mixing training samples (α∈[1.2,3.0]) and adding Gaussian noise. Each raw signal is normalised using the 1st and 99th percentiles on the validation set; its sign is flipped if the mean score on pseudo-OOD is lower than on validation (with overrides for known-direction signals like ODIN). The normalised signals are then ranked by their AUROC on the validation set versus the pseudo-OOD set. If the top signal’s AUROC ≥ 0.72, only that signal is used (k=1); otherwise the top two signals are averaged (k=2). The final anomaly score is this average, and it is evaluated on test anomalies.

## Key Results
- Highest mean AUROC on 11 of 12 anomaly types across four datasets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Multi-signal fusion
The model combines eight signals—density (GaussScore, InMaha), geometry (FtMahaP), epistemic uncertainty (MI, Entropy), discriminative (ODIN, USD), and causal (tabular only)—from dual backbones. Each signal captures different violation types. The fusion averages the top‑k signals (k chosen adaptively) to provide robustness when individual signals fail. Evidence: abstract reports highest mean AUROC on 11 of 12 types; ablation shows removing any single signal causes only minor performance drop, confirming fusion is essential. Break condition: if all signals fail similarly on a new anomaly type (e.g., Adult results near random).

### Mechanism 2: Dual-backbone architecture
GaussEnc uses spectral normalisation and Gaussianization loss to stabilise features for density estimation, while PlainNet lacks spectral norm to preserve input geometry for distance metrics. This split allows both stable density estimates and accurate Mahalanobis distances. Evidence: Section 5.2 explains the design; Section 8.2 shows using both backbones yields richer features than either alone. Break condition: if optimal features require both strong density regularisation and raw geometry, a trade‑off might exist if spectral norm distortion is minimal on some datasets.

### Mechanism 3: Adaptive top‑k fusion
Signals are normalised using validation percentiles and direction-corrected via pseudo-OOD. Validation AUROC (vs. pseudo-OOD) ranks signals; if top AUROC ≥ 0.72, use only it (k=1), else top two (k=2). This avoids learned weights and adapts to dataset difficulty. Evidence: Section 5.6 shows it outperforms equal weighting; Section 7.2.2 confirms fusion is essential. Break condition: if pseudo-OOD does not reflect test anomalies (e.g., Adult's subtle covariate shifts), ranking may misselect.

## Foundational Learning

- **Epistemic vs. Aleatoric Uncertainty**  
  Why needed: SPECTRE‑G2 targets epistemic uncertainty (model ignorance) to detect unknown unknowns; aleatoric uncertainty is irreducible noise.  
  Quick check: Does the model’s uncertainty decrease with more training data? (If yes, it’s epistemic.)

- **Spectral Normalisation**  
  Why needed: Constrains layer Lipschitz constants in GaussEnc to stabilise training and encourage Gaussian features via the Gaussianization loss.  
  Quick check: Does removing spectral norm increase feature variance across ensemble members?

- **Mahalanobis Distance in Feature Space**  
  Why needed: FtMahaP and InMaha signals measure distance to class centroids; requires class‑conditional means and tied covariance estimated from training.  
  Quick check: Is the pooled covariance matrix well‑conditioned? If not, Mahalanobis becomes unstable.

- **Pseudo‑OOD Calibration**  
  Why needed: Determines signal direction (higher score = in‑distribution) and computes validation AUROC for fusion, using synthetic outliers (linear mixtures + Gaussians).  
  Quick check: Do pseudo‑OOD points have systematically lower scores than validation data across all signals after correction?

- **Mutual Information Decomposition**  
  Why needed: MI = H[E[p]] - E[H[p]] estimates epistemic uncertainty from ensemble disagreement.  
  Quick check: Does MI increase on test anomalies compared to in‑distribution samples?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
D_train, D_val → Train GaussEnc (5 models) & PlainNet (1 model) → Extract signals (GaussScore, FtMahaP, InMaha, Energy, Entropy, MI, ODIN, USD, [Causal if tabular]) → Calibrate using D_ood (linear mixtures + Gaussians) → Compute validation AUROC per signal → Select top‑k (k=1 if max AUROC≥0.72 else k=2) → Average selected signals → Final anomaly score S(x)

**Critical path**  
1. Train GaussEnc ensemble (with spectral norm and Gaussianization loss) and PlainNet (without spectral norm) on training data.  
2. Generate pseudo-OOD set D_ood by mixing training samples (α ∈ [1.2,3.0]) and adding Gaussian noise (variance = 4× training covariance).  
3. For each sample in D_val and D_ood, compute all eight signals (and causal signal if applicable).  
4. For each signal, compute 1st and 99th percentiles on D_val; normalize to [0,3] with clipping. Flip sign if mean(D_ood) < mean(D_val) (with overrides for ODIN, Energy, Entropy, MI, USD).  
5. Compute AUROC of each normalised signal on (D_val label=0, D_ood label=1). Determine k: if best AUROC ≥ 0.72 then k=1 else k=2.  
6. For test samples, compute normalised signals, select top‑k by validation AUROC, average to get S(x).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Compute vs. robustness: 5 GaussEnc models increase memory but stabilise MI/ensemble signals.  
- Adaptive vs. fixed fusion: Top‑k avoids learning weights but relies on validation ranking; could fail if best signal varies across test batches.  
- Pseudo-OOD simplicity: Linear mixtures are cheap but may not cover all anomaly types (e.g., Adult’s confounder).

**Failure signatures**  
- All signals near 0.5 AUROC on D_val vs. D_ood → poor signal design or calibration.  
- Large drop from validation to test AUROC for top signals → pseudo-OOD mismatch.  
- Adult dataset results ~0.5 AUROC → model may need stronger feature engineering or larger training set.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Replicate ablation: Remove one signal at a time on Synthetic dataset; verify ≤0.002 AUROC drop (Section 7.2.1).  
2. Signal validation: Compute AUROC of each normalised signal on D_val vs. D_ood; ensure top signal >0.72 (or ~0.7) for k=1 case.  
3. Pseudo-OOD sanity check: Verify that after direction correction, mean signal on D_val > mean on D_ood for in-distribution-favoring signals (e.g., GaussScore).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Extending the causal signal to high-dimensional data**  
   The paper explicitly notes that the causal signal is limited to ≤30 features. It remains unresolved because per-variable MLP regressors do not scale to high-dimensional data like images. Evidence that would resolve this: a scalable approximation (e.g., representation‑based) tested on CIFAR‑10 anomalies.

2. **Diversifying pseudo‑OOD for improved robustness**  
   The paper states that USD is trained on simple Gaussian noise and suggests that more diverse pseudo‑OOD could improve robustness. It is unresolved because the current pseudo-OOD may not cover the full spectrum of unknown unknowns, leading to suboptimal calibration. Resolution: evaluate a richer pseudo‑OOD pool (mix of datasets, GAN samples, synthetic anomalies) on benchmark; improved AUROC/FPR95.

3. **Reducing computational cost**  
   The paper acknowledges that the ensemble and multi-backbone training increase computational overhead. This is unresolved due to high cost hindering deployment. Resolution: distill the ensemble into a single model or use efficient architectures; show comparable performance with lower FLOPs/latency.

4. **Optimising the fixed top‑k threshold**  
   The threshold 0.72 was chosen empirically. It is unresolved because the heuristic may not generalise across datasets. Resolution: learn a gating network or cross‑validate k per dataset; demonstrate consistent AUROC gains over the static threshold.

## Limitations
- Incomplete specifications for causal MLP regressors and USD classifier (e.g., activations, optimizer) hinder exact reproduction.
- Pseudo‑OOD generation details ambiguous; may not capture all anomaly types (e.g., Adult confounder).
- Adaptive threshold (0.72) is heuristic and may not generalize to datasets with different characteristics.
