# Crowdsourcing Piedmontese to Test LLMs on Non-Standard Orthography

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14675
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14675
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: LLMs exhibit tokenization inefficiency for Piedmontese but achieve topic classification F1 near high-resource languages; direct translation into Piedmontese is low (chrF++ ~27-35), while translation from Piedmontese is better (chrF++ ~42-66), with pivoting through Italian providing modest gains (+1-2 chrF++).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the scarcity of NLP resources for Piedmontese, an endangered language with no standard orthography, by crowdsourcing 145 natural Italian-Piedmontese parallel translations without enforcing spelling rules. The dataset includes manual word alignments and topic labels. Evaluating open- and closed-weight LLMs zero-shot reveals that Piedmontese suffers a tokenization penalty (parity >1 vs. Italian/English) yet models classify topics with F1 scores approaching those for Italian, French, and English. Machine translation shows asymmetry: generation into Piedmontese is poor, while translation from Piedmontese is better; pivoting through Italian improves some target scores by up to 2 chrF++ points. The dataset is publicly released.

## Method Summary
The authors deployed an Italian-language online questionnaire to recruit Piedmontese speakers via social media and word-of-mouth. Participants translated random sentences from the Flores+ dev/devtest splits, writing naturally without orthographic constraints; a diacritic substitution scheme was provided for physical keyboards. Translations underwent peer review to filter errors and offensive content. The resulting parallel sentences were optionally manually word-aligned using whitespace/apostrophe tokenization. For evaluation, the team trained SentencePiece tokenizers on Glot500 subsets (100k samples per language) to compute tokenization parity, and zero-shot prompted LLMs (Llama 3.3 70B, Gemma 3 27B, Qwen 3 30B, EuroLLM 9B, Tower Plus 9B, Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-4o Mini) on three tasks: topic classification (75 labeled Piedmontese sentences from SIB-200), machine translation (direct and Italian-pivot on all 145 pairs), and word alignment comparison (eflomal vs. SimAlign against manual spans). Metrics included token parity, F1, chrF++, BLEU, TER, and COMET. Greedy decoding with max 100 tokens was used; COMET was not fine-tuned on Piedmontese.

## Key Results
- Tokenizer parity >1 for Piedmontese relative to Italian and English, indicating higher token count per sentence.
- Topic classification: Llama achieved 0.778 F1 for Piedmontese vs. 0.849 for Italian; other models showed a similar gap but still competitive.
- Machine translation: Direct →Pms scores low (chrF++ 27-35); Pms→ scores higher (42-66). Pivoting through Italian yielded +1-2 chrF++ improvement on some target languages.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The crowdsourcing approach captures authentic, non-standard orthographic variation that would be lost under standardisation, exposing tokenizer inefficiencies rooted in subword models' bias toward high-resource, regularised text. Zero-shot evaluations reveal that LLMs can understand Piedmontese sufficiently for classification (likely by mapping to Italian-like patterns) but struggle to generate it, reflecting limited exposure and training data disparities. The Italian pivot partially ameliorates generation by leveraging a high-resource bridge, suggesting that transfer through related languages can mitigate data scarcity for endangered languages.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Tokenizer Parity**  
   *Why needed*: Quantifies the tokenization cost incurred by low-resource or non-standard orthographies compared to reference languages.  
   *Quick check*: Train SentencePiece on equal-sized corpora for two languages and compute the average token ratio on a shared test set.

2. **Non-Standard Orthography**  
   *Why needed*: Represents real-world usage for languages without codified spelling; ignoring it yields unrealistic NLP benchmarks.  
   *Quick check*: Collect informal social media text and measure orthographic entropy (e.g., variation in diacritic usage).

3. **Crowdsourcing for Low-Resource Languages**  
   *Why needed*: Cost-effective method to gather data from speaker communities when professional annotation is infeasible.  
   *Quick check*: Design a simple translation questionnaire and recruit participants via online forums; evaluate response rate and data quality.

4. **Zero-Shot Evaluation**  
   *Why needed*: Tests the inherent multilingual capacity of large pretrained models without task-specific fine-tuning.  
   *Quick check*: Use a pretrained LLM to perform sentiment analysis with a prompt; compare to a fine-tuned baseline.

5. **chrF++ Metric**  
   *Why needed*: A character-n-gram based MT metric that is more sensitive to morphological accuracy and robust to short sentences than BLEU.  
   *Quick check*: Compute chrF++ between a reference and a translation with correct stems but wrong affixes; observe score impact.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Annotators (Piedmontese speakers) -> Online Questionnaire (Italian instructions) -> Raw Translations -> Peer Review -> Filtered Parallel Corpus (Italian-Piedmontese) -> Branches:
  Branch1: Tokenizer Parity (train SentencePiece on Glot500 subsets, compute parity)
  Branch2: Topic Classification (zero-shot prompts on LLMs, map SIB-200 labels, compute F1)
  Branch3: Machine Translation (zero-shot direct and Italian-pivot generation, compute chrF++/BLEU/TER/COMET)
  Branch4: Word Alignment (eflomal/SimAlign vs. manual spans)
Glot500 subsets feed tokenizer training; SIB-200 labels provide topic classes.

Critical path:
1. Recruit annotators and collect translations via questionnaire.
2. Preprocess data: filter invalid/offensive entries, build parallel corpus, manually word-align a subset.
3. Train tokenizers on Glot500 language samples.
4. Run zero-shot classification on the 75 labeled Piedmontese sentences.
5. Run MT experiments (direct and pivot) on all 145 pairs.
6. Compute metrics and conduct statistical analysis (bootstrapping for classification).

Design tradeoffs:
- Crowdsourcing vs. professional translation: Gains natural orthography but introduces noise and demographic bias.
- Italian as source: Enables data collection but risks translationese, potentially inflating model performance on syntactic tasks.
- Zero-shot vs. fine-tuning: Avoids overfitting to small dataset but may understate achievable performance; results reflect out-of-the-box multilingual ability.
- chrF++ as primary MT metric: Captures character-level accuracy but likely penalises non-standard spelling; COMET provides a learned alternative but is not fine-tuned for Piedmontese.
- Small dataset (n=145): Enables rapid iteration but limits statistical power and generalizability; results should be interpreted with caution.

Failure signatures:
- Token parity far above 1.2 suggests severe tokenizer incompatibility with Piedmontese morphology.
- Topic classification F1 below 0.6 or bootstrap intervals overlapping random chance (≈0.14 for 7 classes) indicates models fail to understand Piedmontese.
- Direct MT chrF++ below 20 and pivot yields no improvement may indicate data quality issues or model inability to generate non-standard orthography.
- High variance across models or large confidence intervals for small n suggests unreliable estimates.
- Word alignment F1 below 0.5 indicates alignment tools cannot handle orthographic variation; manual alignment quality may also be questionable.

First three experiments:
1. **Tokenizer Parity Experiment**: Train SentencePiece (BPE/Unigram, vocab=32k) on Glot500 Piedmontese, Italian, and English (100k sentences each). Compute average token ratio on Flores+ test sentences.
2. **Topic Classification Experiment**: Using the 75 labeled Piedmontese sentences, prompt each LLM (with provided templates) to predict one of 7 SIB-200 topics. Compute precision, recall, F1, accuracy with 1000 bootstrap resamples.
3. **Machine Translation Experiment**: For all 145 Italian-Piedmontese pairs, generate direct →Pms and Pms→ translations, and additionally pivot via Italian (Italian→target). Use greedy decoding, max 100 tokens. Compute chrF++, BLEU, TER, and COMET (without fine-tuning). Average scores per model and direction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Annotator demographic bias**: Recruitment relies on social media, skewing younger and potentially missing older speakers' orthographic habits. This affects the diversity and representativeness of the dataset, limiting generalizability. A follow-up targeting underrepresented groups and comparing orthographic variation would help.
2. **Input device influence**: The ease of typing certain characters on smartphones vs. physical keyboards may drive orthographic patterns (e.g., diacritic omission). Without controlling for device, it is unclear whether observed variation reflects natural usage or technical constraints. A within-subject experiment across devices is needed.
3. **Regional variants**: The dataset does not record which regional variant of Piedmontese each annotator uses. Given known regional differences, model performance may vary across variants. Re-annotation with variant labels or new region-specific collections could assess cross-variant generalisation.
4. **Translationese**: Because source texts are Italian, translations may retain Italian syntactic structures, causing models to rely on Italian patterns rather than authentic Piedmontese. Comparing performance on native Piedmontese text (e.g., social media) would clarify this.
5. **Questionnaire terminology**: The instructions used "orthography" and "grammar" interchangeably, potentially confusing annotators about whether to prioritise spelling or grammatical correctness. A redesigned questionnaire with distinct definitions and a comparative study could measure impact on data quality.

## Limitations
- Recruitment & orthographic distribution: Social media-based recruitment introduces demographic bias and unknown orthographic variation coverage, undermining claims about "natural" non-standard writing.
- Closed model inference parameters: Exact generation hyperparameters for proprietary LLMs (besides greedy decoding and max tokens) are unspecified, preventing exact replication.
- Metric sensitivity: chrF++ likely penalizes non-standard orthographic forms; COMET was not fine-tuned on Piedmontese, so both metrics may conflate spelling variation with semantic correctness.

## Next Checks
1. **Orthography-Normalized MT Evaluation**: Compute chrF++ and BLEU after ascii-folding all references and model outputs (e.g., `à→a`, `ë→e`) to isolate semantic translation quality from diacritic/orthographic noise; compare direct vs. pivot scores under normalization.
2. **Closed-Model Parameter Audit**: Retrieve default generation parameters (temperature, top_p, etc.) for `gemini-2.5-flash` and `gpt-4o-mini` from OpenRouter/API docs during the study period. If defaults changed or are unknown, run a parameter sweep (temperature∈{0,0.3,0.7}) on a 10% subset to bound score variability.
3. **Dataset Recruitability Stress Test**: Deploy the questionnaire on two distinct platforms (e.g., targeted Facebook groups vs. Reddit r/piedmontese). Measure orthographic entropy (character-level n-gram distribution) and obtain human quality ratings on 20 samples to quantify platform-specific bias.
