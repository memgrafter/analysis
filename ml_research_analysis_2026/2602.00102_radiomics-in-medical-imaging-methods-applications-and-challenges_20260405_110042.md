# Radiomics in Medical Imaging: Methods, Applications, and Challenges

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.00102
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00102
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** This survey analyzes radiomics pipeline fragility due to interdependent design choices affecting feature stability, model reliability, and translational validity.

## Executive Summary
This review comprehensively examines the radiomics pipeline, from image acquisition through validation, highlighting how sequential design choices create fragility that undermines reproducibility and clinical translation. It synthesizes evidence that retrospective model performance frequently collapses under external validation, driven by feature instability from acquisition/preprocessing variability, high-dimensionality with small cohorts, validation leakage, and domain shift failures. The authors argue that reported improvements often reflect methodological artifacts rather than true predictive power, urging the adoption of standardized pipelines, leakage-free validation, and stability-aware feature selection. Future directions point toward hybrid radiomics–AI frameworks, multimodal fusion, and federated learning supported by standardized benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The paper does not provide explicit reproduction notes; however, it underscores the need for reproducible, leakage‑free validation protocols such as nested cross‑validation and independent test sets. It advocates for pre‑registration of analysis plans and the use of standardized radiomics feature definitions (e.g., IBSI) to mitigate variability.

## Key Results
- Retrospective radiomics models often exhibit significant performance degradation during external validation.
- Feature values are highly sensitive to acquisition parameters and preprocessing steps, leading to instability.
- High dimensionality combined with small sample sizes risks overfitting; validation bias from data leakage inflates reported accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Radiomics pipeline fragility arises from the interdependence of its components. Each step (e.g., reconstruction kernels, interpolation, segmentation) introduces variations that propagate to feature values, compromising stability. Models built on unstable features become overly tuned to the training cohort’s specific conditions, making them vulnerable to domain shift (e.g., different scanners or protocols). This mechanistic chain explains why apparent success in retrospective studies rarely translates to robust clinical tools.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Data Acquisition -> Preprocessing -> Segmentation -> Feature Extraction -> Feature Selection -> Modeling -> Validation  

**Critical Path:** Acquisition choices (scanner type, protocol) set the upper bound of feature stability; preprocessing and segmentation critically mediate the translation from image to mask; feature extraction yields high‑dimensional data; selection and modeling must avoid leakage; validation on independent data reveals true generalizability.  

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Segmentation*: Manual vs. automated – higher reproducibility vs. lower labor.  
- *Feature Set*: Comprehensiveness vs. dimensionality – richer information vs. risk of overfitting.  
- *Validation*: Strict independence vs. sample size – unbiased performance vs. statistical power.  

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Low intra‑class correlation (ICC) for features across scanners or preprocessing pipelines.  
- Significant drop in AUC/accuracy when moving from internal to external cohorts.  
- Discrepancy between nested and non‑nested cross‑validation results indicating leakage.  

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. Stability Benchmark: Apply a standard radiomics pipeline (e.g., PyRadiomics defaults) to a multi‑center dataset and compute ICCs for all feature types under controlled preprocessing variations.  
2. Leakage Audit: Replicate a published radiomics study using a strict pre‑registered protocol with nested cross‑validation and a held‑out external set; compare performance to original report.  
3. Hybrid Stress Test: Build a radiomics‑deep learning hybrid model, train on data from one manufacturer, and test on another; evaluate whether hybrid improves stability over pure radiomics or pure deep learning baselines.

## Limitations
- The review’s evidence base is heavily oncology‑focused; findings may not generalize to non‑oncological imaging domains.  
- It lacks quantitative aggregation of fragility (e.g., expected performance drop per preprocessing variation).  
- Assessment of hybrid radiomics‑AI frameworks is speculative due to limited real‑world utility data.

## Confidence
- Pipeline fragility linkage: **High** – strong consensus that acquisition/preprocessing affect stability.  
- External validation degradation: **Medium** – widely observed but systematic quantification is sparse.  
- Persistent challenges (leakage, high‑dimensionality): **Medium** – well‑documented, yet solutions are inconsistently applied.

## Next Checks
1. **Multi‑center leakage audit:** Re‑run key cited studies with a strict, pre‑registered “no leakage” protocol (e.g., nested CV, separate test sets) on an external multi‑scanner dataset to quantify true vs. inflated performance.  
2. **Feature stability benchmark:** Deploy a standardized radiomics pipeline (e.g., PyRadiomics default settings) across multiple publicly available multi‑center datasets (e.g., IBSI phantoms, TCIA cohorts) to measure ICC for all feature types under controlled preprocessing variations.  
3. **Hybrid framework stress‑test:** For a proposed hybrid model, conduct domain shift validation by training on one manufacturer’s data and testing on another’s, comparing prediction stability against pure deep learning and pure radiomics baselines.
