# Investigation for Relative Voice Impression Estimation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14172  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14172  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Self-supervised speech (SSL) representations (HuBERT) achieve the highest performance on Relative Voice Impression Estimation (RIE), with Pearson correlations up to 0.88 and Concordance Correlation Coefficients up to 0.82, while zero-shot multimodal large language models (MLLMs) prove unreliable for this fine-grained pairwise task.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces Relative Voice Impression Estimation (RIE) to predict perceptual differences in voice impressions between two utterances from the same speaker. The target is a 9-dimensional vector derived from subjective evaluations along antonymic axes (e.g., Dark–Bright). Using a controlled dataset of a single speaker reading the same text in 52 styles, the authors compare three approaches: classical acoustic features (openSMILE), self-supervised speech (SSL) representations (HuBERT), and zero-shot multimodal large language models (MLLMs). SSL-based models consistently outperform the others, particularly on dynamic impressions where classical features fail. In contrast, MLLMs (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro) show unreliable, often poor performance, highlighting a gap in current models for fine-grained comparative paralinguistic reasoning.

## Method Summary
The task is to predict a 9-dimensional difference vector representing relative voice impressions between two audio utterances from the same speaker and text. The dataset comprises 814 pairs from a single female Japanese voice actor across 52 expressive styles (internal, not public). Three methods are evaluated:

1. **Classical acoustic features:** OpenSMILE (eGeMAPSv02) extracts low-level descriptors (e.g., F0, MFCCs). A feature difference vector ϕΔ = ϕ(x_b) − ϕ(x_a) is computed and fed to regressors (Ridge, SVR, etc.) or a neural network.
2. **SSL representations:** A Japanese HuBERT model processes each utterance to yield frame-level embeddings (768-D). These are pooled via weighted sum, bidirectional LSTM, and attention to produce a 128-D utterance embedding. The two embeddings are concatenated and passed through a 3-layer MLP trained with MSE loss and AdamW (lr=0.002, batch=8) under 10-fold cross-validation.
3. **MLLMs (zero-shot):** GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro are prompted in Japanese with both audio clips and instructions to output the 9-dimensional difference. No fine-tuning is applied; few-shot examples are used but prompt details are unspecified.

Performance is measured per dimension using Pearson correlation (r) and concordance correlation coefficient (CCC).

## Key Results
- SSL models achieve the highest correlations, with Pearson r up to 0.88 (Dark–Bright) and CCC up to 0.82, and consistently strong performance (>0.69 Pearson) across all 9 impression dimensions, especially on dynamic ones (e.g., Calm–Restless, Cold–Warm) where other methods struggle.
- Classical acoustic features perform moderately on static descriptors (e.g., High–Low, Youthful–Elderly) but fail on complex dynamic impressions, with CCC below 0.35 for Calm–Restless and Cold–Warm, indicating inability to capture temporal prosodic cues.
- Zero-shot MLLMs show unreliable, often near-zero or negative correlations (e.g., GPT-5 CCC -0.03 on Powerful–Weak), performing at or below classical methods and far worse than SSL, suggesting they are not suited for fine-grained pairwise voice comparison without task-specific adaptation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Classical Acoustic Features
Classical features (openSMILE/eGeMAPSv02) model relative voice impression changes when differences align with simple, static vocal parameters like pitch height. The approach computes a feature difference vector from handcrafted statistics (e.g., mean F0, early MFCCs) and maps it to the 9-dimensional impression difference via regression. The core assumption is that human perceptual shifts along certain antonymic axes correlate reliably with a low-dimensional subset of aggregate vocal parameters, with a learnable relationship. Evidence: Feature–label correlations are moderate (|r| up to ~0.56) for impressions like Dark–Bright and Youthful–Elderly, and regression models achieve Pearson ~0.54–0.60 on these dimensions (Table 2, 3). The method breaks on impressions requiring temporal or contextual prosodic analysis (e.g., Calm–Restless, Cold–Warm), where correlations drop below 0.4, as the feature set lacks dynamic cues.

### Mechanism 2: Self-Supervised Speech Representations
SSL models (HuBERT) capture latent, context-aware acoustic patterns that align with human perception of expressive *change*, especially for dynamic or complex impressions. Frame-level embeddings are pooled with an LSTM and attention to aggregate temporal context, then concatenated for the pair and fed to an MLP. HuBERT’s pre-training (masked unit prediction) forces it to learn speaker- and content-invariant phonetic and prosodic structure, which encodes fine-grained temporal evolutions (e.g., speech rate, phrase-level intonation) relevant to judgments like Powerful–Weak or Cold–Warm. The core assumption is that the SSL representation space is perceptually smooth with respect to the targeted impression dimensions. Evidence: SSL achieves Pearson up to 0.88 and >0.69 on all impressions, with largest gains on previously failing dimensions: Calm–Restless (0.73 vs. 0.37), Powerful–Weak (0.72 vs. 0.37), Cold–Warm (0.75 vs. 0.43). The model would break if perceptual shifts were driven by non-speech artifacts or if SSL invariance were too strong, washing out subtle cues—though the single-speaker dataset controls for speaker identity, cross-speaker generalization remains untested.

### Mechanism 3: Zero-Shot Multimodal Large Language Models
MLLMs (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro) are unreliable for fine-grained RIE. They receive two audio clips and a natural language prompt describing the 9 axes and must output a numerical difference vector. Their internal audio encoder (e.g., CLAP) and language reasoning are expected to abstract and compare paralinguistic cues. The core assumption is that the MLLM has internalized human-like mappings from acoustic patterns to descriptive adjectives and can perform analogical comparison. Evidence: Both models achieve modest or poor scores (e.g., Gemini CCC 0.45 on Dark–Bright; GPT-5 CCC -0.03 on Powerful–Weak), often near zero or negative, and inconsistent across runs. The mechanism fails because the audio encoder may not resolve subtle intra-speaker expressive differences, the language model lacks grounding for the specific 9-dimensional space, and the models do not inherently perform difference reasoning without explicit chain-of-thought prompting.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Acoustic Parameterization (eGeMAPSv02)**  
  *Why needed:* Provides a standardized, interpretable set of low-level descriptors (F0, MFCCs, spectral features) to establish a baseline of what can be captured without deep learning and to diagnose model limitations.  
  *Quick check:* If mean F0 correlates with High–Low, why might the *variance* of F0 be more relevant for an impression like Calm–Restless?

- **Concept: Self-Supervised Learning Pre-training Objectives**  
  *Why needed:* SSL models like HuBERT replace labeled data with a pretext task (masked unit prediction), forcing the model to learn contextual representations useful for phonetic and prosodic modeling, which explains why frame embeddings might encode the temporal dynamics missing from static feature aggregates.  
  *Quick check:* How might predicting masked acoustic units force a model to learn about speaking style, not just phoneme identity?

- **Concept: Zero-Shot Multimodal Reasoning in LLMs**  
  *Why needed:* MLLMs represent a paradigm of massive cross-modal pre-training followed by instruction tuning. Their failure here highlights a gap between general audio understanding and fine-grained, *comparative* paralinguistic reasoning, raising questions about the sufficiency of natural language supervision for this task.  
  *Quick check:* What does it mean for an MLLM to be "zero-shot" on this task, and why might few-shot examples not be enough?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Pair -> Feature Extractor -> Difference/Concat Module -> Predictor -> Target  
  Feature Extractor: (a) openSMILE → ϕ(x); (b) HuBERT → ψ(x) via frame embeddings + pooling; (c) MLLM audio encoder → audio tokens  
  Difference/Concat: (a) ϕΔ = ϕ(x_b) − ϕ(x_a); (b) concatenate [ψ(x_a); ψ(x_b)]; (c) prompt with both audios and instruction  
  Predictor: (a) Regressor/MLP (9 outputs); (c) MLLM text generation → parse numbers  
  Target: 9-D mean Likert difference from human evaluators

- **Critical path:** The high-performing SSL pipeline: utterance x → HuBERT frame embeddings → weighted sum + BiLSTM + attention → 128-D utterance embedding ψ(x) for each utterance → concatenate [ψ(x_a); ψ(x_b)] → 3-layer MLP → 9-D output. Key steps are frame-level encoding preserving prosodic detail, temporal pooling to aggregate change-relevant context, and explicit pairing via concatenation to enforce relative mapping.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  *Interpretability vs. Performance:* Classical features offer direct acoustic insight but hit a ceiling on complex impressions; SSL is a black box but captures dynamics.  
  *Data Efficiency:* Classical/SSL models are trained on ~800 pairs (10-fold CV); MLLMs are zero-shot, favoring scalability over sample efficiency.  
  *Task Formulation:* Using concatenation vs. explicit embedding difference is a design choice; the paper uses concatenation, letting the MLP learn the difference operation.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  *Classical:* CCC < 0.3 on dimensions like Calm–Restless, Cold–Warm; indicates feature set lacks expressive temporal cues.  
  *SSL:* Disproportionate error on dimensions with low training variance or highly non-linear perception across speakers (untested here due to single speaker).  
  *MLLM:* Near-zero or negative correlations; inconsistent outputs; suggests no reliable auditory comparison for fine-grained scales.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Feature-Diagnostic Analysis:** Compute Pearson |r| between every openSMILE feature (and delta) and each impression label; rank to select top-8 per dimension (as in Table 2); validate perceptual plausibility (e.g., F0 for High–Low).  
  2. **SSL Pooling Strategy Ablation:** Compare utterance-level pooling methods (mean, max, weighted sum, LSTM+attention) on validation fold; test if simpler pooling harms dynamic impressions more.  
  3. **MLLM Prompt Stress Test:** Systematically vary prompt phrasing (e.g., "How much more X is B than A?" vs. "Rate the difference on scale -3 to +3"), few-shot example count, and language (Japanese vs. English); measure output variance and correlation to identify minimal effective prompting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Multi-speaker and cross-gender generalization:** How can RIE be extended while disentangling speaker identity from expressive style? *Basis:* Future work explicitly mentions extending to multi-speaker and cross-gender settings, noting new challenges like speaker normalization. *Why unresolved:* Current study uses a single female speaker, limiting generalizability; inherent vocal traits may confound style-based changes. *Evidence needed:* Evaluation on a multi-speaker dataset with diverse genders, employing speaker normalization or adversarial methods, and measuring performance changes.

- **MLLM fine-tuning and prompting:** Can MLLMs achieve competitive performance through fine-tuning or advanced prompting, and what adjustments are necessary? *Basis:* Future work proposes few-shot training for MLLMs; current results show unreliability. *Why unresolved:* MLLMs were evaluated only zero-shot without adaptation; potential for relative reasoning is unexplored. *Evidence needed:* Implement few-shot learning or fine-tuning of MLLMs on RIE data, vary prompts and context examples, compare against SSL baselines.

- **SSL temporal pattern analysis:** What specific temporal and contextual patterns in SSL representations are most predictive of dynamic voice impressions (e.g., Cold–Warm) that elude classical features? *Basis:* SSL improvements on dynamic impressions are attributed to temporal modeling, but precise cues are not analyzed. *Why unresolved:* The paper does not dissect which SSL-derived temporal dynamics drive performance gains. *Evidence needed:* Ablate SSL embeddings (layer-wise probing, alternative pooling) and correlate specific temporal dynamics (e.g., speech rate fluctuations, spectral contours) with dynamic impression labels.

## Limitations
- Dataset is restricted to a single speaker, limiting generalizability to multi-speaker or cross-gender scenarios.
- SSL model is a Japanese-specific HuBERT; results may not transfer to other languages or larger model variants.
- MLLM evaluation was zero-shot with unspecified prompts; performance is likely sensitive to prompting strategy and may not reflect upper bounds.
- No exploration of alternative SSL architectures (e.g., wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT‑large) or design choices (e.g., explicit difference vs. concatenation).

## Confidence
- **SSL superiority:** Medium – strong evidence on single-speaker data but lacks multi-speaker validation and model diversity.
- **Classical feature limitations:** High – consistent underperformance on dynamic impressions across multiple regressors.
- **MLLM unreliability:** Medium – clear failure in the presented zero-shot setup, but could improve with prompt engineering or fine-tuning.

## Next Checks
1. **Multi-speaker replication:** Use a public multi-speaker expressive corpus (e.g., NUS-8, VCTK with style variations) to replicate the SSL pipeline and compare per-dimension correlations against classical features.
2. **MLLM prompt engineering:** Systematically vary instruction clarity, few-shot count, and output format to establish an upper bound for zero-shot MLLMs on RIE.
3. **Architectural ablation:** Replace BiLSTM-attention pooling with simpler methods (mean, max); test concatenation vs. explicit embedding difference; evaluate other SSL backbones (wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT‑large) to identify key components.
