# Hard labels sampled from sparse targets mislead rotation invariant algorithms

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.20967  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20967  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Rotation-invariant algorithms (e.g., standard gradient descent on a single-layer network) suffer a Bayes excess risk lower bound of Ω((d−1)/n) for overconstrained sparse logistic regression with hard labels. In contrast, a non-rotation-invariant spindly network (w = u⊙v) with balanced initialization and early stopping achieves excess risk O(s log d / n) under n ≳ (a* w_min*)⁻² log d, where a* = E[σ'(x⊤_S w_S*)] is population curvature along the signal.

## Executive Summary
This paper reveals a fundamental limitation: when learning a sparse logistic model from hard binary labels, any algorithm that is invariant to rotations of the input space (including standard gradient descent on a single-layer network) incurs an excess risk at least on the order of d/n, even in the overparameterized regime and with infinitely many samples in proportion to dimension. The root cause is that rotational symmetry prevents the algorithm from distinguishing signal coordinates from irrelevant ones; the noise coordinates collectively contribute a large bias. To overcome this, the authors propose a simple architectural modification: a “spindly” two-layer linear network where each output weight is the product of two scalars, w = u⊙v. With a balanced initialization (u(0) = v(0) = α·1) and proper early stopping, gradient descent on this network breaks rotational symmetry and efficiently recovers the sparse target. The dynamics can be reduced to state-dependent coupled Riccati ordinary differential equations that separate signal and noise. Under isotropic Gaussian design and a sparsity prior, the excess risk scales as O(s log d / n) plus a lower-order term, provided the sample size n satisfies n ≳ (a* w_min*)⁻² log d. Experiments confirm the theoretical rates and demonstrate a narrow window for early stopping where inactive coordinates remain suppressed.

## Method Summary
The method compares two algorithms for sparse logistic regression: (1) standard gradient descent (GD) on a single-layer network (w ∈ ℝᵈ), which is rotation-invariant; (2) GD on a spindly network with parameterization w = u⊙v (elementwise product), using balanced initialization u(0) = v(0) = α·1. The spindly network breaks rotational symmetry because its dynamics involve absolute values of weights. Under isotropic Gaussian features x ∼ N(0, I_d) and a sparse true weight vector w⋆ (s-sparse, ‖w⋆‖₂=1), the population gradient flow for the spindly network can be transformed via Stein’s lemma into a system of coupled ODEs: for each coordinate j, ˙w_j = -|w_j|·[w_j a(w) - w_j⋆ a⋆·1_{j∈S}], where a(w) = E[σ'(xᵀ w)] is a shared curvature factor and a⋆ = E[σ'(x_Sᵀ w_S⋆)]. This yields a competitive amplification: signal coordinates (j ∈ S) receive an extra positive push toward w_j⋆, while noise coordinates (j ∉ S) are damped. By bounding a(w) between a⋆ and 1/4, the coupled dynamics are sandwiched between two scalar envelope ODEs with constant curvature, which have closed-form solutions. Early stopping at a time T(ε) — when the weakest active coordinate’s lower envelope reaches (1-ε)w_min^⋆ — ensures all active coordinates are near target and inactive coordinates remain O(1/√d). The resulting excess risk decomposes into an active error O(s log d / n) and an inactive error O(d/(n (a⋆ w_min^⋆)²)). The sample complexity condition n ≳ (a⋆ w_min^⋆)⁻² log d guarantees these bounds hold with high probability.

## Key Results
- **Lower bound**: Any rotation-invariant algorithm suffers Bayes excess risk at least c(d−1)/n with high probability.
- **Upper bound**: The spindly network with balanced initialization and early stopping achieves excess risk O(s log d / n) plus O(d/(n (a⋆ w_min^⋆)²)), under n ≳ (a⋆ w_min^⋆)⁻² log d.
- **Empirical confirmation**: Experiments validate the theoretical rates and show a clear separation window for early stopping.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Rotational Symmetry Breaking via Hadamard Product
- **Claim**: The spindly parameterization w = u⊙v breaks the rotational invariance that plagues standard GD, enabling efficient learning of sparse targets.
- **Mechanism**: Under Hadamard parameterization, gradient descent induces dynamics ˙w = -|w|⊙∇L(w). The coordinate-wise absolute values |w| break orthogonality: rotating the data by an orthogonal matrix U no longer yields a simple transformation Uw(t), so the trajectory can lock onto directions that grow faster. Balanced initialization (u(0)=v(0)=α1) ensures the symmetry-breaking emerges from dynamics rather than initialization.
- **Core assumption**: Data x ∼ N(0,I) and w⋆ is s-sparse with ‖w⋆‖₂=1.
- **Evidence**: Proposition 3.2 proves standard GD is rotation-invariant; Lemma D.2 shows spindly dynamics are not; Section 4 derives coupled Riccati ODEs.

### Mechanism 2: State-Dependent Coupled Riccati Dynamics
- **Claim**: Under isotropic Gaussian design and sparse targets, the spindly dynamics reduce to coupled ODEs where signal coordinates grow while noise coordinates are damped, enabling provable separation.
- **Mechanism**: Stein’s lemma gives the population gradient: for j ∈ S, [∇L(w)]_j = w_j a(w) - w_j⋆ a⋆; for j ∉ S, [∇L(w)]_j = w_j a(w), with a(w)=E[σ'(xᵀ w)] shared curvature. Spindly dynamics become ˙w_j = -|w_j|·[w_j a(w) - w_j⋆ a⋆·1_{j∈S} - ζ_j], where ζ_j is sampling noise. This creates a “winner-take-most” effect: active coordinates have an extra positive term pushing them toward w_j⋆, while inactive ones only experience stochastic damping.
- **Core assumption**: The gradient decomposition holds exactly for Gaussian x; sampling noise ζ satisfies max|ζ_j| ≤ γ with γ ≪ w_min^⋆ a⋆.
- **Evidence**: Theorem 4.1 provides exact gradient coordinates; Equation 26 gives the coupled ODE system; Appendix C.1 details envelope ODEs.

### Mechanism 3: Early Stopping on Separated Dynamics
- **Claim**: There exists a narrow time window (stopping time) where active coordinates are nearly target while inactive coordinates remain O(1/√d), yielding low excess risk.
- **Mechanism**: Bounding a(w) between a⋆ and 1/4 allows sandwiching the coupled ODEs between two scalar envelope ODEs with constant curvature. These envelopes have closed-form solutions. The stopping time T(ε) is chosen so that the weakest active coordinate’s lower envelope reaches (1-ε)w_min^⋆. At that moment, all active coordinates are even closer, and the inactive upper envelope stays ≤ c/d, giving total inactive norm O(1/d). Excess risk then is O(s log d / n) plus a lower-order term.
- **Core assumption**: (1) Signal-to-noise separation: γ/(w_min^⋆ a⋆) ≤ 1/2 - δ; (2) curvature not saturated: a⋆ ≥ (1-ε)/4. Sample size n ≳ (a⋆ w_min^⋆)⁻² log d ensures these with high probability.
- **Evidence**: Theorem 5.1 gives explicit bound; Section 5 explains envelope comparison; Appendix C.4 proves monotonicity and concentration.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept 1: Rotation-Invariant Algorithms
- **Definition**: An algorithm is rotation-invariant if applying the same orthogonal transformation to training data and test input yields identical predictions. Formally: p(x_test; A({(x_i, y_i)})) = p(U x_test; A({(U x_i, y_i)})). The class includes any algorithm that operates only via inner products and uses a rotationally invariant initialization/distribution.
- **Why needed**: The paper’s fundamental limitation result applies to this entire class, explaining why overparameterized models with gradient descent cannot escape the curse of irrelevant dimensions when learning sparse targets from hard labels.
- **Quick check**: If you rotate all training inputs by U and apply a rotation-invariant algorithm, does the predicted probability for a new x_test equal the prediction from the original run on U x_test? Yes, by definition.

### Concept 2: Spindly Network as Symmetry Breaking Architecture
- **Definition**: A two-layer “diagonal” linear network where each output weight w_i is the product of two scalar parameters u_i and v_i. With balanced initialization u(0)=v(0), gradient descent on this architecture is not rotation-invariant because the dynamics depend on absolute values |w_i| rather than w as a vector.
- **Why needed**: This reparameterization is the authors’ constructive solution to bypass the rotational invariance barrier. It introduces an anisotropic inductive bias that aligns with the sparse target structure without requiring explicit prior knowledge (the bias emerges from gradient dynamics).
- **Quick check**: Does the transformation w → u⊙v commute with multiplying all inputs by an orthogonal matrix U? No: (Uw) ≠ (Uu)⊙(Uv) because Hadamard product does not preserve vector rotations.

### Concept 3: Early Stopping for Implicit Regularization
- **Definition**: Stopping gradient descent before full convergence to exploit the transient phase where the iterate lies near a sparse solution. Here the stopping time is derived analytically from the coupled ODE analysis rather than chosen via validation.
- **Why needed**: The spindly dynamics don’t converge to w⋆; they pass near it at a specific time T(ε). Early stopping captures this “sweet spot.” The stopping time depends on curvature a⋆, minimum signal strength w_min^⋆, and is chosen to ensure inactive coordinates remain small.
- **Quick check**: Why not just run until convergence? At convergence, both signal and noise coordinates would be updated until loss minimum is reached, which for hard labels is not w⋆ due to label sampling randomness, and inactive coordinates may grow.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Data (X, y) → Initialize u=v=α1 → At each step: w = u⊙v → predictions σ(Xw) → loss L = (1/n) Σ log(1+exp(-y·Xw)) → gradient g = ∇_w L → updates u ← u - η(v⊙g), v ← v - η(u⊙g) → monitor active norm (or validation loss) → if stopping condition met, return w; else repeat.

**Critical Path**:
1. Initialize u, v with balanced small α (e.g., α = 1/√d).
2. For iteration t = 1,2,...:
   - Compute w = u⊙v.
   - Compute predictions p_i = σ(x_iᵀ w) for training batch.
   - Compute loss L = (1/n) Σ log(1+exp(-y_i x_iᵀ w)).
   - Compute gradient g = ∇_w L = (1/n) Xᵀ (p - y) (elementwise).
   - Update u ← u - η (v⊙g), v ← v - η (u⊙g).
3. Monitor either the minimum absolute value among coordinates in known support S or the validation loss.
4. When min_{i∈S} |w_i| ≥ (1-ε) w_min^⋆ (or validation loss stops improving), break and output w_hat = u⊙v.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- **Support knowledge**: Theory assumes known support S. In practice, either use validation to detect consistent sign patterns or add ℓ₁ regularization. Theory suggests early stopping alone may suffice without explicit ℓ₁.
- **Curvature estimation**: a⋆ = E[σ'(x_Sᵀ w_S⋆)] depends on w⋆. Estimate from data: a_emp = (1/n) Σ σ'(y_i x_iᵀ w(t)) after warmup. Approximate w_min^⋆ from largest active weights.
- **Learning rate & discretization**: Analysis assumes continuous-time flow. Discrete steps require η small enough to not overshoot separation window. Adaptive step size possible: η ∼ 1/(λ_max(XᵀX)·a(w)).
- **Computational cost**: No extra memory over standard GD; same forward/backward. Only additional bookkeeping for active coordinate monitoring.

**Failure Signatures**:
- **Inactive coordinates blowup**: If ‖w_{Sᶜ}‖² exceeds ~c/d (e.g., 0.1 for d=500), separation window missed. Monitor per-coordinate growth; if max_{i∉S} |w_i(t)| increases rapidly, stop earlier.
- **Active coordinates diverge or collapse**: If min_active becomes > 2 w_min^⋆ (overshoot) or decreases (instability), step size or stopping condition is wrong. Violation of curvature bound a(w) ≥ a⋆ due to large increments.
- **No early-stopping benefit**: If validation loss for spindly matches single-layer (no gap), stopping time not in window. Reduce η and/or increase sample size to satisfy n ≫ d condition.

2. **Rate verification with varying d**: Fix n=2000, s=5, sweep d=20..200. For each d, run both algorithms with early stopping, compute excess risk L(w_hat)-L(w⋆) via large test set. On log-log, spindly slope ≈ -1 (O(s log d/n) if log d dominates) and single-layer slope ≈ 0 (Ω(d/n)).
3. **Stopping time sensitivity sweep**: For one dataset, run spindly GD and stop at various times T. Compute excess risk and inactive norm. Expect U-shaped excess risk: high at very small T, minimum in middle window (signal near target but noise suppressed), then rising. Inactive norm should stay flat then blow up after window. Validate analytic T(ε) prediction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can the lower bounds for rotation invariant algorithms be generalized to exponential family distributions beyond logistic (e.g., Poisson, Exponential)? The paper notes: “Can our lower bounds for rotation invariant algorithms be generalized to the case when hard labels are sampled from other exponential family distributions such as the Poisson and Exponential distributions. Our preliminary experiments show that there is again a performance gap between rotation invariant algorithms and GD on the spindlified network. Quantifying and proving this gap for general exponential families is a challenging goal.”
2. Can other non-rotation invariant algorithms (e.g., exponentiated gradient, priming, Lasso) be adapted to sparse logistic regression to achieve the same O(s log d / n) excess risk upper bound? The paper states: “In the previous setting of over-constrained noisy sparse linear regression (Warmuth et al., 2025), a large variety of non-rotation invariant algorithms were shown to decisively beat rotation invariant algorithms. This include two-sided versions of the exponentiated gradient algorithm (a.k.a. mirror descent with the arcsinh link function) and priming (Warmuth & Amid, 2023). Also Lasso achieves the same feat. We believe that a number of these algorithm can be adapted to learn s sparse logistic regression with the same upper excess risk bound.”

## Limitations
- The analysis assumes isotropic Gaussian features and independent coordinates; real data often have correlations that may break Stein’s lemma reduction and alter the coupled dynamics.
- The upper bound requires precise knowledge of the support and signal parameters (a*, w_min*) for optimal stopping; in practice these are unknown, and mis-specification could worsen performance.
- Theoretical guarantees hold for gradient flow (continuous-time); discrete GD with finite step size may miss the narrow separation window or cause instability, especially with minibatch noise.

## Confidence
- **Lower bound Ω(d/n) for rotation-invariant algorithms**: High (rigorous information-theoretic proof).
- **Upper bound O(s log d / n) for spindly network**: High under stated assumptions, but Medium for practical deployment due to strong distributional and parameter requirements.
- **Symmetry breaking via Hadamard product and Riccati dynamics**: High within Gaussian model, but Medium for broader applicability.
- **Generalization to non-Gaussian designs**: Low (analysis heavily relies on Gaussianity via Stein’s lemma).
- **Practical success of spindly network without oracle support**: Medium (experiments suggest early stopping works, but theory assumes known support).

## Next Checks
1. Test robustness to feature correlations: generate data with non-identity covariance (e.g., autoregressive) and compare spindly vs single-layer excess risks.
2. Hyperparameter sensitivity: vary initialization scale α and learning rate η, measure width of effective stopping window and frequency of inactive coordinate blowup.
3. Practical early stopping: implement spindly with validation-based stopping without oracle support; test on high-dimensional sparse logistic regression (d=500, s=10) to see if O(s log d / n) rate still holds.
