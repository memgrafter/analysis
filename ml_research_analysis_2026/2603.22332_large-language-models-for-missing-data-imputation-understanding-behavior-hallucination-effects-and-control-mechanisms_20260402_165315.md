# Large Language Models for Missing Data Imputation: Understanding Behavior, Hallucination Effects, and Control Mechanisms
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22332
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22332
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** LLMs (Gemini 3.0 Flash, Claude 4.5 Sonnet) achieve lower NRMSE than classical methods on real-world datasets, but MICE outperforms all LLMs on synthetic data; LLMs incur substantially higher computational time and monetary costs, with hallucinations more frequent in unfamiliar contexts.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the use of large language models (LLMs) for tabular missing data imputation under a zero-shot prompting paradigm. The authors compare five LLMs (Gemini 3.0 Flash, Claude 4.5 Sonnet, GPT-4.1-Nano, Xiaomi MiMo-V2-Flash, Mistral Devstral 2 2512) against six traditional imputation methods (kNN, MICE, missForest, SAEI, SoftImpute, TabPFN) across 29 datasets (20 real-world, 9 synthetic) with missingness induced at 5%, 10%, and 20% under MCAR, MAR, and MNAR mechanisms. Results show that Gemini and Claude achieve statistically significant reductions in Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE) compared to classical baselines on real-world datasets, whereas on synthetic datasets, MICE consistently outperforms all LLMs. LLM performance correlates with the overlap between the dataset's semantic domain and the LLM's pre-training corpus, suggesting that semantic context, rather than pure statistical reconstruction, drives their advantage. However, LLMs incur orders of magnitude higher computational time and monetary cost due to API calls and token usage.

The study also examines hallucination effects, measured by fallback activations when the LLM output fails validation checks (e.g., wrong shape, non-numeric values). Hallucinations are more prevalent on synthetic and unfamiliar datasets, with GPT-4.1-Nano exhibiting a fallback rate of 14.94% while top models show near-zero fallbacks. The authors discuss a fundamental accuracy-efficiency trade-off: LLMs provide superior imputation accuracy on suitable real-world data but at substantial resource expense. They highlight the importance of prompt design, batch processing, and fallback mechanisms for robust deployment, and outline open research directions for improving cost-effectiveness and generalizability.

## Method Summary
The method employs zero-shot prompting, converting each batch of 40 rows × 10 columns into a natural language prompt that includes a system role, dataset context, task description, constraints, and strict output format. LLM APIs are queried with temperature 0.1; each batch is sent up to three times with retries on failure, and if after three attempts the output is invalid (mismatched shape, NaN, non-numeric), a deterministic fallback to mean imputation is applied. Traditional methods are applied to normalized data ([0,1]) using their default hyperparameters. Missingness is generated using the `mdatagen` library according to MCAR, MAR, MNAR patterns at specified rates. Evaluation across 5-fold cross-validation uses NRMSE as primary metric, and also logs token counts, runtime, and cost. The experimental pipeline uses a fixed batch size of 40×10, chosen empirically to balance context window limits and number of API calls. The study evaluates 29 datasets, including 20 real-world ones selected via an LLM prompt from UCI/OpenML and 9 synthetic datasets created with scikit-learn's `make_classification`.

## Key Results
- Gemini 3.0 Flash and Claude 4.5 Sonnet significantly outperform classical methods (e.g., MICE) on real-world datasets, but MICE surpasses all LLMs on synthetic datasets.
- LLM hallucinations (triggering fallback) are more frequent in unfamiliar synthetic contexts, with GPT-4.1-Nano at 14.94% fallback versus near 0% for top models.
- LLM-based imputation achieves superior accuracy on suitable data but at substantially higher computational and monetary costs, with dataset batch counts driving overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Semantic Context Advantage
**Claim:** LLMs excel at imputation on real-world datasets by leveraging semantic patterns and domain-specific information acquired during large-scale pre-training, rather than purely statistical reconstruction.
**Mechanism:** The model processes tabular data converted into natural language prompts; its internal weights, trained on internet-scale corpora containing many open-source datasets, activate relevant contextual associations and domain knowledge (e.g., feature relationships in "heart disease" or "iris") to generate plausible values.
**Core assumption:** The performance advantage depends on overlap between the training corpus and the target dataset's semantic domain.
**Evidence:** Abstract states effectiveness is driven by semantic context; Section 4.3 notes LLMs demonstrate superior imputation on datasets like Heart Disease and Iris that are widely represented in internet corpora; performance degrades on synthetic or private datasets lacking such exposure (Table 9, Section 4.6).

### Hallucination via Fallback
**Claim:** LLM hallucinations (invalid outputs) are more likely when imputation contexts are unfamiliar or when the model's representations are misaligned with the data's semantic structure.
**Mechanism:** When faced with feature distributions not well-represented in pre-training, the model may generate non-numeric, misshapen, or otherwise invalid responses. The system detects these via validation checks (shape, NaN, formatting) and triggers a deterministic fallback (mean imputation) after three retries.
**Core assumption:** Fallback activation indicates the model's inability to produce a coherent, context-aware imputation within the prompt constraints.
**Evidence:** Abstract notes hallucinations are more frequent in unfamiliar synthetic contexts; Section 4.4 reports that GPT-4.1-Nano did not consistently follow zero-shot prompts, leading to multiple fallback activations.

### Accuracy-Efficiency Trade-off
**Claim:** There exists a fundamental trade-off where LLM-based imputation achieves superior NRMSE on suitable datasets but incurs orders-of-magnitude higher computational time and monetary cost.
**Mechanism:** The batch-based prompt strategy requires multiple API calls per fold, especially for larger datasets (e.g., compass-7k with 93 batches/fold). Each call involves latency and cost proportional to token usage, contrasting with traditional in-memory methods that have fixed overhead.
**Core assumption:** The value of LLM imputation must be weighed against the marginal NRMSE improvement relative to resource consumption.
**Evidence:** Abstract mentions significantly higher computational time and monetary costs; Section 4.5 quantifies that MICE is fastest, while Claude and Gemini achieve excellent performance but with high batch-processing overhead; Table 3 and Table 8 provide detailed cost and time metrics.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Selector -> Amputation Engine -> Prompt Constructor -> Batch Scheduler -> LLM Imputation Client -> Fallback Handler -> Evaluation Aggregator

**Critical path:** Dataset Selection -> Amputation via mdatagen -> Prompt Design & Batch Size Tuning (empirical) -> API Execution Loop with retries and fallback -> NRMSE Calculation.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Batch size (40x10): Larger batches reduce API calls and cost but risk exceeding context window; smaller batches increase calls but preserve global feature relationships; size chosen empirically.
- Normalization: Traditional methods use [0,1] normalized data; LLMs use raw values, assuming they handle raw scales due to pre‑training. This affects direct NRMSE comparability.
- Fallback determinism: Using mean imputation on validation failure ensures continuity but biases NRMSE downward for that batch; trade‑off between robustness and fairness.

**Failure signatures:**
- High fallback rate (>5%): Indicates prompt instability or model inability; monitor raw outputs and validate shape/dtype.
- Performance collapse on synthetic data: LLMs much worse than MICE (Table 9); suggests lack of semantic pre‑training alignment.
- Degradation on small/high‑dim datasets (e.g., Hepatitis, Wisconsin): Signals insufficient prompt context or too many batches.
- Cost/time explosion: Datasets requiring many batches (>50) become prohibitively expensive (e.g., compass-7k).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Prompt Robustness Test:** Run 5 random seeds on one dataset (e.g., wine) with the same prompt for a single LLM (e.g., Gemini). Measure output variance and fallback rate; verifies deterministic settings but reveals API stochasticity.
2. **Batch Size Sweep:** On a medium dataset (e.g., parkinsons), test batch sizes: 20×5, 40×10, 80×10. Compare NRMSE, total tokens, and API calls; validates the chosen trade‑off.
3. **Real vs. Synthetic Contrast:** Impute a synthetic categorical-continuous dataset and a semantically similar real dataset (e.g., iris) at 10% MAR. Compare Gemini vs. MICE NRMSE; expect LLM to win on iris but lose on synthetic, testing the semantic advantage assumption.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How does varying LLM architecture, size, and pricing affect performance and cost?**  
   *Basis:* Authors note financial constraints limited model diversity.  
   *Needed evidence:* Systematic benchmark across a wide range of models with the same protocol.
2. **Can LLM-based imputation generalize beyond tabular data to other modalities (e.g., time series, images, text)?**  
   *Basis:* Authors propose extension to different data modalities.  
   *Needed evidence:* Adapt the batch‑prompt pipeline to other data types and evaluate imputation accuracy.
3. **What batch processing optimizations minimize overhead while preserving accuracy?**  
   *Basis:* Authors identify efficient batch strategies as an open avenue.  
   *Needed evidence:* Controlled study varying batch size, stride, aggregation; derive Pareto frontier for cost vs. accuracy.
4. **Can integrating LLM-based imputation into AutoML pipelines enhance downstream task performance and automation?**  
   *Basis:* Authors suggest integration with AutoML libraries.  
   *Needed evidence:* Implement AutoML with LLM imputation as an operator and compare end‑to‑end predictive accuracy and automation level.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility gaps:** Crucial details such as the dataset selection prompt, exact API response parsing, and normalization differences are not disclosed, making exact replication difficult.
- **Prompt sensitivity:** Only a single zero‑shot prompt was evaluated; performance may vary substantially with minor wording, delimiter, or feature ordering changes.
- **Fallback bias:** The fallback to mean imputation for invalid outputs artificially lowers NRMSE for those batches, potentially skewing the assessment of LLM accuracy.

## Confidence
- **Semantic context advantage (Mechanism 1):** Medium confidence (real-world vs. synthetic gap observed but pre‑training overlap not directly measured).
- **Hallucination pattern (Mechanism 2):** High confidence (fallback rates measured directly and consistently).
- **Cost‑efficiency trade‑off (Mechanism 3):** High confidence (resource consumption quantified across models and datasets).
- **Overall claim that LLMs are useful for imputation:** Medium confidence, limited by model diversity and prompt dependence.

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt robustness:** Systematically vary delimiters, feature order, and constraint phrasing; assess NRMSE and fallback stability across multiple random seeds.
2. **Exposure test:** Fine‑tune a small LLM on a subset of UCI datasets; compare in‑domain (UCI) vs. out‑of‑domain (synthetic) imputation performance to directly measure pre‑training exposure effects.
3. **Batch scaling:** On a large dataset (compass-7k), compare batch sizes 20×5, 40×10, 80×20; compute Pareto frontier of NRMSE vs. cost/time to identify optimal configurations.
