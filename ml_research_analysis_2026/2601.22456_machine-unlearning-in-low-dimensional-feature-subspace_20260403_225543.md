# Machine Unlearning in Low-Dimensional Feature Subspace

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.22456
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22456
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LOFT reduces optimized parameters to <1% of original and runtime to seconds while achieving near-retrained unlearning accuracy (Avg.Gap 0.85 on Tiny-ImageNet) and low membership inference attack success.

## Executive Summary
Machine unlearning (MU) aims to remove the influence of specific data (the forgetting set $D_{\text{fg}}$) from a trained model without full retraining. Existing methods often require repeated raw data access and extensive parameter updates, posing privacy risks and computational burdens. This paper introduces LOFT, a novel MU approach that operates in a low-dimensional feature subspace. LOFT inserts a small projection matrix into a pretrained model and learns it by optimizing a joint objective on the Stiefel manifold, leveraging covariances of features from forgetting and remaining data. The method requires only a one-shot feature extraction (no raw data revisits) and updates only the projection matrix (as few as <1% of total parameters). Experiments across multiple datasets and architectures show that LOFT attains state-of-the-art unlearning accuracy, with a small gap to models retrained from scratch, runtime in seconds, and robust resistance to membership inference attacks.

LOFT’s efficiency comes from its subspace assumption: after exact unlearning, features of forgetting data concentrate in fewer principal directions, making them separable from remaining data in a low-dimensional subspace. By minimizing the variance of forgetting features within this subspace while preserving the reconstruction of remaining features, LOFT effectively forgets without altering pretrained weights. The approach is applicable to both class-wise and instance-wise unlearning and can be extended to continual unlearning scenarios. The results demonstrate a promising direction for practical, privacy-preserving machine unlearning.

## Method Summary
LOFT proceeds as follows. Given a pretrained model $f_{\text{pre}}$ (backbone $g_{\text{pre}}$ + head $h_{\text{pre}}$), one performs a single forward pass on the union of forgetting set $D_{\text{fg}}$ and remaining set $D_{\text{rm}}$ to extract penultimate-layer features. From these features, compute empirical covariance matrices $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$ and $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$ (after mean-centering). Then initialize a projection matrix $U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times s}$ with orthonormal columns and optimize it on the Stiefel manifold to minimize a joint objective $J(U) = J_{\text{fg}}(U) + \lambda J_{\text{rm}}(U)$, where $J_{\text{fg}}$ encourages the projected forgetting features to have minimal variance, and $J_{\text{rm}}$ encourages accurate reconstruction of remaining features from the subspace. The optimization uses Riemannian Adam (Geoopt) with weight decay for a small number of steps (e.g., 50). The learned $U$ defines an orthogonal projector $UU^\top$. At inference, the model is modified to $f_{U}(x) = h_{\text{pre}}(UU^\top g_{\text{pre}}(x))$. The pretrained parameters remain frozen; only $U$ (typically $s \approx 250$ for $d \approx 768$) is stored, making the approach highly parameter-efficient. For continual unlearning, covariances from multiple forgetting rounds are combined additively in the loss.

## Key Results
- LOFT reduces the number of optimized parameters by orders of magnitude (e.g., 0.69% for Swin-T, <1% for others) compared to full fine-tuning or other baselines.
- Unlearning runtime is reduced to seconds on an RTX 4090, while baselines require minutes to hours.
- The gap between LOFT and the exact retrained model (Avg.G.) is as low as 0.85 on Tiny-ImageNet and 1.65 on ImageNet-1K, achieving state-of-the-art unlearning accuracy.
- Membership inference attack success rates are significantly lowered, nearly matching the retrained model’s privacy protection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The core insight is that the feature representations of forgetting data ($D_{\text{fg}}$) and remaining data ($D_{\text{rm}}$) become more separable in a low-dimensional subspace after exact unlearning. Specifically, if we train a model only on $D_{\text{rm}}$ (the exact unlearning target), the covariance of its remaining data features ($\Sigma_{\text{exact}}^{\text{rm}}$) has a rapidly decaying eigenvalue spectrum, while the covariance of forgetting data features ($\Sigma_{\text{exact}}^{\text{fg}}$) decays more slowly. Conversely, in the pretrained model (trained on full data), both covariances exhibit similar spectral decay. Lemma 3.1 formalizes that a subspace spanned by the top eigenvectors of $\Sigma_{\text{exact}}^{\text{rm}}$ can reconstruct $D_{\text{rm}}$ accurately (small $\epsilon_{\text{rm}}$) but captures little energy from $D_{\text{fg}}$ (small $\epsilon_{\text{fg}}$). LOFT exploits this by seeking a subspace where $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$ and $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$ exhibit a similar spectral divergence. Once such a subspace is found, projecting onto it effectively suppresses forgetting-data-related information while retaining most of the remaining-data information. This separation is empirically visualized in Figure 2, which shows distinct eigenvalue decays for exact unlearning versus similar decays for the pretrained model.

To learn the desired subspace, LOFT optimizes a projection matrix $U$ (with orthonormal columns) on the Stiefel manifold. The objective $J(U)$ combines two terms: $J_{\text{fg}}(U) = \big(\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(U^\top \Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}} U)}{\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}})}\big)^2$, which encourages the projected forgetting features to have minimal variance (aligning with PCA directions of smallest variance for $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$), and $J_{\text{rm}}(U) = \big(\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}} - UU^\top \Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}} UU^\top)}{\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}})}\big)^2$, which penalizes the reconstruction error of remaining features when projected back. Proposition 4.1 shows that minimizing $J_{\text{fg}}$ alone yields PCA directions with smallest eigenvalues of $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$. The joint optimization forces $U$ to span a subspace that simultaneously preserves $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$ and discards $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$. Riemannian Adam (Geoopt) is used to respect the orthonormality constraint. Ablation studies (Table 5) confirm that both terms are necessary: $J_{\text{fg}}$ alone suppresses forgetting accuracy but harms remaining accuracy, $J_{\text{rm}}$ alone preserves remaining but fails to forget, while the combination achieves the desired trade-off.

LOFT achieves efficiency and privacy through a one-shot feature fetching process. The covariances $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$ and $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$ are computed from a single forward pass of the frozen pretrained backbone over all forgetting and remaining data, avoiding repeated raw data access. Only the small matrix $U$ (e.g., $250 \times 768 \approx 0.19$M parameters) is optimized, requiring at most a few dozen Riemannian Adam steps. During inference, the learned orthogonal projector is inserted between the backbone and the linear head, requiring no changes to pretrained weights. Thus, LOFT reduces the number of tunable parameters by orders of magnitude (down to <1%) and cuts unlearning time from hours/minutes to seconds. Moreover, because raw data are not revisited and the model parameters remain fixed except for $U$, membership inference attacks find it difficult to distinguish the unlearned model from one retrained from scratch on $D_{\text{rm}}$.

## Foundational Learning

**Principal Component Analysis (PCA)**  
- **Why needed:** LOFT’s subspace learning is PCA‑inspired; understanding eigen‑decomposition of covariance matrices explains why top eigenvectors capture most variance of $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$ and why minimizing variance of $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$ corresponds to selecting directions of smallest eigenvalues.  
- **Quick check:** If a covariance matrix has eigenvalues [10, 2, 0.1], which subspace dimension $s$ captures >95% of total variance? (Answer: $s=2$, because $(10+2)/(10+2+0.1) \approx 99\%$)

**Stiefel Manifold Optimization**  
- **Why needed:** The projection matrix $U$ must satisfy $U^\top U = I$. Optimization on the Stiefel manifold (e.g., via Riemannian Adam) enforces this constraint throughout training, avoiding numerical drift that could occur with standard SGD plus a regularizer.  
- **Quick check:** Why might adding an orthogonal regularization term (e.g., $\|U^\top U - I\|^2$) to a standard SGD loss be less desirable than intrinsic manifold optimization? (Answer: The regularizer approach can still allow intermediate steps to violate orthonormality, leading to instability; manifold optimizers keep $U$ on the constraint set at every iteration.)

**Machine Unlearning Taxonomy**  
- **Why needed:** LOFT targets approximate machine unlearning. Distinguishing exact vs. approximate, and class‑wise vs. instance‑wise unlearning, clarifies the problem’s difficulty and the appropriate evaluation metrics (e.g., accuracy gaps, MIA).  
- **Quick check:** In class‑wise unlearning of many samples from one class, why is the distribution shift potentially larger than in instance‑wise removal of random samples? (Answer: Class‑wise deletion removes an entire semantic group, which can significantly alter class priors and feature distributions; instance‑wise removal deletes scattered points that are less likely to change the overall distribution structure.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Pretrained backbone $g_{\text{pre}}$ → Feature collector (extract penultimate features $Z_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$, $Z_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$) → Covariance calculator (compute $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$, $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$) → Projection optimizer ($U$ on $\operatorname{St}(d,s)$, minimize $J(U)$) → Learned $U$ → Plug‑in module (compute $f_U(x)=h_{\text{pre}}(UU^\top g_{\text{pre}}(x))$). Optional: continual unlearning adds additional $J_{\text{fgp}}$ terms from previous forgetting sets.

### Critical Path
1. **Offline:** Train $f_{\text{pre}} = (g_{\text{pre}}, h_{\text{pre}})$ on full dataset $D$.
2. **Unlearning request** (forget set $D_{\text{fg}}$):
   a. One‑shot feature extraction: run $g_{\text{pre}}$ (without gradients) on all samples in $D_{\text{fg}} \cup D_{\text{rm}}$ to obtain $Z_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$ and $Z_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$.
   b. Center the features and compute empirical covariances $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$, $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$.
   c. Optimize $U \in \operatorname{St}(d,s)$ using Riemannian Adam (e.g., 50–100 steps, lr$\in$[1,10], weight decay 0.05) to minimize $J(U)$.
   d. Deploy the unlearned model $f_U$ with $UU^\top$ inserted before $h_{\text{pre}}$; optionally absorb $U$ into $h_{\text{pre}}$’s weight matrix for efficient inference.
3. **Inference:** For any input $x$, compute $f_U(x)=h_{\text{pre}}(UU^\top g_{\text{pre}}(x))$.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Subspace dimension $s$:** Larger $s$ preserves remaining‑data accuracy better but weakens forgetting (since more variance of $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$ can be captured). The authors recommend choosing $s$ to preserve ~95% of the explained variance of $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$ (i.e., capture its dominant eigenvalues). This heuristic balances retention and forgetting.
- **Layer selection:** LOFT is applied at the penultimate layer by default. Earlier layers have higher feature dimension $d$, increasing $U$’s parameter count, but may contain more low‑level information that could aid preservation. Later layers have smaller $d$ but might already be more task‑specific; the trade‑off is between efficiency and unlearning efficacy.
- **Data requirement:** The method requires a one‑time forward pass over both $D_{\text{fg}}$ and $D_{\text{rm}}$. If only $D_{\text{fg}}$ is available, one could approximate $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$ from statistics of the original training set, but this is not recommended as it may reduce accuracy.

### Failure Signatures
- **High forgetting accuracy ($\text{Acc}_{\text{fg}}$) after LOFT:** Indicates $J_{\text{fg}}$ was not minimized sufficiently; check eigenvalue spectrum of $\Sigma_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$ (if all eigenvalues are similar, no low‑variance subspace exists) and verify $s$ is not too large; also ensure covariances are correctly centered.
- **Low remaining accuracy ($\text{Acc}_{\text{rm}}$):** Suggests $J_{\text{rm}}$ is too high because the subspace is too small or overly constrained by $J_{\text{fg}}$; try increasing $s$ or adjusting $\lambda$ (if used).
- **High MIA success (low gap):** Means $D_{\text{fg}}$ features are still discriminative in the projected space; inspect reconstruction error of $Z_{\text{fg}}^{\text{pre}}$ using $UU^\top$—if error is low, the projector fails to remove their signal; consider larger $\lambda$ for $J_{\text{fg}}$ or different layer.
- **Excessive runtime:** Feature extraction should be one‑shot; if it is repeated during optimization, runtime blows up. Also, if $d$ or $s$ is very large, the Stiefel optimization becomes slower; reduce $d$ by using an earlier layer or smaller $s$.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Theoretical certification:** Can LOFT be certified under a formal privacy notion like differential privacy? The paper notes a rigorous theoretical development remains future work; linking the subspace projection to a DP mechanism and deriving bounds is an open challenge.
2. **Generative models:** How to adapt LOFT to generation tasks (e.g., diffusion, autoregressive models)? The authors caution that LOFT does not update pretrained parameters and may not be directly suitable; extending the method to hidden states in encoders/decoders is an open direction.
3. **Principled $s$ selection:** The current heuristic (preserve ~95% variance of $\Sigma_{\text{rm}}^{\text{pre}}$) works empirically but lacks theoretical justification. An adaptive, data‑driven criterion to choose $s$ that optimally balances retention and forgetting is needed.
4. **High similarity settings:** When $D_{\text{fg}}$ and $D_{\text{rm}}$ are semantically close (e.g., deleting a subset of a class), the subspace separability assumption weakens. Understanding LOFT’s performance degradation and making it robust to such scenarios remains open.

## Limitations
- The separability hypothesis underlying LOFT may fail when forgetting and remaining data share many classes or exhibit only small distribution shifts, leading to poor unlearning.
- One-shot covariance computation assumes static feature distributions; concept drift or time‑varying data could invalidate the learned subspace, requiring recomputation.
- Critical experimental details (e.g., MIA shadow model setup, exact transforms for datasets) are not fully specified, hindering independent verification and reproducibility.

## Confidence
- **Efficiency** (parameter reduction, runtime): High (directly measured, consistent across models).
- **Unlearning efficacy** (accuracy gap to retrained model): Medium (depends on separability and hyperparameter choice; break conditions exist).
- **Privacy** (membership inference resistance): Medium (results are promising but lack external MIA validation; attack implementation not fully detailed).

Run attacks on LOFT‑unlearned models and on retrained baselines; compare attack success rates to verify the claimed privacy advantage.
