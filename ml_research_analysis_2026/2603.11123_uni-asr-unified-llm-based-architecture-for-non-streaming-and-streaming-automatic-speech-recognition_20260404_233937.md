# Uni-ASR: Unified LLM-Based Architecture for Non-Streaming and Streaming Automatic Speech Recognition
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11123
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11123
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** A single LLM-based ASR model achieves competitive non-streaming accuracy (1.93% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean) and robust streaming performance via a joint training paradigm and a latest-token fallback decoding strategy, without architectural modifications.

## Executive Summary
Uni-ASR presents a unified architecture for autoregressive speech recognition that supports both non-streaming (full-utterance) and streaming (low-latency chunk-based) inference modes using a single LLM-based model. The core innovation is a joint training paradigm that interleaves data formatted for both modes, combined with a context-aware streaming training approach and a novel fallback decoding strategy. This design eliminates the need for separate models or architectural changes when switching between modes, while maintaining high accuracy and strict latency constraints. Results on LibriSpeech and AISHELL-2 demonstrate competitive non-streaming performance and significant streaming accuracy improvements, particularly under tight chunk length constraints.

## Method Summary
The method employs a five-stage training schedule. First, an audio encoder (Conformer) and audio adapter are pre-trained. Then, the LLM decoder (Qwen3-1.7B) is supervised in a standard non-streaming sequence-to-sequence manner (stages 1-4). Stage five implements joint training where batches are randomly sampled from three paradigms: non-streaming (NS), standard streaming (SS), and context-aware streaming (CS). For SS/CS, training sequences are constructed by chunking audio and interleaving speech representations with text tokens at a fixed 2:1 ratio, using forced alignment for token-level timing. The CS paradigm masks the last text token of each chunk (`<pad>`) and omits its `<eos>` to simulate the cross-chunk dependency encountered during inference. For streaming inference, the model processes audio incrementally. Upon each new chunk, it employs a fallback strategy: the KV cache from the previous decode step is discarded, the last emitted token is replaced with `<pad>`, and the new chunk's speech is appended. The model then performs a prefill (with the corrected context) followed by decode cycles to regenerate the provisional token and emit new ones within the latency budget.

## Key Results
- Achieved 1.93% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean and 2.60% CER on AISHELL-2 in non-streaming mode.
- Streaming fallback decoding reduced WER by 11.43% to 19.88% relative to greedy decoding across chunk lengths from 1000ms to 320ms.
- The fallback strategy matched or exceeded beam search performance with lower computational overhead.
- Ablation studies confirmed the context-aware streaming training paradigm is crucial for maintaining accuracy under strict latency constraints.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Joint Training for Mode Unification
A single model can perform both non-streaming and streaming ASR without architectural change by interleaving training data from both modes. Non-streaming steps use full utterances with standard teacher-forcing. Streaming steps construct interleaved speech-text sequences from chunked audio using forced alignment (e.g., 2:1 speech:text token ratio). The audio encoder switches between full attention (NS) and dynamic chunk attention (S) within the same weights, while the LLM decoder learns to generate text from a single forward pass (NS) or an incremental, chunk-wise context (S) based on the input sequence format and loss masking. The core assumption is that the LLM, conditioned on the adapter's output, can adapt its generation behavior to the provided context structure. Evidence is provided by the integrated training description and the absence of mode-specific architectures. A break condition is severe data imbalance between modes, which could cause one mode to be neglected.

### Mechanism 2: Context-Aware Streaming Training
The train-inference mismatch for chunk-wise streaming is reduced by explicitly modeling the regeneration of the last token of a chunk during training. In standard streaming training, the last token of a chunk is predicted using its own chunk's speech and preceding text. However, inference with fallback often provisionally emits this token and later re-decodes it with the next chunk's context. The context-aware (CS) paradigm masks this last text token in the input (replaces with `<pad>`) and removes the corresponding `<eos>` from the target for each chunk. This forces the model to learn to generate a token for a position where the preceding text token is missing, directly simulating the cross-chunk re-decoding scenario. The assumption is that this teaches robust boundary handling. Evidence is the explicit masking definition and the stated goal of bridging the gap. A break condition is inaccurate forced alignment, which could corrupt the interleaved sequence and harm learning.

### Mechanism 3: Latest-Token Fallback Decoding
Streaming accuracy under low latency is improved by a bounded re-decoding operation that corrects boundary errors without adding net latency. For each new speech chunk, the system discards the KV cache from the previous step's final token, resets the output sequence by replacing that last token with `<pad>`, and appends the new chunk's speech. The model then performs a new prefill (with the corrected context) followed by decode. This corrects errors from the previous chunk's boundary without re-computing the entire history, as the KV cache for earlier tokens is preserved. The assumption is that the compute cost of this single-chunk prefill is absorbable within the chunk duration, and the accuracy gain outweighs it. Evidence is the consistent WER reduction (11-20% relative) across chunk lengths in experiments and the claim of negligible overhead. A break condition is an extremely tight per-chunk latency budget where even a single prefill operation exceeds the available time.

## Foundational Learning
- **Autoregressive Decoding with Key-Value (KV) Caching:** The LLM decoder's streaming efficiency relies on caching key-value states from previous tokens to avoid re-computation. The fallback strategy specifically manipulates this cache (discarding the last token's state) to enable context correction. *Why needed:* Understanding cache management is critical to grasp the computational properties of the fallback strategy. *Quick check:* How does discarding and rebuilding part of the KV cache affect total compute time per chunk compared to a naive full re-computation?
- **Forced Alignment and Token-Level Speech-Text Interleaving:** An external tool (e.g., MMS FA) aligns text tokens to audio frames. This alignment is used to construct streaming training sequences by interleaving speech representations with text tokens according to a fixed ratio (e.g., 2:1). *Why needed:* This process is fundamental to creating valid, temporally consistent sequences for the streaming paradigm. *Quick check:* If forced alignment has errors at chunk boundaries, how would that specifically impact the loss masking in the context-aware training paradigm?
- **Dynamic Chunk Attention in Conformer Encoders:** The audio encoder must support both full-sequence (non-streaming) and chunk-wise causal (streaming) attention during training. "Dynamic chunk attention" allows the same encoder weights to switch between these modes. *Why needed:* This mechanism enables the unified architecture; without it, separate encoders or complex weight sharing would be needed. *Quick check:* What is the precise difference in attention masking between the "full attention" and "dynamic chunk attention" modes, and how does the model switch between them during a training batch?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Audio Encoder (Conformer) -> Audio Adapter (2xLinear+ReLU) -> LLM Decoder (Qwen3-1.7B). Training Data Pipeline generates NS, SS, and CS sequences. Decoding Controller manages chunk arrival and fallback logic.
- **Critical path:**  
  *Training:* Raw audio -> Encoder (mode-dependent attention) -> Adapter -> LLM input sequence (NS: [speech, `<sos>`, text]; SS/CS: interleaved [speech chunk, text tokens] with masks) -> CE loss (masked) -> Backprop.  
  *Non-streaming Inference:* Full audio -> Encoder (full attn) -> Adapter -> Prefill LLM with [speech, `<sos>`] -> Decode until `<eos>`.  
  *Streaming Inference (per chunk):* New chunk arrives. If first: Prefill [speech_ctx?, `<sos>`, prev_text (last as `<pad>`?)]. If subsequent: Discard KV from last step, replace last output token with `<pad>`, concatenate new chunk speech -> Prefill -> Decode. Emit tokens within latency budget.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Joint Training Complexity vs. Deployment Simplicity:* Single model deployment is simple, but training requires a complex schedule (dual-attention encoder, mixed paradigms) and careful tuning to avoid mode interference.  
  - *Fallback Decoding Accuracy vs. Compute:* Trades a small, bounded prefill cost per chunk for significant WER reduction, especially at short chunks, unlike beam search which multiplies decode cost.  
  - *Context-Aware Training Cost:* Requires precise forced alignment and token-level masking logic, increasing data preprocessing complexity versus standard streaming training.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High streaming WER at short chunks (e.g., 320ms) despite fallback: likely failed training-inference alignment (CS not active), poor forced alignment, or encoder unable to handle severe context loss.  
  - "Stuttering" or repetition in streaming output: may indicate flawed fallback triggering or KV cache reset logic.  
  - Non-streaming performance degrades after joint SFT: suggests catastrophic interference where streaming training overwrites strong NS representations.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Validation:** Train model in non-streaming mode only (stages 1-4) on LibriSpeech/AISHELL-2. Verify WER ~1.93% (test-clean) and CER ~1.44% (AISHELL-1) are matched, confirming core components work.  
  2. **Streaming Sanity Check:** After stage-5 joint training, run streaming inference with 1000ms chunks using greedy decoding *without* fallback. Measure WER/CER. Expect it to be worse than reported streaming numbers, establishing the fallback's necessity.  
  3. **Component Ablation:** In stage-5, ablate the context-aware (CS) paradigm (use only NS and SS). Evaluate streaming performance with fallback decoding across 1000, 640, 320ms chunks. Expect degraded performance, especially at shorter chunks, confirming CS's role.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Optimal interleaving ratio (N:M):** What is the best speech:text token ratio in streaming training, and does it vary across languages or datasets? The paper uses a fixed 2:1 without exploration.
- **Scaling with LLM size:** How does accuracy and latency scale with the parameter size of the underlying LLM decoder? The study only uses Qwen3-1.7B.
- **Robustness to forced alignment errors:** How sensitive is the context-aware streaming paradigm to errors in the external forced alignment tool? The paper assumes perfect alignment.
- **Computational overhead of fallback:** What is the actual FLOPs, memory, and latency overhead of the latest-token fallback strategy relative to greedy and beam search, especially under short chunks? The claim of "negligible" is unquantified.

## Limitations
- Exact pre-trained model weights (Qwen3-1.7B, FireRedASR-AED), tokenization details, and the implementation of "dynamic chunk attention" are not publicly specified, hindering exact replication.
- The joint training paradigm's effectiveness depends heavily on high-quality forced alignment; boundary errors could corrupt streaming sequences and degrade performance.
- The fallback decoding's latency guarantee assumes the per-chunk prefill computation fits within the chunk duration, which may not hold for very tight budgets or larger models.
- The fixed 2:1 speech:text token ratio may not generalize to languages with different tokenization densities or acoustic conditions.

## Confidence
- **Unified architecture without architectural changes:** Medium confidence. Feasibility is demonstrated, but success depends on precise data formatting and training schedule with risk of mode interference.
- **Context-aware training and fallback decoding for low-latency streaming:** Medium confidence. Ablations support efficacy, but reliance on accurate forced alignment and unmeasured latency trade-offs require further validation.
- **Competitive non-streaming performance:** High confidence. Reported results (1.93% WER, 2.60% CER) align with strong AED-LLM baselines and are measured on standard benchmarks.
