# MedClarify: An information-seeking AI agent for medical diagnosis with case-specific follow-up questions
## Quick Facts
- ArXiv ID: 2602.17308
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17308
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Reduced diagnostic errors by ~27 percentage points vs single-shot LLM baseline and improved top-1 accuracy by 5.7 percentage points vs naive multi-turn baseline; endocrinology gains reached +36.7 percentage points.

## Executive Summary
MedClarify is an AI agent designed to improve medical diagnosis by actively seeking missing information through targeted follow‑up questions. Unlike standard LLMs that produce a differential from a static case description, MedClarify iteratively refines its diagnostic belief state by selecting the question expected to maximally reduce uncertainty. It computes a Diagnostic Expected Information Gain (DEIG) metric that simulates patient responses, uses ICD-11 semantic similarity to weight the clinical relevance of ruling out disease branches, and updates probabilities via Bayes’ rule. Evaluated on 469 masked cases across three datasets, MedClarify cut diagnostic errors by ~27 percentage points compared to a single‑shot baseline and showed strong specialty‑specific improvements, especially in endocrinology. The work demonstrates the value of explicit information‑seeking loops for diagnostic AI.

## Method Summary
MedClarify operates as a multi‑turn information‑seeking agent. Given an initial patient case, it first generates a ranked differential diagnosis list. In each turn, it creates a set of candidate follow‑up questions—both discriminative (between the top diagnosis and each lower one) and exploratory (for diseases not yet considered). For every candidate, the system simulates two hypothetical patient answers (“yes” and “no”) and asks the LLM to produce revised differential diagnoses for each outcome. The Diagnostic Expected Information Gain (DEIG) quantifies the expected reduction in the entropy of the diagnosis distribution, enhanced by a divergence term that accounts for semantic similarity between ICD‑11 chapters of the simulated diagnosis sets; this encourages questions that eliminate entire disease branches. The question with the highest DEIG is posed to the (simulated or real) patient. Upon receiving the real answer, MedClarify updates the prior diagnostic distribution using Bayes’ rule, where the likelihood P(e|d) is the LLM’s estimated compatibility of the evidence with each diagnosis, followed by temperature scaling to smooth the distribution. The case text is updated with the Q&A pair, and the cycle repeats until a confidence threshold or turn limit is reached.

## Key Results
- Reduced diagnostic errors by approximately 27 percentage points compared to a standard single‑shot LLM baseline.
- Improved top‑1 accuracy by 5.7 percentage points over a naïve multi‑turn baseline.
- Achieved a +36.7 percentage point gain in endocrinology cases, with notable improvements across other specialties.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Uncertainty Reduction via DEIG
MedClarify selects follow‑up questions by computing the Diagnostic Expected Information Gain (DEIG). For each candidate, it simulates two possible patient responses and obtains two differential diagnoses (`Diag+` and `Diag-`). DEIG measures the expected reduction in the entropy of the diagnosis distribution, modified by a divergence term that weights semantic similarity between ICD‑11 chapters of the two simulated sets. This design favors questions that shift probability between clinically unrelated disease branches (e.g., ruling out all pneumonias) rather than those distinguishing similar sub‑types. *Core assumption*: the LLM can generate plausible, meaningfully divergent differentials for the hypothetical outcomes. *Break condition*: if simulated differentials are nonsensical or nearly identical, DEIG becomes uninformative and question selection degrades.

### Coherent Belief Updating via Bayes
After receiving a real patient answer, MedClarify updates its diagnostic belief state using Bayes’ rule: posterior P(d|e) ∝ likelihood P(e|d) × prior P(d). The likelihood estimates how compatible the evidence `e` is with each diagnosis `d`, as judged by the LLM. Temperature scaling smooths the posterior to avoid over‑confidence in a single diagnosis. This Bayesian layer integrates new information while preserving the reasoning trace across turns. *Core assumption*: the LLM provides well‑calibrated, rank‑preserving likelihood scores. *Break condition*: degenerate likelihoods (e.g., similar scores for all diagnoses) or poorly calibrated priors cause the update to add noise rather than sharpen the distribution.

### ICD‑11 Relatedness as a Discriminativity Proxy
Each candidate diagnosis is mapped to its ICD‑11 code and then to its chapter (e.g., “Diseases of the digestive system”). A pre‑computed LLM‑generated similarity matrix defines the semantic proximity between chapters. In the DEIG divergence term, the similarity between the chapter sets of `Diag+` and `Diag-` penalizes questions that only separate closely related chapters while boosting those that separate distinct ones. This heuristic aims to mimic clinical reasoning where ruling out a whole class of diseases (e.g., cardiac vs. pulmonary) is more valuable than ruling out a specific subtype. *Core assumption*: chapter‑level similarity is a valid proxy for clinical discriminability in history‑taking. *Break condition*: if the ICD mapping fails or the similarity matrix misrepresents true clinical relationships, the divergence term misweights question informativeness.

## Foundational Learning
### Shannon Entropy & Information Gain
**Why needed:** DEIG is grounded in information theory; entropy measures uncertainty in the diagnosis distribution, and expected information gain predicts how much a question will reduce that uncertainty.  
**Quick check:** *If a question’s “yes” and “no” answers both lead to differentials with high, evenly spread probabilities, what would you expect the information gain to be? Why?* (Answer: Low, because entropy remains high in both branches, yielding little expected reduction.)

### Bayesian Belief Updating
**Why needed:** The iterative diagnostic loop requires a mathematically sound way to combine prior diagnostic confidence with new evidence without discarding previous reasoning; Bayes’ rule provides this coherence.  
**Quick check:** *In the formula P(d|e) ∝ P(e|d) × P(d), if evidence e is very common under diagnosis d (high likelihood) but also very common under many other diagnoses, what is the likely effect on the posterior P(d|e)?* (Answer: The posterior will not increase much because the likelihood ratio relative to other diagnoses is small; the evidence is non‑discriminative.)

### ICD‑11 Hierarchical Structure
**Why needed:** The DEIG divergence term uses ICD‑11 chapter similarity to approximate clinical relatedness; questions that separate diseases from different chapters are assumed to be more diagnostically powerful.  
**Quick check:** *Why might a question that distinguishes between Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (Chapter 5) and Hyperthyroidism (Chapter 5) be less valuable for reducing overall diagnostic uncertainty than a question distinguishing Type 1 Diabetes (Chapter 5) from Deep Vein Thrombosis (Chapter 6)?* (Answer: Because both endocrine conditions belong to the same chapter and share many systemic symptoms, ruling out one may still leave many endocrine possibilities; separating across chapters eliminates a whole branch of the differential, reducing uncertainty more broadly.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text sequential flow):**  
Initial Case → Differential Diagnosis Generator → Candidate Question Generator → DEIG Selector → Selected Question → Patient Response → Bayesian Updater → Case Updater → (loop back to Differential Diagnosis Generator unless termination criteria met) → Final Differential Output.

**Critical path:** The core loop comprises generating a differential, creating candidate questions, evaluating them with DEIG (2 simulations per candidate), selecting the best question, obtaining the patient’s answer, and updating the belief state via Bayes. The DEIG evaluation step creates a branching factor of 2k simulations (k = number of candidates) per turn, leading to significant LLM calls.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Computational cost*: DEIG requires 2k+1 LLM calls per turn (k for candidate generation, 2k for simulations, 1 for selection). This is expensive but traded for diagnostic accuracy.
- *Exploration vs. exploitation*: The DEIG score combines information gain (IG), divergence (Div), and concentration (Con) with weights (α=0.5, β=0.35, γ=0.15). Over‑emphasizing Con encourages broad but potentially inefficient questioning; under‑emphasizing it risks missing the correct diagnostic branch.
- *LLM as oracle*: The entire system assumes the LLM can reliably simulate diagnostic shifts and assess evidence compatibility. A weak backbone propagates errors throughout the pipeline.

**Failure signatures:**
- Entropy remains high across turns without decreasing, indicating non‑discriminative questions.
- Repeated or irrelevant questions suggest the question generator ignores history or the Div term fails to penalize already‑ruled‑out paths.
- Large, unexplained swings in top‑diagnosis confidence point to unstable Bayesian updates.
- Noticeable performance drops in specialties relying on visual/exam findings (e.g., dermatology, neurology) reveal the text‑only limitation.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Single‑feature masking: Systematically remove one category of clinical information (e.g., lab tests) to assess how accuracy degrades and how much MedClarify recovers.
2. All‑features masking: Remove all features except demographics and primary symptom; compare MedClarify’s top‑1 accuracy and question efficiency against a naïve multi‑turn baseline.
3. Cross‑specialty & cross‑model analysis: Evaluate MedClarify on cases stratified by medical specialty (e.g., endocrinology vs. dermatology) and with different LLM backbones (GPT‑5.1, Deepseek‑R1‑0528, Llama) to test robustness and identify domain‑specific weaknesses.

## Limitations
- **LLM simulation fidelity** – The DEIG mechanism depends on the LLM’s ability to generate realistic, divergent simulated differentials; no evidence confirms that simulated shifts match real clinician‑observed shifts.
- **ICD‑11 semantic proxy validity** – Using chapter‑level similarity is an unverified heuristic; a within‑chapter question may be highly discriminative, while a cross‑chapter question may be irrelevant in practice.
- **Evaluation environment gap** – Gains are demonstrated on artificially masked text cases; real clinical encounters involve multimodal data (images, physical exam) and the system underperforms in visual specialties, limiting real‑world applicability.

## Confidence
- Overall performance claim (27 p.p. reduction): **High**
- DEIG mechanism efficacy: **Medium**
- Bayesian updating coherence: **Low**
- ICD‑11 chapter similarity utility: **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Simulated Differential Human Evaluation** – Clinicians judge the plausibility and diagnostic divergence of the LLM‑generated `Diag+` and `Diag-` lists for a sample of questions, validating the core DEIG assumption.
2. **Likelihood Calibration Study** – Compare the LLM’s `P(e|d)` scores against frequencies derived from real patient data (e.g., symptom prevalence in confirmed diagnoses) to assess calibration and rank correlation.
3. **ICD Similarity vs. Expert Discriminants** – Collect expert‑rated discriminative power for disease‑pair questions and measure correlation with the paper’s chapter‑based similarity scores to test the heuristic’s clinical validity.
