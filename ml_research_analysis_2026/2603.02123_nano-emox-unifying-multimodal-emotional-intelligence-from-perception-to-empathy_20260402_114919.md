# Nano-EmoX: Unifying Multimodal Emotional Intelligence from Perception to Empathy

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.02123
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02123
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieved state-of-the-art or highly competitive performance on six multimodal affective tasks with a compact 2.2B parameter model.

## Executive Summary
We present Nano-EmoX, a compact multimodal language model designed to unify six core affective tasks across a cognitive hierarchy—from low-level perception to high-level empathetic interaction. The work addresses the fragmentation in existing affective MLMs, where tasks are siloed by cognitive depth, by proposing a three-level hierarchy (perception, understanding, interaction) that guides both architecture and training. Nano-EmoX integrates frozen omni-modal encoders (CLIP-ViT, HuBERT, FaceXFormer) with lightweight heterogeneous adapters and a hierarchical fusion encoder, and is trained via a Perception-to-Empathy (P2E) curriculum that progresses from modality alignment to multitask instruction tuning. Despite its modest 2.2B parameters, the model matches or surpasses larger counterparts on multiple benchmarks, including MER-UniBench (weighted F1 74.01), EMER (label overlap 5.78), MIntRec (accuracy 58.20), and AvaMERG (hit rate 91.13), demonstrating the efficiency of its design and the generalizability afforded by the hierarchical framework.

## Method Summary
Nano-EmoX builds upon the Qwen2.5-1.5B language model. Visual input is encoded by a frozen CLIP-ViT-Large, audio by HuBERT-Large, and facial expressions by an enhanced FaceXFormer that processes frame sequences with temporal modeling blocks. Each modality’s features are passed through a two-layer Q-Former adapter that resamples and projects them into the language model’s hidden space (64 tokens for vision, 32 for speech, 4 for face). A hierarchical fusion encoder then combines the audio and visual modality tokens via three experts operating on features extracted from specific layers of the speech and vision backbones; a gating network dynamically weights the experts’ outputs to produce a single fused token. During training, the P2E curriculum unfolds in three phases: (1) modality alignment—adapters are trained on unimodal tasks (FERV39K/CAER for vision/face, CREMA-D/M3ED for speech); (2) cross‑modal fusion pre‑training—fusion encoder and adapters are trained on the MIntRec intent recognition task; (3) multitask instruction tuning—LoRA is applied to the LM, and all components are trained together on a mixture of six affective tasks (MER, OV‑MER, MIR, ERI, ERG) with a target ratio of 18:28:5:31:18. AdamW optimizer is used throughout.

## Key Results
- Weighted F1-score of **74.01** on MER‑UniBench (covering MSA, MER, OV‑MER)
- Emotion Wheel Hit Rate **5.78** on EMER (ERI)
- Accuracy **58.20** on MIntRec (MIR)
- Hit rate **91.13** on AvaMERG (ERG)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Nano-EmoX’s effectiveness stems from the synergistic interplay of several design choices. The three‑level cognitive hierarchy imposes a structure that mirrors human emotional processing: perception extracts raw emotional cues, understanding interprets multimodal contexts, and interaction generates empathetic responses. This structure naturally guides task selection and fusion, ensuring that lower-level features are robustly encoded before higher-level reasoning. The P2E curriculum reinforces this by first aligning each modality in isolation, then learning cross‑modal interactions on a simpler task (intent recognition), and finally fine‑tuning on the full suite. This gradual progression prevents catastrophic interference and allows the model to build increasingly abstract representations. The adapter‑based design keeps the majority of the pretrained encoders frozen, preserving their rich feature extraction capabilities while introducing only minimal trainable parameters. The hierarchical fusion encoder with dynamic gating further enhances performance by allowing the model to weight expert outputs according to the demands of each task and sample, rather than relying on a fixed fusion strategy. Finally, LoRA on the LM enables efficient instruction tuning for the diverse output formats required by the six tasks without overwriting the LM’s general knowledge. Together, these mechanisms yield strong generalization from a compact model.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input (Video Frames, Audio Spectrogram, Facial Crop, Optional Text) → CLIP‑ViT‑Large / HuBERT‑Large / FaceXFormer → Q‑Former Adapters (per modality) → Hierarchical Fusion Encoder (3 experts + gating) → Qwen2.5‑1.5B LM (with LoRA) → Task‑specific Outputs

### Critical Path
1. Extract modality features using frozen encoders.  
2. Apply Q‑Former adapters to obtain fixed‑length token sequences (vision=64, speech=32, face=4).  
3. Fuse speech and visual tokens (and optionally face) via hierarchical fusion encoder to produce a single fused token.  
4. Concatenate modality tokens and fused token with an instructional prompt token.  
5. Process the combined sequence through the LM (with LoRA) and generate the answer.  

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Frozen encoders vs. full fine‑tuning**: Freezing preserves pretrained knowledge and drastically reduces trainable parameters, but may limit adaptation to new affective domains.  
- **Hierarchical fusion vs. simple fusion**: The expert‑gating design offers task‑adaptive fusion at the cost of additional parameters and potential training instability; simpler attention‑based fusion might be easier but less effective.  
- **Token budget**: Short token lengths (e.g., 32 for speech) keep memory low but risk losing fine‑grained emotional details; increasing tokens improves performance (as shown in ablation) but hurts efficiency.  
- **Curriculum vs. multitask**: A carefully staged curriculum yields better generalization, yet requires more hyperparameter tuning and longer total training time than simultaneous multitask learning.  

### Failure Signatures
- **Dimension mismatch**: Crashes when concatenating tokens due to adapter outputs not matching LM hidden size.  
- **Uniform gating**: G1≈G2≈G3≈0.33, indicating the gating network fails to learn meaningful routing; likely harms performance.  
- **Curriculum collapse**: After Phase 2 or 3, performance on earlier tasks drops sharply (catastrophic forgetting).  
- **Coherence loss in ERG**: Generated responses lack the required `<think>` structure or contain repetitive/illogical content.  

### First Experiments
1. **Fusion encoder sanity**: Initialize the fusion module, feed random feature tensors, and confirm that the gating network produces a non‑uniform distribution (e.g., entropy > 0.5 over 100 samples).  
2. **Phase‑1 learning**: Train adapters on a tiny subset (<2% of FERV39K+CAER) for 500 steps; verify that the loss decreases by >20% and that the facial adapter achieves >0% accuracy on a held‑out mini‑batch (vs. zero‑shot).  
3. **Benchmark replication**: After full P2E training, evaluate on MER‑UniBench; the weighted F1 should be within ±0.5 of the reported 74.01 to account for random seed variance.

## Limitations
- **Incomplete specifications**: The enhanced FaceXFormer’s temporal modeling blocks and the internal FFN structure of fusion experts are not fully described, hindering exact replication.
- **Missing training hyperparameters**: Key details such as per‑phase batch size, learning‑rate scheduler, dropout rates, and precise task‑mixture formulation are omitted.
- **Inference‑time ambiguities**: The token resampling strategy for Q‑Former outputs and the decoding settings (beam size, sampling) for generation tasks are not provided.
- **No official code**: The absence of a public repository prevents direct verification of implementations and reported numbers.
