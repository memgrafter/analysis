# Same Error, Different Function: The Optimizer as an Implicit Prior in Financial Time Series

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.02620  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02620  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Different architecture-optimizer pairs achieve statistically indistinguishable out-of-sample error (NMSE ~0.575), yet optimizer choice induces functional divergence that impacts trading implementability.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates neural network underspecification in financial time series forecasting, specifically next‑day volatility prediction for S&P 500 stocks. Despite identical test errors (NMSE ≈ 0.575) across four architectures and three optimizers, the authors find that the optimizer acts as an implicit prior, shaping the learned function’s structure. Using impulse‑response and SHAP analyses, they show that SGD yields simpler, flatter response surfaces, while adaptive optimizers (Adam, Muon) produce more complex, nonlinear functions with longer temporal dependencies. These invisible differences manifest in downstream trading: volatility‑ranked portfolios built from adaptive optimizers exhibit up to 3× higher turnover compared to SGD at similar Sharpe ratios, revealing a near‑vertical Sharpe‑turnover frontier. The study underscores that when leaderboards tie, the optimizer selects which admissible function is deployed, with real implications for strategy implementability.

## Method Summary
The authors use CRSP data for S&P 500 constituents from 2000 to 2024 to construct a dataset predicting next‑day log‑variance from the past 100 days of Garman‑Klass volatility. After winsorization and standardization (using training statistics only), the data are split chronologically into 60% train, 20% validation, and 20% test. Four neural architectures (MLP, CNN, LSTM, Transformer) are trained with three optimizers (SGD, Adam, Muon). Hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay) are tuned per architecture‑optimizer pair via Optuna on validation NMSE. Final models are retrained on combined train+validation data with early stopping (patience 10, max 50 epochs, batch 512) across 13 random seeds. Functional diagnostics include impulse response (perturbing one lag), SHAP lag importance, and difference surfaces between optimizer pairs. Downstream evaluation constructs daily rebalanced, equal‑weight portfolios sorted into volatility quintiles, measuring turnover and annualized Sharpe ratio.

## Key Results
- All 12 architecture‑optimizer models achieve near‑identical test NMSE (~0.575), confirming underspecification.
- Optimizer choice drives functional divergence: SGD produces flatter, simpler response surfaces; Adam and Muon learn more nonlinear functions with longer lag dependencies (as shown by impulse responses and SHAP).
- In volatility‑sorted portfolios, adaptive optimizers yield up to 3× higher turnover than SGD at comparable Sharpe ratios, indicating a stark Sharpe‑turnover trade‑off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The optimizer’s update geometry induces implicit bias toward different degrees of solution sharpness, which correlates with functional complexity in low‑signal volatility forecasting.
- **Mechanism:** SGD’s isotropic steps under fixed step size operate near the Edge of Stability, where the constraint λ_max ≈ 2/η penalizes high curvature and thus favors flatter minima. Adaptive optimizers (Adam, Muon) precondition the gradient, effectively raising the stability boundary and allowing convergence into sharper regions of the loss landscape.
- **Core assumption:** Sharpness (maximum Hessian eigenvalue) at convergence predicts qualitative features of the learned function such as nonlinear impulse responses and lag importance patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “SGD produces simpler, flatter response surfaces while adaptive optimizers (Adam, Muon) learn more complex, nonlinear functions.”  
  - [Section 4.3] Adam converges to solutions with λ_max 2–5× larger than SGD (MLP: 111.5 vs 63.1; CNN: 239.9 vs 51.5). Intervention experiments (Figure 6) show that swapping from Adam to SGD collapses the response toward the simpler SGD attractor.  
  - [corpus] “Implicit Bias of Mirror Flow for Shallow Neural Networks in Univariate Regression” (arXiv:2410.03988) and “Systematic Investigation of Sparse Perturbed Sharpness‑Aware Minimization Optimizer” (arXiv:2306.17504) provide related evidence linking optimizer choice to curvature bias.
- **Break condition:** If all optimizers yielded solutions with statistically indistinguishable sharpness, the mechanism could not explain the observed functional divergence.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The training dynamics are constrained by the Edge of (Stochastic) Stability, and this constraint acts as an implicit prior that selects different points from the Rashomon set.
- **Mechanism:** During training, the optimizer’s step size interacts with local curvature; when the maximum eigenvalue approaches 2/η, progress becomes stability‑limited. The effective stability boundary differs between optimizers due to preconditioning (adaptive methods exhibit an Adaptive Edge of Stability). As a result, each optimizer is confined to a distinct region of the loss surface, even though all regions yield similar empirical risk.
- **Core assumption:** The loss landscape in financial volatility forecasting contains many near‑optimal minima with varying functional properties; the optimizer’s stability constraint determines which subset is reachable under a given training budget.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “When leaderboards tie, the optimizer selects which admissible function is deployed.”  
  - [Section 4.3] The authors estimate the stability horizon for the full dataset to be ∼130,000 steps using a scaling law (Equation 6, Figure A12). The models trained for 50 epochs exceed this horizon, so training operates in the EoS regime.  
  - [Section E.1] The “central flow” viewpoint frames EoS as a constraint that shapes the effective hypothesis class explored.  
  - [corpus] Andreyev & Beneventano (2025) “Edge of Stochastic Stability” (arXiv:2412.20553) provides a framework linking batch sharpness to instability events.
- **Break condition:** If all optimizers exhibited the same effective stability threshold (e.g., after rescaling step sizes), they would converge to similar functions.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The optimizer directly shapes the temporal dependence structure of the learned predictor, leading to differences in lag importance and receptive field that persist even when aggregate error is equal.
- **Mechanism:** Because the optimization trajectory selects different regions of parameter space, the internal representations (e.g., hidden states in an LSTM, attention weights in a Transformer) end up emphasizing different lags. SHAP analysis shows that SGD concentrates its explanation weight on the most recent lags, while Adam and Muon incorporate longer‑term patterns (e.g., quarterly cycles). This is not a simple scaling but a reweighting of the input‑output mapping.
- **Core assumption:** Feature attribution differences are primarily caused by optimization dynamics rather than architectural constraints; holding architecture fixed isolates the optimizer’s effect.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “Impulse‑response and SHAP analyses reveal that SGD produces simpler, flatter response surfaces while adaptive optimizers learn more complex, nonlinear functions with distinct temporal dependencies.”  
  - [Section 4.2] For LSTM models, Muon shows strong SHAP values at distant lags (∼t–60), Adam focuses on recent lags, and SGD restricts almost entirely to the last few days (Figure 4b). For CNNs the pattern is similar but less pronounced (Figure 4a).  
  - [Section 4.1] Difference surfaces D(x)=ˆy_Muon(x)−ˆy_Adam(x) are non‑planar across all architectures (Figure 3), confirming structural divergence in the input‑output mapping.
- **Break condition:** If SHAP values and impulse responses were nearly identical across optimizers within an architecture, the mechanism would not explain functional divergence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Underspecification & Rashomon Effect**  
  - *Why needed here:* The study operates in a low‑signal regime where many predictors achieve statistically indistinguishable test error. Recognizing this underspecification is crucial because it means the usual leaderboard metrics cannot uniquely select a model.  
  - *Quick check:* If two models have validation loss within 0.001, are their predictions on new data interchangeable?
- **Implicit Bias of Optimization**  
  - *Why needed here:* The optimizer is not neutral; it imposes an inductive bias that steers training toward certain regions (e.g., flatness) even when the scalar loss is the same. This explains why SGD and Adam can produce qualitatively different functions.  
  - *Quick check:* Does changing only the optimizer (keeping architecture, data, and loss fixed) alter the model’s feature importance or response to input perturbations?
- **Edge of Stability (EoS)**  
  - *Why needed here:* Training often proceeds at the boundary where the step size barely avoids divergence. This “edge” acts as a constraint that limits how sharp a solution can become, and this constraint differs between optimizers, yielding distinct inductive biases.  
  - *Quick check:* During training, does the maximum Hessian eigenvalue approach 2/η? If so, does crossing that boundary correlate with changes in model behavior?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  - Data layer: CRSP download → cleaning (winsorization, standardization) → fixed train/val/test splits.  
  - Model layer: PyTorch implementations of MLP, CNN, LSTM, Transformer.  
  - Training layer: Optuna hyperparameter search for learning rate and weight decay per (architecture, optimizer) pair; early stopping; final retraining on combined train+val.  
  - Diagnostics layer: impulse response surfaces, SHAP (DeepExplainer or permutation), difference surfaces, ensemble construction.  
  - Downstream layer: volatility‑ranked equal‑weight portfolios, daily turnover and Sharpe calculation.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Construct dataset (Appendix B).  
  2. For each (arch, opt) pair: run Optuna to select lr, wd → train final model on combined set.  
  3. Compute test NMSE to confirm equivalence.  
  4. Run functional diagnostics (impulse, SHAP, difference) on the median‑seed model.  
  5. Build volatility‑ranked portfolios and summarize turnover/Sharpe.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Batch size:** Larger batches reduce noise but may weaken SGD’s implicit regularization; paper used 512.  
  - **Hyperparameter ranges:** Must be wide enough to capture optimizer‑specific sweet spots (e.g., Muon needs higher weight decay).  
  - **Diagnostic cost:** SHAP and impulse responses are expensive; use a single representative model per condition.  
  - **Reproducibility vs exploration:** Fixed seeds aid debugging but Monte Carlo across 13 seeds is needed to assess variability.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Test NMSE varies widely across optimizers (>0.01 gaps) → data preprocessing or hyperparameter tuning not followed correctly.  
  - SHAP plots show similar lag patterns across optimizers → either model not trained long enough or diagnostic mis‑implemented.  
  - Difference surfaces appear planar → optimizer swap not executed properly or model not at EoS regime.  
  - Portfolio turnover nearly identical across optimizers → downstream pipeline mis‑aligned (e.g., using predictions directly instead of volatility ranking).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **NMSE parity check:** Replicate Table 1. Train all 12 models with 13 seeds, report mean ± std. If means differ by >0.001 from the paper, investigate data splits or regularization.  
  2. **Impulse response divergence:** For the CNN with Adam, Muon, SGD, plot R(k, δ) for k = t−1 over δ∈[−4,4]. Verify that Adam/Muon show a sigmoidal dampening while SGD is nearly linear.  
  3. **Optimizer swap intervention:** Train an Adam model to convergence, then re‑initialize the optimizer with SGD (zero momentum) and run additional epochs. Compare the impulse response before and after swap; it should collapse to SGD’s simple attractor (Figure 6).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Under what conditions are metrically equivalent predictors functionally interchangeable in financial decision‑making?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors pose "Question 1: Are models with identical test loss actually interchangeable?" and demonstrate via impulse‑response and SHAP analyses that optimizer‑induced functions differ despite equal NMSE, leading to diverging portfolio turnover.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper establishes non‑interchangeability in volatility forecasting but leaves open the boundary conditions—for instance, whether some downstream tasks (e.g., linear allocation rules) or market regimes show functional equivalence, or if interchangeability scales with signal strength.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic study across financial tasks (volatility, returns, credit risk), decision rules (ranking, thresholding, nonlinear weighting), and signal‑to‑noise environments (high/low volatility periods, asset classes) to map where test‑loss ties imply behavioral divergence.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the Edge of Stochastic Stability (EOSS) wholly explain optimizer‑induced functional divergence in underspecified settings?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 4.3 conjectures that EOSS constraints implicitly bias solutions (linking sharpness to function complexity), supported by curvature traces and optimizer‑intervention experiments, but states "we do not claim causality" and "further work is needed."
- **Why unresolved:** The correlation between sharpness and functional complexity is suggestive but not definitive; it remains unclear whether EOSS is necessary or sufficient, or merely a correlate of deeper geometric biases. The role of adaptive optimizers’ preconditioning is also not fully disentangled.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments that decouple sharpness from optimizer choice (e.g., applying sharpness‑aware minimization to SGD‑induced solutions) or directly perturb the stability boundary (via learning‑rate scheduling, batch‑size modulation) while measuring functional divergence.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How large and structured is the Rashomon set of near‑optimal predictors in financial time series?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors invoke Breiman’s Rashomon effect and underspecification, and show via heterogeneous ensembling that errors are not perfectly correlated, implying multiple distinct functions within the same error tolerance. However, geometric characterization of the set is absent.
- **Why unresolved:** The ensemble gain indicates complementarity, but the volume, shape (e.g., connected vs. disjoint basins), and optimizer‑dependent navigation of the set remain unknown. This limits our ability to understand the scope of functional multiplicity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing sampling or approximation methods for the Rashomon set (e.g., extensive hyperparameter sweeps, Nash equilibrium search, or set‑covering algorithms) and quantifying diversity via prediction disagreement, SHAP variance, or functional distance metrics across the set.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can ensemble methods over optimizer‑induced predictors reduce downstream decision instability (e.g., turnover) without sacrificing predictive accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4.4 shows that averaging across optimizer‑induced solutions improves NMSE, but the paper does not test whether ensembles also align volatility rankings or lower turnover in portfolio construction.
- **Why unresolved:** Ensembling may average out conflicting signals, smoothing rankings and reducing turnover, or it may combine divergent forecasts that increase rank churn. The trade‑off between error reduction and behavioral stability is unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Constructing ensembles (simple or weighted) from models trained with different optimizers and evaluating both NMSE and turnover in volatility‑sorted portfolios. Analyzing whether ensembles can target stability directly (e.g., via variance penalization) while maintaining accuracy.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** To what extent do optimizer‑induced functional differences generalize across diverse financial prediction tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study isolates volatility forecasting for S&P 500 stocks. The authors note they "do not claim causality, nor do we assert that this phenomenon generalizes beyond the present task," acknowledging potential task‑specificity.
- **Why unresolved:** Volatility’s strong autocorrelation and clustering may interact uniquely with optimizer bias. Other tasks (e.g., return prediction, default risk) have different signal structures (e.g., near‑zero autocorrelation, binary outcomes) that could alter the functional divergence pattern.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the architecture‑optimizer comparison on other financial datasets (e.g., equity returns, bond spreads, crypto volatility) with varied frequencies and target definitions, measuring whether functional divergence (impulse‑response differences, SHAP profiles) and decision‑level consequences (turnover, Sharpe dispersion) persist.

## Limitations
- Train/val/test split dates are unspecified; different splits could shift NMSE values slightly.
- Muon optimizer implementation details (e.g., β parameters, weight‑decay handling) are ambiguous, risking reimplementation divergence.
- Portfolio rebalancing mechanics are unclear, so turnover and Sharpe estimates may vary.
- Generalizability is limited to S&P 500 volatility forecasting; other asset classes and tasks remain untested.
