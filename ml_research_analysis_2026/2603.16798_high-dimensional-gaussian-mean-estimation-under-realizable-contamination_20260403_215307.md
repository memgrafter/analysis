# High-Dimensional Gaussian Mean Estimation under Realizable Contamination

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.16798
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16798
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Establishes an information–computation gap for Gaussian mean estimation under realizable ε‑contamination and gives a polynomial‑time algorithm (up to a doubly‑exponential factor) with sample complexity \(n = O((kd)^{O(k)}/\varepsilon^2)\) and accuracy \(\delta\).

## Executive Summary
This paper studies the problem of estimating the mean of a high-dimensional spherical Gaussian in the realizable ε‑contamination model, where the observed distribution is a mixture: with probability \(1-\varepsilon\) it is the clean Gaussian, and with probability \(\varepsilon\) it is an arbitrary distribution that may depend on the sample values. Even for \(\varepsilon \ge 1/2\), consistent estimation remains possible under this realizable setting.  
The authors' contributions are twofold. First, they prove an information‑computation gap in the Statistical Query (SQ) model: any SQ algorithm must either make an exponential number of queries or use at least one query with tolerance \(d^{-\Omega(m)}\), where \(m = \Theta((1/\delta \log(1+\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)))^2\). This implies that either the runtime or the sample complexity must be super‑polynomial in the dimension \(d\). Second, they present a polynomial‑time algorithm (modulo a dimension‑independent \(\exp(\exp(O(k))/\varepsilon^2)\) factor) with sample complexity \(n = O((kd)^{O(k)}/\varepsilon^2)\) that achieves error \(\delta\), matching the SQ lower bound up to polylog factors. The techniques extend to Low‑Degree Polynomials and Polynomially‑Thresholded Functions, offering a comprehensive picture of computational hardness in this model.

## Method Summary
The algorithm begins with a warm‑start: a list‑decodable estimator under strong ε‑contamination returns a small list of candidate means, and a tournament improvement procedure selects a single candidate \(\hat{\mu}_0\) with error \(O(\sqrt{\log(1/(1-\varepsilon))})\). A fresh dataset is drawn and centered by subtracting \(\hat{\mu}_0\). For each order \(t = 1,\dots,k\) (with \(k = \Theta((1/\delta\log(1+\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)))^2\) even), the empirical Hermite tensor \(\hat{T}_t\) of the centered data is computed. Each tensor is flattened into a matrix \(M\) and its top left singular vectors with singular values above \(\eta = \Theta(\varepsilon/(k+1)^{k/2})\) define a subspace \(V_t\). The combined subspace \(V = \operatorname{span}(V_1,\dots,V_k)\) has dimension \(\widetilde{O}(k\varepsilon^{-2})\) independent of \(d\) thanks to a norm bound on the tensors. Finally, a brute‑force mean estimation is performed within \(V\) using a \(\delta\)‑net and a robust one‑dimensional estimator, producing an estimate \(\hat{\mu}_V \in V\). The final output is \(\hat{\mu} = \hat{\mu}_0 + \hat{\mu}_V\). The overall sample complexity is \((kd)^{O(k)}/\varepsilon^2\) and the runtime is polynomial in the sample size but carries an extra \(\exp(\exp(O(k))/\varepsilon^2)\) factor due to the net‑based search.

## Key Results
- **SQ lower bound**: Any SQ algorithm requires either an exponential number of queries or a query with tolerance \(d^{-\Omega(m)}\) where \(m = \Theta((1/\delta \log(1+\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)))^2\), implying super‑polynomial runtime or sample complexity \(d^{\Omega(m)}\).
- **Polynomial‑time algorithm**: There exists an algorithm with sample complexity \(n = O((kd)^{O(k)}/\varepsilon^2)\) and accuracy \(\delta\), running in time \(\exp(\exp(O(k))/\varepsilon^2) \cdot \operatorname{poly}(n,d)\).
- **Extension**: The same information‑computation gap holds for Low‑Degree Polynomials and PTF tests, and the algorithm adapts to these models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: High‑order Hermite moments separate the distributions
For a Gaussian shifted by a mean \(\mu\) with projection \(\delta\) onto some unit direction, the Hermite polynomials \(H_t\) (orthonormal under Gaussian measure) satisfy \(\mathbb{E}[H_t(\langle Z,\mu/\|\mu\|\rangle)]=0\) for odd \(t\) and grow with \(\delta\) for even \(t\). More concretely, the raw moment \(\mathbb{E}[(\delta+Z)^k]\) for \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) is amplified by binomial coefficients. For \(k \ge 3(1/\delta\log(1+2\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)))^2\) and \(\delta\) small, the ratio of this moment to the standard Gaussian moment exceeds \(1+2\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)\). Under realizable ε‑contamination, the adversary can only downweight any clean moment by at most \(1-\varepsilon\); hence the observed conditional expectation still deviates by at least \(\varepsilon\) from the clean Gaussian. Consequently, some even order \(t \le k\) yields a detectable signal. The guarantee fails if \(\delta^2 > \log(1+\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon))\) (makes \(k\) super‑polynomial) or if \(\varepsilon\) is near 1 and the log term dominates. (Proposition 4.1, Corollary 4.2)

### Mechanism 2: Bounded tensor norms yield a low‑dimensional subspace
Let \(T_t = \mathbb{E}_{P'}[H_t(x)-\mathbb{E}H_t(x)]\) be the centered \(t\)-th order Hermite tensor under the observable distribution after subtracting \(\hat{\mu}_0\). Using the warm‑start error bound \(\|\mu\| = O(\sqrt{\log(1/(1-\varepsilon))})\) and Gaussian shift bounds, one can show \(\|T_t\|_F \le \widetilde{O}(k)\) for all \(t \le k\). Flattening \(T_t\) into a matrix \(M\) of size \(d \times d^{t-1}\), the variational characterization of spectral norm implies that the number of singular values exceeding \(\eta = \Theta(\varepsilon/(k+1)^{k/2})\) is at most \(\|T_t\|_F^2/\eta^2 = \widetilde{O}(k\varepsilon^{-2})\). Hence the span of all left singular vectors across orders, \(V = \operatorname{span}_{t\le k} V_t\), has dimension at most \(\widetilde{O}(k\varepsilon^{-2})\), regardless of \(d\). (Lemma 4.7)

### Mechanism 3: Flattening captures the true direction
Directly finding a unit vector \(v\) with large \(\langle v^{\otimes t}, T_t\rangle\) is NP‑hard. Flattening provides an equivalence: \(\langle v^{\otimes t}, T_t\rangle = \langle v, M u_v\rangle\) where \(u_v\) is the flattening of \(v^{\otimes(t-1)}\) (unit norm). If \(|\langle v^{\otimes t}, T_t\rangle| > \eta\), then \(\|M^\top v\| \ge \eta\), meaning \(v\) has a non‑trivial component in the span of left singular vectors with singular value \(> \eta\). Thus the true mean offset \(\mu - \hat{\mu}_0\) lies in \(V = \operatorname{span}_{t\le k} V_t\). This reduction turns an intractable tensor problem into a polynomial‑time SVD computation combined with a brute‑force search over a low‑dimensional space. (Section 4.2)

## Foundational Learning
- **Statistical Query (SQ) model**: SQ algorithms query the expected value of bounded functions up to additive noise; used to prove computational lower bounds. *Why needed*: The SQ lower bound establishes the information‑computation gap by showing any SQ estimator must either use exponential queries or have tiny tolerance. *Quick check*: "What does the tolerance \(\tau\) of a statistical query correspond to in terms of sample complexity for a direct implementation via averaging?"
- **Hermite polynomials and tensors**: Form an orthogonal basis for \(L^2\) under the Gaussian measure; the \(t\)-th Hermite tensor encodes moments up to order \(t\). *Why needed*: The structural guarantee and norm bound rely on the properties of Hermite tensors. *Quick check*: "What is the inner product \(\langle h_i, h_j\rangle_{N(0,1)}\) between normalized probabilists’ Hermite polynomials?"
- **Tensor flattening**: A technique that reshapes a high‑order tensor into a matrix to apply singular‑value decomposition. *Why needed*: Direct tensor decomposition is NP‑hard; flattening reduces the problem to polynomial time while still preserving the relevant signal via the subspace guarantee. *Quick check*: "What is the main computational advantage of working with the flattened matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d^{t-1}}\) instead of the original \(t\)-tensor?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: List‑decodable estimator (Fact 4.3) -> Tournament improvement (Fact 4.4) -> \(\hat{\mu}_0\) -> Re‑centering of fresh samples -> Hermite tensor computation (orders \(t=1..k\)) -> Flattening & SVD -> Subspace aggregation \(V = \operatorname{span}\{V^1,\dots,V^k\}\) -> Brute‑force mean estimator restricted to \(V\) (via \(\delta\)‑net and 1‑D robust estimator) -> Final estimate \(\hat{\mu} = \hat{\mu}_0 + \hat{\mu}_V\).

**Critical path**: The final error depends on (i) warm‑start accuracy \(\|\hat{\mu}_0-\mu\| \le O(\sqrt{\log(1/(1-\varepsilon))})\), (ii) concentration of empirical Hermite tensors, (iii) the correctness of the structural lemma that \(\mu-\hat{\mu}_0 \in V\), and (iv) the dimension of \(V\) staying small due to the tensor norm bound. A failure in any step compromises the overall guarantee.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Choice of \(k\)*: Larger \(k\) strengthens the moment‑divergence guarantee but increases sample complexity \((kd)^{O(k)}/\varepsilon^2\) and the runtime factor \(\exp(\exp(O(k)))/\varepsilon^2\); the paper picks the minimal \(k\) satisfying the lemma.
- *Hermite vs. raw moments*: Hermite tensors have zero mean under the clean Gaussian and linear expectation under shifts, simplifying the norm analysis.
- *Flattening vs. full tensor decomposition*: Flattening yields polynomial‑time SVD; the proof shows it does not sacrifice correctness because the true direction lies in the span of top singular vectors.
- *Warm‑start algorithm*: Must handle \(\varepsilon \ge 1/2\); the tournament step introduces an additive \(\delta/2\) error but isolates a single candidate.

**Failure signatures**:
- Warm‑start error too large: if \(\|\hat{\mu}_0-\mu\| \gg \sqrt{\log(1/(1-\varepsilon))}\), the tensor norm bound may fail, causing \(\dim(V)\) to blow up.
- Insufficient samples: noisy empirical tensors may lead to a subspace \(V\) that does not contain the true offset.
- Threshold \(\eta\) mis‑set: Too small makes \(\dim(V)\) huge; too large may drop the true direction.
- Numerical overflow: For large \(k\), Hermite values can exceed floating‑point range.
- Missing \(\bot\) symbols: Forgetting to filter out missing values crashes tensor computation.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Moment divergence test**: Generate ε‑contaminated samples from \(N(\delta,1)\) and clean \(N(0,1)\). Compute empirical Hermite moments up to \(k=10\); verify that for some \(t\) the absolute difference exceeds \(\eta\).
2. **Subspace dimension scaling**: For \(d\) ranging from 10 to 50, run the algorithm and record \(\dim(V)\). Confirm it remains within \(\widetilde{O}(k\varepsilon^{-2})\) and does not grow with \(d\).
3. **End‑to‑end baseline comparison**: Benchmark the algorithm’s runtime and error against an exponential‑time cover baseline (exhaustively applying the 1‑D estimator on an exponential set of directions) for \(d \le 30\). Verify comparable accuracy and polynomial scaling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Sample‑time tradeoffs for other tasks**: What are the tradeoffs between sample and computational complexity for other fundamental statistical tasks (e.g., sparse linear regression, PCA, covariance estimation) under the realizable ε‑contamination model?
2. **Structured subclasses of subgaussian distributions**: For which structured subclasses of subgaussian (or other) distributions is consistent mean estimation achievable under realizable ε‑contamination, and what are their computational complexities? (Full generality is impossible.)
3. **Generality of the computational gap**: Does the information‑computation gap persist for all polynomial‑time algorithms, or is it an artifact of the SQ and related models? Could a non‑SQ algorithm achieve optimal sample complexity polynomially in \(d\)?
4. **Optimality of exponential factors**: Are the super‑polynomial factors in the runtime (\(\exp(\exp(O(k)))\)) and sample complexity (\(d^{O(k)}\)) of the polynomial‑time algorithm inherent, or can they be improved to polynomial in \(k\) and \(\varepsilon^{-1}\)?

## Limitations
- **Missing subroutines**: The algorithm relies on a polynomial‑time list‑decoder for strong ε‑contamination and a robust 1‑D estimator for the realizable model; these are not provided.
- **Constant factors**: Exact values of the parameter \(C\) in \(k\) and the threshold \(\eta\) are not specified, making implementation delicate.
- **Exponential complexity**: The runtime and sample complexity have doubly‑exponential or high‑order polynomial dependencies on \(k\), which may be prohibitive in practice.
