# UI-Voyager: A Self-Evolving GUI Agent Learning via Failed Experience

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.24533  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24533  
- **Reference count**: 19  
- **Primary result**: A 4B-parameter model achieves 81.0% Pass@1 on AndroidWorld, exceeding the reported human performance of 80.0%.

## Executive Summary
UI-Voyager introduces a self-evolving framework for training GUI agents that effectively learns from failures. It combines Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT), which iteratively curates successful trajectories via a rule-based verifier and fine-tunes the policy, with Group Relative Self-Distillation (GRSD), which identifies exact steps where failed and successful rollouts diverge on identical screen states and uses those to generate corrective step-level supervision. On the AndroidWorld benchmark, a 4-billion parameter model attains an 81.0% success rate, surpassing larger baselines and human performance. Ablations confirm the critical role of GRSD and the overall efficacy of the approach.

## Method Summary
The method operates in two stages. **Stage 1: Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT).** Starting from a base model, the agent generates multiple trajectories for synthesized tasks created by perturbing seed task parameters. A rule-based verifier filters only successful trajectories, which are then used for supervised fine-tuning. This closed loop repeats for several iterations, progressively improving the policy. **Stage 2: Group Relative Self-Distillation (GRSD).** Using the final RFT model, multiple rollouts are collected per task. For each failed rollout, a fork point detector (based on SSIM screen matching) finds the step where it diverged from a successful rollout that shared the same screen state. This yields a corrective sample: the failed context up to the divergence point combined with the successful action. The model is fine-tuned on a mixture of original successful trajectories and these self-corrective samples. The two stages together enable efficient learning from failures without external supervision.

## Key Results
- Pass@1 of 81.0% on AndroidWorld (vs. human 80.0%).
- Outperforms larger baselines with a 4B-parameter model.
- Ablation studies show that removing GRSD leads to a significant performance drop, confirming its importance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) for Autonomous Data Curation
- **Claim**: RFT enables continuous co-evolution of training data and model policy by iteratively generating trajectories, filtering successful ones via a verifier, and fine-tuning, leading to progressive performance gains without human annotation.
- **Mechanism**: A closed-loop pipeline: (1) the current policy generates multiple trajectories for synthesized tasks; (2) a rule-based verifier (using adb) filters only successful trajectories; (3) these high-quality traces are used for supervised fine-tuning; (4) the updated policy repeats the process. The seed task generator creates novel tasks by perturbing parameters.
- **Core assumption**: The rule-based verifier accurately distinguishes success from failure; successful trajectories are structurally correct and provide reliable supervision; task generation yields diverse yet solvable instances.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) iteratively generates, filters, and refines trajectories in a closed loop, enabling continuous co-evolution of data and models."
  - [section 3.2]: Describes Trajectory Generation, Rejection Sampling, and Iterative Training. Reports Pass@1 improvement from 37% to 73% over three iterations.
  - [corpus]: Neighboring works (e.g., MagicGUI-RMS, Self-Consolidation) explore self-evolving agents but do not use RFT with rule-based filtering; thus the specific automated loop is novel.
- **Break condition**: If the verifier is inaccurate (e.g., misses edge‑case successes or accepts failures), the training data becomes corrupted. If the seed generator produces unsolvable tasks, the policy may degrade. Performance could plateau if the policy already saturates the task distribution.

### Mechanism 2: Fork Point Detection via SSIM Matching
- **Claim**: Fork point detection pinpoints exact steps in failed trajectories where the agent observed the same screen state as in a successful trajectory but chose a divergent action, enabling precise corrective supervision.
- **Mechanism**: For a pair of successful τ⁺ and failed τ⁻ trajectories: (1) Preprocess screenshots: crop status bar, resize to thumbnail, grayscale. (2) Define SAME(oᵃ,oᵇ) = [SSIM(ϕ(oᵃ),ϕ(oᵇ)) ≥ θ]. (3) For each failed step j, find successful steps i such that SAME(o⁺ᵢ,o⁻ⱼ) holds and either (a) it is the final step or (b) next states differ (DIVERGE). (4) Enforce monotonic index to preserve temporal order. (5) Select i* with highest SSIM (and smallest index) as teacher step.
- **Core assumption**: SSIM (with preprocessing) is a reliable proxy for semantic state equivalence in GUI screens; matching states implies the action taken at that state is the key determinant of divergence.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.3.1]: Provides full algorithm (Algorithm 1) and mathematical definitions of SAME and DIVERGE.
  - [section 4.3]: Figures 5 and 6 visualize fork points in BrowserMaze and SystemBluetoothTurnOff tasks, showing identical screens with divergent actions.
  - [corpus]: Corpus shows other methods (e.g., EvoCUA) use external VLMs for fork detection; UI‑Voyager’s SSIM‑based approach is lightweight and avoids extra models.
- **Break condition**: SSIM may fail to match states that are semantically identical but visually altered by dynamic elements (e.g., animations, clocks, toasts). Temporal misalignment in live execution can also cause misses. Overly strict threshold θ yields few fork points; too low yields noisy matches.

### Mechanism 3: Group Relative Self‑Distillation (GRSD) for Step‑Level Supervision
- **Claim**: GRSD converts sparse binary rewards into dense step‑level supervision by distilling correct actions from successful rollouts at fork points into failed rollouts, thereby resolving credit assignment and enabling efficient learning from failures.
- **Mechanism**: After fork detection, for each matched pair (failed step j, teacher step i*), construct a training sample: prompt = failed context up to step j; response = successful action at i*. Train via standard next‑token loss only on response tokens. The policy learns to take the correct action at the exact critical state.
- **Core assumption**: The successful action at the matched state is optimal for that state; injecting that single correction suffices to steer the trajectory toward success; the model can integrate this without forgetting other skills.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Group Relative Self-Distillation (GRSD) detects fork points where successful and failed rollouts share screen states but diverge in actions; it then distills correct actions from successful trajectories as step-level supervision to correct the failed ones."
  - [section 3.3.2]: Equations (6) and (7) define sample construction and loss.
  - [section 4.2]: GRSD achieves 81.0% Pass@1 vs. GRPO/PPO at ~76%; Figure 8 shows faster and higher final performance.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors typically use RL (GRPO/PPO) or external reward models; GRSD’s pure self‑distillation is a distinctive alternative.
- **Break condition**: Inaccurate fork detection propagates wrong actions. If successful trajectories themselves contain suboptimal steps that pass the verifier, those may be distilled. The method assumes isolated step corrections are sufficient; highly sequential dependencies may require multi‑step adjustments.

## Foundational Learning
- **Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)**  
  Why needed here: GUI interaction is formalized as a POMDP where observations (screenshots) are partial, and history matters. This framing explains why credit assignment is hard and why RFT/GRSD focus on specific steps.  
  Quick check question: In a POMDP, how does the agent’s history context mitigate partial observability, and why does this complicate RL‑based credit assignment?

- **Reinforcement Learning (Policy Gradients, PPO, GRPO)**  
  Why needed here: The paper contrasts GRSD with standard on‑policy RL methods (GRPO/PPO) that suffer from sparse rewards. Understanding these baselines clarifies GRSD’s advantages.  
  Quick check question: Why do trajectory‑level rewards cause ambiguous credit assignment in long‑horizon GUI tasks, and how do algorithms like GRPO attempt to mitigate it?

- **Knowledge Distillation and Self‑Distillation**  
  Why needed here: GRSD is a form of self‑distillation where the agent learns from its own successful rollouts. Understanding KD principles helps analyze why distilling from peers can be effective without external teachers.  
  Quick check question: What risks arise when the teacher and student policies are identical (self‑distillation), and how does GRSD’s focus on fork points alleviate them?

- **Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)**  
  Why needed here: SSIM is the core metric for matching screen states. Knowing its strengths and weaknesses explains design choices (preprocessing, threshold).  
  Quick check question: How does SSIM differ from pixel‑wise MSE, and why might it still fail to match semantically identical GUI screens with minor visual variations?

- **Rule‑Based Verifier**  
  Why needed here: The verifier provides automatic success labels for RFT. Its reliability directly impacts data quality.  
  Quick check question: What are the limitations of a rule‑based verifier for complex GUI tasks, and how could errors in the verifier affect the RFT loop?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
- **Trajectory Generator**: Runs current policy in AndroidWorld to produce (observation, action) sequences.
- **Rule‑Based Verifier**: Uses adb commands to check app states and assign binary success.
- **Seed Task Generator**: Perturbs original task templates (e.g., time, quantity, file names) to create diverse training tasks.
- **Fork Point Detector**: Processes pairs of successful/failed trajectories; computes SSIM on preprocessed screenshots; applies SAME/DIVERGE logic; outputs matched step pairs.
- **Self‑Distillation Trainer**: Builds corrective SFT samples (failed context + successful action) and updates the policy via next‑token loss.

### Critical Path
1. Initialize base model (e.g., Qwen3‑VL‑4B‑Instruct).
2. RFT loop: generate trajectories → verify → keep successes → SFT → repeat (3 rounds).
3. After RFT, collect multiple rollouts per task; split into success/failure groups.
4. For each failed rollout, find matching successful rollout (e.g., same task instance) → run fork point detection → construct corrective samples.
5. Train policy on self‑corrective samples (possibly mixed with RFT data).
6. Evaluate on AndroidWorld.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **SSIM vs. learned visual matching**: SSIM is fast, deterministic, and requires no extra training, but may miss matches under visual noise. A learned matcher could be more robust but adds complexity and risk of overfitting.
- **Rule‑based verifier**: Low‑cost and fully reproducible, but may not capture all success criteria (e.g., subtle state changes). Manual verification of a subset is advisable.
- **Focusing only on fork points**: Efficient and targeted, but might overlook errors that accumulate without a single clear fork. Could miss cases where multiple small mistakes lead to failure.

### Failure Signatures
- **RFT**: No improvement after iteration → verify verifier correctness; check diversity of generated tasks; ensure SFT is properly configured.
- **Fork detection**: Extremely few fork points found → SSIM threshold θ too high or preprocessing too aggressive; try visual inspection of matched pairs. Many matches with no transition divergence → adjust DIVERGE condition.
- **GRSD**: Training loss on corrective samples does not decrease → likely noisy matches; increase SSIM threshold or require longer context agreement. Performance drops after GRSD → overfitting to spurious corrections; consider filtering low‑confidence matches or mixing in original successful data.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Run one RFT iteration and compare Pass@1 of the fine‑tuned model against base; expect single‑digit improvement (e.g., 37% → 45%).
2. Manually inspect fork point matches for 5–10 task pairs: do the matched screens look semantically identical? Do the divergent actions clearly affect outcome?
3. Ablate GRSD by replacing fork‑point pairs with random negative samples; training should be less effective, confirming the importance of precise matching.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Cross-platform generality**: How can UI-Voyager be effectively extended to diverse GUI platforms (e.g., iOS, web, desktop) beyond AndroidWorld? The current design is tightly coupled with AndroidWorld’s environment, action space, and verifier.
2. **Robust fork detection**: Can fork point detection be made robust to real-time asynchronous execution and transient UI dynamics while maintaining efficiency? SSIM-based matching assumes aligned frames and may fail with dynamic elements.
3. **Continuous gesture handling**: How does the limited discrete action space hinder transfer to real-world scenarios with continuous gestures, and can hierarchical action modeling mitigate this? The abstraction underestimates real-world failure modes related to gesture duration, trajectory shape, release timing, and actuation noise.
4. **Adaptive self-correction**: Can more adaptive self-correction mechanisms, triggered by uncertainty rather than solely by fork points, improve performance on complex, unseen tasks? The paper suggests exploring proactive self-correction beyond deterministic peer rollouts.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameters and implementation details (e.g., number of RFT iterations, GRSD SSIM threshold, sample counts) are unreported, hindering exact replication.
- SSIM-based matching may fail on visually dynamic elements (animations, toasts) or due to temporal misalignment, potentially missing valid fork points.
- The rule-based verifier may not capture all success criteria (e.g., subtle state changes) and could erroneously label failed trajectories as successful, corrupting the RFT loop.

## Confidence
- **High**: Claims of achieving 81.0% Pass@1 on AndroidWorld; the benchmark and metric are standard.
- **Medium**: RFT iterative loop effectiveness (reported pass improvement from 37% to 73% over three iterations); core mechanism is clear but hyperparameters are missing.
- **Medium**: GRSD fork‑point detection improving credit assignment; SSIM reliability is asserted but not stress‑tested.
- **Medium**: Surpassing human performance (80.0%); direct comparison is stated but human evaluation protocol is unclear.
