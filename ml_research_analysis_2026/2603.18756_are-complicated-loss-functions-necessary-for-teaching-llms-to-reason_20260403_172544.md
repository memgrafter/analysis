# Are complicated loss functions necessary for teaching LLMs to reason?

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.18756
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18756
- **Reference count**: 26
- **Primary result**: Removing PPO‑style clipping from GRPO yields RGRA (REINFORCE with Group Relative Advantage), a simpler algorithm that outperforms GRPO on most reasoning benchmarks and demonstrates that many complex components are unnecessary.

## Executive Summary
This paper asks which parts of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) are truly needed to teach large language models to reason. By systematically ablating GRPO—training only on positive advantages, discarding the clipping operation (producing RGRA), and using raw rewards—the authors show that negative feedback is essential to avoid reward collapse, PPO‑style clipping is redundant when using group‑relative advantages, and advantage estimation itself is critical. RGRA, a minimalist variant, surpasses GRPO on 17 of 27 tasks, suggesting that overly complicated loss functions may be unnecessary for effective reasoning post‑training.

## Method Summary
The study trains small instruction‑tuned LLMs (Qwen2.5‑0.5B/1.5B, Llama3.2‑1B) on 1,800 GSM8K problems using reinforcement learning. The authors compare four algorithms: full GRPO (with group‑relative advantage and PPO clipping), RGRA (GRPO without clipping), GRPO‑pos (only positive advantages), and REINFORCE with raw rewards. All setups use LoRA for parameter efficiency, a group size of 8, a sparse reward combining a format bonus (0.1) and a correctness indicator (1), and a KL penalty (β=0.005) to stay close to the reference model. Key ablations isolate the contributions of negative feedback, clipping, and advantage estimation. Models are evaluated on a suite of math and STEM benchmarks.

## Key Results
- Positive‑only training causes severe reward collapse, proving negative gradients are indispensable.
- RGRA (no clipping) achieves higher average scores than GRPO: e.g., Qwen2.5‑1.5B gets 38.3 vs 37.3 on English math, 69.3 vs 65.7 on Chinese math, and 50.7 vs 45.7 on STEM.
- REINFORCE with raw rewards fails catastrophically, underscoring the necessity of advantage estimation.
- Overall, RGRA outperforms GRPO on 17 of 27 evaluated tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Incorporating negative feedback is essential for stable policy learning and prevents reward collapse.
- **Mechanism**: Training exclusively on positive advantages removes corrective signals that guide the policy away from degenerate behaviors. Without negative gradients, the model exploits reward sparsity by converging to trivial, short outputs that minimally satisfy the reward function.
- **Core assumption**: A functional policy gradient requires both positive reinforcement for desirable actions and negative reinforcement to suppress undesirable ones, especially when initializing from a competent pretrained model where the baseline (group mean) is meaningful.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "training solely on actions above a baseline limits learning" and "GRPO‑pos…exhibit severe instability…reward collapse".
  - [section 4] "RAFT and positive‑only GRPO show worse performance…training collapse and response truncation."
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak; neighboring papers focus on GRPO variants, not the positive‑only ablation.
- **Break condition**: If a strong enough prior (e.g., heavy supervised fine‑tuning) already suppresses low‑reward behaviors, positive‑only training might not collapse, but this paper shows collapse even in 1.5B models, suggesting the breakpoint is not scale‑dependent here.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: PPO‑style clipping and policy ratio constraints are not necessary for effective reasoning post‑training; group‑relative advantage estimation alone provides sufficient stability.
- **Mechanism**: Group‑relative normalization (centering and scaling advantages within a sampled group) inherently bounds the gradient scale relative to the batch's reward distribution. This reduces the need for explicit trust‑region constraints like clipping, which were designed for unstable off‑policy learning in canonical RL but are redundant when starting from a strong pretrained policy and using relative advantages.
- **Core assumption**: The instability concerns that motivate PPO clipping are less severe in LLM post‑training because (a) the initial policy is high‑performing, (b) the group‑relative baseline reduces variance, and (c) the KL penalty already regularizes deviation from the reference model.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "PPO‑style constraints…are not required" and "RGRA…removes PPO‑style clipping…surpasses GRPO."
  - [section 4] "RGRA achieves the highest average performance…surpassing GRPO in 17 out of 27 individual comparisons."
  - [corpus] "Sharpness‑Guided Group Relative Policy Optimization…" and "Adaptive Group Policy Optimization…" cite GRPO's stability issues, indirectly supporting that simpler variants can work.
- **Break condition**: If the group size is very small or the reward signal is extremely noisy, clipping might be needed to prevent large policy updates; this paper uses G=8 and rule‑based rewards, so the finding may not generalize to settings with high‑reward variance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Advantage estimation (group‑relative or otherwise) is indispensable; raw reward signals alone destabilize learning even with strong initialization.
- **Mechanism**: Raw rewards lack a baseline, leading to high‑variance gradient estimates. This causes policy oscillation or collapse because updates are overly influenced by stochastic outcome noise rather than consistent performance trends. Advantage estimation reduces variance by subtracting an estimate of expected return (here, group mean), focusing learning on relative improvements.
- **Core assumption**: The variance reduction from advantage estimation is critical in the LLM setting despite the model's initial strength, because the reward landscape (from sparse correctness signals) remains rugged.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "REINFORCE with direct rewards fails, highlighting that advantage estimation is critical."
  - [section 4] "Training with direct REINFORCE on raw rewards collapses even in the larger 1.5B model."
  - [section 2.2] defines GRPO's advantage as `ri - mean(group) / std(group)`, contrasting with raw reward `ri`.
- **Break condition**: If rewards are dense, low‑variance, and well‑scaled (e.g., per‑token process rewards), raw rewards might suffice; this paper uses sparse final correctness + format rewards.

## Foundational Learning

### Policy Gradient Methods (REINFORCE)
- **Why needed**: RGRA is a variant of REINFORCE; understanding the basic policy gradient update `∇θ log π(τ)·G(τ)` is prerequisite to seeing how GRPO modifies it with group advantages and clipping.
- **Quick check**: What is the effect of using a full trajectory return `G(τ)` versus a per‑token advantage `A_t` on credit assignment?

### Advantage Estimation & Baselines
- **Why needed**: The core innovation across GRPO and RGRA is replacing a learned value function with a group‑relative baseline. This requires understanding how baselines reduce variance without biasing gradients.
- **Quick check**: Why does subtracting the group mean from each reward produce an "advantage" with zero expectation within the group?

### KL Regularization as a Trust Region
- **Why needed**: Both GRPO and RGRA include a KL penalty `βD_KL[πθ ∥ π_ref]` to prevent the policy from diverging too far from the reference model, acting as an alternative/complement to PPO's clipping.
- **Quick check**: How does a KL penalty differ from PPO's clipping in constraining policy updates? What are the trade‑offs?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Policy model (πθ) -> Reference model (π_ref) -> Group sampler -> Reward function -> Advantage calculator -> Loss composer -> Optimizer (Adam with LoRA).

### Critical path
Prompt → Sample G completions → Compute per‑token rewards → Calculate group‑relative advantages → Compute RGRA loss (no ratio/clip) → Backward pass → Update πθ.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Simplicity vs. safety**: RGRA removes clipping but relies on group‑relative normalization and KL penalty to bound updates.
- **Group size (G)**: Larger G gives better advantage estimates (lower variance) but increases memory/compute; paper uses G=8.
- **Reward shaping**: Only format + correctness rewards are used; richer rewards might change the necessity of clipping.

### Failure signatures
- **Reward collapse**: average reward and response length plummet early (seen in GRPO‑pos, REINFORCE raw, RAFT).
- **Length over‑optimization**: policy generates excessively long outputs without accuracy gains (not prominent here).
- **High gradient variance**: loss spikes or NaNs during training, likely with raw rewards or small group sizes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. Beyond mathematical reasoning, does RGRA's superiority over GRPO generalize to diverse reasoning domains (e.g., commonsense, logic, coding) and non‑reasoning tasks (e.g., dialogue, summarization)? The authors explicitly state future work will explore tasks outside the mathematical domain.

2. What is the precise mechanistic reason why PPO‑style clipping is unnecessary for reasoning‑focused LLMs when using a strong pretrained policy, and does this hold when starting from weaker base models? The paper shows RGRA (no clipping) outperforms GRPO but does not directly test varying base model strengths or provide theoretical analysis.

3. Is the KL regularization term (βD_KL) essential for the stable performance of RGRA, or could it be removed or replaced with a simpler constraint (e.g., entropy bonus) without collapse? The paper isolates clipping and advantage estimation but does not ablate the KL term itself.

4. Why does RGRA perform significantly better on Qwen2.5 models than on Llama3.2 in some benchmarks (e.g., Chinese math), and what architectural or pretraining differences cause this variance in algorithmic preference? Results show inconsistent cross‑model benefits, but the paper does not analyze the interaction between base model characteristics and RL algorithm efficacy.

5. Is the group‑relative normalization (subtracting mean, dividing by std of group rewards) the critical factor enabling stable REINFORCE‑style learning, or would alternative advantage normalization or baseline designs (e.g., learned baseline per token) work equally well? The paper shows raw rewards fail and group‑relative advantages succeed but does not isolate why this specific normalization is superior.

## Limitations
- Missing hyperparameters (PPO clipping ε, total training steps, optimizer details) impede exact replication.
- Experiments are restricted to models ≤1.5B and sparse final‑answer rewards; generalizability to larger LLMs or dense reward signals is unknown.
- Training is performed exclusively on GSM8K prompts; domain transfer is not evaluated.

## Confidence

| Claim | Confidence | Rationale |
|-------|------------|-----------|
| Negative feedback indispensable (positive‑only ablation fails) | High | Consistent reward collapse across three model families under positive‑only GRPO. |
| PPO‑style clipping unnecessary (RGRA ≥ GRPO) | Medium | RGRA outperforms GRPO on 17/27 tasks, but only for G=8 and sparse rewards; boundary conditions not explored. |
| Advantage estimation critical (raw REINFORCE fails) | High | Raw reward training collapses even at 1.5B scale. |
| RGRA as a simpler, better alternative to GRPO | Medium | Gains are modest and may not hold beyond evaluated scale/datasets; no statistical significance tests. |

## Next Checks
1. **Group size stress test**: Train RGRA with G ∈ {4, 16} and compare to GRPO; monitor stability and performance to identify if smaller groups re‑introduce need for clipping.
2. **Scale and reward density probe**: Replicate best RGRA configuration on a 7B+ model and with dense token‑level process rewards (e.g., per‑step correctness) to assess whether findings hold for larger capacities and richer signals.
3. **KL penalty ablation**: Sweep β ∈ [0, 0.05] without clipping to characterize the trade‑off between stability and learning speed; test if a sufficiently high β can substitute for clipping under high‑variance conditions.
