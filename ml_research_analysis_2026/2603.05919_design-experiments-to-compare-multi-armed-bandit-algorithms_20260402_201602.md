# Design Experiments to Compare Multi-armed Bandit Algorithms

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05919
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05919
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Artificial Replay (AR) reduces the number of real-environment interactions needed to compare two bandit policies from ~2T to T + o(T) and achieves sublinear variance growth in the estimator, whereas the naive independent-run design has linear variance growth.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Artificial Replay (AR), a novel experimental design for comparing multi-armed bandit algorithms more efficiently. AR couples the execution of two policies by first running one policy and storing its trajectory, then reusing stored rewards when the second policy selects the same arm, thereby sharing data. The authors develop a shared-reward-stack analytical framework and prove that AR yields an unbiased estimator of the average treatment effect with nearly half the real-environment interactions compared to naive independent runs, and with sublinear variance growth. Numerical experiments confirm significant variance reduction and cost savings.

## Method Summary
The naive approach runs two policies independently for T steps each, requiring 2T real interactions and yielding an estimator whose variance grows linearly with T. AR proceeds in two phases: Phase 1 runs policy π₀ for T steps, recording every (arm, reward) pair in a replay buffer organized per arm (FIFO queues). Phase 2 runs policy π₁ for T steps; at each step, if π₁ chooses an arm a that still has unused recorded rewards in the buffer, the next (oldest) reward from that arm is replayed; otherwise, a new reward is sampled from the environment. The key innovation is the shared-reward-stack model, which decouples policy randomness from reward generation and makes the coupled process analytically tractable, enabling proofs of unbiasedness, reduced interactions (T + o(T)), and sublinear variance under the condition that both policies have sublinear regret.

## Key Results
- AR estimator is unbiased for the average treatment effect (ATE).
- AR requires only T + o(T) real-environment interactions, approximately half the 2T needed by the naive design.
- AR’s estimator variance grows sublinearly in T, whereas the naive estimator’s variance grows linearly, leading to narrower confidence intervals and more reliable comparisons.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Positive correlation through shared reward replay
- **Claim:** AR reduces estimator variance by creating positive correlation between two bandit trajectories through shared reward replay.
- **Mechanism:** AR first runs a control policy π₀, logging all action-reward pairs. When the treatment policy π₁ runs, it reuses (replays) any reward from π₀'s history whenever it selects an arm that π₀ previously selected (and that replay hasn't been used yet). This induces dependence: when both policies pull the same optimal arm frequently, their cumulative rewards become positively correlated, which cancels variance in their difference.
- **Core assumption:** Both policies have sublinear regret, implying they allocate many pulls to the optimal arm a∗ with high probability, enabling substantial replay overlap. (See abstract and Section 3, Algorithm 2).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "requires only T + o(T) real user interactions... its variance grows sub-linearly in T, whereas the naive estimator has a linearly-growing variance."
  - [section]: Algorithm 2 (Steps 10-17) defines replay logic. Theorem 5 proves asymptotic variance reduction.
  - [corpus]: Moderate support; neighboring papers discuss UCB/TS variance properties, but not AR's coupling mechanism directly.
- **Break condition:** If policies' arm selections have negligible overlap (e.g., one policy focuses on a set of arms the other almost never plays), replay frequency drops, reducing correlation and thus variance reduction.

### Mechanism 2: Shared-reward-stack analytical model
- **Claim:** A "shared-reward-stack" probability model provides a tractable analytical representation that is distributionally equivalent to AR's complex canonical model.
- **Mechanism:** In this model, separate random uniform variables (η₀, η₁) control the two policies' internal randomness, while they share a pre-generated stack of rewards X_{a,t} for each arm a. Each policy, when it pulls arm a, simply takes the next unused reward from that arm's stack. This decouples policy randomness from environmental outcomes, making the coupled process analyzable via stopping times and martingales.
- **Core assumption:** Rewards for each arm are i.i.d. across time and independent across arms (standard bandit assumption). The equivalence to the canonical AR model holds exactly under this. (See Section 4, definition before Theorem 1).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section]: "We introduce a shared-reward-stack probability model and establish its distributional equivalence to the canonical model underlying the AR experiment (Theorem 1)."
  - [section]: Figure 6 illustrates the shared reward stacks. Equation (10) formalizes the interaction.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct support; neighboring papers use "reward stack" concepts for single policies (Lattimore & Szepesvári reference), but not for coupled designs.
- **Break condition:** If reward distributions for an arm are not i.i.d. (e.g., non-stationary rewards), the stack representation becomes invalid, as the order of revealed rewards matters non-trivially.

### Mechanism 3: Filtration making arm pull counts stopping times
- **Claim:** A novel filtration {F^a_T,t} makes the number of pulls of a specific arm a stopping time, enabling martingale analysis of arm-specific cumulative rewards under the shared-stack model.
- **Mechanism:** The natural filtration σ(H^π-s_t) is too coarse to make N^π-s_a(T) a stopping time. The new filtration F^a_T,t captures all information after the first t rewards in arm a's stack are revealed, plus all other stacks and internal randomness. This makes N^π-s_a(t) measurable with respect to F^a_T,{N^π-s_a(r): r≤t}, satisfying stopping time conditions. This allows applying the optional stopping theorem to martingales like S_T,a_t (centered cumulative rewards on stack a).
- **Core assumption:** The reward stacks and internal randomness (η₀, η₁) are independent and pre-generated. The martingale property of S_T,a_t holds under this independence. (See Section 4, after Proposition 1).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section]: "We define the filtration... such that N^πi-s_a(t) is a stopping time." (Equation (13)).
  - [section]: Proposition 1-(i) proves N^πi-s_a(r) is a bounded stopping time w.r.t. {F^a_T,t}. Proposition 1-(ii) establishes martingale properties for I_T,a_t(B), S_T,a_t, V_T,a_t.
  - [section]: Theorem 5 proof uses this filtration via Corollary 2, which derives from Proposition 1.
  - [corpus]: Weak support; stopping-time techniques exist in bandit theory but this specific filtration for coupled policies is novel per paper.
- **Break condition:** If the reward stacks are not independent of the policy selection processes (e.g., if rewards depend on which arms were pulled previously by either policy beyond the stack order), the martingale S_T,a_t may not be a Martingale under the constructed filtration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multi-armed bandit fundamentals**
  - **Why needed here:** Understanding how policies like UCB and Thompson Sampling adapt over time (tracking means, balancing exploration/exploitation) is essential to see why naive experiments need many restarts: one trajectory is a single dependent sample.
  - **Quick check question:** Why does the cumulative reward Σ_{t=1}^T R^{π}_t from a single run of a bandit algorithm have high variance relative to i.i.d. samples?
- **Concept: Policy evaluation vs. off-policy evaluation**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper contrasts AR (coupled online execution of both policies) with off-policy evaluation (OPE), which tries to evaluate a target policy from another policy's data and faces high variance or bias for deterministic policies. AR avoids OPE's pitfalls by letting both policies interact (with sharing).
  - **Quick check question:** For a deterministic bandit algorithm like UCB, why might standard OPE methods (like importance sampling) fail or be high-variance?
- **Concept: Variance reduction through covariance**
  - **Why needed here:** The AR estimator's variance advantage comes from positive covariance between the two cumulative rewards (Equation 16 vs 15). Understanding how coupling creates this covariance is key to the design's theory.
  - **Quick check question:** If two random variables X and Y each have variance σ², by how much can Var(X-Y) be reduced if Cov(X,Y) = σ²?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Policy Implementations (π₀, π₁) → Real Environment  
Policy Implementations (π₀) → Replay Buffer (Phase 1)  
Replay Buffer → AR Controller  
AR Controller → Real Environment (Phase 2 for missing rewards)  
AR Controller → Estimator Compute  

**Critical path:**  
1. Implement π₀, π₁ as stateful agents (tracking counts/means internally).  
2. Run π₀ for T steps, storing each (A^π⁰_t, R^π⁰_t) in per-arm FIFO queues.  
3. Initialize π₁. For t=1..T: π₁ selects A^π¹_t; if queue for A^π¹_t non-empty, reuse next reward from that queue; else pull A^π¹_t from environment to get new reward. Append (a, reward) to π₁’s history.  
4. Compute bθ_AR(T) = Σ_t R^π¹_t - Σ_t R^π⁰_t.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Symmetric vs. deployment order: AR is symmetric (Theorem 2), so swapping policies does not affect theoretical guarantees; naive independent runs are also symmetric but lack coupling benefits.  
- Sample efficiency vs. implementation complexity: AR reduces real interactions from ~2T to T + o(T) (Theorem 3) but requires managing replay buffer and matching logic.  
- Variance reduction vs. replay dependency: Gains rely on frequent reward reuse (common pulls on same arms). Policies with very different exploration/exploitation trade-offs reduce benefit.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Low replay rate:** If average min_{a} N^π⁰_a(T), N^π¹_a(T) is tiny, AR approaches naive cost/variances.  
- **Dependent rewards across arms:** If reward distributions P_a are not independent (e.g., arms are correlated), shared-stack model assumptions break, theoretical guarantees may fail.  
- **Non-sublinear regret:** If either policy has linear regret (e.g., ε-greedy with fixed ε), expected pulls on suboptimal arms may be Θ(T), increasing real interactions and violating condition for Theorem 5 (E[N_a(T)] = o(T)).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Example 1 (Bernoulli, UCB vs UCB):** Validates unbiasedness and variance reduction with two sublinear-regret policies. Check confidence intervals narrowing with AR vs naive.  
2. **Example 2 (Bernoulli, UCB vs TS):** Tests AR across different policy families (UCB vs Thompson Sampling). Should still show variance reduction.  
3. **Example 3 (Gaussian, TS vs ε-greedy):** Stress test: ε-greedy violates sublinear regret condition. Observe AR still helps but less dramatically; real interactions grow ∝ 1.09T not T + O(log T). Checks robustness beyond theory's assumptions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can the artificial replay (AR) design be effectively adapted for contextual bandit problems with high-dimensional contextual features where exact replay of identical context-action pairs is infeasible?
2. Can the AR coupling principle be extended to improve treatment effect estimation for more complex adaptive policies in reinforcement learning (MDPs) beyond multi-armed bandits?
3. Does Thompson Sampling violate the variance assumptions of Theorem 5 in practice, and if so, does AR still provide meaningful variance reduction for such algorithms?
4. How does the AR design perform in non-stationary environments where reward distributions drift over time or new arms are introduced, and how should the replay mechanism be adapted?

## Limitations
- Policy similarity dependency: AR’s efficiency gains rely on substantial action overlap between policies; if policies explore/disperse differently, replay rates drop, diminishing returns.
- Reproducibility gaps: Tie-breaking rules for UCB/ε-greedy, handling of unpulled arms’ empirical means, and Gaussian TS’s variance assumption are unspecified, causing potential variability in finite-T performance.
- Real-world assumptions: Stationary i.i.d. rewards and independent arms are standard but often violated in practice; the shared-stack model’s equivalence breaks if reward distributions evolve.

- **Reduced interactions (`T + o(T)`):** Medium
