# Evaluating Robustness of Large Language Models in Enterprise Applications: Benchmarks for Perturbation Consistency Across Formats and Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06341  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06341  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Minor prompt perturbations reduce LLM performance by up to 40 percentage points; multilingual and positional changes cause the largest drops, and model size does not guarantee robustness.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a comprehensive benchmark to evaluate the robustness of large language models (LLMs) in enterprise settings under realistic prompt perturbations. The benchmark isolates five perturbation categories—General, Positional, Format, Multilingual, and Cross-lingual—across four enterprise tasks, testing 11 models of varying sizes. Results reveal that even small changes to prompt structure, language, or format can degrade performance dramatically, with multilingual and positional perturbations proving most damaging. Notably, robustness does not scale monotonically with model size; smaller, well-tuned models can outperform much larger ones. The study highlights persistent format-specific failures (e.g., XML, YAML) and provides a two-stage evaluation metric that measures both content shifts and task-quality degradation.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed a benchmark using 420 synthetic IT incident seeds, expanded into four enterprise tasks (case/chat summarization, Q&A, entity slot-filling). They applied five independent perturbation categories to prompt inputs or templates: general noise (whitespace, spelling), positional reordering, format changes (JSON/YAML/XML/Markdown/HTML), multilingual translation (8 languages), and cross-lingual mismatches. For each model, outputs on original and perturbed prompts were generated (5 runs, temperature 0/fixed seed). Robustness was quantified via a two-stage metric: first, an LLM judge assessed content similarity between outputs (1–3 scale); second, for pairs with meaningful content change, task-specific quality (faithfulness, completeness, etc.) was evaluated. The overall score combined the frequency of content shifts and the severity of quality drops: `1 − (ΔContent × ΔQuality)`. This design isolates each perturbation’s effect while controlling for stochasticity via baseline consistency checks.

## Key Results
- Minor perturbations cause up to 40-point performance drops; multilingual and positional perturbations yield the largest average declines (-15.14 and -17.99 points).
- Model size does not predict robustness: an 8B model (Ministral 3 8B) outperforms a 120B model (GPT OSS), and two 8B models differ by 19.88 points.
- Format-specific failures are common, especially for XML and YAML, even among top performers.
- Within-family scaling improves robustness, but architectural and training choices dominate raw parameter count.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Isolating perturbations one at a time allows clean attribution of performance changes to specific prompt variations.  
- **Mechanism:** The benchmark applies each perturbation type independently to identical base inputs. By comparing model outputs on the original vs. singly-perturbed prompt, the evaluation separates the effect of that specific variation from other noise sources and inherent model stochasticity (estimated via baseline repeats).  
- **Core assumption:** Real-world enterprise prompt variations can be decomposed into orthogonal, additive factors for diagnostic clarity.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: "We introduce a benchmark suite that evaluates robustness across multiple perturbation types... one at a time."  
  - Section 3: "We introduce independent common enterprise variations... one at a time... to isolate the effect of each factor on model behavior."  
- **Break condition:** If perturbations interact synergistically (e.g., multilingual + format) such that combined effect ≠ sum of parts, isolated testing underestimates real-world failure rates.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Using a two-stage metric (content similarity + quality delta) distinguishes between superficial output changes and harmful task-performance degradation.  
- **Mechanism:** An LLM judge first assesses semantic alignment (1–3 scale) between original and perturbed outputs. Only pairs with meaningful content shifts (score <3) proceed to task-specific quality evaluation (faithfulness, completeness, etc.). The overall robustness score penalizes both the frequency of content change (ΔContent) and the severity of quality impact (ΔQuality) via multiplicative combination.  
- **Core assumption:** Not all semantic changes equally harm enterprise utility; quality metrics capture user-relevant performance drops.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 4: "We assess robustness using a two-stage evaluation process that focuses on content similarity and task-specific response quality."  
  - Section 4.3: "Overall Score = 1 − (∆Content × ∆Quality)... penalizes both frequent and impactful content changes."  
- **Break condition:** If LLM-as-a-Judge fails to correlate with human assessments of enterprise task success, the derived scores misrepresent actual harm.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Training methodology and architectural choices can override parameter count in determining robustness.  
- **Mechanism:** The benchmark reveals non-monotonic size-robustness curves: within-family scaling helps (e.g., GPT 5 Nano → 5.2: +6.66 pts), but cross-family comparisons show an 8B model (Ministral 3 8B) exceeding a 120B model (GPT OSS). This indicates factors like data quality, instruction tuning, and architecture innovations (e.g., MoE, better tokenization) dominate raw scale for robustness.  
- **Core assumption:** Robustness is a learned capability not automatically emergent from scale; it depends on training data diversity and objective design.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: "the relationship between model size and robustness is more nuanced... Ministral 3 8B outperforms most larger models... another 8B model (Llama 3.1 8B) performs worst overall."  
  - Section 5.3: "Within-family scaling helps... Architecture and training dominate scale... the 19.88-point gap between two 8B models."  
- **Break condition:** If robustness gains from scale eventually dominate across all architectures at sufficient parameter counts (e.g., beyond 120B), the observed non-linearity is a mid-range artifact.

## Foundational Learning
- **Perturbation isolation for diagnostic evaluation**  
  Why needed: The core method relies on applying one variation at a time to disentangle causes of failure; without this principle, observed effects would be confounded.  
  Quick check: If you simultaneously apply "whitespace" and "JSON format" perturbations, can you determine which caused a specific output change? Why or why not?  

- **LLM-as-a-Judge for semantic and quality assessment**  
  Why needed: The benchmark’s metrics (content similarity, faithfulness, completeness) are all judged by an LLM (GPT 4.1). Understanding its role, limitations (e.g., bias, non-perfect human alignment), and prompt design is crucial to interpreting scores.  
  Quick check: What is a key risk of using an LLM judge to evaluate "faithfulness" on a task where the judge model itself may have been trained on similar data?  

- **Enterprise task definition and metrics**  
  Why needed: Robustness is defined relative to task-specific quality (e.g., "completeness" for summarization, "citations" for Q&A). Knowing what "good" looks like for each task is necessary to understand what robustness protects.  
  Quick check: For the Entity Slot-filling task, why is Exact Match F1 used instead of an LLM judge for the primary metric?  

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Base Dataset Generator → Perturbation Engine → Evaluation Runner → Judge Module → Score Aggregator  

- **Critical path:** Base issue → task-specific prompt generation → perturbation application → model inference → LLM-judge scoring → delta calculation → robustness score. **Key dependency:** Judge prompts must be validated to align with human evaluation for each metric (per section 7 limitation).  

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Synthetic vs. real data:* Synthetic data ensures scale and controlled seeds but may lack real-world noise complexity and domain diversity.  
  - *Isolated vs. compound perturbations:* Isolated testing gives clean diagnosis but misses real-world compounding effects (a noted limitation).  
  - *LLM-as-a-Judge vs. human:* Scalable and consistent but may miss nuanced errors; human validation only for judge prompt tuning, not full dataset.  
  - *Aggregate vs. granular scores:* Overall score enables comparison but obscures format/language-specific failures (e.g., Ministral’s YAML failure).  

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **High variance in repeated runs** (e.g., GPT OSS 20B: ±18.93 on Positional) indicates unstable internal mechanisms for handling instruction order.  
  - **Format-specific collapse** (e.g., Gemma 3 12B: N/A on XML; Mistral Small 24B: 35.00±22.36 on XML) reveals structural generation flaws not apparent from aggregate scores.  
  - **Multilingual quality delta spikes** (e.g., Japanese: mean delta 44.32) suggest tokenization or training-data gaps for specific languages.  
  - **Low baseline consistency** (high variance in identical prompt repeats) would invalidate perturbation comparisons; all models show near-perfect baselines (≥92.8), so this is not an issue here.  

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Consistency Check:** Run 5 repeats on unperturbed prompts for each model/task. Verify Content Delta near 0% and low variance (<1%). This isolates perturbation effects from inherent stochasticity.  
  2. **Perturbation Sensitivity Heatmap:** For each model, compute overall scores across the 5 perturbation categories. Identify categories where score drops >10 points from baseline. Expect Positional/Multilingual to be hardest, General easiest.  
  3. **Format-Specific Stress Test:** For a top performer (e.g., GPT 5.2) and a struggling model (e.g., Ministral 8B), run only Format perturbations (JSON/YAML/XML/Markdown/HTML). Validate whether format weaknesses are model-specific (Ministral fails YAML) or universal (all struggle with XML).  

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How do multiple simultaneous perturbations (e.g., multilingual input combined with positional changes) affect LLM robustness compared to isolated perturbations?**  
   Real-world inputs often contain mixed variations; isolated testing may not capture compounding effects.

2. **Do the robustness findings generalize beyond IT support issues to other enterprise domains (e.g., finance, healthcare, legal)?**  
   The benchmark uses only IT-related synthetic data; domain-specific language and tasks might interact differently with perturbations.

3. **How does deployment-time sampling temperature (and inherent non-determinism) influence measured robustness to prompt perturbations?**  
   The study used temperature 0; higher temperatures used in practice could alter robustness profiles.

4. **What is the robustness profile of other leading closed-source/frontier models (e.g., GPT-4-series, Claude, Gemini) across the five perturbation categories?**  
   Evaluations were limited to a small set of OpenAI models; patterns may not hold across other providers.

## Limitations
- Synthetic dataset & perturbation code not released; replication relies on re-creation.
- LLM-judge alignment with human enterprise quality judgments unverified.
- Isolated perturbations ignore potential synergistic compound effects.
- Synthetic IT issues may lack real-world complexity.

## Confidence
- Isolation for attribution: **High** (design clear, no external dependencies)  
- Two-stage metric (ΔContent×ΔQuality): **Medium** (depends on LLM-judge validity)  
- Architecture > scale for robustness: **Medium** (empirical across 11 models, but families limited; beyond 120B unknown)  
- Format failures (XML/YAML): **High** (consistently observed)  
- Multilingual/positional biggest drops: **Medium** (translation/reordering definitions may vary)
