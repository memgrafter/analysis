# Development and multi-center evaluation of domain-adapted speech recognition for human-AI teaming in real-world gastrointestinal endoscopy

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01705  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01705  
- **Reference count:** 34  
- **Primary result:** EndoASR reduces character error rate from 20.52% to 14.14% and increases medical term accuracy from 54.30% to 87.59% in retrospective evaluation; prospective multi-center validation shows CER improvement from 16.20% to 14.97% and Med ACC from 61.63% to 84.16%, while achieving a real-time factor of 0.005.

## Executive Summary
Gastrointestinal endoscopy demands real-time, accurate speech recognition to facilitate human-AI collaboration. This paper introduces EndoASR, a domain-adapted automatic speech recognition system built by fine-tuning a pretrained Paraformer model on synthetic endoscopy reports. A two-stage fine-tuning strategy first adapts the model to medical terminology using clean synthetic speech, then enhances robustness to procedural noise via noise-augmented synthetic data. In a retrospective study of 600 recordings from six endoscopists, EndoASR lowered character error rate by 31% and raised medical term accuracy by 33 percentage points. Prospective validation across five independent centers confirmed these gains, albeit with smaller improvements, while maintaining a real-time factor of 0.005 suitable for edge deployment. The work demonstrates that synthetic data-driven domain adaptation can achieve clinically viable ASR in specialized procedural environments.

## Method Summary
EndoASR is based on the Seaco-Paraformer model (220 million parameters) pretrained on general Mandarin speech. The authors created a synthetic training corpus by converting authentic structured endoscopy reports into speech using a text-to-speech system, yielding both clean and noise-augmented versions. The latter mixed in recordings of endoscopy-room sounds (e.g., tower equipment, suction) at signal-to-noise ratios of 20–28 dB. Fine-tuning proceeded in two stages: stage-1 used the clean synthetic data to teach domain-specific vocabulary and syntax; stage-2 continued training with the noisy synthetic data to improve acoustic robustness. The system was evaluated on a retrospective set (600 intraprocedural recordings from six endoscopists at a single center) and a prospective multi-center set (300 recordings from five independent centers spanning six clinical content categories). Metrics included character error rate (CER), BLEU-1, BERTScore, and medical term accuracy (Med ACC). Training used the Adam optimizer (learning rate 2×10⁻⁴), up to 50 epochs, token-level batching (max 6000 tokens), early stopping on validation loss, and averaging the top-10 checkpoints. Important reproduction details remain unspecified: the exact size, speaker diversity, and medical term coverage of the synthetic dataset, as well as the precise medical term list used for Med ACC, are not provided.

## Key Results
- Retrospective evaluation: CER dropped from 20.52% to 14.14%, Med ACC rose from 54.30% to 87.59%.
- Prospective multi-center validation: CER improved from 16.20% to 14.97%, Med ACC from 61.63% to 84.16%.
- EndoASR-noise achieves a real-time factor of 0.005, enabling efficient edge deployment for live transcription.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Domain-specific language adaptation via synthetic speech from structured endoscopy reports improves recognition of medical terminology.
- **Mechanism:** A two-stage fine-tuning process first adapts the Paraformer backbone to the linguistic patterns of endoscopic reporting by training it on clean synthetic speech generated from authentic clinical reports. This exposes the model to domain-specific vocabulary, syntax, and standardized expressions without acoustic confounders.
- **Core assumption:** Synthetic speech accurately reproduces the pronunciation and lexical distribution of real endoscopic dictation, and the gap between synthetic and real acoustic conditions can be addressed in a subsequent stage.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "adapting to domain-specific terminology and procedural noise through a two-stage fine-tuning strategy on synthetic endoscopy reports."  
  - [section 2.3] Table 2 shows EndoASR (stage-1) reduces CER from 8.15% to 3.71% on clean synthetic speech, indicating linguistic adaptation.  
  - [section 4.1] Details synthetic speech generation from clinical reports using TTS, preserving medical terms.  
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers focus on general ASR challenges (dysarthric, Arabic call), not endoscopic domain adaptation; direct corpus evidence for this specific mechanism is absent.
- **Break condition:** If the synthetic speech fails to capture the spontaneous, accented, or context-dependent variations of real clinician speech, the linguistic gains may not transfer to real-world recordings, limiting Med ACC improvement.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Noise-aware fine-tuning with augmented synthetic speech enhances robustness to procedural acoustic disturbances.
- **Mechanism:** The stage-2 model (EndoASR-noise) is further fine-tuned on synthetic speech mixed with recorded endoscopy-room noise (e.g., tower sounds, suction). This teaches the model to disentangle speech from background interference while preserving domain-specific language knowledge from stage-1.
- **Core assumption:** The noise characteristics (SNRs, spectral profiles) from a single center’s recordings are representative enough of multi-center procedural noise to confer general robustness.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "procedural noise" and "noise-aware fine-tuning" are cited as key to real-world deployment.  
  - [section 2.3] EndoASR-noise achieves lower CER on noise-mixed synthetic test (3.29% vs 3.75% for EndoASR), showing added noise adaptation benefit under controlled conditions.  
  - [section 4.2] Describes stage-2 using noise-augmented data with SNRs (20–28 dB) derived from real endoscopy-room recordings.  
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers on noise-robust ASR (e.g., [28] in references) provide general methodologies but not endoscopic-specific validation; corpus does not directly evidence this mechanism’s effectiveness.
- **Break condition:** If prospective centers exhibit acoustically heterogeneous noise patterns not captured in the single-center training noise, the stage-2 gains may diminish, as suggested by the smaller Med ACC improvement from stage-1 to stage-2 in the multi-center evaluation (82.27% → 84.16%).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The lightweight Paraformer architecture (220M parameters) enables real-time, edge-deployable inference without sacrificing domain-adapted accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** Paraformer employs a non-autoregressive decoder that predicts all tokens in parallel, avoiding the sequential bottleneck of autoregressive models, and an efficient encoder that balances capacity and speed. This design yields low latency while maintaining sufficient modeling power for domain-adapted performance.
- **Core assumption:** The non-autoregressive approach and moderate parameter count provide adequate capacity for the endoscopic domain while meeting strict real-time constraints on edge hardware.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] reports a real-time factor of 0.005, demonstrating feasibility for live transcription.  
  - [section 2.2] describes the Paraformer architecture, highlighting its parallel decoding and efficiency.  
  - [section 4.3] confirms that EndoASR-noise retains accuracy comparable to larger models while achieving the reported speed.
- **Break condition:** If deployment environments impose even tighter resource budgets or if the domain requires significantly larger model capacity, the trade-off between speed and accuracy may no longer hold.

## Foundational Learning
- **Domain adaptation for ASR**  
  Why needed: To transfer a general-purpose speech recognizer to the specialized vocabulary and syntax of gastrointestinal endoscopy, which is not covered by off-the-shelf models.  
  Quick check: Does the adapted model outperform the base model on in-domain test data (e.g., lower CER or higher Med ACC)?

- **Synthetic speech generation**  
  Why needed: Provides a scalable, privacy-preserving source of domain-specific training speech that can be automatically aligned with text, avoiding costly manual transcription.  
  Quick check: Is the synthetic corpus derived from authentic clinical reports and does it cover the target medical lexicon?

- **Noise-robust ASR**  
  Why needed: Endoscopy rooms contain constant background noises (e.g., equipment, suction) that can degrade recognition; training on noisy data improves real-world robustness.  
  Quick check: Does the model maintain performance when evaluated on recordings with realistic SNR levels (e.g., 20–28 dB)?

- **Non-autoregressive sequence modeling**  
  Why needed: Enables parallel token prediction, drastically reducing inference latency for real-time applications.  
  Quick check: Is the real-time factor consistently below 1.0 across typical input lengths on the target hardware?

- **Medical term accuracy (Med ACC)**  
  Why needed: Standard word error metrics do not capture correct recognition of critical medical entities; Med ACC directly measures clinical usability.  
  Quick check: Is there a curated list of medical terms, and does the model’s output match these terms exactly (or with acceptable morphological variants)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Audio input -> Feature extraction (e.g., log-mel filterbanks) -> Encoder (Conformer/Transformer stack) -> Non-autoregressive decoder (length predictor + token predictor) -> Output token sequence.

**Critical path**  
Raw audio is converted to features, processed by the encoder into contextual representations, the decoder predicts the number of tokens and their identities in parallel, and the final tokens are converted to text. The parallel decoding step is the key to low latency.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Non-autoregressive vs. autoregressive: speed vs. potential accuracy on complex or long sequences.  
- Model size (220M) vs. computational resources: larger models may improve accuracy but increase latency and memory footprint.  
- Synthetic vs. real training data: synthetic data scales easily but may not fully capture real speaker variability; adding noise augmentation improves robustness but can slightly harm clean-speech accuracy.

**Failure signatures**  
1. Sudden increase in CER for utterances containing rare medical terms or out-of-vocabulary words.  
2. Degraded performance when test noise differs significantly from the single-center training noise (e.g., new equipment sounds).  
3. Latency spikes on long utterances due to inaccurate length prediction or inefficient batching.  
4. Inconsistent terminology usage if the TTS used for synthetic data does not cover all clinician accents or speaking styles.

**First experiments**  
1. Baseline check: Evaluate the pretrained Seaco-Paraformer on the clean synthetic test set to confirm it adapts to domain language after stage-1 fine-tuning.  
2. Noise robustness check: Compare stage-1 and stage-2 models on a held-out noise-mixed synthetic set to quantify the benefit of noise-aware fine-tuning.  
3. Deployment check: Measure real-time factor (RTF) on edge hardware to ensure ≤0.005 under expected workloads.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Does incorporating multi-center noise recordings during noise-aware fine-tuning improve EndoASR-noise's robustness and medical term accuracy across diverse acoustic environments compared to the single-center noise used in this study?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly state as a limitation that "noise recordings used for noise-aware adaptation were collected from a single center, which may limit robustness to unseen acoustic conditions."  
**Why unresolved:** The prospective multi-center evaluation showed heterogeneous performance, with noise adaptation providing marginal gains in some centers. It remains unclear whether this was due to the noise data mismatch or inherent limits of the adaptation strategy. Without multi-center noise data, the model's generalizability to truly unseen acoustic profiles is not fully validated.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct an ablation study where stage-2 fine-tuning uses noise data collected from multiple centers (versus single-center), then evaluate on the prospective multi-center dataset. Performance metrics (CER, Med ACC) stratified by center and acoustic embedding clusters (as in Fig. 4a) would reveal if multi-center noise data reduces inter-center variability and boosts terminology accuracy.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** To what extent does supplementing synthetic formal-endoscopy-report speech with spontaneous, conversational clinician dialogue (e.g., intraoperative team communications, filler words, repairs) improve recognition of non-technical speech and overall clinical usability?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note that "linguistic adaptation relied primarily on synthesized speech derived from formal endoscopy reports, emphasizing professional terminology while underrepresenting spontaneous conversational patterns," and identify this as a key limitation for future work.  
**Why unresolved:** The current synthetic data pipeline captures structured reporting language but may not model the disfluencies, colloquialisms, and multi-speaker interactions common in real endoscopy suites. This could limit performance on non-terminology utterances (e.g., instructions to assistants, off-topic remarks) that still impact workflow.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Collect and transcribe a corpus of spontaneous intraoperative dialogue (with privacy safeguards), integrate it into training (either as additional synthetic data via TTS with varied prosody or as limited real annotated samples), and evaluate on a spontaneous-speech subset of the prospective dataset. Metrics should include not only Med ACC but also recognition of non-medical tokens and overall narrative coherence.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What factors primarily cause the diminishing returns in medical term recognition accuracy as synthetic training data scale increases (as shown in Fig. 4b), and can targeted data curation (e.g., accent variation, rare term frequency) overcome these plateaus more effectively than sheer volume?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors observe that performance gains "gradually saturate as the data size increases" and speculate that remaining errors are due to "pronunciation variability and acoustic diversity" beyond vocabulary coverage. However, they do not dissect whether saturation is due to terminological saturation, acoustic mismatch, or model capacity limits.  
**Why unresolved:** Without error analysis across different data scales (e.g., by term frequency, phonetic complexity, or noise conditions), it is unclear whether the plateau is caused by unseen medical terms, pronunciation variants, or acoustic robustness. This impacts how resources should be allocated for further improvements.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Perform fine-grained error analysis on models trained with varying synthetic data scales, categorizing errors by term type (common vs. rare), pronunciation difficulty, and acoustic SNR. Additionally, conduct controlled experiments where data scale is held constant but composition is altered (e.g., oversampling rare terms, augmenting with varied accents), measuring Med ACC shifts to isolate the primary bottleneck.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Why does domain adaptation yield significantly larger improvements in medical term accuracy for lesion-related and intra-procedural content compared to pre-procedural content, and can model architecture modifications (e.g., terminology-aware attention, subword tokenization) further narrow this gap?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper shows in Fig. 3b that Med ACC gains are "particularly pronounced" in lesion-related categories, while pre-procedural (PreOp) content shows smaller differences. The authors attribute this to "differing linguistic characteristics" (higher terminology density in lesion content) but do not investigate whether the model's representation learning differentially captures these categories.  
**Why unresolved:** It is not established whether the residual errors in PreOp content stem from genuine domain mismatch (e.g., general conversational phrases) or from architectural limitations in handling mixed general-medical language. Without category-specific diagnostics, it is unclear if further gains require architectural changes or simply more domain data of that type.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct per-category error analysis and attention visualization for EndoASR-noise versus baseline, focusing on token-level predictions in PreOp vs. Lesion content. Test modified architectures (e.g., terminology-aware loss weighting, subword units for medical terms) on a balanced multi-category test set to see if PreOp accuracy can be raised without sacrificing Lesion performance.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** How does real-time transcription latency (beyond the reported RTF of 0.005) quantitatively affect clinician-AI interaction dynamics, workflow efficiency, and trust in endoscopic settings, given that even small delays may disrupt high-acuity procedures?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper emphasizes real-time deployment (RTF 0.005) and shows a qualitative video demo suggesting that "visible text appearing within approximately 200 ms" enables fluid interaction. However, no quantitative user study measures how varying latency levels impact clinical task performance, error correction, or adoption意愿.  
**Why unresolved:** While low RTF is necessary, it is not sufficient to guarantee usability. Clinicians might tolerate sub-200ms delays if accuracy is high, or conversely, even fast transcription may be rejected if errors require active correction. The interplay between speed, accuracy, and cognitive load in procedural teams remains unmeasured.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a mixed-methods user study with endoscopists using the system under controlled latency manipulations (e.g., artificial delays at 50ms, 200ms, 500ms). Measure objective outcomes (procedure time, report completeness, error correction rate) and subjective metrics (NASA-TLX workload, trust scores, system usability). Correlate these with ASR accuracy metrics to define acceptable latency-accuracy trade-offs for clinical deployment.

## Limitations
- The synthetic training dataset’s size, speaker diversity, and term coverage are unreported, and the medical term list used for Med ACC is not provided, hindering exact metric verification.
- Noise recordings for training were collected from a single center, potentially limiting robustness to the broader acoustic variability across different endoscopy suites.
- The study evaluates only Mandarin gastrointestinal endoscopy; generalizability to other languages or procedural domains remains untested.

## Confidence
- Domain adaptation via synthetic speech improves CER and Med ACC: **Medium** (retrospective gains are robust, but prospective gains are moderate and dataset details are missing).
- Noise-aware fine-tuning adds robustness: **Medium** (controlled experiments show benefit, but multi-center acoustic diversity may reduce impact).
- Real-time inference feasibility: **High** (architecture is fixed and RTF 0.005 is reported, matching efficiency expectations).
- Downstream LLM integration benefits: **Low** (the claim lacks quantitative support in the paper).

## Next Checks
1. Release the synthetic corpus and medical term dictionary, then independently replicate both training stages and evaluate on the retrospective and prospective test sets to verify reported metrics.
2. Expand noise data collection to multiple centers, train a corresponding noise-robust model, and evaluate on a held-out multi-center test set to quantify generalization improvements.
3. Perform an ablation study on noise augmentation parameters (SNR range, noise types) with per-term accuracy analysis, especially for low-frequency medical terms, to identify optimal augmentation strategy.
