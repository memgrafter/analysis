# Silicon Bureaucracy and AI Test-Oriented Education: Contamination Sensitivity and Score Confidence in LLM Benchmarks

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.21636
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21636
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Across 12 models, systematically degrading question information via a router-worker audit frequently leads to *higher* accuracy than a clean baseline, indicating contamination-related memory activation rather than genuine generalization.

## Executive Summary
This paper argues that standard LLM benchmark scores reflect a dangerous conflation: they measure competence in "test-oriented education" (e.g., pattern matching on known question formats) alongside genuine generalization. To disentangle these, the authors introduce an audit framework using a router-worker architecture. By comparing model performance when given a pristine question (clean baseline) versus aggregated outputs from multiple routers that delete, rewrite, or perturb the question, they seek evidence of "contamination sensitivity." Across 12 models, noisy conditions often outperform clean ones, especially with more routers, and exhibit a directional improvement pattern (wrong→correct transitions rise, correct→wrong fall). This suggests models are reactivating latent, benchmark-related memory traces from fragmented cues, not reasoning more effectively. The findings imply that raw benchmark scores alone are inadequate for assessing capability; score confidence must be evaluated separately.

## Method Summary
The core method is a controlled audit. A fixed set of benchmark questions is processed in two regimes: (1) a **Clean Baseline**, where a single "router" transmits the original question verbatim to a "worker" model, which must answer; (2) a **Noisy Aggregation** regime, where `m` parallel routers each independently degrade the question (via random deletion, paraphrasing, and noise injection). Their outputs are aggregated and fed to the worker. The worker never sees the original question. For each model and router count `m` (1-9), the difference in accuracy between noisy and clean conditions (`gain`) is measured. Persistent positive `gain` ("violations") across increasing `m` is interpreted as evidence that the aggregated noise fragments reassemble semantic cues (`Ξ(q)`) from the model's training data, contaminating its performance. Analysis also tracks per-question transitions (improve: clean wrong → noisy correct; degrade: clean correct → noisy wrong) to test for directional, non-random effects.

## Key Results
*   **Widespread Violations:** Noisy conditions frequently outperform the clean baseline. Violations (gain > 0) increase with router count; at 8 routers, 10/12 models show positive excess, with a mean positive excess up to 0.086.
*   **Significant Heterogeneity:** Model sensitivity to these contamination cues varies drastically. For example, Qwen3-Next-80B violates in all 9 router settings, while Qwen3.5-35B reaches a maximum positive excess of 0.260, and DeepSeek-Chat violates only once.
*   **Directional Transition Pattern:** As router count increases, the number of questions that switch from wrong to correct (improve) rises, while those switching from correct to wrong (degrade) falls. At higher router counts (8,9), improve exceeds degrade, indicating a systematic improvement from cue reassembly rather than random fluctuation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Cue Reassembly from Contamination Memory
*   **Claim:** Noisy aggregation across multiple routers can reassemble latent benchmark-related semantic cues, causing performance that exceeds the clean baseline, which is anomalous if the baseline represents maximal information transmission.
*   **Mechanism:** The clean baseline delivers the full problem. In noisy conditions, each router corrupts the problem locally. However, if the model has been exposed to benchmark-related information during training, the diverse, partial fragments from multiple routers may collectively overlap with stored contamination traces (`Ξ(q)`). The worker, processing only this aggregated, noisy input, may then retrieve the correct answer via these reactivated memory traces, leading to `Gₘ(θ) > 0`.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract states noisy conditions "frequently outperform clean... indicating that benchmark-related cues may be reassembled and can reactivate contamination-related memory." Section 3.3 derives that as `m` increases, `κ(Tₘ,Ξ(q))` (overlap with contamination cues) may accumulate, making positive gain more naturally explained by contamination than by pure noise.
*   **Break Condition:** If noisy conditions consistently underperform the clean baseline across all models and router counts, the reassembly mechanism is not supported.

### Mechanism 2: Heterogeneous Contamination Sensitivity
*   **Claim:** Contamination sensitivity (CS(θ)) is heterogeneous across models, meaning similar benchmark scores can mask very different levels of credibility regarding genuine generalization.
*   **Mechanism:** Models differ in their internal representations and how they respond to semantically neighboring contamination cues. This leads to variable violation breadth (number of router settings with gain > 0) and magnitude of positive excess. A model with high CS(θ) has a score more dependent on contamination cues, hence lower score confidence.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract notes "model heterogeneity is substantial," citing Qwen3-Next-80B (violates in all 9 settings) vs. Qwen3.5-35B (max excess 0.260). Section 5.2 shows clear per-model trajectory differences.
*   **Break Condition:** If all models show nearly identical violation patterns (breadth and magnitude), heterogeneity is low, and raw scores might uniformly reflect a single contamination effect.

### Mechanism 3: Directional Improvement from Cue Accumulation
*   **Claim:** Above-baseline anomalies are driven by a directional process of cue reassembly, evidenced by a router-count-dependent shift in question-level transition patterns.
*   **Mechanism:** Random noise would symmetrically increase both "improve" (wrong→correct) and "degrade" (correct→wrong) transitions. The observed pattern—where improve rises and degrade falls as `m` increases, eventually with improve > degrade—suggests aggregated noise constructively reassembles partial contamination clues. More routers provide more fragments, increasing the chance of accumulating enough cues to flip a previously incorrect answer, while having a less systematic effect on breaking a correct one.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.3 (Figure 4) show improve counts rising (e.g., 112 to 180) and degrade counts falling (e.g., 150 to 116) with `m`, reversing order by `r=8,9`.
*   **Break Condition:** If improve and degrade remain symmetric or net improve stays negative/constant across router counts, the directional reassembly inference is not supported.

## Foundational Learning

*   **Concept: Contamination Sensitivity (CS)**
    *   **Why needed:** It is the core dependent variable the audit measures, quantifying how much a model's score depends on contaminated cues vs. clean information.
    *   **Quick Check:** How would you operationally define and measure a model's sensitivity to benchmark contamination cues beyond checking for exact memorization?

*   **Concept: Router-Worker Audit Architecture**
    *   **Why needed:** This is the fundamental experimental methodology, creating controlled conditions (clean vs. noisy) to isolate cue reassembly effects.
    *   **Quick Check:** Why is it critical that the worker cannot access the original question? What would invalidate the comparison if it could?

*   **Concept: Score Confidence vs. Absolute Score**
    *   **Why needed:** The paper's thesis is that these are separable. A high score does not imply high confidence in its validity as evidence for genuine generalization.
    *   **Quick Check:** Give an example of two models with identical benchmark scores but different score confidences based on this framework.

## Architecture Onboarding

Clean Baseline -> Worker -> Answer
Noisy Routers (m x) -> Aggregation (A_m) -> Worker -> Answer
Scoring & Transition Tracker compares Clean vs. Noisy answers.

**Critical Path:**
1.  Select a fixed random subset (n=100) of benchmark questions.
2.  For each model, run the Clean Baseline (1 router) on all questions; record accuracy.
3.  For each router count `m` in {1..9}: for all questions, generate `m` independent noisy router outputs, aggregate them via `A_m`, run the worker, record accuracy.
4.  Compute gain (noisy_acc - clean_acc), positive excess, and per-question transitions (improve, degrade).
5.  Analyze aggregate violation patterns, per-model trajectories, and transition trends.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   **Noise Design:** Must balance degradation and retention. Too weak: minimal degradation, signal may be noisy. Too strong: destroys all information, masking reassembly. Chosen level ensures clean baseline is robust.
*   **Router Count `m`:** Proxy for cue aggregation intensity. More routers increase chance of reassembling dispersed traces but also increase total noise. Non-monotonic violation rates suggest complex interactions.
*   **Worker Constraint:** Worker *must* rely only on router output. Any access to the original question invalidates the comparison by handicapping noisy conditions artificially.
*   **Sample Size (`n`=100):** Limited statistical power for per-question analysis but sufficient for aggregate trends. Larger `n` improves reliability at higher compute cost.

**Failure Signatures:**
*   No above-baseline anomalies: all models show `gain <= 0` for all `m`. Interpretation: benchmark clean or noise too destructive.
*   Symmetrical transition pattern: `improve` and `degrade` counts remain equal across `m`. Suggests random noise, not systematic reassembly.
*   Uniform model response: All models have identical violation patterns. Contradicts heterogeneity hypothesis.
*   Monotonic performance decline: Noisy accuracy strictly decreases with `m`. Expected if noise is purely destructive; paper's key finding is this often fails.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1.  Replicate core violation for a single high-sensitivity model (e.g., Qwen3-Next-80B) across `m=1..9` and verify `gain > 0` occurs commonly (e.g., at `m=8`).
2.  For that model, plot `improve` and `degrade` counts vs. `m`. Verify that at higher `m` (e.g., 8,9), `improve > degrade`.
3.  Compare two models with similar clean scores but different predicted sensitivity (e.g., Qwen3.5-35B vs. DeepSeek-Chat). Confirm the high-sensitivity model shows more frequent/larger positive gains and/or broader violation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1.  How can the proposed audit framework be operationalized and institutionalized within the existing LLM benchmarking ecosystem (e.g., leaderboards, model cards, evaluation standards)? The paper establishes the need but not the governance mechanisms or standard protocols.
2.  What specific model properties (e.g., architecture, training data composition, scale, alignment methods) causally determine heterogeneous contamination sensitivity across models with similar benchmark scores? The paper documents heterogeneity but does not investigate its roots.
3.  For which types of benchmark tasks or question formats is the router-worker audit most sensitive to semantic-neighbor contamination, and do certain benchmark designs inherently produce greater false-positive contamination signals? The paper tests only one multiple-choice benchmark.
4.  Can the router-worker setup reliably distinguish contamination-driven performance gains from gains due to other forms of model robustness or reasoning (e.g., robustness to input perturbation, chain-of-thought-style aggregation of partial information)? The core inference assumes the clean baseline is "full-information," an untested assumption.

## Limitations
*   **Implementation Unknowns:** Exact benchmark dataset, prompt templates, noise parameters, and aggregation function (`A_m`) are not specified, blocking exact replication.
*   **Sample Size:** Fixed n=100 may limit statistical power for per-question transition analysis and increase variance in violation rates.
*   **Causal Attribution:** The link between positive excess and *contamination memory* (vs. other forms of robustness or artifact) remains indirect; a control on a definitively uncontaminated benchmark is absent.

## Confidence
*   **Reassembly Mechanism (Noisy > Clean):** Medium. Strong aggregate pattern across 12 models and 9 router settings, but causal attribution to contamination memory is indirect; alternative explanations not fully eliminated.
*   **Heterogeneous Sensitivity:** High. Clear, consistent model-specific violation breadth and excess magnitude (e.g., Qwen3-Next-80B vs. DeepSeek-Chat).
*   **Directional Transition Trend:** Medium. Asymmetric improve/degrade shift is evident in aggregates but could be sensitive to noise design; requires replication with varied perturbation strategies.

## Next Checks
1.  **Replicate with a New Model:** Audit a model not in the original 12 (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B) using the described protocol. Verify positive gain occurs at high router counts (e.g., r=8), testing generalizability.
2.  **Noise Strength Break Condition:** Systematically vary noise intensity (e.g., deletion rate). Prediction: sufficiently strong noise should eliminate positive excess (gain ≤ 0). Failure to observe this suggests clean baseline degradation, not cue reassembly.
3.  **Statistical Power via Sample Expansion:** Repeat the primary audit with a larger question sample (e.g., n=500) for 2–3 representative models. Check if the directional improve>degrade trend at high `m` remains statistically significant, addressing small-n reliability.
