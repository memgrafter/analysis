# PRISM: Exploring Heterogeneous Pretrained EEG Foundation Model Transfer to Clinical Differential Diagnosis

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.02268
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02268
*   **Reference Count:** 30
*   **Primary Result:** Pretraining on geographically and acquisition-system diverse EEG data yields representations that, while less linearly separable, adapt more effectively via fine-tuning and provide a substantial (+12.3 pp) performance boost on a challenging clinical differential diagnosis task compared to models pretrained on narrow clinical archives, with benchmark rankings being highly sensitive to evaluation protocol choices.

## Executive Summary
This work investigates how the diversity of pretraining data affects the transferability of EEG foundation models. Using a fixed masked autoencoder architecture (PRISM), the authors compare a model pretrained on narrow-source EU/US clinical archives (TUH, PhysioNet) against one pretrained on a more diverse pool that includes multi-center South Asian recordings. They find a key trade-off: the narrow-source model performs better under linear probing (frozen encoder), while the diverse-source model matches or exceeds it under full fine-tuning. The advantage of diverse pretraining is most pronounced on a clinically novel and challenging task—distinguishing epilepsy from diagnostic mimickers using interictal EEG—where it achieves a +12.3 pp boost in balanced accuracy. Furthermore, they demonstrate that rankings between leading models (e.g., PRISM vs. REVE) can reverse by up to 24 percentage points depending on evaluation protocol details like split construction, segment length, and head choice, highlighting major confounding factors in EEG foundation model benchmarking.

## Method Summary
The study uses a masked autoencoder with a fixed REVE-derived architecture. Pretraining data consists of two pools: D1 (narrow-source: TUH + PhysioNet) and D2 (diverse: D1 + 4170 hours from South Asian centers across multiple EEG systems). All data undergoes identical preprocessing: resampled to 200 Hz, bandpassed (0.5–99.5 Hz), notched, per-channel Z-scored, clipped to ±15σ, and mapped to a 19-channel 10–20 montage. The model uses block masking (55% ratio) and a dual L1 reconstruction loss. Downstream evaluation covers standard motor imagery, sleep staging, and pathology detection benchmarks, plus a new 200-subject epilepsy vs. mimickers dataset. Performance is measured under multiple adaptation regimes (linear probing, full/partial fine-tuning) and with different classification heads (MLP, attention/average pooling). Comparisons are made against the REVE model under two published benchmarking frameworks (EEG-Bench, EEG-FM-Bench).

## Key Results
*   N arrow-source pretraining (D1) consistently outperforms diverse-source (D2) under **linear probing** across most benchmarks, while **full fine-tuning** reverses this trend, with D2 matching or exceeding D1 on 5/6 tasks.
*   On the novel clinical **epilepsy vs. mimickers** task, the diverse checkpoint achieves **+12.3 pp higher balanced accuracy** than the narrow-source checkpoint—the largest performance gap observed.
*   Model rankings between PRISM and REVE **reverse by up to 24 pp** between EEG-Bench and EEG-FM-Bench protocols due to differences in split construction, checkpoint selection, segment length, normalization, and head choice.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Diverse pretraining yields representations that are less aligned with any single distribution's statistics but more adaptable via fine-tuning, while narrow-source pretraining yields representations optimized for linear separability on distribution-matched benchmarks.
**Mechanism:** Exposure to heterogeneous recording systems (different amplifiers, referencing) during masked autoencoder pretraining forces the encoder to disentangle consistent neural content from acquisition-specific artifacts to achieve reconstruction. This creates a more general embedding space where discriminative features for downstream tasks require nonlinear readjustment (fine-tuning) but are ultimately more robust.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "narrow-source excels in linear probing but diverse matches or exceeds it under fine-tuning"; [section 4.1] Table 1 shows D2 under full fine-tuning matches/exceeds D1 on 5/6 tasks, while under linear probing D1 leads on 4/6.
### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Inconsistencies between EEG-Bench and EEG-FM-Bench evaluation protocols cause non-additive ranking reversals because models implicitly optimize for protocol-specific structures rather than generalizable EEG representations.
**Mechanism:** Each protocol's specific choices (train/validation split construction, segment length, normalization, head type) create different optimization landscapes during fine-tuning. A model's architecture and pretraining objective interact with these choices in compounding ways, such that a model that exploits a particular protocol's structure (e.g., subject-level leakage via segment-level splits) appears superior under that protocol but inferior under a stricter one.
**Evidence anchors:** [section 4.5] "six concrete sources including split construction, checkpoint selection, segment length, and normalization, showing these factors compound non-additively."; [section 4.5] Table 5 shows rankings reverse between protocols (e.g., REVE vs PRISM on PhysioNet-MI).
### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The advantage of diverse pretraining is most pronounced on clinically challenging differential diagnosis tasks (epilepsy vs. mimickers) because such tasks rely on subtle, sparse features that are easily confounded by acquisition artifacts, which diverse pretraining helps eliminate.
**Mechanism:** The epilepsy vs. mimickers task from interictal EEG has low signal-to-noise ratio and lacks gross physiological markers. A model pretrained on a narrow distribution may have learned shortcuts correlated with that specific distribution's artifact patterns. Diverse pretraining, by forcing reconstruction across varied systems, filters out these distribution-specific artifacts, preserving the subtle neural signatures that differentiate epilepsy from mimickers.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "On a clinically challenging and previously untested task—distinguishing epilepsy from diagnostic mimickers via interictal EEG—the diverse checkpoint outperforms the narrow-source checkpoint by +12.3 pp balanced accuracy, the largest gap across all evaluations."; [section 4.4] Table 4 shows the 12.3 pp gap is substantially larger than on standard benchmarks.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Representation Quality vs. Distribution Matching**
    *   *Why needed here:* To understand why narrow-source pretraining looks better under linear probing but diverse pretraining catches up under fine-tuning.
    *   *Quick check:* If a model's frozen encoder yields high accuracy on a benchmark, does that mean its learned features are generally useful, or just well-aligned with that benchmark's specific data distribution?
*   **Concept: Self-Supervised Pretraining Objective (Masked Autoencoding)**
    *   *Why needed here:* The reconstruction task is the mechanism that drives the encoder to learn signal structure; fixing this objective isolates the effect of pretraining data diversity.
    *   *Quick check:* Does the masked autoencoder's need to reconstruct arbitrary masked patches force it to build a richer internal model of EEG generation (including neural and artifact sources) compared to a contrastive objective?
*   **Concept: Confounding Variables in Model Comparison**
    *   *Why needed here:* The paper argues that comparing dataset counts (e.g., 92 vs 3) without controlling architecture, objective, and data composition is meaningless.
    *   *Quick check:* When Model A trained on 100 datasets outperforms Model B trained on 10, is the gain due to (a) more data, (b) different data, (c) better architecture, or (d) a different pretraining task? How would you design an experiment to isolate (a)?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Raw EEG → Channel-wise patch embedding → 4D positional encoding (spatial coords + temporal index) → 12-layer encoder (masked tokens only) → 4-layer decoder (with learned mask tokens) → L1 loss on reconstructed patches. Auxiliary path: pooled intermediate encoder outputs → global embedding → independent reconstruction. Downstream: frozen or fine-tuned encoder + classification head (MLP, avg-pool, or attn-pool).
*   **Critical path:** Data goes in -> patchify -> add positional encoding -> encoder sees only visible patches -> decoder combines visible encoded tokens + learned mask tokens -> reconstruct masked patches. The auxiliary path runs in parallel during pretraining only.
*   **Design tradeoffs:** Fixed architecture (REVE-based) allows isolating *data diversity* effects but precludes testing if a different architecture would show the same trends. 4D positional encoding enables arbitrary montages but adds complexity. Masking ratio (0.55) is not ablated.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    1.  **Large LP-FT gap for diverse checkpoint:** Indicates representations are not linearly separable on the target task, requiring significant adaptation. Expected if diverse training works.
    2.  **High sensitivity to classification head choice:** Suggests the encoder's features are not naturally aligned with the task; the head becomes a major performance lever.
    3.  **Protocol-dependent ranking reversals:** Model performs well on one benchmark framework but poorly on another with identical data, indicating optimization for protocol-specific artifacts.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Pretraining pool ablation:** Train two models (D1 narrow, D2 diverse) with identical hyperparameters/architecture. Compare under LP and FT on standard benchmarks to test the core trade-off.
    2.  **Classification head ablation:** On a single checkpoint (e.g., D2), evaluate performance across MLP, average pooling, and attention pooling heads under full fine-tuning to quantify head sensitivity.
    3.  **Novel clinical task validation:** Evaluate both checkpoints on the epilepsy vs. mimickers task. A significant and large gap (as found) provides the strongest evidence for clinical relevance of pretraining diversity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question:** Does geographic diversity or acquisition-system heterogeneity primarily drive the performance gains from multi-source pretraining?
    *   **Basis:** The authors state: “Future work should disentangle the relative contributions of geographic representation and acquisition-system diversity.”
    *   **Why unresolved:** The multi-source pretraining condition combined both factors, preventing attribution of improvement to either one.
*   **Question:** How does model scale interact with pretraining population diversity across linear probing and fine-tuning regimes?
    *   **Basis:** The limitation section notes: “We use a single model size; scaling effects remain unexplored.”
    *   **Why unresolved:** All models shared identical architecture; the influence of scaling on the diversity effect is unknown.
*   **Question:** Do the benefits of diverse pretraining generalize to non-South Asian populations for the epilepsy vs. mimickers differential diagnosis task?
    *   **Basis:** The authors acknowledge: “Our epilepsy–mimicker dataset is drawn from South Asian clinical centers; generalization to other demographic groups requires separate validation.”
    *   **Why unresolved:** The evaluation cohort was exclusively South Asian, leaving open whether the 12.3 pp gain is population-specific.
*   **Question:** Is the MLP head’s superiority over attention/average pooling consistent across other EEG foundation models?
    *   **Basis:** The paper notes that EEG-FM-Bench reports similar head sensitivity across models, but the PRISM ablation is the only direct test; they call for further investigation.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Head ablations were performed only on PRISM; generalizability to other architectures remains unknown.

## Limitations
*   The test set for the key epilepsy vs. mimickers task originates from South Asia—the same region partially included in diverse pretraining—so the 12.3 pp gap may partly reflect distribution matching rather than pure artifact robustness.
*   The claim that data diversity trumps sheer dataset count (PRISM: 3 corpora vs. REVE: 92) lacks a volume-matched control ablation, confounding diversity with quantity.
*   Using a single fixed REVE-derived architecture limits generalizability; trends may not hold for other model families (e.g., transformers with criss-cross attention).

## Confidence
*   **LP/FT trade-off (Diverse vs. narrow):** High. Controlled experiments show consistent reversal across six benchmarks.
*   **Epilepsy task advantage:** Medium. Large effect but in-distribution test; dataset unreleased for external verification.
*   **Protocol sensitivity:** High. Systematic ablation (split, segment length, etc.) demonstrates up to 24 pp ranking changes.
*   **Diversity > dataset count:** Medium. Non-apples-to-apples comparison with REVE; lacks volume-matched controls.

## Next Checks
1.  **Out-of-distribution clinical validation:** Release the epilepsy–mimickers dataset and have an independent team replicate results on test cohorts from excluded regions (e.g., Europe/North America) to isolate artifact robustness from geographic matching.
2.  **Volume-controlled diversity ablation:** Train a model on a downsampled diverse pool (matching narrow-source hours) and compare to both D1 and D2. This isolates diversity from data quantity effects.
3.  **Unified protocol re-evaluation:** Re-run all model comparisons (PRISM, REVE, etc.) under a single strict protocol (subject-level splits, 4s windows, standardized MLP head, last checkpoint) to determine if protocol-adjusted rankings persist.
