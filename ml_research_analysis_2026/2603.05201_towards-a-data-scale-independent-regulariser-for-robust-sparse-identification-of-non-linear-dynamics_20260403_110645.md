# Towards a data-scale independent regulariser for robust sparse identification of non-linear dynamics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05201
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05201
- **Reference count:** 35
- **Primary result:** STCV replaces magnitude-based thresholding with a scale-invariant statistical metric (Coefficient Presence) and achieves high success rates on normalized, noisy data where STLSQ and E-SINDy fail completely.

## Executive Summary
Standard sparse regression for equation discovery (e.g., SINDy) relies on magnitude-based thresholding to select model terms. This approach catastrophically fails when data is normalized, because scaling distorts coefficient magnitudes and measurement noise causes spurious terms to acquire magnitudes comparable to true physical terms. The proposed Sequential Thresholding of Coefficient of Variation (STCV) algorithm introduces a dimensionless metric—Coefficient Presence (CP)—derived from Bayesian linear regression posterior statistics. CP measures the consistency of a coefficient across data subsets and is invariant to linear scaling. Benchmarks on canonical dynamical systems and an engineering experiment show that STCV robustly recovers correct sparse models from normalized, noisy data, while competing methods produce dense, unphysical results.

## Method Summary
STCV iteratively sparsifies a candidate library by thresholding on CP values. Starting from an initial STLSQ fit (with near-zero threshold to activate all terms), it computes CP for each active coefficient using Bayesian linear regression with a ridge penalty. CP is defined as √m · μ/σ, where μ and σ are the posterior mean and standard deviation of the coefficient, and m is the number of data points. True terms exhibit low relative variance (high CP), whereas spurious terms exhibit high variance (low CP). The algorithm then eliminates terms below a CP threshold λ_CP. Both the ridge penalty γ and λ_CP are ramped over n_steps to stabilize variance estimation and guide convergence to a sparse, scale-invariant model. The final output is a sparse coefficient matrix.

## Key Results
- STLSQ achieves 0% success rate on normalized, noisy Lorenz data (0.1% noise) while unscaled data yields high success, demonstrating the failure mode.
- STCV attains >90% success rate on the same normalized noisy Lorenz benchmark, significantly outperforming STLSQ and E-SINDy.
- On real mass‑spring‑damper experimental data, STCV recovers the correct sparse model, whereas STLSQ and E-SINDy produce dense, unphysical models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### 1. Normalization and noise invalidate magnitude thresholds
- **Claim:** Data normalization, combined with measurement noise, makes magnitude-based thresholding unreliable because scaling rescales true coefficients and noise inflates spurious coefficients to comparable magnitudes.
- **Mechanism:** Normalization rescales all state variables (e.g., to [-1,1]), which directly rescales the true ODE coefficients. Measurement noise, when scaled with the trajectory, causes overfitted spurious terms to have coefficient magnitudes similar to true terms. The separation that exists in unscaled data (large stable true coefficients vs. small variable spurious ones) collapses.
- **Evidence:** The paper shows 0% STLSQ success on normalized noisy Lorenz across sampling parameters (Fig. 1). Corpus work acknowledges noise/scaling challenges but not their interaction.
- **Break condition:** Failure occurs whenever normalized data contains non‑negligible noise; severity increases with noise level.

### 2. CP provides scale-invariant term selection
- **Claim:** CP (√m·μ/σ) measures coefficient consistency via the coefficient of variation (CV), making it robust to linear scaling.
- **Mechanism:** CP uses the posterior standard deviation σ from Bayesian linear regression (with a ridge prior). True terms have low CV (μ large relative to σ), yielding high CP. Spurious terms have high CV, yielding low CP. Since CV is a ratio, it is invariant to multiplying the data by a constant.
- **Evidence:** On experimental mass‑spring‑damper data, STCV’s CP threshold cleanly isolates true terms while STLSQ/E‑SINDy fail (Table 2). CP is analogous to inclusion probability in E‑SINDy but derived from closed‑form BLR.
- **Break condition:** If noise variance is so high that even true coefficients become inconsistent (CV approaches spurious level), or if multicollinearity destabilizes σ estimates, discrimination fails.

### 3. Iterative ramped thresholding stabilizes solution
- **Claim:** STCV’s loop with ramped ridge penalty γ and CP threshold λ_CP gradually guides the model from a conservative stable fit to a sparse final model.
- **Mechanism:** A high initial γ stabilizes the BLR variance estimates, yielding reliable initial CP values. Decreasing γ and increasing λ_CP over steps removes inconsistent terms while avoiding premature elimination of true terms. The ramping (akin to simulated annealing) balances stability and sparsity.
- **Evidence:** Benchmarks show STCV (with ramping) achieves high success where STLSQ fails. The cascaded STCV‑STLSQ variant (with larger γ) further improves results.
- **Break condition:** If the ramping schedule is too aggressive (γ drops too fast, λ_CP rises too fast), true terms may be eliminated early and not recovered due to hard thresholding.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Inputs (X, dX/dt, Θ) -> Preconditioner (scale for numerical stability) -> Initial Fitter (STLSQ with near‑zero threshold) -> Bayesian Linear Regression Engine (compute μ, σ for active coefficients given current γ) -> CP Calculator (√m·μ/σ) -> Thresholding Gate (remove terms with |CP| < λ_CP) -> Hyperparameter Scheduler (ramp γ and λ_CP) -> Convergence Check -> Output (final Ξ). The loop (steps 4–7) repeats for n_steps.

### Critical path
1. Precondition data.
2. Initial STLSQ fit (all terms active).
3. For each step k = 1…n_steps:
   a. Set current γ_k and λ_CP,k (ramped).
   b. Fit active terms with STLSQ using penalty γ_k.
   c. Compute BLR posterior (μ, σ) using γ_k and residual noise estimate.
   d. Compute CP for active coefficients.
   e. Eliminate terms with |CP| < λ_CP,k.
   f. Check convergence (no change in active set? break).
4. Return final sparse Ξ.

### Design tradeoffs
- *CP threshold vs. sparsity:* Higher λ_CP yields sparser models but risks dropping weak true terms; requires manual grid search.
- *Ridge penalty scheduling:* High initial γ stabilizes variance estimates but biases coefficients; ramping must balance stability and unbiasedness.
- *BLR vs. Bootstrapping:* Closed‑form BLR is computationally cheap but assumes Gaussian noise; bootstrapping (E‑SINDy) is heavier but distribution‑free.

### Failure signatures
1. **Dense final model:** λ_CP too low or γ schedule too weak → spurious terms retained.
2. **Missing true terms:** λ_CP too high or initial γ too high → true terms eliminated early.
3. **Oscillation/non‑convergence:** λ_CP near borderline term CP values → terms repeatedly added/removed.
4. **Failure on noise‑free normalized data:** Numerical integration errors create consistent spurious terms with low variance → CP misidentifies them as true. Remedy: inject small noise.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate Lorenz degradation:** Generate normalised noisy Lorenz data (0.1% noise) and show STLSQ achieves 0% success across sampling grids, confirming the failure mode.
2. **STCV on normalised Lorenz:** Run STCV on the same data with CP threshold range [0.001, 1] and show >90% success, validating the solution.
3. **CP metric inspection:** For a single noisy normalised Lorenz dataset, output CP values for all terms; visualise that true terms have high CP and spurious terms low CP, unlike raw magnitudes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Integration with weak‑form SINDy:** How can STCV be combined with weak‑form SINDy (WSINDy) to address both derivative estimation and scaling issues?
2. **Automated hyperparameter selection:** Can λ_CP and γ be chosen automatically from data properties without manual grid search?
3. **Rigorous uncertainty quantification:** How can STCV be extended to provide distribution‑free uncertainty measures, e.g., via conformal prediction?
4. **Noise‑free normalised data failure:** Why does STCV underperform on perfectly noise‑free normalised data, and how can this be mitigated without artificial noise injection?

## Limitations
- **Implementation gaps:** BLR noise variance estimation method, exact ridge ramping schedule for pure STCV, and preconditioning details are unspecified.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** CP threshold λ_CP requires manual tuning; no automatic selection method provided.
- **Assumptions:** Relies on Gaussian noise for BLR variance; requires artificial noise injection for noise‑free normalised data due to numerical integration errors.
- **Scope limits:** Benchmarking restricted to polynomial libraries; only one experimental dataset; performance on PDEs or other basis functions unknown.
