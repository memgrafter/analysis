# CREDO: Epistemic-Aware Conformalized Credal Envelopes for Regression

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06826
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06826
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** CREDO maintains 90% marginal coverage and significantly improves outlier adaptation (highest interval length ratio among baselines) on 12 regression tasks, with competitive scaled mean interval score.

## Executive Summary
Overconfident prediction intervals in extrapolation regions are a key challenge for conformal regression. CREDO addresses this with a two-step framework: first, it constructs a covariate-dependent credal envelope that widens in data-sparse regions by adaptively trimming posterior predictive endpoints; then it applies split conformal calibration to guarantee marginal coverage. On 12 regression benchmarks, CREDO maintains target 90% coverage and outperforms baselines (CQR, UACQR, EPICScore) in outlier adaptation, especially on small datasets. Theory provides finite-sample coverage guarantees and asymptotic consistency under correct specification.

## Method Summary
CREDO takes any regression dataset and splits it into training (56%), calibration (24%), and test (20%) sets. It uses a Bayesian model (e.g., Quantile Neural Network or BART) trained on the training set to draw posterior samples. For a given covariate x, it computes a credal envelope [ℓ(x), u(x)] by taking quantiles of the posterior predictive distribution endpoints at level α₀=α, trimmed by an adaptive γ(x) that depends on local data density (via k‑NN scarcity score). After computing nonconformity scores s_i = max{ℓ(x_i)−y_i, y_i−u(x_i)} on the calibration set, it estimates the calibration quantile ˆτ as the appropriate order statistic. The final prediction interval is C(x)=[ℓ(x)−ˆτ, u(x)+ˆτ], which enjoys guaranteed marginal coverage under exchangeability. An uncertainty decomposition separates aleatoric (average posterior interval width) and epistemic (envelope width minus aleatoric) contributions.

## Key Results
- CREDO maintains target 90% coverage on all 12 regression tasks.
- It achieves significantly higher interval length ratio (outlier vs. inlier) than baselines CQR, UACQR, and EPICScore, particularly on small datasets, demonstrating superior outlier adaptivity.
- Its scaled mean interval score (SMIS) is competitive with state‑of‑the‑art methods.
- Theoretical guarantees: finite‑sample marginal coverage and asymptotic consistency under correct model specification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Credal envelope via endpoint trimming
Claim: The credal quantile envelope [ℓ(x), u(x)] is built by trimming extreme posterior predictive endpoints at level α₀, ensuring that for any distribution in the local credal set, at least 1−α₀ probability lies inside. Mechanism: For each x, draw posterior samples θ; for each θ, compute α₀/2 and 1−α₀/2 quantiles of F_θ(·|x); then take the γ/2 and 1−γ/2 quantiles across these endpoint draws. This defines a credal set F₀(x) of predictive distributions whose central endpoints are not extreme. Core assumption: The posterior captures parameter uncertainty; trimming level γ controls credal set size. Evidence: Section 2.1 details the algorithm; relation to conformalized credal sets established in literature. Break condition: Posterior mis‑specification may prevent widening in sparse regions; over‑/under‑trimming harms informativeness.

### Mechanism 2: Adaptive credal inflation via data‑density‑dependent γ(x)
Claim: Envelope width is modulated by covariate‑dependent γ(x) that decreases (less trimming) in data‑sparse regions and increases (more trimming) in data‑dense regions. Mechanism: Define scarcity score sc(x) from k‑NN radius in representation space ϕ(x). Map sc(x) to γ(x) via logistic function with parameters (γ_min, γ_max, m_γ, τ_γ). Smaller γ(x) retains more posterior draws, widening envelope where support is low. Core assumption: Local data density (via k‑NN) is a valid proxy for model confidence; ϕ(x) preserves relevant geometry. Evidence: Abstract and Section 2.2 describe adaptive rule; Figure 3 visualizes effect. Break condition: In high dimensions, k‑NN distances concentrate, reducing discriminative power; poor choice of ϕ(x) misrepresents density.

### Mechanism 3: Split conformal calibration with distance‑to‑envelope score
Claim: Using split conformal calibration with score s(x,y)=d(y, [ℓ(x),u(x)]) yields final interval C(x)=[ℓ(x)−ˆτ, u(x)+ˆτ] with guaranteed marginal coverage 1−α under exchangeability. Mechanism: Compute scores s_i = max{ℓ(x_i)−y_i, y_i−u(x_i)} on calibration set; let ˆτ be the ⌈(m+1)(1−α)⌉‑th order statistic. Core assumption: Exchangeability; credal envelope conditionally valid (covers at least 1−α₀ under any F∈F₀(x)). Evidence: Section 2.3; Theorem 3.3 proves distribution‑free guarantee. Break condition: Exchangeability violation (e.g., drift) invalidates coverage; severe envelope under‑coverage forces ˆτ inflation, losing adaptivity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Split Conformal Prediction:** Needed to achieve distribution‑free marginal coverage via calibration of a nonconformity score. Quick check: In split conformal regression, the 1−α interval is [q̂(α/2) − ˆτ, q̂(1−α/2) + ˆτ] where ˆτ is the (⌈(m+1)(1−α)⌉)-th score order statistic; score choice (e.g., absolute residual) affects conditional efficiency.
- **Bayesian Uncertainty (Epistemic vs. Aleatoric):** CREDO’s credal envelope explicitly represents epistemic uncertainty via posterior variation; distinguishing it from aleatoric noise is crucial for interpreting envelope width and decomposition. Quick check: A Bayesian credible interval for the mean captures parameter uncertainty (epistemic) but not observation noise; a posterior predictive interval for a new observation captures both epistemic and aleatoric.
- **Imprecise Probabilities and Credal Sets:** CREDO models epistemic uncertainty as a set of plausible predictive distributions (credal set). The quantile envelope is derived from this set. Quick check: Given a set of CDFs ℱ, the lower cumulative belief function 𝔽̲(y)=inf_{F∈ℱ} F(y). A central credible interval at level 1−α can be defined as [inf{y: 𝔽̲(y)≥α/2}, sup{y: 𝔽̄(y)≤1−α/2}] where 𝔽̄(y)=sup_{F∈ℱ} F(y).

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Data Splitter -> Bayesian Model Trainer -> Envelope Builder (with Adaptive γ(x) computation) -> Score Calculator (on calibration set) -> Calibration Quantile Estimator -> Predictor -> (Optional) Decomposition Module

Critical path: Training (posterior samples) -> Compute envelope on calibration points -> Compute scores -> Estimate ˆτ -> Predict on test points.

Design tradeoffs:
- Fixed vs adaptive γ: Fixed simpler; adaptive requires choosing ϕ, k, scarcity parameters but improves efficiency in varying density; may be unstable in high‑D.
- Model choice: Bayesian QNN (scalable, approximate) vs BART (more formal, slower); posterior quality directly impacts envelope validity.
- Calibration set size: Larger m reduces ˆτ variance but shrinks training data; tradeoff critical in small‑data regimes.
- Computational bottleneck: Posterior sampling (MCMC/MC‑dropout) dominates runtime.

Failure signatures:
- Marginal coverage below target: Check exchangeability, verify envelope covers ≥1−α₀ under posterior draws; ensure correct order statistic.
- Negative epistemic contribution: Envelope narrower than average aleatoric interval; cause: γ(x) too large or aleatoric estimate biased. Fix: increase γ_min or check aleatoric quantile calc.
- Erratic adaptive γ(x) widths: Inspect scarcity scores; high‑D features may need dimensionality reduction for ϕ; verify k not too small.

First 3 experiments:
1. Basic coverage test: Synthetic Bayesian linear regression; fixed γ; confirm empirical coverage ≈1−α; vary γ to see width impact.
2. Adaptivity validation: Toy piecewise mean with data‑sparse gap; compare fixed‑γ vs adaptive‑γ CREDO; measure ILR; expect adaptive higher ILR in gap.
3. Benchmark replication: Small real dataset (e.g., concrete); implement CREDO (QNN) vs CQR (CatBoost); compare SMIS and ILR; expect CREDO similar/better SMIS and higher ILR.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Heterogeneous model ensembles:** How would using multiple model classes (BART, NNs, GPs) for credal set construction affect performance vs single posterior? Basis: Paper suggests F₀(x) could be built from multiple priors or model families. Unresolved: No experiments; unknown if gains in outlier adaptivity come at efficiency cost.
2. **Skewed/multimodal conditionals:** Does CREDO’s central quantile envelope become uninformative for skewed/multimodal Y|X? Could IHDR-style regions be better? Basis: Paper acknowledges possibility to replace envelopes with HPD/IHDR. Unresolved: Theory assumes symmetric unimodal; score may not suit non‑convex sets; needs empirical validation.
3. **Sensitivity of adaptive γ(x):** How robust is γ(x) to hyperparameters (k, γ_min/max, τ_γ, m_γ) and choice of ϕ(x) in high‑dimensional settings? Basis: Adaptive scheme heuristically maps kNN scarcity to γ; no ablation study. Unresolved: kNN unreliable in high‑D; need systematic experiments across dimensions and representations.
4. **Validity with data‑dependent γ(x):** Does coverage guarantee hold if γ(x) is tuned on calibration set? Basis: Proof relies on split conformity; any reuse of D_cal in envelope construction violates exchangeability. Unresolved: Practical tuning may blur lines; no theory for double‑use scenarios.
5. **High‑dimensional and large‑scale performance:** How does CREDO compare to other epistemic‑aware methods on high‑dimensional data? Basis: Experiments on tabular data with p≤141; no evaluation on images/text/genomics. Unresolved: kNN density may fail; QNN posterior calibration unknown; need conditional coverage approximations.

## Limitations
- Coverage guarantee requires exchangeability and a credal envelope that minimally covers 1−α₀; model misspecification can invalidate both.
- Adaptive γ(x) relies on k‑NN density, which becomes unreliable in high dimensions due to distance concentration, potentially degrading adaptivity.
- Reproduction is hindered by missing details (random seeds, exact splits, t‑SNE parameters, baseline hyperparameters), preventing exact replication of reported numbers.

## Confidence
- **Coverage:** High (Theorem 3.3 proves marginal coverage under exchangeability).
- **Adaptivity (ILR):** Medium (strong empirical gains but depends on heuristic density estimate that may fail in high‑D).
- **SMIS:** Medium (competitive but sensitive to α₀ and model choice).
- **Decomposition:** Low (plausible but lacks ground‑truth validation; confounded by posterior quality).

## Next Checks
1. **High‑dimensional robustness test:** Generate synthetic data with varying dimensions (d=10,50,100). For each, run CREDO and measure ILR; expect adaptivity to degrade as d increases if k‑NN density becomes uninformative.
2. **Ablate α₀:** Vary the credal envelope level α₀ (0.05,0.1,0.2) while keeping target α=0.1. Examine coverage, SMIS, and ILR to determine optimal α₀ relative to α and assess sensitivity.
3. **Validate uncertainty decomposition:** Use synthetic Bayesian models with known aleatoric and epistemic components (e.g., Bayesian linear regression with known observation variance). Compare CREDO’s estimated aleatoric and epistemic widths to ground truth for both inliers and outliers.
