# Provably Efficient Algorithms for S- and Non-Rectangular Robust MDPs with General Parameterization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11387  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11387  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Provides the first sample complexity guarantees for average-reward robust MDPs with general policy parameterization and handles non‑rectangular uncertainty via gradient‑based methods, achieving improved sample efficiency through a multilevel Monte Carlo estimator and reduction to entropy‑regularized discounted robust MDPs.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses robust Markov decision processes (RMDPs) under both s‑rectangular and non‑rectangular uncertainty sets, moving beyond tabular representations to general policy parameterization. The authors introduce a novel algorithmic framework that reverses the traditional min‑max optimization order, enabling direct policy updates via gradient‑based methods over expressive policy classes. For the average‑reward setting—where strong duality often fails—they reduce the problem to an entropy‑regularized discounted robust MDP, restoring a minimax structure amenable to analysis. Key technical contributions include Lipschitz and smoothness bounds that are independent of state space size and a multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) gradient estimator that achieves an \(\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})\) sample complexity improvement over prior fixed‑horizon estimators. The work provides sample complexity bounds: \(O(\varepsilon^{-5})\) for discounted s‑rectangular RMDPs, \(O(C_f^2 \varepsilon^{-4})\) for discounted non‑rectangular, and \(O(C_f^2 \varepsilon^{-10.5} H^{5.5})\) for average‑reward non‑rectangular. It also extends to infinite state spaces via linear transition parameterization, marking the first guarantees for general parameterization beyond (s,a)-rectangularity and the first for average‑reward RMDPs.

## Method Summary
The core method reverses the min‑max optimization order, transforming the robust MDP into a direct policy optimization problem. For the average‑reward setting, the authors reduce the problem to an entropy‑regularized discounted robust MDP by choosing the discount factor \(\gamma = 1 - \Theta(\varepsilon/H)\), thereby restoring a strong duality property. This reduction allows the use of gradient‑based methods: for s‑rectangular uncertainty, projected gradient descent updates the policy; for non‑rectangular uncertainty, Frank‑Wolfe handles the linear constraints arising from the uncertainty set. Gradient estimation employs a multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) approach that averages nested expectations across multiple sample sizes, yielding a variance that does not depend on the horizon and thus an \(\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})\) sample complexity improvement. The analysis establishes that the robust value function is Lipschitz and smooth with respect to policy parameters, with bounds independent of the state space size—a crucial property for general function approximation.

## Key Results
- Establishes Lipschitz and smoothness properties for the robust value function that are independent of the size of the state space, enabling gradient‑based optimization with general function approximation.
- Introduces a multilevel Monte Carlo gradient estimator with \(\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})\) sample complexity, improving upon prior fixed‑horizon estimators by an \(O(\varepsilon^{-2})\) factor.
- Provides sample complexity guarantees: \(O(\varepsilon^{-5})\) for discounted s‑rectangular RMDPs; \(O(C_f^2 \varepsilon^{-4})\) for discounted non‑rectangular; \(O(C_f^2 \varepsilon^{-10.5} H^{5.5})\) for average‑reward non‑rectangular. The work claims to be the first to offer such guarantees for general policy parameterization and for average‑reward robust MDPs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach succeeds because reversing the usual min‑max order avoids solving a hard inner maximization repeatedly; instead, the policy is updated directly using gradients of the worst‑case value. The reduction to an entropy‑regularized discounted robust MDP restores strong duality, which is essential for deriving gradient estimators and convergence proofs. The Lipschitz and smoothness bounds (independent of \(|S|\)) ensure that gradient steps are stable and that the function approximation error does not explode with state‑space size. The MLMC estimator controls variance by telescoping across levels, avoiding the horizon‑dependent variance that plagues fixed‑horizon methods. For s‑rectangular uncertainty, the policy optimization problem remains convex, justifying projected gradient descent. For non‑rectangular uncertainty, the feasible set becomes polytope‑like, making Frank‑Wolfe appropriate. The analysis carefully handles the coupling between policy updates and the inner robust evaluation, maintaining sufficient accuracy with limited samples.

## Foundational Learning
- **Robust MDPs** – Model uncertainty in transition probabilities using an uncertainty set; objective is to maximize worst‑case performance. *Why needed:* Captures model misspecification and provides safety. *Quick check:* Verify that for a given policy, the worst‑case value is obtained by solving a linear program over the uncertainty set.
- **s‑rectangular vs non‑rectangular uncertainty** – s‑rectangular decomposes per state; non‑rectangular couples states, yielding larger, more expressive sets. *Why needed:* Trade‑off between expressiveness and computational tractability. *Quick check:* Check if the uncertainty set is a product over states (s‑rectangular) or contains coupled constraints (non‑rectangular).
- **Entropy regularization** – Adds \(-\eta \sum_a \pi(a|s)\log\pi(a|s)\) to the objective; encourages exploration and yields smooth gradients. *Why needed:* Restores strong duality in average‑reward robust MDPs and enables gradient methods. *Quick check:* Confirm that the regularized problem remains concave in the policy for a fixed state.
- **Policy gradient methods** – Update policy parameters in the direction of the performance gradient. *Why needed:* Allows optimization over large/continuous action spaces. *Quick check:* Estimate gradient via likelihood ratio or perturbation methods and verify it is unbiased.
- **Multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC)** – Variance reduction technique that averages nested Monte Carlo estimates across multiple sampling levels. *Why needed:* Achieves \(\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})\) sample complexity for gradient estimation in long‑horizon problems. *Quick check:* Show that the total variance is sum of variances per level and that the cost per level is balanced.
- **Average‑reward vs discounted MDPs** – Average‑reward optimizes steady‑state performance; discounting weights future rewards exponentially. *Why needed:* Different application settings; average‑reward often lacks strong duality. *Quick check:* For a given MDP, verify that discounted value with \(\gamma \to 1\) converges to average‑reward under certain conditions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Policy network \(\pi_\theta\) → Robust Value Evaluation (inner maximization over uncertainty set) → Gradient Computation (MLMC estimator) → Parameter Update (Projected Gradient Descent for s‑rectangular; Frank‑Wolfe for non‑rectangular) → New policy \(\theta'\)

**Critical path:** The main loop consists of (1) evaluating the worst‑case value for the current policy (via inner optimization), (2) estimating the policy gradient with MLMC, (3) updating policy parameters using the chosen update rule, and (4) repeating until convergence.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Choosing between projected gradient descent (requires convex feasible set, faster convergence) and Frank‑Wolfe (handles linear constraints, slower but simpler projection).  
- The reduction to discounted setting introduces dependence on the optimal policy span \(H\), increasing sample complexity; a direct average‑reward analysis could avoid this but is currently an open problem.  
- Step size and number of MLMC levels must balance bias and variance; too few levels increase variance, too many increase computational cost.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Gradient estimator variance diverges (e.g., mixing assumptions violated).  
- Inner maximization not solved accurately, causing bias in policy updates.  
- Policy collapses to deterministic early, leading to poor exploration and high variance.  
- Numerical instability when discount factor \(\gamma\) is very close to 1.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Validate MLMC gradient estimator variance on a small tabular s‑rectangular RMDP and compare against a fixed‑horizon estimator.  
2. Compare convergence speed of projected gradient descent and Frank‑Wolfe on a synthetic non‑rectangular robust MDP.  
3. Evaluate the effect of varying the discount factor (through the \(\gamma = 1 - \Theta(\varepsilon/H)\) schedule) on sample complexity in an average‑reward setting, measuring the achieved \(\varepsilon\) accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the ergodicity assumption in the MLMC gradient estimator be relaxed to the unichain assumption for robust MDPs with general policy parameterization?
- **Basis in paper:** [Inferred] From Section H (Limitations and Future Work): "Our current analysis requires the ergodicity assumption... to establish mixing time bounds. A natural extension would be to relax this to the weaker unichain assumption."
- **Why unresolved:** Ergodicity ensures uniform mixing and finite mixing time, crucial for MLMC variance bounds. Unichain MDPs may have multiple recurrent classes or non-uniform mixing, which could invalidate current concentration guarantees.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the MLMC convergence analysis to unichain settings, possibly by adapting techniques from Satheesh & Aggarwal (2026) for non-robust average-reward MDPs to the robust case, and proving sample complexity guarantees under unichain.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What are the information‑theoretic lower bounds on sample complexity for robust MDPs with general policy parameterization and non‑rectangular uncertainty sets?
- **Basis in paper:** [Inferred] From Section H: "the gap between our current bounds and information‑theoretic lower bounds remains an interesting open question."
- **Why unresolved:** No minimax lower bounds exist for this general setting; existing lower bounds apply only to tabular or restricted cases (e.g., (s,a)-rectangular), leaving optimality of the proposed algorithms unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Proving minimax lower bounds for sample complexity in terms of problem parameters (e.g., state space size, action space size, horizon, accuracy \(\varepsilon\)) for both s‑rectangular and non‑rectangular robust MDPs with general function approximation, potentially using information‑theoretic tools like Fano’s inequality or Bayesian reductions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the sample complexity of average‑reward robust MDPs be improved by developing a direct analysis that avoids the reduction to the discounted setting?
- **Basis in paper:** [Explicit] From Section H: "(i) direct average‑reward analysis that avoids reduction to the discounted setting" is listed as a critical future direction.
- **Why unresolved:** The current reduction introduces a dependency on the optimal policy span \(H\) via \(\gamma = 1 - \Theta(\varepsilon/H)\), leading to large sample complexity \(O(C_f^2 \varepsilon^{-10.5} H^{5.5})\). Direct analysis might eliminate or reduce this dependence.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Designing new algorithms or analytic techniques for average‑reward robust MDPs without discount reduction, and proving sample complexity bounds with improved dependence on \(H\) and \(\varepsilon\) (e.g., polynomial in \(1/\varepsilon\) without \(H\) factors), or showing that such improvement is impossible via lower bounds.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can adaptive discount factor schemes be employed to reduce the sample complexity of reduced average‑reward robust MDPs?
- **Basis in paper:** [Explicit] From Section H: "(iii) adaptive discount factor schemes that reduce samples needed in early iterations" is proposed as a future improvement.
- **Why unresolved:** The fixed choice \(\gamma = 1 - \Theta(\varepsilon/H)\) is conservative, as it assumes worst‑case span \(H\) throughout learning. Adaptive schemes could start with larger \(\gamma\) (smaller effective horizon) and adjust as learning progresses, potentially reducing early‑stage sample requirements.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing algorithms that dynamically update \(\gamma\) based on learning progress (e.g., via confidence intervals on value gaps), and proving theoretical guarantees showing improved sample complexity (e.g., reduced dependence on \(H\) or \(\varepsilon\)), supported by empirical validation on benchmark problems.

## Limitations
- Theoretical sample complexity for non‑rectangular average‑reward setting has high polynomial dependencies (e.g., \(\varepsilon^{-10.5} H^{5.5}\)); these may be pessimistic and not reflect practical performance.
- The reduction to entropy‑regularized discounted robust MDPs introduces an extra regularization parameter; its effect on solution quality and need for careful tuning are unclear.
- Claims of “first” guarantees for general parameterization and average‑reward RMDPs might be overstated given related concurrent work (e.g., policy gradient for non‑rectangular uncertainty, robust average‑reward MDPs).
- Analysis assumes exact oracles for inner maximization; practical implementations could incur significant overhead not captured by sample complexity.
- No empirical evaluation provided; real‑world behavior of the algorithms with function approximation remains unknown.
