# Alignment Reduces Expressed but Not Encoded Gender Bias: A Unified Framework and Study
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24125
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24125
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Supervised fine-tuning reduces expressed gender bias in outputs but does not remove underlying latent gender associations, which persist and can be reactivated.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether aligning large language models (LLMs) for gender bias mitigation truly reduces underlying bias or merely controls its expression. The authors propose a unified framework that measures both intrinsic bias (latent representations) and extrinsic bias (generated outputs) using identical neutral prompts. Experiments on three instruction-tuned LLMs (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1, gemma-7b-it) fine-tuned with LoRA on bias mitigation datasets show that supervised fine-tuning significantly reduces expressed bias in structured tasks but fails to eliminate latent gender-polarized information. Latent and expressed bias are strongly correlated, and directional ablation confirms that latent associations functionally contribute to output bias. Notably, debiasing effects do not generalize to open-ended story generation, indicating that output-level evaluations may overestimate alignment effectiveness.

## Method Summary
The study employs a unified protocol where the same set of neutral prompts (e.g., “My friend is a [entity]”) is used to collect both model outputs (for extrinsic bias) and final-token hidden states (for intrinsic bias). Extrinsic bias is quantified via a polarization score (Biaspol) based on the gender distribution of generated completions. Intrinsic bias is measured by a latent polarization score (Slatent) derived from projections onto a estimated gender direction (vg). The framework is applied to base and fine-tuned models across concepts with varying gender associations (e.g., Professions, Sports, Months). To test causality, directional ablation removes the gender component from representations before regeneration. Experiments compare structured prompts with realistic tasks (ESL exercises, story generation) and include a “jailbreak” prompt to assess bias reactivation.

## Key Results
- Supervised fine-tuning significantly reduces expressed polarization (Biaspol) for gendered concepts in structured tasks, but latent polarization (Slatent) persists post-tuning.
- Latent and expressed bias scores are strongly correlated across layers for gendered concepts, suggesting latent associations drive output bias.
- Debiasing effects observed on structured benchmarks fail to generalize to open-ended story generation, where fine-tuned models show no reduction in expressed bias.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Supervised fine-tuning reduces expressed gender bias via behavioral control that suppresses output without erasing latent gender associations.
- **Mechanism:** Alignment introduces steerable parameters that gate generation away from stereotypes, but internal representations retain gender-polarized information, which can be reactivated under adversarial conditions.
- **Core assumption:** Latent gender associations are functionally used during generation, as directional ablation affects bias.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “supervised fine-tuning significantly reduced expressed bias… but latent gender associations persisted.”
  - [section 4.3]: “After fine-tuning, latent polarization is attenuated but persists… well above the random reference distribution.”
  - [corpus]: Weak; corpus neighbors discuss alignment persistence (e.g., “Refusal in language models is mediated by a single direction”), but direct evidence is from paper's ablation.
- **Break condition:** If latent bias were fully removed, directional ablation after fine-tuning would not further reduce polarization, but it does (e.g., Fig. 6).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Under identical prompts, intrinsic latent gender scores and extrinsic expressed bias scores are strongly correlated, indicating encoded bias influences output.
- **Mechanism:** The gender direction in latent space biases token prediction, leading to systematic gender associations in completions; Spearman correlation captures rank consistency across entities.
- **Core assumption:** Final-token representations condition generation, and gender direction is a stable linear axis.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “we find a consistent association between latent gender information and expressed bias when measured under the unified protocol.”
  - [section 4.4]: “For gendered concepts in the Base-Base configuration, correlation is high from the earliest layers.”
  - [corpus]: Mixed; prior work [11,4] reported weak correlations, but this paper's unified protocol resolves heterogeneity.
- **Break condition:** Correlation near zero across layers for gendered concepts would sever the link, but it remains high in Base-Base and FT-Base configs.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Debiasing on structured benchmarks fails to generalize to open-ended tasks because persistent latent bias dominates under complex generation conditions.
- **Mechanism:** Fine-tuning's behavioral control is effective in constrained settings but insufficient for open-ended generation where latent associations more strongly drive output.
- **Core assumption:** Task complexity modulates the expression of latent bias.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “debiasing effects observed on structured benchmarks did not generalize, e.g., to the case of story generation.”
  - [section 5.2]: “fine-tuning fails to reduce expressed bias” in story generation, while it reduces bias in ESL tasks.
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; assumption based on paper's realistic task results.
- **Break condition:** If latent bias were minimal, fine-tuning effects would generalize across tasks, but they do not (story generation shows no reduction).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Base LLM -> LoRA-fine-tuned model -> Prompt generator (neutral personas + entities) -> Output generator (completions) & Representation extractor (final-token hidden states) -> Gender classifier (LLM-as-judge) & Gender direction estimator (mean-difference) -> Bias calculators (Biaspol, Slatent) -> Correlation module -> Ablation module

**Critical path:**
Input entities & personas construct prompts -> for each prompt: generate output (extrinsic) & extract representation (intrinsic) -> compute entity-level bias/latent scores -> aggregate to concept-level polarization -> correlate across entities -> apply directional ablation to representations -> regenerate outputs & remeasure polarization.

**Design tradeoffs:**
Identical prompts enable clean comparison but may not capture prompt variation in real use; binary gender focus simplifies analysis but excludes non-binary and other biases; structured prompts allow controlled measurement but may overestimate alignment effectiveness compared to open-ended tasks.

**Failure signatures:**
(1) Low/no correlation between intrinsic and extrinsic scores for gendered concepts; (2) Directional ablation after fine-tuning does not reduce polarization; (3) Fine-tuning reduces latent polarization (contradicts persistence finding); (4) Polarization reduction on structured prompts but not on realistic tasks like story generation.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Measure extrinsic polarization (Biaspol) on structured prompts (e.g., “My friend is a [profession]”) for base model and after fine-tuning.
2. Measure intrinsic latent polarization (Slatent) on same prompts across layers, compare to random-direction baseline.
3. Compute Spearman correlation between entity-level latent scores and extrinsic bias scores; perform directional ablation to test if removing gender direction reduces extrinsic polarization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the proposed unified framework be validly extended to languages with grammatical gender (e.g., French, Spanish) to measure intrinsic and extrinsic bias?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state in the conclusion: “Future work could extend the proposed framework to other languages. However, constructing truly neutral prompts may be difficult in strongly gendered languages such as French or Spanish, where gender markers can appear not only in entity terms but also in persona descriptions.”
- **Why unresolved:** The framework relies on neutral prompts to isolate implicit social bias, but grammatical gender in such languages may conflate linguistic gender with social bias, potentially confounding measurements. The paper only evaluates in English, so cross-lingual validity is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the framework to multiple gendered languages, design prompts that control for grammatical gender (e.g., using gender-neutral forms where available), and compare intrinsic/extrinsic bias scores to the English baseline. Human evaluations would be needed to confirm prompt neutrality.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can the framework be adapted to measure non-binary and intersectional biases (e.g., race, age, disability) that may not be captured by a single latent direction?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note in the conclusion: “Extending the framework to other biases (e.g., race, age, disability) also raises additional challenges: unlike binary gender, these attributes may not reduce to a single latent direction, and defining relevant social groups is more complex than for gender, with potential intersectional interactions across dimensions.”
- **Why unresolved:** The current method assumes gender is encoded along one direction via a mean-difference approach. Intersectional and non-binary biases likely involve multiple, non-orthogonal dimensions, making the single-direction projection inadequate. Defining social groups and measuring their associations without oversimplification is also problematic.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop multi-dimensional bias metrics (e.g., using multiple contrastive pairs or concept activation vectors), test on datasets with intersectional annotations, and validate through correlation with extrinsic bias across diverse tasks. Investigate whether latent space contains independent, interpretable directions for each bias attribute.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do debiasing effects from supervised fine-tuning on structured benchmarks fail to generalize to open-ended story generation, and what task characteristics determine bias reactivation?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper finds that fine-tuning reduces expressed bias in structured settings and ESL exercises but not in story generation (Section 5.2). The authors suggest this indicates output-level evaluation may overestimate alignment effectiveness, but the underlying reasons are not explored.
- **Why unresolved:** Only two realistic tasks (ESL and story generation) were compared, so the specific features of story generation (e.g., need for creativity, narrative stereotypes, longer context) that trigger persistent bias remain unclear. The paper does not analyze generated content for stereotype markers or model capabilities that might mediate bias.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically vary task types (e.g., narrative vs. instructional, short vs. long generations), analyze outputs for stereotypical content, and correlate task complexity with bias scores. Test whether model capabilities (e.g., creativity, contextual coherence) predict bias expression beyond fine-tuning status.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does supervised fine-tuning for bias reduction modify the model's underlying knowledge of gender stereotypes, or does it primarily implement output-level behavioral control without altering internal representations?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors conclude that alignment “acts as a behavioral control mechanism rather than removing the underlying gender-related knowledge” based on persistent latent associations and jailbreak reactivation. However, they do not directly probe the model's factual knowledge about gender stereotypes (e.g., via QA tasks).
- **Why unresolved:** The study shows latent gender associations remain post-fine-tuning and can be reactivated, but it does not test whether the model's explicit “knowledge” (e.g., answering “Who is more likely to be a nurse?”) has changed. Output suppression could mask unchanged knowledge, or knowledge itself might be partially altered.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate the fine-tuned model on factual tasks probing gender stereotype knowledge (e.g., stereotypical associations in cloze tests or QA), separate from generative bias. Compare changes in factual accuracy with shifts in latent representations and output bias. Use techniques like knowledge editing to see if targeted changes affect both intrinsic and extrinsic metrics.

## Limitations
- Unspecified neutral persona prompts and generation hyperparameters may affect reproducibility.
- Details of LoRA fine-tuning dataset construction and gender direction estimation are underspecified.
- Study limited to three models, binary gender focus, and synthetic prompts; generalizability to other biases or languages is unclear.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (SFT suppresses output without removing latent associations):** High confidence. Supported by directional ablation and consistent results across models.
- **Claim 2 (Latent and expressed bias are strongly correlated):** Medium confidence. Correlation depends on unified protocol and stable gender direction; prior work shows weaker links.
- **Claim 3 (Debiasing fails to generalize to open-ended tasks):** Medium confidence. Evidence from one story generation task; needs more tasks and models for robust generalization.
