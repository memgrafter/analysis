# Revisiting Northrop Frye's Four Myths Theory with Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.15678  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15678  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Proposed a universal character function framework (protagonist, mentor, antagonist, companion) specialized into 16 genre-specific roles; validated via six LLMs across 40 narratives, achieving 82.5% balanced accuracy and strong inter-model agreement (Fleiss' κ=0.600).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of computational attention to character functions within Northrop Frye’s four narrative genres (comedy, romance, tragedy, satire). It introduces a framework that derives four universal character functions from Jungian archetypes and further specializes them into sixteen genre-specific roles. The framework is evaluated through a multi-model study using six large language models to score character-role correspondences across a corpus of 40 narrative works, including both archetypally valid and deliberately subverted samples. Results show substantial mean balanced accuracy of 82.5% with strong agreement among models, though performance varies by genre and role, with satire companion roles proving particularly challenging (52.5%) due to intentional archetypal subversion. The work demonstrates the potential of LLMs to operationalize literary theory while highlighting limitations in reproducibility, corpus diversity, and evaluation design.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed a corpus of 40 narrative works representing Frye’s four genres, with a mix of valid (archetypal) and invalid (subverted) character assignments. Six LLMs were prompted to map characters to the 16 specialized roles derived from the four universal functions (protagonist, mentor, antagonist, companion). Model outputs were aggregated and compared against expert-derived gold standards, yielding balanced accuracy and Fleiss’ κ as primary metrics. No human-LLM benchmarking was performed, and the LLMs themselves served both as framework operationalizers and evaluators.

## Key Results
- Derived 16 genre-specific character roles from 4 universal Jungian functions.
- LLMs achieved overall balanced accuracy of 82.5% and Fleiss’ κ = 0.600.
- Genre accuracies: romance (89.9%), tragedy (86.4%), comedy (77.6%), satire (72.7%).
- Role accuracies: protagonist (99.2% in tragedy) to satire companion (52.5%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach works by grounding character classification in two widely recognized theoretical layers: (1) Jungian archetypes provide a set of culturally pervasive, semantically rich prototypes that are stable across narratives; (2) Frye’s genre conventions impose specific constraints and expectations on how those prototypes manifest. This combination reduces the ambiguity of role assignment. Pre-trained LLMs have been exposed to a vast array of narratives during training, enabling them to implicitly recognize these archetypal patterns and their genre-specific variations. The specialization into 16 roles further sharpens distinctions, allowing models to achieve high agreement despite the complexity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Jungian Archetypes**  
  *Why needed:* Supply universal character prototypes (e.g., Hero, Mentor) that anchor the four universal functions.  
  *Quick check:* Can you map “trickster” to the companion function? (Usually yes, but context matters.)
- **Frye’s Four Narrative Genres**  
  *Why needed:* Define genre-specific conventions that transform universal functions into specialized roles.  
  *Quick check:* Does a character who escapes bondage and wins a happy marriage fit comedy or tragedy? (Comedy)
- **Character Function vs. Trait**  
  *Why needed:* Distinguishes narrative role (what a character *does* in the plot) from psychological attributes, enabling systematic annotation.  
  *Quick check:* Is “loyalty” a function? No—it’s a trait; a function would be “sidekick who supports protagonist.”
- **Balanced Accuracy**  
  *Why needed:* Provides a fair performance measure when role frequencies are uneven.  
  *Quick check:* If a model always predicts the most common role, balanced accuracy approaches the reciprocal of the number of classes; it is not high.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Corpus (40 works) → Character-role Annotation Schema (4 universal × 4 genres = 16 roles) → LLM Ensemble (6 models) → Role Assignment Queries → Prediction Aggregation → Metrics (balanced accuracy, Fleiss’ κ)  

**Critical path:**  
1. Define universal functions and genre-specific specializations.  
2. Build gold-standard annotations for the corpus.  
3. Design prompts and query each LLM.  
4. Collect predictions and resolve disagreements.  
5. Compute performance statistics.  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Multiple LLMs increase robustness but raise computational cost and complexity.  
- Including subverted (invalid) samples tests framework limits but may penalize accuracy and require careful interpretation.  
- Using LLMs for both framework instantiation and evaluation introduces circularity; human validation would strengthen claims.  

**Failure signatures:**  
- Very low accuracy in a specific genre (e.g., satire) signals possible mismatch between LLM priors and genre conventions.  
- Poor performance on a particular role (e.g., companion) indicates ambiguity in its definition or insufficient textual cues.  
- Low Fleiss’ κ among models suggests the framework may be underspecified or that prompts are unstable.  

**First experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Run the exact prompts from the paper on all six LLMs; verify reported overall accuracy and κ.  
2. **Ablation – valid-only:** Remove all subverted samples to measure pure archetypal recognition.  
3. **Error analysis – satire companion:** Manually inspect all misclassifications for that role to identify systematic patterns (e.g., sarcasm detection, role blending).

## Limitations
- Reproducibility gap: insufficient details on LLM prompts, sampling seeds, and statistical tests.
- Corpus bias: likely skewed toward modern Western narratives, limiting applicability to Frye’s broader mythic scope.
- Evaluation circularity: LLMs both operationalize and judge the framework; absence of human expert validation.

## Confidence
- Framework derivation (Jung → Frye → 16 roles): **Medium**  
- LLM-based validation (overall 82.5% accuracy): **Low**  
- Genre performance (stable genres): **Medium‑High**; satire companion: **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt & metric auditing:** Replicate the study using exactly the same prompts and scoring criteria; report variance across random seeds and model versions.  
2. **Diverse corpus expansion:** Test the framework on 100+ narratives spanning non‑Western traditions (e.g., *Mahabharata*, *The Tale of Genji*) and pre‑20th‑century texts.  
3. **Human‑LLM benchmarking:** Collect expert literary scholar annotations on a stratified sample (e.g., 5 works per genre) and compute concordance with LLM judgments to guide prompt refinement.
