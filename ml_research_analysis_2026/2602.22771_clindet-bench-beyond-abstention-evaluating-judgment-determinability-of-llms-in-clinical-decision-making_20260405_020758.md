# ClinDet-Bench: Beyond Abstention, Evaluating Judgment Determinability of LLMs in Clinical Decision-Making

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22771
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22771
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs fail to identify whether a clinical judgment is determinable from incomplete information, exhibiting a trade-off between premature conclusions on undeterminable cases and unnecessary abstention on determinable ones.

## Executive Summary
Clinical LLMs must not only provide answers but also recognize when available information is insufficient for a reliable decision—a capability termed judgment determinability. This paper introduces ClinDet-Bench, a benchmark built from 16 clinical scoring systems to evaluate this skill. Scenarios are logically classified as complete, incomplete-determinable (conclusion fixed despite missing items), or incomplete-undeterminable (outcome may vary). Results show models explain scoring systems accurately and perform near-perfectly with complete data, but struggle severely with incomplete cases, often imputing missing values or abstaining incorrectly. This reveals a critical safety gap: LLMs do not reliably assess whether a decision can be made, beyond simple uncertainty expression.

## Method Summary
ClinDet-Bench comprises 94 synthetic scenarios derived from 16 rule-based clinical scoring systems (e.g., CHADS2, Wells Score). Each scenario lists present/absent items and asks whether a criterion is "Met," "Not met," or "Unable to determine." Ground truth determinability is computed via score ranges: given missing items, the minimum possible score (assuming all missing items absent) and maximum (assuming all present) are compared to the threshold. If both fall on the same side, the case is determinable; otherwise undeterminable. Models first undergo an Explanation Task to verify knowledge of each scoring system. Only systems correctly explained proceed to the Clinical Decision Task, run under three prompts: Base, Chain-of-Thought (CoT), and "Safe." Performance is measured by accuracy within each information condition, and error analysis on CoT responses classifies failures as imputation (assigning plausible values to missing items) or abstention based on incompleteness.

## Key Results
- Models achieved 88% accuracy explaining scoring systems and >0.93 accuracy on complete-information cases.
- On incomplete-undeterminable cases, accuracy plummeted (e.g., GPT-5.2: 0.11); on incomplete-determinable cases, models often abstained unnecessarily.
- A negative trade-off exists (Spearman r = -0.45) between accuracy on determinable and undeterminable cases.
- Error analysis of CoT responses: 81.6% involved imputation of missing values; 14.4% were abstentions based solely on incompleteness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1  
- **Claim:** The benchmark unambiguously labels determinability by exploiting the monotonic, additive structure of clinical scoring systems.  
- **Mechanism:** For incomplete cases, compute the minimum possible score (*S*<sub>min</sub>) by treating all missing items as absent (0 points) and the maximum (*S*<sub>max</sub>) by treating them as present (full points). The judgment is determinable if *S*<sub>min</sub> ≥ threshold (must be "Met") or *S*<sub>max</sub> < threshold (must be "Not met"); otherwise undeterminable.  
- **Core assumption:** Scoring rules assign non-negative points and rely solely on total score vs. a fixed threshold.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [Section 3.3.3] describes the score-range logic; [abstract] mentions decomposing scenarios into determinable/undeterminable conditions.

### Mechanism 2  
- **Claim:** Evaluating only on correctly explained scoring systems isolates failures of reasoning under incomplete information from knowledge deficits.  
- **Mechanism:** The Explanation Task acts as a filter; models must demonstrate understanding of a system's items and scoring before facing incomplete scenarios. Thus, subsequent errors reflect inability to handle missing data, not ignorance of the rules.  
- **Core assumption:** A correct explanation implies sufficient operational knowledge to apply the system under complete information.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [Section 3.4.2] explicitly states this filtering; [abstract] notes models were evaluated only on systems they explained correctly.

### Mechanism 3  
- **Claim:** LLMs default to imputing plausible values for missing items rather than considering all possible value combinations, causing premature judgments.  
- **Mechanism:** In undeterminable cases, correct reasoning requires acknowledging that missing items could take extreme values (0 or max points) that might alter the conclusion. Models instead assume likely (intermediate) values, leading to false certainty.  
- **Core assumption:** Human expert reasoning systematically explores the full hypothesis space of missing values; autoregressive LLMs favor statistically probable completions.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [Section 4.3] error analysis shows 81.6% imputation; [Section 5.2] hypothesizes bias from next-token prediction training.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Clinical scoring systems**  
  *Why needed:* The benchmark’s building blocks are rule-based tools with itemized points and thresholds; understanding their additive nature is key to grasping determinability logic.  
  *Quick check:* For CHADS2, list components, point values, and stroke risk threshold.

- **Concept: Judgment determinability**  
  *Why needed:* This is the novel evaluation axis—distinct from uncertainty about ground truth, it asks whether available information alone fixes the decision.  
  *Quick check:* Given a patient with known items summing to 3 points and one missing item worth up to 2 points, is the decision determinable? Why?

- **Concept: Model abstention vs. determinability**  
  *Why needed:* Prior work focuses on abstention; this paper shows abstention is not a reliable proxy for determinability—models must recognize both when to answer and when to withhold judgment.  
  *Quick check:* What is the difference between a case where the model should abstain (undeterminable) and one where it should answer (determinable) despite missing data?

## Architecture Onboarding
Scoring system definitions -> Scenario generator -> Prompt templates -> Evaluation harness -> Scoring module -> Error analysis toolkit

**Critical path:**  
1. For each LLM, run Explanation Task on all 16 systems; keep only correctly explained ones.  
2. For each kept system, generate its 6 scenario types (or 5 for low-threshold systems).  
3. Run Clinical Decision Task on these scenarios using Base, CoT, and Safe prompts; record judgments and CoT reasoning.  
4. Aggregate accuracy by condition (Complete, Determinable, Undeterminable); compute trade-off correlation.  
5. Perform error analysis on incorrect CoT responses (physician annotation).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Synthetic cases ensure logical control but lack real-world clinical heterogeneity.  
- Limited to scoring systems with clear thresholds and additive points; may not generalize to nuanced diagnosis.  
- Scenario count (94) is modest but sufficient for signal; larger scale might improve robustness.  
- Only three prompt variants tested; other prompts could shift results.  
- Temperature fixed at 1.0; deterministic sampling might change performance.

**Failure signatures:**  
- In incomplete-undeterminable: outputs "Met" or "Not met" instead of "Unable to determine."  
- In incomplete-determinable: outputs "Unable to determine" despite fixed conclusion.  
- CoT traces show imputation (assigning specific values to missing items) in >80% of errors.  
- Self-evaluation does not improve accuracy; self-rated "correct" responses remain wrong in undeterminable cases.  
- Negative correlation between determinable and undeterminable accuracy across models.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *Replication:* Run the benchmark on GPT-4o with all prompts; confirm near-perfect Complete accuracy and low Undeterminable accuracy, matching main negative result.  
2. *Prompt ablation:* Compare Base, CoT, and Safe prompts; quantify how abstention rate changes and whether Undeterminable accuracy improves at the cost of Determinable accuracy, testing the trade-off.  
3. *Error classification:* For 50 random incorrect CoT responses in undeterminable cases, independently annotate as "imputation" or "abstention based on incompleteness"; verify the imputation rate aligns with ~81.6%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
Can LLMs be trained or prompted to generalize judgment determinability identification from structured scoring systems to complex, unstructured clinical reasoning tasks like differential diagnosis?  
*Basis:* The authors state: "First, we focused on clinical scoring systems with clearly defined input items and thresholds; generalizability to more complex clinical tasks such as diagnostic reasoning remains to be examined." (Limitations)  
*Why unresolved:* The benchmark uses simplified, rule-based systems. Real-world tasks involve ambiguous, multi-step reasoning with incomplete narratives, possibly requiring different cognitive processes.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Develop a benchmark with realistic, incomplete clinical cases (e.g., progressive history-taking) and test transfer from ClinDet-Bench. Analyze reasoning traces to see if models apply the same "consider all hypotheses" logic.

**Open Question 2**  
Do advanced prompting techniques (e.g., few-shot, self-consistency) or model training (e.g., fine-tuning on determinability tasks) mitigate the trade-off between premature judgment and unnecessary abstention?  
*Basis:* The authors note: "Third, not all approaches were evaluated; prompt optimization, few-shot prompting, and training-time methods such as supervised fine-tuning may improve performance." (Limitations)  
*Why unresolved:* The study tested only Base, CoT, and Safe prompts at temperature 1.0. It is unknown if methodological interventions can break the observed negative correlation.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically evaluate prompting strategies (e.g., explicit "list possible missing values" instructions) and fine-tuning on a determinability dataset. Measure if the trade-off persists.

**Open Question 3**  
When LLMs produce correct judgments under incomplete information, do they use robust reasoning (considering all missing-value hypotheses) or flawed shortcuts (e.g., imputation of likely values)?  
*Basis:* The authors state: "Fifth, the error analysis was conducted only on incorrect responses; models that reached correct conclusions may still have relied on flawed reasoning." (Limitations)  
*Why unresolved:* Correct outcomes could arise from lucky guesses or biased heuristics, not genuine determinability assessment. This hidden failure mode risks overestimating reliability.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct fine-grained analysis of *all* correct responses by having experts annotate reasoning chains for exhaustive hypothesis consideration. Compare to randomized ablations where critical missing items are flipped.

**Open Question 4**  
Is the failure to identify determinability fundamentally due to LLMs' autoregressive training objective (favoring plausible completions) rather than a lack of knowledge or reasoning steps?  
*Basis:* Inferred from discussion: "LLMs, trained to predict the most likely continuation, may be biased toward plausible completions. The error analysis supports this: the predominant errors involved treating unmentioned findings as absent rather than considering alternative possibilities."  
*Why unresolved:* The paper shows models fail at a task requiring consideration of *unlikely* hypotheses, but does not isolate whether this stems from the training objective versus architectural or data limitations.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Compare models trained with objectives encouraging exhaustive reasoning (e.g., reinforcement learning for logical coverage) or use controlled synthetic tasks where "plausible" imputation leads to wrong answers, testing if performance drops relative to tasks where plausible = correct.

## Limitations
- **Synthetic benchmark validity:** Scenarios are generated from simplified, additive scoring systems; real clinical decisions involve ambiguity and context not captured here.
- **Unclear explanation rubric:** The physician's criteria for a "correct" explanation in the filtering step is not detailed, risking inconsistency in which systems are evaluated per model.
- **Generalizability gap:** Findings may not extend to complex, unstructured clinical reasoning tasks like differential diagnosis.

## Confidence
- **High:** LLMs perform near-perfectly on complete-information cases but fail on incomplete ones, revealing a determinability gap. *Evidence: Direct experimental results (Table 3).*
- **Medium:** A trade-off exists between avoiding premature judgments (in undeterminable cases) and making unnecessarily cautious abstentions (in determinable cases). *Evidence: Reported Spearman correlation, but requires independent replication for robustness.*
- **Low:** The primary failure mode is "imputation" (assuming plausible values for missing items). *Evidence: Based on post-hoc CoT analysis by a single physician; inter-rater reliability for error classification is not established.*

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate the core trade-off:** Using the public ClinDet-Bench dataset, run the Clinical Decision Task for at least two models (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude Opus) with Base, CoT, and Safe prompts. Compute Spearman correlation between accuracy on Incomplete-Determinable and Incomplete-Undeterminable cases to verify the reported negative relationship.
2. **Verify error classification scheme:** On 50 random incorrect CoT responses in undeterminable cases, independently annotate using a rubric: (a) "Imputation" if reasoning assigns a specific value to any missing item, (b) "Abstention based on incompleteness" if refusal to decide solely due to missing items without score-range analysis. Compare imputation proportion to the paper's 81.6%.
3. **Test prompt sensitivity:** For fixed sets of determinable and undeterminable scenarios, run a model with the original "Base" prompt and a modified prompt explicitly instructing: "First compute the minimum and maximum possible score given the known items before answering." Measure if this prompt closes the gap between determinable/undeterminable accuracy, testing the hypothesized mechanism of failed range consideration.
