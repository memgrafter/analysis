# ClimateCheck 2026: Scientific Fact-Checking and Disinformation Narrative Classification of Climate-related Claims

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26449  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26449  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Systems achieved up to 0.451 Recall@5, 0.740 claim verification F1, and 0.625 narrative macro F1 using multi-stage retrieval and hierarchical reasoning.

## Executive Summary
ClimateCheck 2026 is a shared task that expands previous efforts by tripling training data and introducing a novel disinformation narrative classification component alongside scientific claim verification. Participants developed systems combining dense retrieval pipelines, cross-encoder ensembles, and large language models with hierarchical reasoning to retrieve relevant scholarly abstracts, verify claims against them, and classify narratives from the CARDS taxonomy. The top-performing approaches demonstrated substantial progress in both retrieval (Recall@5 up to 0.451) and verification (F1 up to 0.740), while narrative classification reached a macro F1 of 0.625. Cross-task analysis revealed that verification difficulty is strongly tied to narrative type, with claims attacking the reliability of climate science (Group 5) being the hardest to refute using scholarly literature alone. The study also adapted the Ev2R framework to address incomplete gold annotations, exposing systematic biases in conventional metrics and highlighting the value of unannotated but relevant abstracts.

## Method Summary
The shared task comprised two core datasets: (1) claim-abstract pairs for retrieval and verification, and (2) climate-related claims labeled with 33 fine-grained disinformation sub-narratives organized into five top-level groups. To accommodate more complex reasoning, the training corpus was tripled relative to the previous edition. Participants built end-to-end pipelines that typically initiated with sparse retrieval (BM25) to cast a wide net, followed by dense semantic re-ranking using models such as E5, and a final cross-encoder stage (e.g., MiniLM) for fine-grained claim-abstract interaction. Stance verification was treated as an NLI problem using fine-tuned DeBERTa classifiers to label each retrieved abstract as *Supports*, *Refutes*, or *NEI*. Narrative classification was approached either through hierarchical reasoning (predicting a coarse group first, then sub-labels) or flat multi-label models. The organizers further introduced an adapted Ev2R evaluation that mitigates bias from incomplete annotations by aligning retrieved abstracts with gold references via fact-alignment and a verifier’s confidence scores.

## Key Results
- Multi-stage retrieval ensembles reached Recall@5 up to **0.451**.
- Claim verification systems achieved an F1 score of **0.740**.
- Narrative classification macro F1 peaked at **0.625**.
- Cross-task analysis showed verification difficulty is narrative‑dependent: Group 5 (undermining science reliability) claims are systematically hardest to refute.
- The adapted Ev2R framework revealed that some systems retrieve high‑quality *unannotated* abstracts, a pattern missed by standard metrics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Multi‑stage retrieval superiority
- **Claim:** A multi‑stage retrieval pipeline with dense re‑ranking outperforms single‑method retrieval for scholarly abstracts.
- **Mechanism:** Sparse retrieval (BM25) ensures broad lexical coverage; dense semantic re‑ranking (E5) captures topical relevance; a cross‑encoder models fine‑grained claim‑abstract interactions.
- **Core assumption:** No single model optimally balances lexical precision and semantic recall for domain‑specific, lengthy abstracts.
- **Evidence:** Top systems (e.g., ClimateSense, Ant Bridge) used BM25 → E5 → MiniLM pipelines. Baseline section (§6) describes this architecture, and error analysis shows a single claim with R@5=0 due to lexical mismatch.
- **Break condition:** When claim jargon is absent from abstracts, even dense re‑ranking fails (e.g., extreme lexical divergence).

### Mechanism 2: Hierarchical reasoning for narrative classification
- **Claim:** Predicting coarse narrative groups before fine sub‑labels improves performance on fine‑grained multi‑label disinformation classification.
- **Mechanism:** A two‑stage or chain‑of‑thought approach first classifies the claim into one of five top‑level groups (Coan et al. taxonomy), then predicts specific sub‑narratives within that group, reducing label space complexity.
- **Core assumption:** Human annotator agreement is higher at the top level (α=0.697) than at the sub‑level (α=0.651); top‑level distinctions are more reliable and can constrain sub‑label predictions.
- **Evidence:** Team ahilbert’s system uses CoT prompting with claim decomposition → group selection → sub‑label assignment (§7). Figure 4 shows systems struggle when crossing top‑level boundaries.
- **Break condition:** Claims that legitimately span multiple top‑level groups cause cascade errors; hierarchical systems under‑predict labels, suggesting reluctance to commit to a single top‑level group.

### Mechanism 3: Ev2R adaptation for incomplete annotations
- **Claim:** The adapted Ev2R framework mitigates bias in standard retrieval metrics caused by incomplete gold annotations by evaluating unjudged abstracts via proxy‑reference alignment.
- **Mechanism:** For an unannotated retrieved abstract *r*, find the gold abstract *g* with maximum fact‑alignment (F1 of decomposed atomic statements). Use a fine‑tuned DeBERTa verifier’s confidence on *g*’s label as a proxy for *r*’s quality.
- **Core assumption:** An abstract’s evidential value can be approximated by its alignment to the *best‑matching* gold abstract, even if *r* itself was not originally annotated.
- **Evidence:** §5.2 defines `Sref(r) = max_g∈Gc F1(r,g)` and `Sproxy(r) = P_θ(y*|c,r)`. Table 4 shows ytsoneva’s official R@5=0.094 but Ev2R(r)=0.672, indicating high‑quality unannotated retrieval.
- **Break condition:** If *r* aligns with a gold abstract of a *different* veracity label (e.g., “Refutes” vs. “Supported”), `Sproxy` becomes misleading. Also fails if all gold abstracts for a claim are NEI (excluded from evaluation).

## Foundational Learning
- **Retrieval‑Augmented Generation for Domain‑Specific Evidence**  
  *Why needed:* Claims must be verified against scholarly abstracts, not parametric LLM knowledge; hallucinations must be avoided by grounding in retrieved text.  
  *Quick check:* Given a claim about glacier mass balance, does your retrieval return the specific abstract containing the quantitative estimate, not just a general climate abstract?

- **Multi‑Label Hierarchical Classification**  
  *Why needed:* Disinformation narratives overlap; the 33‑label taxonomy has a clear 5‑group → sub‑label hierarchy, making flat classification prone to label sparsity and co‑occurrence errors.  
  *Quick check:* If a claim denies human causation (Group 2) *and* says impacts are beneficial (Group 3), does your system predict both top‑level groups correctly before sub‑labels?

- **Evidence‑Grounding vs. Verdict Prediction**  
  *Why needed:* The task evaluates claim‑abstract pairs (CAPs), not an overall claim verdict; systems must judge *each retrieved abstract’s* stance rather than synthesizing across abstracts.  
  *Quick check:* For a claim with 3 retrieved abstracts (2 “Supports”, 1 “Refutes”), does your output label each CAP individually? Do not output a single “Refutes” verdict for the claim.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input Processor → Retrieval Module → Verification Module → Narrative Classifier → Scoring/Output Formatter  

- **Critical path:**  
  Claim → Retrieve 5 abstracts → Verify each abstract → Classify narrative from claim  

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Retrieval:* Compute vs. recall. BM25 is cheap but low recall for semantic matches; dense re‑rankers improve recall at cost; cross‑encoders most accurate but slowest. Trade‑off: number of candidates sent to cross‑encoder (e.g., 150 in baseline) affects latency.  
  - *Verification:* Model capacity vs. specialization. LLMs (GPT‑4o‑mini) show promise but are costly; smaller DeBERTa fine‑tuned on NLI is cheaper and robust. Trade‑off: zero‑shot generalization vs. fine‑tuned domain adaptation.  
  - *Narrative:* Hierarchical vs. flat. Two‑stage improves macro‑F1 but adds pipeline complexity and error propagation. Flat multi‑label (ModernBERT) is simpler but may underperform on rare sub‑narratives.  

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Task 1.1 (retrieval):* Recall@5 near 0 for specific claims → likely lexical mismatch or missing evidence in corpus. Check claim phrasing vs. abstracts.  
  - *Task 1.2 (verification):* High *NEI → Supports* confusion → model interprets partial relevance as support. Tune classification threshold or add “weak support” label.  
  - *Task 2 (narrative):* Errors crossing top‑level boundaries → flat classifier or poor hierarchical decomposition. Validate top‑level group accuracy first.  
  - *System‑wide:* If Ev2R(r) is high but official Recall@5 is low (like ytsoneva), the system retrieves valuable *unannotated* abstracts; gold set is incomplete, not the system.  

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Retrieval sanity check:** On 50 random claims, manually inspect top‑5 BM25 vs. E5 re‑ranked results. Does E5 bring in more on‑topic abstracts? Compute gain in R@5 over BM25 alone.  
  2. **Verification calibration:** Run DeBERTa verifier on a sample of gold CAPs. Plot confidence scores for *Supports*/*Refutes*/*NEI*. Is there overlap? Adjust decision threshold to minimize *NEI → Supports* errors.  
  3. **Narrative hierarchy test:** Implement two‑stage classifier: (a) predict top‑5 group probabilities; (b) for top‑1 group, run sub‑label classifier. Compare macro‑F1 against single‑stage multi‑label on validation split. Measure if top‑level accuracy improves.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Cross‑task integration:** Does incorporating disinformation narrative classification into the verification pipeline improve accuracy, especially for narrative groups resistant to refutation (e.g., Groups 4 and 5)?  
2. **Evidence for Group 5:** What evidence sources or reasoning approaches are needed to effectively verify claims that attack the reliability of climate science itself, given the self‑referential issue of using scientific literature as evidence?  
3. **Retrieval metrics refinement:** How can retrieval evaluation metrics be improved to mitigate biases from incomplete annotations, and what are the trade‑offs of automated frameworks like Ev2R versus human judgment?  
4. **Fine‑grained narrative labeling:** What methods can improve both inter‑annotator agreement and system performance on fine‑grained disinformation narratives, given the subjectivity inherent in labeling?

## Limitations
- The evaluation corpus exhibits weak external validation (0 average citations, FMR ~0.47), limiting generalizability claims.  
- The adapted Ev2R framework’s proxy‑realignment assumption—that an abstract’s evidential value can be derived from its best‑matching gold standard—risks misalignment if stance labels differ.  
- Narrative classification macro F1 of 0.625 indicates persistent difficulty with underrepresented sub‑narratives and claims spanning multiple top‑level groups, suggesting hierarchical decomposition may not fully capture multi‑label complexity.  
- All results are tied to the specific CARDS taxonomy and climate‑abstract corpus.

## Next Checks
1. **Retrieval lexical gap analysis:** Manually inspect 100 claims with low R@5, quantifying the proportion where gold abstracts contain zero n‑gram overlap with the claim. Validates the lexical mismatch break condition.  
2. **Verification threshold calibration:** On a held‑out set of gold claim‑abstract pairs, plot DeBERTa verifier’s confidence distribution by true label (Supports/Refutes/NEI). Tune threshold to minimize *NEI → Supports* confusion (Figure 3).  
3. **Hierarchical vs. flat classifier ablation:** Implement both a two‑stage (group → sub) and a single‑stage multi‑label classifier on the same validation split. Measure performance disparity on claims with >1 top‑level group to test the cascade‑error hypothesis.
