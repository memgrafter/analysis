# SRRM: Improving Recursive Transport Surrogates in the Small-Discrepancy Regime

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.18781
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18781
- **Reference count**: 36
- **Primary result**: SRRM mitigates the last-mile bias in recursive transport surrogates, achieving state-of-the-art matching fidelity (e.g., 85.7% accuracy on ModelNet10 with n=1000) and a theoretical convergence rate for anchored empirical RRM.

## Executive Summary
Recursive transport surrogates such as Recursive Rank Matching (RRM), Hilbert Curve Partitioning (HCP), and Binary Space Partitioning (BSP) suffer from a "last-mile problem" where a persistent non-zero bias remains even when the two distributions are very close. This bias stems from address-restricted mismatches: points that are nearest neighbors may be separated into different leaves by the recursive partitioning, causing them to be matched incorrectly. The authors propose Selective Recursive Rank Matching (SRRM), a framework that iteratively introduces anchor points to absorb ambiguous matches, performs multiple RRM runs with random rotations to diversify match hypotheses, merges the resulting permutations to identify reliable cross-distribution pairs early, and finally completes the transport plan via the Hungarian algorithm. Theoretically, they prove consistency and explicit convergence rates for anchored empirical RRM under the L2 cost. Empirically, SRRM consistently outperforms competing baselines (BSP, HCP, SW, GSW, MMD, Sinkhorn) on synthetic benchmarks, Wasserstein flow approximation, color transfer, and 3D point cloud classification (ModelNet10), achieving higher accuracy with lower or comparable computational cost.

## Method Summary
SRRM builds upon the RRM baseline. RRM recursively partitions the space along cycling coordinates at the median to equalize mass, assigns binary path codes to points, sorts them lexicographically, and matches by order. SRRM extends this with an iterative screening procedure: Initially, all points are unresolved. In each round, for the remaining points, SRRM generates a set of synthetic anchor points (via small Gaussian jitter around each unresolved point). Both source and target point sets are augmented with these anchors. Then, K independent RRM runs are performed on the augmented sets after applying random rotations; the resulting permutations are merged by constructing a weight matrix counting co-occurrences and solving a maximum-weight bipartite matching, yielding a merged transport plan T. From T, pairs where both points come from the original unresolved sets are deemed reliable ("good" pairs) and are fixed in the global plan; points matched to anchors remain unresolved. After several rounds, the Hungarian algorithm finalizes the assignment for any remaining points. This process gradually resolves easy matches while isolating ambiguous ones, thus avoiding the last-mile bias.

## Key Results
- Theoretical: Established consistency and explicit convergence rates for anchored empirical RRM under L2 cost, formalizing the benefits of anchor points.
- Empirical (synthetic): SRRM significantly reduces the bias floor on small-discrepancy data (e.g., line-segment experiments) compared to RRM and other baselines, and remains effective across varying mixture weights and slopes.
- Empirical (real-world): On ModelNet10 3D point cloud classification with n=1000, SRRM achieves 85.7% accuracy in 545.8s CPU time, surpassing BSP (84.6%, 1102.7s). It also demonstrates superior performance in Wasserstein flow approximation and color transfer tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The last-mile bias in recursive methods arises because the partitioning tree prematurely splits nearest-neighbor pairs across different leaves, forcing them to be matched with distant points. SRRM addresses this by injecting anchor points that act as buffers: ambiguous matches are diverted to anchors instead of being forced into incorrect cross-distribution pairs. Multiple RRM runs with random rotations produce diverse match candidates; merging these candidates via maximum-weight bipartite matching effectively performs a voting scheme that highlights consensus matches (the good pairs). These consensus pairs are then fixed early, preventing them from being corrupted by later partitioning errors. The iterative nature allows the algorithm to progressively resolve the easier part of the transport plan while leaving the hardest points for the final Hungarian step, which is exact but applied to a much smaller set. This two-phase strategy (screening then completion) reduces the bias without sacrificing computational efficiency, as the expensive Hungarian step is only applied to a residual subset.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Input(X,Y) -> AnchorSampler -> MultiRunRRM (K runs with rotations) -> MergedMatching (max-weight bipartite) -> PointSelector (extract good pairs) -> [loop until unresolved set empty] -> HungarianFinalizer -> Output(π)

Critical path:
1. Initialize global plan π and unresolved sets C_X, C_Y.
2. While unresolved points remain:
   a. Generate anchors Z for current unresolved points.
   b. Augment source and target with Z.
   c. Perform K independent RRM runs on rotated augmented sets.
   d. Merge permutations into weight matrix, compute max-weight matching T.
   e. Select good pairs (both from original unresolved) and add to π; update unresolved sets.
3. Apply Hungarian to remaining unmatched points and add to π.

Design Tradeoffs:
- Anchor set size k: Larger k provides more flexibility but increases augmentation size (O(k n)) and computational cost; too small may not absorb all ambiguous matches.
- Number of runs K: More runs improve diversity and robustness of merging but linearly increase runtime; diminishing returns beyond a small K (e.g., 5).
- Stopping rounds R: More rounds allow finer screening but may over-resolve and leave less for Hungarian; typical R=10 suffices.

Failure Signatures:
- No improvement over baseline RRM: Might indicate anchors or merging not effective; check weight matrix sparsity.
- Unresolved set size does not decrease: Anchors too few or too noisy; monitor |C_X| per round.
- Runtime explosion: Augmentation size too large (excessive k or K); profile per-round cost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to unequal weights and sample sizes**: The paper focuses on discrete measures with equal weights and same support size. Extending SRRM to unbalanced settings would require integrating fractional transport plans (e.g., via entropy-regularized OT) while preserving the screening benefits. This is nontrivial and left future work.
2. **Curse of dimensionality**: The authors note that high-dimensional settings may degrade performance due to increased sparsity and partitioning inefficiency. The theoretical analysis assumes fixed dimension; how constants scale with d and whether dimensionality reduction can mitigate this remain open.
3. **Statistical properties of full SRRM**: Theory is provided only for anchored empirical RRM, not the iterative SRRM procedure. A full analysis (consistency, rates) is needed to guarantee that SRRM indeed eliminates the last-mile bias asymptotically.
4. **Unified bias expression for general recursive methods**: While the paper explains the bias for RRM via premature splitting, a general framework that quantifies the bias floor for HCP, BSP, and other tree-based surrogates is lacking. Such a theory would unify understanding and guide algorithm design.

## Limitations
- Implementation details (anchor generation method, merging strategy, tree stopping criterion, rotation distribution) are not fully specified, making exact reproduction challenging.
- The theoretical guarantees cover only anchored empirical RRM; the full SRRM algorithm remains heuristic without formal bounds.
- The method currently handles only discrete measures with equal weights and identical sample sizes, limiting applicability to unbalanced real-world data.

## Confidence
- **Bias mitigation**: Medium (conceptually sound but sensitive to implementation details and hyperparameters).
- **Theory**: High (proofs appear complete under Assumption 1, which ties to anchor generation).
- **Empirical results**: Medium (benchmark numbers are plausible but require faithful recreation of ambiguous components; replication of ModelNet10 numbers is a key validation).
