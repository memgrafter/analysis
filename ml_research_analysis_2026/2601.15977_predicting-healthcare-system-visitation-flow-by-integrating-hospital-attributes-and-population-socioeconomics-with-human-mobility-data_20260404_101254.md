# Predicting Healthcare System Visitation Flow by Integrating Hospital Attributes and Population Socioeconomics with Human Mobility Data
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2601.15977
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15977
*   **Reference count:** 0
*   **Primary result:** The Deep Gravity model most accurately predicted healthcare visitation flows (NRMSE 0.62, SMAPE 59.71, CPC 0.74) by integrating hospital attributes, population SES, and human mobility data.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the determinants of healthcare visitation patterns by combining multiple data sources: hospital attributes (capacity, occupancy, ratings), population socioeconomic status (SES), and human mobility data. Using four years of SafeGraph visitation data and Google Maps reviews for Houston, Texas, the authors trained and compared five predictive models. The deep learning-based Deep Gravity model achieved the highest accuracy. Analysis revealed that driving time is the strongest negative predictor (distance decay). The influence of hospital quality (ratings) and resource (capacity, occupancy) attributes interacts with travel distance: for short trips, convenience and capacity signals dominate, while for long trips, quality signals become paramount. Furthermore, the sensitivity to online ratings varies significantly by the demographic and socioeconomic composition of the originating neighborhood, with Asian and highly educated populations weighting ratings more heavily even for nearby hospitals. These findings provide a nuanced, data-driven basis for designing targeted interventions to improve equitable healthcare access.

## Method Summary
The study constructed a spatial interaction dataset pairing ~2,800 Census Block Groups (CBGs) with 35 general acute care hospitals in Harris County, TX. The target variable was the normalized "visitation percentage" (a CBG's visits to a specific hospital divided by its total outgoing visits to all hospitals). Features included CBG-level SES demographics from the U.S. Census ACS, hospital attributes from CovidCareMap and Google Maps (beds, occupancy, ratings, review count), and drive time computed via the Google Distance Matrix API. Five models were evaluated: a naïve regression baseline, gradient boosting, a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), the Deep Gravity model (which encodes origin and destination features separately before combining them), and a heterogeneous graph neural network (HGNN) that explicitly models the bipartite CBG-hospital graph structure. Models were trained and validated using a 90/10 random split on CBG-hospital pairs, with performance measured by Normalized Root Mean Squared Error (NRMSE), Symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error (SMAPE), and Pearson Correlation (CPC). The best-performing Deep Gravity model was interpreted using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) for global feature importance and Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs) to visualize nonlinear effects and interactions, particularly between hospital attributes and travel distance.

## Key Results
*   **Deep Gravity achieved superior predictive accuracy** (NRMSE 0.62, SMAPE 59.71, CPC 0.74), outperforming other ML models and demonstrating the value of its specific architecture for this spatial flow problem.
*   **Driving time was the single strongest negative predictor**, confirming a robust distance decay effect where longer travel times monotonically reduce visitation probability.
*   **Hospital attribute effects are distance-dependent:** for short trips (<~20 minutes), higher ICU occupancy and review count (convenience/reliability signals) increase visitation, while for longer trips, higher star ratings (quality signal) become the dominant positive driver.
*   **Population SES moderates sensitivity to ratings:** areas with higher Asian populations and higher educational attainment show a stronger positive effect from hospital ratings even for very short trips, whereas White-majority and less-educated areas exhibit lower rating sensitivity locally.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Distance Decay Dominance
*   **Claim:** Travel distance (drive time) exerts a strong, negative effect on healthcare visitation flow probability.
*   **Mechanism:** As the travel time between a residential census block group (CBG) and a hospital increases, the likelihood of residents from that CBG choosing that hospital decreases monotonically. This reflects a general cost-benefit trade-off where time and effort costs outweigh perceived benefits for more distant facilities.
*   **Core Assumption:** Mobility decisions incorporate travel friction as a primary disincentive, consistent across populations and hospital types in the studied range.
*   **Evidence Anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "...driving time was the strongest negative predictor."
    *   [section] Section 4.2 (SHAP Summary Plot) and Section 4.3 (PDP distance decay curves) show a dominant negative SHAP value for driving time and a consistent decaying pattern.
*   **Break Condition:** The monotonic decay could flatten or reverse for uniquely exceptional, irreplaceable care (e.g., a world-renowned trauma center), but the paper's analysis does not show such a reversal within the studied range.

### Mechanism 2: Distance-Dependent Trade-off Between Convenience and Quality
*   **Claim:** The influence of hospital quality (ratings) and resource (capacity, occupancy) attributes on visitation is nonlinear and interacts with travel distance, exhibiting a trade-off "inflection point."
*   **Mechanism:** For short-distance trips, visitation is more sensitive to convenience/stability signals (e.g., high ICU occupancy, high review count), interpreted as indicators of availability or reliability. For long-distance trips, visitation becomes more sensitive to perceived excellence signals (higher star ratings), as patients require stronger quality justifications for enduring longer travel.
*   **Core Assumption:** Patients employ a compensatory decision strategy: when proximity is high, they optimize for ease/capacity; when proximity is low, they require higher quality assurances. The inflection point marks where the marginal cost of distance begins to outweigh the marginal benefit of convenience-focused attributes.
*   **Evidence Anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "...short trips prioritized convenience (high occupancy), long trips prioritized quality (high ratings)."
    *   [section] Section 4.3, particularly Figure 9, shows PDP curves for ratings, occupancy, and capacity that change slope around specific drive time thresholds (e.g., ratings' positive effect emerges after ~19 minutes).
*   **Break Condition:** If a population segment has severely constrained mobility (e.g., no vehicle), the convenience threshold might be so low that quality never becomes dominant. If a highly-rated hospital is also the only nearby option, quality and convenience effects could be perfectly collinear.

### Mechanism 3: SES-Moderated Rating Sensitivity for Local Trips
*   **Claim:** The sensitivity to hospital ratings (a quality signal) for short-distance visits is moderated by the socioeconomic and demographic composition of the originating CBG.
*   **Mechanism:** Cultural norms, health literacy, and prior experience associated with different demographic/SES groups lead to varying reliance on publicly available quality cues (ratings). For instance, populations with higher average education or Asian-majority areas incorporate ratings into local decisions, while White-majority and less-educated areas show lower sensitivity for nearby options, possibly relying more on habit, physician referrals, or other non-digital information sources.
*   **Core Assumption:** The translation of a digital proxy (Google rating) into a decision-making weight is not universal but is filtered through group-specific socioeconomic and cultural lenses.
*   **Evidence Anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "White-majority and less-educated areas showed lower sensitivity to ratings for short trips, while Asian and highly educated populations prioritized ratings even locally."
    *   [section] Section 4.4 and Figure 10 show PDP-based simulations where the "inflection point" for ratings occurs much earlier (8 min) in Asian areas vs. later (32 min) in White-majority areas.
*   **Break Condition:** If rating data itself is systematically biased (e.g., certain demographics are underrepresented in review submission), the observed effect might reflect a data artifact rather than a true preference.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Gravity Models & Distance Decay
    *   **Why needed:** The task is a spatial interaction (flow) problem. The traditional gravity model (flow ∝ origin mass * destination mass / distance^β) provides the foundational theoretical expectation that flows decrease with distance. All models, especially Deep Gravity, are built upon or contrasted against this principle.
    *   **Quick Check:** What does a SHAP value for "Driving Time" tell you about the model's adherence to the distance decay principle?
*   **Concept:** SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) for Model Interpretation
    *   **Why needed:** The complex models (Deep Gravity, HGNN) are "black boxes." SHAP deconstructs predictions to understand the contribution of each input feature (e.g., ICU occupancy, % Hispanic) across many instances, revealing global importance and directional effects.
    *   **Quick Check:** If the SHAP summary plot shows a feature with a long red bar (high SHAP value) and the red dots (high feature values) are on the right side of the bar, what is the relationship between that feature and predicted visitation?
*   **Concept:** Heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks (HGNN)
    *   **Why needed:** The visitation problem is a bipartite graph (CBGs ↔ Hospitals). An HGNN can explicitly model different node types and edge types, capturing structural dependencies that standard tabular ML might miss. The paper tests if this complexity yields accuracy gains.
    *   **Quick Check:** In the HGNN structure, what do the `weight_B`, `weight_H`, and `weight_D` parameters control in the final feature combination?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component Map:**
    Census Block Groups (CBGs) with SES features -> Edges with Drive Time -> Hospitals with attribute features. Tabular Path (Naive, GB, MLP): Concatenates all CBG features + all Hospital features + Drive Time into a single vector per pair. Deep Gravity Path: Encodes CBG and Hospital features via separate MLPs, concatenates with Drive Time, passes through a decoding MLP. HGNN Path: Uses heterogeneous graph convolution (SAGEConv) to propagate messages between CBG and Hospital nodes based on visit edges, integrating node attributes and edge attributes (Drive Time); final prediction uses a weighted sum of transformed CBG, Hospital, and Drive Time embeddings.
*   **Critical Path:**
    1.  **Data Construction:** For each CBG-hospital pair (~98k pairs), compute Drive Time, assign normalized visitation percentage as label, attach SES to CBG, hospital attributes to Hospital.
    2.  **Train/Test Split:** Randomly split pairs 90/10 (note: potential spatial leakage).
    3.  **Model Selection & Training:** Train all 5 models on training set; Deep Gravity and HGNN trained for fewer epochs (400) than MLP (800) based on convergence.
    4.  **Interpretation:** Use trained Deep Gravity for global SHAP analysis and scenario-based PDP analysis.
*   **Design Tradeoffs:**
    *   **Deep Gravity vs. HGNN:** Deep Gravity is shallower, explicitly models pairwise interactions, and proved more accurate for this bipartite, relatively sparse flow network. HGNN is more complex, designed for richer graphs, but may have over-parameterized for this simpler two-node-type problem, leading to worse performance (higher SMAPE) despite sophistication.
    *   **Normalization Choice:** Using "Visitation Percentage" (CBG visits / CBG total outgoing visits) controls for CBG population size but makes absolute hospital capacity effects harder to interpret separately from CBG size.
*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   **Data Leakage:** Random pair split (not CBG-level) risks leakage; CBGs appear in both train/test, potentially inflating performance by learning CBG-specific constants rather than generalizable rules.
    *   **Sampling Bias in SafeGraph:** Raw visitation counts are a sample of mobile devices. Normalization attempts mitigation, but if sampling rates differ systematically by CBG demographics, biased patterns could be learned.
    *   **Model Underfit:** Naïve Regression's poor performance confirms linear, additive effects are insufficient.
    *   **Model Overfit/Instability:** HGNN's higher variance in SMAPE (±2.17 vs Deep Gravity's ±1.03) and its failure to beat a simpler model suggest potential overfitting or sensitivity to graph construction.
*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  **Baseline Replication:** Implement a standard gravity model (log-linear regression of flow ~ log(pop) + log(capacity) + log(drive_time)). Expect poor performance vs. ML models, confirming need for nonlinear methods.
    2.  **Feature Ablation via SHAP:** Using the trained Deep Gravity model, compute SHAP values for each feature group (e.g., all hospital capacity metrics, all SES metrics). Systematically remove the top 1-2 most important feature groups and re-evaluate test set performance to quantify their marginal contribution.
    3.  **PDP Interaction Verification:** Replicate the key PDP curve for "Hospital Rating" vs. "Visitation %" at different drive times. Then, manually create two subsets: one where CBG has %White > 80%, another where %Asian > 50%. Generate PDPs for each subset. Verify the claimed shift in inflection point (earlier for Asian-majority CBGs).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1.  **How would incorporating individuals' insurance coverage data (type, status) improve the predictive accuracy and explanatory power of healthcare visitation flow models?** Insurance is a key enabling factor in Andersen's Behavioral Model, directly affecting financial barriers and provider network access. Its omission is a major limitation.
2.  **Do the identified relationships between hospital attributes, SES, and visitation patterns (particularly the distance-based inflection points) remain consistent when comparing urban core versus suburban/peripheral zones?** The analysis pools all flows across Harris County. Spatial heterogeneity in density, transportation, and facility distribution may mask distinct decision-making logics.
3.  **How do visitation flow determinants differ when the destination is a specialty hospital (e.g., pediatric, cancer, psychiatric) compared to a general acute care hospital?** The study exclusively analyzes General Acute Care Hospitals; patient choice for specialty care is often more referral-driven and less sensitive to general convenience factors.
4.  **Does the use of normalized visitation percentage (proportion of CBG's total outgoing visits) as the dependent variable, compared to using raw visit counts, alter the interpretation of factor importance and distance decay effects?** Normalization changes the objective from predicting total volume to predicting market share allocation, potentially re-interpreting factors like capacity as competitive advantages.
5.  **To what extent did the COVID-19 pandemic disrupt the stable, long-term relationships between predictors and visitation flows that the four-year averaged model assumes, and have post-pandemic patterns re-stabilized?** The model assumes time-invariant relationships, but the pandemic drastically altered utilization patterns. The paper notes an overall increase in long-distance visits post-2021, suggesting potential instability.

## Limitations
*   **Data Access & Bias:** Raw SafeGraph data requires licensed access; sampling bias may affect SES groups if device penetration differs. Google ratings may be systematically biased by demographic review submission rates.
*   **Spatial Leakage:** Random 90/10 pair split (not CBG-level) risks leakage; CBGs appear in both train/test, potentially inflating performance by learning CBG-specific constants rather than generalizable rules.
*   **Hyperparameter Opacity & Causal Gap:** Key training settings (learning rates, layer sizes) are unreported, hindering exact replication. Models predict correlation, not causation; claimed "priority" shifts are inferred from PDPs, not experimental manipulation.

## Confidence
*   **Distance Decay Mechanism:** High Confidence. Strongly supported by SHAP dominance and consistent PDP decay curves; aligns with gravity model theory.
*   **Distance-Dependent Hospital Attribute Trade-off:** Medium Confidence. PDP curves show clear inflection points, but causal direction (preference vs. constraint) is ambiguous; limited external validation.
*   **SES-Moderated Rating Sensitivity:** Low Confidence. PDP shifts by demographic group are suggestive but based on observational model interactions; susceptible to omitted variable bias (e.g., internet access, physician referral networks); weak corpus signals.

## Next Checks
1.  **Spatial Cross-Validation:** Re-split data by CBG clusters (e.g., by ZIP code or spatial block) to prevent leakage. Compare performance drop vs. random split to quantify overfitting.
2.  **Rating Bias Audit:** For a subset of hospitals, manually audit Google ratings for completeness by linking to CBG demographics. Test if observed SES-rating interactions persist after controlling for rating volume/review text sentiment.
3.  **Inflection Point Robustness:** For the PDP "inflection" between convenience and quality, fit a broken-stick regression model on the PDP curve to estimate the threshold and its confidence interval. Re-check stability across bootstrapped samples of the test set.
