# Latent Forcing: Reordering the Diffusion Trajectory for Pixel-Space Image Generation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.11401
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11401
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** SOTA on ImageNet 256×256: unconditional FID-50K 13.36, conditional FID-50K 4.18 via joint diffusion of pixels and deterministic latents (DINOv2) with separate schedules, denoising latents first.

## Executive Summary
Pixel‑space diffusion models produce high‑fidelity images but are computationally demanding and suffer from information loss when compressed into separate latent tokenizers. Latent Forcing introduces a method that jointly diffuses raw pixels and deterministic latent features (from a pre‑trained self‑supervised encoder like DINOv2) within a single Diffusion Transformer (DiT). By assigning independent noise schedules and ordering the denoising so that latents are processed before pixels, the model uses the semantic structure from latents as a “scratchpad” to guide the generation of fine‑grained pixel details. This avoids the reconstruction‑generation tradeoff of standard latent diffusion while requiring only ~0.5% extra parameters and operating end‑to‑end without a decoder. On ImageNet 256×256, Latent Forcing with DiT‑L achieves state‑of‑the‑art FID scores (13.36 unconditional, 4.18 conditional), significantly outperforming prior pixel‑space diffusion models and demonstrating that the order of information processing is a critical design dimension.

## Method Summary
Latent Forcing modifies DiT to handle two modalities: pixel patches and deterministic latent tokens from a frozen pre‑trained encoder (DINOv2 or Data2Vec2). Pixel patches are embedded via a linear bottleneck; latents are similarly embedded; the per‑patch embeddings are summed to form a single token stream. Separate time‑step embeddings are generated from `t_pixel` and `t_latent` and used in adaLN‑Zero blocks. Training employs a joint schedule that favors cascaded steps: either latent‑only (noisy `t_latent`, `t_pixel=0`) or pixel‑only (noisy `t_pixel`, `t_latent≈1`). The loss combines x‑prediction (v‑loss) for both streams with weighting λ. Optionally, final transformer layers can be split into separate output experts at no extra parameter cost. Inference follows a fixed cascaded trajectory: first denoise latents (using CFG‑Interval for conditional generation), then denoise pixels with AutoGuidance, discarding latents at the end.

## Key Results
- Unconditional FID‑50K: **13.36** (vs. JiT+REPA 24.40)
- Conditional FID‑50K: **4.18** (vs. JiT+REPA 4.57)
- Parameter overhead: **~0.5%** (DiT‑L: 459 M → 465 M)
- SOTA on ImageNet 256×256 with pixel‑space diffusion at matched compute

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Ordering the denoising trajectory to process latents before pixels improves generation quality by using latents as a structured “scratchpad” that guides pixel generation.
- **Mechanism:** Two separate noise schedules are defined for pixel and latent tokens. At each global timestep `t_global`, per‑modality times `t_latent = f(t_global)` and `t_pixel = g(t_global)` are computed. By scheduling `t_latent < t_pixel` during much of the trajectory (e.g., `t_latent` near 1.0 while `t_pixel` is still noisy), the model first denoises the latent representation (e.g., DINOv2 features). This latent provides semantic and structural guidance for the subsequent, more challenging task of denoising high‑frequency pixel details. The joint model is trained on pairs `(z_latent, t_latent), (z_pixel, t_pixel)` with a combined loss, allowing gradients to flow between the two streams.
- **Core assumption:** The information content (approximated by SNR) in self‑supervised latents is easier to denoise early in the trajectory and provides a more useful conditioning signal for later pixel denoising than noisy pixels themselves. The deterministic relationship `latent = f_encoder(pixels)` means modeling `P(pixels | latent)` is easier than `P(pixels)` directly at high noise levels.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract]: “jointly diffusing pixels and deterministic latents (e.g., DINOv2 features) with separate noise schedules, denoising latents first to guide pixel generation.”  
  [section 3.2]: Formalizes the multi‑time diffusion process and generation order `O(t_global)` as the trajectory of SNRs. States: “With non‑overlapping time schedules, the generation process factors as P(Y)P(X|Y)... we focus on generating latents Y output by a deterministic function of the pixels of X.”  
  [section 4.5, Fig 3 & Table 1]: Empirical validation across multiple schedules (cascaded, linear offset, variance shift) and latent types (DINOv2, Data2Vec2) showing consistent FID improvements when `t_latent` advances faster than `t_pixel`.
- **Break condition:** This mechanism would fail if the latent space is poorly aligned with the pixel space (e.g., a weak encoder) or if the latent’s information becomes stale/incorrect as `t_pixel` decreases. The paper notes this is mitigated by adding small latent noise during pixel denoising steps (Table 6).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Performance gains come from *reordering* information within a single, high‑capacity pixel‑space model, not from compression or distillation benefits of a separate tokenizer.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of compressing pixels into a lossy latent code for a separate diffusion model (standard latent diffusion), Latent Forcing keeps the pixel stream throughout. The “latent” stream is derived from a frozen, pre‑trained self‑supervised encoder (e.g., DINOv2), which is *not* trained for compression. By diffusing this high‑dimensional, semantically rich stream first, the model gets the benefits of a “semantic prior” without losing the fine‑detail information present in the original pixel space. The final image is decoded solely from the pixel stream; latents are discarded. This avoids the reconstruction‑generation tradeoff inherent in training a lossy tokenizer.
- **Core assumption:** The primary bottleneck in pixel diffusion is the difficulty of predicting coherent semantic structure from highly noisy pixels at early timesteps. A pre‑trained, semantically meaningful representation can provide this structure more easily. The model’s capacity must be sufficient to handle the high‑dimensional pixel stream.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract]: “achieves the efficiency of latent diffusion while operating on raw natural images... avoids information loss from tokenization while maintaining efficiency.”  
  [section 1]: Contrasts with latent diffusion: “they discard information during image encoding... the reconstruction‑generation tradeoff... losing more information about the pixel space tends to improve generation quality [in latent diffusion]. Our work instead pushes in the other direction.”  
  [section 5.7 & Table 11]: Shows state‑of‑the‑art results with *lower* PSNR (meaning less compression) than any latent diffusion model (e.g., RAE 18.09 PSNR, LightningDiT 25.29 PSNR vs. LF‑DIT “∞” indicating lossless). Outperforms JiT+REPA, demonstrating gains from ordering are distinct from REPA’s distillation.
- **Break condition:** Would fail if the pre‑trained encoder (DINOv2) is not sufficiently semantically aligned with the target data distribution or if the pixel‑space model (DiT) lacks the capacity to effectively use the latent guidance to generate high‑frequency details. The paper’s success is partly attributed to JiT’s x‑prediction target, which helps with high‑dimensional output.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A single diffusion transformer can be minimally modified to handle multiple, asynchronous noise schedules with negligible parameter overhead, making the approach practical and scalable.
- **Mechanism:** The core architecture is a Diffusion Transformer (DiT). Modifications are: 1) Two separate time‑step embedding MLPs (one for pixels, one for latents) instead of one, increasing parameters by ~0.5%. 2) Pixel and latent patch embeddings are added together *before* entering the transformer, keeping the token count identical to standard DiT. 3) Optionally, the last few transformer layers can be split into “output experts” (separate linear projections for latent vs. pixel predictions), which uses identical FLOPs and adds no parameters. The training objective sums the `x`‑prediction losses for both modalities, weighted (`λ_i`) to balance gradient magnitudes.
- **Core assumption:** Standard transformer architectures can process multimodal inputs (pixels + latents) effectively when their embeddings are combined additively. The shared transformer trunk can learn to route information and apply modality‑specific computation via the conditioning from the two time embeddings and the final output projections.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 4.2]: “Latent Forcing requires minimal architecture changes... we add a second time embedding MLP... we simply add these embeddings together... Optionally, we take the last M... transformer layers... and split them into two M/2‑layer output experts. This modification adds no parameters and uses identical FLOPs.”  
  [section 5.1 & Table 2]: Ablation on `p_latent` (probability of sampling a latent‑only training step) shows performance is sensitive to this training schedule hyperparameter, implying the model’s training dynamics must align with the intended inference ordering.  
  [Table 11]: LF‑DIT‑L has 465M parameters (vs. JiT‑L 459M), confirming minimal overhead.
- **Break condition:** This lightweight design would fail if the latent and pixel streams require fundamentally different intermediate representations that cannot be handled by a single shared trunk, or if the additive embedding combination causes interference. The optional output experts are a partial mitigation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Diffusion Model Noise Schedules (e.g., logit‑normal, variance shifting)**  
  **Why needed here:** The entire method hinges on defining and manipulating separate noise schedules (`f_i(t_global)`) for different token streams. Understanding how SNR varies with `t`, and how scaling a data distribution is equivalent to shifting its schedule (Eq. 4), is central to designing the cascaded and variance‑shifted trajectories.  
  **Quick check question:** If you multiply a latent vector by 2 (doubling its variance), how must its noise schedule be modified to keep the effective SNR at a given global timestep `t` constant?  
  **Answer:** Apply `f(t) = t^2 / (1 + (4-1)t)` from Eq. 4 with α=4.

- **Concept: Tokenization in Latent Diffusion vs. Pixel‑Space Diffusion**  
  **Why needed here:** The paper’s motivation and analysis contrast the “reconstruction‑generation tradeoff” in latent diffusion with its lossless pixel approach. One must understand that latent diffusion trains a separate autoencoder (with reconstruction loss, often perceptual/adv losses) whose latent code is the diffusion target. This process discards information (high PSNR = more loss) to improve “diffusability.”  
  **Quick check question:** Why does a lower PSNR tokenizer (more lossy) typically lead to better FID in a latent diffusion model?  
  **Answer:** The compressed latent space is a smoother, lower‑dimensional manifold that is easier for the diffusion model to learn, at the cost of reconstruction fidelity.

- **Concept: Flow‑Based Diffusion / v‑loss and x‑prediction**  
  **Why needed here:** The paper builds on JiT, which uses flow‑based diffusion with an `x`‑prediction target and `v`‑loss weighting (Eq. 5). This is crucial for high‑dimensional pixel prediction, as predicting `x` (denoised sample) is more stable than predicting `ϵ` or `v` in high dimensions. The mechanism and loss definition (Eq. 1, 5) rely on this formulation.  
  **Quick check question:** What is the purpose of the `t_clip` term in the JiT loss (Eq. 5), and why is it needed for high‑dimensional data?  
  **Answer:** It prevents division by near‑zero `(1‑t)` at very low noise levels, which would cause numerical instability and huge loss values when predicting clean data `x`.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
1. **Input Stage:** Image → Patchify → `x_pixel` (256 tokens, 16×16 patches). Additionally, pass full image through frozen pre‑trained encoder (DINOv2‑Base+Registers or Data2Vec2‑Large) → `x_latent` (256 tokens, aligned patches).
2. **Embedding:** `x_pixel` passed through a small linear bottleneck (128‑dim → hidden dim). `x_latent` passed through a similar bottleneck. The two embedding vectors are **summed** per patch to form the final token embedding `z_t` fed to the DiT.
3. **Time Conditioning:** Two independent MLPs generate time embeddings from `t_pixel` and `t_latent`. These are used in the DiT’s adaLN‑Zero blocks.
4. **DiT Backbone:** Standard DiT blocks (with RoPE, etc.). Processes the combined token sequence.
5. **Output Stage:** Final hidden states are projected *separately*. One linear head predicts `v_pixel` for the pixel stream. Another linear head (or a shared expert, Sec 4.2) predicts `v_latent` for the latent stream.
6. **Training Loop:** Sample `(t_pixel, t_latent)` from a defined joint schedule (e.g., cascaded: either `(t_latent~U, t_pixel=0)` or `(t_latent=1, t_pixel~logit‑normal)`). Compute noised inputs `z_pixel, z_latent`. Compute loss `L = λ_pixel * L_pixel + λ_latent * L_latent` using Eq. 5 with `t_clip`.
7. **Inference Loop:** Define a global trajectory `t_global` (e.g., 50 steps from 0 to 1). For each step, compute `t_pixel = f_pixel(t_global)`, `t_latent = f_latent(t_global)` (e.g., cascaded: first 25 steps `f_latent` advances, `f_pixel` stays at 0; last 25 steps `f_pixel` advances, `f_latent` stays at 1). Run one Euler/Heun update per modality using Eq. 2. Discard `z_latent` after final step; decode `z_pixel` to image.

### Critical Path
- **Schedule Design:** The choice of `f_latent` vs. `f_pixel` (cascaded, variance‑shifted) is the single most critical design decision. It directly defines the “generation order” and must be explored via multi‑schedule models first.
- **Loss Balancing (`λ`) and `t_clip`:** Must be tuned so gradients from pixel and latent losses have comparable magnitude, especially at low noise levels (hence `t_clip=1/3` in multi‑schedule, Appendix A.1).
- **Guidance Strategy:** AutoGuidance vs. CFG/CFG‑Interval, and on which timesteps to apply it (latent vs. pixel), significantly impacts final FID. Requires sweeping guidance values and intervals.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Cascaded vs. Joint Denoising:** Cascaded (`t_latent` finishes before `t_pixel` starts) is simpler and showed best results (Table 8). But it risks “cascaded error.” Joint denoising (e.g., variance‑shift) is more robust to schedule misalignment but slightly worse in ablations.
- **Latent Noise During Pixel Steps:** Adding small noise to latents during pixel‑only training steps (`t_latent ∈ U[1‑β, 1]`) is a crucial regularization that prevents overfitting to high‑frequency latent details and improves final performance (Table 6). However, this noise must be *zero* at inference (Table 7).
- **Output Experts:** Splitting final layers into separate experts adds no params/FLOPs and improves FID (Table 5). This trades a tiny bit of implementation complexity for better capacity separation.

### Failure Signatures
- **Poor latent alignment:** If DINOv2 features are not properly normalized (per‑patch global norm) or if the wrong layer is used, the latent stream provides no useful signal, and performance degrades to near JiT baseline.
- **Schedule mismatch:** Training on a uniform joint schedule but inferring with a strong cascade can lead to poor results. The single‑schedule model must be trained *on* the intended inference distribution.
- **Loss imbalance:** If `λ_latent` is too high/low, the model may ignore the latent stream or fail to learn pixel denoising. Check training loss curves for both modalities.
- **Guidance collapse:** Using CFG on latents with a strong interval may degrade performance if the latent already strongly encodes class (AutoGuidance preferred). Watch for FID increases when sweeping guidance values.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Multi‑Schedule Trajectory Sweep:** Train a multi‑schedule model (Sec 4.5) with uniform `t_pixel, t_latent` sampling (after variance‑shift). At inference, evaluate FID‑10K for fixed trajectories: (a) `t_latent = t_pixel` (no ordering), (b) `t_latent = 0.5*t_pixel` (latent earlier), (c) `t_latent = 2*t_pixel` (pixel earlier). Expect (b) to win, confirming ordering matters (Fig 3).
2. **Cascaded Training Schedule Ablation:** Implement single‑schedule cascaded training. Sweep `p_latent` (probability of sampling a latent‑only training step) from 0.3 to 0.8 (Table 2). Sweep `μ_latent` (logit‑normal mean) for latent steps (Table 3). Identify the sweet spot (~0.5, μ≈1.2). This trains the model for the intended inference order.
3. **Output Expert Ablation:** Train two cascaded models: one with final linear projections only, one with final 4 layers split into 2 experts each (Sec 4.2). Keep all else identical. Compare FID‑10K unguided. Expect the expert model to be better (Table 5). This validates the capacity allocation hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does per‑timestep tokenizer reconstruction quality determine the diffusability of a latent space?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper argues diffusability should be evaluated per‑timestep rather than by global compression metrics, showing in Figure 5 that DINOv2 and pixel PSNR evolve differently across noise levels. However, it stops short of providing a causal or quantitative model linking reconstruction at specific timesteps to diffusion training dynamics.
- **Why unresolved:** Without a mechanistic understanding, it remains difficult to design tokenizers optimized for diffusion or to predict which tokenizers will yield high‑quality generation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments with tokenizers having matched global PSNR but different per‑timestep reconstruction curves; measuring gradient norms or loss contributions at each timestep; theoretical analysis connecting tokenizer noise schedules to ELBO gradients.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does strictly denoising latents before pixels (cascaded generation) outperform other ordering strategies in Latent Forcing?
- **Basis in paper:** Extensive ablations (Table 8, Figure 3) demonstrate cascaded generation achieves the lowest FID. The authors hypothesize latents act as a “scratchpad” for intermediate computation, but this mechanism is not tested.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether the benefit arises from easier early learning signals, better conditioning of pixel diffusion, or fundamental information‑theoretic advantages of the ordering.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablations with frozen latents of varying quality; analyzing the model’s reliance on latent predictions at different pixel timesteps; comparing optimization landscapes (e.g., loss curvature, gradient variance) for different orders.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can Latent Forcing achieve comparable performance without relying on fixed pretrained encoders (e.g., DINOv2), perhaps by learning the latent space end‑to‑end?
- **Basis in paper:** The method uses DINOv2/Data2Vec2 and claims ordering is fundamentally different from REPA‑style distillation, but no experiments explore jointly trained encoders. This leaves open whether gains are specific to these pretrained representations or general to any suitably ordered latent.
- **Why unresolved:** If benefits are tied to external pretrained features, Latent Forcing remains dependent on them; joint training would enable a unified, self‑supervised framework.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training Latent Forcing with a learnable encoder (maybe with REPA‑like objectives) and comparing performance to the pretrained‑encoder baseline; analyzing whether the learned encoder converges to features resembling DINOv2.

## Limitations
- **Latent normalization** is underspecified: “global norm to μ=0, σ=1” leaves unclear whether it is per‑patch across channels or dataset‑level, and whether additional scaling (e.g., by pixel std) is applied. This critically affects SNR alignment between streams.
- **Time schedule mapping** is incomplete: the functions converting global time to per‑modality t are not given, only summary statistics (μ, σ of logit‑normal). Replicating the exact training distribution may be inaccurate.
- **Conditional guidance** details are missing: the paper’s mix of CFG‑Interval and AutoGuidance lacks precise intervals, scales, and which steps each applies to. This hinders exact reproduction of the reported conditional FID.

## Confidence
- **Latent Forcing achieves SOTA FID on ImageNet 256×256 with matched compute:** High
- **Performance gain primarily stems from reordering denoising (latents before pixels):** Medium
- **Approach requires minimal architectural overhead (~0.5% parameters) and operates end‑to‑end:** High
