# Do We Need Adam? Surprisingly Strong and Sparse Reinforcement Learning with SGD in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07729
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07729
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: SGD matches or exceeds AdamW in RLVR with extreme sparsity (<0.02% of parameters updated) and up to 15.7 GB memory reduction.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning from verifiable reward (RLVR) for large language models (LLMs) conventionally employs the AdamW optimizer, which carries substantial memory overhead due to its momentum and adaptive learning rate states. This paper challenges the necessity of Adam-specific mechanisms for RL, demonstrating through extensive experiments across multiple models (Qwen, Llama), domains (math, coding, RLVE), and RL algorithms (GRPO, PPO) that plain SGD—without momentum or per-parameter adaptation—achieves comparable or superior performance. Notably, SGD updates fewer than 0.02% of parameters without any sparsity regularization, over 1000× fewer than AdamW, and reduces GPU memory consumption by up to 15.7 GB. Ablation studies reveal that neither momentum nor adaptive learning rates consistently improve outcomes; momentum frequently degrades performance. The extreme sparsity of SGD updates is attributed to its lack of adaptive scaling, which otherwise amplifies minute gradient movements that would be lost to floating-point precision. These results indicate that RLVR optimization operates in a surprisingly low-dimensional subspace, enabling far more efficient training than previously assumed.

## Method Summary
The study conducts comparative experiments using the `verl` framework to implement RLVR via GRPO and PPO algorithms. Models include Qwen3-1.7B/8B and Llama3.1-8B, trained on datasets such as NuminaMath-CoT (math), APPS+CodeContests+TACO+Codeforces (coding), and RLVE (260 tasks). Each optimizer—AdamW (lr=1e-6), SGD (lr=0.1), SGD with momentum (lr swept), and RMSProp (lr swept)—is evaluated under identical conditions: global batch size 256, KL penalty coefficient 0.001, and response length limits per domain (3072 for math, 4096 for code, 8192 for RLVE). Training spans approximately 270 steps for math/coding and 500 for RLVE. Performance is measured via accuracy on benchmarks (MATH-500, AMC, AIME, OlympiadBench, GPQA) and code generation (HumanEval, MBPP pass@1/10). Update sparsity is quantified as the fraction of parameters with absolute changes exceeding 1e-5, and effective rank analysis uses SVD on linear layer updates to determine the number of singular values required to explain 99% of spectral energy.

## Key Results
- SGD matches or exceeds AdamW performance across all tested models, domains, and RL algorithms.
- SGD updates fewer than 0.02% of model parameters without any sparsity-promoting regularization, representing a >1000× reduction compared to AdamW.
- Memory usage is reduced by up to 15.7 GB due to elimination of optimizer states (momentum and adaptive moments).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: RLVR optimization inherently operates in a low-dimensional subspace, so an optimizer that avoids artificially densifying updates (SGD) can match or exceed AdamW.
**Mechanism**: SGD’s lack of adaptive learning rates prevents amplification of tiny gradient components that would be lost to floating-point precision, resulting in extremely sparse updates (~0.02% of parameters). RL updates concentrate in off‑principal directions, yielding a low‑rank effective subspace where simple updates suffice.
**Core assumption**: The useful gradient signal in RLVR is concentrated in a small subset of parameters/directions; the rest are noise or irrelevant.
**Evidence anchors**: 
- [abstract] "SGD updates fewer than 0.02% of model parameters without any sparsity‑promoting regularization, more than 1000× fewer than AdamW."
- [section 5] "Across all experiments, SGD produces orders‑of‑magnitude sparser parameter updates than AdamW." and "SGD produces updates with substantially lower rank than AdamW."
- [corpus] Contrast with "Why Transformers Need Adam: A Hessian Perspective" which argues Adam is essential for supervised learning due to block heterogeneity; this work’s RL findings suggest a qualitatively different landscape.
**Break condition**: If RL required dense updates across many parameters, SGD’s sparsity would cause underfitting; if the low‑rank structure vanished, AdamW’s adaptivity would become necessary.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Momentum, which integrates past gradients, is detrimental in RLVR due to non‑stationary policy updates.
**Mechanism**: RL’s on‑policy nature causes the data distribution and loss landscape to evolve rapidly. Momentum’s moving average accumulates gradients from earlier policy versions, often pointing in directions misaligned with the current gradient. This misalignment can induce overshooting or oscillation, whereas SGD uses the immediate gradient that reflects the current landscape.
**Core assumption**: The non‑stationarity in RLVR is rapid enough that the first‑moment estimate becomes stale.
**Evidence anchors**:
- [abstract] "momentum often degrades performance."
- [section 3] "momentum computes a moving average of past gradients... causing accumulated moment estimates to point in directions misaligned with the current policy gradient."
- [section 4.2] "Comparing SGD + Momentum vs. SGD, we see that momentum hurts the performance in all but one case."
- [appendix E] Cosine similarity between momentum buffer and current gradient: SFT ~0.997 vs RL ~ -0.007, indicating near‑zero alignment in RL.
- [corpus] "Correcting momentum in temporal difference learning" (Bengio et al., 2021) also identifies momentum issues in RL.
**Break condition**: In environments with slower non‑stationarity (e.g., off‑policy methods with replay buffers), momentum might become beneficial; resetting momentum frequently could also mitigate harm.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: Adaptive learning rates (AdamW’s per‑parameter scaling) provide less benefit in RLVR because the second‑moment heterogeneity across parameters is dramatically lower than in SFT.
**Mechanism**: AdamW normalizes updates by √v, where v is a running average of squared gradients. If v is similar across parameters, this normalization has little effect and AdamW approximates SGD with a global LR. The paper shows RLVR’s √v standard deviation is ~22× lower than SFT’s, indicating low heterogeneity; thus, adaptivity is unnecessary.
**Core assumption**: AdamW’s advantage over SGD primarily stems from handling heterogeneous curvature; homogeneous curvature reduces the need for adaptivity.
**Evidence anchors**:
- [abstract] "neither momentum nor adaptive learning rates consistently improve results."
- [section 3] "SFT exhibits approximately 22× higher standard deviation in √v compared to RLVR (σ_SFT = 5.11×10^{-6} vs σ_RL = 2.29×10^{-7})."
- [section 4.2] RMSProp (adaptive LR, no momentum) shows mixed results, sometimes underperforming SGD.
- [corpus] "Why Transformers Need Adam: A Hessian Perspective" claims Adam is needed for Transformers due to block heterogeneity, but this paper’s RL data contradicts that, highlighting regime‑specific optimizer requirements.
**Break condition**: If RL tasks become more heterogeneous (e.g., multi‑domain training), adaptive rates might regain advantage; if second‑moment variance increases, AdamW could outperform.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Policy network (LLM) -> Rollout sampler -> Verifiable reward -> Advantage estimator -> Optimizer -> Distributed communication (FSDP)

### Critical Path
1. Sample prompts from dataset.
2. Generate responses via policy (multiple rollouts per prompt).
3. Compute verifiable rewards.
4. Normalize rewards to advantages (using baseline or group statistics).
5. Compute policy loss: \( -\log \pi_\theta(y|x) \times \text{advantage} + \text{KL penalty} \).
6. Backpropagate loss, apply optimizer step.
7. Repeat.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Memory vs. performance: SGD eliminates momentum/adaptive states, saving ~2× optimizer memory; this can enable larger models or batch sizes but may require higher LR tuning.
- Sparsity vs. convergence: SGD’s sparse updates might slow convergence if the useful subspace is denser than observed; however, the paper finds it works well.
- Stability: Momentum can stabilize training in stationary regimes but harms RL due to non‑stationarity; adaptive rates (RMSProp/AdamW) densify updates, increasing memory and possibly introducing noise.

### Failure Signatures
- Training reward stagnation: Likely LR too low (SGD) or too high (divergence). Perform LR sweep.
- Unexpectedly dense updates: Check optimizer state (AdamW vs SGD); ensure threshold for sparsity measurement accounts for dtype (bfloat16 tolerance).
- High memory usage: Verify optimizer state storage; SGD should use only master weights.
- Gradient norms near zero: May indicate reward scaling issues or saturation; inspect advantage distribution.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **LR sweep comparison**: Train a small model (e.g., Qwen3‑1.7B) with SGD over LRs in {1e‑3, 1e‑2, 1e‑1, 1, 10} and AdamW at 1e‑6; verify SGD converges best at high LR (around 0.1) and matches/exceeds AdamW (replicate Figure 1/5).
2. **Sparsity measurement**: After a few training steps, compute update sparsity (ℓ₀ norm with threshold 1e‑5) for both optimizers; confirm SGD updates <0.5% of parameters vs. AdamW’s ~10%.
3. **Momentum alignment check**: For SFT and RL runs, compute cosine similarity between momentum buffer \( m_{t-1} \) and current gradient \( g_t \) (Appendix E method); expect near‑zero in RL vs. high positive in SFT.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question**: What precise mechanism causes SGD to produce extreme update sparsity (≤0.02% of parameters) in RLVR, and why does adaptive learning rate scaling in AdamW/RMSProp prevent this?
**Basis in paper**: The paper conjectures that adaptive learning rates amplify small gradient movements, preventing them from being lost to floating-point precision, but does not empirically disentangle this from other factors like gradient distributions or numerical thresholds.
**Why unresolved**: The analysis compares distributions of second moments but does not directly measure the threshold at which updates become nonzero under different optimizers or isolate adaptive scaling from other algorithmic components.
**What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments with synthetic gradients of varying magnitudes, or ablation studies that inject known gradient distributions into optimizers while measuring sparsity thresholds under different floating-point precisions.

### Open Question 2
**Question**: What is the geometric and functional structure of the low-dimensional subspace (low effective rank) in which RLVR updates occur? Which model components (layers, heads, features) are modified and how does this relate to reasoning capabilities?
**Basis in paper**: The paper repeatedly emphasizes that RL updates are confined to a low-dimensional subspace and shows lower effective rank for SGD, but does not characterize the subspace itself.
**Why unresolved**: While update sparsity and rank are measured, the paper does not probe which parameters are updated or whether these updates correspond to specific reasoning pathways.
**What evidence would resolve it**: Layer-wise and neuron-level analysis of updated parameters, correlation with downstream task performance, and probing to determine whether updates primarily affect certain attention heads, MLP layers, or feature representations.

### Open Question 3
**Question**: Do the favorable properties of SGD (performance parity, extreme sparsity) generalize beyond GRPO/PPO and dense transformer architectures to other RL algorithms (e.g., DPO, KTO, offline RL) and modern architectures (e.g., Mixture‑of‑Experts, multimodal models)?
**Basis in paper**: The study is limited to GRPO and PPO on Qwen and Llama dense models; the conclusion speculates about broader applicability but lacks validation.
**Why unresolved**: The paper acknowledges that RL practices may differ across algorithms and architectures, but experiments are not conducted across a wider spectrum. Findings may not hold for algorithms with different update rules or for sparse architectures where parameter efficiency is already inherently high.
**What evidence would resolve it**: Replication of the optimizer comparison on diverse RL algorithms (e.g., Direct Preference Optimization, Kahneman‑Tversky Optimization) and architectures (e.g., Mixture‑of‑Experts, vision‑language models), measuring both final performance and update sparsity.

## Limitations
- Findings are limited to dense transformer architectures (Qwen, Llama) and on‑policy RL algorithms (GRPO, PPO); generalization to other RL methods (DPO, KTO, offline RL) and sparse architectures (Mixture‑of‑Experts) remains unvalidated.
- The extreme sparsity threshold (1e‑5) may be sensitive to floating‑point precision and could vary across hardware or training regimes.
- SGD requires a relatively high learning rate (~0.1) that may need per‑task tuning, and its stability over very long training horizons (>1000 steps) is not thoroughly assessed.

## Confidence
- **SGD matches/exceeds AdamW in RLVR**: High for tested settings (Qwen/Llama on math/coding/RLVE), Medium for broader RLVR scenarios.
- **Extreme sparsity (<0.02% updates without regularization)**: High.
- **Momentum harms RLVR performance**: High for on‑policy methods (GRPO/PPO), Medium for other RL algorithms.
- **Adaptive learning rates unnecessary due to low curvature heterogeneity**: High for homogeneous tasks (single‑domain math/coding), Low for heterogeneous multi‑domain training.
- **Optimization operates in a low‑dimensional subspace**: Medium (based on effective rank metrics).
