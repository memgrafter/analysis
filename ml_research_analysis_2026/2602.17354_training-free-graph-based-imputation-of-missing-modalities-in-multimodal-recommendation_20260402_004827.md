# Training-free Graph-based Imputation of Missing Modalities in Multimodal Recommendation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17354
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17354
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Graph-based imputation methods (MULTIHOP, PERSPAGERANK, HEAT) significantly outperform traditional baselines, with Recall@20 improvements up to +42.97%.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the challenge of missing item modalities (e.g., visual or textual features) in multimodal recommendation. The authors formalize the problem and propose four training-free, graph-based imputation techniques that propagate available features across the item-item co-purchase graph. These methods serve as a model-agnostic preprocessing step compatible with any multimodal recommender. Evaluated on seven datasets using fourteen recommendation models, the proposed graph imputations consistently beat traditional (zeros, random, global mean) and autoencoder-based baselines, preserving or even widening the performance gap between traditional and multimodal systems. Sensitivity analyses underscore the importance of hyperparameter tuning and the influence of feature homophily in the graph.

## Method Summary
The problem is to impute missing multimodal feature vectors for items before training a recommender. Inputs are an implicit user-item interaction matrix R and an incomplete feature tensor F (items × modalities × feature dimension). The method constructs an item-item co-purchase graph from R via R_I = R^T R, then sparsifies to keep only the top-N edges per node (TOPN). For each modality with missing vectors, the missing entries are initially set to zero. Then a graph propagation function is applied: NEIGHMEAN averages neighbor features (F = D_I^{-1} R_I F); MULTIHOP performs T steps of normalized diffusion with a reset of observed features at each step; PERSPAGERANK solves a linear system with restart probability a; and HEAT applies the heat kernel exp(-b L), where L is the normalized Laplacian. The resulting values replace zeros, yielding a complete feature tensor. Any multimodal recommender can then be trained on this enriched data. The entire process is training-free and model-agnostic.

## Key Results
- Graph-based imputation (MULTIHOP, PERSPAGERANK, HEAT) significantly outperforms traditional baselines (zeros, random, global mean) and autoencoder-based approaches across seven datasets and fourteen models.
- Recall@20 improvements reach up to +42.97% (NGCF-M vs NGCF on Office) compared to using dropped items.
- The best graph methods consistently achieve higher Recall@20 and nDCG@20; performance is sensitive to hyperparameters TOPN and number of hops, and positively correlated with feature homophily in the item-item graph.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism exploits the homophily principle in the co-purchase graph: items that are frequently bought together tend to share similar multimodal characteristics (e.g., products in the same category have similar visual styles or textual descriptions). By propagating available features along graph edges, missing values are filled with information from semantically related items. The graph-based smoothing acts as a non-parametric imputer that does not require additional training, thereby avoiding overfitting on sparse modality data. Moreover, the model-agnostic nature ensures that any downstream recommender can benefit from richer item representations without architectural changes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
User-Item Interactions -> Item-Item Graph Construction -> Graph-based Imputation -> Multimodal Recommender

**Critical Path**  
The pipeline begins with the interaction matrix R, which is transformed into R_I. This graph, together with the incomplete F, drives the imputation step. The resulting complete F is then used to train any multimodal recommendation model. The critical path is linear; each component must succeed for the next to function.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Graph sparsity: Too few edges limit propagation reach; too many introduce noise and computational overhead.  
- Propagation depth: MULTIHOP and similar methods balance between local smoothing (shallow) and global information (deep), but risk over-smoothing after many hops.  
- Computational method: Exact matrix inversion/exponential for PERSPAGERANK/HEAT may be infeasible for large graphs; approximations trade off accuracy for scalability.  
- Training-free vs. learned imputation: Graph methods are fast and interpretable but may underperform if non-linear dependencies exist that autoencoders capture.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Disconnected graph: A large fraction of items isolated (check largest component size); imputation fails for isolated nodes.  
- Performance collapse with hops: Validation Recall@20 decreases sharply after a few hops, indicating over-smoothing.  
- Low feature homophily: When edge-wise feature similarity is near zero, graph propagation yields negligible gains; may need alternative imputation.  
- Hyperparameter mismatch: Fixed TOPN/hops that work on one dataset drastically underperform on another; rigorous validation required.

**First Experiments**  
1. Neighborhood Mean Verification: Construct a 5-item toy graph with known features, run NEIGHMEAN, and manually verify that each missing entry equals the average of its neighbors’ observed features.  
2. Baseline Comparison: On Amazon Office Products, impute using MULTIHOP (T=2, TOPN=50) and train LightGCN-M; compare Recall@20 against ZEROS to confirm a substantial improvement (>30%).  
3. Hyperparameter Sweep: Vary TOPN among {10, 30, 50, 100} and hops T in {1,2,3,5} on the same dataset; plot Recall@20 to identify the U-shaped trend and optimal region.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
While the paper does not have a dedicated section enumerating open questions, the limitations and discussion point to several important questions for future research:
- How can exact graph propagation methods (PERSPAGERANK, HEAT) be scaled efficiently to very large graphs (>100k items) without relying on approximations?
- What are the optimal ranges for hyperparameters (restart probability a, diffusion coefficient b) across different datasets and modalities?
- To what extent do graph-based imputation methods generalize to modalities beyond the studied ones (e.g., audio, video) and to datasets with more than three modalities?

## Limitations
- Scalability of exact PERSPAGERANK/HEAT computations for large graphs (>100k items) is unclear; approximations may affect performance.
- Hyperparameter ranges (restart probability a, diffusion coefficient b) are not reported, requiring additional tuning.
- The study covers only 2–3 modalities; generalizability to more modalities or different feature types (e.g., audio) remains unexamined.
