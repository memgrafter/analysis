# Lost in Translation? A Comparative Study on the Cross-Lingual Transfer of Composite Harms

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07963
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07963
- **Reference count:** 3
- **Key outcome:** Adversarial harms in Indic languages see up to 4-5x higher attack success than in European languages; English-centric safety alignment fails sharply under translation.

## Executive Summary
This study reveals a significant fragility in multilingual LLM safety: English-aligned safety mechanisms break down when prompts are translated into morphologically distant Indic languages. The authors introduce CompositeHarm, a benchmark combining adversarial and contextual English harms, and evaluate three lightweight multilingual models across five Indic languages. Results show that adversarial harms transfer poorly, with attack success rates rising sharply (over 45% in some languages), while contextual harms transfer more moderately. Binary safety metrics miss many nuanced "soft failures," especially in Indic languages, exposing gaps in current evaluation approaches.

## Method Summary
The authors create CompositeHarm by combining adversarial prompts from AttaQ and contextual prompts from MMSafetyBench, then translating them into Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, and Kannada. They evaluate three lightweight multilingual models using a lightweight inference setup, measuring attack success rates and refusal rates. The study compares cross-lingual transfer performance across Indic and European languages, focusing on binary safety outcomes and identifying a significant increase in adversarial attack success for Indic languages relative to European ones.

## Key Results
- Attack success rates (ASR) for adversarial harms rise sharply in Indic languages, exceeding 45% in Gujarati and Kannada for LLaMA-3-8B.
- Contextual harms transfer more moderately than adversarial harms across languages.
- Binary metrics (ASR/refusal rate) miss many "soft failures" (evasive or guardrail-blocked responses), especially in Indic languages.
- Compared to European languages, Indic ASR increases 4-5x, demonstrating severe degradation of English-centric safety alignment for morphologically distant scripts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The failure of English-centric safety alignment under translation is attributed to the morphological and syntactic distance between English and Indic languages. Safety mechanisms trained on English data do not generalize well when prompts are translated into languages with different scripts, grammar, and cultural contexts. This results in a higher success rate for adversarial attacks and a greater prevalence of evasive or non-refusal responses that binary metrics fail to capture.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cross-lingual transfer**: Needed to assess safety robustness beyond English; check by evaluating prompts in diverse language families.
- **Binary safety metrics**: Provide simple pass/fail measures but miss nuanced failures; check by supplementing with qualitative analysis.
- **Adversarial prompt translation**: Critical for testing robustness; check by using multiple translation methods and back-translation.
- **Morphological distance**: Explains why safety alignment degrades more in Indic than European languages; check by comparing script and grammar complexity.
- **Composite harm benchmarks**: Allow systematic evaluation of both adversarial and contextual harms; check by ensuring comprehensive prompt coverage.
- **Lightweight inference**: Enables scalable evaluation; check by verifying resource usage and reproducibility.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** CompositeHarm (benchmark) -> Translation engine -> Multilingual LLM -> Safety metric evaluator -> Results aggregator

## Limitations
- The study is limited to five Indic languages, so results may not generalize to other language families or scripts.
- Only three lightweight models are evaluated, which may not reflect performance of larger or more capable models.
- Binary safety metrics miss nuanced "soft failures," and the study lacks a systematic approach to quantify these gaps.

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** English-centric safety alignment degrades sharply for Indic languages; cross-lingual transfer failures are reproducible.
- **Medium confidence:** The 4-5x increase in attack success rates for Indic versus European languages is robust but may vary with different protocols.
- **Low confidence:** The extent and nature of "soft failures" in Indic languages are not well characterized, limiting conclusions about overall safety robustness.

## Next Checks
1. Expand language coverage: Evaluate the same benchmark on additional non-Indic low-resource languages (e.g., African or Southeast Asian languages) to test the generality of observed transfer failures.
2. Model scaling study: Test a broader range of model sizes and architectures (including frontier models) to assess whether observed fragility is model-dependent or universal.
3. Robustness to translation artifacts: Conduct experiments with varied translation quality, back-translation, or adversarial prompt transformations to isolate the impact of translation artifacts on safety alignment.
