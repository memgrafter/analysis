# RBF-Solver: A Multistep Sampler for Diffusion Probabilistic Models via Radial Basis Functions
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.13330
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13330
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: RBF-Solver achieves FID 2.87 at NFE=15 and 2.48 at NFE=40 on CIFAR-10; on ImageNet 256×256 it reduces FID by 16.12–33.73% relative to polynomial‑based samplers in the low‑NFE range (5–10) at guidance scale 8.0.

## Executive Summary
Diffusion probabilistic models generate high‑quality samples but require many function evaluations (NFE), making sampling computationally expensive. Existing polynomial multistep samplers, such as Adams‑Bashforth/Moulton, accelerate inference yet suffer from instability at higher orders due to Runge oscillations. RBF‑Solver addresses these limitations by interpolating model evaluations using Gaussian radial basis functions with learnable shape parameters. This localized interpolation maintains stability even at fourth order and beyond, while the learnable parameters allow the sampler to follow an optimal reference trajectory derived from high‑NFE samples. Consequently, RBF‑Solver delivers superior sample quality at moderate NFE and demonstrates robust performance across datasets and guidance conditions.

## Method Summary
RBF‑Solver is a predictor‑corrector multistep method for solving the probability‑flow ODE of diffusion models. At each step it constructs an interpolant from the most recent p model evaluations using Gaussian radial basis functions (RBFs) plus a constant basis to satisfy the coefficient summation condition. The interpolation coefficients are obtained by solving a small linear system that enforces exact integration of the basis functions over the time interval. Two shape parameters per step—γ_pred for the predictor and γ_corr for the corrector—are tuned offline via grid search to minimize the mean‑squared error between a two‑step ahead prediction and a reference trajectory obtained by a high‑NFE sampler (UniPC with 200 steps). At inference the tuned parameters are reused without additional overhead. The method reduces to Euler (DDIM) at first order and converges to the Adams method as γ→∞, ensuring compatibility with established solvers.

## Key Results
- **CIFAR‑10 (Score‑SDE)**: FID 2.87 at NFE = 15; improves to 2.48 at NFE = 40.
- **ImageNet 256×256 (Guided Diffusion)**: RBF‑Solver lowers FID by 16.12–33.73% compared to polynomial‑based samplers for NFE = 5‑10 at guidance scale 8.0.
- **Stability**: Unlike polynomial samplers that deteriorate after order 4, RBF‑Solver remains stable up to order 8 and beyond.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Gaussian RBF interpolation replaces global polynomials with localized basis functions, thereby avoiding Runge oscillations that cause instability in high‑order polynomial multistep methods. The shape parameter γ controls the width of each Gaussian; appropriate tuning ensures the interpolant remains well‑conditioned and non‑oscillatory even when many steps are used.

The shape parameters are learned from a reference trajectory generated by a high‑NFE sampler. This offline optimization steers the solver toward a target that closely approximates the true ODE solution, improving accuracy at moderate NFE. The learned parameters are then fixed and reused, avoiding any runtime cost.

The construction satisfies the coefficient summation condition, preserving consistency for constant integrands. As γ→∞, the Gaussian RBFs tend to flat (global) polynomials and RBF‑Solver recovers the Adams method; at order 1 it exactly matches Euler (DDIM). These limits guarantee that RBF‑Solver inherits favorable properties of existing solvers when the shape parameters are extreme.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
RBF basis module → Kernel matrix builder → Linear solver → Integral calculator → Shape‑parameter optimizer (offline) → Predictor‑corrector loop.

**Critical path**  
1. Collect the p most recent model evaluations at step i.  
2. Build the kernel matrix Φ(p) using current γ_pred (or γ_corr) and the λ‑grid.  
3. Compute the integral vector l(p) in closed form (or via quadrature).  
4. Solve c(p) = Φ(p)⁻¹ l(p).  
5. Update the sample x̃_{t_{i+1}} using c(p)·[previous evaluations].  
6. Optionally perform the corrector step with p+1 evaluations.  
7. Repeat until t = 0.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Interpolation flexibility vs. computational cost*: Tunable γ offers more expressivity than fixed polynomials but requires an offline optimization pass.  
- *Order vs. stability*: Polynomial methods become unstable around order 4; RBF‑Solver maintains stability up to order 6+ due to localized basis, at the cost of solving a small linear system each step.  
- *Constant basis inclusion*: Needed for the summation condition; omitting it may slightly improve low‑NFE FID but harms high‑NFE consistency.

**Failure signatures**  
- Shape parameters hitting the search bound (|log γ| > 2) → fallback to Adams; may indicate too smooth reference trajectory.  
- Ill‑conditioned kernel matrix when γ → 0 (flat Gaussians) → nearly singular Φ; switch to equal‑coefficient mode.  
- Large discrepancy between predictor and corrector coefficients → potential instability; monitor coefficient magnitudes.  
- Target trajectory mismatch (different sampler or NFE) → overfitted shape parameters → degraded FID.  
- Inadequate warm‑up steps for early iterations (i < p‑1) → large initial errors; use lower‑order integrators for first steps.

**First three experiments**  
1. **Unconditional CIFAR‑10**: Compare RBF‑Solver against DPM‑Solver++ and UniPC on Score‑SDE; report FID vs NFE. Shows lower FID for NFE ≥ 15.  
2. **Conditional ImageNet 256×256**: Test with Guided‑Diffusion and CFG across guidance scales 2.0–8.0; RBF‑Solver reduces FID by 16–34% relative to polynomial samplers in the low‑NFE range (5‑10).  
3. **High‑order stability**: Run RBF‑Solver and UniPC at orders 3‑8. UniPC fails after order 4 while RBF‑Solver remains stable up to order 8, confirming the benefit of localized RBF basis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. *Non‑Gaussian RBFs*: Would kernels such as multiquadric or Wendland’s compactly supported functions perform as well as Gaussian RBF‑Solver? The study only examined Gaussian kernels.  
2. *Online adaptation*: Can shape parameters adapt during sampling without offline optimization? Adaptive runtime schemes that adjust γ on‑the‑fly remain unexplored.  
3. *Optimal target trajectory*: Is the forward‑diffusion target trajectory (x_target_ti = α_ti x_target_0 + σ_ti x_target_T) truly optimal? The paper reports it outperforms model‑based trajectories but does not explain why or whether better targets exist.  
4. *Guidance‑dependent advantage*: Why does RBF‑Solver’s relative improvement concentrate in low‑NFE, high‑guidance conditional sampling? The relationship between guidance strength, trajectory nonlinearity, and solver performance is not analyzed.

## Limitations
- **Generalization**: Experiments are limited to Score‑SDE (CIFAR‑10) and Guided‑Diffusion (ImageNet 256); performance on other architectures (e.g., latent diffusion, EDM) or datasets remains unknown.  
- **Reference trajectory bias**: The offline optimization uses a UniPC‑200 trajectory, which itself is an approximation. If this reference deviates from the true ODE solution, the learned shape parameters may be suboptimal.  
- **Computational overhead**: Each step requires solving a (p+1)×(p+1) linear system; for high orders this cost is not reported but may affect wall‑clock time. The tuning grid search also adds upfront cost that is not counted in NFE‑FID comparisons.

## Confidence
- Stability at high orders: **Medium** (empirical evidence but no theoretical proof).  
- Learnable shape parameters: **Medium** (FID gains shown but sensitivity to reference choice not fully quantified).  
- Recovery of known methods: **High** (mathematical derivations and internal validations confirm the limits).

## Next Checks
1. **Stability stress test**: Evaluate RBF‑Solver at orders 3–10 on at least three diffusion models (e.g., NCSN++, EDM, Stable Diffusion) and compare divergence rates against polynomial multistep samplers.  
2. **Cross‑dataset generalization**: Apply γ values tuned on CIFAR‑10/ImageNet to two new datasets (e.g., LSUN Bedroom, CelebA‑HQ) without retuning; measure FID at low NFE to detect overfitting.  
3. **Reference trajectory ablation**: Generate target trajectories using an exact ODE solver (or a very high‑NFE numerical method like 1000‑step RK45) instead of UniPC‑200; re‑tune γ and compare FID to assess sensitivity to the reference.
