# Vision Transformer Finetuning Benefits from Non-Smooth Components

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.06883
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06883
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Finetuning high-plasticity components (Multi-head Attention and feedforward layers) yields superior accuracy and stability compared to smooth components (LayerNorms) across 11 vision benchmarks, challenging the notion that smoothness is always beneficial.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates which components of Vision Transformers (ViTs) should be prioritized during finetuning. It introduces *plasticity*—the average rate of change of a component’s outputs relative to its inputs—as a measure of non-smoothness, with higher plasticity indicating greater sensitivity. Theoretical analysis derives upper bounds on plasticity for transformer modules, ranking them: Multi-head Attention (highest), Feedforward layers (first then second), then LayerNorms (second then first). Empirical measurements on ViT-Base and ViT-Huge confirm this ordering and show that attention and feedforward layers have plasticity >1 (amplifying changes) while LayerNorms have <1 (contracting changes). Isolated finetuning experiments across 11 downstream tasks reveal that tuning higher-plasticity components consistently yields better test accuracy and more stable performance across a range of learning rates. The best results come from tuning attention modules, which achieve statistically significant improvements over other components on most datasets. The findings demonstrate that non-smooth components with high plasticity facilitate faster optimization through larger gradient norms and steeper loss descent, challenging the prevailing emphasis on smoothness for generalization.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a two-phase study. First, they define *plasticity* as the expected ratio of output perturbation to input perturbation (using Frobenius norm) over pairs of samples from pretraining and downstream distributions. They derive theoretical Lipschitz-based upper bounds for each component type (MHA, FC1, FC2, LN) that yield a strict ranking of plasticity. Second, they empirically measure plasticity on pretrained ViT models and validate the theoretical ordering. Then, they conduct isolated finetuning experiments: for each component type, they freeze all other parameters and train only that component (across all layers) plus a randomly initialized classification head. Using SGD with momentum, cosine decay, gradient clipping, and a sweep of learning rates, they evaluate on 11 classification benchmarks (CIFAR-10/100, corrupted variants, DomainNet, Flowers102, Pets). They compare test accuracy, track gradient norms, and monitor validation loss curves to correlate plasticity with finetuning performance.

## Key Results
- Theoretical and empirical plasticity ranking: Multi-head Attention > first Feedforward layer > second Feedforward layer > second LayerNorm > first LayerNorm. Attention and feedforward layers exhibit plasticity >1, while LayerNorms have <1.
- Isolated finetuning of high-plasticity components (especially MHA) achieves significantly higher accuracy and stability across learning rates than tuning low-plasticity components; on most datasets, MHA tuning yields the best performance with statistical significance.
- High-plasticity components produce larger gradient norms and steeper validation loss descent during finetuning, confirming the mechanism that non-smoothness accelerates adaptation. These results challenge the universal desirability of smoothness in neural networks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Gradient amplification**  
Finetuning components with high plasticity (P(f) > 1) amplifies input variations, leading to larger singular values in the Jacobian. During backpropagation, this yields larger gradient norms for the component’s parameters, enabling more substantial updates per step and faster convergence. Evidence: gradient norm and loss curves in Section 5.2 show MHA and FC1 exhibit larger norms and steeper descent. Break condition: excessively high learning rates cause divergence; very small datasets or minimal distribution shift may lead to overfitting.

**Mechanism 2: Theoretical ranking validity**  
The theoretical analysis (Section 4) uses module-specific properties to derive Lipschitz upper bounds that produce the ranking MHA > FC1 > FC2 > LN2 > LN1. Empirical plasticity measurements (Section 5.1) confirm the same ordering, and finetuning results (Section 5.2) show performance decreasing with plasticity rank. This validates that the bounds capture intrinsic adaptability despite being loose. Break condition: architectural changes (e.g., activation functions, attention variants) could alter the ranking.

**Mechanism 3: Smoothness–adaptation trade-off**  
Conventional wisdom links smoothness (low Lipschitz) to in-distribution generalization and stability. However, transfer learning requires adapting to new data distributions. Smooth components resist output changes, limiting adaptability, whereas non-smooth components amplify changes, allowing representations to reshape. The consistent outperformance of high-plasticity components across 11 benchmarks suggests that smoothness is not universally beneficial; its value depends on the distributional shift. Break condition: when pretraining and downstream distributions are very similar, smoothness may suffice and high plasticity could overfit.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input image → patch embedding → token sequence. Each transformer block: LayerNorm1 (attn_norm) → Multi-head Attention (MHA) → residual add → LayerNorm2 (ffn_norm) → Feedforward (FC1 → GeLU → FC2) → residual add. After final block: LayerNorm (output_norm) → classification head. The paper focuses on MHA, FC1, FC2, LN2, LN1 within blocks; classification head is separate.

**Critical path:** During finetuning, forward pass outputs from a tuned component influence the loss; backward pass computes gradients via chain rule: loss gradient → component output gradient → component input gradient → parameter gradients. High plasticity means the component's Jacobian amplifies input perturbations, leading to larger parameter gradient norms.

**Design tradeoffs:** High-plasticity components (MHA, FC1) yield better adaptation and stability across learning rates but involve many parameters (~28M each). Smooth components (LayerNorms) have few parameters (~18K) but adapt poorly; tuning them is inefficient. Practical choice: prioritize tuning MHA or FC1 for best performance; if compute is limited, consider tuning only a subset of these high-plasticity layers.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Poor accuracy: Finetuning a low-plasticity component (e.g., LN) yields little improvement over linear probing.  
- Exploding loss/gradients: Learning rate too high for a high-plasticity component; monitor gradient norms.  
- Vanishing gradients: Very low gradient norms across all components suggest learning rate too small or component plasticity underestimated.  
- High variance across seeds: Inconsistent performance may indicate that plastic components are sensitive to initialization; use multiple seeds.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Plasticity measurement replication:** For a pretrained ViT-Base (ImageNet-21k), compute P(f) for each component type using image pairs from pretraining (ImageNet) and downstream (e.g., Cifar10). Verify ranking: MHA > FC1 > FC2 > LN2 > LN1. Use 12,800 image pairs, compute ∥f(x)−f(y)∥/∥x−y∥ averages.  
2. **Isolation finetuning replication:** Finetune each component type separately on Cifar10 with SGD (momentum 0.9, batch 512, cosine decay, 10k steps). Sweep learning rates {1e-3, 3e-3, 1e-2, 3e-2}, 3 seeds. Expect MHA and FC1 to achieve >98% accuracy, FC2 slightly lower, LN1/LN2 <98%.  
3. **Gradient norm correlation:** During experiment (2), track gradient norms per component step. Confirm MHA/FC1 have larger norms and steeper validation loss descent. Plot norms and loss; check monotonicity with plasticity rank.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Transfer to LLMs:** How do plasticity rankings and finetuning benefits transfer to large language models (LLMs) compared to vision transformers? The authors note that their analysis is limited to vision transformers and could serve as groundwork for LLMs. LLMs have different architectures (causal attention, decoder-only) that may alter theoretical bounds and empirical rankings. Resolving this would require measuring plasticity across transformer components in LLMs and conducting finetuning experiments on downstream tasks.

2. **Combined effects of multiple components:** The paper isolates component tuning, but practical finetuning often adjusts multiple components. The additive or synergistic effects of tuning both high-plasticity (MHA, FFN) and low-plasticity (LN) components remain unknown. Evidence to resolve: extend experiments to include combinations (e.g., MHA+FFN, MHA+LNs, all components) across datasets and compare performance with single-component tuning.

3. **Plasticity vs. generalization beyond accuracy:** The paper connects plasticity to optimization benefits but only evaluates classification accuracy. Implications for robustness, calibration, or out-of-distribution generalization are unexplored. Evidence: evaluate finetuned models on robustness benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet-C, adversarial attacks), calibration metrics (ECE), and OOD detection; correlate these with plasticity.

4. **Tighter theoretical bounds:** Current upper bounds are loose, especially for attention; they overestimate empirical plasticity by orders of magnitude. Developing tighter average-case bounds would provide more accurate predictions. Evidence: prove new theorems bounding the expectation in Definition 1 directly, using probabilistic analysis of input distributions or properties of pretrained weights; validate that tighter bounds match empirical values across layers and scales.

5. **Interaction with PEFT methods:** The paper studies full-component tuning, but Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA modify only a small subset of parameters. It is unknown whether plasticity predicts the effectiveness of applying PEFT to a component. Evidence: run PEFT experiments where LoRA/Adapter modules are applied exclusively to MHA, FFN1, FFN2, or LNs across tasks and ranks; compare performance to random rank allocation.

## Limitations
- Theoretical bounds are loose and only guarantee ordering, not exact plasticity values.
- Plasticity measurements depend on data distribution and pair sampling; results may vary with different pretraining/finetuning data.
- Experiments are restricted to ViT architectures on classification tasks; generalization to other vision models (e.g., CNNs) or modalities (e.g., language) is unknown.
- Isolation finetuning ignores interactions between components; the effects of simultaneously tuning multiple components are not studied.
- The link between plasticity and gradient norms is correlational; other factors like curvature could also influence optimization speed.

## Confidence
- Plasticity ranking (MHA > FC1 > FC2 > LN2 > LN1): **High** (strong theoretical derivation and consistent empirical confirmation).
- Isolated finetuning performance advantage of high-plasticity components: **High** (reproducible gains across 11 benchmarks).
- Mechanism linking high plasticity to larger gradient norms and faster optimization: **Medium** (evidence from gradients and loss curves but causality not fully isolated).
- Challenge to the universal desirability of smoothness: **Medium** (compelling results in transfer learning but limited to ViT classification tasks).

## Next Checks
1. Extend analysis to diverse architectures (Swin, DeiT) and modalities (BERT) to test the generality of the plasticity ranking and isolation benefits.
2. Conduct a controlled intervention: actively modify component smoothness (e.g., by adding noise or regularization) and measure resulting changes in gradient norms and finetuning performance to establish causality.
3. Investigate component combinations: tune pairs or groups (e.g., MHA+FC1, all non-LN) to uncover interaction effects and refine practical tuning recipes for real-world finetuning scenarios.
