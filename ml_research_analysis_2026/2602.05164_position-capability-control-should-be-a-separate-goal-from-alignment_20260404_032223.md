# Position: Capability Control Should be a Separate Goal From Alignment

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.05164
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05164
*   **Reference count:** 33
*   **Primary result:** Capability control (imposing hard limits) is conceptually distinct from alignment and requires a defense-in-depth approach across data, learning, and system layers.

## Executive Summary
The paper argues that controlling a model's fundamental capabilities—imposing hard, absolute limits on what it can do—is a separate problem from aligning its behavior to human preferences in context. It categorizes control mechanisms into three layers across the model lifecycle: data-based (shaping the training distribution), learning-based (direct weight/representation interventions), and system-based (post-deployment guardrails). Each layer alone has inherent failure modes, such as compositional re-synthesis or prompt injection. The core proposal is a defense-in-depth strategy that composes complementary controls from all layers to robustly mitigate risks like adversarial elicitation and dual-use knowledge leakage. The paper identifies key open challenges, including evaluating true capability removal, securing open-weight models, and managing the fundamental trade-off between safety and utility.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a conceptual framework rather than a single empirical method. It synthesizes existing control techniques into a three-layer taxonomy. A minimum viable reproduction of one layer (learning-based) involves: (1) collecting a dataset of harmful queries and refusal responses, (2) performing supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a base model with this dataset, and (3) evaluating the model's refusal rate on harmful queries, its performance on benign benchmarks, and its robustness to adversarial fine-tuning or jailbreak prompts. This tests for suppression versus removal but does not provide a formal protocol for certifying erasure.

## Key Results
*   Control mechanisms are organized into three distinct layers: data-based, learning-based, and system-based.
*   Each layer has inherent failure modes when used in isolation (e.g., data filtering fails against compositional generalization).
*   A defense-in-depth approach, composing complementary controls across all layers, is necessary for robust capability control.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Data-Based Capability Control
*   **Claim:** Capability control can be achieved by intervening on the training data distribution to prevent the acquisition of undesired capabilities at the source.
*   **Mechanism:** Filter, curate, or synthesize training data to exclude content that contributes to harmful capabilities, then train a new model on the intervened dataset.
*   **Core assumption:** Model capabilities are primarily determined by the training distribution, assuming a sufficiently powerful learning algorithm.
*   **Break condition:** Fails if the capability can be re-derived from remaining benign data (compositional generalization) or if identification of harmful data is incomplete (low recall), leaving residual traces.

### Mechanism 2: Learning-Based Capability Control
*   **Claim:** Capability control can be implemented by modifying model weights or representations during training or at inference to suppress or remove undesired behaviors.
*   **Mechanism:** Apply fine-tuning, unlearning, adversarial training, or representation engineering (e.g., activation steering) to directly alter the model's parameters or internal activations.
*   **Core assumption:** Undesired capabilities are encoded in specific weights or representations that can be targeted for modification without catastrophic forgetting of benign capabilities.
*   **Break condition:** Interventions may only suppress capabilities, not remove them, leaving them recoverable via fine-tuning or adversarial elicitation; also risks performance degradation on benign tasks.

### Mechanism 3: System-Based Capability Control
*   **Claim:** Capability control can be enforced post-deployment via external guardrails on inputs, outputs, and actions, providing a last line of defense independent of model weights.
*   **Mechanism:** Deploy wrapper systems that apply input/output filtering, system prompts, chain-of-thought monitoring, or information-flow policies to block harmful behaviors.
*   **Core assumption:** The model's outputs and actions can be intercepted and mediated by external components, offering deterministic guarantees even if the model is compromised.
*   **Break condition:** Vulnerable to prompt injection, adversarial examples, bypass by users with open-weight models, and may increase latency; monitors can fail or be deceived.

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**  
  Data Layer (Dataset Curation/Filters) -> Training Layer (Fine-tuning/Unlearning/Steering Scripts) -> System Layer (Input/Output Classifiers, CoT Monitors, Policy Engines)
*   **Critical path:**  
    1.  Data preparation: Identify target capabilities → construct filtered dataset → retrain or continue pretraining.  
    2.  Learning intervention: Apply chosen method (e.g., unlearning set) → evaluate suppression/removal.  
    3.  System integration: Deploy with guardrails → monitor for elicitation → iterate on data/learning layers.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**  
    *Data:* High recall filtration may remove too much data, hurting general capabilities; low recall leaves residual traces.  
    *Learning:* Strong removal may cause alignment tax; inference-time steering is cheaper but only suppresses.  
    *System:* Deterministic policies increase safety but add latency and may be brittle to jailbreaks; flexibility vs. robustness.
*   **Failure signatures:**  
    *Data:* Unexpected capabilities re-emerge after retraining, indicating incomplete filtering or compositional resynthesis.  
    *Learning:* Model responds to adversarial fine-tuning or jailbreak prompts, suggesting mere suppression.  
    *System:* Guardrail bypass via prompt injection, role-playing, or indirect requests; high false-positive rates causing over-refusal.
*   **First 3 experiments:**  
    1.  **Data filtering efficacy:** Train two models (full vs. filtered data) on a targeted harmful capability (e.g., detailed bioweapon instructions). Measure elicitation success via standard red-teaming prompts.
    2.  **Unlearning robustness:** Apply an unlearning algorithm to remove the harmful capability, then attempt recovery via fine-tuning on a small harmful dataset and adversarial prompting. Compare to a baseline model.
    3.  **System guardrail stress test:** Deploy a model with input/output classifiers and CoT monitoring; run a suite of known jailbreak attacks (e.g., DAN, role-playing). Record bypass rate and latency overhead.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** What metrics and evaluation protocols can reliably distinguish between true capability removal (erasure) and temporary suppression in foundation models, such that latent capabilities are detected even under adversarial elicitation?
    *Basis in paper:* Section 6.1 explicitly states "Evaluating the effectiveness of a control mechanism is notoriously difficult... The core challenge in capability control is distinguishing between suppression (hiding a capability) and removal (erasing it)."
*   **Open Question 2:** For open-weight models where users have full parameter access, what data-centric training interventions can create tamper-resistant safeguards that survive subsequent fine-tuning and adversarial attacks without relying on post-deployment system controls?
    *Basis in paper:* Section 6.2 discusses securing open-weight models, noting "enforcing robust system-level control in open-weight models is inherently challenging."
*   **Open Question 3:** How can we formalize and optimize the trade-offs between retaining dual-use foundational knowledge necessary for model utility and removing hazardous capability subsets, particularly when the same underlying knowledge enables both benign and malicious applications?
    *Basis in paper:* Section 6.3 states "most fundamental capabilities are inherently dual-use... it is difficult to define absolute guardrails for removing harmful capabilities without compromising the core knowledge required for benign tasks."
*   **Open Question 4:** To what extent can compositional generalization in large foundation models re-synthesize hazardous capabilities from remaining safe knowledge components, and what architectural or training interventions can prevent this recomposition without degrading general intelligence?
    *Basis in paper:* Section 6.4 notes "Even if specific harmful knowledge is successfully removed, large models may be able to resynthesize it by composing remaining fundamental concepts..."

## Limitations
*   Major uncertainty: No empirical metrics for "true capability removal"—distinguishing suppression from erasure lacks standardized tests.
*   Open-weight model security is highlighted as a critical challenge but remains unsolved; leakage via fine-tuning is a known risk but not quantified.
*   The dual-use trade-off is acknowledged qualitatively but no utility-safety Pareto curves are provided for any control layer; the framework is conceptual, not validated empirically.

## Confidence
*   **Core thesis** (capability control ≠ alignment): Medium. The conceptual distinction is clear, but practical separation may be fuzzy.
*   **Three-layer categorization**: High. The organization is descriptive and logically coherent, accurately reflecting existing intervention classes.
*   **Defense-in-depth necessity**: Medium. The argument that single-layer controls fail is well-supported by cited failure modes, but the efficacy of layered composition is unproven.

## Next Checks
1.  **Standardized removal evaluation:** Develop an adversarial fine-tuning benchmark where models post-control are fine-tuned on a tiny harmful dataset; measure capability re-emergence rate to quantify residual knowledge.
2.  **Compositional re-synthesis test:** For a capability like bioweapon design, evaluate if filtered training data still allows step-wise inference of harmful sub-components via chain-of-thought prompting.
3.  **Open-weight guardrail bypass:** Test whether system-level guardrails (e.g., output classifiers) can be circumvented by modifying model weights locally (e.g., LoRA) without triggering monitoring—simulating a user with open-weight access.
