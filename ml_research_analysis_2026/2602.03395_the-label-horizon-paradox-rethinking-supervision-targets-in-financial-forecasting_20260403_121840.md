# The Label Horizon Paradox: Rethinking Supervision Targets in Financial Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03395
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03395
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Bi-level adaptive supervision uncovers an optimal intermediate horizon, yielding substantial gains (e.g., CSI 500 LSTM IC ↑ 0.845→1.029, +22%).

## Executive Summary
This paper uncovers the Label Horizon Paradox: in financial forecasting, the optimal supervision horizon for training labels often differs from the target prediction horizon due to a trade-off between marginal signal realization and linear noise accumulation. Using a linear factor model, the authors derive that generalization hinges on the signal‑noise balance and provide a closed‑form condition for the optimal horizon. To operationalize this, they propose a bi‑level optimization framework: an inner loop updates model parameters using a weighted combination of losses across intermediate horizons, while an outer loop tunes these weights to maximize performance on the final target, aided by entropy regularization. A warm‑up phase prevents the model from collapsing to short horizons. Extensive experiments on three CSI indices (300/500/1000) with ten architectures consistently show improvement; for CSI 500 with LSTM, IC rises from 0.845 to 1.029 and ICIR from 0.724 to 0.861.

## Method Summary
The method consists of a warm‑up phase followed by a bi‑level optimization. In warm‑up (3 epochs), the model is trained on the arithmetic mean of standardized intermediate returns, establishing robust features. In the main loop, each batch is split into support and query sets. The inner loop computes a loss as a weighted sum (λ) of MSE between predictions and each standardized intermediate return on the support set, then performs one gradient step on the model parameters (inner LR = 1e‑6) while keeping λ fixed. The outer loop evaluates the updated model on the query set against the standardized final‑target return, adds an entropy regularization term on λ, and updates λ via gradient ascent (outer LR = 1e‑3). Cross‑sectional standardization is applied throughout. This process autonomously discovers the optimal supervision horizon within a single training run.

## Key Results
- For CSI 500 with LSTM (Δ = 240 min): IC ↑ from 0.845 to 1.029, ICIR ↑ from 0.724 to 0.861, RankIC ↑ 0.792→0.859, Sharpe ↑ 3.534→3.660.
- Improvements are consistent across ten architectures (LSTM, GRU, Transformer, Mamba, etc.) and all three CSI indices, with average IC gains of 10–20%.
- Learned horizon weights align with brute‑force horizon sweeps, confirming the paradox and the efficacy of the bi‑level approach.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Signal‑Noise Trade‑off**: Under an extended APT model, returns at horizon δ are rδ = α(δ) w*⊤ s + εδ. The signal factor α(δ) grows with diminishing returns, while noise variance increases linearly. The derived performance metric ln J(δ) = 2 ln α(δ) – ln[α(δ)² + K(δ+δ₀)] shows that the optimal δ* satisfies α’(δ)/α(δ) > 1/(2(δ+δ₀)) and often lies far from the target horizon.

**Bi‑level Optimization**: Gradient flow from the outer query loss through the inner update directly optimizes λ for the final target. The inner weighted loss lets the model exploit multiple horizons, while the outer loop shifts weight toward the most beneficial horizons. Entropy regularization (‑γ ∑λ log λ) prevents λ collapse to a single horizon.

**Warm‑up**: Short‑horizon signals are less noisy and can trap optimization in trivial local minima. Training initially on a mean‑field label provides a generic feature extractor, after which the bi‑level loop can specialize without overcommitting to early horizons.

## Foundational Learning
### Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) and Linear Factor Models
- **Why needed**: Forms the basis of the theoretical analysis by modeling returns as a linear combination of factor exposures plus noise, with time‑dependent signal realization α(δ) and linear noise accumulation.
- **Quick check**: In rδ = α(δ) w*⊤ s + εδ, α(δ) represents the increasing but concave strength of the predictable signal; it differs from static APT where signal is constant.

### Bi‑level Optimization
- **Why needed**: Core algorithm for adaptive label horizon selection; inner/outer loops and gradient‑through‑optimization are essential for implementation.
- **Quick check**: The entropy term encourages a smooth weight distribution, preventing λ from degenerating to a single horizon (collapse).

### Financial Forecasting Evaluation Metrics
- **Why needed**: Performance is primarily measured by the Information Coefficient (IC), which links directly to forecast skill and the Fundamental Law of Active Management.
- **Quick check**: IC is the Pearson correlation between predicted scores and realized returns; maximizing IC improves expected active returns.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Data Loader → Model fθ → Compute losses on support → Inner loop (θ update) → Evaluate on query → Outer loop (λ update) [with Warm‑up preceding main loop].

**Critical Path**
1. Initialize θ and λ (softmax over learnable parameters).
2. Warm‑up: Train for N_warm epochs with loss = MSE(pred, mean(standardized intermediate returns)).
3. Per batch:
   a. Split batch into support and query sets.
   b. Inner: weighted sum of intermediate‑horizon MSE on support, one gradient step on θ (λ fixed).
   c. Outer: query loss on final target + entropy regularizer, gradient ascent on λ.
4. Post‑training: δ* = argmax λ.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- Inner steps (M): paper uses M=1; more steps better approximate full adaptation but increase cost.
- Entropy weight γ: must balance between collapse and over‑flat distribution; tuned on validation.
- Learning rates: outer LR (1e‑3) >> inner LR (1e‑6) to adapt λ faster after warm‑up.
- Batch split: 50/50 support/query with date‑based randomisation to avoid leakage.

**Failure Signatures**
- λ collapse (one‑hot) → increase γ or verify warm‑up.
- λ instability (wild swings) → lower inner LR or increase M.
- No gain over Δ baseline → check if signal‑noise trade‑off is flat; plot fixed‑horizon IC curve.
- Overfitting to query → apply early stopping on validation, reduce outer LR.

**First 3 Experiments**
1. **Paradox Verification**: Train models with fixed supervision horizons δ = 1…Δ on a held‑out set; plot IC vs. δ to confirm non‑monotonicity and locate optimal δ*.
2. **Bi‑level vs Standard**: Compare the adaptive bi‑level model against a standard Δ‑horizon model; expect consistent improvements when δ* ≠ Δ.
3. **Ablation on Warm‑up**: Train bi‑level with and without warm‑up; observe λ distribution and test IC. Without warm‑up, λ should concentrate on short horizons and performance drops.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization across markets**: Does the paradox and bi‑level framework hold for non‑CSI markets (e.g., S&P 500, FX, cryptocurrencies)?
2. **Theoretical extension to deep models**: Can the linear‑factor‑model‑based generalization bounds be rigorously extended to deep neural networks?
3. **Regime adaptation**: How does the optimal supervision horizon evolve under volatile market regimes, and can the bi‑level method adapt online to regime shifts?
4. **Computational shortcut**: Can a one‑shot estimator (e.g., based on correlation or SNR) replace bi‑level optimization to identify the optimal horizon at lower compute cost?

## Limitations
- Feature engineering pipeline is not fully specified (exact statistical descriptors and total dimensionality missing).
- Hyperparameters for each backbone architecture (hidden size, layers, dropout) are unreported, impacting replication.
- Experiments are confined to Chinese A‑share minute‑level data; external validity to other assets and frequencies is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical derivation (signal‑noise trade‑off)**: Medium (rigorous but assumes monotonic α(δ) and linear noise).
- **Empirical gains across architectures**: Medium (consistent improvements reported, but sensitive to undisclosed feature/hyperparameter choices).
- **Method robustness (warm‑up & entropy)**: High (strongly supported by ablations and alignment with brute‑force optimum).
