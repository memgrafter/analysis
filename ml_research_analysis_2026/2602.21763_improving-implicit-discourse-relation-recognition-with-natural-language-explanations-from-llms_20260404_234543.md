# Improving Implicit Discourse Relation Recognition with Natural Language Explanations from LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21763
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21763
- **Reference count:** 5
- **Primary result:** EIDRR achieves 73.14% accuracy and 68.01% F1 on the PDTB benchmark, improving over prior SOTA by 1.34% and 1.14% respectively, while providing high-quality interpretable explanations.

## Executive Summary
Implicit Discourse Relation Recognition (IDRR) suffers from limited interpretability and suboptimal performance. This paper introduces EIDRR, a framework that distills reasoning capabilities from large language models (LLMs) into lightweight IDRR models. For each training instance, an LLM (Qwen‑long) generates a two‑part natural language explanation conditioned on the gold label. A shared RoBERTa encoder is then trained multi‑task to predict the discourse relation and to generate the same explanation via a transformer adapter and a T5 decoder. On the PDTB corpus, EIDRR sets a new state‑of‑the‑art with 73.14% accuracy and 68.01% F1, and human evaluation confirms the generated explanations are interpretable (average 4.20/5). Faithfulness tests indicate alignment between explanations and model decisions, and the approach yields modest gains on related tasks like sentiment classification and natural language inference.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two stages. First, for every training example (arguments and gold label), an LLM is prompted (using a Chain‑of‑Thought template) to produce a structured explanation: a restatement of the arguments followed by a rationale. Second, a lightweight model is built on top of an existing prompt‑based IDRR system (e.g., DiscoPrompt). It shares a RoBERTa encoder that processes a classification prompt and an explanation prompt (which includes the predicted relation). The encoder’s output is fed through a single randomly initialized transformer layer (adapter) to bridge the gap to a T5 decoder, which generates the explanation. The total loss combines classification loss (cross‑entropy) and generation loss (cross‑entropy) with dynamic weights: Stage 1 emphasizes generation (α=0.4, β=0.6) to stabilize the explanation task, then Stage 2 shifts to classification (α=0.8, β=0.2). This plug‑and‑play wrapper adds minimal parameters and can be applied to various prompt‑based IDRR architectures.

## Key Results
- **SOTA on PDTB:** 73.14% accuracy (+1.34%) and 68.01% macro‑F1 (+1.14%) over prior best.
- **High‑quality explanations:** Human evaluation yields an average score of 4.20/5 (interpretability, factuality, fluency), with 98% factuality and 97% fluency.
- **Faithfulness:** Feature importance agreement and robustness equivalence tests show generated explanations reflect model reasoning.
- **Generalization:** ~1% improvement on aspect‑term sentiment classification and natural language inference tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** LLM‑generated explanations provide structured reasoning traces that serve as richer supervision signals than categorical labels alone.  
**Mechanism:** The system prompts an LLM (e.g., Qwen‑long) with in‑context examples to produce a two‑part natural language explanation for each training instance, conditioned on its gold label. During training, the lightweight IDRR model is supervised not only to predict the correct label (classification loss) but also to reproduce the LLM’s explanation (generation loss). The explanation acts as a “thought process” scaffold, forcing the model to learn deeper semantic alignments between the arguments and the relation.  
**Core assumption:** High‑quality, gold‑label‑conditioned explanations from a capable LLM encode semantic reasoning patterns that are transferable to a smaller, supervised model, and that learning to reproduce these patterns improves the model’s internal representations for the primary classification task.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “distill the reasoning capabilities of LLMs into lightweight IDRR models by generating natural language explanations for training instances and using them as additional supervision.”  
- [section 3] Details the prompt template design and the selection/validation of Qwen‑long for explanation generation.  
- [corpus] Neighbor papers explore LLM‑generated data for IDRR, supporting LLM knowledge transfer.  
**Break condition:** If LLM‑generated explanations are systematically inconsistent with the gold labels they are conditioned on, or introduce spurious patterns, the additional supervision would mislead the student model. The paper notes minimal manual correction was needed (< 100 cases), suggesting this did not occur.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A shared encoder with an intervening transformer module effectively harmonizes the conflicting representational needs of a discriminative classification task and a generative explanation task.  
**Mechanism:** The framework uses a single RoBERTa‑based encoder. The classification head directly uses the encoder’s output. For explanation generation, the encoder’s final hidden states are passed through a randomly initialized transformer layer (“transformer module”) before being fed to a T5 decoder. This adapter mitigates the modality mismatch between RoBERTa’s representation space (optimized for understanding/classification) and the T5 decoder’s expectation (optimized for generation). The multi‑task loss (`αL_c + βL_g`) with dynamic weighting coordinates the two objectives.  
**Core assumption:** The core semantic representation needed for predicting a discourse label and for generating a faithful rationale is largely shared, but the output‑space requirements (logits vs. token sequences) create a representational gap that a small, trainable adapter can bridge without destroying the shared knowledge.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “introduce a novel classification‑generation framework… with a shared encoder… transformer module to alleviate the potential mismatch.”  
- [section 3.2] Explicitly states the mismatch problem and introduces the transformer module.  
- [section 4.1] Ablation shows EIDRR w/o Exp underperforms, and the baseline e‑INFERSENT (without the transformer module) produces poor explanations (score 1.71 vs. 4.20).  
**Break condition:** If the semantic requirements for the two tasks are fundamentally divergent or adversarial, the shared encoder would be forced into a compromised representation that harms both. The performance gains and improved explanation quality suggest this was avoided, likely due to the complementary nature of predicting a label and articulating why that label holds.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The plug‑and‑play design allows the distillation of LLM reasoning to be applied as a lightweight wrapper around existing, high‑performing prompt‑based IDRR models, avoiding the need to design a new model from scratch.  
**Mechanism:** For a given existing prompt‑based IDRR model (which uses a template `T_a` and a verbalizer on a `<mask>` token from a RoBERTa‑like encoder), the method minimally augments it by: (1) defining a second prompt template `T_b` for explanation input that appends the predicted relation, (2) stacking a transformer module on the encoder’s last hidden states, and (3) attaching a T5 decoder for auto‑regressive generation. The model is then fine‑tuned multi‑task. The original classification pathway (`T_a` → RoBERTa → Verbalizer) remains intact and trainable.  
**Core assumption:** The feature representations learned by a state‑of‑the‑art prompt‑based IDRR model’s encoder are already strong for classification and provide an excellent foundation for the additional generation task. The adapter and decoder add minimal parameters that can be trained effectively on the existing dataset with the new explanation supervision.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “Our framework is plug‑and‑play, enabling easy integration with most existing IDRR models.”  
- [section 3.2] Details integration with prompt‑based models using Equation (1) and (2), showing how `T_b` uses the predicted `˜y` and only new components are added.  
- [section 4.1] EIDRR builds upon and significantly outperforms DiscoPrompt, demonstrating compatibility and efficacy of the wrapper on a strong existing architecture.  
**Break condition:** If the existing IDRR model’s encoder representations are fundamentally incompatible with sequence generation (e.g., if they discard positional information crucial for generation), or if the model’s architecture prevents cleanly extracting intermediate representations, the plug‑and‑play integration would fail or require significant re‑engineering. Success with DiscoPrompt suggests this break condition was not met for modern transformer‑based prompt learners.

## Foundational Learning
### Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Prompting
**Why needed here:** The quality of LLM‑generated explanations is paramount. The paper uses a CoT‑inspired template (restate arguments → give rationale) to elicit more structured, higher‑quality reasoning from the LLM than a simple “explain” instruction. This directly impacts the utility of the distillation signal.  
**Quick check:** How does structuring an explanation into discrete thought steps (like restating premises) improve the quality and consistency of LLM‑generated rationales compared to asking for an explanation in one shot?

### Multi‑Task Learning with Dynamic Loss Weighting
**Why needed here:** The framework jointly trains classification and generation. These tasks have different convergence rates and potential conflicts (e.g., a label might be predictable without a perfect rationale). The paper uses a two‑stage schedule (first emphasize generation, then classification) to coordinate learning. Understanding this scheduling is key to reproducing results.  
**Quick check:** Why might emphasizing the generation task (`β > α`) in early training epochs be necessary when the ultimate goal is improved classification performance?

### Prompt‑Based Discourse Relation Recognition
**Why needed here:** The target models being enhanced (e.g., DiscoPrompt) are prompt‑based. To integrate EIDRR, one must understand how these models work: using a template to query a PLM for a connective (`<mask>`), then mapping connectives to PDTB senses. The onboarding requires familiarity with this paradigm.  
**Quick check:** In a typical prompt‑based IDRR model, what is the role of the “verbalizer,” and how does it convert a predicted token (a connective like “but”) into a discourse relation label (like “Comparison”)?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
**Input:** Two arguments (`arg1`, `arg2`) and a gold label `y`.  
**Explanation Generation Stage (offline):** LLM + Prompt Template (`T_explanation`) → Natural Language Explanation `e`.  
**Model Core (trainable):**  
1. **Shared Encoder:** RoBERTa‑like PLM. Processes `T_class(arg1, arg2)` for classification and `T_expln(arg1, arg2, ˜y)` for generation.  
2. **Classification Head:** (Existing from base model) Takes `[CLS]` or `<mask>` representation → Verbalizer → Predicted label `˜y`.  
3. **Adapter:** One randomly initialized Transformer layer. Takes encoder’s last hidden states `H_last` → `Ĥ`.  
4. **Generation Decoder:** T5‑like PLM decoder. Autoregressively generates explanation `˜e` conditioned on `Ĥ`.  
**Loss:** `L_total = αL_c(y, ˜y) + βL_g(e, ˜e)`, with staged weighting.

### Critical Path
1. **Prepare dataset:** For each `(arg1, arg2, y)` in PDTB, obtain pre‑computed explanation `e` from LLM.  
2. **Initialize:** Load pre‑trained `robertabase` and `t5base`. Insert one `nn.TransformerEncoderLayer` between them.  
3. **Training Loop (Two stages):**  
   - **Stage 1 (Generation‑focused):** Set `α=0.4, β=0.6`. For each batch, forward pass: get `H_last` from RoBERTa with `T_class` for `˜y`; compute `L_c`. Pass `H_last` through adapter → `Ĥ`; decode with T5 using `T_expln` and `˜y` as input prefix; compute `L_g`. Backpropagate `L_total`.  
   - **Stage 2 (Classification‑focused):** Lower LR for non‑RoBERTa modules. Set `α=0.8, β=0.2`. Continue training.  
4. **Inference:** Use only the classification pathway (`T_class` → RoBERTa → Verbalizer) to predict `˜y`. Generation pathway active only during training or if an explanation is desired post‑hoc.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Adapter Capacity vs. Overfitting:** One transformer layer is sufficient; more layers gave no gains. This keeps parameters minimal, preserving the base model’s knowledge.  
- **Task Weighting (`α`, `β`):** Must be scheduled. Constant equal weighting harms convergence. The paper’s schedule (gen‑first, cls‑second) is a heuristic based on observed convergence speeds.  
- **Explanation Template (`T_b`):** Must include the predicted label `˜y` as a prefix (Predict‑AND‑Explain). Explain‑ThenPredict (generate `e` first, then use `e` for `y`) was found inconsistent and harmful.

### Failure Signatures
- **Poor Explanation Quality (Human eval score <3.0):** Likely due to bad LLM explanations (inconsistent with label), inadequate adapter (failure to bridge modality gap), or training instability (e.g., `β` too low in Stage 1).  
- **Classification Performance Degradation (vs. base model):** Indicates catastrophic interference. Check if `α` became too dominant too early, or if the generation task is creating conflicting gradients. Monitor `L_c` and `L_g` separately.  
- **Label‑Explanation Misalignment (low Feature Importance Agreement):** Explanations change when classification‑relevant features are occluded, or vice versa. Suggests the two tasks are not sharing meaningful representations; likely adapter is ineffective or tasks are not being coordinated by the shared encoder.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline Replication & w/o Explanation Ablation:** Train the base prompt‑based IDRR model (e.g., DiscoPrompt) on PDTB. Then, train EIDRR *without* the generation loss (`β=0`). Expect EIDRR (full) > EIDRR w/o Exp > Base model, confirming the contribution of the explanation task.  
2. **Adapter Necessity Test:** Compare full EIDRR against a variant where the T5 decoder is fed directly `H_last` (no transformer module, mimicking e‑INFERSENT). Expect significantly lower explanation quality (human eval) and possibly lower classification F1, validating the adapter’s role.  
3. **Explanation Quality Human Evaluation:** As in paper (Section 4.2). Sample 100‑200 correctly predicted test instances. Have annotators score generated explanations on Interpretability, Factuality, Fluency (0‑2,0‑2,0‑1). Expect average score >4.0/5, with high Factuality (>98% score 1+) and Fluency (>97% score 1). This confirms the LLM’s reasoning was successfully distilled.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How can we develop more effective and automatic methods to evaluate the faithfulness of generated explanations in implicit discourse relation recognition?  
*Basis:* The conclusion states that “developing more effective methods to evaluate the faithfulness of generated explanations” is a key direction for future work. Current evaluation relies on indirect proxies like feature importance agreement and robustness equivalence, which may not fully capture whether explanations truly reflect the model’s reasoning process.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* New evaluation frameworks or benchmark datasets with gold‑standard human rationales, or probing‑based techniques that directly compare explanation content with internal model states.

### Open Question 2
To what extent do the generated explanations align with the model’s actual reasoning process, and how can we interpret the model’s internals to understand its discourse relation recognition?  
*Basis:* The conclusion cites recent work (Miao and Kan 2025; Mondorf and Wold 2025) and calls for “further exploring the interpretability of model internals in discourse relation recognition.” The paper demonstrates fidelity via feature noise and removal studies but does not analyze the model’s neural circuits or decision‑making pathways to confirm alignment.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Applying circuit‑level analysis or probing to the trained EIDRR model to identify which components activate for specific discourse relations and comparing them to explanation content.

### Open Question 3
What is the optimal configuration for the multi‑task training strategy (e.g., loss weighting α/β, two‑stage schedule, transformer module size) to effectively distill LLM reasoning into lightweight IDRR models?  
*Basis:* The method section describes a specific two‑stage training with α=0.4/β=0.6 then α=0.8/β=0.2 and a single transformer layer, but provides no ablation or sensitivity analysis.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic experiments varying loss weights, number of transformer layers, and training schedules to measure impact on both classification accuracy and explanation quality.

### Open Question 4
How does the quantity and quality of LLM‑generated explanations impact the performance and generalization of the distilled model to other tasks?  
*Basis:* The paper notes that gains on ATSC and NLI were achieved “with only limited explanations” and required minimal human post‑processing, but does not study scaling effects. It is unknown whether performance improvements scale with more or higher‑quality explanations, or if there is a saturation point; also, the transfer efficiency to new tasks is not thoroughly analyzed.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Training EIDRR with varying amounts of explanation‑augmented data (e.g., subsets of PDTB explanations) and evaluating on IDRR, ATSC, and NLI to determine data efficiency and transfer limits.

## Limitations
- Exact prompt templates, verbalizer mappings, and Qwen‑long access details are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.  
- Explanations are generated once offline; quality depends on the chosen LLM and may not generalize to other LLMs.  
- The plug‑and‑play claim is validated only on DiscoPrompt; broader compatibility remains uncertain.  
- Human evaluation covered only correctly predicted instances; explanation quality for errors is unknown.  
- Generalization gains to sentiment/NLI are modest (~1%) and may not robustly transfer.
