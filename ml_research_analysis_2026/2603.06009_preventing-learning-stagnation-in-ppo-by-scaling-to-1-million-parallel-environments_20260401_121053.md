# Preventing Learning Stagnation in PPO by Scaling to 1 Million Parallel Environments

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06009  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06009  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Scaling PPO to over 1 million parallel environments, while fixing minibatch size and learning rate and increasing epochs, prevents learning plateaus and enables continuous improvement up to one trillion interactions, doubling the solve rate on the hardest Kinetix tasks.

## Executive Summary
Learning plateaus in Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) are caused by an outer step size that is too large relative to gradient noise during policy updates. The outer loop of PPO behaves like stochastic gradient ascent, where the effective step size depends on regularization (clipping or proximal policy) and the number of samples per update. Increasing parallel environments raises the sample count, which simultaneously reduces gradient noise and the outer step size (via increased "aging" of the reference policy). The authors propose a stable scaling recipe: keep minibatch size and learning rate fixed, and increase the number of epochs as parallelization grows. Applied to the Kinetix benchmark, scaling to over 1 million environments sustained monotonic improvement for up to one trillion environment interactions, doubling the solve rate on the hardest tasks. Validation confirms that the outer step size governs plateauing, and tuning the inner learning rate cannot compensate for a poorly sized outer step.

## Method Summary
The paper combines theoretical analysis and empirical scaling of PPO. The core method models PPO’s outer loop as stochastic optimization, identifying a tradeoff between outer step size (controlled by regularization parameters like the clipping threshold ϵ or the center of mass (COM) in PPO-EWMA) and gradient noise (inversely proportional to the number of transitions collected per update). To prevent plateaus, one must reduce the outer step size relative to the noise. This is achieved by increasing the number of parallel environments, which increases the batch size per update. Crucially, hyperparameters must be co-scaled: the minibatch size and inner learning rate are kept constant, while the number of epochs (passes over the collected batch) is increased. This preserves the inner optimization dynamics while fully utilizing the larger dataset. The approach is validated through targeted hyperparameter sweeps on Jax2D locomotion, comparisons of scaling strategies on Isaac Gym robotics, and a large-scale run on Kinetix with up to 1,048,576 parallel environments.

## Key Results
- Increasing parallel environments (hence batch size) reduces both outer step size and gradient noise, preventing the oscillation-and-stagnation failure mode characteristic of PPO plateaus.
- The recommended scaling recipe—fix minibatch size and learning rate, increase epochs—outperforms alternative co-scaling strategies (e.g., scaling minibatch size without epoch adjustment) across multiple tasks.
- On the Kinetix benchmark, scaling to over 1 million parallel environments enabled continuous improvement for up to one trillion interactions, doubling the solve rate on the hardest tasks (from ~0.2 to ~0.4).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Learning plateaus occur when the outer step size (policy update magnitude) is too large relative to the gradient noise.
- **Mechanism:** The outer loop of PPO is analogous to stochastic gradient ascent in policy space. The effective step size depends on regularization strength (via clipping ϵ or proximal policy COM) and the number of samples used to estimate the gradient. If the step is too large, small errors in the gradient estimate cause the policy to oscillate around a local optimum instead of converging.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] Plateaus arise because sample-based loss estimates become poor proxies when the outer step size is too large relative to the noise.  
  - [section 3.1] Large outer step size leads to "thrashing around a local optimum"; Figure 2 shows high gradient norms but stagnant solve rate.  
  - [section 3.2] Figure 3 checkpoint retraining: adjusting COM (outer step size) immediately resumes learning from a plateau, demonstrating causality.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Increasing the number of parallel environments reduces both the outer step size and the gradient noise.
- **Mechanism:** More parallel environments produce more transitions per outer iteration (larger batch). Larger batch size lowers gradient variance (less noise). Indirectly, it reduces the outer step size because the behavior policy ages relative to the reference policy when the same number of minibatch updates is applied, effectively increasing the COM.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] Increasing samples per update via more parallel environments reduces both step size and noise.  
  - [section 4.3] Larger minibatches are more robust to weak regularization; Figure 7 shows slower or no plateau with larger batches.  
  - [section 4.4] Data-to-Divergence Ratio (DDR) optimal value increases with compute budget, implying larger batches are needed as training scales.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** When scaling parallel environments, the safest hyperparameter co-scaling recipe is to keep minibatch size and learning rate fixed and increase the number of epochs.
- **Mechanism:** Changing minibatch size or learning rate alters inner optimization dynamics, which non-linearly shifts the effective outer step size. By fixing the inner loop (Adam LR, minibatch size) and only increasing epochs, one increases the number of policy improvements per collected batch without changing the step size per gradient step, preserving the noise–step size relationship.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 5] Keeping the inner optimization unchanged performs best; scaling minibatch size without learning rate adjustment causes severe degradation.  
  - [section 3.2] Figure 4 shows learning rate tuning cannot compensate for a poor outer step size (COM), suggesting inner/outer loop decoupling.  
  - [section 5.1] Figure 10: on Isaac Gym, fixing minibatch/LR and increasing epochs preserves performance, while scaling minibatch or learning rate degrades results.

## Foundational Learning
- **Stochastic Optimization (SGD Step Size–Noise Tradeoff):** PPO’s outer loop is modeled as stochastic gradient ascent where convergence requires the step size to be balanced against gradient noise variance. Larger batches reduce noise but may require step size adjustment.  
  *Why needed:* This framework explains why plateaus occur (step size too large relative to noise) and why increasing parallel environments helps.  
  *Quick check:* In SGD, if you double the batch size while keeping learning rate constant, what happens to gradient variance and the safe learning rate range?

- **PPO Clipping and Reference Policy:** PPO limits policy updates via a clipping parameter ϵ or via a proximal center of mass (COM) in variants like PPO-EWMA. These directly control the outer step size by bounding the KL divergence between current and reference policies.  
  *Why needed:* These regularization mechanisms are the knobs that set the outer step size; tuning them prevents overshooting optima.  
  *Quick check:* How does the PPO clipping term zero out gradients when the probability ratio exceeds 1±ϵ, and what is its effect on maximum KL divergence?

- **Data-to-Divergence Ratio (DDR):** DDR = (transitions per update) / (target KL divergence). It compactly relates batch size (data) to regularization strength (divergence), guiding how to scale parallelization with compute budget.  
  *Why needed:* DDR predicts that as compute budget grows, the optimal batch size per update should increase, motivating massive parallelization.  
  *Quick check:* For a fixed KL budget, if you increase parallel environments (hence batch size), what happens to DDR and what does the theory predict about plateau risk?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Parallel environments -> Rollout buffer (transitions, advantages) -> Inner loop (shuffle into minibatches) -> PPO loss (clip ratio, value, entropy) -> Backward + Adam update -> (optional) EWMA reference update -> Next outer iteration (new rollouts with updated policy).

- **Critical path:** Environment stepping → buffer population → GAE computation → minibatch sampling → forward/loss computation → backward pass → parameter update → (optional) reference policy update → next collection cycle.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  *Parallelism vs aging:* More environments increase batch size (less noise) but also age the reference policy longer before updates, reducing outer step size.  
  *Minibatch scaling:* Increasing minibatch size improves hardware utilization but without learning-rate scaling (√ rule) changes the effective outer step size and may destabilize.  
  *Epoch count:* More epochs exploit the fixed dataset more fully but risk overfitting to stale data if regularization is weak.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  Solve rate/return plateaus while gradient norms remain high (Fig. 2).  
  KL divergence between current and behavior policies spikes and stays elevated.  
  Performance drop when scaling parallel environments if minibatch size is increased without learning rate adjustment (Fig. 9).  
  Stagnation at suboptimal return under weak regularization (low COM or large ϵ).

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Sweep COM/ϵ on Jax2D locomotion; validate that reducing outer step size (higher COM) resumes learning from a plateau (Section 3.1, Figures 2–3).  
  2. Compare scaling strategies on Isaac Gym: fix minibatch/LR and increase epochs vs. scale minibatch/LR; the former preserves performance while the latter degrades (Section 5.1, Figure 10).  
  3. Apply recommended recipe to scale SFL on Kinetix to 1M parallel environments; observe monotonic improvement up to 1 trillion steps, while baseline plateaus after ~10B steps (Section 6, Figure 11).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Will the outer step size-plateau relationship and scaling approach hold in sparse-reward environments with hard exploration challenges?  
2. Can adaptive methods for outer step size control (e.g., based on gradient noise or learning progress) achieve comparable or better performance than fixed scaling while reducing need for massive parallelization?  
3. Does the outer step size framework and scaling recipe apply to other on-policy RL algorithms beyond PPO (e.g., TRPO, A2C, IMPALA)?  
4. Can we derive a theoretical formula linking optimal DDR to training budget (e.g., number of update steps, environment horizon) to guide scaling without extensive hyperparameter sweeps?

## Limitations
- **Generality:** Results are demonstrated on dense-reward continuous control tasks (Jax2D, Kinetix, Isaac Gym); applicability to discrete actions, sparse rewards, or partial observability is untested.
- **Algorithm specificity:** The analysis and scaling recipe are tailored to PPO’s clipping/EWMA structure; other policy gradient methods may not exhibit the same outer step size/noise tradeoff.
- **Compute intensity:** The 1M environment scale requires massive parallelism; benefits may be inaccessible to researchers with limited resources.
- **Epoch overfitting risk:** Increasing epochs without bound may lead to overfitting to stale data; the breaking point is not fully explored.

## Confidence
- **High:** Outer step size governs plateauing (causal evidence via checkpoint retraining and COM sweeps); Kinetix success with 1M envs (clear monotonic improvement).
- **Medium:** Scaling parallel environments reduces step size and noise (theoretical and empirical support but task-dependent); fixed minibatch/LR with increased epochs is optimal (empirical superiority in comparisons but no universal proof).
- **Low:** Generality to other algorithms or sparse-reward domains (no evidence provided).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate on diverse domains: evaluate on Atari (discrete) and MiniGrid (sparse rewards) to test generality of the outer step size framework and scaling recipe.
2. Ablate maximum epochs: identify where extra epochs degrade performance due to data staleness and compare to DDR predictions.
3. Cross-method validation: apply the same scaling recipe to A2C and TRPO to see if the outer step size/noise explanation and DDR scaling hold.
