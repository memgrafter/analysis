# Quant Experts: Token-aware Adaptive Error Reconstruction with Mixture of Experts for Large Vision-Language Models Quantization
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.24059  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24059  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: On Qwen2VL-72B under W4A6 quantization, QE achieves a 5.09% absolute accuracy gain, approaching full-precision performance across multiple benchmarks, with 3.5×–4.5× hardware acceleration.

## Executive Summary
Deploying large vision-language models (VLMs) efficiently requires aggressive compression. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is a practical solution, but standard PTQ methods apply static, global error compensation that fails to capture the dynamic variation of important channels across tokens and modalities. This paper introduces Quant Experts (QE), a token-aware adaptive error reconstruction framework that leverages a mixture of experts (MoE) to overcome this limitation. QE first identifies per-token important channels during calibration and partitions them into token‑independent (handled by a shared low‑rank adapter) and token‑dependent (clustered by co‑occurrence and handled by multiple routed low‑rank adapters). A lightweight router selects the appropriate routed expert at inference time.

QE is evaluated on Qwen2VL (2B, 7B, 72B) and InternVL2 (2B, 8B) across a broad suite of benchmarks. It consistently outperforms strong baselines like MBQ and LQER. Notably, on the 72B model under W4A6 quantization, QE recovers 5.09% absolute accuracy, nearly matching full‑precision performance, while incurring minimal overhead and achieving 3.5×–4.5× hardware acceleration.

## Method Summary
QE consists of three phases: calibration, expert construction, and inference. During calibration, a small dataset (128 image-caption pairs) is used to collect per-layer activations. For each layer, channel importance is computed as the product of activation magnitude and weight row mean; the top‑k channels per token are marked as important. The frequency of each channel across tokens determines its grouping: high‑frequency channels are token‑independent, low‑frequency are token‑dependent. For token‑independent channels, a shared low‑rank adapter is built by applying whitening and SVD to the quantized weight residual. For token‑dependent channels, a co‑occurrence matrix is constructed, converted to an NPMI‑based similarity graph, and spectral clustering yields multiple groups; each group yields a routed low‑rank adapter via weighted SVD and a corresponding router column (mean absolute residual). Optional refinement fine‑tunes only the routed experts and router with a combined reconstruction and clustering loss. At inference, for each token, the router scores each routed expert and selects the one with minimal predicted error; the output is the sum of the quantized linear operation and contributions from the shared and selected routed experts (masked to their channel sets).

## Key Results
- QE consistently outperforms strong PTQ baselines (MBQ, LQER) on a diverse set of benchmarks for Qwen2VL and InternVL2 models.
- On the 72B Qwen2VL model under W4A6 quantization, QE achieves a 5.09% absolute accuracy gain, nearly recovering full‑precision performance.
- The method incurs minimal parameter overhead and yields 3.5×–4.5× hardware acceleration in end‑to‑end inference.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The positions of important channels vary across tokens and modalities, making a static global compensation strategy insufficient for accurate VLMs quantization.
- **Mechanism:** From calibration data, compute per‑token important channels as the top‑k channels with largest product of activation magnitude and weight row mean (Eq. 2). Aggregate these over all tokens to obtain frequency $f_c$ for each channel (Eq. 3). Channels with high $f_c$ are labeled token‑independent; low‑$f_c$ channels are token‑dependent.
- **Core assumption:** The frequency of a channel’s appearance across tokens correlates with its global versus local influence on quantization error.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] States: “We observe that the distributions and occurrence frequencies of important channels vary significantly both across modalities and among tokens, even within the same modality. QE divides the important channels into token‑independent and token‑dependent groups.”
  - [section 2.1 & 2.2] Provides visualizations (Fig. 2, 3) and equations (1)–(3) showing token‑wise variation and the long‑tail frequency distribution.
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., MoBiQuant arXiv:2602.20191) explore token‑adaptive quantization but do not analyze channel frequency partitioning; this observation is a novel contribution of this work.
- **Break condition:** If channel frequencies were uniform, the partitioning would not separate distinct behaviors and the method would collapse to a single global expert, losing adaptivity.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Token‑independent channels dominate the global quantization error, which can be effectively stabilized by a shared low‑rank adapter applied uniformly to all tokens.
- **Mechanism:** For token‑independent set $C_s$, build a shared expert. Compute mean activation of $C_s$, weight the quantization error matrix $E$ accordingly, and apply whitening via Cholesky of the activation covariance. Perform SVD on the whitened error to obtain low‑rank matrices $L_{SA}, L_{SB}$ (each rank $r/2$). At inference, this adapter is always used: $\Delta = L_{SA} L_{SB} \cdot x_t$ is added to the quantized linear output.
- **Core assumption:** The error pattern of token‑independent channels is relatively constant across tokens, so a single low‑rank approximation suffices.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] Algorithm 3 details the whitening SVD and shared expert construction.
  - [abstract] “a shared expert is designed for most tokens to compensate for global quantization error using a low‑rank adapter.”
  - [corpus] Low‑rank reconstruction appears in LQER (a baseline), but LQER applies a single global adapter to all channels; QE restricts it to token‑independent channels, a delimitation not present in the corpus.
- **Break condition:** If token‑independent channels exhibit high residual variance after shared compensation, the low‑rank structure becomes inadequate, requiring more expressive (e.g., higher‑rank) modeling.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Token‑dependent channels exhibit co‑occurrence patterns that cluster into groups; each group can be handled by a specialized routed low‑rank adapter, and a lightweight router can select the appropriate adapter per token.
- **Mechanism:** For token‑dependent set $C_r$, construct a co‑occurrence matrix $O$ (Eq. 7) from calibration tokens. Convert to similarity matrix $S$ using Normalized Pointwise Mutual Information (NPMI, Eqs. 8–10). Perform spectral clustering on the normalized Laplacian of $S$ to obtain $N_r$ clusters. For each cluster, weight the residual error $E_S$ (after shared expert) by a cluster‑specific vector $\omega$ derived from mean activations of tokens in that cluster, then SVD yields $L_{RA}, L_{RB}$. A router $R$ (linear layer) predicts a score for each expert from the current token; the expert with smallest score (i.e., lowest predicted error) is selected and applied.
- **Core assumption:** Channels that frequently co‑occur have correlated quantization error patterns, and a simple router trained on calibration data can generalize to unseen tokens.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3] Algorithm 2 and Equations (7)–(10) describe clustering and routed expert creation.
  - [abstract] “routed experts including multiple routed low‑rank adapters are elaborated to compensate for local quantization error related to specific tokens.”
  - [corpus] MoEQuant (arXiv:2505.03804) and Mixture Compressor (arXiv:2410.06270) use expert routing for MoE model quantization, but they do not address token‑dependent channel clustering; QE’s application to VLMs is distinct.
- **Break condition:** If clustering yields incoherent groups (low intra‑cluster similarity) or the router mis‑ranks experts, the selected adapter may increase error rather than reduce it.

- **Why needed here:** QE is a PTQ method; understanding PTQ’s goal (compressing models without retraining) and its core challenge (outlier‑driven error) is prerequisite.

### Concept: Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
- **Why needed:** Each expert in QE is a low‑rank adapter ($L_A L_B$) that approximates an error matrix with few parameters; grasping LoRA’s parameterization clarifies the efficiency gain.
- **Quick check:** If a full $d \times d$ matrix has $d^2$ parameters, how many parameters does a rank‑$r$ LoRA adapter ($d \times r$ and $r \times d$) use? Why is this beneficial?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Calibration dataset → activation statistics → Channel importance estimator → per‑token top‑k important channels and global frequency → Partitioning module → splits channels into token‑independent ($C_s$) and token‑dependent ($C_r$) sets → Shared expert builder → creates low‑rank adapter $(L_{SA}, L_{SB})$ from $C_s$ → Routed experts builder → clusters $C_r$, creates multiple $(L_{RA,i}, L_{RB,i})$ and router $R$ → Inference wrapper → integrates quantized weights, shared expert, router, and selected routed expert.

Critical path:
1. For each token $x_t$, compute router scores $s_i = R(|x_t|)$ for all routed experts.
2. Select expert $i^* = \arg\min_i s_i$ (or top‑1).
3. Compute shared compensation: $\Delta_s = L_{SA} L_{SB} x_t$.
4. Compute routed compensation: $\Delta_r = L_{RA,i^*} L_{RB,i^*} x_t$.
5. Output: $y = W_q x_t + \Delta_s + \Delta_r$.

Design tradeoffs:
- Shared vs. routed rank: total rank $r$ is fixed; more rank for shared improves global error suppression, less for routed reduces local adaptivity.
- Number of routed experts $N_r$: larger $N_r$ yields finer token‑specific adaptation but increases memory and router latency; also risks overfitting on limited calibration.
- Router complexity: a linear router is cheap but may not capture complex token‑expert affinity; a deeper router could improve selection at the cost of overhead.

Failure signatures:
- Accuracy collapse on rare token semantics → mis‑clustering or insufficient $N_r$.
- High variance across different calibration sets → unstable frequency estimation.
- Router always selects the same expert → routing degenerates, effectively removing token‑dependent compensation.
- Memory overhead exceeds expected → rank or $N_r$ too large for target hardware.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Ablation study:** Remove either shared expert or routed experts, or use random routing/clustering, to quantify each component’s contribution (Table 4).
2. **Scalability test:** Apply QE to models of 2B, 7B, and 72B parameters under W4A6/W4A8 to verify consistent gains (Tables 1–3).
3. **Sensitivity analysis:** Vary $N_r$ (number of routed experts) and $k$ (important channels) to examine trade‑offs between accuracy and overhead (Tables 6, 13).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Is the fixed number of important channels (k=32) optimal across all layers and tasks, or does it require adaptive selection?
2. Can the lightweight router's simple error-based scoring be improved with more sophisticated, learned routing mechanisms?
3. How sensitive is the refinement stage to its hyperparameters (τ, α, β), and do they require per-layer tuning?
4. To what extent does QE generalize to VLM architectures with different modality fusion strategies (e.g., cross-attention vs. concatenation)?

## Limitations
- Quantifier ambiguity: The base PTQ scheme (e.g., SmoothQuant vs. pure RTN) is not specified, which complicates fair comparison with baselines.
- Router details: The exact router input (absolute activation, dequantized activation, etc.) and scoring formulation remain unclear, hindering reproducibility.
- Efficiency evidence: The claimed 3.5×–4.5× hardware acceleration lacks end‑to‑end profiling data; actual overhead might differ.
