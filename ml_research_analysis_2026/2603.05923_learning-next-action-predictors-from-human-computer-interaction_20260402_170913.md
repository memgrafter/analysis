# Learning Next Action Predictors from Human-Computer Interaction
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05923
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05923
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LongNAP outperforms SFT by 79% and prompted baselines by 39% on held-out next action prediction; 17.1% of predicted trajectories are well-aligned (LLM-judge ≥0.5), rising to 26% for high-confidence predictions.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces NAPsack, a pipeline that passively annotates longitudinal human-computer interaction data using vision-language models, releasing a dataset of 360K actions across 1,800 hours from 20 users. It then proposes LongNAP, a retrieval-augmented model that combines parametric and in-context learning via policy gradients to generate user-specific reasoning traces, retrieve relevant past traces, and predict future actions. Evaluated with an LLM-as-judge metric, LongNAP significantly improves over supervised finetuning and prompting baselines, demonstrating that learning to reason and retrieve from interaction history enhances next action prediction.

## Method Summary
LongNAP operates in two phases: first, it generates a retrieval query (“reasoning to retrieve”) describing the user’s current activity from a context window; this query retrieves similar past reasoning traces from a memory store via BM25 with temporal decay and MMR. Second, it integrates the retrieved traces to produce a refined reasoning (“reasoning to predict”) and then predicts the next sequence of actions. The entire system is trained end-to-end with group relative policy optimization (GRPO), using an LLM-as-judge to compute a similarity reward between predictions and ground-truture future actions. The memory is continuously updated with high-reward reasoning traces per context, enabling personalization without storing full histories.

## Key Results
- LongNAP achieves a 79% relative improvement over supervised finetuning and 39% over prompted baselines in LLM-judge similarity score.
- 17.1% of its predicted trajectories receive a well-aligned score (≥0.5), increasing to 26% among high-confidence predictions.
- Ablations show that removing either the reasoning trace or the retriever degrades performance, confirming the hybrid design’s contribution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Passive supervision via vision-language models enables scalable annotation of naturalistic, longitudinal human-computer interaction data.
- **Mechanism:** NAPsack continuously records screenshots and input events, compresses them by grouping adjacent interactions into bursts and saving only active frames, then chunks the stream and uses a VLM to annotate each chunk with a high-level action description, merging everything into a time-ordered sequence of labeled events.
- **Core assumption:** VLMs can reliably generate accurate, consistent natural-language action descriptions from multimodal interaction traces without human labeling.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper reports labeling over 360K actions across one month of continuous phone usage from 20 users (abstract, section 3.1). Validation via LLM-judge and human evaluation supports label quality; however, no independent large-scale human verification is provided.
- **Break condition:** If VLMs fail to maintain consistent granularity or accuracy across diverse user behaviors, the noisy labels will degrade downstream model performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** LongNAP’s hybrid parametric+in-context retrieval architecture overcomes limitations of pure finetuning or prompting by learning to generate, retrieve, and apply user-specific reasoning traces.
- **Mechanism:** The policy π (initialized from a VLM) first generates a retrieval query `z_retrieve` from recent context; this query retrieves relevant past `(context, reasoning)` entries from a memory store via BM25 with temporal decay and MMR. The retrieved traces are then used to refine reasoning (`z_predict`) and predict future actions. Both phases are optimized end-to-end via policy gradients (GRPO) with a temporal reward from an LLM-as-judge.
- **Core assumption:** Generating explicit reasoning traces improves retrieval relevance and allows the model to flexibly reuse past inferences for new contexts, mitigating “latent learning” failures.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper’s architecture description (section 4.1) and ablations (removing reasoning causes -0.07 absolute drop) support the mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If reasoning traces become degenerate or the memory accumulates low-quality traces, retrieval may degrade performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Atemporal reward shaping—using an LLM-as-judge to compute semantic similarity between predicted and actual future trajectories—provides a scalable, dense training signal for long-horizon action prediction.
- **Mechanism:** For each rollout, the LLM judge outputs a continuous similarity score (0–1) against the ground-truth future actions. This reward is used in GRPO to optimize both the reasoning-to-retrieve and reasoning-to-predict phases without hand-crafted metrics or human annotations at training time.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM judge’s score correlates with true alignment of user intent and remains stable enough to guide policy optimization.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper’s abstract and section 4.2 describe the reward; internal validation (human eval matches LLM judge on a subset) provides初步 evidence.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM judge becomes biased or fails to capture nuanced intent, the policy may optimize for judge-pleasing but misaligned predictions.

## Foundational Learning

  *Why needed:* Defines the core problem: predicting multimodal future actions (screenshots + actions) from a history window. All data and modeling approaches are built around this formalism.

- **Concept: Passive supervision and event-based compression**  
  *Why needed:* Enables scalable dataset creation without manual annotation. Understanding event bursts and frame filtering is key to replicating NAPsack.  
  *Quick check:* How does NAPsack reduce storage by ~70% while maintaining caption quality, and what role do input events (I/O) play?

- **Concept: Retrieval-augmented in-context learning with memory**  
  *Why needed:* LongNAP’s architecture centers on maintaining a memory of past (observation, reasoning) pairs and learning to retrieve from it. Grasping this is essential to understand why it outperforms pure finetuning.  
  *Quick check:* In LongNAP’s two-phase generation, what is the purpose of the “reasoning to retrieve” trace, and how does it differ from a typical RAG query?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data Collection Layer → Preprocessing → Training Data; Memory Store ↔ Training Loop ↔ Policy Network; Evaluation → Metrics

### Critical Path
1. Install NAPsack and record a personal interaction session (or obtain dataset).  
2. Generate ground-truth labels for validation (e.g., via human annotation or VLM).  
3. Train LongNAP on a single user’s chronological data (first weeks).  
4. Evaluate on held-out data using LLM-judge pass@1.  
5. Run ablations (remove reasoning, retriever, shuffle data) to verify components.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Storage vs. Quality in NAPsack:** Compression (event-based) reduces storage by 70% with minimal loss; adding I/O improves quality but requires extra logging.
- **Memory size vs. Retrieval noise:** Larger memory increases coverage but may introduce irrelevant traces; temporal decay and MMR mitigate this.
- **Parametric vs. In-context learning:** Pure finetuning (SFT) lacks flexible reuse; pure prompting lacks adaptation. LongNAP balances both via learned retrieval.

### Failure Signatures
- Very low LLM-judge scores (<0.2) across baselines suggest poor data quality or highly unpredictable user.
- Large ablation drop (>0.05 absolute) when removing reasoning indicates failure to generate useful retrieval queries.
- Cross-user generalization gap >0.15 signals overfitting to user-specific patterns.
- High intra-cluster variance combined with low pass@1 indicates model uncertainty.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **NAPssack validation:** Record a 10-minute session, generate human ground truth, run all four NAPsack conditions (naive, split, compress, IO), compute LLM-judge scores and human win rates. Expect IO+split+compress to score ~0.70.  
2. **Single-user LongNAP baseline:** Train on first two weeks of a user, validate on week 3, test on week 4. Compare LLM-judge score vs. SFT and prompted baselines. Expect LongNAP to outperform SFT by ~0.17 absolute.  
3. **Retriever ablation impact:** Train LongNAP with retriever disabled (use only recent context) on 5 users. Measure absolute drop in LLM-judge score. Expect ~0.06 drop on average.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can LongNAP interleave retrieval and reasoning in one generation pass?** The current two-phase pipeline may limit flexibility; a more integrated approach could improve efficiency and accuracy.
2. **How does LongNAP performance scale with more users and longer histories?** The paper evaluated only 20 users over one month; scaling to hundreds of users and years of data remains open.
3. **What efficient personalization methods can serve many users with individual models?** Storing full LoRA per user is costly; methods like adapter merging or meta-learning need exploration.
4. **What rewards avoid hacking when using LLM-as-judge for NAP?** The judge alone may be prone to reward hacking; alternative rewards (human feedback, ensemble of judges) could improve robustness.

## Limitations
- **VLM annotation quality:** NAPsack’s passive labels rely on Gemini Flash; no large-scale human verification, risking systematic noise.
- **LLM-as-judge reliability:** The primary metric may not capture nuanced user intent; human evaluation was limited to a small subset.
- **Cross-user generalization:** Models trained on one user’s data likely fail to generalize; the paper only reports single-user results.

## Confidence
- Passive supervision enabling scalable data: **Medium** (internal validation exists but lacks independent human verification)
- LongNAP’s hybrid architecture: **Medium** (ablations support contribution but reasoning traces are not deeply interpretable)
- LLM-as-judge as training reward: **Low** (no evidence of alignment with long-term user satisfaction; high reward hacking risk)

## Next Checks
1. **Human label verification:** Sample 1,000 NAPsack events across 5 users; have experts annotate ground-truth actions; compute VLM label accuracy against human consensus.
2. **Judge metric correlation:** Conduct human evaluation on 200 LongNAP predictions vs. baselines; compute Spearman correlation between LLM-judge scores and human preference win rates.
3. **Reasoning trace analysis:** Cluster sampled `z_retrieve`/`z_predict` strings; manually inspect for coherence, specificity, and retrieval relevance; compare against random baselines.
