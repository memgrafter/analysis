# Continuous-time reinforcement learning: ellipticity enables model-free value function approximation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.06930
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06930
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Under uniform ellipticity, the Sobolev‑prox fitted Q‑learning algorithm attains oracle error bounds that reduce continuous‑time RL with general function approximation to the statistical and computational complexity of supervised learning.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses off‑policy reinforcement learning for continuous‑time Markov diffusion processes observed at discrete timesteps. The core difficulty is that the Bellman operator is only a contraction in the supremum norm, making model‑free value approximation unstable. By exploiting uniform ellipticity of the diffusion, the authors establish new positive‑definiteness and boundedness properties of the Bellman operators in the Sobolev space \(H^1(\rho)\). This motivates the Sobolev‑prox fitted Q‑learning algorithm, which alternates between a least‑squares update for the advantage function and a proximal update for the value function under the empirical Sobolev norm. Theoretical analysis yields non‑asymptotic oracle inequalities: the total error decomposes into (i) best approximation error, (ii) statistical complexity (critical radii), (iii) exponentially decaying optimization error, and (iv) a discretization error of order \(\eta^{1/2}\). Consequently, under ellipticity, RL with general function approximation for Markov diffusions is no harder than supervised learning.

## Method Summary
The algorithm operates on an offline dataset of trajectories generated by a behavior policy \(\pi_0\). Each trajectory consists of state‑action‑reward tuples at discrete intervals of length \(\eta\). The goal is to learn optimal value and advantage functions within convex function classes \(F^v\) and \(F^q\) (with the latter satisfying a zero‑mean constraint). The iterative procedure is:

1. **Advantage update (least‑squares):** For each iteration \(t\), using the current value estimate \(\hat{v}^t\) and stored transitions, compute the regression target  
   \[
   R_{k\eta} + e^{-\beta\eta}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\eta}(v(X_{(k+1)\eta})-v(X_{k\eta})) + \max_{a'} q(X_{(k+1)\eta},a')\bigr),
   \]
   and solve a least‑squares problem to obtain \(\hat{q}^{t+1} \in F^q\).

2. **Value update (Sobolev‑prox):** Construct an empirical Sobolev inner product \(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_n[\cdot,\cdot]\) that incorporates gradients of the value function. Then solve  
   \[
   \hat{v}^{t+1} = \arg\min_{v\in F^v} \tfrac{1}{2}\|v-\hat{v}^t\|_{\hat{H}^1_n}^2 + \tfrac{\alpha}{\eta} \hat{\mathbb{E}}_n[(\hat{v}^t - \hat{T}\hat{v}^t,\hat{q}^{t+1}), \, v-\hat{v}^t],
   \]
   where \(\hat{T}\) is an empirical Bellman operator.

The algorithm is run for \(N\) iterations, ideally using sample‑splitting (disjoint subsets for each iteration) to keep the analysis clean. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3) states that the final estimates satisfy an oracle inequality bounding the \(H^1\) and \(\nu\) errors by the four terms mentioned above.

## Key Results
- Under uniform ellipticity, the Bellman operators \(T_v\) and \(T_q\) admit a monotonicity property in \(H^1(\rho)\), i.e., \(\langle f, (\beta - A^{\pi_0})f\rangle_\rho \ge \frac{\beta}{2}\|f\|_\rho^2 + \lambda_{\min}\|\nabla f\|_\rho^2 - c\eta\).
- The Sobolev‑prox update (12b) yields exponential convergence of the value iterate in the \(H^1\) norm, with rate determined by \(\lambda_{\min}\) and step size \(\alpha\).
- The overall algorithm achieves a non‑asymptotic bound that decomposes into best approximation error, statistical critical radii, exponentially small optimization error, and discretization error \(O(\eta^{1/2})\), thereby equating the difficulty of continuous‑time RL to that of supervised learning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success of the approach hinges on three interconnected mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1 (Elliptic coercivity ⇒ Sobolev monotonicity).**  
Uniform ellipticity (\(\Lambda(x) \succeq \lambda_{\min}I\)) together with smooth coefficients yields the key estimate (Lemma 4)  
\[
\langle f, (\beta - A^{\pi_0})f\rangle_\rho \ge \tfrac{\beta}{2}\|f\|_\rho^2 + \lambda_{\min}\|\nabla f\|_\rho^2 - c\eta,
\]  
which makes the operator \(\beta - A^{\pi_0}\) positive definite in the Sobolev space \(H^1(\rho)\). This serves as a surrogate for the usual \(\mathbb{L}^2\) contraction, allowing control of projected Bellman residuals.

**Mechanism 2 (Sobolev‑prox aligns with elliptic geometry).**  
The value update (11b) is a proximal gradient step in \(H^1(\rho)\). Combined with the monotonicity from Mechanism 1, it produces a recursion (Lemma 9) where the \(H^1\) error contracts for sufficiently small step size \(\alpha\). In contrast, using an \(\mathbb{L}^2\) proximal step would not leverage the gradient term and can lead to instability.

**Mechanism 3 (Oracle reduction to supervised learning).**  
With sample‑splitting and concentration arguments, the empirical iterates inherit the population contraction. The final error (Theorem 3) is the sum of:  
(i) the best achievable approximation error in \(F^v\) and \(F^q\),  
(ii) statistical complexity measured by critical radii,  
(iii) an exponentially decaying optimization error, and  
(iv) a discretization term \(O(\eta^{1/2})\).  
Thus, the statistical and computational burden matches that of standard supervised regression on the same function classes.

## Foundational Learning
**Continuous‑time diffusion processes and semigroups**  
The environment is a controlled diffusion \(dX_t = b_a(X_t)dt + \Lambda(X_t)^{1/2}dB_t\). Its discrete‑time counterpart arises from a step‑size \(\eta\) discretization of the stochastic differential equation, leading to transition operators \(P^a_\eta\) and generator \(A^a\). Understanding these objects is essential to connect continuous‑time dynamics with the discrete‑time Bellman operator.  
*Quick check:* For a smooth test function \(f\), what is \(A^a f(x) = \lim_{t\downarrow0} \frac{\mathbb{E}[f(X_t)|X_0=x, A_0=a]-f(x)}{t}\) in terms of \(b_a\) and \(\Lambda\)?

**Uniform ellipticity**  
Ellipticity means the diffusion matrix \(\Lambda(x)\) has eigenvalues uniformly bounded below by \(\lambda_{\min}>0\). This condition is crucial for the coercivity estimate \(\langle f, A^{\pi_0}f\rangle_\rho \ge \lambda_{\min}\|\nabla f\|_\rho^2\) (up to lower‑order terms). Without \(\lambda_{\min}>0\) the coercivity term vanishes and the monotonicity bound fails.  
*Quick check:* Which property of the eigenvalues of \(\Lambda(x)\) is required, and what breaks if \(\lambda_{\min}=0\)?

**Sobolev spaces and the \(H^1(\rho)\) norm**  
The Sobolev norm \(\|f\|_{H^1(\rho)}^2 = \|f\|_\rho^2 + \|\nabla f\|_\rho^2\) incorporates the gradient component that appears naturally from elliptic coercivity. Using only the \(\mathbb{L}^2(\rho)\) norm would miss this geometric structure and would not yield a contraction‑like inequality.  
*Quick check:* Why does the gradient term \(\|\nabla f\|_\rho^2\) become essential in turning the Bellman error into a monotone operator?

**Projected Bellman equations**  
Because the true optimal value \(v^*\) may not lie in the chosen function class \(F^v\), we target the solution of the projected fixed‑point equations, e.g. \(v = \arg\min_{u\in F^v} \| \beta u - \mathcal{T}u \|_{\rho}^2\) (and similarly for the advantage). These define the best approximations within the hypothesis classes and serve as the benchmark for the error bounds.  
*Quick check:* What projections are used in the equations (10) and under which norms are they defined?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Offline dataset \(D\) (trajectories from \(\pi_0\)) → Advantage approximator \(F^q\) (zero‑mean constraint) → least‑squares update → \(\hat{q}^{t+1}\).  
  In parallel, value approximator \(F^v\) → empirical Sobolev inner product \(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_n\) (requires state gradients) → proximal update using Bellman residual → \(\hat{v}^{t+1}\).  
  The two updates alternate in a loop: advantage first, then value.

- **Critical path (iteration loop):**  
  1. Initialize \(\hat{v}^{(0)}\in F^v\), \(\hat{q}^{(0)}\in F^q\).  
  2. For \(t = 0,\dots,N-1\):  
     a. Compute regression targets for all stored transitions using current \(\hat{v}^{(t)}\) and \(\hat{q}^{(t)}\).  
     b. Solve least‑squares: \(\hat{q}^{(t+1)} = \arg\min_{q\in F^q} \sum (\text{target} - q(X,A))^2\).  
     c. Build empirical Sobolev inner product \(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_n[\cdot,\cdot]\) by evaluating \(\nabla\hat{v}^{(t)}\) at state points.  
     d. Compute empirical Bellman operator term \(\hat{B}_n(\hat{v}^{(t)},v-\hat{v}^{(t)}; \hat{q}^{(t+1)})\).  
     e. Solve prox: \(\hat{v}^{t+1} = \arg\min_{v\in F^v} \tfrac{1}{2}\|v-\hat{v}^{(t)}\|_{\hat{H}^1_n}^2 + \tfrac{\alpha}{\eta} \hat{B}_n(v-\hat{v}^{(t)})\).  
  3. Return \((\hat{v}^{(N)}, \hat{q}^{(N)})\).

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  *Sobolev vs. \(\mathbb{L}^2\) proximal:* Sobolev leverages elliptic geometry and yields stability but requires computing gradients of \(v\); \(\mathbb{L}^2\) is simpler but may diverge.  
  *Sample‑splitting:* Theory assumes \(2N\) disjoint subsets to preserve independence; this multiplies data needs. Reusing data (e.g., replay buffer) is practical but may increase statistical error or require variance reduction.  
  *Function class richness:* Larger classes reduce approximation error but increase statistical complexity (critical radii). Convexity is assumed for exact projections; non‑convex models (e.g., deep nets) need different treatment.  
  *Hyperparameters:* Step size \(\alpha\) must satisfy \(\alpha \le c\lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max}^2\); too large destroys contraction. Discretization step \(\eta\) trades off \(O(\eta^{1/2})\) error against effective horizon \(\sim 1/\eta\).

- **Failure signatures & diagnostics:**  
  *Blowing up/oscillating \(v\):* Likely \(\alpha\) too large or \(\lambda_{\min}\) too small. Check \(\| \hat{v}^{t+1} - \hat{v}^t \|_{H^1}\); reduce \(\alpha\).  
  *Noisy or biased \(q\):* Poor coverage of \(\pi_0\) or violated zero‑mean constraint. Verify \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_0}[q(\cdot,a)]\approx0\) across states.  
  *Discretization dominated:* Error decreases only slowly when \(\eta\) is large; try smaller \(\eta\) and observe the \(O(\eta^{1/2})\) trend.  
  *Sobolev gradient instability:* With non‑smooth \(F^v\) (e.g., ReLU), estimated gradients can be biased. Use smooth networks or finite‑difference approximations and monitor gradient norms.

- **First 3 experiments for reproducible validation:**  
  1. **LQG benchmark:** 2‑D Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck with linear control; exact solution available. Compare Sobolev‑prox FQI against standard FQI; verify linear convergence and effect of \(\eta\).  
  2. **Non‑linear drift with tunable ellipticity:** 3‑D diffusion with cubic drift and scaled diffusion matrix \(\Lambda = \lambda_{\min} I\). Vary \(\lambda_{\min}\) to test the predicted dependence of convergence speed and error on ellipticity.  
  3. **High‑dimensional RKHS estimation:** 10‑dimensional nonlinear drift; use Gaussian kernel for \(F^v\), \(F^q\). Vary sample size \(n\) and kernel bandwidth to confirm statistical rates governed by critical radii (log‑log error vs. \(n\)).

## Limitations
- Uniform ellipticity is a strong assumption; singular or degenerate diffusions break the coercivity argument.
- Sample‑splitting multiplies data requirements by \(2N\); this is prohibitively expensive for large \(N\) and motivates developing variance‑reduced or data‑reuse variants.
- The Sobolev proximal step necessitates accurate gradient estimation, which may be challenging for non‑smooth function classes (e.g., ReLU networks).
- Theoretical bounds demand stringent step‑size conditions and convexity; extending to stochastic first‑order methods with non‑convex approximators remains open.
- The analysis assumes known behavior policy with good coverage; distribution shift or insufficient exploration can degrade performance.

## Confidence
- **Elliptic Sobolev monotonicity (Lemma 4):** High – derived from standard elliptic PDE techniques.
- **Sobolev‑prox exponential convergence (Theorem 2):** Medium – hinges on precise step‑size selection and convexity; empirical behaviour likely robust but finite‑sample effects may appear.
- **Oracle equivalence (Theorem 3):** Medium – relies on strong assumptions (convexity, sample‑splitting, smoothness); extensions beyond these are needed for broader applicability.

## Next Checks
- **Ellipticity stress test:** In a controlled diffusion, vary \(\lambda_{\min}\) over a wide range and measure the impact on convergence rate and final error to validate the theoretical dependence.
- **Sample‑splitting vs. reuse:** Compare the original algorithm (with disjoint subsets) against a version that reuses the full dataset each iteration, quantifying the increase in statistical error or need for additional variance reduction.
- **Gradient estimation for ReLU nets:** Replace analytic gradients with autodiff (which may be noisy for piecewise‑linear functions) and assess the stability of the Sobolev‑prox update; consider using smooth surrogate activations.
