# Glitter: Visualizing Lexical Surprisal for Readability in Administrative Texts
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.05411
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05411
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: Glitter color-codes token-level surprisal from GPT-2, showing that formulaic legal phrases are surprisingly predictable yet difficult for non-experts, indicating a misalignment between pretrained model probabilities and human comprehension.

## Executive Summary
Glitter is a tool that computes lexical surprisal—the negative log probability of a token given its context—from a pretrained GPT-2 model and visualizes it via a blue-to-red color gradient. This approach helps editors identify words that may hinder readability in administrative texts. Evaluations on manually clarity‑edited documents reveal that improved versions tend to have lower surprisal at key tokens, supporting surprisal as a proxy for readability issues. However, the study also uncovers a critical limitation: formulaic legal language, though highly predictable (low surprisal), remains opaque to lay readers, demonstrating that pretrained models do not always capture human comprehension difficulty.

## Method Summary
Glitter processes input text by tokenizing it and passing it through GPT‑2 small (124M). For each token, the model’s conditional probability is extracted; the negative logarithm yields the surprisal value. These values are normalized and mapped to a color scale (blue = low surprisal, red = high surprisal). The text is then displayed with each token highlighted accordingly. The authors compared surprisal patterns in original administrative texts and their clarity‑edited counterparts and manually inspected formulaic phrases to assess the relationship between surprisal and human difficulty.

## Key Results
- Formulaic legal phrases (e.g., “party of the first part”) exhibit very low lexical surprisal yet are notoriously difficult for non‑experts, indicating that pretrained model probabilities do not always reflect human understanding.
- Text versions edited for clarity show distinct surprisal patterns, often with reduced surprisal at key decision points, suggesting that human editors implicitly lower cognitive load by using more predictable wording.
- The Glitter visualization effectively highlights tokens that may contribute to comprehension difficulty, providing a practical tool for editors of administrative content.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Surprisal quantifies how unexpected a word is given its context, which correlates with processing effort for human readers. By leveraging a large language model trained on broad data, Glitter obtains an automatic, context‑sensitive measure without manual annotation. The color‑coding translates numeric surprisal into an immediate visual signal, drawing attention to potentially problematic words. The observed correlation between reduced surprisal and human edits suggests that editors simplify text by choosing more predictable vocabulary, thereby lowering cognitive load. The exception—formulaic legal language—reveals that domain‑specific exposure can make tokens highly probable to the model while still being inaccessible to non‑experts due to specialized jargon and missing world knowledge. Thus, surprisal is not a perfect proxy, but it remains a useful indicator for many readability issues.

## Foundational Learning
Core concepts underlying Glitter:

- **Lexical Surprisal**: Negative log probability of a token given its context; lower values mean higher predictability. Why needed: Directly linked to processing difficulty. Quick check: Compute `-log2(p(token | context))`.
- **Conditional Probability in Language Models**: The model’s estimate of the next‑token distribution. Why needed: Provides the base for surprisal. Quick check: Ensure all next‑token probabilities sum to 1.
- **Readability Metrics**: Traditional (e.g., Flesch–Kincaid) and cognitive measures of text understandability. Why needed: Surprisal is proposed as a new proxy. Quick check: Compare metric scores with human judgments on a sample.
- **Color‑Based Visual Encoding**: Mapping numeric values to colors for rapid perception. Why needed: Communicates surprisal at a glance. Quick check: Verify perceptual uniformity and color‑blind accessibility of the chosen gradient.
- **Domain‑Specific Language**: Specialized vocabularies (e.g., legal) that may be frequent in training data but opaque to general audiences. Why needed: Explains mismatches between surprisal and actual difficulty. Quick check: Identify low‑surprisal tokens that are jargon and test recognition among non‑experts.

## Architecture Onboarding
Glitter’s processing pipeline:

Raw text -> Tokenization -> GPT-2 forward pass -> Probability extraction -> Surprisal calculation -> Normalization -> Color mapping -> Highlighted UI

*Component map*: Text input -> Tokenizer -> GPT-2 model -> Logits -> Softmax -> Token probabilities -> Negative log -> Normalization -> Color scale -> Rendered display

*Critical path*: The core path flows from GPT-2 inference to surprisal computation; UI rendering depends directly on the resulting color values.

*Design tradeoffs*:
- Using GPT-2 small (124M) prioritizes speed and low resource usage over potentially more accurate probabilities from larger models.
- A fixed blue‑red gradient is simple and intuitive but may not be optimal for color‑blind users; alternative palettes could improve accessibility.
- Token‑level surprisal ignores discourse‑level structures, which could also affect readability.

*Failure signatures*:
- Extremely rare or out‑of‑vocabulary tokens generate huge surprisal values that may dominate the color scale, obscuring other tokens.
- Formulaic legal phrases produce low surprisal despite causing comprehension problems, revealing a systematic blind spot.
- Poor normalization can make common function words appear overly salient, cluttering the visualization.

*First experiments*:
1. Replicate the original manual evaluation on a new, diverse administrative corpus to verify the sustainment of low‑surprisal/high‑difficulty patterns for formulaic phrases.
2. Correlate token‑level surprisal with fine‑grained human readability ratings (e.g., sentence‑level complexity scores from expert annotators) to quantify the proxy’s strength and boundaries.
3. Conduct a user study where non‑expert editors revise documents with and without Glitter’s visualization to measure impacts on comprehension speed and editing quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
While the paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions, its findings and limitations point to several unresolved issues:

- How can surprisal be adapted to account for domain-specific knowledge gaps? The misalignment for formulaic legal phrases indicates that pretrained model probabilities may not capture human comprehension barriers due to jargon and missing world knowledge.
- To what extent do model scale and domain adaptation affect the surprisal-readability relationship? The study uses GPT‑2 small; larger or fine‑tuned models might yield different patterns.
- Can discourse‑level information be integrated with token‑level surprisal to improve readability prediction? The current approach ignores higher‑order structures that influence processing effort.
- What is the generalizability of the observed correlation between reduced surprisal and human edits across diverse administrative domains and text genres? The corpus is small and not fully diverse, raising questions about external validity.

These questions emerge from the paper’s core discovery, its methodological choices, and its acknowledged constraints.

## Limitations
- The studied administrative text corpus is small and not fully diverse, limiting how broadly the results can be generalized.
- The analysis depends on GPT-2 small; other models (larger or domain‑adapted) might yield different surprisal patterns and alter conclusions.
- Details of the manual evaluation (e.g., number of annotators, inter‑rater reliability, editing guidelines) are omitted, reducing confidence in the reported distinct patterns.
