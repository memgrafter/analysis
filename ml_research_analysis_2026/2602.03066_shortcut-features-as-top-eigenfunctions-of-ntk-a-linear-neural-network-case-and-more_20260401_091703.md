# Shortcut Features as Top Eigenfunctions of NTK: A Linear Neural Network Case and More

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03066  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03066  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Shortcut features correspond to top NTK eigenfunctions and dominate network output due to cluster variances, providing a spectral explanation for persistent shortcut bias.

## Executive Summary
Shortcut learning—where neural networks rely on spurious correlations—is poorly understood. This paper analyzes shortcut features through the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework, treating features as eigenfunctions. Using linear networks on clustered Gaussian data with imbalanced shortcut clusters, the authors show that shortcut features align with NTK eigenfunctions having larger eigenvalues, causing them to be learned faster. Moreover, due to within-cluster variances, these features continue to dominate the network output even after convergence, a phenomenon that persists under strong margin regularization and is not solely due to max-margin bias. Empirical extensions to ReLU networks and ResNet-18 across five datasets confirm that shortcut features exhibit higher availability (alignment with top NTK eigenvectors) but lower predictability (correlation with ground-truth) compared to core features. The study introduces availability and predictability as metrics and demonstrates that the NTK spectrum explains shortcut preference.

## Method Summary
The paper combines rigorous theoretical analysis with extensive empirical validation. Theoretically, the authors consider a linear network trained on a Gaussian mixture model where some clusters correspond to shortcuts. They derive closed-form expressions for the NTK eigenvalues, showing they scale with cluster weights, and for the converged function coefficients, showing they scale inversely with within-cluster variances. This yields two mechanisms: spectral bias (larger eigenvalues → faster learning of shortcuts) and variance-driven dominance (larger influence of high-weight clusters after convergence). Empirically, they train linear, two-layer ReLU, and ResNet-18 models on datasets with known biases (Patched-MNIST, Colored-MNIST, Waterbirds, CelebA, Dogs vs. Cats). They compute empirical NTK (eNTK) on sampled training points and define availability (alignment of a feature label with the top eigenspace of eNTK) and predictability (correlation with ground-truth labels). These metrics are compared for ground-truth and manually identified shortcut labels across different losses (CE, MSE, SD).

## Key Results
- Shortcut features have larger NTK eigenvalues, leading to faster learning (spectral bias) in linear networks and empirical models.
- Even after convergence, shortcut features dominate network outputs due to cluster variances; this bias persists under margin-regularization (SD), beyond mere max-margin effects.
- Across five datasets, shortcut features consistently show higher availability (NTK alignment) but lower predictability (ground-truth alignment) than core features.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Shortcut features correspond to NTK eigenfunctions with larger eigenvalues, causing them to be learned faster (spectral bias).
- **Mechanism**: In a clustered Gaussian mixture with imbalanced cluster weights, eigenvectors aligned with high-weight clusters yield larger eigenvalues in the NTK spectrum. Gradient descent prioritizes these directions first.
- **Core assumption**: Data follows a clustered Gaussian mixture; network operates in the NTK (infinite-width lazy) regime; linear kernel (dot-product) for analysis.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "shortcut features correspond to features with larger eigenvalues when the shortcuts stem from the imbalanced number of samples in the clustered distribution."
  - Section 3.2: Proposition 3.1 formally derives eigenvalues scaling with cluster weights πₖ; saliency maps in Figure 3 empirically show large-eigenvalue features focus on shortcuts (patches, backgrounds).
  - Corpus: Neighbors discuss NTK eigenstructure and feature learning but do not directly link cluster imbalance to shortcut eigenvalues; evidence for this specific mechanism is primarily from the paper's own analysis.
- **Break condition**: If cluster means are orthogonal and variances are negligible (σ²→0), eigenvalue differences vanish (Proposition 3.1), weakening spectral bias for shortcuts.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Even after convergence, shortcut features dominate network outputs, not solely due to max-margin bias but also due to cluster variances.
- **Mechanism**: The optimal (MSE-minimizing) function decomposes into eigenfunctions with coefficients wₖ that scale with cluster weight πₖ and inversely with total variance (Proposition 3.2). Larger πₖ and non-tiny σ² amplify wₖ for shortcut clusters, tilting the decision boundary.
- **Core assumption**: Data distribution has non-negligible within-cluster variance; linear network; MSE loss (or CE with strong SD regularization).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "features with larger eigenvalues still have a large influence on the neural network output even after training, due to data variances in the clusters."
  - Section 3.3: Proposition 3.2 gives wₖ formula; Figure 4 shows decision boundary tilting toward high-weight clusters only when variances are large.
  - Corpus: Corpus shows general work on NTK and Fisher information, but not this specific variance effect on post-convergence influence.
- **Break condition**: If within-cluster variances are extremely small (σ²≈0), wₖ becomes independent of πₖ, so cluster weight imbalance no longer biases the decision boundary (Figure 4c,f).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Shortcut features exhibit higher "availability" (alignment with top NTK eigenvectors) than ground-truth features across real datasets, explaining their persistent use.
- **Mechanism**: Availability metric (Definition 4.2) measures alignment between a label vector and top eigenspace of empirical NTK. Imbalanced data generates top eigenvectors that correlate strongly with shortcuts, leading to higher availability.
- **Core assumption**: Empirical NTK on finite training data approximates the theoretical NTK eigenstructure; shortcut label can be identified (manually or via prediction bias).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "Shortcut features have a lower predictability but a higher availability."
  - Section 4.2: Figures 6,8,9 show consistently higher availability for shortcut labels across networks/datasets/losses; Figure 7 shows availability increases with shortcut strength.
  - Corpus: Corpus includes papers on empirical NTK dynamics and feature alignment, but not the specific availability metric or its correlation with shortcut prevalence.
- **Break condition**: If ground-truth features themselves have strong spectral alignment (e.g., due to pretraining on diverse data), availability may not flag shortcuts (Appendix A.5, A.7).

## Foundational Learning
- **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)**  
  Why needed: Framework to define features as eigenfunctions; governs training dynamics via spectral bias.  
  Quick check: How does the NTK matrix (or operator) decompose, and what does an eigenvalue control during gradient descent?

- **Spectral bias**  
  Why needed: Explains why large-eigenvalue directions (including shortcuts) are learned first.  
  Quick check: In the NTK regime, what property of an eigenfunction determines its convergence rate?

- **Gaussian Mixture Model (clustered data)**  
  Why needed: Analytical tractability; models imbalanced data distribution that induces shortcuts.  
  Quick check: How do cluster mixing weights πₖ and variances σ²ₖ affect the NTK eigenvalues and optimal function coefficients?

- **Margin regularization (SD)**  
  Why needed: To test whether max-margin is the sole cause of shortcut learning.  
  Quick check: What does Corollary 3.3 imply about the ratio of feature weights under strong SD vs. MSE loss?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: The theoretical core (Gaussian mixture + Linear network) yields NTK eigendecomposition and eigenvalue/weight formulas. The empirical extension replaces the linear model with ReLU/ResNet, computes empirical NTK on sampled training points, defines availability/predictability metrics for ground-truth and shortcut labels, and validates against supervised test groups.

- **Critical path**:  
  1. Derive NTK eigenstructure for linear case (Proposition 3.1).  
  2. Derive converged output decomposition (Proposition 3.2) and SD limit (Corollary 3.3).  
  3. Empirically validate: (a) eigenvalue magnitude vs. shortcut focus (saliency maps), (b) decision boundary tilt with cluster variance (Figure 4), (c) availability metric across datasets/models.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Analytical tractability vs. realism: Gaussian/linear assumptions allow closed-form proofs but may not capture nonlinear feature learning in deep nets.  
  - Metric design: Availability uses eNTK on finite samples; may miss later-learning shortcuts if multiple biases exist.  
  - Shortcut label definition: Manually identified per dataset; may not generalize to unseen bias types.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Availability metric fails to detect a shortcut if another stronger bias dominates early learning (Appendix A.7).  
  - Theoretical results assume infinite width and NTK constancy; with moderate width, feature learning beyond NTK may alter spectral alignment.  
  - Pretraining can reshape initial NTK, reversing shortcut availability (Appendix A.5).

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Eigenvalue alignment check**: Train a linear network on a synthetic clustered Gaussian dataset; compute NTK eigenvectors; verify that top eigenvectors align with directions of high-weight clusters (Proposition 3.1).  
  2. **Decision boundary under variance**: For a 2D 4-cluster GMM, train a ReLU network with varying cluster variances; observe boundary tilt toward high-weight clusters only when variances are large (Figure 4).  
  3. **Availability vs. predictability**: For a real dataset (e.g., Waterbirds), compute availability of ground-truth vs. shortcut labels for a ResNet-18; expect higher availability for shortcut despite lower predictability (Figure 6).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does the discrepancy between eigenvalue scaling (with mean magnitude) and influence scaling (inversely with mean magnitude) affect the NTK spectrum's ability to predict post-training shortcut bias?
- **Basis in paper**: "while Proposition 3.1 indicates that eigenvalues scale with the magnitude of the mean vectors, Proposition 3.2 implies that the contribution of a feature on the decision boundary scales inversely with the magnitude of the mean vectors. This could potentially limit the ability of using the NTK spectrum to assess the influence of shortcut bias after network convergence."
- **Why unresolved**: The two properties suggest that a feature with a large eigenvalue may not necessarily have large final influence if its mean magnitude is also large, making it unclear whether spectral alignment alone suffices to diagnose shortcut dominance.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic experiments varying mean norms and cluster weights, measuring both NTK eigenvalues and final feature influence; theoretical analysis linking the two quantities across distributions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can the theoretical findings for linear networks (e.g., shortcut features as top eigenfunctions, variance-driven influence) be formally extended to deep non-linear networks, possibly under mean-field or other asymptotic regimes?
- **Basis in paper**: "First, our theoretical analysis is based on a simple case of training a linear neural network on a Gaussian Mixture model." They state this as a limitation and only provide empirical extensions.
- **Why unresolved**: Linear networks allow closed-form eigendecomposition; non-linearities and depth break these forms, leaving the underlying mechanism conjectural for realistic architectures.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop a theoretical framework for NTK (or related kernels) in deep networks under structured data (e.g., Gaussian mixtures) and prove analogous spectral bias and influence results; compare with extensive empirical validation.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Under exactly what conditions does the "availability" metric fail to detect a true shortcut, and how can it be improved to handle multiple confounding biases?
- **Basis in paper**: "Another limitation is that, as shown in Appendix A.7, availability might not detect certain shortcut labels if the ground-truth label is predominated by a stronger shortcut." Appendix A.7 demonstrates a case where a later-emerging shortcut is not the top eigenfunction alignment.
- **Why unresolved**: Availability measures alignment with top NTK eigenvectors; if several shortcuts exist, the top eigenvectors may correspond to the earliest/most prevalent bias, while a secondary but still impactful shortcut may have lower availability, causing false negatives.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Characterize the relationship between label predictability, shortcut strength, and eigenvector spectrum; design a metric that integrates contributions across a broader eigenbasis or uses a multi-component decomposition.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Why does pretraining often reduce reliance on shortcut features, and how does this interact with the NTK's spectral bias during fine-tuning?
- **Basis in paper**: Appendix A.5 shows that a pretrained ResNet-18 on Dogs vs Cats has availability higher for ground-truth labels than shortcut labels, contrary to the untrained case. The paper notes this but provides no explanation.
- **Why unresolved**: Pretraining changes the initial parameterization and possibly the NTK at initialization. The effect on spectral alignment and subsequent shortcut learning is not understood within their framework.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Study how pretraining shifts the NTK eigenspectrum relative to the data distribution; track availability changes during fine-tuning with various pretraining objectives and architectures.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis assumes infinite-width NTK regime and linear networks; its extension to finite-width nonlinear architectures remains unproven.  
- Empirical NTK approximations (e.g., last-layer vs. full) and heuristic shortcut label construction lack precise specifications, potentially biasing measurements.  
- The availability metric depends on the sampled training subset and eigendecomposition; it may fail to detect secondary shortcuts when multiple biases are present.

## Confidence
- Shortcut features correspond to top NTK eigenfunctions in linear networks: **High**  
- Shortcut dominance persists post-convergence due to cluster variances (beyond max-margin): **Medium**  
- Across real datasets, shortcuts show higher availability but lower predictability: **Medium**
