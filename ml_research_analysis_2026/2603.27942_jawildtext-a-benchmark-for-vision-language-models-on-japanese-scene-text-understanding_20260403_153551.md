# JaWildText: A Benchmark for Vision-Language Models on Japanese Scene Text Understanding

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.27942
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27942
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** The best open-weight VLM (Qwen3-VL-8B) achieves an overall score of 0.64 on the new benchmark, with recognition—especially of kanji—being the dominant error source.

## Executive Summary
JaWildText is a new benchmark designed to address the lack of standardized evaluation for Japanese scene text understanding by vision-language models (VLMs). It introduces 2,961 real-world images and 1.12 million annotated characters across three complementary tasks: Dense STVQA, Receipt KIE, and Handwriting OCR. The benchmark’s core innovation is its ability to disentangle recognition failures from reasoning and formatting errors through task diversity and fine-grained, script-aware annotations. Evaluation of 14 open-weight VLMs reveals a persistent recognition bottleneck, particularly for kanji, with the top model scoring only 0.64 overall, indicating significant room for improvement.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed JaWildText by capturing 2,961 images in real Japanese environments (e.g., streets, stores) using smartphones, preserving natural artifacts like clutter, creases, and perspective distortion. These are split into three tasks: 745 images for Dense STVQA with multi-region reasoning questions, 1,151 receipt images for key information extraction (KIE), and 1,065 handwriting samples for verbatim transcription. Annotations include 95,705 quadrilateral text boxes with character-level transcriptions (3,643 unique types) and, for STVQA, minimal evidence region links. Models are evaluated zero-shot with strict output formats; metrics are task-specific (LLM-judge accuracy, field-level F1, character error rate). An error analysis taxonomy uses separate transcription prompts to classify failures as Recognition, Reasoning, or Format errors.

## Key Results
*   The benchmark comprises 2,961 images and 1.12M annotated characters across 3,643 unique types.
*   The best model, Qwen3-VL-8B, achieves an overall score of 0.64 (Dense STVQA ~0.62, Receipt KIE F1 ~0.53, Handwriting OCR 1-CER ~0.79).
*   Error analysis shows recognition is the primary bottleneck (e.g., 44.7–67.3% of errors for major models in Dense STVQA), with kanji having the highest character error rate (CER) across all tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Task diversity disentangles recognition from reasoning failures.
*   **Mechanism:** By varying visual organization (cluttered signboards to structured receipts), output format (free-form QA to verbatim transcription), and writing style (printed vs. handwritten), the benchmark isolates whether errors stem from character recognition or higher-order reasoning.
*   **Core assumption:** Different tasks have different dominant failure modes; e.g., Handwriting OCR is recognition-dominant, while Dense STVQA requires multi-region reasoning.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states "three complementary tasks that vary in visual organization, output format, and writing style." Section 3 details task variations. Corpus notes existing multilingual benchmarks lack this disentanglement focus for Japanese.
*   **Break condition:** If error patterns across tasks are highly correlated (a model bad at Dense STVQA is also bad at Handwriting OCR for same scripts), disentanglement fails.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Script-aware annotation enables fine-grained diagnosis of language-specific bottlenecks.
*   **Mechanism:** Character-level transcriptions across 3,643 unique types allow computing error rates (CER) by script category (kanji, hiragana, katakana, ASCII), revealing which scripts are most problematic.
*   **Core assumption:** Error rates vary by script due to differences in character inventory size, visual similarity, and training data exposure.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "fine-grained, script-aware diagnosis." Section 4.3 shows kanji CER highest, ASCII digits lowest. Corpus contrasts with benchmarks like MTVQA that lack script-level breakdowns.
*   **Break condition:** If CER does not differ significantly across script categories, the script-aware analysis provides no diagnostic value.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Real-world image collection with natural artifacts tests robustness to in-the-wild conditions.
*   **Mechanism:** Images are captured with smartphones in Japan, preserving clutter, occlusion, reflections, creases, and perspective distortions—common in everyday use but absent in scanned documents.
*   **Core assumption:** Performance on clean, curated images does not generalize to noisy, real-world scenarios; robustness requires testing under natural degradations.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Sections 3.1, 3.2 describe smartphone photography and retained artifacts. Abstract states "in-the-wild scene text." Corpus highlights that existing Japanese datasets (e.g., JDocQA) focus on scanned documents.
*   **Break condition:** If model performance is unaffected by presence/absence of natural artifacts, the collection method does not test robustness.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Vision-Language Models (VLMs)**
    *   **Why needed:** The paper evaluates VLMs that perform end-to-end image-to-text tasks, contrasting with traditional modular OCR pipelines. Understanding this shift is key to seeing why disentangling recognition and reasoning is urgent.
    *   **Quick check:** What is the core difference between a VLM and a traditional OCR + NLP pipeline when answering questions about scene text?
*   **Japanese Script Complexity**
    *   **Why needed:** The benchmark targets mixed scripts (kanji, hiragana, katakana, ASCII), vertical writing, and a large character set (~3,600 types). These features cause recognition failures that are language-specific.
    *   **Quick check:** List three ways Japanese written language differs typologically from English, and how each could challenge a VLM.
*   **End-to-End Evaluation vs. Modular Diagnosis**
    *   **Why needed:** The paper stresses that aggregate accuracy on downstream tasks (e.g., VQA) conflates recognition and reasoning errors. Disentangling them requires curated annotations (evidence regions, transcriptions).
    *   **Quick check:** Why is a high score on Receipt KIE not sufficient to conclude a model can read Japanese receipts well?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Data ingestion -> Annotation layer -> Evaluation engine -> Analysis module
    *   Data ingestion: 2,961 images with metadata.
    *   Annotation layer: Quadrilateral boxes + transcriptions; QA pairs linked to evidence regions (STVQA); field annotations (KIE); line-level transcriptions (OCR).
    *   Evaluation engine: Task-specific parsers (JSON, \boxed{}, plain text); LLM judge for STVQA; character alignment for OCR CER.
    *   Analysis module: Error taxonomy (recognition, reasoning, format); script-type CER breakdown; per-field KIE accuracy.
*   **Critical path:**
    1. Collect diverse images with specified conditions (lighting, layout, artifacts).
    2. Annotate with rigorous quality control (dual labeling, author review).
    3. Run model inference with standardized prompts and strict output formatting.
    4. Compute metrics; use ground-truth transcriptions and evidence regions to categorize errors.
    5. Perform script-type and task-specific breakdowns.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   Diversity vs. control: Handwriting OCR uses controlled prompts but diverse media; Dense STVQA uses uncontrolled real-world scenes.
    *   Granularity cost: Region-level annotations enable fine-grained diagnosis but require 95k boxes—high cost.
    *   Task balance: Each task ~1k instances for comparable precision, but text density varies (Dense STVQA up to 2k chars/image).
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Dominant Recognition Errors in Dense STVQA for most models (e.g., 44.7–67.3% in InternVL3.5, Gemma3).
    *   Kanji CER highest across models, correlating with overall OCR score.
    *   Layout reasoning gaps in Receipt KIE: `store_name` and `store_address` accuracies ≤0.55 even for top models.
    *   Format Errors (e.g., Phi-4-Multimodal) indicate instruction-following issues.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1. **Baseline performance sweep:** Evaluate all 14 VLMs on the three test splits to reproduce Table 5 and verify Qwen3-VL-8B's ~0.64 overall.
    2. **Dense STVQA error taxonomy:** For a top model, run a separate full transcription pass; compare evidence region recognition vs. final answer to quantify recognition vs. reasoning error proportions (as in Figure 6).
    3. **Handwriting OCR script-CER:** Compute per-script CER for a model using aligned predictions; verify kanji > hiragana > katakana > ASCII pattern and compare to Figure 7.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How can VLMs be improved to overcome the persistent kanji recognition bottleneck, given its large inventory and high CER?
    *   **Basis:** Error analysis shows recognition as primary failure; kanji CER highest; dataset includes 2,866 unique kanji, exceeding Jōyō.
    *   **Why unresolved:** No intervention tested; benchmark diagnoses but does not solve.
    *   **Needed evidence:** Ablations on training data augmentation (synthetic kanji, font diversity) or architecture changes (script-aware tokenization) measured on kanji-specific CER.
*   **Open Question 2:** Why do general-purpose VLMs outperform OCR-specialized models on Handwriting OCR despite the latter's design for document text?
    *   **Basis:** Table 7 shows best OCR model (olmOCR-2-7B, 0.74) underperforms best VLM (Qwen3-VL-8B, 0.79). Paper notes OCR models trained on scanned documents vs. benchmark's diverse media.
    *   **Why unresolved:** No analysis of training data/architectural differences causing reversal; unclear if failure is due to domain shift (handwriting vs. print) or variability handling.
    *   **Needed evidence:** Controlled evaluations on matched subsets (e.g., only lined paper) to isolate factors like writing style, media, image quality.
*   **Open Question 3:** Does Japanese-centric pretraining provide uniform benefits across all Japanese scene text tasks, or are its advantages task-specific?
    *   **Basis:** Sarashina2.2-Vision-3B (Japanese-centric) matches much larger models on Dense STVQA but only smaller ones on KIE/OCR, suggesting confined benefit.
    *   **Why unresolved:** No ablation isolating effect of Japanese training data; unclear if advantage stems from better recognition, layout understanding, or both, and why it doesn’t transfer.
    *   **Needed evidence:** Fine-tuning a non-Japanese-centric model on controlled portions of Japanese scene text data and evaluating across all three tasks.
*   **Open Question 4:** Can JaWildText's diagnostic approach (disentangling recognition via evidence regions, script breakdown) be extended to other complex-script languages (e.g., Chinese, Korean)?
    *   **Basis:** Authors hope it encourages diagnostic evaluation in other typologically diverse languages; methodology is presented as transferable.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Only evaluated on Japanese; untested if same error taxonomy and script analysis yield analogous insights for Chinese/Hangul.
    *   **Needed evidence:** Building and evaluating a comparable benchmark for another script-complex language using the same evidence-region method.

## Limitations
*   LLM judge prompt and KIE JSON schema are undisclosed, preventing exact replication of STVQA and KIE scoring.
*   Model-specific preprocessing details (resizing, tiling) are not fully documented, introducing input representation variability.
*   The dataset size (2,961 images) may be insufficient to cover all in-the-wild scenarios, limiting generalizability claims.
