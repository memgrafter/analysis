# Grad-ELLM: Gradient-based Explanations for Decoder-only LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03089
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03089
- **Reference count**: 23
- **Primary result**: Grad-ELLM produces faithful per-token attributions for decoder-only LLMs by combining gradient-based channel importance with softened attention maps, achieving state-of-the-art performance on the proposed π‑Soft‑NS and π‑Soft‑NC metrics across multiple models and tasks.

## Executive Summary
Grad-ELLM addresses the challenge of generating faithful input attribution explanations for decoder-only large language models (LLMs). Existing model‑agnostic attribution methods often misalign with the transformer's internal mechanisms, leading to unfaithful explanations. The proposed method computes channel importance via gradients of the output logit with respect to each attention layer's output and spatial importance via 0‑1 normalized attention similarities, yielding dense heatmaps without architectural changes. To fairly compare attribution distributions, the authors introduce the π‑Soft‑NS and π‑Soft‑NC metrics, which normalize scores to a common retention probability. Extensive experiments on Llama and Mistral models across sentiment classification, question answering, and open‑generation tasks show Grad-ELLM consistently outperforms a range of baselines, setting new records on the π‑Soft metrics.

## Method Summary
Grad-ELLM generates token attributions by decomposing the output logit into per‑layer contributions. For a given generation step *t*, it first captures the output *o*<sup>(*k*)</sup><sub>*t*</sub> of each transformer layer *k*. Channel importance weights *w*<sub>*c*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> are derived as the gradient of the logit with respect to *o*<sup>(*k*)</sup><sub>*t*</sub> (first‑order Taylor approximation). Spatial importance weights λ<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> are obtained by applying 0‑1 normalization to the raw query‑key similarities *q*<sub>*t*</sub> · *k*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>⊤</sup>, softening the otherwise sparse softmax distribution. The per‑token attribution from layer *k* is then *H*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> = ReLU(∑<sub>*c*</sub> *w*<sub>*c*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> λ<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> *v*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>[<sub>*c*</sub>]), where *v*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> are the value vectors. The final heatmap aggregates these across selected layers (default: all 32). For evaluation, the π‑transform adjusts raw attribution scores to a target retention probability π before applying the Soft‑NS/NC perturbation protocol, which estimates faithfulness by measuring the Hellinger distance between the original and perturbed output distributions.

## Key Results
- Grad-ELLM achieves π‑Soft‑NS AUC 0.572 and π‑Soft‑NC AUC 1.115 on Llama, the highest among all compared methods.
- On Mistral, it leads with π‑Soft‑NS 0.383 and π‑Soft‑NC 0.491.
- The method consistently surpasses baselines (including attention, saliency, Integrated Gradients, DeepLIFT, etc.) across sentiment classification, question answering, and open‑generation tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Grad-ELLM produces faithful token attributions by deriving channel importance weights from the gradients of the output logit with respect to the value vectors in each attention layer.
- **Mechanism:** For a given generation step, the method approximates the logit contribution from a specific layer's output *o*<sub>*t*</sub> as a linear combination of its channel features *f*(*o*<sub>*t*</sub>) ≈ Σ<sub>*c*</sub> *w*<sub>*c*</sub> *o*<sub>*t*</sub>[*c*]. The channel weights *w*<sub>*c*</sub> are set to the partial derivative ∂*f*/∂*o*<sub>*t*</sub>, which matches the first‑order Taylor expansion. This gradient indicates how sensitively the logit responds to changes in each feature channel, providing a direct, model‑internal measure of channel importance.
- **Core assumption:** The logit function is locally linear around *o*<sub>*t*</sub>, so a first‑order approximation is sufficient for attributing output contributions to intermediate features.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 3.2] The derivation from Equation 6 to Equation 9 explicitly defines *w* = ∂*f*/∂*o*<sub>*t*</sub> as the solution to minimizing the difference in first derivatives between *f* and its linear approximation *f*̃.
  - [Section 3.2] Equation 12 shows the final attribution for token *i* incorporates Σ<sub>*c*</sub> *w*<sub>*c*</sub> λ<sub>*i*</sub> *v*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>*c*</sup>, where *w*<sub>*c*</sub> are these gradient‑derived channel weights.
  - [Abstract] Mentions "aggregating channel importance from gradients of the output logit with respect to attention layers."
- **Break condition:** If the gradient ∂*f*/∂*o*<sub>*t*</sub> vanishes or becomes highly unstable (e.g., due to saturation in the final projection or extreme activation magnitudes), the derived channel weights lose signal, making the attribution uninformative or noisy.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Grad-ELLM computes token (spatial) importance using a softened, 0‑1 normalized version of the raw attention similarity scores (*q*·*k*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>⊤</sup>), rather than the standard softmax, to distribute attribution more broadly and reveal less‑attended but semantically similar tokens.
- **Mechanism:** Standard softmax (λ<sub>*i*</sub> = softmax(*q*·*k*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>⊤</sup>/√*d*)) is overly sparse and peaky, concentrating almost all weight on a single key token. Grad‑ELLM replaces it with λ<sub>*i*</sub> ≈ Φ(*q*·*k*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>⊤</sup>), where Φ applies 0‑1 normalization across all token similarities. This loosening step preserves the relative ordering of similarities while ensuring non‑zero weights for all tokens, especially those with moderate similarity to the query. This denser spatial map better captures the holistic context used by the model.
- **Core assumption:** The raw dot‑product similarity *q*·*k*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>⊤</sup> is a meaningful, untransformed indicator of token relevance before the competitive normalization of softmax. Softmax's competitive nature artificially suppresses useful attribution signals from non‑peak tokens.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 3.2] Explicitly states: "we also apply 0‑1 normalization on the similarities [*q*<sub>*t*</sub> *k*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>⊤</sup>]<sub>*i*</sub> and use it to replace the original softmax... Without this loosening step, the softmax operation will produce sparser heat maps, due to the peakiness of softmax."
  - [Section 3.2] Claims the loosening "helps to reveal unattended input tokens that are similar to the attended token, which likely contain similar information."
  - [Abstract] References "spatial importance from softened attention maps (0‑1 normalized similarities)."
- **Break condition:** If the query‑key similarity distribution is extremely uniform (low variance), 0‑1 normalization may fail to distinguish relevant from irrelevant tokens, leading to noisy, uniformly distributed attributions that lack discriminatory power.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Grad-ELLM aggregates importance signals across multiple transformer layers via a recursive linear approximation of the logit, allowing it to capture hierarchical feature representation from early to late layers.
- **Mechanism:** The output logit *l*<sub>*t*</sub> is decomposed as the sum of layer‑wise contributions *l*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> (Equation 5). For each layer *k*, the method computes an attribution heatmap using that layer's output *o*<sup>(*k*)</sup><sub>*t*</sub> and its derived channel (*w*<sub>*c*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>) and spatial (λ<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>) weights. The final heatmap can be a sum or selective combination of these per‑layer heatmaps. This leverages the transformer's hierarchical processing: early layers capture surface features, later layers capture contextual and task‑specific refinements.
- **Core assumption:** The approximation *l*<sub>*t*</sub> ≈ Σ<sub>*k*</sub> *l*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> holds, meaning the contribution of each layer's output to the final logit can be independently estimated and summed without significant interaction effects being lost.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 3.2] Derives Equation 5: *l*<sub>*t*</sub> ≈ Σ<sub>*k*</sub> *l*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> as an aggregation of features from each layer, based on recursively decomposing the transformer block outputs.
  - [Section 3.2] States: "Based on (5), we can select the desired number of layers to aggregate information from different layers to obtain the heatmap."
  - [Section 4.3] Notes: "By default, we set the number of layers to 32 to get the most comprehensive information," implying aggregation across many layers is a key design choice.
- **Break condition:** If the linear approximation error \|*l*<sub>*t*</sub> − Σ<sub>*k*</sub> *l*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>\| is large for a particular layer (e.g., due to strong non‑linear interactions between layers), that layer's contribution to the heatmap may be misleading. The method assumes error is manageable across all layers.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
The core attribution pipeline flows as: Forward Pass Engine (captures *o*<sup>(*k*)</sup><sub>*t*</sub> from each layer) → Gradient Calculator (computes ∂*l*<sub>*t*</sub>/∂*o*<sup>(*k*)</sup><sub>*t*</sub> to get *w*<sub>*c*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>) → Attention Processor (extracts *q*<sub>*t*</sub>, *k*<sub>*i*</sub>, computes raw similarities, applies 0‑1 normalization to produce λ<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>) → Aggregation Module (combines *w*<sub>*c*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>, λ<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>, and *v*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> into per‑layer heatmaps *H*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>, then sums across selected layers to produce final heatmap *H*<sub>*i*</sub>). The evaluation pipeline adds: π‑Transformer (adjusts raw scores to target retention probability) → Soft perturbation (Bernoulli masking of embeddings) → Forward passes on perturbed inputs → Hellinger distance computation → Soft‑NS/NC score aggregation.

**Critical path:** For faithful attribution on a generated token: (1) Run forward pass to obtain logit *l*<sub>*t*</sub> for the target token and intermediate activations *o*<sup>(*k*)</sup><sub>*t*</sub> from all layers. (2) Perform backward pass from *l*<sub>*t*</sub> to compute gradients ∂*l*<sub>*t*</sub>/∂*o*<sup>(*k*)</sup><sub>*t*</sub>. (3) Extract attention weights (queries and keys) for the current token across layers, compute softened spatial weights λ<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>. (4) Retrieve value vectors *v*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>. (5) Compute per‑layer token scores *H*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> = ReLU(∑<sub>*c*</sub> *w*<sub>*c*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> λ<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> *v*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup>[<sub>*c*</sub>]). (6) Average or sum *H*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>(*k*)</sup> across layers to get final heatmap. (7) (Optional) Apply π‑transform for evaluation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Density vs. Sparsity:* The 0‑1 normalization yields denser heatmaps than softmax, capturing wider context but potentially introducing noise; sparser methods may excel on insertion/deletion metrics that reward sharp top‑k focus.  
- *Layer aggregation scope:* Using all layers (default 32) provides richer hierarchical information at the cost of higher memory and possible noise accumulation; limiting to late layers is cheaper but may miss early feature formation.  
- *Evaluation metric choice:* π‑Soft metrics measure distributional faithfulness (score calibration), whereas insertion/deletion AUC measures ordering correctness; the two can disagree, so metric selection should align with the intended use of explanations.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Vanishing gradients in deep layers cause channel weights to become uninformative, reducing attribution to the spatial component.  
- Overly uniform raw similarities after 0‑1 normalization produce near‑constant λ<sub>*i*</sub>, yielding noisy heatmaps.  
- π‑transformation may become numerically unstable when attribution scores contain extreme values (zeros or ones) or are very flat.  
- Memory overflow when storing activations/gradients for many layers and long sequences; mitigated by gradient checkpointing or layer‑by‑layer computation.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *Single‑layer ablation:* Run Grad‑ELLM using only the last layer’s output and compare π‑Soft‑NS/NC scores against the default multi‑layer aggregation to isolate the contribution of hierarchical aggregation.  
2. *Loosening ablation:* Replace the 0‑1 normalized attention with raw softmax in the spatial weight computation and evaluate on a small dataset (e.g., 50 IMDb examples). Expect π‑Soft‑NS/NC scores to drop, confirming the benefit of softened attention.  
3. *π‑Soft sanity check:* On a simple sentiment example with a clear polarity word, compute π‑Soft‑NS curves for Grad‑ELLM and a random baseline at π=0.1. Grad‑ELLM’s curve should lie well above random’s at low retention, verifying the pipeline’s ability to identify important tokens.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to multimodal LLMs** – The method currently targets text‑only decoder models; adapting it to architectures with vision encoders or cross‑attention is non‑trivial and untested.  
2. **Bias mitigation integration** – It is unclear whether Grad‑ELLM’s dense attributions can effectively identify biased tokens or improve fairness interventions; no experiments on bias benchmarks have been conducted.  
3. **Density–sparsity trade‑off** – The softened attention produces denser heatmaps, which may be suboptimal for tasks requiring sparse, sharp explanations. The paper does not explore alternative softening strategies or quantify this trade‑off.  
4. **Proprietary model adaptation** – Grad‑ELLM requires internal access to gradients and attention weights, which closed‑source APIs do not provide. Developing gray‑box proxies (e.g., gradient estimation via finite differences) remains an open challenge.  
5. **Generality of π‑Soft metrics** – The π‑Soft‑NS/NC metrics were designed for text LLMs; it is unknown whether they ensure fair comparison for other architectures (e.g., CNNs, RNNs) or data types (e.g., images, audio).

## Limitations
- Requires full access to model internals (activations, gradients, attention), limiting use with closed‑source models.
- Computationally expensive for long sequences and many layers due to multiple backward passes and per‑layer storage.
- Gradient‑based channel importance may suffer from vanishing/exploding gradients in deep networks.
- The 0‑1 normalization heuristic lacks theoretical justification and may behave poorly on similarity distributions with low variance.
- π‑Soft evaluation, while fairer, adds complexity and may not correlate perfectly with human judgment of explanation quality.

## Confidence
- Gradient‑based channel importance yields faithful attributions: **Medium** (sound theory but gradient instability concerns).  
- Softened attention maps improve distributional faithfulness: **Medium** (intuitive but ad‑hoc).  
- Grad‑ELLM outperforms baselines on π‑Soft‑NS/NC: **High** (consistent results across multiple tasks and models).  
- Method generalizes across tasks and model scales: **Medium** (evaluation limited to two model families and three task types).  

## Next Checks
1. Ablate layer aggregation: compare using only the last layer vs. all layers on π‑Soft‑NS/NC AUC to validate the design choice of aggregating across many layers.
2. Test alternative spatial weightings (e.g., temperature‑scaled softmax, top‑k masking, sigmoid normalization) and measure both π‑Soft scores and attribution sparsity to quantify the density–faithfulness trade‑off.
3. Conduct a small human evaluation: present annotators with input–heatmap pairs from Grad‑ELLM and a strong baseline (e.g., Integrated Gradients) for sentiment examples, and measure agreement that highlighted tokens are relevant; correlate these ratings with π‑Soft scores.
