# Stabilized Fine-Tuning with LoRA in Federated Learning: Mitigating the Side Effect of Client Size and Rank via the Scaling Factor

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.08058
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08058
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: SFed-LoRA introduces scaling factor γz=α√(N/r) to prevent gradient collapse in federated LoRA, achieving lower perplexity (~3.0 on Alpaca) and higher accuracy (+6.47 on GLUE MNLI-m) at high ranks without extra inference cost.

## Executive Summary
Federated learning (FL) of large language models via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) suffers from gradient collapse when adapter rank r is high, due to aggregation-induced variance that scales with the number of clients N. Existing LoRA scaling factors (α/r) and Rank-Stabilized LoRA (α/√r) ignore this aggregation effect, leading to instability in multi-client settings. This paper proposes SFed-LoRA, a theoretically grounded solution that adjusts the scaling factor to γz = α√(N/r). The derivation follows an asymptotic stability analysis, ensuring that the moments of the adapter output and gradients remain invariant to N and r. Empirical evaluations on instruction tuning (Alpaca), math reasoning (GSM8K), and NLU (GLUE) with LLaMA2-7B and RoBERTa-large demonstrate that SFed-LoRA maintains stable gradient norms across ranks, converges faster, and attains substantially lower perplexity and higher accuracy compared to FedSA-LoRA and FedSA-rsLoRA, particularly at high ranks (e.g., r=512). The method incurs no additional communication or inference overhead.

## Method Summary
SFed-LoRA builds upon the FedSA-LoRA framework, where each client maintains a local low-rank adapter with matrices B_i (up-projection) and A_i (down-projection). Only the A_i matrices are uploaded to the central server for aggregation; B_i remains local and is never shared. The server computes the global average ¯A = (1/N) Σ A_i and broadcasts it back. The key modification lies in the scaling factor applied to the adapter output: instead of the standard γ=α/r (LoRA) or γr=α/√r (rsLoRA), SFed-LoRA uses γz = α√(N/r). This scalar is applied during the forward pass on each client, yielding an effective adapter γz·B_i·¯A. Local training proceeds with standard optimizers (SGD or AdamW), updating both A_i and B_i using the loss. The scaling factor is recomputed each round based on the current N (number of participating clients) and fixed rank r. No additional parameters or server-side computations are introduced, preserving the communication efficiency of FedSA-LoRA and eliminating any inference cost after merging the adapter into the base model.

## Key Results
- SFed-LoRA maintains consistent gradient norms across a wide range of ranks (r=4 to 512), while baselines exhibit exponential decay or stratification.
- On Alpaca with N=20, r=512, SFed-LoRA reaches a final perplexity of ~3.0, significantly lower than FedSA-LoRA and FedSA-rsLoRA.
- On GLUE MNLI-matched with r=512, SFed-LoRA improves accuracy by +6.47 points over the baselines, demonstrating superior performance at high ranks.
- The method stabilizes training irrespective of client count, showing robustness to increasing N.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Aggregation-Induced Variance Accumulation**  
Gradient collapse in federated LoRA at high ranks stems from statistical variance introduced by averaging adapter updates across N clients. In FedSA-LoRA, only A matrices are aggregated, but each client’s local gradient depends on its private data and its local B matrix. When these updates are summed, the variance compounds linearly with N because client data are heterogeneous (non-IID). This aggregation-variance scaling ultimately causes gradient norms to collapse as rank increases. Break condition: if all client updates were identical (perfect IID), aggregation variance would be zero and standard scaling would not fail.

**Mechanism 2: Suboptimal Scaling Factor Interacts with Aggregation**  
Prior scaling factors (α/r and α/√r) are derived for single-client LoRA training and only consider rank-dependent variance (∝ 1/r or 1/√r). In FL, the aggregated update’s variance also scales with client count N. The mismatch between these factors and the federated aggregation dynamics leads to instability. The optimal scaling must simultaneously dampen the rank-driven component (∝ 1/r) and compensate for aggregation-driven variance (∝ √N). Break condition: if aggregation did not add variance (e.g., perfect joint aggregation of A and B, which is algebraically impossible under FedSA), the single-client scaling might suffice.

**Mechanism 3: Corrected Scaling Restores Variance Preservation**  
SFed-LoRA’s derived factor γz = α√(N/r) stabilizes training by making the adapter output a variance-preserving transformation across N and r. By increasing γ with √N and decreasing with √r, the second moment (variance) of γz·B·A becomes invariant to the specific choices of N and r. This prevents gradient norms from collapsing (high r, high N) or exploding, maintaining consistent signal strength throughout training. The approach is grounded in an asymptotic stability definition (Definition 4.1) that requires output and gradient moments to scale as Θ(1) in N and r. Evidence: empirical plots show SFed-LoRA keeps gradient norms flat across ranks, whereas baselines decay exponentially. Break condition: if input data moments themselves scale with N or r, the Θ(1) requirement may misalign with optimal feature learning; the theory prioritizes numerical stability over potential feature quality gains.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Pre-trained frozen model W0. Each client i holds local adapter: B_i (up-projection, zero-initialized, never uploaded) and A_i (down-projection, random-initialized, uploaded). Server aggregates uploaded A_i to global ¯A = (1/N) Σ A_i. Scaling factor module computes γz = α√(N/r) as a scalar applied during forward pass. Effective adapter on client: γz · B_i · ¯A. No new parameters; inference cost unchanged after merging.

**Critical Path**  
1. Local Update: Client i computes gradient from loss L(γz·B_i·A_i·x) and updates both B_i and A_i via SGD/AdamW.  
2. Upload: Client i sends its updated A_i to the server.  
3. Aggregate: Server computes new global ¯A as the average of all received A_i.  
4. Download & Merge: Clients receive ¯A and form the effective adapter γz·B_i·¯A for the next round. B_i remains local throughout.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Communication vs. Expressiveness:* Uploading only A reduces communication but confines B to be purely local, trading off potential expressiveness for analyzability.  
- *Simplicity vs. Optimality:* Using the theoretically derived γz is more complex than heuristic scalings but is necessary for stability at high ranks and large N.  
- *No Inference Cost:* The scaling factor is folded into training; post-training, the adapter is merged into W0, incurring zero runtime overhead.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Gradient norms collapse to near zero (<1e-6) as rank increases, especially after 20–30 rounds.  
- Validation perplexity plateaus at a high value (e.g., >10 on Alpaca) or becomes NaN/Inf.  
- Clear performance gap between low-rank (r=4,8) and high-rank (r=512) configurations; high rank underperforms.  
- Performance degrades noticeably as N increases (e.g., from 5 to 20) at fixed high rank, indicating scaling mismatch.

**First Experiments**  
1. *Stability Sweep:* Fix N=3 (IID), vary r∈{4,8,32,128,512}. Compare gradient norms of FedSA-LoRA vs. SFed-LoRA. Expect SFed-LoRA to show stable norms across r.  
2. *Client Count Sweep:* Set r=512, vary N∈{5,10,15,20}. Plot validation perplexity vs. rounds. Expect SFed-LoRA to converge to similar low perplexity regardless of N, while baselines worsen with more clients.  
3. *Cross-Task Generalization:* Apply the same sweeps to GSM8K (with LLaMA2-7B) and GLUE (with RoBERTa-large, non-IID Dir(0.5)). The advantage of SFed-LoRA at high ranks should generalize, confirming scalability across tasks and architectures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Architectural and protocol generalization**: How can the SFed-LoRA stability principles and scaling factor γz be extended to other federated large model architectures (e.g., encoder-decoder, multimodal transformers) and more complex aggregation protocols (e.g., weighted averaging, hierarchical aggregation)? The paper mentions this as future work but provides no analysis.

2. **Feature quality dependence**: How does variation in feature quality—which may depend on client size N and adapter rank r—affect the optimality of γz? The theoretical derivation assumes input data moments scale independently of N and r; if feature quality varies significantly with these parameters, the derived scaling may be suboptimal.

3. **Joint aggregation settings**: For FL frameworks that aggregate both A and B matrices (unlike FedSA-LoRA’s selective A-aggregation), can γz be directly applied, or is a fundamentally different scaling approach required? The current analysis is tailored to the FedSA design; the interaction between scaling and joint aggregation is unexplored.

4. **Extreme heterogeneity and local training regimes**: Under what conditions of extreme data heterogeneity (e.g., Dirichlet α < 0.5) or local training configurations (e.g., few local steps, varying compute resources) does γz fail to prevent gradient collapse? The experiments use moderate heterogeneity (α=0.5) and 10 local steps, leaving more challenging settings untested.

## Limitations

- The theoretical stability analysis relies on infinite-width (NTK) assumptions and assumes input data moments are independent of N and r; these may not hold in finite, real-world fine-tuning.
- Key hyperparameters for GSM8K and GLUE experiments (local epochs, batch size, learning rate schedules, validation splits) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- Evaluation is limited to decoder-only (LLaMA) and encoder-only (RoBERTa) models; applicability to other architectures (e.g., MoE, diffusion) remains untested, giving low confidence in generality.

## Confidence

- Aggregation-variance scaling (∝ N): High
- Derived scaling factor (γz ∝ √(N/r)): Medium
- Cross-task efficacy (Alpaca/GLUE/GSM8K): Medium
- Theoretical assumptions (infinite-width, moment independence): Medium
- Hyperparameter gaps: Medium
- Generality scope (beyond evaluated architectures): Low
