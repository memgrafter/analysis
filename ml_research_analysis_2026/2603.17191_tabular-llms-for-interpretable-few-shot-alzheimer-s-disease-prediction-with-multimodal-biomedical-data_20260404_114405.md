# Tabular LLMs for Interpretable Few-Shot Alzheimer's Disease Prediction with Multimodal Biomedical Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv**: 2603.17191  
- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17191  
- **Citations**: 40  
- **Key result**: TAP-GPT achieves mean F1=0.89 on QT-PAD few-shot tabular AD classification, outperforming classical baselines and rivaling general-purpose LLMs.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces TAP-GPT, a framework that fine‑tunes the tabular‑specialized TableGPT2 model for few‑shot Alzheimer’s disease (AD) prediction using structured tabular prompts. TAP‑GPT addresses the challenge of accurate diagnosis from small, incomplete tabular biomarker data, where deep learning often underperforms classical methods. Evaluated on four ADNI‑derived datasets—including the multimodal QT‑PAD and unimodal imaging (amyloid PET, tau PET, structural MRI)—TAP‑GPT achieves a mean F1 score of 0.89 on QT‑PAD in the few‑shot tabular setting (k=8), competing with state‑of‑the‑art LLMs and outperforming traditional ML. On unimodal imaging it remains competitive (tau PET F1=0.68). The model demonstrates robustness to missing data without imputation, generates interpretable, modality‑aware reasoning aligned with AD biology, and maintains stable predictions under self‑reflection.

## Method Summary
TAP‑GPT employs domain adaptation via QLoRA fine‑tuning of TableGPT2, keeping the tabular encoder frozen while updating the decoder (Qwen2.5‑7B backbone) for binary AD classification. Inputs are structured tabular prompts that preserve column‑wise relationships, contrasting with serialized text. For high‑dimensional imaging data, feature selection (LASSO) reduces dimensionality before prompt construction. The model uses few‑shot in‑context learning (k examples) and constrained decoding to output predictions. Training uses supervised binary cross‑entropy loss, with hyperparameter tuning via Optuna. Interpretability is achieved via chain‑of‑thought prompting for JSON reasoning without architectural changes.

## Key Results
- Achieves mean F1=0.89 on QT‑PAD (multimodal biomarker) few‑shot tabular setting (k=8).
- Competitive on unimodal imaging: tau PET F1=0.68 (optimized p=16, k=4).
- Robust to simulated/real missingness without imputation; generates biologically plausible reasoning; stable under self‑reflection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: TAP‑GPT leverages tabular prompts (structured tables) instead of serialized text to preserve permutation invariance and column‑wise relationships inherent in biomedical tables, which improves few‑shot learning for AD prediction.
- **Mechanism**: The model's TableGPT2 encoder processes explicit table structure (rows/columns) while the LLM decoder consumes this structured latent representation. This contrasts with serialization, which flattens tables into sequential text, losing alignment and increasing token length.
- **Core assumption**: Structured tabular input maintains feature interactions critical for small, high‑dimensional biomedical data where column order is semantically meaningful (e.g., ROI features).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "using structured tabular prompts" and "tabular‑specialized LLM"
  - [section II] "tabular prompts rather than plain texts" and Figure 2 format comparison
  - [corpus] Weak direct corpus support; neighboring papers focus on multimodal fusion or tabular attention, not prompt structuring.
- **Break condition**: Performance degrades with high‑dimensional imaging features (p=72) unless feature selection reduces dimensionality (Fig. 7), indicating tabular prompts struggle with excessive column count.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Domain adaptation via QLoRA fine‑tuning on TableGPT2's decoder layer injects AD‑specific diagnostic patterns while freezing the pretrained tabular encoder, balancing biomedical knowledge transfer with structural integrity.
- **Mechanism**: The frozen TableGPT2 encoder retains generalized tabular representations from business‑domain pretraining. QLoRA updates low‑rank adapters in the decoder (Qwen2.5‑7B backbone) to map structured features to AD/CN labels using few‑shot examples.
- **Core assumption**: TableGPT2's encoder already captures tabular semantics (e.g., column relationships); only the decoder needs adaptation for the clinical classification task.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section II.B] "keep the tabular encoder fixed and update only the decoder parameters during adaptation"
  - [section III.A] Stepwise gains: Qwen2.5 → TableGPT2 (tabular specialization) → TAP‑GPT (task fine‑tuning)
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Longitudinal Progression Prediction..." use tabular foundation models but not domain‑adapted LLMs; limited direct comparison.
- **Break condition**: Performance depends on backbone quality; TableGPT‑R1 (Qwen3‑based) underperformed in imaging few‑shot settings (Section III.B.3), suggesting decoder capacity or fine‑tuning alignment matters.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Interpretable, modality‑aware reasoning emerges from prompting strategies that exploit the LLM's pretrained biomedical semantics and feature naming, enabling cross‑modal justification without architectural changes.
- **Mechanism**: Prompts instruct CoT reasoning (e.g., "Let's think step by step") and request structured JSON outputs. The model leverages feature names (e.g., "hippocampus", "tau PET") and in‑context examples to generate explanations referencing biologically plausible regions.
- **Core assumption**: The LLM's pretraining corpus contains sufficient AD/neuroscience knowledge to produce clinically coherent reasoning when conditioned on tabular evidence.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "generates interpretable, modality‑aware reasoning aligned with AD biology"
  - [section III.C] Qualitative examples (Fig. 8) show correct region references (e.g., "normal hippocampal volume" for CN)
  - [section III.C.2] GPT‑4.1‑mini rankings match known AD regions (hippocampus, entorhinal cortex)
- **Break condition**: Reasoning can be inconsistent—e.g., misinterpreting feature directionality (higher FDG as AD indicator) or hallucinating values—requiring output validation (Section III.C.1). Self‑reflection stability varies by model (Fig. 10).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Tabular data properties (permutation invariance, small‑N large‑p, heterogeneous missingness)  
  - **Why needed here**: Biomedical tables have few patients (N) but many features (p), columns are unordered, and missing values are common; models must generalize from limited labeled examples without assuming feature order.  
  - **Quick check question**: "If you shuffle column order, does model performance drop significantly?"
- **Concept**: In‑context learning (ICL) with few‑shot examples  
  - **Why needed here**: AD datasets are small; TAP‑GPT conditions predictions on k labeled examples in the prompt rather than gradient updates, enabling adaptation without full retraining.  
  - **Quick check question**: "How does performance change when k increases from 2 to 8 (QT‑PAD) or 4 (imaging)?"
- **Concept**: Parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (QLoRA)  
  - **Why needed here**: Full fine‑tuning of 7B+ LLMs is costly; QLoRA updates low‑rank matrices while quantizing base weights, preserving pretrained knowledge with minimal overhead.  
  - **Quick check question**: "Is the tabular encoder frozen? Which layers receive LoRA adapters?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: 1. Data loader: Splits ADNI data into train/val/test and separate ICL pools (per modality). 2. Feature selector: Applies LASSO to reduce p for imaging data. 3. Prompt builder: Formats selected features + k ICL examples into tabular/serialized prompts with diagnosis token. 4. TableGPT2 encoder: Frozen semantic table encoder processing structured table. 5. Decoder (Qwen2.5‑7B): QLoRA‑updated LLM head for binary classification. 6. Output parser: Constrained decoding to {0,1} or JSON for interpretability.
- **Critical path**: Raw data → feature selection → prompt construction → encoder → decoder → prediction. *Must maintain strict ICL pool separation to avoid data leakage*.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Tabular vs. serialized prompts: Tabular preserves structure but may hit context limits; serialized uses natural language but can lose alignment with many examples (QT‑PAD serialized few‑shot collapsed).  
  - Feature selection (p) vs. ICL examples (k): Trade prompt length for signal; imaging data needs p=16, k=4; QT‑PAD uses all 19 features, k=8.  
  - Backbone choice: Qwen2.5‑based TableGPT2 works; newer Qwen3‑based TableGPT‑R1 did not improve few‑shot imaging performance, possibly due to RL tuning for reasoning, not low‑shot classification.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - High‑dimensional collapse: p=72 imaging features cause near‑zero F1 for TAP‑GPT and TableGPT‑R1.  
  - Serialization degradation: Few‑shot serialized prompts underperform tabular on QT‑PAD.  
  - Self‑reflection instability: GPT‑4.1‑mini performance drops under self‑critique; TAP‑GPT stable.  
  - Reasoning hallucination: Model misinterprets biomarker directionality or confuses patient rows.
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. Baseline reproduction: Train TAP‑GPT on QT‑PAD with k=8 tabular prompts; verify mean F1 ~0.89. Check that unfinetuned TableGPT2 underperforms.  
  2. Imaging modality ablation: For tau PET, run p×k grid (p∈{8,16,32}, k∈{4,8,12}); confirm p=16, k=4 optimal. Compare against TabPFN and XGBoost in few‑shot tabular setting.  
  3. Missingness stress test: Apply 30–50% uniform random masking to QT‑PAD test inputs; verify TAP‑GPT retains F1 >0.75 while classical models drop. Repeat with real missingness cohort.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question**: How does updating the backbone LLM of TAP‑GPT to more recent models (e.g., Qwen3) affect performance on AD prediction tasks?  
   - **Basis**: "Future work could explore reconstructing a similar tabular‑LLM architecture using more modern backbones for clinical prediction tasks."  
   - **Why unresolved**: The current TAP‑GPT uses a fixed TableGPT2 architecture (Qwen2.5 backbone), limiting potential gains from newer, more capable LLMs.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Ablation studies comparing TAP‑GPT variants with updated decoder backbones while keeping the tabular encoder and training pipeline constant, evaluated across all modalities.

2. **Question**: Can TAP‑GPT be extended to jointly process multiple imaging modalities (e.g., amyloid PET, tau PET, MRI) in a unified tabular representation to improve AD classification accuracy?  
   - **Basis**: "It is possible that moving towards a multimodal analysis of the imaging data would further improve TAP‑GPT’s performance on these datasets, similar to what we observed in the multimodal dataset QT‑PAD."  
   - **Why unresolved**: The imaging experiments were conducted separately per modality; synergistic effects from multimodal fusion remain unexplored.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Construct multimodal tabular prompts combining features from all imaging types and evaluate TAP‑GPT performance against unimodal baselines, measuring gains from cross‑modal interactions.

3. **Question**: How does TAP‑GPT perform in iterative multi‑agent settings beyond single‑step self‑reflection, such as multi‑round debates or collaborative diagnosis with other agents?  
   - **Basis**: "This stability is particularly relevant for multi‑agent chains of communication... TAP‑GPT’s stability here indicates reduced susceptibility to drift during debate." But: "although this represents a simple single round of self‑reflection..."  
   - **Why unresolved**: The study only tested one round of self‑reflection; true multi‑agent dynamics with multiple critique‑revision cycles are unexamined.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Simulate multi‑agent systems where multiple TAP‑GPT instances (or heterogeneous agents) iteratively discuss a case, measuring prediction stability, consensus convergence, and final accuracy compared to single‑agent baselines.

4. **Question**: What is the reliability of TAP‑GPT's generated reasoning explanations, and can we develop methods to validate or improve their clinical correctness?  
   - **Basis**: "These errors underscore the need to validate generated explanations and caution against interpreting reasoning strings as causal evidence."  
   - **Why unresolved**: The model produces plausible but sometimes incorrect reasoning (e.g., misinterpreting biomarker directionality), which could mislead clinicians if trusted.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Create a dataset of AD cases with gold‑standard clinician rationales, then evaluate alignment between model reasoning and clinical truth; develop training objectives or post‑hoc checks to flag inconsistent explanations.

## Limitations
- ADNI preprocessing pipeline (FreeSurfer version, ROI definitions) underspecified.
- ICL pool sampling strategy and deterministic formatting details missing; risk of data leakage.
- Hyperparameter search spaces and optimal values omitted; hinders exact replication.
- Model collapses on high‑dimensional imaging (p=72) and can produce reasoning hallucinations.

## Confidence
- Tabular prompts preserve structure: Medium
- QLoRA adaptation: High
- Interpretable reasoning: Low

## Next Checks
1. Permutation test: Shuffle column order in QT‑PAD prompts (k=8); expect F1 drop >5% if tabular structure is critical.
2. ICL stratification: Evaluate with balanced, all‑AD, and random ICL pools; high variance indicates fragility in few‑shot setup.
3. Encoder unfreezing: Unfreeze TableGPT2 encoder during QLoRA on tau PET; performance gain would contradict frozen‑encoder assumption.
