# Blinded Radiologist and LLM-Based Evaluation of LLM-Generated Japanese Translations of Chest CT Reports: Comparative Study

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2604.02207
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02207
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Radiologists showed poor inter-rater agreement (QWK 0.01–0.06) while LLM judges strongly favored LLM translations (70–99% preference) and rated them as more radiologist-like (>93%), with near-zero human-LLM agreement.

## Executive Summary
This study compared DeepSeek-V3.2-generated Japanese translations of chest CT reports to human-edited versions, assessing educational suitability. Two blinded radiologists (one board-certified, one resident) and three LLM judges evaluated 150 translation pairs across four criteria: terminology accuracy, readability, overall quality, and radiologist-style authenticity. Radiologists exhibited poor inter-rater agreement (QWK 0.01–0.06) and diverged in their preferences, while all LLM judges strongly favored the LLM-generated translations (70–99% preference) and rated them as more authentic (>93%), showing near-zero agreement with radiologists (QWK -0.04 to 0.15). The findings reveal that LLM-as-a-judge evaluations systematically prefer fluent outputs and disagree with domain experts, indicating that automated quality assessment alone is insufficient for ensuring translation quality in radiology education.

## Method Summary
The study used the CT-RATE-JPN dataset containing 150 chest CT reports, each with an original English report, a human-edited Japanese translation, and a DeepSeek-V3.2-generated Japanese translation. The LLM translation was produced with temperature 0 using a fixed system prompt. Two blinded radiologists (one board-certified, one resident) and three LLM judges (DeepSeek-V3.2, Mistral Large 3, GPT-5) independently evaluated each translation pair in random order across four criteria: terminology accuracy, readability/fluency, overall quality, and radiologist-style authenticity (the latter with an additional "Uncertain" option). Evaluators chose "A better", "B better", or "Equivalent". LLM judges produced structured JSON outputs with a winner and brief rationale. Inter-rater agreement was quantified using quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) on an ordinal scale (human-edited < tie < LLM preferred). Linguistic analysis compared character counts, sentence segmentation (using Janome), and type-token ratio (TTR), applying paired statistical tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank, t-test).

## Key Results
- Radiologists' inter-rater agreement was poor (QWK 0.01–0.06), with one favoring the LLM translation and the other preferring human-edited.
- All LLM judges strongly favored the LLM-generated translations, with preference rates ranging from 70% to 99% across criteria.
- LLM judges rated the LLM translations as more radiologist-like than human-edited (>93% preference), but agreement with radiologists was negligible (QWK -0.04 to 0.15).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Divergent Linguistic Properties
- **Claim**: Divergent linguistic properties (sentence length, structure) of LLM-generated vs. human-edited translations lead to systematically different human and LLM evaluation patterns.
- **Mechanism**: DeepSeek generates shorter, more numerous, and lexically diverse sentences. Radiologists prioritize clinical density and conventional phrasing, while LLMs value surface fluency and conciseness, creating a bifurcation in preferences.
- **Evidence**: The abstract and linguistic analysis sections report significantly shorter character counts, more sentences, and higher TTR for DeepSeek outputs (p < 0.001). Corpus support is limited but aligns with known fluency biases.
- **Break condition**: If linguistic normalization (e.g., post-editing LLM output to match human sentence structure) eliminates the preference gap between evaluator types.

### Mechanism 2: LLM-as-a-Judge Self-Preference Bias
- **Claim**: LLM-as-a-judge exhibits a strong self-preference, systematically favoring outputs from models of its own lineage or with similar training objectives.
- **Mechanism**: LLM judges are fine-tuned on human preferences that reward natural, fluent, and concise text. When evaluating translations from a powerful model like DeepSeek-V3.2, they recognize and reward patterns aligned with their own generation distribution, overriding nuanced clinical judgments.
- **Evidence**: All three LLM judges strongly and systematically favored the LLM translation (70–99% preference) and rated it as more radiologist-like (>93%). The corpus includes studies documenting self-preference and fluency over adequacy biases.
- **Break condition**: If LLM judges are explicitly fine-tuned on radiology expert preferences or provided with gold-standard style guides, their agreement with radiologists improves significantly.

### Mechanism 3: Subjectivity of Radiologist-Style Authenticity
- **Claim**: The construct "radiologist-style authenticity" is subjective and inconsistently applied even among human experts, making it an unreliable anchor for automated evaluation.
- **Mechanism**: Criteria for an authentic radiology report (e.g., use of loanwords, sentence complexity, hedging) are not universally agreed upon. One radiologist preferred the LLM's concise style, the other the human-edited version, demonstrating that "authenticity" reflects personal or institutional norms rather than fixed properties. LLMs, lacking professional socialization, latch onto superficial features like conciseness, which one human rater also values, creating spurious alignment but not field-wide consensus.
- **Evidence**: Radiologist inter-rater agreement on style authenticity was extremely low (QWK = 0.012). LLM judges agreed with the radiologist who favored conciseness (near 100% preference for LLM), but not with the other. No shared operational style guide exists for Japanese radiology reports.
- **Break condition**: If a consensus style guide for Japanese radiology reports is established and used to train both human raters and LLM judges, higher inter-rater agreement should follow.

## Foundational Learning

- **Medical Translation Equivalence Trade-offs**: Translation quality in medicine is multi-dimensional (terminology, readability, style, clinical adequacy). Improving one dimension (e.g., fluency) can degrade another (e.g., conventional phrasing). *Why needed*: Understanding these trade-offs is essential to interpret divergent evaluator preferences. *Quick check*: If an LLM translation is shorter and uses more common vocabulary but omits a conventional Japanese radiology hedge phrase, which dimension should dominate for "educational suitability" and why?

- **LLM-as-a-Judge Systematic Biases**: Known biases include self-preference, fluency over adequacy, and position bias. *Why needed*: The study's core finding is that automated judging fails; knowing these biases helps design safeguards and interpret extreme preferences. *Quick check*: Beyond self-preference, what two other common LLM judge biases might explain why all three judges strongly favored the DeepSeek output even when terminology errors were present?

- **Inter-Rater Reliability Metrics in Subject Domains**: Quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) penalizes larger ordinal disagreements but assumes ordered categories. *Why needed*: Low human-human QWK on a preference task can be informative because "ground truth" is inherently subjective; a high QWK might mask real disagreement. *Quick check*: Why is a low QWK between two radiologists on a "preference" task potentially more informative than a high QWK would be, in this context?

## Architecture Onboarding

*Component map*:
The evaluation pipeline consists of:
- **DatasetLoader**: Fetches paired English and Japanese reports from CT-RATE-JPN.
- **LinguisticAnalyzer**: Computes character counts, sentence segmentation, and TTR using Janome.
- **TranslationGenerator**: Interface to DeepSeek-V3.2 (or other LLMs) with controlled prompts and temperature=0.
- **HumanEvaluatorUI**: Presents blinded translation pairs (randomized A/B) to radiologists and captures selections on terminology, readability, quality, and style authenticity.
- **LLMJudgeOrchestrator**: Serves the same pairs (with A/B randomized) to LLM judges, enforces structured JSON output (winner, rationale).
- **AgreementCalculator**: Computes quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) and raw percentage agreement for all rater pairs on the ordinal scale.
- **QualitativeDisagreementAnalyzer**: Identifies cases where all LLMs vs. both humans disagree, extracts rationales for thematic analysis.

The flow is:
DatasetLoader → LinguisticAnalyzer → (HumanEvaluatorUI and LLMJudgeOrchestrator in parallel) → AgreementCalculator → QualitativeDisagreementAnalyzer → Conclusion.

*Critical path*:
1. Load dataset (with or without LLM translations; if missing, generate via TranslationGenerator).
2. Perform linguistic analysis on both translation versions.
3. Conduct human and LLM evaluations in parallel.
4. Calculate inter-rater agreement metrics.
5. Identify and analyze disagreement cases.
6. Synthesize conclusions.

*Design tradeoffs*:
- **Human evaluation**: High nuance and clinical validity, but low scalability and expensive; only two radiologists limits inter-rater reliability estimation.
- **LLM-as-judge**: Highly scalable and reproducible, but exhibits systematic biases (self-preference, fluency focus) and lacks domain-specificity; three judges provide a cross-section but no guarantee of unbiasedness.
- **Metrics**: QWK handles ordinal data and penalizes larger disagreements, but assumes categories are ordered and equally spaced; for subjective style authenticity, this assumption may not hold.

*Failure signatures*:
1. LLM judges show >80% preference for one translation source across all criteria (indicates strong bias).
2. Human inter-rater QWK < 0.1 on terminology accuracy (suggests task ambiguity or rubric inadequacy).
3. LLM judge rationales contain generic praise ("concise", "natural") without referencing specific clinical content, even when terminology errors are present.

*First 3 experiments*:
1. **Replication & Baseline**: Run the exact pipeline on the provided CT-RATE-JPN validation set (n=150) with the same prompts to confirm primary findings: (a) poor human-human agreement (QWK <0.1), (b) extreme LLM preference for LLM output (>70%), (c) near-zero human-LLM agreement.
2. **Bias Isolation**: Swap the LLM translation source. Generate translations with a different model (e.g., GPT-4o) and have the same three LLM judges evaluate both DeepSeek vs. Human and GPT-4o vs. Human pairs. Test if judges systematically prefer the output from the model they are most aligned with (e.g., GPT-5 prefers GPT-4o, DeepSeek prefers DeepSeek), quantifying self-preference strength.
3. **Prompt Sensitivity**: For a fixed set of 50 disagreement cases, vary the LLM judge prompt: (a) Original (prioritize fidelity & medical safety), (b) Add explicit negative examples of common LLM errors (e.g., "Avoid translating 'tree-in-bud' literally"), (c) Require justification referencing the English source text. Measure if agreement with the radiologist who preferred the human-edited translation improves under stricter, source-grounded prompts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. Does learning from LLM-translated radiology reports (vs. human-edited) lead to measurable differences in trainee knowledge acquisition, terminology retention, or interpretation accuracy in educational contexts? The current study only measured evaluator preferences, not downstream educational effects. This could be resolved by randomized controlled trials comparing trainee outcomes after using either translation type.

2. What specific factors (e.g., self-preference bias, overvaluation of fluency, insensitivity to clinical register conventions) primarily drive the systematic divergence between LLM-as-a-judge evaluations and expert radiologist judgments? The paper hypothesizes but did not test these mechanisms. Resolution requires experiments manipulating translation features (e.g., injecting errors, altering structure) and measuring judge vs. radiologist scores, with rationale analysis.

3. Can the design of the LLM-as-a-judge prompt and rubric be optimized (e.g., by incorporating radiology-specific guidelines, weighted criteria, or reference examples) to achieve meaningful alignment with radiologist assessments? The study used a generic prompt; it is unclear if radiology-tailored prompts can bridge the gap. Resolution requires iterative prompt redesign and measurement of agreement with radiologists.

4. How do the observed linguistic differences between LLM-generated and human-edited translations correlate with radiologists' subjective judgments of "radiologist-style authenticity"? The paper presented linguistic metrics and style disagreement separately without linking them. Resolution requires a post-hoc correlational analysis mapping linguistic features to radiologist style preferences, possibly augmented by qualitative analysis.

## Limitations
- Radiologist evaluation protocol (interface, randomization, handling of "Uncertain") is unspecified, potentially affecting reproducibility of human results.
- QWK calculation for style authenticity (criterion 4) with inclusion of "Uncertain" responses is unclear, introducing ambiguity in agreement metrics.
- Access to GPT-5 and Mistral Large 3 may be restricted, requiring substitutions that could alter the observed LLM judge biases and limit generalizability.

## Confidence
- LLM-as-judge self-preference: High
- Poor radiologist inter-rater agreement: Medium-High
- Linguistic divergence as cause of evaluation differences: Medium
- Subjectivity of radiologist-style authenticity: Medium
