# Cognitive Mismatch in Multimodal Large Language Models for Discrete Symbol Understanding

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.18472
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18472
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** State-of-the-art MLLMs exhibit a "recognition-reasoning inversion," performing worse on foundational symbol recognition (Level 1) than on higher-level compositional reasoning (Levels 2/3), contrasting with human cognitive trajectories.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates a fundamental gap in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs): their ability to process discrete symbolic information (e.g., formulas, characters) versus continuous natural scenes. The authors introduce "Visual Semiosis-13K," a large hierarchical benchmark spanning five domains and three cognitive levels. Evaluating nine leading MLLMs reveals a counterintuitive "recognition-reasoning inversion"—models are less accurate at basic symbol perception than at complex reasoning. Human performance shows the opposite, declining with task complexity. The findings indicate MLLMs rely on linguistic priors and procedural imitation rather than robust visual grounding for symbols, with performance heavily domain-dependent. The paper concludes that current visual encoders (e.g., ViTs) and training objectives are misaligned with the precise, structural demands of discrete symbol understanding.

## Method Summary
The study constructs and evaluates on "Visual Semiosis-13K," a benchmark of 13,148 samples across Language (Chinese characters, emojis), Culture, Mathematics, Physics, and Chemistry. Tasks are hierarchically organized: Level 1 (Perception & Recognition), Level 2 (Combination & Reasoning), Level 3 (Association & Critical Thinking). Data is sourced from existing public datasets (e.g., VisualC3, MathVista, ChemBench) and augmented with new handwritten error samples. Metrics are domain-specific (e.g., exact match, F1, edit distance). Nine MLLMs (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-4o, Qwen2.5-VL) are evaluated in a zero- or few-shot setting without training on the benchmark. The analysis compares average performance trajectories across the three levels to identify the inversion pattern and performs qualitative error analysis.

## Key Results
*   **Recognition-Reasoning Inversion:** All evaluated MLLMs consistently achieve lower accuracy on Level 1 (basic perception) than on Level 2/3 (reasoning) tasks across most domains, violating a canonical cognitive hierarchy.
*   **Domain-Specific Proficiency:** Models excel in structured formal systems (Mathematics, Chemistry) where symbols are dense and rules are explicit, but struggle with sparse, culturally nuanced symbols (Language, Culture) at all levels.
*   **Human-Model Divergence:** Human accuracy monotonically declines from Level 1 to 3, starkly contrasting the inverted model trajectories, confirming a fundamental cognitive mismatch in how symbols are processed.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: ViT as a Spatial Low-Pass Filter
*   **Claim:** The Vision Transformer's patch-based, global self-attention with coarse positional encoding inherently blurs the precise topological structures (connectivity, angles, stroke endpoints) critical for discrete symbols, acting as a spatial low-pass filter.
*   **Mechanism:** ViTs optimize for low-frequency, holistic features in natural scenes. For sparse, high-frequency symbol details (e.g., a single chemical bond, a stroke in a character), this representation averages or loses critical information, where a missing pixel can change semantic meaning.
*   **Core assumption:** The visual processing bottleneck in standard ViTs is too coarse for symbol-level fidelity.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [Page 19] Architectural description; [Page 12-16] Qualitative failures on sparse structures; inferred from failure patterns discussed in related work on symbolic reasoning.
*   **Break condition:** Introduction of a structural bottleneck (e.g., symbolic parsing layer, graph extractor) that explicitly preserves topology before LLM integration.

### Mechanism 2: Jump Mapping via Training Objectives
*   **Claim:** Current MLLM training objectives (e.g., VQA, captioning) encourage a "jump mapping" from raw pixels directly to high-level linguistic concepts, bypassing the construction of a stable, compositional visual symbol representation.
*   **Mechanism:** Models learn to map an image region to a word/concept (e.g., "blob" → "benzene ring") without verifying visual primitive correctness. For discrete symbols, this shortcut fails because the "blob" must be *exactly* right; strong language priors fill in plausible interpretations from flawed inputs.
*   **Core assumption:** Learning signal is biased toward final semantic alignment, not intermediate visual fidelity.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] Statement on linguistic priors; [Page 11] Discussion of jump mapping; [Page 10-11] Case examples where models read text labels instead of analyzing shapes.
*   **Break condition:** Training objectives that include auxiliary losses for symbolic element detection, count, and structural consistency before question answering.

### Mechanism 3: Reasoning Compensation Masking Perception
*   **Claim:** A model's powerful in-context reasoning and procedural knowledge can compensate for deficient visual perception, creating the illusion of understanding on higher-level tasks.
*   **Mechanism:** For Level 2/3 tasks, models apply known rules (e.g., "sum of angles in a triangle") or reasoning chains based on linguistic patterns, not the actual image. Success on these tasks does not indicate correct visual interpretation, as language-only knowledge can simulate the output of a correct vision-reasoning pipeline.
*   **Core assumption:** Language knowledge can simulate correct outputs without accurate visual grounding.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] Domain-dependent performance; [Page 17] Explicit statement on compensation; [Page 10] Case where model ignored visual clues for symbolic algebra.
*   **Break condition:** Evaluation tasks that decouple perception from reasoning by forcing an intermediate structured visual representation output.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Discrete Semantic Space**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper's core thesis contrasts continuous natural scenes (distributed meaning) with discrete symbolic spaces (precise, combinatorial meaning). This distinction explains why small visual perturbations cause total semantic shifts for symbols but not for natural images.
    *   **Quick check:** Why does a missing stroke in a Chinese character or a single wrong bond in a chemical formula cause a total semantic shift, whereas a minor texture change in a natural image often does not?
*   **Concept: Hierarchical Cognitive Levels (Perception → Reasoning → Critical Thinking)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The entire benchmark and the inversion finding are structured around this hierarchy. It operationalizes the claim that models lack foundational perceptual skills.
    *   **Quick check:** Give an example of a Level 1 (Perception) and a Level 3 (Critical Thinking) task for a chemical equation. Why is the Level 3 task cognitively harder for a human?
*   **Concept: Linguistic Priors vs. Visual Grounding**
    *   **Why needed here:** This is the proposed explanation for the inversion. Models default to strong text-based knowledge when visual evidence is ambiguous or poor, bypassing true symbol parsing.
    *   **Quick check:** In the faked character detection task (Level 1), what is the difference between a model that uses visual grounding and one that uses a linguistic prior to "correct" the character?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Input Image -> Vision Transformer (ViT) Patch Encoder -> Global Self-Attention -> Vision-Language Projector -> [Interleaved Visual & Text Tokens] -> Large Language Model (LLM) Decoder -> Output Text.
*   **Critical path:** The failure occurs in the ViT's global attention stage, which produces coarse representations that lose precise spatial relationships of discrete symbols. The subsequent LLM stage then applies powerful linguistic priors to these degraded visual tokens.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Resolution vs. Compute:** Higher resolution preserves symbol detail but linearly increases patch count and memory.
    *   **Modality Fusion:** Early fusion (projecting patches into LLM space) may allow the LLM to "correct" bad vision, exacerbating the inversion.
    *   **Task Generalization:** Training on diverse natural images improves noise robustness but may dilute the signal needed for precise symbolic parsing.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    1.  **Recognition-Reasoning Inversion:** Benchmark shows Level 1 accuracy < Level 2/3 accuracy.
    2.  **Linguistic Hallucination:** Outputting plausible text that ignores visual constraints (e.g., calling a non-monotonic function monotonic).
    3.  **Template-based Correction:** For error tasks, outputting generic excuses or replacing anomalous symbols with the most common visual-literal match.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Isolate Perception:** Run all Level 1 benchmark tasks. Expect low scores on sparse symbols (chemistry bonds, circuit symbols), confirming baseline perceptual deficiency.
    2.  **Probe Compensation:** For a successful Level 2/3 task, ablate the image input (use a text description). If performance remains high, the model was using linguistic priors, not visual reasoning.
    3.  **Attention Diagnostics:** For a Level 1 failure case (e.g., faked character), visualize ViT attention maps on the altered stroke. Expect diffuse or misaligned attention on critical high-frequency details.

## Limitations
*   The "Visual Semiosis-13K" benchmark dataset is unreleased, blocking independent verification of the inversion phenomenon.
*   Model evaluation protocol (exact prompts, few-shot examples, decoding parameters) is incompletely specified, hindering exact result replication.
*   Mechanistic claims (ViT low-pass filter, jump mapping) rely on indirect evidence from failure patterns; no controlled ablations directly test these hypotheses.

## Confidence
*   **Recognition-reasoning inversion:** High (large-scale, consistent across 9 models).
*   **ViT low-pass filter hypothesis:** Medium (supported by qualitative patterns, not direct measurement).
*   **Jump mapping effect:** Medium (observed behavior aligns, but no training ablation).
*   **Reasoning compensation:** Medium (inferred from task success despite perception errors).
*   **Need for structural bottleneck:** Low (theoretical inference, no experimental validation).

## Next Checks
