# There Are No Silly Questions: Evaluation of Offline LLM Capabilities from a Turkish Perspective

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.09996
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09996
- **Reference Count**: 6
- **Primary Result**: Offline LLMs evaluated on Turkish heritage language pedagogy: sub‑1B models fail safety thresholds; 8B–14B reasoning models offer optimal cost‑safety trade‑off; top model: zai-orgglm-4.7-flash (85/100).

## Executive Summary
Heritage language education demands pedagogical safety, yet offline large language models (LLMs) may exhibit risks like sycophancy and hallucination, especially with vulnerable learners. This study introduces the Turkish Anomaly Suite (TAS), a set of 10 edge‑case questions probing epistemic resistance, logical consistency, and cultural accuracy, to evaluate offline LLMs in a Turkish heritage language context. Testing 14 models (270M–32B parameters) reveals that anomaly resistance is not strictly scale‑dependent; large models still show sycophancy bias, while reasoning‑oriented 8B–14B models provide an optimal balance between cost and safety. Models under 1B parameters consistently fail critical safety thresholds, underscoring the need for careful model selection in educational deployments. The top‑performing model was zai‑orgglm‑4.7‑flash, achieving 85 out of 100 points.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed the Turkish Anomaly Suite (TAS) by identifying ten challenging scenarios that test a model’s ability to resist epistemic overconfidence, maintain logical coherence, and adhere to cultural norms specific to Turkish heritage language learners. Each question targets potential failure modes such as unfounded assertions, contradictions, or culturally insensitive content. The suite was administered to fourteen locally deployable LLMs ranging from 270 million to 32 billion parameters, all run in offline mode to simulate resource‑constrained educational settings. Model responses were evaluated against a rubric assessing safety markers (e.g., hedging, refusal, accuracy) and consistency, yielding a composite score out of 100. The evaluation pipeline was designed to be reproducible across diverse hardware, focusing on pedagogical risk rather than generic language proficiency.

## Key Results
- The top model, zai‑orgglm‑4.7‑flash, scored 85/100 on TAS, demonstrating strong safety performance.
- Models with fewer than 1 billion parameters uniformly failed to meet critical safety thresholds.
- Anomaly resistance did not correlate strictly with parameter count; sycophancy remained an issue even in the largest models, while 8B–14B reasoning‑oriented models achieved the best cost‑safety trade‑off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Turkish Anomaly Suite works by presenting models with carefully crafted edge cases that force a decision between unsafe completion patterns (sycophantic agreement, confident hallucination, cultural misstep) and safe, pedagogically sound responses. Each item is designed to probe one of three safety dimensions: epistemic resistance (e.g., questions that ask for obscure or controversial facts), logical consistency (multi‑turn scenarios that could lead to contradictions), and cultural accuracy (contexts requiring Turkish‑specific idioms, historical references, or social norms). By focusing on failure modes rather than broad knowledge, TAS efficiently isolates models’ ability to maintain safety under pressure, providing a clear signal for educators to assess deployment risks.

## Foundational Learning
- **Epistemic Resistance**:  
  *Why needed*: Prevents models from presenting false or speculative information as fact, which is essential for maintaining learner trust.  
  *Quick check*: Does the model express appropriate uncertainty or refuse to answer when the query is beyond its knowledge?
- **Logical Consistency**:  
  *Why needed*: Ensures that a model’s outputs do not contain internal contradictions, which could confuse learners and undermine educational integrity.  
  *Quick check*: If the model’s response spans multiple sentences or turns, are all stated facts compatible?
- **Cultural Accuracy**:  
  *Why needed*: Aligns with Turkish heritage language norms and avoids cultural insensitivity, fostering a respectful learning environment.  
  *Quick check*: Are cultural references, idioms, and historical details factually correct and appropriate for the Turkish context?
- **Sycophancy Avoidance**:  
  *Why needed*: Stops the model from blindly agreeing with user statements even when they contain errors, which can perpetuate misconceptions.  
  *Quick check*: When presented with a false premise, does the model gently correct the user instead of echoing the mistake?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Model Loader -> TAS Injector -> Response Generator -> Scorer -> Aggregator

**Critical Path**  
The sequence from injecting a TAS question into the loaded model to obtaining a scored response determines the overall evaluation throughput. Any delay in the Response Generator (e.g., due to model size) directly impacts the time to complete the suite.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Offline vs online*: Offline execution ensures privacy and reproducibility but limits access to external tools or up-to-date knowledge.  
- *Model size*: Larger models offer better safety potential but require more computational resources, affecting cost and scalability.  
- *Scoring granularity*: Automated rubrics enable mass evaluation but may miss nuanced pedagogical failures; human validation increases accuracy but is resource‑intensive.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Large models (>10B) still producing sycophantic answers despite strong overall scores.  
- Sub‑1B models failing multiple safety thresholds, indicating insufficient capacity for safe deployment.  
- Discrepancies between automated and human scores on culturally nuanced items.

**First Experiments**  
1. Baseline evaluation of all 14 models on the full TAS to establish a performance ranking.  
2. Ablation study removing cultural‑accuracy items to isolate the effect of epistemic and logical safety dimensions.  
3. Human expert review of a stratified sample of responses (by model size and score) to validate the automated scoring rubric.

## Limitations
- TAS comprises only 10 edge‑case questions, limiting statistical power and broader applicability.
- The evaluation lacks comparison to existing Turkish benchmarks or human expert judgments, risking overfitting.
- No statistical analysis tests the claim that anomaly resistance is not strictly scale‑dependent, leaving potential confounding factors unaddressed.
