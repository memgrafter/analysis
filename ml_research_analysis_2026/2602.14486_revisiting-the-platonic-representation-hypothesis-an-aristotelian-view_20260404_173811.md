# Revisiting the Platonic Representation Hypothesis: An Aristotelian View

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14486  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14486  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** After correcting for width and depth confounders via permutation-based null calibration, global spectral similarities (e.g., CKA) between neural networks no longer show convergence, while local neighborhood alignments (e.g., mKNN) remain significant across vision-language and video-language models—supporting an Aristotelian view of convergent local structure rather than global geometry.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH)—the idea that neural networks across modalities converge to shared global representation geometries—by identifying two pervasive statistical confounders: **width** (embedding dimension inflates baseline spectral similarity) and **depth** (selecting the best layer-pair across many layers inflates scores). The authors introduce a **permutation-based null-calibration framework** that corrects any similarity metric for these effects, producing calibrated effect sizes with valid p-values. When reapplied to vision-language and video-language model pairs, calibrated global metrics (CKA) lose their apparent convergence trend, whereas local neighborhood metrics (mKNN, cycle-kNN) retain robust alignment. This pattern suggests that neural representations converge to similar **local neighborhood structures** rather than global geometric alignment, motivating an Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis focused on shared topological consistency rather than isometric geometry.

## Method Summary
The core method is a **permutation-based null-calibration** pipeline that transforms any raw similarity score into a calibrated effect size. For a given model pair, all layer-wise representations are extracted. For each similarity metric, an observed statistic (e.g., max CKA across layer pairs) is computed. Then, *K* permutations of sample indices are generated, applying each permutation **identically across all layers** to maintain the depth aggregation structure under the null. The combined distribution of observed + null statistics yields a threshold τ_α (e.g., 95th percentile), defining a zero point. The calibrated score is a max‑preserving rescaling: `s_cal = max((s_obs - τ_α) / (s_max - τ_α), 0)`, with a permutation p‑value also derived. This procedure corrects both width‑induced baseline shifts (by centering on a data‑driven null) and depth‑induced selection inflation (by calibrating the aggregated statistic directly). The framework is model‑ and metric‑agnostic, requiring only exchangeability of sample pairs under the null.

## Key Results
- **Width confounder:** Raw spectral similarities (CKA, RSA) increase spuriously with embedding dimension *d* even under independence, scaling as *O(d/n)*.
- **Depth confounder:** Uncalibrated maximum similarity across *M* layer pairs inflates by roughly √log(*M*) under the null; deeper models show higher raw scores by chance.
- **Calibrated outcome:** After correction, global spectral measures lose their positive scaling trend with model size, while local neighborhood metrics (mKNN, cycle‑kNN) retain significant alignment across all tested model families and modalities (vision‑language, video‑language).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Width confounder in spectral metrics
- **Claim:** Raw spectral similarity metrics (e.g., CKA, CCA) are systematically inflated by the width (embedding dimension) of neural representations, creating a spurious positive baseline even when the true relationship is absent.
- **Mechanism:** In high‑dimensional regimes where the number of features *d* is not negligible compared to the number of samples *n*, the expected squared Frobenius norm of the cross‑covariance matrix ‖𝐶̂‖_F² under independence equals *d_x d_y/(n‑1)*. This yields a null baseline that scales as *O(d/n)*. Since many spectral metrics aggregate such energies (e.g., ‖𝐶̂‖_F² or its singular values), their raw scores increase with *d/n* under the null.
- **Core assumption:** Under *H₀* (no relationship beyond marginals), the data rows are i.i.d. with zero mean and identity covariance, and the centering operator reduces but does not eliminate the high‑dimensional fluctuation effect.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract explicitly identifies “higher embedding dimensions induce spurious baseline similarity”.  
  - Section 4.1 Theorem Proposition 4.1 derives the *d_x d_y/(n‑1)* null expectation.  
  - Corpus: neighboring papers discuss convergence but do not isolate this width confounder.

### Mechanism 2: Depth confounder from layer‑wise selection
- **Claim:** Summarizing model similarity by selecting over many layer pairs (e.g., taking the max CKA across all layer‑pair combinations) inflates reported alignment in a depth‑dependent manner, even if the representations are unrelated.
- **Mechanism:** Given *M = L_A L_B* layer pairs, the expected maximum of *M* similarities under *H₀* grows at least as *μ + Cσ√(log M)* for sub‑Gaussian fluctuations (Hoeffding/EVT). Deeper models have larger *M*, thus higher raw “best‑aligning layer” scores by chance alone.
- **Core assumption:** The null similarity scores for different layer pairs are bounded (or sub‑Gaussian in the right tail) and share a common mean *μ*; dependence between layers only reduces effective comparisons but does not remove the monotonic inflation.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract states that “selection over many layer pairs inflates scores” and calls this the “depth confounder”.  
  - Section 4.2 Proposition D.6 formalizes the √log(*M*) inflation bound.  
  - Corpus: no explicit neighbor addresses this selection effect.

### Mechanism 3: Permutation‑based null‑calibration
- **Claim:** A permutation‑based null‑calibration framework removes both width and depth confounders for any similarity metric, turning raw scores into a calibrated effect size with a valid p‑value.
- **Mechanism:** Under the exchangeability assumption (Assumption 3.1), permuting sample correspondences generates an empirical null distribution of the similarity statistic. The right‑tail threshold τ_α (or τ^agg_α) of this combined (observed+null) distribution defines a principled zero point, and the rescaled excess `[s‑τ_α]_+/(s_max‑τ_α)` yields an effect size in [0,1]. For summaries like the maximum over layers, the same permutation is applied consistently to all layers before computing the aggregate, ensuring the null distribution matches the selected statistic.
- **Core assumption:** The row‑wise pairing of samples is exchangeable under *H₀*; that is, the joint distribution of (*X,Y*) is invariant to random relabeling of the sample correspondences.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract highlights the “permutation‑based null‑calibration framework” and its ability to “transform any similarity metric into a calibrated score with statistical guarantees”.  
  - Section 5 provides full derivations (Corollary 5.1, Proposition D.4) proving super‑uniformity of the p‑value and validity of the calibrated score.  
  - Corpus: permutation tests are standard in neuroimaging, but application to cross‑modal representational similarity with aggregation‑aware correction is novel.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept:** Exchangeability and permutation inference  
  - **Why needed here:** The calibration relies on randomly re‑assigning corresponding rows between two sets; this is valid only if the distribution of paired samples does not depend on which sample index is linked to which.  
  - **Quick check question:** What condition must hold for a permutation test to produce a valid p‑value? (Answer: Under *H₀*, the joint distribution is invariant to permutations of the sample labels.)

- **Concept:** Selection bias and multiple‑testing correction  
  - **Why needed here:** When searching across many layer pairs (depth confounder), the probability of observing a high similarity by chance increases, inflating the false‑positive rate. The aggregation‑aware calibration corrects this by calibrating the final selected statistic rather than each layer pair individually.  
  - **Quick check question:** Why does taking the maximum over many layer pairs require special calibration? (Answer: Because the distribution of the maximum under the null depends on the number of comparisons.)

- **Concept:** Spectral vs. neighborhood representational similarity metrics  
  - **Why needed here:** Metric families capture different aspects: spectral metrics (CKA, CCA) compare global geometry (eigen‑structures), while neighborhood metrics (mKNN, cycle‑kNN) compare local topology. Their null baselines differ in scaling with width (Section 4.1), and they respond differently to calibration (Section 6.3).  
  - **Quick check question:** How does the null expectation of mKNN scale with sample size and *k*? (Answer: *k/(n‑1)*, independent of embedding dimension.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input modules (X, Y) -> Similarity function (e.g., CKA, mKNN) -> Permutation engine (draw K permutations, consistent across layers) -> Aggregation operator (optional, e.g., max) -> Threshold estimator (compute τ_α from combined distribution) -> Calibrated score module (apply max‑preserving rescaling) -> Multiplicity handler (optional FDR correction)
- **Critical path:** Load pair (X,Y) -> Compute observed statistic s_obs (or layer‑wise matrix S) -> For k=1…K: permute Y identically across all layers, compute s^(k) (or S^(k)) -> Collect nulls + observed; compute τ_α (or τ^agg_α) -> Compute calibrated score(s) and permutation p‑value(s) -> Apply FDR correction if many tests.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Scalar vs. aggregation‑aware:** Scalar calibration is O(K); aggregation‑aware requires O(K·L_A·L_B) but can be parallelized.  
  - **Metric‑agnostic vs. analytic:** Permutation works for any metric but needs many permutations for stable thresholds; analytic debiasing is faster but metric‑specific.  
  - **Choice of K:** Larger K improves threshold accuracy but linearly increases runtime; K≈200 is recommended.  
  - **Boundedness assumption:** The max‑preserving calibrated score formula requires a known upper bound s_max (usually 1); for unbounded metrics report unnormalized effect size [s‑τ_α]_+.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Width confound: raw spectral scores increase with *d/n* even under *H₀* (Figure 3, left panels).  
  - Depth confound: uncalibrated max‑aggregates rise with the number of layer pairs (Figure 5, left).  
  - Type‑I inflation: calibrated scores under *H₀* exceed zero substantially when permutation count is too low (K<50).  
  - Violated exchangeability: clustered data without restricted permutations can yield anti‑conservative p‑values; check by comparing marginal distributions across blocks.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Null drift elimination:** Generate independent Gaussian X, Y with varying *d/n*, compute raw and calibrated CKA, RSA, mKNN. Expect: raw scores increase with *d/n* for CKA/RSA; calibrated scores stay at zero.  
  2. **Depth inflation check:** Create two synthetic models with increasing depth *L*, sample independent layers, compute raw max‑CKA. Expect: raw max rises with *L*; aggregation‑aware calibrated max remains flat.  
  3. **Revisit PRH:** Apply calibration to real vision‑language model pairs from Huh et al. (2024). Expect: (a) global metrics (CKA) lose their positive scaling trend after calibration, (b) local metrics (mKNN) retain significant alignment and scaling trend. Verify divergence across multiple model families and video‑language extensions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Why do scaled neural networks exhibit convergent local neighborhood structure but not global spectral alignment after calibration?** The paper presents empirical patterns but no theoretical mechanism; it is unclear whether local convergence is a consequence of optimization, data properties, or architectural inductive biases. Resolving this requires deriving learning dynamics that predict preservation of neighbor rankings or controlled experiments decoupling local from global structure.
- **Is the Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis universally valid across all modalities (e.g., audio, robotics) and for the largest frontier models beyond those tested?** The hypothesis is motivated by vision‑language and video‑language experiments, but its scope is claimed broadly; however, the paper does not test audio, reinforcement learning agents, or state‑of‑the‑art multimodal systems. Without evidence from a wider range of modalities and architectures, the hypothesis remains a tentative generalization.
- **How can permutation null‑calibration be adapted to data with complex dependence (e.g., time‑series, clustered samples) while preserving computational feasibility and statistical guarantees?** The paper’s framework assumes simple row exchangeability; many real‑world datasets violate this (e.g., temporal, spatial, subject clusters), and it is unknown how to perform valid calibration efficiently in such cases. Development of restricted permutation schemes for common dependence structures, with theoretical proof and empirical validation, is needed.

## Limitations
- **Exchangeability:** Permutation test assumes i.i.d. sample pairs; clustered data (e.g., subjects) violate this without block permutations, not addressed.
- **Scalability:** Aggregation‑aware calibration is O(K·L_A·L_B); for very deep models (L>100) and many comparisons, runtime may be prohibitive. Paper uses K=200 but no stress test.
- **Generality:** Empirical results limited to vision‑language/video‑language models on WIT/PVD datasets; unknown for other modalities (audio, robotics) or architectures.
- **Residual confounders:** Width and depth are corrected, but other factors (activation sparsity, normalization, training loss) might still bias metrics.

## Confidence
- **Width confounder (theoretical + empirical):** High  
- **Depth confounder (theoretical + empirical):** High  
- **Permutation calibration validity (under exchangeability):** Medium  
- **Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis (empirical pattern across tested models):** Medium
