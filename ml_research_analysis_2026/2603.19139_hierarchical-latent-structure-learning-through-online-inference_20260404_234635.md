# Hierarchical Latent Structure Learning through Online Inference

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19139
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19139
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: HOLMES learns hierarchical latent structure online, matching flat models in accuracy (84‑100%) while using more compact representations, enabling superior one‑shot transfer (e.g., 62.1% vs 35.5% recall at 5 levels).

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces HOLMES, a particle filter that combines a depth‑decayed sticky nCRP prior with online inference. HOLMES discovers hierarchical latent causes from a sequential stream, balancing structural complexity via decayed concentration and temporal persistence via stickiness. Across compositional tasks (2–5 latent levels), HOLMES achieves outcome prediction comparable to flat models (84–100%) but with lower representational entropy and dramatically better one‑shot transfer (up to 62.1% vs 35.5% recall). In a context‑dependent task with nested temporal structure, HOLMES outperforms flat models in accuracy (80.3% vs 48.1%) and efficiency (within‑state entropy 1.8 vs 2.6). The results demonstrate that hierarchical Bayesian nonparametrics can be scaled to online settings with particle filtering, yielding reusable abstractions that facilitate generalization.

## Method Summary
HOLMES uses a particle filter with P = 200 particles, each maintaining its own latent tree. The prior is a nested Chinese Restaurant Process with depth‑adjusted concentration αℓ = α·exp(−α·ℓ) to penalize deep branching and a stickiness parameter Ω added to self‑transitions at each level, encouraging temporal reuse of the same branch. At each trial, a particle samples a path from the root to a leaf: at level ℓ>0 it may stop with probability 1/(1+αℓ); otherwise it chooses a child using the CRP rule augmented by stickiness. The leaf node holds Beta‑Bernoulli statistics (pseudocount Ω). After computing the likelihood (as particle weight), the leaf’s feature counts are updated and the edge counts along the path are incremented. Particles are resampled each step. The flat baseline uses the same particle filter with a single‑level CRP (concentration α) and no stickiness. Hyperparameters α and Ω are shared; trees have maximum depth 20 and maximum children 20. Inference is purely online, processing each trial once.

## Key Results
- **Compositional tasks (L=2‑5)**: outcome accuracy matched flat models (84–100%) while achieving lower within‑category entropy. One‑shot transfer improved with hierarchy depth (e.g., 5‑level: recall 62.1% vs 35.5%).
- **Context‑dependent task**: HOLMES achieved 80.3% outcome accuracy vs 48.1% for flat; within‑state entropy 1.8 vs 2.6; used fewer clusters per state (15.1 vs 32.3).
- Hierarchical advantage grows systematically with task complexity and is most pronounced at deeper (more specific) latent levels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Depth‑Decayed Sticky nCRP Prior
- **Claim**: The prior induces hierarchical clustering that favors reusable abstractions while controlling structural complexity.
- **Mechanism**: The base concentration α decays exponentially with level ℓ (αℓ = α·e^(−αℓ)), penalizing branching at deeper levels. Stickiness adds Ω to self‑transitions, encouraging persistence of the same branch across time. Together, these yield trees where higher‑level nodes capture widely shared regularities and lower‑level nodes capture fine distinctions without an explicit depth limit.
- **Core assumption**: The environment’s latent structure is hierarchical and persistent; a bounded segmentation budget (via decay) is appropriate.
- **Evidence**: Section 4.2 derives αℓ and stopping probability (Eqs. 10, 12). Figure 2 shows HOLMES learns compact trees even as L increases.
- **Break condition**: If the true hierarchy is shallow, depth‑decay may over‑constrain branching, preventing necessary finer distinctions.

### Mechanism 2: Particle Filter for Online Inference
- **Claim**: Particle filtering enables tractable trial‑by‑trial posterior inference over an expanding, combinatorially large space of hierarchical trees.
- **Mechanism**: A population of particles carries competing tree hypotheses. Each trial, particles sample a path according to the nCRP prior, weight by observation likelihood, and are resampled. This forward‑only process approximates the full Bayesian posterior without revisiting past data.
- **Core assumption**: An approximate, sequential inference scheme can effectively track the posterior over tree‑structured partitions under limited computation.
- **Evidence**: Section 3.2 describes the SMC loop; Fig. 1C illustrates the process. Results show particle filter learns online as well as batch methods (Supplementary Fig. S1).
- **Break condition**: Particle degeneracy if P is too low or likelihood overly peaked; resampling can collapse diversity, leading to suboptimal trees.

### Mechanism 3: Hierarchical Representation Enables One‑Shot Transfer
- **Claim**: The learned tree’s intermediate nodes align with true latent categories, allowing a single exemplar to generalize across observations that share an ancestor.
- **Mechanism**: Observations assigned to the same leaf share all higher‑level ancestors. For a given latent level ℓ, the ancestor node at depth ℓ−1 serves as a category handle. One labeled example at that node’s leaf instantly labels all leaves under that node, enabling transfer across stimuli that differ only in lower‑level features.
- **Core assumption**: The outcome rule depends on a conjunction of latent factors at multiple levels, and the true hierarchy is discoverable from observational data alone.
- **Evidence**: Fig. 3 shows hierarchical models consistently outperformed flat models in one‑shot recall across levels, with advantage increasing at deeper (more derived) levels. Entropy reductions (Fig. 2D,E) confirm more efficient clustering.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bayesian Nonparametrics (CRP)**: Provides a prior over the number of clusters that grows with data. *Why needed*: Avoids pre‑specifying capacity; the model discovers the appropriate number of latent causes. *Quick check*: How does the CRP new‑cluster probability α/(N+α) balance creation vs. reuse as N increases?
- **Sequential Monte Carlo / Particle Filtering**: Approximates posterior online via weighted samples. *Why needed*: Allows trial‑by‑trial updates without batch retraining, matching sequential experience of agents. *Quick check*: What is the role of resampling in preventing degeneracy, and what is a main downside (loss of diversity)?
- **Beta‑Bernoulli Likelihood**: Conjugate prior for binary features; enables tractable online updates. *Why needed*: Supports efficient computation of sufficient statistics (n_f, b_f) at each cluster. *Quick check*: Why are symmetric pseudocounts Ω used for both positive and negative outcomes?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: nCRP Prior Module → Particle Ensemble (tree nodes + counts) → Likelihood Evaluator → Particle Manager (normalize, resample) → Depth/Stop Controller → Canonical Node Registry (optional reuse)
- **Critical path**: For each trial t: ∀ particle p: sample hierarchical path c_t^(p) using current tree & nCRP rules → identify leaf node → compute likelihood w_t^(p) from leaf’s Beta stats → update particle weight → after all particles: normalize weights → resample ensemble → (optionally) update global registry → increment counts along sampled paths.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - α (base concentration): larger α encourages more clusters, risking overfitting and shallower trees due to rapid depth‑decay; smaller α promotes reuse but may underfit.
  - Ω (pseudocount/stickiness): larger Ω increases prior strength and persistence, reducing sensitivity; smaller Ω speeds adaptation but may be noisy.
  - P (particles): more particles reduce variance but increase compute; too few causes degeneracy.
  - Max children/depth: necessary for tractability but may truncate true deep hierarchy.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - High within‑state entropy: model not learning meaningful clusters (α too high or Ω too low).
  - Poor one‑shot transfer: tree depth does not reach relevant level (check α decay or max depth).
  - Particle collapse (low ESS): increase P, adjust resampling, or decrease α.
  - Outcome accuracy at chance in context task (flat fails) → hierarchical tree not instantiating correct context/value nodes; check parameter settings.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Sanity check: 2‑level compositional task; expect HOLMES and flat ≈100% accuracy, lower entropy for HOLMES.
  2. Parameter sweep across L=2‑5; verify accuracy remains matched, entropy advantage persists across >95% of (α,Ω) combinations, and transfer advantage grows with L.
  3. Nested temporal task; confirm HOLMES significantly exceeds flat in outcome accuracy (~80% vs ~48%) and shows lower within‑state entropy/cluster count.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does HOLMES remain effective on real‑world data with continuous, high‑dimensional features and ambiguous hierarchical structure?**  
   Basis: The paper notes its evaluation is limited to synthetic binary tasks. Robustness to realistic complexities is untested. Resolution: Apply HOLMES to real hierarchical datasets (e.g., visual object taxonomies, parsing) and compare performance.
2. **How can HOLMES be extended to non‑stationary environments where latent hierarchy changes over time?**  
   Basis: The model lacks forgetting or pruning mechanisms, which may be needed when structure shifts. Resolution: Incorporate node decay, changepoint detection, or adaptive resampling and test in tasks with shifting contexts or emerging categories.
3. **Is the discrimination‑generalization tradeoff (negative correlation between outcome prediction and transfer) fundamental to hierarchical Bayesian online inference, or can it be mitigated?**  
   Basis: Both architectures showed a negative correlation, suggesting a possible tradeoff. Resolution: Systematically vary priors (depth‑decay schedules, concentration adaption) and inference designs to see if correlation weakens without sacrificing tractability.
4. **Can hierarchical representations from HOLMES support broader transfer beyond one‑shot categorization (e.g., compositional recombination, rule transfer)?**  
   Basis: The discussion situates HOLMES as a foundation for compositional reasoning, but such capabilities were not tested. Resolution: Design tasks requiring recombination of learned subtrees or applying discovered rules to novel contexts; compare HOLMES to flat models and human data.

## Limitations
- HOLMES is evaluated only on synthetic discrete tasks; scaling to high‑dimensional real data remains an open issue.
- The depth‑decay schedule and stickiness parameters require careful tuning; results may degrade if true hierarchy differs from the expected bias.
- Particle filtering remains computationally expensive (P = 200) and may suffer degeneracy without careful implementation.

## Confidence
- **Key outcomes (accuracy, transfer)**: High confidence; presented with clear quantitative results and error bars.
- **Mechanism (depth‑decayed nCRP + particle filtering)**: High confidence; the paper provides full equations, algorithmic details, and ablations.
- **Generalisability to real‑world or non‑stationary settings**: Low confidence; paper identifies these as open questions with no empirical evidence.
