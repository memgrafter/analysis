# OmniCustom: Sync Audio-Video Customization Via Joint Audio-Video Generation Model

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.12304  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12304  
- **Reference Count**: 40  
- **Primary Result**: A tuning‑free DiT‑based framework for synchronized audio‑video customization that achieves state‑of‑the‑art identity preservation, timbre cloning, and video quality on a new 1M‑sample benchmark.  

## Executive Summary
OmniCustom introduces the novel task of **sync audio‑video customization**: generating a video that matches the visual identity of a reference image and an audio track that imitates the timbre of a reference audio, while freely specifying the spoken content via a text prompt. The proposed framework builds atop the OVI joint audio‑video generator, incorporating separate Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA) modules for identity and timbre conditioning, and a contrastive learning objective to enhance fidelity. Trained on the newly collected OmniCustom‑1M dataset (≈1 M single‑person portrait clips), it outperforms existing video customization and text‑to‑speech methods across multiple metrics and also produces contextual background sounds. A user study further validates superior performance in identity consistency, audio‑video synchronization, and overall video quality.

## Method Summary
OmniCustom is a **tuning‑free** diffusion transformer (DiT) model that extends OVI, a twin‑backbone audio‑video generator. Reference image and audio are first encoded into token sequences. During training, low‑rank LoRA adapters are injected into the QKV projections of self‑attention layers: one set for the video branch (identity) and another for the audio branch (timbre). These reference tokens are concatenated with noisy latent tokens, allowing the model to steer the diffusion process toward the desired attributes. Additionally, face embeddings (ArcFace) and timbre embeddings (Naturalspeech 3) are added as global tokens for stronger conditioning. The training objective combines standard flow‑matching losses for video and audio with **contrastive losses** that maximize dissimilarity between the velocity fields produced with and without reference conditions. At inference, the learned LoRA modules guide the reverse flow‑matching sampling (50 steps) to produce synchronized audio‑video outputs. The model is trained on the OmniCustom‑1M dataset, which comprises carefully filtered and transcribed single‑person portrait clips.

## Key Results
- Achieves SOTA identity preservation (FaceSim‑Arc 0.60, FaceSim‑Cur 0.62) and timbre cloning (Speaker‑Sim 0.47) with high video quality (FID 95.57, FVD 440.49).  
- Generates natural audio (FAD 3.44, WER 2.51 %) and contextual background sounds.  
- Outperforms existing video customization and TTS models; user study confirms superior audio‑video sync and overall quality.  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Separate reference identity and audio LoRA modules
- **Claim**: Zero‑shot identity and timbre customization without full fine‑tuning.  
- **Mechanism**: Reference image and audio are encoded into token sequences. In the video branch’s self‑attention, the QKV projections are augmented with LoRA matrices specific to the reference image tokens; the audio branch analogously uses LoRA for reference audio tokens. Reference tokens and base tokens mutually attend, steering the diffusion toward the desired identity/timbre.  
- **Core assumption**: The base OVI model has sufficient capacity to integrate reference signals via low‑rank updates while preserving its pretrained audio‑visual alignment.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions “separate reference identity and audio LoRA modules”; Section 4.2.2 details Reference LoRA (Eqs. 5‑6).  
- **Break condition**: Low LoRA rank, lack of identity cues (profile face, noisy audio) or excessive contrastive loss weight can cause conditioning failure.

### Mechanism 2: Contrastive learning objective
- **Claim**: Enhances identity and timbre fidelity by forcing the model to distinguish reference‑conditioned from non‑reference outputs.  
- **Mechanism**: Alongside flow‑matching loss (Eq. 7), contrastive losses \(L_I^{CL}\) and \(L_A^{CL}\) (Eq. 8) push apart the velocity fields predicted with reference conditions (positive) and without reference (negative). This regularization encourages reliance on reference signals.  
- **Core assumption**: Maximizing dissimilarity between reference and non‑reference flows does not degrade generation quality, and the non‑reference condition serves as an effective negative sample.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions “contrastive learning objective added to the flow‑matching loss”; Section 4.2.3 presents the contrastive losses and total loss (Eq. 9).  
- **Break condition**: Improper contrastive weight (\(\lambda\)) may overpower the flow loss (mode collapse) or be too weak; a poorly representative negative condition can mislead training.

### Mechanism 3: Large‑scale OmniCustom‑1M dataset
- **Claim**: The dataset is essential for learning robust identity‑timbre correlations and generalizing to diverse contexts.  
- **Mechanism**: The dataset (~1 M single‑person portrait clips) is extracted from SpeakerVid‑5M and filtered by sync confidence, aesthetics, and clip length. For each clip, a reference image (random frame) and reference audio (first 4 s) are paired with a training segment (last 5 s) of different speech content. Audio captions and text prompts are added, forcing the model to ignore speech content while preserving identity/timbre.  
- **Core assumption**: The dataset is representative of real‑world human speech and appearance, and fine‑tuning OVI on it does not cause catastrophic forgetting.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states “trained on the newly constructed 1M‑sample OmniCustom‑1M dataset”; Section 5 details sources, filtering, and statistics.  
- **Break condition**: Dataset biases (demographic, accent, quality) may lead to underperformance on unseen groups; low‑quality pairs could inject noise. The 5‑second duration and English‑only scope limit broader applicability.

## Foundational Learning

  Why needed: OmniCustom inherits OVI’s transformer‑based diffusion backbone; DiT replaces UNet convolutions with attention layers for efficient spatiotemporal modeling.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Reference encoders (image VAE, audio 1D VAE) → Reference LoRA modules (in QKV projections of video/audio branches) → Fusion blocks (parallel video/audio transformers with self‑attention, text cross‑attention, paired cross‑attention) → Additional embeddings (face/timbre) → Loss modules (flow‑matching + contrastive) → Decoders (video VAE, audio VAE + vocoder)

### Critical Path
1. Encode reference image & audio into token sequences (timestep 0).  
2. Sample noise and corrupt clean video/audio latents (Eq. 1).  
3. Concatenate reference tokens with noised latents (and optional embedding tokens).  
4. Process through fusion blocks: self‑attention applies LoRA to fuse reference info; text cross‑attention conditions on prompt; paired cross‑attention synchronizes modalities.  
5. Predict velocity fields for video and audio.  
6. Compute flow‑matching loss against target (\(z_1 - z_0\)). In parallel, run a no‑reference version (reference masked, embeddings zeroed) to compute contrastive loss.  
7. Back‑propagate total loss (Eq. 9) to update LoRA parameters (and possibly linear layers for embeddings); base model frozen.  
8. At inference, use learned LoRA and embeddings to guide reverse sampling (Eq. 3) with 50 steps, denoising to generate synchronized audio‑video.

### Design Tradeoffs
- LoRA rank: higher rank strengthens adaptation but adds parameters and overfitting risk (paper uses rank 128).  
- Contrastive loss weights (\(\lambda_I,\lambda_A\)): balance fidelity vs. sample quality; require careful tuning.  
- Face/timbre embeddings: provide strong signal but may restrict pose/microphone variations.  
- Tuning‑free vs. tuning‑based: OmniCustom avoids per‑identity fine‑tuning but relies on expressive LoRA; tuning‑based methods may achieve higher fidelity at the cost of flexibility.  
- Base model constraints: OVI limits video length to 5 s and language to English; adopting a more flexible base could broaden scope but might affect synchronization.

### Failure Signatures
- Low FaceSim / high FID → identity degradation (blurry or mismatched faces).  
- Low Speaker‑Sim / high FAD → poor timbre cloning or audio quality.  
- High LSE‑D / low LSE‑C → lip‑speech misalignment.  
- Missing background sounds → model may ignore environmental context.  
- Cross‑gender timbre drop → implicit gender bias in reference mapping.  
- Training instability (loss spikes) → often from excessive contrastive weight or poor gradient scaling between branches.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Qualitative comparison with SOTA video customization (ID‑Animator, ConsisID, Phantom, V ACE) and audio‑driven methods (HunyuanCustom, Humo) on identity, prompt following, and video quality (Section 6.2, Fig. 3, App‑Fig. 4‑5).  
2. Quantitative evaluation on a constructed 100‑sample benchmark using FaceSim, FID, FVD for video and Speaker‑Sim, FAD, WER for audio; also reports background sound capability (Section 6.3, Table 2).  
3. Ablation study isolating contributions of face/timbre embeddings and contrastive learning (Baseline vs. +Embeddings vs. +Contrastive) showing improvements in identity/timbre metrics (Section 6.4, Table 2). A user study (Section 6.5) further validates audio‑video sync and quality.  

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What specific constraints in the base OVI model prevent OmniCustom from supporting non‑English languages, and what architectural or data modifications would be necessary to enable multilingual sync audio‑video customization?  
- **Basis in paper**: Conclusion explicitly states OmniCustom “currently supports only English” due to constraints of the base joint audio‑video generation model.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not diagnose whether the limitation stems from OVI’s English‑only training data, text‑to‑speech components, or architectural assumptions about linguistic features.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic evaluation of OVI models trained on multilingual datasets, ablations replacing text‑conditioning modules with multilingual alternatives, and cross‑linguistic performance profiling across diverse phonetic inventories.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Is the 5‑second video duration limitation fundamental to the DiT‑based architecture or merely a consequence of training data characteristics and computational constraints?  
- **Basis in paper**: Conclusion acknowledges “video generation of 5‑second duration” as a current limitation.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not investigate whether this limit arises from DiT attention span, temporal receptive field, maximum clip length in training data, or GPU memory constraints.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Training OmniCustom with varying temporal window sizes, measuring attention patterns and coherence over extended sequences, and testing strategies like autoregressive generation or video looping.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Why does timbre transfer accuracy degrade in cross‑gender scenarios, and does this reflect dataset biases or inherent limitations in the disentanglement of identity and timbre representations?  
- **Basis in paper**: Appendix B demonstrates cross‑gender transfer with reduced Speaker‑Sim scores and speculates the model encodes “priors regarding the gender of reference identity and its corresponding timbre range.”  
- **Why unresolved**: The speculation lacks empirical validation—the degradation could stem from training data imbalances, insufficient capacity in the separate LoRA modules to decouple gender, or fundamental acoustic mismatches. No analysis quantifies gender‑related biases.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Creating gender‑balanced evaluation sets, visualizing latent spaces of identity and timbre embeddings stratified by gender, controlled experiments with systematically varied gender pairs, and measuring gender information leakage across pathways.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What causes OmniCustom’s failure to preserve identity when using profile face reference images, and can this be resolved through data augmentation, better face embeddings, or pose‑aware conditioning?  
- **Basis in paper**: Appendix D.2 shows clear failures with profile faces and suggests “employing multi‑view reference images during training” as a solution without testing it.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not analyze whether the failure is due to ArcFace’s reduced accuracy on profile angles, scarcity of profile faces in OmniCustom‑1M, or attention bias toward frontal faces. The proposed remedy remains hypothetical.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Quantifying pose distribution in the training dataset, benchmarking face embedding quality across pose angles, ablating with pose‑augmented reference images, and incorporating explicit pose estimation as additional conditioning.

## Limitations
- OVI base model is unreleased, forcing reimplementation from incomplete specifications.  
- OmniCustom‑1M construction details (SyncNet thresholds, aesthetics filter) are omitted, risking hidden biases and reproducibility issues.  
- Ambiguities in reference token injection (ordering, timestep) and contrastive negative sampling hinder faithful replication.

- LoRA‑based reference conditioning: **Medium**  
- Contrastive learning objective: **Medium**  
- Dataset scale & quality: **Medium‑Low**
