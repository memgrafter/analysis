# Extreme-value forest fire prediction A study of the Loss Function in an Ordinality Scheme

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03327
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03327
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Ordinal supervision, particularly the proposed Weighted Kappa Loss (WKLOSS), improves prediction of extreme wildfire severity by >0.1 IoU on the rarest classes while maintaining calibration.

## Executive Summary
The study tackles extreme wildfire severity prediction in an operational French forecasting context with highly imbalanced data. It introduces an ordinal classification scheme (5-level risk) and compares several ordinal-aware loss functions, including the proposed TDeGPD probabilistic loss and Weighted Kappa Loss (WKLOSS), against standard cross-entropy across multiple neural architectures. WKLOSS achieves the best overall performance, delivering a substantial IoU gain (>0.1) on the most extreme severity classes (3 and 4) and preserving calibration. Nevertheless, accuracy on the rarest events remains limited due to their extremely low representation.

## Method Summary
The authors reconstruct a spatiotemporal dataset (2017–2023) from BDIFF and other sources, aggregating variables to 2 km rasters and then by French departments. They generate ordinal targets by clustering fire occurrence and burned area (square-root transformed) into five ordered classes using K-means. The data are split by year: training (2017–2021), validation (2022), test (2023). Six model architectures (MLP, GRU, LSTM, DilatedCNN, GraphCastGRU, GraphCastGRUGAT) are trained with various loss functions: standard cross-entropy (CE), Weighted Kappa Loss (WKLOSS), Generalized Wasserstein Dice Loss (GWDL), all-threshold binary CE (BCE), MCEWK composite, and the proposed TDeGPD (truncated discrete eGPD). Class imbalance is mitigated by undersampling class 0 to proportions ranging from 15% to 100% and selecting the proportion that maximizes validation extreme IoU. Performance is evaluated using global binary F1, macro‑averaged IoU, ordinal AUC, calibration error, and extreme‑class (3–4) binary metrics; results are averaged over multiple seeds.

## Key Results
- Ordinal loss functions substantially improve extreme‑class performance; WKLOSS yields >+0.1 IoU gain on classes 3 and 4 compared to CE.
- TDeGPD captures tail behavior but underperforms overall and exhibits poor calibration.
- Despite gains, extreme‑class recall remains low due to extremely few training samples (e.g., ~12 class‑4 test cases).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Ordinal-aware loss (WKLOSS)  
- **Claim:** Penalizing misclassifications in proportion to their ordinal distance encourages “near‑miss” predictions for rare high‑severity fires.  
- **Mechanism:** WKLOSS uses a weighted confusion matrix where penalties scale with class distance (e.g., ωᵢⱼ = |i−j|/(J−1) for linear weighting). Gradients thus align with the ordered severity levels.  
- **Core assumption:** The five risk classes are meaningfully ordered, and errors between adjacent classes are less harmful than those between distant classes.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4.2; paper reports >+0.1 IoU gain on extreme classes using WKLOSS.  
- **Break condition:** If classes are not truly ordered or extreme‑class samples are too scarce for stable penalty estimation.

### TDeGPD probabilistic loss  
- **Claim:** Explicitly modeling the tail of the strictly‑positive fire severity via an exponentiated Generalized Pareto Distribution (eGPD) allocates probability mass to extreme classes.  
- **Mechanism:** The network predicts σ (scale), κ (exponent), and ξ (shape). For y≥0, the eGPD CDF F⁺(y) = [H(y;σ,ξ)]^κ is discretized and truncated to {0,…,4}. Training minimizes the negative log‑likelihood of this truncated discrete distribution.  
- **Core assumption:** Strictly positive burned areas follow an eGPD in the tail, and discrete truncation adequately represents the ordinal scale.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.6 and Section 6; TDeGPD can identify samples from classes 3–4 but yields lower F1 and IoU overall.  
- **Break condition:** If the eGPD tail assumption fails or truncation discards relevant information beyond class 4.

### Class‑0 undersampling  
- **Claim:** Reducing the overwhelming proportion of class 0 samples prevents the model from being biased toward the majority class and improves sensitivity to rare fires.  
- **Mechanism:** The training set is undersampled so that class 0 proportion varies between 15% and 100%; the optimal proportion is chosen based on validation extreme IoU.  
- **Core assumption:** The dataset naturally contains many non‑fire samples, which dominate learning; balancing the class distribution helps the model learn decision boundaries for minority classes.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3; the paper reports the selection procedure and its effect on performance.  
- **Break condition:** If undersampling discards valuable spatial/temporal context or extreme classes remain too sparse even after resampling.

## Foundational Learning
- **Ordinal vs. Nominal Classification:** Treating severity levels as ordered utilizes the monotonic relationship between classes; otherwise, a class‑3 vs. class‑4 error is treated equivalently to class‑0 vs. class‑4, wasting the inherent structure. Quick check: Why is predicting “High” (3) instead of “Extreme” (4) less harmful than predicting “No fire” (0) instead of “Extreme” in operational forecasting?
- **Extreme Value Theory (Generalized Pareto Distribution):** GPD models the tail of a distribution separately from the bulk, as standard distributions fail to capture rare, high‑intensity events. Quick check: What does a positive shape parameter ξ > 0 in the GPD imply about the tail heaviness of burned‑area data?
- **Class Imbalance in Hazard Forecasting:** Wildfire data are dominated by zero‑fire days; models biased toward the majority class will miss rare but critical extreme fires. Quick check: How might oversampling the minority extreme classes (e.g., via SMOTE) risk overfitting given only a dozen class‑4 test samples?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data -> Preprocessing (aggregation, K‑means clustering) -> Feature set -> Model (MLP/GRU/LSTM/DilatedCNN/GraphCastGRU/GraphCastGRUGAT) -> Loss layer (CE/WKLOSS/MCEWK/BCE/GWDL/TDeGPD) -> Metrics (binary F1, macro IoU, ordinal AUC, ECE, extreme‑class metrics)

**Critical path:**  
1. Reconstruct the spatiotemporal dataset (2017–2023) from BDIFF and auxiliary sources.  
2. Generate ordinal targets via department‑wise K‑means (5 classes) on fire occurrence and sqrt(burned area).  
3. Apply temporal split (train:2017‑2021, val:2022, test:2023) and undersampling of class 0 to balance.  
4. Train each architecture with each loss, tuning the class‑0 proportion on validation extreme IoU.  
5. Evaluate on test set with global and extreme metrics, and assess calibration.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Loss choice:* WKLOSS offers simplicity and strong extreme‑class gains; TDeGPD provides probabilistic tail modeling but suffers from calibration and implementation complexity.  
- *Model complexity:* Graph‑based models capture spatial dependencies but may already encode ordinality, reducing benefit from ordinal losses; simpler recurrent models benefit more.  
- *Data vs. model:* With extreme scarcity of high‑severity samples (<15 total), high‑capacity models risk overfitting; simpler architectures may generalize better.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Calibration plots deviate from diagonal for classes 2‑4, indicating unreliable probabilities.  
- Confusion matrices show low true‑class recall for extreme classes despite high precision.  
- TDeGPD yields extreme overconfidence (high predicted probability → low observed frequency) for rare classes.  
- Training loss plateaus or NaN values in TDeGPD CDF.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Baseline:** Train all architectures with standard CE; record global and extreme IoU to establish a performance ceiling.  
2. **Ordinal loss benchmark:** Replace CE with WKLOSS (the paper’s best) and MCEWK; compare extreme‑class IoU and calibration improvements.  
3. **Probabilistic loss test:** Implement TDeGPD; inspect whether it increases predicted probabilities for extreme classes and assess calibration stability versus WKLOSS.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Seasonal dynamics and uncertainty:** How can seasonal cycles and label uncertainty be integrated into training to improve reliability of extreme‑event prediction?  
   *Basis:* Authors mention incorporating seasonality and uncertainty as future work. Unresolved because current models ignore temporal cycles and treat labels as certain. Resolving would require empirical gains from methods that encode seasonal patterns (e.g., cyclical embeddings) and probabilistic labels, evaluated on out‑of‑season data.

2. **Data augmentation for rare classes:** What augmentation or sampling techniques can boost representation of the rarest severity classes without overfitting, given their extremely low counts?  
   *Basis:* Discussion notes that SMOTE could overfit due to only ~12 class‑4 test samples. Unresolved because standard oversampling risks memorization. Resolving would demand novel spatio‑temporal augmentation (e.g., conditional generative models) and demonstration of improved extreme‑class metrics without majority‑class degradation.

3. **Spatial generalization:** Why do ordinal losses yield only modest improvements in spatial generalization across departments, and what architectural/training modifications could enhance spatial robustness?  
   *Basis:* Area‑normalized scores remain low (e.g., F1≈0.16); ordinal losses stabilize but do not solve regional variability. Unresolved because models likely overfit to departmental patterns; solutions might involve hierarchical or multi‑task learning with department‑specific parameters or domain adaptation.

4. **Calibration of minority classes:** How can we improve probability calibration for minority severity classes while preserving ordinal consistency?  
   *Basis:* Calibration analysis shows poor reliability for classes 2‑4, persisting across losses. Unresolved because imbalance and ordinal structure hinder calibrated probability estimation. Resolving would require calibration techniques respecting ordinality (e.g., temperature scaling, Dirichlet calibration) with low ECE on tail classes and maintained or improved IoU.

## Limitations
- Dataset contains only ~12 extreme (class 4) test samples, causing high variance in extreme‑class metrics and limiting statistical confidence.
- Critical implementation details are missing: exact train/val/test years (2022–2024 overlap suggests error), optimal class‑0 undersampling proportions, GWDL background class, TDeGPD specifics (activations, ε, κ learning), training hyperparameters, and ordinal AUC formula.
- The paper is the sole source of evidence; no independent verification or external corpus supports the mechanisms.

## Confidence
- *Ordinal losses (WKLOSS) boost extreme IoU:* Medium (depends on undisclosed undersampling and scarce extremes).  
- *TDeGPD models tail effectively:* Medium (theoretically sound but inferior metrics and calibration issues; implementation unclear).  
- *Undersampling class 0 improves balance:* Medium (standard approach, but optimal proportion unreported; risk of discarding context).
