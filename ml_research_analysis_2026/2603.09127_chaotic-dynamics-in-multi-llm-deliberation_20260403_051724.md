# Chaotic Dynamics in Multi-LLM Deliberation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09127
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09127
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Multi-LLM committee deliberation exhibits positive empirical Lyapunov exponents (λ̂ > 0) even at temperature zero, with instability driven by role differentiation and model heterogeneity, and partially mitigated by structured roles in mixed-model settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the stability of five-agent LLM committees deliberating on policy scenarios. Under deterministic (temperature-zero) conditions, repeated executions of the same committee often produce diverging preference trajectories, a symptom of chaotic dynamics. The authors model the process as a random dynamical system and quantify instability using an empirical Lyapunov exponent (λ̂) derived from the exponential divergence of mean preference paths across replicates. A factorial experiment varying role assignments and model homogeneity reveals two independent instability amplifiers: assigning differentiated roles increases λ̂ in homogeneous committees, while mixing model families without roles yields the highest instability. Notably, adding roles to mixed-model committees non-additively reduces λ̂ relative to mixed no-role groups. Ablations identify the Chair role as the strongest single amplifier, and shortening the argument memory window attenuates divergence. The findings suggest that instability is an inherent design property of multi-LLM governance protocols, not merely sampling noise, necessitating stability audits for reliable deployment.

## Method Summary
The study employs a deterministic "windowed-summary" deliberation protocol across 12 policy scenarios. Five agents (each assigned a role or none) receive a scenario prompt, a sliding window of the last *k* arguments, and a committee state table. Each round, agents output a brief argument plus a structured `STATE` line containing their preference vector, confidence, and tags. A parser extracts preferences; one auto-repair attempt is allowed on failure. After 20 rounds, private ballots are aggregated by a clerk agent to produce a final decision. The primary metric is the empirical Lyapunov exponent λ̂, computed as the slope of log(mean pairwise L2 distance *D(t)* between replicate committee mean preference trajectories) over rounds 3–20. A 2×2 factorial design is implemented: roles (NoRoles/Roles) × model composition (uniform/mixed models). For mixed-model committees, specific model families (GPT-4.1, Claude, Gemini, Grok) are mapped to roles. Each condition is replicated *R*=20 times from identical initial prompts at temperature 0.

## Key Results
- Role assignment in homogeneous committees increases λ̂ from 0.0221 (no-roles) to 0.0541 (roles).
- Model heterogeneity without roles yields the highest instability (λ̂ = 0.0947).
- Combined mixed+roles condition shows a non-additive interaction: λ̂ = 0.0519, lower than mixed+no-role.
- Chair-role ablation most strongly reduces λ̂; shortening memory window (*k*) further attenuates divergence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Role Differentiation as Instability Amplifier
- **Claim:** Assigning differentiated institutional roles (e.g., Chair, Welfare, Rights) to homogeneous model agents increases trajectory divergence (λ̂) relative to a no-role baseline, even at T=0.
- **Mechanism:** Role mandates introduce divergent optimization targets (e.g., Welfare prioritizes cost-benefit, Rights defends deontological constraints). These create systematic disagreements that, through iterative feedback, amplify small initial perturbations into macroscopic trajectory splits.
- **Core assumption:** Role labels alter agent behavior beyond superficial framing—they induce persistent strategic or ideological divergence in preference updating.
- **Evidence anchors:** Role differentiation in homogeneous committees increases λ̂ from 0.0221 to 0.0541; role-specific system prompts enforce distinct mandates.
- **Break condition:** If role mandates are removed (ablation) and λ̂ decreases substantially, as seen in Chair ablation reducing λ̂ most strongly.

### Mechanism 2: Model Heterogeneity Without Roles
- **Claim:** Mixing model families (GPT-4.1, Claude, Gemini, Grok) in a no-role committee yields the highest observed instability (λ̂ = 0.0947), exceeding homogeneous-role committees.
- **Mechanism:** Different foundation models possess inherent response distribution biases and reasoning patterns. In the absence of role-guided convergence pressure, these diverse "agent philosophies" interact unpredictably, causing greater divergence.
- **Core assumption:** Model families have stable, intrinsic behavioral differences that manifest as divergent preference trajectories when unconstrained by roles.
- **Evidence anchors:** Mixed-model no-role condition yields λ̂ = 0.0947; mixed-model lineup specified.
- **Break condition:** Adding roles to mixed-model committees reduces λ̂ (0.0519 vs. 0.0947), implying roles impose convergent structure that suppresses heterogeneity-driven chaos.

### Mechanism 3: Chair as Primary Amplifier & Interaction Modulation
- **Claim:** The Chair role is the strongest single amplifier of divergence, and role structure can *mitigate* heterogeneity-induced instability via non-additive interaction.
- **Mechanism:** The Chair's mandate to synthesize views forces it to dynamically adjust preferences based on group discourse, acting as a chaotic "feedback integrator." However, when roles are present in mixed-model committees, role-specific mandates create convergent cross-pressures that partially counteract model heterogeneity.
- **Core assumption:** The Chair's synthesis behavior is inherently more sensitive to initial conditions than other roles, and role structures can create stabilizing cross-cutting constraints.
- **Evidence anchors:** Chair-role ablation reduces λ̂ most strongly; mixed+roles (λ̂=0.0519) < mixed+no-role (0.0947).
- **Break condition:** Chair ablation or extremely limited memory (k=1) suppresses λ̂.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Empirical Lyapunov Exponent (λ̂)**
  - *Why needed:* Quantifies inter-run sensitivity: positive λ̂ means exponential divergence of preference trajectories, the paper's core instability metric.
  - *Quick check:* If two runs start with nearly identical states, how does pairwise distance *D(t)* grow over rounds? A linear fit to log *D(t)* yields λ̂.

- **Concept: Random Dynamical Systems**
  - *Why needed:* The paper models multi-LLM committees as systems with deterministic protocol but stochastic elements (API non-determinism), requiring tools from dynamical systems theory.
  - *Quick check:* What distinguishes a random dynamical system from a purely stochastic process? How does the protocol act as the deterministic skeleton?

- **Concept: Windowed-Summary Deliberation Protocol**
  - *Why needed:* The experimental architecture; defines how agents exchange arguments and update states. Memory depth (*k*) is a key intervention lever.
  - *Quick check:* How does the sliding transcript window couple agent decisions across rounds? What happens when *k*=1 vs. *k*=15?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Scenario Packet + System Prompt + Sliding Window + Committee State -> Agent (role/model) -> Argument + STATE Line -> Parser -> Extracted Preferences -> State Tracker (p_t, D(t)) -> After 20 Rounds -> Clerk (aggregates private ballots) -> Final Decision. Replicate Controller orchestrates *R* independent runs.

- **Critical path:**
  1. Initialize committee with roles/models and scenario.
  2. For each of 20 rounds: each agent (fixed order) reads context (window + state + prompt), generates argument + STATE; parser extracts preferences; state tracker updates mean trajectory; window slides.
  3. After 20 rounds, agents cast private ballots; clerk aggregates to final decision.
  4. Repeat for *R* replicates; compute λ̂ from pairwise distance trajectories.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Roles vs. no-roles:** Roles increase instability but may improve deliberative quality (untested).
  - **Memory depth (*k*):** Larger *k* preserves more context, amplifying feedback loops (higher λ̂); smaller *k* attenuates divergence but may lose coherence.
  - **Homogeneous vs. mixed models:** Mixed models increase baseline instability; roles can partially suppress this.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - High λ̂ (>0.05 in HL-01 conditions) indicates chaotic amplification.
  - Low majority formation rate/time-to-majority shows consensus delay.
  - High "flip rate" (non-modal final decisions) signals outcome unpredictability.
  - Parse failures >2% suggest protocol fragility.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate core 2×2 matrix: run all four conditions across 12 scenarios at T=0, R=20, compute λ̂. Verifies two-route pattern and interaction.
  2. Role ablation: for each role, run full committee with that role's mandate removed. Compare λ̂ to full-roles baseline. Identifies dominant amplifiers (expect Chair).
  3. Memory window intervention: run baseline (*k*=15) vs. reduced memory (*k*=3, *k*=1). Test if λ̂ decreases, supporting feedback-memory amplification pathway.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does λ̂ correlate with decision quality (accuracy, calibration, harm)?
- Can interventions that lower λ̂ preserve decision quality?
- Do instability patterns generalize to other committee sizes, task domains, and protocols?
- What explains the non-additive interaction (mixed+roles less unstable than mixed+no-role)?

## Limitations
- Scenario prompts and committee state table not fully described; replication relies on reconstructions.
- API non-determinism at T=0 may contribute to λ̂; unclear how much is inherent to protocol.
- No assessment of decision quality, fairness, or bias; stability-utility trade-offs unexplored.

## Confidence
- Chaotic dynamics exist (λ̂>0 at T=0): **High**
- Two instability routes (roles, model heterogeneity) and interaction: **Medium**
- Chair role amplifies and memory mitigates: **Medium**
