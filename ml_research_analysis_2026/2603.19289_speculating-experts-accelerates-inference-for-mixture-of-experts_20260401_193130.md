# Speculating Experts Accelerates Inference for Mixture-of-Experts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19289  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19289  
- **Reference count:** 5  
- **Primary result:** Prefetching future experts based on current-layer internal representations reduces time per output token by up to 14% and, with a lightweight neural estimator, recovers accuracy on reasoning benchmarks for high-drift MoE models.

## Executive Summary
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models often offload expert weights to CPU memory to fit within GPU constraints, but this creates a severe CPU‑GPU data transfer bottleneck during inference. This work proposes an expert prefetching scheme that predicts the next layer’s expert selections from the current layer’s “quasi‑hidden state,” asynchronously copies those weights, and speculatively executes the prefetched experts. This overlap reduces the time per output token (TPOT) by up to 14% across multiple MoE architectures. For models with high representational drift in early layers (e.g., Qwen3‑30B‑A3B), the basic predictor suffers low hit rates, leading to accuracy loss. To mitigate this, a lightweight neural estimator trained on a small number of tokens improves prediction accuracy, restoring downstream task performance on benchmarks like GSM8k and HumanEval while preserving most of the speedup.

## Method Summary
Implemented in the YALIS system, the method constructs a quasi‑hidden state \( q_l = \text{LN}(d_l + r_l) \) from the current layer’s normalized residual stream \( r_l \) and a precomputed default vector \( d_l \). This state is fed to an expert predictor: either the next layer’s router (router‑PF) or a small neural network estimator (Est‑PF). The predictor outputs the indices and gating weights of the next layer’s experts. The system then asynchronously copies the corresponding expert weights from CPU to GPU using a double‑buffered cache, overlapping the copy with the current layer’s MoE computation. At the start of layer \( l+1 \), the prefetched experts are already resident and are executed speculatively (with the predicted gating) without verifying against the router’s true selection. For models where \( q_l \) is a poor predictor (high drift), the estimator is applied selectively (Hybrid‑PF) to boost hit rates and maintain accuracy.

## Key Results
- TPOT reduced by **up to 14%** compared to on‑demand loading on an A6000 GPU across multiple MoE models and long contexts.
- Speculative execution with router‑PF preserves accuracy on low‑drift models (e.g., GPT‑OSS series) but causes noticeable degradation on high‑drift models like Qwen3‑30B‑A3B.
- A lightweight neural estimator, trained on only 4–5 M tokens, increases early‑layer hit rates by ~25% and recovers baseline accuracy on GSM8k and HumanEval for Qwen3‑30B‑A3B while retaining most of the TPOT improvement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Prefetching future experts by predicting router selections from the current layer's internal representations (quasi‑hidden state) overlaps CPU‑GPU data transfers with GPU computation, reducing TPOT.
- **Mechanism:** The system constructs a "quasi‑hidden state" \( q_l \) from the current layer's normalized residual stream \( r_l \) and the layer‑level default vector \( d_l \). This state is used to predict the experts \( E_{l+1} \) and gating weights \( G_{l+1} \) for the next layer. The prediction triggers an asynchronous, non‑blocking CPU‑to‑GPU copy of the predicted experts' weights. This copy stream runs in parallel with the compute stream executing the current layer's MoE operation. By the time layer \( l+1 \) starts, its experts are already resident on the GPU, avoiding a synchronous transfer on the critical path.
- **Core assumption:** The internal representations at layer \( l \) contain a sufficiently predictive signal of the router's selection at layer \( l+1 \).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "We propose an expert prefetching scheme that leverages currently computed internal model representations to speculate future experts, enabling memory transfers to overlap with computation."  
  [section 3.2] Defines \( q_l = \text{LN}_{l+1}(d_l + r_l) \) and shows in Figure 3 that it has higher cosine similarity to the true next‑layer router input \( s_{l+1} \) than the current router input \( s_l \) for GPT‑OSS models.  
  [section 5.1] Algorithm 1 and Equation 1 formalize the overlap and the TPOT improvement bound \( \Delta T = \sum \min(t_{\text{copy}}(E_{l+1}), t_{\text{compute},l}) \).
- **Break condition:** If the prediction error (hit rate) is too low, the speculative prefetch may load incorrect experts, and the correct experts will still need to be fetched on‑demand later, reducing overlap gains. This is evident for early layers in Qwen3‑30B‑A3B.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Executing the prefetched experts speculatively (using their predicted gating weights) instead of discarding them upon a miss preserves downstream task accuracy and fully eliminates the re‑fetch penalty for true positives.
- **Mechanism:** When the predicted experts for layer \( l+1 \) are resident on the GPU at the start of that layer's computation, the system directly uses them with their predicted mixing weights. Crucially, it does *not* check if they match the router's ground‑truth selection. For models like GPT‑OSS, the dominant experts (high routing weight) are often predicted correctly (Figure 5), so using the prefetched set yields nearly identical outputs to the baseline. This means no synchronous transfer for the true experts is ever needed, maximizing compute‑memory overlap.
- **Core assumption:** The MoE activation with a partially correct expert set (high hit rate for top‑k experts) produces logits sufficiently close to the ground‑truth activation for the task, due to the low contribution mass of lower‑ranked experts.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "We also demonstrate that executing prefetched experts generally maintains downstream task accuracy, thus preserving more effective compute‑memory overlap by eliminating the need to re‑fetch true router‑selected experts."  
  [section 4.1] Table 1 shows that "Router‑PF" (using quasi‑hidden state predictions and executing them) has minimal accuracy loss on GPT‑OSS models (e.g., GPT‑OSS‑120B average 0.857 vs. 0.849 baseline) but significant loss on Qwen3‑30B‑A3B (0.641 vs. 0.817).  
  [section 4.1] Figure 5 shows high hit rates for the highest‑ranked experts across most layers for GPT‑OSS, supporting the assumption that dominant expert preservation maintains accuracy.
- **Break condition:** For architectures with high inter‑layer representational drift in early layers (e.g., Qwen3‑30B‑A3B), the top‑k expert hit rate drops significantly (Figure 4, 5). Speculative execution then causes a measurable accuracy degradation on sensitive tasks (GSM8k, AIME in Table 1).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A lightweight neural estimator, trained via distillation on a small number of tokens, can improve expert prediction hit rates in high‑drift layers (e.g., early layers of Qwen3‑30B‑A3B), mitigating the accuracy loss from speculative execution while maintaining performance benefits.
- **Mechanism:** A shallow feed‑forward network (Appendix A, Figure 11) is trained to map the quasi‑hidden state \( q_l \) directly to the next layer's router logits. It is applied selectively (Hybrid‑PF) or universally (Est‑PF). By learning a more robust mapping than the fixed mathematical relation \( q_l = \text{LN}(d_l + r_l) \), it corrects for representational drift patterns that the quasi‑hidden state alone cannot capture. This increases the hit rate specifically where the baseline prefetcher is weak (Figure 10), leading to more accurate speculative executions and recovered task accuracy (Table 1, Qwen3‑30B‑A3B Hybrid‑PF).
- **Core assumption:** The representational drift causing low prefetch hit rates is learnable from a small, representative set of input tokens and generalizes to the full decoding distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "For MoEs where speculative execution alone yields suboptimal accuracy, we further examine lightweight estimators that improve expert prediction hit rates, thereby reducing performance degradation."  
  [section 6.2] Shows the estimator reaches ~90% average hit rate for Qwen3‑30B‑A3B with only 4 M training tokens (Figure 9) and improves per‑layer hit rate by up to ~25% in early layers (Figure 10).  
  [section 6.3] Hybrid‑PF (estimator only on low‑hit‑rate layers) recovers most of the baseline accuracy for Qwen3‑30B‑A3B on GSM8k and other benchmarks (Table 1).
- **Break condition:** The estimator requires a separate, small training phase. Its effectiveness is bounded by the capacity of its lightweight architecture and the representativeness of its training data. If drift patterns are highly non‑stationary or the estimator is too shallow, gains may be limited.

## Foundational Learning
- **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Architecture.**  
  *Why needed here:* To understand *what* is being offloaded and prefetched (expert FFN weights) and *why* selection is sparse (routing function selects Top‑K experts per token). The prefetching logic is tightly coupled to the MoE layer structure (pre‑norm, router placement).  
  *Quick check question:* In a pre‑norm MoE block, what components are typically kept on the GPU vs. offloaded to CPU in a memory‑constrained setting, and why?

- **CPU‑GPU Data Transfer Bottleneck.**  
  *Why needed here:* The entire problem premise is that synchronous CPU→GPU copies of expert weights dominate TPOT (84–88% in Figure 7). Understanding this bottleneck is essential to see why overlapping it with compute is valuable.  
  *Quick check question:* If compute time is 10 ms and copy time is 40 ms per layer under on‑demand loading, what is the ideal TPOT after perfect overlap? What limits achieving this ideal?

- **Representational Drift Across Layers.**  
  *Why needed here:* The core mechanism relies on the signal in \( q_l \) predicting \( s_{l+1} \). Drift (change in hidden state distribution) between layers determines prediction difficulty. High drift (early Qwen3 layers) breaks the simple quasi‑hidden state predictor, necessitating the estimator.  
  *Quick check question:* How is "representational drift" operationalized in this paper (e.g., via Figure 3), and why does it specifically impact early layers in models like Qwen3‑30B‑A3B?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Router -> Quasi‑Hidden State Calculator -> Expert Predictor -> Prefetch Scheduler -> MoE Kernel  
(Default Vectors feed into Quasi‑Hidden State Calculator)

### Critical path
The critical path per layer is \( \max(t_{\text{compute},l}, t_{\text{copy}}(E_{l+1})) \). The goal is to fully hide the copy time of next‑layer experts within the compute time of the current layer.

### Design tradeoffs
- *Prefetch depth:* Currently one layer ahead. Deeper prefetching could increase overlap but raises memory pressure and miss penalty.
- *Predictor complexity:* Router‑based (parameter‑free, no training) vs. neural estimator (lightweight training, higher hit rate). Trade‑off is estimator overhead vs. accuracy recovery.
- *Speculative execution:* Always execute prefetched experts (preserves overlap, risks accuracy) vs. treat as cache hints only (safe accuracy, loses overlap on misses). The paper shows the former is often safe.
- *Cache size:* GPU expert buffer size. Must fit \( k \) experts per layer. Larger \( k \) or expert size reduces how many layers can be cached.

### Failure signatures
1. Low hit rate in early layers: Accuracy collapse on reasoning tasks (GSM8k, AIME) for models like Qwen3‑30B‑A3B with Router‑PF.
2. No TPOT improvement: If \( t_{\text{copy}}(E_{l+1}) > t_{\text{compute},l} \) for most layers, prefetching cannot fully hide transfer latency (e.g., on faster GPUs).
3. High idle time in traces: GPU waiting despite prefetching, indicating synchronization issues or estimator inference becoming a serial bottleneck.

### First 3 experiments
1. Baseline TPOT & Copy Fraction: Run on‑demand loading across context lengths; confirm CPU‑GPU copy dominates TPOT (≥84%) and that compute time increases with sequence length.
2. Prefetch Hit Rate Validation: For fixed prompts, at each layer \( l \) compare predicted \( E_{l+1} \) from \( q_l \) against true router‑selected \( E_{l+1} \); compute recall@k (active experts per token). Should replicate Figure 4.
3. Full Inference Accuracy with Speculative Execution: Run generation with Router‑PF/Est‑PF on a benchmark suite (HumanEval, GSM8k, etc.); compare accuracy to baseline on‑demand execution. Must replicate Table 1 pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can a single neural estimator fully replace per‑layer routers in MoE inference without degradation in downstream accuracy or generation quality, while also reducing serial overhead?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper proposes a lightweight estimator to improve prediction hit rates and notes in Section 6.4 that "router execution is not strictly required at inference time once the sl representation is available," suggesting a potential router‑free path. The Future Work section also mentions "router‑free inference" as a direction.
- **Why unresolved:** The estimator was evaluated only selectively (e.g., on high‑drift layers for Qwen3‑30B‑A3B) and not as a complete router replacement across all layers and models. The impact on long‑sequence generation, diverse tasks, and overall system parallelism remains unmeasured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comprehensive end‑to‑end evaluation replacing all routers with a trained estimator across multiple MoE architectures, measuring accuracy on a broad suite of benchmarks (including non‑reasoning tasks), generation quality metrics (e.g., perplexity), and TPOT under realistic workloads.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What architectural or training dynamics cause certain MoE models (e.g., Qwen3‑30B‑A3B) to exhibit high representational drift between adjacent early layers, and can this be mitigated at model design time?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper identifies that Qwen3‑30B‑A3B suffers from significant drift in early layers (Figure 3), leading to lower prefetch hit rates and accuracy degradation under speculative execution (Table 1, Figure 5). This drift is presented as a key limitation but its root cause is not explored.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis treats high drift as an observed model‑specific property without investigating why it occurs. Possible factors could include initialization, routing function topology, expert specialization patterns, or training objectives. Understanding the cause could inform the design of future MoEs that are more amenable to prefetching.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies or architectural analyses comparing high‑drift and low‑drift MoEs (e.g., GPT‑OSS vs. Qwen3) to isolate contributing factors (e.g., expert placement, router training, normalization schemes). Simulations or training interventions to reduce early‑layer drift and measure resulting prefetch accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does multi‑layer prefetching—speculatively loading experts for more than one future layer—combined with on‑device expert caching yield super‑additive improvements in compute‑memory overlap for long‑context decoding?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Future Work section states: "motivated by the representational similarities observed across many layers, multi‑layer prefetching may further improve compute‑memory overlap in conjunction with on‑device expert caching." The paper currently focuses on single‑layer prefetching.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only implements and evaluates one‑layer‑ahead prefetching. Multi‑layer prefetching risks higher memory pressure on GPU if many experts are cached, and prediction errors could compound. The trade‑off between prefetch depth, cache size, and accuracy is unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementation of a multi‑layer prefetching system with a cache management policy (e.g., LRU or utility‑based). Empirical evaluation across varying sequence lengths and expert pool sizes, measuring TPOT, cache hit rates, and accuracy to quantify net gains and optimal prefetch depth.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How effective is the proposed speculative prefetching scheme in severely memory‑constrained environments where offloading targets slow storage (e.g., SSD) rather than CPU RAM, and what modifications are needed?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Future Work section explicitly mentions extending to "resource‑constrained deployments (e.g., smartphones, robotic platforms, and embedded systems), where prefetching may be applied to offloading involving disk‑CPU memory transfers."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper evaluates only CPU→GPU offloading over PCIe/NVLink. Disk‑based offloading introduces orders‑of‑magnitude higher latency and non‑uniform access times. The current prediction accuracy and overlap strategy may not transfer directly.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Port the prefetching system to a disk‑offloading setup (e.g., model weights stored on SSD). Measure TPOT improvement relative to on‑demand disk loading, analyze the impact of higher transfer latency on the optimal prefetch window, and potentially develop hierarchical prefetching (CPU cache then disk).

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does speculative execution of prefetched experts maintain fidelity across a wider range of language tasks beyond the reasoning‑heavy benchmarks (e.g., GSM8k, HumanEval), such as open‑ended dialogue, summarization, or instruction following?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper evaluates accuracy on a narrow set of reasoning and coding benchmarks (Table 1). The Future Work section notes the need to "explore tasks beyond reasoning" to validate generalizability.
- **Why unresolved:** Reasoning tasks may be more robust to expert mismatches due to their structured nature. Open‑ended generation tasks could be more sensitive to subtle distribution shifts introduced by executing incorrect experts, potentially degrading coherence, factual accuracy, or style.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate router‑based and estimator‑based prefetching on a diverse suite of tasks (e.g., dialogue quality via human evaluation, summarization metrics like ROUGE, instruction adherence benchmarks). Compare degradation patterns across task types to understand task‑specific sensitivity to expert speculation.

## Limitations
- Estimator training details (hyperparameters, architecture) and default vector computation protocol are underspecified, hindering faithful reproduction and analysis of trade‑offs.
- Evaluation uses single‑token decoding; batched generation, which is common in practice, could alter prefetch dynamics and remains uninvestigated.
- TPOT gains are hardware‑dependent; on newer GPUs with faster compute (e.g., H100), overlap benefits may shrink significantly.

## Confidence
- Prefetch overlap reduces TPOT: **High**
- Speculative execution preserves accuracy (without estimator): **Medium**
- Neural estimator recovers accuracy for high‑drift models: **Medium**

## Next Checks
