# Segmentation of Gray Matters and White Matters from Brain MRI data
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.29171
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29171
- **Reference count**: 23
- **Primary result**: Coronal model achieves Dice 0.8751 and IoU 0.7935 on 1000-slice test set; orientation-specific models outperform unified.

## Executive Summary
Accurate segmentation of gray and white matter from T1‑weighted brain MRI is essential but existing tools have limitations: traditional methods like FSL FAST require task‑specific adjustments, and foundation models such as MedSAM are restricted to binary segmentation. This work extends MedSAM to three‑class segmentation by modifying its mask decoder to output channels for background, gray matter, and white matter while freezing the image encoder. Using the IXI dataset, the authors preprocess MRI volumes with FSL BET (skull stripping) and FSL FAST (tissue probability maps), generate 2D axial, sagittal, and coronal slices, and create multi‑class labels by thresholding probability maps at 0.5. Fine‑tuning only the prompt encoder and mask decoder on orientation‑specific slice sets yields a best Dice of 0.8751 (IoU 0.7935) for the coronal model; sagittal and axial models perform lower, and a unified model combining all orientations fails to surpass the orientation‑specific ones. The study highlights the promise of SAM‑style models for multi‑class brain tissue segmentation but notes limitations from pseudo‑label reliance and evaluation restricted to healthy subjects.

## Method Summary
The method builds on MedSAM, a medical segment‑anything model. The authors obtain T1‑weighted NIfTI volumes from the IXI dataset. For each volume, they perform skull stripping with FSL BET, then compute gray/white matter probability maps using FSL FAST. The 3D volumes are sliced along axial, sagittal, and coronal planes; each slice and its corresponding probability maps are resized to 256×256. Multi‑class labels are created by thresholding the probability maps: voxels with gray matter probability > 0.5 are labeled 1, white matter > 0.5 labeled 2, and all others (including background) labeled 0. The pre‑trained MedSAM model is adapted by replacing the final convolutional layer of its mask decoder with a three‑channel output layer. All image encoder parameters are frozen; the prompt encoder and modified mask decoder are fine‑tuned. Models are trained per orientation (axial, sagittal, coronal) and also on a unified dataset mixing all orientations. Training uses AdamW (learning rate 1e⁻⁴, batch size 2) with a weighted cross‑entropy loss to handle class imbalance, early stopping on validation loss, and 10 epochs maximum. Performance is evaluated on a held‑out set of 1 000 slices using Dice Similarity Coefficient and Intersection over Union.

## Key Results
- **Coronal model** achieved the highest Dice score (0.8751) and IoU (0.7935).
- **Sagittal and axial models** performed lower (Dice 0.8604 and 0.8440, respectively).
- **Unified model** did not outperform any orientation‑specific model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Three‑channel mask decoder
- **Claim**: Extending MedSAM’s mask decoder to output three channels enables multi‑class segmentation (background, gray matter, white matter) without retraining the image encoder.
- **Mechanism**: The original decoder produces a single‑channel binary mask logit. Replacing its final convolutional layer with a three‑channel layer generates separate logit maps for each class. The argmax across channels assigns each pixel to the highest‑scoring class.
- **Core assumption**: The pre‑trained image encoder’s features are general enough to support multiple tissue classes; the binary segmentation objective can be cleanly extended via the decoder.
- **Evidence**: [abstract], [section 3.1] describe the modification; feasibility is assumed from MedSAM’s decoder design (no direct validation in the corpus).
- **Break condition**: If the encoder’s features are overly specialized for binary distinction, the three‑channel decoder may fail to disentangle gray and white matter without encoder retraining.

### Mechanism 2: Pseudo‑labels from FAST probability maps
- **Claim**: Using FSL FAST probability maps thresholded at 0.5 provides effective pseudo‑labels for training, despite not being manual ground truth.
- **Mechanism**: FAST generates probabilistic tissue maps (gray, white, CSF). Thresholding (≥ 0.5) creates discrete, multi‑class masks where each voxel is assigned to one class, which then supervise pixel‑wise classification.
- **Core assumption**: FAST’s segmentations are sufficiently accurate and consistent; the 0.5 threshold cleanly separates gray and white matter voxels.
- **Evidence**: [abstract], [section 3.2.2] detail preprocessing and thresholding. [section 5.2] acknowledges that results reflect agreement with FAST‑derived labels rather than true anatomical accuracy.
- **Break condition**: If FAST contains systematic biases or the threshold produces ambiguous boundaries, the model will learn and propagate these inaccuracies.

### Mechanism 3: Orientation‑specific training advantage
- **Claim**: Training models on orientation‑specific 2D slice datasets (axial, sagittal, coronal) yields higher segmentation performance than on a unified dataset combining all orientations.
- **Mechanism**: Each anatomical orientation provides a distinct 2D projection with unique contextual relationships and boundary appearances. Orientation‑specific models learn features tuned to that view, while a unified model must learn a more complex, orientation‑invariant mapping from a mixed distribution, which can dilute specialized feature learning.
- **Core assumption**: The optimal feature representation for segmenting gray/white matter differs meaningfully across 2D planes; a single model cannot efficiently learn all three specialized mappings simultaneously.
- **Evidence**: [section 5.1] reports coronal Dice 0.8751, sagittal 0.8604, axial 0.8440; [section 5.2.4] explains the dilution effect due to increased variability. Corpus contains related orientation‑specific segmentation work but not a direct comparison.
- **Break condition**: If the unified model’s performance gap closes with significantly more data, advanced augmentations (e.g., random rotation), or a multi‑view fusion architecture, the dilution effect may be overcome.

## Foundational Learning
- **Fine‑tuning a frozen encoder**  
  *Why needed*: Preserves robust pre‑trained features while adapting only task‑specific parameters, reducing overfitting on the limited brain MRI data.  
  *Quick check*: What would likely happen if the image encoder were also fine‑tuned? (Expected: potential overfitting and loss of generalizable features, higher resource consumption.)
- **Pseudo‑labeling with model‑based preprocessing**  
  *Why needed*: Enables scalable creation of training masks without costly manual annotation; FSL FAST acts as a teacher model.  
  *Quick check*: What is a key risk of using another model’s outputs as training labels? (Expected: error propagation; the student inherits the teacher’s biases and limitations.)
- **Handling class imbalance with weighted loss**  
  *Why needed*: Prevents background pixels from dominating the loss, ensuring the model learns to segment the smaller but critical gray and white matter regions.  
  *Quick check*: How does assigning a lower weight to the background class change the model’s learning dynamics? (Expected: increases gradient contribution from gray/white matter errors, forcing the model to prioritize those classes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
2D slice → Frozen Image Encoder (ViT‑B) → Prompt Encoder → Modified Mask Decoder (3‑channel output) → Argmax → Segmentation map.

**Critical path**:
1. Preprocess each 3D NIfTI volume: apply FSL BET for skull stripping, then FSL FAST for tissue probability maps.
2. Slice the volume and probability maps along axial, sagittal, and coronal planes; resize to 256×256.
3. Threshold probability maps at 0.5 to create multi‑class labels (0 = background, 1 = gray matter, 2 = white matter).
4. Load pre‑trained MedSAM and modify its mask decoder’s final convolutional layer to output 3 channels.
5. Freeze all image encoder parameters; set prompt encoder and mask decoder as trainable.
6. Train each orientation‑specific dataset (and unified) using AdamW (lr = 1e⁻⁴, batch = 2) with weighted cross‑entropy loss; stop early based on validation loss.
7. Evaluate the best model on a held‑out 1000‑slice test set using Dice and IoU.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **2D vs 3D**: 2D slices are efficient and reuse MedSAM but ignore inter‑slice context; 3D models could capture volumetric cues but need more memory and architectural changes.
- **Orientation‑specific vs Unified**: Separate models may achieve higher per‑view accuracy but require three models; unified model simplifies deployment but risks performance dilution from mixed orientations.
- **FAST pseudo‑labels vs Manual**: FAST labels are abundant and free but may contain systematic biases; manual labels would be more accurate but prohibitively expensive for large datasets.

**Failure signatures**:
- Blurry or indistinct tissue boundaries indicating label ambiguity.
- Systematic mislabeling in certain regions (e.g., ventral prefrontal gray matter misclassified as white matter in axial slices).
- Unified model underperforming all single‑orientation models.
- Model predicting only the background class (catastrophic failure).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Decoder sanity check**: Verify that the modified decoder outputs a 3‑channel tensor and that loss decreases on a small batch.
2. **Single‑orientation baseline**: Train the coronal model for 1 epoch on a small subset (e.g., 1 000 slices) and confirm Dice > 0.5 on validation slices.
3. **Orientation comparison**: Train all three orientation‑specific models for the full 10 epochs; compare validation Dice scores and confirm expected ordering (coronal > sagittal > axial).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **3D extension**: Can the modified MedSAM architecture be effectively extended to 3D volumetric segmentation, and how does its performance compare to the 2D slice‑based approach?
2. **Generalization to pathology**: How does the model’s segmentation accuracy generalize to MRI scans with pathological conditions (e.g., tumors, neurodegenerative diseases) compared to healthy subjects?
3. **FAST vs manual agreement**: To what extent do FSL FAST‑derived probability maps agree with manual expert annotations for gray and white matter segmentation, and does training on FAST labels affect performance on tasks requiring high anatomical precision?
4. **Unified model improvement**: Why does training a unified model on all three orientations fail to outperform orientation‑specific models, and what modifications enable effective multi‑orientation learning?
5. **Encoder fine‑tuning**: Is freezing the pre‑trained image encoder optimal, or would adaptive fine‑tuning improve accuracy, especially with larger and more diverse datasets?

## Limitations
- Labels are derived from FSL FAST probability maps (threshold 0.5), not manual annotations; reported Dice scores reflect agreement with FAST rather than true anatomical accuracy, and FAST biases may be inherited.
- Training details are missing: exact class weights for the weighted cross‑entropy loss and the slice exclusion criterion for minimal brain tissue.
- Evaluation protocol is ambiguous: composition of the common test set for the unified model and the testing procedure for orientation‑specific models are not fully specified.

## Confidence
- **High**: The decoder modification to 3‑class output is straightforward and well documented; freezing the encoder follows standard fine‑tuning practice for SAM derivatives.
- **Medium**: The claimed superiority of orientation‑specific models over unified models is supported by results but lacks statistical validation; the hypothesized dilution effect remains untested.
- **Low**: Confidence in generalizability of the reported Dice scores is low because the test data (IXI healthy adults) is narrow and pseudo‑labels are not a gold standard.

## Next Checks
1. **Manual‑label validation**: Manually annotate a diverse subset of ~50 slices across orientations from the IXI test set and re‑evaluate all models to quantify true vs. FAST‑agreement performance.
2. **External robustness test**: Apply the best coronal model to an external T1 dataset with different scanner vendors or pathology (e.g., ADNI) and measure the drop in Dice to assess overfitting to IXI/FAST characteristics.
3. **Unified model intervention**: Retrain the unified model with heavy random rotations (e.g., ±30°) or an orientation‑aware module (e.g., prepend an orientation token) and compare validation Dice to the original unified model to test if orientation dilution is mitigable.
