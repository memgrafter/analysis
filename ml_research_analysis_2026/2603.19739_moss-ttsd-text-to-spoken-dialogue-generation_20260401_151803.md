# MOSS-TTSD: Text to Spoken Dialogue Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19739
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19739
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art multi-party spoken dialogue generation with up to 60-minute single-pass synthesis, achieving high speaker attribution accuracy (ACC=0.9626) and similarity (SIM=0.7326) on English.

## Executive Summary
MOSS-TTSD addresses core challenges in spoken dialogue synthesis—accurate turn-taking, cross-turn acoustic consistency, and long-form stability—by using a fully discrete speech generation paradigm. It combines a Qwen3-8B large language model backbone with a low-bitrate audio tokenizer (MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer) to generate up to 60 minutes of multi-party dialogue in a single forward pass, supporting up to five speakers and zero-shot voice cloning from short reference audio. A novel evaluation framework, TTSD-eval, leverages forced alignment to measure speaker attribution accuracy and similarity without diarization tools. Objective tests on Chinese and English datasets show MOSS-TTSD surpasses both open-source and proprietary baselines. Subjective evaluations confirm superior human-perceived quality in speaker consistency, rhythm, and overall preference.

## Method Summary
MOSS-TTSD treats speech as a sequence of discrete tokens produced by a neural audio codec. The system uses a Qwen3-8B LLM to autoregressively predict only the first 16 layers of a 32-layer Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) tokenizer, operating at a low bitrate (2 kbps, 12.5 Hz). This reduction in sequence length enables modeling of contexts up to 3600 seconds in a single pass. Training follows a three-stage curriculum: Stage 1 focuses on single-speaker and two-speaker data while introducing speaker tags and voice cloning; Stage 2 filters for high audio quality (DNSMOS ≥ 3.4, ≥24 kHz) and reduces the proportion of single-speaker samples; Stage 3 adds real and synthetic multi-speaker (3–5 speakers) data to improve turn-switching stability and long-range speaker attribution. At inference, the model conditions on both speaker-tagged text (continuation) and per-speaker reference audio tokens (voice cloning), yielding robust zero-shot speaker similarity.

## Key Results
- Achieves state-of-the-art results on Chinese and English test sets: English ACC = 0.9626, SIM = 0.7326, WER = 9.88%, outperforming both open-source and proprietary baselines.
- Supports up to 60‑minute single-pass synthesis and multi-party dialogue with five speakers.
- Subjective evaluations show significant preference over comparators in speaker consistency, rhythm, and overall quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Low-bitrate RVQ modeling for long contexts
**Claim:** Modeling only the first 16 RVQ layers enables robust long-context, multi-speaker dialogue generation without stitching artifacts.  
**Mechanism:** The MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer operates at a low bitrate (2 kbps, 12.5 Hz). By autoregressively predicting just the first 16 layers via a multi-head delay pattern, the sequence length is drastically reduced, allowing the Qwen3‑8B LLM to manage contexts up to 3600 seconds in a single pass while maintaining acoustic coherence across long turns and multiple speakers. The core assumption is that the most perceptually critical information for speaker identity and prosody resides in the lower RVQ layers, making higher‑layer modeling less essential in this low-bitrate regime.  
**Evidence:** [abstract], [section 4.1] state that the tokenizer's low‑bitrate performance enables robust long‑context modeling; neighboring work (e.g., ZipVoice‑Dialog) explores non‑autoregressive alternatives but does not confirm this specific discrete‑layering trade‑off.  
**Break condition:** If lower RVQ layers prove insufficient for preserving necessary timbral or prosodic nuances in longer (>60 min) or higher‑fidelity (>32 kHz) scenarios, the single‑pass advantage would degrade, forcing chunked inference and risking stitching artifacts.

### Mechanism 2: Curriculum learning for multi‑party robustness
**Claim:** A three‑stage curriculum progressively shifts from single‑speaker TTS to natural, high‑quality multi‑party dialogue, improving turn‑taking stability and cross‑turn speaker consistency.  
**Mechanism:** Stage 1 extends context and introduces speaker tags/voice cloning on mostly single/two‑speaker data. Stage 2 filters for high audio quality (DNSMOS ≥ 3.4, ≥24 kHz) and reduces the single‑speaker data ratio to prioritize dialogue quality. Stage 3 adds real and synthetic multi‑speaker (3–5 speakers) data, directly training on complex turn‑switching and long‑range speaker attribution. This staged increase in task complexity prevents catastrophic forgetting of single‑speaker quality while building multi‑party robustness. The core assumption is that coherent multi‑speaker dialogue generation is a composite skill that can be bootstrapped from single‑speaker TTS by gradually increasing speaker and context management difficulty.  
**Evidence:** [section 4.2] details the three stages, filtering criteria, and the use of synthetic concatenated data to improve stability; [section 3.2] describes synthetic data creation (interleaving single‑speaker segments). Corpus evidence for this specific curriculum is weak; neighboring multi‑party dialogue work (e.g., MPCEval) focuses on evaluation, not training curricula.  
**Break condition:** If the distribution shift from Stage 2 (high‑quality, mostly ≤2 speakers) to Stage 3 (lower‑quality real multi‑speaker data + synthetic concatenations) is too abrupt, naturalness and intelligibility gains from Stage 2 could be degraded.

### Mechanism 3: Dual conditioning for zero‑shot voice cloning
**Claim:** Combining reference‑conditioned voice cloning with autoregressive continuation during inference significantly improves zero‑shot speaker similarity and attribution accuracy compared to either method alone.  
**Mechanism:** The model is trained on data where speaker identity is conditioned via explicit speaker tags in the text prompt (continuation) and via per‑speaker reference audio tokens in a designated prompt slot (voice_clone). At inference, providing both the reference audio embeddings and the speaker‑tagged text prompt allows the LLM to jointly leverage: (1) the timbral anchor from the reference audio tokens, and (2) the sequential dialogue context from the text tags. This dual conditioning grounds speaker identity more robustly across long turns and speaker switches. The core assumption is that speaker identity (timbre/prosody) and dialogue context (turn order, linguistic content) are separable conditioning signals that, when combined, yield superior performance over a single modality.  
**Evidence:** [section 4.3] reports substantial improvement from combining the paradigms; [Appendix C] tables show that `voice_clone_and_continuation` achieves the best SIM scores (e.g., EN SIM 0.7326 vs. 0.7228 for voice_clone alone, 0.7107 for continuation alone) and comparable/improved ACC and WER. Corpus evidence is absent; neighboring models (e.g., FlashLabs Chroma) focus on personalized cloning but not this combination.  
**Break condition:** If the reference audio is of very poor quality (e.g., extreme noise, very short duration <1 s), the added conditioning might inject noise or cause timbre degradation, potentially harming similarity more than continuation alone.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Encoder -> Audio Tokenizer (MOSS‑Audio‑Tokenizer) -> LLM Backbone (Qwen3‑8B‑base) -> Audio Token Decoder  
*(TTSD‑eval toolkit is a separate post‑training component.)*  

**Critical path:**  
1. Data Engineering: Collect long dialogues with diarized transcripts; filter by DNSMOS and language consistency.  
2. Curriculum Training: Train the Qwen3‑8B+tokenizer pipeline through three stages, gradually increasing speaker count and data quality.  
3. Inference: Condition on both speaker‑tagged text and reference audio tokens; generate up to 60 min in a single pass.  
4. Evaluation: Use TTSD‑eval (forced alignment + speaker embeddings) to compute ACC, SIM, WER.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Low-bitrate (16 RVQ layers) vs. Fidelity:* Reduces sequence length for long contexts but discards high‑frequency detail; acceptable for dialogue but may limit high‑fidelity monologue.  
- *Single‑pass (60 min) vs. Memory/Stability:* Requires very long context windows; any error propagates. Chunked fallback introduces boundary artifacts.  
- *Zero‑shot cloning (short reference) vs. Speaker Consistency:* Works robustly for 1–5 speakers but likely fails for unseen speaker characteristics or very poor references.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Speaker Attribution Collapse:** ACC drops sharply when (a) number of speakers > 5, (b) reference audio is missing or very short, or (c) turns are very rapid with minimal pause.  
- **Long‑form Degradation:** Increasing WER or SIM variance after ~45–50 minutes, indicating context window saturation or prediction drift.  
- **Cross‑turn Inconsistency:** Same speaker’s timbre/prosody changes noticeably between early and late turns in a long dialogue.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Ablation on RVQ Layers:** Train/model with N = 8, 16, 24, 32 layers on a fixed 5‑min multi‑speaker test set. Measure ACC, SIM, and memory usage. Expect 16 to be Pareto‑optimal for the 60‑min goal.  
2. **Curriculum Stage Ablation:** Train ablations skipping Stage 2 (high‑quality filter) or Stage 3 (synthetic data). Evaluate on a hard 4–5 speaker, 30+ min test set. Expect Stage 3 ablation to harm ACC most.  
3. **Inference Paradigm Comparison:** On a held‑out set, generate audio using `voice_clone` only, `continuation` only, and `voice_clone_and_continuation`. Compute TTSD‑eval SIM and run human ABX tests on speaker consistency. Expect combined to win on SIM; check for any WER increase.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can we construct large-scale, high-quality training data for multi-party spoken dialogues with accurate speaker annotations for more than 5 speakers?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors state that annotating speaker identities in long multi‑speaker scenarios is challenging, leading them to retain only a small fraction of real multi‑speaker clips.  
**Why unresolved:** Current diarization and ASR systems produce errors, especially with many speakers, limiting data quantity and quality for >5 speakers.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a robust, end‑to‑end system for joint diarization and ASR with high accuracy on long multi‑party dialogues, or a manually curated dataset with gold‑standard speaker labels for 6+ speakers.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** What is the impact of limiting the audio tokenizer to the first 16 RVQ layers on the quality of synthesized speech, particularly for prosody and speaker characteristics, in long‑context multi‑speaker generation?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper explains that modeling only the first 16 RVQ layers enables robust long‑context generation at a low bitrate, but does not analyze the quality trade‑off.  
**Why unresolved:** Higher RVQ layers likely encode finer acoustic details; ignoring them may degrade prosodic naturalness or speaker similarity, especially in complex dialogues.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study varying the number of RVQ layers (e.g., 8, 16, 24, 32) and measuring objective (e.g., SIM, WER) and subjective quality on long dialogues.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Does the forced‑alignment based evaluation framework TTSD‑eval produce reliable speaker attribution scores when the generated dialogue contains disfluencies or departs from the input script?  
**Basis in paper:** TTSD‑eval relies on forced alignment between the script and audio, assuming perfect correspondence; it does not account for improvisations or disfluencies.  
**Why unresolved:** Real dialogues often include filled pauses, repetitions, or non‑lexical tokens, which could cause misalignment and biased ACC/SIM scores.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate TTSD‑eval on dialogues with controlled deviations (e.g., inserted “um”, repetitions) and compare its outputs with human annotations of speaker turns.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How does the model’s performance in speaker consistency and dialogue naturalness scale with the number of speakers beyond the current limit of five?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper states support for “up to 5 speakers” but provides no results for larger groups, leaving scalability untested.  
**Why unresolved:** Multi‑party conversations with many participants (e.g., crowded scenes) are common; the architecture’s capacity to maintain distinct speaker embeddings and manage turn‑taking with >5 speakers is unknown.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct experiments with synthetic dialogues containing 6 to 10 speakers, using TTSD‑eval and subjective tests to measure degradation in ACC, SIM, and naturalness.

## Limitations
- Low‑bitrate tokenization (first 16 RVQ layers) may lose timbral/prosodic details; scalability beyond 60 min or higher sampling rates is unproven.
- Curriculum synthetic concatenations may introduce unnatural abrupt transitions; quality filters (DNSMOS ≥ 3.4) could exclude diverse voices.
- TTSD‑eval assumes perfect script correspondence; misalignments from generation errors (e.g., stuttering, repetitions) could invalidate speaker attribution metrics.
- Unknown reference audio preprocessing (length, normalization) and inference hyperparameters hinder reproducibility and optimal voice cloning.
- Evaluation restricted to Chinese and English; generalization to other languages or >5 speakers is unverified.

## Confidence
- **SOTA performance on TTSD benchmarks:** High (clear metrics, multiple baselines; limited to provided test sets).
- **60‑minute single‑pass generation via reduced RVQ layers:** Medium (demonstrated; beyond 60 min unproven).
- **Necessity of three‑stage curriculum:** Medium (ablation not reported; qualitative evidence only).
- **Dual conditioning (voice‑clone + continuation) superiority:** High (quantitative results in Appendix C).
- **TTSD‑eval validity:** Medium (innovative but not cross‑validated against diarization‑based metrics).
