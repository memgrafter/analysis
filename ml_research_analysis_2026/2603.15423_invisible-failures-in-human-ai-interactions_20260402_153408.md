# Invisible failures in human-AI interactions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15423  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15423  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** AI systems fail silently in 78% of problematic interactions, revealing eight invisible failure archetypes; 91% of failures stem from interactional dynamics and 94% are likely to persist with more capable models.

## Executive Summary
The widespread adoption of large language models (LLMs) in conversational AI has highlighted a critical yet understudied problem: invisible failures, where the AI produces a flawed response but the user does not provide any explicit signal of dissatisfaction. This paper analyzes 196,704 English-language conversations from the WildChat dataset (GPT-4 driven) to characterize such failures. Through an automated annotation pipeline using Claude, the authors assign 28 quality signals per transcript and define eight failure archetypes based on signal combinations. They distinguish invisible failures from visible ones (e.g., user complaints) and investigate whether these failures are due to capability limitations or persistent interactional dynamics.

The analysis reveals that 78% of all failures are invisible. The most prevalent archetypes are “The drift” (AI gradually diverges from the user’s goal) and “The confidence trap” (AI presents incorrect information with high confidence). Retrospective validation with Claude Opus indicates that 91% of failures arise from interactional patterns—most notably the AI’s default to generate rather than clarify ambiguity—and that 94% of these would likely persist even with a more capable model. Moreover, failure patterns vary across domains: software development shows higher rates of visible failures, while creative writing and UX design are more susceptible to drift. This taxonomy provides a scalable foundation for monitoring and mitigating subtle failures in real-world AI systems.

## Method Summary
The study uses the WildChat dataset, comprising 196,704 English conversations between users and ChatGPT (GPT-4). An annotation pipeline with Claude Sonnet 4.5 assigns each transcript an overall quality label (good, acceptable, poor, critical) and 28 fine-grained signal tags (e.g., `ai_false_confidence`, `user_abandonment`). Failures are identified by the presence of the `goal_failure` signal. Invisible failures are those lacking any signal from the `VisibleSignals` set (e.g., explicit user complaints). The eight invisible failure archetypes are derived from specific combinations of signal tags (e.g., `goal_failure` + `ai_off_topic_drift` → “The drift”). To determine the origin of failures, a stratified sample of failure transcripts is re-evaluated by Claude Opus, which judges whether each failure is due to a capability gap, an interactional dynamic, or both, and whether it would likely persist with a more capable model. Transcripts are also labeled with a primary domain (from a fixed ontology), and co-occurrence between domains and archetypes is measured using positive pointwise mutual information (PPMI) to identify domain-specific risk patterns.

## Key Results
- **Invisible failure dominance:** 78% of all failures across the dataset are invisible, meaning the AI’s error goes undetected by the user during the conversation.
- **Key archetypes:** The drift (off-topic drift) and the confidence trap (confident inaccuracies) are the two most prevalent invisible failure archetypes.
- **Interactional roots and persistence:** 91% of failures are attributed to persistent interactional dynamics (e.g., the AI generating rather than clarifying), and 94% of these are estimated to persist even with more capable models. Domain analysis shows software development has higher visible failure rates, while creative writing and UX design are prone to drift.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The most common driver of invisible failure is the AI's default behavior to "generate rather than clarify," where it produces fluent output instead of surfacing ambiguity or requesting missing information.
- **Mechanism:** This creates a silent mismatch between user intent and AI response because the AI fills gaps with plausible but misaligned content, and the user, receiving a coherent answer, has no explicit signal to correct it.
- **Core assumption:** This generative default is a structural property of LLM training and chat interface design, not a transient capability limitation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "The single most common pattern, appearing in 79% of failures, is generate rather than clarify."
  - [section 5.2] "The most prevalent interaction dynamic is generate rather than clarify... This pattern appears in 79% of all failure transcripts."
  - [corpus] The pattern is derived from retrospective validation of 1,044 failure transcripts.
- **Break condition:** If future model architectures or interaction designs systematically incentivize clarification over generation when ambiguity is detected, this mechanism could diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** 91% of failures arise from persistent interactional dynamics (e.g., how the AI presents information, how users communicate intent) rather than pure capability gaps, and 94% of these are estimated to persist even with more capable models.
- **Mechanism:** Failures are structural to the interaction loop: the AI's behavioral defaults (false confidence, topic drift) combine with user communication patterns (passive acceptance, implicit correction) to create failure modes that are invisible during the interaction but evident in retrospective analysis.
- **Core assumption:** Capability improvements will not automatically resolve these dynamics because they are orthogonal to knowledge or reasoning ability.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "91% involve interactional dynamics, and we estimate that 94% of such failures would persist even with a more capable model."
  - [section 5.2] "58% of failures are classified as primarily interaction and 33% as substantially both... 94% of failures are judged as likely to persist."
  - [corpus] Based on Claude Opus's retrospective analysis of 1,038 transcripts.
- **Break condition:** If a new model class fundamentally alters its response policy to prioritize verification, hedging, or clarification in ambiguous contexts, the persistence rate could drop significantly.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Failure archetypes cluster by domain because different tasks have different inherent characteristics that amplify specific interaction dynamics (e.g., open-ended creative tasks amplify drift; verifiable code tasks amplify visible failures).
- **Mechanism:** Domain-specific user goals and evaluation criteria shape which interaction failures remain invisible. For example, in creative writing, a drifted narrative can still feel coherent and thus invisible; in software development, broken code is immediately visible.
- **Core assumption:** The archetype-domain associations reflect stable properties of the tasks themselves, not just current model quirks.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Failure patterns vary by domain: software development has higher visible failure rates, while creative writing and UX design are prone to drift."
  - [section 6] "creative_writing and design_ux show unexpectedly high rates of [The drift]... the only very large value for Visible failure is in software_development."
  - [corpus] PPMI analysis of co-occurrence between 10 top domains and 9 archetypes across full dataset.
- **Break condition:** If domain norms or AI tooling change drastically (e.g., AI-native creative processes that inherently validate coherence), the domain-archetype mapping could shift.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Failure Archetype Taxonomy
- **Why needed here:** Provides a composable, signal-based framework for categorizing invisible failures. Without this, failures are just noise.
- **Quick check question:** Which archetype is defined by `ai_off_topic_drift` in the signal set? (Answer: "The drift")

### Concept: Interactional vs. Capability-Driven Failures
- **Why needed here:** Determines whether failures will persist with model improvements. This dichotomy is central to the paper's predictive claim.
- **Quick check question:** According to the retrospective validation, what percentage of failures were classified as "primarily interaction"? (Answer: 58%)

### Concept: Domain-Specific Risk Profiling
- **Why needed here:** Allows product teams to anticipate which failure modes are most likely in their application area, guiding monitoring emphasis.
- **Quick check question:** Which domain shows the highest PPMI association with "The drift"? (Answer: creative_writing)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Data Ingestion -> Signal Annotator -> Archetype Engine -> Domain Classifier -> Aggregation & Reporting

### Critical path
Raw transcript → Signal Annotator → Archetype Engine → Domain Classifier → Aggregation → Alerts/Reports

### Design tradeoffs
- **Precision vs. Recall:** The paper calibrated for precision (section 3.1). A monitoring system might tune for higher recall (catching more failures) but will increase false positives.
- **Model dependence:** Using a frontier model (like Claude Opus) for annotation gives high-quality signals but is expensive and creates a moving target as models change. A cheaper, fixed model may degrade over time.
- **Real-time vs. Batch:** Archetypes can be computed in real-time per transcript, but domain profiling and co-occurrence analysis require batch aggregation.

### Failure signatures
The eight archetypes themselves are the primary signatures. Secondary signals are the 28 quality tags (e.g., `ai_false_confidence`, `user_abandonment`). A spike in `The drift` rate in a specific domain is a concrete signature.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replication:** Run the full pipeline on a new sample of WildChat (or similar) conversations. Verify that the overall 78% invisible failure rate and top archetype distribution (drift ~37%, confidence trap ~26%) hold within confidence intervals.
2. **Persistence Test:** Take 100 transcripts from each archetype from a newer model (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.7). Use a strong evaluator model to re-apply the signal/archetype tagging. Hypothesis: The same archetype distributions should appear, confirming persistence.
3. **Domain Drill-down:** For a specific high-stakes domain (e.g., "software_development"), analyze the archetype mixture. Compare the rate of `Visible failure` vs. `The drift` vs. `The confidence trap`. Use this to recommend which archetype-specific mitigations (e.g., code validation tools for visible failures, goal-confirmation prompts for drift) should be prioritized.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the invisible failure taxonomy generalize to non-English conversations, voice-based interactions, or multi-agent systems?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the limitations section, the authors state: "The signal taxonomy was developed and calibrated for the English-language subset of WildChat. Applying it to conversations with different characteristics (e.g., voice-based AI, multimodal interactions, multi-agent systems) would require recalibration." They also note that future work should apply the framework to transcripts from more recent models to test persistence predictions.
- **Why unresolved:** The taxonomy is derived from English text-based ChatGPT (GPT-4) interactions from 2023–2024. Different modalities, languages, or interaction structures (e.g., multi-agent) may exhibit distinct failure patterns not captured by the eight archetypes or 28 signals.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Collect and annotate parallel datasets from diverse modalities (voice, video), languages, and multi-agent setups using the same signal tagging protocol. Compare archetype prevalence and co-occurrence patterns, and revise the taxonomy based on new signal tags or archetype splits that emerge.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do the annotated quality categories (good, acceptable, poor, critical) correlate with actual user satisfaction, retention, and task success in real-world deployments?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the limitations: "The quality ratings reflect the judgment of the classifier and the analysis team, not ground-truth user satisfaction." The authors highlight the "acceptable" tier as a hidden risk where conversations succeed technically but carry latent issues, and they note that a conversation rated acceptable may have been "perfectly fine for the user’s actual needs, or it may have caused problems that surfaced later."
- **Why unresolved:** The quality labels are proxy metrics derived from conversation text; it is unclear whether they align with user perceptions, behavioral outcomes (e.g., repeat usage), or downstream task success. For example, "acceptable" conversations with high signal density might still yield user dissatisfaction not captured overtly in the transcript.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct studies that pair the annotation labels with direct user feedback (surveys, ratings), behavioral metrics (session duration, return rate), and expert evaluations of task outcomes. Analyze whether high-signal "acceptable" transcripts predict negative long-term user experiences.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What specific failure dynamics underlie the "mystery failure" archetype, and why are they concentrated in domains like creative_writing and content_production?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Mystery failure is defined as φ={goal_failure} with no other signals. The authors explicitly state: "The mystery failure should probably be treated as a high-priority area for further tagging and analysis." They find it most prevalent in creative_writing, content_production, and personal_lifestyle, noting these may be domains where "we lack enough insight even to get robust signals of the nature of the failure."
- **Why unresolved:** The category is a catch-all for failures with no identifiable signals, making it opaque. Its domain concentration suggests either missing signals in the current taxonomy or domain-specific failure modes (e.g., subjective quality, creative intent) that evade automated tagging.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform deep qualitative analysis (manual review, user interviews) on a sample of mystery failure transcripts from high-prevalence domains. Identify recurring patterns or new signal tags (e.g., aesthetic mismatch, unmet implicit expectations) and test whether augmenting the annotation scheme captures these cases.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do the identified interactional dynamics (e.g., "generate rather than clarify") persist in human-AI interactions with state-of-the-art models like GPT-5 or Claude Opus 4.6?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The retrospective validation used Claude Opus to judge that 94% of failures would persist with a more capable model, and they note: "Future work could test this prediction directly by applying the same framework to transcripts from more recent models." They also observe that when using GPT-5.2 for annotation, the "mystery failure" rate increased, possibly due to annotation capability gaps.
- **Why unresolved:** The persistence claim is based on a model's retrospective judgment of older GPT-4 transcripts, not on empirical data from interactions with newer models. Actual model improvements (e.g., better ambiguity handling) might reduce certain interactional failures, but this remains untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Collect fresh interaction data using the latest frontier models (e.g., GPT-5, Claude 4), annotate transcripts with the same signal tags and archetype definitions, and compare failure distributions and interaction dynamics (like clarification-seeking rates) against the WildChat baseline. Measure changes in specific patterns like "generate rather than clarify."

## Limitations
- **Annotation opacity:** The annotation pipeline relies on proprietary Claude models with unreported prompts and settings, hindering reproducibility.
- **Domain labeling ambiguity:** The method for assigning primary domains to transcripts is not described, making it difficult to verify the domain–archetype associations.
- **Lack of user feedback:** The invisible failure determination is retrospective and lacks validation with actual user satisfaction, behavioral outcomes, or task success metrics.
