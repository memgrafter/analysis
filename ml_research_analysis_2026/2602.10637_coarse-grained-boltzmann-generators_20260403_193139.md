# Coarse-Grained Boltzmann Generators

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10637
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10637
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** CG-BGs recover equilibrium distributions with low Jensen-Shannon divergence (~0.008) and substantial speedups (e.g., inference time from 13.9 min to 0.84 min) by combining coarse-grained generative modeling with importance sampling via a learned potential of mean force.

## Executive Summary
Coarse-Grained Boltzmann Generators (CG-BGs) address the scalability limitations of exact Boltzmann Generators and the lack of reweighting in coarse-grained methods. They operate in a reduced-dimensional space defined by coarse-grained beads, using a normalizing flow to propose configurations and a learned potential of mean force (PMF) obtained via enhanced-sampling force matching to provide the target energy for importance reweighting. This approach yields asymptotically exact equilibrium estimates even when trained on short biased trajectories, as demonstrated on the M¨uller–Brown potential and alanine dipeptide. The method outperforms implicit solvent baselines and enables simulation-free evaluation of coarse-grained potentials, offering a scalable path to unbiased sampling of larger molecular systems.

## Method Summary
CG-BGs unify coarse-graining with Boltzmann Generator training. First, a deterministic mapping $\Xi$ projects atomistic coordinates $r$ onto coarse-grained beads $R$. The target equilibrium distribution is the marginal $p(R) \propto e^{-\beta U(R)}$, where $U(R)$ is the potential of mean force. The PMF $U_\eta(R)$ is trained via enhanced-sampling force matching (ESFM) on rapidly converged biased trajectories (e.g., well-tempered metadynamics), leveraging the fact that if the bias depends only on $R$, the projected forces remain unbiased samples of the true mean force. Separately, a continuous normalizing flow $q_\theta(R)$ is trained by conditional flow matching to approximate the (unnormalized) CG marginal. At inference, samples $R \sim q_\theta$ are reweighted by $w(R) \propto \exp(-\beta U_\eta(R))/q_\theta(R)$, optionally with clipping, to estimate equilibrium observables. The entire pipeline avoids long unbiased MD, with computational gains from the lower-dimensional CG space.

## Key Results
- On M¨uller–Brown and alanine dipeptide, CG-BGs achieve Jensen-Shannon divergences of ~0.008 and PMF errors around 0.25, even when trained on short biased trajectories.
- The method significantly outperforms implicit solvent baselines in recovering reference distributions.
- Coarse-graining reduces inference time by over an order of magnitude (e.g., 13.9 min → 0.84 min for alanine dipeptide), demonstrating scalability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Coarse-graining reduces the effective dimensionality of the molecular system, making generative modeling tractable while preserving the essential thermodynamics.
- **Mechanism:** A deterministic mapping $\Xi$ projects full atomistic coordinates $r$ onto a smaller set of CG beads $R$. The target distribution becomes the marginal $p(R) \propto e^{-\beta U(R)}$, where $U(R)$ is the PMF. The flow model operates directly in this $R$‑space, so its dimensionality and Jacobian cost scale with the number of CG beads rather than total atoms.
- **Core assumption:** The chosen CG variables retain the slow degrees of freedom that dominate equilibrium statistics. The mapping does not introduce irrecoverable information loss.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “Coarse-grained surrogates enable the modeling of larger systems by reducing effective dimensionality.”
  - [section 2.3]: Defines coarse‑graining mapping and the resulting PMF.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers confirm coarse-graining is standard for scaling; CG‑BG integration is novel.
- **Break condition:** If coarse-graining is too aggressive (e.g., Core Beta for complex systems), the conditional mean force exhibits high variance (Section 4.2). The flow may then struggle to cover the target distribution, and even exact reweighting cannot recover correct statistics because the marginal $p(R)$ no longer captures the true atomistic equilibrium.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Importance sampling with a learned PMF yields asymptotically exact equilibrium estimates from the flow‑generated proposals.
- **Mechanism:** After training, the flow defines a proposal density $q_\theta(R)$. Importance weights are $w(R) \propto \exp(-\beta U_\eta(R))/q_\theta(R)$. If the learned PMF $U_\eta$ approximates the true $U$ on the support of $q_\theta$ and the effective sample size (ESS) is non‑degenerate, the self‑normalized estimator converges to $\mathbb{E}_{p}[O]$.
- **Core assumption:** (i) The PMF is accurate enough on the regions where the flow places mass; (ii) the flow has non‑negligible overlap with the target.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “a learned potential of mean force (PMF) … provides the target energy for reweighting.”
  - [section 3.3]: Explicit weight formula and ESS definition.
  - [corpus]: Established Boltzmann Generator and reweighting literature; CG‑BG adapts it to CG space.
- **Break condition:** Low ESS due to poor proposal‑target overlap or unphysical PMF extrapolation. The paper mitigates this with weight clipping, but severe mismatch (e.g., flow trained on highly biased data without sufficient diversity) can still cause high variance or bias after clipping.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The PMF can be accurately learned from rapidly converged biased trajectories via enhanced sampling force matching (ESFM), eliminating the need for long unbiased simulations.
- **Mechanism:** ESFM exploits Proposition 2: if the bias potential $V(R)$ depends only on CG coordinates, the conditional distribution $p(r|R)$ is unchanged. Therefore, the projected forces $F_{\text{proj}}(r)$ from biased data remain unbiased samples of the true mean force $-\nabla U(R)$. Minimizing the force‑matching loss on such data yields the same optimum as using unbiased data (Proposition 3).
- **Core assumption:** The bias is a function of CG coordinates only; the biased dataset is rapidly converged in CG space.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “learned potential of mean force (PMF) obtained via enhanced‑sampling force matching.”
  - [section 3.2]: Propositions 2 and 3 with proofs; loss definition $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ESFM}}$.
  - [corpus]: Force matching is established in coarse‑graining; ESFM’s use of enhanced sampling is a specific contribution.
- **Break condition:** The bias potential depends on fine‑grained details (not solely $R$), violating Proposition 2. Alternatively, if the biased simulations do not sufficiently sample rare but important $R$ values, the learned PMF may be inaccurate in those regions, leading to reweighting failures.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Coarse‑graining module (Ξ) → PMF network (U_η) & Flow model (v_θ) → Reweighting engine (w(R), ESS)
- **Critical path:**  
  1. *Data generation*: Run enhanced‑sampling MD (e.g., WT‑MetaD) on atomistic system, save $\{r, F_{\text{proj}}(r)\}$.  
  2. *PMF training*: Minimize $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ESFM}}$ on biased dataset (Alg. 1).  
  3. *Flow training*: Sample CG pairs from data, train via CFM (Alg. 2).  
  4. *Sampling*: Draw latent $z$, solve ODE to get $R \sim q_\theta$, compute $w(R)$, clip, estimate ESS and observables (Alg. 3).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *CG resolution*: Fewer beads → faster training/inference but risk degeneracy and PMF errors.  
  - *Flow expressivity*: Deeper/wider networks better match complex targets but cost more compute and may overfit.  
  - *Weight clipping*: Aggressive clipping (10–20%) raises ESS but introduces bias; too little leaves variance high. Use ~1% as safe default.  
  - *PMF architecture*: Equivariant GNNs (MACE) respect rotational symmetry, improving data efficiency vs. MLPs on 3‑D beads.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Extremely low ESS (< 0.01) after reweighting → poor proposal‑target overlap or PMF extrapolation.  
  - Large JS divergence (> 0.2) or PMF error vs. reference → PMF inaccuracy or flow mismatch.  
  - Flow proposals populate regions with very high $U_\eta$ → out‑of‑distribution extrapolation; check energy histograms.  
  - Training curves: PMF loss plateauing at high value → insufficient model capacity or poor force‑matching coverage.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Müller‑Brown test*: Coarse‑grain to 1‑D $x$, train on unbiased/biased data, verify reweighted $p(x)$ matches exact marginal (Fig. 2).  
  2. *Alanine dipeptide (Heavy Atom, 10 beads)*: Train on 500 ns explicit solvent unbiased data + WT‑MetaD for PMF. Compare reweighted $\phi$ dihedral free energy to explicit MD reference; target JS ~0.008 (Tab. 2). Benchmark vs. implicit solvent baselines.  
  3. *Alanine dipeptide (Core Beta, 6 beads)*: Repeat with coarser mapping; confirm similar JS (∼0.008) and measure 5× speedup in inference (Tab. 1). Perform ablation on weight clipping to show stability (Tab. 6).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the selection of collective variables for coarse-graining in CG-BGs be fully automated to handle complex molecular systems without manual intervention?
2. Can CG-BGs maintain low distributional error (e.g., Jensen‑Shannon divergence <0.01) and high effective sample size (>0.2) when applied to molecular systems with thousands of atoms using highly coarse-grained representations, and what are the practical computational speedups?
3. How can experimental data (e.g., NMR, SAXS) be systematically incorporated into the CG-BG framework to refine the learned potential of mean force and improve agreement with real‑world measurements?
4. Under what conditions does enhanced sampling force matching (ESFM) fail to accurately learn the potential of mean force, and how can these failures be detected and corrected?

## Limitations
- The assumption that coarse-graining preserves slow degrees of freedom may fail for complex biomolecules with entangled allosteric motions, leading to irrecoverable information loss.
- ESFM relies on bias potentials that depend only on CG coordinates; real enhanced-sampling biases (e.g., well‑tempered metadynamics) may depend on collective variables misaligned with $\Xi$, potentially violating the invariance condition.
- Weight clipping heuristics (e.g., 1%) may introduce bias for distributions with heavy tails; optimal thresholds likely depend on target overlap and are not theoretically derived.

## Confidence
- **Coarse-graining reduces dimensionality while preserving thermodynamics**: Medium (validated on small systems, but break condition noted for aggressive CG).
- **Importance sampling yields asymptotically exact equilibrium estimates**: Medium‑High (theoretical foundation solid, but practical ESS sensitivity and clipping‑induced bias leave empirical uncertainty).
- **ESFM learns accurate PMFs from biased trajectories**: High (theoretical guarantees; empirical results support, but validation beyond two systems is limited).

## Next Checks
1. **Scale to larger systems**: Apply CG-BG to multi-domain proteins (e.g., insulin) with varying CG resolutions; track JS divergence and ESS scaling with bead count to test the "slow degrees of freedom" assumption.
2. **Stress ESFM bias assumptions**: Train PMFs using biased data where $V$ depends on both $R$ and local atomistic descriptors (e.g., pairwise distances); quantify PMF error and reweighting bias to map the boundary of Proposition 2.
3. **Ablate weight clipping thresholds**: Systematically vary clipping percentiles (0.1–20%) on alanine dipeptide and a larger test system; report JS divergence vs. ESS to derive a data‑driven selection rule.
