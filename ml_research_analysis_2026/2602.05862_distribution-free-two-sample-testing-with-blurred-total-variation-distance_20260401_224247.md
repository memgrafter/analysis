# Distribution-free two-sample testing with blurred total variation distance
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05862
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05862
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Classical total variation admits no non‑trivial distribution‑free upper confidence bound; smoothing via kernel convolution yields "blurred TV," enabling valid finite‑sample inference without distributional assumptions.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles a fundamental limitation in two‑sample testing: without assumptions on the underlying distributions, it is impossible to construct non‑trivial distribution‑free upper confidence bounds for the total variation (TV) distance. The authors overcome this by introducing the *blurred TV distance*, which smooths each distribution by convolving it with a kernel (bandwidth `h`). This relaxation permits the derivation of both lower and upper confidence bounds (DF‑LCB and DF‑UCB) that hold uniformly over all distributions, with explicit finite‑sample guarantees. The error scales as √(log(1/α)(1/n+1/m)) and the bounds can be computed efficiently via Monte Carlo. Notably, in high dimensions the difficulty depends on the intrinsic (effective) dimension of the distributions, not the ambient dimension, thereby avoiding a curse of dimensionality when data lie near low‑dimensional structure. The paper also proves a complementary impossibility: any distribution‑free UCB for classical TV must be trivial (value 1 with high probability).

## Method Summary
Assumptions: The kernel is Gaussian (ensuring monotonicity and finite shift modulus) and the samples are i.i.d. The core construction for a fixed bandwidth `h` combines the empirical blurred TV estimator with two error terms: a deterministic truncation derived from the expected discrepancy between split empirical measures and a McDiarmid concentration term, both scaling as √((1/n+1/m) log(1/α)). The empirical blurred TV is estimated by Monte Carlo: draw points from the pooled empirical distribution, add Gaussian noise `hξ`, and evaluate the ratio of density differences. Monte Carlo approximation adds an extra O(1/√B) term, which is controlled by increasing the number of draws `B`. To achieve validity across all `h`, the authors define a monotonized blurred TV (for Gaussian kernels) and replace the pointwise estimator with its supremum over a logarithmic grid of `h`. This yields a bandwidth‑adaptive bound that automatically tightens as blur increases. A data‑driven variance proxy based on the kernel's shift modulus further scales the error, leading to the final DF‑UCB/DF‑LCB expressions.

## Key Results
- Blurred TV enables **distribution‑free** confidence bounds; classical TV cannot admit such bounds.
- Fixed‑`h` bounds have error ~ √(log(1/α)(1/n+1/m)), computable via Monte Carlo.
- **Intrinsic dimension:** In high dimensions, the difficulty depends on the effective dimension of the distributions; for linear subspaces the concentration depends only on the subspace dimension (assumption: linear subspace support).
- **Uniform‑over‑`h`** extension (for Gaussian kernels) yields adaptive intervals that tighten with larger `h`.
- **Negative result:** any distribution‑free UCB for classical TV is trivial (equal to 1 w.h.p.) under general distributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Blurring via convolution ( `P*ψₕ` ) smooths the distributions, making the empirical blurred TV converge to its population counterpart without smoothness assumptions, and its bounded sensitivity (via the shift modulus) allows the use of McDiarmid’s inequality to obtain distribution‑free deviation bounds. The bandwidth `h` controls the bias‑variance trade‑off: too small yields near‑1 empirical TV and trivial bounds; too large shrinks the distance to 0 and loses power. The DF‑UCB/DF‑LCB then combine the empirical blurred TV with explicit deterministic terms capturing the concentration and the split‑sample bias. For Gaussian kernels (assumption: Gaussian), the blurred TV is monotone in `h` and invariant under projection; if distributions lie on a `k`‑dimensional subspace, all finite‑sample bounds depend on `k` rather than the ambient dimension `d`. The Monte Carlo approximation is safe because adding an extra √(log(1/α)/(2B)) term preserves the overall coverage guarantee.

## Foundational Learning
- **Total Variation distance** – The canonical metric measuring worst‑case discrepancy; distribution‑free estimation fails because continuous data are almost surely mutually singular. *Why needed:* Sets up the core limitation the paper solves.
- **Convolution and kernel smoothing** – The key operation: `P*ψₕ` adds independent noise `hξ` to samples, smoothing the distribution. *Why needed:* Defines blurred TV and makes the functional estimable without assumptions.
- **Bounded differences (McDiarmid) inequality** – Provides finite‑sample concentration for functionals with bounded sensitivity to a single sample change. *Why needed:* Central to deriving distribution‑free confidence intervals.
- **Shift modulus** – A property of the kernel quantifying its maximum change under input shifts, used to bound the variance of the blurred TV estimator. *Why needed:* Enables a data‑driven scaling of the error term in the confidence bounds.
- **Intrinsic dimension** – The effective dimensionality of the distribution’s support. *Why needed:* Blurred TV concentration depends on intrinsic dimension, enabling high‑dimensional inference when data lie near low‑dimensional manifolds.
- **Distribution‑free inference** – Guarantees that hold uniformly over all distributions in a class. *Why needed:* The paper’s goal is to achieve this for two‑sample TV.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input samples X₁…Xₙ~P, Y₁…Yₘ~Q → Compute Monte Carlo estimator \(\hat{d}_h^{\text{TV}}(B)\) (sample W from pooled empirical, add hξ) → Compute variance proxy \(\hat{\Sigma}_h\) from pairwise shift moduli → Assemble DF‑UCB: \(\hat{d}_h^{\text{TV}} + C_{\alpha}\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_h + 1/(n∧m)}\); DF‑LCB uses split‑sample estimates and similar margin.

Critical path: Monte Carlo estimation dominates runtime; pairwise calculations for variance proxy cost O(n²+m²) and can be memory‑intensive. For uniform‑over‑`h` bounds, evaluate over a grid of `h` values, multiplying Monte Carlo cost and requiring careful vectorization.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Monte Carlo sample size `B`:** Larger `B` reduces approximation error (~1/√B) but increases compute.
- **Kernel choice:** Gaussian ensures monotonicity and simplifies shift modulus; other kernels may be cheaper but may require envelope for uniform bounds.
- **Bandwidth grid:** Finer grid improves uniform bound tightness but scales cost linearly; adaptive grid or multi‑start refinement mitigates this.

Failure signatures:
- DF‑UCB near 1 for all `h`: `h` too small, effective dimension too high, or kernel mismatch.
- Numerical instability: denominator in MC estimator extremely small → add small floor or increase `h`.
- Overly conservative intervals: McDiarmid constants are loose, especially for small `n,m`; consider tuning or empirical Bernstein variants.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can we automatically discover low‑dimensional projections or functions for high‑dimensional data without prior knowledge, while preserving distribution‑free validity?
2. Will assumption‑free blurred TV bounds prove practically useful for evaluating generative models, and how do they relate to existing metrics like FID?
3. What is the minimax optimal rate for estimating blurred TV in a distribution‑free setting? The current error scaling ~ √(log(1/α)/n) may not be optimal.
4. For which kernels does blurred TV decrease monotonically with bandwidth `h`? Non‑monotonicity could affect uniform‑in‑`h` bound tightness.

## Limitations
- Theoretical requirements: Results assume a known kernel (Gaussian for monotonicity) and a fixed bandwidth; adaptive choice of `h` is not fully addressed.
- Monte Carlo error: The MC approximation introduces an extra O(1/√B) term; very high dimensions may demand large `B`.
- Intrinsic dimension advantage: Proven only for linear subspaces; extension to general low‑dimensional manifolds is open.
- Conservative constants: McDiarmid‑based constants are loose, leading to overly wide intervals for small sample sizes.
- Negative result scope: The impossibility for classical TV holds over the broad class of all distributions; under restrictions (e.g., smoothness) non‑trivial bounds may still be possible.
