# A vision-language model and platform for temporally mapping surgery from video

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22583
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22583
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Halsted achieves high accuracy across multiple temporal scales (99% steps, 91% procedure ID, 70-76% action/instrument) and outperforms prior state-of-the-art on external benchmarks (AUROC 1.00 vs 0.68, 5.5× improvement on RARP-50).

## Executive Summary
The authors present Halsted, a vision-language model and web platform for automatically temporally mapping surgical activity from video. Halsted is trained on the Halsted Surgical Atlas, a multi-specialty dataset of over 650,000 annotated video clips across eight surgical specialties, expanded four-fold via an iterative self‑labelling framework. The model uses a frozen VideoMAE encoder and a two‑layer transformer decoder to generate sequences of surgical component tags conditioned on textual instructions, enabling a single model to perform diverse tasks (procedure identification, step recognition, fine‑grained action/instrument tagging, proficiency assessment). On held‑out data, Halsted reaches 99% accuracy for surgical steps, 91% for procedure identification, and 70‑76% for actions and instruments, surpassing prior SOTA (e.g., AUROC 1.00 vs 0.68 on activity recognition). The deployed platform processes hour‑long videos in minutes via a coarse‑to‑fine inference workflow, making high‑fidelity surgical mapping practical for clinical and educational use.

## Method Summary
Halsted adopts a generative vision‑language architecture. Input short video clips (1–30 s) are encoded by a frozen pre‑trained VideoMAE model; each clip is split into spatio‑temporal cubes (2×14 × 14 patches, M=8) whose embeddings are averaged to form a clip embedding. A task‑specific instruction (e.g., “map dissection activity”) is tokenized into up to 8 learned embeddings. The clip embedding and instruction embeddings are concatenated as a prefix sequence fed to a two‑layer transformer decoder with causal self‑attention. The decoder autoregressively generates a sequence of surgical tags drawn from a unified vocabulary. Training is multitask: all annotated clips from the Halsted Surgical Atlas (650k+) are mixed, and the model minimizes cross‑entropy loss on next‑token prediction. The dataset was expanded from 160k manual annotations (v1) to 650k clips (v2) by self‑labeling: the preliminary model infers on unlabelled full‑length videos, predictions are filtered by confidence thresholds and temporally merged (identical actions within 1 s), then added to the training pool. For full procedure mapping, the platform first scans 30‑second non‑overlapping windows to detect coarse phases (e.g., “suturing”). Only windows containing the target phase are re‑processed at finer temporal granularity (2‑5 second windows) to extract detailed actions, instruments, and proficiency, reducing total processing time to about 15 minutes per hour of video.

## Key Results
- Halsted achieves 99% accuracy on surgical step recognition, 91% on procedure identification, and 70‑76% on fine‑grained action and instrument recognition.
- On a constrained benchmark, it outperforms the prior state‑of‑the‑art (SAIS) with AUROC 1.00 vs 0.68 for activity recognition.
- On the external RARP‑50 dataset, Halsted demonstrates a 5.5× improvement over random chance, validating cross‑dataset generalizability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A single generative model can map multiple surgical components across temporal scales and specialties without task‑specific retraining.
- **Mechanism:** Halsted uses a transformer‑based vision‑language architecture conditioned on textual task instructions. Given a video clip embedding from a frozen pre‑trained encoder (VideoMAE) and an instruction token sequence, the decoder autoregressively generates a sequence of surgical component tags. This unified formulation allows the same model parameters to perform fundamentally different tasks by simply changing the input instruction.
- **Core assumption:** Surgical semantics across tasks and specialties share enough common structure that a single parameter set can learn a sufficiently general representation when trained jointly on all annotated data.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper states Halsted is “a generative model with outputs controlled by task‑specific instructions… jointly trained across all tasks and specialties” and that the architecture “enables a wide range of tasks to be performed within a single unified framework.”
- **Break condition:** If a new surgical task requires feature representations that are fundamentally disjoint from those learned for existing tasks (e.g., haptic feedback), joint training may cause catastrophic interference or poor performance on all tasks.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** An iterative self‑labelling strategy can expand a manually annotated surgical video dataset by ~4× while maintaining or improving model performance, enabling scaling to hundreds of thousands of clips.
- **Mechanism:** Starting with a curated manual annotation set (HSA v1), a preliminary model is trained and then deployed on unlabelled full‑length videos to generate AI‑derived annotations for 1‑second clips. These AI labels undergo post‑processing (confidence thresholding, temporal merging) to filter low‑quality predictions before being added to the training pool. The expanded dataset (HSA v2) is then used to train the final model.
- **Core assumption:** The model’s self‑generated labels, after quality control, are sufficiently accurate and diverse to serve as useful training data, effectively bootstrapping dataset growth.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper describes the self‑labelling approach and reports that “Incorporating AI‑labelled clips into training yields a statistically significant improvement in performance.”
- **Break condition:** If the model’s initial performance is too poor (<~80% accuracy as a heuristic), its self‑labels will introduce excessive noise that cannot be fully corrected by post‑processing, degrading the final model.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A hierarchical, multi‑stage inference workflow on the deployment platform can efficiently map hour‑long surgical videos within minutes by leveraging coarse‑to‑fine temporal processing.
- **Mechanism:** For a full procedure video, the model first processes large (30‑second), non‑overlapping windows to identify coarse surgical segments (e.g., “suturing”). Only the time windows flagged as containing the target activity are then re‑processed with smaller (2‑5 second) windows and more granular instructions to extract fine‑grained components (actions, instruments, proficiency). This avoids naïvely applying fine‑grained analysis to the entire video.
- **Core assumption:** Coarse surgical components are reliably detectable at long temporal scales and can serve as an effective spatiotemporal filter to focus computational resources.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper outlines the multi‑stage workflow and notes it “reduces the time‑taken to process the video… averaging 15 minutes to map a 1‑hour video.”
- **Break condition:** If the coarse‑grained detector has low precision (many false positives), the pipeline will waste resources on irrelevant segments; if it has low recall, critical fine‑grained analysis will never occur.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Vision‑Language Models (VLMs) for Sequential Output**  
  *Why needed here:* Halsted is a VLM that generates a sequence of structured tags, not just a single label. This requires understanding of autoregressive decoding (like language models) applied to video‑conditioned token generation.  
  *Quick check question:* How does conditioning on a video embedding influence the token probability distribution at each decoding step in an autoregressive VLM?

- **Concept: Multi‑Task Learning via Instruction Tuning**  
  *Why needed here:* The model is trained simultaneously on procedure classification, step recognition, action/instrument tagging, and proficiency assessment. This requires grasping how a shared parameter set can optimize for diverse objectives and how task instructions steer the model’s output head.  
  *Quick check question:* What are the key architectural components (e.g., tokenization, loss masking) that allow a single decoder to perform both classification‑like and sequence‑generation tasks?

- **Concept: Self‑Supervised Video Representation (VideoMAE)**  
  *Why needed here:* The video encoder is frozen and pre‑trained via masked autoencoding. Understanding its inductive biases (temporal modeling, spatial attention) is crucial to interpreting why it provides a strong initialization and why a lightweight decoder suffices.  
  *Quick check question:* What properties of masked autoencoding pre‑training make VideoMAE features suitable as a generic video embedding for multiple downstream surgical tasks?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Video Input -> Video Encoder (frozen VideoMAE) -> Clip Embedding -> Instruction Encoder -> Prefix Construction -> Transformer Decoder (2‑layer) -> Greedy Decoding -> Tag Sequence -> (Platform) Multi‑stage Inference -> Aggregated Report/Overlay

### Critical Path
1. **Video input** split into short clips.  
2. **VideoMAE** processes each clip; embeddings averaged from spatio‑temporal cubes.  
3. **Instruction** tokenized into learned embeddings; concatenated with clip embedding to form decoder prefix.  
4. **Transformer decoder** autoregressively generates surgical token sequence.  
5. **Greedy decoding** selects most likely token at each step until EOS.  
6. **Platform workflow** orchestrates coarse (30 s windows) and fine (2‑5 s windows) passes, then aggregates results across full video.

### Design Tradeoffs
- *Decoder size:* 44M (2‑layer) vs. 1B (Llama). Larger decoder offered no significant gain, favoring efficiency.
- *Task diversity vs. specialization:* Joint training on 650k clips across 8 specialties/16 procedures yields a generalist model but may underperform on rare procedures (e.g., cardiac) due to data imbalance.
- *Inference latency vs. comprehensiveness:* The multi‑stage platform workflow trades slightly more total compute (two passes) for dramatically reduced wall‑clock time and cost versus processing every clip at maximum granularity.

### Failure Signatures
- **Task confusion:** Model generates tags from the wrong task vocabulary (e.g., procedure name in an action recognition task). Likely cause: insufficient or ambiguous instruction tuning during training.
- **Temporal smearing:** Fine‑grained action boundaries are inaccurate or overly long. Likely cause: reliance on 1‑2 second clips without explicit temporal modeling; failure in the platform’s coarse‑to‑fine filtering.
- **Specialty blind spot:** Very low performance on procedures/specialties with minimal training data (e.g., cardiac). Evidenced by AUROC drop in Fig 2c.
- **Platform pipeline stall:** Failure in the coarse‑grained step (e.g., never detecting “suturing”) leads to no fine‑grained analysis output.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Unit test a single task:** Freeze all but the decoder head for “micro‑activity” (action/instrument). Evaluate accuracy on a held‑out set to confirm the core autoregressive tagging mechanism works in isolation before multi‑task training.
2. **Validate the self‑labelling impact:** Train two models: one on only HSA v1 (manual) and one on HSA v2 (manual + AI). Compare performance on a common manually‑annotated held‑out test set to isolate the effect of AI‑labeled data.
3. **Benchmark coarse filter precision/recall:** On a validation set, run the platform’s first stage (30‑sec windows, “suturing” detection). Measure how many windows containing suturing are detected (recall) and how many detected windows actually contain suturing (precision). This quantifies the efficiency gain of the multi‑stage approach.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to missing specialties:** How does Halsted perform on surgical specialties absent from the Halsted Surgical Atlas (e.g., neurosurgery, orthopaedics)? The model lacks exposure to unique visual features, instruments, and workflows, limiting its ability to map procedures in these domains.
- **Impact on surgeon proficiency and patient outcomes:** What is the causal effect of Halsted’s automated surgical mapping on surgeon technical proficiency, patient outcomes (e.g., complications), and surgical education? Current evidence is observational; prospective clinical trials are needed.
- **Effect of AI‑generated label noise:** To what extent do errors in AI‑generated annotations (from self‑labelling) degrade Halsted’s performance on critical tasks, and can noise‑robust training mitigate this without proportional increases in manual annotation? The cumulative impact of label noise remains unknown.
- **Zero‑shot mapping of new procedures:** Do the procedural similarity embeddings learned by Halsted reflect clinically meaningful relationships (e.g., shared anatomy, instruments), and can this enable zero‑shot mapping of entirely new surgical procedures? The model has not been tested on held‑out procedures.
- **Robustness to real‑world video variations:** How robust is Halsted to real‑world video variations (e.g., poor lighting, occlusions, open surgery) compared to the curated HSA dataset, which is largely from public domain sources? External validation is limited to a specific robotic procedure benchmark.

## Limitations
- Video preprocessing details (frame rate, resolution, cube parameters) and the adjustment to an embedding dimension of 1408 (different from standard VideoMAE) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Self‑labelling hyperparameters (confidence thresholds, temporal merging rules) are not disclosed, preventing faithful reconstruction of the dataset expansion.
- The public HSA‑27k subset may under‑represent rare procedures, risking overfitting and limiting assessment of performance on less common surgeries. Platform failure modes (e.g., coarse detector errors) are not fully characterized.

## Confidence
- **Single generative model for all tasks:** Medium (strong multi‑task results but no direct ablation against specialist models).
- **Self‑labelling expansion:** High (paper reports statistically significant performance gain from AI‑labeled clips).
- **Coarse‑to‑fine pipeline:** Medium (efficiency claim plausible but lacks comparison to exhaustive fine‑grained processing).

## Next Checks
1. **Self‑labelling ablation:** Train on HSA v1 vs. HSA v2 with identical architecture; evaluate on a fixed manual test set to isolate the impact of AI labels.
2. **Specialist vs. unified model:** For a subset of tasks, train separate single‑task models and compare accuracy/efficiency to the unified generative model.
3. **Coarse filter characterization:** Measure precision/recall of the 30 s window detector for each phase on a validation set; correlate errors with final fine‑grained coverage to quantify pipeline robustness.
