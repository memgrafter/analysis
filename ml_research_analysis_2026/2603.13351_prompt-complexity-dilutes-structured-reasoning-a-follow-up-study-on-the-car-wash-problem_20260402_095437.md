# Prompt Complexity Dilutes Structured Reasoning: A Follow-Up Study on the Car Wash Problem
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.13351
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13351
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: The STAR reasoning framework, which solved the implicit-constraint car wash problem with 100% accuracy in isolation, dropped to 0–30% accuracy when embedded in a complex 60-line production prompt.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether the STAR reasoning framework, previously shown to solve the car wash problem with near-perfect accuracy, maintains its effectiveness when embedded within a realistic production prompt. Testing three conditions—STAR-only, a production prompt with an Anthropic profile, and a production prompt with a default profile—on Claude Sonnet 4.6, the authors found STAR achieved 100% accuracy in isolation. However, when placed within a 60-line prompt that included directives like "Lead with specifics," its accuracy degraded to 0% (Anthropic profile) and 30% (default profile). The paper identifies two primary mechanisms: conclusion-first ordering forced by production directives, which reverses STAR's reason-then-conclude sequence, and attention competition in long contexts that diminishes the STAR instruction's influence. A cross-model comparison also revealed that model upgrades improve STAR's isolated performance (85% → 100%) but do not prevent dilution in complex prompts. The work demonstrates that prompt complexity via instruction competition and output ordering can completely undermine structured reasoning, turning a 100% effective technique into a 0–30% one in production environments.

## Method Summary
The experiment used the implicit-constraint car wash problem: "I want to wash my car. The car wash is 100 meters away. Should I walk or drive?" The correct answer is "drive" because the constraint is that the car must be at the car wash to be washed. Three system prompt conditions were tested: (C) a concise 10-line STAR-only prompt; (A) and (B) a 60-line production prompt that included STAR instructions (mid-prompt), persona setups, style guidelines, and the directive "Lead with specifics." Conditions A and B varied by profile (Anthropic vs. default). The model was Claude Sonnet 4.6 with temperature 0.7 and max tokens 512. Condition C was run with n=100 trials to confirm high accuracy; conditions A and B were run with n=20 each. Responses were scored against predefined pass/fail patterns to determine accuracy and constraint detection.

## Key Results
- STAR-only prompt achieved 100% accuracy (n=100).
- Production prompt with Anthropic profile yielded 0% accuracy; with default profile, 30% accuracy (n=20 each).
- Cross-model comparison showed STAR-only accuracy increased from 85% on Sonnet 4.5 to 100% on Sonnet 4.6.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Conclusion-First Ordering**  
- Claim: Production directives like "Lead with specifics" force a conclusion-first output order, reversing STAR's reason-then-conclude sequence.  
- Mechanism: Autoregressive generation conditions all subsequent tokens on early outputs. When the model is instructed to lead with specifics, it emits a conclusion (e.g., "Walk") before executing STAR's Situation/Task steps that would reveal the implicit constraint. The subsequent STAR reasoning becomes post-hoc rationalization, not genuine inference.  
- Core assumption: STAR's effectiveness depends on constraint identification occurring before conclusion generation.  
- Evidence anchors: Abstract states directive forces conclusion-first ordering. Section 7.1 shows model output "Short answer: Walk." followed by a correct STAR breakdown, proving reasoning was intact but misordered.  

**Mechanism 2: Attention Competition in Long Contexts**  
- Claim: In a 60+ line prompt, STAR's mid-prompt position subjects it to the "lost in the middle" effect, reducing its attention weight relative to surface-level formatting directives.  
- Mechanism: Transformer attention distributes focus across tokens; mid-positioned content receives less effective attention. STAR instructions compete with numerous concrete, actionable rules (word counts, exact numbers) that may attract disproportionate attention due to specificity and urgency.  
- Core assumption: Instruction priority is determined by implicit factors (position, specificity) rather than declared hierarchy.  
- Evidence anchors: Section 7.3 notes STAR sits in the middle between persona setup and communication style rules, and that instructions with surface-level urgency may receive disproportionate weight. Section 2.3 summarizes literature showing instruction priority is architecturally unreliable and influenced by position and specificity.  

**Mechanism 3: Instruction Interference and Priority Failure**  
- Claim: Competing instructions create proactive interference in the model's working memory, causing STAR to be deprioritized despite being explicitly present.  
- Mechanism: The 60-line prompt contains multiple instruction types (persona, style, format, content rules). Without a robust internal hierarchy, the model cannot reliably prioritize reasoning framework instructions over format directives. "Lead with specifics" and word-count rules win because they are easier to satisfy and align with pretraining patterns for direct answers.  
- Core assumption: LLMs lack a native mechanism to weight instructions by declared importance; they follow a first-order approximation of salience.  
- Evidence anchors: Abstract mentions "surrounded by competing instructions" and "instruction dilution." Section 2.3 states that without explicit training, LLMs treat all instruction sources equally and that explicit instructions to "focus" yield only marginal improvement. Section 7.3 notes that instructions are deprioritized relative to those that are more format-specific and easier to satisfy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Implicit constraint reasoning** – The car wash problem fails because the constraint "car must be at the car wash" is implicit. STAR works by forcing the model to articulate the Task goal before the Action, surfacing this hidden constraint. Quick check: Why does a model that correctly identifies the constraint in STAR's Task step still output "Walk" if it generates the conclusion first?  
- **Autoregressive commitment and path dependence** – Once a token is generated, it becomes part of the conditioning context for all future tokens. A premature conclusion anchors the generation trajectory, making subsequent reasoning a justification rather than a discovery. Quick check: If a model outputs "Walk" as the first word, what must its next tokens condition on, and how does this prevent constraint-driven correction?  
- **Prompt engineering as a competitive attention landscape** – A prompt is not a static instruction set but a dynamic arena where instructions compete for limited attention and working memory. Adding "helpful" directives can drown out crucial reasoning steps. Quick check: What happens to STAR's effectiveness when you add 50 lines of formatting rules, style guides, and persona details around it, and why?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
User Question Input → Profile Selector → System Prompt Assembler (`_get_system_prompt`) → LLM Inference Call → Streaming Response Parser

**Critical path**  
User question → System prompt assembly (STAR embedded mid-prompt) → LLM generation → First tokens determine answer commitment → STAR reasoning (if any) occurs after commitment.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Feature accretion vs. reasoning fidelity: Adding features (word counts, exact numbers, persona specifics) improves interview coaching but cumulatively crowds out structured reasoning.  
- Generality vs. control: The same prompt must handle dozens of question types; broad directives ("Lead with specifics") override specialized reasoning instructions.  
- Model upgrade leverage: Newer models (Sonnet 4.6) execute STAR more reliably in isolation, but this gain evaporates in complex prompts.

**Failure signatures**  
- Pattern 1: Answer token appears at generation start (e.g., "Short answer: Walk.") followed by a correct STAR breakdown.  
- Pattern 2: 0–30% pass rate on the implicit constraint problem while formatting rules are obeyed.  
- Pattern 3: Performance varies by profile content (Anthropic 0% vs default 30%), indicating semantic context modulates instruction priority.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Replication baseline: Run Condition C (STAR-only) on Sonnet 4.6 with n=100 to confirm 100% starting point.  
2. Production embedding: Run Conditions A/B (full prompt + profiles) with n=20 each to reproduce 0%/30% dilution; record first 10 tokens of every response.  
3. Directive ablation: Take production prompt, remove "Lead with specifics" and word-count constraints, keep STAR. Test if accuracy recovers toward 100%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Prompt complexity vs. accuracy curve** – Only 10-line and 60-line prompts were compared; testing intermediate complexities (20, 30, 40, 50 lines) will reveal whether degradation is linear, threshold-based, or exponential.  
2. **STAR position within prompt** – STAR occupied a mid-prompt position; moving it to the beginning or end might mitigate dilution. Positional ablation experiments are needed to distinguish between instruction conflict and positional attention bias.  
3. **Generalization to other reasoning frameworks** – It is unknown whether dilution affects Chain-of-Thought, ReAct, or Tree of Thoughts similarly. Replicating the experiment with alternative frameworks on implicit constraint tasks will test generality.  
4. **Cross-model robustness** – All experiments used Claude Sonnet; dilution may be model-specific. Testing on GPT-4, Gemini, and open-source models will determine if the phenomenon is universal.  
5. **Mechanistic cause of conclusion-first ordering** – Is it driven by attention competition, positional bias, or instruction hierarchy breakdown? Interpretability methods (e.g., attention visualization) and targeted interventions are needed to isolate the cause.

## Limitations
- Small sample sizes (n=20) for production conditions limit statistical power.
- Experiments only on Claude Sonnet; generalizability to other model families is unknown.
- Exact prompt strings and scoring patterns are not publicly released, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- STAR-only effectiveness: High
- Production dilution effect: Medium
- Conclusion-first ordering mechanism: High
- Attention competition mechanism: Medium
- Cross-model improvement (Sonnet 4.5 → 4.6): High

## Next Checks
1. Prompt reordering: Move the STAR block to the very beginning of the 60-line prompt; if accuracy recovers toward 100%, it confirms conclusion-first ordering as the primary failure mode.  
2. Directive ablation: Remove "Lead with specifics" and all word-count constraints while keeping STAR; a significant accuracy increase would isolate instruction interference from length effects.  
3. Cross-model replication: Repeat all three conditions on GPT‑4 and Claude Opus with n=100 each to test robustness across model families and improve statistical resolution.
