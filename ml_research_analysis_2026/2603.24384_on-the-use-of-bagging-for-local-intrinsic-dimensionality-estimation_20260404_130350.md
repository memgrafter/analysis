# On the Use of Bagging for Local Intrinsic Dimensionality Estimation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24384
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24384
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Bagging (subbagging) with appropriate hyperparameters reduces variance of local intrinsic dimensionality estimators, yielding lower mean squared error, especially when combined with neighborhood smoothing.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method‑agnostic bagging wrapper to reduce variance in local intrinsic dimensionality (LID) estimation. By constructing subsamples of the dataset and averaging LID estimates across them, the approach mitigates the high variance associated with small neighborhoods while carefully balancing the trade‑off with nonlocal bias. Theoretical analysis and experiments on 19 benchmark manifolds demonstrate that bagging significantly lowers mean squared error compared to baseline estimators, and further gains are achieved by incorporating neighborhood smoothing. The method is applicable to various LID estimators (MLE, TLE, MADA) and requires only a modest ensemble size (B=10).

## Method Summary
The proposed method wraps any existing local LID estimator in a bagging ensemble. For a given sampling rate \(r\) (0<r≤1), the algorithm creates \(B\) subsamples (bags) of size \(m = r·n\) without replacement. For each query point, the LID estimator is applied within each bag (excluding the query if present), producing \(B\) independent estimates which are averaged to obtain the final LID estimate. To control the effective neighborhood size, the base \(k\)-NN parameter is kept small; the interplay between \(k\) and \(r\) determines locality (effective radius ~ \(k/r\)). Additionally, optional smoothing steps—either applied within each bag before aggregation (pre‑smoothing) or after aggregation (post‑smoothing)—further reduce variance by averaging LID values over local neighborhoods. The method is agnostic to the base LID estimator and requires only moderate \(B\) (e.g., 10) and appropriate \(r\) to achieve variance reduction without incurring nonlocal bias.

## Key Results
- Bagging reduces variance and mean squared error for small \(k\) and moderate sampling rate \(r\), with minimal bias increase.
- Neighborhood smoothing (pre, post, or combined) further improves performance.
- Gains hold across multiple LID estimators (MLE, TLE, MADA) and require as few as \(B = 10\) bags.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Variance Reduction via Subbagging with Controlled Overlap**  
Subbagging reduces estimator variance by averaging decorrelated models, but the sampling rate \(r\) simultaneously controls covariance and expands the effective \(k\)-NN radius. Lowering \(r\) shrinks bag size \(m = rn\), which (i) reduces covariance between bag estimates (Theorem 1) and (ii) increases the distance needed to gather \(k\) neighbors. The former lowers variance; the latter may introduce nonlocal bias, increasing bias. The net effect on MSE depends on whether the variance reduction outweighs the bias inflation. The analysis assumes the conditional covariance \(\gamma(h,m)\) between two bag estimates given overlap \(h\) scales roughly as \(h/m\). Evidence: The abstract proposes the bagging wrapper; Theorem 1 bounds asymptotic covariance as an increasing function of \(r\) and Theorem 2 shows variance decreases with \(B\) and depends on inter‑bag correlation. Bagging fails when \(k/r\) is too high, causing excessive radius and dominant bias (observed in Figure 5).

**Mechanism 2: Neighborhood Smoothing Exploits Local Continuity**  
Smooth­ing LID estimates by averaging over a point’s \(k_s\)-NN graph further reduces variance by leveraging the local smoothness of LID on a manifold. Because LID varies slowly locally, averaging suppresses random fluctuations. Smoothing can be applied inside each bag before aggregation (pre‑smoothing), after aggregation (post‑smoothing), or both; each yields cumulative gains. The core assumption is that nearby points on the same manifold share similar intrinsic dimensions. Evidence: The abstract mentions systematic additional gains from combining bagging with smoothing; Section 5.4 defines the variants and Figure 3 shows a consistent performance ordering (baseline < bagged < smoothed < bagged+post < bagged+pre < bagged+pre+post). Smoothing is a well‑known variance‑reduction technique (e.g., scale‑space filtering). Excessive smoothing (large \(k_s\)) can blur manifold boundaries and increase bias.

**Mechanism 3: Joint Hyperparameter Control of Locality and Variance**  
The interaction between base neighborhood size \(k\) and sampling rate \(r\) governs the effective locality of the bagged estimator. Intuitively, the effective radius scales as \(k/r\) because the bag’s point density is proportional to \(r\). A smaller \(r\) inflates the radius for fixed \(k\), threatening locality; a smaller \(k\) shrinks it. Thus, keeping \(k/r\) modest ensures the neighborhood remains local while benefiting from variance reduction via lower \(r\) and sufficient \(B\). This relation assumes local uniformity: the expected distance to the \(k\)-th neighbor in a subsample of size \(m\) is \(\propto (k/m)^{1/d} = (k/(rn))^{1/d}\). Evidence: The abstract emphasizes the interplay among \(r\), \(k\), and \(B\); Figure 5 visualizes MSE differences across \((k,r)\) pairs, showing a roughly linear boundary in \(\log(k/r)\) separating regimes where bagging helps (blue) and hurts (red). The ratio \(k/r\) is not theoretically derived but correlates with performance in experiments.

## Foundational Learning
- **Local Intrinsic Dimensionality (LID):** LID measures the effective number of degrees of freedom in an infinitesimal neighborhood around a query point. - **Bias‑Variance Tradeoff in LID Estimation:** \(k\)-NN‑based LID estimators face a fundamental compromise: small \(k\) preserves locality (low bias) but uses few points (high variance); large \(k\) reduces variance but risks mixing points from different manifolds (high bias). *Quick check:* “If you halve the neighborhood size \(k\) while keeping the dataset fixed, what happens to the bias and variance of the LID estimate?”

## Architecture Onboarding
The method wraps any LID estimator in a bagging ensemble with optional smoothing.

**Component Map:**  
Input (dataset, base LID estimator, hyperparameters) → Bag Sampler (creates B subsamples) → LID Estimator (applied per bag per query) → Aggregator (averages B estimates) → (optional) Smoother (pre‑ or post‑smoothing) → Output LID values.

**Critical Path:** For a given query \(q\): (1) For each bag \(i=1..B\): if \(q\) is in the bag, compute LID on the bag without \(q\); otherwise use the bag as is. Find the \(k\)-nearest neighbors of \(q\) within the bag and apply the base LID formula. (2) Average the \(B\) results to obtain the bagged estimate. (3) If smoothing is requested, apply pre‑smoothing within each bag before step 2, or apply post‑smoothing to the final average, or both.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- \(B\): Larger \(B\) reduces variance with diminishing returns; runtime increases linearly.  
- \(r\): Lower \(r\) increases decorrelation (good) but inflates effective neighborhood radius (bad). Recommended range \([r_{\min}, 0.5]\); lower bound ensures bags remain representative.  
- \(k\): Smaller \(k\) preserves locality; paper suggests \(k \in [5,10]\). Larger \(k\) may be used with higher \(r\) to keep \(k/r\) modest.  
- Smoothing: Pre‑smoothing yields larger gains but requires per‑bag neighbor searches (higher memory/compute); post‑smoothing is cheaper but less effective; combining both is additive.  
- Complexity: Naïve \(k\)-NN gives \(O(B r n^2)\); indexing reduces to \(O(B n \log(rn))\). Smoothing adds \(O(B k_s)\) per query for pre‑smoothing.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Bagged MSE > baseline: likely \(k/r\) too high; check heatmap (e.g., Figure 5).  
- High variance across bags: insufficient \(B\) or \(r\) too close to 1 (bags highly correlated). Increase \(B\) or decrease \(r\).  
- Smoothing hurts performance: \(k_s\) too large, oversmoothing across manifolds; reduce \(k_s\) or use geodesic distances.  
- Memory explosion: storing \(B\) full bags may be heavy; sample bags on the fly or reuse a single set of bags across queries.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **Variance‑\(r\) curve:** Fix \(k=10\), vary \(r\) across a range (e.g., 0.04–0.6), compute MSE, variance, and bias for the bagged estimator (replicating Figure 4). Confirm variance decreases monotonically with \(r\) while bias rises slightly.  
2. **\(k\)‑\(r\) interaction grid:** Sweep \(k\) (e.g., 5–72) and \(r\) (0.04–0.6) on a dataset, compute \(\log(\text{MSE}_\text{baseline}/\text{MSE}_\text{bagged})\), and reproduce the heatmap in Figure 5. Identify the blue region where bagging improves MSE and verify it aligns with small \(k/r\).  
3. **Smoothing combination test:** Compare baseline, bagged, standalone smoothed, bagged+post‑smoothing, bagged+pre‑smoothing, and bagged+pre+post‑smoothing on 2–3 manifolds. Verify the performance ordering (Figure 3) and measure runtime overhead of each smoothing configuration.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis relies on local uniformity and covariance assumptions that may not hold on real‑world data.  
- Evaluation is restricted to 19 synthetic manifolds; behavior on noisy, high‑dimensional, or mixed‑manifold datasets remains unknown.  
- Hyperparameters (\(k\), \(r\), \(B\), smoothing neighbors) are sensitive; robust default ranges for diverse data distributions are not fully established.  
- Bagging with smoothing significantly increases computational and memory costs, limiting scalability to large \(n\) or high‑dimensional feature spaces.

## Confidence
- Bagging reduces variance while slightly increasing bias: **High** confidence.  
- Neighborhood smoothing yields systematic additional gains: **Medium** confidence.  
- The ratio \(k/r\) governs effective locality and performance: **Low** confidence.
