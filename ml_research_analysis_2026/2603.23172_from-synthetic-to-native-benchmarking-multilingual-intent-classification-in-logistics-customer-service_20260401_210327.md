# From Synthetic to Native: Benchmarking Multilingual Intent Classification in Logistics Customer Service

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23172
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23172
- **Reference Count:** 37
- **Primary Result:** Machine-translated test sets consistently overestimate real-world multilingual intent classification performance on native customer queries, with accuracy drops of up to 5–10% for long-tail intents and zero-shot transfer.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a critical evaluation gap in multilingual intent classification for logistics customer service: standard benchmarks using machine-translated text overestimate model robustness compared to deployment on real, noisy native user queries. To address this, the authors introduce a new benchmark derived from ~30K de-identified real customer logs, featuring a two-level intent taxonomy (13 parent, 17 leaf intents) and paired native/translated test sets across multiple languages. Experiments reveal that performance on translated data is inflated, particularly for long-tail intents and zero-shot cross-lingual transfer. Notably, lightweight causal language models (e.g., Gemma) demonstrate smaller robustness gaps and often match or exceed the performance of larger multilingual encoders (e.g., XLM-R) under native evaluation conditions.

## Method Summary
The study builds a benchmark from 600K historical logistics customer service logs, yielding ~30K de-identified queries split 20:1:1 into train, development, and test sets. The taxonomy defines 13 parent and 17 leaf intents. The training set covers English, Spanish, and Arabic (seen languages), while test sets include native and machine-translated (via TranslateGemma-27B) versions for these and zero-shot languages (Indonesian, Chinese, Thai). The native test set consists of 1.3K human-verified multilingual examples. Models evaluated include multilingual encoders (mBERT, XLM-R, mmBERT), decoder-only SLMs (Gemma 3, Qwen 3), and closed-source LLMs. Training follows standard fine-tuning protocols with task-specific hyperparameters; for hierarchical intent classification, constrained decoding is applied for SLMs. Primary metrics are accuracy and micro-F1, with macro-F1 used to assess long-tail performance.

## Key Results
- Machine-translated evaluation consistently overestimates performance on native queries, with accuracy drops of 5–10% observed across languages and model architectures.
- The synthetic-native gap is most pronounced for long-tail leaf intents and zero-shot cross-lingual transfer, where models trained on clean or translated data struggle with the lexical noise in real user messages.
- Lightweight causal SLMs (e.g., Gemma 3 270M) often match or outperform similarly sized masked encoders (e.g., mmBERT) on native test sets and exhibit smaller robustness gaps.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Machine-translated evaluation overestimates real-world robustness because synthetic text lacks the lexical noise (shorthand, misspellings, colloquialisms) present in native user queries.
- **Mechanism:** The benchmark's paired native/translated test sets isolate a distributional shift. Translated queries are generated from a pre-filtered, high-quality source subset and normalized by translation systems, removing surface-level variability that production systems must handle.
- **Core assumption:** Native customer queries inherently contain more linguistic noise and non-standard forms than their translated counterparts.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states translated text is "cleaner and more standardized" than native requests. Section 3.2 describes the paired design, noting translated sets "preserve intent semantics while removing much of the noise." Corpus context shows prior work on customer service NLU, but evidence is primarily from the constructed benchmark.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The synthetic-to-native gap is amplified for long-tail intents and zero-shot transfer due to reduced lexical/semantic support in limited training data.
- **Mechanism:** Long-tail classes have fewer examples, making models rely on superficial lexical cues. Native queries for these intents exhibit greater variability and domain-specific shorthands not captured in cleaner translated sets. For zero-shot transfer, models lack in-language exposure to native noise patterns entirely.
- **Core assumption:** Model performance on low-resource intents is more brittle to input distribution shifts.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes the gap is "especially for long-tail intents and cross-lingual transfer." Section 5.2 discusses higher translated performance in zero-shot settings. Section 3.3 characterises the 17-way leaf task as more long-tailed with "naturally occurring noise."

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Architectural shifts toward lightweight causal SLMs can mitigate the robustness gap relative to traditional multilingual masked encoders.
- **Mechanism:** Causal SLMs, even when small, are trained on more diverse, naturally occurring text and generate structured outputs natively, improving generalisation to noisy native distributions compared to encoders fine-tuned on cleaner parallel corpora.
- **Core assumption:** Pre-training data diversity and generation-based decoding provide implicit robustness to lexical variation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.2 shows Gemma 3 270M outperforms similarly sized mmBERT in Chinese native evaluation and notes SLMs "often matched or outperformed" encoders. It also highlights that causal LLMs natively support hierarchical decoding.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Hierarchical Intent Classification**
  - **Why needed:** Logistics systems use a two-level taxonomy (parent/leaf intents) for coarse-to-fine routing. Models must handle both flat 17-way prediction and structured hierarchy decoding.
  - **Quick check:**Why might predicting a leaf intent under a parent constraint improve operational reliability compared to a flat 17-way model?

- **Concept: Zero-Shot Cross-Lingual Transfer**
  - **Why needed:** Deployment often requires supporting new languages before collecting labeled data. Models must leverage seen-language training to generalise to unseen native queries.
  - **Quick check:**What factors explain why zero-shot transfer performance drops more on native queries than on translated ones for the same unseen language?

- **Concept: Synthetic Data Gap in Multilingual Evaluation**
  - **Why needed:** Using machine-translated test sets is common but misleading. Understanding this gap is crucial for realistic robustness estimation.
  - **Quick check:**How does the paper's "business-rewritten" native-style variant help disentangle translation effects from native noise?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Pipeline -> Model Zoo -> Evaluation Engine
- **Critical path:** 
  1. Replicate train/dev splits from released data.
  2. Fine-tune baseline model (e.g., Gemma 3 270M) on seen languages (EN/ES/AR) using provided prompts/hyperparameters.
  3. Run inference on native and translated test sets for all target languages (including zero-shot ones).
  4. Compute synthetic-native gaps per language/task; validate against Table 2 patterns.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Flat vs. Hierarchical: Flat classification is simpler but ignores operational taxonomy; hierarchical decoding improves fine-grained accuracy but requires constrained generation.
  - Encoder vs. SLM: Encoders offer low-latency fine-tuning; SLMs provide flexible zero-shot and native hierarchy support but may have higher inference cost.
  - Synthetic vs. Native Eval: Synthetic sets are controlled and cheap; native sets are essential for realism but expensive to curate.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Model shows >5% accuracy drop from translated to native test on seen languages.
  - Zero-shot transfer to native unseen languages performs near random on long-tail leaf intents.
  - Encoder-based models (mBERT, XLM-R) show larger native robustness gaps than similarly sized SLMs (Gemma 3 270M).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce synthetic-native gap on Chinese: Fine-tune Gemma 3 270M on EN/ES/AR train. Evaluate on ZH translated vs native 17-way flat. Expect ~1.9% drop.
  2. Test hierarchical decoding benefit: Compare 17-way flat vs. hierarchical on native ZH test. Expect minor gain or parity.
  3. Zero-shot native robustness check: Evaluate mmBERT and Gemma 3 270M on Indonesian native 17-way test. Expect Gemma to outperform mmBERT.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How do different machine translation systems and source filtering strategies affect the synthetic-to-native robustness gap?
- Which specific noise characteristics in native logistics queries most degrade model accuracy, and how does this interact with intent frequency?
- What architectural or training factors explain the superior robustness of causal LMs over masked LMs on noisy native queries?

## Limitations
- Domain generalisability is unknown; logistics queries may not represent other customer service domains.
- Synthetic sets depend on a single translator (TranslateGemma-27B); other MT systems might yield different gaps.
- Full replication is blocked by unavailable models (mmBERT, EmbeddingGemma) and a small native test set (1.3k).
- The specific noise types lost in translation are not systematically measured.

## Confidence
- **Synthetic overestimation:** High (consistent drops across languages/models).
- **Long-tail/zero-shot amplification:** Medium (supported but limited by test size).
- **SLM robustness advantage:** Medium (contingent on training recipes; not proven universal).

## Next Checks
1. Quantify lexical differences between native and synthetic queries (e.g., OOV rate, typo frequency, slang detection) to confirm noise removal.
2. Augment encoder training with simulated native noise (misspellings, abbreviations) and measure synthetic-native gap reduction.
3. Replicate paired evaluation using multiple MT systems (e.g., M2M-100, ChatGPT) to test gap robustness across translators.
