# On the Sample Efficiency of Inverse Dynamics Models for Semi-Supervised Imitation Learning

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.02762  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02762  
- **Reference count:** 37  
- **Primary result:** VM-IDM and IDM labeling converge to the same policy with infinite unlabeled data; IDM-based methods are more sample-efficient than behavior cloning due to lower complexity and reduced stochasticity of the IDM.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles semi-supervised imitation learning (SSIL) where only a small labeled demonstration set and a large unlabeled interaction set are available. The authors focus on approaches that learn an inverse dynamics model (IDM) to convert unlabeled trajectories into labeled training data. They present a theoretical analysis proving that two widely used methods—VM-IDM (which employs a video model to infer actions) and IDM labeling (which directly learns an IDM from pseudo-labels)—converge to the same policy under the assumption of infinite unlabeled data and model realizability. Through experiments on ProcGen (discrete control), Push-T, and Libero (continuous control), they find that IDM-based policies consistently outperform standard behavior cloning. They attribute this sample efficiency to two factors: the ground-truth IDM typically resides in a lower complexity hypothesis class than the expert policy, and it is less stochastic. These properties make the IDM easier to learn accurately and produce cleaner labels for policy training. Building on these insights, the authors propose LAPO+, which unifies video and action modeling in a single architecture, showing improved performance over prior latent action methods. The work provides both theoretical and empirical evidence for the benefits of IDM-based SSIL and suggests future directions for scaling to more realistic settings.

## Method Summary
The paper studies two pipelines for leveraging unlabeled data in imitation learning. In **IDM labeling**, an inverse dynamics model is first trained on a small labeled set or on pseudo-labels derived from a warm-started IDM. This model then annotates the entire unlabeled dataset with action labels. A policy network is finally trained via behavior cloning on the combined labeled data. In **VM-IDM**, a video model predicting next state given state and action is learned. Actions for unlabeled transitions are inferred by optimizing the video model to produce the observed next state, effectively generating high-quality pseudo-labels. Both approaches yield an IDM-based policy. The authors further introduce **LAPO+**, which uses a unified model that simultaneously predicts future states and actions, allowing shared representation learning and more stable training. Theoretical results show that, under realizability and with infinite unlabeled data, both VM-IDM and IDM labeling converge to the same optimal policy. Empirically, the methods are evaluated on ProcGen, Push-T, and Libero, with analyses linking performance to IDM complexity and stochasticity.

## Key Results
- **Theoretical convergence:** VM-IDM and IDM labeling produce identical policies given infinite unlabeled data.
- **Two-factor explanation:** The sample efficiency advantage of IDM-based methods over behavior cloning correlates with (1) lower complexity of the IDM hypothesis class relative to the policy, and (2) lower stochasticity of the IDM compared to the expert policy.
- **LAPO+ improvement:** A unified video-action model yields gains over prior latent action algorithms, confirming the benefit of shared representations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism behind the sample efficiency of IDM-based SSIL is twofold. First, the true inverse dynamics function is often simpler than the expert policy; neural networks thus learn it more accurately from limited data, partly due to an implicit bias toward simpler functions. Second, the IDM is typically more deterministic (less stochastic) than the policy, which reduces noise in the pseudo-labels used to train the final policy. These properties ensure that the unlabeled data, once labeled by the IDM, provides a higher-quality training set than raw demonstrations alone, leading to better sample efficiency. The convergence result further assures that both VM-IDM and IDM labeling ultimately exploit these properties to arrive at the same optimal policy under sufficient data.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** DU (unlabeled trajectories) -> [Dynamics Model] -> Pseudolabels -> BC -> Policy. The Dynamics Model can be an IDM (for IDM labeling) or a Video Model + Action Inference (for VM-IDM). LAPO+ uses a Unified Model that combines both roles.

**Critical path:** Unlabeled data is fed into a dynamics model to generate action labels; these labels are merged with the small labeled set; a policy network is trained via behavior cloning on the merged dataset. The dynamics model may be trained iteratively (IDM labeling) or jointly with video prediction (LAPO+).

**Design tradeoffs:** VM-IDM typically produces more accurate pseudo-labels because the video model can be learned reliably and action inference is flexible, but it incurs computational overhead during inference. IDM labeling is simpler and faster but can suffer from error propagation if the initial IDM is poor. LAPO+ attempts to get the best of both by sharing representations, but may require careful tuning and can underperform when data is abundant.

**Failure signatures:** (1) Performance collapse when unlabeled data contains suboptimal or out-of-distribution trajectories, as the IDM may produce incorrect labels. (2) Instability in VM-IDM if video model predictions are inaccurate, leading to poor action inference. (3) LAPO+ may not scale to high-data regimes where simpler methods suffice.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What explains the empirical performance gap between VM-IDM and IDM labeling when the unlabeled dataset size is finite (as opposed to the infinite limit case where they are proven equivalent)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "Finally, our work focuses on the sample efficiency of IDM learning w.r.t. action labels. We did not investigate the difference in generalization between VM-IDM and IDM labeling in the finite DU setting, which might be crucial to fully understand the differences between both methods." (Section 6, Discussion)
- Why unresolved: The paper proves VM-IDM and IDM labeling converge to the same policy in the limit of infinite unlabeled data, but empirically they show different performance in finite data regimes (Section 5.2 notes IDM labeling trails VM-IDM). Understanding this gap is important for method selection in practice.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying DU size while measuring both methods' performance, theoretical analysis of finite-sample generalization differences, and ablation studies isolating factors like VM generalization vs. IDM labeling noise propagation.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How robust is the sample efficiency advantage of IDM-based policies when the unlabeled dataset DU is collected from a different distribution (e.g., different expert, environment variations) than the labeled dataset DL?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "First, our modeling and experiments assume a curated DU which only includes expert data sampled precisely from the task of interest. A more practical setting would be when DU is sampled from an environment/expert that differs from the one of interest used to sample DL." (Section 6, Discussion)
- Why unresolved: The paper's theoretical analysis and experiments assume DU is sampled from the same expert distribution as DL. In real-world scenarios, DU often comes from different sources (e.g., internet videos) with distribution shifts, which could break the IDM sample efficiency advantage.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with synthetic distribution shifts in ProcGen/Push-T/Libero, theoretical bounds on IDM performance under covariate shift, and empirical studies on diverse video datasets with varying quality/expertise levels.

### Open Question 3
- Question: To what extent does the implicit simplicity bias of overparameterized neural networks contribute to the sample efficiency of IDM learning compared to BC, and can this bias be quantified or controlled?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper discusses the simplicity bias of neural networks as an explanation for why high-capacity VM-IDM outperforms high-capacity BC (Section 4.1), but notes "the reasons why neural networks are implicitly biased towards simpler functions are not fully understood." They hypothesize this bias favors the simpler h∗ over π∗.
- Why unresolved: While the simplicity bias is a known phenomenon, its precise interaction with the relative complexities of IDM vs policy is not quantified. This could help explain when IDM advantages will appear with modern architectures.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical measurements of simplicity (e.g., function smoothness, norm) for learned IDMs vs policies across tasks, controlled experiments with architectures that have different implicit biases, or theoretical analysis linking implicit bias to the complexity gap.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What architectural or algorithmic modifications can maximize the sample efficiency gains from the lower complexity and stochasticity of IDMs in semi-supervised imitation learning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes LAPO+ and explores UV A, but states: "These present promising avenues for future work." (Section 6) They also note LAPO/LAPO+ underperform in high-data regimes, suggesting trade-offs.
- Why unresolved: While they identify two factors (complexity, stochasticity), the translation of these insights into practical improvements is still preliminary. Understanding how to best exploit the IDM's properties could lead to more efficient SSIL algorithms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic architecture search for IDM-friendly designs, theoretical guidance on capacity allocation between IDM and policy, and extensive benchmarks measuring sample efficiency across diverse tasks to identify patterns.

### Open Question 5
- Question: How do the complexity and stochasticity factors vary across different domains (e.g., vision-based vs. state-based, discrete vs. continuous actions) and can they predict when IDM-based methods will significantly outperform BC?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] In Section 5, they analyze complexity and stochasticity across ProcGen environments and find correlations with performance gaps, but they note: "This presents a valuable framework for understanding when and to what extent IDM-based learning can outperform BC." However, they only study limited domains.
- Why unresolved: Their analysis is primarily on ProcGen (discrete actions) and Push-T/Libero (continuous), but not exhaustive. A more comprehensive study could establish predictive principles for method selection.
- What evidence would resolve it: A large-scale study across diverse benchmarks measuring the complexity/stochasticity of π∗ and h∗, correlating with IDM vs BC performance, and possibly deriving theoretical bounds based on these properties.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes infinite unlabeled data and exact optimization; real-world finite-sample and optimization errors are not addressed.
- The explanation for IDM sample efficiency (lower IDM complexity, reduced stochasticity) relies on empirical correlations; causality and exhaustive factor coverage remain open.
- Experimental validation is confined to three simulated benchmarks, limiting generalizability to real-world or high-dimensional domains.
