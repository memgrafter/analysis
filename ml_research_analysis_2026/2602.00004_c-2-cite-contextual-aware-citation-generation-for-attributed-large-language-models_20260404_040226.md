# C$^2$-Cite: Contextual-Aware Citation Generation for Attributed Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.00004
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00004
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** C²-Cite++ achieves a 5.8% average improvement in Citation-F1 score and a 17.4% improvement in response correctness over the SOTA baseline Front on the ALCE benchmark across three datasets using Llama3-8B.

## Executive Summary
Attributed large language models (LLMs) often treat citation markers as inert placeholders, resulting in disjointed references and poor integration of retrieved knowledge. The C²-Cite framework addresses this by introducing a contextual citation alignment mechanism that imbues each citation token with the semantic content of its referenced document and trains the model to actively align markers to their sources. On the ALCE benchmark, C²-Cite++ (an enhanced variant) yields substantial gains: a 5.8% rise in Citation-F1 and a 17.4% boost in response correctness compared to the previous state-of-the-art. Ablation studies confirm the essential roles of its contextual-aware embeddings and attention augmentation, demonstrating that making citations “active knowledge pointers” significantly improves both attribution accuracy and answer quality.

## Method Summary
The paper does not include explicit reproduction notes; however, the methodology is fully described in Section 3. C²-Cite comprises three core innovations: (1) Contextual-Aware Embedding, which replaces the standard embedding of a citation token `<c_i>` with a mean-pooled representation of its corresponding retrieved document `d[i]`; (2) a trainable Citation Router that classifies each generated token as either a default word or a citation and, for the latter, aligns it to the correct source document through a learned similarity metric; and (3) an Attention Augmentation loss that encourages stronger attention from subsequent non‑citation tokens to the immediately preceding citation token, thereby tightening the link between claims and evidence. During fine‑tuning, these components are optimized jointly with a weighted sum of language modeling, router classification, citation alignment, and attention losses. At inference, the model processes the query and retrieved documents with enriched citation tokens and generates responses where any produced citation markers are decoded by the router to point to specific sources, providing explicit provenance.

## Key Results
- Average 5.8% improvement in Citation-F1 score across three ALCE datasets.
- 17.4% improvement in response correctness over the SOTA baseline Front.
- Ablation studies show that both Contextual-Aware Embedding and Attention Augmentation are critical; removing either causes a marked drop in performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Replacing generic citation placeholder tokens with embeddings that encode the semantic content of their referenced document mitigates the model's tendency to treat citations as inert symbols.
- **Mechanism:** During fine-tuning, the standard embedding for a citation marker (e.g., `<c_i>`) is replaced by a mean-pooled embedding of the tokens from its corresponding retrieved document `d[i]`. This forces the model's forward pass to directly process source document semantics when it encounters a citation token, establishing a learned association between the token's position and the content it is meant to reference.
- **Core assumption:** The model's generation quality is degraded because pre‑training does not provide a meaningful learned representation for special citation tokens; injecting document semantics directly into the token's embedding creates a usable signal.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "...explicitly integrates the semantic relationships between citation markers and their referenced content... encodes the retrieved document contexts into the symbol representation of citations..."
  - [section 3.3] "We normalize citation markers... we compute the contextual embedding from the corresponding document d[i] to replace its symbolic embedding... this operation mitigates potential citation errors..."
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., *HLM-Cite*, *LongCite*) also explore enriched token representations for citations, supporting the premise that standard embeddings are insufficient.
- **Break condition:** If the retrieved document context is noisy or irrelevant, the injected semantic signal would be misleading, potentially harming generation more than using a neutral placeholder.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A trainable citation router that classifies each decoded token as either a default word or a citation marker, and aligns the latter to a specific source document via a learned similarity metric, teaches the model the functional role of citation tokens.
- **Mechanism:** A binary classifier (Eq. 3) predicts token type. For tokens classified as citations, their hidden state is transformed (`z_t`) and compared via dot product (Softmax) against a matrix of all contextual citation embeddings (`C`). The loss (`L_citation`) forces the model to produce a hidden state for a citation token that is most similar to the embedding of the *correct* source document it should point to.
- **Core assumption:** The model can learn to generate a specific "pointer" signal in its hidden state for a citation token that corresponds to a particular document's context, separate from generating natural language.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "...aligns the marker numbers by decoding information from a citation router function... transform citation markers from generic placeholders into active knowledge pointers."
  - [section 3.4] "The citation router is optimized by a binary cross‑entropy loss... we compare all contextual‑aware citation embeddings... to classify the citation token into the true citation marker number."
  - [corpus] The *SelfCite* neighbor paper uses self‑supervised alignment for attribution, indicating this router/alignment approach is part of a recognized design pattern.
- **Break condition:** If the model's capacity is insufficient or training data lacks clear citation boundaries, the router may fail to learn a discriminative mapping, leading to incorrect document alignment.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** An attention augmentation loss that increases attention weights from non‑citation tokens to immediately preceding citation tokens strengthens the local semantic coherence between a claim and its cited support.
- **Mechanism:** During training, for any default token `t_j` generated after a citation token `t_c`, the model's attention score `S(t_j, t_c)` is pushed towards a target value `Â(t_j, t_c)` that is inversely proportional to the token distance (Eq. 10). This explicitly biases the model's attention pattern to link evidence to the statements it supports.
- **Core assumption:** The native self‑attention mechanism in LLMs does not reliably create strong, immediate links between a citation marker and the textual claim it is annotating, leading to disjointed text. Directly optimizing this link improves integration.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "...transforming citation markers from passive placeholders into active knowledge pointers."
  - [section 3.5] "The attention score... is constrained to approximate the target attention score... using inverse distance weighting... This ensures that our model better integrate the contextual information of citations when generating subsequent tokens."
  - [section 5.4/Fig. 6] The visualized heatmaps show stronger `S2 -> S1` attention in C²-Cite vs. the native model when a citation separates the sentences.
- **Break condition:** Over‑enforcement could make the model overly dependent on the immediately preceding citation, reducing its ability to integrate information from multiple sources for a single claim or to generate fluid text without constant citation scaffolding.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**  
  *Why needed here:* C²-Cite is fundamentally an *attributed* RAG system. Understanding the baseline RAG paradigm (retrieve‑then‑generate) is essential to see where C²-Cite innovates: not in retrieval quality, but in *how the retrieved content is grounded* through citation symbols during generation.  
  *Quick check question:* In a standard RAG pipeline, when and how is the retrieved document content typically introduced to the LLM? How does C²-Cite change this integration?

- **Concept: Transformer Token Embeddings & Special Tokens**  
  *Why needed here:* The core technical innovation manipulates the *embedding* of a special token (`<c_i>`). One must understand that in a standard Transformer, each token ID maps to a fixed, learned embedding vector. C²‑Cite dynamically overrides this for citation tokens, a non‑standard modification to the input embedding layer.  
  *Quick check question:* How does a standard decoder‑only LLM like Llama3 represent a special token (e.g., [UNK], [SEP]) during processing? What would it mean to "replace" that representation on the fly for specific instances?

- **Concept: Supervised Fine‑Tuning Loss Functions (CE, BCE)**  
  *Why needed here:* The framework combines multiple loss functions: standard cross‑entropy (`L_default`) for language modeling, binary cross‑entropy (`L_router`) for token type classification, and another cross‑entropy (`L_citation`) for document alignment. An engineer must grasp how these losses are aggregated (Eq. 12) and what each component trains the model to do.  
  *Quick check question:* If `L_citation` is the dominant loss term, what behavior would you expect the model to prioritize at the potential expense of fluent language generation?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (data flow):**  
Input Processor -> Contextual Embedding Module -> Modified Transformer Decoder -> (Citation Router Head & Citation Alignment Head) -> Loss Aggregator  
*Auxiliary:* Decoder attention scores feed into Contextual Attentive Augmentation Module, whose loss also contributes to Loss Aggregator.

**Critical path (training flow):**  
1. Prepare fine‑tuning data: (query, retrieved docs `D`, response `R` with `[i]` markers).  
2. For each batch, construct input IDs with special tokens `<c_i>`.  
3. Pre‑embedding hack: Dynamically compute contextual embeddings for each `<c_i>` from its `d[i]` and override the embedding lookup for that specific token position.  
4. Forward pass through decoder.  
5. For every output position `t`:  
   - Get `h_t`.  
   - Compute `r_t` (router logits) → `L_router`.  
   - If `r_t` indicates citation: compute `z_t`, compare to fixed matrix `C` → `p_c` → `L_citation`.  
   - If `r_t` indicates default: standard LM head → `L_default`.  
6. Compute `L_attn` by scanning generated token pairs and comparing their actual attention to the distance‑based target.  
7. Sum losses, backpropagate. The contextual embeddings `c_i` (from mean‑pooling) are detached and fixed; only decoder, router, and alignment head weights are updated.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Precision vs. fluency: Heavy weighting on `L_citation`/`L_router` may improve citation accuracy but disrupt `L_default`, harming response correctness.  
- Context length vs. cost: All retrieved documents must be processed to compute their `c_i` embeddings before generation, increasing memory; mean‑pooling is a cheap compression, more sophisticated fusion would be costlier.  
- Static vs. dynamic context: Document context is encoded once into `c_i` at the start and does not evolve during generation, potentially missing nuanced, claim‑specific extracts.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. **Citation token hallucination:** Model generates `[1]` but router confidence is low, or alignment incorrectly maps `[1]` to the wrong document. Check router confidence scores and `p_c` distributions.  
2. **Choppy/ incoherent response:** Excessive focus on `L_attn` or poor `L_default` loss weighting causes overuse of citations or broken narrative flow. Monitor fluency metrics or human evaluation.  
3. **Context mismatch:** If retrieved docs are poor, injected `c_i` embeddings contain misleading semantics; the model may generate factually incorrect statements that are well‑cited to a bad source. Citation quality (F1) might stay high while correctness drops.

**First 3 experiments (from paper logic):**  
1. **Ablation Study (Section 5.2, Table 2):** Remove Contextual‑Aware Embedding (w/o CAE) and Contextual Attentive Augment (w/o Attn). Directly tests the causal contribution of Mechanisms 1 and 3. Expect large drop in Citation‑F1, especially for CAE removal (~25%).  
2. **Main Results Comparison (Section 4.2, Table 1):** Compare C²‑Cite++ against SOTA (Front) and other paradigms (P‑ICL, I‑ICL). Validates that the integrated mechanism yields superior joint performance on citation quality and correctness.  
3. **Attention Visualization (Section 5.4, Fig. 6):** Compare attention heatmaps of native LLM vs. C²‑Cite++. Provides qualitative evidence for Mechanism 3, showing stronger cross‑sentence attention across citation boundaries.

## Limitations
- Generalization: Performance is only evaluated on the ALCE benchmark with Llama3‑8B; results may not hold for other model architectures, languages, or domains.  
- Context simplification: Mean‑pooling of retrieved documents into a single embedding may oversimplify semantics, especially for long documents, and can propagate noise from imperfect retrieval.  
- Attention rigidity: The attention augmentation loss might force overly rigid patterns, potentially degrading narrative fluency when multiple citations support a single claim.

## Confidence
- **Core improvement claim (C²‑Cite++ outperforms SOTA):** High  
- **Independent contributions of the three mechanisms:** Medium  
- **Broad generalizability beyond evaluated settings:** Low
