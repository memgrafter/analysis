# From Weighting to Modeling: A Nonparametric Estimator for Off-Policy Evaluation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09436
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09436
- Reference count: 10
- Primary result: Nonparametric weighting (NW) reduces variance compared to IPW without increasing bias; model-assisted NW (MNW) further lowers RMSE relative to doubly robust estimator, and both are robust to errors in estimated logging policy probabilities.

## Executive Summary
Off-policy evaluation in contextual bandits aims to estimate the performance of a target policy using historical data logged by a different behavior policy. Standard inverse probability weighting (IPW) suffers from high variance due to extreme importance weights, especially when the behavior policy assigns very small probabilities to some actions. This paper introduces a nonparametric weighting (NW) approach that replaces the unstable inverse probabilities with a smooth, nonparametric model of the relationship between weighted rewards and behavior policy probabilities. Using P-splines, NW estimates the function fπ(p) such that π_a r ≈ fπ(p_a) and evaluates the target value by averaging fitted values over actions. A model-assisted extension (MNW) incorporates a reward prediction model to further reduce variance, akin to doubly robust methods but without relying on its specific robustness guarantees. Theoretical analysis provides convergence rates for bias and mean squared error. Empirical results on multi-class classification benchmarks show that NW achieves markedly lower RMSE than IPW while maintaining comparable bias, and MNW improves upon doubly robust estimators. Both methods demonstrate robustness to errors in the estimated behavior policy probabilities, making them attractive for practical off-policy evaluation.

## Method Summary
The core idea is to model the target policy's value nonparametrically rather than directly inverting estimated propensities. For the nonparametric weighting (NW) estimator, the authors posit that the product of the target policy probability and reward, π_a r, is a smooth function fπ of the behavior policy probability p_a. Given logged data, they construct the response y = π_a r and regress it on p_a using P-splines, yielding an estimate f̂π(p). The off-policy value estimate is then the average of f̂π(p_a) over all actions and contexts. The model-assisted version (MNW) first fits a reward model μ̂_a to predict rewards, then uses the residual π_a (r - μ̂_a) as response in the same nonparametric regression, obtaining ĝπ(p). The final estimate combines ĝπ(p_a) with the reward model predictions: V̂ = n^{-1}∑_{i,a} (ĝπ(p_{ia}) + π_{ia} μ̂_{ia}). This hybrid approach leverages the explanatory power of a reward model to reduce residual variance, leading to further efficiency gains. Both methods rely on the smoothness of the underlying functions and avoid the multiplicative instability of IPW.

## Key Results
- NW achieves significantly lower root mean squared error (RMSE) than IPW across benchmark datasets while maintaining similar bias.
- MNW attains lower RMSE than the doubly robust estimator, demonstrating the benefit of model assistance without strict doubly robust guarantees.
- Both NW and MNW exhibit robustness to errors in the estimated behavior policy probabilities, outperforming IPW and DR when propensities are noisy or biased.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Nonparametric Weighting (NW) reduces variance compared to IPW by modeling the systematic relationship between `π_ia r_ia` and the behavior policy probability `p_ia`, rather than directly using unstable inverse probabilities `1/p_ia`.
- **Mechanism:** Under model `π_ia r_ia = f_π(p_ia) + ε` (Eq. 4), the target value is `V_π = E[∑_a f_π(p_ia)]`. `f_π(·)` is estimated nonparametrically (e.g., via P-splines) from the weighted rewards `π_ia r_ia` regressed on `p_ia`. The final estimator averages these fitted values over actions. This smooths extreme weights because the estimated function `f_π(p)` varies continuously with `p`, avoiding large multiplicative inverses.
- **Core assumption:** The function `f_π(p)` lies in a smooth function space (e.g., Sobolev space `W^q`) allowing consistent nonparametric estimation. The action space size `K` grows slower than `n^{q/(1+2q)}` for convergence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "NW approach uses P-splines to model the relationship between weighted rewards and behavior policy probabilities, avoiding unstable inverse probabilities."
  - [section 3.1.2] "We allow for a flexible structure of `f_π(·)` without imposing a specific functional form... By employing the nonparametric estimation procedure..."
  - [corpus] Corpus shows related work on variance reduction (e.g., "clipping", "density ratio") but does not directly anchor this specific modeling mechanism; evidence is weak.
- **Break condition:** If the true `f_π(p)` is discontinuous or highly irregular, P-spline smoothing may introduce bias, or if `K` is too large relative to `n` (e.g., `K = ω(n^{q/(1+2q)})`), convergence fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Model-assisted Nonparametric Weighting (MNW) further reduces variance by incorporating a reward model `μ̂_ia` to capture systematic reward variation, then nonparametrically modeling the residuals `π_ia (r_ia - μ̂_ia)`.
- **Mechanism:** MNW fits `π_ia (r_ia - μ̂_ia) = g_π(p_ia) + ξ` (Eq. 11). The estimator is `n^{-1} ∑_i ∑_a (ġ_π(p_ia) + π_ia μ̂_ia)`. By subtracting `μ̂_ia`, the response variable has lower variance if `μ̂_ia` explains some reward variation, making `g_π(p_ia)` easier to estimate accurately with less noise.
- **Core assumption:** The residual reward `r_ia - μ̂_ia` (or its expectation) still has a nonlinear relationship with `p_ia` that can be captured by a smooth `g_π(p)`. The reward model error `μ_ia - E[μ̂_ia]` is manageable (bias-variance trade-off).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "To further reduce variance, we incorporate reward predictions – similar to the DR technique – resulting in the Model-assisted Nonparametric Weighting (MNW) approach."
  - [section 4] "We extend the NW approach by integrating the DM estimator... We model the relationship between `π_ia (ria - μ̂_ia)` and the probability `p_ia` using the following nonparametric model..."
  - [corpus] Corpus mentions doubly robust methods but not MNW's specific hybrid approach; evidence is indirect.
- **Break condition:** If `μ̂_ia` is severely misspecified (high bias), the residual may still contain large unexplained variation, making `g_π(p)` estimation noisy. If `μ̂_ia` is already very accurate, the gain from MNW over NW may be minimal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Both NW and MNW are robust to errors in the estimated behavior policy probabilities `p̂_ia` because the nonparametric regression smoothly maps noisy inputs to outputs, diluting the impact of individual probability estimation errors.
- **Mechanism:** The estimators depend on `f_π(p̂_ia)` or `g_π(p̂_ia)`. If `p̂_ia` is a noisy but consistent estimate of `p_ia`, and `f_π` is Lipschitz continuous, then `f_π(p̂_ia) ≈ f_π(p_ia) + small error`. The smoothing in nonparametric regression averages over nearby points, further mitigating the effect of noise in individual `p̂_ia`.
- **Core assumption:** The function `f_π` or `g_π` is sufficiently smooth (Lipschitz). The estimation error in `p̂_ia` is `o_p(1)` and not systematically biased in a way that breaks the conditional independence `E[π_ia r_ia | p_ia]`.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Both methods remain robust to errors in estimated logging policy probabilities."
  - [section 3.4] "Using a flexible regression model – such as a nonparametric method – allows us to approximate... the flexibility of the regression function mitigates the impact of the bias."
  - [corpus] Not directly addressed in corpus; this robustness is a claimed property of the method.
- **Break condition:** If `p̂_ia` has large systematic bias (e.g., `p̂_ia = h(p_ia)` where `h` is not approximately identity or invertible) and `h(p_ia)` is not sufficient for `p_ia` with respect to `π_ia r_ia`, then the fitted function may learn a different relationship, leading to bias.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW)
  - Why needed here: NW is explicitly motivated as a variance-reduced alternative to IPW. Understanding IPW's formula `n^{-1} ∑_i (π_ai r_iai / p̂_ai)` and its vulnerability to small `p̂_ai` is essential to grasp the problem NW solves.
  - Quick check question: Why does operating directly on `1/p̂_ai` cause high variance, and how does NW avoid this multiplicative instability?

- **Concept:** Nonparametric Regression (P-splines / B-splines with penalty)
  - Why needed here: NW and MNW rely on P-spline smoothing to estimate `f_π` and `g_π`. Key elements: basis expansion, penalty on differences of coefficients, smoothing parameter `λ_n`.
  - Quick check question: What is the role of the penalty term `λ_n ∑ (Δβ_k)²` in P-splines, and how does it trade bias vs. variance?

- **Concept:** Doubly Robust (DR) Estimation
  - Why needed here: MNW is inspired by DR's idea of combining weighting and reward modeling but does not aim for the classic doubly robust property. Contrasting helps understand MNW's design goal (variance reduction without strict robustness guarantees).
  - Quick check question: What is the standard DR estimator formula, and what does "doubly robust" mean in terms of consistency conditions?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: 
Input (logged dataset S, target policy π, behavior probabilities p̂) -> Response construction (compute y) -> Nonparametric fitting (P-splines to estimate f̂ or ĝ) -> Aggregation (compute V̂).
For MNW: additionally, Reward model fits μ̂ from S, which feeds into Response construction (compute residual y).

Critical path: Response construction (computing y correctly) -> Nonparametric fitting (accurate f̂/ĝ) -> Aggregation (summing over actions). The correctness of the reward model for MNW also critically affects the final estimate.

Design tradeoffs:
- Choosing P-splines over other nonparametric methods (e.g., kernels, neural nets) balances flexibility, interpretability, and theoretical guarantees; P-splines offer stable smoothing and well-understood rates.
- The number of actions K must be controlled relative to sample size n to avoid the curse of dimensionality in the sum over actions; the theory requires K = o(n^{q/(1+2q)}).
- In MNW, the reward model complexity trades off bias and variance: too simple a model leaves large residuals, while too complex a model may overfit and introduce additional variance.

Failure signatures:
- If the true relationship fπ(p) or gπ(p) is highly non-smooth, P-spline smoothing will incur substantial bias, degrading estimator accuracy.
- When K is large (e.g., thousands of actions), the summation over actions may still yield high variance even after smoothing, and computational cost rises.
- In MNW, a poorly specified reward model (e.g., linear when true rewards are nonlinear) can introduce bias into residuals, leading to a distorted estimate of gπ and thus V̂.
- Improper selection of the smoothing parameter λ (too small) yields high variance; (too large) yields high bias; cross-validation is recommended but not trivial with weighted responses.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Synthetic variance comparison (Example 1):** Compare NW against IPW on a K-armed bandit with controlled correlation between rewards and propensity probabilities, verifying NW achieves lower RMSE via variance reduction while maintaining comparable bias.
2. **Model assistance validation (Example 2):** Compare MNW to NW when a baseline reward model μ(x)=βx² is provided (both correctly and incorrectly specified), demonstrating MNW’s ability to further reduce RMSE by explaining residual variation.
3. **Real-data robustness test:** Apply NW and MNW to multi-class classification benchmarks (e.g., Page dataset) under true, perturbed, and estimated logging policies. Compare against IPW and doubly robust estimators, confirming NW/MNW maintain low bias and RMSE even when propensity estimates are noisy, while IPW/DR degrade.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can neural network-based nonparametric models improve the flexibility and predictive accuracy of the NW and MNW estimators compared to P-splines?
- How can the model framework (4) be adapted for binary or discrete rewards, and does this adaptation improve estimation accuracy in such settings?
- Can the NW approach be extended to combinatorial contextual bandits with structured action spaces, and does it maintain variance reduction benefits over IPW in these complex settings?
- How can NW and MNW estimators be integrated into sequential reinforcement learning (e.g., MDPs) for off-policy evaluation, and do they improve sample efficiency and robustness compared to sequential DR methods?
- Does the NW approach scale effectively to large action spaces (e.g., thousands of actions), and how does it mitigate the extreme variance issues of IPW in such regimes?

## Limitations
- The nonparametric weighting approach relies on smoothness assumptions about fπ and gπ; violations may introduce bias.
- Theoretical convergence conditions require the number of actions K to grow slower than a function of sample size n (K = o(n^{q/(1+2q})), which may limit applicability to large action spaces.
- MNW's performance depends on the quality of the auxiliary reward model; misspecification can harm accuracy.

## Confidence

- Robustness to p̂ errors: Medium
