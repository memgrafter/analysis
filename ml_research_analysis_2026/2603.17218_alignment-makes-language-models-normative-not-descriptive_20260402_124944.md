# Alignment Makes Language Models Normative, Not Descriptive

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.17218
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17218
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Alignment shifts LLMs from descriptive to normative behavior predictors; base models beat aligned in multi-round games (213 vs 22 wins, p<1e-40), while aligned beat base in one-shot games (4.1:1) and lotteries (2.2:1).

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how post-training alignment (e.g., RLHF, DPO) changes the behavioral predictions of large language models compared to their unaligned base versions. Across 120 base-aligned model pairs from 23 families and over 10,000 human decisions in strategic games, the authors find that base models are far superior at predicting human choices in multi-round interactive settings, where behavior is shaped by history-dependent dynamics like reciprocity and retaliation. In contrast, aligned models excel at one-shot games and lottery choices, which align more closely with normative equilibrium predictions. The effect is robust across prompts, model families, and scales up to 70B parameters, with the base advantage growing with size. The authors attribute these findings to KL-regularized alignment concentrating probability mass on annotator-approved actions, thereby suppressing the diverse, descriptive strategies that base models retain. The results highlight a trade-off: alignment improves normative alignment but can degrade descriptive accuracy for modeling real human strategic behavior.

## Method Summary
The study compares base and aligned models on human decision data from four families of multi-round strategic games (bargaining, persuasion, negotiation, repeated matrix games) and two boundary-condition tasks (one-shot 2×2 games, binary lotteries). For each of 120 same-provider model pairs, models are prompted in their native formats (plain completion for base, chat template for aligned) to predict the next action at each human decision point. A structured prompt includes a system message, dialogue history, and a JSON suffix (e.g., `{"decision": "`) to extract token log-probabilities for decision tokens (e.g., "accept"/"reject"). The normalized probability `p_accept` is computed by applying a softmax over the decision-token logits, requiring the total probability mass on these tokens to be at least 0.8 to ensure reliability. The model’s predictions are then correlated with actual human binary choices using Pearson correlation per pair. Pairs where both models’ correlation falls below 0.3 are excluded. The primary outcome is the number of wins (higher correlation) for base versus aligned models across pairs, analyzed with binomial tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Additional analyses examine round-by-round dynamics, prompt robustness (10 variants), and scaling across model sizes. Data sources include the GLEE benchmark for multi-round games, Zhu et al. (2025) for one-shot 2×2 games, and Marantz & Plonsky (2025) for lotteries. The evaluation pipeline reflects typical model usage while enabling fair comparisons; native formats yield the strongest effects, though forcing a common format still preserves the pattern.

## Key Results
- In multi-round strategic games, base models outperform aligned models by a factor of 9.7:1 (213 vs 22 wins, p<1e-40) across 120 pairs.
- Aligned models dominate in one-shot 2×2 games (4.1:1 win ratio, p<1e-6) and binary lotteries (2.2:1, p=2.19e-4).
- Within multi-round games, aligned models only win at round 1; the base advantage emerges sharply from round 2 onward.
- The effect is consistent across model families, prompt variants, and scales up to 70B parameters (larger models show a bigger base advantage).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Distributional Narrowing via KL-Regularized Alignment
- **Claim**: Alignment rewards cause probability mass to concentrate on annotator-approved actions, suppressing low‑reward descriptive strategies.
- **Mechanism**: KL‑regularized RL optimizes $E[r] - \beta KL(\pi||\pi_0)$, yielding $\pi^* \propto \pi_0 \exp(r/\beta)$. High‑reward actions increase probability; low‑reward actions (often descriptive strategies like retaliation) are downweighted or lost.
- **Core assumption**: Human annotators rate responses based on normative preferences (helpfulness, harmlessness) rather than on how humans actually behave.
- **Evidence anchors**: The abstract states alignment introduces a normative bias, helping prediction only when behavior conforms to normative theories. Section 2.2 derives the exponential tilt and notes the collapse of tail probabilities (Korbak et al., 2022). The corpus literature (e.g., "Beyond Preferences in AI Alignment") argues preference‑based alignment may not capture the full distribution of human behavior.
- **Break condition**: If alignment uses an entropy regularizer or other method to preserve tail probabilities, distributional collapse may not occur.

### Normative Reinforcement through Preference Optimization
- **Claim**: Aligned models favor normative solutions (e.g., Nash equilibrium), which align with human one‑shot choices but not with history‑rich play.
- **Mechanism**: Preference training rewards responses judged reasonable, which often coincide with normative solutions (cooperation, fair splits). This shifts model outputs toward equilibrium. In one‑shot games, human behavior approximates equilibrium, so aligned models excel; in multi‑round games, human behavior deviates due to descriptive dynamics, hurting prediction.
- **Core assumption**: One‑shot human decisions are relatively well‑described by normative theories, while multi‑round decisions are driven by descriptive dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract notes aligned models dominate on one‑shot games and at round one of multi‑round games. Section 5 shows aligned models are systematically more NE‑aligned than base models (mean r=0.41 vs. 0.28; aligned closer in 59 of 76 pairs, p<1e‑6). Corpus work (e.g., "Differences in the Moral Foundations of Large Language Models") shows alignment pushes models toward specific normative frameworks.
- **Break condition**: If humans in one‑shot games also exhibit strong descriptive biases, the aligned advantage would disappear.

### Loss of History‑Dependent Strategy Capacity
- **Claim**: Base models retain the ability to incorporate interaction history (e.g., reciprocity), which is critical for predicting human choices in multi‑round games; alignment degrades this capacity.
- **Mechanism**: Multi‑round strategic behavior often involves contingent strategies (tit‑for‑tat, retaliation). These are low‑reward in preference training because they may appear uncooperative. The KL‑regularization prevents large deviations, but the reward tilt already suppresses such strategies, leading to misprediction.
- **Core assumption**: Descriptive dynamics (reciprocity, retaliation) are present in the base distribution and are essential for accurate prediction of human behavior in repeated settings.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract describes behavior shaped by reciprocity, retaliation, and history‑dependent adaptation. Section 4 (Round‑by‑round dynamics) shows aligned models win only at round 1, with advantage reversing from round 2 onward. Corpus (e.g., "Evolving Diverse Red‑team Language Models in Multi‑round Multi‑agent Games") emphasizes the need for diverse strategies, including conditional ones.
- **Break condition**: If alignment techniques are modified to reward appropriate conditional responses (e.g., via outcome‑based rewards that value reciprocity), the base advantage might shrink.

## Foundational Learning
### Distinguishing Normative and Descriptive Behavior
- **Why needed**: To interpret the observed trade‑off, one must grasp that normative theories prescribe ideal behavior while descriptive accounts capture actual human actions. Alignment pushes models toward the former, away from the latter.
- **Quick check**: Give an example where a normative prediction (e.g., always cooperate in a one-shot Prisoner’s Dilemma) diverges from a descriptive finding (e.g., many people defect).

### KL‑Regularized Alignment (RLHF/DPO)
- **Why needed**: Understanding how KL regularization shapes the policy distribution explains why alignment can collapse diversity; the exponential tilt formula is key.
- **Quick check**: Derive the optimal policy $\pi^*$ from the objective $E[r] - \beta KL(\pi||\pi_0)$ and explain what happens to low‑reward actions as $\beta$ decreases.

### History‑Dependent Strategies in Repeated Games
- **Why needed**: Multi‑round games evoke dynamic behaviors (reciprocity, retaliation) that are not captured by static equilibrium. The base models' advantage hinges on preserving these strategies.
- **Quick check**: Describe how a tit‑for‑tat strategy works in an iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma and why it might be penalized by a preference‑based reward model.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Model loaders → Prompt builder (uses Datasets) → Inference engine → Normalizer → Evaluator (uses Filters and Datasets) → Statistical comparison

### Critical Path
1. Select a same‑provider base–aligned pair (from Appendix A).
2. Construct the game‑specific prompt in the model’s native format (standard for base, chat template for aligned), including system message, dialogue history, and a JSON suffix for decision token extraction.
3. Run a single forward pass to obtain next‑token logits at the decision position; extract log‑probabilities for the set of decision tokens (e.g., "accept"/"reject").
4. Compute normalized p_accept = softmax(decision_tokens) / sum(decision_tokens mass); require total mass ≥ 0.8.
5. Calculate Pearson correlation between p_accept and the ground‑truth human binary choices across all decision points in that game family.
6. Repeat steps 1‑5 for both models; record which correlation is higher (base win or aligned win).
7. Aggregate wins across many pairs and families; compute statistical significance (binomial test on win counts, Wilcoxon signed‑rank on correlation differences).
8. For boundary conditions, repeat with one‑shot 2×2 games and lotteries using aggregate human choice rates per problem.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Model format**: Native format (base = plain completion, aligned = chat) yields the strongest effect; forcing both to a common format reduces effect size slightly but still robust. Native format reflects typical deployment.
- **Filtering thresholds**: Mass threshold 0.8 and correlation threshold 0.3 are chosen to eliminate unreliable pairs. Lower thresholds include more pairs but introduce noise; higher thresholds risk excluding valid pairs. Robustness checks confirm the base advantage holds across a wide range.
- **Evaluation granularity**: Multi‑round games use per‑decision correlation (unique histories); one‑shot and lottery datasets use per‑problem aggregate correlation (multiple participants). Metric choice matches the data structure.

### Failure Signatures
- **Low decision token mass (<0.8)**: Model does not focus on decision tokens; may need prompt reformatting (e.g., enforce JSON suffix, adjust system message).
- **Both models fail min correlation filter**: Neither captures human behavior; suggests dataset misalignment, incorrect prompt format, or that the game family is unsuitable for language modeling.
- **High variance across prompt variants**: If the base advantage disappears for certain prompts, results may be prompt‑sensitive; adhere to native format and the validated variants from Table 1.
- **Chat template mismatches**: Aligned models require correct special tokens; misapplication can severely degrade performance.
- **Out‑of‑memory errors**: Large models need sufficient GPU memory; use quantization or batching.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate main result**: Pick one model family (e.g., Qwen2.5‑7B) and evaluate its base vs. aligned on the four GLEE families. Verify base wins with high ratio and p<1e‑6.
2. **Test boundary conditions**: Same pair on one‑shot 2×2 games and binary lotteries; expect aligned to win (ratio >2:1, p<0.001). Also compare round 1 vs. rounds ≥2 within multi‑round games.
3. **Prompt robustness**: Evaluate the same pair across the 10 valid prompt variants from Table 1; confirm base win rate stays >90% and all variants individually significant (p<0.01).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Which aspects of multi‑round play drive the base advantage?**  
   The paper establishes the robust base advantage but does not isolate whether opponent modeling, history integration, or trajectory novelty is the key driver.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Systematically manipulate these factors independently and measure the base‑aligned performance gap.

2. **Does the base advantage generalize to continuous action spaces?**  
   All evaluated games involve discrete decisions; continuous strategic interactions (e.g., bargaining with variable offers, auctions) might behave differently.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Collect human behavioral data in continuous strategic games and compare base vs. aligned predictive accuracy.

3. **How can alignment preserve the full empirical distribution of human behavior while adding helpfulness?**  
   Standard alignment (RLHF/DPO) with KL regularization concentrates probability on high‑reward modes and collapses diversity. No existing method demonstrably adds helpfulness without narrowing the distribution.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Develop and evaluate novel alignment algorithms (e.g., entropy bonuses, constrained RL) that maintain diversity and meet helpfulness/safety benchmarks.

4. **Does the normative shift diminish at larger model scales?**  
   The effect increases with scale up to 70B, but it is unknown whether it persists, saturates, or reverses at extreme scales (500B, 1T).  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Evaluate the largest accessible base and aligned models on the same benchmarks and plot the performance gap across sizes.

## Limitations
- **Dataset accessibility**: Raw human decision datasets (GLEE, Zhu et al., Marantz & Plonsky) are not confirmed public, which may hinder exact replication.
- **Prompt & tokenization specificity**: Precise native formats and chat templates vary across model families; deviations can affect decision token mass and outcomes.
- **Causal attribution**: The proposed mechanisms are inferred from correlations; direct ablation studies (e.g., varying KL penalty) are needed to establish causality.

## Confidence
- **Core empirical findings** (base advantage in multi‑round games; aligned advantage in one‑shot/lotteries): **High**
- **Mechanistic explanation** (KL‑induced distribution narrowing and loss of descriptive strategies): **Medium**
- **Boundary conditions** (aligned wins only at round 1; effect scales with model size up to 70B): **High**
- **Causal link between alignment and normative bias**: **Medium**
- **Generalizability to continuous action spaces**: **Low**
