# Evaluating Adjective-Noun Compositionality in LLMs: Functional vs Representational Perspectives

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09994  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09994  
- Reference count: 17  
- Primary result: LLMs encode stable internal compositional representations but fail to translate them reliably into functional task success across model variants.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) exhibit compositionality in adjective‑noun combinations through two complementary lenses: functional task performance (entailment accuracy/F1 on AddOne, PLANE, COMPCOMB) and representational probing of internal states. Across model families (LLaMA‑2, CodeLlama, Qwen2.5‑Coder, Gemma2), functional scores vary substantially—often decreasing with instruction tuning or scaling—while internal compositional signals remain consistently above chance and peak in intermediate layers. The divergence reveals that LLMs acquire compositional semantic structure during pretraining, yet subsequent training or scaling can disrupt the read‑out from these representations to outputs, or functional evaluations may be sensitive to prompt formulation. The study highlights the importance of contrastive evaluation and calls for causal analyses to determine whether internal compositional signals are actually used.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate adjective‑noun compositionality via functional benchmarks and representational probing. Functionally, they measure weighted F1 on AddOne (substitutivity) and PLANE (systematicity) and accuracy on COMPCOMB (overgeneralization), using both generation‑based multiple‑choice QA and comparative log‑probability scoring; COMPCOMB employs three prompt variants. Representationally, they extract hidden states from every fifth transformer layer, train a linear classifier to predict entailment labels for AddOne/PLANE, and compute cosine similarity between embeddings for COMPCOMB. The analysis covers base, instruction‑tuned, and scaled variants of four model families, with weights from HuggingFace and datasets from public sources (COMPCOMB to be released). This dual approach allows contrasting behavioral outcomes with internal signals.

## Key Results
- Functional performance drops markedly with instruction tuning and scaling on AddOne and COMPCOMB (e.g., LLaMA‑2‑7B AddOne F1 = 77.97 → 7B‑chat F1 = 18.01), while PLANE remains stable.  
- Representational probing yields above‑chance scores for all models, with a clear peak in intermediate layers, and these signals remain stable across variants.  
- The divergence indicates LLMs encode compositional structure internally but fail to harness it reliably for functional tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Instruction tuning alters read‑out  
**Claim:** Instruction tuning optimizes for instruction‑following, which may degrade functional compositional performance even though internal representations stay compositional, because the mapping from representations to outputs is altered.  
**Mechanism:** Pretraining builds compositional semantics; SFT/RLHF introduces objectives that prioritize compliance, potentially overwriting or bypassing those structures during generation.  
**Core assumption:** Compositional knowledge is stable, but the read‑out is malleable.  
**Evidence:** [abstract] notes LLMs develop compositional representations but fail to translate; [section 2.2] shows AddOne and COMPCOMB often decrease with instruction tuning, PLANE stable.  
**Break condition:** If instruction‑tuned models match base functional accuracy or if representational signals deteriorate with tuning, this mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2: Intermediate‑layer signal dissipation  
**Claim:** Compositional information peaks in intermediate layers, but later layers are optimized for next‑token prediction, so functional evaluations relying on final‑layer outputs may suffer.  
**Mechanism:** Transformer representations become increasingly task‑specific toward the top; intermediate layers preserve abstract semantic relations, while final layers adapt to LM objectives, distorting the signal.  
**Core assumption:** Functional performance depends on final‑layer representations; probing can access any layer.  
**Evidence:** [abstract] reports compositional signals peak in intermediate layers; [section 2.3] shows layer‑wise F1 scores peak around middle layers for all models.  
**Break condition:** If functional performance correlates with final‑layer signals, or if boosting intermediate signals improves function without architectural changes, the mechanism holds; if no layer peak exists, it fails.

### Mechanism 3: Prompt sensitivity  
**Claim:** Functional evaluations are sensitive to prompt formulation; variability across model variants may reflect differences in prompt interpretation rather than compositional competence.  
**Mechanism:** Prompt‑based tasks expose models to surface cues and instruction biases; instruction‑tuned models follow instructions more rigidly, causing inconsistent outcomes. Probing feeds fixed inputs, avoiding this.  
**Core assumption:** Same compositional knowledge can produce divergent outputs under different prompts.  
**Evidence:** [abstract] highlights contrastive evaluation; [section 2.2] uses two setup variations to mitigate prompt sensitivity and notes PLANE stable while AddOne/COMPCOMB are not.  
**Break condition:** If controlling prompt design yields consistent functional performance aligned with probing, the mechanism holds; otherwise other mechanisms apply.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Tokenizer → Embedding layer → Transformer stack (N layers of self‑attention + feed‑forward) → Final hidden states → LM head

**Critical path**  
1. Tokenize input (e.g., a question with candidates).  
2. Forward pass through the model to obtain hidden states.  
3a. Functional: Compute conditional probabilities of candidate completions or generate outputs; pick the higher‑probability option.  
3b. Representational: For each target layer, feed the state into a linear probe or compute cosine similarity between token embeddings.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Probing classifier: Linear vs nonlinear; using only the last token vs mean pooling over tokens.  
- Functional prompts: Choice of template, few‑shot examples, and decoding parameters (temperature, top‑p) heavily influence results.  
- Layer extraction: Sampling every fifth layer reduces cost but may miss nuances; benchmarking multiple intervals is advisable.

**Failure signatures**  
- High variance across prompt templates for the same model indicates prompt sensitivity.  
- Probing accuracy well above chance but functional accuracy near random suggests a read‑out bottleneck.  
- Scaling leads to worse functional performance while probing improves points to output‑level overfitting.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Replicate functional evaluation on LLaMA‑2‑7B using both generation and log‑prob setups for AddOne, PLANE, COMPCOMB; report F1/accuracy.  
2. Run layer‑wise probing on the same model: extract hidden states every 5 layers, train a linear classifier on AddOne/PLANE train split, evaluate on test; for COMPCOMB compute cosine similarity; plot layer‑wise scores.  
3. Cross‑variant comparison: Repeat steps 1–2 for the tuned (7B‑chat) and scaled (13B) variants; observe trends across instruction tuning and scale, focusing on the divergence between functional and representational results.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What factors cause the divergence between internal compositional representations and functional task performance?** The paper establishes a correlation but does not pinpoint whether altered read‑out, layer‑peak bottlenecks, or prompt sensitivity are causal. Controlled interventions (e.g., activation patching) are needed.  
2. **Do these patterns generalize beyond adjective‑noun compositionality?** The study is limited to English AN phrases; it remains unclear whether similar divergences occur for subword morphology, phrasal verbs, clause‑level syntax, or multilingual models.  
3. **What is the causal relationship between internal compositional structure and outputs?** Linear probing shows compositional information is present, but not whether the model actually uses it. Causal methods (e.g., mediation analysis) could establish whether the signals are epiphenomenal or functional.  
4. **How do different representational analysis methods affect conclusions?** The paper relies on linear probing and cosine similarity; more expressive or causal methods might yield different estimates and better predict functional success.

## Limitations
- Undisclosed prompt templates and decoding settings hinder precise replication.  
- Insufficiently detailed probing protocol (token selection, classifier hyperparameters) may affect representational results.  
- Evaluation limited to adjective‑noun compositionality; generalizability to other compositional constructions is unknown.  
- No causal interventions to directly test the hypothesized read‑out bottleneck.

## Confidence
- Representational stability: **High**  
- Functional decline with instruction tuning/scaling: **Medium**  
- Read‑out alteration mechanism: **Medium**  
- Layer‑peak bottleneck mechanism: **Low**  
- Prompt sensitivity mechanism: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. Systematically vary prompts and decoding parameters across all functional tasks to quantify their impact on score variance.  
2. Conduct a read‑out intervention: train linear probes on intermediate representations, use them to predict functional labels, then fine‑tune the LM head on probe outputs to test recovery of function.  
3. Perform comprehensive final‑layer probing (linear and non‑linear) and extend evaluation to other composition types (e.g., verb‑argument, noun‑noun) to verify signal dissipation and scope.
