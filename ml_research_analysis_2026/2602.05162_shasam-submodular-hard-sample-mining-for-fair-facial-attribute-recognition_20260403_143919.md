# SHaSaM: Submodular Hard Sample Mining for Fair Facial Attribute Recognition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05162
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05162
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art fairness (Equalized Odds) improvement up to 2.7 points and Top-1 Accuracy up to 3.5% on CelebA and UTKFace, with fewer training epochs than existing methods.

## Executive Summary
Facial attribute recognition models often inherit biases present in training data, leading to unfair performance across sensitive attribute groups (e.g., gender, age). SHaSaM addresses this by proposing a unified combinatorial framework that jointly tackles group imbalance and spurious correlations. The method consists of two stages: SHaSaM-MINE uses submodular subset selection to mine hard positive and negative samples in a balanced manner, while SHaSaM-LEARN employs a family of loss functions based on Submodular Conditional Mutual Information to maximize separation between target classes while minimizing influence from sensitive attributes. Experiments on CelebA and UTKFace demonstrate that SHaSaM outperforms prior arts, achieving superior fairness (Equalized Odds) and accuracy within reduced training time.

## Method Summary
SHaSaM adopts a two-stage training procedure. In Stage 1, a feature extractor (e.g., ResNet-18 or ViT) is trained using the SHASAM loss. For each epoch, a random subset of the training data is used as a ground set. SHaSaM-MINE then selects subsets for a randomly chosen target class and sensitive class pair: diverse anchors (via Facility-Location or LogDet), hard positives (same target, different sensitive, maximizing conditional gain), and hard negatives (different target, same sensitive, maximizing mutual information). All subsets are of equal cardinality \(k\), guaranteeing balanced exposure regardless of data distribution. Stage 2 freezes the learned encoder and trains a linear classifier with cross-entropy. The SHASAM-LEARN loss is defined as a Submodular Conditional Mutual Information measure over the selected batch, encouraging features of anchors and hard positives to be similar across sensitive groups while pushing apart anchors and hard negatives. The selection and loss are tightly coupled via a combinatorial viewpoint, differentiating SHaSaM from pairwise contrastive approaches.

## Key Results
- Improves Equalized Odds by up to **2.7 points** and Top-1 Accuracy by up to **3.5%** on CelebA and UTKFace.
- Achieves state-of-the-art fairness while requiring **fewer training epochs** compared to methods like FSCL.
- Ablations confirm that both SHaSaM-MINE (balanced hard sampling) and SHaSaM-LEARN (SCMI loss) contribute essential gains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Submodular hard sample mining mitigates attribute group imbalance by ensuring balanced selection of hard positives and negatives per training iteration.
- **Mechanism:** SHaSaM-MINE uses submodular maximization (e.g., Facility-Location) under a cardinality constraint to select diverse anchors, then selects hard-positives via Submodular Conditional Gain (dissimilar to anchors but same target) and hard-negatives via Submodular Mutual Information (similar to anchors but different target). This forces balanced sets (\(|A_{ij}|=|A_{ip}|=|A_{in}|=k\)) regardless of original data distribution.
- **Core assumption:** Data imbalance (both in target and sensitive attributes) drives unfairness; balanced hard-sample exposure during training reduces bias.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "SHaSaM-MINE uses submodular subset selection to mine hard positive and negative samples, mitigating group imbalance."
  - [section 3.4]: Algorithm details "maximizing LogDet selects diverse examples" and "maximizing SCG results in selection of samples which have maximum dissimilarity to the ones in \(A_{ij}\) but share the same \(T_i\)."
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers focus on contrastive fair learning (e.g., FSCL) but not submodular hard mining.
- **Break condition:** If submodular selection fails to find sufficiently diverse anchors (e.g., in extremely rare sensitive groups), the balanced set guarantee may not hold, potentially perpetuating imbalance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The Submodular Conditional Mutual Information (SCMI) loss in SHaSaM-LEARN enforces fairness by maximizing feature overlap within target classes (across sensitive attributes) while maximizing separation between target classes (within the same sensitive attribute).
- **Mechanism:** Minimizing \(L_{\text{SHASAM}}(\theta) \propto I_f(A_{ij}; A_{in} \mid A_{ip})\) simultaneously maximizes \(I_f(A_{ij}; A_{ip})\) (anchors and hard-positives share target, different sensitive) and minimizes \(I_f(A_{ij}; A_{in})\) (anchors and hard-negatives share sensitive, different target). This creates a decision boundary invariant to sensitive attributes.
- **Core assumption:** Sensitive attribute information can be suppressed by encouraging feature similarity among same-target samples regardless of sensitive attribute, and dissimilarity among different-target samples within the same sensitive group.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "SHaSaM-LEARN introduces a family of combinatorial loss functions based on Submodular Conditional Mutual Information to maximize the decision boundary between target classes while minimizing the influence of sensitive attributes."
  - [section 3.5]: "minimizing \(L_{\text{SHASAM}}\) increases the decision boundary between [hard positives and negatives]... enforcing fairness in the learning process."
  - [corpus]: Weak; neighbors like FairViT use architectural changes, not SCMI losses.
- **Break condition:** If feature extractor \(F\) cannot separate sensitive attributes in representation space (e.g., due to high intra-group variance), SCMI minimization may fail to decouple them, leaving fairness gains limited.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The unified set-based combinatorial viewpoint—modeling batches as ground sets and mining/learning via submodular functions—enables simultaneous mitigation of imbalance and explicit fairness regularization.
- **Mechanism:** By treating each training batch as a collection of sets (\(V = \cup T_i = \cup S_j\)), SHaSaM applies submodular optimization to select subsets (anchors, positives, negatives) and then defines a loss as a submodular information measure (SCMI) over these sets. This couples selection and learning in a way pairwise contrastive methods (e.g., SupCon) do not.
- **Core assumption:** Fairness can be optimized at the set level; interactions across sets (via submodular functions) better capture group-level fairness than instance-level losses.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "models fairness-driven representation learning as a submodular hard-sample mining problem" with a "unified formulation."
  - [section 3.1]: "introducing a combinatorial viewpoint... allowing us to leverage the benefits of submodular functions."
  - [section 3.3]: Equation (2) couples selection (A) and (B) with learning (C).
  - [corpus]: Moderate; neighbors like SCoRe (Majee et al. 2024) use submodular combinatorial representation learning, but not specifically for fairness.
- **Break condition:** If the set cardinality constraint (\(k\)) is too small, selected subsets may not represent the full attribute diversity, weakening the fairness signal.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  FeatureExtractor \(F(\cdot;\theta)\) (ResNet-18/ViT) -> Projection head -> SHASAM loss -> Encoder parameters  
  Encoder (frozen) -> Classifier \(Clf(\cdot)\) -> Linear head -> Predictions
- **Critical path:**  
  1. At iteration t: sample mini‑batch V (random subset per epoch).  
  2. For random \((T_i,S_j)\) pair with \(T_i\cap S_j\neq\emptyset\): run SHaSaM‑MINE to get \(A_{ij}, A_{ip}, A_{in}\) via greedy submodular maximization (Algorithm 1 lines 19–21).  
  3. Forward pass: compute features \(h_A, h_P, h_N = F(A_{ij};\theta), F(A_{ip};\theta), F(A_{in};\theta)\).  
  4. Compute \(L_{\text{SHASAM}}(\theta)\) via SCMI (Eq. 3/Table 1).  
  5. Backpropagate \(\nabla_\theta L_{\text{SHASAM}}(\theta) \approx \nabla_\theta L_{\text{SHASAM}}(\hat{A}_{ij},\hat{A}_{ip},\hat{A}_{in};\theta)\) (per Sec. 3.2/Eq. 12).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Selection budget \(k\):** Larger \(k\) improves fairness/accuracy (Tab. 5) but increases compute (Fig. 6); paper uses \(k=16\).  
  - **Submodular function choice:** FL (Facility-Location) models representation, yielding better accuracy/fairness than LogDet (diversity) per Tab. 3/H.4.  
  - **Backbone architecture:** ViT vs. ResNet: ViT gives higher accuracy but SHaSaM works with both; FLCMI consistently best.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High EO (>10) on CelebA/UTKFace (Tab. 2) indicates SCMI not disentangling sensitive attributes.  
  - Slow convergence in stage 1 (>50 epochs to match FSCL) suggests ineffective hard-sample mining.  
  - Low accuracy drop when replacing SHaSaM-MINE with random (Tab. 3) indicates selection not providing gradient signal.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Ablation on components (Tab. 3):** Replace random mining with SHaSaM-MINE and/or random learning with SHaSaM-LEARN to isolate contribution of each stage.  
  2. **Synthetic dataset visualization (Fig. 4/8/9/10):** Run SHaSaM-MINE on 2D imbalanced/overlapping clusters; verify hard positives/negatives lie at cluster boundaries.  
  3. **Convergence study (Fig. 5):** Train stage 1 for varying epochs; plot accuracy/EO vs. epochs to confirm SHaSaM reaches target performance in fewer epochs than FSCL.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the computational complexity of SHaSaM be reduced to enable its application to very large datasets without sacrificing fairness performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] from the limitations discussed in Appendix I, where the authors note the "computational cost and wall-clock time overhead" of SHaSaM-MINE and the inefficiency of random subset selection for large-scale applications.
- **Why unresolved:** The current approach uses random subset sampling to mitigate compute costs, which may introduce sampling bias and is not guaranteed to scale efficiently. The overhead stems from submodular optimization and set-based operations that grow with batch size.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a more scalable variant (e.g., using streaming submodular optimization or approximate greedy methods) evaluated on large-scale datasets (e.g., larger versions of CelebA) showing similar fairness gains (e.g., EO improvement) with significantly reduced training time per epoch.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can SHaSaM be extended to directly handle continuous sensitive attributes (e.g., age as a real number) without requiring binarization?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] from the statement in Appendix I that "continuous attributes like 'age' were simplified into binary categories, a step that may not be suitable for all real-world scenarios."
- **Why unresolved:** Binarization discards granular information and introduces arbitrary threshold choices, which may not capture nuanced biases. Fairness definitions for continuous attributes are less mature, and it is unclear if the submodular formulation can adapt.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A continuous version of SHaSaM (e.g., by discretizing into multiple bins or integrating continuous fairness constraints) tested on datasets with continuous sensitive attributes (e.g., original age values in UTKFace) showing improved fairness metrics without loss in accuracy compared to binarized baselines.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the combinatorial submodular approach in SHaSaM yield fairness improvements in non-visual domains (e.g., text classification or tabular data)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] from the exclusive evaluation on facial attribute recognition benchmarks (CelebA and UTKFace) with no discussion of cross-domain applicability.
- **Why unresolved:** The submodular hard sample mining relies on feature embeddings from convolutional or transformer networks; other domains (e.g., NLP, tabular) have different data structures, bias patterns, and may require different similarity kernels or submodular functions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Application of SHaSaM to standard fairness benchmarks in other modalities (e.g., bias in sentiment analysis on text data, or fairness in tabular datasets like COMPAS) demonstrating consistent reductions in Equalized Odds and accuracy gains compared to domain-specific fairness methods.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What theoretical guarantees can be provided regarding SHaSaM's impact on fairness metrics (e.g., does minimizing its loss imply bounds on Equalized Odds)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] from the absence of any theoretical analysis; the paper presents empirical results without proofs connecting the Submodular Conditional Mutual Information objective to fairness guarantees.
- **Why unresolved:** Without theoretical bounds, it is difficult to understand the conditions under which SHaSaM provably improves fairness, or to tune hyperparameters (e.g., selection budget, submodular function) with confidence. This limits trust and adoption in high-stakes applications.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formal analysis establishing relationships between the SHaSaM loss and fairness metrics (e.g., showing that under certain distributional assumptions, minimizing the loss upper-bounds the difference in true positive rates across sensitive groups), supported by experiments that validate these bounds in practice.

## Limitations
- **Pool dynamics:** Unclear whether features are recomputed within epoch; stale gradients may hinder training.
- **Scalable optimization:** Greedy submodular maximization on large candidate pools (~40k) is not detailed, posing reproducibility risk.
- **Hard‑positive definition mismatch:** Algorithm uses \(T_i \cap S_j\) while text states different sensitive attribute; this inconsistency may affect fairness outcomes.
