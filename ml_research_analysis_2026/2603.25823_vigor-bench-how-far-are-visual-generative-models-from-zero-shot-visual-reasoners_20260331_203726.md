# ViGoR-Bench: How Far Are Visual Generative Models From Zero-Shot Visual Reasoners?
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.25823
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25823
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: ViGoR-Bench uncovers a "logical desert" in visual generative models: despite high visual fidelity, models fail at physical, causal, and spatial reasoning; proprietary models lead but even they lag, and RL on out-of-distribution complex data dramatically improves reasoning success.

## Executive Summary
ViGoR-Bench is a unified benchmark for evaluating zero‑shot visual reasoning in generative models. It moves beyond superficial fidelity metrics by assessing physical, causal, and spatial reasoning across 918 samples spanning image‑to‑image, sequential image‑to‑image, and video tasks. The core evaluation employs an automated judge (Gemini‑2.5‑Pro) with task‑specific prompts, validated to align with human judgments (78.6% accuracy). A dual‑track design separately scores the generative process (0‑100 scales) and final result (binary success), enabling fine‑grained diagnostics. Experiments on over 20 models reveal severe deficits: proprietary models vastly outperform open‑source ones, video models exhibit an “illusion of reasoning” (high temporal coherence but near‑zero success), and explicit chain‑of‑thought improves interpretability but not final accuracy. Post‑training experiments show that reinforcement learning on out‑of‑distribution, high‑complexity data (e.g., 8×8 maze navigation) yields dramatic gains in reasoning success (up to 97%) and better generalization than supervised fine‑tuning. The benchmark thus exposes a critical gap and points toward effective training strategies.

## Method Summary
ViGoR‑Bench comprises 20 sub‑domains across Physical, Knowledge, and Symbolic reasoning, totaling 918 samples. Data sources include generative synthesis, real‑world collection, and algorithmic construction to ensure diversity. The framework supports three modalities: image‑to‑image, sequential image‑to‑image (with intermediate frames), and video generation. For each sample, a task‑specific template formats the instruction, input image(s), model output, and optional ground‑truth references. An automated judge based on Gemini‑2.5‑Pro processes the template and returns a JSON with scores for four dimensions: Background Consistency (SBC), Rule Obey (SRO), Visual Quality (SVQ), and Reasoning Accuracy/Beneficial Action (SRA). Process metrics are graded on a 0‑100 scale; result metrics are binary (0/1) for each dimension, with an average reported. The judge’s human alignment was validated using ground‑truth references, achieving 78.6% accuracy. The benchmark also includes post‑training experiments: models were fine‑tuned via supervised learning (SFT) or reinforcement learning (RL with GRPO) on maze navigation tasks of increasing grid sizes (4×4, 6×6, 8×8). RL on out‑of‑distribution, high‑complexity mazes (8×8) led to substantial improvements in reasoning success and Generalization compared to SFT and even proprietary models.

## Key Results
- Significant reasoning deficits across state‑of‑the‑art visual generative models; proprietary models (e.g., Nano Banana Pro) outperform open‑source by a large margin (68.4% vs 34.6% Avg Score).
- Video generation models exhibit an “illusion of reasoning”: high temporal coherence (Sora 2 Pro Process VQ 85.5%) but very low reasoning success (1.6%).
- Post‑training with RL on out‑of‑distribution, high‑complexity data (8×8 mazes) dramatically improves reasoning success (up to 97.0% RS, 99.0 Avg) and generalizes better than SFT.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s dual‑track evaluation separates process coherence from final accuracy, preventing models from “gaming” metrics through superficial quality. The use of a powerful LLM judge (Gemini‑2.5‑Pro) with task‑specific prompts—including CoT for process scoring—provides evidence‑grounded, human‑like assessment that scales. The four diagnostic dimensions pinpoint whether failures stem from rule violations, background inconsistencies, visual artifacts, or lack of beneficial actions. By including tasks of varying complexity and modalities, the benchmark tests true rule understanding rather than memorization. The RL experiments further show that training with an objective that rewards correct reasoning (via success signals) on challenging, out‑of‑distribution tasks forces models to internalize constraints, closing the logical desert.

## Foundational Learning
- **Zero-shot Visual Reasoning**  
  - *Why needed*: To evaluate models' ability to apply physical, causal, and symbolic rules in generation without any task‑specific examples.  
  - *Quick check*: Given an image and the instruction “move the red block to the left”, does the output image reflect the correct transformation?

- **Dual-Track Evaluation**  
  - *Why needed*: Separating process and result metrics exposes cases where intermediate steps look plausible but the final output is wrong.  
  - *Quick check*: For a video task, are intermediate frames consistent with physical laws even if the final frame fails?

- **Evidence‑Grounded Automated Judge**  
  - *Why needed*: Scalable assessment aligned with human judgments is essential for large‑scale evaluation.  
  - *Quick check*: Does the judge achieve ≥75% agreement with human annotators on a held‑out set?

- **Granular Diagnostic Decomposition**  
  - *Why needed*: A single score masks specific failure modes; breaking down into consistency, rule adherence, quality, and reasoning guides targeted improvements.  
  - *Quick check*: Is SVQ high while SRO is low? That suggests the model prioritizes aesthetics over rule following.

- **Complexity‑Driven Generalization**  
  - *Why needed*: Simple tasks can be solved by pattern matching; increasing difficulty tests deep reasoning and rule induction.  
  - *Quick check*: Does performance drop sharply as the number of elements (e.g., Sudoku grid size) increases?

- **Reinforcement Learning vs Supervised Fine‑Tuning**  
  - *Why needed*: RL with a success‑based reward encourages exploration and rule learning, whereas SFT may lead to mimicry of limited demonstrations.  
  - *Quick check*: Does an RL‑trained model generalize to larger mazes better than an SFT‑trained one?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Data Repository -> Task Templater -> Judge Service (Gemini‑2.5‑Pro) -> Response Parser -> Metric Calculator -> Diagnostic Analyzer

**Critical Path**  
The evaluation pipeline relies on the sequence: Task Templater builds the prompt (instruction, images, GT), which is sent to Judge Service; Response Parser extracts scores; Metric Calculator aggregates them. Failure at any step stops the benchmark.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- *Automated Judge*: Gemini‑2.5‑Pro offers flexibility and human alignment but adds cost, API dependency, and opacity; a custom rule‑based judge would be transparent but less adaptable.
- *Binary vs Graded Metrics*: Result metrics are binary to reflect strict success criteria, while process metrics use a 0‑100 scale to capture gradual progress. Binary metrics simplify but lose nuance (e.g., partial compliance).
- *Ground‑Truth References*: Including GT improves judge accuracy (78.6%) but is not always available; the benchmark works without GT but expects lower alignment.
- *Dual‑Track Requirement*: Capturing intermediate frames for sequential tasks increases burden on video models but yields richer diagnostics; a final‑output‑only evaluation would miss the “illusion of reasoning.”

**Failure Signatures**
- *Illusion of Reasoning*: High process visual quality (SVQ >80%) with near‑zero reasoning success (SRS <5%) in video models.
- *Judge Inconsistency*: High score variance on identical samples across runs (temperature mis‑setting) or significantly lower accuracy when GT references are omitted.
- *CoT Error Propagation*: Models with explicit chain‑of‑thought may show improved process metrics but unchanged/degraded result accuracy, indicating accumulating intermediate errors.
- *Complexity Collapse*: A sharp drop in reasoning success (e.g., >30% decrease) when task difficulty crosses a threshold (e.g., maze size >6×6).

**First Experiments**
1. *Baseline Replication*: Evaluate a well‑known open‑source model (e.g., Bagel) on the Sorting task; verify scores match the paper’s range (Avg Score ~34%).
2. *CoT Impact Test*: Compare the same model with and without chain‑of‑thought on Maze Navigation 4×4; observe increases in process scores but potential stagnation in reasoning success.
3. *Generalization Stress Test*: Train an RL agent on 6×6 mazes and evaluate on 8×8 mazes; expect a smaller performance gap compared to an SFT agent.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Why do video models show high process VQ but near-zero reasoning success ("illusion of reasoning")?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Table 3: Sora 2 Pro Process VQ 85.5% vs Result RS 10.1%; Kling 1.6 77.0% vs 1.6%.
- **Why unresolved**: The coexistence of high temporal coherence and extremely low reasoning success suggests models mimic logical flow without true constraint understanding; underlying cause unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Probe internal representations for rule adherence; add explicit rule‑checking modules and measure RS changes; cross‑architecture comparisons.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Under what conditions does explicit Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) improve final accuracy in visual generative models?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Paper reports CoT enhances interpretability but not necessarily accuracy; some CoT models have lower Result scores (Table 3).
- **Why unresolved**: CoT's benefit for accuracy is ambiguous for vision generation; identifying enabling factors could guide design.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematically vary CoT depth, task complexity, and model size; correlate process metrics (e.g., SProcess) with Result RS across diverse tasks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Why does RL on OOD high‑complexity data outperform SFT for reasoning generalization?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Section 4.3: RL on 8x8 mazes yields up to 97% RS, surpassing SFT and proprietary models; OOD training forces rule learning.
- **Why unresolved**: Mechanism of RL's advantage unclear; insights could improve training regimes.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Compare learning dynamics and representation similarity of SFT vs RL in maze tasks; test transfer to other reasoning domains (physical, knowledge).

## Limitations
- Dataset details (instructions, per‑task sample counts, ground‑truth references) are not publicly available.
- Proprietary model inference settings (guidance scale, CFG, steps, CoT planner prompts) remain undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
- The benchmark’s reliance on a single LLM judge (Gemini‑2.5‑Pro) introduces potential bias and cost barriers; the reported RL benefits are limited to maze navigation tasks, with broader generalization unverified.
