# Transforming User Defined Criteria into Explainable Indicators with an Integrated LLM AHP System

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05267  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05267  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** UniScore achieves higher correlation with ground-truth quality signals (Spearman ρ up to 0.4303) than baseline methods, while maintaining real‑time inference speed and producing interpretable weights with low consistency ratios.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of converting user‑defined textual quality criteria into a single, explainable score suitable for real‑time web applications. It introduces UniScore, a system that uses lightweight language models to score individual criteria, quantifies each criterion’s ability to discriminate between high‑ and low‑quality examples via Jensen–Shannon distance, and aggregates these into a final weighted score using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). Evaluated on Amazon reviews and depression‑tweet datasets, UniScore outperforms regression, neural networks, and large LLM baselines in correlation with external quality signals, while remaining efficient and producing human‑interpretable, domain‑consistent weights.

## Method Summary
UniScore follows a data‑driven pipeline: (1) partition the dataset into high‑ and low‑quality groups using an available quality signal (e.g., helpful votes); (2) score each text on every user‑defined criterion using a small LLM prompted for Likert‑scale judgments; (3) for each criterion, estimate the probability distribution of scores in both groups (via smoothed histograms) and compute Jensen–Shannon distance (JSD) to measure discriminative power, also noting which group scores higher on average; (4) construct an AHP pairwise comparison matrix by mapping the differences in JSD values to Saaty’s scale, extract the principal eigenvector to obtain consistent positive weights, and multiply by the direction sign; (5) at inference, compute a new text’s UniScore as the signed linear combination of its per‑criterion LLM scores. The approach requires a supervision signal for initial weighting but yields an interpretable, real‑time predictor.

## Key Results
- Superior predictive correlation: UniScore achieves the highest Spearman ρ (up to 0.4303) on Amazon reviews, significantly outperforming linear regression, random forests, and neural networks, with lower variance and skew.
- Operational efficiency: Using Qwen‑1.7B, evaluating 100 samples takes ~2.25 minutes, comparable to or faster than large proprietary LLM ensembles while matching or exceeding their correlation.
- Explainable and consistent weighting: AHP produces low consistency ratios (mean CR ≈ 0.01–0.02, all < 0.04), and final weights align with domain expectations in case studies (e.g., “expertise” receives positive weight for medical Q&A).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
 Measuring discriminative power via Jensen–Shannon distance (JSD) yields robust criterion weights even when LLM outputs suffer from central‑tendency bias. JSD quantifies the distributional separation between high‑ and low‑quality groups on a per‑criterion basis; because it focuses on the shape of the score distributions (converted to PMFs) and is bounded and symmetric, it remains sensitive to relative differences even if all scores are compressed. If a criterion’s JSD is near zero, it fails to discriminate and is effectively ignored by the final model. Evidence includes the formal definition in Section 3.1 and practical computation details in Section 4.3, with the break condition being near‑identical PMFs across groups.

### Mechanism 2
 Deriving aggregation weights through the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) from pairwise comparisons of discriminative differences yields consistent, interpretable coefficients without assuming linearity of the raw score distributions. For each pair of criteria, the difference in their JSD values (Δ) is linearly mapped to an entry in the pairwise matrix (a_ij = 1 + 8Δ or its reciprocal), respecting Saaty’s scale [1/9, 9]. The principal eigenvector of this matrix provides positive weights; a sign term based on which group has the higher mean score is later applied. Consistency ratios in experiments are well below the 0.1 threshold, confirming reliable judgments. The break condition occurs when many Δ values are near zero, leading to nearly uniform weights, or if CR exceeds 0.1.

### Mechanism 3
 Final aggregation as a signed linear combination of per‑criterion LLM scores produces a quality indicator that is both predictive of external signals and interpretable via explicit criterion weights. The linear form enables real‑time inference (O(m) per text), while the weights reveal which criteria matter and in which direction. This design assumes that criterion interactions are negligible and that the weighted sum sufficiently captures the underlying quality construct. Evidence includes the highest correlation results across datasets (Section 5.4) and a case study showing UniScore’s weights aligning with domain knowledge, unlike regression which produced counter‑intuitive weights. The break condition would be strong non‑linear interactions in the ground truth or high noise in LLM scores.

## Foundational Learning
- **Jensen–Shannon Divergence (JSD)**: A symmetric, bounded measure of difference between two probability distributions. *Why needed*: It robustly quantifies how well a criterion separates high‑ and low‑quality groups, even if LLM scores are compressed. *Quick check*: How does JSD handle zero probabilities, and why is its square root used as a metric?
- **Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)**: A structured technique for deriving priority weights from pairwise comparisons. *Why needed*: It converts relative discriminative powers (JSD differences) into consistent, normalized weights with a provable consistency ratio. *Quick check*: What is the role of the consistency ratio (CR) in AHP, and what threshold is considered acceptable?
- **LLM‑as‑Judge for Criterion Scoring**: Using a language model to evaluate individual aspects of a text via prompted Likert scales. *Why needed*: Decomposes evaluation into lightweight tasks that small models can perform reliably, avoiding the latency of monolithic evaluation. *Quick check*: What design elements (definition, guidelines, scale description, JSON output) are required in a criteria prompt to ensure consistent LLM scores?

## Architecture Onboarding
Group Partitioning -> Criteria Scoring -> Distribution Estimation -> AHP Weight Derivation -> Inference Engine  
**Critical path**: The system requires an initial quality signal to partition the data into high/low groups; without such a signal, discriminative power cannot be estimated.  
**Design tradeoffs**:  
- LLM size vs. cost/latency: Small models enable real‑time scoring but may increase noise; JSD‑AHP aggregation is designed to be robust to this noise.  
- Deterministic vs. stochastic: Temperature=0 ensures reproducibility but may reduce variability; averaging runs was avoided to keep latency low.  
- Supervised vs. unsupervised: UniScore needs a supervision signal for partitioning; it is not fully unsupervised.  
- Linear aggregation vs. black‑box: Linear model sacrifices potential non‑linear interactions but gains interpretability and speed.  
**Failure signatures**:  
- High CR (> 0.1) after AHP → inconsistent weight matrix; investigate JSD values.  
- Very low JSD across criteria → criteria do not separate groups; consider adding more discriminative criteria.  
- Parsing failures from LLM (non‑JSON output) → retry mechanism may fail; fallback scores can bias results.  
- Inference time exceeds budget → check LLM call latency (O(m) per text) and consider model compression.  
**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Main performance comparison: Compute predictive metrics (Spearman ρ, Kendall τ, Pearson r, Welch p‑value, F1, accuracy) across three datasets (Amazon Reviews, RoSE XSum, Depression Tweet) against baselines (Single LLM, Random Weights, Regression, Random Forest, Neural Network). UniScore consistently achieves highest or near‑highest correlations with lower variance.  
2. Flagship model benchmark: Evaluate UniScore (Qwen‑1.7B) against large proprietary LLMs (GPT‑5, Claude 4) on Amazon Reviews, measuring correlation and wall‑clock time for 100 samples. UniScore matches or exceeds correlation while being orders of magnitude faster (2.25 min vs. ~15 min for ensembles).  
3. Ablation on percentile threshold p: Vary p (0.001%, 0.1%, 1%, 3%, 5%, 7%, 10%) on Amazon Reviews and observe correlation stability; performance peaks in [0.1%, 5%] and degrades at extremes, confirming the trade‑off between statistical robustness and computational cost.

## Limitations
- Generalizability limited to two domains (Amazon reviews, depression tweets); performance may not extend to other genres or languages.  
- Dependency on the quality of the partition signal; noisy or biased quality signals impair JSD‑based weight estimation.  
- Linear aggregation assumption may overlook important interactions among criteria, potentially limiting predictive accuracy.

## Confidence
- JSD‑based discriminative weighting: Medium  
- AHP weight consistency: High  
- UniScore predictive performance: Medium  
- Operational efficiency: High  

## Next Checks
1. Cross‑domain generalization: Evaluate UniScore on at least three additional domains (e.g., restaurant reviews, code quality, academic peer review) and report correlation with appropriate ground‑truth signals.  
2. Bias robustness test: Systematically induce central‑tendency bias in LLM scores (e.g., via temperature adjustments or score clipping), then measure JSD stability and resulting UniScore correlation to validate claimed robustness.  
3. Non‑linear aggregation exploration: Replace linear weighted sum with an interpretable non‑linear model (e.g., rule‑based or shallow neural network respecting criterion ordering) and compare predictive performance and weight interpretability.
