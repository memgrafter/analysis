# EComStage: Stage-wise and Orientation-specific Benchmarking for Large Language Models in E-commerce

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.02752
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02752
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** EComStage reveals that no LLM consistently excels across all three reasoning stages (Perception, Planning, Action) and both orientations; top models achieve ~84-85% average, with Perception scores high but Planning/Action variable, and merchant tasks expose weaknesses even in GPT-4o.

## Executive Summary
EComStage introduces a novel benchmark for evaluating large language models in e-commerce scenarios. Unlike prior benchmarks that focus solely on final task success, EComStage decomposes e-commerce reasoning into three intermediate stages—Perception, Planning, and Action—and evaluates both customer-oriented and merchant-oriented interactions. The dataset comprises 4,804 human-annotated instances across seven tasks, with rigorous data sanitization to ensure quality and safety. The evaluation uses accuracy for classification tasks and cosine similarity (via Qwen3-Embedding-8B) for open-ended generation. Results on over 30 LLMs (1B to 200B+ parameters) show that while all models perform well on Perception, they exhibit significant variance in Planning and Action. Moreover, merchant-oriented tasks highlight previously unnoticed weaknesses, such as GPT-4o’s relative drop in performance. No single model dominates across all stages and orientations, underscoring the need for targeted agent optimization. The benchmark provides fine-grained insights into stage-specific and orientation-specific capabilities, enabling more precise development of e-commerce agents.

## Method Summary
The EComStage benchmark is built from real-world e-commerce service data, annotated by professionals. The construction pipeline includes: (1) collecting authentic business scenarios; (2) human annotation of input-output pairs; (3) task-specific filtering to anonymize and remove sensitive identifiers; and (4) global filtering that employs LLM-based consistency checks, translation, and security screening. The final dataset contains 4,804 instances across seven tasks: Query Rewrite, Attitude Classification, Query Match, Intent Recognition, Scenario Route, Solution Decision, and RAG-QA, each aligned with either customer or merchant orientation. Evaluation is performed with fixed generation parameters (temperature 0.1, top-p 0.001, max tokens 512) on 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs. Closed-ended tasks are scored by exact-match accuracy; open-ended tasks use cosine similarity between the model output and reference embeddings from Qwen3-Embedding-8B. The benchmark harness supports batch inference and reports stage-wise and orientation-wise averages.

## Key Results
- No model excels across all stages and orientations; top performers Claude Sonnet 4 (84.21 avg) and Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct (85.61 avg) still show stage-specific gaps.
- All models achieve high scores on Perception tasks, but Planning and Action exhibit greater performance variance, indicating larger challenges.
- Merchant-oriented tasks reveal weaknesses in otherwise strong models; for example, GPT-4o scores notably lower on merchant scenarios compared to customer ones.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Stage-wise reasoning decomposition.** The benchmark divides e-commerce reasoning into three stages—Perception, Planning, and Action—and assigns tasks accordingly. This design exposes intermediate reasoning weaknesses that would be hidden in an end-to-end evaluation. By measuring each stage separately, the benchmark identifies which stage is responsible for failures, as confirmed by results showing uniformly high Perception scores but substantial variance in Planning and Action across all models. If scores were uniformly high and tightly correlated, this diagnostic value would vanish.

**Mechanism 2: Dual orientation assessment.** Most prior benchmarks focus exclusively on customer-facing interactions. EComStage introduces merchant-oriented tasks, thereby uncovering orientation-specific performance gaps. The evaluation reveals that even top models like GPT-4o exhibit relative weakness on merchant scenarios, a finding that would have been missed by a single-orientation benchmark. If models performed equally well on both orientations, the inclusion of merchant tasks would not provide additional insights.

**Mechanism 3: Rigorous data construction pipeline.** The dataset is derived from real e-commerce business data, professionally annotated, and subjected to a two-level filtering process: task-specific filters remove sensitive identifiers, and global filters perform consistency checks, translation, and security screening. This multi-step sanitization preserves authenticity while ensuring safety and high quality, thereby producing trustworthy evaluation results. Should the filtering inadvertently leak private data or over-sanitize and bias the data, the benchmark’s validity would be compromised.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Task definitions (7 tasks mapping to 3 stages and 2 orientations) → Dataset instances (4,804 human-annotated samples) → Annotation guidelines and quality-check procedures → Filtering scripts (task-specific and global) → Evaluation harness (prompt templates, inference API wrappers) → Metric modules (accuracy for closed-ended, cosine similarity using Qwen3-Embedding-8B for open-ended) → Results aggregation and reporting utilities

Critical path:  
1. Obtain dataset files (train/test splits) from the official repository.  
2. Configure target LLM access (API key or local model path).  
3. For each task, select the appropriate prompt template and format inputs.  
4. Run batch inference with deterministic settings (temperature 0.1, top-p 0.001, max tokens 512).  
5. Compute metrics against human-provided references.  
6. Aggregate scores by stage (Perception/Planning/Action) and orientation (customer/merchant).  
7. Validate that scores align with expected ranges (e.g., Perception > 80%).

Design tradeoffs:  
- Closed-ended vs open-ended metrics: Accuracy is objective but coarse; cosine similarity captures semantic alignment but depends on the embedding model.  
- Stage-wise separation vs integrated evaluation: Separate tasks enable fine-grained diagnosis but may not reflect end-to-end error propagation.  
- Human annotation cost vs scale: Limited to 4,804 instances for high quality; synthetic benchmarks could be larger but less reliable.  
- Dual orientation: Increases comprehensiveness but complicates analysis and requires balanced splits.

Failure signatures:  
- Low inter-annotator agreement (if measured) indicates ambiguous task definitions.  
- Unusually high scores on specific tasks suggest data leakage or overfitting.  
- Inconsistent orientation patterns (e.g., a model shows no gap when a gap is reported) may indicate mis-grouping.  
- NaN or extreme cosine similarity values reflect embedding model failures or empty responses.  
- Repeated time‑outs or memory errors during inference due to long contexts or large model sizes.

First 3 experiments:  
1. Smoke test – Run the full pipeline on a tiny subset (5 instances per task) with Qwen3-4B-Instruct. Verify prompt formatting, inference, metrics, and that Perception accuracy exceeds 80%.  
2. Stage-wise variance replication – Evaluate the full test set, compute stage averages, and perform statistical tests (paired t-test or ANOVA) to confirm significant differences between stages (p < 0.05).  
3. Orientation gap analysis – For each model, compute the absolute difference between customer and merchant averages. Rank by gap size and verify GPT-4o shows a notable merchant penalty, matching the paper.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Capturing error propagation across stages.** How can we design evaluation methodologies that capture error propagation across the Perception, Planning, and Action stages in LLM-based e-commerce agents? (Limitation: stages are evaluated separately, so error chains are unobserved.) Resolution requires integrated cross-stage annotations or pipelines tracking error chains.  
2. **Expanding domain coverage.** How can EComStage be extended to cover a broader range of e-commerce domains and scenarios to improve evaluation breadth? (Limitation: current tasks are finite and not fully representative.) Resolution involves systematic data collection from additional domains (e.g., travel, digital goods, B2B) and validation of model stability.  
3. **Explaining stage performance disparity.** Why do current LLMs exhibit high performance on Perception tasks but greater variance in Planning and Action stages? (Observation: all models show this pattern.) Resolution would come from controlled experiments ablating model size, training data, and fine-grained error analysis.  
4. **Merchant-oriented task difficulty.** What factors contribute to the difficulty of merchant-oriented tasks for state-of-the-art LLMs, even when they excel on customer tasks? (Finding: merchant tasks reveal weaknesses.) Resolution requires comparative failure analysis, data audits for representation gaps, and targeted fine-tuning.  
5. **Achieving balanced multi-stage capabilities.** What architectural or training modifications can lead to LLMs that consistently excel across all three reasoning stages and both orientations? (Observation: no model dominates.) Resolution calls for new training objectives or multi-task learning setups that jointly optimize stage-specific performance.

## Limitations
- Dependency on unreleased official code and precise prompt templates; without them, exact replication is ambiguous.  
- Dataset size is modest (4,804 instances), limiting domain diversity and statistical robustness.  
- No reported inter-annotator agreement, leaving data quality difficulty to assess.
