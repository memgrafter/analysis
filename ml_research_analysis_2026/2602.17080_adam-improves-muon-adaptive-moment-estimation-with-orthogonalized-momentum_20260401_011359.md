# Adam Improves Muon: Adaptive Moment Estimation with Orthogonalized Momentum

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17080
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17080
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: NAMO and NAMO-D combine orthogonalized momentum with adaptive moment estimation, achieving superior performance over AdamW and Muon on GPT-2 pretraining with optimal theoretical convergence.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of merging an effective deterministic update direction with robust noise adaptation in stochastic optimization for deep learning. The proposed methods, NAMO and NAMO-D, integrate Muon’s orthogonalized momentum with Adam’s norm-based adaptive moment estimation. NAMO applies a single adaptive scalar to the orthogonalized momentum, while NAMO-D uses column-wise diagonal scaling with clamping to enable neuron-wise adaptivity. Theoretically, both achieve optimal convergence: O(T⁻¹/²) deterministically and O(T⁻¹/⁴ plus a variance-dependent term) stochastically. Empirically, on GPT-2 models of 124M and 355M parameters, both methods outperform AdamW and Muon in final training and validation loss, with NAMO-D providing additional gains via its clamping hyperparameter.

## Method Summary
NAMO modifies Muon by scaling the orthogonalized momentum matrix \(O_t = \text{Orth}(M_t)\) by a scalar \(\alpha_t \propto \|M_t\|_F / \sqrt{v_t}\), where \(v_t\) is an exponential moving average of gradient norm squared. This preserves orthogonality while introducing adaptive stepsize control. NAMO-D extends this to per-column scaling: a diagonal matrix \(D_t\) with entries proportional to column-wise momentum norms divided by corresponding second-moment estimates, followed by entrywise clamping to bound the condition number. Both methods follow an iterative procedure: compute stochastic gradient, update momentum \(M_t\) and second moment \(v_t\), approximate orthogonalization (e.g., via Newton-Schulz), compute adaptive scaling, and apply the update with optional decoupled weight decay. The clamping in NAMO-D balances fine-grained noise adaptation against ill-conditioning.

## Key Results
- NAMO and NAMO-D achieve lower final training and validation loss compared to AdamW and Muon on GPT-2 pretraining (124M and 355M parameters).
- NAMO-D yields additional improvements through a clamping hyperparameter \(c\) that regulates the trade-off between orthogonality benefits and neuron-wise noise adaptation.
- Theoretical analysis guarantees optimal convergence rates: deterministic \(O(T^{-1/2})\) and stochastic \(O(T^{-1/4} + \text{variance term})\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Orthogonalized Momentum for Deterministic Direction
Orthogonalized momentum (from Muon) maps the momentum matrix to the nearest orthogonal matrix, which aligns with the steepest descent direction under the spectral norm. This leverages structure in loss landscapes (e.g., correlated singular directions) to accelerate convergence in deterministic settings. However, under high stochastic noise, orthogonalization can amplify perturbations and destabilize updates without adaptive scaling.

### Mechanism 2: Norm-Based Adaptive Scaling for Noise Stabilization
NAMO and NAMO-D scale the orthogonalized momentum by factors derived from second-moment estimates: a scalar \(\alpha_t\) for NAMO (using \(\|M_t\|_F\)) or a per-column vector for NAMO-D (using column norms). This mimics Adam’s adaptivity, shrinking stepsizes when local gradient scales (or noise) are large. The approach assumes bounded gradient variance and reliable second-moment approximations, but may falter with heavy-tailed noise or when second-moment estimates approach zero.

### Mechanism 3: Clamping for Well-Conditioned Neuron-Wise Adaptation
NAMO-D clamps its per-neuron adaptive stepsizes to a range \([c \cdot \text{avg}, \text{avg}/c]\), ensuring the condition number of the scaling matrix \(D_t\) is bounded by \(1/c^2\). This prevents any neuron from having an excessively large or small stepsize while retaining enough adaptivity. The hyperparameter \(c\) must be tuned: too small over-regularizes, too large risks ill-conditioning. The clamping balances orthogonality preservation with robust noise adaptation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Matrix orthogonalization via polar/SVD**: Generates the orthogonal update direction \(O_t = \text{Orth}(M_t)\), central to Muon and NAMO. Quick check: If \(M\) is already orthogonal, \(\text{Orth}(M) = M\); if \(M\) is zero, \(\text{Orth}(M)\) is typically the zero matrix (or undefined in exact arithmetic).
- **Adaptive moment estimation (Adam-style)**: Provides noise adaptation via scaled updates using second-moment estimates. Quick check: As \(v_t\) grows, the effective stepsize decreases; \(\varepsilon\) prevents division by zero and adds a minimal stepsize floor.
- **Convergence rates for nonconvex stochastic optimization**: Theoretical guarantees measure progress via average gradient norm. Quick check: \(O(T^{-1/4})\) implies many iterations for modest improvement; increasing batch size \(b\) reduces the variance term in the stochastic rate as \(1/\sqrt{b}\).

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Inputs (loss \(L\), stochastic gradient \(G_t\)) → Update first moment \(M_t\) → Update second moment \(v_t\) (scalar or vector) → Orthogonalization \(O_t = \text{Orth}(M_t)\) → Adaptive scaling (\(\alpha_t\) for NAMO, \(d_t/D_t\) for NAMO-D) → Parameter update \(\Theta_t\) (plus weight decay).

### Critical Path
1. Compute stochastic gradient \(G_t\) on minibatch.
2. Update momentum: \(M_t \leftarrow \mu_1 M_{t-1} + (1-\mu_1) G_t\).
3. Update second moment: \(v_t \leftarrow \mu_2 v_{t-1} + (1-\mu_2) \cdot \|G_t\|^2\) (scalar for NAMO, per-column for NAMO-D).
4. Compute orthogonalized momentum \(O_t \approx \text{Orth}(M_t)\) (e.g., via Newton-Schulz iterations).
5. Compute adaptive scaling: for NAMO, \(\alpha_t = \|M_t\|_F / (\sqrt{v_t} + \varepsilon)\) with bias correction; for NAMO-D, compute per-column \(d_t\), clamp to \(D_t\).
6. Apply update: \(\Theta_t \leftarrow \Theta_{t-1} - \eta \cdot (\alpha_t O_t)\) or \(\Theta_t \leftarrow \Theta_{t-1} - \eta \cdot (O_t D_t)\), with optional weight decay.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **NAMO vs. NAMO-D**: NAMO preserves exact orthogonality and is simpler (only scalar state) but lacks per-neuron adaptivity. NAMO-D offers finer-grained noise adaptation at the cost of breaking orthogonality and requiring tuning of clamping parameter \(c\).
- **Computational/memory**: Both add negligible cost over Muon. NAMO adds \(O(1)\) state; NAMO-D adds \(O(n)\) vector state (\(n\) = number of columns). This is more efficient than Adam’s per-element second moment (\(O(mn)\)).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: NAMO-D’s \(c\) varies with model size (e.g., 0.1 for 124M, 0.9 for 355M), indicating tuning complexity.

### Failure Signatures
- **Divergence or oscillation**: Caused by poor orthogonalization approximation (too few Newton-Schulz iterations) or exploding stepsizes when \(v_t\) becomes too small. Monitor \(\|G_t\|\) and \(\|M_t\|\).
- **Slow convergence**: In NAMO-D, if \(c\) is too small, clamping over-regularizes per-neuron adaptivity; if too large, the update becomes ill-conditioned. Track condition number of \(D_t\) (should be \(\leq 1/c^2\)).
- **Poor generalization**: Excessive adaptation to noisy gradients may harm validation performance. If training loss drops but validation loss plateaus, reduce adaptivity (use NAMO or increase \(c\) in NAMO-D).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Hyperparameter sweep**: For each optimizer (AdamW, Muon, NAMO, NAMO-D), sweep learning rate \(\eta\) on 124M model for 10K steps. Select optimal \(\eta\) based on training/validation loss at step 10K. For NAMO-D, also sweep clamping \(c\).
2. **Extended training**: Using optimal hyperparameters, train 124M for 50K steps and 355M for 10K steps. Compare final training/validation losses; expect NAMO-D to improve over NAMO on longer runs.
3. **Ablation on clamping \(c\)**: For 355M model, run NAMO-D with various \(c\) values (e.g., 0.12, 0.40, 0.75, 0.90) at optimal \(\eta\). Plot loss curves to show sensitivity and identify best trade-off.

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (smoothness, bounded variance) may not hold in practice; orthogonalization can amplify noise under high stochasticity, and implicit regularization effects are not captured.
- NAMO-D’s clamping parameter \(c\) is sensitive and requires model-specific tuning, with optimal values differing across scales.
- Evaluation is restricted to GPT-2 pretraining; generalizability to other architectures (e.g., CNNs) and tasks (e.g., classification) remains unknown.

## Confidence
- Theoretical convergence guarantees: Medium (proofs likely sound but assumptions questionable in practice).
- Empirical superiority on GPT-2: High (supported by extensive hyperparameter sweeps and consistent results across model scales).
- Clamping mechanism benefits: Medium (depends on appropriate \(c\) choice; limited ablation provided).
