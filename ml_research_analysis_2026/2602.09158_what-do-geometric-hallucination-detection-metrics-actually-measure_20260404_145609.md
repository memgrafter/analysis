# What do Geometric Hallucination Detection Metrics Actually Measure?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09158
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09158
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** Hidden Score and Attention Score detect irrelevant hallucinations, while Matrix Entropy detects incoherent hallucinations; perturbation normalization reduces domain shift and improves detection AUROC by up to +34 points.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates what geometric hallucination detection metrics (Hidden Score, Matrix Entropy, Attention Score) actually measure by evaluating them on a synthetic dataset that isolates specific hallucination types: factual incorrectness, under-confidence, irrelevance, incoherence, and incompleteness. The core finding is that different metrics are sensitive to different types: Hidden Score and Attention Score detect irrelevance, while Matrix Entropy detects incoherence. A major secondary finding is severe domain shift: metrics trained or evaluated on one domain (e.g., math) fail on others (e.g., history), causing AUROC to drop dramatically in multi-domain settings. To mitigate this, the authors propose a perturbation normalization method, which compares a response's geometric statistic to those of perturbed versions of the same response. This normalization aligns distributions across domains, boosting detection AUROC by +34 points (e.g., Attention Score from 0.60 to 0.94) for factual incorrectness in the multi-domain "all" setting.

## Method Summary
The study constructs a synthetic dataset covering three domains: math (400 integer multiplications), history (70 event–year QA pairs), and counting (80 word-count QA pairs). For each domain, correct and hallucinated responses are generated by applying controlled transformations corresponding to different hallucination types and severity levels. Using Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct, the model processes prompt-response pairs under teacher forcing, and hidden states and attention maps are extracted for the response token span. Three geometric statistics are computed per layer: Hidden Score (log-det of the Gram matrix of hidden vectors), Matrix Entropy (Shannon entropy of the eigenvalue distribution of the Gram matrix), and Attention Score (mean log of diagonal attention entries). A perturbation normalization technique is introduced: for a given response, six perturbed variants are created by adding small numeric offsets to the answer; the original statistic is normalized to a z-score relative to the perturbation distribution. Detection performance is evaluated using AUROC for each statistic across layers, with best-layer results reported for each hallucination type and domain.

## Key Results
- Hidden Score and Attention Score are highly effective at detecting irrelevant hallucinations (AUROC up to 0.96) but show no sensitivity to incoherence.
- Matrix Entropy reliably detects incoherent hallucinations, achieving near-perfect AUROC, while it is less effective for irrelevance.
- Raw geometric metrics suffer from severe domain shift; in the combined multi-domain "all" setting, detection AUROC drops below 0.60. Perturbation normalization aligns distributions across domains and boosts AUROC by up to +34 points (e.g., Attention Score from 0.60 to 0.94).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Hidden Score (HS) and Attention Score (AS) are sensitive to *irrelevant* hallucinations.
- **Mechanism:** HS measures the log-volume of the parallelotope formed by hidden state vectors (via log-determinant of the Gram matrix), while AS measures the average log self-attention (diagonal entries of attention maps). Irrelevant responses, where the prompt question differs from the response question, cause the model's internal representations to become more diffuse (higher volume) and induce atypical attention patterns (higher self-attention on non-answer tokens), increasing both scores.
- **Core assumption:** Irrelevance disrupts the model's focused representation on the correct answer token sequence, increasing geometric dispersion or self-attention concentration.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "Hidden Score and Attention Score detect irrelevance."
  - [section 3.1]: "HS and AS achieve AUROCs of 0.94 and 0.96 for detecting irrelevant hallucinations on all."
  - [corpus]: Weak direct support; neighboring papers focus on other hallucination aspects (e.g., RAG faithfulness, factuality metrics). Corpus does not contradict the claim.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails for *incoherent* hallucinations (repeated, inconsistent responses), where HS and AS show *lower* scores for hallucinations, opposite to the "higher=hallucination" detection pattern used for incorrectness.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Matrix Entropy (ME) is sensitive to *incoherent* hallucinations.
- **Mechanism:** ME computes the Shannon entropy of the eigenvalue distribution of the hidden state Gram matrix. Incoherent responses (multiple inconsistent answer repetitions) create highly non-uniform or divergent eigenvalue spectra, leading to higher entropy. This captures a different aspect of geometric "disorder" than volume (HS) or attention (AS).
- **Core assumption:** Incoherence induces a specific pattern of dispersion across singular vectors that increases the entropy of the representation's covariance structure, distinct from the sheer volume increase measured by HS.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "Matrix Entropy detects incoherence."
  - [section 3.1]: "ME is highly receptive to incoherent hallucinations, achieving near perfect AUROC for this category."
  - [section C, Figure 3g, 3k]: ME shows strong separation for incoherence across layers, while HS/AS do not.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism is insensitive to *irrelevance*, where ME does not show strong separation (AUROC 0.76-0.99 on "all" but much lower on single domains for history/counting).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Perturbation normalization mitigates domain shift by converting geometric statistics into *outlier scores* relative to locally perturbed responses.
- **Mechanism:** For a given response, the normalized score `f*(Hl)` is the z-score of the original statistic `f(Hl)` compared to `k` perturbed versions of the same response (e.g., answer ± small offset). This removes domain-specific baseline shifts in absolute statistic values (e.g., different hidden state scales in math vs. counting) because a *correct* response should be an outlier (lower score) relative to its *incorrect* perturbations, a relationship that holds cross-domain. An incorrect response's statistic will be less of an outlier.
- **Core assumption:** For a correct response, the true answer's internal representation is geometrically more "stable" or "canonical" than nearby incorrect answers, creating a consistent within-example signal independent of the domain's absolute representation scale.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "Our normalization aligns distributions across domains, boosting detection AUROC by +34 points."
  - [section 4]: Detailed method and result that "HS-Norm and ME-Norm achieve AUROCs of 0.96... while AS-Norm achieves an AUROC of 0.89" on multi-domain "all", compared to <0.60 without normalization.
  - [section 4, Figure 2a, 2c]: Visual and quantitative evidence of domain alignment and performance gain.
- **Break condition:** The method fails if the perturbation set is not meaningful for the domain (e.g., perturbations that don't alter the answer's validity or that induce new, irrelevant hallucinations), or if the "correct" response is itself geometrically unstable (e.g., very low-confidence generation).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Geometric Statistics of LLM Internals**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's core analysis uses HS, ME, and AS—all derived from hidden state Gram matrices or attention maps. Understanding these as measures of representation volume (HS), entropy/dispersion (ME), and self-attention concentration (AS) is prerequisite to interpreting their sensitivity patterns.
  - *Quick check question:* How does the log-determinant of a Gram matrix relate to the volume occupied by token vectors, and why might an "incorrect" answer increase this volume?
- **Concept: Hallucination Taxonomy (Fine-Grained)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's experiment disentangles hallucination into orthogonal properties: incorrectness, confidence, irrelevance, incoherence, incompleteness. A learner must grasp these distinctions to understand why a metric might fire for one type but not another.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the operational difference between an *irrelevant* hallucination (different prompt question) and an *incoherent* one (repeated inconsistent answers) in the synthetic dataset?
- **Concept: Domain Shift in Representation Geometry**
  - *Why needed here:* The major secondary finding is that absolute geometric statistic values vary drastically across domains (math vs. history), breaking detectors. Understanding this as a covariate shift problem in the feature space (the statistic itself) is key to motivating the normalization solution.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might the scale/variance of hidden state Gram matrices differ fundamentally between a domain like "integer multiplication" and "historical year lookup"?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** The system comprises: 1) LLM Engine (Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct) generating token representations and attention maps under teacher forcing; 2) Metric Extractors computing HS, ME, AS per layer; 3) Synthetic Data Generator creating controlled prompt-response pairs; 4) Perturbation Normalizer generating k perturbed responses and computing z-scores; 5) Evaluator computing AUROC.
- **Critical path:** Input PR pair → LLM forward pass (teacher forcing) → Extract[H_l, A_l] at answer token span → Compute f(H_l) or f*(H_l) → AUROC vs. hallucination label. Perturbation normalization is inserted between extraction and scoring.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Normalization vs. Compute:** Adds k forward passes per sample; paper uses k=6.
  - **Perturbation Design:** Perturbations must be domain-appropriate; wrong set breaks the outlier assumption.
  - **Layer Selection:** Optimal detection layer varies by domain (e.g., layer 30 for math, 16 for history/counting). System must either select per-domain or use a late layer as default.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low AUROC (<0.70) on multi-domain "all" without normalization.
  - A metric showing high AUROC for one type but near-random or inverse for another (e.g., HS low for incoherence).
  - Normalization degrading performance on single-domain data if perturbations are too large.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replicate Single-Domain Baselines:** On math domain for incorrectness level 3, verify HS/ME ~0.92 AUROC at layer 30 and HS/AS ~0.00 AUROC for incoherence.
  2. **Demonstrate Domain Shift:** Apply the same incorrectness detector (e.g., HS at layer 30) on math, history, counting individually (expect >0.90 AUROC) then on combined "all" (expect drop to ~0.57).
  3. **Apply Perturbation Normalization:** For "all" setting, compute HS-Norm and verify AUROC jumps to ~0.96; visualize distributions to confirm domain alignment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What characteristics of hallucinations do geometric detection metrics actually capture?** The paper establishes empirical correlations but lacks a theoretical explanation for why these metrics are sensitive to particular types.
2. **How do geometric hallucination detection metrics generalize across different model architectures, sizes, and training regimes?** The study is limited to Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct; it is unclear if findings hold universally.
3. **Can perturbation normalization be applied in black-box settings where the correct answer is unknown, and how should perturbations be designed for arbitrary output types?** The paper uses numeric offsets; generic strategies for text or other modalities remain open.
4. **How do geometric metrics perform on naturally occurring hallucinations, which often combine multiple flaw types, compared to isolated synthetic hallucinations?** Real-world hallucinations are messy and may overlap types; metric sensitivities may not translate.

## Limitations
- **Ambiguities in implementation details:** Tokenization (BOS/EOS handling, inclusion of prompt tokens), exact prompt templates for history/counting, and numerical stability in eigenvalue/Gram computation are unspecified, potentially affecting reproducibility.
- **Synthetic data scope:** Hallucinations are isolated and generated with simple numeric operations; generalizability to real-world, multi-faceted hallucinations is uncertain.
- **Perturbation normalization constraints:** The method relies on domain-specific perturbations (numeric offsets) and the assumption that correct responses are outliers relative to perturbations; this may not hold for non-numeric or highly ambiguous tasks.
