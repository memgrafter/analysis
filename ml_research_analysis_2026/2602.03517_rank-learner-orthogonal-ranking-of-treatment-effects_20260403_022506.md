# Rank-Learner: Orthogonal Ranking of Treatment Effects

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03517
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03517
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: A method that directly optimizes a pairwise ranking objective using a Neyman-orthogonal loss, achieving superior ranking performance (higher AUTOC and policy value) compared to CATE-based methods, especially in small-sample regimes and under limited overlap.

## Executive Summary
Rank-Learner addresses the critical problem of ranking individuals by their conditional average treatment effect (CATE) from observational data. Instead of first estimating full CATE—a harder, pointwise task—it directly optimizes a pairwise ranking objective. Its core innovation is a Neyman-orthogonal loss function, which makes the learning robust to errors in estimated nuisance parameters (propensity scores and response surfaces). The method is model-agnostic and employs cross-fitting to ensure valid nuisance estimates. Empirical results on synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets show it significantly outperforms standard CATE estimators (T-learner, DR-learner) and a non-orthogonal plug-in ranker on ranking metrics, with notable gains in small samples and under positivity violations.

## Method Summary
Rank-Learner is a two-stage procedure. Stage 1 uses cross-fitting to estimate nuisance functions: the conditional mean outcomes under treatment and control (µ₁(x), µ₀(x)) and the propensity score (e(x)). These out-of-sample estimates are combined to form doubly robust scores. Stage 2 trains a scoring function g(x) by minimizing an orthogonal ranking loss on randomly subsampled pairs of observations. The loss uses pseudo-labels constructed from the doubly robust scores and a smoothing parameter κ, ensuring the gradient is first-order insensitive to nuisance estimation errors. The final ranking is obtained by sorting test points according to g(x).

## Key Results
- On synthetic data with n=500, Rank-Learner achieves a test AUTOC of 1.30 vs. 1.28 for DR-learner.
- On semi-synthetic datasets (MOVIELENS, MIMIC-III, CPS) with n=1,000, it consistently achieves the highest AUTOC and mean policy value.
- Performance is robust to drastic reductions in the number of training pairs (e.g., using only 1% of all possible pairs) and degrades least under limited overlap (positivity violations).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

#### Mechanism 1: Neyman-Orthogonal Loss Construction
The orthogonal ranking loss `L_orth` is constructed to be first-order insensitive to errors in the estimated nuisances (`ê`, `µ̂1`, `µ̂0`). Starting from a smooth pairwise ranking loss `L_soft`, an influence-function-based correction term is added to the pseudo-label. This term cancels the bias that would otherwise arise from plugging in estimated nuisances. The correction is weighted by `ω_τ`, which scales the doubly robust score difference and concentrates adjustments on pairs where the ranking is ambiguous. This orthogonality property (proven in Theorem 5.1) ensures the loss's gradient remains accurate even when the nuisance estimates converge slowly, yielding `√n`-consistent estimation of the optimal ranking function. The guarantee relies on the doubly robust scores being unbiased for the true CATE conditional on X and on the loss being smooth in the CATE.

**Break condition**: If the overlap (positivity) condition fails, the doubly robust scores become unstable, degrading the orthogonality guarantee and overall performance.

#### Mechanism 2: Direct Pairwise Optimization vs. CATE Estimation
The ranking task only requires recovering the *ordering* of τ(x), not its pointwise magnitudes. A pairwise ranking loss (`L_bin` or `L_soft`) is therefore sufficient and solves a statistically easier problem than minimizing squared error for full CATE estimation (`L_cate`). The population minimizer of `L_bin` is any strictly increasing transformation of τ(x), whereas `L_cate` uniquely identifies τ(x) itself. The orthogonal loss `L_orth` inherits the same minimizers as `L_soft` (τ(x) up to an affine transformation), so it targets ranking directly without unnecessary pointwise identification. This avoids the curse of dimensionality and higher variance associated with full CATE estimation.

**Break condition**: If the downstream policy depends on precise effect magnitudes (e.g., a fixed threshold for treatment assignment), a ranking-only solution may be insufficient, and a full CATE estimate could be necessary.

#### Mechanism 3: Cross-Fitted Nuisance Estimation with Model-Agnostic Architecture
Cross-fitting is used to obtain out-of-sample predictions for the nuisance functions. The data is split into K folds; nuisance models are trained on K-1 folds and evaluated on the held-out fold. These independent estimates are then used to construct the pseudo-labels in the second stage. This prevents the scoring model g from overfitting to the same data used to estimate the nuisances, which would otherwise induce bias. The procedure is model-agnostic—any flexible ML model (e.g., neural networks) can be used for both nuisance estimation and the final scorer. The combination of cross-fitting and the orthogonal loss provides debiased machine learning (DML) guarantees for the ranking objective.

**Break condition**: If the second-stage model is overly complex relative to the data, or if the subsampling of pairs is too aggressive, the finite-sample variance of the pseudo-labels can become large and destabilize training.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Neyman-Orthogonality  
  *Why needed*: Guarantees the gradient of the loss is first-order insensitive to nuisance estimation errors, enabling `√n`-consistent estimation even when nuisances are learned at slower rates.  
  *Quick check*: If you perturb the estimated propensity score `ê` by a small amount, how much does the gradient of the loss change? For an orthogonal loss, the change should be second-order (negligible to first order).

- **Concept**: Pairwise Ranking vs. Pointwise Regression  
  *Why needed*: Ranking depends solely on the ordinal relationship τ(x) > τ(x'). A pairwise loss (`L_bin`) is identification-agnostic (minimized by any monotone transformation of τ), making the learning problem easier than full pointwise regression (`L_cate`).  
  *Quick check*: What is the population minimizer of `L_bin`? Any strictly increasing function h(τ(x)), not necessarily τ(x) itself.

- **Concept**: Cross-Fitting for Debiased Machine Learning  
  *Why needed*: Prevents overfitting when plugging in estimated nuisances (`η̂`) into a second-stage loss. By using out-of-sample nuisance predictions, the second-stage sample is independent of `η̂`, mitigating bias and enabling valid asymptotics.  
  *Quick check*: Why not estimate nuisances once on the full dataset and then train the ranking model? Because the ranking model would then be evaluated on data correlated with `η̂`, leading to spurious correlations and biased loss estimates.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Observational Data D -> (Stage 1: Cross-Fitting) -> Out-of-Sample Nuisance Estimates `η̂` (ê, µ̂1, µ̂0) -> (Stage 2: Pair Subsampling & Pseudo-labeling) -> Orthogonal Ranking Loss `L_orth(g, η̂)` -> Trained Scoring Function `g(x)` -> Inferred Ranking (sort by `g(x)`)

**Critical path**: Accurate nuisance estimation (`η̂`) -> correct pseudo-label construction (`t̃`) -> stable training of `g`. Failure at any stage compromises final ranking.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **κ (smoothing parameter)**: Small κ (→0) makes the loss more purely ranking-focused (invariant to scaling) but amplifies noise from the orthogonal correction in finite samples. Large κ (→∞) approaches `L_soft`, losing orthogonality benefits. Tuned via validation AUTOC.
- **Pair subsampling fraction**: Using all O(n²) pairs is computationally infeasible. Subsampling (e.g., 1% for n=1000) maintains performance while reducing cost, but too few pairs may prevent learning.
- **Nuisance model complexity**: Flexible models (e.g., neural nets) improve `η̂` quality but risk overfitting. Cross-fitting and early stopping are essential safeguards.

**Failure signatures**:
- Poor AUTOC despite large n: Nuisance models are underfitting (check MSE/BCE for `ê`, `µ̂`). Solution: increase model capacity or collect more data.
- High variance across random seeds: κ is too small, making pseudo-labels noisy from amplification. Solution: increase κ or stabilize `η̂`.
- No improvement over a non-orthogonal plug-in ranker: κ is too large, so `L_orth` ≈ `L_soft`. Solution: decrease κ.
- Early saturation in pair subsampling plot: Performance limited by `η̂` quality, not pair count. Solution: improve nuisance estimation.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Synthetic benchmark varying n**: Train on increasing sample sizes (e.g., n=100, 250, 500, 1000). Expect Rank-Learner's advantage to be largest at small n where nuisance errors are most impactful, and to scale favorably.
2. **Pair subsampling study**: Fix n=1000, vary the fraction of pairs used per training epoch (e.g., 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, 100%). Expect test AUTOC to saturate early (around 1%); using all pairs gives minimal gain at huge cost.
3. **Overlap sensitivity**: Vary the strength of the overlap condition (e.g., via a parameter α in the propensity model) while keeping other data-generating process fixed. Measure test AUTOC across methods. Expect all methods to degrade as overlap worsens, but Rank-Learner's orthogonalization should provide the most robust performance.

## Limitations
- **Data generation code unreported**: Exact implementation for semi-synthetic benchmarks (MOVIELENS, MIMIC-III, CPS) is not detailed, hampering exact replication.
- **Validation metric opacity**: The method for approximating AUTOC on validation data (from Chernozhukov et al., 2025) is not specified, making hyperparameter tuning without ground-truth τ unclear.
- **Theoretical conditions**: Guarantees rely on standard DML conditions (e.g., nuisance functions in Donsker classes, positive density of X). These may be violated in practice with complex ML models or severe positivity violations, potentially degrading finite-sample performance.

## Confidence
**Claim-Cluster Confidence**:
- *Orthogonal loss robustness*: **High**. Theorem 5.1 proves Neyman-orthogonality; experiments consistently show reduced sensitivity to nuisance errors, especially in small samples.
- *Direct pairwise optimization sufficiency*: **Medium**. Theorem 5.2 establishes ordering-only identification for the ranking task. However, the break condition—that some policies require precise magnitudes—is a valid theoretical caveat not fully explored empirically.
- *Cross-fitted model-agnostic architecture*: **Medium**. Adapts well-established DML cross-fitting. Finite-sample interactions with the ranking loss and aggressive pair subsampling are primarily supported by empirical results rather than deep theory.
