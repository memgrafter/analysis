# Error Propagation and Model Collapse in Diffusion Models: A Theoretical Study

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16601
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16601
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The paper derives that the accumulated distributional divergence in recursively trained diffusion models scales with a geometrically discounted sum of past score estimation errors, and shows that model collapse is avoided iff these errors are summable.

## Executive Summary
Score-based diffusion models are vulnerable to error accumulation when trained recursively on their own synthetic data. This paper provides a theoretical analysis of this phenomenon, focusing on the setting where each generation is trained on a mixture of fresh real data and samples from the previous generation. Using tools from stochastic calculus (Girsanov’s theorem) and divergence measures, the authors derive explicit two-sided bounds linking the distributional drift between generated and target distributions to the score estimation error. The core findings reveal two key mechanisms: (1) the intra-generation drift scales quadratically with the score error energy, modulated by an “observability coefficient” that captures how much of the path-wise error projects onto the terminal state; (2) across generations, the total drift accumulates as a geometrically discounted sum of past errors, where the discount factor is \((1-\alpha)^2\) with \(\alpha\) being the proportion of fresh real data. The theory predicts that if the sequence of score errors is summable, the model converges to a stable distribution; otherwise, it collapses. Empirical validation on Gaussian mixtures and image datasets confirms the predicted memory decay and the role of \(\alpha\).

## Method Summary
The authors consider a recursive training loop: starting from real data \(p_{\text{data}}\), each generation \(i\) produces a model distribution \(\hat{p}_i\) by training a score network on a mixture \(q_i = \alpha p_{\text{data}} + (1-\alpha)\hat{p}_{i-1}\) (with \(\hat{p}_0 = p_{\text{data}}\)). The score network approximates the score of \(q_i\) and is used to sample \(\hat{p}_{i+1}\) via the reverse-time SDE. The theoretical analysis proceeds by:
1. Modeling the forward diffusion as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and the reverse SDE as its time-reversal.
2. Applying Girsanov’s theorem to relate the law of the reverse SDE under an imperfect score to the ideal law, introducing a likelihood ratio expressed as a functional of the score error process. This yields a path-space coupling.
3. Projecting the path-space divergence onto the terminal distribution to obtain bounds on \(\chi^2(\hat{p}_{i+1} \| q_i)\) in terms of the integrated squared score error and an observability coefficient \(\eta_i\).
4. Deriving a recursion for the accumulated divergence \(D_i = \chi^2(\hat{p}_i \| p_{\text{data}})\) that incorporates the contraction due to mixing fresh data (\((1-\alpha)^2\) factor) and the innovation error from the current generation.
5. Summing the recursion to express \(D_{N+1}\) as a discounted sum of past score errors plus a bias term. Stability requires that the series of \(\varepsilon_{\star,i}^2\) be summable; otherwise \(D_{N+1}\) diverges.
Empirical evaluation uses synthetic Gaussian mixtures (with analytically tractable scores) and image datasets (Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10) to measure the predicted quantities (divergences, score error energy, observability) across generations.

## Key Results
- **Intra-generation drift bound:** \(\chi^2(\hat{p}_{i+1} \| q_i) \asymp \varepsilon_{\star,i}^2\), where \(\varepsilon_{\star,i}^2\) is the pathwise score error energy, and the constant involves an observability coefficient \(\eta_i\).
- **Accumulated drift:** \(D_{N+1} \approx \sum_{k=1}^{N} (1-\alpha)^{2(N-k)} \varepsilon_{\star,k}^2\) plus a constant bias. The effective memory length scales as \(1/(1-\alpha)^2\).
- **Collapse criterion:** If \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \varepsilon_{\star,k}^2 = \infty\), then \(D_{N+1} \to \infty\) (model collapse). If the errors are summable and sufficiently small, \(D_{N+1}\) remains bounded and the process stabilizes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The analysis uncovers three interconnected mechanisms that explain how errors propagate and when they lead to failure.

### Mechanism 1: Intra-generation divergence via score error energy
The reverse SDE with an imperfect score introduces a drift perturbation equal to the score error. Under the small-error regime, Girsanov’s theorem gives a Radon–Nikodym derivative between the law of the reverse SDE with the true score and with the learned score as an exponential of a stochastic integral of the error. The marginal \(\chi^2\) divergence between the endpoint distributions \(\hat{p}_{i+1}\) and \(q_i\) is then bounded above and below by the expectation of a square of this integral, which equals the integrated score error energy multiplied by an observability coefficient \(\eta_i\). Thus, the quality of generation within one step depends on how much of the path error is “observable” in the final state. If errors are purely temporal or orthogonal to the terminal state, \(\eta_i\) may vanish and the bound becomes vacuous.

### Mechanism 2: Contraction from fresh data mixing
Each generation’s training distribution \(q_i\) includes a fraction \(\alpha\) of fresh real data. Because \(\chi^2(q_i \| p_{\text{data}}) = (1-\alpha)^2 \chi^2(\hat{p}_i \| p_{\text{data}})\), this mixing acts as a contraction on the overall divergence. When the score network produces a new distribution \(\hat{p}_{i+1}\) that is close to \(q_i\) (intra-generation error), the accumulated divergence \(D_{i+1}\) approximately satisfies \(D_{i+1} \approx (1-\alpha)^2 D_i + \frac{\alpha}{2} I_i\), where \(I_i = \chi^2(\hat{p}_{i+1} \| q_i)\). This recursion reveals a trade-off: a smaller \(\alpha\) preserves memory (slower contraction) but also accumulates innovation errors more slowly; a larger \(\alpha\) forgets faster but reduces the impact of past errors.

### Mechanism 3: Geometric memory and summability
Solving the recursion yields \(D_{N+1} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} (1-\alpha)^{2(N-k)} I_k + (1-\alpha)^{2N} D_0\). Substituting the intra-generation bound \(I_k \asymp \varepsilon_{\star,k}^2\) gives the main accumulation law: \(D_{N+1} \asymp \sum_{k=1}^{N} (1-\alpha)^{2(N-k)} \varepsilon_{\star,k}^2\). This is a geometrically discounted sum of historical score errors. The total is finite if and only if the series of \(\varepsilon_{\star,k}^2\) is summable (since the discount factor does not affect convergence for infinite sums). Therefore, long-term stability (bounded divergence) requires that the score errors across generations be summable; otherwise the model diverges (collapse).

## Foundational Learning
These concepts underpin the theoretical and empirical analysis.

- **Score-based diffusion models**
  - *Why needed:* The generative process is defined by learning the score (gradient of log-density) of a forward diffusion and then using the reverse-time SDE for sampling. All error analysis is set in this framework.
  - *Quick check:* For the forward OU process \(dX_t = -\frac12 X_t dt + dB_t\), write the ideal reverse SDE. If the estimated score is perturbed by \(\delta s\), how does the terminal distribution change?

- **Girsanov’s theorem for diffusion processes**
  - *Why needed:* It provides the exact change of measure between the law of the reverse SDE with the true score and with the learned score, enabling the derivation of divergence bounds.
  - *Quick check:* State the conditions (e.g., Novikov) needed to apply Girsanov’s theorem to two diffusions with the same diffusion coefficient but different drifts.

- **f-divergences (χ², KL)**
  - *Why needed:* The analysis uses χ²-divergence because it contracts linearly under the data mixture operation and interacts nicely with the Girsanov density. The bounds often involve χ², and its relationship to KL is used for interpretation.
  - *Quick check:* Prove that for \(q_i = \alpha p_{\text{data}} + (1-\alpha)\hat{p}_i\), one has \(\chi^2(q_i \| p_{\text{data}}) = (1-\alpha)^2 \chi^2(\hat{p}_i \| p_{\text{data}})\). How does the analogous KL relation differ?

## Architecture Onboarding
The recursive training pipeline consists of the following components operating in a loop:

Data Mixture Module -> Score Network (s_θ_i) -> Reverse SDE Sampler -> Divergence Estimator -> Error Diagnostics -> (back to Data Mixture Module with updated distribution)

**Critical Path:** The core computation flows as: score estimation on \(q_i\) yields \(s_θ_i\); reverse SDE sampling produces \(\hat{p}_{i+1}\); intra-generation divergence \(I_i = \chi^2(\hat{p}_{i+1} \| q_i)\) is estimated; mixing fresh data forms \(q_{i+1}\); the recursion updates \(D_{i+1}\). Each component must operate correctly to close the loop.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Fresh data proportion \(\alpha\):** Larger \(\alpha\) increases contraction (faster forgetting) but requires more real data collection. Smaller \(\alpha\) increases memory and risks accumulation but is cheaper. The stability threshold depends on the sum of scores errors.
- **Score network capacity:** Higher capacity reduces per-generation score error \(\varepsilon_{\star,i}^2\) but increases computational cost and may overfit to the synthetic mixture, potentially amplifying distributional biases.
- **Diffusion schedule (\(T, t_0\)):** A larger total time \(T\) and smaller starting time \(t_0\) improve asymptotic score learning but require more integration steps. The theory suggests the score error scales like \((1/t_0)^{d/2}\), so \(t_0\) must be chosen carefully in high dimensions.

**Failure Signatures:**
- The accumulated divergence \(D_i\) grows systematically across generations without saturation.
- The observability coefficient \(\eta_i\) drops close to zero, indicating that errors are not manifesting in the marginal distribution (a hidden failure mode).
- The memory contribution heatmap (e.g., Fig. 10 in the paper) shows a broad, non-decaying pattern, implying long memory and risk of non-summable errors.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Intra‑generation bounds validation:** On a 10‑dimensional Gaussian mixture, compute the true score, train a KDE estimator on mixtures \(q_i\), and verify that \(I_i\) stays within constant factors of \(\varepsilon_{\star,i}^2\) over 20 generations. Check that \(\eta_i > 0\) by regressing the martingale \(M_i\) on terminal states.
2. **Memory decay heatmap:** For a fixed \(\alpha\), ablate each generation’s score error in the final model and measure the resulting change in \(D_N\). Compare to the predicted discounted contribution \((1-\alpha)^{2(N-i)} \varepsilon_{\star,i}^2\). The heatmap should show a geometrically decaying pattern along generations.
3. **Critical \(\alpha\) threshold sweep:** Run the recursive loop for \(\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5,0.9\}\) on CIFAR‑10. Track \(D_i\) and determine the smallest \(\alpha\) for which \(D_i\) stabilizes rather than diverging. This threshold should correlate with the sum \(\sum \varepsilon_{\star,i}^2\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The authors identify several unresolved directions:

1. **Beyond the small-error regime:** The current analysis requires the score error energy to be sufficiently small to linearize the Girsanov density. How do the bounds extend to large errors that occur in realistic training? A lower bound for one generation with finite error is a key challenge.
2. **Existence and form of the limiting distribution:** Is there a fixed‑point distribution \(\hat{p}_\infty\) for the recursive training operator? If so, how does it depend on \(\alpha\) and \(p_{\text{data}}\)? The paper provides stability criteria but no fixed‑point analysis.
3. **Uniform positivity of the observability coefficient \(\eta_i\):** What factors determine \(\eta_i\), and can it be bounded away from zero uniformly across generations? If \(\eta_i\) vanishes, the lower bound becomes vacuous and errors might accumulate undetected.
4. **Intrinsic‑dimension‑dependent sample complexity:** The score estimation error likely depends on the intrinsic dimension \(d_\star\) of the data manifold rather than the ambient dimension \(d\). Can theory be developed to obtain rates scaling with \(d_\star\)? This is crucial for high‑dimensional image data.

## Limitations
- The theoretical bounds rely on a perturbative assumption (small score error) and the use of \(\chi^2\) divergence. They may not hold for large errors or distributions with heavy tails.
- The observability coefficient \(\eta_i\) is derived under specific martingale conditions; if it becomes near zero, the lower bound offers no guarantee, yet the model might still fail silently.
- Empirical validation on image datasets uses a feature‑space \(\chi^2\) estimator (based on a classifier) whose consistency is not proven and may introduce bias.
- The recursive training protocol assumes perfect mixing and i.i.d. data; real‑world scenarios with non‑i.i.d. or streaming data could violate the contraction factor \((1-\alpha)^2\).

## Confidence
We assess confidence in the major claims as follows:

- **Claim 1:** Intra‑generation divergence scales quadratically with score error energy (with constant involving \(\eta_i\)). **Confidence:** High for Gaussian mixtures (analytic), Medium for images (due to estimator approximations).
- **Claim 2:** Accumulated divergence is a geometrically discounted sum of past score errors. **Confidence:** Medium; the proof assumes exact contraction and linearity, which may be affected by optimization noise.
- **Claim 3:** Model collapse occurs iff the sum of score errors diverges. **Confidence:** Medium; depends on long‑term behavior of the score learner, which is not fully explored.
- **Claim 4:** Fresh data proportion \(\alpha\) controls forgetting rate. **Confidence:** High; the algebra of the mixture is exact and the effect is clearly demonstrated.

## Next Checks
To further validate and extend the findings, we propose:

1. **Dense \(\alpha\) sweep and decay rate measurement:** Run 10 seeds per \(\alpha\) on CIFAR‑10, compute the empirical decay rate of the memory contributions, and compare to \((1-\alpha)^2\). Identify the precise \(\alpha\) threshold where summation diverges.
2. **Observability in high dimensions:** Train on isotropic Gaussian data with increasing ambient dimension \(d\) (up to 100) and measure \(\eta_i\). Test whether \(\eta_i\) remains uniformly positive as \(d\) grows.
3. **Alternative divergence estimator:** On synthetic data, replace the classifier‑based \(\chi^2\) estimator with a kernel‑based density ratio estimator to rule out biases specific to the neural classifier.
