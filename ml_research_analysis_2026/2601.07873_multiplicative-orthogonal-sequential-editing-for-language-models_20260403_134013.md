# Multiplicative Orthogonal Sequential Editing for Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.07873
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07873
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Primary result:** MOSE achieves a 12.08% improvement in editing performance while retaining 95.73% of the model’s general abilities on downstream tasks after up to 4000 sequential edits.

## Executive Summary
MOSE tackles the instability caused by additive knowledge updates in large language models when edits are applied sequentially. It introduces a multiplicative paradigm: the target weight matrix is left‑multiplied by an orthogonal update matrix, which mathematically preserves the matrix Frobenius norm and condition number—key indicators of numerical stability. The orthogonal matrix is derived from a constrained least‑squares objective (orthogonal Procrustes problem) using SVD, guaranteeing a closed‑form solution that precisely injects new knowledge while preserving existing representations. A data‑driven layer selector identifies the most relevant feed‑forward network layer for each fact, and edits are applied to that layer and its immediate neighbours. Across LLaMA3‑8B, LLaMA2‑13B, and Qwen2.5‑7B on multiple editing benchmarks, MOSE outperforms state‑of‑the‑art methods, especially in long sequential scenarios, by a margin of 12.08% in editing quality while maintaining 95.73% performance on downstream tasks such as QA and summarization.

## Method Summary
MOSE replaces the standard additive update $W \leftarrow W^0 + \Delta W$ with a multiplicative orthogonal transformation $W \leftarrow R W^0$. The orthogonal matrix $R$ solves the constrained optimization $\min_R \|R W^0 K^0 - W^0 K^0\|_F^2 + \|R W^0 K^E - V^E\|_F^2$ subject to $R^\top R = I$, where $K^0$ and $K^E$ are the preserved and new key representations, and $V^E$ is the target value vector. This is equivalent to the orthogonal Procrustes problem with $A = [\sqrt{\lambda} W^0 K^0,\, W^0 K^E]$ and $B = [\sqrt{\lambda} W^0 K^0,\, V^E]$; the solution $R = U V^\top$ is obtained via the SVD of $M = B A^\top$. A layer selection heuristic (Eq. 22) computes, for an input $x$, the normalized activation magnitude of each FFN layer. The layer $l^*$ minimizing $\|V^E - W^0 K^E\|_F / \|W^0\|_2 \cdot \sigma(x \cdot W^0_{fc})$ is chosen as the primary edit target, and both $l^*$ and its adjacent layers are edited to capture distributed knowledge.

## Key Results
- **Editing performance:** In sequential editing (up to 4000 edits), MOSE improves reliability/generalization metrics by 12.08% over existing methods.
- **Retention of general abilities:** After extensive edits, MOSE retains 95.73% of the original model’s performance on downstream tasks (NLI, QA, summarization, sentiment analysis).
- **Stability:** MOSE maintains the Frobenius norm and condition number of edited weight matrices, preventing the degradation observed with additive updates (Figure 2).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Left‑multiplying a parameter matrix by an orthogonal matrix preserves its Frobenius norm and condition number, thereby maintaining numerical stability during sequential edits.
- **Mechanism:** Orthogonal matrices $R$ satisfy $R^\top R = I$. Multiplying $W' = R W$ leaves singular values unchanged: $\sigma_i(W') = \sigma_i(W)$. Since norm ($\|W\|_F = \sqrt{\sum \sigma_i^2}$) and condition number ($\kappa = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$) depend solely on singular values, they remain invariant under such transformations.
- **Core assumption:** Numerical stability indicators (norm, condition number) are the primary determinants of editing performance and retention of general abilities in sequential editing.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] States that additive updates damage “key numerical stability indicators (such as condition number and norm)”, and MOSE “mathematically preserves the matrix’s Frobenius norm and condition number”.
  - [section 4.2] Provides formal proofs (Equations 7–14) showing $\|RW\|_F = \|W\|_F$ and $\kappa_2(RW) = \kappa_2(W)$.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., “Norm Growth and Stability Challenges…”, “Perturbation‑Restrained…”) also identify norm/condition number growth as core failure modes in additive editing.
- **Break condition:** If the orthogonal constraint $R^\top R = I$ is violated during optimization (e.g., due to numerical error in SVD), invariance guarantees no longer hold, potentially re‑introducing instability.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Reformulating knowledge editing as a constrained least‑squares problem with an orthogonal transformation matrix ($\min_R \|RA - B\|_F^2$ s.t. $R^\top R = I$) yields a closed‑form solution via the orthogonal Procrustes method, ensuring the update precisely injects new knowledge while preserving existing representations.
- **Mechanism:** The optimization (Eq. 17) minimizes output error for both preserved knowledge ($K^0$) and new facts ($K^E$) under the constraint that $R$ is orthogonal. This is equivalent to the orthogonal Procrustes problem: $\min_R \|RA - B\|_F^2$ with $A = [\sqrt{\lambda}W^0K^0,\; W^0K^E]$ and $B = [\sqrt{\lambda}W^0K^0,\; V^E]$. The solution $R = U V^\top$ comes from SVD of $M = B A^\top$ (Eqs. 19–20), which is numerically stable.
- **Core assumption:** The target knowledge $V^E$ and preserved representations $W^0K^0$ can be effectively modeled as linear transformations of the original activations via the weight matrix.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.1] Explicitly derives the constrained problem and its closed‑form SVD solution, linking it to MEMIT’s objective but with a multiplicative (rather than additive) update.
  - [section 5.1] Notes that $K^0$, $K^E$, $V^E$ are computed as in prior work (Meng et al. 2022, 2023), relying on localized knowledge representations in MLP layers.
  - [corpus] Neighbor “O‑Edit: Orthogonal Subspace Editing…” also uses orthogonal constraints but in a subspace formulation; MOSE’s novelty is the direct multiplicative application to the full weight matrix.
- **Break condition:** If the matrices $A$ or $B$ become rank‑deficient (e.g., due to redundant/conflicting edit requests), SVD may yield a poorly conditioned $R$ or fail to capture the desired transformation, leading to editing failure.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A data‑driven layer selection algorithm that identifies the most relevant feed‑forward network (FFN) layer for a given fact (based on activation magnitude normalized by layer norm) and edits that layer plus its neighbors, optimizes the trade‑off between edit efficacy and preserving general abilities.
- **Mechanism:** For an input $x$, compute activation strength $\|\sigma(x\cdot W^0_{fc})\|_2 / \|W^0_{fc}\|_2$ for each layer’s FFN. The layer $l^*$ minimizing $\|V^E - W^0 K^E\|_F / \|W^0\|_2 \cdot \sigma(x\cdot W^0_{fc})$ (Eq. 22) is selected as the primary edit target. Editing $l^*$ and adjacent layers ($l^*\pm1$) captures knowledge distributed across layers without excessive disruption.
- **Core assumption:** Knowledge in LLMs is localized but not confined to a single layer; editing a small neighbourhood around the most activated layer balances precision and stability.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.2] Presents the layer selection criterion (Eq. 22) and the rationale for editing three consecutive layers.
  - [section 6.4] Ablation in Figure 4 shows that multi‑layer editing (selected layer + neighbours) outperforms single‑layer or fixed‑layer baselines in batch‑sequential editing.
  - [corpus] Neighbours like “WilKE” and “Perturbation‑Restrained…” also use layer‑wise activation analysis, supporting the premise that layer selection matters.
- **Break condition:** If the activation‑based metric fails to correlate with actual knowledge storage (e.g., for highly distributed or abstract concepts), $l^*$ may be misidentified, causing edits to occur in irrelevant layers and degrading performance.

## Foundational Learning
### Orthogonal Matrices and Their Properties
- **Why needed here:** MOSE’s core innovation relies on proving that left‑multiplication by an orthogonal matrix leaves $\|W\|_F$ and $\kappa(W)$ unchanged. Understanding why this holds (via $R^\top R = I$ and SVD) is essential to grasp the stability guarantee.
- **Quick check:** If $R$ is orthogonal and $W$ has singular values $[5, 2, 0.1]$, what are the singular values of $RW$? What is $\|RW\|_F$ relative to $\|W\|_F$?

### Constrained Optimization and the Orthogonal Procrustes Problem
- **Why needed here:** MOSE formulates editing as $\min_R \|RA - B\|_F$ s.t. $R^\top R = I$, solved via SVD of $B A^\top$. This replaces additive updates with a stable closed‑form solution. Without this, the orthogonal constraint would be intractable.
- **Quick check:** Given $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, how does the SVD of $M = B A^\top$ yield the optimal $R$ satisfying $R^\top R = I$ for minimizing $\|RA - B\|_F$?

### Knowledge Localization in LLMs
- **Why needed here:** MOSE assumes edits target specific FFN layers where factual associations are stored (per Meng et al. 2022). The layer selection algorithm (Eq. 22) depends on the premise that activation magnitude in an FFN correlates with its involvement in representing a fact.
- **Quick check:** In a transformer MLP, what do the “key” and “value” vectors represent in the context of factual knowledge editing? Why might editing the FFN (rather than attention) be more effective for fact updates?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input fact → Layer selector → Knowledge extractor → Orthogonal update solver → Applier → Output

### Critical Path
Input fact → layer selection → extract $K^0$, $K^E$, $V^E$ → compute $R$ via SVD → update $W_{l^*}$ (and neighbours) → evaluate edit success and general abilities.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **SVD cost vs. stability:** SVD on matrices of size up to several thousand is expensive (~0.5 s per edit per layer) but provides a globally optimal orthogonal solution. Approximations (e.g., randomized SVD) could speed up at the risk of violating orthogonality.
- **Layer selection sensitivity:** The metric in Eq. 22 uses activation magnitude normalized by $\|W\|_2$. If $\|W\|_2$ varies widely across layers, normalization may be insufficient. Alternative norms or learned weights could improve robustness.
- **Multi‑layer editing:** Editing $l^*$ and neighbours improves retention (Figure 4) but increases risk of collateral damage if neighbouring layers are not actually involved in the fact. A more adaptive neighbourhood size might help.

### Failure Signatures
- **Norm/condition number growth:** If $R$ deviates from orthogonality (e.g., due to SVD truncation), $\|W_{\text{new}}\|_F$ or $\kappa(W_{\text{new}})$ may increase. Monitor these after edits—MOSE should keep them flat (Figure 2).
- **Edit failure:** Low reliability/generalization on edited facts (Table 1) suggests $R W^0 K^E$ does not match $V^E$. Check residual $\|R W^0 K^E - V^E\|_F$; high values indicate poor fit.
- **General ability drop:** Decline in downstream task scores (Figure 3) indicates overfitting to edits. Likely cause: incorrect layer selection or insufficient regularization ($\lambda$ too low).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Norm invariance test:** On a random $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times p}$, generate $R$ via QR decomposition. Verify $\|R W^0\|_F \approx \|W^0\|_F$ and $\kappa(R W^0) \approx \kappa(W^0)$ within numerical tolerance. This validates the core mechanism in isolation.
2. **Layer selection sanity check:** For a known fact (e.g., “Paris is capital of France”), run layer selector on clean input. Confirm $l^*$ matches the layer identified by prior work (e.g., ROME’s causal mediation analysis). Ablate: fix $l^*$ to a random layer and measure drop in edit success.
3. **Additive vs. multiplicative comparison:** Apply 100 sequential edits using MOSE and an additive baseline (e.g., RECT) on CounterFact. Track $\|W\|_F$ and $\kappa(W)$ per edit. MOSE should show flat trends; additive should show growth. Also measure reliability after 100 edits—MOSE should stay >0.95 while additive drops.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How can the computational overhead of SVD in MOSE be reduced to enable efficient editing for very large models (e.g., >70B parameters) or extremely long edit sequences (>10,000 edits) without sacrificing numerical stability?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6.6 acknowledges that MOSE’s editing time is higher than additive methods due to SVD computation, and scalability is implied by experiments only up to 4000 edits on models ≤13B parameters.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not explore approximations, distributed SVD, or alternative orthogonalization techniques that could scale. Without such analysis, MOSE’s practicality for cutting‑edge LLMs or lifelong editing remains unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmarking MOSE with optimized SVD libraries (e.g., randomized SVD) or proposing a cheaper orthogonal parameterization (e.g., Householder reflections) on larger models and longer sequences, showing comparable stability and performance.

### Open Question 2
Does MOSE’s multiplicative orthogonal paradigm generalize to non‑transformer architectures (e.g., CNNs, RNNs) or multimodal models (e.g., vision‑language models), where parameter matrices may have different structural roles and stability properties?
- **Basis in paper:** Experiments are confined to decoder‑only transformer LLMs (LLaMA, Qwen). The mathematical analysis (Section 4.2) assumes general matrices but is validated only on FFN weight matrices in transformers.
- **Why unresolved:** The preservation of norm and condition number is mathematically general, but the editing formulation (Eq. 17) relies on specific key/value representations from transformer MLPs. Other architectures may lack analogous localized knowledge representations, making the method’s application non‑trivial.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Adapting MOSE to edit convolution kernels or recurrent weights in other models, with appropriate definitions of “knowledge units” (analogous to key‑value pairs), and demonstrating similar stability gains on sequential editing benchmarks for those architectures.

### Open Question 3
What is the sensitivity of MOSE to the regularization parameter $\lambda$ and the layer selection heuristic? Can these be automatically determined (e.g., via validation edits) to robustly handle diverse knowledge types (factual vs. conceptual) and model scales?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 5.1 introduces $\lambda$ as a trade‑off parameter without discussion of tuning. Section 5.2 proposes a layer selection score based on activation strength but does not evaluate its robustness across datasets or model sizes beyond brief ablations (Section 6.4).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper uses fixed $\lambda$ (implied by lack of analysis) and a single‑layer selection criterion. Different knowledge may embed in different layers; poor layer choice or $\lambda$ mismatch could degrade performance, but the limits are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic studies varying $\lambda$ across edit batches and analyzing layer selection accuracy (e.g., against ground‑truth editing layers if available) on multiple datasets. Developing an adaptive $\lambda$ schedule based on edit sequence progress or knowledge type.

### Open Question 4
To what extent does the orthogonality constraint limit MOSE’s ability to edit knowledge that requires non‑orthogonal updates (e.g., overriding strongly conflicting prior facts), and can hybrid approaches (e.g., partially orthogonal updates) balance stability with edit success?
- **Basis in paper:** MOSE enforces strict orthogonality ($R^\top R = I$) to preserve stability. The orthogonal Procrustes solution (Eq. 20) is optimal under Frobenius norm minimization, but this assumes the desired edit $V^E$ lies in the column space of $R W^0 K^E$. For highly conflicting knowledge, the optimal $R$ might need to be near‑singular, violating orthogonality to achieve reliability.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows MOSE outperforms baselines on standard datasets, but these may not contain extremely conflicting edits. The theoretical guarantee (norm/condition number preservation) comes at the cost of restricting the update subspace, potentially reducing editability for hard cases.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Designing controlled experiments with deliberately conflicting edits (e.g., reversing high‑confidence facts) and measuring edit reliability vs. stability trade‑offs. Exploring relaxations like allowing $R$ to be near‑orthogonal ($R^\top R \approx I$) via soft constraints and comparing performance.

## Limitations
- Missing hyperparameters ($\lambda$, layer selection details) hinder full reproducibility.
- High computational cost per edit due to SVD limits scalability to very large models and long sequences.
- Orthogonality constraint may limit editability for highly conflicting facts.
