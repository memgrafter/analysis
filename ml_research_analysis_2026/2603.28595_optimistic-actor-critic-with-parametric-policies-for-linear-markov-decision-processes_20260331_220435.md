# Optimistic Actor-Critic with Parametric Policies for Linear Markov Decision Processes
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.28595
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28595
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: An optimistic actor-critic algorithm with explicit log-linear policies achieves sample complexities of \(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4})\) (on-policy) and \(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})\) (off-policy) for linear MDPs, with significantly reduced computational costs compared to prior implicit-policy methods.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes an optimistic actor-critic framework for linear Markov decision processes (MDPs) that bridges the gap between theory and practice. The algorithm uses a projected natural policy gradient for the actor with a tractable logit-matching regression objective and an approximate Thompson sampling critic via Langevin Monte Carlo. The approach achieves state-of-the-art sample complexity bounds while being computationally efficient, with reduced memory and per-episode costs. Empirical evaluations on linear MDPs and Atari benchmarks demonstrate its effectiveness and sample efficiency.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two main components: an actor that updates a parametric log-linear policy using a projected natural gradient step based on a logit-matching regression objective, and a critic that maintains an optimistic value estimate using approximate Thompson sampling implemented via Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC). The critic samples plausible reward functions from a posterior and computes corresponding optimal actions, avoiding explicit confidence set constructions. The algorithm operates either on-policy or off-policy with a replay buffer, and uses a coreset \(D_{\text{exp}}\) to simplify the actor update. The overall framework yields an optimistic actor-critic that enjoys strong theoretical guarantees and practical tractability.

## Key Results
- Achieves sample complexity \(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4})\) for on-policy and \(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})\) for off-policy linear MDPs, matching state-of-the-art.
- Reduces computational costs: memory and per-episode cost drop from \(\mathcal{O}(d_c^2 H |A| T)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d_a H |A|)\) compared to implicit-policy methods.
- Experiments confirm efficacy on both synthetic linear MDPs and Atari benchmarks, showing improved sample efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success hinges on two key innovations:
- **Actor**: The projected natural policy gradient with logit-matching regression provides a stable and tractable update for explicit log-linear policies. The projection step ensures feasibility and the natural gradient accounts for the policy's geometry.
- **Critic**: Approximate Thompson sampling via LMC yields optimistic value estimates without explicit confidence sets. By sampling reward functions from a posterior, the critic implicitly explores and avoids optimistic biases that can derail learning.
These components together maintain optimism for effective exploration while keeping the policy parameterization explicit and computationally manageable.

## Foundational Learning
We now list several core concepts underpinning the work. For each, we state why it is needed and a quick mental check.

1. **Linear MDP**: Assumes transition and reward functions are linear in a known feature map. Needed to derive sample complexity bounds and design linear function approximations. Check: Can we represent the MDP as \(\phi(s,a)^\top \theta\)?
2. **Log-linear policies**: Policies of the form \(\pi(a|s) \propto \exp(\psi(a)^\top \eta(s))\). Needed for explicit parameterization that is both expressive and tractable. Check: Does the policy class have bounded logarithmic linearity?
3. **Natural policy gradient**: Uses Fisher information matrix to define update direction. Needed for stable and efficient optimization in policy space. Check: Is the update \(\nabla \mathbb{E}[A] F^{-1}\) approximated tractably?
4. **Thompson sampling**: Maintains posterior over reward functions and samples to guide exploration. Needed to achieve optimism without explicit UCB-style confidence intervals. Check: Is the posterior updated after each trajectory?
5. **Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC)**: MCMC method for approximate sampling from posterior. Needed to implement Thompson sampling when analytic sampling is infeasible. Check: Does LMC converge quickly enough in high dimensions?
6. **Projected gradient**: Ensures policy parameters stay within a feasible set (e.g., bounded). Needed to maintain stability and theoretical guarantees. Check: Is projection step convex and efficiently computable?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Replay buffer -> Critic (LMC) -> Optimistic Q -> Actor (logit-matching) -> Policy -> Environment -> Replay buffer
Replay buffer -- coreset --> Actor

### Critical path
1. Collect trajectories using current policy and store in replay buffer.
2. Critic performs LMC to sample reward functions and compute optimistic Q-values.
3. Actor updates policy via projected natural gradient using a coreset drawn from replay buffer and the optimistic Q-values.
4. Repeat.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Explicit vs implicit policies**: Explicit policies are computationally efficient but may be less expressive; implicit policies can be more flexible but are harder to sample from and analyze.
- **Thompson sampling vs UCB**: Thompson sampling via LMC avoids explicit confidence set construction but introduces approximation error; UCB provides explicit bounds but may be more complex to implement.
- **Coreset usage**: Using a coreset simplifies the actor update and reduces computation but introduces dependence on the coreset’s distribution, which may not match the current state distribution.

### Failure signatures
- LMC divergence: unstable sampling due to too large steps or insufficient iterations.
- Policy collapse: actor update becomes overly greedy, leading to suboptimal deterministic actions.
- Coreset mismatch: coreset does not cover relevant state distributions, causing biased policy updates.
- Linear model misspecification: in nonlinear MDPs, linear features fail to capture dynamics, leading to high approximation error.

3. **Ablation on coreset**: Vary coreset size and sampling method to assess impact on performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Can the theoretical guarantees be extended to settings where the policy and environment use nonlinear function approximations (e.g., neural networks)? Basis: [explicit] "We also aim to investigate the actor-critic algorithms in more realistic setups (e.g., infinite-horizon discounted MDPs) with more general function approximation schemes beyond linear models for both the environment and the policy." (Section 8) Why unresolved: The sample complexity analysis heavily relies on linearity of both MDP transitions/rewards and policy representations. Nonlinear function approximation introduces additional approximation and optimization errors that are not analyzed. What evidence would resolve it: A new algorithm that replaces linear functions with expressive yet bounded function classes (e.g., two-layer networks under NTK regime) and derives comparable sample complexity bounds, possibly using more advanced complexity measures (e.g., Bellman rank, low-rank MDPs).
- **Open Question 2**: Can the dependence on the coreset \(D_{\text{exp}}\) and distribution \(\rho_{\text{exp}}\) be removed in the theoretical analysis while maintaining the same sample complexity? Basis: [explicit] "eliminating the dependence on \(D_{\text{exp}}\) and \(\rho_{\text{exp}}\) will result in a more practical algorithm." (Section 8) Why unresolved: The use of \(D_{\text{exp}}\) and \(\rho_{\text{exp}}\) simplifies the actor update but may be unnatural in practice; the core theoretical challenge is controlling projection error without a pre-defined subset. Current analysis does not address an adaptive or random sampling approach. What evidence would resolve it: An alternative actor update that samples mini-batches directly from the replay buffer and a proof that the projection error is still bounded under appropriate in-distribution assumptions, or a uniform convergence argument over the policy parameter space.
- **Open Question 3**: Does the proposed optimistic actor-critic method extend to infinite-horizon discounted MDPs without sacrificing sample efficiency? Basis: [explicit] "investigate the actor-critic algorithms in more realistic setups (e.g., infinite-horizon discounted MDPs)." (Section 8) Why unresolved: The finite-horizon analysis leverages a backward induction for value estimation and specific properties of the episodic structure. Infinite-horizon problems require different techniques (e.g., contraction mappings) and the optimism bias may behave differently. What evidence would resolve it: A designed algorithm for infinite-horizon linear MDPs using similar optimistic principles (e.g., UCB or LMC on action-value functions) with a proven sample complexity bound matching or improving upon existing discounted results.
- **Open Question 4**: How does the method perform on complex, high-dimensional deep RL benchmarks beyond Atari, particularly tasks with continuous actions or long horizons? Basis: [explicit] "further evaluate their empirical performance in more challenging large-scale deep RL applications." (Section 8) Why unresolved: The experiments in Section 7.2 only cover Atari (discrete actions, moderate length). Continuous control domains (e.g., MuJoCo) or 3D environments may pose new challenges like policy expressiveness and stability of the actor update. What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive evaluation across standard continuous control suites and procedural generation environments, with hyperparameter-swept results showing competitive performance against strong deep RL baselines (SAC, TD3, etc.).

## Limitations
- The theoretical guarantees require the MDP and policy to be linear, which is rarely true in complex real-world tasks.
- The off-policy analysis assumes a concentrability condition that is often unrealistic in practice.
- Empirical evaluation on Atari uses deep neural networks, which violate the linear assumptions, making the connection to theory tenuous.
