# Human-AI Collaborative Autonomous Experimentation With Proxy Modeling for Comparative Observation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12618
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12618
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** px‑BO leverages human pairwise comparisons via a Bradley‑Terry model to guide Bayesian optimization, achieving ~75–84% AI vote accuracy and ≥75% reduction in human effort while discovering physically superior experimental samples.

## Executive Summary
Traditional Bayesian optimization (BO) for autonomous experiments depends on predefined scalar metrics, which can misrepresent complex noisy data and overlook novel phenomena by ignoring qualitative human insights. This paper introduces proxy‑modelled Bayesian optimization (px‑BO), where human experts compare experimental outcomes on the fly. These pairwise preferences train a Bradley‑Terry model, creating a quantitative utility function that replaces hand‑crafted metrics. The trained model also acts as an AI agent to automate future voting, with periodic human validation to correct and retrain it. Evaluated on simulated ferroelectric images and experimental BEPS spectroscopy data (PbTiO₃ thin films), px‑BO discovered optimal samples with physically superior features (e.g., cleaner spectral loops) compared to numerically derived optima. With validation every 5–10 iterations, the AI agent achieved ~75–84% accuracy, reducing required human intervention by at least 75% while maintaining or improving discovery quality.

## Method Summary
px‑BO integrates human comparative judgment into the BO loop. Initially, a small set of random samples is collected, and domain experts perform pairwise comparisons among them. These comparisons are fitted with a Bradley‑Terry (B‑T) model, assigning a latent utility to every explored sample. The utility values become the target for a BO surrogate (Gaussian Process or Deep Kernel Learning). Using an acquisition function (Expected Improvement), the surrogate proposes the next experimental parameter. After measurement, an AI agent selects a historically similar “proxy” sample (via structural similarity) and uses the trained B‑T model to predict whether the new sample is preferred over a subset of existing ones, thereby generating an automated vote. Every *m* iterations, a batch of AI votes is presented to a human for validation; errors are corrected and fed back to retrain the B‑T model. The utilities for *all* samples are then recomputed, stabilizing the preference landscape as more data arrives. This cycle balances automated exploration with human oversight.

## Key Results
- px‑BO identified samples with physically superior features (e.g., cleaner BEPS spectral loops) compared to optima found by traditional scalar metrics.
- The AI agent achieved ~75–84% accuracy in predicting human preferences under periodic validation (every 5–10 iterations).
- Human workload decreased by at least 75% relative to a fully manual pairwise-comparison approach, without sacrificing discovery quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Human Comparisons → Quantitative Utility
Recorded pairwise expert judgments are modeled by a Bradley‑Terry likelihood, yielding a latent utility score for each sample. This utility becomes the BO objective, capturing qualitative aspects of experimental quality that scalar metrics miss. *Core assumption:* Human experts consistently encode domain‑relevant attributes (e.g., “cleaner spectral loops”) in their comparisons. *Evidence:* Methodology described in Section 2. *Break condition:* Inconsistent human votes or insufficient comparisons lead to an ill‑conditioned B‑T model that fails to produce a meaningful utility ordering.

### Mechanism 2: AI Agent via Proxy Modeling
A trained B‑T model automates future voting. For a new sample, the system finds a historically measured “proxy” with high structural similarity (e.g., SSIM). The B‑T model then predicts the preference between the proxy and a subset of existing samples, effectively simulating a human vote. Periodic human validation (every *m* iterations) corrects AI errors, and these corrections are used to retrain the B‑T model. *Core assumption:* Structural similarity between the new sample and its proxy strongly correlates with human preference as encoded in the B‑T model. *Evidence:* Reported AI accuracies of 75–84% for *m* = 5, 10 (Section 3). *Break condition:* For novel patterns lacking a good proxy, the similarity metric fails and systematic misvotes occur.

### Mechanism 3: Global Utility Re‑estimation Stabilizes Search
After each new (validated) comparison, the entire comparison dataset is augmented and the B‑T model is refit, recalculating utilities for *all* explored samples. This re‑estimation allows early misjudgments to be corrected as more data accumulates, preventing premature convergence to suboptimal regions. The BO surrogate is updated accordingly. *Core assumption:* The underlying preference structure is stationary; additional comparisons refine the global ranking. *Evidence:* Decreasing human correction rates across iterations (Figure 7). *Break condition:* If human preferences drift over time, re‑estimation may inject inconsistency rather than improvement.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bayesian Optimization (BO)** – Sequential decision‑making framework combining a surrogate model (GP/DKL) and an acquisition function (Expected Improvement) to propose high‑performing experiments. *Why needed:* Provides the exploration‑exploitation backbone of px‑BO. *Quick check:* How does Expected Improvement balance exploiting known high‑utility regions versus exploring uncertain regions, and what role does the current best utility *u⁺* play?
- **Bradley‑Terry Model** – Statistical model for pairwise comparisons; assigns a latent utility *u(i)* to each item, with preference probability *P(i > j) = exp(u(i)) / [exp(u(i)) + exp(u(j))]*. *Why needed:* Transforms discrete human preferences into a continuous objective for BO. *Quick check:* In the B‑T model, what does *u(i)* represent, and how is *P(i > j)* calculated from *u(i)* and *u(j)*?
- **Deep Kernel Learning (DKL)** – GP variant where a neural network maps high‑dimensional inputs to a feature space before applying a kernel. *Why needed:* Enables BO to handle high‑dimensional data (e.g., spectra, images). *Quick check:* What is the key architectural difference between a standard GP and DKL that allows DKL to scale with high‑dimensional inputs?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Initial random sampling → Human pairwise comparisons → B‑T model (estimates utilities U) → GP/DKL surrogate trained on (locations, U) → Expected Improvement selects next location → Measurement → AI agent: structural similarity finds proxy → B‑T generates AI vote → (Every *m* iterations) Human validation/correction → Augment comparisons → Retrain B‑T → Update U for all samples → loop.

**Critical Path:** 1) Initialize with random samples and human comparisons. 2) Fit B‑T model, compute utilities. 3) Train GP/DKL on (X, U). 4) Select next x via EI. 5) Acquire data. 6) AI agent produces proxy vote (or human votes during validation). 7) If validation step, incorporate human corrections. 8) Augment comparison dataset, retrain B‑T, recompute all utilities. 9) Repeat from step 3.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- *Validation frequency (m)*: Smaller *m* improves AI alignment but increases human burden; larger *m* reduces burden but risks drift. Paper reports good performance at *m* = 5 or 10.
- *Subset size (q)*: Larger *q* yields more robust utility updates for new samples but adds computational overhead. Paper uses *q* = 3.
- *Structural similarity metric*: Choice (e.g., SSIM) directly impacts proxy selection quality; misalignment with human perception degrades AI accuracy.

**Failure Signatures:**
- Persistently high human correction rate (>30–40%) indicating unstable preference learning.
- px‑BO optimal locations diverge from physically meaningful regions identified by experts.
- Poorly conditioned initial comparison matrix (e.g., near‑zero utility separation) due to vague or contradictory human votes.
- Proxy selection failure when new samples are highly novel and lack a similar historical counterpart.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Replicate simulated image test** – Use provided VAE‑generated ferroelectric phase images; initialize with 10 random samples and 20 comparisons; run px‑BO for 20 iterations with fully human voting; verify the final utility map highlights the “clean single domain wall” region and that the optimal sample surpasses the numerically optimal one.
2. **Simulate human‑AI teaming** – Same setup as Exp 1 but implement AI agent with validation interval *m* = 4 (every 4th BO iteration); log AI accuracy vs. human corrections; confirm final discovery quality matches fully human run and observe decreasing correction rates.
3. **Run on provided BEPS data** – Apply px‑BO to the PbTiO₃ BEPS dataset using DKL surrogate; follow paper’s initialization (j = 10, n = 20); run 50 iterations with *m* = 5 and *m* = 10; reproduce comparison of optimal locations/spectra from px‑BO vs. numerically optimal (loop area); quantify physical superiority (loop cleanliness); generate human correction plot; verify average correction ~16–25%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Optimal validation frequency:** What interval *m* best balances discovery quality and human effort? The paper tested *m* = 5 and 10 but did not optimize it nor explore adaptive scheduling.
2. **Generalization to other domains:** How well does px‑BO transfer to experimental problems with different data structures (e.g., synthesis parameters, multimodal sensor outputs)? The study only covers ferroelectric spectroscopy and simulated images.
3. **Robustness to human variability:** How does the framework handle inter‑expert disagreement or intra‑expert inconsistencies? The methodology assumes a single consistent expert and does not quantify sensitivity to preference noise.
4. **Computational overhead:** What are the runtime and memory costs of the proxy modeling steps (B‑T fitting, structural similarity calculations) relative to standard BO? This trade‑off is not reported.

## Limitations
- The paper omits detailed procedures for GP/DKL hyperparameter tuning and global utility re‑estimation, hindering faithful reproduction.
- The structural similarity metric for spectral data is unspecified; its choice likely affects proxy selection and AI accuracy.
- The approach assumes stationary human preferences and does not address robustness to expert variability or drift.
- Claims of “physical superiority” rely on expert qualitative assessment without blind, quantitative benchmarks.
