# Probability Hacking and the Design of Trustworthy ML for Signal Processing in C-UAS: A Scenario Based Method

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08086
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08086
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: The paper identifies mandatory pre-registration of ML research designs as the key requirement to prevent probability hacking, establishing transparency and contestability for trustworthy ML in C-UAS.

## Executive Summary
Counter-UAS (C-UAS) systems increasingly use Machine Learning (ML) for signal processing tasks like detection and tracking. However, "probability hacking" (p-hacking)—where performance metrics are manipulated via biased training data (implicit) or deliberate metric tweaking (explicit)—creates untrustworthy accuracy claims that undermine Rule of Law principles such as transparency and contestability. The authors propose a scenario-based method to analyze capability gaps within operational contexts (both civil and military), deliberately exposing how p-hacking leads to system failure and eroded accountability. The primary outcome is the identification of mandatory pre-registration of ML research designs as a foundational requirement. When integrated with technical paradigms like MLOps, this requirement creates an immutable audit trail of the development process, enabling stakeholders to verify claims and decisions, thereby fostering justified trust for Human-Autonomy Teaming in high-stakes C-UAS operations.

## Method Summary
The scenario-based method consists of four steps: (1) capability gap analysis along the C-UAS kill chain to identify suitable ML applications; (2) design of detailed operational scenarios that incorporate legal, environmental, and threat variables; (3) requirement identification, linking Rule of Law tenets (transparency, contestability) to technical paradigms (e.g., MLOps); and (4) validation and implementation planning. The showcase demonstrates the method with an RNN for radar-based sUAS detection. Two failure scenarios are examined: (a) training solely on fixed-wing sUAS data, causing failure against rotary-wing sUAS in urban clutter (implicit p-hacking); (b) training on clear-weather data only, causing failure during a hailstorm (explicit p-hacking). These scenarios expose how training data distribution mismatches or post-hoc metric manipulation invalidate claimed accuracy. The derived requirement—mandatory pre-registration—aims to institutionalize transparency and contestability from the outset of ML development.

## Key Results
- Mandatory pre-registration of ML research designs is the central measure to prevent both implicit and explicit p-hacking.
- Integration of pre-registration into MLOps pipelines and procurement governance creates an audit trail that enables verification of performance claims and decisions.
- This approach fosters justified trust in ML systems for Human-Autonomy Teaming, aligning commander intent with mission intent while upholding Rule of Law constraints.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Implicit P-hacking Exposure via Capability Gaps
The method’s first step—capability gap analysis—requires defining a specific gap (e.g., detecting rotary-wing sUAS in urban clutter) and embedding an ML solution (e.g., RNN) into a detailed operational scenario. This exposes how unrepresentative training data (e.g., only fixed-wing sUAS) directly causes failure under scenario conditions. The core assumption is that operational performance is conditional on the match between deployment context and training data distribution; aggregate accuracy metrics alone are insufficient. Evidence is provided in Section 4.1, where the failure scenario is explicitly described. The mechanism breaks if the scenario fails to capture the full variance of real-world conditions (e.g., only rural environments), leaving data representativeness issues undiscovered.

### Contestability via Mandatory Pre-registration
Mandatory pre-registration—documenting hypotheses, data sources, evaluation metrics, and analysis plan before development begins—creates an immutable audit trail. This allows stakeholders (procurement officers, commanders, courts) to verify whether reported performance was derived from the pre-registered procedure or from post-hoc tweaking (explicit p-hacking). The assumption is that transparency into the development process is a prerequisite for contestability of outputs, a fundamental Rule of Law tenet. Section 3 explicitly argues that pre-registration would contribute to contestability and acts as ‘legal protection by design’. The mechanism fails if pre-registration is not mandated or enforced by institutional bodies (R&D funders, procurement agencies), leaving no audit trail.

### Justified Trust through Transparent Development
By preventing p-hacking, the method ensures reliability claims are trustworthy, providing “justified trust” for Human-Autonomy Teaming. Commanders, who cannot assess ML performance in real time during cognitive overload, rely on institutional confidence in the transparent development and validation process. This aligns autonomy with legal and mission constraints. Section 5 links justified trust to governance-driven requirements that align commander intent with mission intent. The mechanism fails if required safeguards (e.g., pre-registration) are ignored or not integrated into MLOps, leaving trust based on unverified claims and risking accountability gaps.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Scenario Engine -> Capability Gap Analyzer -> Requirement Translator -> Compliance Artifact Store -> Procurement & R&D Governance

**Critical path**:  
1. Define operational C-UAS scenario  
2. Identify capability gap  
3. Propose ML solution  
4. Stress-test solution within scenario to expose p-hacking vulnerabilities  
5. Derive requirement  
6. Map requirement to MLOps checkpoint & legal/procurement policy  
7. Validate via red-teaming the scenario with/without the requirement

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Speed vs. Transparency*: Pre-registration adds overhead to R&D/Procurement cycles.  
- *Generality vs. Specificity*: Scenario-derived requirements may be overly context-specific; extracting reusable principles is challenging.  
- *Innovation Inhibition*: Overly rigid compliance by design could stifle novel ML approaches; the scenario method aims to contextualize requirements to avoid this.

**Failure signatures**:  
- *Scenario Blindness*: Capability gap or scenario fails to capture a key real-world variance, leaving implicit p-hacking undetected.  
- *Artifact Disconnect*: Pre-registration exists bureaucratically but is not integrated into MLOps or procurement criteria, rendering it ineffective.  
- *Cumulative Causality liability*: Post-failure, audit trail is incomplete or ignored, obscuring whether cause was flawed system or flawed operator decision.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Replication with real data: Use a public radar-UAS dataset to train an RNN on a homogeneous subset (e.g., only fixed-wing sUAS) and evaluate on heterogeneous test sets (including rotary-wing). Measure performance degradation to quantify implicit p-hacking.  
2. Pre-registration protocol stress test: Create a minimal pre-registration template (hypotheses, data splits, metrics). Simulate procurement where some vendors submit pre-registrations and others only final accuracy reports. Assess whether evaluators can detect and discount submissions with post-hoc metric manipulation.  
3. Cumulative causality simulation: Model the hailstorm failure scenario as a Bayesian network. Compare uncertainty in legal responsibility attribution when development history is opaque versus when a complete pre-registration audit trail exists.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Operational Integration of Pre-registration**: How can mandatory pre-registration of ML research designs be integrated into military procurement and R&D processes (e.g., NATO Responsible AI) without hindering urgent innovation? The paper identifies the need but not the operational steps, especially given trade secret concerns and multinational timelines.  
2. **Detection Metrics for P-hacking**: What specific technical and procedural metrics can reliably detect both implicit (data bias) and explicit (metric manipulation) p-hacking in deployed ML signal processing systems? The paper notes p-hacking is hard to discover after the fact but offers no detection mechanisms.  
3. **Balancing Transparency and Confidentiality**: How can the tension between Rule of Law transparency (requiring disclosure) and commercial/military confidentiality (trade secrets, operational security) be balanced in pre-registration and auditing? The paper argues for transparency but does not address legitimate secrecy claims.  
4. **Generalization Beyond the Showcase**: To what extent do the p-hacking risks and solutions (pre-registration, MLOps integration) generalize to other ML-enhanced C-UAS signal processing tasks (e.g., classification, intent designation) or to other Emerging Disruptive Technologies (EDTs) beyond ML? The analysis is scenario-specific; broader applicability is untested.

## Limitations
- The methodology lacks quantitative validation using real radar data; scenarios remain conceptual and unverified with empirical performance metrics.
- The mandatory pre-registration requirement is abstract; its concrete integration into existing MLOps pipelines and procurement governance is not explored.
- Scenario design may not capture the full variance of operational contexts (limited to urban clutter, fixed/rotary-wing sUAS), potentially missing other sources of implicit p-hacking.

## Confidence
