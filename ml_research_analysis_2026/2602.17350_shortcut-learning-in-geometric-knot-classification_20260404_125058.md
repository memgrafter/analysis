# Shortcut learning in geometric knot classification

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.17350  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17350  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Machine‑learning models trained on molecular dynamics data for knot classification achieve >99% accuracy but fail on a geometrically unbiased dataset, revealing reliance on non‑topological shortcuts such as total writhe.

## Executive Summary
The paper examines whether ML models truly learn topological invariants for knot classification or exploit geometric shortcuts present in molecular dynamics (MD) training data. Using a mutual‑information‑based shortcut probe, the authors show that MD datasets contain strong correlations between geometric functionals (e.g., total writhe) and knot labels, enabling simple neural networks to reach near‑perfect accuracy on MD test sets. However, these models perform poorly (~50–70%) on the newly introduced GEOKNOT dataset, which is sampled to eliminate such correlations. The failure extends to published knot‑classification models. Further analysis reveals that decisions are based on non‑invariant features like writhe and that models are not invariant under topology‑preserving deformations. In contrast, a topological feature derived from writhe matrices (approximating Vassiliev invariants) achieves high accuracy on GEOKNOT, indicating that topological information is present but not learned by standard ML. The work underscores the risk of shortcut learning in geometric deep learning for topology.

## Method Summary
The study uses two dataset families: (1) MD‑generated polygonal knot embeddings (100 vertices) from LAMMPS with bending and excluded‑volume potentials at low/high temperatures, and (2) GEOKNOT, produced by a biased Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler that uniformly explores geometries while preserving topology (verified via Alexander polynomial and Vassiliev invariants). Input representations are raw 3D coordinates and discretized writhe matrices. Models are four‑layer feed‑forward neural networks (320 neurons per layer) trained with Adam (lr = 1e‑3) and cross‑entropy loss. A shortcut probe computes mutual information between geometric functionals (total writhe, average crossing number, etc.) and labels; high values flag potential shortcuts. The “shortcut index” τ measures the ratio of accuracy when training only on high‑MI features to full‑model accuracy. Saliency analysis (gradient‑based) identifies influential geometric features. Finally, isotopy invariance is tested by deforming misclassified unknots from GEOKNOT toward a minimized shape using KnotPlot and tracking predictions along the deformation path.

## Key Results
- MD‑trained models achieve >99% accuracy on MD test sets but only 50–70% on unbiased GEOKNOT; published models similarly fail (14–52%).
- MD datasets exhibit high mutual information (e.g., I(total writhe; label) ≈ 0.69) between geometric features and knot type, while GEOKNOT shows near‑zero mutual information.
- Shortcut index τ approaches 1 for low‑temperature MD data, confirming near‑total reliance on shortcut features.
- Saliency analysis identifies total writhe as the dominant feature for low‑T MD models.
- Models are not invariant under topology‑preserving deformations: prediction probability changes sharply as writhe decreases along an isotopy.
- A topological proxy (Vassiliev invariants approximated from writhe matrices) yields ~98% accuracy on GEOKNOT, showing that topological information is extractable but missed by standard ML.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
#### Mechanism 1: Data Distribution Induces Spurious Correlation
Molecular dynamics simulations produce knot embeddings with narrow geometric distributions due to force‑field constraints (bending stiffness, excluded volume). This forces different topologies to occupy distinct regions in geometric feature space (e.g., total writhe, pairwise distances), creating high mutual information between these non‑topological features and knot labels. Evidence: MD datasets show I(Ω⁺; y)=0.69 for low‑temperature data, while GEOKNOT shows near‑zero MI (Table II). The shortcut disappears when using an unbiased sampler like GEOKNOT.

#### Mechanism 2: Supervised Learning Exploits Available Shortcuts
Standard feed‑forward neural networks trained on MD data readily exploit high‑MI features because gradient descent finds low‑dimensional decision boundaries based on them. Models trained solely on shortcut features achieve virtually identical accuracy to full‑coordinate models, yielding a shortcut index τ ≈ 1. Evidence: Shortcut‑only models reach ~99.9% on low‑T MD (Table III); saliency shows total writhe dominates (Fig. 4). Without such features (as in GEOKNOT), performance collapses to near random.

#### Mechanism 3: Lack of Topological Invariance
True topological classifiers must be invariant under ambient isotopy (smooth, cut‑free deformations). Shortcut‑dependent classifiers base decisions on non‑invariant geometric features (e.g., writhe). As a knot is deformed to a simpler geometry (e.g., via energy minimization), these features cross the learned decision boundary, flipping predictions. Evidence: MD‑trained models misclassify complex unknots from GEOKNOT as trefoils; as these unknots are continuously minimized, prediction probability drops sharply when writhe falls below ~3 (Fig. 5).

## Foundational Learning
- **Ambient isotopy & topological invariants**: A classifier must assign the same label to any two embeddings that can be smoothly deformed into each other without cutting. Understanding invariant vs. variant features is key to diagnosing shortcuts. *Quick check*: If you continuously untwist a trefoil, does total writhe necessarily stay constant? (No.)
- **Molecular dynamics sampling bias**: MD force fields (bending stiffness, excluded volume) restrict the conformational space explored, causing geometric distributions to differ across knot types. *Quick check*: Would increasing temperature in an MD simulation of a polymer chain broaden or narrow the distribution of its radius of gyration? (Broaden.)
- **Mutual information as a shortcut probe**: Computing I(geometric feature; label) quantifies whether a feature carries information about the knot label in the dataset, regardless of causality. *Quick check*: If I(Ω⁺; y) ≈ 0 for a dataset, does that guarantee no shortcut exists? (It only means this feature isn’t correlated; other features might still be.)

**Component map:** Datasets (MD, GEOKNOT) → Representations (3D coordinates / writhe matrix) → Models (4‑layer FFNN, published architectures) → Diagnostics (mutual information, saliency, isotopy test)

**Critical path:**  
1. Train a feed‑forward network on low‑temperature MD coordinates; expect >99% accuracy on MD test set.  
2. Evaluate the same model on GEOKNOT; expect accuracy drops to ~50–70%.  
3. Compute mutual information I(Φ; y) for geometric functionals Φ on MD; expect high scores (e.g., total writhe >0.5).  
4. Train shortcut models using only high‑MI features; compute shortcut index τ ≈ 1 for MD.  
5. For misclassified GEOKNOT unknots, generate isotopy paths via KnotPlot and plot prediction probability vs. writhe; expect a sharp transition near writhe = 3.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Simplicity vs. plausibility: Simple FFNNs demonstrate that even minimal architectures exploit shortcuts; more complex models likely will too but were not thoroughly tested.  
- Computational cost: Verifying topology at each MCMC step (Alexander polynomial, Vassiliev invariants) is expensive, limiting dataset size and knot complexity.  
- Representation choice: Raw coordinates are intuitive but messy; writhe matrices encode more topological structure but standard ML still fails to extract it effectively.

**Failure signatures:**  
- In‑distribution success / out‑of‑distribution failure (MD test >99%, GEOKNOT ~50%).  
- Shortcut index τ near 1 (accuracy with shortcut‑only features ≈ full‑model accuracy).  
- Low mutual information on unbiased data (I(ϕ; y) ≈ 0 on GEOKNOT).  
- Prediction non‑invariance along isotopy (confidence changes with writhe).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions; however, it highlights several unresolved issues through its "Limitations" and "Next Checks" sections. These implicitly raise the following open questions:

- Does shortcut learning generalize to more complex knot types (e.g., figure‑eight, 5₁) and varying polygon lengths, given the study's restriction to unknots and trefoils with 100 vertices?
- Would more expressive or equivariant architectures (e.g., SE(3)-Transformers) exhibit the same reliance on geometric shortcuts when trained on MD data, suggesting the problem is fundamentally data‑driven?
- Can an automated isotopy validation framework generate many deformation paths to statistically separate writhe dependence from true topological invariance, providing a robust diagnostic for shortcut learning?

These questions aim to extend the findings and improve the robustness of topological classifiers.

## Limitations
- The exact MD simulation parameters (force‑field constants, temperature control) are not fully specified, making exact replication difficult.
- GEOKNOT sampling hyperparameters (e.g., Wang‑Landau bin sizes) are not detailed, which could affect the claimed uniformity.
- The study is restricted to two knot types (unknot, trefoil) and a fixed polygon length (100 vertices); generalization to more complex knots or varying lengths is unknown.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑knot generalization:** Train on MD datasets with additional knot types (e.g., figure‑eight, 5₁) and test on corresponding GEOKNOT splits to verify that shortcut reliance extends beyond unknots and trefoils.
2. **Architecture stress test:** Evaluate equivariant graph neural networks (e.g., SE(3)-Transformers) on MD vs. GEOKNOT. A similar performance drop would confirm the issue is data‑driven rather than model‑specific.
3. **Automated isotopy validation:** Use a controlled MCMC sampler to generate many isotopy paths for misclassified GEOKNOT unknots, tracking predictions and multiple geometric functionals to statistically separate writhe dependence from true topological invariance.
