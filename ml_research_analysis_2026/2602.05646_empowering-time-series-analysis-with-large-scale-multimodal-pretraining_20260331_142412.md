# Empowering Time Series Analysis with Large-Scale Multimodal Pretraining

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05646  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05646  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** HORAI achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot forecasting (29.6% MSE reduction vs. ROSE) and anomaly detection (13.4% AUC-ROC improvement over DADA) across multiple datasets.

## Executive Summary
Existing time series foundation models are unimodal, limiting their ability to capture complex temporal dynamics. This paper addresses the problem by introducing a multimodal pretraining paradigm that combines time series with endogenous modalities (derived images and text) and exogenous knowledge (real-world news). The authors build MM-TS, a large-scale multimodal dataset with up to one billion points across six domains, and propose HORAI, a frequency-enhanced multimodal foundation model. HORAI uses a Frequency-enhanced Cross-Modality Encoder to align low-frequency time series components with text and mid/high-frequency components with images via Flow-Attention, and a Time-Frequency Decoder with a router that leverages both temporal and frequency features for expert selection in a Mixture of Experts layer. Pretrained autoregressively on MM-TS, HORAI demonstrates strong zero-shot performance on forecasting and anomaly detection tasks, with significant improvements over prior unimodal and multimodal baselines.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two core contributions: (1) the MM-TS dataset, which integrates three modalities—time series, images (line plots generated from the series), and text (endogenous descriptions of trends/seasonality plus exogenous news from GDELT). The dataset covers six diverse domains and is curated with LLM-based filtering to ensure logical consistency. (2) HORAI, an autoregressive transformer decoder that processes multimodal inputs. It first encodes the time series via patching and splits it into low-frequency and mid/high-frequency components using FFT. Low-frequency embeddings are aligned with text embeddings, and mid/high-frequency with image embeddings, using Flow-Attention—a linear-complexity attention mechanism that treats information as a flow to optimize conservation. The aligned modalities are fused adaptively (gated weighting) and combined with the original time series embedding. The decoder applies causal self-attention followed by a Time-Frequency MoE-FFN: a router that combines per-token temporal (MLP) and frequency (FFT+MLP) features to route tokens to one of several experts. The model is trained to predict the next time series token autoregressively.

## Key Results
- **Zero-shot forecasting:** HORAI ranks first on all nine test datasets, reducing MSE by 29.6% compared to the previous SOTA (ROSE).
- **Zero-shot anomaly detection:** HORAI achieves the best result in 13 out of 15 cases, improving AUC-ROC by 13.4% over DADA.
- **Cross-modal generalization:** Ablations confirm that both text and image modalities contribute, and that frequency-guided alignment is critical for performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Frequency-enhanced cross-modality alignment
- **Claim:** Matching time series frequency bands to complementary modalities improves representation learning—low-frequency trends align with text (global semantics), mid/high-frequency details align with images (local patterns).
- **Mechanism:** FFT decomposes the time series into low-frequency (long-term) and mid/high-frequency (short-term) components. Low-frequency embeddings are fused with text embeddings, and mid/high-frequency with image embeddings, using Flow-Attention. Flow-Attention treats attention as a conservation flow, enabling efficient cross-modal fusion with linear complexity.
- **Core assumption:** Low-frequency dynamics reflect global temporal concepts that can be described textually, while high-frequency variations correspond to local structures that are visually salient.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract, section 3.2.1] The decomposition and alignment process is described; [corpus] Unimodal baselines lack this frequency conditioning.
- **Break condition:** If frequency decomposition fails to separate distinct patterns, or if textual content describes local details rather than global trends, the alignment may degrade performance.

### Mechanism 2: Time-Frequency MoE router
- **Claim:** Incorporating frequency features into the router of a Mixture of Experts layer improves expert assignment for heterogeneous temporal patterns, boosting generalization.
- **Mechanism:** For each token, the router computes a temporal feature via an MLP on the token embedding and a frequency feature via FFT followed by an MLP. A learned gate adaptively combines these features to produce a routing representation, which is used for Top‑K expert selection. Frequency cues provide complementary signals, helping coherent grouping of similar patterns.
- **Core assumption:** Frequency‑domain representations offer information orthogonal to temporal features, making routing more robust across diverse domains.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract, section 3.2.2] The router formulation is given as `H^r = G_router ⊙ MLP(H) + (1-G_router) ⊙ MLP(FFT(H))`; [corpus] Prior MoE‑based time series models (e.g., MOIRAI) do not use frequency in routing.
- **Break condition:** Noisy frequency features or an imbalanced gate may cause expert collapse or misrouting. Monitoring expert utilization is necessary.

### Mechanism 3: Multimodal pretraining with endogenous and exogenous data
- **Claim:** Jointly pretraining on time series, derived images, and text (both endogenous descriptions and exogenous news) yields a multi‑view representation that enhances zero‑shot time series understanding.
- **Mechanism:** MM‑TS combines three modalities: (1) raw time series, (2) line‑plot images generated from each series, (3) text that includes LLM‑generated pattern descriptions (endogenous) and news summaries from GDELT (exogenous). Quality control via LLM filtering ensures logical consistency. The model learns to reconstruct the next token by integrating signals from all modalities.
- **Core assumption:** Different modalities capture complementary aspects of temporal phenomena; integrating them during pretraining produces more robust features than training on a single modality.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract, section 3.1] The data pipeline and quality‑control steps are outlined; [corpus] Ablations (Figure 6) show that replacing text with random strings degrades performance.
- **Break condition:** If exogenous news is irrelevant or contradictory, or endogenous text is noisy, spurious correlations may be learned, harming performance.

  Why needed: Enables efficient cross‑modal fusion with linear complexity, replacing quadratic attention for long sequences.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
TS Series → TS Encoder (patch + MLP) → E_ts  
Text → Text Encoder (Qwen‑0.5B) → E_text  
Image → Image Encoder (ViT‑Base) → E_img  
E_ts → FFT → split into Low (E_low) and Mid‑High (E_mh)  
(E_low + E_text) → Flow‑Attention → Aligned_Text  
(E_mh + E_img) → Flow‑Attention → Aligned_Image  
Aligned_Text & Aligned_Image → Adaptive Fusion (gated) → E_mm  
E_mm + E_ts → E_fused  
E_fused → Decoder (Causal Self‑Attention + Time‑Frequency MoE‑FFN) → H_moe  
H_moe → Token Projection → predictions

### Critical Path
1. Input time series is normalized, patched, and encoded by the TS encoder to produce E_ts.  
2. In parallel, E_ts is transformed via FFT, binary masks (with α=0.05) separate low‑frequency and mid/high‑frequency components, which are inverse‑FFT back to the time domain and patched to obtain E_low and E_mh.  
3. Text and image inputs are encoded separately by pretrained Qwen‑0.5B and ViT‑Base encoders to yield E_text and E_img.  
4. Cross‑modality alignment: E_low and E_text are fused via Flow‑Attention to produce Aligned_Text; E_mh and E_img similarly yield Aligned_Image.  
5. An adaptive gating mechanism combines the two aligned embeddings into a multimodal embedding E_mm.  
6. E_mm is added to the original E_ts to form E_fused.  
7. E_fused passes through causal self‑attention.  
8. For each token in the attention output, the Time‑Frequency Router computes a temporal feature (MLP) and a frequency feature (FFT+MLP), gates them, and uses the result to select Top‑K experts (K=2/3) within the MoE‑FFN.  
9. The aggregated expert output is projected to the token dimension for next‑token prediction.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Frequency threshold α:** Controls the low/mid‑high split; α=0.05 was optimal. Smaller α merges high‑frequency into low, larger α leaves less in low.
- **Number of experts K:** More experts increase capacity but risk under‑utilization; K=2 or 3 performed best.
- **Encoder size:** Larger pretrained text/vision encoders may improve semantics but increase memory; trade‑off between gain and cost.
- **Flow‑Attention:** Linear complexity vs. possible loss of fine‑grained interactions; suitable for long sequences.
- **Modality inclusion:** Both text and image contribute; removing either hurts, with text more important for trend‑heavy datasets.

### Failure Signatures
- **Poor alignment:** If Flow‑Attention fails to cross‑match modalities, performance drops on datasets where correspondence is strong (e.g., Agriculture). Monitor via embedding visualizations.
- **Router collapse:** All tokens assigned to one expert → MoE reduces to a single FFN. Check expert utilization histograms; should be roughly uniform.
- **Data quality degradation:** Noisy or irrelevant exogenous/endogenous text introduces spurious correlations; random text ablation (Figure 6) significantly increases MSE.
- **Overfitting to pretrain distribution:** Zero‑shot on out‑of‑distribution domains may require fine‑tuning; performance improves with more downstream data (Figure 5).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Zero‑shot forecasting:** HORAI is evaluated on 9 datasets and compared to unimodal and multimodal foundation models; MSE/MAE reductions are reported (e.g., 29.6% vs. ROSE).
2. **Zero‑shot anomaly detection:** HORAI is tested on 5 datasets against DADA and other baselines; AUC‑ROC improvements are measured (e.g., 13.4% over DADA).
3. **Ablation study:** Ablations remove individual modalities, swap alignment schemes, remove the MoE‑FFN, or omit frequency from the router; performance degradations validate each component’s contribution (Figures 4, 6, Table 8).

## Limitations
- The exact MM‑TS construction pipeline (e.g., GDELT query formulation, LLM judging thresholds) is not fully disclosed, hindering faithful replication.  
- The assumed frequency‑modality correspondence (low‑freq ↔ text, mid/high‑freq ↔ image) lacks independent verification and may not generalize to all domains.  
- Zero‑shot evaluation risks overfitting to the pretraining distribution; cross‑domain robustness beyond the six covered domains remains untested.

## Confidence
- Frequency‑enhanced cross‑modality alignment: **Medium**  
- Time‑Frequency MoE router: **Medium**  
- Multimodal pretraining gains: **Medium‑High**

## Next Checks
1. Recreate a minimal MM‑TS subset: fetch raw series, generate endogenous text with GPT‑4 using the described prompt, retrieve news via GDELT; measure modality consistency (e.g., embedding similarity).
2. Conduct frequency split sensitivity: vary α from 0 to 0.2 and test swapped alignment (low‑freq with image, high‑freq with text) on a held‑out domain to validate the frequency‑modality hypothesis.
3. Monitor expert utilization during pretraining: log per‑layer router assignment histograms; compute entropy and correlate with downstream performance to detect router collapse or expert imbalance.
