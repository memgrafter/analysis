# HONEST-CAV: Hierarchical Optimization of Network Signals and Trajectories for Connected and Automated Vehicles with Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.18740  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18740  
- **Reference count:** 26  
- **Primary result:** At 60% CAV penetration, average speed ↑7.67%, fuel consumption ↓10.23%, idling time ↓45.83% vs. Webster baseline in a 4×4 Hangzhou grid; gains increase with CAV rate, EVs further reduce energy.

## Executive Summary
HONEST-CAV presents a hierarchical framework for joint optimization of traffic signal control and CAV eco‑driving in mixed traffic. The upper layer uses decentralized MARL with VDN to adapt signal timings, while the lower layer employs a machine‑learning‑based trajectory planner (MLTPA) for CAVs. A novel SPaT prediction module coordinates the two by forecasting upcoming green windows. Evaluated on a real‑world 4×4 Hangzhou network, the system achieves significant improvements in mobility, energy, and idling compared to a Webster fixed‑time baseline, with scaling benefits as CAV penetration grows and additional savings from EVs.

## Method Summary
The framework comprises two coupled layers. The upper layer implements decentralized traffic signal control via MARL. Each intersection runs a VDN‑based agent using Multi‑Agent Soft Actor‑Critic (MASAC). The state per agent is a 144‑dimensional vector encoding occupancy, average speed, queue length, and CAV penetration for each direction/lane over three aggregated time windows. Agents output a 5‑dimensional continuous action modifying cycle length ratio and green splits; a global common cycle is obtained by averaging and rescaling. The global reward sums passed vehicles minus a weighted queue length (ω=12) across all intersections and time steps. Training uses asynchronous multi‑processing (8 workers), shared actor‑critic networks, a replay buffer of size 100k, and standard hyperparameters (lr=0.0003, γ=0.99, batch size=32) over 150 episodes. The lower layer governs CAV trajectories through an MLTPA, an imitation‑learning model trained on near‑optimal trajectories generated by a Graph‑Based Trajectory Planning Algorithm (GBTPA). Coordination is achieved by an SPaT Prediction Algorithm that estimates the next cycle’s green interval for an approaching CAV by blending the MARL‑predicted phase with historical patterns using a time‑dependent weight β = min(τ/t_cyc, 1.0). The entire pipeline runs in SUMO using a calibrated 4×4 Hangzhou network with human‑driven vehicles (Krauss model) and CAVs.

## Key Results
- At 60% CAV penetration vs. Webster baseline: average speed ↑7.67%, fuel consumption ↓10.23%, idling time ↓45.83%.  
- Performance improvements grow with CAV penetration rates (10%–90%).  
- Replacing internal combustion engine vehicles with EVs yields additional energy savings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The hierarchical design isolates slow‑evolving network‑level signal optimization from fast vehicle‑level trajectory planning, reducing computational complexity. MARL enables intersections to collaboratively maximize a global throughput‑based reward, leading to adaptive signal plans that respond to real‑time traffic conditions, including CAV presence. The MLTPA provides CAVs with smooth, fuel‑optimal trajectories that target green windows, minimizing stops. The SPaT predictor gives CAVs advance knowledge of future signal states, aligning their trajectories with the adaptive plan. This creates a positive feedback loop: signals adapt to CAV behavior (since CAVs are part of the traffic stream), and CAVs exploit those adaptations, yielding synergistic gains that increase with CAV market share.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi‑Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)**  
  *Why needed:* Enables decentralized control at each intersection while optimizing a global objective through credit assignment (VDN).  
  *Quick check:* Verify that the summed individual Q‑values approximated by VDN approximate the global Q‑value from a centralized critic during training.

- **Value Decomposition Network (VDN)**  
  *Why needed:* Decomposes the global Q‑function into a sum of individual agent Q‑functions, allowing independent action selection with joint optimality guarantees.  
  *Quick check:* Monitor the mixing network’s ability to reconstruct the global Q‑value from individual outputs; the mixing error should decrease during training.

- **Imitation Learning (for MLTPA)**  
  *Why needed:* Avoids costly online optimization by learning a direct mapping from contextual features (passing window, distance, speed) to near‑optimal trajectories.  
  *Quick check:* Evaluate the MLTPA’s mean squared error against GBTPA trajectories on a held‑out dataset; aim for <5% error in fuel consumption estimates.

- **Graph‑Based Trajectory Planning (GBTPA)**  
  *Why needed:* Generates optimal training data by solving a time‑space shortest‑path problem with vehicle dynamics and signal constraints.  
  *Quick check:* Ensure GBTPA trajectories satisfy all constraints (e.g., maximum acceleration) and yield lower fuel consumption than a simple cruising profile.

- **SPaT Prediction**  
  *Why needed:* Provides CAVs with reliable future phase information despite imperfect V2I communication, enabling eco‑driving.  
  *Quick check:* Compare predicted vs. actual green start/end times across many approaches; target >80% of CAVs to arrive within the predicted window.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
SUMO simulation -> State extraction (per intersection) -> MARL agents (VDN+MASAC) -> Action transformation (cycle/splits) -> Global signal plan -> SPaT predictor (blends policy & history) -> MLTPA (per CAV) -> Trajectory execution -> New state & reward -> Replay buffer -> Asynchronous network updates (training loop).  

Critical path: During each simulation cycle, the system collects traffic states, runs the MARL agents to compute signal adjustments, broadcasts these to intersections, predicts green windows for approaching CAVs, and calls MLTPA to generate speed profiles. CAVs then follow these trajectories. Meanwhile, experiences are stored in a replay buffer and used to update the shared networks asynchronously across multiple workers.  

Design tradeoffs:  
- *Decentralized MARL* scales to many intersections but requires careful credit assignment (VDN) and may suffer from non‑stationarity.  
- *Continuous action space* offers fine‑grained control but increases sample complexity compared to discrete phase selections.  
- *Hierarchical separation* simplifies each layer but introduces coupling through SPaT prediction; inaccurate forecasts can cascade.  
- *Imitation learning* for MLTPA is data‑efficient but may not generalize to scenarios outside the GBTPA training distribution.  

Failure signatures:  
- Global reward plateaus or diverges → check reward scaling, entropy coefficient α, and action clipping.  
- High rate of CAVs missing green windows → examine SPaT prediction error (MAE > 2 s) and adjust β weight.  
- Training instability or NaNs → verify replay buffer size, batch size, and gradient clipping.  

First three onboarding experiments:  
1. **Baseline replication:** Build a synthetic 4×4 grid in SUMO, implement Webster fixed‑time control, and record speed, fuel consumption, and idling time to establish reference numbers.  
2. **MARL‑only training:** Replace the CAV model with a simple IDM, disable SPaT prediction (use historical only), and train the signal agents for 50 episodes. Confirm global reward improvement and observe cycle/split adaptations.  
3. **Full integration test:** Load a pre‑trained MLTPA (or substitute a rule‑based eco‑driver), enable SPaT blending, run a few episodes, and compare the three key metrics against the Webster baseline to verify the expected direction and magnitude of gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **City‑scale scalability:** Can HONEST‑CAV be applied to city‑level networks via a zone division approach? The authors mention this as future work; scaling introduces inter‑zone coordination and increased state/action spaces that remain unexplored.  
2. **Generalization to topologies:** Does the framework perform on irregular, radial, or hybrid networks beyond the regular 4×4 grid? The modular design suggests potential, but validation on diverse topologies is lacking.  
3. **Robustness to disturbances:** How does the hybrid SPaT predictor handle non‑recurrent events (accidents, special events) that alter traffic flow and phase patterns? The study assumes normal conditions only.  
4. **Impact of communication constraints:** The framework assumes perfect V2I communication; real‑world latency and packet loss could degrade coordination and performance.  
5. **Comparison with other MARL algorithms:** HONEST‑CAV uses VDN and benchmarks only against Webster and IRL; it does not compare with alternatives like QMIX or MADDPG under the same eco‑approach strategy, leaving algorithmic choices unevaluated.

## Limitations
- The exact Hangzhou 4×4 network files and calibrated demand are unavailable, forcing approximation in any reproduction.  
- MLTPA architecture and its training data generation (GBTPA) are not described, preventing exact reconstruction of the trajectory planner.  
- Several hyperparameters (e.g., entropy coefficient α, action noise schedule, SPaT blending weight β) are omitted and must be tuned empirically.

## Confidence
- **Framework effectiveness (improved speed, fuel, idling):** Medium – methodology is sound but unverified without original code/data.  
- **Quantified gains (e.g., 7.67% speed increase):** Medium – numbers depend on network specifics and implementation choices.  
- **Scaling with CAV penetration:** Medium – trend aligns with theory, but exact improvement percentages are uncertain.  
- **EV energy reduction:** Low – claim lacks detailed experimental support; likely extrapolated.

## Next Checks
1. **Reproduce MARL convergence:** Train the signal agents with the reported hyperparameters; if global reward does not improve as in Fig. 4, systematically vary entropy regularization α and action scaling, then document sensitivity.  
2. **Assess SPaT prediction accuracy:** After training, log >1000 CAV approaches; compute mean absolute error between predicted and actual green intervals. If MAE > 2 s, recalibrate β using a validation set and re‑evaluate metrics.  
3. **Validate on a synthetic grid:** Implement a generic 4×4 grid (e.g., 300 m links, 2 lanes, calibrated demand), run the full HONEST‑CAV pipeline, and compare against Webster. If improvements are within ±3 percentage points of the reported values, the approach generalizes; larger discrepancies suggest missing details.
