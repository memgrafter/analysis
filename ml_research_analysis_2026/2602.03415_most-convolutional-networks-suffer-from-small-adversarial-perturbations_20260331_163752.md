# Most Convolutional Networks Suffer from Small Adversarial Perturbations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03415  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03415  
- **Reference count:** 4  
- **Primary result:** Random CNNs with Xavier initialization admit ℓ₂ adversarial perturbations of size Θ(∥x∥/√d), and a single gradient descent step finds such an example with probability ≥0.95 under mild architectural constraints.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the existence of adversarial examples in randomly initialized convolutional neural networks (CNNs), extending previous results for fully connected networks. The authors develop a Fourier‑analytic approach to analyze the singular values of random convolutional operators on finite abelian groups, obtaining tight high‑probability bounds. These bounds enable them to prove that for typical CNNs with smooth activations, there always exists an ℓ₂ perturbation of size Θ(∥x∥/√d) that flips the prediction of a linear classifier read out from the network. Moreover, they show that with constant probability at least 0.95, a single step of gradient descent with step size O(1) suffices to find such an adversarial example. The analysis relies on assumptions of constant depth, non‑increasing widths, and activation functions that are twice differentiable with a uniform lower bound on a combination of derivatives.

The results highlight a fundamental fragility: even randomly wired CNNs, without any training, are vulnerable to small adversarial perturbations. The Fourier decomposition technique reduces a high‑dimensional random operator to independent low‑dimensional blocks, making the singular value analysis tractable. However, the theoretical guarantees involve hidden constants that may require large model widths and group sizes to hold in practice, and the smoothness assumption excludes widely used non‑smooth activations like ReLU.

## Method Summary
The methodology centers on representing the input space \(L^2(G,\mathbb{R}^d)\) of a finite abelian group \(G\) as a direct sum of isotypic components \(V_\chi \otimes \mathbb{R}^d\) indexed by characters \(\chi\). A random convolutional operator \(\Lambda\) with i.i.d. Gaussian weights (Xavier initialization) acts independently on each subspace. This structure allows the application of standard random matrix concentration (Gaussian width, \(\epsilon\)-nets) to bound the operator’s singular values uniformly over all characters, yielding high‑probability upper and lower bounds with constant factors. The network is composed of \(t\) such layers separated by a pointwise activation \(\sigma\) that is assumed \(C^2\) and satisfies \((\sigma'(r))^2 + (\sigma'(-r))^2 \ge 2c^2\) for all \(r\). The Jacobian of the final layer’s output factorizes as \(J = D_t \Lambda_t \cdots D_1 \Lambda_1\), where \(D_\ell\) is a diagonal matrix of activation derivatives. By lower bounding each term’s contribution and telescoping, the authors derive a lower bound on \(\|J\|_F^2\) proportional to \((0.1c^2)^t d_t |G|\). They further show that the gradient \(\nabla H_b(f)\) of the binary classifier \(H_b(f)=\langle u, h^{(t)}(f)\rangle\) inherits a non‑vanishing norm. Finally, using mean‑value expansions and the fact that the backpropagated vector’s \(\ell_\infty\) norm vanishes as \(|G|\to\infty\), they prove that the gradient is locally Lipschitz with a constant dominated by the gradient norm within a ball, which guarantees that a single gradient descent step of length \(O(1)\) flips the label with probability at least 0.95.

## Key Results
- Adversarial perturbations of size \(\Theta(\|x\|/\sqrt{d})\) exist in random CNNs; this matches an information‑theoretic lower bound and is achieved with high probability.
- Under the stated assumptions, one step of gradient descent with Euclidean step size \(O(1)\) finds an adversarial example with success probability \(\ge 0.95\).
- The Frobenius norm of the network’s Jacobian satisfies \(\|J\|_F^2 \ge \frac{1}{2}(0.1c^2)^t d_t |G|\), ensuring a non‑trivial gradient signal.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Fourier Decomposition for Singular Value Bounds**  
- **Claim:** The convolutional operator’s singular values are tightly bounded with high probability, enabling optimal adversarial perturbation analysis.  
- **Mechanism:** Fourier decomposition of \(L^2(G,\mathbb{R}^d)\) into isotypic components \(V_\chi \otimes \mathbb{R}^d\) for each character \(\chi\) of the abelian group \(G\). The operator \(\Lambda\) acts independently on each low‑dimensional (\(\le 2d\)) subspace, allowing standard random matrix concentration (Gaussian width, \(\epsilon\)-nets) to be applied per character. A union bound over \(|G|\) characters then yields uniform singular value bounds for the full operator.  
- **Core assumptions:** \(G\) finite abelian; filter weights i.i.d. Gaussian (Xavier); input/output dimensions satisfy \(d \ge c_w q\) and \(\min(d,q) \ge c_G \log|G|\).  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions Fourier decomposition; Section 4.2.1 details the decomposition and union bound.  
- **Break condition:** Non‑abelian \(G\) or non‑Gaussian weight distribution invalidates the decomposition, potentially destroying uniform singular value bounds.

**Mechanism 2: Jacobian Frobenius Norm Lower Bound**  
- **Claim:** The Frobenius norm of the network’s Jacobian \(J\) is lower bounded by a constant, ensuring \(\|\nabla H_b(f)\|\) is sufficiently large.  
- **Mechanism:** Jacobian factorizes as \(J = D_t \Lambda_t \cdots D_1 \Lambda_1\). Using a layer‑wise bound \(\|D_\ell \Lambda_\ell v\|^2 \ge 0.1 c^2 \frac{d_\ell}{d_{\ell-1}} \|v\|^2\) (via Gaussian quadratic form lower bound applied to output channel groups) and telescoping across constant depth \(t\), we get \(\|J v\|^2 \ge (0.1 c^2)^t \frac{d_t}{d_0} \|v\|^2\). The Frobenius norm follows from \(\|J\|_F^2 = \mathbb{E}_r[\|J r\|^2]\) with Rademacher vector \(r\).  
- **Core assumptions:** Activation \(\sigma\) is \(C^2\) with \(\forall r: (\sigma'(r))^2 + (\sigma'(-r))^2 \ge 2c^2\); constant depth \(t\); widths satisfy \(d_{\ell-1} \ge c_w d_\ell\).  
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 6 (Lemmas 6.1–6.4, Theorem 6.4) derives \(\|J\|_F^2 \ge \frac{1}{2}(0.1c^2)^t d_t |G|\).  
- **Break condition:** If activation derivative condition fails (e.g., flat regions), the layer‑wise amplification collapses and Jacobian norm may vanish.

**Mechanism 3: Local Lipschitzness of the Gradient**  
- **Claim:** The gradient \(\nabla H_b(f)\) is locally Lipschitz with a small constant within a ball \(B(f,\Delta)\), so one gradient descent step of length \(O(1)\) flips the label.  
- **Mechanism:** Bound \(\|\nabla H_b(f') - \nabla H_b(f)\|\) by product of (i) max singular value \(M_s\) of any \(\Lambda_\ell\), (ii) max \(\ell_\infty\) norm \(M_\infty\) of backpropagated vector \(\Phi_i(f)\), and (iii) variation \(M(f,\Delta)\) of activation derivative across the ball. Lemma 7.3 shows \(M(f,\Delta) \le \|\sigma''\|_\infty (\|\sigma'\|_\infty M_s)^t (2\Delta)\). Lemma 7.4 proves \(M_\infty = O(1/\sqrt{N_t})\) w.h.p. as \(|G| \to \infty\). For large enough \(|G|\), the Lipschitz constant \(c_2(\Delta)\) becomes much smaller than the gradient lower bound \(c_1 = \|\nabla H_b(f)\|\).  
- **Core assumptions:** \(\sigma \in C^2\) with bounded derivatives; constant depth; \(|G|\) sufficiently large relative to depth.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 7 (Lemmas 7.2–7.4) derives Lipschitz bound and \(M_\infty \to 0\); Section 8 combines to show \(c_2(\Delta) \le c_1/3\) for large \(|G|\).  
- **Break condition:** If \(|G|\) is too small, \(M_\infty\) may not vanish and \(c_2(\Delta)\) could exceed \(c_1\), breaking the one‑step guarantee.

## Foundational Learning
- **Representation theory of finite abelian groups** (characters, isotypic decomposition)  
  Why needed: The Fourier decomposition via characters \(V_\chi \otimes \mathbb{R}^d\) reduces analysis of the large convolutional operator to independent, low‑dimensional random matrices.  
  Quick check: Why does the action of a convolutional operator preserve each isotypic component \(V_\chi \otimes \mathbb{R}^d\)?

- **Random matrix theory for Gaussian matrices** (singular value concentration, \(\epsilon\)-nets, Gaussian quadratic forms)  
  Why needed: To bound singular values of the restricted operators \(\Lambda_\chi\) and lower bound quadratic forms like \(\sum (\alpha_i^\top v)^2\).  
  Quick check: How does the dimension \(2d\) of \(V_\chi \otimes \mathbb{R}^d\) compare to the full space dimension \(d|G|\), and why is the former crucial for the net argument?

- **Gradient‑based adversarial perturbation analysis** (Jacobians, Lipschitz gradients)  
  Why needed: Proving that a small Euclidean step in the gradient direction changes the network output sign hinges on lower bounding \(\|\nabla H_b(f)\|\) and controlling its variation.  
  Quick check: What identity links the Frobenius norm of the Jacobian to the expectation over Rademacher vectors?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**  
  Input (function \(f \in L^2(G,\mathbb{R}^d)\) with \(\|f\|_\infty \le 1\)) -> Convolutional layers (\(\Lambda_\ell\) with Xavier weights, then \(\sigma\)) -> Final fully connected layer (vector \(u \in \mathbb{R}^{d_t|G|}\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal{N}(0,1/(d_t|G|)\)) -> Decision \(\text{sign}(H_b(f))\).

- **Critical path**  
  Random initialization -> Singular value bounds (Theorem 3.2) -> Jacobian Frobenius norm lower bound (Theorem 6.4) -> Gradient norm lower bound (Lemma 6.6) -> Gradient Lipschitz constant (Lemmas 7.2–7.4) -> One‑step gradient descent analysis (Theorem 8.2).

- **Design tradeoffs**  
  - Width vs. depth: Proof requires constant depth \(t\) and non‑increasing widths \(d_{\ell-1} \ge c_w d_\ell\); prior work allows increasing widths but gives non‑optimal perturbation size.  
  - Activation smoothness: \(C^2\) with derivative condition excludes ReLU; smooth variants allowed; second derivatives used for Lipschitz control.  
  - Group size: \(|G|\) must be large enough relative to depth to force \(M_\infty \to 0\); small \(|G|\) breaks Lipschitz argument.

- **Failure signatures**  
  - Activation derivative condition fails (e.g., saturated sigmoid), causing Jacobian norm collapse.  
  - Widths increase sharply, making telescoping product gain factors <1 and possibly making \(\|J v\|\) sub‑constant.  
  - \(|G|\) too small (e.g., constant while depth grows) keeps \(M_\infty\) large, making \(c_2(\Delta) > c_1\).  
  - Weight distribution deviates from Gaussian; singular value bounds may not hold.

- **First 3 experiments**  
  1. **Empirical singular values:** Compute top/bottom singular values of each \(\Lambda_\ell\) for random CNNs; verify they cluster around theoretical constants as \(d,q,|G|\) vary.  
  2. **Gradient descent step:** Perform one gradient descent step on \(H_b(f)\) with step size \(\eta = 2a/\|\nabla H_b(f)\|^2\) (where \(a \approx 10\|\sigma'\|_\infty^t\)). Check label flip and \(\|f'-f\|_2 = O(\|f\|_2/\sqrt{d})\). Repeat to estimate success probability.  
  3. **Activation derivative condition:** Sample pre‑activations from a random layer; compute \(\min_r (\sigma'(r))^2 + (\sigma'(-r))^2\). Verify it is bounded away from zero for smooth ReLU variants; for plain ReLU, it is zero, indicating failure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Can the existence of adversarial examples with optimal ℓ₂‑distance be proven for CNNs with ReLU activation functions?  
  - *Basis:* The authors conjecture similar ideas may work but note \(C^2\) requirement excludes ReLU.  
  - *Why unresolved:* Current proof relies on twice differentiability to control derivatives; ReLU is not differentiable at zero.  
  - *What evidence would resolve:* A new technique handling non‑smooth activations (e.g., via subgradients).

- **Question 2:** Can the optimal adversarial perturbation bounds be achieved without constant depth and non‑increasing width assumptions?  
  - *Basis:* The paper acknowledges this limitation compared to prior work.  
  - *Why unresolved:* The Fourier analysis and bounds rely on these assumptions; relaxing them may require new insights.  
  - *What evidence would resolve:* Extending singular value bounds (Theorem 3.2) to more general layer dimensions or depth.

- **Question 3:** Is the Fourier decomposition approach applicable beyond standard CNNs with abelian group structures, such as non‑abelian group convolutions or attention?  
  - *Basis:* The method heavily uses the abelian group representation theory; the paper defines \(G\) as finite abelian.  
  - *Why unresolved:* Non‑abelian groups have more complex representation theory; clean decomposition into low‑dimensional subspaces may not exist.  
  - *What evidence would resolve:* Developing analogous decomposition for non‑abelian groups or alternative analyses for those architectures.

## Limitations
- Hidden constants (e.g., \(c_w, c_G\)) may necessitate impractically large widths and group sizes for the theoretical bounds to hold.
- The Lipschitz guarantee and one‑step success are asymptotic in \(|G|\); finite networks with moderate \(|G|\) may not satisfy \(M_\infty \to 0\).
- The smoothness assumption excludes non‑differentiable activations like ReLU, limiting applicability to common architectures.
- The proof requires constant depth and non‑increasing widths, which are restrictive compared to modern CNNs.

## Confidence
- Fourier singular value control: **High** (rigorous representation‑theoretic reduction and random matrix concentration).  
- Existence of ℓ₂ perturbations ~∥x∥/√d: **Medium** (theoretically sound but constant tightness and finite‑size effects uncertain).  
- One‑step gradient descent success (≥0.95): **Medium‑Low** (depends on \(M_\infty \to 0\), which requires large \(|G|\); may fail for moderate group sizes).  
- Jacobian Frobenius norm lower bound: **Medium** (relies on activation derivative condition; fragile for activations with near‑zero derivatives).

## Next Checks

2. **Activation sensitivity test:** Compare gradient descent success on a smooth ReLU variant (e.g., Softplus) vs standard ReLU. Verify the condition \(\min_r[(\sigma'(r))^2+(\sigma'(-r))^2] > 0\) and measure success probability as a function of \(|G|\).  
3. **Width ratio exploration:** Systematically vary the ratio \(r = d_{\ell-1}/d_\ell\) (e.g., 1.5, 2, 4, 8) at fixed depth. For each \(r\), run one‑step gradient descent trials to estimate success probability and average perturbation norm; identify empirical threshold below which guarantees fail.
