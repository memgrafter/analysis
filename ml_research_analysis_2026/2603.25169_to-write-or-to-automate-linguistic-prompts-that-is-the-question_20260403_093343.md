# To Write or to Automate Linguistic Prompts, That Is the Question

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25169
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25169
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** Manual expert prompts and GEPA-optimized DSPy signatures achieve comparable performance on most tasks, with GEPA consistently improving base signatures and unified LQA systems beating decomposed pipelines.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the trade‑offs between manually crafted linguistic prompts and automatically optimized prompts using the GEPA optimizer within the DSPy framework. Across three tasks—terminology insertion, translation, and language quality assessment (LQA)—and five large language models, the authors compare three conditions: hand‑crafted expert prompts, base DSPy signatures, and GEPA‑optimized DSPy signatures. Evaluation relies on automatic metrics such as BLEU, HTER, TMR, and detection F1. Key findings indicate that GEPA reliably enhances base signatures, that manual and optimized prompts are largely statistically indistinguishable though with task‑ and model‑specific nuances, and that unified single‑stage LQA systems outperform decomposed two‑stage pipelines on error detection across all models. The work highlights the potential of lightweight programmatic prompt optimization to approach expert‑level performance while also exposing limitations in metric alignment and validation representativeness.

## Method Summary
The study conducted systematic experiments on three linguistic tasks using five LLMs: GPT‑4.1‑mini, GPT‑5.4‑mini, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Flash‑Lite, and one additional model. For each task, gold‑standard test sets and a validation split were prepared. Expert prompts were developed iteratively by linguists without any access to the validation data, relying solely on domain knowledge and qualitative testing. Base DSPy signatures were minimal specifications of input and output fields (e.g., source text, glossary, and target translation). GEPA optimization ran in “light” mode: a reflection model (usually a more capable LLM) proposed textual prompt variations, which were evaluated on the validation set using a task‑specific composite metric (e.g., BLEU + HTER for translation). The highest‑scoring prompt was selected and then evaluated on the held‑out test set. For LQA, the authors additionally compared unified (single‑stage) and decomposed (two‑stage: detect‑then‑characterize) pipelines. All results were derived from automatic metrics; no human evaluation was performed.

## Key Results
- GEPA optimization consistently yields improvements over base DSPy signatures across all three tasks and five models, demonstrating the effectiveness of programmatic prompt search.
- Expert prompts and GEPA‑optimized prompts are generally not statistically significantly different, but performance varies by task: terminology insertion yields similar translation quality (BLEU/HTER) with optimization boosting term match rates for some models; translation shows model‑specific preferences (newer models often favor manual prompts); LQA gives manual prompts an advantage in error detection while optimization improves error characterization.
- For language quality assessment, unified single‑stage systems achieve higher detection F1 than decomposed two‑stage pipelines for every model tested, indicating that joint optimization avoids error propagation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** GEPA optimization elevates minimal DSPy signatures by programmatically searching over validation splits to find prompts that maximize task‑specific metrics.
- **Mechanism:** The optimizer iteratively proposes and evaluates textual prompt variations using a reflection model and gold‑standard feedback, refining prompts based on composite scores (e.g., BLEU+HTER for translation) to navigate the discrete prompt space.
- **Core assumption:** A prompt that performs well on a representative validation set will generalize to the test set.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "GEPA elevates minimal DSPy signatures"
  - [section 3.1] "GEPA optimization searches programmatically over gold‑standard splits"
  - [table 1‑3] Consistent gains of Opt. DSPy over Base DSPy across models/tasks.
- **Break condition:** If the validation metric poorly correlates with true task success (e.g., over‑optimizing for term match rate at the cost of fluency), or if the validation set is too small/non‑representative, the optimized prompt may overfit and fail to generalize.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Manual expert prompts retain an advantage for certain capability dimensions (e.g., error detection in LQA) because they encode domain heuristics and implicit quality judgments not fully captured by the automated metrics used for optimization.
- **Mechanism:** Experts use iterative, qualitative testing and linguistic knowledge to craft prompts that target nuanced aspects of quality (e.g., "detect critical errors") that are difficult to formalize into a differentiable or combinable metric for GEPA.
- **Core assumption:** Expert intuition identifies quality dimensions that automated metrics miss or weight incorrectly.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "In LQA, expert prompts achieve stronger error detection"
  - [table 3] Manual unified prompts achieve highest Detection F1 in 3/5 models, with significant wins for GPT‑4.1‑mini and GPT‑5.4‑mini.
  - [section 4.3] "Manual expert prompts retain an advantage on error detection."
- **Break condition:** If the optimization objective is redesigned to better align with human error detection (e.g., using a more capable judge or a hybrid metric), the manual advantage may diminish. The break condition is also task‑dependent; for terminology insertion, manual did not significantly outperform on detection‑equivalents (TMR).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Unified single‑stage systems generally outperform decomposed multi‑stage pipelines because decomposition introduces error propagation between stages without sufficient regularization or error correction.
- **Mechanism:** In a pipeline (e.g., detect‑then‑characterize for LQA), an error in the first stage (missed error detection) irrevocably prevents correct characterization, accumulating failures. An end‑to‑end unified model learns to jointly optimize all sub‑tasks, potentially avoiding brittle intermediate decisions.
- **Core assumption:** The supervisory signal from the final gold label can train a unified model to implicitly handle sub‑tasks more robustly than a chain of specialized prompts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "unified single‑stage systems generally outperform decomposed pipelines"
  - [table 3] Unified systems have higher Detection F1 than decomposed in all 5 models (differences 0.05–0.20).
  - [section 4.3] "This finding generalizes robustly: in all five models, unified systems outperform their two‑stage counterparts on Detection F1."
- **Break condition:** For tasks where sub‑tasks are highly separable and errors in one stage can be easily corrected by the next (e.g., with high‑confidence gating), a decomposed design might match or exceed unified performance. The paper finds no such evidence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Asymmetric Data Utilization**
  - *Why needed here:* The core comparison is asymmetric: GEPA uses labeled validation data for automated search, while expert prompts rely on human expertise without requiring the same gold‑standard splits. Understanding this asymmetry is critical for interpreting "comparable performance" claims.
  - *Quick check question:* If GEPA and manual prompts achieve similar scores, does that imply equal development cost? Why or why not?
- **Concept: Task‑Specific Prompt Sensitivity**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper’s key finding is that the manual‑vs‑optimized relationship is task‑dependent (terminology insertion: similar quality; translation: model‑specific; LQA: manual better at detection). This counters any monolithic view of prompt optimization.
  - *Quick check question:* Based on Tables 1‑3, for which task and model did manual prompts show the most *statistically significant* advantage over optimized prompts?
- **Concept: The DSPy Programming Framework**
  - *Why needed here:* DSPy is the substrate for both base and optimized comparisons. It treats prompts as compilable modules (`dspy.Predict`). Understanding signatures, optimizers (GEPA), and the "light" mode constraint is key to replicating the architecture.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the functional difference between a DSPy *signature* and a traditional hand‑crafted prompt?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input (source text, MT output, glossary/style guide, locale code) → Core System (DSPy module with signature) → Model output.  
Optimizer (GEPA light) uses Scaffolding (validation splits, reflection model) to iteratively refine the Core System’s prompt.  
Evaluation applies automatic metrics (BLEU, HTER, TMR, ChrF3, detection F1, category/severity F1) to outputs on the test set.

**Critical path:**  
Data preparation → Define DSPy Signature → Run GEPA optimization on validation set (with reflection) → Select best prompt → Evaluate on test set.  
Manual baseline bypasses the optimization step.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Compute vs. Data: GEPA requires more API calls (compute) but less human time; manual uses human time but may not exploit labeled data.
- Metric Alignment: Optimizing for a composite metric (e.g., HTER+ChrF3) may trade off nuances a human expert would prioritize (e.g., fluency over strict edit distance).
- Model Choice: Results are model‑specific; a prompt optimized for GPT‑4.1‑mini may not transfer to Gemini 3.1 Flash‑Lite.

**Failure signatures:**
- Optimized prompt achieves high validation score but low test score (overfitting to metric/validation set).
- Unified system performs worse than pipeline for a specific task/model (violates the general pattern, indicating possible task mis‑specification).
- Optimization objective leads to degenerate outcomes (e.g., high TMR but nonsensical translations).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline Replication:** For a single task‑model pair (e.g., GPT‑4.1‑mini on terminology insertion), compare Manual vs. Base DSPy vs. Opt. DSPy. Verify the pattern: Opt. improves over Base, may match Manual.
2. **Unified vs. Decomposed Stress Test:** For LQA with Claude Sonnet 4.6, run both unified and decomposed pipelines. Confirm unified Detection F1 > decomposed, as per Table 3.
3. **Optimization Robustness Check:** For GPT‑5.4‑mini on translation, run GEPA with different random seeds or small validation subset variations. Check if the "best" prompt (Table 2: Manual wins) is consistently found or if optimization flip‑flops, indicating instability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** To what extent does the asymmetry in labeled data usage—GEPA optimization exploits gold‑standard splits while expert prompts require no labeled data—bias the validity and interpretation of performance comparisons?
- **Basis in paper:** Abstract and Limitations explicitly state the comparison is asymmetric due to differing labeled data requirements.
- **Why unresolved:** Equal performance may mask different resource trade‑offs; the optimal approach likely depends on available labeled data and human expertise.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying labeled data access for both approaches, paired with cost‑benefit metrics (compute, human hours), across tasks/models.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can hybrid workflows that combine expert prompt engineering with automatic optimization (e.g., expert‑seeded GEPA or manual refinement of optimized prompts) yield synergistic gains, outperforming pure manual or pure optimized baselines?
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations note manual and automatic approaches are not mutually exclusive and may combine; Future Work explicitly calls for evaluating hybrid workflows.
- **Why unresolved:** The study only tested pure strategies; potential complementary effects remain unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic comparison of hybrid variants (expert→GEPA, GEPA→expert) against pure baselines across all tasks and models, measuring performance and development effort.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do alternative automatic prompt optimizers (not just GEPA) and non‑light optimization modes compare to expert prompts on linguistic tasks, and do results generalize beyond the `dspy.Predict` setup?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Work explicitly suggests comparing additional optimizers and optimization modes.
- **Why unresolved:** Only GEPA in "light" mode was evaluated; other optimizers may produce different performance patterns or cost trade‑offs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the study with multiple optimizers (e.g., OPRO, TextGrad) and modes, using identical tasks, models, and evaluation protocols.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do human quality judgments of outputs (e.g., translation fluency, error detection) align with the automatic metrics (BLEU, HTER, etc.) used in this study, and would such alignment change the conclusions about prompt strategies?
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations state all evaluation was automatic without human judgments, and human evaluation could shift results.
- **Why unresolved:** Automatic metrics may not capture production‑relevant nuances (e.g., severity perception, terminology appropriateness).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct human evaluations with professional linguists on system outputs, correlate with automatic scores, and analyze discrepancies that affect practical utility.

## Limitations
- **Validation representativeness:** GEPA’s reliance on limited validation splits risks overfitting to metric quirks, especially for nuanced tasks like LQA where human judgments diverge from automated scores.
- **Metric alignment:** Composite metrics (e.g., BLEU + HTER) may not fully capture quality dimensions experts prioritize (e.g., fluency, critical error detection), potentially biasing optimization.
- **Generalization across models:** Findings are model‑specific; prompts optimized for one model may not transfer, limiting broad applicability claims.

## Confidence
- **Unified systems outperform decomposed pipelines for LQA detection:** High
- **GEPA elevates base DSPy signatures:** Medium
- **Manual prompts match optimized prompts in terminology insertion/translation:** Low
