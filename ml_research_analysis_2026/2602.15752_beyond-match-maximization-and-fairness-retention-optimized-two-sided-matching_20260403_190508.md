# Beyond Match Maximization and Fairness: Retention-Optimized Two-Sided Matching

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.15752
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15752
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: MRet, a dynamic learning-to-rank algorithm, directly optimizes user retention by jointly modeling retention gains for both sides, achieving higher retention than match maximization and fairness baselines in synthetic and real-world online dating data.

## Executive Summary
Two-sided matching platforms often suffer from user churn due to imbalanced exposure when maximizing total matches. This paper proposes a direct retention optimization approach via MRet, a dynamic learning-to-rank algorithm. MRet learns a personalized concave retention function for each user from profile and interaction history. For each arriving user, it scores candidates by summing the retention gain for the receiver and the candidate, scaled by position weights. Under concavity, this simplifies to an efficient per-candidate score. Experiments on synthetic data and a large Japanese online dating platform show MRet significantly improves retention compared to baselines like Max Match and FairCo, even with fewer total matches. The method closely approximates the NP-hard optimum and is robust across conditions.

## Method Summary
MRet consists of offline training and online ranking. Offline, it trains a user-specific retention function \(f(x, m)\) (e.g., with XGBoost) to predict churn probability given user features \(x\) and cumulative matches \(m\), assuming \(f\) is concave in \(m\). Online, when a user \(x\) arrives, for each candidate \(y\) from the opposite side, MRet computes:
\[
\text{Score}(y) = \frac{1}{A} f(x, m(x) + A r(x,y)) + \frac{1}{\alpha_{\max}} \left[ f(y, m(y) + \alpha_{\max} r(x,y)) - f(y, m(y)) \right],
\]
where \(A = \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k\), \(\alpha_{\max} = \max_k \alpha_k\), \(r(x,y)\) is the match probability, and \(m(\cdot)\) are current match counts. Candidates are sorted by Score and the top \(K\) are recommended. Match counts are updated using position weights \(\alpha_k\). The concavity assumption enables reduction of the NP-hard optimal ranking to this scoring, allowing efficient implementation.

## Key Results
- On synthetic data, MRet used only ~70% of the matches allocated by Max Match but achieved higher user retention.
- On real-world sparse data from a Japanese dating platform, MRet notably outperformed Max Match, FairCo (fairness), and Uniform baselines in retention.
- MRet approximates the NP-hard optimal solution well and remains robust across varying arrival rates and position biases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Personalized Retention Modeling
- **Claim:** MRet learns a user-specific retention function \(f(x, m)\) that predicts the probability a user stays given cumulative matches \(m\).
- **Mechanism:** The function is trained offline via regression (e.g., XGBoost) on historical data \(\{x, m, u\}\) where \(u\) is churn (0/1). At runtime, for a user \(x\), \(f(x, \cdot)\) is queried with current match count \(m^{1:\tau}(x)\) and candidate match probabilities.
- **Core assumption:** Retention behavior is stable and can be captured by user features and past match counts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "MRet dynamically adapts recommendations by jointly considering the retention gains of both the user receiving recommendations and those who are being recommended."
  - [section 3]: "MRet first learns a personalized retention curve for each user based on profile and interaction history."
  - [corpus]: Neighbors like Tomita & Yokoyama (2409.00720) model user preferences but not retention directly—weak anchor.
- **Break condition:** Concept drift in user satisfaction or insufficient training data leads to inaccurate \(f\) estimates, degrading recommendations.

### Mechanism 2: Joint Retention Gain Optimization
- **Claim:** MRet ranks candidates by the total retention probability gain for both the receiver and the candidate, promoting matches where both sides are likely to stay.
- **Mechanism:** For an arriving user \(x\), the score for candidate \(y\) is  
  \[
  \text{Score}(y) = \frac{1}{A} f(x, m^{1:\tau}(x) + A r(x,y)) + \frac{1}{\alpha_{\max}} \left[ f(y, m^{1:\tau}(y) + \alpha_{\max} r(x,y)) - f(y, m^{1:\tau}(y)) \right],
  \]  
  where \(A = \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k\) and \(\alpha_{\max} = \max_k \alpha_k\). Candidates are sorted by this score.
- **Core assumption:** Retention gains are additive and separable; a match affects only the two users involved.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3, Eq 13]: Exact score definition derived from the objective in Eq 9.
  - [abstract]: "...jointly considering the retention gains of both the user receiving recommendations and those who are being recommended."
  - [corpus]: No direct anchor; neighbors focus on fairness or match maximization.
- **Break condition:** Network effects (e.g., a match changes a user’s future attractiveness or the platform’s ecosystem) violate additivity.

### Mechanism 3: Concavity-Enabled Efficient Ranking
- **Claim:** Under concave retention functions, MRet reduces the NP-hard optimal ranking problem to a per-candidate scoring and sorting problem.
- **Mechanism:** Using Jensen’s inequality (Lemma 1) and a linear bound from concavity (Lemma 2), the original objective is lower-bounded by \(\sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k \text{Score}(\sigma_{\tau,k})\). Sorting by Score maximizes this bound.
- **Core assumption:** \(f(\cdot)\) is concave in \(m\) (diminishing marginal retention gains).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1]: "Under Assumption 1, the receiver-side term in Eq. (9) admits a Jensen-type lower bound." and Lemma proofs.
  - [section 3.1]: "By the rearrangement inequality, assigning higher-score candidates to positions with larger visibility weights \(\alpha_k\) maximizes this lower bound."
  - [corpus]: Concavity is common in engagement models (e.g., Zou et al., KDD 2019) but not explicitly in two-sided fairness—weak anchor.
- **Break condition:** Non-concave retention (e.g., increasing returns at low match counts) invalidates the bound; MRet may still work empirically (as shown in real data) but theoretical guarantee is lost.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Retention Model (f̂) -> Match Probability Matrix (R) -> State Tracker -> Scoring Engine -> Ranker -> Simulation/Online Loop. The Retention Model predicts retention probability; R stores pairwise match probabilities; State Tracker maintains cumulative match counts; Scoring Engine computes Score(y); Ranker sorts candidates; Simulation processes arrivals and updates states.
- **Critical path**:
  1. Collect historical logs: user features, interaction history, churn labels.
  2. Train f̂ (e.g., XGBoost) on \(\{x, m, u\}\) triples.
  3. Build/load match matrix R (from observed matches or ALS imputation).
  4. Initialize state tracker with \(m^{0}(\cdot) = 0\).
  5. For each arriving user \(x\): fetch \(f̂(x)\) and \(m^{1:\tau}(x)\); for each candidate \(y\), fetch \(f̂(y)\) and \(m^{1:\tau}(y)\), compute \(\text{Score}(y)\); return top-K sorted by score; after interaction, update \(m^{1:\tau}(x)\) and \(m^{1:\tau}(y)\) using \(\alpha_k \cdot r(x,y)\).
  6. Periodically re-evaluate churn in simulation or monitor retention online.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Retention model complexity: simple models may underfit; complex (XGBoost, deep nets) risk overfitting on sparse data; start with XGBoost (200 trees, depth 6).
  - Match probability source: raw observed matches yield sparse R; imputation (ALS) introduces bias; MRet is sensitive to errors—consider smoothing or Bayesian methods.
  - Position weights \(\alpha_k\): typically decay (e.g., \(1/k\)); steeper decay intensifies bias; test with uniform (\(\alpha_k=1\)) to check robustness.
  - Concavity enforcement: train \(f̂\) without constraints; if residuals show non-concave patterns, apply post-hoc smoothing or piecewise concave fitting.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Retention model collapse: validation AUC for \(f̂\) < 0.7.
  - Cold-start for rare users: unreliable \(f̂\); need fallback to population average or fairness-based ranking.
  - Popularity drift: R becomes stale; periodic recomputation essential.
  - One-sided arrival bias: extreme \(\rho\) causes less-arriving side’s \(m^{1:\tau}\) to update slowly, leading to stale scores.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Synthetic replication: Reproduce Figure 3 with default params (\(|X|=|Y|=1000, K=5, \kappa=0.5, \rho=0.5, \alpha_k=1/k\)). Verify MRet achieves higher retention than Max Match and FairCo using fewer matches.
  2. Real data baseline: Use provided real-world setup (Section 4.2) with \(f̂\) trained on \(n=5000\) samples. Confirm MRet outperforms baselines in sparse-match regime.
  3. Ablation on concavity: On synthetic data, replace concave \(f\) with non-concave (e.g., U-shaped). Compare MRet performance vs. oracle to quantify sensitivity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can MRet be extended to jointly estimate unknown match probabilities while optimizing retention, instead of assuming the matching matrix is given? (Basis: Paper notes this as an important extension, since preferences are often estimated separately in practice.)
2. Can an online version of MRet that incrementally updates user retention functions based on streaming interactions improve long-term retention compared to the offline approach? (Basis: Paper suggests online adaptation to handle concept drift and maintain model accuracy.)
3. How should MRet weight retention gains across the two user sides when the platform's business model assigns different monetary values to retaining users on each side? (Basis: Current formulation treats both sides equally; platforms may need side-specific multipliers to align with profit.)
4. Does MRet retain its performance guarantees when the user retention function is non-concave, and how might the algorithm be adapted? (Basis: Theoretical derivation depends on concavity; real-data robustness observed but not analyzed for non-concave cases.)

## Limitations
- Synthetic data parameters \(M_a, M_b\) are unreported, preventing exact replication of synthetic experiments.
- Real-world data preprocessing (ALS imputation hyperparameters, k-means clustering configuration, smoothing of per-cluster retention curves) is underspecified.
- Training details for \(f̂\) (e.g., retraining schedule, distribution match between training and deployment) are unclear; FairCo hyperparameter \(\lambda=100\) may be untuned.

- Robustness across conditions: Medium (supported by synthetic ablations; real validation partial)
- Concavity of retention functions: Low-Medium (concave curves fitted offline; assumption may not hold for all segments)
