# Online decoding of rat self-paced locomotion speed from EEG using recurrent neural networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18637
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18637
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Decoded rat locomotion speed from non-invasive EEG using LSTMs, achieving a median correlation of 0.88 and R² of 0.78 across 225 sessions.

## Executive Summary
This study presents an asynchronous brain-computer interface that continuously decodes self-paced treadmill locomotion speed from cortex-wide EEG in head-fixed rats. Using long short-term memory (LSTM) recurrent neural networks trained on over 133 hours of 32-channel recordings, the decoder achieved high accuracy (median Pearson r = 0.88, R² = 0.78), outperforming previous invasive approaches. The decoding relied predominantly on visual cortex electrodes and low-frequency (<8 Hz) oscillations. While models generalized across sessions for the same animal with minimal fine-tuning, they failed to transfer between animals, indicating strong subject-specific neural signatures. Furthermore, cortical EEG encoded both past and future locomotion dynamics up to 1000 ms, with stronger retrospective decoding.

## Method Summary
The task is asynchronous regression: given a sliding window of multi-channel EEG, predict instantaneous treadmill speed. Inputs are 32-channel skull-surface EEG (0.01–45 Hz, downsampled to 100 Hz) and synchronized continuous speed. Each 200 ms window (20 timesteps) is normalized channel-wise using training-set statistics. Models are trained with mean squared error loss, using a sequential 80%/10%/10% train/validation/test split per session. The primary architecture is an LSTM followed by dense layers. Comparisons include linear regression, random forest, feed-forward neural network, LSTM, and encoder-only transformer. Transfer learning evaluates zero-shot and fine-tuned (10% of new data, only final dense layers) performance across sessions and subjects.

## Key Results
*   Decoding accuracy is high (median r = 0.88, R² = 0.78) and surpasses prior invasive studies.
*   Performance is driven almost entirely by visual cortex electrodes and delta/theta (<8 Hz) oscillations.
*   Pre-trained models generalize across sessions for the same animal (zero-shot r = 0.61) but fail across animals (zero-shot r = 0.24); fine-tuning on 10% new data helps only within-subject.
*   Cortical signals encode locomotion history and forecast up to 1000 ms, with retrospective decoding outperforming prospective beyond 300–400 ms.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Visual cortex as primary source
**Claim:** Visual cortex activity is the dominant cortical correlate of self-paced locomotion speed.  
**Mechanism:** During locomotion, the visual cortex exhibits state-dependent modulation (e.g., enhanced visually evoked responses, theta peaks) that covaries with speed. The LSTM learns a mapping from distributed visual cortical patterns—reflecting visuomotor integration and arousal—to instantaneous speed.  
**Core assumption:** Even in head-fixed, non-visually guided spontaneous running, visual cortex carries a speed-correlated signature via internal movement-related signals or reafference.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Decoding was primarily driven by visual cortex electrodes"; [section 3.3] "Using visual cortex electrodes alone performed almost as well as using all 32 electrodes".  
**Break condition:** Silencing or lesioning visual cortex should collapse decoder performance to baseline (e.g., linear regression levels), independent of motor cortex signals.

### Mechanism 2: Low-frequency oscillations as carrier
**Claim:** Delta and theta (<8 Hz) EEG oscillations contain the majority of locomotion-relevant information.  
**Mechanism:** Locomotion upregulates delta/theta power across cortex (especially visual), creating a broadband spectral signature that scales with speed. The LSTM exploits the amplitude and phase relationships of these slow rhythms to estimate speed.  
**Core assumption:** Slow cortical oscillations index locomotor drive or arousal state, which covaries with self-selected speed.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "low-frequency (<8 Hz) oscillations"; [section 3.4] "delta and theta bands produced similar decoding results... to those of a model with access to the entire spectrum"; [section 2.5.3] "power increases with speed, with the largest differences at low frequencies".  
**Break condition:** Filtering EEG to exclude frequencies below 8 Hz should cause a drastic (>50%) drop in decoding correlation.

### Mechanism 3: Subject-specific stable signatures
**Claim:** Locomotion-related EEG signatures are idiosyncratic to each animal but stable across sessions, enabling within-subject transfer learning.  
**Mechanism:** Each rat develops unique cortical representations of its locomotion dynamics (e.g., EEG-speed coupling patterns). These patterns are conserved over time (same electrodes, skull), allowing a model pre-trained on one session to initialize weights for another session of the same animal. Fine-tuning then adapts to session-specific drifts.  
**Core assumption:** Inter-subject neural variability exceeds intra-subject session-to-session variability for this task.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "pre-trained models generalized across sessions for the same animal... but failed to transfer across animals"; [section 3.2] "cross-session zero-shot decoding (r=0.61)... cross-subject... performed worst (r=0.24)"; [section 2.5.2] Fine-tuning cross-session models improved vs. training from scratch on 10% data; cross-subject fine-tuning did not.  
**Break condition:** If a rat undergoes significant pharmacological manipulation (e.g., sedatives) or brain injury between sessions, cross-session transfer performance should drop to near cross-subject levels.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Sliding-window time series segmentation**  
  **Why needed here:** Continuous EEG must be framed into fixed-length input tensors (200 ms windows, 10 ms stride) for the RNN. This converts a high-rate stream (100 Hz) into discrete sequences preserving temporal context while enabling batch training.  
  **Quick check question:** How would increasing the window size from 200 ms to 500 ms likely affect decoding latency and performance, based on the paper's rationale for choosing 200 ms?

- **Concept: Channel-wise z-score normalization**  
  **Why needed here:** EEG amplitudes vary across electrodes and sessions due to impedance differences, skull conductivity, etc. Normalizing each channel using training-set statistics (mean, std) ensures stable gradients and prevents high-amplitude channels from dominating the loss.  
  **Quick check question:** If you computed normalization statistics (mean/std) across the entire session instead of just the training split, what type of data leakage would occur and how would it inflate test performance?

- **Concept: Transfer learning via partial fine-tuning**  
  **Why needed here:** Subject-specific patterns require calibration, but collecting 80% of a new session's data is impractical. Pre-training on a source session provides locomotion-relevant feature extractors; fine-tuning only the final dense layers on 10% of the target session adapts the readout to the new subject's neural signature.  
  **Quick check question:** Why does fine-tuning a cross-subject pre-trained model yield minimal benefit over training from scratch on 10% data, according to the paper's interpretation?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input (200 ms × 32 channels) -> LSTM layer(s) -> Dense layer(s) -> Scalar speed output
- **Critical path:**
  1. Acquire synchronized 32-channel EEG (0.01–45 Hz, 100 Hz) and treadmill speed.
  2. For each 200 ms window (20 timesteps), extract a [20, 32] matrix.
  3. Apply channel-wise z-score using training-set parameters.
  4. Feed sequence through LSTM to obtain final hidden state.
  5. Pass hidden state through dense layers to predict speed.
  6. Repeat every 10 ms (stride) for continuous output.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Window size: Larger windows capture longer context but increase latency and memory; 200 ms was sufficient.
  - Recurrence vs. attention: LSTM slightly outperformed transformer; bidirectional attention violates causality for real-time use.
  - Bandwidth: Full-band (0.01–45 Hz) vs. band-limited: Delta/theta alone nearly matched full-band, suggesting computational savings.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Non-zero baseline during immobility: Decoder outputs small positive speeds during true rest.
  - Future prediction decay: Prospective (±1000 ms) correlation decays faster than retrospective.
  - Cross-subject failure: Zero-shot performance (r=0.24) is worse than trivial mean predictor (R² negative).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Benchmark model selection: Train and compare Linear Regression, Random Forest, Feed-Forward NN, LSTM, Transformer on single sessions; confirm LSTM median r=0.88, R²=0.78.
  2. Transfer learning evaluation: Apply pre-trained models from other sessions of same rat and from other rats; test zero-shot and with 10% fine-tuning; validate that cross-session fine-tuning helps but cross-subject does not.
  3. Spatial/spectral ablation: Retrain LSTM using only single brain region electrodes or isolated frequency bands; quantify performance drop vs. full-band cortex-wide input; confirm visual cortex dominance and delta/theta sufficiency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How can the offline decoder be engineered into a safe real-time BCI for locomotion control?  
  **Basis in paper:** The Discussion identifies needed refinements: speed thresholding to eliminate immobility outputs, output smoothing, and latency compensation via future prediction.  
  **Why unresolved:** Current decoder produces non-zero speeds during rest and jagged trajectories, posing safety risks for prosthetic or exoskeleton use.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and test these modifications in closed-loop experiments with a robotic gait-assist device or virtual environment, quantifying accuracy, latency, stability, and safety metrics.

- **Question 2:** What causes the failure of zero-shot cross-subject transfer, and can it be mitigated?  
  **Basis in paper:** Zero-shot cross-subject performance was near chance (median r=0.24, R²=-0.39) while cross-session within the same animal remained moderate (r=0.61); fine-tuning gave only marginal improvements.  
  **Why unresolved:** Subject-specific neural signatures hinder rapid BCI deployment across animals; understanding their origin could enable generalized decoders.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform representational similarity analysis to compare EEG patterns across rats; test domain adaptation or meta-learning methods; systematically vary electrode montages to identify robust features.

- **Question 3:** Why does cortical EEG provide more accurate reconstruction of past than future locomotion speeds?  
  **Basis in paper:** EEG-based past predictions remained strong up to 1000 ms (r~0.59) but future predictions decayed faster (r~0.34), unlike symmetric autocorrelation baselines.  
  **Why unresolved:** Asymmetry suggests cortical dynamics encode a rich history but weakly represent prospective states; underlying neural processes (e.g., forward models, efference copy) are unknown.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Record during tasks that dissociate movement planning from execution; perturb motor areas during planning to test causal contributions; compare with motor cortex and cerebellum.

- **Question 4:** Does visual cortex encode self-paced locomotion speed via visual feedback or intrinsic motor signals?  
  **Basis in paper:** Models using only visual cortex electrodes achieved r=0.85; authors argued against pure stimulus-locking but did not rule out visual feedback.  
  **Why unresolved:** Clarifying whether visual areas are multisensory integration hubs or merely reflect visual input would reshape understanding of distributed locomotion control and BCI electrode selection.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Test decoding in complete darkness or with manipulated optic flow independent of treadmill speed; optogenetically silence visual cortex to assess causal impact.

## Limitations
*   Exact LSTM hyperparameters and transfer learning protocol are unreported, blocking precise reproduction without the code repository.
*   Causal mechanisms (e.g., visual cortex driving speed) are inferred from correlations, not manipulation.
*   Generalizability beyond laboratory conditions (e.g., freely moving rats) is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Decoding accuracy (r=0.88, R²=0.78):** High (empirically measured, large dataset).
- **Visual cortex/delta-theta dominance:** High (robust spatial/spectral ablations).
- **Subject-specific signatures & transfer limits:** High (clear cross-session vs. cross-subject results).
- **Mechanistic interpretations (e.g., visual cortex as primary source):** Medium (correlational; break conditions untested).

## Next Checks
1. **Causal test of visual cortex:** Pharmacologically or optogenetically silence visual cortex during locomotion; decoder performance should collapse to baseline (linear model levels).
2. **Spectral manipulation:** Band-pass filter EEG to exclude frequencies below 8 Hz; replication should show >50% drop in correlation, confirming delta/theta necessity.
3. **Cross-session stability breach:** Introduce a sedative or mild brain injury between sessions for the same rat; cross-session fine-tuned transfer should degrade to cross-subject levels (r≈0.24).
