# CPGPrompt: Translating Clinical Guidelines into LLM-Executable Decision Support

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03475
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03475
- **Reference Count**: 25
- **Primary Result**: CPGPrompt achieved perfect binary recall (1.00 ± 0.00) and multi‑class F1 scores ranging from 0.47 (headache) to 0.77 (prostate cancer) on synthetic patient vignettes across three clinical domains.

## Executive Summary
CPGPrompt introduces a framework for converting narrative clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) into structured, LLM‑executable decision trees. The system automatically parses guidelines into JSON trees and then uses an LLM to traverse these trees by answering yes/no queries about patient cases. Evaluated on headache, lower back pain, and prostate cancer guidelines with synthetic vignettes, CPGPrompt achieved near‑perfect recall for referral decisions and strong but variable performance on fine‑grained pathway assignment. The work demonstrates the feasibility of leveraging LLMs to operationalize clinical guidelines, while highlighting challenges in negation, temporal reasoning, and subjective assessments.

## Method Summary
The CPGPrompt pipeline comprises two main stages:  
1. **Guideline‑to‑tree conversion**: An LLM agent parses the source CPG (text or diagram) into a JSON decision tree where each node represents a feature check (simple or multi‑criteria) with defined yes/no branches. The tree is manually validated and corrected for logical fidelity.  
2. **Execution engine**: Starting at the root, the engine dynamically prompts the LLM with binary queries derived from each node, records the responses, and branches accordingly until a terminal action (e.g., referral pathway) is reached.  
The system was evaluated on synthetic patient vignettes spanning four categories (single‑criteria, multi‑criteria, contrastive, exclusion) across three medical domains. Five independent runs with GPT‑4o (temperature=0.2) produced metrics for binary referral classification and multi‑class pathway assignment, alongside traversal efficiency analysis.

## Key Results
- Binary specialty‑referral classification: Recall = 1.00 ± 0.00, F1 = 0.85–1.00.
- Multi‑class pathway assignment F1 scores: Headache 0.47, Lower back pain 0.72, Prostate cancer 0.77.
- Traversal analysis revealed a conservative bias (earlier termination than optimal), particularly in exclusion‑criteria scenarios.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Structured Tree Representation
Claim: Translating narrative CPGs into a structured JSON decision tree yields an auditable, LLM‑executable representation of guideline logic.  
Mechanism: An LLM parses guideline text or diagrams into a tree where each node is a simple or multi‑criteria feature check with a `yes_action` and a `no_node` reference. Segmentation and merging handle lengthy guidelines; manual validation ensures fidelity.  
Core assumption: The guideline’s diagnostic and referral logic can be decomposed into binary or thresholded queries that map directly to patient findings.  
Evidence: Abstract and Section 2.2 describe the automated generation of JSON trees.  
Break condition: Ambiguous language, non‑hierarchical logic, or complex temporal relations may require substantial manual correction.

### Mechanism 2: LLM‑Driven Tree Traversal
Claim: Traversing the guidance tree with a dynamically prompted LLM reproduces guideline‑adherent decisions while maintaining full transparency via step‑by‑step logs.  
Mechanism: The execution engine starts at the root node. For each node, it constructs a yes/no query (e.g., “Does the patient have a thunderclap headache?”). The LLM’s response selects the branch; all queries and answers are recorded, enabling audit and error analysis.  
Core assumption: The LLM can reliably interpret unstructured patient vignettes and answer the structured feature questions.  
Evidence: Abstract and Section 2.3 detail the dynamic prompting and logging.  
Break condition: Misinterpretation of negations, temporal phrases, or multi‑criteria thresholds leads to incorrect branching.

### Mechanism 3: Conservative Bias for Safety
Claim: A safety‑oriented conservative bias, inherent in the tree’s urgency ordering and early termination on positive findings, ensures near‑perfect recall for referral decisions.  
Mechanism: Red‑flag criteria are placed near the root; a single positive answer immediately triggers a referral and halts traversal. The system prioritizes sensitivity, minimizing missed urgent cases.  
Core assumption: In a triage context, a false negative is more harmful than a false positive.  
Evidence: Section 3.2 reports perfect recall and Section 3.4 shows earlier termination than optimal paths.  
Break condition: In resource‑constrained settings, excessive over‑referral may outweigh safety benefits.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Guideline Analyzer → Tree Builder → Execution Engine ↔ LLM Interface; Audit Logger attached to Execution Engine.

### Critical path
1. Load CPG → Guideline Analyzer → raw JSON subtrees.  
2. Merge and validate → final guidance tree.  
3. For each patient vignette: initialize current node = root; loop: construct query based on node type, call LLM, record result; if “yes” execute yes_action (terminate) else move to no_node.  
4. Output final action; persist logs.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Automation vs accuracy**: Automatic tree extraction risks errors; manual verification ensures correctness but reduces scalability.  
- **Safety vs efficiency**: Conservative early referral boosts recall but may increase false positives and system load.  
- **LLM variability**: Even with low temperature, semantic nuances cause inconsistent answers.  
- **Granularity**: Finer‑grained trees improve interpretability but increase traversal steps and cost.

### Failure signatures
- Traversal length much shorter than optimal → over‑conservative behavior.  
- Binary recall < 1.0 → missed positive criteria, often due to negation handling.  
- Low multi‑class F1 → misassignment of pathways, possibly from incorrect priority ordering or multi‑criteria mis‑count.  
- High variance across runs → LLM instability.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Smoke test**: Run an obviously positive vignette (e.g., thunderclap headache) and verify expected emergency referral and log path.  
2. **Full benchmark**: Process all synthetic vignettes for one domain, compute binary recall and multi‑class macro F1, compare to paper’s reported ranges.  
3. **Category‑specific error analysis**: Pick a poorly performing category (e.g., exclusion‑criteria for headache), inspect logs to identify whether the LLM misclassified negated symptoms or halted prematurely.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can CPGPrompt be improved to accurately handle negated clinical features in narrative guidelines and patient vignettes, given persistent challenges in the headache domain's exclusion‑criteria cases?
- **Basis**: The paper explicitly notes negation handling as a key difficulty for headache referrals.
- **Why unresolved**: Conservative referral behavior in presence of negations leads to high over‑referral, reducing precision. This stems from LLM difficulty interpreting negated subjective symptoms.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Evaluate negation‑specific interventions (prompt engineering with explicit negation cues or fine‑tuning on clinical negation datasets). Measure changes in F1‑score for exclusion‑criteria vignettes while monitoring recall.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What architectural enhancements are necessary for CPGPrompt's execution engine to robustly process temporal reasoning requirements in clinical guidelines, as seen in the lower back pain domain's timeline‑based staging?
- **Basis**: The paper highlights timeline‑based clinical cues as a primary difficulty for lower back pain.
- **Why unresolved**: The LLM struggles to parse and combine temporal phrases into correct decision branches, causing misclassification.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Integrate a temporal information extraction module that normalizes time expressions into structured timestamps/durations. Test on vignettes with varied temporal markers, comparing accuracy against baseline and analyzing errors in time‑based decision nodes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How should CPGPrompt manage uncertainty and subjective clinical assessments that require nuanced human judgment, given the system's consistent challenges with such cases across domains?
- **Basis**: The paper states that cases requiring clinical judgment or subtle cues were consistently challenging, suggesting need for hybrid human‑AI workflows.
- **Why unresolved**: Rule‑based traversal cannot fully capture ambiguous or subtle clinical cues, leading to reduced performance and potential over‑reliance on conservative referral.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Explore uncertainty quantification techniques (e.g., LLM confidence scores) and develop deferral mechanisms to human clinicians when uncertainty exceeds a threshold. Conduct simulation studies with clinicians to evaluate acceptable workflows and measure outcomes like referral accuracy and trust.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: To what extent does CPGPrompt's performance generalize to real‑world clinical notes containing noise, omissions, and informal documentation, given the evaluation relied solely on synthetic vignettes?
- **Basis**: The paper explicitly notes this as a key limitation: real clinical notes were not used due to privacy and security risks.
- **Why unresolved**: Synthetic vignettes are curated and may lack the linguistic variability, missing data, and shorthand typical of real EHRs; robustness to such factors remains unproven.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Obtain de‑identified real clinical notes under data use agreements and run CPGPrompt on them. Compare performance metrics with synthetic results, and perform error analysis to identify real‑world challenges.

## Limitations
- Exact LLM model/prompt for tree construction and manual validation criteria are unspecified, hindering faithful replication.
- Domain‑specific failures (negation, temporal reasoning, test reliability) may limit generalizability to real‑world clinical notes.
- Synthetic patient vignettes may not capture the full complexity and noise of actual clinical narratives.

## Confidence
- **Structured tree representation (Mechanism 1)**: Medium
- **LLM‑driven traversal (Mechanism 2)**: Medium
- **Conservative bias for safety (Mechanism 3)**: High
