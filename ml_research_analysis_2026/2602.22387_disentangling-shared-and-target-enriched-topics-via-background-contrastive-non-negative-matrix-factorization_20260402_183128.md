# Disentangling Shared and Target-Enriched Topics via Background-Contrastive Non-negative Matrix Factorization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.22387
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22387
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: bcNMF dramatically improves recovery of condition-specific signals over standard NMF across diverse biological and image datasets, with ARI increases from ~0.03 to ~0.86 in challenging cases.

## Executive Summary
Standard dimensionality reduction methods like PCA and NMF often fail to isolate condition-specific signals because they are overwhelmed by dominant shared variation. This paper introduces background-contrastive NMF (bcNMF), a method that jointly factorizes a target dataset and a matched background dataset using a shared non-negative basis. A contrastive objective minimizes reconstruction error for the target while penalizing reconstruction of the background, effectively suppressing topics that are prominent in the background and highlighting those enriched in the target. The approach uses efficient multiplicative updates, supports minibatch training for scalability, and demonstrates substantial improvements in recovering known biological and image conditions across multiple benchmarks.

## Method Summary
bcNMF factorizes a target matrix `X` and a background matrix `Y` (samples × features) into a shared basis `W` (features × K) and dataset-specific coefficients `H_X` and `H_Y`. The optimization minimizes a contrastive objective: `J = L(X, W H_X) - α * L(Y, W H_Y)`, where `L` is a negative log-likelihood (Gaussian, Poisson, NB, or ZINB) and `α ≥ 0` controls the strength of background suppression. Multiplicative updates, derived from a majorization-minimization scheme, ensure non-negativity of all factors and monotonic decrease of `J`. The algorithm supports minibatch computation for large datasets, with per-batch updates for `H_X`/`H_Y` and epoch-level accumulation for `W`. The number of topics `K` and contrast weight `α` are selected heuristically (e.g., via line search on stability or reconstruction contrast).

## Key Results
- On binary MNIST (digits 0 vs. 1) with ImageNet backgrounds, bcNMF achieved an ARI of 0.8628 vs. 0.0298 for standard NMF.
- On ten-digit MNIST, bcNMF yielded an ARI of 0.3699 vs. 0.0201 for NMF.
- For mouse Down syndrome protein data (using control mice as background), ARI was 0.789 vs. 0.108 for NMF, recovering known markers like SOD1 and PSD95.
- In leukemia scRNA‑seq (pre‑ vs. post‑transplant, with healthy bone marrow as background), ARI reached 0.591 vs. 0.336 for NMF.
- For TP53 drug response in cancer cell lines, ARI was 0.621 vs. 0.058.
- In MDD brain transcriptomics, bcNMF achieved an ARI of 0.1306 vs. 0.0363, identifying immune‑inflammatory and extracellular matrix pathways.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: bcNMF isolates target-specific latent topics by jointly factorizing target and background datasets with a shared non‑negative basis and a contrastive objective that penalizes background reconstruction.
- **Mechanism**: The method minimizes a composite loss: reconstruction error for the target minus a weighted term α times the reconstruction loss for the background. This encourages basis vectors to explain variation present in the target but not in the background, effectively suppressing shared/confounding topics.
- **Core assumption**: Target and background data can be expressed using a common set of non‑negative basis vectors; unwanted variation dominates the background so that subtracting its reconstruction removes it from the target representation.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: “jointly factorize a target dataset and a matched background using shared non‑negative bases under a contrastive objective that suppresses background‑expressed structure.” Section 2.1: “minimizing a contrastive objective: min_{W,H_X,H_Y≥0} L(X,WH_X)−α L(Y,WH_Y)”.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Multiplicative updates derived from a majorization‑minimization scheme guarantee non‑negativity of factors and monotonic decrease of the contrastive objective, leading to stable convergence.
- **Mechanism**: For each block variable (H_X, H_Y, W) the gradient is decomposed into positive and negative parts. Updates use element‑wise division of the negative part by the positive part scaled by the current value (θ ← θ ∘ ∇^−θ J / ∇^+θ J). This preserves non‑negativity and, under standard regularity conditions, ensures the objective does not increase.
- **Core assumption**: The chosen loss yields a convex subproblem in each block; the multiplicative updates are derived from a valid majorization function for that loss.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 4.1: “We optimize this objective using multiplicative updates derived from a general principle: θ←θ◦ ∇_θ^− J / ∇_θ^+ J ... These updates ensure non‑negativity by construction and promote convergence under mild conditions.” The extension of classical NMF convergence theory (Lee & Seung) supports the update rules.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The contrastive weighting parameter α tunes the trade‑off between faithful target reconstruction and background suppression; a suitable α yields target‑enriched topics.
- **Mechanism**: α scales the background loss term. Larger α increases penalty for explaining background, forcing basis to avoid topics prominent in the background. Smaller α prioritizes target fit, risking insufficient removal of confounding variation. Tuning is done via intrinsic criteria (e.g., stability of reconstruction contrast across α) or downstream validation.
- **Core assumption**: There exists an intermediate range of α where the contrastive objective effectively disentangles shared from target‑specific variation.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 4.2: “We employ a coarse‑to‑fine line search ... small to moderate values of α (typically 0.5–5) yield stable factorizations that suppress shared variation while preserving target‑enriched topics.” Section 2.2 notes α was selected to minimize reconstruction error in binary MNIST.

  - *Why needed here*: The algorithm optimizes W, H_X, H_Y sequentially via multiplicative updates derived from majorization‑minimization, ensuring stability.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input target X and background Y (matched features) -> Shared basis W (features × K) -> Target coefficients H_X (K × N_X) and background coefficients H_Y (K × N_Y). Optimization loop: Update H_X, H_Y per batch/minibatch; accumulate W gradients across all batches; update W at epoch end.
- **Critical path**:
  1. Preprocess X and Y identically (normalization, HVG selection).
  2. Choose K, α, and loss type; initialize W, H_X, H_Y with small positive values.
  3. For each epoch: shuffle minibatches; update H_X and H_Y in each batch using multiplicative rules; accumulate W numerator/denominator terms across all batches; update W via element‑wise ratio.
  4. Monitor contrastive objective J until convergence (e.g., relative change < 0.01%).
  5. Extract W (topics) and H_X (sample embeddings); downstream analysis (clustering, gene set enrichment).
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Shared basis vs. separate bases*: Shared basis enforces explicit contrast but may discard target‑private structure; separate bases increase parameters and require additional regularizers.
  - *Likelihood choice*: Squared error is simple but may not fit sparse counts; Poisson/NB/ZINB add realism at the cost of extra dispersion parameters.
  - *α selection*: Intrinsic criteria avoid downstream tuning but may be less robust; grid search with stability checks is safer but more manual.
  - *Minibatch vs full batch*: Minibatch reduces memory but introduces stochasticity; requires careful gradient accumulation and possibly more epochs.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - *Objective divergence or many zero factors*: α too large or poor initialization; reduce α or reinitialize with smaller random values.
  - *Low ARI/poor separation*: α too low or background does not capture relevant confounding; test a range of α values; verify background suitability via variance overlap or cPCA.
  - *Minibatch instability*: Incorrect gradient accumulation (e.g., averaging vs. summing); ensure W update uses summed numerators/denominators across all batches before division.
  - *Overfitting to noise*: K too large; use model selection (silhouette, downstream validation) to choose K.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Binary MNIST vs. ImageNet backgrounds: Demonstrates dramatic ARI improvement (0.8628 vs. 0.0298).
  2. Mouse Down syndrome protein data: Using control mice as background, recovers DS‑associated markers (ARI 0.789 vs. 0.108).
  3. Leukemia transplant scRNA‑seq: With healthy bone marrow as background, identifies treatment‑specific programs (ARI 0.591 vs. 0.336).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the contrastive parameter α be selected automatically and data‑drivenly, rather than by manual tuning or cross‑validation? The authors state “principled and fully data‑driven strategies for adaptive selection of α remain an important area for future work.”
2. Can the shared‑basis assumption be relaxed to allow separate basis matrices for target and background while still encouraging contrast? They propose “relaxing the shared‑basis assumption and learning separate basis matrices for the target and background datasets (W_target and W_background), coupled through alignment or regularization constraints.”
3. How can bcNMF be extended to integrate multi‑modal data (e.g., scRNA‑seq and scATAC‑seq) in a contrastive framework? They note “One direction is the integration of multi‑modal or multi‑view data, where modalities such as scRNA‑seq and scATAC‑seq ... are analyzed jointly within a shared contrastive framework.”
4. What are the theoretical properties (e.g., identifiability, convergence) of non‑negative contrastive factorization? They observe “While bcNMF does not directly inherit the linear subspace guarantees of these methods, these theoretical developments provide a principled lens ... motivate further theoretical analysis.”
5. Can bcNMF be adapted to online or streaming data scenarios where data arrive sequentially? They mention “bcNMF can in principle be adapted to online or streaming settings in which data are acquired sequentially and factorization must be updated incrementally.”

## Limitations
- Background dataset selection is not formally guided; an inappropriate background may cause poor separation or over‑suppression of target signal.
- The contrastive weight α requires an underspecified coarse‑to‑fine line search, leading to potential irreproducibility across runs.
- Minibatch implementation details (order of updates, gradient accumulation normalization) are not fully described, risking variability.
- The shared basis assumption may not hold in all biological scenarios, possibly discarding target‑specific variation.
- No principled method is provided for choosing the number of topics K.
- Baselines are limited to standard NMF; comparisons with other contrastive methods are missing.

## Confidence
- **Condition‑specific recovery**: High – Strong empirical evidence from six diverse benchmarks shows large ARI improvements.
- **Contrastive mechanism**: Medium – Mechanistically sound and supported by experiments, but depends on proper α and background; lacks theoretical guarantees.
- **Biological relevance**: Medium – Enriched pathways align with known biology but lack independent cohort validation.
- **Scalability and efficiency**: High – Multiplicative updates are well‑established; minibatch extension is standard and likely correct.
- **General applicability**: Medium‑High – Tested across data types (images, counts, continuous) but only in the presented domains.
