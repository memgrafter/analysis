# Humanizing AI Grading: Student-Centered Insights on Fairness, Trust, Consistency and Transparency

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.07754
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07754
*   **Reference Count:** 29
*   **Primary Result:** Students perceived AI feedback as clear and consistent but less fair, trustworthy, and nuanced than human TA feedback, recommending a supplementary model with human oversight.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates student perceptions of AI-generated grading feedback in an undergraduate block-based programming course. Comparing feedback from ChatGPT Team and human teaching assistants, students rated AI feedback highly for clarity, transparency, and consistency but preferred human feedback for fairness and trust. Qualitative insights revealed that while AI provided structured, rubric-aligned comments, it lacked contextual understanding, personalization, and empathy. The core finding is that AI is viewed as a transparent but less trusted tool, best deployed as a supplementary system under human supervision to combine efficiency with nuanced judgment.

## Method Summary
The research employed a comparative case study design with 27 students. Anonymized final project submissions were graded using two methods: the course’s standard human TA process and an AI engine (ChatGPT Team) prompted with the course description and rubric. Students then received both feedback versions and completed a survey. The survey used 5-point Likert scales to measure perceived fairness, transparency/clarity, consistency, and trust, alongside open-ended questions. Quantitative data were analyzed via means and percentage comparisons, while qualitative responses underwent inductive thematic analysis to explore nuances behind the scores.

## Key Results
*   Students rated AI feedback favorably on fairness (4.2), clarity/transparency (4.12), consistency (4.08), and trust (4.03) on a 5-point scale.
*   In direct comparisons, a majority perceived TA feedback as fairer (60%) and more trustworthy (55%), while most found AI feedback more transparent (57%) and noted consistency between the two (63%).
*   Thematic analysis identified key student concerns: AI’s lack of contextual understanding, absence of personalization/empathy, and preference for human judgment despite the AI’s structural clarity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** AI grading systems achieve high perceived transparency and clarity through structured, rubric-aligned feedback.
*   **Mechanism:** The AI processes the rubric and student code to generate point-by-point feedback organized by assessment criteria.
*   **Core assumption:** Students equate "transparency" with explicit, organized feedback rather than insight into the AI's reasoning process.
*   **Break condition:** When evaluation requires interpreting beyond explicit criteria (e.g., creative problem-solving), the structured output fails to address nuance, reducing perceived transparency.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** AI achieves perceived consistency by eliminating human rater variability but may sacrifice contextual accuracy.
*   **Mechanism:** The AI applies uniform rubric logic to all submissions, avoiding human inconsistency.
*   **Core assumption:** Students interpret "consistency" as surface-level agreement with human graders, not deep conceptual alignment.
*   **Break condition:** For tasks requiring inference of implementation strategy, effort, or iterative learning, AI consistency breaks down against human judgment.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Student trust in AI feedback is contingent on perceived alignment with human judgment and empathy, not just accuracy.
*   **Mechanism:** Trust requires a perception of benevolent, context-aware evaluation, which humans signal via personalized comments. AI's lack of this socio-emotional cue erodes trust.
*   **Core assumption:** Trust in assessment is both cognitive and socio-emotional.
*   **Break condition:** When AI feedback is accurate and clear but omits encouragement, acknowledges struggle, or references progress, trust remains low compared to human feedback.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Ethical Principles Framework (Jobin, 2019)**
    *   **Why needed:** The study analyzes four core principles (fairness, trust, transparency, consistency). This framework contextualizes why these specific constructs are measured and how they interrelate in AI ethics.
    *   **Quick check:** How might "non-maleficence" (avoiding harm) manifest in an AI grading system beyond the four studied principles?
*   **Concept: Thematic Analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006)**
    *   **Why needed:** The qualitative insights (themes like "lack of contextual understanding") are the primary evidence for nuanced student perspectives. Understanding inductive theme derivation is key to evaluating claims about nuance and empathy.
    *   **Quick check:** If a student comment says "The AI was clear but felt robotic," what latent theme might this code to, and what does it reveal about the transparency-trust link?
*   **Concept: Block-Based Programming (e.g., Scratch, Blockly)**
    *   **Why needed:** The domain matters. Block-based code has visual, spatial properties that text-based AI might poorly capture. The "key elements" AI misses are domain-specific.
    *   **Quick check:** Why might an AI trained on text-based code struggle to assess the "clarity" of a block-based program's visual layout?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Submission Store -> (AI Grading Engine & TA Pipeline) -> Feedback Comparator/Delivery System -> Survey & Analytics Platform.
*   **Critical path:** Student project submission is stored and processed in parallel by the AI Engine (ChatGPT Team with rubric) and the Human TA Pipeline. The Comparator bundles the AI feedback with a survey link for delivery. Student survey responses are aggregated and analyzed in the Analytics Platform.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Efficiency vs. Nuance:** AI provides scalable, uniform feedback but cannot replicate human contextual reasoning, leading to a supplementary design.
    *   **Control vs. Automation:** Using a licensed ChatGPT Team version offers data control but limits fine-tuning to course-specific rubrics.
    *   **Measurement Depth:** Single-item Likert measures sacrifice psychometric depth for brevity and response rate.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Quantitative: Mean scores for "fairness" or "trust" drop below 3.5.
    *   Qualitative: Emergent theme "AI feedback is misleading" appears in >20% of responses.
    *   Systemic: Significant grade discrepancy between AI and TAs (>15% of submissions) without corresponding low clarity scores.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Rubric Alignment Stress Test:** Run 50 past submissions through AI and TAs; calculate Cohen's Kappa per criterion to identify "context-sensitive" zones where AI should defer.
    2.  **Feedback Tone A/B Test:** Randomly assign students to receive AI feedback with (A) neutral/structured or (B) empathetic/encouraging language; measure differential impact on trust and preference scores.
    3.  **Longitudinal Trust Study:** Track trust scores for the same students across two projects using AI feedback; assess if trust increases with exposure or if frustration persists.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1:** How generalizable are these findings to other undergraduate courses and diverse student populations? The study was confined to a single course at one institution (n=27). Resolution requires replication across multiple courses, institutions, and larger, more demographically varied samples.
*   **Question 2:** How can AI grading systems be designed to incorporate human-like empathy, contextual understanding, and personalization? Current AI was perceived as clear but lacking nuance and personal touch. Resolution requires developing and user-testing models that explicitly consider student context and adopt individualized, encouraging tones.
*   **Question 3:** What alignment techniques can reduce discrepancies between AI-generated grades and human evaluative standards, particularly for subjective criteria? The study noted mismatches where AI missed key elements or applied different thresholds. Resolution requires comparative analysis to identify problematic criteria and iterative refinement of AI prompts or fine-tuning.
*   **Question 4:** How does the integration of human oversight with AI grading affect student trust, perceived fairness, and acceptance? The study compared pure AI and pure human feedback but did not evaluate hybrid models. Resolution requires experimental studies comparing conditions (AI-only, human-only, human-AI collaboration).

## Limitations
*   **Sample & Generalizability:** Small, single-course sample (n=27) from a specific domain (block-based CS) limits statistical power and broad applicability.
*   **Instrument Specificity:** Exact survey items and open-ended prompts are unreported, preventing precise replication and raising construct validity concerns.
*   **AI Configuration Opacity:** Specific prompts, system instructions, and few-shot examples given to ChatGPT Team are unknown, making it difficult to isolate drivers of observed outcomes.

## Confidence
*   **High Confidence:** Students perceive AI feedback as **transparent/clear** and **consistent** in a surface-level, rubric-aligned sense. Supported by high quantitative means (4.12, 4.08) and aligned qualitative themes.
*   **Medium Confidence:** Students **prefer human feedback for fairness and trust** (60%, 55% direct comparison). Supported by survey percentages and themes on "human judgment/empathy," but limited by sample size and single-item measures.
*   **Medium Confidence:** The recommended **"supplementary under human oversight"** model. This is an interpretive claim logically extending from mixed results, but not directly tested as a system configuration.

## Next Checks
1.  **Rubric-Alignment Replication with Gold-Standard Audit:** Re-run the study using the exact rubric and prompt engineering on a new, larger sample (n>100). Have experts blind-evaluate AI vs. TA feedback for rubric adherence to quantify the "contextual understanding" gap.
2.  **Prompt Engineering Ablation Study:** Systematically vary the AI's system prompt (e.g., adding "explain reasoning," "adopt an encouraging tone") and measure differential impact on the four perceptual constructs to identify levers for acceptance.
3.  **Longitudinal Exposure & Calibration Study:** Survey the same cohort across multiple grading cycles (3+ projects) to track changes in trust and fairness perceptions. Determine if exposure calibrates expectations or intensifies "lack of empathy" frustration.
