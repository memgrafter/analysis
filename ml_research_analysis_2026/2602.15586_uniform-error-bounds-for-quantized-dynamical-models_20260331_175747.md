# Uniform error bounds for quantized dynamical models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15586
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15586
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Provides uniform high-probability error bounds for quantized dynamical models learned from β‑mixing dependent data, introducing fast‑rate variance‑adaptive bounds that scale with total bits Bp and achieve O(1/µ') convergence under small empirical risk.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of deriving statistical error guarantees for dynamical models learned from temporally dependent data, while explicitly accounting for model quantization (bit precision B) and imperfect optimization. The authors develop two families of uniform error bounds: (i) slow‑rate bounds based on block decomposition that handle β‑mixing dependence, and (ii) fast‑rate, variance‑adaptive bounds via a novel spaced‑point strategy. Both bounds incorporate the model’s bit complexity Bp, linking hardware constraints to statistical guarantees. Theoretical results are validated on an AR(1) process (n = 200,000) and a switched linear system (n = 80,000), where the fast‑rate bound consistently outperforms prior art, reducing confidence intervals by over 60% in the hybrid system.

## Method Summary
The core methodology revolves around uniform convergence under β‑mixing assumptions. For slow rates, the time series is partitioned into blocks of size a; only odd‑indexed blocks are retained to form an effectively independent subsample of size µ ≈ n/(2a). Hoeffding’s inequality and a union bound over the finite class of B‑bit quantized models (size ≤ 2^{Bp}) yield a bound with a mixing penalty term β(a). For fast rates, a subsample of µ' ≈ n/a spaced points (positions 1, 1+a, 1+2a, …) is extracted. Bernstein’s inequality is applied to an i.i.d. copy of this subsample; the variance term is coupled to the true risk via the envelope bound σ²_f ≤ M·L(f), producing a variance‑adaptive bound that scales as √(empirical risk) and converges as O(1/µ') when empirical error is small. Both approaches require the spacing a to be chosen such that the mixing penalty does not exceed the confidence budget δ.

## Key Results
- Two bound families: slow‑rate (O(1/√µ)) and fast‑rate (variance‑adaptive O(1/µ') under small empirical risk).
- Fast‑rate bound (Theorem 5) yields substantially tighter intervals in experiments; e.g., confidence interval drops from 27.8 to 10.4 for a switched linear system.
- Bounds scale explicitly with total bits Bp, quantifying the statistical cost of hardware quantization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Block Decomposition for Dependent Data
The sequence is divided into µ blocks of size a; two subsequences (odd/even blocks) are formed. Successive blocks in a subsequence are 2a apart, reducing dependence. Lemma 6 bounds the expectation difference between the dependent and independent block sequences by (µ−1)β(a). Hoeffding’s inequality is applied to the independent version, and a union bound over 2^{Bp} models yields a slow‑rate bound with mixing penalty. **Core assumptions:** β‑mixing with coefficient β(a), block size a satisfying δ′ = δ − 2(µ−1)β(a) > 0, and bounded loss. **Break condition:** if 2(µ−1)β(a) ≥ δ, the bound becomes vacuous.

### Mechanism 2: Spaced‑Point Fast Rates
A subsample of µ' spaced points (positions 1, 1+a, 1+2a, …) is selected. Lemma 7 bounds the expectation difference between this dependent subsample and an i.i.d. copy by (µ'−1)β(a). Bernstein’s inequality for the i.i.d. copy gives a tail involving variance. Using the envelope bound σ²_f ≤ M·L(f) couples variance to the true risk. Solving the quadratic inequality yields a fast‑rate bound of the form L(f) ≤ ˆL_spaced + √(2M(A)/µ')√(ˆL_spaced) + 4M(A)/µ', where A = Bp ln 2 + ln(1/δ''). **Core assumptions:** β‑mixing, δ'' = δ − (µ'−1)β(a) > 0, bounded loss, envelope variance bound. **Break condition:** δ'' ≤ 0, or if empirical risk is large the fast‑rate advantage diminishes.

### Mechanism 3: Quantization‑to‑Bits Complexity
The model class is defined as all functions with parameters quantized to B bits (Hypothesis 2). This makes the class finite with cardinality ≤ 2^{Bp}. A union bound over all such models introduces a term √(Bp ln 2 / n) in the independent case and appears inside the logarithm for dependent cases. **Core assumption:** the hypothesis class exactly equals the set of B‑bit quantized parameters. **Break condition:** if the effective dimension is much smaller than Bp, the union bound may be loose.

## Foundational Learning
- **β‑mixing coefficients**: Measure temporal dependence decay in stationary processes. *Why needed*: Quantify how “independent” spaced or blocked samples are, enabling concentration under dependence. *Quick check*: Given β(k) ≤ Cρᵏ, what does β(a) represent and how does it enter the bound?
- **Uniform convergence**: Bounds must hold simultaneously for all f in the class. *Why needed*: The learning algorithm may not find the global optimum, so worst‑case over F is required. *Quick check*: Why can’t we apply concentration to a fixed f and then take supremum after seeing data?
- **Hoeffding’s inequality**: Provides exponential tail for bounded independent variables. *Why needed*: Foundation for slow‑rate bounds where variance adaptation is not exploited. *Quick check*: If ℓ∈[0,M], what’s the Hoeffding bound for deviation > ε with n samples?
- **Bernstein’s inequality**: Incorporates variance, giving faster rates when variance is small. *Why needed*: Enables variance‑adaptive fast rates in the spaced‑point strategy. *Quick check*: How does Bernstein differ from Hoeffding and when is it tighter?
- **Envelope variance bound**: For X∈[0,M], Var(X) ≤ M·E[X]. *Why needed*: To replace unknown population variance σ²_f in Bernstein’s inequality with a term involving true risk L(f). *Quick check*: Prove Var(X) ≤ M·E[X] for X∈[0,M].

## Architecture Onboarding
*Component map*:  
Complexity calculator → Mixing estimator → Spacing optimizer → Risk evaluator → Bound assembler → Validation routine

*Critical path*:
1. Quantify model: determine p, set B (hardware) → compute Bp.
2. Characterize mixing: for system, compute β(a) bound (e.g., β(a)≤Cρᵃ).
3. Select strategy: need fast rates? → spaced‑point; otherwise block.
4. Optimize spacing: start with smallest a satisfying δ′>0 (or δ′′>0) per Remark 4.
5. Compute empirical risks: ˆLn for Theorem 4; ˆL_spaced for Theorem 5.
6. Assemble bound: include A = Bp ln 2 + ln(1/δ′) term.
7. Validate: run experiment, check if actual risk ≤ bound.

*Design tradeoffs*:
- **Spacing a**: Smaller a → larger µ′/µ but larger β(a). Optimal a balances 1/√µ (or 1/µ) vs β(a). Rule: start at minimal a s.t. δ′>0.
- **Confidence δ**: Lower δ increases log term but may force larger a. Typical δ=0.05.
- **B vs Bp**: Higher B reduces quantization error but increases bound factor. Balance with n.
- **Empirical risk estimator**: Spaced‑points use fewer points (µ′ vs n) but should be similar if mixing slowly; verify by comparing ˆLn vs ˆL_spaced.

*Failure signatures*:
- **δ′ ≤ 0 or δ′′ ≤ 0**: Mixing too strong/a too small → increase a until positive.
- **Bound >> empirical risk**: Possibly overestimated Bp, poor β(a) bound, or suboptimal a.
- **Spaced empirical risk differs greatly from full**: Suggests a too large → reduce a.
- **Bound increases with n**: Wrong a scaling; a should grow slowly (logarithmically) with n.

*First 3 experiments*:
1. **AR(1) verification**: θ=0.5, n=200k, B=32, p=1. Sweep a from minimal feasible to large. Plot both bounds vs a. Confirm fast‑rate bound lower and both decrease as a→optimal.
2. **Switched system replication**: C=3, na=nb=2, n=80k. Compute bounds (Theorems 4/5) and compare to Massucci bound. Expect fast‑rate total ~10.4 vs 27.8.
3. **Spacing sensitivity**: For fixed n and β estimate, compute bound as function of a. Should have clear minimum; verify optimal a gives δ′′>0 with margin. Test robustness if β(a) underestimated.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can the error bounds be extended to non‑stationary stochastic processes?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The conclusion states “Future work may explore extensions to non‑stationary processes…”.  
   *Why unresolved*: The current analysis relies on stationarity for mixing coefficients and risk definitions.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Generalize mixing coefficients to time‑varying versions and re‑derive the block/spaced‑point bounds; validate on non‑stationary benchmarks.

2. **What is a data‑driven method for selecting the spacing parameter ‘a’?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The paper notes the trade‑off and suggests choosing the smallest ‘a’ satisfying δ′′ > 0, but this is heuristic.  
   *Why unresolved*: Optimal ‘a’ depends on unknown mixing properties; no theoretical guidance for adaptive selection.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: An algorithm that estimates mixing rate from data to optimize ‘a’ for bound tightness, with theoretical regret analysis.

3. **How can β‑mixing coefficients be practically estimated for complex dynamical systems?**  
   *Basis in paper*: Experiments state that estimating β‑mixing is complex and out of scope; they use McDonald et al. (2011) for a simple AR(1).  
   *Why unresolved*: Existing methods may not scale to high‑dimensional or nonlinear systems.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Development of scalable β‑mixing estimators leveraging system structure or data‑driven proxies, validated across diverse models.

4. **Can the bounded output assumption (Hypothesis 1) be relaxed?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The paper mentions possible adaptation to subgaussian/subexponential settings without details.  
   *Why unresolved*: Many systems (e.g., unstable linear) have unbounded states; current bounds require bounded outputs.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Derive analogous bounds using subgaussian concentration, account for unbounded losses; test on unstable AR models or heavy‑tailed noise.

## Limitations
- **β‑mixing estimation uncertainty**: The method (McDonald et al., 2011) is not detailed; inaccurate estimates can invalidate δ′/δ′′ conditions.
- **Heuristic algorithm unspecified**: The training procedure for the switched system is only described as “heuristic”, hindering reproducibility of Table 2.
- **Union bound looseness**: The complexity term Bp assumes the quantized class is exactly all 2^{Bp} encodings; effective complexity may be smaller, leading to pessimistic bounds.
- **Fast‑rate dependence on small empirical risk**: The variance‑adaptive advantage requires ˆL_spaced to be small; for larger errors the bound may be loose.
- **Clipping may disrupt stationarity**: Bounding outputs to [−r, r] could violate stationarity assumptions underlying β‑mixing, but this effect is not analyzed.

## Confidence

- **Practical tightness of bounds (spacing choice, mixing penalty)**: Medium
- **Empirical superiority of fast‑rate bound**: Medium (one experiment lacks algorithmic details)
