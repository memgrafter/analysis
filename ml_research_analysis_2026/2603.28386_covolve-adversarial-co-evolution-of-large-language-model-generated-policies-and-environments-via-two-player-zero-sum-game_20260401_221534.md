# COvolve: Adversarial Co-Evolution of Large-Language-Model-Generated Policies and Environments via Two-Player Zero-Sum Game

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.28386
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28386
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: COvolve uses an LLM‑driven co‑evolutionary framework where environment and policy designs are adversarial; the mixed‑strategy Nash equilibrium meta‑policy achieves robust performance across co‑evolved and unseen benchmarks (e.g., 1.00 vs 0.62 on CARLA Town02).

## Executive Summary
COvolve tackles the limitation of static training environments in reinforcement learning by introducing a co‑evolutionary framework that jointly generates tasks and solutions using large language models. The interaction is cast as a two‑player zero‑sum game: the environment designer proposes a level that minimizes the expected return of the current mixed‑strategy Nash equilibrium (MSNE) policy mixture, while the policy designer produces an improving policy for that level. The MSNE meta‑policy, computed over the empirical payoff matrix, maximizes worst‑case performance across the archive of generated environments, preventing catastrophic forgetting.

Experiments across MiniGrid mazes, PyGame navigation, and CARLA driving show that the environments grow in complexity and the MSNE meta‑policy consistently outperforms greedy and uniform baselines on both the archive and standardized benchmarks. The approach remains effective even with weaker LLMs, demonstrating the promise of LLM‑driven adversarial curriculum generation.

## Method Summary
COvolve iteratively updates two populations: environments (Python code) and policies (function code). Each iteration: (1) Compute the MSNE distribution p* over the current policy set using the payoff matrix M (policy returns on each environment) via a linear program. (2) The environment designer uses an LLM to mutate the latest environment and selects K candidates; it evaluates each under p* (weighted sum of returns) and chooses the one with minimal expected return as the new environment. (3) The policy designer mutates the highest‑weighted policy under p* for the new environment, evaluates K candidates on that environment, and selects the best performer. (4) Update M by evaluating the new policy on all environments and all policies on the new environment. The loop repeats, building an archive of paired tasks and solutions. The MSNE meta‑policy is robust to forgetting because it optimizes the minimum expected return across all archived environments.

## Key Results
- The MSNE meta‑policy maintains high performance on the full environment archive as it grows, avoiding the catastrophic forgetting observed in greedy baselines.
- On standardized unseen benchmarks, the final MSNE policy achieves perfect success (1.00) on CARLA Town02 and MiniGrid‑MultiRoom‑N6, while baselines score 0.62 and 0.86 respectively.
- Ablations confirm that the curriculum and game‑theoretic equilibrium are essential; weaker LLMs (GPT‑4.1) still benefit from the MSNE mixture, though overall performance degrades gracefully.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Adversarial co‑evolution via a two‑player zero‑sum game automatically generates a curriculum of increasingly complex environments.
- **Mechanism**: The environment designer generates a new environment that minimizes the expected return of the current policy mixture, while the policy designer generates a best‑response policy. This arms race drives difficulty upward.
- **Core assumption**: The environment designer's best response yields meaningful challenges and remains solvable (enforced by feasibility helpers).
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "We model the interaction ... as a two‑player zero‑sum game, ensuring adversarial co‑evolution ..."; [section 4.2] "The Environment Designer generates a new level as a best response to the current MSNE policy. Its goal is to minimize the expected return of the mixture policy, thereby revealing its weaknesses."; similar to MAESTRO [1] but with LLM code generation.
- **Break condition**: If the environment designer repeatedly fails to generate solvable levels (high failure_feedback) or generates trivial levels, curriculum progression stalls.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Computing a mixed‑strategy Nash equilibrium (MSNE) over the policy population prevents catastrophic forgetting by maximizing worst‑case performance across all seen environments.
- **Mechanism**: The payoff matrix M records returns. The MSNE distribution p* solves max_p min_j Σ_i p_i M_{ij}. Sampling from p* ensures the agent performs well on the weakest environment in the archive.
- **Core assumption**: The empirical payoff matrix accurately reflects policy performance and the environment designer will continue to propose worst‑case environments for the current mixture.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "To guarantee robustness and prevent forgetting ... we compute the mixed‑strategy Nash equilibrium (MSNE) of the zero‑sum game, thereby yielding a meta‑policy."; [section 4.1] Equation (1) defines p* as the solution to the max‑min problem.; [section 5.2] "UED‑Greedy policies ... exhibit reduced performance on earlier environments. In contrast, the MSNE mixture maintains performance across the full environment set as it grows over iterations."
- **Break condition**: Computational cost solves an LP that grows with matrix size; if the environment designer's best response is constrained, the equilibrium may not be truly minimax.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Using LLMs to generate executable Python code for environments and policies enables open‑ended, modular, and interpretable task and solution design beyond fixed parametric families.
- **Mechanism**: The LLM is prompted with domain‑specific constraints and helper functions; it mutates previous code structures to produce new environment classes or policy functions. Generated code is validated and executed.
- **Core assumption**: LLMs can produce valid, progressively more complex code when guided by the minimax objective and feasibility constraints.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "We address this with COvolve ... that leverages large language models (LLMs) to generate both environments and agent policies, expressed as executable Python code."; [section 1] "Such programmatic representations provide advantages over neural encodings, including improved generalizability ... greater modularity ... stronger verifiability and interpretability."; [section 4] Algorithm 1 and Appendix C prompts detail the mutation process.
- **Break condition**: LLM generates syntactically invalid or non‑executable code; or generates environments that do not increase complexity, leading to curriculum stagnation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Unsupervised Environment Design (UED)**
  - **Why needed**: COvolve operates within the UED paradigm, automatically generating tasks rather than relying on handcrafted or randomly sampled ones.
  - **Quick check**: What key limitation of domain randomization does UED address? (Answer: Lack of adaptivity and tendency to produce trivial or unsolvable tasks.)

- **Mixed‑Strategy Nash Equilibrium (MSNE)**
  - **Why needed**: The MSNE is the core mechanism that combats catastrophic forgetting by optimizing the worst‑case performance across the environment archive.
  - **Quick check**: In COvolve, what objective does the MSNE distribution p* optimize? (Answer: It maximizes the minimum expected return across all environments in the archive.)

- **Programmatic (Code‑Based) Policy Representations**
  - **Why needed**: Representing policies as executable code influences generation, evaluation, and interpretation, and is central to COvolve's LLM‑driven approach.
  - **Quick check**: Name two advantages of code‑based policies over neural network policies as stated in the paper. (Answer: Improved generalizability to unseen scenarios; greater modularity and reuse; stronger verifiability and interpretability.)

## Architecture Onboarding
The high‑level component map: **LLM Environment Designer** → new environment → **Execution & Evaluation Engine** → returns → **Payoff Matrix M** → **MSNE Solver** → distribution p* → next iteration. Concurrently, **LLM Policy Designer** mutates highest‑weighted policy → new policy → evaluated on all environments → updates M. The archive of environments L and policies P grows each iteration.

### Critical Path (per iteration)
1. Compute p* = argmax_p min_j Σ_i p_i M_{ij} (MSNE).
2. Environment designer generates K candidates from previous environment θ_{t-1}; evaluates each under p* (weighted sum of policy performances); selects worst as θ_t.
3. Policy designer generates K candidates from the policy with highest p* weight; evaluates each on θ_t; selects best as π_t.
4. Evaluate π_t on all environments in L and θ_t on all policies in P to extend M.
5. Append π_t to P and θ_t to L.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **LLM mutation vs. parameterized mutation**: LLMs allow expressive structural changes but risk invalid code; mitigated by feasibility helpers and retries.
- **Full MSNE recomputation vs. incremental**: Full recomputation is robust but O(n³); incremental could be faster but may accumulate error.
- **Code execution safety**: Running arbitrary code is dangerous; sandboxing is needed, trading safety for flexibility.

### Failure Signatures
- High environment failure rate: self.failure_feedback set frequently, retries exhausted → curriculum stall.
- Degenerate MSNE: p* has near‑zero weight on all but one policy → indicates lack of diversity or environment designer not exploiting weaknesses.
- Policy best‑response fails: New policy π_t does not significantly outperform previous ones on the new environment → environment too hard or policy designer ineffective.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Co‑evolution dynamics**: Compare MSNE, UED‑Greedy, and UED‑Uniform on archive performance to demonstrate that MSNE prevents forgetting as the environment set grows.
2. **Generalization**: Evaluate the final MSNE meta‑policy on unseen standardized benchmarks (MiniGrid‑MultiRoom‑N6‑v0, CARLA Town02) to assess robustness.
3. **Ablation**: Run zero‑shot evaluation on the hardest environment and compare performance with a weaker LLM (GPT‑4.1 vs GPT‑5.2) to show that the curriculum and MSNE benefits persist under weaker generators.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: *How can the environment designer's mutation process be formally guided to guarantee curriculum progression without relying on domain‑specific feasibility heuristics?*  
  *Basis*: The paper notes that unconstrained LLM‑generated environments can be infeasible; they use predefined helpers to filter unsolvable levels.  
  *Why unresolved*: This lacks theoretical guarantees; the curriculum may stagnate if the mutation space is not controlled.  
  *Resolution evidence*: Integrating a regret‑based adversary and showing monotonic difficulty increase and improved learning outcomes would provide a formal solution.

- **Open Question 2**: *To what extent do the benefits of the MSNE meta‑policy depend on the diversity and quality of the policy population generated by the LLM, and how does this scale with model capability?*  
  *Basis*: The paper only ablates with one weaker model (GPT‑4.1). The interaction between LLM capability, policy diversity, and MSNE effectiveness is not systematically studied.  
  *Why unresolved*: The relationship could be non‑linear; with very weak models the policy set may be too homogeneous for MSNE to add value.  
  *Resolution evidence*: A systematic study across multiple LLM families/sizes measuring diversity metrics (e.g., payoff entropy, behavioral clustering) and final MSNE performance would quantify the conditions for benefit.

- **Open Question 3**: *Does the adversarial co‑evolution lead to open‑ended generation of environments, or does it converge to a local optimum, and what mechanisms could sustain indefinite novelty?*  
  *Basis*: Experiments run only 10 iterations; long‑term dynamics are unknown. The myopic minimax feedback may cause stagnation.  
  *Why unresolved*: No metrics for sustained novelty or long‑term complexity growth are reported.  
  *Resolution evidence*: Running COvolve for many more iterations (e.g., 100+) and tracking continuous measures of environment diversity/ complexity over time would reveal whether the process plateaus or remains open‑ended.

## Limitations
- **Prompt and model opacity**: The exact prompt templates and LLM generation settings are unreported, hindering precise replication and potentially masking tuning.
- **Domain‑specific heuristics**: The curriculum’s validity depends on ad‑hoc feasibility helpers (e.g., `check_solvable`); more principled control of difficulty is needed for broader applicability.
- **Scalability and generalization**: The cubic cost of solving the full MSNE linear program limits archive size, and evaluation is confined to three domains, raising questions about scalability and transfer.

## Confidence
- **Adversarial co‑evolution produces a meaningful curriculum**: Medium
- **MSNE prevents catastrophic forgetting**: Medium‑High
- **LLM‑driven code generation enables open‑ended task design**: Low‑Medium
