# Assessing the quality and coherence of word embeddings after SCM-based intersectional bias mitigation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.04393
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04393
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: SCM-based intersectional debiasing preserves local semantic coherence (ECT ~0.998-0.999) while balancing analogy performance; Partial Projection best for coherence, Linear/Subtraction improve analogies at modest stability cost.

## Executive Summary
This paper extends Stereotype Content Model (SCM)-guided bias mitigation from single‑group to intersectional settings in static word embeddings. Compound representations for gender‑race pairs are created via summation or concatenation and debiased using Subtraction, Linear Projection, or Partial Projection. Evaluation with the Embedding Coherence Test (ECT) and Embedding Quality Test (EQT) across Word2Vec, GloVe, and ConceptNet Numberbatch shows that Partial Projection most strongly preserves local neighborhoods (ECT ≈ 0.998–0.999), while Linear Projection and Subtraction can boost analogy completion accuracy, revealing a trade‑off between coherence and utility. The optimal aggregation method (summation vs. concatenation) is found to be model‑dependent, demonstrating that intersectional SCM debiasing is practical for static embeddings with clear guidance for balancing competing objectives.

## Method Summary
The pipeline starts with pretrained static embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe, ConceptNet Numberbatch) fine‑tuned on the WikiText‑103 corpus. For each gender‑race identity pair, a compound vector is constructed either by summation of the individual embeddings or by concatenation, thereby creating a joint representation. SCM bias directions for warmth and competence are derived by computing difference vectors between curated antonym pairs and extracting the first principal component for each dimension. Each compound embedding is then debiased using one of three operators: Subtraction (removing the bias direction), Linear Projection (orthogonal projection to discard bias), or Partial Projection (a tunable attenuation that retains some bias‑direction component). Finally, the debiased embeddings are evaluated intrinsically with ECT (Spearman rank correlation of cosine similarities to attribute words) and EQT (accuracy on analogy completion), measuring local neighborhood preservation and global relational quality respectively.

## Key Results
- ECT scores remain extremely high (≈ 0.998–0.999) after debiasing, indicating robust preservation of local semantic neighborhoods.
- EQT performance varies by model and debiasing method; Linear Projection and Subtraction often yield higher analogy accuracy than Partial Projection, confirming a coherence–analogy trade‑off.
- Partial Projection achieves the highest ECT, while the optimal aggregation (summation vs. concatenation) is model‑dependent, with no universal winner.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Subtraction**
- **Claim:** Subtraction attenuates intersectional SCM bias by removing a fixed bias direction from all compound vectors, offering a computationally simple baseline.
- **Mechanism:** For a compound embedding `w`, the debiased vector is `w' = w - v_B`, where `v_B` is the bias direction (first principal component of warmth or competence difference vectors). This directly nullifies the component along the target SCM axis.
- **Core assumption:** The primary bias signal for a given SCM axis is captured by a single linear direction `v_B`; subtracting it removes the dominant stereotype projection without requiring a mean center.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper defines the operation in Section 4.4 and notes that Subtraction remains competitive (abstract).
- **Break condition:** If bias is distributed across multiple non‑collinear directions or is nonlinear, a single subtraction will incompletely remove bias and may distort semantics.

**Mechanism 2: Linear Projection**
- **Claim:** Linear Projection more precisely targets the bias subspace by projecting embeddings orthogonal to `v_B`, preserving components unrelated to the SCM axis.
- **Mechanism:** For each `w`, compute `w' = w - proj_vB(w)`, where `proj_vB(w)` is the orthogonal projection of `w` onto `v_B`. This ensures `w'` has zero dot product with `v_B`, removing bias along that axis while retaining other geometric relationships.
- **Core assumption:** The bias is primarily encoded in the projection onto `v_B`; orthogonal components are bias‑free and semantically meaningful.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper describes this as “more assertive” (abstract) and shows in Ablation A2 (Table 8) that Linear Projection often yields higher EQT than Partial Projection for Word2Vec and GloVe.
- **Break condition:** If the bias subspace is not truly one‑dimensional or useful semantic variance also lies along `v_B`, orthogonal projection may discard meaningful signal, harming analogy performance.

**Mechanism 3: Partial Projection**
- **Claim:** Partial Projection provides a tunable trade‑off by projecting embeddings only partially orthogonal to `v_B`, aiming to balance bias removal with semantic retention more conservatively.
- **Mechanism:** `w' = μ + r(w) + β * f(||r(w)||) * v_B`, where `r(w) = w - proj_vB(w)` is the orthogonal component, `μ` is the mean of bias subspace vectors, `β` controls attenuation strength, and `f` is a smoothing function. This allows `w'` to retain some bias‑direction component (`β < 1`) while reducing it.
- **Core assumption:** Complete orthogonal projection is too aggressive; a partial projection better preserves neighborhood coherence (ECT) by limiting distortion.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper reports Partial Projection “most strongly preserves neighborhood coherence” (abstract) and achieves the highest ECT scores across models (Table 8).
- **Break condition:** For applications demanding maximal bias removal, Partial Projection’s conservative attenuation may be insufficient.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Static Word Embeddings**
- **Why needed here:** The study operates on pre‑trained, non‑contextual vectors; understanding their linear geometry (e.g., `king - man + woman ≈ queen`) is essential to grasp how vector‑space operations affect semantics.
- **Quick check question:** Why might the arithmetic `king - man + woman ≈ queen` hold in static embeddings but not in contextual models?

**Concept: Stereotype Content Model (SCM)**
- **Why needed here:** SCM provides two interpretable bias axes (warmth, competence) from social psychology; the method constructs `v_B` from curated antonym pairs along these axes, enabling social‑group‑agnostic mitigation.
- **Quick check question:** How does using principal component analysis on multiple antonym pair differences help create a robust bias direction compared to using a single pair?

**Concept: Embedding Coherence Test (ECT) and Embedding Quality Test (EQT)**
- **Why needed here:** These are the primary intrinsic metrics. ECT measures rank stability of similarities to warmth/competence terms (local neighborhood preservation); EQT measures analogy completion accuracy (global relational preservation). Understanding their trade‑off is central to interpreting results.
- **Quick check question:** Why might a debiasing method improve EQT (analogies) while lowering ECT (neighborhood coherence), or vice versa?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Prep → Compound Construction → Bias Subspace Estimation → Debiasing Application → Evaluation

**Critical path:** The bias subspace must be estimated before debiasing; compound vectors are the debiasing targets.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Summation vs. concatenation: Summation preserves original dimensionality but may lose information; concatenation preserves all signals but doubles dimensionality, requiring careful handling of the bias direction and evaluation.
- Debiasing method: Partial Projection maximizes ECT (stability); Linear Projection/Subtraction can boost EQT (analogies) but risk neighborhood distortion. Choice depends on application priority.
- Model dependence: Aggregation method optimality varies by embedding family; no universal winner.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low ECT (<0.95) after debiasing indicates neighborhood collapse; likely due to over‑aggressive projection or poor bias direction estimation.
- EQT near zero suggests analogy logic broken; possibly caused by improper handling of concatenated vectors (dimensionality mismatch) or incorrect projection.
- Model‑specific EQT collapse (e.g., Word2Vec) may reflect fragility of locally trained embeddings to vector‑space operations.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replicate single‑group baseline: Use only gender or only race terms (no compounds) and verify that ECT/EQT match prior work (Table 10). Failure indicates implementation drift before intersectional extension.
2. Test concatenation dimensionality handling: For GloVe, compare (a) projecting each 300‑d half of the concatenated vector separately onto the 300‑d bias direction and recombining, vs. (b) duplicating the bias direction for the 600‑d space and projecting the full vector. Large discrepancies in ECT/EQT would confirm ambiguity.
3. Parameter sweep for Partial Projection: On Word2Vec with summation compound vectors, vary β ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 0.9} and test linear vs. sigmoid smoothing functions. Verify that as β→0 the results approach Subtraction and as β→1 they approach Linear Projection; then choose β that maximizes ECT while keeping EQT >0.01.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** What evaluation metrics should be adopted as a standard for assessing intersectional bias mitigation in static word embeddings?
- *Basis:* The authors note the lack of a commonly agreed metric at the time of writing.
- *Why unresolved:* Without a standard, comparisons across studies are difficult, and it is unclear which metrics best capture the multifaceted nature of intersectional bias.
- *Resolving evidence:* A community‑wide benchmark suite including both bias‑specific and utility metrics, validated through correlation with human judgments and downstream fairness outcomes.

**Open Question 2:** How does SCM‑based intersectional debiasing influence performance on downstream NLP tasks?
- *Basis:* The discussion explicitly mentions that downstream task impact was not measured.
- *Why unresolved:* ECT and EQT measure intrinsic properties but do not guarantee fairer or more accurate predictions in real applications.
- *Resolving evidence:* Systematic experiments using debiased embeddings in tasks like sentiment analysis, coreference resolution, or hate speech detection, evaluating both accuracy and fairness metrics for intersectional groups.

**Open Question 3:** Can we automatically generate semantically meaningful contrastive word pairs for compound intersectional identities to improve debiasing?
- *Basis:* Section 4.2 explains that constructing contrastive pairs for compound embeddings is challenging; random pairing was used, which does not ensure semantic relations.
- *Why unresolved:* Random pairing may introduce noise and fail to capture intended contrasts, potentially weakening debiasing.
- *Resolving evidence:* Develop an automated method using lexical resources or embedding clustering to create contrastive pairs, and demonstrate improved bias reduction compared to random pairing.

**Open Question 4:** Are the warmth and competence dimensions of SCM universally valid for intersectional bias mitigation across different cultures?
- *Basis:* The discussion notes that SCM axes reflect broad social judgments and may vary by culture.
- *Why unresolved:* The debiasing relies on SCM axes derived from Western‑centric antonym pairs; cultural variations could limit effectiveness.
- *Resolving evidence:* Cross‑cultural studies deriving SCM axes from culturally specific resources and applying debiasing to embeddings trained on corpora from those cultures.

## Limitations
- **Missing implementation specifics:** Exact identity term lists, attribute word pairs, Partial Projection parameters (β, f), and unambiguous handling of concatenation’s dimensionality are not provided, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Model‑dependent optimality:** The best aggregation (sum vs. concat) and debiasing method differ across embedding families, limiting generalizability to new models or domains.
- **Ecological validity:** The study uses static embeddings, which are increasingly supplanted by contextual models; it remains unclear whether SCM‑based intersectional mitigation transfers to modern architectures.
