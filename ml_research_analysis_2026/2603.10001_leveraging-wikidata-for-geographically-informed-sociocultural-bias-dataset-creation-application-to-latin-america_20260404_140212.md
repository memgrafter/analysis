# Leveraging Wikidata for Geographically Informed Sociocultural Bias Dataset Creation: Application to Latin America

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.10001
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10001
*   **Reference Count:** 33
*   **Primary Result:** Created LatamQA, a multilingual benchmark revealing systematic performance gaps: all evaluated LLMs score higher in their native language (Spanish/Portuguese) than in English, and higher on Iberian Spanish content than Latin American Spanish, with significant variation across Latin American countries.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of resources to measure sociocultural bias in Large Language Models (LLMs) for Latin America by introducing LatamQA, a benchmark of over 26,000 multiple-choice questions. The dataset is constructed by leveraging the structured ontology of Wikidata to scrape culturally-relevant Wikipedia articles, which are then filtered and transformed into questions using expert sociocultural definitions and LLM-based generation. Evaluation across multiple models shows consistent performance asymmetries: models perform better in their native language and on Iberian (Spanish) culture compared to Latin American cultures, with performance also varying significantly between different Latin American nations.

## Method Summary
The methodology involves a multi-stage pipeline. First, Wikipedia's category graph is recursively scraped from seed "Culture of [Country]" categories, with top-level subcategories manually validated by a sociologist to ensure cultural relevance. Next, a fine-tuned Longformer classifier, trained on 500 expert-tagged articles, filters the scraped corpus. An LLM then generates multiple-choice questions from filtered articles using a prompt based on a sociologist-selected "general cultural exploration" definition, followed by a second LLM generating distractors. The final dataset spans Spanish, Portuguese, and English (via translation) and covers 18 Latin American countries plus Iberian Spain, categorized by a sociocultural elements taxonomy.

## Key Results
*   All models evaluated performed better when queried in their native language (Spanish or Portuguese) compared to English.
*   All models demonstrated superior performance on Iberian Spanish cultural content relative to Latin American Spanish content.
*   Model performance varied significantly across questions pertaining to different Latin American countries.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Wikidata's category ontology, combined with recursive scraping and expert curation, enables scalable extraction of culturally-grounded Wikipedia articles for a specific region.
*   **Mechanism:** The system starts from a seed "Cultura de [Country]" category, recursively traverses subcategories to a depth (`MAX_DEPTH`), and collects articles. A sociologist manually validates top-level subcategories to remove non-cultural ones (e.g., general language categories). This filters the raw article pool to those more likely to contain region-specific sociocultural content.
*   **Core assumption:** Wikipedia's category structure, when pruned by domain experts, reliably separates broadly encyclopedic content from locally-relevant cultural content.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "We propose to leverage the content of Wikipedia, the structure of the Wikidata knowledge graph, and expert knowledge from social science..."
    *   [section 2.1] "We start from a mother category... and recursively collect the links... A manual validation of the main subcategories from a sociologist helps removing the categories that are not relevant..."
*   **Break condition:** If the initial category ontology is poorly structured for a target culture (e.g., missing key subcategories) or if expert validation is not performed, the scraped articles will have low cultural pertinence, introducing noise at the dataset's foundation.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Fine-tuning a classifier on expert-annotated articles and using LLM prompts based on a sociologist-selected cultural definition transforms raw articles into high-quality, culturally-relevant multiple-choice questions (MCQs).
*   **Mechanism:** 1) A sociologist annotates 500 articles as positive/descriptive/negative based on a cultural elements taxonomy. 2) This data fine-tunes a Longformer classifier to filter the remaining articles. 3) An LLM generates Q/A pairs from filtered articles using a prompt based on the "general cultural exploration" definition, which was manually selected as most pertinent. 4) A second LLM generates distractors.
*   **Core assumption:** A sociologist's judgment on a small sample can generalize to filter a large corpus, and an LLM can reliably extract and formulate sociocultural knowledge from a filtered article without hallucination.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "...expert knowledge from social science... transformed into multiple-choice questions..."
    *   [section 2.1] "500 articles were manually tagged and used to fine-tune a pre-trained multilingual Longformer... reaching a precision of 87.5% with respect to the positive class."
    *   [section 2.2] "To select the definition leading to the most pertinent questions, they were manually validated by a sociologist... The general cultural exploration approach was judged the most relevant."
    *   [section E] "We validated quantitatively the answers’ grounding to the article on 100 examples, and found no case of hallucination."
*   **Break condition:** If the fine-tuned classifier's precision drops on new data (evidenced by low validation scores on a held-out set), irrelevant articles will proceed to generation. If the chosen cultural definition is too narrow or broad, generated Q/As will miss key cultural elements or become too generic.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Evaluating multilingual LLMs on this geographically and linguistically segmented benchmark reveals systematic performance asymmetries that operationalize epistemic injustice in model knowledge.
*   **Mechanism:** Models are prompted in Spanish/Portuguese (native) and English (translated) on subsets for specific Latam countries and for Iberian Spain. Performance differences are measured along three axes: 1) Native vs. English language, 2) Latam Spanish vs. Iberian Spanish, 3) Between different Latam countries.
*   **Core assumption:** Performance on these MCQs is a valid, operationalizable proxy for the depth and accuracy of a model's sociocultural knowledge about a region, and differences are attributable to training data representation biases rather than model architecture quirks.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "We use this MCQ to quantify the degree of knowledge of various LLMs and find out (i) a discrepancy in performances between the Latam countries... (ii) that the models perform better in their original language, and (iii) that Iberian Spanish culture is better known than Latam one."
    *   [section 3] "All the models are performing better in their native language (ES or PT)... All evaluated LLMs perform better in Iberian Spanish than in Latin American Spanish."
*   **Break condition:** If the MCQ format introduces artifacts (e.g., distractor quality varies by language, questions are translation-biased), measured performance gaps may not reflect true knowledge asymmetry but test-taking competency. A break condition would be if performance inversions are observed when controlling for question difficulty and distractor plausibility across language subsets.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** **Wikidata Category Ontology as a Cultural Map**
    *   **Why needed here:** Understanding that Wikipedia categories are a pre-existing, community-curated hierarchical structure is fundamental. The paper's entire data collection relies on navigating this graph to find culturally-specific article clusters. Without this, the scalable "geographically informed" approach fails.
    *   **Quick check question:** What is a key limitation of starting a "Culture of X" scrape from a general Wikipedia category like "Culture of Mexico" instead of a more specific, expert-validated subcategory?
*   **Concept:** **Operationalizing "Cultural Elements" via Taxonomy**
    *   **Why needed here:** The Espindola & Vasconcellos (2006) taxonomy (e.g., TOPO, ANTHR, FOOD) is the bridge between abstract social science concepts and computable article tags. It defines what "cultural" means for this engineering task and drives both filtering and analysis (Figure 4).
    *   **Quick check question:** If an article is tagged as "FOOD" but describes a globally ubiquitous dish with no regional specificity, how would the paper's methodology likely classify it after the expert-driven filtering stage?
*   **Concept:** **MCQ as a Knowledge Probe (vs. Preference/Attitude Probe)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper explicitly aims to measure *knowledge* ("degree of knowledge"), not bias in the sense of stereotypical associations. This design choice (factual, article-grounded QA) is critical for interpreting results as gaps in memorized/synthesized information rather than toxic output generation.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why would a dataset like BLEnD (Myung et al., 2024), which may probe social norms, yield different performance patterns than LatamQA, which probes factual knowledge from encyclopedic sources?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Scraper -> Expert Filter (Human-in-the-loop) -> Article Classifier -> QA Generator -> Distractor Generator -> Validator -> Evaluator
*   **Critical path:** Scrape Seed Category -> Expert Subcategory Validation -> Recursive Scrape -> Classifier Filter -> QA/Distractor Generation -> Human Validation (sample) -> Evaluation Pipeline
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Scale vs. Cultural Specificity:** Deeper `MAX_DEPTH` increases article count but decreases cultural relevance. Fixed at 5 after empirical testing (Section 2.1).
    *   **Automation vs. Expert Labor:** Heavy reliance on LLM generation for scale, but critical validation steps (category curation, 500-tag training set, prompt definition selection) require scarce expert time.
    *   **Multilinguality:** Creating parallel Spanish/Portuguese/English sets via translation (from native language source) risks losing cultural nuance, but is necessary for the language-performance analysis.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **Data Collection:** Low overlap between scraped articles and expert-defined cultural elements; poor classifier precision on validation set (<80%).
    *   **Generation:** High rate of "hallucinated" answers (answers not grounded in article) in validation sample; Q/As that score low (<3) on sociocultural dimensions.
    *   **Evaluation:** Performance differences vanish when controlling for question difficulty metrics (e.g., length, entity rarity), suggesting test artifacts rather than true knowledge gaps.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Classifier Sanity Check:** Manually label a held-out set of 200 articles. Compute precision/recall for the "positive" class. Target: >85% precision as achieved in training.
    2.  **Prompt Robustness Test:** For 50 articles, generate Q/As using all 5 initial cultural definition prompts. Have a sociologist rank the resulting 250 Q/As for "sociocultural pertinence." Confirm the "general cultural exploration" prompt consistently ranks highest.
    3.  **Baseline Model Run:** Evaluate a small, widely-available model (e.g., Llama 3.1-8B) on a 500-question pilot subset in Spanish and English. Verify the expected pattern (Spanish > English) emerges before full-scale evaluation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How should LLM cultural bias benchmarks evolve beyond multiple-choice QA to capture nuanced sociocultural understanding?
    *   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "cultural knowledge cannot be adequately captured through simple prompt-based question answering alone" and call for moving "beyond basic multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) benchmarks," suggesting human-in-the-loop construction and analysis of interactional data (e.g., Wikipedia Talk Pages).
    *   **Why unresolved:** Current benchmarks like LatamQA measure factual recall but may miss interpretive, context-dependent cultural competence, risking superficial assessments.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and validation of interactive benchmarks (e.g., dialogue-based, role-play) with stakeholder participation, measuring models on tasks requiring contextual reasoning, empathy, or negotiation of cultural norms.
*   **Open Question 2:** Why do regionally specialized LLMs (e.g., PatagonIA) fail to outperform general-purpose models of comparable scale on regional cultural knowledge?
    *   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 3 shows PatagonIA (Chilean Spanish-specialized) does not surpass Mistral-medium or GPT-4.1-mini despite regional pre-training, contradicting the expectation that specialization improves cultural knowledge.
    *   **Why unresolved:** The paper notes this but does not analyze causes—possible explanations include insufficient regional data diversity, training objective misalignment, or benchmark limitations.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled ablation studies comparing training data distributions (region-sourced vs. generic), fine-tuning effects, and error analysis across model architectures to isolate factors influencing cultural knowledge retention.
*   **Open Question 3:** What factors explain performance disparities across different cultural elements (e.g., FICT vs. FOOD) in LatamQA?
    *   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Figure 4 reveals varying accuracy by element (e.g., Brazilian Portuguese excels in FICT and DIAL), but the paper does not investigate root causes such as data sparsity, concept complexity, or training-data prevalence.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Understanding element-specific gaps could inform more representative dataset construction and reveal biases in model training toward certain knowledge types.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlational analysis linking element performance to (1) Wikipedia article volume/complexity per element, (2) frequency in training corpora, and (3) human difficulty ratings for culturally grounded questions.
*   **Open Question 4:** Under what conditions does prompting language affect LLM performance on cultural QA—and why do findings conflict across studies?
    *   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors observe superior performance in native languages (Spanish/Portuguese) vs. English, but note contradictions with prior work (e.g., Tanwar et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025), without resolving the discrepancy.
    *   **Why unresolved:** The relationship between language, cultural knowledge retrieval, and model architecture remains unclear; unresolved variables include prompt formulation, training data mix, and cultural element type.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Multifactorial experiments varying prompt language, model language exposure, and cultural content type (factual vs. interpretive), coupled with probing internal representations to isolate language-specific activation patterns.

## Limitations
*   The entire dataset depends on the initial "Culture of X" Wikipedia category being a sufficiently comprehensive and unbiased map for the target region, which is unvalidated for all 18 countries and risks missing subcultures or non-standard topics.
*   Critical filtering and definition steps (category pruning, 500-article annotation, prompt selection) rely on a single sociologist's expertise, introducing a high-variance, non-replicable bottleneck where different experts could produce a substantively different dataset.
*   The core claim—that performance gaps on these MCQs directly measure asymmetric *sociocultural knowledge*—rests on the assumption that test artifacts (translation bias, distractor plausibility, question difficulty) are negligible and controlled, which is not fully demonstrated.

## Confidence
*   **Claim 1 (Wikidata/Scraping Mechanism):** **High.** The described recursive scraping with expert validation is a standard, transparent knowledge graph extraction technique. Its failure mode (poor initial category) is clear and testable.
*   **Claim 2 (LLM-based Filtering/Generation):** **Medium.** The reported classifier precision (87.5%) is strong but on a small, expert-labeled sample. The "no hallucination" check on 100 examples is reassuring but not statistically comprehensive. The pipeline's robustness to different LLM backbones or expert definitions is unknown.
*   **Claim 3 (Performance Asymmetries as Knowledge Gaps):** **Medium-Low.** The observed patterns (native language > English, Iberian > Latam) are consistent empirical findings. However, confidence in the *interpretation* as epistemic injustice (vs. test artifacts) is lower, as the dataset design cannot fully disentangle linguistic familiarity from actual knowledge depth.

## Next Checks
1.  **Inter-Expert Annotation Agreement:** Recruit 2-3 additional sociologists with regional expertise to annotate a stratified sample of 200-300 articles using the same taxonomy. Quantify Cohen's Kappa for the "positive/descriptive/negative" labels. A Kappa < 0.7 would severely undermine the generalizability of the primary expert's filter.
2.  **Controlled Distractor Quality Audit:** For a balanced subset of questions (e.g., 100 per language/country bucket), have bilingual experts evaluate distractor plausibility *independently* of the correct answer. Measure variance in distractor quality scores across the Spanish/Portuguese/English versions of the same question. Significant variance would invalidate direct performance comparisons.
3.  **Counterfactual Knowledge Probe:** Run a separate evaluation using a dataset like DLAMA (which probes factual knowledge via cloze-style prompts) on the same set of models. Compare the performance gap pattern (Latam vs. Iberian, native vs. English) against LatamQA's pattern. Convergence would strengthen the claim of a true knowledge gap; divergence would suggest LatamQA's design introduces unique biases.
