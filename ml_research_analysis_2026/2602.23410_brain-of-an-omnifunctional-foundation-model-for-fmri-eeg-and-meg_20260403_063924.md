# Brain-OF: An Omnifunctional Foundation Model for fMRI, EEG and MEG

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23410
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23410
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** First model jointly pretrained on fMRI, EEG, and MEG, achieving state-of-the-art on 7 of 9 downstream tasks.

## Executive Summary
Brain-OF is the first omnifunctional foundation model for brain imaging, jointly pretrained on fMRI, EEG, and MEG to overcome the modality-specific limitations of prior work. It introduces an Any-Resolution Neural Signal Sampler to unify heterogeneous spatiotemporal resolutions, a backbone combining DINT attention with a Sparse Mixture of Experts to capture both shared and modality-specific features, and a Masked Temporal-Frequency Modeling pretraining objective. Pretrained on ~40 datasets (5.8M samples), it sets new benchmarks across diverse tasks like emotion recognition and Alzheimer's diagnosis, with multimodal fusion further enhancing performance.

## Method Summary
The core innovation lies in three components: (1) the Any-Resolution Neural Signal Sampler projects varying spatial resolutions (voxels, electrodes, sensors) into a shared embedding space; (2) a backbone using DINT attention for sequential dependencies and a Sparse Mixture of Experts (MoE) to dynamically route inputs to modality-invariant or -specific experts; (3) a pretraining task that reconstructs masked segments in both temporal and frequency domains. This architecture enables processing of any combination of the three modalities via a serial ARNESS fusion mechanism, followed by task-specific heads.

## Key Results
- Surpasses single-modality and task-specific baselines on 7 of 9 downstream tasks.
- Achieves Cohen's Kappa of 26.36% on SEED-V (EEG emotion) and Balanced Accuracy of 71.79% on ADNI (fMRI Alzheimer's diagnosis).
- Multimodal fusion reduces MAE to 7.87 on CamCAN MEG brain-age prediction.
- Largest variant: 178 GFLOPs, 5.5 ms latency on A100 GPU.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
- **Multimodal Pretraining:** Training across fMRI, EEG, and MEG exposes the model to complementary spatial and temporal perspectives, fostering robust, generalizable representations that single-modality models cannot capture.
- **Dual-Domain Reconstruction:** Masked Temporal-Frequency Modeling forces the model to learn meaningful patterns in both time and frequency, crucial for interpreting neural signals which are often analyzed in either domain.
- **Modality Routing via MoE:** The Sparse Mixture of Experts allows the backbone to allocate parameters efficiently, using shared experts for invariant features (e.g., general neural dynamics) and specialized experts for modality-specific characteristics (e.g., hemodynamic vs. electrophysiological signals).

## Foundational Learning
- **Omnifunctional Modeling:** Pretraining a single model on heterogeneous brain modalities to serve multiple tasks. *Why needed:* Breaks silos of single-modality research, enabling cross-modal insights and efficient deployment. *Quick check:* Does performance on a novel task (unseen during pretraining) improve when using multimodal data versus unimodal?
- **Any-Resolution Sampling:** Unified embedding for variables spatial resolutions (e.g., 1mm fMRI voxels vs. EEG electrodes). *Why needed:* Brain signals are sampled at vastly different scales; without this, joint learning is impossible. *Quick check:* Can the sampler maintain performance when resolution is artificially altered within a modality (e.g., downsampled fMRI)?
- **Masked Dual-Domain Modeling:** Reconstructing masked segments in both time and frequency space. *Why needed:* Neural signals have complementary information in both domains; single-domain masking loses critical patterns. *Quick check:* Does ablating frequency-domain masking degrade performance on tasks known to rely on spectral features (e.g., resting-state fMRI)?
- **Sparse Mixture of Experts (MoE):** Dynamically activating sub-networks per input. *Why needed:* Manages computational cost while allowing specialization for different modalities/tasks. *Quick check:* Are different experts consistently activated for unimodal (EEG) vs. multimodal (EEG+fMRI) inputs?
- **Serial ARNESS Fusion:** A simple, modality-agnostic fusion strategy. *Why needed:* Provides a baseline for multimodal integration without complex cross-modal attention. *Quick check:* Does performance drop significantly if fusion order is randomized (e.g., MEG→EEG vs. EEG→MEG)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Input Modalities (fMRI/EEG/MEG) -> Any-Resolution Sampler -> DINT-MoE Backbone -> Serial ARNESS Fusion -> Task-Specific Head

**Critical Path:**  
1. Sampler normalizes spatial dimensions to fixed token count.  
2. DINT attention processes temporal sequences; MoE router selects experts.  
3. Fusion combines features from each modality sequentially.  
4. Task head maps to output (e.g., classification, regression).

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- **Sampler vs. Information Loss:** The sampler must compress without losing modality-specific spatial patterns; aggressive downsampling could erase fine-grained topographical information.
- **Serial Fusion Simplicity:** ARNESS is computationally cheap but may miss nonlinear cross-modal interactions that simultaneous, attention-based fusion could capture.
- **MoE Sparsity:** Sparse activation reduces compute but risks underfitting if the router misroutes inputs or if dataset imbalance biases expert usage.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Performance collapse on a modality with scarce pretraining data (likely MEG due to dataset scarcity).
- High variance across datasets from different sites/scanners, indicating poor harmonization.
- Expert activation patterns that do not differentiate between unimodal and multimodal inputs, questioning the claimed separation of invariant/specific features.

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. **Sampler Isolation Test:** Train a unimodal fMRI model, artificially downsample its spatial resolution to EEG density, and compare performance to a model using the Sampler. Quantify the Sampler's contribution versus raw data resizing.
2. **Expert Activation Audit:** Run inference on unimodal (fMRI-only, EEG-only) and multimodal (fMRI+EEG+MEG) inputs. Log activated experts and correlate with known modality-specific biomarkers (e.g., alpha-band power for EEG, default-mode network for fMRI).
3. **Fusion Mechanism Swap:** Replace serial ARNESS with a cross-modal transformer fusion module. Evaluate on multimodal benchmarks (e.g., CamCAN fMRI+MEG) to test if more expressive fusion yields statistically significant gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extending to New Modalities:** How can Brain-OF incorporate additional neuroimaging modalities (e.g., fNIRS, PET) and general multivariate time series? *Basis:* Limitations section explicitly mentions this. *Why Unresolved:* New modalities introduce heterogeneity and pretraining data is imbalanced (fMRI scarce). Straightforward extension risks negative transfer. *Resolution Evidence:* A variant that integrates at least one new modality with comparable performance and effective handling of data imbalance.
- **More Expressive Fusion:** Can adaptive gating or cross-modal attention improve fine-grained inter-modality dependencies beyond serial ARNESS? *Basis:* Limitations suggest exploring adaptive gating or cross-modal attention. *Why Unresolved:* Serial fusion may not exploit complex, nonlinear interactions. *Resolution Evidence:* Implementation of a more expressive fusion module showing statistically significant gains on multimodal benchmarks vs. original.
- **Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT):** To what extent can LoRA reduce computational/memory costs without sacrificing performance? *Basis:* Authors note full finetuning was used and future work will investigate PEFT. *Why Unresolved:* Full finetuning is expensive; PEFT is untested for Brain-OF. *Resolution Evidence:* Applying PEFT across tasks showing similar performance to full finetuning with substantial reductions in memory and training time.

## Limitations
- **Dataset Heterogeneity:** Unclear how 40 datasets with varying scanners, electrode layouts, and preprocessing pipelines were harmonized; potential confounding biases may inflate generalization claims.
- **Sampler Opacity:** The Any-Resolution Sampler's mechanism for mapping disparate spatial resolutions into a shared space without information loss is not deeply elaborated.
- **MoE Specialization Unproven:** The claim of capturing both modality-invariant and -specific features lacks direct evidence; it is not shown which experts specialize in which modality or task.

## Confidence
- **High:** Engineering scale (5.8M samples, GFLOPs/latency figures) and empirical SOTA results on multiple benchmarks.
- **Medium:** Overall benefit of multimodal pretraining and fusion, supported by ablation studies showing complementary gains.
- **Low:** Strength and interpretability of the "omnifunctional" claim; success may reflect massive data rather than true unification of neural signal semantics. Robustness to novel/out-of-distribution modalities or tasks is unevidenced.

## Next Checks
1. **Dataset Harmonization Audit:** Request full preprocessing and metadata (scanner parameters, electrode/sensor models, QC) for all 40 datasets. Replicate a subset of the training pipeline to assess variance from source domains.
2. **Resolution Sampler Ablation:** Isolate the Any-Resolution Sampler. Train a version using only fMRI data but artificially downsampling spatial resolution to match EEG/MEG density, and vice-versa. Measure performance drop to quantify the sampler's contribution versus dataset biases.
3. **Expert Specialization Analysis:** Inspect activated MoE pathways during inference on unimodal vs. multimodal inputs. Correlate expert usage patterns with known modality-specific anatomical/physiological signals to validate separated invariant/specific features.
