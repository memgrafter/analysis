# SAMAS: A Spectrum-Guided Multi-Agent System for Achieving Style Fidelity in Literary Translation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19840
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19840
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: SAMAS achieves state-of-the-art semantic accuracy (e.g., 96.93 XCOMET en→xx with GPT-5) and statistically significant style fidelity, with human preference rates of 57%–70% over strong baselines including GPT-5 and TACTIC.

## Executive Summary
SAMAS introduces a novel multi-agent framework for literary translation that explicitly targets authorial style preservation. It treats style as a signal by extracting an 81‑dimensional Stylistic Feature Spectrum (SFS) from the source text’s word‑length sequence via Wavelet Packet Transform. This SFS deterministically routes the text through a dynamic workflow of specialized translation agents. Evaluations on FLORES‑200 and WMT24 show that SAMAS not only matches but often exceeds the semantic accuracy of large single‑model baselines while achieving a statistically significant advantage in style fidelity. Human evaluations consistently prefer SAMAS outputs, demonstrating the practical value of its design.

## Method Summary
The system operates in three stages. First, a SFS extractor converts the source text into a numeric sequence of word lengths, then applies a 4‑level Wavelet Packet Transform to compute 81 statistical features (Relative Wavelet Energy, Wavelet Entropy, and moments across sub‑bands). Second, a deterministic router uses thresholded values (e.g., Wavelet Entropy > 0.85 and cumulative low‑frequency energy > 0.6) to classify the text’s stylistic complexity (e.g., “Faulkner‑esque” vs. “Hemingway‑esque”). Third, a predefined sequence of six specialized agents—Core Translation, Linguistic Structure, Metaphor, Emotion, Rhythm & Prosody, and Consistency—process the text in order, each addressing a distinct style dimension. The final agent ensures coherence across the contributions.

## Key Results
- SAMAS achieves state‑of‑the‑art semantic accuracy on FLORES‑200 and WMT24 (e.g., 96.93 XCOMET for en→xx with GPT‑5).
- Human evaluations show a clear preference for SAMAS outputs over strong baselines, with preference rates between 57% and 70%.
- The system demonstrates a statistically significant improvement in style fidelity, validating the efficacy of its spectrum‑guided approach.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Stylistic Feature Spectrum (SFS) as Control Signal
- **Claim**: Quantifying authorial style into an 81‑dimensional SFS via Wavelet Packet Transform on word‑length sequences creates a deterministic, language‑agnostic control signal capturing rhythmic and structural properties.
- **Mechanism**: Word‑length sequences are non‑stationary signals. WPT decomposes them into multiple frequency sub‑bands. Statistical features (Relative Wavelet Energy, Wavelet Entropy, moments) are extracted from each sub‑band, forming a compact vector that encodes multi‑scale rhythmic patterns.
- **Core assumption**: Word‑length sequences are a robust, language‑agnostic proxy for literary style’s rhythm and structural complexity; WPT is suitable for extracting multi‑scale features.
- **Evidence anchors**: The abstract and Section 2.1 describe the 81‑D vector construction. The assumption is cited to general stylometry literature.
- **Break condition**: If word‑length statistics do not correlate with perceived stylistic felicity for a language pair, the SFS becomes a misleading signal, leading to misrouting.

### Mechanism 2: Deterministic Routing via Thresholds
- **Claim**: A rule‑based router uses SFS thresholds (e.g., H > 0.85, E_low > 0.6) to dynamically select a pre‑optimal multi‑agent workflow for each input segment.
- **Mechanism**: The SFS vector is compared against empirically derived thresholds. Based on the outcome (e.g., “Faulkner‑esque” high‑complexity or “Hemingway‑esque” simple rhythm), a specific ordered sequence of specialized agents is activated.
- **Core assumption**: High‑complexity/high macro‑structure and low‑complexity/short rhythmic pace are two dominant, separable style clusters; optimal pipelines for each can be manually designed.
- **Evidence anchors**: The abstract and page 2 describe the thresholds and validation on 200 segments. The thresholds were chosen to enable controlled analysis.
- **Break condition**: Thresholds derived from a limited validation set (2 authors, 200 segments) may fail to generalize to broader literary style distributions, causing systematic misclassification.

### Mechanism 3: Specialized Agents in a Sequential Pipeline
- **Claim**: A pool of specialized, role‑prompted LLM agents executes the dynamically assigned workflow, with each focusing on a distinct stylistic dimension, and their outputs are sequentially integrated to preserve fidelity.
- **Mechanism**: The routing assigns a chain of agents (e.g., Linguistic Structure before Core Translation). Each agent processes the text (or intermediate output) with a prompt tailored to its role (e.g., Rhythm & Prosody handles cadence). A final Consistency and Stylistic Fidelity Agent ensures global coherence.
- **Core assumption**: Literary style fidelity can be decomposed into orthogonal, sequentially processable sub‑tasks (semantic, syntactic, rhetorical, emotional, rhythmic, cohesive), and a pipeline of specialized prompts outperforms a single monolithic prompt.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Section 2.2.1 list the six agents and their roles. The decomposition is inspired by neighboring multi‑agent translation work.
- **Break condition**: Style dimensions may not be orthogonal; sequential processing risks error propagation or misses synergistic effects a single model could capture.

## Foundational Learning
- **Wavelet Packet Transform (WPT) for Signal Analysis**: WPT decomposes a non‑stationary signal into multiple frequency sub‑bands, providing both frequency and positional resolution. It is needed here to transform a simple word‑length sequence into a rich multi‑scale feature vector (SFS). *Quick check*: How does decomposing into sub‑bands at different scales capture both overall sentence length pattern (low‑frequency) and local clausal phrasing (high‑frequency)?
- **Deterministic Routing vs. Learned/Adaptive Control**: SAMAS uses rule‑based thresholds, not a learned policy. This trade‑off prioritizes interpretability and control over potential adaptability. *Quick check*: What is a key risk of using a small set of manually defined thresholds compared to a system that learns routing from thousands of examples?
- **Decomposition of Stylistic Phenomena for Specialized Agents**: The system assumes style can be broken into concrete, addressable sub‑problems (rhythm, metaphor, emotion). This informs prompt design and handoff logic. *Quick check*: If the Rhythm agent alters sentence length and the Emotion agent changes word choices, how must the Consistency agent reconcile potentially conflicting edits?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Source Text -> Input & Signalizer (word‑length) -> SFS Extractor (WPT) -> Routing Logic (thresholds) -> Workflow Executor (ordered agents) -> Consistency Agent -> Output.
- **Critical path**: Text -> SFS -> Routing Decision -> Agent1 -> Agent2 -> … -> AgentN -> Output. SFS extraction and routing are single points of failure.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Interpretability vs. Coverage: Deterministic thresholds are transparent but likely only cover two broad archetypes; nuanced styles may be misrouted.
  - Specialization vs. Error Propagation: Sequential agents risk early errors compounding; iterative consensus could mitigate but adds complexity.
  - Static Library vs. Online Generation: Workflows are predefined; adaptive on‑the‑fly construction is more flexible but harder to control.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Routing Failure: SFS vector near decision boundaries; no single fixed workflow in ablations matches dynamic routing performance.
  - Agent Incompetence: Isolating a specific agent degrades quality, especially for texts emphasizing that style dimension.
  - Incoherence: Output has jarring register shifts or broken syntax; Consistency agent reports high conflict rates.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **SFS Validation**: On a held‑out multi‑author corpus with known stylistic labels, train a classifier on the 81‑D SFS. Report separation accuracy for style dimensions.
  2. **Routing Threshold Sensitivity**: Grid‑search over H and E_low thresholds; plot translation quality (e.g., XCOMET) against thresholds; confirm paper’s thresholds are near optimum.
  3. **Agent Isolation & Contribution**: Disable one agent at a time (e.g., bypass Rhythm) and measure delta in style metrics; qualitatively check each agent’s marginal contribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Are the SFS routing thresholds generalizable to authors beyond Faulkner and Hemingway, or do they oversimplify stylistic variation?
2. Is the word‑length based SFS sufficient to capture all relevant stylistic features, or are additional signals needed?
3. Does the fixed pool of six specialized agents cover the full range of literary stylistic techniques, or are certain styles underserved?

## Limitations
- The assumption that word‑length sequences robustly capture literary style across languages/authors is weakly validated; evidence is limited to two authors.
- Routing thresholds are derived from a tiny validation set (200 segments, 2 authors) and may not generalize to the broad style spectrum in standard benchmarks.
- The system depends on unspecified prompt engineering for the six agents; reproducing these prompts without guidance is challenging and may require extensive trial‑and‑error.

## Confidence
- **High**: Multi‑agent decomposition improves style fidelity (supported by human evaluation preference over GPT‑5/TACTIC).
- **Medium**: SFS extraction is mathematically sound and yields a deterministic control signal (implementation is specified, but stylistic validity is an assumption).
- **Low**: The specific binary threshold‑based routing is generally applicable (evidence is extremely narrow; likely an illustrative example, not a complete solution).

## Next Checks
1. **SFS stylistic validity test**: On a held‑out, multi‑author, multi‑language literary corpus with independent stylistic ratings, train a classifier using the 81‑D SFS. Report accuracy for separating style dimensions (e.g., complexity, rhythm) cited in literary theory.
2. **Routing threshold sensitivity & failure mode analysis**: For a diverse set of source texts, plot H vs. E_low, identify clusters, systematically vary thresholds, and re‑run SAMAS on boundary texts. Does performance degrade smoothly or catastrophically?
3. **Prompt reconstruction & agent isolation**: With base model access, attempt to reconstruct agent prompts via few‑shot examples of their described functions. Run ablations: (a) single full‑prompt agent vs. sequential pipeline; (b) disable each agent in sequence. Measure delta in style metric and qualitative coherence.
