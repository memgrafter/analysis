# Practical Efficient Global Optimization is No-regret

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.25311
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25311
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Practical EGO with a nugget achieves sublinear cumulative regret and is a no‑regret algorithm for common kernels; the nugget’s effect on the bound is non‑monotonic.

## Executive Summary
Practical Efficient Global Optimization (EGO) is a popular Bayesian optimization method that uses a Gaussian process (GP) surrogate and Expected Improvement (EI) acquisition. In practice, a small nugget (ϵ) is added to the GP covariance matrix for numerical stability, but this modification had lacked theoretical regret guarantees. This paper resolves that gap by showing that the nugget ensures a positive lower bound on the posterior variance at all points, which in turn yields a lower bound on EI and controls the instantaneous regret. Under the assumption that the objective function lies in the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) with bounded norm and that the domain is compact, the authors prove a sublinear cumulative regret bound of O(√(T log T γ_T)), where γ_T is the maximum information gain. This establishes that practical EGO is a no‑regret algorithm for common kernels such as the squared exponential and Matérn with ν > 1/2, for which γ_T grows sublinearly.

Additionally, the analysis reveals a nuanced effect of the nugget size ϵ on the regret bound: the bound is not monotonic in ϵ, indicating that an intermediate range of ϵ values yields tighter guarantees. The authors provide theoretical conditions under which the bound decreases or increases with ϵ, and they validate these insights through numerical experiments on standard benchmark functions. The work bridges theory and practice for a widely used algorithm and offers guidance on nugget selection.

## Method Summary
Practical EGO proceeds iteratively. At each step, a GP is fitted to the observed data, with a small positive nugget ϵ added to the diagonal of the covariance matrix to ensure positive definiteness. The GP posterior mean and variance define the Expected Improvement acquisition function, which is maximized (e.g., via gradient‑based optimization) to select the next evaluation point x_t. The true objective f(x_t) is observed, the data is updated, and the process repeats. The nugget is fixed throughout the optimization.

The paper’s theoretical contribution is a regret analysis that accounts for the nugget. The key insight is that the nugget provides a uniform lower bound on the posterior variance, which combined with an RKHS norm bound gives a positive lower bound on EI at any point. This lower bound is then used to control the instantaneous regret, especially in cases where the current incumbent value is not improved. The cumulative regret is bounded by splitting the sum into exploitation and exploration terms: the exploitation sum telescopes to a constant, while the exploration sum is bounded using the maximum information gain γ_T. The resulting regret bound depends on γ_T and exhibits a non‑monotonic relationship with ϵ due to competing effects on variance and information gain.

## Key Results
- Practical EGO with a small nugget ϵ > 0 achieves cumulative regret O(√(T log T γ_T)), proving it is a no‑regret algorithm for kernels with sublinear γ_T (e.g., squared exponential, Matérn ν > 1/2).
- The nugget guarantees a strictly positive lower bound on the posterior variance, which translates to a lower bound on Expected Improvement and thus controls instantaneous regret.
- The dependence of the regret bound on ϵ is non‑monotonic; there exists an optimal intermediate range for ϵ that minimizes the bound, as shown by case analysis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Nugget‑induced variance lower bound
**Claim:** Adding a small positive nugget ϵ > 0 to the GP covariance matrix ensures a positive lower bound on the posterior variance at all points, which in turn provides a lower bound on expected improvement (EI) and controls the instantaneous regret.  
**Mechanism:** The posterior variance formula σ_t²(x) = k(x,x) − k_t(x)⊤ (K_t + ϵI)⁻¹ k_t(x) is bounded below by ϵ/(t+ϵ) (Lemma B.6). Because EI(x) = σ_t(x) τ(z_t(x)) and under the RKHS norm bound |f(x)−μ_t(x)| ≤ B σ_t(x) (Lemma B.7) we have z_t(x) ≥ −B and thus τ(z_t(x)) ≥ τ(−B). Hence EI_t(x^*) ≥ τ(−B)√(ϵ/(t+ϵ)), a uniform positive lower bound. This bound is used to control the term μ_{t−1}(x_t)−f_{t−1}^+ in the case f_{t−1}^+ ≤ f(x_t) (Lemma 3.2).  
**Core assumption:** Assumption 3.1 – f belongs to the RKHS of the kernel with bounded norm B, the kernel is bounded (k(x,x)=1), and the domain C is compact.  
**Evidence anchors:** Lemma B.6 (variance lower bound); Lemma 3.2 (instantaneous regret bound using that lower bound). The corpus contains prior work on nuggets in GP modeling, but the specific link to EI lower bound and regret is established anew here.  
**Break condition:** If ϵ decays too fast relative to t the lower bound becomes negligible; if the kernel’s maximum information gain γ_T grows linearly (e.g., for non‑smooth kernels or poor length‑scale) the cumulative regret may not be sublinear.

### Mechanism 2: Sublinear cumulative regret
**Claim:** Practical EGO achieves sublinear cumulative regret R_T = O(T^{1/2} log^{1/2}(T) √γ_T), and is therefore a no‑regret algorithm for common kernels (squared exponential, Matérn ν > 1/2) where γ_T = O(log^{d+1} T) or O(T^{d/(2ν+d)} log^{2ν/(2ν+d)} T).  
**Mechanism:** The instantaneous regret bound (Lemma 3.2) splits into an exploitation term max{f_{t−1}^+ − f(x_t),0} and an exploration term proportional to σ_{t−1}(x_t). The sum of exploitation terms over T is bounded by 2B using a telescoping argument on a subsequence of improving points (Lemma 3.5). The sum of σ_{t−1}(x_t) is upper‑bounded by √(C_γ(ϵ) T γ_T(ϵ)) via the maximum information gain (Lemma A.4). Combining yields R_T ≤ 2c_{B1}B + poly(B,ϵ) √(T γ_T).  
**Core assumption:** The RKHS bound and compact domain as above; additionally γ_T(ϵ) must be sublinear, which holds for the specified kernels (Lemma A.5).  
**Evidence anchors:** Lemma 3.5 (cumulative bound); Theorem 3.6 (asymptotic rates). Recent papers derive regret for EI but without nugget; this work extends to the practical nugget‑adjusted case.  
**Break condition:** If γ_T grows super‑linearly (e.g., for a kernel with poor inductive bias on the target function) the term √(T γ_T) may become linear or worse, losing the no‑regret property.

### Mechanism 3: Non‑monotonic effect of the nugget
**Claim:** The nugget ϵ does not monotonically improve the cumulative regret bound; instead its effect is non‑monotonic, implying an optimal intermediate range for ϵ to achieve the tightest bound.  
**Mechanism:** The bound R_T ≤ C_1 + √(2 c_T(ϵ) T) contains three ϵ‑dependent pieces: (i) c_{Bϵ}(ϵ,T) ∼ ½ log((T+ϵ)/(2πϵ τ²(−B))) decreases with ϵ; (ii) C_γ(ϵ)=2/ log(1+1/ϵ) increases with ϵ; (iii) the information gain bound γ_T(ϵ) decreases with ϵ for SE and Matérn (Lemma E.1). Their product c_T(ϵ) is therefore not monotone. Theorems 4.2 and 4.3 give sufficient conditions under which the bound decreases (Case 1) or increases (Case 2) with ϵ, showing that an optimal trade‑off exists.  
**Core assumption:** T/ϵ ≫ 1 to use asymptotic approximations; constants from Lemma E.1/E.2/E.3 are fixed for a given dimension d, length‑scale l, and kernel.  
**Evidence anchors:** Theorem 4.2 and Theorem 4.3 state the two‑case behavior; Figure 2 demonstrates the non‑monotonic curve. The nugget‑regret interplay appears to be a novel theoretical insight.  
**Break condition:** The case‑analysis relies on explicit bounds for γ_T(ϵ); if those bounds are loose or if T is very small, the conditions may not classify the behavior correctly. Also the analysis assumes a fixed length‑scale; if l is estimated adaptively the constants change.

## Foundational Learning
- **Gaussian Process posterior with nugget**: The nugget ϵ is added to the diagonal of the covariance matrix to ensure numerical stability and positive definiteness. It modifies the posterior variance formula, introducing a term that scales with ϵ.  
  *Why needed here*: Understanding how ϵ affects posterior variance is essential to grasp the core mechanism of the paper.  
  *Quick check*: "How does adding ϵI to the covariance matrix change the posterior variance at a previously sampled point?"

- **Expected Improvement (EI) and its properties**: EI is the acquisition function that balances exploitation (difference between incumbent and predicted mean) and exploration (posterior standard deviation). Its mathematical form involves the standard normal PDF and CDF. Properties used include monotonicity in σ and the inequality EI(x) ≥ σ(x)·τ(−B) under an RKHS norm bound.  
  *Why needed here*: EI drives the selection of points; bounding EI from below directly leads to regret control.  
  *Quick check*: "Why is EI non‑negative for all x, and how does the incumbent f_t^+ create a trade‑off?"

- **Maximum Information Gain (γ_T)**: γ_T = max_{x_1,…,x_T} [½ log |I + σ⁻² K|] measures the reduction in uncertainty about the function after T evaluations. It determines how much the posterior variance can decrease and appears in the bound on the sum of σ_t(x_t). For common kernels, γ_T grows sublinearly (e.g., logarithmically for SE), which is key to achieving sublinear regret.  
  *Why needed here*: The sum of exploration terms is bounded in terms of γ_T; its growth rate directly impacts the cumulative regret.  
  *Quick check*: "What does γ_T measure, and why does it grow slower for smoother kernels?"

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
GP surrogate (with kernel k and nugget ϵ) → compute posterior mean μ and variance σ → EI acquisition function → maximize EI to choose next point x_t → evaluate true objective f(x_t) → augment data and update GP (recompute with K+ϵI). This forms an iterative loop.

### Critical path
At iteration t (starting from data D_{t-1}):
1. Update GP posterior: compute K_{t-1}+ϵI, obtain μ_{t-1}(·) and σ_{t-1}(·).
2. Compute EI_{t-1}(x) = (f_{t-1}^+ − μ_{t-1}(x)) Φ(z) + σ_{t-1}(x) φ(z), with z = (f_{t-1}^+ − μ_{t-1}(x))/σ_{t-1}(x).
3. Select x_t = argmax_{x∈C} EI_{t-1}(x).
4. Observe y_t = f(x_t) (noiseless).
5. Append (x_t, y_t) to D_t.
6. Nugget ϵ remains fixed; it is added to the covariance matrix during Cholesky factorization.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Nugget size**: Too small risks numerical instability (Cholesky failure); too large inflates variance and slows convergence. The theoretical bound suggests an optimal intermediate range.
- **Kernel choice**: Smoother kernels (e.g., SE) yield slower γ_T growth → tighter regret, but may be less expressive; rougher Matérn (ν ≤ 1/2) may lead to linear γ_T and thus linear regret.
- **Initial design**: Number and distribution of initial points affect early GP conditioning; too few points can exacerbate nugget effects and lead to poor early exploration.

### Failure signatures
- **Numerical crashes**: Cholesky decomposition fails when ϵ is too small and points cluster; catch LinAlgError.
- **Stagnation**: Excessive exploration due to large ϵ leads to slow reduction in f(x_t).
- **Theoretic breach**: If the true function’s RKHS norm B is much larger than assumed, constants blow up and the bound becomes loose.
- **Non‑no‑regret**: For kernels with linear γ_T (e.g., ν ≤ 1/2 for Matérn), cumulative regret may be linear.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Is classic EGO without a nugget (ϵ=0) a no‑regret algorithm?**  
   The analysis relies on a positive nugget to guarantee a variance lower bound; for ϵ=0 the posterior variance can vanish at previously sampled points, breaking the proof. Determining whether EGO is no‑regret without a nugget remains open.

2. **How should one practically select the nugget ϵ in real‑world Bayesian optimization to minimize cumulative regret, given the theoretical non‑monotonic dependency and unknown problem‑dependent constants?**  
   The theory indicates an optimal intermediate range but requires knowledge of constants (e.g., C1_dl) that are typically unknown. No adaptive or data‑driven tuning rule is provided.

3. **How does the nugget ϵ interact with kernel hyperparameters (e.g., length‑scale) during typical Bayesian optimization where hyperparameters are estimated from data, and does this affect the no‑regret guarantee?**  
   The analysis assumes fixed kernel hyperparameters and a fixed RKHS norm bound B. When hyperparameters are learned, these assumptions may be violated; the interplay and its impact on regret are unstudied.

4. **For Matérn kernels with smoothness ν ≤ 1/2, does practical EGO with a nugget achieve sublinear cumulative regret?**  
   The sublinear guarantee hinges on γ_T being sublinear. For ν ≤ 1/2, γ_T may grow polynomially (potentially linearly), which could lead to linear regret. This regime is not covered by the current results.

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (RKHS membership, bounded norm, compact domain, bounded kernel) may not hold for many real‑world black‑box functions.
- The nugget’s effect on regret is derived using asymptotic bounds for γ_T that are often loose; consequently the theoretically optimal ϵ range might differ in practice.
- Empirical validation is limited to noiseless synthetic functions; performance under observation noise or stochasticity remains unknown.

## Confidence
- Nugget ensures positive posterior variance lower bound → **High** confidence (directly proved in lemmas).
- Practical EGO achieves sublinear cumulative regret O(√(T log T γ_T)) → **Medium** confidence (bound is established but constants may be large and γ_T bounds are loose).
- Nugget’s non‑monotonic effect on regret → **Medium** confidence (theoretical case analysis provides conditions but relies on potentially loose bounds; empirical verification is limited).
