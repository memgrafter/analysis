# ThaiSafetyBench: Assessing Language Model Safety in Thai Cultural Contexts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04992
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04992
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** Thai-specific attacks achieve higher Attack Success Rate (ASR) than general Thai attacks, exposing a critical cultural vulnerability in LLM safety alignment.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces ThaiSafetyBench, the first large-scale safety evaluation suite for Thai language models that includes culturally-specific malicious prompts. The dataset comprises 1,954 prompts spanning six risk areas, with a split between general Thai and Thai-specific cultural attacks. Using GPT-4.1 and Gemini-2.5-Pro as judges, the authors evaluate 24 LLMs and find a consistent performance gap: models are more vulnerable to Thai-specific attacks, revealing that current safety alignment fails to capture region-specific norms. To enable scalable future evaluation, they fine-tune a DeBERTa classifier (ThaiSafetyClassifier) that matches judge agreement with a weighted F1 of 84.4% at low cost. The work highlights the need for culturally-grounded safety training and provides a foundation for benchmarking non-English LLMs.

## Method Summary
ThaiSafetyBench was constructed through a multi-stage pipeline: initial prompts sourced from translations, existing Thai datasets, and generation, then refined by Thai annotators and labeled across a taxonomy of six risk areas (e.g., Illegal Activity, Thai Socio-Cultural Harm, etc.). The dataset is split into general Thai prompts and Thai-specific prompts that embed local cultural references (e.g., monarchy, regional issues). For evaluation, each target model's response to a prompt is binary-classified as safe or harmful by two strong multilingual LLMs (GPT-4.1 and Gemini-2.5-Pro) in an LLM-as-a-judge framework; the Attack Success Rate (ASR) is the proportion of responses judged harmful, averaged across judges. To avoid recurring API costs, the authors train an open-source DeBERTa-based classifier on 46,893 judge-labeled prompt-response pairs. Using LoRA fine-tuning with a class-balanced loss, the resulting ThaiSafetyClassifier achieves 84.4% weighted F1, effectively replicating the judge's decisions and enabling community-wide, low-cost evaluation.

## Key Results
- Thai-specific attacks yield a significantly higher ASR than general Thai attacks across all evaluated models, confirming a cultural safety gap.
- ThaiSafetyClassifier attains a weighted F1 of 84.4%, matching the agreement level of GPT-4.1 and providing a cost-effective evaluation tool.
- Evaluation of 24 LLMs shows that even Thai-tuned models struggle with culturally contextualized harms, underscoring the need for targeted safety data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Culturally specific malicious prompts expose safety vulnerabilities not captured by general non-English prompts.
- **Mechanism:** By embedding Thai socio-cultural references (e.g., monarchy, local norms) into prompts, the benchmark tests whether safety alignment generalizes beyond surface-level translation to deeper cultural schemas. A higher ASR on these prompts indicates that the model's alignment fails to recognize culturally bound harmful intents.
- **Core assumption:** Safety alignment trained primarily on English or universal harm categories underfits region-specific knowledge and norms.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states "a consistently higher Attack Success Rate (ASR) for Thai-specific, culturally contextualized attacks compared to general Thai-language attacks, highlighting a critical vulnerability." Section 5 reports the ASR gap (Fig. 4a). Neighboring papers on Thai LLMs also indicate an underexplored cultural risk.
- **Break condition:** If models show no ASR gap between Thai-specific and general prompts after targeted cultural safety tuning, the mechanism's explanatory power for the initial gap weakens.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Using strong multilingual LLMs (GPT-4.1, Gemini-2.5-Pro) as judges provides a reliable, automated proxy for human safety judgment in Thai.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM-as-a-judge framework leverages high-capacity models presumed to have robust multilingual understanding to perform binary classification of responses. High inter-evaluator correlation (Spearman's ρ=0.974) and classifier agreement lend credibility, enabling scalable, reproducible evaluation without labor-intensive human annotation.
- **Core assumption:** State-of-the-art LLMs can reliably interpret harm in Thai linguistic and cultural contexts with accuracy approximating human experts.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions GPT-4.1 and Gemini-2.5-Pro as evaluators; Section 4 describes the dual-judge setup; Appendix A reports ρ=0.974.
- **Break condition:** If subsequent human evaluation on a held-out Thai subset reveals significant disagreement with the LLM judges (e.g., Cohen's κ < 0.7), the reliability of the automated evaluation mechanism is compromised.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A fine-tuned DeBERTa classifier can match LLM-judge agreement at a fraction of the computational cost, enabling sustainable benchmark maintenance.
- **Mechanism:** The classifier is trained on prompt-response pairs labeled by the LLM judges. By achieving a weighted F1 of 84.4% that matches GPT-4.1 judgments, it learns to replicate the judge's decision boundary, creating a low-cost, open-source tool for community evaluation without recurring API costs.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM-judge's labels are a sufficiently accurate "gold standard" and DeBERTa can capture the required semantic nuance for Thai safety judgment.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports the DeBERTa classifier's 84.4% weighted F1; Section 6 details training setup and metrics.
- **Break condition:** If the classifier's predictions systematically diverge from LLM-judge labels on specific Thai cultural subcategories despite high overall F1, its utility for fine-grained analysis diminishes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Attack Success Rate (ASR):** The primary metric; proportion of malicious prompts that elicit harmful responses. Needed to interpret vulnerability. *Quick check:* If a model rejects 8 out of 10 culturally-specific prompts, its ASR is 0.2 (20%); a high ASR indicates poor safety alignment.
- **LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation:** Using powerful LLMs to judge response safety. Benefits: scalable, multilingual. Risks: judge bias, cultural misinterpretation. *Quick check:* Potential biases: (1) GPT-4.1 may have been trained mainly on English data, leading to misinterpretation of Thai nuances; (2) inherent model biases could skew harm classification. Correlation checks help but don't fully resolve.
- **Cultural Contextualization in NLP Safety:** Concept that harm is mediated by local symbols, history, and social norms. Needed to understand why Thai-specific prompts are harder. *Quick check:* A prompt about lèse-majesté (insulting the Thai monarchy) is a uniquely high-risk category in Thailand because of severe legal and social penalties, whereas similar content might not be considered harmful in many English-centric benchmarks.
- **Fine-tuning a Classifier for Proxy Evaluation:** Transfer learning from a teacher LLM to a student model (DeBERTa) for cost-effective labeling. Needed to maintain the benchmark sustainably. *Quick check:* The classifier inherits any biases or blindspots of GPT-4.1; if GPT-4.1 misjudges certain Thai cultural harms, the classifier will replicate those errors.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Curation Pipeline -> Evaluation Engine -> Classifier Training Stack -> Leaderboard & Community Interface

**Critical path:**  
1. Dataset construction (ensuring cultural validity) →  
2. Model evaluation (obtaining judge scores) →  
3. Classifier training (achieving judge parity) →  
4. Leaderboard deployment (enabling community use). A failure in dataset cultural authenticity breaks the entire chain.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Judge reliability vs. cost: Two powerful closed-source judges increase confidence but are costly and create dependency. The classifier trades some accuracy for openness and cost.
- Dataset size vs. depth: 1,954 prompts is modest; the tradeoff is between breadth (many categories) and depth (many samples per nuanced scenario).
- Release scope vs. regulation: Full dataset withheld (Monarchy category) for Thai compliance, reducing utility but adhering to local law.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low inter-judge correlation (<<0.9) on specific categories indicates judge instability.
- Classifier F1 drops significantly on Thai-specific subcategories vs. general ones.
- Leaderboard submissions without clear methodology (e.g., no judge or classifier details) leading to irreproducible scores.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Reproduce baseline ASR: Run the provided evaluation script on one closed-source (e.g., GPT-4o) and one open-source Thai-tuned model (e.g., Typhoon2-8B) using the public dataset subset. Verify ASR numbers within 2% of paper's Figure 1.
2. Classifier sanity check: Load ThaiSafetyClassifier, run it on 100 prompt-response pairs from the paper's Appendix. Check that its "harmful" predictions align with at least one judge's "harmful" label for >80% of cases.
3. Cultural gap probe: Select the "Thai Socio-Cultural Harm" risk area. Compute ASR for your chosen model on only those prompts vs. on "Information Hazards" prompts. Confirm the ASR gap direction matches the paper's trend (higher ASR on cultural harms). If not, investigate model-specific behavior.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why do Thai-specific cultural attacks achieve consistently higher Attack Success Rates (ASR) compared to general Thai-language attacks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5 ("Thai-Specific vs. General Content Attacks") reports a significant disparity in ASR, with Thai-specific prompts outperforming general ones across models.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies this as a "critical cultural vulnerability" but does not dissect the underlying causes—whether due to insufficient cultural representation in safety training data, the models' lack of contextual understanding of Thai norms, or limitations in current alignment techniques for region-specific nuances.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled ablation studies comparing models fine-tuned on datasets with varying volumes of Thai-cultural examples versus general Thai safety data, coupled with error analysis of failure cases to pinpoint whether the issue stems from knowledge gaps, reasoning failures, or misaligned reward modeling.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What specific factors in continual pretraining (CPT) data quality and curation determine whether Southeast Asia or Thai-tuned models achieve safer performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5 ("Base vs. Continual Pretrained (CPT) Models") concludes that CPT's impact on ASR is inconsistent and hypothesizes that data quality—such as inclusion of safety-focused datasets or filtering of malicious content—is key, but this is not empirically validated.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper observes no consistent trend across CPT models but does not isolate or measure the specific data characteristics (e.g., proportion of culturally relevant safety examples, toxicity filtering rigor) that might explain the variance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A retrospective analysis of the training corpora used for the evaluated CPT models (e.g., SeaLLMs, Typhoon2, OpenThaiGPT), quantifying features like cultural content diversity, safety-related data volume, and adversarial sample exposure, then correlating these metrics with ASR on ThaiSafetyBench.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do more sophisticated, culturally targeted jailbreak techniques—beyond simple prompt-based attacks—affect LLM safety in Thai contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 9 ("Limitation and Future Work") explicitly states that evaluation used only "simple prompt based jailbreak attempts" and identifies exploring "more sophisticated jailbreaking techniques, particularly those targeting culturally sensitive LLM safety" as a critical future direction.
- **Why unresolved:** The benchmark's methodology is limited to static, single-turn malicious prompts, leaving unassessed the model's resilience against iterative, multi-modal, or context-aware attacks that exploit deep cultural scripts or real-time conversational manipulation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and evaluation of a suite of advanced jailbreak methods (e.g., role-playing with culturally specific authority figures, narrative-based coercion, or code-switching obfuscation) on ThaiSafetyBench, measuring ASR differentials and analyzing attack pathways that bypass existing safeguards.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent does the exclusive focus on malicious prompts in ThaiSafetyBench obscure the trade-off between safety (harm rejection) and helpfulness (task utility) in Thai-tuned LLMs?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 9 ("Limitation and Future Work") acknowledges that the benchmark "does not account for overly restrictive models that may achieve high scores by rejecting most prompts but lack practical utility" and does not evaluate "the usefulness or informativeness of jailbroken responses."
- **Why unresolved:** High ASR might indicate robustness or simply over-alignment that cripples usability; without measuring model performance on benign Thai-language tasks, it is impossible to gauge whether safety tuning has degraded general capability or introduced unnecessary refusal bias in culturally normative queries.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting a paired evaluation where models are scored on both ThaiSafetyBench ASR and a suite of helpfulness metrics (e.g., accuracy, coherence, completeness) on a set of benign, culturally relevant Thai queries, then analyzing correlations and failure modes where safety and utility conflict.

## Limitations
- **Cultural validity:** Thai-specific prompts were curated by annotators but lack independent expert validation of authenticity and harmfulness; the withheld "Monarchy" category further limits coverage.
- **Judge reliability:** Though GPT-4.1 and Gemini-2.5-Pro show high inter-correlation, neither was explicitly evaluated against human Thai safety judgments; their understanding of nuanced cultural harm is unverified.
- **Classifer generalizability:** The ThaiSafetyClassifier's 84.4% weighted F1 overall may mask category-specific failures, especially on culturally nuanced subareas.

## Confidence
- Thai-specific attacks expose unique vulnerabilities: **Medium**
- LLM-as-a-judge provides reliable automated evaluation: **Low to Medium**
- DeBERTa classifier matches judge cost-effectively: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Human audit of prompt cultural specificity:** Recruit 3–5 Thai cultural experts to rate 100 random Thai-specific prompts for authenticity and harmfulness; compute Krippendorff's α > 0.8 to validate cultural grounding.
2. **Human-LLM judge alignment study:** Collect human safety judgments on 200 balanced prompt-response pairs; measure Cohen's κ between humans and each judge; κ > 0.7 needed to support judge reliability.
3. **Category-wise classifier failure analysis:** Evaluate ThaiSafetyClassifier on the held-out test set per risk area; if any category's F1 drops >4 percentage points from overall, examine misclassifications to determine cultural nuance failures.
