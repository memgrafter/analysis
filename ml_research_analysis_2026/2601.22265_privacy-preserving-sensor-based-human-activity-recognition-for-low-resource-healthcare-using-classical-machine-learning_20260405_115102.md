# Privacy-Preserving Sensor-Based Human Activity Recognition for Low-Resource Healthcare Using Classical Machine Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.22265
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22265
- **Reference count**: 1
- **Primary result**: STM achieves 96.67% test accuracy (98.50% CV) and outperforms SVM (93.33%) and others (91.11%); federated model reaches 98.74% accuracy, demonstrating high performance with on-device training for low-resource healthcare.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles inadequate medical infrastructure for home-based elderly care by proposing a low-cost human activity recognition (HAR) framework using wearable inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensors. The core method introduces a Support Tensor Machine (STM) that operates on tensor representations to preserve spatio-temporal dynamics of multi-axis sensor data, and integrates Federated Learning (FL) to keep raw patient data private. On the UCI HAR dataset, STM achieves 96.67% test accuracy and 98.50% cross-validation accuracy, significantly surpassing vector-based classifiers like SVM (93.33%). The federated variant attains 98.74% accuracy, indicating that on-device training without data centralization is viable for resource-constrained healthcare settings.

## Method Summary
The HAR task uses accelerometer and gyroscope data from wearable sensors, though the study focuses on accelerometer-only for simplicity. Data is drawn from the UCI HAR dataset, segmented into fixed-length windows (e.g., 128 samples at 50 Hz). Preprocessing includes a moving average filter and normalization. Classical classifiers (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, SVM) are evaluated as baselines. The proposed Support Tensor Machine (STM) represents each window as a multi-order tensor (samples × time‑steps × axes) and learns a classifier via iterative mode‑wise optimization that maintains temporal and axis relationships. For privacy, a Federated Learning simulation distributes training across 10 clients (subject-wise non‑IID partitions) using FedAvg, with local models trained on-device and only weights aggregated centrally. The system is designed for low‑cost hardware (NodeMCU ESP8266) and emphasizes computational feasibility.

## Key Results
- STM test accuracy: **96.67%**, cross‑validation accuracy: **98.50%**, significantly outperforming SVM (93.33%) and other classical models (91.11%).
- Federated learning with STM (or a classical local model) achieves **98.74%** global accuracy.
- The framework targets low‑resource settings by using inexpensive sensors and classical ML, avoiding heavy deep learning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Tensor Representation Preserves Spatio‑Temporal Structure
- **Claim:** The Support Tensor Machine (STM) outperforms vector‑based classifiers (e.g., SVM) by preserving the intrinsic spatio‑temporal structure of multi‑axis IMU sensor data through tensor operations.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of flattening multi‑dimensional time‑series data (e.g., `[x_acc, y_acc, z_acc]` over time) into a long feature vector, STM represents the data as a third‑order tensor (samples × time‑steps × axes). It performs classification by learning a tensor‑based hyperplane via iterative mode‑wise optimization (in a Tucker decomposition context), maintaining relationships between temporal steps and sensor axes that are lost during vectorization.
- **Core assumption:** The discriminative dynamics of human activities (e.g., the coordinated pattern of `x,y,z` acceleration during walking vs. stairs) are best captured by a multi‑way data structure, and this structure is predictive.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states "STM leverages tensor representations to preserve spatio‑temporal motion dynamics"; Section II.B & II.C provide mathematical formulation of tensors (Eq. 8), tensor distance (Eqs. 9‑12), and STM optimization (Eqs. 13‑16) that operates directly on `χ ∈ R^{I1×I2×…×IN}`.
- **Break condition:** If per‑class confusion matrices (e.g., between "Walking Upstairs" and "Walking Downstairs") remain high despite tensor modeling, it would indicate the tensor structure alone is insufficient to capture subtle discriminatory features.

### Mechanism 2: Federated Learning Enables Privacy‑Preserving Distributed Training
- **Claim:** Federated Learning (FL) enables privacy‑preserving, scalable model training for low‑resource settings by keeping raw patient data on‑device and only sharing model updates.
- **Mechanism:** Each client device (e.g., patient's smartphone) trains a local model on its private IMU data. Only the model weights/gradients are sent to a central server for aggregation (FedAvg). The global model is then redistributed. This prevents raw sensitive motion data from leaving the user's device.
- **Core assumption:** Model updates (gradients/weights) do not inadvertently leak reconstructable information about the original training data (membership inference attacks are infeasible).
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract mentions "Federated Learning for privacy" and "on‑device training suitable for low‑resource healthcare"; Section II.A & III.I describe the FL simulation across 10 clients, FedAvg, and final accuracy of 98.74%.
- **Break condition:** If empirical privacy audits (e.g., gradient inversion attacks) demonstrate that raw activity sequences can be reconstructed from shared model updates, the privacy claim is broken.

### Mechanism 3: Classical ML and Lightweight FL Ensure Low‑Resource Feasibility
- **Claim:** The combination of classical ML models and lightweight FL makes the system computationally feasible for low‑resource, edge‑deployed healthcare scenarios.
- **Mechanism:** Classical models (LR, RF, SVM, STM) have lower computational and memory footprints compared to deep neural networks. FL distributes training load across client devices, avoiding centralized compute costs. The IMU sensor (NodeMCU ESP8266) and preprocessing (moving average) are low‑power.
- **Core assumption:** The feature space derived from raw IMU data (via preprocessing) is linearly/tensor‑separable enough that complex, compute‑heavy deep learning is unnecessary for high accuracy.
- **Evidence anchors:** The introduction and Section II.A explicitly contrast with image‑based methods (high cost/compute) and state the use of "classical classifiers" and an inexpensive NodeMCU ESP8266.
- **Break condition:** If test accuracy drops significantly (e.g., >5%) when deploying the full pipeline (preprocessing + STM inference) on the actual target edge hardware, the low‑resource claim is invalidated.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Sensing Layer (IMU on NodeMCU ESP8266) -> Edge Client Layer (Mobile app) -> Server/Aggregation Layer (Central server) -> Model Layer (STM and classical baselines) -> Application Layer (Alert/Feedback)

- **Critical path:** Sensor data collection → Wireless transmission → Mobile app preprocessing → (FL: Local STM training → Update upload → Server aggregation → Global model download) → Inference → User alert

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Accuracy vs. Privacy/Compute: STM vs. Deep Learning. STM offers high accuracy with lower compute, but deep models might capture more complex patterns at higher cost.
  - Centralized vs. Federated: Centralized training (reported STM 96.67%) is simpler but requires data pooling. FL (98.74%) adds communication overhead and complexity for privacy.
  - Sensor selection: Using only accelerometer reduces data dimension and power vs. fusing gyroscope/magnetometer, potentially sacrificing some discriminatory power.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - High inter‑class confusion in specific activities (e.g., stairs) → Model or feature set inadequate.
  - FL convergence stagnation or accuracy drop vs. centralized → Poor local data distribution (Non‑IID), communication overhead, or aggregation issues.
  - High latency in mobile inference → STM implementation not optimized for mobile CPU/memory.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate centralized STM: Implement STM using a tensor library (e.g., TensorFlow/PyTorch tensor ops), train on the paper's feature set, and verify test accuracy ~96.67% on the same data split.
  2. Validate FL simulation: Simulate 10 clients with Non‑IID data partitions (by participant), run FedAvg for 10 rounds, and confirm final global model accuracy reaches ~98.74%. Monitor per‑client accuracy to diagnose data heterogeneity issues.
  3. Profile on‑target inference: Deploy the trained STM model (converted to TFLite/ONNX) on a device matching NodeMCU ESP8266 capabilities. Measure inference latency and memory footprint to validate "low‑resource" claim.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Generalization to diverse elderly populations** – How well does STM perform on larger, more heterogeneous groups with varying mobility impairments (e.g., Parkinson’s, arthritis)? The study used only 15 participants, limiting robustness claims for the target population.
2. **Impact of excluding gyroscope data** – Does accelerometer‑only suffice for distinguishing complex activities like walking upstairs vs. downstairs? The paper claims it does but provides no rigorous comparison against fused accelerometer‑gyroscope tensors; persistent stair confusion suggests gyroscope could help.
3. **Real‑world feasibility of federated STM** – What are the privacy‑utility trade‑offs and system performance (battery, latency) when deploying on low‑cost, battery‑powered wearables with intermittent connectivity? The simulation assumes ideal conditions; empirical deployment studies are needed.

## Limitations

- **STM implementation unspecified** – Tensor construction (order, reshape from UCI features) and hyperparameters (C, γ, solver) are unclear, hindering faithful replication of the 96.67% result.
- **FL privacy and accuracy skepticism** – The reported 98.74% FL accuracy is exceptionally high and may indicate simulation flaws (e.g., IID partitioning, data leakage). No privacy audit (e.g., gradient inversion attacks) supports the "privacy‑preserving" claim.
- **Low‑resource claim unverified** – Computational feasibility is assumed but not measured; actual on‑device inference latency, memory footprint, and power consumption during continuous HAR are missing.
