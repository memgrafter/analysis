# BD-Merging: Bias-Aware Dynamic Model Merging with Evidence-Guided Contrastive Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03920
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03920
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Under severe corruption (Level L3), BD-Merging (layer-wise) achieves 75.36% average accuracy, reducing the performance drop by >1.8% over Twin-Merging; on unseen tasks, it attains 55.01% average accuracy, outperforming Twin-Merging (53.03%) and AdaMerging (49.83%).

## Executive Summary
BD-Merging addresses the unreliability of existing Model Merging (MM) methods under test-time distribution shift, such as data corruption or unseen tasks. It introduces a bias‑aware framework that integrates a joint evidential head to model uncertainty over a unified label space, an Adjacency Discrepancy Score (ADS) to quantify local evidential alignment among neighboring samples, and a discrepancy‑aware contrastive learning mechanism. This trains a debiased router that dynamically allocates merging weights on a per‑sample basis. Evaluated on eight image classification benchmarks with realistic corruptions and unseen tasks, BD‑Merging shows superior robustness and generalization, approaching the performance of individual fine‑tuned models at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Method Summary
BD‑Merging comprises three core components. First, a joint evidential head is attached to a pretrained backbone and trained on unannotated auxiliary data to output Dirichlet concentration parameters over a unified label space, capturing cross‑task semantic dependencies and sample‑level uncertainty via an entropy loss and an Inverse Correlation Loss. Second, for each sample, an Adjacency Discrepancy Score (ADS) is computed against its feature‑space neighbors, combining measures of evidence sharpness, semantic divergence, and belief‑level disagreement to quantify local evidential alignment. Third, a lightweight router (two‑layer MLP) is trained using a contrastive loss that partitions neighbors into positive and negative sets according to ADS, aligning consistent samples and separating conflicting ones while regularizing with an unsupervised entropy loss. The router then produces sample‑wise merging weights, which are combined with pre‑computed task vectors to form a dynamic merged model for each input.

## Key Results
- Under Level L3 corruption, the layer‑wise variant of BD‑Merging achieves 75.36% average accuracy, reducing the performance drop by over 1.8% compared to the strongest baseline (Twin‑Merging).
- On unseen tasks, BD‑Merging attains 55.01% average accuracy, significantly outperforming Twin‑Merging (53.03%) and AdaMerging (49.83%).
- The approach approaches the performance of individual fine‑tuned models while requiring substantially lower computational cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The robustness of BD‑Merging stems from three synergistic mechanisms.

1. **Joint evidential head captures cross‑task dependencies and uncertainty.**  
   The head outputs Dirichlet parameters α for L unified classes, trained with an entropy loss and an Inverse Correlation Loss that forces the Inter‑class Evidential Contrast (IEC) to be inversely related to total uncertainty u. IEC incorporates competition among top‑k predicted classes, making uncertainty sensitive to semantic ambiguity across tasks. This enables the model to express fine‑grained uncertainty under distribution shift. Evidence: the abstract and Section 4.2 define IEC and `LInv`; ablations show the evidential head improves calibration.

2. **Adjacency Discrepancy Score (ADS) quantifies local evidential alignment.**  
   For a sample *xᵢ*, ADS with neighbor *xₖ* is the product of three factors computed over the adjacency set *Aᵣ(xᵢ)*: Sharpness (overall evidence concentration), Divergence (semantic mismatch between *xᵢ* and neighbors), and Confidence (belief‑level disagreement weighted by mutual confidence). High ADS indicates potential conflict or shift, serving as a proxy for sample reliability. Evidence: Section 4.3 defines ADS; Table 3 ablation shows removing `Div` hurts corruption robustness most.

3. **Discrepancy‑aware contrastive learning trains the debiased router.**  
   Guided by ADS, each anchor’s neighbors are partitioned into positives (low ADS) and negatives (high ADS). A contrastive loss pulls together representations of positives and pushes apart negatives, while an unsupervised entropy loss regularizes the router’s merging weights. This optimizes the router to allocate weights dynamically, aligning consistent samples and separating conflicting ones, thus mitigating bias. Evidence: abstract and Section 4.4 describe the loss; Figure 6 shows router weight adaptation; ablations (Table 3) confirm the importance of `L_Dis`.

## Foundational Learning
- **Evidential Deep Learning (EDL)** – Provides a principled way to quantify uncertainty via evidence (Dirichlet parameters). Needed because BD‑Merging’s core relies on fine‑grained uncertainty estimation over a unified space. Quick check: How does the Dirichlet concentration parameter *S* relate to confidence and uncertainty in EDL?
- **Contrastive Learning** – Enables learning representations by distinguishing positive from negative pairs. Needed because BD‑Merging uses contrastive loss (`L_Dis`) to train the router. Quick check: What is the effect of the temperature parameter τ, and how does BD‑Merging’s partition function differ from standard contrastive setups?
- **Dynamic/Per‑Sample Routing in Multi‑Task Models** – Allows conditional computation based on input features. Needed because static merging weights cannot handle test‑time shift; the router must adapt to each sample. Quick check: Why can’t a single set of merging weights (e.g., Task Arithmetic) effectively handle distribution shift?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Pretrained backbone θ₀ → Joint evidential head (outputs α, embeddings H) → ADS module (computes dᵢₖ over neighbors in H‑space) → Debiased router R(H) (outputs sample‑wise weights wₖ) → Merged parameters θ* = θ₀ + Σ wₖ τₖ.

**Critical path:**  
1. Train evidential head on auxiliary dataset D_aux using unsupervised loss `L_Head`.  
2. For all samples in D_aux, compute H (last‑layer tokens) and evidential outputs.  
3. Build adjacency sets Ar(xᵢ) via radius‑r k‑NN search in H‑space; compute ADS matrix {dᵢₖ}.  
4. Train router R by minimizing `L_BD = L_Unsup + η L_Dis`; for each anchor, partition neighbors into positives/negatives using threshold ϵ, compute contrastive loss, backprop through R and the merged model f_θ*.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Adjacency radius *r*: Small *r* increases sensitivity to noise; large *r* mixes distinct semantics.  
- ADS threshold ϵ: Controls positive/negative split; should be set as a quantile (e.g., 20th percentile) of ADS distribution on a clean validation set.  
- Router capacity: A two‑layer MLP is used; deeper routers risk overfitting to D_aux.  
- Computation: ADS requires pairwise distances O(N²); for large D_aux, approximate nearest‑neighbor search (e.g., FAISS) is needed, introducing possible noise.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Evidential head collapse: all samples exhibit high uncertainty and low IEC → ADS becomes uninformative.  
- ADS non‑discriminative: most dᵢₖ values similar → contrastive partition fails.  
- Router weight stagnation: all wₖ nearly identical → router does not adapt.  
- Degradation on unseen tasks: performance falls below individual fine‑tuned models, indicating overfitting to seen auxiliary domains.

**First 3 experiments for a new instance:**  
1. **Evidential calibration check:** On a held‑out clean set from D_aux, plot reliability diagrams for predictive probabilities. Verify that uncertainty *u* rises monotonically with synthetic corruption level.  
2. **ADS behavior under controlled shift:** For a clean sample and its neighbors, corrupt the anchor only and recompute ADS; ADS should increase significantly relative to clean neighbors.  
3. **Router adaptation ablation:** Train router ablating either `L_Dis` or `L_Unsup`; expect removal of `L_Dis` to most harm robustness, removal of `L_Unsup` to harm overall performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Theoretical bounds for robustness:** What theoretical factors bound the robustness of model merging under distribution shift, and can evidential uncertainty provide probabilistic guarantees for merged predictions? The paper lacks formal analysis linking evidence to robustness bounds.
2. **Auxiliary data influence:** How does the distributional alignment and size of the auxiliary dataset affect the debiased router’s generalization to unseen tasks? The router may overfit to seen domains; systematic study of auxiliary data characteristics is needed.
3. **Unified label space interference:** For tasks with high semantic dissimilarity, does the joint evidential head’s unified label space introduce interference that degrades uncertainty estimation and merging performance? The paper assumes cross‑task dependencies are always beneficial and does not test task similarity effects.

## Limitations
- Task vector composition is ambiguous: it is unclear whether merging includes only backbone parameters or also task‑specific heads; heads have incompatible dimensions across tasks.
- Hyperparameters for ADS (radius *r*, threshold ϵ) and contrastive temperature τ are not specified; they must be set manually or tuned.
- ADS computation scales quadratically with auxiliary set size; approximation details (e.g., using FAISS) are omitted and may introduce noise.
- The unified label space assumption may not hold for tasks with disjoint ontologies, potentially harming evidential head calibration.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Joint evidential head): Medium – supported by loss design and ablations, but calibration depends on auxiliary data and label space.
- Mechanism 2 (Adjacency Discrepancy Score): Medium – equations and ablations back it, yet sensitive to *r*, ϵ, and feature smoothness.
- Mechanism 3 (Contrastive router): Medium – contrastive loss improves robustness, but relies heavily on ADS quality and thresholding.
