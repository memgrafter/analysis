# BiMotion: B-spline Motion for Text-guided Dynamic 3D Character Generation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.18873
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18873
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: State-of-the-art text-guided motion generation for 3D characters with continuous B-spline representation, achieving top VBench scores and user preference.

## Executive Summary
BiMotion introduces a novel continuous B-spline motion representation to overcome limitations of fixed-length discrete frame inputs in text-guided 3D character animation. By compressing variable-length motion into a fixed set of control points via a Laplacian-regularized solver, the method enables the generation of sequences of arbitrary length within a VAE-latent diffusion framework. Integrated components—multi-level control-point embedding, normal fusion, and geometry-aware losses—ensure fidelity to the input shape and motion semantics. Trained on a new 39K-sequence dataset (BIMO), BiMotion significantly outperforms prior work across automatic metrics (VBench) and user studies, while reducing computational overhead.

## Method Summary
BiMotion represents per-vertex displacement trajectories using cubic B-spline curves. A closed-form Laplacian-regularized least-squares fit compresses variable-length raw motion into a fixed number (k=16) of control points. These control points undergo a hierarchical multi-level embedding that separates coarse and fine motion components. Surface normals are fused with positional features via a cosine-similarity weighting to preserve geometric detail, while correspondence and local rigidity losses enforce adherence to the source mesh. The B-spline VAE encodes control points into a latent space. A diffusion model (flow matching) conditioned on the initial mesh latent and text prompts generates new motion latents, which are decoded back to control points and reprojected to vertex sequences of any length. Training uses the new BIMO dataset (38,944 sequences) with losses: reconstruction (Charbonnier), correspondence, rigidity, KL divergence, and flow-matching.

## Key Results
- VBench: Overall Consistency (OC) 0.187, Subject Consistency (SC) 0.948, Temporal Flickering (TF) 0.995, Aesthetic Quality (AQ) 0.529, Dynamic Degree (DD) 0.800.
- User study: Text-Agreement (TA) 4.095, Motion Plausibility (MP) 4.062, Motion Expressiveness (ME) 4.048 (5-point scale).
- Efficiency: reduced generation time and GPU memory compared to AnimateAnyMesh, GVFDiffusion, V2M4.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
We detail three core mechanisms that enable BiMotion's performance.

### Mechanism 1: Continuous B-spline compression
Claim: Representing motion with continuous differentiable B-spline curves compresses variable-length sequences into a fixed set of control points while preserving full motion semantics.
Mechanism: Each vertex's displacement over time is modeled as a cubic B-spline. Variable-length raw displacements are fit via a least-squares problem with Laplacian regularization, yielding a fixed number of control points. The B-spline basis permits sampling at any temporal resolution, enabling generation of arbitrary-length sequences from the same representation.
Evidence: Abstract and Section 3.4 present the closed-form solver; related work (AnimateAnyMesh, GVFDiffusion) rely on discrete frames, underscoring the novelty. Ablations (Fig. 8) show superior detail preservation.
Break conditions: Highly discontinuous motions or extremely long sequences with intricate detail may require more control points or risk oversmoothing.

### Mechanism 2: Multi-level hierarchical control-point embedding
Claim: Decomposing control points into multiple resolutions captures both global trends and local variations more effectively than standard positional encodings.
Mechanism: Control points are hierarchically decomposed using linear transport operators derived from the B-spline basis. High-frequency residuals from each level are stacked with the coarsest coefficients, yielding a feature vector that explicitly separates motion across scales.
Evidence: Section 3.5 describes the decomposition and matrices; Fig. 8 shows that frequency-based position encoding fails on fine details (e.g., lion's tail) while the proposed method succeeds.
Break condition: Predominantly low-frequency motions may not benefit from extra levels, incurring overhead.

### Mechanism 3: Normal fusion and geometry-aware losses
Claim: Normal fusion and the correspondence and local rigidity losses ensure generated motion adheres to the input shape's geometry and maintains plausible local structure.
Mechanism: Surface normals are embedded via an MLP and fused with point coordinate features using per-point cosine-similarity weights. Correspondence loss minimizes reprojected vertex differences against ground truth; local rigidity loss preserves pairwise distances between neighboring vertices (inspired by skeletal rigging).
Evidence: Abstract and Section 3.5 define these components; Figs. 7 & 9 ablations show that removing normal fusion causes parts to stick together and removing rigidity loss causes unnatural stretching.
Break condition: Motions with significant elastic deformation may be overly restricted by rigidity; noisy or missing normals degrade fusion.

## Foundational Learning
Three key concepts underpin BiMotion:

- **B-spline curves**: Continuous polynomial curves defined by control points and basis functions. *Why needed*: They provide a compact, differentiable representation of motion that can be sampled at arbitrary temporal resolutions. *Quick check*: What are the continuity and support properties of cubic B-splines that make them suitable for smooth motion fitting?

- **Variational Autoencoder (VAE) and latent diffusion**: Probabilistic generative models that learn a latent space and denoise via diffusion. *Why needed*: The VAE compresses motion control points into a latent code; the diffusion model generates new latents conditioned on text and the initial mesh. *Quick check*: How does the VAE handle variable-length motion through the B-spline representation, and how is the latent normalized for diffusion training?

- **Point cloud processing (FPS, attention)**: Techniques to handle unstructured point sets. *Why needed*: After normal fusion, the dense point cloud is compressed via farthest point sampling (FPS) to a fixed number of tokens, and cross-attention fuses local and global context. *Quick check*: Why is FPS applied to the normal-fused features rather than raw vertex positions, and how does cross-attention integrate spatial compression?

## Architecture Onboarding
The pipeline processes an initial mesh and text to produce an animated sequence.

**Component map:**
Initial mesh (vertices V⁰, faces F⁰) + text → B-spline fitting on ΔV_t → control points Pᵏ → multi-level embedding + normal fusion → point features F₀, Fₖ → FPS → n′=512 tokens → cross-attention → VAE encoder → latent zᵏ (and z⁰ from initial mesh) → latent normalization → DiT velocity network (flow matching) conditioned on z⁰ and text → sampled latent ẑᵏ → VAE decoder → predicted control points P̂ᵏ → B-spline reprojection B_{T,k} → ΔV̂^{1:T} → final mesh sequence M^{1:T} = V⁰ + ΔV̂.

**Critical path:**
1. Train B-spline VAE: For each batch, fit B-splines to ground-truth ΔV to obtain control points; compute multi-level embedding, normal fusion, FPS; encode to latent; optimize reconstruction (Charbonnier), correspondence, rigidity, and KL losses.
2. Train diffusion generator: Normalize VAE latents (running mean/std); train DiT with flow-matching loss using concatenated [z⁰, z_τ] and CLIP text embeddings; classifier-free guidance dropout.
3. Inference: Encode initial mesh to z⁰; sample z̃ᵏ from noise via ODE; decode to control points; reproject to ΔV for target T.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Control points k: Larger k improves fidelity but increases latent dimension and memory; fixed k=16 used.
- Normal fusion vs. mesh connectivity: Normals are topology-independent and robust; may lose thin-structure details compared to mesh edges.
- Flow matching vs. DDPM: Faster sampling but requires careful latent normalization; guidance strength γ=3.0.
- B-spline degree (cubic): Ensures C² continuity; lower degrees give more local control but less smoothness.

**Failure signatures:**
- Oversmoothing: Motion too fluid, losing sharp transitions → likely too few control points or high Laplacian weight μ.
- Shape collapse: Generated meshes distort or lose identity → check VAE reconstruction loss, normal fusion integrity, rigidity loss weighting.
- Text misalignment: Motion doesn’t match prompt → inspect text embedding dropout, classifier-free guidance, conditioning noise.
- Topology artifacts: Strange deformations on meshes with degenerate normals → validate normal consistency and outward orientation.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Reconstruction vs. control points**: Vary k (4,8,16,32) on validation; plot L1 reprojection error vs. k to characterize diminishing returns.
2. **Normal fusion ablation**: Train VAE without normals, with naive concatenation, and with proposed cosine-weighted fusion; compare reconstruction loss and downstream VBench scores.
3. **Laplacian regularization effect**: Fit B-splines to short sequences (T<k) using (i) no regularization, (ii) L2 ridge, (iii) Laplacian; visualize interpolated motion to assess naturalness and artifact suppression.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **OQ1**: How can BiMotion handle topological changes (e.g., cloth tearing) while keeping a compact B-spline representation? The fixed-mesh assumption limits such motions.
- **OQ2**: What is the optimal strategy for choosing the number of control points? A fixed k=16 may be suboptimal for different motion frequencies or complexities.
- **OQ3**: Can the framework scale to very long sequences (thousands of frames) without losing coherence? Current evaluations cap at 200 frames.
- **OQ4**: Are there alternative continuous representations (e.g., NURBS, neural implicits) that could outperform uniform cubic B-splines for this task?

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters (μ, model dimensions, loss weights, training schedule) are unreported, hindering exact replication.
- Normal fusion details (e.g., weighting function) are ambiguous; performance on meshes with poor normals is uncertain.
- B-spline parameterization (uniform in frame index vs. continuous time) not fully specified.
- The BIMO dataset is not public; surrogate data may not match its distribution, possibly affecting comparisons.
- The method assumes fixed mesh topology and may degrade on discontinuous or highly elastic motions.

## Confidence
- B-spline compression: High – clear mathematical formulation and closed-form solver.
- Multi-level embedding: Medium – ablations show benefit but hierarchical choices not fully justified.
- Normal fusion & geometry losses: High – strong ablation evidence and well-described mechanisms.
- SOTA claims: Medium – rely on unreleased BIMO and unspecified hyperparameters; reproduction with other data may not match metrics.

## Next Checks
1. **μ Sensitivity Sweep**: Fit B-splines on a fixed validation set across μ ∈ {1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1}. Measure reprojection L1 error and visual smoothness to identify optimal range and oversmoothing thresholds.
2. **Normal Fusion Ablation**: Compare three settings: (a) no normals, (b) naive concatenation, (c) proposed cosine-weighted fusion. Evaluate VAE reconstruction (Charbonnier) and VBench subject consistency to isolate fusion impact.
3. **Laplacian vs. Ridge Regularization**: For sequences with abrupt transitions, fit B-splines using (i) Laplacian, (ii) L2 ridge, (iii) none. Quantify temporal gradient smoothness and user-rated plausibility to validate Laplacian’s advantage.
