# Benchmarking Legal RAG: The Promise and Limits of AI Statutory Surveys

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03300
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03300
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** STARA achieves 83% accuracy on LaborBench, substantially outperforming Westlaw AI (58%) and Lexis+ AI (64%); corrected accuracy rises to 92% after accounting for omissions in the Department of Labor ground truth.

## Executive Summary
This study benchmarks three legal retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems on LaborBench, a dataset of 1,647 Boolean questions about U.S. state unemployment insurance laws. STARA, a domain-specific system that preserves statutory hierarchy and uses strategic regex filtering, achieves 83% accuracy, while commercial tools Westlaw AI and Lexis+ AI perform significantly lower. Error analysis reveals that 75% of STARA’s apparent false positives are actually valid statutory provisions missing from the expert-compiled ground truth, raising its corrected accuracy to 92%. The findings demonstrate that architecture tailored to legal structure yields more reliable multi-jurisdictional surveys than generic AI, and highlight the inherent incompleteness of even expert legal benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses LaborBench, featuring questions with contextual prompts and binary labels derived from a Department of Labor (DOL) manual compilation. STARA preprocesses a corpus of state statutes (StatReg) to preserve hierarchical structure (titles, chapters, sections, cross-references), applies regex filters to narrow candidate provisions per question batch, and uses an LLM to classify relevance. Commercial systems are tested via their standard user interfaces/APIs without modification. Primary metrics are accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 against DOL labels, complemented by manual verification of false positives to separate system errors from ground truth omissions.

## Key Results
- STARA: 83% accuracy (81% F1); after re-verification, 92% corrected accuracy.
- Westlaw AI: 58% accuracy (64% F1), hampered by a 300-character input limit.
- Lexis+ AI: 64% accuracy (41% F1), with inconsistent answers across query modes.
- 75% of STARA’s apparent false positives (135/181) reflect provisions omitted from the DOL compilation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Statutory hierarchy preservation
- **Claim:** Domain-specific preprocessing that maintains statutory hierarchy and cross-references improves retrieval accuracy for multi-jurisdictional legal questions.
- **Mechanism:** STARA parses and segments statutes while retaining hierarchical metadata (titles, chapters, sections, subsections) and augments provisions with contextual elements like definitions and cross-references. This enriched representation allows the LLM to interpret legal concepts in their structural context (e.g., an exception in a subsection modifying the parent rule).
- **Core assumption:** Statutory meaning is shaped by nested organization and referential networks; flattening structure loses essential semantics.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract explicitly notes domain-specific preprocessing outperforms generic methods; Section 2.3 details the multi-stage process preserving hierarchy and adding context.
- **Break condition:** If statutory structures vary wildly across jurisdictions or if legal reasoning depends primarily on extrinsic materials (case law, regulations) not in statutes.

### Mechanism 2: Regex-based filtering trade-off
- **Claim:** Strategic regex filters balance computational feasibility with retrieval completeness in large-scale statutory analysis.
- **Mechanism:** STARA applies optional regex patterns to narrow the search space to provisions likely relevant to a batch of questions (e.g., unemployment insurance terms, keywords like "voluntary contribution"). This reduces the volume of provisions requiring expensive LLM classification while attempting to retain all genuinely relevant text.
- **Core assumption:** Statutory language often contains consistent technical terms and formulaic phrasing, allowing lexical filters to approximate semantic relevance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.1 describes the filter application and its speed/completeness tradeoff; Appendix B provides concrete filter examples.
- **Break condition:** If statutory drafting shows high lexical variance—provisions use unexpected synonyms or phrasing—filters may systematically exclude relevant text (as seen for part-time work search questions).

### Mechanism 3: Ground truth fallibility
- **Claim:** Perceived accuracy of legal AI systems is constrained by ground truth completeness; apparent errors may reflect omissions in expert compilations rather than system failures.
- **Mechanism:** Manual re-verification of STARA’s apparent false positives against raw statutes found many to be valid provisions missed by the DOL compilation. Correcting for these omissions boosted STARA’s accuracy from 83% to 92%.
- **Core assumption:** Even exhaustive, expert-compiled statutory surveys are incomplete due to fifty-state scale, drafting variation, and scattered placement of concepts.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.3.2 quantifies omissions (135/181 false positives); notes variation in drafting and scale as root causes.
- **Break condition:** If ground truth compilations use systematic, verified methods that exhaustively cross-check all jurisdictions and sections, and AI systems still underperform after re-verification.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Statutory hierarchy and cross-reference resolution
- **Why needed here:** Legal codes are deeply nested (titles > chapters > sections > subsections) with numerous internal references. Misinterpreting a provision’s scope by ignoring its parent or referenced sections causes reasoning errors. STARA’s design explicitly preserves this graph-like structure.
- **Quick check:** If a statute says "Except as provided in § 456, employers must...", why must the system retrieve and analyze § 456 to answer whether an employer must comply?

### Concept: Retrieval-compute trade-offs in large corpora
- **Why needed here:** Processing all statutes for fifty states is expensive. STARA uses regex filters to limit the candidate set, introducing a precision-recall trade-off. Understanding this trade-off is key to interpreting false negatives versus computational constraints.
- **Quick check:** Would a narrower regex filter (requiring the exact phrase "voluntary contribution") increase or decrease the chance of missing a provision using synonymous language like "optional payment"?

### Concept: Ground truth fallibility in legal benchmarks
- **Why needed here:** The DOL compilation was treated as gold standard but contained omissions. Accuracy metrics measure agreement with a fallible reference, not absolute correctness. Recognizing this prevents overconfidence in benchmark scores.
- **Quick check:** If an AI system finds a provision clearly in the statute but absent from the benchmark’s answer key, should this be counted as a system error? Why or why not?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Raw Statutes -> Input Preprocessor -> Preprocessed Corpus (with structure) -> RegEx Filter Engine -> Filtered Candidate Set -> Semantic Classifier -> Provision Classifications -> Output Formatter -> Final Answer Table (state-level True/False)

**Critical path:** 
1. Parse raw statutes into hierarchical provisions with cross-references.
2. For each question batch, apply regex filters to select candidate provisions.
3. Use LLM to classify each candidate’s relevance with hierarchical context.
4. Aggregate classifications into per-state binary answers and generate citations.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Filter specificity vs. recall: Tighter regex reduces compute but increases false negatives (missed provisions); looser filters increase cost and noise.
- Structure preservation vs. token limits: Including full parent and cross-referenced provisions provides context but may exceed LLM context windows, requiring truncation strategies.
- Ground truth alignment vs. legal completeness: Optimizing for DOL-style answers may under-report provisions legally valid but omitted by DOL’s methodology.

**Failure signatures:**
- Sudden recall drop on specific question types (e.g., part-time work search) suggests filter over-exclusion or linguistic mismatch.
- High false positive rate with verbose, tangential citations (as in Westlaw AI) indicates poor semantic discrimination or keyword over-reliance.
- Inconsistent answers across query modes (as in Lexis+ AI’s 50-state vs. 3-state) reveal fragility in prompt engineering or corpus scope handling.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Filter sensitivity test:** Run STARA on a single question batch with progressively broader regex filters. Measure recall change against a manually verified statute subset to assess if current filters are too narrow.
2. **Structure ablation test:** For a sample of provisions, run the classifier with and without hierarchical context (parent text, cross-references). Quantify accuracy change to validate structure preservation value.
3. **Ground truth audit:** Take a random sample of STARA’s apparent false positives and a sample of questions where STARA got "False" but DOL got "True". Manually verify both against raw statutes to estimate true DOL omission rate vs. STARA error.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Ground truth completeness:** How can we construct exhaustive and verified ground truth for multi-jurisdictional statutory surveys, given that expert compilations like the DOL report contain significant omissions? The study found 75% of STARA’s apparent false positives were valid provisions missed by DOL attorneys. Manual compilation across 50 states is inherently error-prone; no existing benchmark is known to be complete. Resolution would require a standardized protocol using multiple independent experts and AI-assisted cross-checking to produce a gold-standard benchmark for UI law.
2. **Generalizability:** Does the performance advantage of structure-aware retrieval generalize to other legal domains with different statutory structures and drafting conventions? Evaluation was limited to unemployment insurance; legal domains vary in cross-reference density, terminology, and jurisdictional consistency. Resolution requires replicating the LaborBench methodology in at least two other domains (e.g., state tax codes, environmental regulations).
3. **Evaluation frameworks:** What evaluation frameworks beyond binary accuracy can effectively measure reasoning quality, citation precision, and completeness in AI statutory surveys? Current metrics do not distinguish correct answers for wrong reasons, assess whether all relevant provisions are cited, or evaluate logical steps. Resolution would involve designing a multi-dimensional rubric (answer correctness, citation specificity, reasoning coherence) validated by legal experts and applying it to AI and human responses.
4. **Commercial tool failures:** What are the root causes of systematic reasoning errors in commercial legal AI tools (e.g., conflating legal concepts, citing irrelevant statutes), and can architectural or prompting changes mitigate these failures? Commercial systems are black boxes; errors may stem from retrieval, prompt engineering, training data biases, or input limits. Resolution requires vendor transparency (logs, confidence scores) or open-source replicas with modular components to isolate failure modes.

## Limitations
- Ground truth incompleteness: The DOL compilation, though expert-created, contains omissions, so metrics are relative to a fallible reference.
- Corpus and API stability: Results depend on the specific StatReg corpus version and commercial API behaviors (black boxes) that may change.
- Domain narrowness: LaborBench focuses on UI Boolean questions; performance on other legal domains or complex reasoning tasks is unknown.
- Scalability trade-off: STARA’s regex filtering introduces a precision-recall trade-off; optimal filter design for other statutory domains is unvalidated.

## Confidence
- **High:** Domain-specific statutory hierarchy preservation improves accuracy (strong internal evidence & ablation potential). Ground truth is fallible (direct error analysis).
- **Medium:** Regex filters provide a viable compute-accuracy trade-off (described but not rigorously benchmarked). Commercial tools are constrained by input limits (observed but not exhaustively tested).
- **Low:** STARA’s architecture generalizes beyond UI law (untested). Commercial tool failures are primarily due to input limits vs. model capability (correlation, not proven causation).

## Next Checks
1. **Filter sensitivity analysis:** Run STARA on a stratified sample of LaborBench questions with systematically relaxed regex filters. Measure recall change for question types with known low performance (e.g., part-time work search) to quantify omission rates.
2. **Hierarchical context ablation:** For a random sample of provisions, classify with/without full parent/cross-reference context. Compute accuracy delta to isolate the contribution of structure preservation.
3. **Cross-source ground truth audit:** Manually verify STARA’s apparent false positives against two sources: (a) raw state statutes and (b) an independent, updated DOL-style compilation. Quantify true omission rate vs. system error.
