# Report-based Recommendations for Policy Making and Agency Operations: Dataset and LLM Evaluation

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.20287
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20287
*   **Reference count:** 0
*   **Primary result:** LLMs (especially GPT-4o zero-shot) can generate policy recommendations rated highly by human experts for relevance to evidence (avg 2.79–2.85/3) and actionability (60% deemed actionable), but traditional lexical metrics (BLEU) fail to capture this quality.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces PubRec-Bench, a novel benchmark of 110 agency reports and 493 evidence-recommendation pairs from UK and US social care sectors, to evaluate LLMs on generating actionable policy recommendations. It finds that while zero-shot LLMs like GPT-4o produce outputs humans rate highly for evidence alignment and actionability, standard automatic evaluation metrics (e.g., BLEU) show poor correlation with human judgment. The study concludes that domain-specific prompting and evaluation are crucial for this high-stakes task, demonstrating LLMs' potential as assistive tools for policy analysts while highlighting the need for better automated assessment methods.

## Method Summary
The authors curated the PubRec-Bench dataset by scraping public reports from UK Care Inspectorate, US Children's Bureau, and NSPCC, extracting evidence passages and human-written recommendations. Three LLMs (GPT-4o, Command R+, LLaMA 3 8B) were prompted using a generic prompt (Prompt 1) and an expert-designed prompt (Prompt 2) in zero-shot and one-shot settings. Generated recommendations were evaluated via automatic metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-L, BERTScore), LLM-as-judge scoring for factual alignment, and human expert assessment across five criteria (fluency, coherence, relevance to evidence, relevance to human rec, actionability). The analysis focused on correlating automatic scores with human judgments.

## Key Results
*   GPT-4o zero-shot with the expert-designed prompt achieved the highest human-rated performance, averaging 2.79–2.85/3 for "relevance to evidence" and 60% of outputs deemed "actionable."
*   Traditional lexical metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) showed very low scores, indicating a mismatch between surface similarity and functional quality for this task.
*   LLM-based evaluators showed moderate correlation with humans but exhibited self-bias (models rated their own outputs higher), questioning their reliability as standalone judges.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Domain-tailored prompting significantly improves recommendation generation quality.
*   **Mechanism:** SME-designed prompts provide clearer structural guidance and task framing, helping the LLM focus on generating actionable policy language rather than generic text. This leverages the LLM's instruction-following capabilities while reducing ambiguity.
*   **Core assumption:** The LLM possesses relevant domain knowledge from pre-training but requires precise prompt scaffolding to access and format it appropriately for this specialized task.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 4.1]: Prompt 2 (SME-designed) consistently outperforms the generic Prompt 1 across automatic metrics and human evaluation.
    *   [abstract]: Notes the need for "domain-specific evaluation metrics," implying prompt design is a key adaptation layer.
    *   [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; mechanism is inferred from experimental results.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** LLMs can generate functionally relevant recommendations by aligning with evidence, even when lexical similarity to human-written references is low.
*   **Mechanism:** The models perform evidence-based abstraction and reformulation, identifying key constraints or actions implied in the source text and generating novel but contextually appropriate policy language. This relies on semantic understanding beyond surface n-gram matching.
*   **Core assumption:** Human "gold standard" recommendations are not the only valid output; different phrasings can be equally valid if rooted in the evidence.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 5.2]: Human evaluators rated "Relevance to the evidence" (avg 2.79-2.85/3) higher than "Relevance to the human-created recommendation" (avg ~2.5/3).
    *   [abstract]: States traditional metrics showed low scores while human evaluators rated outputs highly for relevance and actionability.
    *   [corpus]: Corpus provides evidence-recommendation pairs; the mechanism is that the LLM learns this mapping during pre-training and applies it in-context.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Performance is partly driven by the models' exposure to similar regulatory/inspection report structures and language during pre-training.
*   **Mechanism:** LLMs have ingested vast quantities of public documents, including governmental and institutional reports. This provides implicit knowledge of report conventions, recommendation phrasing patterns, and policy domain semantics, activatable via prompting.
*   **Core assumption:** The specific report types (UK Care Inspectorate, US Children's Bureau, NSPCC) or their content are represented in the models' training corpora.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 5.1]: Notes better performance on the US Children's Bureau dataset, speculating it may be due to "better represented location within the training set."
    *   [limitations]: Explicitly states "it is possible that portions of these reports were included in the pre-training data."
    *   [corpus]: No direct evidence; this is a hypothesized influence from training data distribution.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: In-Context Learning (Prompting)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper uses zero- and one-shot prompting due to limited annotated data and to mirror realistic deployment. Understanding how prompt formulation steers the model is central to the findings.
    *   **Quick check question:** Given the finding that one-shot often underperformed zero-shot, what might be a risk of using a single poorly chosen example in a high-stakes domain?
*   **Concept: Evaluation Metric Validity for NLG**
    *   **Why needed here:** The core thesis challenges the sufficiency of traditional reference-based metrics (BLEU, ROUGE). Learning why these fail and why LLM-based/human metrics correlate differently is critical.
    *   **Quick check question:** If BERTScore shows moderate correlation with humans but LLM-based evaluators show self-bias, what does this imply about choosing an "oracle" metric for this task?
*   **Concept: Human-AI Collaboration in High-Stakes Domains**
    *   **Why needed here:** The task's purpose is to "facilitate rather than replace" experts. Understanding the LLM as a different-perspective generator (higher "relevance to evidence") versus a mimic is key.
    *   **Quick check question:** Based on the human evaluation criteria, what trade-off might occur if an LLM is optimized only for "actionability" at the expense of "coherence"?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Pipeline -> Prompt Template Store -> LLM Inference API -> Evaluation Suite -> Correlation Analysis Module.

**Critical path:** 1) Curate/preprocess a new report into an evidence passage. 2) Select SME-designed prompt template. 3) Query chosen LLM (start with zero-shot). 4) Run automatic metrics. 5) For high-stakes outputs, route to human SME review using the 5-criteria form. 6) Log all inputs, outputs, and scores for correlation analysis.

**Design tradeoffs:** Speed vs. Quality (zero-shot for drafts, one-shot for refinement); Model choice (GPT-4o best but costly/closed vs. LLaMA 3 open but weaker); Evaluation depth (cheap LLM-as-judge risks self-bias; human eval gold-standard but slow/expensive). A tiered system is implied.

**Failure signatures:** 1) High BLEU/ROUGE but low human "relevance to evidence" → model copying surface patterns. 2) LLM-as-judge gives high score only to its own model's outputs → self-bias detected. 3) High "fluency/coherence" but low "actionability" → output is well-written but generic.

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Replicate Prompt Comparison:** Use Prompt 1 and Prompt 2 on a held-out sample from your target domain. Measure delta in LLM-based and human scores for "relevance to evidence" and "actionability."
2.  **Zero-shot vs. One-shot Degradation Test:** For one model (e.g., GPT-4o), generate recommendations in both settings for the same evidence set. Calculate the percentage where one-shot is rated lower by an LLM judge. If degradation >30%, investigate example mismatch.
3.  **Metric Correlation Sanity Check:** For 50 generated recommendations, compute all automatic metrics. Have one SME rate "relevance to evidence." Compute Spearman correlation. Expect BERTScore to have highest (but moderate) correlation; expect LLM-as-judge from same model to show inflated self-scores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How can evaluation metrics for policy recommendation generation be improved to better align with human judgments like actionability?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Section 6 and 5.2 note current metrics show weak/moderate correlation and fail to capture domain-specific quality.
    *   **Why unresolved:** No metric achieves high human agreement; task-specific purpose is hard to quantify automatically.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** A new metric or framework validated on PubRec-Bench with Spearman correlation >0.7 with human judgments across multiple criteria.
*   **Open Question 2:** What adaptation strategies beyond zero/one-shot prompting can improve LLM performance on evidence-based policy recommendation generation?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Section 5.1 and Limitations state prompting yields inconsistent results and more dynamic strategies are needed.
    *   **Why unresolved:** One-shot underperformance suggests naive in-context learning is insufficient for high-variability task.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical comparison of strategies (fine-tuning, RAG, dynamic example selection) showing significant gains in human-evaluated quality over zero-shot baseline.
*   **Open Question 3:** How can self-bias in LLM-based evaluators, where models rate their own outputs higher, be mitigated?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Section 5.1 documents self-bias in GPT-4 and Cohere models and calls for further research.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Self-bias threatens reliability of automated evaluation; multi-model approach still shows bias.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** A debiasing method (e.g., model ensemble, calibration) that eliminates self-preference statistically, with self-ratings aligning with third-party ratings.
*   **Open Question 4:** Do LLM-generated policy recommendations generalize to non-English or non-Western policy contexts, and how does performance vary?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Limitations state datasets are English only and findings may not generalize to non-English/Western contexts.
    *   **Why unresolved:** All data from UK/US; policy-making practices and language differ globally.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluation on a comparable non-English/Western dataset showing performance metrics; analysis of adaptation needs (e.g., multilingual fine-tuning).

## Limitations
*   Dataset availability uncertain; one-shot example selection criteria not detailed; API access for proprietary models may be costly/rate-limited.
*   LLM-as-judge prompts incompletely specified and show self-bias; traditional lexical metrics poorly correlate with human judgment.
*   Potential pre-training contamination of report data uncontrolled; human evaluation sample limited in domain and size.

## Confidence
*   Domain-tailored prompts improve recommendation quality: **High**
*   LLMs can produce evidence-aligned recommendations despite low lexical similarity: **Medium**
*   Performance partly due to exposure to similar report structures in pre-training: **Low**
*   GPT-4o zero-shot achieves high relevance/actionability ratings: **High**
