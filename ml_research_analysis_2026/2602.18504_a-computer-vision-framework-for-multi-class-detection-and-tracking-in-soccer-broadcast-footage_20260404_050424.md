# A Computer Vision Framework for Multi-Class Detection and Tracking in Soccer Broadcast Footage

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18504
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18504
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Multi-class detection and tracking pipeline from broadcast footage achieves mAP@0.5 0.871, but ball recall is only 0.511.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents a single-camera computer vision pipeline to extract soccer analytics from standard broadcast footage, addressing the barrier of expensive specialized hardware for lower-budget clubs. The system integrates YOLOv8s for multi-class object detection (players, referees, goalkeepers, ball), ByteTrack for multi-object tracking, and an unsupervised team segmentation method leveraging CLIP embeddings and K-Means clustering. The model is trained on a custom dataset from Roboflow's Football Players Detection Dataset and additional broadcast matches, using a high input resolution of 1280×1280 to improve detection of small objects.

Results show strong overall performance (mAP@0.5 0.871, precision 0.884, recall 0.828). Class-specific metrics are excellent for players (mAP@0.5 0.993, recall 0.978), goalkeepers (0.931, 0.897), and referees (0.946, 0.925). However, ball detection lags with recall 0.511 and mAP@0.5 0.616, attributed to its small size, fast motion, and frequent occlusions. The pipeline demonstrates that affordable, scalable soccer analytics are feasible from a single broadcast camera, though ball tracking remains a critical limitation for future work.

## Method Summary
The pipeline uses a YOLOv8s detector fine-tuned on a custom soccer dataset (sourced from Roboflow and supplemented with NCAA/UPSL matches) with an input resolution of 1280×1280 to enhance small object detection. During inference, frames are sampled (stride=30) and passed to YOLOv8s to obtain bounding boxes, class labels, and confidence scores. Player detections are cropped and processed by a pretrained CLIP image encoder to produce 512‑dimensional embeddings. These embeddings are reduced to three dimensions via UMAP and clustered using K‑Means (k=2) to assign team labels per frame. ByteTrack then associates detections across time, maintaining consistent track IDs. Training uses AdamW optimizer for 25 epochs with a batch size of 6, starting from Ultralytics' pretrained weights. All components except the detector are used off-the-shelf without further training.

## Key Results
- Overall mAP@0.5: 0.871; precision: 0.884; recall: 0.828.
- Players: mAP@0.5 0.993, recall 0.978; Goalkeepers: mAP@0.5 0.931, recall 0.897; Referees: mAP@0.5 0.946, recall 0.925.
- Ball: mAP@0.5 0.616, precision 0.914, recall 0.511.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The pipeline reliably detects and tracks players, goalkeepers, and referees by leveraging a modern, single-stage detector (YOLOv8s) fine-tuned on high-resolution soccer footage, combined with a robust association tracker (ByteTrack).
- **Mechanism:** YOLOv8s processes each sampled frame to generate class-specific bounding boxes. Its multi-scale feature pyramid (via C2f, SPPF, concatenation) allows it to detect objects at various sizes—critical for full-body players versus the small ball. ByteTrack then associates these detections across time using motion cues and appearance similarity, maintaining consistent IDs through brief occlusions.
- **Core assumption:** The training dataset provides sufficient diversity in player poses, kit designs, and camera angles for YOLOv8s to learn robust, class-specific features. ByteTrack's association logic effectively handles the typical densities and motions in broadcast soccer.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...combines a YOLO object detector with the ByteTrack tracking algorithm to identify and track players, referees, goalkeepers, and the ball..."
  - [section] PAGE 6, "Model Architecture" details C2f, SPPF, concatenation for multi-scale detection; PAGE 7 notes increasing resolution to 1280x1280.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., *SoccerNet-v3D*, *AutoSoccerPose*) confirm multi-scale detection and tracking as active challenges, supporting this architectural approach.
- **Break condition:** Performance degrades severely in densely packed scenes (e.g., set pieces) where inter-object occlusion exceeds ByteTrack's association threshold, causing ID switches or missed detections, especially for smaller objects.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Unsupervised team segmentation is achieved by using CLIP's pre-trained semantic embeddings to group players by jersey color, bypassing the need for manual team labeling in training.
- **Mechanism:** Detected player crops are encoded via CLIP's image encoder into a 512-dimensional appearance vector. UMAP reduces this to 3D for clustering. K-Means then partitions players into two dominant clusters (teams) based on visual similarity in the embedding space, where jersey color/texture differences are captured.
- **Core assumption:** The CLIP model's representations are semantically aligned such that differences in primary jersey color create a clusterable separation in the embedding space, even under varying illumination and minor pose variations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...using CLIP embeddings and K-Means clustering for team segmentation."
  - [section] PAGE 4, "Overall Pipeline" describes cropping, CLIP encoding, UMAP projection, and K-Means clustering.
  - [corpus] No direct support; neighbor work (e.g., *FOOTPASS*) focuses on multi-agent context, not team ID. [corpus] evidence is weak; assumption is primarily paper's own.
- **Break condition:** Under strong directional sunlight or with highly reflective kits, CLIP embeddings become dominated by lighting/shadow features rather than jersey color, causing cluster collapse or misassignment. Frame-wise clustering without temporal smoothing also allows team labels to flip.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** High-resolution input (1280x1280) specifically improves ball detection by preserving fine-grained pixel information for a small, fast-moving target.
- **Mechanism:** Upscaling from YOLO's default 640x640 increases the number of pixels representing the ball, making its contour, texture, and color more discernible to the convolutional backbone. This directly aids the detection head in localizing the ball's small bounding box with higher confidence.
- **Core assumption:** The ball's visual features (white, round, with distinct texture) are sufficiently distinctive at higher resolution to be separated from background clutter (e.g., feet, field lines) by the model.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section] PAGE 7, "Training Procedure": "To better detect small objects—specifically the ball—the input resolution was increased from the default 640×640 to 1280×1280 pixels. This higher resolution preserved finer spatial details and significantly improved ball visibility."
  - [results] PAGE 9-10, ball metrics show high precision (0.914) but low recall (0.511), indicating that when the ball is visible and resolved, it is correctly identified, but many instances remain undetected due to occlusion or size.
- **Break condition:** When the ball is heavily occluded (by players) or when motion blur becomes prominent at high speeds, increased resolution alone cannot recover the necessary pixel evidence; recall remains low. Computational cost also becomes prohibitive for real-time.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Single-Stage Object Detection (YOLO family)**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire pipeline depends on a fast, accurate detector that can localize multiple classes (players, ball, refs) per frame. Understanding anchor-free detection and the trade-off between speed (YOLOv8s) and accuracy (YOLOv8x) is key to replicating the design choice.
  - *Quick check question:* What architectural components in YOLOv8 (e.g., C2f, SPPF) specifically help with detecting objects at multiple scales, like a player at midfield versus a ball at the goalkeeper's feet?
- **Concept: Multi-Object Tracking (MOT) Association Metrics**
  - *Why needed here:* ByteTrack's core innovation is using both high- and low-confidence detections for association. The engineer must understand IoU, cosine similarity for appearance (if used), and the "matching cascade" to debug tracking failures.
  - *Quick check question:* Why does ByteTrack pair a detection with a track even if the detection confidence is low, and how does this help with handling occlusions?
- **Concept: Transfer Learning with Vision-Language Models (CLIP)**
  - *Why needed here:* CLIP's pre-trained knowledge about "jersey" or "team uniform" concepts is exploited for feature extraction without training. The engineer must know how to extract and use embeddings for a downstream clustering task.
  - *Quick check question:* If CLIP was trained on internet images of diverse objects, why should its embeddings be meaningful for clustering soccer players by jersey color, and what is a key limitation of this approach (hint: lighting)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Broadcast video → Frame Sampler (stride=30) → YOLOv8s (1280×1280) detection → Cropping → CLIP image encoder → 512-D embedding → UMAP reduction → K-Means (k=2) → ByteTrack association → Output (track_id, class, bbox, team_label)
- **Critical path:** Detector performance is the single point of failure. If YOLOv8s misses a detection (especially the ball), everything downstream fails for that object. The path is: High‑res frame → YOLOv8 detection → CLIP embed (players) → Cluster → ByteTrack ID.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Resolution vs. Speed: 1280×1280 improves ball detection but reduces FPS; 640×640 is faster but ball recall drops sharply.
  - Supervised vs. Unsupervised Team ID: CLIP/K-Means avoids labeling effort but is frame‑wise and lighting‑sensitive; a supervised team‑classifier would be more robust but requires labeled data.
  - Frame Sampling: Stride=30 reduces compute but may miss fast ball events between frames.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Ball: High precision, very low recall. Output shows sporadic ball detections; long gaps where ball is present but undetected.
  - Teams: Team labels flip rapidly for the same player across consecutive frames in sunny conditions.
  - Tracking: ID switches for players during tight scrums or after exiting/re‑entering frame.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline Detection Test:** Run YOLOv8s (pretrained soccer weights) on a sample clip at 640×640 and 1280×1280. Quantify ball recall difference to validate resolution impact.
  2. **ByteTrack Isolation:** Feed ground‑truth detections (or high‑confidence YOLO detections) into ByteTrack. Measure IDF1 and ID switches to evaluate tracker's pure association performance on this dataset's motion patterns.
  3. **CLIP Clustering Robustness:** Extract CLIP embeddings for a set of well‑lit and a set of backlit/sun‑glared player crops. Visualize UMAP projections. Does the 2‑cluster separation hold under lighting stress? This tests the core team segmentation assumption.

## Limitations
- Ball detection remains poor (recall 0.511) due to small size, frequent occlusion, and motion blur, despite high-resolution input.
- Team segmentation is performed per‑frame without temporal smoothing, leading to label flips and sensitivity to lighting conditions.
- The paper does not report quantitative accuracy for team segmentation and omits key reproduction details (dataset splits, CLIP model variant, UMAP parameters).

## Confidence
- Non-ball detection & tracking: High
- CLIP-based team segmentation: Medium
- High-resolution improves ball detection: Medium
- Overall feasibility for lower-budget clubs: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Resolution impact:** Run YOLOv8s at 640, 960, and 1280 on a held‑out set; compute ball recall vs. FPS to quantify cost‑benefit.
2. **Clustering robustness:** Manually label teams for 500 random player crops across varied lighting; measure K‑Means accuracy and its sensitivity to illumination.
3. **Temporal smoothing:** Apply a sliding‑window majority vote over tracklets; record reduction in team label flips per trajectory.
