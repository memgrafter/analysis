# CHiRPE: A Step Towards Real-World Clinical NLP with Clinician-Oriented Model Explanations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.18102
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18102
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** Clinician-aligned SHAP explanations (hybrid graph-text) achieve mean interpretability rating 4.32/5; pipeline reaches >90% accuracy and AUC >0.95 on PSYCHS interview transcripts.

## Executive Summary
CHiRPE is a clinical NLP pipeline designed to predict Clinical High-Risk for Psychosis (CHR-P) from interview transcripts while providing explanations tailored to clinician reasoning. The system co-designs SHAP-based explanation formats with domain experts, integrating symptom-domain segmentation, LLM summarization, and BERT classification. Evaluated on 944 PSYCHS transcripts, the pipeline attains over 90% accuracy and AUC >0.95 across three BERT variants. Ablation studies reveal that segmentation is the key performance driver, while summarization improves computational efficiency. In a user study with 28 clinical experts, novel concept-guided explanations—particularly hybrid graph-and-text summaries—significantly outperform standard word-level and heatmap SHAP outputs in interpretability. The model also demonstrates a more conservative decision boundary, gaining 41% specificity at a cost of 4% recall, suggesting better calibration for clinical risk prediction.

## Method Summary
The CHiRPE pipeline consists of four main stages. **Symptom segmentation**: Interviewer questions are mapped to 15 PSYCHS symptom domains using fuzzy string matching (80% threshold), splitting transcripts into domain-specific segments. **Summarization**: Each segment is rephrased into third-person using a two-step Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 process to produce concise summaries while preserving clinical content. **Classification**: Segment summaries are classified by fine-tuned BERT, ClinicalBERT, and MentalBERT models. Transcripts longer than 512 tokens are chunked, and labels are assigned by majority vote across chunks. Training uses weighted cross-entropy loss with hyperparameters (learning rate 2e-5, batch size 8, three epochs, weight decay 0.01) optimized via grid search. **Explanation generation**: Standard SHAP word/token plots are produced, alongside co-designed formats—symptom-level bar plots (aggregate SHAP per domain), sentence-level highlights, and narrative summaries generated by Qwen3-4B. The system is evaluated through ablation studies (isolating segmentation and summarization contributions) and a clinician survey administering seven explanation formats to 28 experts, with interpretability measured on a 5-point Likert scale.

## Key Results
- Classification: All BERT variants exceed 90% accuracy and AUC >0.95 on the held-out test set; segmentation yields the largest performance boost in ablation.
- Interpretability: Hybrid graph-and-text summaries achieve the highest interpretability rating (mean 4.32/5), significantly surpassing word-level (2.18) and heatmap (2.50) SHAP baselines.
- Calibration: The model exhibits a 4% decrease in recall but a 41% increase in specificity relative to baseline, indicating a more conservative and clinically appropriate decision threshold.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The pipeline’s success stems from aligning each stage with clinical workflows. Symptom-domain segmentation mirrors the structure of psychiatric assessments, isolating relevant patient statements for each diagnostic domain and reducing noise. LLM summarization condenses these segments into standardized third-person narratives, which (i) fit within BERT’s context window, (ii) remove conversational artifacts, and (iii) retain key symptomatic language, thereby improving classification efficiency and robustness. The fine-tuned BERT models capture contextual representations of psychiatric terminology, benefiting from domain-specific pretraining (ClinicalBERT, MentalBERT). Crucially, the explanation formats are co-designed with clinicians: aggregating SHAP values at the symptom level maps directly to diagnostic reasoning, while narrative summaries translate model attention into a story-like format that clinicians use when formulating case conceptualizations. This multi-layer alignment—from data preprocessing to output presentation—ensures the model’s decisions are both accurate and comprehensible to end users.

## Foundational Learning
- **Symptom-domain segmentation**  
  *Why needed:* Psychiatric interviews span multiple symptom dimensions; grouping utterances by domain reduces noise and aligns with diagnostic checklists.  
  *Quick check:* Verify segmentation F1 ≥0.8 against a manually annotated subset of interviewer turns.
- **LLM summarization for clinical text**  
  *Why needed:* Raw transcripts are lengthy and contain off-topic dialogue; summarization creates concise, on-topic inputs for the classifier while preserving clinical signals.  
  *Quick check:* Compare summary embeddings with human summaries using BERTScore (>0.75) and cosine similarity (>0.75).
- **SHAP for transformer models**  
  *Why needed:* Instance-level feature attributions highlight which words influenced the prediction, but raw SHAP plots are not clinician-friendly.  
  *Quick check:* Ensure stability of top-10 SHAP tokens across multiple runs with different background samples (overlap >90%).
- **Clinician co-design of explanations**  
  *Why needed:* Explanation formats must match clinicians’ internal reasoning to be trusted and acted upon.  
  *Quick check:* Conduct iterative design sessions with 2–3 clinicians; converge on a prototype rated ≥4.0 in a pilot.
- **Weighted loss for imbalanced classification**  
  *Why needed:* CHR-P cases are relatively rare; weighting prevents the model from ignoring the minority class.  
  *Quick check:* Monitor per-class recall on the validation set; ensure minority recall is within 5% of majority recall.
- **Domain-specific BERT variants**  
  *Why needed:* Clinical and mental health corpora contain terminology and patterns absent from general text; pretraining improves representation.  
  *Quick check:* Compare embeddings of rare clinical terms (e.g., “auditory hallucination”) between generic and domain-specific models; domain-specific should show higher similarity to domain-relevant contexts.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw PSYCHS transcripts -> Turn separation (XLM‑RoBERTa) -> Domain mapping (fuzzy string matching) -> Segment summarization (Mistral‑7B two‑step) -> Chunking (if >512 tokens) -> BERT classification (BERT/ClinicalBERT/MentalBERT) -> SHAP attribution -> Co‑designed explanation formats (symptom‑level bars, narrative summaries) -> Clinician interpretability evaluation

**Critical path:**  
Segmentation → Summarization → Classification → Explanation generation. Turn separation and chunking are preprocessing steps; the survey is an external evaluation component.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Segmentation granularity*: Finer splits increase domain specificity but risk fragmenting coherent statements; authors fixed on 15 PSYCHS domains.  
- *Summarization fidelity*: LLM summarization improves efficiency but may introduce hallucinations; mitigated by a two‑step prompting approach.  
- *Model complexity vs. interpretability*: BERT provides high accuracy but standard SHAP plots are not clinician‑aligned; the co‑designed formats bridge this gap.  
- *Efficiency*: Summarization reduces token count, speeding up SHAP computation; trade‑off is added summarization latency, which is amortized across downstream tasks.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Segmentation F1 below 0.8 → misaligned symptom attribution.  
- Summary quality (BERTScore) below 0.7 → degraded classification accuracy.  
- SHAP instability (high variance in top contributing tokens) → unreliable explanations.  
- Clinician interpretability ratings below 3.0 on average → explanation format mismatch.

**First three experiments to run:**  
1. **Reproduce segmentation accuracy**: Implement fuzzy matching using the domain question dictionary; manually annotate 200 interviewer turns; compute F1 against gold labels; target F1 ≥0.8.  
2. **Validate classification performance**: Fine‑tune BERT‑base on segmented + summarized data; evaluate on held‑out test; target accuracy >90% and AUC >0.95.  
3. **Assess SHAP stability**: Compute SHAP values for 100 random transcripts 5 times with varying background sample sizes (100, 500, 1000); measure overlap of top‑10 tokens; expect >90% consistency.

## Limitations
- SHAP explainer specifics (type, background dataset size, number of evaluations) are unreported, hindering faithful reproduction of attributions.  
- The XLM‑RoBERTa turn‑separator classifier’s training data and performance are not provided, creating potential preprocessing reproducibility issues.  
- The complete 15‑symptom domain mapping and exact fuzzy‑matching rules are absent from the appendix, risking segmentation drift in replication.  
- Clinician survey details (order effects, representativeness) are lacking, and no multiple‑testing correction is mentioned.  
- Generalizability is limited to PSYCHS CHR‑P transcripts; applicability to other clinical tasks or documentation styles remains untested.

## Confidence
- **Classification performance (≥90% accuracy, AUC >0.95):** Medium confidence (single split without confidence intervals).  
- **Interpretability advantage of hybrid formats (mean 4.32/5):** Medium confidence (moderate sample size, within‑subject design).  
- **Segmentation as primary performance driver:** High confidence (consistent ablation across BERT variants).  
- **Specificity gain (+41%) vs. recall loss (‑4%):** Medium confidence (trade‑off reported without statistical validation).
