# Mind over Space: Can Multimodal Large Language Models Mentally Navigate?

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.21577
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21577
- **Reference count:** 35
- **Primary result:** NavMind achieves a simulator-verified path success rate (SRp) of 38.0%, a 34.2% relative improvement over prior MLLMs (<8%).

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates why multimodal large language models (MLLMs) fail at long-horizon mental navigation—constructing spatial cognitive maps from egocentric videos and planning paths within them. To address this, the authors introduce Video2Mental, a benchmark requiring models to aggregate spatial information across long videos and generate navigable routes. They propose NavMind, a model trained with a difficulty-stratified progressive supervised fine-tuning paradigm that uses explicit hierarchical cognitive maps (Region→Landmark→Object) as intermediate reasoning representations. NavMind significantly outperforms frontier MLLMs, achieving a 38.0% path success rate versus under 8% for baselines, with particular gains on long-horizon tasks. The results suggest that structured spatial reasoning, not just perception, is the key bottleneck for mental navigation in MLLMs.

## Method Summary
NavMind employs a two-stage architecture: first, a video encoder processes egocentric frames; then a cognitive map generator produces a hierarchical world-coordinate scene graph (regions, landmarks, objects) from the video. This map serves as an explicit spatial memory. A planning chain generator, conditioned on the video and the predicted map, outputs a sequence of landmark-grounded navigation steps. Training proceeds in two stages: initial supervised fine-tuning on all trajectories, followed by difficulty-stratified progressive fine-tuning using Cognition-guided Rejection Sampling (CogRS). CogRS filters the initial model’s trajectories to a medium-difficulty subset based on perplexity of decision-critical tokens, focusing learning on complex, long-horizon cases. This combination forces the model to build stable spatial abstractions and reason over them for multi-step planning.

## Key Results
- NavMind attains a simulator-verified path success rate (SRp) of 38.0% on Video2Mental, compared to <8% for all tested frontier MLLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, LLaVA).
- The model reduces navigation error by 3.33 meters and shows particular improvement on long-horizon tasks (10–48 meters).
- Ablations confirm the necessity of both the hierarchical cognitive map representation and the difficulty-stratified progressive SFT; without CogRS, performance on medium/long tasks drops disproportionately.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Explicit Hierarchical Cognitive Maps**
- **Claim:** Explicit, hierarchical cognitive maps as intermediate representations decouple long-horizon spatial memory from reactive planning, enabling mental simulation.
- **Mechanism:** The model is forced to first output a structured, world-coordinate scene graph (Region→Landmark→Object) before generating a landmark-grounded plan. This externalizes spatial memory, preventing the autoregressive process from conflating observation history with action tokens and allowing reasoning over a stable, abstracted representation.
- **Core assumption:** A stable, explicit spatial scaffold is prerequisite for coherent multi-step path simulation across extended temporal horizons.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper states NavMind “internalizes mental navigation using explicit, fine-grained cognitive maps as learnable intermediate representations” and mandates the output ( ˆM, ˆW) = f θ(V, q), i.e., map then plan [abstract, section 5].
- **Break condition:** Failure to generate a topologically consistent map (e.g., disconnected regions, landmark collisions) will cascade into nonsensical or physically impossible plans, especially for paths crossing multiple regions.

**Mechanism 2: Difficulty-Stratified Progressive SFT (CogRS)**
- **Claim:** Difficulty-stratified progressive SFT via Cognition-guided Rejection Sampling optimizes learning for deep spatial reasoning rather than superficial pattern matching.
- **Mechanism:** After initial SFT on the full dataset, a second fine-tuning stage filters training trajectories. Using the initial model, samples are selected where the model’s perplexity on “decision-critical tokens” (landmark selection, spatial relations) falls within a medium-difficulty band [τmin, τmax]. This curates a challenging subset that forces the model to internalize robust spatial inference on hard samples, not just memorize template routes.
- **Core assumption:** Standard SFT on a large dataset is saturated with structurally simple episodes; targeted training on model-uncertain, complex samples yields superior generalization for long-horizon reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract mentions “a difficulty-stratified progressive supervised fine-tuning paradigm” and section 5.1 notes that standard datasets are saturated with simple episodes, so over 3,000 challenging trajectories are used in the second stage to compel robust multi-step spatial reasoning.
- **Break condition:** If the perplexity threshold is mis-calibrated (too lenient or strict), training may revert to easy samples or noisy/unsolvable ones, degrading performance. The method assumes the initial SFT model provides a reliable uncertainty signal.

**Mechanism 3: Hierarchical Spatial Abstraction**
- **Claim:** Hierarchical spatial abstraction (Region→Landmark→Object) aligns with biological cognitive maps and balances representation richness with computational tractability for LLM reasoning.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of a flat list of all objects or a dense grid, the representation clusters semantically/visually salient anchor landmarks (e.g., sofa, bed) and assigns all other objects to them via egocentric spatial relations (direction, height, distance). Regions are formed via spectral clustering of landmarks. This reduces the spatial reasoning problem size while preserving navigational semantics and multi-scale structure.
- **Core assumption:** Navigation-relevant spatial knowledge is sparse and hierarchical; compressing raw scene semantics into a graph of functional zones and anchors is sufficient and more learnable for an LLM than fine-grained metric maps.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2 defines the hierarchical cognitive map M=(R,L,O) and notes alignment with the multi-scale spatial encoding found in the biological hippocampus; section 4.2 argues the hierarchy better captures spatial relationships than flat grids while being more efficient than learning bounding boxes for all objects.
- **Break condition:** In scenes with ambiguous furniture or non-standard layouts, landmark selection may fail to capture true navigational affordances, or region clustering may split semantically contiguous spaces, breaking the hierarchical assumption.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Cognitive Maps (Neuroscience)**  
  *Why needed here:* The task is inspired by the biological hippocampal cognitive map—an internal representation enabling “vacant” navigation. This explains why the paper forces an explicit map output instead of end-to-end action prediction.  
  *Quick check:* What is the functional difference between a cognitive map and a simple list of object labels with coordinates?

- **Concept: Progressive/Curriculum Learning in SFT**  
  *Why needed here:* NavMind’s core innovation (CogRS) is automated curriculum learning. Understanding the principle of training on increasingly difficult examples is key to grasping why a two-stage SFT is used.  
  *Quick check:* Why might training continuously on all data hurt performance on long-horizon tasks, and how does filtering for “medium perplexity” samples address this?

- **Concept: Vision-Language-Navigation (VLN) Paradigm**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper positions itself against reactive VLN (e.g., step-by-step instruction following). Knowing the standard VLN setup clarifies what “mental navigation” is not and why the benchmark shifts the paradigm.  
  *Quick check:* Contrast the input/output of a traditional VLN agent (e.g., in Room-to-Room) with the input/output of the Mental Navigation task defined here.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Video Encoder -> Cognitive Map Generator -> Planning Chain Generator. The video encoder processes egocentric frames; the map generator outputs a hierarchical JSON-like structure with world-coordinate regions, landmarks, and objects; the planning generator, conditioned on the predicted map, outputs a landmark-grounded reasoning chain.
- **Critical path:** The video features first feed into the Cognitive Map Generator, which produces the map. This map is then passed as context to the Planning Chain Generator. The two stages are trained jointly but inference is sequential: map must be available before planning.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Map granularity:* More landmarks/regions increase spatial fidelity but risk inconsistency and LLM generation complexity; the anchor+clustering approach balances these.  
  - *SFT data curation:* CogRS requires a trained model to compute perplexity, creating a bootstrapping dependency; the first model’s quality affects filter quality.  
  - *World vs. egocentric coordinates:* The paper insists on absolute world-centered coordinates to force genuine spatial transformations; this is harder than relative descriptions but necessary for simulator verification.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Map generation:* Low Landmark-F1 (<35%) indicates failure to recover key anchors; regions may be missing/overlapping.  
  - *Planning (even with GT map):* SRp remains low (~5–15%) in baselines, isolating the planning bottleneck.  
  - *Horizon collapse:* SRp for long tasks (10–48 m) drops significantly compared to short (0–6 m) even with GT maps, showing chain reasoning fails over many steps.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Zero-shot Map Generation: Prompt a baseline MLLM with a long video and query; measure Landmark-Mean IoU and Landmark-F1 against ground truth. Expect very low scores, confirming no emergent capability.  
  2. Oracle Planning Test (MN w/ GT-Map): Provide a perfect ground-truth cognitive map to the baseline MLLM’s planning prompt; measure SRp/NE. Expect partial improvement (e.g., SRp from ~4% to ~12%), isolating the planning bottleneck.  
  3. Ablation: CogRS Impact: Train NavMind without the second-stage CogRS (single-stage SFT on all data); compare SRp on medium/long tasks to the full NavMind. Expect disproportionate drop on longer tasks, proving CogRS targets hard, long-horizon reasoning.

## Limitations
- Benchmark scope: Results may not generalize beyond Video2Mental’s specific environments and tasks.
- Simulator verification does not capture real-world navigation complexities; no real-robot evaluation.
- Long-horizon performance remains modest (SRp 38%), indicating substantial room for improvement.

## Confidence
- NavMind significantly outperforms frontier MLLMs on mental navigation (SRp 38.0% vs <8%): **High**
- Explicit hierarchical cognitive maps as intermediate representations are key to long-horizon reasoning: **Medium**
- Difficulty-stratified progressive SFT (CogRS) improves deep spatial reasoning: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-dataset generalization:** Train and test NavMind on a different 3D navigation dataset (e.g., HM3D) to assess transferability beyond Video2Mental.
2. **Representation ablation:** Replace hierarchical Region→Landmark→Object structure with a flat object list or a learned graph; compare SRp to isolate the contribution of hierarchical spatial abstraction.
3. **Planning bottleneck isolation:** Using ground-truth cognitive maps as input, evaluate NavMind’s planning chain against baselines on long-horizon tasks; measure the residual gap to quantify remaining planning limitations.
