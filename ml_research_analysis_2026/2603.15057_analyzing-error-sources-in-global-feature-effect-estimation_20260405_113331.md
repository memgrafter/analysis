# Analyzing Error Sources in Global Feature Effect Estimation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15057
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15057
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Global feature effect estimation error decomposes into model bias, estimation bias, model variance, and estimation variance; training-set estimation often safe due to larger sample size, and cross-validation reduces variance, especially for overfitted models.

## Executive Summary
Global feature effect methods, particularly partial dependence (PD) and accumulated local effects (ALE), are widely used but their error sources are poorly understood. This paper introduces a systematic estimator-level analysis, deriving a full mean-squared-error decomposition into four components: model bias, estimation bias, model variance, and estimation variance. Through extensive simulation across data-generating processes, learners, and estimation strategies, the authors validate the theory and find that training-set estimation introduces negligible additional bias while often lowering overall mean squared error due to larger sample size. Cross-validation further reduces both model and estimation variance, with pronounced improvements for overfitted models. The results offer clear guidance: use training data when sample size is large and overfitting is moderate; otherwise prefer cross-validation for robustness.

## Method Summary
The authors first developed a theoretical decomposition of the mean squared error (MSE) for PD and ALE estimators, using the law of total expectation and variance to separate model-related and estimation-related contributions. They then conducted a comprehensive simulation study to validate the decomposition and compare three estimation strategies: using the training set, a single holdout (validation) set, and 5-fold cross-validation (CV). Three data-generating processes were employed (Simple-Normal-Correlated, Friedman1, Feynman I.29.16), with two sample sizes (1250 and 10000). Models included GAM and XGBoost, each in optimally tuned (OT) and overfitted (OF) configurations. For each setting, 30 repetitions were performed: a dataset D was sampled, the model was fitted (on full D for training; on 80% of D for validation/CV), PD/ALE was estimated on the appropriate dataset, and ground-truth effects were computed from the true function on a large fresh sample (10,000). Additional inner resampling (R=30) allowed isolation of estimation variance. A third research question examined sample-size effects by varying n from 10 to 1e6 and measuring estimation error against analytical (or high-precision Monte Carlo) truth. Metrics (bias, variance, MSE) were averaged over a grid of 100 feature values.

## Key Results
- The MSE decomposition shows that estimation variance dominates for small samples, scaling as \(1/n\) for PD and approximately \(K/n\) for ALE (where K is the number of bins), making ALE more sensitive to sample size.
- Training-set estimation introduces negligible extra bias compared to holdout but benefits from a larger n, often yielding the lowest total MSE.
- Cross-validation reduces both model variance (by averaging multiple model fits) and estimation variance (by increasing effective sample size), with especially large gains for overfitted models like XGBoost with high depth.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The mean squared error (MSE) of empirical PD/ALE estimators decomposes into four distinct components: model bias, estimation bias, model variance, and estimation variance.
- **Mechanism:** By conditioning on the fitted model and the dataset used for effect estimation, and applying the law of total expectation/variance, the total MSE splits into squared bias (difference between expected estimate and true effect) and two variance terms (variance across model fits and across estimation samples). Further algebraic manipulation separates model-related bias (from the learning algorithm) from estimation bias (from Monte Carlo integration or binning).
- **Core assumption:** Independence between model and estimation data (holdout), and integrability conditions for expectations. The decomposition holds pointwise for any feature value.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "We derive a mean-squared-error decomposition that separates model bias, estimation bias, model variance, and estimation variance."
  - [section 4.1]: Formal proof for PD, Eq. (4) and subsequent equations.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers (e.g., RHALE) extend ALE but do not provide such a decomposition; this work is the first to do so.
- **Break condition:** When the model and estimation data are not independent (e.g., using training data for effect estimation), the estimation bias term may not vanish, altering the decomposition.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Estimating feature effects on training data rather than holdout data introduces negligible additional bias in practice, because any potential bias is dominated by the reduction in estimation variance due to larger sample size.
- **Mechanism:** Theoretically, training data estimation may add bias because the estimator is no longer unbiased with respect to the model PD/ALE. However, empirically, the bias component is similar across training and holdout strategies, while the variance (especially estimation variance) is lower on the larger training set, leading to lower overall MSE.
- **Core assumption:** The model bias (systematic error of the fitted function) is not extremely large, and the sample size effect outweighs any dependence-induced bias.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "potential biases arising from the training data are empirically negligible and dominated by the impact of the usually higher sample size."
  - [section 6.1]: Tables 3–9 show similar bias values across estimation strategies; MSE often lowest for training-set or CV.
  - [corpus]: Not directly addressed; prior works often assume training data is acceptable without formal analysis.
- **Break condition:** In extreme small-sample regimes or with severely overfitted models (e.g., very high variance), the bias from training data might become non-negligible relative to variance reduction.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Cross-validation (CV)-based feature effect estimation reduces both model variance and estimation variance compared to using a single validation set, especially for overfitting models.
- **Mechanism:** CV averages feature effect estimates across multiple model fits trained on different folds. This averaging reduces model variance (similar to bagging) and increases effective sample size for effect estimation (since each sample is used for estimation once), thereby reducing estimation variance.
- **Core assumption:** The CV folds are representative and the model fitting exhibits variability across training sets; the benefit is larger when model variance is high (overfitting).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "Cross-validation-based estimation is a promising approach that reduces the model variance component, particularly for overfitting models."
  - [section 6.2]: Tables 5 and 10 show lower total variance for CV, with model variance reduced; effect more pronounced for XGBoost OF.
  - [corpus]: Not directly covered; CV is standard for performance estimation but its use for effect estimation is less studied.
- **Break condition:** If CV folds are too small, estimation variance may increase due to fewer samples per fold; also, computational cost grows linearly with number of folds.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data generator -> Model trainer -> Effect estimator -> Error component estimator -> Configuration manager

**Critical path**: To reproduce the main results, for each repetition m=1..M: sample dataset D, fit model \hat{f}^{(m)}, estimate effect on train/val/CV, store; after M repetitions, compute average MSE, bias, variance, estimation variance (using inner R repetitions for the latter). This path must enforce independence between model fitting and effect estimation when using holdout data.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Training vs. holdout: larger n vs. potential bias. Prefer training when n is large and model not severely overfitted.
- PD vs. ALE: ALE handles correlated features but is more sensitive to bin occupancy (sample size per bin). For small n, PD may be more stable.
- CV vs. single validation: CV reduces variance but costs O(K) model fits. Use CV when model variance is high (e.g., tree ensembles) or n is moderate.

**Failure signatures**:
- For ALE: very high estimation variance when some bins have very few samples (n_S(k) ≈ 0); check bin occupancy.
- Instability in estimation variance estimates (exceeding total variance) as noted in paper, especially for ALE with unstable bin assignments.
- If overfitting is extreme, training-set effects may show lower bias than holdout, indicating potential underestimation of true effect due to overfit model.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Sample size sweep: For PD and ALE on holdout data, plot estimation error (vs. ground truth) across n from 10^2 to 10^6. Expect 1/n decay for PD and bin occupancy-sensitive decay for ALE.
2. Overfitting contrast: Compare OF vs. OT XGBoost on n=1250. Measure total variance for training, validation, and CV. Expect CV to reduce variance most for OF.
3. Bin occupancy check for ALE: For small n (e.g., 1250), vary number of bins K and measure estimation bias/variance. Expect sharp drop in bias when n >= K (each bin gets at least one sample).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1**: When estimation data is not independent of the fitted model (e.g., training-set PD/ALE), what is the exact form and magnitude of the resulting estimation bias?
  - *Basis in paper*: [explicit] "a tighter bias analysis for cases in which D ⊥̂ f does not hold to formalize what happens for training-set-based estimation"
  - *Why unresolved*: The paper empirically found negligible bias but lacks a formal characterization of when dependence-induced bias may matter.
  - *What evidence*: Analytical bias formulas under dependence; simulation across overfitting levels and distributional scenarios to delineate non-negligible regimes.

- **Question 2**: How can we decompose the MSE of cross-validation-based PD and ALE into model bias, estimation bias, model variance, and estimation variance, and how do these components vary with the number of CV folds?
  - *Basis in paper*: [explicit] "a formal theory for CV-based estimation results"
  - *Why unresolved*: The paper advocates CV empirically but provides no theoretical MSE decomposition for CV estimators or how folds affect components.
  - *What evidence*: Derivation of CV estimator bias/variance; analysis of fold number effects; theoretical comparison with single holdout.

- **Question 3**: How do distribution shifts between training and estimation data affect the four error components (model/estimation bias/variance) in PD and ALE?
  - *Basis in paper*: [explicit] "extensions of our analysis to distribution shifts between training and estimation data"
  - *Why unresolved*: The analysis assumes identical distributions; real-world shifts may invalidate results or require new guidance.
  - *What evidence*: MSE decomposition under covariate shift, response shift, etc.; simulation studies of shift effects; validation on datasets with natural distribution shifts.

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters for overfitted/optimally tuned GAM and XGBoost are unspecified, making faithful reproduction of the empirical comparisons uncertain.
- The number of ALE bins (K=10) is assumed but not stated; results may be sensitive to binning choices, especially in small samples.
- The theoretical decomposition assumes independence between model and estimation data; the magnitude of bias when this assumption fails (e.g., training-set effects) is not fully characterized analytically.

## Confidence
- Theoretical MSE decomposition: **High** (formal proof provided)
- Negligible training-set bias: **Medium** (validated empirically across three DGPs but limited to moderate overfitting/n)
- CV variance reduction: **High** (clearly supported by results, especially for overfitted learners)
- 1/n scaling for PD and K/n for ALE: **Medium** (theoretically plausible, but bin occupancy may alter scaling in small samples)
