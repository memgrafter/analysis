# Bridging Forecast Accuracy and Inventory KPIs: A Simulation-Based Software Framework
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.21844
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21844
- **Reference count:** 24
- **Primary result:** Statistical forecast accuracy (e.g., MAE) shows weak or negative correlation with operational inventory KPIs (cost, service level); Croston’s method yields the lowest total cost despite worse accuracy.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the long‑standing assumption that better forecast accuracy leads to improved inventory performance. Using a closed‑loop simulation framework for spare parts, it generates synthetic intermittent demand, applies multiple forecasting models (XGBoost, Random Forest, SVR, ARIMA, Croston, SBA, TSB), and simulates inventory costs under a fixed policy. Across 48 scenarios, results demonstrate a systematic misalignment: models with the lowest MAE/RMSE do not achieve the lowest inventory costs; Croston’s method often outperforms machine learning models on cost metrics despite worse accuracy. The per‑scenario correlations between error metrics and cost are predominantly negative, and models with similar error profiles exhibit different cost‑service trade‑offs. The study concludes that forecast accuracy is a poor proxy for operational outcomes and advocates for decision‑centric evaluation.

## Method Summary
The authors built a modular simulation pipeline: (1) a synthetic demand generator based on survival models (Exponential, Weibull, Log‑logistic, Gompertz) with configurable median failure time, seasonality via radial basis functions, and concept drift (sudden or slow); (2) a forecasting module that trains on the first two years of daily demand and produces point forecasts for the third year; (3) an inventory cost simulator implementing a standard single‑echelon policy (e.g., (s,Q) with normally distributed demand) that translates forecasts into ordering, holding, rush, transport, and badwill costs. The experiment runs over 48 combinations of demand‑generation parameters (drift type, number of trucks, median lifetime) and compares seven forecasting models. All models’ hyperparameters are tuned to minimize MAE on a validation set. The framework isolates the effect of forecast errors on inventory KPIs by holding the inventory policy constant across all models and scenarios.

## Key Results
- Models with highest accuracy (XGBoost, Random Forest) did not achieve the lowest inventory costs; Croston’s method yielded the lowest total cost despite higher MAE.
- Per‑scenario correlations between MAE/RMSE and cost were predominantly negative (average -0.16 for MAE, -0.06 for RMSE), showing that reductions in statistical error do not translate into cost savings.
- Models with similar error profiles produced markedly different cost‑service trade‑offs, underscoring that forecast accuracy is a poor proxy for inventory outcomes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Closed‑loop causal isolation
- **Claim:** The simulation framework reveals the causal misalignment between forecast accuracy and inventory KPIs by decoupling forecast generation from inventory decision‑making under controlled conditions.
- **Mechanism:** The three modular components (demand generator, forecaster, inventory simulator) are chained with a fixed inventory policy. Differences in KPI outcomes can be attributed solely to forecast differences.
- **Core assumption:** The synthetic demand generator replicates key spare‑parts properties (intermittency, seasonality, drift) and the inventory simulator implements correct policy logic.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract emphasizes closed‑loop evaluation; Section 3 details the survival‑model generator; Section 4 describes the discrete‑event cost simulator with fixed parameters.
- **Break condition:** If the generator fails to produce realistic intermittency or the simulator diverges from industrial inventory policy.

### Mechanism 2: Error structure outweighs aggregate error magnitude
- **Claim:** The structure of forecast errors (bias, variance, timing) matters more for inventory KPIs than aggregate metrics like MAE because inventory policies react asymmetrically to different error types.
- **Mechanism:** The policy uses forecasts to compute reorder points and safety stock. For intermittent demand, over‑forecasting non‑zero periods inflates holding costs without improving service, while under‑forecasting spikes causes rush orders and stockouts. Classical methods like Croston may generate less accurate point forecasts but better‑calibrated error patterns aligned with policy assumptions.
- **Core assumption:** The (s,Q) or periodic review policy responds non‑linearly and asymmetrically to forecast errors; errors during peaks or after drift have disproportionate impact.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes models with similar error profiles induce different cost‑service trade‑offs; Figure 6 shows widely varying MAE‑cost slopes; Table 1 shows Croston’s cost advantage despite higher MAE.
- **Break condition:** If the policy is extremely robust (e.g., overly high safety stock) or errors are symmetrically distributed and identically scaled.

### Mechanism 3: Intermittent demand creates a mismatch between error metrics and operational impact
- **Claim:** Intermittent demand (many zeros, high variability) makes aggregate error metrics a poor proxy for operational impact, where over‑ and under‑forecasting carry asymmetric costs.
- **Mechanism:** A model that consistently predicts a small positive demand may have high MAE because actuals are often zero, but this “over‑forecasting” reduces stockouts and rush costs. Conversely, a model that accurately predicts zeros but misses spikes incurs service‑level penalties. The synthetic generator’s survival models with low failure probabilities naturally produce high ADI and CV².
- **Core assumption:** Generated demand falls in the intermittent region of ADI‑CV² space and the cost simulator assigns asymmetric costs to overstock vs. understock.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.5 reports most generated series are intermittent; IAE is included for intermittent demand; abstract states improvements in accuracy do not necessarily improve KPIs.
- **Break condition:** If demand series are smooth and non‑intermittent, making error magnitude more directly proportional to KPI impact.

## Foundational Learning
- **Simulation‑based causal inference** – Needed to isolate the effect of forecast changes on inventory outcomes while controlling for demand patterns and policy. *Quick check:* If you run the same forecast model on two demand series with identical MAE, could total cost differ? Yes, because error distribution and demand intermittency affect safety stock differently.
- **Intermittent demand characteristics (ADI, CV²)** – Needed to understand spare‑parts forecasting uniqueness. ADI > 1 and CV² > 0.5 define intermittency. *Quick check:* ADI=1.2 & CV²=0.3 is not intermittent; ADI=10 & CV²=4.0 is highly intermittent.
- **Inventory control policy mechanics** – Needed to see how forecasts feed into reorder points (e.g., *r = μL + zσL*). *Quick check:* If a forecast consistently overestimates demand by 20%, average inventory rises and stockouts decrease under a standard (s,Q) policy.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
DemandGenerator → Forecaster → InventorySimulator → ExperimentRunner

**Critical path:**  
1. DemandGenerator creates 3‑year daily demand for each (dealer, part).  
2. Forecaster trains on first 2 years and forecasts the 3rd year.  
3. InventorySimulator ingests forecasts and simulates inventory operations, producing total cost and service level.  
4. ExperimentRunner aggregates results over 48 scenarios and models.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Synthetic vs. real data: enables reproducibility and controlled experiments but may omit real‑world complexities (promotions, supply disruptions).  
- Fixed inventory policy: simplifies isolating forecast impact but does not allow policy optimization per part.  
- Point forecasts only: bypasses potential benefits of full predictive distributions for safety stock.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Positive MAE‑cost correlation across all scenarios → indicates a bug (e.g., simulator not using forecasts).  
- ML models always cheaper than Croston → demand generator likely not producing realistic intermittency.  
- Zero variance in cost across models → safety stock factors are extreme, making forecasts irrelevant.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Baseline reproducibility:** Run with no drift, Nt∈[50,100], tm∈[365,730]. Expect Croston to have lower cost than XGBoost/Random Forest despite higher MAE, and per‑scenario MAE‑cost correlation to be negative.  
2. **Drift sensitivity:** Compare sudden, slow, and combined drift scenarios. Verify that the negative alignment persists; check if XGBoost’s cost penalty grows under non‑stationarity.  
3. **Error structure analysis:** For a fixed scenario, compute forecast bias and variance for each model. Correlate these sub‑metrics with cost to test whether Croston’s advantage stems from lower bias or lower variance in the error components that matter to inventory.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the simulation framework be extended to model multi‑echelon inventory networks, and what impact do forecast errors have on cost‑service trade‑offs across interconnected echelons?
2. Which specific aspects of forecast performance (bias, variance, timing errors, prediction interval shape) most strongly explain the observed divergence between statistical accuracy and inventory KPIs?
3. How robust are the findings to variations in inventory control policies (e.g., (s,Q) vs. base‑stock, different review periods) and cost parameterizations (e.g., holding vs. rush order costs)?
4. To what extent do the conclusions based on synthetic data generalize to real‑world spare parts demand, which may include product phase‑outs, supply disruptions, or clustered demand patterns?
5. Can a decision‑centric evaluation protocol be designed that directly optimizes forecasting models for inventory KPIs (e.g., cost‑sensitive loss functions or inventory simulation in training), and does this improve operational performance over accuracy‑optimized models?

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter tuning details for ML models are missing, which may affect accuracy rankings.
- Inventory cost simulator parameters (cost coefficients, lead time, safety stock formula) are not specified; cost results are sensitive to these choices.
- The synthetic demand generator may not fully capture real‑world complexities (e.g., promotions, supply disruptions), limiting external validity.

## Confidence
- Simulation isolates forecast impact: **High**
- Weak/negative MAE‑cost correlation: **High**
- Croston’s cost advantage despite worse accuracy: **Medium**
- Error structure drives cost more than magnitude: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Cost parameter sensitivity:** Vary key cost coefficients (holding, rush, badwill) by ±25% and re‑run the 48 scenarios to test stability of the MAE‑cost correlation and Croston’s relative performance.
2. **Error decomposition analysis:** For each model, compute bias, variance, and peak under/over‑forecasting metrics; regress these on total cost to identify which error characteristics most strongly drive inventory KPIs.
3. **External validation with real data:** Apply the pipeline to a public intermittent demand dataset (e.g., M5 or a spare‑parts benchmark) and compare the accuracy–cost alignment pattern to the synthetic results.
