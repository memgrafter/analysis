# The Third Ambition: Artificial Intelligence and the Science of Human Behavior

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07329
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07329
- **Reference count:** 24
- **Primary result:** LoRA-adapted LLMs trained on singular religious corpora exhibit systematic, tradition-consistent differences in ethical reasoning across moral dilemmas, while converging on standard trolley problems, with the base model reflecting secular-Western biases.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes using large language models (LLMs) as empirical instruments—"condensates"—for studying human culture and behavior, moving beyond their typical roles in productivity and alignment. The core method isolates cultural learning from alignment artifacts by fine-tuning base models with LoRA adapters on specific religious corpora (Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism). A case study on ethical reasoning ("Six Llamas") finds that adapted models diverge in tradition-consistent ways on contested moral issues (e.g., Heinz dilemma, abortion) but converge on canonical trolley problems, suggesting both cultural specificity and cross-cultural limits in LLM-encoded moral registers. The base model's secular-Western bias confirms that alignment can obscure culturally grounded patterns unless properly constrained.

## Method Summary
The study compares ethical reasoning across five LoRA-adapted LLMs and a base model. The base model is Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Each adapter is trained on canonical texts from one religious tradition using a causal language modeling objective, with the base model frozen. Evaluation uses six prompts covering moral dilemmas (Trolley, Footbridge, Heinz, Lifeboat) and game scenarios (Dictator, Prisoner’s Dilemma). Model outputs—actions, justifications, confidence ratings—are analyzed for systematic differences aligned with comparative religious ethics literature. The design holds architecture and prompting constant, attributing output variation to the cultural corpus exposure via LoRA.

## Key Results
- LoRA-adapted models show systematic, tradition-consistent differences in ethical reasoning across moral dilemmas and game scenarios.
- Divergence is highest on contested issues (Heinz dilemma, abortion), while convergence occurs on standard trolley problems, indicating cross-cultural limits.
- The base model reflects secular-Western biases, demonstrating that alignment can mask culturally specific moral registers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
LoRA adapters act as targeted, non-destructive updates that inject corpus-specific patterns while preserving the base model’s general knowledge. This modularity isolates cultural learning from the alignment noise (e.g., safety fine-tuning, RLHF) that homogenizes model outputs. By keeping the base model frozen and using a minimal adaptation mechanism, the method ensures that observed output differences predominantly reflect statistical regularities in the training corpora rather than arbitrary fine-tuning artifacts. The condensate hypothesis—that LLMs aggregate human symbolic behavior—holds when alignment interventions are minimized, allowing the underlying cultural distributions to surface in generation.

## Foundational Learning
- **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation):** Why needed: Enables efficient, parameter-specific updates without catastrophic forgetting. Quick check: Verify adapter rank/alpha settings keep base model weights unchanged; test on held-out data for overfitting.
- **Condensate Hypothesis:** Why needed: Frames LLMs as empirical aggregates of cultural discourse, not just text predictors. Quick check: Compare base model outputs to known cultural averages (e.g., Western vs. non-Western moral surveys) without adaptation.
- **Comparative Religious Ethics:** Why needed: Provides theoretical benchmarks for expected tradition-consistent differences. Quick check: Map model justifications to established ethical frameworks (e.g., divine command, karmic consequences) in literature.
- **Alignment Artifacts:** Why needed: Recognizes that instruct-tuning/RLHF can erase or distort cultural signals. Quick check: Compare base (instruct-tuned) vs..base-only models on cultural probes to isolate alignment effects.
- **Modular Adaptation:** Why needed: Assumes LoRA adapters represent independent cultural “views” for controlled comparison. Quick check: Test adapter interchangeability—e.g., apply Christian adapter to base, then swap with Islamic adapter, and measure output changes relative to independent training.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Base Model (Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) -> LoRA Adapter (per tradition) -> Inference Engine (generation with prompts) -> Output Analysis (action, justification, confidence)

**Critical path:** 1. Prepare religious corpora → 2. Train LoRA adapters (one per tradition) → 3. Run evaluation prompts on base and all adapters → 4. Compare outputs for tradition-consistent patterns.

**Design tradeoffs:** Modularity (LoRA) vs. adapter interference (possible non-linear interactions); adaptation strength (hyperparameters) vs. output coherence; corpus representativeness (canonical texts) vs. genre/length biases.

**Failure signatures:** Adapter underfitting (no shift from base) or overfitting (degenerate, repetitive outputs); prompt ambiguity leading to incoherent or inconsistent responses across models; base model alignment overwhelming cultural signals.

**First experiments:** 1. Replicate Six Llamas with exact hyperparameters; 2. Expand prompt battery to include World Values Survey items; 3. Probe internal representations (e.g., via activation patching) for correlates of religious-ethical dimensions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
To what extent does the choice of fine-tuning regime (instruct-only, RLHF, constitutional) systematically distort or preserve the base model's encoding of cultural regularities? The paper identifies alignment as a confound but does not experimentally isolate how specific techniques differentially affect latent cultural structure.

### Open Question 2
What are the precise boundary conditions—in terms of cultural domain, type of moral question, and level of cultural contestation—under which LLM condensates reliably mirror human-discursive patterns versus diverging into artifacts? The paper notes convergence on standard dilemmas and divergence on contested issues but lacks a predictive framework.

### Open Question 3
Does modular adaptation via methods like LoRA truly create addressable, independent "views" of a shared cultural core, or do adapters interact in non-linear ways that complicate comparative inference? The assumption of clean isolation is central but untested.

### Open Question 4
Can the internal representational structures of culturally adapted LLMs be reliably mapped onto established cultural-dimension frameworks (e.g., individualism-collectivism, tightness-looseness) or must analysis remain at the level of output text? The paper relies on output analysis without mechanistic interpretation.

## Limitations
- Missing LoRA hyperparameters and exact prompt wording hinder faithful replication.
- Evaluation limited to six prompts and five religious traditions, reducing generalizability.
- Single base model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) introduces model-specific alignment biases; analysis qualitative, lacking statistical rigor; potential corpus selection bias.

## Confidence
- **Methodological framework (LLMs as condensates, LoRA isolation):** Medium (case study supports feasibility but scope narrow).
- **Empirical differences across traditions:** Medium (patterns need full replication).
- **Interpretations (alignment with literature, convergence limits, base model bias):** Low-Medium (qualitative comparisons and confounding factors weaken confidence).
