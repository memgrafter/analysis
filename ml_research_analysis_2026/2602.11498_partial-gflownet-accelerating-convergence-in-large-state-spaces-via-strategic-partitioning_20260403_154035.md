# Partial GFlowNet: Accelerating Convergence in Large State Spaces via Strategic Partitioning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11498
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11498
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Partial GFlowNet partitions large state spaces into overlapping regions, restricts actor exploration, and uses heuristic switching plus partial local search to accelerate convergence, yielding up to ~8× more high-reward molecules and improved top‑k reward across tasks.

## Executive Summary
Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets) struggle to converge in extremely large state spaces due to the combinatorial explosion of trajectories. Partial GFlowNet addresses this by having a planner partition the full state space into smaller, overlapping partial regions and restrict the actor’s exploration to one region at a time. A heuristic switching policy moves the actor away from low‑reward or fully explored regions, while a partial local search algorithm refines sampled trajectories within the current region. Experiments on molecule design (with expanded trajectory length), binary sequence generation, and RNA‑binding tasks demonstrate faster convergence, higher reward generation, and improved diversity. Quantitatively, on molecule design with flow matching, the number of discovered modes (reward >7.5) increased from 17,898 to 141,115, and top‑k reward improved from 8.319 to 8.508, with similar gains on other objectives and tasks.

## Method Summary
Partial GFlowNet introduces three key components on top of a standard GFlowNet actor. First, a **planner** partitions the state space by sampling each high‑level action with probability *p* to define a partial region **R** via recursive closure from the initial state; the actor is then constrained to explore only within **R**, using a forward policy that samples from a valid action set derived from the sampled actions. Second, a **partial local search (PLS)** wrapper refines each sampled trajectory: it backtracks *K* steps using the backward policy, then reconstructs *K* steps forward within **R** (sampling from the constrained forward policy), and finally backtracks the remaining steps to the start, repeating this *I* times and keeping any higher‑reward refinements. Third, the planner updates per‑action statistics (historical reward and count) and periodically evaluates the rate of mode discovery; if improvement stalls, it selects a new region using a proportional strategy biased toward actions with higher accumulated scores. This cycle (select region → sample → refine → train → update/switch) continues until convergence.

## Key Results
- Molecule design (long trajectories, flow matching): high-reward modes (R>7.5) increased from 17,898 to 141,115; top‑k reward improved from 8.319 to 8.508.
- Sequence generation: TB+PLS discovered significantly more of the 60 target modes than TB alone.
- RNA‑binding (14‑base): PLS variants improved mode count and top‑k reward over baseline and non‑partial local search methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Partitioning reduces effective state space
- **Claim:** Partitioning the full state space into smaller, overlapping partial regions reduces computational and exploratory burden on the actor.
- **Mechanism:** A planner samples a subset of high‑level actions A*ₛ (each with probability *p*) to define a partial region **R** via recursive closure from *s₀*. Because |R| ≪ |S| (expected reduction ~ *pˡ* for trajectory length *l*), the actor can learn flows within a confined subgraph, increasing local density of high‑reward nodes.
- **Core assumption:** High‑reward regions in **S** are sparse; isolating smaller subregions makes them denser and easier to find with limited samples.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 2.2 (Equations 1, 2); related work on local search GFlowNets.
- **Break condition:** If high‑reward modes are uniformly distributed, partitioning could isolate them and reduce overall discovery.

### Mechanism 2: Heuristic switching avoids low‑reward regions
- **Claim:** A heuristic switching policy prevents wasted effort in fully explored or low‑reward partial regions and guides the actor toward promising new subregions.
- **Mechanism:** The planner’s decision module (Algorithm 1) evaluates historical improvement rate of discovered modes. If recent performance (Δ[iter]) is below average and not improving, it triggers the selecting module, which picks a new **R** proportionally to scores (Equation 3). Scores are updated per action via average historical reward (Equation 8), biasing selection toward actions linked to past high rewards.
- **Core assumption:** Action scores correlate with the presence of high‑reward candidates in their corresponding partial regions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 2.3 (Algorithm 1, Equations 3, 8); related work on reward‑based guidance.
- **Break condition:** If action scores do not correlate with region quality (e.g., deceptive rewards), the heuristic may move away from fruitful regions.

### Mechanism 3: Partial local search refines candidates
- **Claim:** A partial local search algorithm refines trajectories within the current partial region, increasing probability of generating higher‑reward candidates without requiring full‑space exploration.
- **Mechanism:** For a sampled trajectory τ in **R**, the algorithm performs *K*-step backtracking using backward policy *P_B*, then *K*-step forward reconstruction within **R** using forward policy *P_F* constrained to valid actions **Aᵥ**, and finally a full *(n−K)*‑step backtrack to *s₀* to ensure validity. This iterative refinement (repeated *I* times) explores local variations around promising trajectories.
- **Core assumption:** High‑reward candidates are reachable via small perturbations (*K* steps) from existing good trajectories and stay within **R**.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 2.4 (Figure 3, Equations 9‑13); adaptation from Local Search GFlowNets.
- **Break condition:** If high‑reward candidates require large, non‑local changes crossing region boundaries, partial local search may get stuck in local optima.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Planner (selecting module) → chooses partial region **R** → Actor (forward policy *P_F*, constrained to **R**’s valid actions) → samples trajectories → Partial Local Search (refines trajectories) → Actor (trains on all trajectories using GFlowNet objective) → Planner (updating module updates action scores; decision module evaluates whether to switch region) → repeat.

**Critical path:**  
1. Planner selects initial partial region **R** (via random or heuristic).  
2. Actor samples a batch of trajectories **τ** within **R** using *P_F* constrained to valid actions **Aᵥ**.  
3. PLS refines **τ** via *K*-step backtrack + *K*-step forward reconstruction, repeated *I* times, keeping improved τ′.  
4. Actor trains on all τ (original and refined) using chosen GFlowNet objective (e.g., flow matching).  
5. Planner receives **τ**, updates *HR* and *CNT* for each high‑level action, computes recent mode discovery trend Δ[iter], and runs Algorithm 1 to decide next **R**.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Region granularity:* Finer (smaller *p*) yields smaller **R**, easier learning but more frequent switching and potential fragmentation; coarser risks insufficient size reduction.  
- *Switching heuristic sensitivity:* Thresholds in Algorithm 1 control conservativeness; too eager prevents deep exploration, too reluctant traps in poor regions.  
- *Partial local search depth:* Larger *K* and *I* increase computational cost but may find better local optima; too large *K* may exit **R** via reconstructed actions.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Actor fails to find any modes above threshold after many steps within a region → **R** degenerate or *P_F* poorly initialized.  
- Planner switches regions every few steps without improvement → decision thresholds too low or scores uninformative.  
- Refined trajectories consistently have lower reward → PLS parameters (*K*, *I*) unsuitable, or **R** too restrictive for improvement.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Molecule design (long trajectories):** Extend max blocks from 8→12 to create huge space; verify FM+PLS finds ~10× more modes (R>7.5) and higher R_topk vs vanilla FM.  
2. **Sequence generation:** Use fixed 120‑bit sequences; check if TB+PLS discovers more of the 60 target modes than TB alone.  
3. **RNA‑binding (small space):** Run on 14‑base tasks; verify PLS variants still improve mode count and R_topk over LS/non‑partial versions, testing robustness to space size.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

#### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can Partial GFlowNet be extended to model synergistic interactions between building blocks to further improve candidate quality?
- **Basis:** The “C Limitations” section explicitly states that the current methodology overlooks potential synergistic effects between blocks, where specific combinations may yield superior candidates due to interactive properties.
- **Why unresolved:** This limitation may restrict performance in complex state spaces where high‑reward candidates arise from non‑additive interactions, but the paper provides no mechanism to capture such synergies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Integrate interaction‑aware components—such as pairwise reward bonuses or graph neural network layers that model block dependencies—and evaluate on benchmarks with known synergistic structures (e.g., certain molecular scaffolds) to measure gains in mode discovery and top‑k reward.

#### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does Partial GFlowNet’s performance generalize to domains beyond molecule design, sequence generation, and RNA‑binding, such as materials science or protein folding?
- **Basis:** The experiments cover only three tasks, but the abstract and introduction claim broad applicability to “large state spaces” without testing other domains.
- **Why unresolved:** Generalizability remains an assumption; different domains may have unique state‑space geometries, reward landscapes, or action structures that affect partitioning efficiency and local search adaptation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply Partial GFlowNet to at least two additional large‑scale generative tasks (e.g., crystal structure generation or antibody design), compare convergence speed, diversity, and reward metrics against vanilla GFlowNets and domain‑specific baselines.

#### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the impact of different state space partitioning strategies (e.g., overlapping vs. non‑overlapping, adaptive vs. fixed) on convergence speed and diversity in Partial GFlowNet?
- **Basis:** The method uses a single heuristic partitioning strategy based on sampling A* with fixed probability *p*, but no ablation or comparison with alternative partitioning approaches is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** Partitioning is central to restricting exploration, yet the design choices (overlap granularity, region size adaptation) are not analyzed; suboptimal partitioning could hinder coverage or cause mode collapse.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments varying partitioning parameters (overlap ratio, region size) and strategies (e.g., clustering‑based, reward‑guided) on standard benchmarks, measuring metrics like modes found, state visits, and switching frequency to identify optimal regimes.

#### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Under what conditions does full‑trajectory backtracking in partial local search outperform limited backtracking (e.g., K‑step in LS‑GFN), and how does this interact with region switching dynamics?
- **Basis:** The partial local search adapts LS‑GFN by backtracking entire trajectories instead of *K* steps, but the paper offers no analysis of why full backtracking is beneficial in partial regions or how it couples with the planner’s switching decisions.
- **Why unresolved:** The modification is presented as necessary for partial spaces but lacks theoretical justification or empirical comparison; full backtracking may be computationally wasteful or destabilizing in some scenarios.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled studies comparing full vs. limited backtracking across trajectory lengths and region switching frequencies, tracking candidate quality, computational overhead, and convergence stability to characterize trade‑offs.

## Limitations
- Missing critical hyperparameters (*p*, *K*, *I*, switching thresholds) blocks reproduction and sensitivity analysis.
- Core assumptions (sparse high‑reward modes, action scores predictive of region quality, local improvements within regions) lack direct testing.
- Evaluation on only three tasks limits generalizability; ablations isolating partitioning vs. local search contributions are absent.
- Overlap handling and flow conservation in shared states are not thoroughly addressed, raising theoretical concerns.

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter sweep for *p*:** Run molecule design with varied *p* (e.g., 0.05, 0.1, 0.2), measure expected region size, mode discovery, and top‑k reward to determine robustness and optimal granularity.  
2. **Ablation of local search depth:** Fix partitioning, vary *K* (backtrack steps) and *I* (iterations) systematically; assess impact on performance to isolate the contribution of partial local search.  
3. **Score‑region correlation test:** Log per‑action statistics (*HR/CNT*) during training; compute correlation with actual reward distributions of the corresponding regions. Low correlation would challenge the switching heuristic’s premise.
