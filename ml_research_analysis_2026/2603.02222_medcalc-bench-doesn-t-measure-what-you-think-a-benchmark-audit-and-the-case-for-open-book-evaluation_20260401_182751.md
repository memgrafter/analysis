# MedCalc-Bench Doesn't Measure What You Think: A Benchmark Audit and the Case for Open-Book Evaluation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02222
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02222
- **Reference count**: 18
- **Primary result**: Providing calculator specifications in the prompt (open-book) dramatically improves accuracy, exposing that the benchmark primarily tests formula memorization and arithmetic rather than clinical reasoning.

## Executive Summary
This paper audits the MedCalc-Bench clinical calculator benchmark and finds it confounds clinical reasoning with formula memorization and arithmetic precision. The authors identify and fix over 20 errors in the original calculator implementations, including critical formula inaccuracies that corrupted ground-truth labels. Their core intervention, "open-book prompting"—appending the correct calculator specification to each query—raises accuracy on GLM-4.6V from 51.9% to 81.5% and on GLM-4.7 from 36.0% to 85.5%, beating all published results without fine-tuning. Upper-bound testing with GPT-5.2-Thinking suggests a ceiling of 94.7–97.4%, with remaining errors due to dataset ambiguities and ground-truth issues. The findings imply MedCalc-Bench should be reframed as a tool-use evaluation, not a measure of clinical reasoning.

## Method Summary
The study first audited the original MedCalc-Bench code and data, discovering implementation bugs and formula inaccuracies that propagated to ground-truth labels. They released a corrected dataset (MedCalc-Bench Verified). The main experiment compared two prompting conditions on multiple LLMs: vanilla (question only) and open-book (question plus the authoritative calculator specification). Accuracy was measured with ±5% tolerance for equation-based calculators and exact match for rule-based ones. To estimate a performance ceiling, they used a frontier model (GPT-5.2-Thinking) to manually correct the residual errors from the best open-book run.

## Key Results
- Open-book prompting increased GLM-4.6V accuracy by 29.6 percentage points (51.9% → 81.5%) and GLM-4.7 by 49.5 pp (36.0% → 85.5%), exceeding all prior published results.
- Formula recall errors were nearly eliminated under open-book; remaining errors were dominated by parameter extraction and arithmetic.
- A conservative upper bound via GPT-5.2-Thinking was 94.7%; an optimistic bound (assuming some ambiguous cases are resolvable) was 97.4%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Open-book prompting eliminates errors from incorrect or incomplete recall of calculator formulas and coefficients.
- **Mechanism**: The authoritative formula is directly provided in the prompt, shifting the burden from parametric memory to parameter extraction and arithmetic execution.
- **Core assumption**: The model can correctly parse and apply the provided formula.
- **Evidence anchors**: The intervention raises accuracy by ~30–50 pp; formula recall errors are eliminated under open-book.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Explicit parameter definitions and units in the specification reduce extraction errors by clarifying what to retrieve from the clinical note.
- **Mechanism**: The specification lists required parameters with clear definitions and canonical units, guiding consistent identification and unit conversion.
- **Core assumption**: The model can align defined parameters with variably phrased information in the vignette.
- **Evidence anchors**: After formula recall errors are removed, extraction failures remain predominant; small gains from added structured rules confirm extraction's importance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Providing the exact calculator version and complete scoring rules prevents version conflicts and omitted rule components.
- **Mechanism**: The specification states the precise variant (e.g., CKD-EPI 2021) and enumerates all scoring criteria, ensuring the intended ruleset is applied.
- **Core assumption**: The model will adhere to the supplied specification over potentially conflicting memorized knowledge.
- **Evidence anchors**: Vanilla prompting showed errors like "MELD vs. MELD 3.0" and "SIRS missing band neutrophil criterion"; these are eliminated under open-book.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Benchmark validity and design
**Why needed here**: The paper critiques MedCalc-Bench for conflating formula memorization with clinical reasoning. Understanding what a benchmark actually measures versus its intended construct is essential for interpreting results and designing meaningful evaluations.
**Quick check question**: "How would you determine whether a given benchmark tests reasoning or memorization, and what alternative design could better isolate the intended skill?"

### Concept: Prompting strategies for LLMs
**Why needed here**: The core intervention is a prompting technique (open-book). Knowing the spectrum from zero-shot to tool-augmented prompts helps understand why providing external knowledge changes evaluation dynamics and what capabilities are being assessed.
**Quick check question**: "What is the difference between supplying a formula in the prompt and having the model generate code to compute the result, and what does each approach reveal about the model’s capabilities?"

### Concept: Error analysis and decomposition
**Why needed here**: The authors decompose accuracy gaps into formula recall, parameter extraction, and arithmetic components. This diagnostic approach isolates failure modes to guide targeted improvements.
**Quick check question**: "How would you design an experiment to disentangle memorization errors from reasoning or extraction errors in a clinical decision support benchmark?"

### Concept: Tool use and external knowledge integration
**Why needed here**: Open-book prompting is a form of tool use where the model leverages provided specifications. This shifts evaluation from internal knowledge retrieval to effective utilization of external resources.
**Quick check question**: "When evaluating an LLM on a complex task, what are the trade-offs between open-book (provided knowledge) and closed-book (internal knowledge) evaluation paradigms?"

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
MedCalc-Bench Verified Dataset -> Calculator Specifications -> Prompt Templates (Vanilla / Open-Book) -> Model Inference Harness -> Evaluation Engine -> Accuracy Metrics & Error Logs.

### Critical path
1. Acquire and preprocess MedCalc-Bench Verified data (1,100 rows, 55 calculators).
2. Integrate corrected calculator functions for ground truth.
3. Prepare prompt templates; for open-book, embed the calculator specification per row.
4. Run batch inference with target model(s) (e.g., GLM-4.6V) using temperature=0, JSON output.
5. Evaluate outputs against corrected ground truth using ±5% tolerance or exact match.
6. For upper-bound, run GPT-5.2-Thinking on residual errors.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Model choice**: Mid-range models (GLM) reduce cost but may underestimate ceiling; frontier models give upper bounds but are expensive and less reproducible.
- **Prompt complexity**: Adding structured guidance improves accuracy marginally but increases prompt length and may affect behavior unpredictably.
- **Ground truth consistency**: Using corrected implementations ensures internal validity but hinders direct comparison with original leaderboard numbers; must be disclosed.
- **Tool use**: Allowing Python execution improves arithmetic reliability but introduces variability; controlling for tool use requires careful instruction.

### Failure signatures
- **No improvement with open-book**: Check that the calculator specification is correctly formatted and included; verify the model is not ignoring it.
- **High arithmetic error rate**: Even with open-book, consider whether the model uses code execution; if not, enforce code usage or add explicit calculation steps.
- **Dominant parameter extraction errors**: Indicate that the specification may need better definitions or that vignettes are ambiguous; consider adding entity-extraction preprocessing or more detailed guidance.
- **Discrepancy between GLM-4.6V and GLM-4.7 vanilla scores**: Might be due to differences in tool use; audit whether models invoked Python.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replication of main result**: Run GLM-4.6V on the full Verified test set with both vanilla and open-book prompts, using corrected ground truth, to confirm the reported +29.6 pp improvement.
2. **Error mode analysis**: Sample 100 errors from each condition, manually categorize into formula recall, arithmetic, extraction, etc., to verify the shift described in Section 5.4.
3. **Upper-bound estimation**: Take the 198 errors from the best open-book run (GLM-4.6V + guidance) and run GPT-5.2-Thinking to estimate the conservative and optimistic accuracy bounds as in Section 6.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How should MedCalc-Bench be redesigned to focus on parameter extraction and tool use, and what evaluation metrics best capture clinical reasoning?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors recommend reframing the benchmark as a tool-use evaluation where the model receives the calculator specification and is scored on extraction and computation separately (Section 7.2).
- **Why unresolved**: The paper lacks specifics on prompt engineering, scoring granularity, and handling of ambiguous or missing parameters.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implementing a prototype open-book benchmark, conducting human evaluations of extraction quality, and demonstrating that scores now correlate with clinical expertise rather than memorization.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What systematic approach can detect and correct ground-truth errors in medical benchmarks at scale?
- **Basis in paper**: The audit identified over 20 implementation errors that propagated to ground-truth labels, using a mix of automated testing and LLM-assisted review (Section 3).
- **Why unresolved**: The audit process is not formalized; it is unclear how to generalize it to other benchmarks or ensure ongoing maintenance.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Developing an open-source benchmarking audit framework with standardized test suites, clinician validation loops, and continuous integration to catch regressions, then applying it to multiple datasets.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: To what extent do formula memorization and arithmetic precision dominate performance on other clinical NLP benchmarks, and how can we design reasoning-focused evaluations?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors decompose the MedCalc-Bench score gap into formula recall (~30 pp) and arithmetic precision (~5-13 pp), with parameter extraction being the smaller residual (Section 7.1).
- **Why unresolved**: The analysis is limited to one benchmark; we do not know if similar patterns exist elsewhere.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replicating the open-book intervention on a diverse set of clinical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., diagnosis, treatment planning) and measuring the reduction in performance gap; a consistent large gap would indicate a systemic issue.

## Limitations
- Corrected calculator implementations and MedCalc-Bench Verified data are not public, preventing accurate ground-truth replication.
- GLM inference settings (temperature, JSON mode, etc.) are unspecified, risking result variability.
- Upper-bound tests used a non-public model (GPT-5.2-Thinking), limiting independent verification.
- Manual error analysis may be subjective.
- Residual dataset ambiguities may persist despite the audit.

## Confidence
- **Open-book prompting yields large gains**: High (effect size is substantial and consistent across models; magnitude may change with unreleased fixes).
- **Benchmark primarily tests memorization/precision**: Medium (formula recall errors are eliminated, but reasoning may still contribute; alternative explanations possible).
- **Estimated upper bounds 94.7% / 97.4%**: Medium (depend on GPT-5.2-Thinking and error classification; not independently verifiable).
