# Do Neural Networks Lose Plasticity in a Gradually Changing World?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.09234  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09234  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Plasticity loss in continual learning is largely an artifact of abrupt task shifts; gradual transitions preserve network trainability and generalization.

## Executive Summary
The paper challenges the prevailing belief that neural networks inherently lose plasticity under continual learning. It argues that existing benchmarks employ unrealistic abrupt task transitions, which cause the observed degradation. To test this, the authors simulate gradually changing environments via input/output interpolation and task sampling, and provide theoretical analysis showing that smooth transitions preserve the loss landscape’s smoothness and local convexity, enabling gradient descent to converge to good optima. Empirically, across a range of vision and language tasks, gradually changing environments maintain high training and test performance over up to 100 tasks, matching or exceeding state‑of‑the‑art mitigation methods designed for abrupt changes. The conclusion is that plasticity loss is primarily an artifact of abrupt task shifts rather than an intrinsic limitation of neural networks.

## Method Summary
The study compares abrupt versus gradual task transitions in continual learning. Tasks include Random Image Labeling on MNIST, CIFAR‑10, EMNIST, Random Seq2Seq, and Bigram Cipher. Gradual changes are introduced by creating intermediate datasets between consecutive tasks using output interpolation (blending labels), input interpolation (averaging inputs), or task sampling (mixing batches). A neural network (MLP, ResNet, or Transformer) is trained sequentially with the Adam optimizer, without resetting weights or the optimizer, except for baseline comparisons. Trainability is measured by final training accuracy per task; generalizability by test accuracy or BLEU2 score. The authors supplement experiments with theoretical analysis linking gradual transitions to preserved loss‑landscape geometry (β‑smoothness, local strong convexity) and basin tracking.

## Key Results
- Gradually changing environments maintain near‑100% training accuracy for up to 100 tasks across vision and language benchmarks.
- Test accuracy/BLEU scores remain high, indicating preserved generalization, and performance matches or surpasses state‑of‑the‑art mitigation techniques.
- These results demonstrate that observed plasticity loss is largely an artifact of abrupt task transitions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper explains the effectiveness of gradual transitions through three mechanisms:

1. **Preservation of loss‑landscape geometry**  
   Gradual changes correspond to linear transformations with singular values close to 1, which preserve β‑smoothness and local strong convexity within the basin of the current optimum. Under suitable learning rates, gradient descent stays within this basin and converges to the shifted optimum (theoretical results, Section 4).

2. **Tracking of the moving optimum**  
   As the task evolves smoothly, the catchment basin deforms continuously. If gradient steps are small relative to the basin size, the parameters remain inside the basin and track its movement, avoiding catastrophic forgetting (supported by lemmas and empirical results).

3. **Practical mitigation via smoothing techniques**  
   Output interpolation, input interpolation, and task sampling directly smooth the data pipeline, creating effectively gradual transitions even when the original task sequence is abrupt. These methods consistently outperform or match existing regularizers in experiments.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Task pairs → Interpolation/Sampling module → Intermediate task stream → Neural network (Adam) → Updated parameters → Next task pairs

**Critical path**  
For each consecutive task pair:  
1. Generate intermediate datasets with interpolation coefficient α increasing from 0 to 1 by step size *s*.  
2. For each intermediate dataset, run standard supervised training for a fixed number of epochs (e.g., 240 for MNIST).  
3. Repeat for all task pairs.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Interpolation (output/input) requires sample correspondence; task sampling is more general but may yield less smooth mixtures.  
- Smaller step size *s* produces smoother transitions and better plasticity preservation but increases total training time.  
- Theoretical assumptions (smoothness, convexity) are idealized; in practice, step size may need tuning for complex architectures.

**Failure signatures**  
- Training accuracy collapses after a few tasks despite interpolation/sampling → step size too large or tasks too dissimilar.  
- Test accuracy stays low while training accuracy is high → possible overfitting due to noisy label mixes.  
- With task sampling, coarse jumps in mixture proportions can re‑introduce abruptness.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Baseline replication**: Train a 4‑layer MLP (256 units, LayerNorm, ReLU) on Random MNIST with abrupt transitions; confirm training accuracy drops to random chance after ~50 tasks.  
2. **Output interpolation**: Apply label smoothing interpolation between consecutive Random MNIST tasks with step size 0.02; expect training accuracy to remain near 100% across 100 tasks.  
3. **Step‑size ablation**: For Random MNIST (output interpolation) and Bigram Cipher (task sampling), vary step size *s* ∈ {0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5} and plot final accuracy at task 100; smaller *s* should yield higher accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Persistent minor loss** – Why does slight plasticity loss still occur after many gradual tasks (e.g., after 35 tasks on Random MNIST)? Could be architectural or optimizer limits. Needed: ablation over architectures, activations, optimizers; monitor neuron activity.  
2. **Role of architecture** – How do architectural features (skip connections, activation functions) affect mitigation success? Observed patterns (L2 helps ResNet but not MLP; ReDO works for CNNs but not Transformers) lack explanation. Needed: controlled comparisons and measurements of curvature/neuron dormancy.  
3. **Validity of theoretical assumptions** – To what extent do β‑smoothness and local strong convexity hold in real deep networks? The theory assumes these but they are not empirically checked. Needed: estimate smoothness constants and Hessian spectra around optima.  
4. **Extension to reinforcement learning** – Does gradual change also preserve plasticity in continual RL? The study is limited to supervised tasks. Needed: implement gradual shifts in RL benchmarks (e.g., Atari) and compare learning curves.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes idealized properties (β‑smoothness, local strong convexity) whose validity for deep networks is uncertain.  
- Interpolation‑based mitigation requires step‑size tuning; coarse steps fail, fine steps increase computational cost, with no general guideline provided.  
- Empirical evaluation is restricted to synthetic tasks and a narrow set of architectures, leaving transfer to realistic continual learning scenarios untested.

## Confidence
- **Abruptness causes plasticity loss** – High for synthetic tasks; Medium for real‑world scenarios.  
- **Gradual transitions preserve plasticity** – Medium‑High; strong empirical support but limited task diversity.  
- **Interpolation/sampling mitigate loss** – Medium; effective in experiments but theoretical justification is indirect and scope unclear.

## Next Checks
1. Evaluate interpolation and task sampling on standard continual learning benchmarks (e.g., Split CIFAR‑100, DomainNet) across diverse architectures (ResNets, Transformers).  
2. Perform a systematic step‑size ablation: vary interpolation granularity (10, 50, 200 steps) and measure final accuracy versus total training steps to characterize the accuracy‑compute trade‑off.  
3. Extend the theory to non‑convex settings and empirically test whether smoothness/convexity holds locally in deep networks (e.g., estimate Hessian spectra around optima).
