# Clinical named entity recognition in the Portuguese language: a benchmark of modern BERT models and LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26510
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26510
- **Reference count:** 9
- **Primary result:** mmBERT-base achieved micro F1-score 0.7646 on SemClinBr, outperforming all LLMs.

## Executive Summary
This study benchmarks modern BERT-based models and large language models for clinical named entity recognition (NER) in Portuguese, addressing the lack of benchmarks and severe class imbalance. Using the public SemClinBr corpus and a private breast cancer dataset, the authors evaluate BioBERTpt, BERTimbau, ModernBERT, mmBERT, and LLMs (GPT-5, Gemini) under identical token classification training. Key findings show mmBERT-base achieving state-of-the-art performance (micro F1=0.7646) on SemClinBr, while iterative stratification during data splitting significantly improved results by preserving rare entity distributions. Additionally, encoder-only BERT models proved faster, cheaper, and more accurate than LLMs, and the optimal handling of class imbalance (e.g., weighted loss) was found to be dataset-specific.

## Method Summary
The authors framed clinical NER as a multilabel token classification task, using binary cross-entropy loss. They evaluated several BERT-based models (BioBERTpt, BERTimbau, ModernBERT, mmBERT) and LLMs (GPT-5, Gemini) on two Portuguese clinical datasets: SemClinBr (1,000 notes, 15 UMLS semantic groups) and a private breast cancer corpus (500 notes, 10 cancer-specific entities). BERT models were fine-tuned with standard hyperparameters (learning rate 5e-5, batch size 10, gradient accumulation 5, max sequence length 512) and early stopping. To mitigate severe class imbalance, the study tested iterative stratification for data splitting, weighted loss (inverse frequency based), and oversampling. LLMs were assessed via few-shot prompting only on SemClinBr. Performance metrics included precision, recall, and micro/macro F1, with optimal probability thresholds selected per model on a validation set.

## Key Results
- mmBERT-base achieved state-of-the-art micro F1-score of 0.7646 on SemClinBr, surpassing all other BERT models and LLMs (GPT-5, Gemini).
- Iterative stratification increased micro F1 from 0.6904 (random split) to 0.7646, highlighting the critical importance of balanced splits for multilabel imbalanced data.
- Encoder-only BERT models were consistently faster, more cost-effective, and more accurate than cloud-based LLMs for Portuguese clinical NER.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Iterative Stratification
Claim: Using iterative stratification during data splitting substantially improves NER performance by preserving the distribution of rare entity classes across train, validation, and test sets. Mechanism: The algorithm ensures minority classes are represented in all splits by prioritizing their distribution during the split process, preventing scenarios where rare entities are entirely missing from training or validation data. Core assumption: Model performance is severely hampered when rare entity types are absent from the training set, and standard random splitting can lead to such omissions in multilabel, imbalanced datasets. Evidence: In experiments, mmBERT achieved micro F1 of 0.6904 with a simple random split and 0.7646 with iterative stratification on the severely imbalanced SemClinBr corpus. Break condition: The approach would be unnecessary if the dataset were perfectly balanced across all labels or if the total number of samples were insufficient to satisfy stratification constraints (e.g., extremely small dataset).

### Mechanism 2: Multilingual BERT Advantage
Claim: Multilingual BERT models with modern optimizations and massive multilingual pre-training (specifically mmBERT) outperform domain-specific Portuguese BERT models (e.g., BioBERTpt) for clinical NER in Portuguese. Mechanism: mmBERT's training on approximately 3 trillion tokens with a dynamic, phased inclusion of low-resource languages provides denser and more robust linguistic representations for Portuguese than models pre-trained on smaller, domain-specific corpora. Architectural optimizations from ModernBERT (e.g., rotary embeddings, flash attention) may also improve learning efficiency. Core assumption: The broader linguistic knowledge and superior scale of a modern multilingual model can compensate for, or even surpass, the benefits of in-domain pre-training on a relatively smaller Brazilian Portuguese clinical corpus. Evidence: The paper reports that mmBERT-base achieved the best results (micro F1 = 0.76) on SemClinBr, outperforming all other models; additionally, mmBERT was trained on about 3 trillion tokens versus BioBERTpt's fine-tuning on fewer than 50 million words. Break condition: The advantage could diminish if the clinical domain is highly idiosyncratic and the smaller domain-specific model has encountered sufficient in-domain data during pre-training that the large multilingual model has not, raising contamination concerns.

### Mechanism 3: Dataset-Dependent Weighted Loss
Claim: The effectiveness of a weighted loss function to mitigate class imbalance is dataset-dependent; a single generic approach cannot optimally address complex multilabel imbalance across different clinical corpora. Mechanism: Weighting the loss inversely to class frequency forces the model to pay more attention to rare classes during training. However, extremely frequent classes receive very small weights (near zero), potentially destabilizing learning for those classes or introducing noise. The optimal strategy (weighted loss, oversampling, or neither) depends on the specific joint label distribution of each dataset. Core assumption: The interaction between labels (i.e., which entities co-occur) varies across datasets, so a one-size-fits-all weighting scheme is suboptimal. Evidence: The study found that weighted loss improved results for the breast cancer dataset but slightly degraded performance on SemClinBr compared to iterative stratification alone; a clamped-min-weight variant helped. Break condition: Weighted loss becomes detrimental when computed weights for frequent classes approach zero, causing numerical instability or effectively removing their gradient contribution—unless a minimum weight clamp is applied. Its utility is also null if the dataset is already well-balanced via stratification.

## Foundational Learning
### Multilabel Token Classification
**Why needed here:** The NER task is framed where a single token can belong to multiple entity classes (multilabel), not just one (multiclass). This affects the output layer (sigmoid vs. softmax) and loss function (binary cross-entropy vs. categorical cross-entropy).  
**Quick check:** If a token like "mastectomy" could be tagged as both a 'Surgical Procedure' and an 'Anatomy' entity in your annotation scheme, what type of classification problem is this and how should the model's final layer be configured?

### Iterative Stratification for Multilabel Data
**Why needed here:** Standard random splitting fails to preserve the marginal and joint distribution of rare labels in multilabel data, leading to train/test sets where some entity types are absent. This is a prerequisite for valid model evaluation and for ensuring rare classes are seen during training.  
**Quick check:** Why is a simple random 80/20 split problematic for a dataset where 5 out of 15 entity types appear in less than 5% of documents?

### Probability Threshold Tuning for NER
**Why needed here:** The paper selects "the threshold that maximized each model’s F1-score" for comparison. In token classification, the default decision threshold (0.5) is rarely optimal, especially with imbalanced data. Tuning this threshold on a validation set is a critical post-processing step to maximize the chosen metric.  
**Quick check:** After training your model, you get raw logits for each class on a token. How do you convert these into final 'B-XXX', 'I-XXX', 'O' tags for evaluation?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input Text -> Tokenizer -> BERT Encoder (mmBERT-base/BioBERTpt/etc.) -> Token-level Linear Classification Head -> Sigmoid Activations (per class) -> Thresholded Labels

### Critical Path
1. Text is tokenized into subwords with a special [CLS] token.
2. The BERT encoder produces contextual embeddings for each token.
3. A task-specific linear layer maps each token embedding to a vector of logits, one per entity class.
4. A sigmoid function is applied to each logit, producing a probability per class.
5. A tuned threshold (e.g., 0.45) is applied to each probability; if above threshold, the corresponding tag is assigned.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Model Size vs. Resources:** Larger BERT models (e.g., `large`) may yield marginal gains but require significantly more VRAM and slower inference. The paper shows `mmBERT-base` often outperforms larger variants on this task.
- **IO vs. BIO Scheme:** BIO doubles the number of labels (adds 'B-' tags). The paper finds BIO hurt macro F1 more for some models, suggesting simpler IO may be more robust when many fine-grained classes are rare.
- **Local BERT vs. Cloud LLM:** BERT models are fast, cheap to run, and privacy-preserving (local). LLMs (GPT-5, Gemini) are slow, costly, inferior in accuracy for this task, and require sending data to third parties.

### Failure Signatures
- **Poor performance on rare entities:** Check per-class precision/recall. If rare classes have recall ~0, the split likely omitted them or the model isn't learning them (weighted loss needed?).
- **Macro F1 << Micro F1:** Indicates the model is doing well on frequent classes but failing on minority classes. This is a classic sign of unresolved class imbalance.
- **High validation loss / low validation F1 early in training:** Could indicate poor learning rate, model failing to converge on the complex multilabel task, or that the chosen stratification/weighting strategy is harming learning for majority classes.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate Baseline:** Train `mmBERT-base` with the paper's reported hyperparameters (LR=5e-5, batch=10, grad accum=5, max_len=512) using simple random split and no class weighting on the SemClinBr dataset (IO scheme). Verify you can achieve ~0.69 micro F1, establishing a lower-bound baseline.
2. **Implement & Test Stratification:** Replace the random split with iterative stratification (using `scikit-multilearn`). Retrain with identical hyperparameters. Expect micro F1 to rise to ~0.76. This confirms the primary paper finding.
3. **Apply Dataset-Specific Balancing:** On the breast cancer dataset, use iterative stratification plus a weighted loss (with clamped min weight of 1.0). Compare results to stratified-only training to observe the reported improvement, validating the claim that optimal balancing is dataset-dependent.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does continued domain-specific pretraining of mmBERT on Portuguese biomedical corpora improve its clinical NER performance beyond the current multilingual pretraining?**  
   - *Basis:* The authors explicitly state that future work should explore fine-tuning mmBERT on biomedical data in Portuguese.  
   - *Why unresolved:* mmBERT was pretrained on general multilingual data; the impact of targeted biomedical Portuguese pretraining on downstream clinical NER tasks remains untested.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Perform continued pretraining of mmBERT on a large Portuguese biomedical corpus (e.g., PubMed PT, SciELO), then fine-tune and evaluate on both SemClinBr and the private breast cancer dataset, comparing performance against the original mmBERT.

2. **To what extent does data contamination in mmBERT’s pretraining corpus explain its performance advantage over BioBERTpt on the public SemClinBr dataset?**  
   - *Basis:* The authors note that the possibility of data contamination cannot be excluded as a cause of the difference in performance between BioBERTpt and mmBERT, given mmBERT was trained on 3 trillion tokens published after SemClinBr’s release.  
   - *Why unresolved:* The study did not investigate whether SemClinBr texts were included in mmBERT's pretraining data, which could artificially inflate results; this threatens the validity of the benchmark.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct a contamination audit by searching mmBERT’s pretraining data (if accessible) for overlap with SemClinBr texts, or replicate the benchmark on a new, held-out Portuguese clinical dataset not publicly available at the time of mmBERT’s training.

3. **Are the observed benefits of iterative stratification over simple random splitting statistically significant across multiple clinical NER datasets, and how do they interact with weighted loss and oversampling?**  
   - *Basis:* The authors state that the study did not include hypothesis testing to rigorously compare the different techniques for handling class imbalance, which limits generalizability.  
   - *Why unresolved:* While iterative stratification improved micro F1 in experiments, the lack of statistical tests means the reported improvements could be due to chance, and dataset-specific interactions remain unclear.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Perform a rigorous experimental design with multiple random seeds (e.g., 30 runs) for each data-splitting and imbalance-handling combination across both datasets, then apply appropriate statistical tests (e.g., ANOVA with post-hoc analysis) to determine significant differences and interactions.

## Limitations
- **LLM evaluation incomplete:** LLMs were evaluated only on SemClinBr, not on the private breast cancer dataset, limiting the generalizability of BERT vs. LLM comparisons.
- **No statistical significance testing:** Reported improvements (e.g., iterative stratification) lack confidence intervals or p-values, preventing assessment of reliability.
- **Hyperparameters fixed without tuning:** Learning rate, batch size, etc., were not optimized per model, potentially masking performance differences.

## Confidence
- **Iterative stratification improves NER performance:** High
- **Multilingual mmBERT outperforms domain-specific Portuguese BERTs:** Medium
- **Weighted loss effectiveness is dataset-dependent:** Medium
- **Encoder-only BERTs are more accurate and resource-efficient than LLMs:** High
