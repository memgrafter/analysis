# Do Large Language Models Reflect Demographic Pluralism in Safety?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07376  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07376  
- **Reference count:** 11  
- **Primary result:** Balanced thresholds (δ=0.5, τ=10) yield high reliability (ICC=0.87) and low demographic sensitivity (DS=0.12), with GPT-4o achieving the lowest DS (0.119), demonstrating scalable demographically robust safety evaluation.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the lack of demographic pluralism in LLM safety evaluations, which traditionally rely on narrow annotator pools. The authors present Demo-SafetyBench, a framework that models pluralism directly at the prompt level by reclassifying and expanding the DICES dataset across 14 safety domains while retaining demographic metadata, producing 43,050 samples. Pluralistic sensitivity is then measured using LLMs-as-Raters (Gemma-7B, GPT-4o, LLaMA-2-7B) under zero-shot conditions. The study finds that carefully chosen thresholds (δ=0.5, τ=10) achieve high inter-rater reliability (ICC=0.87) and low demographic sensitivity (DS=0.12), with GPT-4o performing best (DS=0.119). These results indicate that scalable, demographically robust safety evaluation is feasible.

## Method Summary
Demo-SafetyBench employs a two-stage pipeline. In Stage I, the original DICES prompts are reclassified into 14 safety domains using a Mistral-7B classifier with a threshold δ=0.5, preserving each prompt’s demographic metadata. For domains with fewer than 100 instances, additional queries are generated using Llama-3.1-8B, conditioned on the target domain and demographic samples drawn from an empirical prior p_demo estimated from the data. All prompts (original and synthetic) are then deduplicated via SimHash with a Hamming threshold τ=10. Stage II evaluates safety perception on the resulting corpus Q₂: each prompt–demographic pair is presented in a structured zero‑shot input to three rater models (Gemma‑7B, GPT‑4o, LLaMA‑2‑7B). The models output a binary safety judgment and a confidence score; these are aggregated into a tensor S and used to compute pluralistic metrics including Demographic Sensitivity (DS), Inter‑Rater Correlation (ICC), and fairness gaps (DPD, EOD).

## Key Results
- Balanced thresholds (δ=0.5, τ=10) achieve high reliability (ICC = 0.87) and low demographic sensitivity (DS = 0.12).  
- GPT‑4o attains the lowest demographic sensitivity (DS = 0.119) among the raters.  
- The final corpus contains 43,050 prompt–demographic pairs covering 14 safety domains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Modeling pluralism at the prompt level (decoupling value framing from responses) yields a scalable, demographically robust safety evaluation.  
- **Mechanism**: Reclassify DICES prompts into 14 domains while retaining demographic metadata `d`; then evaluate each prompt–demographic pair using LLMs-as-Raters under zero‑shot conditions.  
- **Core assumption**: Safety perception is primarily shaped by the prompt’s framing and its associated demographic context, independent of any generated response.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] “Demo‑SafetyBench addresses this gap by modeling demographic pluralism directly at the prompt level, decoupling value framing from responses.”  
  - [section 3.1] Details the reclassification of Q₀ into Q₁ with preserved `d` and multi‑label domain assignment.  
  - [corpus] DICES dataset provides explicit demographic metadata (gender, race, age, education) enabling prompt‑level grounding.  
- **Break condition**: If the demographic metadata is strongly biased or systematically missing, the prompt‑level isolation fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Balanced thresholds (`δ = 0.5` for classification, `τ = 10` for SimHash) produce a high‑reliability, low‑sensitivity benchmark.  
- **Mechanism**: `δ` controls multi‑label assignment trade‑off (false positives vs. false negatives); `τ` controls deduplication aggressiveness; together they shape the final corpus Q₂.  
- **Core assumption**: A single global threshold set can simultaneously optimize error rates and semantic diversity across all 14 domains.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] “Balanced thresholds (δ = 0.5, τ = 10) achieve high reliability (ICC = 0.87) and low demographic sensitivity (DS = 0.12).”  
  - [section 4.3] Shows error trade‑offs for varying δ and τ, identifying δ = 0.5 and τ = 10 as optimal.  
  - [corpus] Corpus size and label distributions (Table 1) reflect the impact of these thresholds.  
- **Break condition**: If threshold sensitivity is highly domain‑specific, global thresholds may over‑ or under‑classify in certain categories, harming validity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: LLMs-as‑Raters can effectively capture pluralistic safety judgments without task‑specific fine‑tuning.  
- **Mechanism**: Each rater model `m` receives a structured prompt–demographic pair and outputs a safety score (`0` safe, `1` unsafe); scores are aggregated to compute DS, ICC, DPD, EOD.  
- **Core assumption**: Large language models, when prompted zero‑shot, can simulate diverse demographic perspectives with consistent internal reasoning.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] “Pluralistic sensitivity is then evaluated using LLMs‑as‑Raters—Gemma‑7B, GPT‑4o, LLaMA‑2‑7B—under zero‑shot inference.”  
  - [section 3.2] Defines the structured input and deterministic inference protocol.  
  - [section 4.1] Reports GPT‑4o’s DS = 0.119 and ICC = 0.87, supporting the claim.  
- **Break condition**: If rater models inherit strong demographic biases from their training data, the scores may not reflect true pluralism but rather model‑specific priors.

## Foundational Learning

### Demographic Pluralism in AI Safety
- **Why needed**: The entire framework assumes safety judgments vary across demographic groups; misunderstanding this leads to misinterpreting DS results.  
- **Quick check**: Give an example of two demographic groups that might disagree on the safety of a prompt about alcohol consumption, and explain why.

### Prompt‑Level vs. Response‑Level Evaluation
- **Why needed**: The paper’s novelty lies in isolating framing; confusing it with response evaluation obscures the methodological contribution.  
- **Quick check**: Why might evaluating model responses introduce confounders unrelated to the prompt’s inherent framing?

### Multi‑Label Classification with Thresholding
- **Why needed**: Prompts can belong to multiple safety domains; the chosen `δ` determines label sets and impacts downstream analysis.  
- **Quick check**: Describe how increasing `δ` from 0.5 to 0.7 affects false positives, false negatives, and the proportion of multi‑label examples.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
- Stage I (Data Construction): DICES → Mistral‑7B classifier (δ=0.5) → low‑resource domain identification → Llama‑3.1‑8B generator (conditioned on domain & p_demo) → SimHash deduplication (τ=10) → final corpus Q₂.
- Stage II (Benchmarking): Q₂ + demographic vectors → structured input → rater models (Gemma‑7B, GPT‑4o, LLaMA‑2‑7B) → safety scores (binary + confidence) → score tensor S → metrics (DS, ICC, DPD, EOD).
- Supporting computation: Empirical prior p_demo estimation from metadata.

### Critical path
1. Load DICES with metadata.
2. Run Mistral‑7B classification with δ=0.5.
3. Identify domains with count < 100; generate synthetic queries using Llama‑3.1‑8B conditioned on domain and sampled `d ≽ p_demo`.
4. Apply SimHash (τ=10) to remove duplicates.
5. For each (prompt, d) pair, prompt each rater model zero‑shot, parse confidence.
6. Compute metrics across demographic subgroups and domains.

### Design tradeoffs
- Automation vs. bias: Fully automated pipeline avoids human annotation costs but inherits biases from the classification and generation models.
- Threshold selection: δ=0.5 balances FP/FN but is arbitrarily chosen; domain‑specific tuning might improve accuracy at the cost of complexity.
- Rater choice: Larger models (GPT‑4o) give lower DS but higher compute; smaller models enable scaling but may amplify demographic noise.

### Failure signatures
- Unexpectedly high DS (>0.3) across all raters → indicates metadata misalignment or flawed prompt‑level assumption.
- Low ICC (<0.6) → suggests inconsistent domain labeling or unstable rater behaviour.
- SimHash duplicate rate remains high → τ too large or poor hash quality.
- GPU memory/time blow‑up in Stage II → model size or batch size too large for available hardware.

### First 3 experiments
1. *Classification sanity check*: Randomly sample 100 reclassified prompts, manually verify domain labels and multi‑label assignments, compute agreement with Mistral‑7B.
2. *Threshold sweep*: Run Stage I with δ ∈ {0.4,0.5,0.6,0.7} and τ ∈ {8,10,12,15}; measure resulting DS, ICC, and duplicate fractions to confirm reported optimal points.
3. *Rater consistency*: Run all three raters on a fixed 1 000‑query subset; compute pairwise Kendall’s τ and per‑domain DS; verify that GPT‑4o shows lowest DS and highest ICC as in Table 2.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. *Do computational efficiency metrics of LLMs-as-raters hold across diverse hardware platforms?*  
   Limitations note that efficiency metrics were estimated under controlled GPU environments; cross‑hardware validation remains future work.

2. *How does stochastic decoding (temperature, top-p) influence demographic sensitivity and inter‑rater agreement?*  
   All evaluations used t=0, top‑p=1; sampling could change pluralistic judgments.

3. *Are the chosen thresholds (δ=0.5 for classification, τ=10 for SimHash) universally optimal across all safety domains?*  
   Section 4.3 selected thresholds based on aggregate error rates, but no per‑domain analysis was performed.

4. *Does demographic pluralism in safety persist in multimodal (e.g., image‑text) evaluation settings?*  
   Pluralistic sensitivity was measured at the prompt level, without extending to contexts where demographic cues may interact differently.

## Limitations
- DICES demographic metadata may be systematically biased or incomplete, threatening the prompt‑level pluralism assumption.  
- Global thresholds (δ=0.5, τ=10) may not generalize across all 14 safety domains, risking over/under‑classification.  
- LLM raters may reflect model‑training biases rather than true human demographic variance, undermining DS as a pluralism metric.

## Confidence
- Prompt‑level pluralism modeling: **Medium**  
- Balanced threshold selection: **Medium**  
- LLMs‑as‑Raters validity: **Low‑to‑Medium**
