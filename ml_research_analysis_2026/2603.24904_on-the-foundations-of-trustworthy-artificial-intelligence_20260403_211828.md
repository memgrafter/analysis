# On the Foundations of Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24904
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24904
- **Reference Count:** 0
- **Primary Result:** Proves platform-deterministic inference is necessary and sufficient for trustworthy AI and constructs a pure integer arithmetic inference engine that achieves bitwise identical outputs across ARM and x86 architectures for models up to 6.7B parameters.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes that the trustworthiness of AI systems—encompassing verification, reproducibility, auditability, and certification—fundamentally collapses to the property of platform-deterministic inference: the requirement that an inference function `f(m, x)` produces identical outputs on all hardware platforms. The authors prove this necessity and sufficiency formally, introducing *trust entropy* to quantify the exact verification failure probability introduced by non-determinism. Empirically, they construct a 99,000-line Rust inference engine using only pure integer arithmetic (INT8 quantized weights, Q16 activations) with a fixed evaluation order. This engine demonstrates bitwise identical outputs across ARM and x86 in 82 cross-architecture tests (0 hash mismatches up to 512 tokens), with corroborating attestations from four geographically distributed nodes and succinct 152-byte STARK proofs for layer computations. The work argues that AI trust reduces to the engineering problem of arithmetic determinism.

## Method Summary
Reproduction requires building the `arc-chain` Rust project from the specified commit (`cfb4780`), which implements a pure integer transformer inference engine. The engine loads GGUF-quantized models (e.g., Llama-2-7B Q4_K_M) and executes all operations—matrix multiplication, normalization, attention—using fixed-width two’s complement integers with a fixed, platform-agnostic evaluation order. The primary validation is a cross-platform hash match: run the `bench_int8` example on ARM and x86 systems with identical prompts and sequence lengths, then compare the BLAKE3 hash of the generated token sequence. Successful reproduction yields identical hashes across all documented test cases (78 prompts for TinyLlama and Llama-2-7B, sequence lengths up to 512). Additional validation involves verifying the constant 152-byte size of generated STARK receipts and the consistency of on-chain attestation transactions from distributed nodes.

## Key Results
- Platform-deterministic inference is proven necessary and sufficient for trustworthy AI (verification, reproducibility, auditability, certification).
- A pure integer arithmetic inference engine achieves bitwise identical outputs across ARM and x86 in 82 cross-architecture tests (0 hash mismatches for sequence lengths ≤512 tokens).
- Four geographically distributed nodes produced identical outputs, verified by 356 on-chain attestation transactions.
- Succinct STARK proofs of dense layer computations yield constant 152-byte on-chain receipts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper's core thesis rests on three interconnected mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1: Determinism as Foundation**
Platform-deterministic inference (identical outputs `f(m, x)` across all hardware) is both necessary and sufficient for trust properties. Under determinism, verification simplifies to a single hash comparison: a verifier re-executes `f` and checks the output hash matches the prover's. The proof shows verification complexity collapses to O(1) (Theorem 16). The mechanism relies on integer arithmetic's associative ring structure (`ℤ/2^nℤ`) and a fixed evaluation order. Break condition: if integer operations overflow inconsistently or compilers reorder independent ops, bitwise determinism fails.

**Mechanism 2: Trust Entropy Quantifies Failure**
Non-determinism introduces *trust entropy* `H_T` (Rényi collision entropy over the output distribution). The probability that two honest parties (prover, verifier) independently sampling hardware agree on the output is exactly `2^{-H_T}`. Thus, honest verification failure probability is `1 - 2^{-H_T}` (Theorem 11). This links a quantitative measure of non-determinism to a fundamental limit on verification completeness, separating soundness (cryptographic) from completeness (determinism-dependent). Break condition: the "exact" formula assumes a perfect random oracle hash and a discrete output space; hash collisions or continuous distributions would make it an approximation.

**Mechanism 3: Integer Arithmetic Enforces Determinism**
Using pure fixed-width two’s complement integer operations (INT8 weights, Q16 activations) with a fixed reduction tree order yields platform-deterministic inference by algebraic necessity, bypassing IEEE 754 floating-point non-associativity. All transformer operations—dense matmul, RMSNorm (integer Newton-Raphson), RoPE (precomputed Q16 tables), SiLU (integer lookup)—are implemented with integer ops. Evidence: 82 cross-platform tests with zero hash mismatches (Section 9.1). Break condition: an integer overflow in accumulators or a data race in parallel sections (e.g., attention head reduction) could introduce divergence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Platform-Deterministic Inference**
  - *Why needed:* The central thesis; all trust properties (V, R, A, C) presuppose it.
  - *Quick check:* "If an AI model's output differs on ARM vs. x86 for the same input, can it be certified for safety-critical deployment across both platforms?"

- **Trust Entropy (Rényi Collision Entropy)**
  - *Why needed:* Quantifies the *cost* of non-determinism exactly, linking Shannon theory to verification failure probability.
  - *Quick check:* "If a non-deterministic inference system produces 3 distinct outputs uniformly, what is the honest verifier rejection rate? What if it produces 10^6 distinct outputs uniformly?"

- **Associativity as Algebraic Foundation**
  - *Why needed:* Explains why integer arithmetic works and floating-point fails; the engineering effort reduces to selecting an associative arithmetic substrate.
  - *Quick check:* "Why does changing SIMD width (reduction tree shape) change the sum of floating-point numbers but not integers?"

## Architecture Onboarding
The system's dataflow and components are:

Model Loader -> Quantization Layer (INT8 weights, Q16 scales) -> Layer Implementations (Dense, RMSNorm, RoPE, SiLU, Attention) -> KV Cache (Q16) -> Execution Engine (fixed order) -> Attestation Service (BLAKE3 hash) -> DAG Consensus Node -> STARK Prover (optional) -> Verifier Client

**Critical Path:** Input → Tokenize → Embedding Lookup → [x L times: Attention → RMSNorm → Dense → Residual] → Output Logits → Greedy Argmax → Token Sequence → BLAKE3 Hash → Attestation Transaction → DAG Finality. Every forward-pass operation must be integer and ordered.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Precision vs. Determinism:** INT8 weights yield memory efficiency but increase perplexity (e.g., 144 vs. 5.47 on WikiText-2). Mixed-precision (INT16 attention, INT8 FFN) is a natural optimization but must preserve determinism.
- **KV Cache Size:** Full Q16 KV cache (~2x activations) is memory-intensive but necessary; lower precision caused attention failures after ~10 tokens.
- **Proof Scope:** Current STARK proofs cover only dense layers. Extending to attention, normalization, and activations is an open engineering challenge (AIR complexity, integer softmax).

**Failure Signatures:**
- **Hash mismatch on same hardware:** Indicates non-determinism in the integer engine (e.g., data race, uninitialized memory).
- **Hash mismatch across architectures:** Indicates violation of integer assumptions (e.g., overflow, compiler reordering, accidental floating-point use).
- **STARK proof generation failure:** Usually due to trace size exceeding NTT limits; requires sharding or AIR redesign.
- **Consensus node not finalizing:** Likely network/DAG issue; determinism ensures *what* is attested is consistent.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Cross-Platform Hash Match:** Run `bench_int8` on ARM and x86 with the same GGUF model (Llama-2-7B Q4_K_M) and prompt; output BLAKE3 hashes must be identical.
2. **Multi-Node Consensus Replay:** Submit identical inference to two DAG nodes on different architectures; their attestation transactions must contain the same `output_hash`.
3. **STARK Proof Reproducibility:** Generate proofs for a 7B model's dense layers twice; the 152-byte commitment receipts must be byte-for-byte identical.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extending STARK Proofs:** Can STARK proofs cover all transformer operations (attention, integer softmax, RMSNorm, RoPE, activations) for ≥50B models with ≤200-byte on-chain receipts?
- **Deterministic Training:** Is deterministic training possible for LLMs while preserving convergence and final quality, given sources like SGD, data shuffling, and distributed all-reduce?
- **Mixed-Precision Quantization:** What mixed-precision integer scheme (e.g., INT16 attention, INT8 FFN) optimally closes the perplexity gap while maintaining platform determinism?
- **Formal Verification:** Can the 99k-line Rust implementation be machine-checked (Coq/Lean) to prove correct integer inference for at least one transformer variant?

## Limitations
- Incomplete empirical reporting: 4 of 82 cross-architecture tests are not itemized in the appendix.
- Succinct verification (STARK) currently covers only dense layers; attention and normalization are excluded.
- Theoretical proofs assume ideal conditions (integer associativity, perfect hash random oracle) that may not hold in edge cases (overflow, compiler reordering).
- The INT8-only engine exhibits a large perplexity gap (144 vs. 5.47); the trade-off between precision and trust is not fully characterized.

## Confidence
- **Platform-determinism as necessary & sufficient:** Medium (internal proofs, no independent validation).
- **Bitwise identical outputs across platforms:** Medium-High (0/82 reported mismatches, raw data not public).
- **STARK receipts (152-byte constant):** Medium (proof generation not independently benchmarked).
- **Distributed attestation integrity:** Medium (on-chain claims not audited externally).
