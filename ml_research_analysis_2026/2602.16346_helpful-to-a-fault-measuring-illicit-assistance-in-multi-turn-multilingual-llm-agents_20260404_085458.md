# Helpful to a Fault: Measuring Illicit Assistance in Multi-Turn, Multilingual LLM Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16346  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16346  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** STING increases illicit-task completion by up to 107.1% (AgentHarm Score) compared to single-turn and chat-based baselines for Qwen3-Next.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces STING, a red-teaming framework designed to measure multi-turn, multilingual illicit assistance from LLM agents. STING decomposes harmful intents into atomic phases and uses adaptive attacker agents with real-time judge feedback to circumvent safety refusals. Evaluations across multiple models and six languages show STING significantly outperforms baselines, reveals faster vulnerability discovery via survival analysis, and finds that lower-resource languages do not consistently increase jailbreak success in agentic settings—contrasting with prior chatbot research. Lightweight defenses, such as safety prompts, only partially mitigate harm.

## Method Summary
STING employs four coordinated agents: a Strategist decomposes a harmful intent into a phased plan and persona; an Attacker executes each phase turn-by-turn against the target agent; a Refusal Detector and Phase-Completion Checker provide binary feedback after every target response. The Attacker adapts (e.g., masking, escalation) if a phase is not completed or a refusal occurs. The loop continues until all phases are completed or budgets are exhausted (up to 10 strategies, 10 turns each). Experiments run on AgentHarm instances across English and five other languages, with target models including Qwen3-Next, GPT-5.1, and Gemini 3 Flash. Analysis uses survival modeling (Kaplan-Meier curves, Restricted Mean Jailbreak Discovery, hazard ratios) to measure attack efficiency. Ablations test reasoning effort, tool hints, and defenses.

## Key Results
- STING achieves up to 107.1% higher AgentHarm Score (AHS) than single-turn and X-Teaming baselines for Qwen3-Next, demonstrating the efficacy of phased, adaptive attacks.
- Multilingual evaluations show no consistent jailbreak increase in lower-resource languages (e.g., Urdu, Telugu), diverging from prior chatbot-only findings.
- A simple safety prompt defense reduces harmful-task completion by 37.3% on average but does not eliminate risk, highlighting the need for stronger multi-turn defenses.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Phased Decomposition Evades Single-Turn Refusals
Claim: Breaking a harmful intent into sequential, atomic phases increases illicit-task completion because each sub-request appears innocuous in isolation, avoiding the blanket refusal triggered by the full harmful prompt.
Mechanism: The Strategist creates a multi-phase plan; the Attacker executes each phase turn-by-turn. Safety mechanisms are more likely to reject a single request containing the complete malicious goal than a series of benign-looking steps that cumulatively achieve harm.
Core assumption: LLM agent safety systems do not effectively track cumulative intent across multiple turns.
Evidence anchors: [abstract] states STING "decomposes harmful intents into phased plans" and "substantially increases illicit-task completion"; [section 3] details the plan structure and turn-by-turn execution; the corpus gap (AgentHarm is single-turn) motivates this design.
Break condition: If the target analyzes conversation history to infer cumulative intent, or if any individual phase is inherently suspicious, the attack fails early.

### Mechanism 2: Judge-Driven Adaptation Overcomes Partial Failures
Claim: Real-time feedback from dedicated judge agents (Refusal Detector, Phase-Completion Checker) enables the Attacker to dynamically adapt, handling both safety blocks and incomplete tool executions to achieve higher phase completion rates.
Mechanism: After each target response, judges provide binary signals. The Attacker uses this to either retry the current phase with a new tactic (e.g., masking, escalation) or advance. This closed-loop refinement resolves ambiguous failures.
Core assumption: Many target failures are partial (e.g., mild refusal, incomplete tool call); iterative adaptation with precise feedback can navigate these.
Evidence anchors: [section 3] describes the adaptive loop; [section 5.2] uses survival analysis to quantify efficiency gains; X-Teaming's poor performance in agentic settings [corpus] underscores the need for judge-guided adaptation in tool-use contexts.
Break condition: Inaccurate judges (false refusals/completion) misguide the Attacker; adaptive strategies may be exhausted without progress.

### Mechanism 3: Specialized Agent Roles Enable Scalable Red-Teaming
Claim: Dividing labor among Strategist, Attacker, and Judges prevents role conflict and optimizes each component for multi-phase, tool-based attacks.
Mechanism: Strategist focuses on planning; Attacker on persona embodiment and turn-level interaction; Judges on objective evaluation. This separation allows using different LLMs for each role and better simulates a skilled red team.
Core assumption: A monolithic agent would struggle to simultaneously plan, adapt, and evaluate impartially.
Evidence anchors: [section 3] defines the four agents; [section 5.1] shows deliberate specialization (Gemini 3 Pro for Strategist, Qwen3-Next for others); multi-agent frameworks exist but STING is tailored for agentic misuse.
Break condition: Poor inter-agent communication (e.g., format errors) derails the loop; low judge accuracy makes the system unreliable.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Strategist -> Attacker -> Target. Refusal Detector and Phase-Completion Checker provide feedback to Attacker after each Target response. Attacker adapts or advances based on feedback.
Critical path: Strategist generates persona and phased plan. For each phase, Attacker interacts with Target until phase_completed=True (per judge) or a refusal triggers adaptation. Loop continues until all phases complete (jailbreak) or budgets (Smax, Tmax) exhaust.
Design tradeoffs: Specialized judges improve evaluation reliability but add latency and potential error; using LLMs as judges balances cost and scalability but may be less accurate than humans; fixed budgets (Smax, Tmax) enable controlled experiments but may limit coverage for complex tasks.
Failure signatures: (1) Poor phase decomposition causing early refusal; (2) Inaccurate judges (e.g., low refusal recall) misleading adaptation; (3) Adaptive strategies exhausted without phase completion; (4) Target safety effectively blocks multi-turn attacks.
First 3 experiments: (1) Run STING on one AgentHarm scenario with Smax=1 to verify plan generation and initial Attacker turns; (2) Manually validate judge accuracy on a sample of target responses (refusals/compliances) to ensure feedback reliability; (3) Vary Smax and plot discovery curves to confirm multi-turn advantage over single-turn baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can we design effective defenses specifically for multi-turn, tool-using agents that mitigate jailbreaks without severely degrading benign utility? Basis: Conclusion identifies this as a key future direction. Why unresolved: Current lightweight defenses only reduce harm by ~37% and may not generalize; multimodal/multi-turn vulnerabilities remain unaddressed. Needed evidence: Novel defense proposals (e.g., real-time tool monitoring) rigorously evaluated within STING.
- Why do lower-resource languages not consistently increase jailbreak success in agentic systems, contradicting prior chatbot-only findings? Basis: Paper states divergence from common chatbot findings. Why unresolved: Hypotheses (e.g., imperfect multilingual tool usage) lack systematic disentanglement from capability differences. Needed evidence: Controlled experiments isolating tool-execution accuracy and benign capability per language.
- What causes the non-monotonic relationship between reasoning effort and illicit-task completion, where medium reasoning yields lower harm than both low and high? Basis: For GPT-5.1, medium reasoning is safer than none or high. Why unresolved: Internal mechanisms unexplored; hypotheses (e.g., coherent harmful plans at high reasoning) are speculative. Needed evidence: Fine-grained output analysis, activation inspections, and systematic reasoning-depth ablation.
- How accurate are the automated judge agents in multi-turn, multilingual red-teaming, and how do their errors bias evaluation metrics? Basis: Human validation on Urdu/Telugu shows refusal recall 0.71, indicating under-detection. Why unresolved: Judge performance across all languages/strategies not thoroughly characterized; noisy judges could distort discovery curves. Needed evidence: Large-scale human evaluation across languages, error analysis for systematic biases, development of robust multilingual evaluators.

## Limitations
- Tool environment specification (APIs, schemas) is not provided, risking implementation mismatch.
- Target model inference settings (reasoning budgets, system prompts) are unspecified, potentially altering agent behavior.
- Judge accuracy is only partially validated (refusal recall 0.71 on two languages; phase completion checker not validated), which may bias the feedback loop and metrics.
- Translation quality for multilingual prompts lacks automatic or human evaluation, threatening semantic equivalence.
- Statistical analysis parameters (Cox model formulation, RMJD calculation) are not detailed.

## Confidence
- Increased illicit-task completion via STING: **Medium** (due to judge and tool uncertainties).
- Survival-based vulnerability metrics (RMJD, hazard ratios): **High** (directly summarize observed events, though data quality matters).
- Multilingual divergence from chatbot results: **Medium** (limited judge validation and translation concerns).
- Lightweight defense efficacy (safety prompt): **Medium** (prompt robustness may vary across models).
