# Rethinking Music Captioning with Music Metadata LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03023
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03023
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Two-stage metadata-based approach achieves comparable caption quality to end-to-end models with 46.3% less training time; prompt engineering yields >20% improvement; metadata imputation boosts performance up to 21%.

## Executive Summary
Music captioning often suffers from limited high-quality audio-caption pairs and rigid output styles. This paper introduces a novel two-stage pipeline that first predicts structured music attributes (genre, mood, instruments, keywords) from audio, then leverages a pre-trained large language model (LLM) to transform these attributes into fluent, stylized captions. Experiments show that the method matches end-to-end baselines in SBERT similarity while requiring 46.3% less training time. Additional prompt engineering further improves caption quality by over 20%. The approach also supports metadata imputation, delivering up to 21% gain when only partial attributes are available—a feature absent in traditional captioners.

## Method Summary
The proposed system decouples music captioning into two independent stages. Stage 1 trains an audio-to-metadata model on datasets with labeled attributes (genre, mood, instruments, keywords). The model maps an audio clip to a set of structured predictions. Stage 2 feeds these predicted attributes into a pre-trained LLM via carefully designed prompts to generate natural language captions at inference. Because the LLM is not fine-tuned on audio, the pipeline only needs audio-metadata pairs for training, which are more abundant than full audio-caption pairs. Moreover, the LLM stage is highly flexible: prompt engineering can control caption style, and missing attributes can be imputed by the LLM’s language priors, enabling robust performance with incomplete metadata.

## Key Results
- Comparable SBERT similarity to end-to-end baselines with 46.3% less training time.
- Post-hoc prompt engineering improves caption quality by over 20%.
- Metadata imputation with partial inputs boosts prediction performance by up to 21% on average.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The two-stage design isolates the complex task of generating fluent language from the audio domain. Predicting interpretable metadata (e.g., genre, instruments) is a well-defined, often easier problem that can be trained on moderately sized labeled datasets. Once reliable metadata is available, a powerful pre-trained LLM can compose diverse, stylistically adaptable captions by conditioning on that metadata. This separation reduces the need for large-scale paired audio-caption data and allows prompt-based control over output style. The LLM’s internal knowledge also helps to fill in gaps when some metadata is missing, effectively performing imputation without additional training.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Audio Input -> Audio Encoder -> Metadata Predictor -> Structured Metadata -> Pre-trained LLM -> Caption Output  
**Critical path:** Accurate audio feature extraction → reliable metadata prediction → effective LLM prompting. Failure at any stage degrades caption relevance.  
**Design tradeoffs:** 
- *Modular vs end-to-end:* Modularity allows independent dataset usage and prompt control but introduces error propagation; end-to-end training may yield better optimization but requires large audio-caption pairs.
- *Metadata granularity:* More attributes give richer context but increase prediction difficulty and computational cost.
- *LLM choice:* Larger models improve fluency but raise inference latency and cost; smaller models may require fine-tuning.
**Failure signatures:** 
- High metadata prediction error leads to irrelevant or factually incorrect captions.
- Poorly crafted prompts yield generic, repetitive, or off-topic text.
- Imputation fails when crucial attributes are missing, resulting in hallucinated details.
**Three first experiments:** 
1. Quantify metadata prediction accuracy per attribute on a held-out audio-metadata dataset.
2. Compare caption quality (SBERT) across different LLM backbones (e.g., GPT-3, T5) using a fixed prompt.
3. Evaluate imputation robustness by systematically dropping metadata fields and measuring SBERT drop and caption consistency.

## Limitations
- Evaluation relies solely on SBERT similarity; lacks human evaluation and complementary metrics such as BLEU or diversity scores.
- Training time reduction claim is not fully substantiated due to unspecified baseline architectures and hardware differences.
- Prompt engineering improvement and imputation benefits are vaguely reported and may not generalize to diverse music domains or real-world incomplete metadata.
