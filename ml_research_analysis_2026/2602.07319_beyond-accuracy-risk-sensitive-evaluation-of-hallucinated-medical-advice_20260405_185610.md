# Beyond Accuracy: Risk-Sensitive Evaluation of Hallucinated Medical Advice

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07319
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07319
- Reference count: 6
- Models with similar factual accuracy can exhibit dramatically different risk profiles when evaluated for risk-bearing hallucinations

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a risk-sensitive evaluation framework for assessing hallucinations in patient-facing medical question answering systems. Instead of measuring factual correctness, the framework quantifies the presence and severity of risk-bearing medical language—such as treatment directives, contraindications, urgency cues, and high-risk medications—using a continuous Risk-Sensitive Hallucination Score (RSHS). By combining RSHS with a relevance measure (QASim) between the query and response, the framework identifies high-risk, low-grounding failures that standard metrics miss. Applied to three instruction-tuned language models on controlled patient-facing prompts, the results show that models with similar surface-level behavior exhibit substantially different risk profiles, with larger models producing more risk-bearing language in the upper tail.

## Method Summary
The framework generates 200 synthetic patient-facing prompts as safety stress tests and uses pattern-based detection of risk-bearing language categories with manually assigned ordinal weights. It computes the Risk-Sensitive Hallucination Score (RSHS) by aggregating pattern weights with length normalization, and calculates query-response relevance (QASim) using cosine similarity of sentence embeddings. The approach is applied to responses from three instruction-tuned FLAN-T5 models (small, base, large) and one decoder-only Phi-3 model to identify high-risk, low-grounding failure modes.

## Key Results
- Models with similar factual accuracy can exhibit dramatically different risk profiles when evaluated for risk-bearing hallucinations
- Larger language models show increased prevalence of risk-bearing language in the upper tail of the RSHS distribution
- Evaluation validity is critically dependent on task and prompt design, with management-oriented prompts substantially increasing risk-bearing language

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Risk-bearing language patterns serve as actionable safety signals independent of factual correctness.
- Mechanism: The framework identifies specific linguistic cues (e.g., "stop," "urgent," "contraindicated") that carry potential harm if acted upon, regardless of whether the advice is medically correct.
- Core assumption: Language patterns associated with clinical risk can be reliably identified through keyword matching without requiring medical expertise or ground truth validation.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Rather than assessing clinical correctness, our approach evaluates the potential impact of hallucinated content if acted upon."
  - [section] "We define a set of risk-bearing language categories that have been widely identified as safety-critical in medical NLP and clinical decision support literature"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus shows related work on hallucination detection but not specifically on pattern-based risk scoring
- Break condition: Pattern matching fails when risk-bearing language is expressed through complex or nuanced phrasing that doesn't match simple keyword templates.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Combining risk scoring with relevance detection identifies the most dangerous failure modes.
- Mechanism: High-risk responses that are poorly grounded in the query represent particularly dangerous failures, as they may appear plausible but be disconnected from the actual patient need.
- Core assumption: The combination of RSHS and QASim captures a distinct failure mode that neither metric alone would identify.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We further combine risk scoring with a relevance measure to identify high-risk, low-grounding failures."
  - [section] "By jointly analyzing RSHS and QASim, we identify a particularly concerning class of failures: responses that exhibit high risk and low relevance"
  - [corpus] Weak - corpus shows relevance detection work but not specifically combined with risk scoring
- Break condition: Relevance measures fail when they cannot capture semantic grounding in complex medical contexts or when high-risk language is actually appropriate for the query.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Evaluation validity depends critically on task and prompt design.
- Mechanism: The framework demonstrates that hallucination risk is highly sensitive to how prompts are framed, with management-oriented prompts significantly increasing risk-bearing language.
- Core assumption: Safety-critical behaviors only emerge under specific prompt conditions that encourage actionable guidance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "evaluation validity is critically dependent on task and prompt design"
  - [section] "Prompts that invite management or urgency considerations substantially increase the prevalence of risk-bearing language"
  - [corpus] Moderate - corpus shows related work on prompt sensitivity but not specifically in medical risk contexts
- Break condition: If real-world usage patterns differ substantially from evaluation prompts, the safety assessment may not generalize.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Pattern matching engine -> Scoring module -> Relevance engine -> Analysis pipeline -> Evaluation framework

- Critical path:
  1. Generate controlled patient-facing prompts
  2. Submit prompts to language models
  3. Apply pattern matching to model responses
  4. Compute RSHS and QASim scores
  5. Analyze joint risk-relevance distributions

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Pattern specificity vs. coverage: More specific patterns reduce false positives but may miss novel risk expressions
  - Weight calibration: Ordinal weights provide transparency but lack clinical validation
  - Embedding choice: Pre-trained embeddings offer convenience but may not capture medical semantics optimally

- Failure signatures:
  - False negatives: Risk-bearing language expressed through complex phrasing or domain-specific terminology
  - False positives: Medical jargon or standard practice language incorrectly flagged as risky
  - Prompt bias: Safety signals only emerge under specific evaluation conditions

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Test pattern matching on a small sample of responses to verify coverage and precision
  2. Validate RSHS scoring on responses with known risk levels to calibrate weight sensitivity
  3. Compare QASim scores across prompt-response pairs to ensure relevance detection works as expected

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How well do pattern-based risk-bearing language heuristics generalize to diverse patient queries and model architectures beyond the tested settings?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors acknowledge that pattern matching may miss subtle forms of unsafe guidance and that findings are limited to synthetic prompts and a single model family.
- Why unresolved: The framework relies on predefined keyword and phrase lists; real-world queries may contain novel or implicit risk-bearing language not covered by these patterns, and different model architectures may produce different failure modes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation across diverse real-world medical queries, multiple model families (including decoder-only, multimodal, and specialized clinical models), and validation against human expert annotations of risk-bearing language.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the relationship between the presence of risk-bearing language and actual clinical harm if such advice were followed?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state that RSHS does not assess clinical correctness, appropriateness, or patient outcomes and is intended for evaluation, not deployment.
- Why unresolved: RSHS quantifies risk-bearing language presence and severity but does not link these signals to real-world harm or clinical consequences, leaving the clinical validity of the score unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies correlating RSHS scores with documented patient outcomes, adverse events, or clinician assessments of harm in real or simulated clinical scenarios.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How should evaluation metrics balance sensitivity to risk-bearing language with the need to avoid false positives that could over-censor clinically appropriate guidance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors highlight that risk-bearing language is treated as a signal of potential harm regardless of factual accuracy, and that higher RSHS scores indicate greater presence of such language, but do not address trade-offs with false positives.
- Why unresolved: Without calibration against clinical appropriateness, the framework may flag benign or correct medical advice as high-risk, raising concerns about over-censoring in safety-critical applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies measuring false positive rates of RSHS against expert-annotated datasets of both harmful and clinically appropriate advice, and analysis of the impact of false positives on downstream decision-making or model deployment.

## Limitations
- Pattern coverage limitations: The framework relies on keyword-based pattern matching which may miss nuanced or domain-specific expressions of risk.
- Clinical validity concerns: The ordinal weights assigned to pattern severity lack formal clinical validation, potentially misrepresenting true risk levels.
- Evaluation prompt bias: Safety assessment appears highly sensitive to prompt design, raising questions about generalization to real-world usage patterns.

## Confidence

**High confidence**: The core insight that risk-sensitive evaluation is necessary for medical AI safety assessment is well-supported. The identification of high-risk, low-grounding failure modes as particularly dangerous is theoretically sound and practically important.

**Medium confidence**: The specific implementation of RSHS and its effectiveness in quantifying risk-bearing language across different model sizes shows promise, but the pattern-based approach may have coverage gaps that affect reliability.

**Low confidence**: Claims about evaluation validity being "critically dependent" on prompt design require further validation, as real-world usage patterns may differ substantially from controlled evaluation prompts.

## Next Checks

1. **Pattern coverage validation**: Test the pattern matching system on a small sample of human-annotated medical responses with known risk levels to identify gaps in pattern coverage and false positive rates.

2. **Clinical expert review**: Have medical professionals review a stratified sample of responses across different RSHS ranges to validate whether the risk scoring correlates with actual clinical safety concerns.

3. **Prompt generalization study**: Evaluate the same models on a separate set of real-world medical queries (rather than synthetic prompts) to assess whether the risk profiles observed in controlled conditions generalize to actual usage scenarios.
