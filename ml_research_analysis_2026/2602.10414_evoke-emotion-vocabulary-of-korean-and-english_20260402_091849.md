# EVOKE: Emotion Vocabulary Of Korean and English

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10414
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10414
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Comprehensive bilingual lexicon of 1,427 Korean and 1,399 English emotion words with systematic annotations for polysemy, metaphor, and translational mappings, revealing high translational coverage (~96%+) and substantial inter-annotator agreement for Korean (κ=0.74) but moderate for English (κ=0.60).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces EVOKE, a cross-linguistic emotion word dataset designed to address the scarcity of theory-agnostic resources with systematic annotations for polysemy and metaphor. The authors compiled emotion words from prior studies, established many-to-many translational mappings via bilingual experts using dictionaries, and applied a multi-criteria annotation scheme (acceptability, semantic experiencer, exclusion, polysemy) by native speakers. The resulting resource documents extensive translational equivalence, identifies language-specific words, and captures polysemous/metaphorical relations, providing a foundation for comparative linguistic analysis of emotion lexicon structure.

## Method Summary
The construction of EVOKE followed three main phases. First, a base set of emotion words was compiled from existing Korean and English emotion lexicons and studies. Second, bilingual experts created many-to-many translational mappings between Korean and English words using dictionary resources, allowing for multiple equivalents and gaps. Third, native speaker annotators evaluated each word against multiple criteria: acceptability (whether the word emotions), semantic experiencer (whether the emotion is experienced or caused), exclusion (whether it denotes a non-emotion), and polysemy (number of distinct meanings). For Korean, 819 adjectives/verbs were annotated; for English, 924. Inter-annotator agreement was measured on a subset (~10% of items) using Cohen’s κ.

## Key Results
- EVOKE contains 1,427 Korean and 1,399 English emotion words, with 96%+ having at least one translational equivalent and an average of ~1.6 mappings per word.
- Annotation coverage focused on adjectives/verbs: 819 Korean and 924 English items.
- Inter-annotator agreement is substantial for Korean (mean κ=0.74) and moderate for English (mean κ=0.60); agreement is highest for the exclusion criterion and lowest for causality judgments (caused vs. causing states).
- The dataset reveals language-specific emotion terms (e.g., Korean “답답하다”) and documents polysemous and metaphorical extensions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The multi-criteria annotation scheme captures multiple dimensions of emotion word semantics (acceptability, experiencer, exclusion, polysemy), enabling a theory-agnostic documentation of lexical properties. Bilingual expert mappings using dictionaries ensure that translational equivalents are identified comprehensively, including many-to-many relations and lexical gaps. Native speaker annotation grounds judgments in intuitive language use, while measuring inter-annotator agreement provides a reliability check. This systematic approach balances breadth (large word lists) with depth (nuanced semantic annotation), creating a resource that reveals both common structures and language-specific patterns.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Word Compilation → Bilingual Expert Mapping → Native Speaker Annotation (multi-criteria) → Inter-Annotator Agreement Analysis → Dataset Release

**Critical Path**:  
1. Gather base word lists from existing literature.  
2. Bilingual experts generate translational mappings using dictionaries.  
3. Native annotators apply criteria to each word.  
4. Compute IAA on a held-out subset.  
5. Integrate annotations into final lexicon.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *Breadth vs. Depth*: Including many words from diverse sources improves coverage but may limit detailed sense inventories.  
- *Single vs. Multiple Annotators*: Using one annotator per language maximizes coverage but reduces reliability checks; IAA measured on only ~10% of items.  
- *Dictionary-based vs. Corpus-based Mapping*: Dictionaries provide systematic coverage but may miss contextual equivalences; corpus evidence was not used.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Low IAA on causality (κ~0.45) indicates criterion is too subjective or underspecified.  
- Opaque bilingual expert selection risks introducing systematic bias in mappings.  
- Restricting annotation to adjectives/verbs ignores other parts-of-speech that convey emotion (e.g., nouns like “grief”).

**First Experiments**:  
1. Replicate annotation on a 10% subset with 3–5 new native annotators per language to measure consistency and refine guidelines.  
2. Conduct a behavioral study asking bilingual speakers to judge translational equivalence for “gap” words (e.g., Korean “답답하다”) to test whether they are truly lexical or conceptual gaps.  
3. Integrate EVOKE into a multilingual emotion classification model and compare performance against existing lexicons (e.g., NRC, EmoLex) to quantify extrinsic utility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question**: How do patterns of polysemy and metaphorical extension in emotion words distribute within a language and differ across typologically distinct languages?  
  *Basis*: [explicit] Gaps in existing work leave open questions about how emotion words develop multiple meanings and metaphorical extensions, how such patterns distribute within languages, and how they differ across languages.  
  *Why unresolved*: Systematic, large-scale documentation of polysemy and metaphor in emotion lexica is rare; prior work focuses on small, predefined sets of metaphorical mappings or general polysemy, not emotion specifically.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Cross-linguistic comparative analyses using datasets like EVOKE (or more) that annotate multiple senses and their metaphorical relationships across a wide range of emotion words in diverse languages.

- **Question**: What methodological improvements can increase the reliability of annotating abstract semantic properties like causality (caused vs. causing states) in emotion words?  
  *Basis*: [inferred] Annotation results show consistently low agreement for causality judgments (exp7-8), particularly exp8 (causing; κ=0.46 Korean, 0.45 English), and the authors caution these criteria "should be applied cautiously."  
  *Why unresolved*: The criteria require complex, subjective reasoning about semantic relations and may depend on annotators' individual experiences and theoretical leanings, leading to substantial disagreement.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Developing more concrete annotation guidelines with extensive exemplars, incorporating behavioral pilot studies to calibrate judgments, or exploring alternative operationalizations (e.g., using prompted sentence generation or corpus-based distributional evidence).

- **Question**: To what extent are the lexical gaps (words without translations) and annotation patterns observed in EVOKE specific to the Korean-English pair, versus generalizable to other language pairs?  
  *Basis*: [inferred] The dataset covers only two languages, and the limitations note "there are many other languages to investigate." Furthermore, most data was annotated by a single annotator per language, with agreement assessed on only ~10% of items.  
  *Why unresolved*: Cross-linguistic variation in emotion lexicon is expected, but the relative contributions of cultural-specificity versus methodological artifacts (e.g., single-annotator bias, specific theoretical criteria) are unclear.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Replicating the EVOKE methodology with multiple annotators per language across several additional, typologically and culturally diverse language pairs, comparing rates of lexical gaps, acceptability patterns, and polysemy annotation.

- **Question**: Do lexical gaps in emotion vocabulary (e.g., Korean "답답하다") reflect underlying conceptual gaps, or are they merely lexicalization gaps that can be expressed via multi-word paraphrases?  
  *Basis*: [inferred] The authors identify words without translational equivalents as potential "lexical gaps" that "could potentially indicate conceptual gaps," but also note some gaps have phrasal equivalents (e.g., English "moody" vs. Korean phrase).  
  *Why unresolved*: It is unclear whether the absence of a single-word equivalent prevents speakers from efficiently conceptualizing or communicating that emotional state, or if the concept is simply lexicalized differently.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Behavioral experiments testing recognition, production, or conceptual priming for these "gap" emotions in speakers of the source and target languages, combined with analysis of paraphrasing frequency and naturalness in large corpora.

## Limitations
- Bilingual expert selection and dictionary sources for translational mappings lack transparency, risking selection bias.
- Annotation procedures are underspecified (number and profile of native annotators, training details), and moderate English IAA (κ=0.60) with low causality agreement undermines reliability for subtle semantic properties.
- Annotation restricted to adjectives/verbs excludes other emotion-bearing categories (e.g., nouns, interjections), limiting comprehensiveness.
- No extrinsic validation demonstrates practical utility in downstream NLP tasks.

## Confidence
- **Translational coverage statistics (96%+ equivalence, ~1.6 mappings)**: *Medium* – raw counts are high-confidence, but the 96%+ figure depends on opaque mapping criteria.
- **Annotation reliability (overall κ: Korean 0.74, English 0.60)**: *High* for reported κ values, but *Low* for consistency on polysemy/metaphor given moderate English agreement.
- **Linguistic insights (language-specific words, polysemy/metaphor relations)**: *Low* without external validation or broader cross-linguistic replication.
- **Dataset completeness (1,427 Korean, 1,399 English words)**: *High* – based on transparent compilation from prior sources.

## Next Checks

2. Full inter-annotator agreement breakdown by criterion, annotator demographics, and item difficulty to diagnose low causality agreement.
