# Structured Prompting for Arabic Essay Proficiency: A Trait-Centric Evaluation Approach
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19668
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19668
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** A three-tier prompt framework applied to LLMs achieves trait-level scoring agreement (QWK=0.28, CI upper=0.41) for Arabic essays without fine-tuning, with rubric-guided prompting showing consistent gains.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the scarcity of scalable, trait-specific Automated Essay Scoring (AES) tools for Arabic by evaluating a structured prompt engineering framework across eight Large Language Models (LLMs). Using the QAES dataset of 195 Arabic essays annotated for seven linguistic traits, the authors test zero-shot and few-shot prompting strategies. The core finding is that detailed, rubric-aligned prompts—particularly those including trait-specific definitions and scored exemplars—systematically improve agreement between LLM predictions and human scores, with discourse-level traits showing the largest gains. The model Fanar-1-9B-Instruct achieved the best performance, demonstrating that careful prompt design, not just model scale, can enable effective Arabic AES in a zero-resource setting.

## Method Summary
The methodology involves three incremental prompt levels applied in zero- and few-shot configurations. Level 1 (Standard) gives a single instruction to score all traits. Level 2 (Hybrid) simulates five specialist raters, each scoring a subset of traits, with final scores aggregated via a weighted average. Level 3 (Rubric-Guided) provides a detailed Arabic rubric per trait and three scored exemplars (low, mid, high) within the prompt. Trait scores (1–5 scale, except Relevance 0–2) are extracted from model outputs and compared to human annotations using Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals. Evaluation runs on the QAES test set across eight multilingual and Arabic-focused LLMs.

## Key Results
- Rubric-guided prompting (Level 3) consistently outperformed simpler prompts across all models and traits, with the most significant improvements for Development and Style.
- Fanar-1-9B-Instruct achieved the highest overall trait-level agreement (QWK = 0.28, 95% CI upper bound = 0.41).
- Smaller models often exhibited parsing failures or null outputs under the complex Level 3 prompt, while larger Arabic-optimized models maintained better structural compliance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Rubric-Aligned Prompting
Structured prompts with explicit, trait-specific evaluation criteria and scored exemplars constrain the LLM's output space to align with human scoring logic, reducing ambiguity. The rubric serves as a decision-making scaffold that activates the model's latent knowledge of linguistic proficiency norms for Arabic.
- **Break condition:** If rubrics are culturally/linguistically misaligned with Arabic proficiency norms or the model's Arabic instruction-following is insufficient, gains fail to materialize.

### Mechanism 2: Hybrid Multi-Agent Decomposition
Simulating specialist raters decomposes holistic scoring into five focused sub-tasks, reducing cognitive load and trait interference. Each simulated rater handles a specific subset of traits, mimicking human expert assessment where different skills are evaluated in sequence.
- **Break condition:** Invalid or overlapping trait-to-rater mappings (e.g., Development mapped to multiple raters) can introduce aggregation inconsistency.

### Mechanism 3: Few-Shot Calibration
Providing low/mid/high exemplars creates implicit anchor points, enabling in-context learning where the model analogically generalizes scoring standards from examples to new essays. This calibrates the model's internal scale to match human distributions, especially for abstract discourse traits.
- **Break condition:** Unrepresentative or biased exemplars lead to poor calibration and skewed predictions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK)** – Weighted agreement metric for ordinal scales (1–5). Penalizes larger disagreements more heavily, making it suitable for rubric-based scoring where a 1-point difference is less severe than a 4-point difference.
  - *Quick check:* Why is QWK preferred over accuracy for AES, and how does its weighting formula reflect ordinal score distances?
- **Trait-Based vs. Holistic AES** – Trait-based scoring evaluates distinct linguistic skills (e.g., Vocabulary, Organization) separately, providing diagnostic feedback. Holistic scoring produces a single overall mark without granular insight.
  - *Quick check:* What pedagogical advantages does trait-based scoring offer for language learners and instructors?
- **Prompt Engineering for LLMs** – Crafting instructions, examples, and output specifications to elicit desired structured responses without model fine-tuning. Few-shot prompts include exemplars; zero-shot do not.
  - *Quick check:* How does the structure of a few-shot prompt differ from zero-shot, and why might few-shot yield better calibration for nuanced tasks like trait scoring?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Load QAES Essay → Select Prompt Level & Model → Construct Prompt (inject rubrics/exemplars) → Query LLM → Parse Output to Scores → Aggregate (if Hybrid) → Compute QWK vs Human

- **Critical path:** Input essay and prompt configuration determine the constructed prompt; model inference produces raw text; parser extracts numeric scores; aggregator combines specialist scores (Level 2); evaluator calculates QWK against human annotations.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Prompt complexity vs. model capability: Complex L3 prompts require strong Arabic instruction-following; small models may fail.
  - Trait granularity vs. interdependence: Decomposition reduces focus but forces arbitrary mappings that ignore trait interactions.
  - Rubric detail vs. conciseness: Detailed rubrics improve alignment but increase prompt length, risking context limit overflow.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Parsing failure: Model outputs malformed JSON or refuses scoring (yields QWK=0).
  - Degraded performance with complexity: QWK drops from L1 to L2/L3, indicating insufficient model capability.
  - High CI width: Bootstrapped confidence intervals >0.3 reflect dataset size or unstable predictions.
  - Trait-specific collapse: Persistently low QWK for Style/Structure suggests rubric or model deficiency.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline Replication: Run all 8 models with Level 1 (Standard) prompt; verify Fanar/Qwen3-VL lead and overall low scores (<0.2).
  2. Hybrid Decomposition Test: For Fanar (high) and Qwen1.5 (low), compare per-trait QWK between L1 and L2; confirm L2 helps weaker model on some traits.
  3. Few-Shot Calibration Test: Implement L3 for Fanar on Development trait; validate JSON output and QWK improvement. Then corrupt one exemplar's label to test model sensitivity.

## Limitations
- Exact Arabic rubrics and few-shot exemplars are not provided, blocking precise replication of Level 3.
- QAES dataset is small (n=195), limiting statistical power and generalizability.
- Peak QWK of 0.28 indicates only modest agreement, questioning practical effectiveness.

## Confidence
- Rubric-guided prompting improves agreement: **Medium**
- Hybrid multi-agent simulation helps: **Low**
- Few-shot exemplars calibrate scaling: **Medium**
- Fanar-1-9B-Instruct superiority: **Medium**
- Structured prompting enables effective Arabic AES: **Low**
