# AceGRPO: Adaptive Curriculum Enhanced Group Relative Policy Optimization for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07906
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07906
- **Reference count:** 17
- **Primary result:** Ace-30B achieves 100% valid submission rate on MLE-Bench-Lite, outperforms larger open-source baselines like DeepSeek-V3.2 by 12.13% in medal rate, and approaches proprietary frontier models while demonstrating sustained self-evolution.

## Executive Summary
Autonomous machine learning engineering (MLE) demands sustained iterative optimization, but existing LLM agents often stagnate due to frozen parameters. Reinforcement learning offers a path to continuous improvement but faces challenges of high execution latency and inefficient data selection. AceGRPO introduces two key innovations: an Evolving Data Buffer that converts every execution trace into a step-wise training task, and an Adaptive Sampler guided by a Learnability Potential function that prioritizes tasks at the agent's learning frontier. This combination enables efficient, continuous policy updates without requiring full trajectory reruns. Trained from Qwen3-30B, the resulting Ace-30B model achieves perfect validity on MLE-Bench-Lite, surpasses larger open-source baselines, and nears the performance of proprietary models, demonstrating robust self-evolution over time.

## Method Summary
AceGRPO reformulates autonomous MLE as a step-wise reinforcement learning problem. The agent interacts with the MLE-Dojo environment: at each step, it receives a state (task description, current code, history) and proposes a code update. The environment executes the code, computes a performance score (HumanRank), and returns a reward that combines absolute score and relative improvement over a baseline. The training loop employs Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a group size of 8, using group-relative advantages to reduce variance.

The system's core is an Evolving Data Buffer that stores states along with their task type (DRAFT, DEBUG, IMPROVE) and a Learnability Potential. After each rollout, new derivative states are appended to the buffer, continuously expanding the training distribution. An Adaptive Sampler selects which state to process next based on a rank-based curriculum that emphasizes frontier states with high potential, as measured by within-group reward dispersion and improvement headroom. The sampler also incorporates a cooling mechanism to avoid over-sampling. Rollout workers and learners operate asynchronously, maximizing GPU utilization while the buffer grows organically from the agent's own experience.

## Key Results
- Ace-30B achieves 100% valid submission rate on MLE-Bench-Lite.
- It outperforms larger open-source baselines (e.g., DeepSeek-V3.2) by 12.13% in medal rate.
- Performance approaches that of proprietary frontier models and demonstrates robust sustained self-evolution over training.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The Evolving Data Buffer transforms costly long-horizon executions into a scalable, step-wise training distribution that fuels continuous policy improvement.
- **Mechanism:** Each execution `(x, y)` deterministically creates a new derivative state `x' = Φ(x, y, f)`, which is added to the buffer `B_t`. This turns every intermediate state—whether from a failed debug or a suboptimal improvement—into a reusable training task. The buffer thus grows organically from the agent's own experience, providing on-policy data without requiring full trajectory reruns.
- **Core assumption:** Intermediate states are valid and valuable starting points for single-step RL updates, and their diversity preserves exploration while avoiding redundant full-trajectory rollouts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "Evolving Data Buffer that continuously repurposes execution traces into reusable step-wise training tasks."
  - [section 4.1]: "By expanding B_t on-the-fly with the agent’s own outputs, AceGRPO ensures that the training distribution continuously shifts towards the agent’s evolving frontier."
  - [corpus]: Neighbors focus on GRPO variants or curriculum for other domains; direct evidence for this *specific* buffer design is primarily from this paper.
- **Break condition:** If execution latency remains prohibitive even for single steps, or if the buffer accumulates大量low-quality states that do not generalize, the mechanism fails to provide efficient learning signals.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Adaptive Sampling guided by Learnability Potential directs the limited execution budget toward states with high expected gradient magnitude, preventing wasted computation on mastered or intractable tasks.
- **Mechanism:** The potential `P(x)` combines within-group reward *variance* (indicating non-deterministic outcomes at the frontier) and *improvement headroom* (penalizing states near max/min rewards). States are sampled via a rank-based curriculum `w_base(x,t)` with a cooling factor `C(x,t)` to balance focus and diversity. This explicitly constructs an adaptive curriculum over the evolving buffer.
- **Core assumption:** Within-group reward dispersion in GRPO (`σ(r_x)`) is a proxy for the magnitude of the policy gradient update; prioritizing high-dispersion states maximizes learning per unit of execution cost.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "Adaptive Sampling guided by a Learnability Potential function... dynamically prioritizes tasks at the agent's learning frontier to maximize learning efficiency."
  - [section 4.2]: Defines `P(x)` as `clip(σ(r_x),0,δ_max) + λ·clip(1-µ(r_x),0,1)` and explains its role in mitigating "vanishing within-group reward dispersion."
  - [section 3.3]: Argues that uniform sampling leads to states where `r_i - µ(x) → 0`, making `∇θL_GRPO ≈ 0`.
- **Break condition:** If the potential function miscalibrates (e.g., `λ` mis-set) or the buffer's state distribution drifts such that high-potential states become unreachable, the sampler may starve necessary exploratory tasks or overfit to narrow sub-regions.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A shaped reward that mixes absolute performance and relative improvement provides stage-consistent incentives, enabling the agent to first achieve validity and then pursue incremental gains.
- **Mechanism:** `R(x,y) = (1-α)*s + α*max(0, (s - s_p)/(1 - s_p + ε))`. For `DRAFT` (`s_p=0`), reward is purely absolute. For `DEBUG/IMPROVE`, reward includes a normalized improvement bonus over the previous baseline `s_p`, scaled by `α`.
- **Core assumption:** Separating the incentive for first-achieving validity from subsequent optimization matches the natural curriculum of MLE, where early failure is costly and later gains become harder.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1]: "This shaping strategy creates a stage-consistent incentive: early training focuses on achieving valid solutions... later stages are explicitly driven to exploit the diminishing improvement headroom."
  - [section 4.1]: The reward is defined explicitly in Equation 6.
- **Break condition:** If `α` is too high early on, the agent may ignore achieving any valid submission; if too low later, it may stagnate on suboptimal plateaus. The normalization denominator must remain stable.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**
  - **Why needed here:** AceGRPO is built on GRPO; understanding how advantages are computed as `(r_i - µ)/σ` within a group is essential to see why variance collapse is fatal and how `P(x)` targets it.
  - **Quick check question:** Why does GRPO use group-relative advantages instead of a learned value baseline, and what happens when `σ(x) ≈ 0`?

- **Concept: Step-wise vs. Trajectory-based RL**
  - **Why needed here:** AceGRPO explicitly reformulates long-horizon MLE as step-wise optimization to avoid prohibitive latency. This is a fundamental design shift from standard RL.
  - **Quick check question:** What are the trade-offs of optimizing immediate reward `R(x,y)` versus full-trajectory return in a domain with minutes-per-step execution?

- **Concept: Adaptive Curriculum Learning**
  - **Why needed here:** The Adaptive Sampler is a form of automatic curriculum generation over the buffer. It requires understanding how difficulty or learning value can be inferred from online statistics.
  - **Quick check question:** How does `P(x)` differ from simple "replay priority" in prioritized experience replay, and why is the rank-based schedule with cooling necessary?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  1.  **Evolving Data Buffer (`B_t`)**: Shared memory/data structure storing `(x, κ, P)` tuples. Append-only, with `P` updated on sampling.
  2.  **Adaptive Sampler (`Q_t`)**: Computes sampling probabilities `Qt(x) ∝ w_base(x,t) * C(x,t)` from buffer metadata. Implements stage logic based on `|B_t|`.
  3.  **Rollout Workers**: Asynchronously sample `x ~ Q_t`, query policy `π_θ` for `G` responses, execute `y_i`, compute `r_i` and `f_i`, spawn `x'_i`, update `P(x)` from group stats, and push `x'_i` to buffer.
  4.  **Learner Actors**: Consume batches of `(x, {y_i, r_i})` from a queue, compute GRPO loss `L_GRPO`, perform gradient update `θ ← θ + η∇`, and broadcast new `π_θ` to workers.
  5.  **Task Executor**: Sandboxed environment (MLE-Dojo) that runs generated code, catches errors, and computes metric `s` and reward `R`.

- **Critical path:**
  `Sample x from Q_t` → `Generate {y_i} from π_θ` → `Execute y_i (high-latency)` → `Compute r_i` → `Update P(x)` → `Spawn x'_i into B_t` → `Asynchronously push (x, {y_i,r_i}) to learner queue` → `Learner updates π_θ`. The buffer expansion and sampler must operate without blocking the learner; the learner must operate without waiting for all rollouts to finish.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Buffer size vs. stale data:** Large buffers risk including many low-potential states, wasting sampler overhead; small buffers limit diversity. The stage-based `ρ(t)` and top-percentile clipping attempt to manage this.
  - **Potential update frequency:** `P(x)` is updated only when `x` is sampled. This is cheap but means `P` can be stale if the policy shifts dramatically. A tradeoff between metadata freshness and update overhead.
  - **Asynchrony vs. consistency:** Decoupling rollouts and learning maximizes GPU utilization but introduces staleness: the policy `π_θ` used for rollouts may be older than the one used for the latest gradient step. The paper does not quantify this staleness bound.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Vanishing gradient:** Monitor average `σ(r_x)` of sampled batches. A sustained drop toward zero indicates the sampler is not finding learning-zone states.
  - **Buffer pollution:** Growth of `|B_t|` without corresponding improvement in `Any Medal` rate suggests the buffer is filling with irreparable states (e.g., always-failing debugging contexts) that cannot be learned from.
  - **Task-type imbalance:** If `κ(x)=DRAFT` states dominate the buffer or sampler, the agent may fail to develop robust debugging/improvement skills. Track the proportion of each `κ` in `Q_t` samples.
  - **Revisit starvation:** The cooling mechanism `C(x,t)` might prevent necessary re-sampling of high-potential states if `γ` or `∆_hard` are misconfigured, leading to plateaus.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Buffer Growth & Composition Analysis:** Track `|B_t|` over training steps and the fraction of `κ ∈ {DRAFT, DEBUG, IMPROVE}`. Confirm streaming expansion occurs and that `IMPROVE` states increase as the agent gains capability.
  2.  **Potential Calibration Ablation:** Run an ablation with `P(x)` replaced by uniform sampling, random sampling, or a naive "recently visited" priority. Measure `Any Medal` rate and training stability (advantage variance over time).
  3.  **Stage Transition Sensitivity:** Vary the buffer-size thresholds for `ρ(t)` stages and the `Top percentile` parameter. Evaluate the impact on convergence speed and final performance to validate the chosen schedule.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Does AceGRPO generalize to other long-horizon agentic tasks beyond machine learning engineering?
- **Basis in paper:** (inferred) The paper evaluates Ace-30B solely on MLE-Bench-Lite (Section 5) and does not test on domains such as software engineering or scientific discovery.
- **Why unresolved:** The adaptive curriculum and Learnability Potential may be tailored to MLE-specific reward structures; transferability remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Run AceGRPO on established benchmarks from other fields (e.g., SWE-bench, SciBench) and compare against baselines to measure cross-domain effectiveness.

### Could alternative Learnability Potential functions further improve sample efficiency?
- **Basis in paper:** (inferred) The potential combines standard deviation and mean reward with equal weights (α0=λ=0.5) and fixed clipping (Eq. 7), but no justification or alternatives are explored.
- **Why unresolved:** The current design is a heuristic; different weightings, non-linear transformations, or learned predictors might yield higher gradient signals.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct an ablation varying α0, λ, clipping thresholds, or replace the hand-crafted potential with a meta-learned model; assess training speed and final performance.

### Are the manually scheduled parameters of the rank-based curriculum (focusing coefficient ρ(t), stage thresholds) optimal across task distributions?
- **Basis in paper:** (inferred) Table 3 shows three stages with fixed ρ values and buffer-size boundaries; these were chosen empirically without sensitivity analysis.
- **Why unresolved:** The schedule may be brittle to different dataset sizes or difficulty mixes; an adaptive schedule could reduce tuning effort and improve robustness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform systematic experiments perturbing ρ(t) schedules and stage thresholds; measure variance in performance. Alternatively, design a dynamic adaptation rule based on real-time buffer statistics.

### Does the strict step-wise decoupling sacrifice important long-horizon dependencies, and would incorporating multi-step returns yield better final performance?
- **Basis in paper:** (explicit) The authors state that full-trajectory RL is intractable and therefore adopt step-wise optimization (Section 3.3). They do not compare with any trajectory-based method.
- **Why unresolved:** Step-wise updates ignore credit assignment over more than one step; the agent may fail to learn strategies requiring sequential coordination.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a variant that uses n-step returns (e.g., 3-step) or a hybrid approach that occasionally performs short trajectory rollouts; compare convergence speed and final scores against the strictly step-wise baseline.

## Limitations

- **U1:** MLE-Dojo execution API details (sandboxing, timeout, error parsing, score extraction) are underspecified; replication depends on unreleased infrastructure.
- **U2:** Reward shaping parameters (α=0.3) and HumanRank normalization (N) are stated but lack sensitivity analysis; performance may be fragile to these choices.
- **U3:** The "sustained self-evolution" claim lacks ablations showing causal necessity of the Evolving Data Buffer vs. a static replay buffer.
- Evaluation is limited to MLE-Bench-Lite (22 tasks) with no statistical tests or multiple runs reported, limiting reliability.

## Confidence

- **100% valid submission rate and 12.13% medal rate improvement:** Medium (results on a narrow benchmark without statistical tests or multiple runs).
- **Core mechanisms (Evolving Data Buffer, Adaptive Sampling):** Medium (theoretical justification is sound but direct evidence is primarily from self-experiments; no independent validation or broader task generalization).
- **Approaching proprietary frontier models:** Low (comparisons are to older open-source models; no head-to-head with current SOTA like Claude 3.7 or GPT-4.5).

## Next Checks

1. **Buffer Ablation & Analysis:** Train with a random-sampling buffer (no learnability potential) and a static buffer (no new state expansion). Compare final medal rates and buffer composition (κ-type distribution) over 400 steps.
2. **Potential Function Stress Test:** Systematically vary λ in P(x) and monitor per-stage advantage variance σ(r_x). Validate the claim that high-P states yield larger gradient magnitudes by correlating P(x) with measured ||∇L_GRPO|| on sampled batches.
3. **Cross-Benchmark Generalization:** Evaluate Ace-30B on full MLE-Bench (90 tasks) and an out-of-distribution code repair suite (e.g., SWE-bench). A robust curriculum should transfer; failure indicates overfitting to the training task distribution.
