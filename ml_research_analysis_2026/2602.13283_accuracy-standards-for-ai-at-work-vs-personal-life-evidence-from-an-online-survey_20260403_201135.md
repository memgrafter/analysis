# Accuracy Standards for AI at Work vs. Personal Life: Evidence from an Online Survey

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13283  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13283  
- **Reference count:** 7  
- **Primary result:** 24.1% rate AI accuracy as "Extremely important" at work vs. 8.8% for personal life (p<0.001).

## Executive Summary
This study examines how users' tolerance for AI inaccuracy differs between professional and personal contexts using an online survey (N=300, paired analytic sample N=170). Respondents rated the importance of AI accuracy on a 1–5 scale for both domains. The core finding is a substantial and statistically significant gap: a far greater share require "Extremely important" accuracy at work than in personal life. Conversely, reported disruption from AI unavailability is higher in personal routines than at work. Stricter work accuracy standards correlate with heavy professional app use and reliance patterns, though explanatory power is moderate. The work provides evidence that context—not just the technology—shapes user expectations.

## Method Summary
A within-subjects online survey measured perceived importance of AI accuracy on two 5-point Likert items (work vs. personal life), along with usage frequency, reliance, and impact variables. The primary analytic sample consists of 170 participants with complete paired responses. Paired statistical tests—including McNemar’s test, sign test, Wilcoxon signed-rank, and paired t-test—compared distributions across the two contexts. Logistic regressions explored determinants of (a) imposing stricter accuracy standards at work versus personal life and (b) reporting high disruption from unavailability in each domain. No machine learning training was involved; analysis relied on classical statistical inference and summary statistics.

## Key Results
- **Work accuracy demands are substantially higher:** 24.1% rate accuracy as "Extremely important" for work vs. 8.8% for personal life (p<0.001).
- **Paired mean difference of 0.79:** Work mean = 3.86, personal mean = 3.08 on 1–5 scale (p<0.001).
- **Disruption from unavailability is higher in personal routines:** 34.1% report significant impact vs. 15.3% for work (p<0.01).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Stricter work standards driven by stakes and accountability**  
Individuals demand higher accuracy from AI tools in professional contexts than in personal life. The mechanism posits that higher perceived stakes, formal accountability structures, and potential for cascading professional consequences (e.g., reputational damage, financial loss) tighten tolerance thresholds. In contrast, personal errors are perceived as more reversible and convenience-weighted. The core assumption is that respondents accurately introspect their *ex-ante* tolerance and that "accuracy" aligns with the study's operationalization. Evidence includes the robust gap across multiple metrics (top-box 24.1% vs. 8.8%, mean 3.86 vs. 3.08, p<0.001) and theoretical grounding in trust calibration literature. A break condition would be if the gap is primarily an artifact of social desirability bias or if "work" and "personal" portfolios do not meaningfully differ in stakes for the sample.

**Mechanism 2: Determinants of the gap show mixed, moderate associations**  
Stricter work accuracy standards are associated with certain usage patterns but explanatory power is moderate. Heavy professional AI use may correlate with stricter standards via two opposing paths: (a) exposure to high-stakes tasks reinforces precision needs, or (b) heavy reliance creates comfort with errors, lowering reported thresholds. The paper finds the latter for self-rated reliance (`rely_work` negative coefficient). The core assumption is that survey items capture meaningful integration depth and that the logistic model correctly specifies the relationship. Evidence: Model A shows `high_accurate_work` strongly predicts being stricter at work (+2.96, p<0.01), while `rely_work` predicts lower odds (-2.03, p<0.01); pseudo-R² ~0.29 indicates moderate power. Break condition: negative `rely_work` coefficient confounded by unobserved task type or poor reliance proxy.

**Mechanism 3: Higher personal disruption from unavailability despite lower accuracy demands**  
Users report greater disruption when AI/apps are unavailable in personal routines than at work. The mechanism suggests personal routines may rely on narrow, highly integrated apps (e.g., navigation, smart home) with fewer offline fallbacks or procedural substitutes. Professional work often retains human oversight, alternative workflows, or organizational buffers, reducing reported disruption. Core assumption: the binary "significantly impacted" item validly captures perceived dependency, and respondents can isolate the AI component. Evidence: 34.1% report high personal impact vs. 15.3% for work (p<0.01), with support from GPS/navigation dependency literature. Break condition: personal impact overstated due to salience of convenience losses, or professional impact underreported due to normalization of tool failure.

## Foundational Learning
- **Paired Ordinal Data Analysis**: For within-subjects Likert comparisons, use Wilcoxon signed-rank or sign test for the full 1–5 scale; McNemar’s test is only appropriate after dichotomization. Unpaired tests inflate Type I error by ignoring pairing.
- **Self-Report Bias in Technology Use**: Self-reported frequency and reliance may diverge from actual logs due to social desirability and recall limits. Expect overstatement of work seriousness and understatement of personal dependence.
- **Context-Specific Risk Calibration**: Accuracy tolerance is framed by stakes and reversibility. A 5% error in personal travel recommendations may be tolerable due to low stakes and easy correction, but not in a financial summary where losses are high and corrections costly.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Survey Instrument Module -> Paneling & Quota Control Module -> Data Pipeline -> Analysis Engine -> Visualization & Reporting
- **Critical path**: (1) Hypothesis & threshold definition; (2) Survey deployment & QA; (3) Data cleaning & sample construction; (4) Primary analysis execution; (5) Robustness & diagnostics.
- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - Paired N=170 yields high power for large effects but limits detection of subtle interactions in H2.
  - Full 1–5 ordinal scale retains ~1.5x power over top-box but is less interpretable for policy; self-report is cheap and general but noisy vs. behavioral logs.
- **Failure signatures**: 
  - High missingness on key paired items (>15%) reduces effective N and risks bias if not MCAR.
  - Inconsistent paired responses (e.g., both rated "Extremely important") dilute H1 contrast.
  - Logistic regression separation due to sparse outcomes in H2/H3.
  - Low pseudo-R² (≤0.29) signals dominant unobserved confounders; overinterpretation risk.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Threshold Sensitivity Analysis:** Re-run H1 using all dichotomizations (e.g., 5 vs. 1–4; 4–5 vs. 1–3) to map effect size robustness.
  2. **Determinant Power Recalculation:** Using observed pseudo-R² and outcome prevalence, compute sample size needed to detect OR=1.5 for key covariates (80% power) to assess if H2’s null findings are power-limited.
  3. **Task-Level Vignette Pilot:** Conduct a follow-up where participants rate accuracy importance for concrete, matched tasks (e.g., "client email" vs. "family vacation") in work/personal contexts to test gap persistence and unpack mechanisms.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Residual variance in the gap:** What factors explain remaining variation after accounting for usage and reliance? (H2 regressions yield pseudo-R² ≤ 0.29.)
2. **Personal disruption paradox:** Why is disruption from unavailability higher in personal routines despite lower personal accuracy demands? (H3 predictors have pseudo-R² ≈ 0.02.)
3. **Task-level heterogeneity:** How do accuracy standards vary across specific tasks/occupations, and does task-level risk moderate the work-personal gap? (Study aggregates tasks; future work needs task-level data.)
4. **Accuracy dimension specificity:** Which dimensions of "accuracy" (factual correctness, bias, relevance) drive context-specific tolerance? (Generic item likely interpreted diversely.)
5. **Causal mechanisms:** What mechanisms (accountability, loss aversion, norms) underpin stricter work standards? (Correlational design cannot isolate causes.)

## Limitations
- **Data accessibility:** Raw individual-level data unavailable, preventing exact replication and precise coefficient recovery.
- **Self-report validity:** All key measures are self-reported, raising concerns about social desirability, recall accuracy, and introspective limits; mental model of "accuracy" may not align with operationalization.
- **Causal ambiguity:** Cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; observed correlations (e.g., negative `rely_work`) may be confounded by unobserved task criticality.
- **Sample generalizability:** Online panel (Prolific) may not represent broader worker populations, especially in high-regulation or safety-critical industries.

## Confidence
- **H1 (Stricter Work Accuracy Standards):** High – large, statistically significant effect (p<0.001) consistent across multiple paired tests.
- **H2 (Determinants of the Gap):** Medium – moderate explanatory power (pseudo-R² ~0.29) and interpretable but counterintuitive coefficients; vulnerable to omitted variable bias.
- **H3 (Higher Personal Disruption):** Medium – significant reversal with plausible mechanism, but relies on a subjective "significantly impacted" binary item.

## Next Checks
