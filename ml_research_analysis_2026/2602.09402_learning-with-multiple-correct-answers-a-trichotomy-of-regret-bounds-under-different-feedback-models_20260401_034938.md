# Learning with Multiple Correct Answers -- A Trichotomy of Regret Bounds under Different Feedback Models
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.09402
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09402
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** The paper establishes a trichotomy in agnostic regret: linear for mistake-unknown, O(√(T log T)|Y|) for mistake-known, and constant for set-valued feedback (under broad conditions). Realizable mistake bounds are characterized by combinatorial dimensions LDunknown, LDknown, and LDset.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates online multi-label learning where each instance may have multiple valid labels, motivated by language generation. It considers three feedback models: mistake-unknown (only the true label is revealed when wrong), mistake-known (correctness indicator added), and set-valued (full correct label set revealed). The primary contribution is a characterization of optimal realizable mistake bounds via three combinatorial dimensions (LDunknown, LDknown, LDset) that generalize the Littlestone dimension. This yields a trichotomy in agnostic regret: the mistake-unknown model incurs linear regret in the horizon T, the mistake-known model achieves O(√(T log T)|Y|) regret, and the set-valued model attains constant regret for a broad class of hypothesis sets. These results are derived using Standard Optimal Algorithms, reductions to bandit expert advice (EXP4), and online-to-batch conversions. The findings highlight the dramatic impact of feedback richness on learning complexity in multi-label settings.

## Method Summary
The study employs Standard Optimal Algorithms (SOA) in the realizable setting, leveraging combinatorial dimensions (LDunknown, LDknown, LDset) to achieve optimal mistake bounds. For the agnostic case, the mistake-known feedback is handled via reduction to bandit expert advice using EXP4, while the set-valued case uses a Weighted Majority algorithm over SOAs trained on label subsets. The mistake-unknown agnostic setting is shown to have a linear lower bound. Finally, online-to-batch conversion translates these online guarantees into sample complexity bounds that scale with the respective dimensions.

## Key Results
- Realizable mistake bounds for each feedback model are exactly characterized by the corresponding combinatorial dimension: LDunknown, LDknown, or LDset.
- Agnostic regret exhibits a trichotomy: linear in T for mistake-unknown; O(√(T log T)|Y|) for mistake-known; constant for set-valued under broad hypothesis classes.
- Batch learning sample complexity inherits the dependence on these dimensions via standard online-to-batch conversions.

## Why This Works
The mechanisms hinge on problem-specific combinatorial dimensions that capture the online learnability under each feedback model. The Standard Optimal Algorithm (SOA) achieves the optimal mistake bound by always predicting the label that minimizes the worst-case Littlestone dimension of the remaining version space. For mistake-unknown feedback, an offset function is incorporated to account for the limited information when a mistake occurs, leading to the LDunknown dimension. For mistake-known feedback, the extra correctness signal allows a reduction to bandit expert advice (EXP4), which yields √T regret with an additional |Y| factor due to label-wise exploration. In the set-valued case, the full correct set provides sufficient information for a simple majority-like rule, and under conditions that ensure monotonicity of the hypothesis class (e.g., finite LDset), the agnostic regret remains constant regardless of T. The online-to-batch conversion then translates these online guarantees into sample complexity bounds that scale with the respective dimensions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Combinatorial dimensions (LDunknown, LDknown, LDset):** Generalizations of Littlestone dimension to multi-label feedback settings. Why needed: They precisely characterize the optimal mistake counts and regret bounds for each feedback model. Quick check: For a finite hypothesis class H, construct complete Y-ary trees and check shattering conditions as per Definitions 3.1-3.3.
- **Version space:** The set of hypotheses consistent with all observed feedback. Why needed: Maintaining version space allows the SOA to eliminate inconsistent hypotheses and base predictions on the remaining set. Quick check: After each round, filter H by keeping only h that agree with received feedback.
- **Offset function (for LDunknown):** A function off(x) that shifts label predictions to compensate for the asymmetry of mistake-unknown feedback. Why needed: Without offset, the naive halving would not be optimal; the offset reduces the effective tree depth. Quick check: Define off(x) as a mapping from X to subsets of Y and incorporate it when measuring shattering.
- **Bandit expert advice (EXP4):** An algorithm for the bandit setting where only partial feedback is observed. Why needed: The mistake-known model reduces to this setting, enabling √T regret. Quick check: Implement EXP4 with uniform exploration parameter γ and verify weight updates.
- **Monotonicity condition (Theorem 5.4):** A structural property of hypothesis classes (2|h'(x)\h(x)| ≤ |h(x)|) required for the set-valued Weighted Majority algorithm to work. Why needed: It ensures the expected potential does not increase, yielding constant regret. Quick check: For each h and possible predictions y, verify the inequality holds for all h' in H.
- **Online-to-batch conversion:** A technique to convert an online algorithm into a PAC learner by averaging its predictions. Why needed: Extends the online regret bounds to sample complexity results for batch learning. Quick check: Run the online algorithm for T rounds and output the majority vote of all predictors; bound excess risk by expected regret / T.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Hypothesis class H → Compute combinatorial dimension (LDunknown/LDknown/LDset) → Initialize version space or weights → (for each round: predict → receive feedback → update state) → Online mistake/regret accumulation.

**Critical path:** The essential steps are: (1) preprocess H to obtain the relevant dimension and possible offset; (2) initialize the online learner (version space for SOA, weight distribution for EXP4/Weighted Majority); (3) iterate over T rounds: produce prediction based on current state, observe feedback, update state accordingly; (4) for batch learning, apply online-to-batch conversion to the final predictor.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Exact dimension computation is combinatorial and may be infeasible for large H; approximations risk losing optimality.
- For mistake-known agnostic, EXP4's expert set size can be exponential in T/LDknown in the reduction used, imposing computational burdens; careful expert set design or sampling may be necessary.
- Set-valued Weighted Majority requires the monotonicity condition; violating it breaks the constant regret guarantee.
- The offset function for LDunknown introduces an additional search over functions, which may be intractable without additional assumptions.

**Failure signatures:**
- SOA mistakes exceed min(LD, T) on worst-case sequences → likely incorrect tree shattering or consistency checks.
- Expected potential (e.g., total weight) increases during Weighted Majority updates → violation of monotonicity condition 2|h'(x)\h(x)| ≤ |h(x)|.
- EXP4 regret grows faster than O(√(T log T)|Y|) → oversized expert set or misimplementation of bandit feedback (correctness indicator).

**First experiments:**
1. Validate SOA mistake bound: For a small finite H (e.g., threshold functions), compute its LD dimension, build a shattered tree, run SOA on the induced sequence, and confirm mistakes = min(LD, T).
2. Test EXP4 scaling: Implement EXP4 with expert set = H (or a suitable finite family), vary T and |Y|, and measure regret; verify the O(√(T log T)|Y|) dependence and that the logarithmic term is in T.
3. Check set-valued constant regret: Choose a class satisfying monotonicity (e.g., finite LDset), run Weighted Majority on adversarial sequences for increasing T, and confirm total mistakes remain bounded by a constant independent of T.

## Limitations
- The notion of "broad classes" for set-valued feedback is left informal; constant agnostic regret likely depends on strong structural conditions (e.g., finite LDset and monotonicity) that are not fully explicated.
- The |Y| factor in the mistake-known agnostic bound may be an artifact of the expert reduction and might be removable with a more refined analysis.
- For infinite hypothesis classes, the mistake-unknown lower bound construction is only sketched via combinatorial arguments; no explicit worst-case sequence generation is provided, limiting understanding of its scope.

## Confidence
- Realizable mistake bounds characterized by LD dimensions: **High**
- Mistake-unknown agnostic regret linear lower bound: **Medium**
- Mistake-known agnostic regret O(√(T log T)|Y|): **Medium**
- Set-valued constant agnostic regret for broad classes: **Low**
