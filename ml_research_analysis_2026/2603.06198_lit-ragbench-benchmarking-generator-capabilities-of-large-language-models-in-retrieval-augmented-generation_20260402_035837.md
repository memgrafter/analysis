# LIT-RAGBench: Benchmarking Generator Capabilities of Large Language Models in Retrieval-Augmented Generation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.06198
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06198
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: No model exceeds 90% overall accuracy; top performers are GPT-5 (0.872) and Qwen3-235B (0.859).

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces LIT-RAGBench, a new benchmark for evaluating the generator component of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems across five capability categories: Integration, Reasoning, Logic, Table, and Abstention. To ensure models rely solely on provided context, the dataset uses entirely fictional entities and scenarios, preventing reliance on parametric memory. The benchmark comprises 114 human-curated Japanese QA pairs (with English translations), each tagged with 1-2 distinct aspects, often combining multiple capabilities. Evaluation via LLM-as-a-Judge across major LLMs reveals that even the best models achieve below 90% overall accuracy, with specific weaknesses in table interpretation (especially merged cells) and multi-source integration. The analysis also highlights challenges in appropriate abstention, with some models over-abstaining significantly. LIT-RAGBench provides fine-grained diagnostics for RAG generator development and deployment.

## Method Summary
LIT-RAGBench constructs a dataset of 114 questions (originally in Japanese, machine-translated and curated into English) that require understanding of provided document chunks to answer. Each instance includes a question, a ground-truth answer, a set of positive documents (`C+`) containing evidence, and negative distractors (`C-`). The questions are tagged with aspects from five categories (Integration, Reasoning, Logic, Table, Abstention) and combine at most two non-overlapping categories to test multi-capability performance. To prevent parametric recall, all entities (company names, products, etc.) are fictional. During evaluation, the order of `C+` and `C-` is randomized to remove position bias and isolate the generator's evidence-processing ability. The generator model produces an answer given the concatenated context; an LLM judge (GPT-4.1) then judges semantic consistency between the generated answer and the reference, yielding binary labels. Accuracy is computed per category and overall, and an Over-Abstention Rate measures unnecessary refusals.

## Key Results
- Overall accuracy: no model exceeded 90%; GPT-5 achieved 0.872 and Qwen3-235B 0.859.
- Category analysis: Table (especially merged-cell interpretation) and Integration were the most error-prone categories.
- Abstention: Claude-Sonnet-4 exhibited the highest over-abstention rate (0.259), indicating an overly conservative refusal behavior.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark's design rests on three interrelated mechanisms that ensure a rigorous, context‑grounded evaluation.

**Fictional Entity Grounding.** All QA pairs are built around entirely fictional entities (company names, product names, personal names, etc.) that do not exist in the model's pre‑training corpus. This forces the generator to rely exclusively on the supplied positive documents (`C+`) because no real‑world factual basis exists in its parameters. The assumption is that the model's internal knowledge contains no information about these novel synthetic entities. If the fictional entity generation inadvertently creates names or patterns that do appear in training data, this grounding fails and models could answer from memory.

**Systematic Aspect Combination.** Each question is tagged with a set of aspects Ψ(q) drawn from 1‑2 distinct evaluation categories (θ). This design forces the generator to apply multiple skills simultaneously (e.g., multi‑document integration *and* table interpretation), mirroring real‑world RAG tasks that require compound reasoning. By evaluating categories in isolation, one would miss failure modes that emerge under such compound conditions. The constraint of at most two categories per question keeps the dataset manageable but may miss higher‑order interactions involving three or more capabilities.

**Input Context Order Randomization.** For each instance, the concatenation order of the positive and negative chunks is shuffled before being fed to the generator. This removes confounding from retriever ranking and mitigates position bias. The generator must extract relevant information from `C+` wherever it appears in the context window, thereby testing order‑invariant evidence comprehension. However, if a model’s architecture (e.g., with strong positional encoding) fundamentally struggles with arbitrarily ordered context, this mechanism could conflate architectural limits with capability gaps.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Dataset Builder -> Context Formatter -> Generator Runner -> Judge Evaluator -> Metrics Aggregator

**Critical Path:**  
1. Specify evaluation pattern (aspects Ψ(q)) and create fictional scenario.  
2. Construct `C+` (evidence documents) and answer `a_i`, ensuring answer derivable only from `C+`.  
3. Build `C-` (distractors) that are related but non‑supportive.  
4. Verify quality: (1) `Ψ(q)` alignment, (2) derivability from `C+`, (3) no parametric knowledge, (4) factual consistency.  
5. For each model: generate `y_i` with randomized `C` order; evaluate with judge `J`.  
6. Aggregate scores by category and overall.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Sample size vs. curation quality:* The dataset has only 114 questions; intensive human verification ensures each item cleanly tests target aspects but limits statistical power.  
- *Fictional vs. real‑world validity:* Fictional entities guarantee context grounding but may reduce ecological validity compared to real enterprise data.  
- *Aspect co‑occurrence limit:* At most two categories per question keeps the dataset manageable but may not capture scenarios with three or more simultaneous capabilities.  
- *LLM‑as‑a‑Judge:* Evaluation scales well but inherits judge model biases; human validation was limited to dataset creation, not scoring.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- *Judge inconsistency:* Low inter‑annotator agreement between `J` and human judgments would invalidate metrics.  
- *Category ceiling/floor:* If all models score near 100% or 0% on a category, the aspect is too easy/hard or poorly defined.  
- *Over‑abstention correlation:* Models with high Over‑Abstention Rates may appear safer but are less useful; tradeoff between reliability and utility.  
- *Leakage detection:* If any model achieves perfect scores on fictional‑entity questions, inspect `C+` for accidental real‑world overlaps.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. *Sanity check:* Run GPT‑4.1 on 5 ΘMain questions (one per category) with `C` order randomized. Verify `J` scores match expected correctness based on human inspection of `a_i` and `C+`.  
2. *Aspect isolation:* Select a single‑aspect question (e.g., Table HTML) and test a strong model (GPT‑5) vs. a weak model (Llama‑3.1‑8B). Confirm performance gap aligns with expected capability.  
3. *Abstention probe:* For an Insufficient Evidence question (`C+` empty), verify all models output "I cannot answer" or similar. Check `J` correctly scores abstention as correct. Also test a Contradictory Evidence case to see if models detect conflict.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling with balanced aspects** – How can LIT‑RAGBench be expanded to a larger size with balanced aspect representation while maintaining human‑verified quality? The current 114‑instance dataset limits statistical robustness and fair cross‑aspect comparisons.  
2. **Improving multi‑source integration and table handling** – What architectural or training improvements can significantly reduce errors in merged‑cell table interpretation and multi‑source integration? These categories were the most error‑prone in the results.  
3. **Balancing abstention** – How can RAG generators balance appropriate abstention with answer generation to minimize both hallucinations and over‑abstention? Claude‑Sonnet‑4’s high over‑abstention rate (0.259) reveals a trade‑off between safety and usefulness.

## Limitations
- The dataset contains only 114 questions, limiting statistical power and the ability to generalize.  
- Evaluation relies on an LLM‑as‑a‑Judge (GPT‑4.1) without reported inter‑annotator agreement with human judgments, introducing potential bias.  
- Questions are restricted to at most two non‑overlapping capability categories, possibly missing failures involving three or more simultaneous skills.  
- Despite fictional entities, there is a risk of training data contamination if generated names inadvertently appear in model corpora.

## Confidence
- Fictional entity grounding (preventing parametric recall): Medium confidence  
- Two‑category combination design (capturing multi‑capability interactions): Medium confidence  
- Randomization of context order (isolating evidence processing): Medium confidence  
- LLM‑as‑a‑Judge reliability: Medium confidence  
- Overall findings (no model >90% accuracy, weaknesses in Table/Integration, abstention issues): Medium confidence  

## Next Checks
1. **Human evaluation subset** – Perform human annotation on a stratified sample to measure inter‑annotator agreement with the LLM judge and calibrate scores.  
2. **Leakage analysis** – Query target models without context for each fictional entity; any accurate responses indicate contamination and warrant item removal.  
3. **Higher‑order capability test** – Augment the benchmark with 50 questions combining three distinct categories to assess whether performance declines beyond additive effects of two‑category cases, testing for complex interactions.
