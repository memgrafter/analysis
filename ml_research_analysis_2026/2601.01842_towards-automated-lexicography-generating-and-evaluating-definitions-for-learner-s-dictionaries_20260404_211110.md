# Towards Automated Lexicography: Generating and Evaluating Definitions for Learner's Dictionaries

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2601.01842
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01842
*   **Reference Count:** 30
*   **Primary Result:** A validated LLM-as-a-judge evaluation framework and an iterative simplification method (IterSim) can generate Japanese learner's dictionary definitions that use a restricted 16k-word vocabulary (99.8% compliance) while maintaining definitional quality, with few-shot prompting achieving state-of-the-art scores.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the high cost of manually creating definitions for learner's dictionaries (LDDG), which must use a limited "defining vocabulary." It introduces two main contributions: (1) a multi-criteria LLM-as-a-judge evaluation framework (Prometheus-Eval) for truthfulness, coverage, sense specificity, and guideline compliance, which is validated against human annotators; and (2) IterSim, an iterative process that simplifies LLM-generated definitions by systematically replacing complex words. Experiments on a Japanese dataset (D3J) show that few-shot prompting with Claude Sonnet 4.5 produces high-quality definitions, and IterSim can make them almost entirely compliant with a 16k-word vocabulary (TUBE16K) without degrading quality.

## Method Summary
The proposed method is a two-stage pipeline. First, an LLM (e.g., Claude Sonnet 4.5) generates definition sets for given headwords/PoS using a detailed instruction prompt, optionally with 5 few-shot examples. Second, the IterSim algorithm iteratively simplifies each definition: it identifies words outside a defining vocabulary (TUBE16K), then prompts the LLM to paraphrase one complex word at a time while preserving meaning. Success is checked by re-evaluating the definition and ensuring the complex word count decreases. The overall quality and lexical simplicity of the final definitions are measured by a separate LLM evaluator (Prometheus-Eval) configured with specific rubrics and reference definitions.

## Key Results
*   The LLM-as-a-judge evaluation framework (using GPT-5.1) achieves a Kendall's τ of 0.631 with human annotators on overall quality, significantly outperforming text similarity metrics like BLEU (τ=0.174).
*   IterSim increases the TUBE16K ratio (definitions using only the 16k-word vocabulary) from ~90% to 99.8% without statistically significant degradation in the four quality criteria.
*   Few-shot prompting with Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieves state-of-the-art scores (e.g., 91.9% sense specificity) and surpasses human-written Wiktionary baseline definitions on three of the four criteria.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** An LLM-as-a-judge evaluation framework, guided by fine-grained rubrics, can reliably approximate human assessments of definition quality across multiple dimensions.
*   **Mechanism:** The framework ingests generated definitions, reference definitions, and human-authored rubrics. The rubrics operationalize abstract criteria (e.g., "coverage" becomes "proportion of reference senses covered"). The LLM generates verbal assessments per criterion and converts them into percentage scores, anchored to references and explicit guidelines.
*   **Core assumption:** Clear, example-backed rubrics can mitigate the subjectivity of sense division, enabling a consistent evaluation protocol an LLM can follow.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Reports Kendall's τ of 0.631 with GPT-5.1 (abstract, section 6). Validation on a 100-headword subset shows this exceeds BLEU and approaches inter-human agreement (τ=0.521). Low inter-human agreement on some criteria (e.g., truthfulness τ=0.370) is a noted limitation.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Iterative simplification (IterSim) can systematically remove complex vocabulary from LLM-generated definitions while preserving truthfulness, coverage, sense specificity, and guideline compliance.
*   **Mechanism:** Starting from an initial definition, the algorithm segments text, identifies words outside TUBE16K, and iteratively prompts the LLM to simplify one complex word. Each attempt is validated by checking if the revised definition still covers the original sense (via zero-shot re-evaluation) and reduces complex word count. A two-attempt retry with banned words handles failures.
*   **Core assumption:** An LLM can perform local, accurate paraphrasing to replace a target complex word with simpler synonyms without globally distorting meaning or violating other criteria.
*   **Evidence anchors:** IterSim increases TUBE16K ratio to 99.8% without degrading other criteria (abstract, section 8.2). The `SIMPLIFY()` subroutine uses zero-shot prompting with banned words (section 7.2).

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Few-shot prompting with high-quality demonstrations significantly improves an LLM's adherence to complex lexicographic guidelines and sense specificity compared to zero-shot prompting.
*   **Mechanism:** Providing 5 curated input-output pairs from a lexicographer-reviewed set (D3J demo set) within the instruction context allows the LLM to infer required definition structure, style, and granularity, exemplifying sentence types, vocabulary constraints, and sense separation.
*   **Core assumption:** A small set of expert-validated examples effectively communicates tacit lexicographic conventions better than textual instructions.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Few-shot Claude improves overall score (90.8 vs 87.2 zero-shot) and sense specificity (88.0% vs 73.4%) (section 8.1). The demo set (75 headwords) was reviewed by a professional lexicographer (section 5).

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation with Structured Rubrics
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper's validation and comparative analysis depend on the reliability of its automated evaluation. Understanding how rubrics translate subjective criteria into LLM-processible instructions is key to interpreting all results.
    *   **Quick check question:** How do the rubrics for "sense specificity" and "coverage" differently handle the arbitrariness of sense division between system output and reference?
*   **Concept:** Lexical Simplicity Constraints (TUBE16K/TUBE3K)
    *   **Why needed here:** The LDDG task's defining feature is the vocabulary constraint. All generation and evaluation outcomes are measured against these resource-based frequency lists.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why was TUBELEX (YouTube subtitles) chosen over BCCWJ (balanced corpus) to construct the defining vocabulary, and what does a 99.8% TUBE16K ratio practically mean for a learner?
*   **Concept:** Sense Specificity vs. False Polysemy
    *   **Why needed here:** This criterion directly targets a known failure mode in LLM-generated definitions. Its moderate inter-human agreement (τ=0.492) makes it a nuanced evaluation challenge.
    *   **Quick check question:** In the context of the guidelines, what distinguishes two definitions that represent "closely overlapping meanings" (mergable) from two that represent "distinct senses" (should remain separate)?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Input Processor (segments headword/PoS/reading) -> Definition Generator (LLM module: GPT-5.1/Claude/Qwen/Swallow, with/without few-shot) -> [Optional: IterSim Wrapper (FINDCOMPLEXWORDS -> SIMPLIFY loop)] -> Evaluator (Prometheus-Eval with GPT-5.1 judge) -> Metrics Aggregator (TUBE ratios, score averages).
*   **Critical path:** For a new headword: [Generator] -> [Optional IterSim loop] -> [Evaluator] -> [Store scores/vocab stats]. Validation: [Sample headwords] -> [Generate] -> [Human annotation] -> [Compute τ against LLM-judge].
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   *Evaluation cost vs. granularity:* Using a powerful proprietary LLM (GPT-5.1) as judge is expensive but yields human-correlated multi-criterion scores; cheaper metrics (BLEU) correlate poorly.
    *   *Simplicity vs. Coverage:* IterSim prioritizes lexical simplicity; the two-attempt retry risks over-simplifying if the LLM cannot find a suitable paraphrase, potentially lowering coverage/truthfulness.
    *   *Few-shot generalization:* Using 5 fixed examples improves quality but may not cover all PoS or sense patterns; random sampling introduces variance.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **Low guideline compliance score:** Indicates output violates sentence-type rules or `[語法]` marker usage. Check prompt clarity and example diversity.
    *   **High complex word ratio post-IterSim:** `SIMPLIFY` failing repeatedly for certain words; might require increasing `MAX_RETRIES` or improving the simplification prompt.
    *   **Disparity between sense specificity and coverage:** High coverage but low specificity suggests "false polysemy" (redundant senses); the evaluator's merging logic may be inconsistently applied by the judge LLM.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Reproduce evaluation validation:** On the 100-headword subset, run your own GPT-5.1 evaluation and compute Kendall's τ against the provided human scores (Table 7). Verify no calculation errors in score aggregation.
    2.  **IterSim robustness test:** Apply IterSim to zero-shot Claude outputs (Table 9). Does it still achieve ~99.8% TUBE16K without degrading scores? This isolates IterSim's effect from few-shot prompting.
    3.  **Cross-linguistic portability check:** Use the same rubric-and-judge framework on a non-Japanese DDG dataset (e.g., CODWOE). Does GPT-5.1 maintain reasonable τ against human scores when definitions are in English? This tests framework generalizability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How can the inter-annotator agreement for evaluating dictionary definitions be increased, given the low agreement (e.g., 0.370 on truthfulness) reported in the study?
*   **Basis in paper:** The Limitations section explicitly states that inter-human agreement was low and suggests recruiting additional expert annotators and preparing more specific instructions may improve reliability, but further validation is left for future work due to annotation resource constraints.
*   **Why unresolved:** Low agreement threatens the validity of the evaluation framework as a gold standard; without a reliable human baseline, assessing LLM-generated definitions becomes inconsistent and potentially misleading.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** A large-scale annotation study with diverse expert lexicographers, iterative refinement of evaluation guidelines based on pilot studies, and statistical analysis to identify and mitigate sources of disagreement.
*   **Open Question 2:** Why does the LLM-as-a-judge approach show particularly low agreement with humans on the truthfulness and sense specificity criteria (below 0.4 Kendall's τ), and how can prompt design or model selection be improved to address this?
*   **Basis in paper:** Section 6.3 reports that GPT-5.1's agreement with humans on truthfulness and sense specificity is below 0.4, while overall agreement is higher (0.631), leading the authors to caution that scores on these criteria should be interpreted carefully.
*   **Why unresolved:** This discrepancy suggests current LLMs may struggle with nuanced judgment of factual accuracy or distinguishing overlapping senses, which are core to dictionary quality; without improvement, the evaluation framework may not be trusted.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Error analysis comparing LLM and human judgments on a stratified sample, testing alternative evaluator LLMs or prompt templates that emphasize factual verification and sense differentiation, and correlating scores with external metrics like learner comprehension tests.
*   **Open Question 3:** To what extent do the proposed evaluation criteria and iterative simplification method generalize to languages other than Japanese, and what language-specific adaptations are required?
*   **Basis in paper:** The paper focuses solely on Japanese, using a Japanese-specific dataset (D3J) and defining vocabulary (TUBE16K), but claims contributions can be applied to any language; however, no cross-lingual validation is provided.
*   **Why unresolved:** Linguistic and lexicographic differences—such as morphological complexity, sense granularity, or defining vocabulary conventions—may affect criterion applicability and IterSim's effectiveness; the guidelines are tailored to Japanese dictionary styles.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies for typologically diverse languages (e.g., English, Arabic, Finnish), constructing comparable datasets with professional lexicographer input, and measuring framework consistency and IterSim's trade-offs across languages.
*   **Open Question 4:** What is the optimal size for a defining vocabulary in learner's dictionaries across different languages, balancing lexical simplicity and definitional adequacy?
*   **Basis in paper:** The authors select TUBE16K (~16,000 words) for Japanese based on feasibility, noting smaller sizes make definition writing harder, but they do not systematically investigate optimality; they also contrast this with English LDs using ~3,500 words.
*   **Why unresolved:** Vocabulary size directly impacts the trade-off: too constricted may limit expressiveness, too permissive may not aid comprehension; the paper's choice is pragmatic but lacks empirical optimization.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying defining vocabulary sizes (e.g., 3K to 30K words) for multiple languages, evaluating generated definitions on simplicity and quality criteria, and correlating with user studies on learner understanding.

## Limitations
*   **Dataset Access:** Core resources (D3J definitions, TUBE16K/TUBE3K lists) are unreleased, blocking exact replication of lexical simplicity results and reference-based evaluation.
*   **Human Agreement Baseline:** Inter-human agreement is modest (τ=0.370–0.521), especially for truthfulness. The LLM-judge may be consistent but misaligned with true quality, limiting interpretation of "state-of-the-art" scores.
*   **Generalization Risk:** Few-shot examples are drawn from a small (75-headword) lexicographer-reviewed set. Performance may degrade on underrepresented PoS or sense patterns in the 250-headword test set.

## Confidence
*   **LLM-as-Judge Reliability:** Medium (Validated τ=0.631 vs. humans is promising but based on a 100-headword sample; low human agreement on truthfulness undermines anchor strength.)
*   **IterSim Effectiveness:** High (Clear, measured outcome: TUBE16K jumps to 99.8% with no statistical score drop in the reported experiment.)
*   **Few-Shot Superiority:** Low–Medium (Reported gains are strong, but dependent on demo-set representativeness; variance from example selection indicates sensitivity.)
