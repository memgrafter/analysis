# Improving Generalization of Deep Learning for Brain Metastases Segmentation Across Institutions
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00397
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00397
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** Unsupervised VAE-MMD domain adaptation improves segmentation across institutions, raising mean F1 by 11.1% and reducing HD95 by 65.5%.

## Executive Summary
Brain metastases segmentation using deep learning faces significant challenges in generalizing across hospitals due to variations in MRI scanners and acquisition protocols. This paper introduces an unsupervised domain adaptation framework that combines a variational autoencoder (VAE) with maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) loss to align latent features across institutions. Integrated as a preprocessing step for the nnU-Net segmentation model, the approach requires no target-domain labels. Evaluated on 740 T1-weighted post-contrast MRI volumes from four centers, the VAE-MMD preprocessing yields substantial gains: mean F1 score increases by 11.1% (0.700 to 0.778) and mean 95th percentile Hausdorff distance decreases by 65.5% (11.33 to 3.91 mm). Domain classifier accuracy on latent vectors drops from 0.91 to 0.50, confirming effective distribution alignment.

## Method Summary
The method follows a two-stage pipeline. First, a VAE with skip connections and 3D self-attention blocks is trained on all available source-domain images (from the four institutions) using a composite loss: reconstruction losses (L2, L1, SSIM), KL divergence, MMD for latent distribution alignment across domains (multi-scale RBF kernels), and an adversarial loss for realism. The VAE reconstructs images in a standardized intensity range while its encoder produces domain-invariant representations. Second, the reconstructed volumes replace the original images as inputs to nnU-Net (3D full-resolution configuration), which is trained from scratch for segmentation. The baseline is nnU-Net trained on original, unprocessed images. No target-domain labels are used during adaptation, making the approach fully unsupervised.

## Key Results
- Mean F1 score increased by 11.1% (0.700 → 0.778).
- Mean surface Dice increased by 7.93% (0.7121 → 0.7686).
- Mean HD95 decreased by 65.5% (11.33 → 3.91 mm).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The VAE encoder-decoder with skip connections reconstructs input volumes into domain-harmonized representations that preserve anatomical structures while attenuating scanner- and protocol-specific intensity variations.
- **Mechanism:** The encoder compresses the 128³ input into a 512-dimensional latent vector. Skip connections concatenate encoder feature maps at each resolution with decoder upsampling layers, preventing the loss of high-frequency spatial details (e.g., small lesion boundaries) that would otherwise be degraded by the latent bottleneck. The decoder’s tanh output, combined with min-max input normalization, constrains reconstruction to a standardized intensity range.
- **Core assumption:** Domain-specific appearance variations (e.g., contrast, noise) are encoded as high-frequency signals that can be filtered out during compression and reconstruction, while domain-invariant anatomical semantics (e.g., tissue boundaries, lesion location) are preserved in the latent manifold and transferred back via skip connections.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “Reconstructed volumes attained a PSNR greater than 36 dB, maintaining anatomical accuracy.”
  - Section 2.3: “Skip connections... provided high-frequency spatial details... enabling accurate reconstruction of fine structures such as small metastases.”
- **Break condition:** If reconstruction PSNR falls below ~30 dB or segmentation performance degrades after VAE preprocessing, indicating that critical anatomical information was lost during compression.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The MMD loss aligns the latent distributions of all institutional datasets, forcing the encoder to produce features that are statistically indistinguishable across domains.
- **Mechanism:** For each training batch, MMD computes the squared distance between mean embeddings of samples from different institutions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) using multi-scale radial basis function kernels (σ ∈ {0.5, 1.0, 2.0}). Minimizing this loss reduces the discrepancy between domain distributions in the latent space. This operates on the 512-dimensional latent vectors, not pixel space, allowing alignment at the semantic level.
- **Core assumption:** Domain shift manifests as distributional differences in high-level feature space; by aligning these distributions without using domain labels, the encoder learns representations that are invariant to scanner/protocol while retaining task-relevant information for segmentation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “VAE-MMD reduced domain classifier's accuracy from 0.91 to 0.50.”
  - Section 3.1: “t-SNE visualization... samples from all four centers were mixed together... domain classifier accuracy dropped to 50.0%.”
- **Break condition:** If a domain classifier trained on post-VAE latents remains above ~70% accuracy, latent alignment has failed and domain-specific biases persist.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The domain-aligned reconstructions serve as harmonized inputs to nnU-Net, enabling the segmentation network to learn institution-agnostic mappings from image to mask.
- **Mechanism:** The VAE-MMD preprocessing step is applied to all training images before they are fed into nnU-Net. nnU-Net’s auto-configuration (preprocessing, architecture, training) remains unchanged, but now operates on images where inter-institutional intensity and contrast variations have been normalized by the VAE. The segmentation model thus focuses on learning lesion morphology rather than site-specific imaging signatures.
- **Core assumption:** Domain shift primarily affects low-level image statistics; harmonizing these at the input level reduces the burden on the segmentation network to internally account for scanner variability, improving its ability to generalize.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “The combined method raised the mean F1 by 11.1%... reduced the mean HD95 by 65.5%.”
  - Section 2.6: “nnU-Net was trained using both the original images (baseline) and those reconstructed through a VAE (VAE-MMD preprocessing).”
  - Section 3.2: Table 1 shows consistent improvements across F1, sDice, and HD95 for all centers with VAE-MMD.
- **Break condition:** If segmentation performance on target centers does not improve relative to baseline mixed training (e.g., F1 gain < 2%), the harmonization did not translate to better generalization.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text):**  
Input MRI → VAE Encoder → Latent vector (512-dimensional) → VAE Decoder (with skip connections) → Reconstructed MRI → nnU-Net → Segmentation output.  
Additional: MMD loss computed on latent vectors from all domains; reconstruction losses (L2, L1, SSIM), KL divergence, and adversarial loss guide VAE training.

**Critical path:**
1. Preprocess all images to 128³ and min‑max normalize intensities to [-1,1].
2. Train VAE‑MMD end‑to‑end on mixed institutional data, minimizing total loss (reconstruction + KL + MMD + adversarial).
3. Generate VAE‑reconstructed volumes for the entire training and test sets.
4. Train nnU‑Net (3D full‑res) from scratch on either original images (baseline) or VAE‑reconstructed images (proposed).
5. Evaluate segmentation metrics (F1, sDice, HD95) separately on each center’s test set.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Latent dimension (512): Larger preserves more detail but makes MMD alignment harder; smaller risks information loss.
- Skip connections: Essential for boundary preservation but increase memory usage; may also convey some domain‑specific details if not properly aligned.
- MMD kernel scales: Multi‑scale captures both fine and coarse shifts but increases computation; single scale may miss certain shifts.
- Loss weights (λ_MMD=10, λ_adv=5, λ_KL=0.1): High MMD weight prioritizes alignment but could distort latent space if too high; adversarial loss improves realism but risks training instability.

**Failure signatures:**
- High domain classifier accuracy (>70%) after VAE‑MMD training.
- Reconstruction PSNR < 36 dB or visible artifacts in reconstructed volumes.
- No improvement (or degradation) in segmentation HD95/sDice compared to baseline.
- Training instability: loss spikes, discriminator mode collapse.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Latent alignment validation:** Train VAE‑MMD on all centers, plot t‑SNE of latents before/after; compute domain classifier accuracy. Target: accuracy ~50% (chance for 4 classes).
2. **Reconstruction quality check:** Compute PSNR/SSIM on held‑out validation reconstructions; visually inspect difference maps for anatomy preservation. Target: PSNR > 36 dB, near‑black differences.
3. **Segmentation transfer test:** Train nnU‑Net on VAE‑reconstructed vs. original images; evaluate F1, sDice, HD95 on each center’s test set. Target: consistent improvement across all centers, especially in HD95 (>50% reduction).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Can a multi‑modal VAE‑MMD framework that jointly processes T1CE and FLAIR sequences further improve segmentation performance across institutions?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors explicitly suggest exploring multi‑modal VAE architectures.  
*Why unresolved:* The study used only T1CE; FLAIR may provide complementary contrast, especially for non‑enhancing lesions, but its benefit in this framework is unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A controlled experiment applying a multi‑modal VAE‑MMD to the same four‑center dataset, comparing sensitivity, F1, and HD95 against the single‑modal approach.

### Open Question 2
Does VAE‑MMD preprocessing maintain its domain adaptation benefits when deployed prospectively at a completely unseen hospital without any model retraining?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors propose prospective clinical validation studies.  
*Why unresolved:* Evaluation was retrospective with predefined train/test splits; real‑world deployment may encounter scanner models or protocols not represented in training data.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A prospective multi‑center trial where VAE‑MMD preprocessed images from a new institution are segmented by the same nnU‑Net model; performance would be assessed against local manual annotations and baseline nnU‑Net.

### Open Question 3
Can federated learning enable training of the VAE‑MMD across institutions without centralizing data, while achieving comparable domain alignment and segmentation improvements?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors mention federated learning as a privacy‑preserving alternative.  
*Why unresolved:* Current method requires centralized multi‑institutional unlabeled data, posing privacy/regulatory barriers; federated efficacy for this architecture is untested.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Implementation of a federated VAE‑MMD algorithm across the four institutions’ local data, comparing domain classifier accuracy, F1, and HD95 to the centralized approach.

### Open Question 4
Do alternative domain alignment losses (e.g., adversarial discriminators, optimal transport) outperform MMD in the VAE context for brain metastasis segmentation?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors suggest exploring different strategies.  
*Why unresolved:* Only MMD was evaluated; other methods might handle complex shifts more effectively or with better stability.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic comparison replacing MMD with adversarial or optimal transport losses in the same VAE architecture, using domain classifier accuracy and segmentation metrics across all four centers.

## Limitations
- nnU‑Net’s automatic preprocessing (normalization, cropping) may behave differently on VAE‑reconstructed images (tanh‑scaled), potentially confounding the observed segmentation gains.
- Reconstruction quality relies on PSNR (>36 dB) but lacks qualitative validation that small metastases and fine boundaries are preserved; smoothing could impair segmentation of tiny lesions.
- Training details (random seeds, data splits, VAE batch composition) are under‑specified, impeding exact replication of the experiments.

## Confidence
- **Reconstruction harmonization:** Medium – PSNR and t‑SNE mixing support the claim, but no direct evidence that tiny lesions survive compression without distortion.
- **Latent alignment via MMD:** High – Domain classifier accuracy drops to chance (0.50) and t‑SNE shows mixed clusters, strongly confirming distribution matching.
- **Segmentation improvement:** High – Consistent gains across F1, sDice, and HD95 on all four centers demonstrate practical benefit, though the isolated effect of VAE‑MMD vs. nnU‑Net’s auto‑configuration remains partially entangled.

## Next Checks
1. Perform a qualitative reconstruction audit: Visually inspect VAE‑reconstructed volumes per center, focusing on small metastases and boundary fidelity; compute difference maps to detect anatomical distortions.
2. Fixed‑preprocessing ablation: Train nnU‑Net on both original and reconstructed images using identical, non‑adaptive preprocessing (fixed normalization/cropping) to isolate the effect of VAE harmonization.
3. Latent content probing: Train linear classifiers on VAE latent features to predict domain metadata (scanner model, protocol parameters). Success = low accuracy (<60%), confirming removal of domain information while retaining lesion semantics.
