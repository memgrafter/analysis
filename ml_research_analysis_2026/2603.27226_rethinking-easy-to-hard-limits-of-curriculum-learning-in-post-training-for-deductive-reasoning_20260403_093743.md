# Rethinking Easy-to-Hard: Limits of Curriculum Learning in Post-Training for Deductive Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.27226
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27226
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Curriculum learning shows no consistent benefit over random sampling for post-training deductive reasoning in LLMs.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates the effectiveness of curriculum learning (CL) in post-training large language models (LLMs) for deductive reasoning. Using synthetic arithmetic and logical benchmarks where difficulty is defined by reasoning complexity, the authors compare various curriculum schedules (easy-to-hard, hard-to-easy, mixed) against random sampling across multiple model families and post‑training methods (SFT and RL). The key finding is that CL yields no consistent improvement in accuracy or response length compared to random sampling. Reinforcement learning (RL) generally enhances out‑of‑distribution accuracy, while supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) can degrade performance. The invariance of response lengths across curricula suggests that example ordering has negligible impact on compositional generalization in this setting.

## Method Summary
The study employs synthetic datasets (MathGAP, PARTWHOLE, KK) designed to test deductive reasoning, with difficulty defined by the number of required reasoning steps. Models from the Llama3.2, Qwen3, and Gemma2 families are post‑trained using either supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) with LoRA or reinforcement learning (RL) with GRPO/PPO. Training follows a fixed compute budget across different curriculum strategies: standard uniform sampling, single‑difficulty increasing/decreasing, and mixed schedules (UpToDiff, DownToDiff). Models are evaluated on in‑distribution and out‑of‑distribution difficulties, with metrics including accuracy, response length, and format compliance.

## Key Results
- No consistent accuracy gains from curriculum learning over random sampling across all models and post‑training methods.
- RL improves out‑of‑distribution accuracy; SFT sometimes degrades OOD performance relative to zero‑shot baseline.
- Response lengths remain invariant across curricula, indicating limited effect of ordering on reasoning depth.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: The ordering of training examples by structural difficulty has little effect on final accuracy because models learn to approximate the correct reasoning distribution through mechanisms insensitive to sequence.  
**Mechanism**: In both SFT and RL, model updates are driven by total gradient steps rather than example order. For RL, reward signal incentivizes correct final answers regardless of order. For SFT, models may memorize provided chains of thought without building compositional rules.  
**Core assumption**: The learning algorithm is sufficiently powerful that the final distribution of visited examples determines performance.  
**Evidence anchors**: [abstract] no consistent improvement; [section 4] distribution of response lengths similar across curricula.  
**Break condition**: If models were significantly undertrained or curriculum pacing extremely slow, ordering might matter.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: RL with verifiable outcome rewards provides a strong learning signal to uncover reasoning rules without curriculum scaffold.  
**Mechanism**: RL optimizes for correctness of final answer via reward independent of training order. Policy gradient updates encourage exploration of reasoning paths that lead to high rewards, gradually acquiring inference rules even if hard examples appear early.  
**Core assumption**: Reward function accurately reflects desired reasoning process and model capacity is sufficient.  
**Evidence anchors**: [section 4] RL improves OOD accuracy; SFT can degrade.  
**Break condition**: If rewards are sparse or ambiguous, curriculum might be needed.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: Positive correlation between example difficulty and input/CoT length confounds curriculum effect, as models attend to length cues.  
**Mechanism**: Because difficulty tied to reasoning steps and thus length, models may be driven by length‑related features not aligned with curriculum progression. Under uniform training, mix of lengths per batch remains similar across curricula under fixed steps.  
**Core assumption**: Model optimization sensitive to length distribution rather than semantic difficulty.  
**Evidence anchors**: [section A.2] input length increases linearly with difficulty; [section 4] response lengths invariant.  
**Break condition**: If difficulty defined independently of length, curriculum effect might become visible.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Synthetic reasoning datasets (MathGAP with LINEARDEPTH and PARTWHOLE, KK) -> Medium‑sized LLMs (Llama3.2, Qwen3, Gemma2) -> Curriculum strategies (Standard uniform, SingleDiffInc/Dec, UpToDiff, DownToDiff) -> Post‑training methods (SFT with LoRA, RL with GRPO/PPO) -> Evaluation metrics (accuracy, response length, format compliance) -> Implementation framework (VeRL).
- **Critical path**: Define difficulty function → split training difficulties into phases → for each phase, sample examples per curriculum → perform fixed‑budget training (gradient updates) → evaluate on held‑out ID and OOD difficulties → compare metrics across curricula.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Synthetic data vs real benchmarks; fixed training steps vs convergence; static difficulty ordering vs adaptive pacing; difficulty definition (structural vs surface proxies); full‑finetuning vs LoRA; outcome reward vs step‑wise reward.
- **Failure signatures**: Accuracy not significantly above zero‑shot; SFT leads to lower OOD than zero‑shot; response length distributions collapse; format compliance drops on OOD; high seed variance.
- **First 3 experiments**: 1. Zero‑shot evaluation to establish baseline and verify difficulty‑accuracy correlation. 2. SFT with Standard random sampling to measure effect without curriculum. 3. SFT with easy‑to‑hard curriculum (SingleDiffInc) and compare to Standard; repeat for RL (GRPO) to assess interaction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does curriculum learning improve post‑training deductive reasoning on natural language benchmarks?**  
   *Basis*: Synthetic setting avoids contamination and tightly controls difficulty; CL may behave differently on benchmarks like GSM8K/MATH.  
   *Why unresolved*: Negative results limited to synthetic tasks.  
   *Resolution*: Conduct CL experiments on widely used reasoning benchmarks with contamination controls.

2. **Can adaptive curriculum strategies based on model performance yield better generalization?**  
   *Basis*: Only static curricula were evaluated; adaptive pacing remains unexamined.  
   *Why unresolved*: No exploration of performance‑driven adaptation.  
   *Resolution*: Implement dynamic difficulty adjustment (e.g., increase when validation accuracy exceeds threshold) and compare to static curricula.

3. **Do curriculum effects emerge at larger model scales or with increased compute?**  
   *Basis*: Experiments on medium models (≤4B) with fixed budget; scaling might reveal benefits.  
   *Why unresolved*: Limited model size and training budget.  
   *Resolution*: Replicate with >70B models and proportionally larger compute budgets.

4. **What is the mechanism behind curriculum inefficacy—surface pattern learning vs. rule abstraction?**  
   *Basis*: Paper speculates models may internalize surface patterns; internal representations unanalyzed.  
   *Why unresolved*: No probing or analysis of learned representations.  
   *Resolution*: Use activation analysis or test generalization to novel rule combinations to assess abstraction.

## Limitations
- Synthetic benchmarks may not reflect complexity of real‑world reasoning tasks.
- Difficulty confounded with input/CoT length, making effective ordering indistinguishable from random.
- Fixed training steps and medium model scales limit generalizability; benefits might emerge with more compute or larger models.

## Confidence
- **Claim**: Curriculum learning yields no consistent accuracy gains over random sampling. **Confidence**: High within controlled synthetic setting, Medium for broader generalization.
- **Claim**: RL enhances out‑of‑distribution accuracy. **Confidence**: Medium‑High.
- **Claim**: SFT can degrade OOD performance relative to zero‑shot. **Confidence**: Medium.
- **Claim**: Response lengths invariant across curricula. **Confidence**: Medium.

## Next Checks
