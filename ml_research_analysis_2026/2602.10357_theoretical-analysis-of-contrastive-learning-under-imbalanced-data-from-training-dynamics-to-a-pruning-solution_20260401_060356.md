# Theoretical Analysis of Contrastive Learning under Imbalanced Data: From Training Dynamics to a Pruning Solution

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10357
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10357
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Magnitude-based pruning during training amplifies gradient influence of minority features, improving representation balance and achieving up to +7.07% accuracy on CIFAR10-LT (imbalance ratio 100).

## Executive Summary
Contrastive learning under class-imbalanced distributions suffers from inadequate learning of minority (tail) features. This paper provides a theoretical analysis showing that minority features are weakly learned due to their low frequency and reduced neuron specialization. To address this, the authors propose a simple yet effective magnitude-based pruning strategy: during training, neurons with activation magnitudes below a threshold are masked in the forward pass. This pruning amplifies the gradient influence of minority features during backpropagation, leading to stronger updates and encouraging more neurons to specialize in minority patterns. Empirical evaluations on CIFAR10-LT, CIFAR100-LT, and ImageNet-LT confirm substantial accuracy improvements (e.g., +7.07% on CIFAR10-LT with imbalance ratio 100) and a significantly reduced performance gap between head and tail classes. The work thus offers both a theoretical foundation and a practical solution for imbalanced contrastive learning.

## Method Summary
The authors analyze contrastive learning using a simplified single-head Transformer-MLP model. Their theoretical treatment of training dynamics reveals that under imbalance, features belonging to tail classes receive less frequent updates, resulting in neurons that are only weakly specialized to those features. Based on this insight, they introduce a magnitude-based pruning mechanism applied during training: after the forward pass through the MLP, any neuron whose activation magnitude falls below a dynamically set threshold is temporarily set to zero. This masking effectively removes low-magnitude pathways, which are predominantly responsive to majority features, thereby amplifying the gradient flow to the remaining neurons. The amplified updates promote the emergence of neurons that purely specialize in minority features, ultimately balancing the representation. The method is theoretically justified within their framework and implemented without additional hyperparameters beyond the pruning ratio.

## Key Results
- **Significant accuracy gains** on standard long-tailed benchmarks: +7.07% top-1 on CIFAR10-LT (imbalance ratio 100), with large improvements also on CIFAR100-LT and ImageNet-LT.
- **Reduced head-tail gap**: The performance disparity between majority and minority classes decreases markedly across all datasets.
- **Theoretical validation**: The observed improvements align with the theory’s predictions that pruning induces neuron specialization and balanced gradient contributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Pruning operates by masking neurons with activation magnitudes below a chosen threshold. In an imbalanced setting, neurons that predominantly respond to tail-class features tend to have small magnitudes because those features appear infrequently during training. When these small-magnitude neurons are removed, the forward pass propagates through a reduced set of pathways. During backpropagation, the loss gradient must still be distributed to explain the output, but with fewer active neurons, the gradient magnitude per surviving neuron increases. This gradient amplification specifically benefits neurons that were already somewhat responsive to minority features, giving them stronger updates and accelerating their specialization. Over time, this leads to a more balanced representation where a higher proportion of neurons dedicate themselves to tail classes, mitigating the imbalance without sacrificing head-class performance.

As the pruning is applied iteratively throughout training, the continuous amplification creates a positive feedback loop: minority-aligned neurons receive larger gradients, become more active, and are less likely to be pruned in subsequent iterations. Consequently, the network progressively allocates representational capacity to tail classes, resulting in the empirically observed improvements.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Input -> Token Embedding -> Single-Head Self-Attention -> Layer Normalization -> Position-wise MLP -> Output Representation

**Critical Path**: The most influential route for the contrastive loss is the forward pass through the attention and MLP layers, with the pruning operation applied to MLP neuron activations. The output representation fed into the InfoNCE loss depends directly on this path.

**Design Tradeoffs**: The pruning ratio controls the fraction of neurons removed. A higher ratio yields stronger gradient amplification for minority features but risks losing representational capacity and may harm majority performance. The threshold for magnitude can be fixed or adaptive; an adaptive threshold based on the activation distribution might better balance the tradeoff.

**Failure Signatures**: Indicators of a misconfigured pruning strategy include (1) overall accuracy drop, especially on head classes; (2) training instability such as exploding or vanishing gradients; (3) the gap between head and tail failing to shrink or even widening; and (4) the proportion of pruned neurons exceeding expected levels, suggesting over‑pruning.

**First Experiments**:
1. **Baseline verification**: Train a standard contrastive model on balanced data and confirm that pruning (with small ratio) does not degrade accuracy.
2. **Imbalance effect replication**: Train without pruning on imbalanced data and verify that tail class accuracy is significantly lower than head class accuracy.
3. **Pruning impact test**: Apply the proposed magnitude-based pruning at a modest ratio (e.g., 20%) and measure the change in tail accuracy and head‑tail gap; expect improvement without major head accuracy loss.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How precisely does the choice of pruning ratio and pruning criterion affect the ability to mitigate class imbalance in contrastive learning?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state that studying the pruning ratio and scheme is a limitation and future work: "The first concerns studying the pruning ratio and pruning scheme in magnitude-based pruning. Providing a fully precise characterization of how performance varies across different ratios and schemes is highly nontrivial, and doing so would require making more precise assumptions about the data distribution."
- **Why unresolved**: The theoretical guarantees cover a specific regime but do not provide a full characterization across possible ratios or alternative pruning criteria. The impact on representation quality, sparsity, and the trade-offs remain unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Empirical evaluation of test accuracy and tail performance over a wide range of pruning ratios; theoretical extension that explicitly incorporates α into convergence bounds and neuron specialization dynamics, possibly with relaxed data assumptions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can the theoretical analysis be extended to more complex architectures beyond the single-head Transformer-MLP considered, such as multi-head attention and deeper transformers?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "Extending the analysis to more complex or realistic models will be another direction for future work, and may require fundamentally different derivations and analytical tools."
- **Why unresolved**: The simplified model limits the scope of the results; real-world architectures incorporate many additional mechanisms whose theoretical interaction with imbalance is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop a new theoretical framework that accommodates multi-head self-attention, stacking multiple transformer blocks, and alternative activations; validate the extended theory on larger-scale models and compare predicted neuron specialization patterns with empirical observations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Are there alternative strategies beyond magnitude-based pruning that could more effectively promote minority feature learning in imbalanced contrastive learning?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The conclusion notes: "Looking ahead, promising directions include exploring alternative strategies beyond pruning that could further promote minority-feature learning."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper focuses solely on pruning; other plausible approaches (e.g., loss re-weighting, augmentation, regularization) are not explored theoretically or empirically, leaving a gap in understanding the most effective methods.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Propose new methods (e.g., frequency-aware re-weighting, feature-space augmentation) and provide both theoretical analysis and experiments showing superior performance over pruning on standard imbalanced benchmarks.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis relies on simplified assumptions (e.g., linear outputs, Gaussian features) that may not fully capture the behavior of deep, nonlinear contrastive networks.
- Empirical validation is restricted to long-tailed image classification (CIFAR10-LT, CIFAR100-LT, ImageNet-LT); effectiveness on other data modalities (text, audio) or contrastive settings (video, multimodal) remains untested.
- The causal link between pruning and amplified gradient influence for minority features, while suggested by performance gains, lacks direct measurement (e.g., gradient norms for minority-related parameters).

## Confidence
- Minority features are weakly learned under imbalance: **Medium**
- Magnitude-based pruning amplifies gradient influence and induces neuron specialization: **Medium**
- Pruning yields substantial accuracy gains and reduced head‑tail gaps on standard LT benchmarks: **High**
- Pruning is a broadly effective, theoretically grounded solution for imbalanced contrastive learning: **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Gradient influence measurement**: Track gradient norms or parameter updates specifically for neurons that encode minority features (e.g., via probing) before and after pruning to verify amplification.
2. **Cross-domain and cross-objective evaluation**: Apply the same pruning protocol to contrastive learning in NLP (sentence embeddings), audio, and medical imaging, and to objectives other than InfoNCE, testing if balance improvements persist.
3. **Pruning criterion ablation**: Compare magnitude-based pruning against random pruning and alternative saliency criteria (e.g., activation magnitude, weight variance) to isolate why removing small-magnitude neurons benefits minority features.
