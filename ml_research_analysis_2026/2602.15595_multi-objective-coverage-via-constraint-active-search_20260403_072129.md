# Multi-Objective Coverage via Constraint Active Search

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15595  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15595  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** On large-scale protein‑target datasets (1M compounds, 5 objectives), MOC‑CAS outperformed baselines with lower fill distance, more feasible samples, and higher AUP.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the multi‑objective coverage (MOC) problem: selecting a small, diverse set of threshold‑compliant samples to broadly explore the feasible region in objective space, a key need in expensive applications like drug discovery. MOC‑CAS uses independent Gaussian processes (GPs) with upper confidence bounds (UCB) to predict optimistic outcomes. Its acquisition maximizes incremental feasible volume coverage at a fixed resolution *r*, while breaking ties for diversity. A smoothed surrogate acquisition enables closed‑form gradients for efficient gradient‑based optimization. On real‑world datasets (SARS‑CoV‑2 and cancer targets), MOC‑CAS consistently achieves better coverage (lower fill distance), more feasible discoveries, and higher area under the progress curve than existing baselines. Ablations confirm that larger *r* improves coverage quality and a higher initial optimism scale β₀ boosts early discovery.

## Method Summary
MOC‑CAS proceeds iteratively. Initially, 20 samples are selected at random. In each subsequent round, GPs (RBF kernels) are fitted to observed objectives and updated incrementally. Candidate inputs are drawn from a shared shortlist: a pool of 5 000 random candidates, then the top 200 per objective by UCB (μᵢ + √βₜσᵢ), plus 200 additional random points. For each candidate *x*, compute optimistic predictions *U(x)*. The smoothed acquisition combines (a) a feasibility term *p_sat(U)* = ∏ Φ((Uᵢ−τᵢ)/λ), approximating all objectives exceeding thresholds; (b) a novelty term *n(U)* = 1 − Σ_{feasible y_s} cₘ(r) exp(−‖U−y_s‖²/(4r²)), estimating the new volume covered by an *r*‑ball around *U(x)*; and (c) a constant volume factor *Vₘ(r)*. The candidate maximizing acquisition is selected; if several tie, the farthest from existing feasible points in objective space is chosen. After querying the true objectives, the new point is added to the dataset and the process repeats for *T* = 200 iterations.

## Key Results
- MOC‑CAS achieved significantly lower fill distance (tighter spread) than all baselines on four protein‑target datasets with 1 M compounds and 5 objectives.  
- It discovered more threshold‑compliant samples and attained higher Area Under the Progress (AUP) curves, indicating superior cumulative feasible discovery.  
- Ablations showed that larger resolution *r* balances coverage quality, while higher initial β₀ improves early discovery rates; the smoothed acquisition enabled efficient gradient‑based maximization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: UCB‑based optimistic predictions**  
Claim: Independent GPs with UCB guide search toward potentially feasible regions. Mechanism: For each objective *i*, a GP yields posterior mean μₜ⁽ⁱ⁾ and variance σₜ⁽ⁱ⁾; the optimistic estimate is Uₜ⁽ⁱ⁾(x) = μₜ⁽ⁱ⁾(x) + √βₜ σₜ⁽ⁱ⁾(x). The UCB vector balances exploitation (high mean) and exploration (high uncertainty). Evidence: Abstract and Section 4 define GPs and UCB; Algorithm 1 integrates them. Break condition: Poor GP fit, strong objective correlations, or heteroscedastic noise can produce over‑ or under‑confident UCBs, misdirecting search.

**Mechanism 2: Incremental feasible volume coverage**  
Claim: Acquisition maximizes the *r*‑ball volume around optimistic predictions that lies inside the feasible set *S* and is not already covered, promoting diverse coverage. Mechanism: For candidate *x* with UCB *U(x)*, compute Volume(Bᵣ(U(x)) ∩ S \ ⋃_{y_s∈Zₜ₋₁} Bᵣ(y_s)). Ties are broken by selecting the point farthest from *Zₜ₋₁* in objective space. This heuristic assumes ball‑based volume captures diversity, with *r* trading quality vs. sample count. Evidence: Abstract; Section 3.1 defines covered volume and fill distance; Section 4 gives the acquisition formula. Break condition: Highly irregular *S* or poorly chosen *r* can cause over‑coverage (merging clusters) or under‑coverage (leaving gaps).

**Mechanism 3: Smooth surrogate for gradient‑based optimization**  
Claim: Replacing hard geometric/threshold indicators with Gaussian kernels and probit gates yields a differentiable acquisition with closed‑form gradients, enabling fast maximization. Mechanism: (A) The ball indicator ∈{0,1} is replaced by a normalized Gaussian kernel to preserve mass; (B) The feasibility orthant indicator becomes a product of probit CDFs with softness λ; (C) The union of balls becomes a soft sum of kernels. The resulting expectation under a local Gaussian has closed form; gradients follow from GP derivatives (Eq. 7–8). Evidence: Abstract; Section 5 derives the surrogate and gradients. Break condition: Mis‑specified λ or *r* biases the surrogate; gradient‑based optimization may stall in non‑convex landscapes or with poor initial points.

## Foundational Learning
- **Gaussian Processes (GPs)** – Why needed: provide non‑parametric Bayesian models for unknown objectives, yielding predictive means and variances essential for UCB and feasibility estimation. - **Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)** – Why needed: balances exploration and exploitation by adding a scaled standard deviation to the mean, directing search toward promising yet uncertain regions in objective space. Quick check: What role does the confidence schedule βₜ play in MOC‑CAS, and how does annealing it affect early vs. late iterations?
- **Multi‑objective Coverage vs. Pareto Optimization** – Why needed: MOC aims to cover the entire feasible region defined by thresholds, whereas Pareto optimization seeks the trade‑off front; the former is crucial when diverse feasible candidates are required (e.g., drug discovery). Quick check: Why might sample‑space coverage methods fail in objective‑space coverage problems, as argued in the introduction?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Manager → GP Modules → UCB Calculator → Acquisition Engine → Optimizer → Tie‑Breaker → Data Manager

**Critical path:**  
Each iteration: (1) Fit/update independent GPs on current dataset *D*ₜ₋₁; (2) For all candidates in the shortlist, compute UCBs; (3) Evaluate the soft acquisition *𝛼̃*(x) and its gradients; (4) Use a gradient‑based optimizer (e.g., L‑BFGS) to find candidate *x*ₜ maximizing *𝛼̃*; (5) If multiple maximizers exist, pick the point farthest in objective space from existing feasible outcomes; (6) Query true objectives *f(x*ₜ*), add to *D*ₜ and update feasible set.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Independent vs. correlated GPs:* independence simplifies scaling but ignores inter‑objective correlations that could improve feasibility estimates.  
- Fixed resolution *r:* larger *r* increases per‑sample coverage but may merge distinct feasible clusters; smaller *r* gives finer granularity but needs more samples.  
- Soft vs. hard acquisition: the soft surrogate enables gradient optimization but introduces approximation bias; the hard version is exact but non‑differentiable.  
- Shortlist size: larger shortlists increase per‑iteration cost but reduce risk of missing good points; too small may degrade performance.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Fill distance plateaus at a high value → poor spread in objective space.  
- Number of feasible samples grows very slowly → acquisition not finding valid regions.  
- High variance across random seeds → instability, possibly from GP hyperparameter fitting or optimization failures.  
- Optimizer fails to converge or repeatedly returns the same point → acquisition landscape may be flat or poorly conditioned.  
- Acquisition values become negative or NaN → numerical issues (e.g., *n(U)* > 1, underflow in *p_sat*).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic sanity check:** Deploy MOC‑CAS on a 2D synthetic feasible region (e.g., axis‑aligned rectangle) with known GPs; verify that selected points spread evenly and achieve lower fill distance than random sampling.
2. **Hyperparameter ablation:** Systematically vary *r* and initial β₀ on one protein‑target dataset; measure fill distance and AUP to confirm reported sensitivities (higher *r* improves coverage quality, higher β₀ boosts early discovery).
3. **Baseline replication:** Implement all baselines (Random, One‑Step, Straddle, MOO+Cluster) with identical GP kernels and shortlists; ensure MOC‑CAS outperforms in early AUP and fill distance, reproducing patterns from Figure 2/3.

## Limitations
- Dataset access: The proprietary DrugImprover dataset prevents independent verification of results.  
- Hyperparameter gaps: Critical settings (e.g., probit width *λ*, βₜ schedule, shortlist sizes, GP kernel details, noise variance, thresholds *τ*) are unspecified or inferred, hindering faithful reproduction.  
- Model approximations: Independent GPs ignore inter‑objective correlations; smoothing replaces hard constraints with surrogates, introducing potential bias without strong theoretical guarantees.  
- Candidate shortlisting: Pre‑filtering may exclude globally optimal points in high‑dimensional input spaces.
