# Aligned explanations in neural networks

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2601.04378
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04378
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: PiNets, pseudo-linear models with intrinsic explanations, achieve competitive detection scores on image tasks and excel when targets are informative.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a fundamental limitation of post-hoc explanation methods: they often fail to reflect the model’s actual reasoning. It introduces the principle of **explanatory alignment** and **model readability**, arguing that models should be architected so that explanations are immediate and faithful to predictions. To this end, the authors propose **Pointwise-interpretable Networks (PiNets)**, a class of pseudo-linear models that, for each input, compute a representation in an interpretable feature space and then predict via a linear combination. This structure ensures the explanation (feature attributions) is directly derived from the forward pass, eliminating the approximation gap of post-hoc methods. Evaluated on image classification and segmentation with the MARS framework, PiNets achieve meaningfulness comparable to Grad-CAM baselines. The paper further shows that recursive feedback, model ensembling, and strong supervision improve faithfulness, and that PiNets particularly excel when target variables provide richer information than class labels, reducing the need for threshold tuning.

## Method Summary
PiNets are designed as **pseudo-linear prediction functions**: an input \(x\) is mapped by a feature extractor \(\phi\) to an interpretable feature vector \(z\) in a space \(Z\) (e.g., spatial regions or semantic concepts). The prediction is \(y = w^\top z\), where \(w\) are learned weights. The explanation for \(x\) is the per-feature contribution \(w_i \cdot z_i\), available immediately after the forward pass. This architecture enforces explanatory alignment by construction.

To enhance robustness and fidelity, PiNets incorporate a **recursive second-look** mechanism: after the initial prediction and explanation, the model re-examines the input using the explanation to guide attention (e.g., masking low‑attribution areas), producing a refined feature vector \(z'\) and updated prediction. The process may be repeated. Additionally, PiNets can be **ensembled** to stabilize explanations, and **strong supervision** (e.g., segmentation masks) may be used to directly supervise the explanations during training.

## Key Results
- PiNets achieve competitive detection scores (TDR×TAR) compared to Grad-CAM on image classification and segmentation, with optimal post‑processing often matching or exceeding baseline performance.
- Recursive accuracy shifts confirm that PiNet explanations are sufficient and robust; removing top‑attributed features causes a sharp drop in prediction.
- Techniques such as recursive feedback, model ensembling, and strong supervision improve faithfulness metrics; PiNets notably outperform when target variables are more informative than class labels, requiring less threshold fine‑tuning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The pseudo‑linear structure guarantees that each prediction is a sum of contributions from interpretable features, making the explanation a direct read‑off of the model’s computation. This eliminates the noise and saturation issues that arise when approximating gradients for black‑box models. Recursive feedback likely reduces contextual bias by allowing the model to refine its focus based on initial attributions, similar to a reflexive attention process. Ensembling smooths out idiosyncrasies across models, yielding more stable attributions. Strong supervision aligns internal features with human‑recognizable concepts, closing the gap between model reasoning and user expectations. Together, these design choices embed alignment at the architectural level rather than treating it as an afterthought.

## Foundational Learning
- **Explanatory alignment**: Ensuring that an explanation directly corresponds to the model’s decision process. *Why needed*: Post‑hoc methods can be unfaithful, eroding trust. *Quick check*: Is the explanation computed as part of the forward pass rather than by an external approximator?
- **Pseudo‑linear model**: A network whose output is a linear combination of interpretable features. *Why needed*: Linear combinations provide exact feature attributions (coefficients) that are straightforward to interpret. *Quick check*: Are the final layer weights the sole determinants of the prediction over features that carry semantic meaning?
- **MARS (Meaningful Attribution for Reliability and Stability)**: A framework for evaluating explanation meaningfulness via insertion/deletion detection tasks. *Why needed*: Offers a quantitative, dataset‑dependent measure of how well attributions highlight relevant regions. *Quick check*: Does the method achieve high detection scores (e.g., TDR×TAR) on a standard benchmark?
- **Recursive accuracy shift**: A robustness test that iteratively removes top‑attributed features and measures the change in prediction. *Why needed*: Validates that attributions truly reflect influential features; removing important features should drastically alter output. *Quick check*: Does accuracy drop steeply when the most attributed features are masked?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input \(x\) → Feature extractor \(\phi\) → Interpretable feature vector \(z\) → Linear predictor (weights \(w\)) → Prediction \(y\); explanation \(= w \odot z\). Optional loop: Explanation \(\rightarrow\) Refinement (second‑look) \(\rightarrow\) \(\phi\) → refined \(z'\).

**Critical path**: Basic training: forward pass through \(\phi\) to get \(z\), compute \(y = w^\top z\), compute task loss, backpropagate to update \(\phi\) and \(w\). With second‑look: after initial \(y\) and explanation, a second pass through \(\phi\) (possibly with altered input) yields \(z'\) and a new prediction. Ensembling aggregates predictions from multiple independently trained PiNets. Strong supervision adds auxiliary losses on explanations when ground‑truth masks are available.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Pseudo‑linear architecture restricts representational power to gain interpretability; may limit peak accuracy on complex tasks.
- Second‑look improves robustness to context at the cost of extra computation (an additional forward pass) and potential instability if attention is noisy.
- Ensembling boosts performance and explanation stability but increases memory and inference latency.
- Strong supervision requires extra annotations but yields explanations better aligned with human concepts; however, it may bias the model if the supervised concepts diverge from true predictors.

**Failure signatures**:
- Low task accuracy: the interpretable feature extractor \(\phi\) is insufficiently expressive.
- High variance in explanations across training runs: regularization or training dynamics are inadequate.
- Second‑look degrades performance: refinement may overfit to noise; inspect whether attention maps become scattered.
- Strong supervision does not improve MARS: the supervised concepts may not match the model’s actual decision factors.

**Three first experiments**:
1. **Baseline replication**: Train a basic PiNet (no second‑look, ensembling, or strong supervision) on CIFAR‑10. Compare classification accuracy and MARS detection score against a ResNet‑18 baseline with Grad‑CAM explanations.
2. **Recursive feedback ablation**: Disable the second‑look mechanism. Evaluate explanation stability under synthetic context perturbations (e.g., adding random backgrounds) using attribution similarity metrics (e.g., Spearman rank correlation).
3. **Strong supervision scaling**: Train PiNets with varying percentages of pixel‑level masks (0%, 10%, 50%, 100%) on PASCAL VOC segmentation. Report mean IoU and MARS score to assess how explanation quality correlates with segmentation performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can PiNets be generalized to data types beyond images (e.g., audio, text, graphs, genomics), and what are appropriate interpretable feature spaces Z for each?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state: "Extending PiNets to other data structures such as audio, text, graphs or genomic sequences is another promising avenue for future developments."
- **Why unresolved**: PiNets have only been evaluated on image classification and segmentation. Different modalities have distinct characteristics and existing interpretable representations. The pseudo‑linear structure and second‑look mechanism may need significant adaptation.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop PiNet implementations for each modality, defining Z based on domain‑specific features (e.g., spectrogram coefficients for audio, word embeddings for text, node centralities for graphs). Conduct experiments measuring predictive accuracy and MARS faithfulness, and gather qualitative feedback from domain experts on the interpretability of explanations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What operational definition and metric can capture explanatory meaningfulness for cases where attributions should reflect causal influence rather than spatial detection (e.g., tabular data)?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors note: "In many other settings, however, the definition of meaningfulness is less straightforward and may require a more careful conceptualization. For example, oftentimes in the context of tabular data meaningful explanations may need to capture causal relationships."
- **Why unresolved**: The detection score used in the paper relies on binary ground‑truth maps, which are not available for causal attributions. There is no standard way to evaluate whether an explanation correctly identifies causal features, and existing post‑hoc methods do not guarantee causal fidelity.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Introduce benchmarks with known causal structures (e.g., synthetic datasets with causal graphs) and define metrics that compare attributions to true causal influences, possibly via interventions. Validate new metrics by correlation with human assessments of causal explanation quality across multiple xAI methods.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Does recursive feedback in PiNets improve robustness to contextual variations, and can combining it with adversarial training yield explanations that are both accurate and robust under distribution shift?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors call for "a more thorough investigation of robustness to context, along with the effect of stabilizing the explanations recursively," and also propose exploring "the feasibility and effectiveness of incorporating existing robustness‑enhancing techniques, such as adversarial training, into PiNets."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper’s analysis of robustness is limited to recursive accuracy shifts, without systematic evaluation of how explanations change when irrelevant context is modified. The potential synergy between recursive stabilization and adversarial robustness remains unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Design experiments where contextual features (e.g., background, texture) are manipulated while keeping the true predictive signal constant. Measure explanation stability (e.g., via similarity metrics) and predictive accuracy under such perturbations for PiNets with/without recursive feedback and with/without adversarial training. Demonstrating reduced sensitivity to context would answer the question.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can PiNets be trained effectively with weak supervision (e.g., image‑level labels, counts) to produce high‑quality segmentation, and which architectural choices (second look, decoder capacity, ensembling) are most beneficial?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors suggest that PiNets could "bypass the complexities of the multi‑step training procedures required for this type of hybrid supervision" and cite the flood mapping experiment as a proof‑of‑concept, while noting it likely reflects a lower bound on potential.
- **Why unresolved**: The flood mapping results used a simple soft second look and achieved lower IoU than a fully supervised model. It is unknown whether more sophisticated PiNet designs (hard second look, deeper decoders, ensembling, strong supervision with limited pixel annotations) can close the performance gap or even surpass standard weakly supervised segmentation methods.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct comprehensive studies on standard segmentation datasets (e.g., PASCAL VOC, COCO) using only weak labels. Compare PiNet variants (with different second‑look implementations, decoder architectures, and ensembling) against state‑of‑the‑art weakly supervised segmentation techniques. Evaluate both segmentation metrics (IoU) and explanation faithfulness (MARS) to assess overall quality.

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to MARS on two vision tasks; generalizability to other modalities and datasets remains untested.
- Baseline comparisons limited to Grad‑CAM; lacks newer methods such as Integrated Gradients and SHAP.
- No statistical tests or confidence intervals; the significance of reported improvements is unclear.
