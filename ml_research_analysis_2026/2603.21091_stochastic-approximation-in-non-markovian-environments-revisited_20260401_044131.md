# Stochastic approximation in non-markovian environments revisited

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.21091  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21091  
- **Reference count**: 14  
- **Primary result**: Stochastic approximation driven by non‑Markovian, non‑ergodic processes converges to equilibria determined by the tail σ‑field at −∞ via a Markov mimic and Doeblin decomposition.

## Executive Summary
This paper revisits stochastic approximation (SA) when the driving process is both non‑Markovian and non‑ergodic. The core contribution is a representation of the original process by an equivalent time‑homogeneous Markov chain, called the *Markov mimic*. The non‑ergodicity of the original process translates into a Doeblin decomposition of the Markov chain’s state space into distinct ergodic classes. The main theoretical result shows that SA iterates retain asymptotic memory of the distant past through the tail σ‑field at −∞, which selects the operative ergodic class. This framework provides a conceptual bridge linking long‑term memory effects in machine learning—such as those arising in transformer attention and continual learning—to non‑ergodic non‑Markovian dynamics. The work is purely theoretical; no empirical results are presented.

## Method Summary
The method builds a Markov mimic \(\tilde{Z}(n)\) whose transition kernel reproduces the conditional laws of the original non‑Markovian driver \(Z(n)\). This mimic is time‑homogeneous and defined on an augmented state space that encodes the necessary history. Under standard SA assumptions (Robbins‑Monro step sizes, Lipschitz drift \(h\)), the iterates \(x(n)\) track an ordinary differential equation (ODE) derived from the expected drift. The Doeblin decomposition partitions the mimic’s state space into ergodic classes, each associated with an extremal invariant measure. The tail σ‑field \(\sigma_{-\infty}\) of the original process determines which class is operative, thereby explaining how the distant past influences the asymptotic behavior of SA.

## Key Results
- SA driven by a non‑Markovian process can be analyzed via a Markov mimic whose Doeblin decomposition reveals ergodic classes.
- The asymptotic limit of SA iterates depends on the tail σ‑field at −∞, which selects the applicable ergodic class.
- The framework connects long‑term memory in machine learning (e.g., transformers, continual learning) to non‑ergodic non‑Markovian dynamics, offering a theoretical foundation without empirical validation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Markov mimic condenses all relevant history into its state, making the overall process Markovian. This allows the powerful tools of Markov chain theory—尤其是 the Doeblin decomposition—to be applied. The decomposition isolates ergodic classes, each behaving like a closed communicating class with its own stationary distribution. Under SA’s usual conditions, the iterates follow the ODE’s flow, and the ODE’s equilibria correspond to the extremal invariant measures of the mimic. The tail σ‑field at −∞ encodes which ergodic class the process originated from, and this information persists asymptotically, guiding the SA iterates to the appropriate equilibrium. Thus, long‑range memory is mathematically captured by the selection of an ergodic class, rather than by explicit state dependence.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
`Z(n) (non‑Markovian) → Markov mimic \tilde{Z}(n) → Doeblin decomposition → ergodic classes → ODE approximation → SA iterates x(n)`

**Critical path**:
1. Construct the Markov mimic transition kernel \(p_x(dz'|z)\) from the conditional laws of \(Z(n)\).
2. Perform Doeblin decomposition on the mimic's state space to identify ergodic classes.
3. Implement SA recursion with step sizes \(a(n)\) (Robbins‑Monro) and Lipschitz drift \(h(x,z)\).
4. Initialize SA with diverse histories; run iterates.
5. Verify convergence to equilibria associated with distinct ergodic classes.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Compactness*: Assuming a compact state space simplifies moment bounds; non‑compact spaces require additional Lyapunov conditions.
- *Lipschitz drift*: Ensures ODE approximation; violation may cause instability or divergence.
- *State augmentation*: The mimic often requires larger state space to encode history, trading off theoretical elegance for computational overhead.
- *Countable vs. general state*: Doeblin decomposition is classical for countable spaces; extending to general Polish spaces needs measure‑theoretic care.

**Failure signatures**:
- All SA trajectories converge to the same limit despite varied initial histories → ergodic classes are not correctly identified or the mimic is effectively ergodic.
- Iterates become unbounded or oscillate without settling → step sizes violate Robbins‑Monro conditions or Lipschitz assumption on \(h\) fails.
- Memory effects vanish quickly in finite runs → the tail σ‑field has negligible influence, possibly due to a small spectral gap or near‑ergodic behavior.

**First experiments**:
1. Implement a basic SA scheme with a simple \(h\) (e.g., gradient of a multimodal function) and standard step sizes.
2. Simulate a non‑ergodic non‑Markovian driver \(Z(n)\), e.g., a finite‑state process with history dependence, and compute its Markov mimic.
3. Run SA from multiple initial histories and observe whether iterates converge to different equilibria corresponding to distinct ergodic classes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Empirical identification of ergodic classes** – Can the ergodic classes of the Markov mimic be detected from transformer training trajectories, and do they correspond to interpretable long‑term memory patterns? (Currently lacking empirical methodology.)
2. **Spectral gap and convergence rates** – For a Markov mimic derived from a finite‑window non‑Markovian process, what is the spectral gap and how does it quantitatively determine the rate at which SA converges to the operative ergodic class? (No calculations provided for relevant ML models.)

(Finite‑difference methods are mentioned as a workaround, but analysis is missing.)
4. **Extension to non‑compact state spaces** – Does the main convergence result hold when the driver’s state space \(S\) is non‑compact? (The paper assumes compactness to avoid unbounded moment issues; non‑compact cases likely need Lyapunov functions or growth conditions.)

## Limitations
- No empirical validation, especially regarding the claimed link to transformers or continual learning.
- Compactness assumption on the state space limits applicability to unbounded settings.
- The tail σ‑field is a theoretical limit σ‑algebra, not directly estimable from finite data.
- Theoretical assumptions (Lipschitz drift, Robbins‑Monro step sizes) may be violated in practical machine learning training pipelines.
