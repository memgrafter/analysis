# Safety Training Persists Through Helpfulness Optimization in LLM Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02229
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02229
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Safety training gains persist through subsequent helpfulness optimization, with 90–94% of improvement retained; all training configurations lie on a strong linear Pareto frontier (R² = 0.77); simultaneous safety and helpfulness DPO fails to discover “best of both worlds” strategies existing in the dataset.

## Executive Summary
The paper examines whether safety improvements in LLM agents are preserved when the model is subsequently optimized for helpfulness. Using the ToolEmu agentic benchmark, which simulates tool‑use scenarios with an emulator, the authors apply Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to three open‑weight models (Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct, Phi 4). Safety is defined as avoiding harmful actions taken by the agent, while helpfulness measures task completion quality.

Results show that safety gains from an initial safety‑focused DPO are highly robust: sequential helpfulness training retains 90–94% of the safety improvement even at relatively strong DPO strengths. All experimental configurations—whether safety first, helpfulness first, or simultaneous—fall on a linear Pareto frontier between safety and helpfulness deltas (R² = 0.77). In contrast to prior work on chat models, simultaneous optimization does not uncover strategies that excel at both metrics, suggesting that safety training in agentic contexts may stabilize the policy and that the two objectives overlap substantially. The findings imply that safety post‑training can be compatible with performance optimization in tool‑using agents.

## Method Summary
Data collection and evaluation: The authors used the ToolEmu benchmark’s 144 tasks (split 72 train/72 test per seed). They ran 27 LLMs (including the three source models and larger models like GPT‑5 series) to generate trajectories (thoughts, actions, observations). Each trajectory was scored on safety and helpfulness by two separate evaluator LLMs (Qwen 3 32B Thinking and GPT‑5 mini) on a 0–3 scale using rubric prompts. Preference pairs for DPO were formed from trajectory pairs on the same task where the chosen outcome scored at least 2 points higher than the rejected on the target metric.

Training and analysis: The source models were fine‑tuned with LoRA‑based DPO. Experiments included single‑metric safety (S) or helpfulness (H) training, sequential runs (S→H and H→S), and simultaneous combined (S&H). The DPO β parameter was set to 0.05 or 0.1. For sequential runs, the model after the first stage served as the reference for the second stage. After training, models were evaluated on the 72 test tasks with the evaluator they did not train against (cross‑evaluation) to avoid overfitting. The primary analysis computed average safety and helpfulness scores, measured the fraction of safety gain retained after helpfulness training (persistence), and plotted the Pareto frontier of deltas showing a strong linear relationship (R² = 0.77).

## Key Results
- **Safety gains persist:** After safety DPO followed by helpfulness DPO, the model retains 90–94% of the initial safety improvement across three models and β values.
- **Linear Pareto frontier:** All training configurations (S, H, S→H, H→S, S&H) cluster on a line relating safety and helpfulness deltas with R² = 0.77 (p < 10⁻⁹), indicating a trade‑off that is approximately constant ratio.
- **Simultaneous training fails to exceed the frontier:** Joint DPO on both metrics yields a point on the same linear frontier rather than a “best of both worlds” strategy that is present in the DPO dataset, implying that the optimization cannot exploit complementary aspects of the data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Cautious intermediate alignment
**Claim:** Safety training in ToolEmu encourages information‑seeking and risk‑averse behavior (e.g., asking clarifying questions) that is also beneficial for helpfulness, so most safety gains persist when optimizing for helpfulness afterwards.  
**Mechanism:** The safety DPO preferences favor trajectories that avoid harmful actions by first gathering missing information. These intermediate steps are not penalized by helpfulness and often contribute to successful task completion. Consequently, after safety training the model sits in a parameter region where the helpfulness gradient refines execution without discarding the cautious pattern.  
**Core assumption:** The helpfulness evaluator does not strongly penalize extra informative steps, and safe information‑gathering aligns with high helpfulness.  
**Evidence:** The abstract notes that “using tools to gather information and then providing the user with clear options without taking direct action usually scores perfectly for safety and highly for helpfulness.” Figure 7 shows a safe trajectory that asks for dosage confirmation (safety 3, helpfulness 2).  
**Break condition:** If many tasks required refusal (inaction) for safety while helpfulness demanded action, the overlap would vanish and safety would likely degrade during helpfulness training.

### Mechanism 2: Linear regularization from sequential DPO
**Claim:** Sequential DPO with a fixed reference model (the previous stage) constrains updates within a KL ball. Because the safety‑trained model is near a local optimum for safety, the helpfulness gradient shifts both metrics in a roughly constant ratio, producing the observed linear Pareto frontier. Simultaneous combined training also lands on this line because the combined gradient is a linear combination and the KL constraint prevents escaping it.  
**Mechanism:** DPO minimizes a reward‑difference loss with a KL penalty to the reference policy. When training helpfulness after safety, the reference is the safety‑trained model. The safety model already lies on a plateau for safety; the helpfulness gradient therefore moves the policy in a direction that changes safety and helpfulness proportionally, yielding a line of trade‑offs.  
**Core assumption:** The implicit reward models for safety and helpfulness are approximately linear in the policy logits, and the KL penalty dominates the optimization dynamics.  
**Evidence:** The abstract states “all training configurations end up near a linear Pareto frontier with R² = 0.77” and “there was a strong linear relationship between safety and helpfulness gains.” Figure 5 plots delta scores with R² = 0.77 (p < 10⁻⁹); the text notes that S&H training “simply resulted in another point on the frontier.”  
**Break condition:** A very low β (weak KL penalty) or orthogonal gradients would likely produce a non‑linear frontier or allow a “best of both worlds” point.

### Mechanism 3: Agentic safety overlaps with helpfulness unlike chat
**Claim:** In agentic settings, safety means avoiding harmful actions, which often requires the same cautious, information‑gathering behavior that helpfulness rewards. In chat, safety usually means refusing harmful requests, which directly conflicts with helpfulness. This difference explains why safety persists here but not in prior chat studies.  
**Mechanism:** ToolEmu tasks are frequently underspecified or involve implicit assumptions. The safe strategy (e.g., ask for clarification, verify parameters) is also the helpful strategy because it ensures correct task completion. In contrast, chat safety often pits compliance (helpful) against refusal (safe). Thus, the optimal policy regions overlap substantially in the agentic domain, making safety gains robust to subsequent helpfulness optimization.  
**Core assumption:** The distribution of ToolEmu tasks is such that the safe and helpful optimal action sets intersect strongly.  
**Evidence:** The abstract explicitly says “unlike prior work, we find that safety training persists… safety refers to harmful actions directly taken by the LLM.” The Introduction notes: “Even legitimate requests can carry significant risk… using tools to gather information … usually scores perfectly for safety and highly for helpfulness.”  
**Break condition:** Introducing many tasks where the only safe response is to refuse or abort would re‑introduce conflict and likely reduce persistence.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Trajectory collector → Evaluator module → Preference dataset builder → DPO trainer → Cross-evaluator → Analysis scripts

### Critical Path
1. Split: Randomly split the 144 ToolEmu tasks into 72 training and 72 test (seed dependent).
2. Collect: Run 27 LLMs on all 144 tasks to gather 3888 trajectories.
3. Score: Evaluate every trajectory with both evaluators for safety and helpfulness.
4. Build datasets: For each evaluator and metric, construct DPO triplets from trajectory pairs with ≥2 score gap.
5. Train: For each DPO metric sequence (S, H, S,H, H,S, S&H) and β∈{0.05,0.1}, run LoRA DPO, using previous stage output as reference for sequential runs.
6. Evaluate: For each trained model, generate new trajectories on the 72 test tasks and score with the opposite evaluator.
7. Analyze: Aggregate scores over seeds and evaluators; compute persistence and plot the safety‑helpfulness frontier.

### Design Tradeoffs
- LoRA: Low‑rank adaptation reduces compute but may limit the magnitude of policy shifts; could constrain ability to find “best of both worlds” strategies.
- Two evaluators + cross‑eval: Reduces single‑evaluator bias but assumes both are equally valid; if both are flawed, results may be systematically off.
- Fixed hyperparameters (learning rate 5e‑5, 1 epoch): Simplifies experimentation but may not be optimal for all datasets; other values could change dynamics.
- Small test set (72 tasks): Increases statistical noise; bootstrapping helps but still limits power.
- Only open‑weight sources (Llama, Qwen, Phi): Findings may not generalize to proprietary models like GPT‑5, which were used only as evaluators.
- DPO only: Other methods (full‑rank DPO, RLHF) might exhibit different persistence properties.

### Failure Signatures
- Safety training ineffective: Post‑safety safety score remains low (< 2.0 on 0‑3 scale).
- No persistence: After S→H sequential training, safety drops to the H‑only baseline, yielding Persist(S,β) ≈ 0.
- High seed variance: Standard deviation > 0.3 across seeds for the same configuration.
- S&H outperforms frontier: Simultaneous training yields a point clearly above the linear frontier (e.g., higher helpfulness at equal safety).
- Evaluator mismatch: Matched evaluator scores diverge strongly from cross‑evaluator scores, hinting at overfitting.
- LoRA underfitting: Using too low a rank (e.g., r = 4) causes negligible metric changes.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline check:** Run the three source models on ToolEmu without post‑training. Confirm low safety scores (< 1.8) and a “bias for action” (many immediate tool uses without clarification).
2. **Single‑metric sanity:** Apply safety DPO (β = 0.1) to one source model. Verify safety improves by ≥ 0.8 points and helpfulness changes little.
3. **Sequential persistence:** Perform safety DPO (β = 0.1) followed by helpfulness DPO (β = 0.1) on the same model. Compute Persist(S, 0.1); expect > 0.8. If < 0.5, investigate data splits, reference handling, or LoRA setup.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**1. Why does safety training persist through subsequent helpfulness optimization in agentic settings but not in traditional chat settings?**  
Basis in paper: The authors explicitly state they do not know why safety persists here but not in chat settings and call it an “exciting area for future work.”  
Why unresolved: The paper identifies a stark contrast with prior chat‑based findings but provides no mechanistic analysis explaining the difference in post‑training dynamics between the two settings.  
What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments comparing identical models trained on chat versus agentic safety benchmarks with matched DPO data, coupled with analyses of gradient updates, loss landscapes, or objective overlap.

**2. Why does simultaneous safety and helpfulness DPO fail to discover “best of both worlds” strategies (i.e., Pareto‑optimal points) that exist in the dataset?**  
Basis in paper: The authors report that training on both metrics “simply results in another point on the frontier rather than finding a ‘best of both worlds’ strategy, despite the presence of such strategies in our DPO dataset.”  
Why unresolved: It is unclear whether this failure stems from inherent conflict in the objectives, limitations of the DPO algorithm, optimization getting stuck in suboptimal basins, or inadequate reward modeling.  
What evidence would resolve it: Applying more advanced multi‑objective RL or reward modeling techniques from the literature to the same dataset to see if they can recover the missing strategies, and analyzing the DPO loss gradients for safety and helpfulness to see if they compete.

**3. Do the persistence and linear Pareto frontier findings generalize to other agentic benchmarks and model architectures beyond ToolEmu and the three open‑weight models studied?**  
Basis in paper: The study is restricted to a single benchmark (ToolEmu) with 144 tasks and three specific source models; the authors acknowledge this as a key limitation and express hope that their findings “spur future research.”  
Why unresolved: The observed dynamics may be particular to ToolEmu’s task distribution, simulator, or the specific models’ initial “bias for action.”  
What evidence would resolve it: Replicating the entire sequential DPO pipeline on widely used agentic benchmarks (e.g., AgentBench, WebArena) and with a broader set of model families (e.g., decoder‑only vs. encoder‑decoder, different scales) to check for consistent persistence and frontier shapes.

**4. What is the precise mechanism by which initial safety training stabilizes model behavior and creates resistance to subsequent helpfulness post‑training?**  
Basis in paper: The authors hypothesize that “safety DPO may be moving the model to a basin of attraction in weight space that is also locally (near‑)optimal for helpfulness” and contrast this with catastrophic forgetting, but provide no direct evidence.  
Why unresolved: No analysis of weight space geometry, feature representations, or training dynamics is provided; the resistance could also be due to data distribution shifts or regularization effects.  
What evidence would resolve it: Tracking model weights throughout sequential training (e.g., using probing or similarity metrics), ablating the overlap between safety and helpfulness DPO datasets, and measuring gradient norm magnitudes to see if safety training reduces the capacity for further change on helpfulness.

## Limitations
- **Proprietary model dependence:** The reliance on GPT‑5 models for trajectory collection and evaluation introduces reproducibility issues and may embed model‑specific biases that affect the DPO data distribution.
- **Benchmark scope:** ToolEmu may not cover agentic scenarios where safety requires outright refusal, potentially overestimating the overlap between safety and helpfulness.
- **Evaluation limitations:** The small test set (72 tasks) and use of only two evaluators increase variance and may miss systematic biases; LoRA and fixed hyperparameters also limit the ability to fully explore the policy space.

## Confidence
- **Safety persists through helpfulness optimization:** High
- **Linear Pareto frontier:** Medium
- **Cautious intermediate alignment as mechanism:** Medium
- **Generalization to other agentic benchmarks:** Low

## Next Checks
- **Full‑rank DPO ablation:** Replace LoRA with full‑rank updates across β values to test robustness of the linear frontier and persistence against increased capacity for policy shifts.
- **Cross‑benchmark transfer:** Train and evaluate the same sequential DPO pipeline on a different agentic benchmark (e.g., WebArena) with diverse safety threats to assess generalizability of the overlap.
- **Dataset shift analysis:** Split ToolEmu tasks by whether the safe policy requires an informational query versus a refusal, then measure persistence separately to directly test the hypothesis that informational queries drive the overlap.
