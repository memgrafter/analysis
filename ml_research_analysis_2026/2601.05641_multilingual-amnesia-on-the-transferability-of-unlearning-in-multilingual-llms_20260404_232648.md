# Multilingual Amnesia: On the Transferability of Unlearning in Multilingual LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05641
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05641
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Unlearning in multilingual LLMs is predominantly language-specific; cross-lingual transfer is minimal and driven mainly by syntactic similarity, with notable asymmetry where unlearning lower-resource languages can more strongly impact higher-resource ones.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether gradient-based unlearning applied to one language within a multilingual large language model inadvertently affects other languages. Using the Aya-Expanse-8B model and two multilingual benchmarks (TOFU for data unlearning, SeeGULL for concept unlearning) across ten diverse languages, the authors find that forgetting is largely confined to the target language. Cross-lingual transfer is weak, correlates most strongly with syntactic similarity (r ≈ 0.35–0.40), and is asymmetric: unlearning in lower-resource languages often causes greater disruption to high-resource language performance than vice versa. Among three gradient-based methods (GradDiff, GradDiff-KL, NPO), NPO exhibits the most controlled forgetting with minimal spillover, while GradDiff shows broader propagation. The study concludes that unlearning in a single language is insufficient for comprehensive multilingual forgetting, highlighting the need for explicit multilingual unlearning strategies.

## Method Summary
The study uses the 8-billion parameter multilingual instruction-tuned model Aya-Expanse-8B. Two unlearning scenarios are evaluated: data unlearning (TOFU, synthetic author profiles) and concept unlearning (SeeGULL, geocultural stereotypes). Both datasets are machine-translated into ten languages (EN, FR, AR, JA, RU, FA, KO, HI, IW, ID). For each source language, three gradient-based unlearning algorithms are applied during fine-tuning: GradDiff (loss subtraction on forget vs. retain sets), GradDiff-KL (adds KL divergence from the pre-trained model), and NPO (preference-style loss using a reference model). Models are evaluated on all ten languages’ forget/retain/test splits. Primary metrics include normalized answer probability ratios, Truth Ratio (for TOFU), stereotypical answer selection rate (for SeeGULL), and perplexity on mC4 for utility. Cross-lingual transfer strength is correlated with linguistic distances from the URIEL database (syntactic, phonological, inventory) and resource levels.

## Key Results
- Unlearning effects are overwhelmingly language-specific; cross-lingual transfer is minimal and primarily occurs between typologically related languages (e.g., English–French).
- Transfer is asymmetric: unlearning in lower-resource languages (e.g., Farsi, Indonesian) often leads to greater performance degradation in higher-resource languages (e.g., English) compared to the reverse.
- Syntactic similarity is the strongest linguistic predictor of cross-lingual transfer (correlation ~0.35–0.40), outperforming phonological and inventory distances.
- NPO achieves the most controlled forgetting with the least cross-lingual spillover and minimal utility loss; GradDiff exhibits broader propagation.
- For concept unlearning (stereotype removal), English-centered unlearning generally reduces stereotypical responses in other languages, but effectiveness varies by source language; Japanese unlearning consistently yields gains across targets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Unlearning effects are predominantly confined to the language in which the unlearning is applied, with minimal cross-lingual propagation.
- **Mechanism:** Gradient updates from the forget set primarily modify language-specific subspaces within the shared multilingual model parameters. The optimization signal does not sufficiently overwrite representations for other languages, which are learned from distinct data distributions and occupy different regions of parameter space.
- **Core assumption:** The multilingual model’s parameter space contains both shared and language-specific components; unlearning gradients mainly affect the latter unless languages are highly similar.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.1 state that transfer is minimal and confined to typologically related pairs; heatmaps in Figure 2 show low off-diagonal values.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Cross-lingual unlearning transfer is asymmetric, with unlearning in lower-resource languages sometimes impacting higher-resource languages more strongly.
- **Mechanism:** High-resource language representations are more robust and redundant due to abundant training data, making them less vulnerable to perturbation from unlearning in another language. Lower-resource language representations are more fragile and entangled with shared parameters, so unlearning them destabilizes parameters that also support high-resource languages.
- **Core assumption:** Resource availability during pretraining determines robustness and independence of a language’s representation; less-resourced languages rely more on shared parameters.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes asymmetric transfer; Section 5.1 provides examples (e.g., unlearning in Russian affects English probability ratio at 0.20 vs. English unlearning affecting Russian at 0.38) and higher perplexity rise when unlearning low-resource languages.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Syntactic similarity is the strongest linguistic predictor of cross-lingual unlearning transfer, outweighing phonological or inventory distances.
- **Mechanism:** Syntactic structures are deeply embedded in the model’s intermediate representations and attention patterns. Unlearning perturbs these syntactic processing pathways; similar syntax in another language leads to generalization because the same neural circuitry is reused.
- **Core assumption:** The model encodes syntax in a shared manner across typologically similar languages, creating overlapping parameter sensitivity.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.1 report correlations r = 0.347–0.399 for syntactic distance, consistently higher than other distances; metrics from URIEL database.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Machine Unlearning (MU)**
  - **Why needed:** The entire study revolves around MU—removing specific information or behaviors from a trained model without retraining from scratch. Understanding MU paradigms (data vs. concept unlearning) is essential to grasp the paper’s objectives and evaluation.
  - **Quick check:** What is the key difference between *data unlearning* (TOFU) and *concept unlearning* (SeeGULL), and why might they require different evaluation strategies?

- **Concept: Cross-lingual Transfer**
  - **Why needed:** The core question is whether unlearning transfers across languages. This requires understanding how multilingual LLMs share knowledge (e.g., via shared vocabulary, cross-lingual embeddings, unified representations). Without this, patterns of (non-)transfer cannot be interpreted.
  - **Quick check:** Why might knowledge acquired in English appear in a low-resource language like Farsi within a multilingual model, and what factors determine the strength of that transfer?

- **Concept: Gradient-based Unlearning Objectives**
  - **Why needed:** GradDiff, GradDiff-KL, and NPO are the “how” of unlearning. A learner must understand how these objectives differ in balancing forgetting vs. retaining utility, as this influences stability and cross-lingual spillover.
  - **Quick check:** How does NPO’s use of a reference model and preference-style optimization lead to more controlled forgetting compared to GradDiff’s direct loss subtraction?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Base Model (Aya-Expanse-8B) -> Unlearning Algorithms (GradDiff, GradDiff-KL, NPO) -> Evaluation Benchmarks (TOFU, SeeGULL) -> Metrics (Probability Ratios, Truth Ratio, Stereotype Selection Rate, Perplexity). Linguistic analysis (URIEL distances) overlays the evaluation.

- **Critical path:**
  1. Prepare multilingual datasets: translate TOFU/SeeGULL into ten languages; validate splits (forget/retain).
  2. Train a finetuned baseline for each language using complete training data (5 epochs, lr=2e-5).
  3. For each source language, apply each unlearning algorithm for specified epochs (TOFU: 5 epochs; SeeGULL: 1 epoch).
  4. Evaluate the unlearned model on all ten languages’ forget/retain/test sets.
  5. Compute transfer metrics (e.g., forget probability ratio relative to finetuned baseline).
  6. Correlate transfer strengths with linguistic distances and resource levels.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Aggressiveness vs. Stability:** GradDiff achieves stronger forgetting but causes more cross-lingual spillover and utility loss. NPO is more conservative, preserving utility and limiting propagation.
  - **Evaluation granularity:** Using translated benchmarks introduces potential translation artifacts; original English performance is always highest, complicating fair cross-lingual comparison.
  - **Resource vs. Similarity:** Prior work emphasized resource disparity; this paper shows syntactic similarity is a stronger predictor, suggesting model architecture mediates transfer.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - High perplexity degradation on low-resource languages after unlearning (Appendix D), indicating instability.
  - Asymmetric transfer: e.g., unlearning on Indonesian affecting English more than vice versa (Figures 11/12).
  - Concept unlearning inconsistency: Japanese shows consistent debiasing gains regardless of source language.
  - Utility collapse: When retain set performance drops sharply, forgetting robustness degrades (Appendix E).

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline Transfer Test:** Unlearn on English using GradDiff, evaluate forget set probability across all 10 languages. Expect strong forgetting in EN, weak in others; confirm language-specificity.
  2. **Linguistic Correlation:** Compute pairwise syntactic distances (URIEL) between all language pairs. Correlate with cross-lingual forget probability ratios (from heatmaps). Expect positive correlation (~0.35–0.40).
  3. **Method Comparison:** Repeat Experiment 1 with GradDiff-KL and NPO. Compare heatmap patterns and retain set ratios. Expect NPO to show the most diagonal-focused pattern and highest retain set retention.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can we design unlearning methods that propagate forgetting across multiple languages simultaneously?
- What evaluation metrics are sufficient to capture the trade-offs in multilingual unlearning, including forgetting efficacy, retained utility, and cultural fairness?
- How can we create high-quality, culturally diverse multilingual benchmarks for unlearning that avoid translation artifacts?
- What factors determine the asymmetry in cross-lingual unlearning transfer, where unlearning in a lower-resource language sometimes affects higher-resource languages more?

## Limitations
- Translation artifacts in SeeGULL/TOFU may bias transfer measurements; human validation is limited.
- Results are based on a single multilingual architecture (Aya-Expanse-8B); generalizability to other models is uncertain.
- Hyperparameters for unlearning (learning rates, α/β values) are under-specified, potentially affecting stability and reproducibility.
- Retain set construction for concept unlearning (use of TruthfulQA KL) is ambiguous, impacting neutrality and utility trade-offs.

## Confidence
- **Language-specific confinement:** High (direct experimental evidence via cross-lingual evaluation heatmaps).
- **Asymmetric transfer (low-resource → high-resource impact):** High (consistent across multiple language pairs and metrics).
- **Syntactic similarity as top predictor:** Medium (correlational analysis; causality unproven; other linguistic factors may confound).
- **NPO’s controlled forgetting vs. GradDiff spillover:** High (reproducible pattern across experiments).

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity Ablation:** Systematically vary learning rates and unlearning epochs for GradDiff/NPO to quantify their effect on cross-lingual spillover magnitude and utility loss, establishing stable operating ranges.
2. **Translation Robustness Check:** Replicate key experiments using human-translated (vs. machine-translated) SeeGULL/TOFU for a subset of languages (e.g., Arabic, Japanese) to isolate translation-induced noise from true linguistic transfer effects.
3. **Architecture Generalization Test:** Repeat the core unlearning transfer protocol on at least two additional multilingual architectures (e.g., mT5, BLOOM) to verify whether syntactic similarity remains the strongest predictor, or if architectural differences alter the transfer landscape.
