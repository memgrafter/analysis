# M-Loss: Quantifying Model Merging Compatibility with Limited Unlabeled Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.08564
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08564
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: M-TIES, which integrates M-Loss into TIES, achieves near-ensembling performance (e.g., 85.28% vs. 85.56% on ViT-L/14) while significantly outperforming other merging baselines, using only 128 unlabeled samples to compute compatibility.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the absence of theoretical tools to evaluate model merging compatibility without labeled data. It introduces **M-Loss**, a metric that quantifies the discrepancy between the outputs of a parameter-averaged model and an ensemble of models, using limited unlabeled data. M-Loss operates at both layer and node levels, and its small expected value theoretically justifies merging feasibility for models fine-tuned from a common pretrained backbone. The authors incorporate M-Loss into a dynamic pruning scheduler within the TIES merging framework, resulting in **M-TIES**. Empirical validation on Vision Transformers shows M-TIES consistently surpasses standard merging methods (Simple Average, Task Arithmetic, TIES, DARE) and closely matches the performance of expensive model ensembling. The approach is sample-efficient, requiring only 128 unlabeled examples for stable M-Loss estimation.

## Method Summary
M-Loss measures the gap between the output of a merged model (via parameter averaging) and the average output of individual models (ensembling) across layers and nodes, using unlabeled data. Theoretically, under assumptions of large hidden dimension (k >> σ) and specific activation functions (ReLU, GELU, Leaky ReLU), the expected M-Loss is bounded and small for compatible models. This bound suggests that minimizing M-Loss via selective merging (pruning conflicting neurons) yields a merged model that approximates ensembling. M-TIES implements this by computing layer-wise and node-wise M-Loss on unlabeled data, then dynamically pruning parameters with high discrepancy before applying TIES's sign-based consensus merging. The process requires only a small, unlabeled calibration set.

## Key Results
- M-TIES consistently outperforms all baselines (Simple Average, Task Arithmetic, TIES, DARE) on ViT-B/32 and ViT-L/14 across multiple vision tasks.
- On ViT-B/32, M-TIES achieves 73.23% average accuracy vs. ensembling's 78.56%; on ViT-L/14, it reaches 85.28% vs. ensembling's 85.56%, demonstrating near-optimal merging performance.
- The metric stabilizes with as few as 128 unlabeled samples, enabling practical deployment without large calibration datasets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
M-Loss works because it directly estimates the output discrepancy between two merging strategies—parameter averaging and ensembling—at a granular level. For models fine-tuned from a shared pretrained backbone, their parameters and intermediate representations are often aligned, leading to small output differences when averaged. The theoretical analysis shows that under conditions like large neuron count (k) relative to parameter variance (σ), and for common activations (ReLU, GELU, Leaky ReLU), this discrepancy is mathematically bounded. By pruning neurons with high M-Loss, M-TIES removes conflicting directions, ensuring the merged model's behavior stays close to the ensemble. The use of unlabeled data bypasses the need for labels while capturing representational compatibility.

- **Unlabeled calibration**: Using raw data to estimate M-Loss. *Why needed*: Avoids label dependency; leverages readily available data. *Quick check*: Why 128 samples? Is there a theoretical sample complexity?
- **k >> σ assumption**: Hidden dimension (k) much larger than parameter standard deviation (σ). *Why needed*: Ensures central limit-like effects for small expected discrepancy. *Quick check*: What happens if σ grows comparable to k?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Unlabeled Data -> Forward Passes (per model) -> Layer-wise/Node-wise Activation Statistics -> M-Loss Computation -> Pruning Decision (high M-Loss neurons) -> TIES Merging (sign-based consensus) -> Final Merged Model

**Critical Path**: The core flow is: (1) Pass unlabeled samples through each fine-tuned model to collect intermediate activations. (2) For each layer and neuron, compute the discrepancy between averaged parameters' output and averaged individual outputs (M-Loss). (3) Identify and mask neurons with M-Loss above a threshold. (4) Apply TIES on the pruned parameter sets to produce the final merged model.

**Design Tradeoffs**: 
- *Sample efficiency vs. robustness*: Using only 128 samples minimizes cost but may not capture full data distribution, risking unstable M-Loss estimates on diverse data.
- *Granular pruning vs. information loss*: Node-level pruning removes conflicting units but may discard useful features if threshold is too aggressive.
- *Theoretical assumptions vs. real-world complexity*: The k >> σ and activation function constraints may not hold for all architectures, limiting applicability.

**Failure Signatures**:
- High M-Loss values across multiple layers/nodes indicate incompatible models; merging will likely degrade performance.
- Performance drop after merging despite low M-Loss suggests violation of theoretical assumptions (e.g., tasks too dissimilar, activations not covered).
- Inconsistent M-Loss across different unlabeled subsets signals insufficient or unrepresentative calibration data.

**First 3 Experiments**:
1. **Sample size sensitivity**: Vary unlabeled samples from 16 to 1024; measure M-Loss stability and final accuracy on a fixed merging task (e.g., ViT-B/32 on 3 vision tasks).
2. **Cross-architecture test**: Apply M-TIES to merge ResNet-50 models fine-tuned on ImageNet subsets; compare against baselines and ensembling.
3. **Task dissimilarity stress test**: Intentionally merge models fine-tuned on highly divergent tasks (e.g., natural images vs. medical scans); track M-Loss, pruning ratio, and accuracy to probe the k >> σ assumption.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the theoretical expectation of M-Loss derived for ReLU, GELU, and Leaky ReLU activations extend to other common activation functions, such as SiLU or ELU, under the same k >> σ assumption?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper's theoretical analysis in Section 4 and Appendix focuses exclusively on ReLU, GELU, and Leaky ReLU, without addressing other activations that are widely used in modern architectures.
- Why unresolved: The mathematical derivations rely on specific properties of these activations; other functions may have different non-linear characteristics that affect M-Loss bounds, limiting the theory's generality.
- What evidence would resolve it: Perform analogous probabilistic derivations for additional activations, then empirically measure M-Loss on models with those activations to validate the theoretical predictions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the violation of the k >> σ assumption—when merging models fine-tuned on highly dissimilar tasks—affect the reliability of M-Loss as a predictor of merging feasibility?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The assumption "k >> σ" is explicitly stated in Section 4 as a condition for the theoretical expectation to hold, implying its importance for small M-Loss.
- Why unresolved: Experiments only involve models fine-tuned on diverse but related vision tasks; no systematic evaluation with drastically different tasks (e.g., merging a language model and a vision model) tests the assumption's robustness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Design experiments merging source models with increasing task dissimilarity (quantified by metrics like parameter variance σ), compute M-Loss and final performance, and analyze correlation breakdowns when σ approaches k.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can M-Loss and M-TIES be directly applied to non-vision domains, such as natural language processing with transformer-based models, without architectural modifications?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] All empirical evaluations in Section 6 use Vision Transformer (ViT) models; no experiments on NLP models (e.g., BERT, GPT) are conducted, leaving cross-domain applicability unexplored.
- Why unresolved: NLP models feature unique components like multi-head attention and layer normalization, which may alter intermediate representation distributions and M-Loss computation, potentially requiring adjustments to the method.
- What evidence would resolve it: Replicate the experimental setup using pretrained language models fine-tuned on multiple NLP tasks, compute M-Loss with unlabeled text data, and benchmark M-TIES against baselines to assess transferability.

## Limitations
- Theoretical justification assumes fine-tuned models from a common backbone; applicability to heterogeneous training regimes (e.g., multi-source, different initializations) is unverified.
- Empirical evaluation restricted to Vision Transformer models; performance on other architectures (CNNs, LLMs) and non-vision tasks remains unknown.
- The claimed sample efficiency (128 samples) lacks robustness analysis across data distributions, domain shifts, or sample quality variations.

## Confidence

- **Link between small M-Loss and merging feasibility**: Medium (propositional, lacking extensive proof or counterexamples)
