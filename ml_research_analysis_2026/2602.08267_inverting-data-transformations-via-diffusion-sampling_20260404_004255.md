# Inverting Data Transformations via Diffusion Sampling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.08267
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08267
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: TIED significantly outperformed baselines on MNIST classification (96.95% accuracy) and PDE solving (0.043 relative L2 error) by inverting unknown Lie group transformations.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Transformation-Inverting Energy Diffusion (TIED), a method to invert unknown data transformations from general Lie groups at test time, improving robustness of pretrained neural networks. TIED employs a diffusion sampler that operates directly on the Lie group manifold using a right-trivialized forward process and a reverse SDE with a trivialized score computed via a target-score identity. This enables sampling from the Boltzmann posterior over transformations without requiring clean posterior samples. Experiments on MNIST classification under affine and homography transformations and on PDE solving show substantial gains over baselines, with TIED achieving up to 96.95% accuracy and reducing relative L2 error to 0.043. The method handles non-compact, non-Abelian groups like PGL(3,R) and semidirect products, demonstrating broad applicability.

## Method Summary
TIED formulates transformation inversion as sampling from a posterior distribution \(p(g|x) \propto \exp(-E(g))\), where the energy \(E(g)\) is defined using a pretrained model (e.g., classifier confidence or VAE ELBO). The sampler uses a forward diffusion process on the Lie group that adds noise in a right-trivialized manner, guaranteeing updates remain on the manifold. The reverse-time SDE is driven by a score estimate obtained via a Monte Carlo approximation of the trivialized target-score identity, which expresses the score in terms of the energy gradient and the Haar modular function. Implementation requires Lie group primitives (exponential map, action Jacobian), sampling from the forward noise distribution, and integrating the reverse SDE with automatic differentiation for trivialized gradients.

## Key Results
- MNIST affine/homography: up to 96.95% test accuracy (vs. 94.38% for LieLAC) and FID 0.57 (vs. 0.58).
- PDE solving: relative L2 error reduced to 0.043 (vs. 0.066 for LieLAC).
- Handles non-compact, non-Abelian groups (e.g., PGL(3,R), semidirect products) effectively.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: Diffusion sampling on Lie groups stays exactly on the manifold of transformations.  
**How**: The forward noising process uses right-trivialization: it draws noise in the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{g}\) and maps it to the current group element \(g_t\) via the tangent map \(d(L_{g_t})_e\). This ensures updates remain on the manifold. The reverse SDE inherits this property, and its drift term is computed in \(\mathfrak{g}\) via the trivialized score.  
**Evidence**: Abstract and Section 4.2 define SDEs (3) and (4) with \(d(L_{g_t})_e\) guaranteeing manifold preservation; neighbors (Kong & Tao, 2024; Zhu et al., 2025) explore trivialized diffusion.  
**Break condition**: If the exponential map is not surjective for non-compact groups, initialization \(g_M \sim k_1\) might not cover all modes, requiring careful noise schedule design.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: The trivialized target-score identity enables score estimation from an energy function \(E(g)\) without clean samples from the posterior.  
**How**: The identity expresses the trivialized score \(\nabla_g \log p_t(g_t)\) as an expectation over the initial score \(\nabla_g \log p_0\) weighted by \(p_{0|t}\). Since \(p_0 \propto e^{-E}\), this becomes \(-\nabla_g E\). It is recast into a Monte Carlo form using the noise distribution \(k_t\) and modular function \(\lambda(w)\), avoiding the need for \(p_{0|t}\).  
**Evidence**: Proposition 4.2 derives identity (5); Proposition 4.3 gives Monte Carlo form (22–23) with \(\lambda(w)\) handling measure distortion; neighbors (Akhound-Sadegh et al., 2024; De Bortoli et al., 2024) extend target score matching to Euclidean spaces.  
**Break condition**: The estimator (23) is biased (Jensen gap). High noise \(\gamma(t)\) can smooth \(E\) but extreme values may cause numerical instability in \(\nabla_g E\) and \(\lambda(w)\).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: Sampling from the transformation inversion posterior \(p(g|x)\) restores inputs \(g^{-1}\cdot x\) to the training distribution, enabling test-time equivariance for any pretrained model.  
**How**: Theorem 3.1 shows that if \(x \sim p_x\), then \(g^{-1}\cdot x \sim p_x\) when \(g \sim p(g|x)\). Proposition 3.2 proves \(p(g|x)\) is equivariant. Running TIED on \(x\) yields \(g^{-1}\), and applying it and then \(f_\theta\) with \(g\) gives an equivariant randomized predictor \(\tilde{f}_\theta(x)=g\cdot f_\theta(g^{-1}\cdot x)\).  
**Evidence**: Abstract states goal of inverting transformations; Section 3.3 and Proposition 3.2 formalize equivariance; Sections 5.2–5.3 show empirical success; neighbors (Kaba et al., 2023; Shumaylov et al., 2024) explore canonicalization alternatives.  
**Break condition**: If energy \(E\) is mis-specified (e.g., lacks modes for plausible \(g\)), the posterior could be misleading, causing \(g^{-1}\cdot x\) to leave the training manifold.

## Foundational Learning
**Lie Groups and Lie Algebras**  
*Why needed*: Transformations and updates live on curved manifolds \(G\); the algorithm operates in the tangent space \(\mathfrak{g}\) via trivialization to avoid manifold geometry issues.  
*Quick check*: How does the map \(v \in \mathfrak{g} \mapsto g \exp(v)\) relate to updating a group element, and why is \(\nabla_g f(g) = \nabla_v f(g \exp(v))|_{v=0}\) useful?

**Energy-Based Models (EBMs)**  
*Why needed*: \(p(g|x) \propto \exp(-E(g))\). The energy defines the posterior; gradients \(\nabla_g E\) guide sampling. Understanding EBM training/stability is crucial for interpreting noise and gradients.  
*Quick check*: Why can the same energy be used across different transformed inputs \(x\), and what does the determinant Jacobian term in \(E(g)\) account for?

**Diffusion Models / Reverse SDEs**  
*Why needed*: TIED essentially runs a reverse-time diffusion on \(G\). Knowledge of VE-SDEs, score estimation, and noise schedules is required to implement \(\gamma(t)\) and the Euler scheme.  
*Quick check*: How does the forward noising process \(g_t = g_0 w_t\) (with \(w_t \sim k_t\)) help avoid sampling \(p_0\) directly, and what role does \(k_1 \approx p_1\) play?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Pretrained model \(f_\theta\) (energy) → Group \(G\) & Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{g}\) (primitives: exp, Haar module \(\lambda\)) → TIED Sampler (forward \(k_t\), score MC, reverse SDE) → Test-time pipeline (\(x \rightarrow g \rightarrow g^{-1}\!\cdot\!x \rightarrow f_\theta\))

**Critical path**:
A. Implement Lie group primitives: \(\exp\), Jacobian of action \(J_g(x)\), structure constants for \(\lambda(w)\).  
B. Implement forward sampler \(w \sim k_t\) via Eq. (24) and compute \(\log \lambda(w)\) via Proposition B.13.  
C. Implement score estimator (23): for a given \(g_t\), draw \(w^{(i)} \sim k_t\), compute \(E(g_t w^{-1})\) and \(\log \lambda(w^{(i)})\), take \(\nabla_g\) of log-sum-exp, and update \(\hat{g}\) via exponential map.  
D. Integrate reverse SDE (9) with chosen \(\gamma(t)\), \(\Delta t\), and MC sample size \(N\).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Noise schedule \(\gamma(t)\): Larger \(\gamma_{\max}\) yields smoother scores but may lose fidelity; smaller improves detail but risks stuck in local modes.
- MC sample size \(N\): Higher \(N\) reduces bias but increases compute; \(N \approx 5-10\) often suffices; parallelization is key.
- Energy choice: VAE ELBO vs. classifier confidence—classifier may be sharper but have spurious modes; VAE smoother but less discriminative.

**Failure signatures**:
- Numerical overflow/underflow in \(\lambda(w)\) exponential due to large steps; mitigate by adaptive step sizing.
- Score estimator gradient vanishing when all \(E(g_t w^{-1})\) are large/similar; increase \(\gamma(t)\) earlier or use more \(N\).
- Divergence if \(f_\theta\) energy not informed enough along orbits; check via visualizing \(E(g)\) along known transformations.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Synthetic SO(10)**: Validate sampler on known multimodal density \(E(X)=-10 X_{1,1}^2\). Compare distribution of \(X_{1,1}\) to ground truth and kinetic Langevin (Fig. 3). Check convergence speed (steps vs. KL).
2. **MNIST Affine**: Apply TIED to ResNet18 on affNIST. Verify that \(g^{-1}\!\cdot\!x\) visually aligns digits to upright/centered pose. Check accuracy/FID vs. baselines (Tab. 1).
3. **PDE Symmetry (Burgers)**: Use DeepONet + TIED on \(SL(2,\mathbb{R})\ltimes(\mathbb{R}^2,+)\). Plot solutions before/after (Fig. 4b). Ensure relative L2 error drops to near clean-data level (~0.01–0.02).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Efficiency**: How can the computational efficiency of the TIED sampler be improved, particularly through consistency distillation? Unresolved because the current diffusion-based approach requires many sampling steps and Monte Carlo estimates. Evidence would be implementing consistency distillation and measuring sampling speed and final performance on benchmarks.
2. **Other inverse problems**: Can TIED be successfully applied to image registration? Unresolved because only evaluated on classification and PDE. Evidence would be adapting TIED to a registration task (e.g., OASIS dataset), defining an appropriate energy, and evaluating registration accuracy.
3. **Convergence guarantees**: What are the convergence guarantees of the TIED sampler, and how do they depend on Lie group geometry and energy function properties? Unresolved because no theoretical analysis of rates or conditions exists. Evidence would be developing convergence bounds for discretized SDEs on general Lie groups and empirically validating them.

## Limitations
- **PDE experiment details omitted**: Experimental setup for PDE solving (DeepONet architecture, data generation, energy function) not described, hindering replication.
- **Hyperparameter specifications incomplete**: Key sampling hyperparameters (\(\Delta t\), \(N\), \(\gamma(t)\)) not fully documented, making fair comparison difficult.
- **Score estimator bias unaddressed**: Monte Carlo approximation (Eq. 23) may suffer from bias and variance, especially for non-compact groups; impact on sampling quality unexplored.
