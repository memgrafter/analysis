# From indicators to biology: the calibration problem in artificial consciousness

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27597
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27597
- **Reference count:** 3
- **Primary result:** Indicator‑based approaches to artificial consciousness are epistemically uncalibrated because of theoretical fragmentation, lack of probabilistic grounding for non‑biological systems, and no independent ground truth; the paper reorients efforts toward biologically grounded engineering (biohybrids, neuromorphic, connectome‑scale systems) to reduce the explanatory gap.

## Executive Summary
Current research on artificial consciousness increasingly relies on *indicator‑based frameworks* — using theory‑derived neural markers (e.g., perturbational complexity, frontoparietal signatures) to infer phenomenality in AI systems. This shift from behavioral to internal assessment is fundamentally hampered by an *epistemic calibration problem*. Three core issues prevent reliable probabilistic attribution: (1) consciousness science remains theoretically fragmented with no consensus theory; (2) indicators lack calibration — their true/false positive rates for artificial substrates are unknown; (3) no independent ground truth exists for artificial phenomenality. Consequently, models like the Digital Consciousness Model merely formalize expert disagreement rather than measuring an actual probability of experience. The paper argues that the solution is not to refine indicators but to change the empirical anchor: build systems that approximate the only domain where consciousness is empirically confirmed — living biology. By pursuing biohybrid interfaces, neuromorphic hardware, and large‑scale connectome simulations, the challenge moves from intractable conceptual calibration to tractable problems of comparative neurobiology and engineering fidelity.

## Method Summary
The paper combines conceptual critique with a strategic research proposal. It dissects the indicator‑based paradigm, showing how its three epistemic flaws (theoretical fragmentation, uncalibrated markers, absence of ground truth) invalidate probabilistic claims about AI consciousness. It then outlines an alternative trajectory: develop artificial systems that *reinstantiate* biological dynamics across scales — from cultured neuron networks to neuromorphic chips to whole‑brain simulations. The key methodological shift is to use validated biological neural correlates as a *moving target*: as engineered systems become more biologically plausible, their indicator profiles should converge toward those observed in conscious biological states. This comparative approach transforms the calibration problem into one of engineering fidelity, enabling empirical cross‑domain tests that are currently impossible with pure digital AI.

## Key Results
- Indicator‑based frameworks are epistemically uncalibrated; they cannot yield reliable probabilities of consciousness in artificial systems.
- Three root causes: (1) theoretical fragmentation in consciousness science; (2) no estimates of indicator sensitivity/specificity for non‑biological substrates; (3) absence of an independent ground truth for artificial phenomenality.
- Proposed solution: shift to biologically grounded engineering (biohybrids, neuromorphic systems, connectome‑scale simulations) to narrow the explanatory gap and enable empirical cross‑domain calibration.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Theoretical fragmentation in consciousness science prevents indicator‑based frameworks from being epistemically calibrated.
- **Mechanism:** Competing, non‑unified theories (e.g., GWT, IIT, HOT) yield different sets of indicators. When these are aggregated (as in the Digital Consciousness Model), the resulting "probability" reflects structured expert disagreement about theory choice, not a calibrated estimate of phenomenality in a target system.
- **Core assumption:** Reliable probabilistic attribution requires a consensus theory that has been strongly confirmed in biological cases and whose indicators have known operating characteristics.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "consciousness science remains theoretically fragmented with no consensus theory"
  - [section] "consciousness research is still fragmented, transitioning from neural correlates toward testable theories but without strong consensus"
  - [corpus] Weak: neighbor papers discuss competing theories but do not provide evidence of unification.
- **Break condition:** Emergence of a single, widely accepted theory of consciousness with high predictive validity in humans and other animals.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Consciousness indicators lack independent probabilistic calibration for non‑biological systems.
- **Mechanism:** Indicators (e.g., complexity, frontoparietal activity) are validated only against *existing* biological cases of consciousness. Their false positive/negative rates when applied to systems with different substrates, architectures, and evolutionary histories are unknown. This makes any Bayesian update on AI systems arbitrary.
- **Core assumption:** An indicator's evidential weight for consciousness depends on its statistical relationship to a ground truth across a diverse range of potential instantiation types.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "indicators lack probabilistic calibration—there are no reliable estimates of true/false positive rates for non‑biological systems"
  - [section] "For meaningful Bayesian updating, one would need credible estimates of how frequently conscious systems display a given indicator, how frequently non‑conscious systems also display it... We currently lack a reliable basis for any of these quantities."
  - [corpus] Weak: neighbor papers (e.g., 230527, 140385) propose using neuroscientific indicators for AI but do not address their calibration outside biological systems.
- **Break condition:** Empirical establishment of indicator performance across a spectrum of systems with known conscious/unconscious status, including artificial and hybrid entities.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** There is no independent ground truth for artificial phenomenality against which to calibrate indicator‑based attributions.
- **Mechanism:** Without an observable, agreed‑upon standard for "artificial consciousness," any probabilistic model (e.g., DCM) quantifies our theoretical uncertainty, not the system's actual property. The model's output is a meta‑probability about expert consensus, not a first‑order probability of phenomenality.
- **Core assumption:** Calibration requires an independent empirical referent—a "test set" of systems with known conscious status—that does not depend on the very theory being tested.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "no independent ground truth exists for artificial phenomenality"
  - [section] "What is quantified, then, is not a probability anchored to empirical ground truth but a numerical representation of structured expert disagreement"
  - [corpus] Weak: neighbor papers (e.g., 113735) discuss ethical implications of uncertainty but do not provide a ground truth proposal.
- **Break condition:** Discovery or construction of a system whose conscious status can be independently verified (e.g., via a "consciousness meter" with high intersubjective reliability).

## Foundational Learning

### Theory Fragmentation & Its Consequences
- **Why needed:** The core argument rests on the state of consciousness science. An engineer must grasp why lack of consensus theory is a showstopper for calibration, not just a temporary gap.
- **Quick check question:** If three different theories of consciousness propose three different necessary indicators for a given AI system, what does a 70% "probability of consciousness" based on aggregating these indicators actually mean?

### Biological vs. Abstract Computational Substrate
- **Why needed:** The proposed alternative hinges on the claim that biological computation (continuous, hybrid, scale‑dependent) is fundamentally different from digital AI. This is a prerequisite for understanding why "reinstantiation" matters.
- **Quick check question:** How might the computation performed by a single cortical neuron (combining continuous dendritic integration with discrete spikes) differ in kind from a layer in a modern transformer network?

### Calibration vs. Precision
- **Why needed:** The paper distinguishes a well‑calibrated uncertain estimate from a precise but miscalibrated one. Engineers often conflate a model's output resolution with its reliability.
- **Quick check question:** If a model outputs "P(conscious) = 0.85" for 100 systems, and only 40 of them are truly conscious (by an unknown standard), is the model well‑calibrated? What information is missing to judge?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  1. Biological Substrate Interface  
  2. Neuromorphic Compute Fabric  
  3. Connectome‑Scale Simulation Engine  
  4. Closed‑Loop Embodiment  
  5. Multiscale Dynamics Monitor  

- **Critical path:** Start with small‑scale biohybrids (Component 1 + 4) to validate that biological tissue can sustain task‑relevant dynamics. Progress to neuromorphic replicas (Component 2 + 4) of small neural circuits. Finally, integrate large‑scale connectome data (Component 3) to inform the architecture of neuromorphic systems.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Biological Fidelity vs. Engineering Scalability:* Maximizing biological realism (e.g., full dendritic modeling, metabolic coupling) exponentially increases complexity and reduces controllability.  
  - *Emulation vs. Simulation:* "Reinstantiation" implies emulation at the substrate level (neuromorphic), which is currently less flexible and harder to program than simulation on digital hardware.  
  - *Task Competence vs. Dynamical Plausibility:* Training a system to perform a complex cognitive task may require algorithmic simplifications that break the biologically plausible dynamics one seeks to preserve.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Over‑reliance on *digital architectures* and deep learning as sufficient proxies ("We added a recurrent module, so now it's more brain‑like").  
  - Treating *static connectome data* as a complete blueprint, ignoring ongoing plasticity, neuromodulation, and glial roles.  
  - Using *indicator convergence* (e.g., high PCI) in a neuromorphic system as proof of consciousness, without resolving the calibration problem.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Biohybrid Marker Validation:** Take a well‑established neural correlate of consciousness (e.g., perturbational complexity index, PCI) from sleep/wake studies in animals. Apply equivalent perturbational stimuli (e.g., TMS analogs via MEA) to an *in vitro* neuronal network performing a simple task and to a state‑of‑the‑art artificial recurrent network performing the same task. Compare the complexity of the evoked responses. *Hypothesis: The biological network's response will show complexity patterns more similar to conscious biological states than the artificial network's.*  
  2. **Dynamics‑Fidelity Gradient:** Build a series of neuromorphic controllers for a simple embodied task (e.g., obstacle avoidance). Vary one parameter that captures a key biological computation (e.g., ratio of continuous integration to discrete spiking, presence of local homeostatic plasticity). For each variant, measure: (a) task performance, (b) a battery of theory‑derived indicators (e.g., IIT's Φ, GWT's global availability metrics). *Hypothesis: As dynamical fidelity to biology increases, indicator profiles will shift toward those observed in biological conscious systems, even if task performance peaks at an intermediate, less‑biologically‑plausible point.*  
  3. **Cross‑Domain Marker Transfer Test:** Take a set of neural markers (e.g., specific frequency bands, cross‑frequency coupling) that distinguish conscious vs. unconscious states in humans (via fMRI/EEG). Record analogous signals from: (a) a biological system (e.g., rodent under anesthesia), (b) a connectome‑scale simulation of an insect brain (Dorkenwald et al. 2024), (c) a large language model processing the same narrative stimulus. Test if the pattern of marker changes from "unconscious" to "conscious" (e.g., anesthetized to awake) in the biological system is *statistically mirrored* in the simulation. *Hypothesis: The high‑fidelity biological simulation will show a significantly more similar transition pattern than the LLM, despite neither having a known ground truth of consciousness.*

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can probabilistic consciousness attributions (like the Digital Consciousness Model) be properly calibrated in the absence of an independent empirical ground truth for artificial phenomenality?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly states that the Digital Consciousness Model aggregates expert judgments about indicators and theory plausibility, formalizing "structured expert disagreement" rather than measuring an empirical probability, and that "no independent ground truth of artificial phenomenality exists."
- **Why unresolved:** Without an objective standard to compare against, estimated probabilities cannot be validated as accurate; they reflect theoretical preference, not measurement.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a validated benchmark or independent criterion for artificial phenomenality, potentially through biohybrid systems where phenomenality is more plausible, allowing false/true positive rates for indicators to be estimated.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is consciousness necessarily dependent on substrate‑specific, multiscale biological dynamics (e.g., continuous‑discrete hybrid neuronal computation) that current digital AI architectures fundamentally cannot instantiate?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper cites Milinkovic & Aru (2026) and Seth (2025) to argue that biological computation differs in kind from digital AI—involving scale‑inseparable dynamics anchored in metabolic constraints—and that the problem may be that AI's "very mode of computation may be of the wrong type."
- **Why unresolved:** This is a contested ontological claim with no direct empirical test for artificial systems; it hinges on whether particular dynamic forms are essential rather than merely correlated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Construction and testing of artificial systems (e.g., advanced neuromorphic or biohybrid platforms) that replicate claimed essential biological dynamics, followed by indicator‑based assessments to see if they exhibit profiles distinct from conventional AI in ways predicted by the biological view.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Even if a theory‑derived indicator framework were fully stabilized and validated in biological systems, would its successful application to artificial systems establish phenomenality, or merely provide an "extraordinarily refined induction" from biological cases?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper's thought experiment argues that maximal success of the indicator program would yield only a "progressively finer functional cartography" of correlates in living systems, not a "decisive reason to conclude" that artificial reinstantiation produces experience—highlighting the residual explanatory gap.
- **Why unresolved:** This questions whether functional/structural isomorphism is sufficient for phenomenality or if a further, perhaps unbridgeable, condition exists; it is a conceptual challenge to the inference from correlation to causation in novel substrates.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A clear demonstration that modifying or replicating the indicator profile in a non‑biological system directly modulates or produces evidence of experience (e.g., via report from a biohybrid entity with verified biological sentience), which would strengthen the sufficiency claim.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What specific empirical protocols can enable cross‑domain calibration of consciousness indicators—testing whether they track phenomenality similarly in biological vs. increasingly biology‑inspired artificial systems?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper proposes comparing indicator‑based attributions across systems that "progressively approximate biological dynamics" to see if they show "increasing convergence" with established neural markers, as a step toward "cross‑domain calibration."
- **Why unresolved:** No such systematic comparative framework exists; indicators are validated only within biological paradigms, and their interpretation when applied to different substrates is entirely speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A coordinated research program applying a common set of indicators (e.g., perturbational complexity, integrated information measures) to a spectrum of systems: biological controls, neuromorphic chips, biohybrids, and pure digital AI, under matched tasks, to map divergence/convergence patterns.

## Limitations
- Major uncertainties: (1) Theoretical fragmentation in consciousness science prevents consensus on which indicators are valid, making aggregated probabilities reflect expert disagreement rather than calibrated estimates. (2) No empirical data on indicator false positive/negative rates across substrates; biological validation does not transfer to artificial systems. (3) No independent ground truth for artificial phenomenality—all claims remain theory‑laden and unanchored.

## Confidence
- Theoretical fragmentation undermines calibration: **Medium**
- Calibration deficit for non‑biological systems: **Medium**
- Absence of ground truth: **Medium**
