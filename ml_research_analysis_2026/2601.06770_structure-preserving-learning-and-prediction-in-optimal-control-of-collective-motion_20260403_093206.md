# Structure-preserving learning and prediction in optimal control of collective motion

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06770
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06770
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** CO-LPNets accurately reproduce trajectories over hundreds of time steps with machine-precision Casimir conservation and bounded energy errors, using approximately 200 data points per dimension and around 1000 parameters.

## Executive Summary
Predicting collective motion of autonomous vehicles (e.g., drones) from observational data without prior knowledge of the underlying Hamiltonian or interaction details is a significant challenge. This paper introduces Control Optimal Lie-Poisson Neural Networks (CO-LPNets), a machine learning framework that learns phase-space dynamics by composing Poisson maps derived from analytically integrable test Hamiltonians. Operating directly in the reduced momentum space, CO-LPNets preserve the Poisson bracket and Casimir invariants by construction, ensuring physically consistent long-term behavior.

The method is evaluated on three-particle systems evolving on SO(3) (rotating bodies) and SE(3) (drones). Using only about 200 data points per dimension and a modest network size (~1000 parameters), CO-LPNets achieve accurate trajectory predictions over hundreds of steps, with Casimir invariants conserved to machine precision and energy errors remaining bounded. These results demonstrate the data efficiency and structure-preserving capabilities of CO-LPNets for learning optimal control of collective motion.

## Method Summary
CO-LPNets approximate the reduced Lie-Poisson dynamics of a multi-particle system by stacking a predetermined number *K* of Poisson-preserving transformations. Each transformation corresponds to an analytically integrable test Hamiltonian (e.g., linear, quadratic) and yields a closed-form map that exactly respects the Poisson bracket. The composition of such maps is trained to reproduce observed trajectories, with learnable parameters selecting appropriate test Hamiltonians. By working in the co-state (momentum) representation, the network avoids the need for explicit group elements and enforces Casimir invariants automatically. Training minimizes a loss on the predicted momentum sequence to match the observed data.

## Key Results
- Casimir invariants are conserved to machine precision across all tested configurations.
- Energy errors remain bounded over hundreds of time steps, preventing blow-up.
- The approach requires only ~200 data points per dimension and ~1000 parameters to achieve accurate long-term prediction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism lies in constructing the dynamics from exact Poisson maps. Each test Hamiltonian is chosen to be analytically integrable, yielding a symplectic (or Poisson) integrator in closed form. Because Poisson maps are closed under composition, the overall learned map remains Poisson regardless of the coefficients of the test Hamiltonians. Consequently, Casimir invariants are preserved exactly by the architecture, independent of the training objective. The test Hamiltonians span the Lie algebra sufficiently to approximate any smooth Hamiltonian vector field; by adjusting their linear combination through learning, the composed map can closely mimic the true dynamics. The use of momentum space simplifies the Poisson structure and reduces the number of variables relative to a full phase-space description.

## Foundational Learning
### Lie-Poisson brackets
- **Why needed:** The system evolves on a Lie-Poisson manifold; preserving the bracket guarantees that trajectories follow physically admissible paths and that inherent symmetries are respected.
- **Quick check:** Sample points in the momentum space and verify that the learned Poisson tensor satisfies the Jacobi identity up to numerical tolerance.

### Casimir invariants
- **Why needed:** Casimirs are conserved solely due to the Poisson structure; their exact preservation signals that the learned dynamics remains on the correct leaf of the manifold.
- **Quick check:** Compute the variation of known Casimir functions (e.g., squared momentum norms) along predicted trajectories; it should be zero.

### Poisson transformations
- **Why needed:** Each building block of CO-LPNet is a map that preserves the Poisson bracket. Their composition retains this property, providing structure preservation by design.
- **Quick check:** For a single transformation, apply it to random momentum vectors and check that the forward and backward pullbacks of the Poisson bracket coincide.

### Momentum (co-state) representation
- **Why needed:** Lie-Poisson equations are naturally expressed in the dual of the Lie algebra (momentum). This reduces dimensionality compared to the full group representation and simplifies the Poisson structure.
- **Quick check:** Ensure that all inputs and outputs of the network lie in the expected space (e.g., ℝⁿ for so(3) or se(3)).

### Analytically integrable test Hamiltonians
- **Why needed:** They provide explicit Poisson maps, eliminating numerical integration errors and allowing exact enforcement of the Poisson property.
- **Quick check:** For each test Hamiltonian (e.g., linear, quadratic), derive the map and test that it preserves a simple Poisson bracket on a few points.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input µ ∈ 𝔤* → Transformation 1 (using test Hamiltonian H₁) → µ₁ → Transformation 2 (using H₂) → … → Transformation K (using H_K) → Output µ' (next time step)  

**Critical path:**  
During a forward pass, the initial momentum is processed sequentially through K Poisson transformations to produce the next momentum. During training, gradients flow backward through all layers to update the coefficients of the test Hamiltonians.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Number of transformations (K):** Larger K increases expressive power and approximation accuracy but adds parameters and may lead to overfitting.  
- **Test Hamiltonian family:** The choice (e.g., linear, quadratic, higher-order) determines the richness of admissible maps; simpler families are easier to integrate but might limit expressivity.  
- **Structure vs. flexibility:** The architecture guarantees Poisson preservation but relies on learning the right combination of maps; there is a trade-off between strict physics compliance and fitting complex dynamics.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Casimir drift exceeding small thresholds (e.g., >1e-6).  
- Energy (Hamiltonian) growing without bound during rollouts.  
- Validation prediction error rising sharply after a few steps, indicating poor generalization.  

**First experiments:**  
1. **Scaling study:** Train CO-LPNet on the 3‑particle SE(3) system with K = 1, 3, 5, 10; compare Casimir conservation and trajectory MSE over 500 steps.  
2. **Noise robustness:** Add 5% Gaussian noise to the training observations; evaluate performance degradation in terms of invariants and prediction error.  
3. **Baseline comparison:** Implement a Hamiltonian Neural Network (HNN) and a plain MLP with similar parameter counts; compare long‑term stability and sample complexity on the same test data.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can CO-LPNets be directly extended to Lie-Poisson systems on SU(2) for quantum control applications?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "In the future, we would like to extend the current progress to the control of interacting particles on SU(2) group, essential for quantum computing."
- **Why unresolved:** SU(2)'s Lie algebra and exponential map differ from SO(3); the integrability of test Hamiltonians and Poisson transformation formulas may require adaptation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Deriving analytical solutions for test Hamiltonians on su(2), implementing CO-LPNets for a multi-particle quantum system, and verifying structure preservation and accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the CO-LPNet framework generalize to mixed Lie group products where particles evolve on different groups (e.g., SE(3) and SE(2)) with subgroup relationships?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Another problem is the interaction of several particles evolving on different groups... it would be interesting if our machine learning methods extend to these more general... cases" referencing SE(3)×SE(2).
- **Why unresolved:** The current Poisson tensor assumes identical groups with block-diagonal structure; mixed groups would require combining different Lie algebras and handling their interaction within the Poisson bivector and test Hamiltonian design.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formulating the Lie-Poisson reduction for a two-particle system with groups G1⊆G2, constructing appropriate Poisson transformations, and demonstrating learning from data.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How robust are CO-LPNets to scenarios where co-states µ are not directly observable, only partial state information is available?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] "The ground truth data obtained... involves information about the co-states of the system µ. Such information is not readily available from the data collected by an external observer." (Remark 3.1)
- **Why unresolved:** The method operates directly in reduced momentum space; without observed µ, training would require estimation or reformulation to use only observable quantities, potentially degrading accuracy.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulating partially observable data (e.g., only group elements g(t) while µ is hidden), attempting to train CO-LPNets either with estimated µ or by modifying the architecture to handle latent variables, and comparing prediction errors.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What governs long-term energy error growth in CO-LPNets, and can the number of transformations K be chosen to bound energy drift over a desired horizon?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Paper notes "energy errors... bounded" but not conserved; also discusses using many transformations but doesn't analyze energy stability vs K.
- **Why unresolved:** The method does not enforce energy conservation; energy stability emerges from the learned composition. The trade-off between approximation accuracy (increasing with K) and energy drift is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Varying K in experiments across different systems, measuring maximum energy deviation over fixed long times, and possibly deriving analytic bounds based on properties of the Poisson maps.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Is the O(Nn) scaling of Poisson transformations (with N particles and Lie algebra dimension n) necessary for accurate predictions, or can sparser compositions suffice?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The completeness arguments suggest potentially exponential scaling, but practical implementation uses only K=Nn (linear in Nn). The paper claims efficiency but doesn't study the relation between K and accuracy.
- **Why unresolved:** Using fewer transformations reduces parameters and computational cost but may harm fidelity; the minimal K needed for a given accuracy threshold is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically varying K from sub-linear to Nn and measuring prediction error on held-out data for various N and n, to determine empirical scaling relations and saturation points.

## Limitations
- Scalability is unproven beyond three-particle systems; larger collectives may degrade accuracy or increase computational cost unpredictably.
- Real-world robustness remains uncertain, as experiments likely used clean simulated data without measurement noise, missing observations, or unmodeled disturbances.
- Comparative data efficiency and performance against alternative physics-informed or structure-preserving machine learning methods have not been established.
