# On the Superlinear Relationship between SGD Noise Covariance and Loss Landscape Curvature

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05600  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05600  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: SGD noise covariance C is proportional to the second moment of per‑sample Hessians, leading to a power‑law scaling C_ii ∝ H_ii^γ with 1≤γ≤2; empirically γ>1 for cross‑entropy (~1.4) and γ≈1 for mean‑squared error.

## Executive Summary
The paper challenges the common assumption that SGD noise covariance scales proportionally with the Hessian (C ∝ H). Using Activity‑Weight Duality (AWD), it derives a new general relationship: C is proportional to the second moment of per‑sample Hessians, E[h_p²], rather than the first moment H = E[h_p]. This yields a power‑law scaling of diagonal elements C_ii ∝ H_ii^γ, where the exponent 1≤γ≤2 is theoretically bounded by the per‑sample curvature spectra. Empirically, across datasets, architectures, and loss functions, cross‑entropy loss exhibits superlinear scaling (γ ≈ 1.4) while mean‑squared error shows near‑linear scaling (γ ≈ 1). A suppression experiment ties the superlinear behavior to a correlation between large per‑sample curvature eigenvalues and their alignment with global Hessian directions. These findings suggest that SGD noise adaptively amplifies curvature in directions with high outlier eigenvalues, with implications for optimization and preconditioning.

## Method Summary
The authors combine theoretical derivation and empirical validation. Theoretically, AWD maps mini‑batch activity fluctuations to weight perturbations, showing that near a local minimum with vanishing gradients, the gradient noise covariance reduces to C ∝ E[h_p²]. For diagonals in the eigenbasis of the global Hessian H, this implies a power‑law C_ii ∝ H_ii^γ. Empirically, they train multilayer perceptrons and convolutional networks on MNIST and CIFAR‑10 using cross‑entropy (CE) and mean‑squared error (MSE) losses. After convergence, they compute the full Hessian H = (1/N)∑_i ∇²ℓ_i and the empirical noise covariance C ≈ (1/(BN))∑_i ∇ℓ_i∇ℓ_iᵀ (ignoring the mean gradient). Focusing on a specific weight layer (the last hidden‑to‑output matrix), they obtain the top N eigenvectors of H, project C onto this basis to get C_ii, and fit γ by log‑log linear regression. The experiments confirm 1≤γ≤2 in all cases, with γ>1 for CE and γ≈1 for MSE. Additional tests include an AWD approximation check and a suppression experiment to probe the eigenvalue‑alignment correlation.

## Key Results
- SGD noise covariance is proportional to the second moment of per‑sample Hessians (E[h_p²]), not the first moment.
- Diagonal elements follow an approximate power‑law C_ii ∝ H_ii^γ with theoretically bounded exponent 1≤γ≤2.
- Empirically, γ>1 for cross‑entropy loss (≈1.4) while γ≈1 for mean‑squared error; the difference is caused by a correlation between large per‑sample curvature eigenvalues and their alignment with global Hessian directions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1 – C ∝ E[h_p²]**: Activity‑Weight Duality maps mini‑batch‑induced activity fluctuations to equivalent weight perturbations. Under the assumptions of vanishing gradients near a local minimum and isotropic weight perturbations (M_p ≈ σ_w² I), the gradient difference between batches is approximated by h_p Δw. Averaging over batches yields C ∝ E[h_p²] (Theorem 3.4). This avoids reliance on unrealistic Fisher Information approximations.

**Mechanism 2 – Power‑law scaling (1 ≤ γ ≤ 2)**: The global Hessian H is the first moment of projected per‑sample curvatures, while C involves the second moment. For heavy‑tailed per‑sample spectra and bounded eigenvalues, Jensen’s inequality and the Cauchy‑Schwarz bound combine to give γ∈[1,2] (Section 5.1). Superlinear scaling (γ>1) arises when the curvature magnitudes vary significantly across samples, increasing the second moment relative to the first.

**Mechanism 3 – Loss‑dependent γ (CE vs. MSE)**: Cross‑entropy exhibits a positive correlation between the magnitude of the dominant per‑sample Hessian eigenvalue (κ₁⁽ᵖ⁾) and its squared projection onto global Hessian eigenvectors (u₁⁽ᵖ⁾·v_i)². This correlation boosts the second moment, giving γ>1. Mean‑squared error shows independence, leading to γ≈1. A suppression experiment—replacing the first eigenvalue of each per‑sample Hessian with its mean—drives γ→1 for CE but not for MSE, confirming the mechanism (Section 5.3).

## Foundational Learning
- **Activity‑Weight Duality (AWD)**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a model‑agnostic framework to derive SGD noise covariance without relying on Fisher information approximations, enabling the second‑moment relationship.  
  *Quick check*: How does AWD map activity perturbations to weight perturbations, and why is this invariant to loss function?

- **Per‑sample Hessian (h_p)**  
  *Why needed*: Central to defining the first and second moments that distinguish H and C; its spectral properties determine scaling exponent γ.  
  *Quick check*: What is the difference between the global Hessian H and a per‑sample Hessian h_p, and how do their spectra differ?

- **Power‑law scaling in heavy‑tailed spectra**  
  *Why needed*: Explains why C and H exhibit superlinear relationships due to outlier eigenvalues and alignment effects.  
  *Quick check*: Why does a heavy‑tailed spectrum lead to C_ii ∝ H_ii^γ with γ>1 under alignment?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Training data & mini‑batches → AWD module → Per‑sample Hessian calculator → Spectral decomposer → Output (C, γ)

**Critical path**  
1. Sample mini‑batches and compute matched pairs.  
2. Apply AWD to obtain Δw^μ_ν.  
3. Approximate gradient noise as h_p Δw^μ_ν.  
4. Decompose h_p spectrally.  
5. Project onto global Hessian basis.  
6. Average to obtain C_ii.  
7. Fit power‑law to estimate γ.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- AWD assumes small perturbations and vanishing gradients; using full per‑sample Hessians is computationally heavy, necessitating trade‑offs like approximating with dominant eigenvalues.
- The isotropic weight perturbation assumption (M_p ≈ σ_w² I) simplifies derivation but may not hold for all architectures, potentially biasing results.

**Failure signatures**  
- Large gradient norms relative to curvature terms violate Lemma 3.3.
- Non‑heavy‑tailed per‑sample spectra (eigenvalues similar) leads to γ ≈ 1 universally.
- Poor alignment between local and global eigenbases (high off‑diagonal in R) breaks commutativity.
- Non‑convex regions where per‑sample Hessians are not PSD invalidate the theoretical bounds.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Empirical validation of the power‑law**: Train MLP/CNN on MNIST/CIFAR‑10 with CE and MSE; compute C and H from top eigenvalues; fit γ and verify 1 ≤ γ ≤ 2 and γ_CE > 1 > γ_MSE.  
2. **AWD approximation test**: Compare empirical C with AWD‑derived C (Eq. 15) across training stages; assess convergence near minima (Figures 4‑6).  
3. **Suppression experiment**: Modify per‑sample Hessians by suppressing non‑dominant eigenvalues and homogenizing dominant ones; measure resulting changes in C_ii and H_ii to isolate correlation effect (Figures 13‑15).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Mechanism behind loss‑dependent correlation**: What theoretical cause makes the correlation between large per‑sample Hessian eigenvalues and their global alignment differ between cross‑entropy and mean‑squared error losses? The paper empirically links this correlation to γ but does not derive it (Section 5.3).
2. **Generality of bounds 1 ≤ γ ≤ 2**: Do these bounds hold outside the assumed local convexity and positive semi‑definite per‑sample Hessians? The proof in Theorem 5.1 uses these assumptions, but Remark 5.2 notes γ ∈ [1,2] emerges earlier; generalizing remains open.
3. **Persistence in Transformer architectures**: Does the superlinear noise–curvature relationship hold for Transformers? The paper expects it to (Section 6) but provides no empirical validation; it’s unclear how self‑attention and layer normalization affect the underlying assumptions.

## Limitations
- Theoretical derivations rely on strong assumptions (vanishing gradients, isotropic weight perturbations, bounded spectra, approximate alignment), which may not hold during early training or in highly non‑convex regions.
- Empirical validation requires full‑dataset Hessians and per‑sample Hessians, which are computationally prohibitive; the paper does not detail scalable approximations, raising concerns about practicality and potential truncation bias.
- The suppression experiment lacks full specification (e.g., exact manipulation details across seeds), weakening the causal inference linking eigenvalue‑alignment correlation to superlinear scaling.

## Confidence
- **C ∝ E[h²] (AWD relationship)**: Medium  
- **Theoretical bounds (1 ≤ γ ≤ 2)**: High  
- **Empirical γ values (CE > 1, MSE ≈ 1)**: Medium  
- **Correlation mechanism (superlinear scaling due to eigenvalue‑alignment)**: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Direct correlation measurement**: For a trained model, compute per‑sample dominant eigenvalues κ₁⁽ᵖ⁾ and their squared projections onto global Hessian eigenvectors (u₁⁽ᵖ⁾·v_i)²; calculate Pearson correlation across samples to test the proposed link.
2. **Generalization across architectures and losses**: Repeat the power‑law analysis on ResNet‑18 and with losses like Huber and logistic to determine whether γ>1 is specific to cross‑entropy or more widespread.
3. **Truncation sensitivity**: Vary the number N of top eigenvalues used to approximate H and C, and plot γ versus N. A stable γ across a reasonable range confirms robustness; large swings indicate artifacts of spectral truncation.
