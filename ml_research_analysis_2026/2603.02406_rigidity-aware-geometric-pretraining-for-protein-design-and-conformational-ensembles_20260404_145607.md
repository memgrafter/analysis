# Rigidity-Aware Geometric Pretraining for Protein Design and Conformational Ensembles

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.02406
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02406
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** A two-phase geometric pretraining framework (RigidSSL) that front-loads geometry learning, boosting protein designability by up to 43% and improving biophysical realism in conformational ensemble generation.

## Executive Summary
RigidSSL addresses fundamental limitations in protein generative modeling—joint geometry-design optimization, reliance on local rigid representations, and inability to capture conformational dynamics—through a two-phase pretraining strategy. Phase I (RigidSSL-Perturb) learns a robust SE(3)-invariant geometric prior from 432K static AFDB structures using simulated perturbations. Phase II (RigidSSL-MD) refines this representation on 1.3K MD trajectories to capture biophysically realistic motions via a bi-directional rigidity-aware flow matching objective. When transferred to downstream models (FrameDiff, FoldFlow-2), RigidSSL significantly enhances designability, novelty, and diversity for unconditional generation and zero-shot motif scaffolding. For generating GPCR conformational ensembles, RigidSSL-MD achieves state-of-the-art biophysical fidelity on most metrics, demonstrating the value of learning from dynamic data.

## Method Summary
RigidSSL represents each residue as a rigid body (SE(3) frame) defined by its N-Cα-C atoms. All structures are canonicalized into a global inertial frame via center-of-mass alignment and principal axis rotation. Pretraining employs a bi-directional flow matching objective: given two views of the same protein (either synthetically perturbed or from an MD trajectory), the model learns to predict the velocity field that transports one to the other along a straight (LERP) or spherical (SLERP) interpolation path. Phase I applies small SE(3) perturbations (Gaussian translation σ=0.03, IG SO(3) rotation ϵ=0.5) to static AFDB structures. Phase II samples consecutive frames (δ=2ns) from MD trajectories. The encoder is an Invariant Point Attention (IPA) network. After pretraining, the IPA weights are loaded into a downstream generative model (e.g., FrameDiff) and finetuned on PDB structures for backbone generation.

## Key Results
*   RigidSSL boosts designability (fraction of proteins with scRMSD≤2.0Å) by up to 43% in unconditional generation and improves zero-shot motif scaffolding success rate by 5.8%.
*   RigidSSL-MD achieves best biophysical realism on 7/9 metrics for GPCR conformational ensemble generation, including contact Jaccard and cryptically exposed residues Jaccard.
*   RigidSSL-Perturb enables high-fidelity generation of ultra-long chains (700-800 residues) with the lowest Clashscore and MolProbity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Front-loading Geometry Decouples Learning
- **Claim:** Pretraining a generalizable SE(3)-invariant geometric prior before downstream generation improves model generalization and efficiency.
- **Mechanism:** RigidSSL first instills fundamental protein geometry through a self-supervised objective on massive static data (AFDB). This decouples the learning of broad geometric regularities from the later refinement of generation dynamics or flexibility, allowing downstream models to focus on the generative task.
- **Core assumption:** A transferable representation of protein geometry, learned independent of the final generative objective, provides a superior starting point.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract, section 1] Motivation to "front-load geometric understanding"; [section 3] Description of two-phase rigidity-based pretraining.
- **Break condition:** The pretrained geometric prior fails to transfer effectively to the downstream task, resulting in no gain over end-to-end training.

### Mechanism 2: Rigid Body Abstraction Captures Fold-level Geometry
- **Claim:** Modeling residues as rigid SE(3) bodies provides a globally coherent, efficient representation superior to local, non-rigid atomic fragments for learning fold-level geometry.
- **Mechanism:** Fixed bond lengths/angles reduce degrees of freedom. The entire backbone becomes a sequence of SE(3) transformations. This enforces physical plausibility, enables canonicalization into a consistent global frame, and allows efficient, globally consistent interpolation (LERP/SLERP).
- **Core assumption:** Essential fold geometry is adequately captured by the collective poses of rigid backbones, making this abstraction sufficient and superior for generation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Critique of "local, non-rigid atomic representations"; [section 2] Formal definition of residue as rigid body; [section 3.1] Canonicalization process.
- **Break condition:** Critical generative or functional properties depend on non-rigid sidechain or fragment flexibility not captured by the rigid model.

### Mechanism 3: Two-phase Pretraining Instills Stability and Dynamics
- **Claim:** Phase I (Perturb) builds a robust geometric prior from static structures, while Phase II (MD) refines it with realistic dynamics, addressing the static bias of structure databases.
- **Mechanism:** Phase I uses synthetic SE(3) perturbations on AFDB structures to teach reconstruction of geometrically plausible, near-native variations, building noise-invariant stability. Phase II uses MD trajectories to teach the *directions* of biophysically allowed motions and state transitions. The bi-directional flow matching objective maximizes mutual information between conformations, forcing the latent space to disentangle invariant structure from allowed fluctuations.
- **Core assumption:** Mutual information between different conformative views (synthetic or dynamic) is a valid signal for learning a latent space that captures both core structure and permissible variation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Description of two phases; [section 3.2] View construction details; [section 4.3] RigidSSL-MD's success on 7/9 GPCR ensemble metrics.
- **Break condition:** The flow matching objective fails to create a meaningful latent space (e.g., memorization), or the MD distribution is too narrow/biased for design tasks, causing negative transfer.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Mutual Information Maximization via Flow Matching**
    *   **Why needed:** The core pretraining objective is to make latent representations of two protein views maximally informative of each other. Flow matching provides a tractable training objective (minimizing velocity error) that serves as a surrogate for maximizing likelihood, linked to mutual information.
    *   **Quick check:** How does the bi-directional loss (Eq 11) prevent the model from simply memorizing specific training pairs and instead enforce a general mapping?
*   **Concept: SE(3)-Equivariance and Rigid Body Representations**
    *   **Why needed:** Protein structures are invariant to global rotation/translation. The IPA encoder and flow matching objective must respect this symmetry. Residue-level rigid bodies restrict predictions to physically valid SE(3) transformations.
    *   **Quick check:** Why are absolute 3D coordinates insufficient, and how do relative SE(3) transformations within a canonical frame achieve invariance?
*   **Concept: Reference Frame Canonicalization**
    *   **Why needed:** To ensure interpolation paths (LERP/SLERP) and the flow matching objective operate in a consistent coordinate system across all samples. Without it, the model must additionally handle arbitrary global orientations.
    *   **Quick check:** Describe the two-step process (center-of-mass translation, principal axes rotation). What breaks if this step is skipped for the bi-directional objective?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Input (PDB/AFDB coordinates) -> Preprocessor (constructs canonicalized residue frames) -> View Constructor (Phase I: Perturb; Phase II: extract MD pair) -> Encoder (IPA with time conditioning) -> Decoder/Head (predicts translation/quaternion) -> Loss Calculator (LERP/SLERP targets vs. prediction, bidirectional sum). Downstream: Pretrained IPA weights loaded into FrameDiff/FoldFlow-2 -> Finetuned on PDB -> Generation.
*   **Critical Path:** Canonicalization -> View construction -> IPA encoding/decoding -> Flow matching loss -> Parameter update (sequential for Phase I then II).
*   **Design Tradeoffs:**
    *   **Rigid vs. Flexible:** Rigid-body abstraction simplifies learning and ensures validity but may limit modeling of local non-rigid effects (sidechains, loops).
    *   **Perturb vs. MD:** Phase I boosts designability/foldability but may suppress diversity. Phase II boosts diversity/biophysical fidelity for ensembles but may slightly reduce designability and risks negative transfer from MD force field bias.
    *   **Data Scale vs. Cost:** Phase I uses 432K cheap static structures. Phase II uses 1.3K expensive MD trajectories. Tradeoff is broad coverage vs. deep dynamic insight.
*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   High Clashscore/Low MolProbity: Invalid sterics from corrupted interpolation or flow matching. Check Phase I noise scales (σ, ϵ).
    *   Low Designability (scRMSD>2.0Å): Generated structures not self-consistent. Could be due to insufficient Phase I noise, Phase II overfitting, or limited IPA capacity.
    *   Low Diversity/High Novelty: Latent space collapse from overly dominant MI objective. Check if flow matching loss is causing representation convergence.
*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  Validate view construction: Sample AFDB protein, apply Perturb_sigma,epsilon (σ=0.03, ϵ=0.5). Visualize. Verify Cα RMSD ~1-2 Å, preserved bond geometry, plausible thermal fluctuation appearance.
    2.  Sanity check flow matching: For a single (g₀,g₁) pair, run forward pass. Plot model-predicted intermediates (x_τ) against LERP/SLERP targets across τ∈[0,1]. Trajectory should smoothly track targets; deviation >0.5 Å indicates failure.
    3.  Inspect pretrained embeddings: After Phase I, extract final node embeddings for held-out proteins. Reduce (t-SNE/UMAP). Check if similar fold types (e.g., TIM barrels) cluster. Unstructured space indicates failed MI objective.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How do complementary strengths of RigidSSL-Perturb (geometric quality) and RigidSSL-MD (diversity/biophysical fidelity) interact across tasks, and can a unified approach retain both?
*   **Open Question 2:** To what extent do force field biases in MD pretraining propagate to downstream quality, and can reweighting/data selection mitigate this?
*   **Open Question 3:** What are the limits of ultra-long chain generation with RigidSSL, and do architectural modifications become necessary beyond ~800 residues?
*   **Open Question 4:** What target features predict when RigidSSL-Perturb vs. RigidSSL-MD will succeed on motif-scaffolding tasks?
*   **Open Question 5:** Why do RigidSSL models exhibit very low Spearman correlation (ρ≈0.03) for exposed residue mutual information despite improving other ensemble metrics?

## Limitations
*   Exact data splits (432K AFDB, 1.3K ATLAS) and downstream finetuning hyperparameters are unreported, blocking full replication.
*   Evaluation scripts for key GPCR ensemble metrics (e.g., exposure mutual information) are not described, risking inconsistent benchmarking.
*   The trade-off between designability (from Perturb) and dynamic fidelity (from MD) is noted but not systematically ablated; potential negative transfer from Phase II remains a concern.

## Confidence
*   **Mechanism 1 (front-loading geometry):** Medium. Conceptually supported, but empirical gains depend on unverified downstream hyperparameters and transfer efficiency.
*   **Mechanism 2 (rigid body representation):** High. Precisely defined SE(3) formulation is computationally principled and less vulnerable to implementation variance.
*   **Mechanism 3 (two-phase pretraining):** Medium. Benefits hinge on MD data quality and MI objective stability, with acknowledged break conditions.
