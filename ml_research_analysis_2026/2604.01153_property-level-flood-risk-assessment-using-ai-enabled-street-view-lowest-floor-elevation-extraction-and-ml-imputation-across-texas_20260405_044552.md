# Property-Level Flood Risk Assessment Using AI-Enabled Street-View Lowest Floor Elevation Extraction and ML Imputation Across Texas

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01153  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01153  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** AI extracts lowest floor elevation from street-view with 49% success, ML imputation fills missing data (cross‑validated R² up to 0.974), and integration with flood models yields parcel‑level damage estimates for 12,241 Texas homes.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the critical lack of building‑specific lowest floor elevation (LFE) data needed for accurate flood damage estimation. It develops and tests a three‑stage pipeline across 12,241 residential structures in 18 Texas areas: (1) AI extracts LFE and height‑difference from street‑grade (HDSL) using Google Street View imagery, (2) machine learning imputes missing HDSL values using 16 spatial and terrain features, and (3) integrates results with flood hazard models to estimate interior flood depth and property loss. Direct extraction succeeded for 49.0% of structures (5,992), with imagery available for 73.4%. Performance‑gated imputation was applied in 13 of 18 areas, achieving cross‑validated R² values from 0.159 to 0.974; five areas were explicitly excluded due to insufficient model performance. The workflow demonstrates a scalable pathway to generate parcel‑level flood risk metrics where elevation certificates are absent, moving assessments beyond hazard exposure to structure‑specific damage potential.

## Method Summary
The authors implement a three‑stage workflow. First, a computer vision model processes Google Street View imagery to detect building facades and automatically extract the lowest floor elevation (LFE) and the height difference from street grade (HDSL). Extraction relies on identifying reference points such as doors, floors, and grade‑level markers. Second, for structures where extraction fails (due to occlusions, poor angles, or missing imagery), a machine learning model imputes HDSL using 16 spatial and terrain features derived from GIS data (e.g., DEM, slope, distance to water). The imputation model is trained separately on each of the 18 study areas and only applied where cross‑validated performance exceeds a threshold; five areas were excluded. Third, the imputed or extracted HDSL is combined with building‑specific first‑floor heights to compute interior flood depths for various flood return periods, using existing flood hazard surfaces. Depth‑damage functions from USACE Gulf Coast single‑family homes then estimate economic losses. The whole pipeline aims to produce property‑level flood risk metrics at scale.

## Key Results
- Direct extraction of LFE/HDSL succeeded for 49.0% of the 12,241 structures (5,992 buildings), with street‑view imagery available for 73.4% of the total.
- Machine learning imputation was applied in 13 of the 18 areas, achieving cross‑validated R² values between 0.159 and 0.974; five areas were excluded due to insufficient model performance.
- The integrated workflow produced parcel‑level estimates of interior flood depth and property damage, demonstrating a scalable pathway for flood risk assessment without relying on sparse elevation certificates.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Elev‑Vision AI model uses computer vision to detect building features (e.g., doors, windows, foundation) from Street View imagery and compute elevation relative to estimated street grade. For cases where imagery is insufficient, a machine‑learning imputation model predicts HDSL from 16 spatial and terrain features, trained separately per AOI to capture local patterns. The predicted or extracted HDSL is then combined with building first‑floor height to obtain LFE; this elevation is overlaid with flood hazard surfaces (e.g., Fathom) to derive interior flood depths for various return periods. Finally, USACE depth‑damage functions translate depths into economic losses. The cascade compensates for the limitations of each step: imputation fills gaps left by AI extraction, and hazard integration converts static elevations into dynamic risk metrics.

## Foundational Learning
**Elev‑Vision AI Extraction**  
*Why needed:* Automates measurement of building lowest floor elevation from publicly available street imagery, eliminating costly field surveys.  
*Quick check:* On a validation set of 100 buildings with known LFE, achieve mean absolute error < 0.3 m.

**Spatial Feature Engineering for Imputation**  
*Why needed:* Provides predictors that correlate with building elevation (e.g., DEM, slope, hydrography) to impute missing HDSL values.  
*Quick check:* In a training area, selected features should exhibit correlation coefficient > 0.4 with observed HDSL.

**Machine Learning Imputation**  
*Why needed:* Predicts HDSL for structures lacking AI extraction, ensuring nearly complete coverage of the building inventory.  
*Quick check:* Perform 5‑fold cross‑validation; target R² > 0.6 in high‑performing areas; if below, investigate.

**Flood Depth‑Damage Modeling**  
*Why needed:* Converts physical flood depth into economic loss estimates, enabling risk quantification for decision‑making.  
*Quick check:* Validate depth‑damage curve using a sample of historical claims; typical loss ratios should fall within expected ranges (e.g., 20‑80% of market value for 1‑ft depth).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Street View Imagery -> Elev‑Vision AI -> Raw HDSL -> ML Imputation (spatial features) -> Imputed HDSL -> Flood Hazard Model -> Flood Depth -> DDF -> Property Loss

**Critical Path**  
The pipeline’s critical path runs from imagery acquisition through AI extraction to imputation (where needed), then to flood depth calculation and damage estimation. Imputation determines whether an area can be included; the performance gate (R² threshold) is a hard dependency.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Generalization vs. specificity*: Using a single global AI model may reduce accuracy compared to area‑specific fine‑tuning, but improves scalability.  
- *Imputation complexity*: Including 16 features increases predictive power but raises data acquisition costs and risk of overfitting; simpler models may generalize better across areas.  
- *Coverage threshold*: Strict performance gates (e.g., high R² requirement) ensure reliability but discard many structures; a lower threshold yields broader coverage at the cost of increased error.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Low extraction yield (<40%) in an area, indicating poor imagery quality or architectural incompatibility.  
- Imputation R² below 0.4 and high residual bias, suggesting terrain heterogeneity or insufficient predictor variables.  
- Mismatched flood model resolution leading to unrealistic depth estimates (e.g., flat terrain where model shows high variation).

**First Three Experiments**  
1. **Extraction Accuracy Benchmark**: Run the AI extraction on a manually annotated sample of 200 buildings with known LFE (from elevation certificates). Report MAE and pixel‑level errors.  
2. **Imputation Transfer Test**: Train imputation models in two high‑performing areas and apply them to a low‑performing area without retraining. Compare R² to locally trained models to assess transferability.  
3. **Damage Validation**: For a recent flood event (e.g., Hurricane Harvey), match modeled property damages to NFIP claim amounts for a subset of insured homes. Compute correlation and mean absolute percentage error.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the Elev-Vision framework maintain its accuracy and extraction yield when applied to regions with different architectural styles, vegetation density, or Google Street View network coverage outside of Texas?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The study was restricted to 18 Texas AOIs; generalizability to other U.S. regions or countries with different housing stocks and imagery availability remains untested.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates regional deployment within Texas but does not evaluate cross-regional transferability, which is essential for national-scale flood risk assessment.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies in diverse geographic areas (e.g., Northeast, Midwest, coastal vs. inland); analysis of how feature importance (e.g., door detection) shifts across regions with varying facade designs and GSV capture angles.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What specific environmental, spatial, or data-structural factors cause machine learning imputation of HDSL to fail in certain AOIs (e.g., Harris County Pocket 1 with R²_CV = 0.159), and can we develop a predictive model of imputation feasibility before training?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Five AOIs were excluded due to insufficient imputation performance, with cross-validated R² ranging from 0.159 to 0.974 across qualifying AOIs; the paper does not diagnose the root causes of this variability.
- **Why unresolved:** Without understanding why imputation fails in some areas, practitioners cannot reliably anticipate where the pipeline will produce trustworthy estimates, limiting operational adoption.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of excluded vs. included AOIs using metrics like HDSL variance, terrain heterogeneity (e.g., DEM standard deviation), street-view coverage rate, and built-environment age diversity; development of a feasibility index that predicts imputation success from these covariates.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do uncertainties in LFE (from extraction and imputation errors), flood hazard surfaces (e.g., Fathom model inaccuracies), and depth-damage functions propagate to property-level damage estimates, and what are appropriate uncertainty quantification methods for such integrated pipelines?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The discussion acknowledges multiple sources of uncertainty (e.g., DDF applicability to non-standard structures, market value proxies) but presents damage estimates without confidence intervals or sensitivity analysis.
- **Why unresolved:** Damage estimates are used for mitigation prioritization and insurance decisions; without quantified uncertainty, stakeholders cannot assess the reliability of individual property or community-scale loss figures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Monte Carlo simulations propagating LFE error distributions (from extraction/imputation RMSE), flood depth uncertainty bounds, and DDF parameter ranges; comparison of damage estimates under alternative DDFs (e.g., FEMA HAZUS, structure-type-specific curves).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How accurately do the modeled interior flood depths and economic damages correlate with observed outcomes from actual flood events (e.g., NFIP claims or post-disaster damage surveys) in the studied Texas AOIs?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The pipeline is validated only through internal consistency checks (e.g., spatial clustering aligned with FIRMs) and not against real-world loss data, despite the practical goal of informing risk management.
- **Why unresolved:** Without external validation, the practical utility of the method for cost-benefit analysis, buyout prioritization, or insurance underwriting remains speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Geospatial matching of modeled damage estimates to NFIP claim amounts for properties in the 18 AOIs during historical floods (e.g., Hurricane Harvey); collaborative damage assessment studies with local emergency managers to collect ground-truthed inundation and loss data.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does incorporating building-type-specific HDSL distributions and depth-damage functions (e.g., for mobile homes, pier-and-beam, slab-on-grade) significantly improve regional loss estimation accuracy compared to the generic USACE curves applied uniformly?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study uses a single imputation model and USACE DDF calibrated for Gulf Coast single-family homes, assuming homogeneity in structural vulnerability across all AOIs, including rural areas with different housing stocks.
- **Why unresolved:** Mis-specification of damage functions for non-standard construction types likely biases loss estimates, particularly in excluded or rural AOIs where such structures are prevalent, undermining risk characterization in vulnerable communities.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Collection of building type labels from parcel data or street-view imagery; training separate imputation models and applying structure-specific DDFs (e.g., from FEMA HAZUS or empirical studies); quantifying reduction in estimation error when validated against observed claims by building type.

## Limitations
- Extraction yield is only 49% and 26.6% of structures lack any street-view imagery, leading to systematic coverage gaps, especially in rural areas.
- Imputation performance is highly variable (cross-validated R² 0.159–0.974) and fails entirely in 5 of 18 areas, limiting reliability without careful performance gating.
- The study is confined to Texas and has not been validated against actual flood damage claims, so its generalizability and real-world accuracy remain uncertain.
