# MESD: Detecting and Mitigating Procedural Bias in Intersectional Groups

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.13452  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13452  
- **Reference count:** 28  
- **Primary result:** MESD is the first metric to quantify explanation stability disparities across intersectional subgroups; the UEF framework balances utility, outcome fairness, and procedural fairness via multi‑objective optimization.

## Executive Summary
Procedural bias—differences in the stability of model explanations—remains an under‑explored fairness dimension, especially for intersectional groups defined by multiple protected attributes. This paper introduces MESD (Maximum Explanation Stability Disparity), a novel metric that aggregates stability differences across subgroups while emphasizing worst‑case outcomes. MESD leverages label‑aware aggregation, empirical‑Bayes shrinkage for small subgroups, and CVaR weighting to spotlight extreme bias. Coupled with MESD, the authors propose UEF (Utility‑Equity‑Fairness), a multi‑objective optimization framework based on NSGA‑II that jointly optimizes predictive performance (AUC/F1), outcome fairness (Demographic Parity Difference, Equalized Odds Difference), and procedural fairness (MESD). Evaluations on German Credit, Adult Income, and Recidivism datasets show UEF matching or surpassing three baselines (ADL, RW, RD) in all objectives on two datasets and remaining competitive on the third. The results demonstrate that MESD reveals biases invisible to outcome‑only metrics, and CVaR weighting mitigates fairness gerrymandering by surfacing disparities in small intersectional cells.

## Method Summary
The method targets detection and mitigation of procedural bias across intersectional groups. For a given model, explanations (from SHAP/LIME ensembles) are computed for each instance. Stability is measured by generating K perturbed variants of the instance (Gaussian noise and stochastic feature masking) and calculating the inverted Euclidean distance between the original and perturbed explanation vectors. These instance‑level stability scores are aggregated per subgroup and class: label‑aware averages are shrunk via empirical‑Bayes to stabilize estimates for small subgroups, then combined across labels using prevalence weights. Pairwise disparities between subgroups are distilled into a single CVaR‑weighted value, defining MESD (lower is better).

UEF integrates MESD as a third objective in a multi‑objective optimization loop. The model (a two‑hidden‑layer neural network) is parameterized by a small set of hyperparameters (threshold, L2 strength, learning rate, epochs, dropout). NSGA‑II evolves a population of configurations, each evaluated on three objectives: negative AUC (utility maximization), ΔDP and ΔEOD (outcome fairness), and MESD (procedural fairness). The resulting Pareto front captures the trade‑offs; a final model is selected using Chebyshev scalarization with respect to an ideal point.

## Key Results
- MESD captures stability differences missed by outcome fairness metrics, highlighting procedural bias in small intersectional groups.
- UEF consistently outperforms or matches the adaptive reweighting (ADL), random weighting (RW), and random drop (RD) baselines across three datasets, achieving a better balance among utility, outcome fairness, and procedural fairness.
- CVaR weighting reduces fairness gerrymandering by forcing the optimization to consider extreme subgroups rather than averaging away disparities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Procedural bias manifests as variability in explanations when inputs are perturbed; the metric MESD formalizes this as a disparity across intersectional subgroups. By using label‑aware aggregation, MESD avoids conflating stability patterns across different classes, which is crucial for multi‑class problems. Empirical‑Bayes shrinkage borrows strength from the broader population to stabilize estimates for small subgroups, preventing noisy spikes due to data scarcity. CVaR (Conditional Value at Risk) weighting focuses on the tail of the disparity distribution, ensuring that even rare but severe biases are penalized; this directly combats fairness gerrymandering where aggregating over subgroups hides extreme harms.

UEF’s multi‑objective NSGA‑II search explores a broad spectrum of the utility‑fairness trade‑off space. The Pareto front makes explicit the inherent conflicts: improving outcome fairness may worsen procedural fairness or utility. Chebyshev scalarization then picks a model close to the ideal (simultaneously best on all objectives) but respecting the trade‑offs. This design allows decision makers to choose a solution that aligns with their policy priorities, rather than fixing weights a priori. The use of a neural network with regularized hyperparameters provides flexibility to fit complex patterns while controlling overfitting.

## Foundational Learning
- **Procedural Bias**: Systematic differences in how explanations change under perturbations across groups. *Why needed*: Outcome fairness alone does not capture whether a model’s reasoning is consistently reliable for different demographic slices. *Quick check*: Compute stability scores for two subgroups; large disparity indicates procedural bias.
- **Explanation Stability**: Robustness of post‑hoc explanations (e.g., SHAP, LIME) to small input changes. *Why needed*: Unstable explanations erode trust and may hide discriminatory mechanisms. *Quick check*: Perturb an instance slightly and recompute explanations; high divergence signals instability.
- **Intersectional Fairness**: Fairness across combinations of multiple protected attributes (e.g., race and gender). *Why needed*: Single‑attribute fairness can mask biases affecting specific intersecting groups. *Quick check*: Split data by all combinations of protected attributes; evaluate metrics per group.
- **Multi‑Objective Optimization**: Simultaneous optimization of competing goals. *Why needed*: Utility, outcome fairness, and procedural fairness often conflict; a multi‑objective approach reveals the full trade‑off landscape. *Quick check*: Plot two objectives (e.g., AUC vs ΔDP) for a set of models; Pareto‑optimal points indicate no single solution dominates.
- **NSGA‑II**: A popular genetic algorithm for multi‑objective optimization. *Why needed*: It efficiently approximates the Pareto front using selection, crossover, and mutation while maintaining diversity. *Quick check*: Run NSGA‑II on a simple two‑objective problem (e.g., minimizing f1(x)=x, f2(x)=(x‑2)²); verify non‑dominated solutions.
- **CVaR (Conditional Value at Risk)**: Risk measure focusing on the average of the worst α‑fraction. *Why needed*: It penalizes extreme disparities more heavily than averaging, preventing gerrymandering. *Quick check*: Sort a list of disparities; CVaR_0.1 is the mean of the top 10% largest values.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Data → Preprocessing → Model Training → MESD Computation → NSGA‑II Optimization → Pareto Front → Final Model Selection  

**Critical Path**  
1. Load and preprocess data, encode protected attributes, split into train/validation/test sets.  
2. For each hyperparameter candidate in the NSGA‑II population: train the neural network on training data until early stopping on validation.  
3. Compute objectives: (a) negative validation AUC (or F1), (b) ΔDP and ΔEOD on validation set, (c) MESD on validation explanations.  
4. Feed objectives back to NSGA‑II to evolve the population.  
5. After termination, extract the Pareto front of non‑dominated hyperparameter configurations.  
6. Select the final model via Chebyshev scalarization (minimize max distance to ideal point) and evaluate on the test set.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Objective weighting*: Fixed Chebyshev weights may bias the solution; a posteriori decision is preferred but requires understanding of the front.  
- *Explainer choice*: SHAP is more computationally intensive than LIME; ensemble weighting affects MESD stability.  
- *Computational cost*: NSGA‑II requires many model trainings; with three objectives, convergence may need generations.  
- *CVaR α*: Smaller α focuses on very small subgroups, increasing variance; larger α approximates average disparity.

**Failure Signatures**  
- **Pareto collapse**: The front becomes a single point, indicating too much selection pressure or insufficient population diversity.  
- **Exploding MESD**: Extremely large MESD values for subgroups with <200 samples, signaling inadequate shrinkage (λ too low) or insufficient perturbations.  
- **Long training times**: NSGA‑II population size or number of generations too high relative to dataset size.  
- **Instability in explanation variance**: High variance in SHAP/LIME outputs across runs; may require fixing random seeds or increasing background samples.

**First Experiments**  
1. **MESD unit test**: Create a synthetic dataset with two known subgroups (e.g., sizes 500 and 50) and a simple logistic model; manually perturb explanations to induce a stability gap; verify MESD detects the disparity and that CVaR weighting emphasizes the smaller subgroup.  
2. **End‑to‑end UEF on German Credit**: Run UEF with default hyperparameter bounds and NSGA‑II settings; confirm that the Pareto front contains models spanning the utility‑fairness spectrum and that the selected Chebyshev model improves procedure fairness relative to a utility‑only baseline.  
3. **Baseline comparison**: Replicate the three baselines (ADL, RW, RD) on Adult Income; compare the three objectives (AUC, ΔDP, MESD) to ensure UEF does not degrade outcome fairness while improving procedural fairness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
None were explicitly raised by the authors.

## Limitations
- **Incomplete reproducibility specifications**: NSGA‑II parameters (population, generations, mutation) and neural network hyperparameter ranges are not fully provided; implementers must guess reasonable values.
- **Small‑subgroup variance**: MESD can be noisy for intersectional cells with fewer than 200 examples; empirical‑Bayes shrinkage helps but the paper does not justify the chosen λ.
- **Dataset scope**: Evaluation limited to three tabular datasets; scalability to high‑dimensional data (e.g., images) or many protected attributes remains untested.

## Confidence
- **MESD novelty**: High (first metric of its kind).  
- **UEF outperformance**: Medium (strong on two datasets, only competitive on the third; no statistical significance tests).  
- **CVaR mitigation of fairness gerrymandering**: Medium (empirically observed but limited analysis).  
- **Generalizability of findings**: Low‑Medium (due to narrow dataset selection).

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Vary NSGA‑II population size, generations, mutation rate, and neural network architecture across multiple seeds; assess Pareto front stability and hypervolume.  
2. **Explainer robustness**: Replace SHAP/LIME with Integrated Gradients or DeepLIFT; vary kernel size (for SHAP) and number of samples; evaluate consistency of MESD rankings.  
3. **Scalability to more protected attributes and complex data**: Apply UEF to datasets with ≥4 protected attributes (e.g., COMPAS extended) and to image classification tasks; measure computational overhead and fairness‑utility trade‑offs.
