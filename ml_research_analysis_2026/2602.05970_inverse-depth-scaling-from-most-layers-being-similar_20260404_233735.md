# Inverse Depth Scaling From Most Layers Being Similar

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05970
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05970
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** LLMs primarily use depth as an ensemble of similar layers, reducing error by averaging rather than hierarchical composition, leading to inverse depth scaling (loss ∝ ℓ^{-α_ℓ} with α_ℓ ≈ 1).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how loss scales with model depth in large language models (LLMs). By analyzing hidden states across layers in several LLM families, the authors find that for the vast majority of tokens, intermediate layers apply uniformly small angular updates, with only the first and last layers showing larger changes. A decomposed scaling law fitted to Chinchilla data yields a depth exponent α_ℓ ≈ 1, indicating inverse depth scaling. Controlled toy experiments demonstrate that when teacher dynamics are non-smooth (independent layer weights), students replicate the hidden-state pattern and achieve α_ℓ ≈ 1. In contrast, smooth dynamics (tied weights) lead to α_ℓ ≈ 3 after convergence. The evidence points to LLMs using depth mainly as a redundant ensemble, a regime that is robust but depth-inefficient, suggesting future architectures should promote more compositional use of depth.

## Method Summary
The study combines empirical analysis of real LLMs with a teacher-student toy framework. For real LLMs (e.g., Pythia, Qwen, OPT), hidden states are extracted from a dataset (FineWeb) and analyzed using angular metrics (θ(h_l, h_{l+1}), θ(Δh_l, Δh_{l+1})) and PCA to characterize per-token update patterns. A decomposed power-law scaling law (L = c_m m^{-α_m} + c_ℓ ℓ^{-α_ℓ} + c_D D^{-α_D} + L_0) is fitted to public Chinchilla training data to isolate the depth exponent. The toy model uses a deep teacher (ℓ* = 128) with residual MLP blocks; the teacher's weight correlation ρ (tied ρ=1 vs. independent ρ=0) and output temperature T control the smoothness of the target dynamics. Student models of varying depths are trained to mimic the teacher, and their final test loss vs. depth is used to infer α_ℓ, while hidden-state angles are compared to the LLM patterns.

## Key Results
- Most tokens (99.6%) exhibit uniform, incremental updates across middle layers; first/last layers change more drastically.
- Fitted depth scaling exponent on Chinchilla data: α_ℓ ≈ 1.2 (inverse scaling).
- Toy model with independent teacher weights (ρ=0) reproduces LLM hidden-state pattern (uniform updates, weak inter-update correlation) and yields α_ℓ ≈ 1 robustly.
- Toy model with tied teacher weights (ρ=1) and sufficient training at low temperature shows α_ℓ → 3, consistent with procedural assembly (discretization of smooth dynamics), contrasting with real LLMs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper evaluates three hypothesized mechanisms for how depth reduces loss, rejecting two and supporting the third based on evidence from LLMs and toy models.

### Mechanism 1: Compositional Assembly (Rejected)
- **Claim:** Layers build hierarchical abstractions (e.g., syntax → semantics → reasoning), with each layer performing a distinct transformation; depth enables new representational capabilities.
- **Core assumption:** Hidden state trajectories should show early stopping for simpler inputs; representations at the same depth index in models of different depths should be similar for early layers but diverge deeper.
- **Evidence against:** PCA on per-token angle trajectories shows only ~0.4% of tokens exhibit early stopping (first tokens of documents), while 99.6% are "evenly updated in the middle." Neighbors discuss layer redundancy and removal without significant loss, conflicting with distinct hierarchical roles.

### Mechanism 2: Procedural Assembly (Discretization of Smooth Dynamics) (Rejected as Dominant)
- **Claim:** Residual networks approximate neural ODEs; layers perform small, incremental updates along a smooth trajectory, and depth reduces discretization error.
- **Core assumption:** Hidden state updates should be highly correlated between consecutive layers (smooth derivatives); scaling exponent should be α_ℓ = 3 after convergence for smooth underlying functions.
- **Evidence against:** LLM hidden states show weak inter-layer correlation (θ(Δh_l, Δh_{l+1}) large, close to π/2). Toy experiments with tied teacher weights (ρ=1, smooth dynamics) show α_ℓ approaches 3 only after exhaustive training at low temperature; at higher temperature, α_ℓ ≈ 1 due to incomplete training. Under smooth dynamics, the predicted scaling (α_ℓ=3) is not the dominant regime in practice.

### Mechanism 3: Ensemble Averaging (Supported)
- **Claim:** Most layers function as a redundant ensemble of similar subnetworks; depth reduces variance via averaging independent layer-wise errors.
- **Core assumption:** Layer-wise updates should be approximately uniform in magnitude across middle layers; inter-update correlations should be weak (angles near π/2); depth scaling exponent should be α_ℓ ≈ 1.
- **Evidence for:** LLMs show ⟨θ(h_l, h_{l+1})⟩_D,l scales inversely with depth for middle layers, and θ(Δh_l, Δh_{l+1}) are large. PCA shows most tokens are "evenly updated." Toy model with independent teacher weights (ρ=0) replicates these hidden state patterns and robustly yields α_ℓ ≈ 1. The inverse scaling is consistent with summing independent O(1) errors across ℓ layers, leading to L ∼ 1/ℓ via central limit theorem.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Power-law scaling decomposition**
  - **Why needed:** Decomposes total loss L into separate contributions from width (∼ m^{-α_m}), depth (∼ ℓ^{-α_ℓ}), and dataset size (∼ D^{-α_D}), allowing isolation of the depth effect.
  - **Quick check:** If total parameters N ∝ m²ℓ and α_m ≈ α_ℓ ≈ 1, what is the implied optimal width-depth relationship under a fixed N?

- **Concept: Hidden state geometry (angular updates)**
  - **Why needed:** Angles between consecutive hidden states θ(h_l, h_{l+1}) and between updates θ(Δh_l, Δh_{l+1}) are key diagnostics to distinguish smooth dynamics (small angles) from ensemble averaging (large, independent angles).
  - **Quick check:** Why would procedural assembly predict small θ(Δh_l, Δh_{l+1}) while ensemble averaging predicts angles near π/2?

- **Concept: Teacher-student paradigm for controlled dynamics**
  - **Why needed:** Toy models use a deeper teacher to generate data and a shallower student to learn, enabling manipulation of underlying dynamics (smooth vs. non-smooth via weight tying ρ) and target distribution sharpness (temperature).
  - **Quick check:** How does tying teacher weights across layers (ρ=1) induce smooth hidden-state dynamics, and why does independent weighting (ρ=0) break smoothness?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Real LLM analysis pipeline: Load model → tokenize text → extract per-layer hidden states → compute norms, θ(h_l,h_{l+1}), θ(Δh_l,Δh_{l+1}) → accumulate over dataset → PCA on per-token angle trajectories → fit decomposed scaling law L = c_m m^{-α_m} + c_ℓ ℓ^{-α_ℓ} + c_D D^{-α_D} + L_0. Toy model core: Residual MLP blocks with RMSNorm → teacher with depth ℓ* > student depth ℓ → control teacher weight correlation ρ (tied ρ=1 vs independent ρ=0) and temperature T → student trained via KL(π_teacher || π_student) or MSE on final hidden state → measure final test loss vs. ℓ to fit α_ℓ.

- **Critical path:**
  1. Validate hidden state pattern in LLMs: compute θ(h_l, h_{l+1}) across layers; confirm majority of tokens show uniform small updates in middle layers and weak inter-update correlation.
  2. Fit decomposed scaling law to public Chinchilla data; confirm α_ℓ ≈ 1.
  3. Replicate hidden state pattern and α_ℓ ≈ 1 in toy model with independent teacher weights (ρ=0).
  4. Show tied teacher weights (ρ=1) yield α_ℓ ≈ 3 after convergence and smoother hidden state updates, contrasting with LLMs.
  5. Conclude ensemble averaging dominates in LLMs.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Loss metric:** Cross-entropy vs. MSE on final hidden state. CE aligns with LLM training; MSE ensures easier convergence in toy models at low temperature but may not capture distributional sharpness effects.
  - **Teacher design:** Tied weights enforce smooth dynamics but may be unrealistic for next-token prediction; independent weights mimic ensemble but lack inductive bias for composition.
  - **Temperature:** Low T yields peaked targets, filtering gradients and slowing convergence; high T eases training but may obscure depth scaling differences.
  - **Architecture modifications:** Second-order integration scheme (two MLPs per residual block) increases α_ℓ under smooth dynamics, testing discretization order.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - In toy models, low teacher temperature prevents convergence within fixed steps, making α_ℓ estimates unreliable.
  - Hidden state correlations θ(Δh_l,Δh_{l+1}) near zero would indicate procedural assembly, but in practice remain large even under ρ=1 due to limited training or symmetries.
  - Scaling law fit may be unstable if excluding too many high-loss Chinchilla points; residual dataset-size term must still show power-law.
  - LLM script may fail for some architectures (e.g., OPT) due to mismatched attribute names for final_norm and lm_head.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **LLM hidden state sweep:** For a mid-size Pythia model (410M), compute and plot ⟨θ(h_l, h_{l+1})⟩_D vs. normalized layer index for multiple model depths; perform PCA on per-token angle trajectories to quantify fraction of tokens with early stopping.
  2. **Decomposed scaling fit:** Using Chinchilla data (N, D, L and inferred m, ℓ), fit Equation (3) via log-space MSE; check that α_ℓ ≈ 1 and that residual depth term L - c_m m^{-α_m} - c_D D^{-α_D} follows a clear power law.
  3. **Toy model regime check:** Train student (ℓ=6..48) under two teacher conditions: (a) ρ=1 (tied weights), (b) ρ=0 (independent weights), at high temperature (T=1) for sufficient steps. Measure final test loss vs. ℓ and fit α_ℓ; also compute θ(h_l, h_{l+1}) and θ(Δh_l, Δh_{l+1}) to compare with LLM patterns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Can the decomposed scaling law (Equation 3) be derived from first principles, such as from assumptions about data manifolds or training dynamics? The current formulation is empirical; a derivation linking exponents to formal models of training or data properties would resolve this.
- **Open Question 2:** What specific architectural or training factors causally induce ensemble averaging as the dominant depth-utilization regime in LLMs? The paper identifies correlations (e.g., residual architecture, non-smooth teacher dynamics) but does not establish causality; controlled ablations on LLM-scale models are needed.
- **Open Question 3:** How do attention mechanisms, absent in the toy model, alter depth-scaling regimes and hidden-state dynamics in real Transformers? Attention induces spatially coupled dynamics (better described by PDEs); extending the teacher-student framework to include self-attention would test whether ensemble averaging persists.
- **Open Question 4:** Do middle layers in LLMs form multiple distinct functional ensembles (sub-networks) rather than a single homogeneous ensemble, and how does the number of such ensembles scale with depth? The analysis treats middle layers as one cluster via PCA; clustering on update vectors could reveal discrete sub-ensembles and their scaling.

## Limitations
- Toy model hyperparameters are underspecified (activation "ReLU2", training details like batch size, LR schedule, step count), especially at low temperature where convergence may be incomplete.
- LLM analysis specifics (exact FineWeb sample, Pythia checkpoint version, padding/causal masking implementation) are not fully documented, risking reproducibility.
- Chinchilla scaling data relies on a reconstructed dataset (Besiroglu et al., 2024) not provided; the ad‑hoc exclusion of 40 high‑loss points may bias the depth exponent.
- Diagnostic sufficiency: mean angular update scaling ∝ 1/ℓ and weak inter‑update correlations are suggestive of independent errors but could also arise from partially smooth dynamics; the link is indirect.
- Ecological validity: The teacher‑student paradigm with independent MLP layers may not capture complexities of LLM pretraining (next‑token loss, large vocab, attention), possibly over‑simplifying the ensemble interpretation.
