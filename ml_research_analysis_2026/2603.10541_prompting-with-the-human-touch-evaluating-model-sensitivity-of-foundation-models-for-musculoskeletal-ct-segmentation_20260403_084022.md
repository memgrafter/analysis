# Prompting with the human-touch: evaluating model-sensitivity of foundation models for musculoskeletal CT segmentation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10541
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10541
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Human prompt variability causes a statistically significant drop in segmentation performance (2.07% DSC for 2D, 1.06% for 3D) across all evaluated models.

## Executive Summary
This study examines the impact of realistic human prompt variability on the performance of promptable foundation models for musculoskeletal CT segmentation. The authors first evaluated 11 models (2D and 3D) using idealized reference prompts, identifying Pareto-optimal models: SAM and SAM2.1 for 2D; nnInteractive and Med-SAM2 for 3D. They then collected prompts from 20 medical students and found that human prompts, while moderately accurate, exhibit considerable inter-rater variability. When the Pareto-optimal models were tested with human prompts, segmentation performance declined significantly, and all models showed sensitivity to prompt variations. No model was fully robust; nnInteractive with combination prompts demonstrated the least sensitivity. The findings underscore that standard benchmarks using ideal prompts overestimate real-world performance and recommend that model sensitivity to human input become a key evaluation metric.

## Method Summary
The study employs a two-stage evaluation framework. First, eleven promptable foundation models (six 2D, five 3D) are tested on reference prompts automatically derived from ground-truth masks. Performance is measured across Dice (DSC), Normalized Surface Dice (NSD), and 95% Hausdorff Distance (HD95). Pareto-optimal models are selected based on multi-metric dominance and parameter efficiency. Second, these top models are evaluated with human prompts collected from an observer study of 20 medical students. Performance drops are quantified using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction. Model sensitivity is assessed by correlating intra- and inter-rater prompt variability (Euclidean distance for points, IoU for boxes) with segmentation consistency via Spearman’s rank coefficient.

## Key Results
- Reference prompt evaluation yields Pareto-optimal models: 2D – SAM and SAM2.1; 3D – nnInteractive and Med-SAM2.
- Human prompts achieve median point error of 1.50 mm and box IoU of 90.56%, but with considerable inter-rater variability, especially for complex structures.
- All models exhibit statistically significant performance degradation with human prompts: average DSC drop of 2.07% for 2D and 1.06% for 3D.
- nnInteractive with combination prompts demonstrates the lowest sensitivity to inter-rater variability, though no model is completely robust.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Human prompt variability causally degrades segmentation performance compared to idealized reference prompts.
- **Mechanism**: Human prompts deviate from reference coordinates due to intra- and inter-rater inconsistency (e.g., median point error 1.50 mm). These deviations act as input noise, causing output mask shifts. The study quantifies a statistically significant DSC drop (2.07% for 2D, 1.06% for 3D) when switching from reference to human prompts, establishing a causal chain: rater variability → prompt deviation → model sensitivity → segmentation inconsistency.
- **Core assumption**: Prompt placement is the primary driver of segmentation output given deterministic model inference.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract, section 4.4 (statistical decline), and the empirical design directly comparing reference vs. human prompts.
- **Break condition**: If iterative refinement (not tested) can recover the performance gap, the non-iterative causal link may not hold in interactive workflows.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Pareto-optimal model selection via multi-metric trade-offs identifies top candidates for human-driven settings.
- **Mechanism**: Models are evaluated on DSC, NSD, HD95. A model is Pareto-optimal if no other model is strictly better in all metrics and has fewer parameters. This two-stage filtering reduces the model pool to those balancing accuracy and efficiency, enabling feasible human-prompt testing.
- **Core assumption**: Parameter count and the three metrics are sufficient proxies for clinical utility; smaller models are preferable unless dominated.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 3.4.3 (Pareto dominance definition) and section 4.2 (identified fronts).
- **Break condition**: If clinical utility depends on additional factors (e.g., inference speed, memory, prompt type support), the Pareto front may exclude practically optimal models.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Model sensitivity to prompt variations is quantifiable via correlation between prompt variability and segmentation consistency.
- **Mechanism**: For each model/prompt type, compute Spearman’s ρ between (a) intra-rater prompt variability (Euclidean distance/IoU) and (b) segmentation metrics (DSC/NSD/HD95). A significant negative ρ (for DSC/NSD) or positive ρ (for HD95) indicates sensitivity: greater prompt spread yields worse consistency.
- **Core assumption**: Intra-rater variability is a bounded proxy for inter-rater variability; robustness to intra-rater noise generalizes to inter-rater settings.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 3.4.5 (method) and Table 6 (correlation results). All 2D models and 3D models with box prompts showed significant sensitivity; nnInteractive combination prompts and SAM2.1 point-prompted were robust for intra-rater variations.
- **Break condition**: If sensitivity is non-linear or threshold-based, Spearman’s ρ may understate it.

## Foundational Learning
- **Promptable Foundation Models**: Models like SAM, SAM2.1, Med-SAM2 that accept sparse interactive prompts (points, boxes) for segmentation. They typically use an image encoder, prompt encoder, and mask decoder, trained on large-scale datasets (natural or medical). *Why needed*: Understanding their architecture and training regime explains performance differences across models. *Quick check*: Why might a model trained on natural images (SAM) outperform a medical variant on bone CT, or vice versa?
- **Segmentation Metrics**: Dice (DSC) for volume overlap, Normalized Surface Dice (NSD) for boundary accuracy at a tolerance (1.5 mm), and 95% Hausdorff Distance (HD95) for extreme surface discrepancies. *Why needed*: These are the primary outcomes; trade-offs among them determine Pareto-optimality. *Quick check*: How could a model have high DSC but poor HD95, and what clinical risk does that imply?
- **Prompting Strategies**: 2D models process slices independently and can use multiple prompts per slice; 3D models process entire volumes but often restrict to single prompts or limited slices. *Why needed*: Strategy choice impacts performance and comparability; 3D evaluation on whole volumes is more stringent. *Quick check*: Why does a 3D model's evaluation on whole volumes penalize it relative to a 2D model evaluated slice-wise?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input CT volumes → slice selector → axial slices → prompt sources (reference masks or human annotators) → prompt generation (boxes/points) → promptable foundation models (2D/3D, natural/medical variants) → segmentation predictions → evaluation engine (metric calculators, Pareto front constructor, correlation analyzer) → outputs (performance tables, consistency statistics, sensitivity rankings).
- **Critical path**:
  1. Prepare dataset: select 404 axial slices from four anatomical regions (wrist, shoulder, hip, lower leg) combining private scans and TotalSegmentator, ensuring test/train separation.
  2. Conduct observer study: collect human prompts (boxes, points) from 20 medical students on the same slices; include duplicate slices for intra-rater assessment.
  3. Reference evaluation: run all 11 models with algorithmic prompts; compute DSC/NSD/HD95; construct Pareto fronts.
  4. Human prompt evaluation: run Pareto-optimal models with human prompts; compute performance gaps and segmentation consistency on duplicates.
  5. Sensitivity analysis: compute Spearman correlation between prompt variability (intra-/inter-rater) and segmentation consistency.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - 2D vs. 3D: 2D models allow multi-component prompts per slice but ignore inter-slice context; 3D models capture volumetric context but often limit to single prompts and suffer from error propagation.
  - Natural vs. medical pretraining: Medical models (e.g., nnInteractive) may better handle bone/implant contrast; natural models (SAM2.1) sometimes generalize well but may lack domain-specific biases.
  - Prompt type: Bounding boxes generally yield higher accuracy than center points; combination prompts (box+point) can further improve results but increase annotator burden.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Over-segmentation: model predicting beyond anatomical boundaries, common in 3D due to propagation across low-contrast gaps (e.g., joint spaces).
  - Under-segmentation: failure to capture full extent in fading intensity regions.
  - Anatomical ambiguity: inability to separate cortical vs. trabecular bone from a single prompt, leading to merged predictions.
  - Volume cropping failure: models with fixed input crops (e.g., SAM-Med3D) fail when the object exceeds the crop size.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Replicate Pareto selection: run all 11 models with reference prompts (default: bounding box, largest component, single slice for 3D) to verify Table 4 results (2D: SAM, SAM2.1; 3D: nnInteractive, Med-SAM2).
  2. Human prompt performance drop: test Pareto-optimal models with human prompts; confirm significant DSC reduction using Wilcoxon signed-rank with Bonferroni correction (α=0.0083).
  3. Sensitivity quantification: for a top model (e.g., SAM2.1 T point-prompted), compute intra-rater Spearman’s ρ between point Euclidean distance and DSC; expect significant negative correlation (≈ -0.59). Then explore inter-rater threshold effects (Table 7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How does iterative interactive refinement (e.g., negative prompts, multiple rounds) affect model sensitivity and segmentation performance? *Basis*: Limitations state interactive refinement remains unanalyzed. *Why unresolved*: Non-iterative setup used.
- **Open Question 2**: Can text-based prompts (e.g., in SAM3) reduce geometric variability and improve robustness compared to geometric prompts? *Basis*: Authors note text prompts could remove interaction but were not evaluated.
- **Open Question 3**: Does annotator expertise (medical students vs. radiologists) influence human prompt variability and model sensitivity, especially for complex structures? *Basis*: Only students were studied; experts may differ.
- **Open Question 4**: What architectural modifications to promptable foundation models can reduce sensitivity to input prompt variations while maintaining accuracy? *Basis*: All models were sensitive; nnInteractive least but not robust, indicating a fundamental design limitation.
- **Open Question 5**: How generalizable are the findings to other modalities (MRI, ultrasound) and soft tissue segmentation tasks? *Basis*: Study is narrow (bone/implant CT); cross-modality robustness unknown.

## Limitations
- Human prompt dataset is not publicly available; exact replication requires a new observer study.
- Private Amsterdam UMC scans cannot be accessed; public TotalSegmentator only partially covers the examined regions and implant cases.
- Several models (e.g., SAM-Med3D) have reported bugs or constraints (fixed crop size) that hinder reproduction.

## Confidence
- Claim 1 (Human variability causally degrades performance): High confidence. Supported by within-study statistical tests on human vs. reference prompts.
- Claim 2 (Pareto-optimal model selection identifies suitable candidates): Medium confidence. Depends on whether parameter count and three metrics suffice as proxies for clinical utility.
- Claim 3 (Sensitivity is quantifiable via prompt-mask correlation): Low confidence. Correlation may miss non-linear or threshold effects; intra-rater proxy for inter-rater not directly validated.

## Next Checks
