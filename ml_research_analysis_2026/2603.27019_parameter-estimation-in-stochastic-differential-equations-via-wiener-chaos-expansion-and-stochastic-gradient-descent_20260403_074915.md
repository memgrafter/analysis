# Parameter Estimation in Stochastic Differential Equations via Wiener Chaos Expansion and Stochastic Gradient Descent

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27019
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27019
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** A novel framework that uses Wiener Chaos Expansion to convert stochastic differential equation solutions into a deterministic propagator system, enabling parameter estimation via a Tikhonov‑regularized loss minimized with stochastic gradient descent; validated on synthetic SDEs and real‑world data (S&P 500, microbial growth) with accurate recovery and reduced computational cost compared to traditional Monte‑Carlo methods.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the inverse problem of estimating parameters in stochastic differential equations (SDEs) from noisy discrete observations. Classical simulation‑based approaches such as maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) become computationally prohibitive because they require repeated path simulations. The proposed solution leverages Wiener Chaos Expansion (WCE) to spectrally project the random solution onto an orthogonal basis of Hermite polynomials, thereby transforming the SDP into a hierarchical system of deterministic ordinary differential equations called the *propagator*. The estimation problem is then cast as minimizing a Tikhonov‑regularized discrepancy between the propagator’s mean and variance and the empirical statistics computed from the data, solved via stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with Barzilai‑Borwein step sizes. The method is tested on synthetic benchmarks (Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck, Geometric Brownian Motion, Stochastic Logistic Equation) and two real datasets: daily S&P 500 index values and microbial growth curves. In all cases the learned parameters match or closely approximate ground truth or established estimators, and simulated trajectories agree with observed dynamics within confidence bands, all while avoiding the heavy computational burden of Monte‑Carlo sampling.

## Method Summary
The approach begins by expanding the SDE solution \(X(t)\) in a Wiener Chaos basis: \(X(t)=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}} X_m(t)\,\xi_m\), where \(\xi_m\) are orthonormal Hermite polynomials. Under the assumption that the drift and diffusion coefficients factor as \(f(x;\theta_1)=F(x)\,g_1(\theta_1)\) and \(\sigma(x;\theta_2)=\Gamma(x)\,g_2(\theta_2)\) (Hypothesis 3), a Malliavin calculus calculation yields a closed system of deterministic ODEs for the chaos coefficients \(X_m(t)\)—the propagator. From discrete noisy trajectories, empirical mean \(\bar{y}(t)\) and a normalized quadratic variation are computed. The loss function is an integral of two terms: squared error between \(X_0(t)\) and \(\bar{y}(t)\), and squared discrepancy between \(\sum_{|m|\ge1} X_m(t)^2\) and the empirical variance; this construction is mathematically equivalent to a Tikhonov‑Phillips regularization of the underlying stochastic misfit. Analytical derivatives of \(X_m(t)\) with respect to parameters are derived from the propagator ODEs, enabling gradient‑based optimization. SGD with Barzilai‑Borwein step sizes is applied directly to the propagator, iterating until convergence. The result is a set of parameters that reproduces the first two moments of the data without simulating any sample paths.

## Key Results
- **Synthetic benchmarks:** For Geometric Brownian Motion with true parameters \(\alpha=0.35,\;\sigma=0.17\), the method estimates \(\alpha_{\text{est}}=0.3648\) and \(\sigma_{\text{est}}=0.1684\); similar accuracy is achieved for Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck and the Stochastic Logistic Equation.
- **Financial data:** Applied to S&P 500 daily adjusted closes (2017), the estimated drift \(\mu\approx0.159\) compares favorably to an MLE estimate of \(0.1726\); simulations from the learned model capture market dynamics within 95% confidence bands.
- **Biological data:** On microbial growth curves (optical density), the method accurately models nonlinear saturation and inherent biological noise.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Wiener Chaos Expansion de‑randomizes the SDE
The solution is expanded onto an orthonormal basis of Hermite polynomials. Substituting into the SDE and applying the duality between the Skorokhod integral and the Malliavin derivative yields a decoupled system of deterministic ODEs for each chaos coefficient (the propagator). This transformation relies on the square‑integrability of the solution and on the separability of drift and diffusion (i.e., \(f(x;\Theta)=F(x)g_1(\theta_1)\), \(\sigma(x;\Theta)=\Gamma(x)g_2(\theta_2)\)). The propagator depends deterministically on the model parameters, thus converting a stochastic inverse problem into a deterministic one.

### Mechanism 2: Loss function naturally induces Tikhonov regularization
The \(L^2(\Omega)\) norm of the data‑model mismatch is expanded using orthonormality of the chaos basis. The zeroth‑order term yields a squared error between the empirical mean and the model mean; the sum of squares of all higher‑order coefficients forms a strongly convex penalty that controls the variance. Under zero‑mean independent observation noise and a mean‑preserving forward operator, the resulting deterministic functional is exactly a Tikhonov‑Phillips regularized discrepancy. This guarantees existence, uniqueness, and stability of the minimizer.

### Mechanism 3: SGD on the propagator avoids Monte‑Carlo and converges reliably
The propagator provides explicit expressions for \(X_m(t;\Theta)\) and their gradients. The Tikhonov term ensures strong convexity and Lipschitz continuity of the loss gradient, so Barzilai‑Borwein SGD converges to the unique global optimum. Because the loss is evaluated directly from the propagator ODEs, no repeated sampling is needed, drastically reducing computational cost.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input module -> WCE‑Propagator Generator -> ODE Solver -> Loss Evaluator -> Gradient Module -> Optimizer (BB‑SGD) -> Convergence Check
- **Critical path:** 
  1. Initialize parameters \(\Theta\).
  2. Loop:
     - Construct propagator ODEs for current \(\Theta\).
     - Integrate ODEs (e.g., Runge‑Kutta) to obtain all \(X_m(t)\).
     - Evaluate loss \(u(\Theta)\) from \(X_0\) and higher modes.
     - Compute \(\nabla u(\Theta)\) using analytical formulas.
     - Update \(\Theta\) via BB‑SGD step.
  3. Stop when change in loss or parameters falls below tolerance or maximum iterations reached; return final \(\Theta\).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Truncation order \(L\):** Higher \(L\) improves accuracy but increases the number of ODEs (potentially exponential in multi‑D), affecting memory and time.
  - **Step‑size strategy:** Barzilai‑Borwein often speeds convergence but may cause instability; dynamic dampening or clipping is used.
  - **Parameter separability:** Hypothesis 3 simplifies gradients; non‑separable models introduce cross‑derivatives and possible non‑convexity.
  - **Regularization strength:** The \(L\) truncation itself regularizes; if data noise is high, a higher \(L\) may be needed to match variance, otherwise underfitting occurs.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Propagator ODEs produce NaNs or overflow (stiffness, incorrect equations).
  - SGD loss increases or plateaus above optimum (poor step size or gradient error).
  - Empirical quadratic variation negative or infinite (division by near‑zero in normalization).
  - Poor match of empirical mean to \(X_0(t)\) (wrong initial guess or model mismatch).
  - Excessive memory/time due to large number of chaos modes.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck:** Linear drift and constant diffusion; tests basic convergence and sensitivity to initial guesses.
  2. **Geometric Brownian Motion:** State‑dependent diffusion; validates multi‑parameter estimation (both drift and diffusion).
  3. **Stochastic logistic equation:** Nonlinear drift with multiplicative noise; demonstrates handling of saturation dynamics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does the WCE‑SGD framework generalize to high‑frequency volatile financial data, where market microstructure noise and irregular sampling may violate current assumptions?
- How can the method be robustly implemented for stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs), given challenges of spatial discretization, curse of dimensionality in basis functions, and stability?
- What are the scalability limits of WCE‑SGD for higher‑dimensional and coupled SDE systems, considering the exponential growth of Wiener chaos modes?
- Can the framework be extended to non‑separable parameter dependencies (e.g., parameters appearing in both drift and diffusion), which are common in realistic models?

## Limitations
- The orthonormal basis \(\{e_i\}\) of \(L^2([0,T])\) used in the Malliavin derivative is not specified; implementation choices affect the propagator integrals.
- Truncation parameters \(L\) and \(p\) are ambiguous and lack theoretical guidance for selection.
- Preprocessing details for real datasets (S&P 500 source, microbial growth standardization) are missing.
- Numerical implementation details (ODE solver, time step, stopping criterion \(m_{\text{max}}\) or tolerance) are not provided.

## Confidence
- **WCE yields deterministic propagator:** Medium (relies on square‑integrability and separability; violations introduce coupling).
- **Loss equals Tikhonov‑regularized discrepancy:** High (Theorem 1 proves equivalence under noise assumptions).
- **SGD converges robustly without Monte‑Carlo:** Medium (strong convexity is guaranteed, but practical performance depends on truncation and step‑size tuning).
