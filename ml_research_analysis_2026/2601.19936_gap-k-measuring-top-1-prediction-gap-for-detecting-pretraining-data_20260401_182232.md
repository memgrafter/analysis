# Gap-K%: Measuring Top-1 Prediction Gap for Detecting Pretraining Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.19936  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19936  
- **Reference count:** 31  
- **Primary result:** Gap-K% achieves state-of-the-art membership inference performance, with absolute AUROC improvements of 2.4% (WikiMIA original) and 1.7% (WikiMIA paraphrased) over Min‑K%++, and top average performance on the MIMIR benchmark across multiple model sizes.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Gap‑K%, a reference‑free method to detect whether a given text was included in the pretraining corpus of a large language model. It addresses key limitations of existing approaches (e.g., Min‑K%++) by focusing on the normalized gap between the model’s top‑1 predicted token and the actual target token, arguing that training data minimizes this gap due to explicit optimization during pretraining. Furthermore, it incorporates a sliding window to aggregate gap scores across adjacent tokens, capturing local sequential patterns that correlate with memorized text segments. Evaluations on WikiMIA and MIMIR benchmarks demonstrate consistent and significant improvements over strong baselines across autoregressive models of varying sizes and architectures.

## Method Summary
Gap‑K% operates under gray‑box access to a frozen autoregressive LLM. For each token position \(t\) in a sequence, it computes a normalized gap score:
\[
g_t = \frac{\log p(x_t \mid x_{<t}) - \max_{v \in V} \log p(v \mid x_{<t})}{\sigma_t},
\]
where \(\sigma_t\) is the standard deviation of the log‑probability distribution over the full vocabulary \(V\). This per‑token score is then smoothed via a sliding window of fixed size \(w\) (e.g., \(w=3\) for Pythia/Mamba, \(w=6\) for LLaMA), yielding averaged scores \(\bar{g}^{(w)}_t\) for each window position. The final membership score is the average of the bottom‑\(k\%\) (e.g., \(k=20\%\)) of these smoothed scores; higher scores (closer to zero) indicate the sequence likely was in the pretraining set. The method requires no model finetuning and is applied at inference time.

## Key Results
- On WikiMIA (sequence length 64), Gap‑K% yields absolute AUROC improvements of **2.4%** (original) and **1.7%** (paraphrased) over the strongest baseline (Min‑K%++), averaged across five models.
- On the challenging MIMIR benchmark, Gap‑K% attains the **highest average performance** across Pythia model sizes (1.4B to 12B).
- Ablations confirm that sequential smoothing (preserving token order) is crucial; shuffling token order before smoothing collapses performance to near‑random levels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Top‑1 Gap as a Training Signal
Training data minimizes the gap between the model’s top‑1 predicted token and the actual target token due to explicit optimization. In cross‑entropy loss, the gradient for a non‑target token is proportional to its predicted probability; a confident misprediction (high probability on a wrong token) produces a large gradient that the optimizer reduces. This drives the model to align the top‑1 prediction with the ground truth on frequently seen sequences. For a non‑member, the model relies on generalizable patterns, often yielding plausible but incorrect top‑1 predictions that diverge from the single observed token. The signal breaks if the pretraining objective is not next‑token prediction or if optimization converges with persistent confident mispredictions on training data.

### Mechanism 2: Sequential Smoothing for Local Correlations
Local sequential correlations provide a more robust membership signal than isolated token‑level gaps. A sliding window aggregates gap scores over adjacent tokens, smoothing noise and highlighting contiguous regions where the model consistently aligns (or fails to align) with the input, which correlates with memorized text segments. The assumption is that memorization manifests over short phrases, not isolated tokens. Evidence: sequential smoothing (without shuffling) yields substantial AUROC gains over no smoothing or shuffled‑order smoothing. The signal breaks if the input order is randomized or if memorization is non‑contiguous (e.g., only isolated words).

### Mechanism 3: Focusing on Hardest Segments
The most discriminative evidence lies in segments with the largest normalized top‑1 gaps after smoothing. Following Min‑K%, Gap‑K% averages over the bottom‑\(k\%\) smoothed scores, hypothesizing that segments hardest for the model to reconcile (large divergence from its own top prediction) are strong non‑member indicators. Performance peaks around \(k=15\%\), validating selective averaging. However, if member and non‑member gap distributions overlap (e.g., on MIMIR), this selective aggregation may fail.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Autoregressive Language Modeling & Cross‑Entropy Loss**  
- **Why needed:** The method derives from analyzing the gradient of the next‑token prediction objective. Understanding how the loss shapes the probability distribution explains why the top‑1 gap is meaningful.  
- **Quick check:** If the target token \(y_t\) has much lower predicted probability than another token \(v\), what is the sign and magnitude of the gradient \(\partial \ell_t / \partial s_t(v)\) for that non‑target token \(v\), and how does it drive learning?

**Concept: Membership Inference Attacks (MIA) in ML**  
- **Why needed:** Gap‑K% is a reference‑free MIA for LLM pretraining data. The problem formulation and evaluation metrics (AUROC, TPR@FPR) come from the MIA literature.  
- **Quick check:** What is the fundamental difference between a reference‑based and reference‑free MIA, and why is reference‑free necessary for detecting pretraining data where the original dataset is inaccessible?

**Concept: Normalization of Probabilistic Scores**  
- **Why needed:** The gap score \(g_t\) normalizes the raw difference by the standard deviation \(\sigma_t\) of the log‑probability distribution, making scores comparable across contexts with varying confidence.  
- **Quick check:** Why would using the raw, unnormalized gap be problematic? Provide an example: one context where the model is very confident (peaked distribution) and one where it is uncertain (flat distribution), and how the same raw gap might be interpreted differently.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**  
Input & Forward Pass -> Distribution Extraction -> Per‑Token Gap Scoring -> Sequential Smoothing -> K% Selection -> Final Score Aggregation

**Critical Path:**  
Steps 3 (Gap Scoring) and 4 (Smoothing) are the novel computational additions over baselines like Min‑K%++. The final score quality is highly sensitive to the window size \(w\) and the \(k\) parameter.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- **Window size \(w\):** Small \(w\) (e.g., 1–2) captures very local patterns but is noisy; large \(w\) (e.g., >6) provides stability but may dilute the signal from short memorized spans and introduce irrelevant context. Model‑specific tuning is needed (e.g., \(w=3\) for Pythia, \(w=6\) for LLaMA).  
- **\(k\)% ratio:** Small \(k\) (e.g., 5%) focuses on extreme gaps (high precision, low recall); large \(k\) (e.g., 50%) includes more noise. Performance peaks around \(k=15\%\).  
- **Gray‑box dependency:** Requires access to token‑level logits/probabilities. Not applicable to fully black‑box APIs that only return generated text.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- **AUROC ~ 0.5:** No discriminative power (common on very challenging benchmarks like MIMIR for small models).  
- **No separation in score histograms:** Member and non‑member score distributions overlap completely.  
- **Smoothing degrades performance:** If sequential smoothing without shuffling does not improve over no smoothing (or shuffling), the local correlation assumption is violated.  
- **Hyperparameter insensitivity:** Performance does not vary significantly with \(w\) or \(k\), suggesting core mechanisms are not active.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication & comparison:** Replicate Min‑K% and Min‑K%++ on WikiMIA‑64 for a target model (e.g., Pythia‑12B). Verify implementation matches reported AUROC within a small margin. Then run Gap‑K% with default \(w\) and \(k=20\%\) to confirm the stated ~2‑3% AUROC gain.  
2. **Ablation of sequential component:** Run Gap‑K% in three modes: (a) no smoothing, (b) smoothing after shuffling token order, (c) sequential smoothing (default). The performance drop from (c) to (b) must be significant to validate the sequential locality claim; (a) and (b) should underperform (c).  
3. **Hyperparameter sweep for window size:** For fixed \(k=20\%\), sweep \(w\) from 1 to 10 on a single model/dataset (e.g., Pythia‑12B on WikiMIA‑64). Plot AUROC vs. \(w\); expect a peak at a small \(w\) (likely 3) followed by degradation as \(w\) increases. Use this to set the default \(w\) for other models in the same family.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1: Generalization to Newer Architectures**  
- *Basis:* Limitations explicitly state evaluated models "do not include the most recent model families" and that generalization "remains an open question."  
- *Why unresolved:* Training regimes, data mixes, and architectural innovations evolve rapidly, potentially altering optimization dynamics and memorization signals.  
- *Resolution:* Evaluate Gap‑K% on diverse state‑of‑the‑art models released after the paper (e.g., GPT‑4 series, Claude 3, newer Llama variants, Gemini) across multiple pretraining data detection benchmarks.

**Open Question 2: Robustness Against Adversarial Paraphrasing**  
- *Basis:* Limitations acknowledge that the paper "does not explicitly study adversarial settings in which paraphrases are deliberately optimized to evade detection."  
- *Why unresolved:* An attacker with white/gray‑box knowledge could craft perturbations to minimize the Gap‑K% score while preserving semantics, possibly nullifying the method.  
- *Resolution:* Design and evaluate adversarial paraphrase attacks (gradient‑based, RL, heuristic) that explicitly minimize the Gap‑K% score, and measure AUROC drop compared to non‑adversarial paraphrases.

**Open Question 3: Relationship Between Gap Magnitude and Memorization Strength**  
- *Basis:* The method is motivated by gradient analysis (Eq. 4) arguing that training minimizes the gap, but no formal proof or empirical measurement correlates \(g_t\) with memorization strength (e.g., sequence frequency).  
- *Why unresolved:* A small gap could arise from high‑frequency syntactic patterns (strongly learned but not memorized) or exact memorization; disentangling requires controlled experiments.  
- *Resolution:* Conduct controlled experiments on synthetic/curated datasets with known pretraining frequencies, measuring Gap‑K% as a function of frequency to clarify if it proxies memorization or general in‑distribution likelihood.

**Open Question 4: Automatic Calibration of Hyperparameters**  
- *Basis:* The paper fixes \(k=20\%\) and window sizes (\(w=3\) or \(6\)) based on "stable and near‑optimal performance across model sizes within each family," but Fig. 5 shows \(k\) peaks around 15% and Fig. 4 indicates optimal \(w\) varies by architecture.  
- *Why unresolved:* A single global setting may be sub‑optimal for all inputs; optimal \(k\) might depend on sequence difficulty/length, and optimal \(w\) on typical phrase length in the domain.  
- *Resolution:* Develop an adaptive Gap‑K% that selects \(k\) based on entropy of gap distribution or determines \(w\) via heuristics (e.g., predicted sentence boundaries), and compare against fixed‑parameter version on heterogeneous benchmarks like MIMIR.

## Limitations
- Optimal window size \(w\) is model‑family‑specific but not exhaustively tuned for all evaluated sizes (e.g., Pythia‑160M/2.8B, LLaMA‑65B), risking suboptimal performance if defaults are mismatched.
- The precise definition of "bottom \(k\%\)" (whether indices are taken from the \(N-w+1\) smoothed scores or the original \(N\) tokens) is ambiguous, potentially affecting score calculation and AUROC.
- For the paraphrased setting, the exact paraphrasing method and its impact on tokenization/probability computation are unreported, hindering exact replication.
