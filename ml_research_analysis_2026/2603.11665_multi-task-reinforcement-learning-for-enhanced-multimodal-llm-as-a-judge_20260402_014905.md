# Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning for Enhanced Multimodal LLM-as-a-Judge

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.11665
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11665
- Reference count: 16
- Primary result: MT-RL-Judge achieves state-of-the-art Macro-F1 on six in-distribution multimodal evaluation benchmarks and robust out-of-distribution generalization, outperforming single-task and SFT baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the limited generalization of existing MLLM-as-a-Judge systems, which are typically trained for a single evaluation task. The authors propose MT-RL-Judge, a unified framework that jointly trains a multimodal judge across multiple evaluation tasks (alignment, safety, quality) using group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) and a composite reward that enforces structured reasoning. Experiments on six in-distribution benchmarks show consistent SOTA performance, while on out-of-distribution pairwise tasks MT-RL-Judge maintains strong accuracy and safety alignment, contrasting with the collapse of SFT-based unified models. The results demonstrate that multi-task reinforcement learning with reasoning incentives yields transferable judging logic and robust generalization.

## Method Summary
MT-RL-Judge employs a multi-task reinforcement learning approach to train a single multimodal judge (based on Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct) on a unified dataset comprising six evaluation tasks: AGIN-Nat, AGIN-Tech, AGIN-Rat, SeeTRUE, UnsafeBench, and ImageReward. The training uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which avoids a learned value network by computing advantages as the difference between a rollout’s reward and the group average over G=20 rollouts per prompt. The reward is composite: an accuracy term (binary label match) and a format term (presence of <thinking> and \boxed{...} output). The format weight α balances the two. During each training step, a prompt is sampled from the mixed dataset, G outputs are generated, rewards are computed, and the policy is updated with the GRPO objective. The model is trained until validation reward plateaus; the checkpoint with highest accuracy is selected. This unified multi-task training, coupled with the enforced reasoning trace, is hypothesized to internalize general evaluation principles rather than dataset-specific patterns, enabling robust out-of-distribution performance.

## Key Results
- On six in-distribution benchmarks, MT-RL-Judge achieves SOTA Macro-F1 scores, e.g., 83.67 on SeeTRUE and consistently outperforms SFT and single-task RL baselines.
- On the out-of-distribution MJ-Bench pairwise tasks, MT-RL-Judge generalizes robustly with 60.59% Alignment and 82.23% Safety, while the SFT-Unified baseline collapses to 49.40% on Safety.
- Multi-task RL training yields a unified model that does not sacrifice performance on individual tasks and often surpasses single-task specialists, demonstrating positive transfer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Mechanism 1: Multi-task RL with GRPO encourages the model to internalize evaluation logic rather than memorize surface patterns, leading to robust generalization. By jointly training on diverse tasks with a group-relative objective, the model is incentivized to produce explicit reasoning traces (<thinking> blocks) that abstract core criteria (alignment, safety, quality) applicable across contexts. Evidence includes the abstract’s claim and Table 3 showing strong OOD performance; the mechanism breaks if unified training causes per-task degradation or low format compliance.

Mechanism 2: The composite reward (accuracy + format) ensures both correct judgments and structured reasoning. The accuracy reward (RAcc) provides a task-specific correctness signal, while the format reward (RFor) enforces the presence of reasoning and a structured output (JSON, \boxed{}). This separation allows exploration of reasoning without penalizing early attempts, and group-relative scaling stabilizes learning across tasks with different reward scales. Evidence: Equations (2)-(5) define the rewards; RL prompts explicitly require <thinking>. The mechanism would fail if increasing α harms accuracy or if format compliance remains low.

Mechanism 3: Unified multi-task training captures shared evaluation criteria across tasks, preventing overfitting to any single dataset’s superficial cues. Exposure to varied tasks and prompt styles forces the model to extract common principles (e.g., what constitutes “rational” or “safe”), which transfer to unseen formats. Evidence: SFT-Unified already shows improvement over SFT-Single on some tasks (Table 2), and MT-RL-Judge’s OOD success where SFT-Unified fails (Table 3) suggests positive transfer. Negative transfer would be indicated by consistent underperformance of unified models.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) / Policy Gradient Methods**: Why needed: The paper uses RL to train the judge; RL helps avoid the overfitting typical of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) by exploring a broader output space and optimizing for a reward signal rather than a fixed demonstration set. Quick check: What key limitation of SFT does RL address in the context of MLLM-as-a-Judge? (Answer: SFT tends to imitate surface patterns and lacks the ability to refine judgments based on a differentiable reward; RL allows direct optimization of evaluation metrics.)
- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**: Why needed: GRPO is the specific RL algorithm used; it simplifies multi-task training by replacing a learned value network with a group-average baseline, making the advantage computation stable and task-agnostic. Quick check: How does GRPO compute advantages without a learned value function? (Answer: For each prompt, it generates G rollouts, computes the mean reward across the group, and uses each rollout’s reward minus this group mean as the advantage.)
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**: Why needed: The method enforces CoT via the format reward; CoT is central to the mechanism of structured reasoning and interpretability, as it forces the model to decompose its judgment into steps before producing a final verdict. Quick check: Why might requiring an explicit reasoning trace improve generalization compared to direct answer generation? (Answer: Explicit reasoning encourages the model to articulate intermediate criteria, making the evaluation logic more transparent and allowing the model to internalize general principles that apply across tasks.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Unified dataset sampler (mixed tasks) -> MLLM backbone (Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct) -> output parser (JSON + <thinking>) -> reward calculator (RAcc, RFor) -> rollout buffer (G samples per prompt) -> GRPO optimizer (AdamW) -> validation evaluator (periodic check).

Critical path:
1. Initialize model from pre-trained weights.
2. For each training iteration:
   - Sample a batch of prompts from the unified dataset.
   - Generate G rollouts per prompt using the current policy.
   - Compute total reward: Rtotal = (1−α)·RAcc + α·RFor.
   - Compute group mean reward across G rollouts for each prompt; advantage = Rtotal − group_mean.
   - Update model with GRPO loss using advantages.
3. Periodically evaluate validation accuracy/format compliance; keep best checkpoint.

Design tradeoffs:
- Multi-task vs single-task: Unified model reduces deployment cost but risks negative transfer; paper shows synergy but requires careful reward design.
- α (format weight): Higher α improves format adherence but may sacrifice accuracy; needs tuning.
- Rollout size G: Larger G stabilizes reward baselines but increases GPU memory; paper uses G=20.
- Reasoning enforcement: Adds length/latency but yields more robust and interpretable judgments.

Failure signatures:
- Low format compliance → increase α or verify RL prompts (Figures 7–12).
- Poor OOD generalization → insufficient task diversity; consider adding more tasks or adjusting reward weights.
- Training instability (loss spikes, NaNs) → reduce learning rate, increase G, or check reward normalization.
- OOM errors due to large model/resolution → ensure flash-attn enabled; reduce batch size or use gradient accumulation; verify images are resized to ≤4,194,304 pixels.
- Degradation on specific tasks → potential negative transfer; try task-specific sampling weights or separate LoRA adapters.

First 3 experiments:
1. **In-distribution replication**: Train MT-RL-Judge, SFT-Unified, RL-Single, SFT-Single as described; evaluate Macro-F1 on test sets of the six tasks (Table 2). Expect MT-RL-Judge to achieve best overall scores.
2. **OOD generalization test**: Evaluate all trained models on MJ-Bench pairwise tasks (Table 3). Expect SFT-Unified to collapse (e.g., Safety 49.40%) while MT-RL-Judge remains strong (e.g., Safety 82.23%).
3. **Ablation on α**: Train MT-RL-Judge with α ∈ {0.2, 0.5, 0.8}; measure format compliance (percentage of valid <thinking>) and accuracy on validation. Identify the α that balances both without large drops.

## Limitations
- Key hyperparameters (α, RL learning rate, stopping criterion) are not fully specified; reproduction requires assumptions.
- Dataset-specific prompt templates and preprocessing steps (e.g., field mappings for each task) must be reconstructed from figures and are not explicitly detailed.
- The format reward logic (e.g., strictness of JSON syntax) is ambiguous, and the reasoning quality itself is not directly validated.

## Confidence
- In-distribution SOTA claim: Medium (results reported but hyperparameter details may risk overfitting).
- OOD generalization claim: Medium (strong MJ-Bench results but limited external validation).
- Mechanism (structured reasoning via CoT): Low (correlational evidence; no ablation isolating reasoning quality or transferability).

## Next Checks
1. Conduct an α sensitivity sweep: train with α ∈ [0.2, 0.5, 0.8] and report format compliance vs. accuracy trade-off curves on validation sets; identify optimal α that maintains >90% format adherence without >2% accuracy drop.
2. Monitor per-task validation Macro-F1 during RL training; flag any consistent degradation (>5% relative drop) indicating negative transfer, and adjust sampling weights accordingly.
3. Sample 100 rollouts from MT-RL-Judge on OOD tasks; manually inspect <thinking> blocks for task-agnostic principles (e.g., "safety: detect harmful intent"). If <30% show clear cross-task abstraction, the reasoning mechanism hypothesis weakens.
