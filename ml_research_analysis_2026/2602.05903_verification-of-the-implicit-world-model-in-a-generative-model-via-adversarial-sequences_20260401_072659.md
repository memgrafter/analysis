# Verification of the Implicit World Model in a Generative Model via Adversarial Sequences

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05903
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05903
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** No generative sequence model tested (GPT-2/LLaMA variants) learns a sound implicit world model of chess; adversarial sequences reliably force illegal move predictions.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether transformer-based generative sequence models learn a sound implicit world model of chess—i.e., whether they only predict legal moves given a valid game state. The authors construct adversarial sequences that force models to predict illegal continuations. Evaluating 24 models across datasets and training objectives, all fail soundness. Stronger adversaries (Illegal Move Oracle) achieve nearly 100% attack success, while weaker ones have lower rates. Larger training datasets and a probability distribution (PD) objective that predicts all legal moves reduce attack success but do not eliminate failures. Linear probes for board state show weak causal links to next-token prediction, suggesting the extracted state representations are not used by the model. Out-of-distribution sequences further expose fragility, indicating models rely on statistical patterns rather than abstract rules.

## Method Summary
The study trains transformer decoders on chess game sequences tokenized in UCI notation. Models are trained with next-token (NT), probability distribution (PD), or joint probe (JP) objectives. Soundness is tested by generating adversarial games: an adversary (playing White) selects moves to maximize the chance the model (playing Black) predicts an illegal move. Five adversaries are implemented, ranging from random (RM) to the strong Illegal Move Oracle (IMO) that searches over legal moves to find one maximizing probability of an illegal continuation. Attack success rate (ASR) is the fraction of warm-up prefixes after which the model ever predicts an illegal move. Additional analyses probe for board state representations via linear classifiers and measure gradient alignments between probe and next-token losses.

## Key Results
- All 24 models fail soundness; IMO achieves ASR up to 1.0.
- Larger datasets (Random-10M vs. 500k) and PD objective significantly reduce ASR, but models remain unsound.
- Linear board state probes show near-orthogonal gradients with next-token loss (~0.99 cosine) and low BSO attack success, indicating weak causal involvement.
- ASR increases sharply for sequences beyond 150 moves, suggesting overfitting to typical game length.
- Out-of-distribution warm-up sequences increase ASR by up to 0.48, revealing distributional fragility.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Adversarial sequence generation reliably exposes soundness failures in implicit world models.
- **Mechanism:** An adversary constructs a valid chess sequence by iteratively selecting the next legal move that maximizes a heuristic designed to force the target model to assign high probability to at least one illegal continuation. The Illegal Move Oracle (IMO) explicitly maximizes the maximum probability among all illegal black moves for each candidate white move.
- **Core assumption:** A model is unsound if there exists any valid prefix after which it predicts an invalid move; the adversary efficiently searches for such a prefix.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Paper defines adversary framework in Section 3 and implements IMO, BSO, AD.
  - Near-perfect IMO ASR across all models confirms the mechanism exposes failures.
- **Break condition:** If the model were sound, no adversary could succeed. IMO could become computationally infeasible for very long sequences due to exponential growth of illegal move candidates.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Larger training datasets and the probability distribution (PD) objective improve soundness.
- **Mechanism:** More data exposes the model to a broader distribution of legal state transitions. The PD objective explicitly trains the model to output a uniform distribution over all legal next moves at move boundaries, reducing confusion between illegal high-probability moves and legal ones.
- **Core assumption:** Soundness requires accurate learning of transition rules; data scale and appropriate objectives help capture these rules.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract and Section 5.1 show ASR reduction with dataset size and PD vs. NT.
  - Table 1: ASR lower for Random-10M vs. 500k and PD vs. NT.
- **Break condition:** Improvements are marginal; models remain unsound under OOD conditions, indicating PD/data are insufficient for abstract rule learning.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Linear board state probes, even when jointly trained, have no causal role in next-token prediction.
- **Mechanism:** If probes were causally involved, gradients of probe loss and next-token loss would align, and attacks targeting probe accuracy (BSO) would break soundness. However, gradient cosine distances are near 0.99 (orthogonal), and BSO attack ASR is low, showing independence.
- **Core assumption:** Causal involvement is reflected in gradient alignment and influence on downstream task failure.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract and Section 6 report ~0.99 cosine and low BSO ASR.
  - Tables 2–3 quantify gradient orthogonality and BSO performance.
- **Break condition:** If probe architecture were deeper or training pressure different, causal links might emerge; joint training and loss weighting experiments showed no effect.

## Foundational Learning
- **Generative sequence modeling (next-token prediction):** Training transformers to predict the next token in a sequence. Needed to understand NT/PD/JP objectives and why NT may fail to distinguish illegal vs. bad moves.
  - *Quick check:* Why might a next-token model trained on high-quality human chess games still predict illegal moves?
- **Linear probing for interpretability:** Adding a linear layer to model representations to predict a target property (board state). Needed to interpret joint probe training and causality analysis.
  - *Quick check:* How does joint probe training differ from training a probe on a frozen model, and what does each approach reveal?
- **Adversarial attack success rate (ASR):** Fraction of adversarial games where the model makes an illegal move. Central evaluation metric.
  - *Quick check:* Why is IMO expected to be stronger than AD or BSO?
- **Chess rules and UCI notation:** Formal move legality, special moves, and tokenization (e.g., `a7 a8 n` for promotion). Defines the world model.
  - *Quick check:* In UCI, how is a pawn promotion to a knight on the same file represented, and why is it three tokens?
- **Gradient cosine similarity:** Measure of alignment between gradient vectors of two losses. Used to assess causal relationship between probe and next-token objectives.
  - *Quick check:* What does a cosine near 1 (orthogonal) imply about the relationship between two tasks' gradients?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
Base model (12-layer transformer decoder, 86M params) -> Tokenizer (UCI notation, square/promotion tokens) -> Training objectives (NT/PD/JP) -> Joint probe head (linear to 13-class per square) -> Adversaries (IMO/BSO/AD/RM/SMM) -> Evaluation (ASR from 1000 warm-up prefixes)

**Critical path:**
1. Prepare chess game data (≤150 moves), tokenize, split train/test.
2. Train base model with chosen objective (NT/PD/JP) for 3 epochs (AdamW, lr 3e-4).
3. For joint probe, attach linear head and train combined loss; otherwise train separate probe on frozen model (AdamW, lr 1e-3, 1 epoch).
4. For 1000 warm-up prefixes, run each adversary to generate full game; record if model ever predicts illegal move.
5. Compute ASR, analyze move-wise dynamics, compute gradient alignments (cosine between ∇L_NT and ∇L_probe), and perform OOD tests.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Model size:** 86M chosen for manageability; larger models might learn better but are costlier.
- **Dataset choice:** Random games explore state space; curated (human) games are longer but include premature endings. Capping at 150 moves biases distribution.
- **Tokenization:** UCI with single-token squares simplifies but promotion splits into three tokens, affecting PD target design.
- **Adversary cost:** IMO is 10–20× slower than AD/BSO due to evaluating many illegal continuations; limits sequence length.
- **Decoding:** Greedy decoding used for determinism; top-k/p sampling yields similar ASR rankings.
- **Probes:** Linear probes are cheap but may be too weak; deeper probes not explored to isolate causal independence.

**Failure signatures:**
- High ASR (≥0.9 for IMO) indicates unsoundness.
- Sharp ASR increase beyond 150 moves indicates overfitting to typical game length.
- Gradient cosine ≈0.99 between next-token and probe losses indicates orthogonal representations.
- Low BSO attack ASR (<0.3) despite probe errors shows probe not aligned with model decisions.
- OOD warm-up increases ASR by up to 0.48, showing reliance on distributional shortcuts.
- Joint probe fails to reduce ASR relative to NT/PD alone.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline soundness test:** Train GPT-2 on Random-500k with NT. Run all five adversaries. Expect IMO ASR near 1.0, confirming universal failure.
2. **Dataset size effect:** Train PD models on Random-500k, 2M, 10M. Evaluate with IMO. Expect ASR decreases with size (e.g., 0.999 → 0.972).
3. **Probe causality check:** Take PD+JP model on Lichess-16M. Compute average cosine(∇L_NT, ∇L_probe) on 10k sequences. Expect ~0.99. Also, for illegal moves predicted by model, check if same move is legal per probe’s board state prediction; expect low agreement (<0.15).

## Limitations
- Tokenization artifacts (promotion as three tokens) may create spurious correlations for adversaries.
- 150-move cap causes overfitting to typical game length; ASR spikes beyond this.
- IMO adversary becomes computationally prohibitive for very long sequences.
- Only linear probes tested; deeper probes might show different causal relationships.
- OOD fragility indicates statistical shortcuts but precise nature of these shortcuts is not mapped.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Adversarial sequences reliably expose soundness failures):** High. Consistent across 24 models, multiple adversaries, datasets; IMO ASR near 1.0.
- **Claim 2 (Larger datasets and PD objective improve soundness):** Medium. Clear trends in tables, but improvements marginal and OOD failure persists.
- **Claim 3 (Board state probes are causally independent):** Medium. Gradient orthogonality and low BSO ASR support independence, but limited to linear probes.

## Next Checks
1. **Tokenization sensitivity test:** Retrain best models (LLaMA, Lichess-16M, PD) using single-token moves (e.g., `e2e4`). Re-run IMO/AD/BSO. If ASR drops significantly, UCI tokenization is a major confounder.
2. **Length distribution ablation:** Train models on datasets truncated at 50, 100, 150 moves. Evaluate ASR on prefixes from 10–200 moves (no 150 cap). If ASR spike aligns with training length ceiling, overfitting to length is primary driver.
3. **Non-linear probe causality:** Attach 2-layer MLP probe (hidden dim=768) to last-layer representations; train jointly with +JP on Lichess-16M. Compute gradient cosine and BSO ASR. If orthogonality decreases (cosine <<0.99) and BSO ASR rises, linear probes understate possible entanglement.
