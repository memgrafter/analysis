# AI Agents as Policymakers in Simulated Epidemics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04245
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04245
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Providing causal knowledge about epidemic feedback loops to a GPT-5 nano-based policymaker agent reduced cumulative infections by about one-third in a simple policy-only environment, and by half when combined with ensemble averaging; however, ensemble alone without knowledge degraded performance.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether generative AI agents can serve as effective policymakers in simulated epidemics. The authors embedded a GPT-5 nano-based agent, prompted as a city mayor, within a mechanistic SEIR disease model. Each week, the agent set business restriction levels based on recent case data and a recency-weighted dynamic memory. The key interventions were: (1) providing a brief systems-level description of epidemic feedback loops (knowledge), and (2) using ensemble averaging across multiple independent agents.

Results show that augmenting the agent with causal knowledge substantially improved decision quality and stability. In an environment where policy was the primary behavioral lever (World 1), knowledge reduced cumulative infections by approximately one-third. Combining knowledge with an ensemble of agents cut cumulative cases by about 50%. In a more complex environment where the public also adapted its behavior voluntarily (World 2), knowledge alone reduced cases by one-third, while the ensemble benefit was smaller due to existing behavioral suppression. Knowledgeable agents implemented restrictions earlier and more consistently, leading to lower transmission and more accurate forecasts. These findings demonstrate that theory-informed prompting can guide generative agents toward human-like reactive policy behavior in dynamic, feedback-rich systems.

## Method Summary
The experimental framework consists of a generative AI agent operating as a policymaker within a simulated SEIR(b) epidemic model. The environment is discretized into daily timesteps, with the agent making weekly decisions (every 7 days starting at week 6). Each decision determines the restriction level \( G \) (0–100%), which modulates the transmission rate. The agent receives as input the weekly mean reported cases and accesses a dynamic memory storing tuples of past decisions and associated case levels, retrieved with a recency bias. The agent's prompt includes a role description, guiding principles, context (last week's cases and decision), a sampled memory block, and optionally a knowledge paragraph explaining epidemic feedback loops. The agent must output JSON containing the new restriction level, case forecasts, and reasoning.

Two simulated worlds were tested: World 1, where policy is the sole behavioral lever; and World 2, where public behavior adapts voluntarily based on case trends (introducing a behavior factor \( b(t) \)). Four agent configurations were evaluated: Base (no knowledge, single agent), Knowledge (with knowledge paragraph, single agent), Ensemble (10 independent agents, averaged outputs, no knowledge), and Ensemble+Knowledge. For each condition, 10 independent simulations (seeds) were run, with primary outcome being cumulative infections over 365 days, alongside forecast accuracy and policy patterns.

## Key Results
- Knowledge augmentation reduced cumulative infections by ~33% in World 1 and by a similar margin in World 2; combined with ensemble, reductions reached ~50% in World 1 and ~33% in World 2.
- Knowledgeable agents acted earlier and more consistently, tightening restrictions when cases rose and maintaining them longer, which lowered transmission and improved forecast accuracy.
- Ensemble averaging without knowledge actually worsened outcomes relative to the Base agent, indicating that averaging amplifies erratic decisions when individual agents lack sound causal reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Knowledge instills a causal mental model
**Claim:** Providing explicit systems-level knowledge about epidemic feedback loops improves decision quality by enabling earlier and more consistent policy intervention.
**Mechanism:** The knowledge paragraph describes reinforcing (infection spread) and balancing (depletion, behavioral response) loops. This scaffolding helps the agent move beyond mere reaction to anticipate downstream effects, leading to proactive tightening of restrictions when cases rise and sustained suppression.
**Core assumption:** The LLM can internalize and apply simplified causal narratives to adjust its decision heuristic.
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes substantial improvement in decision quality and stability; Section P6 provides the knowledge text; Sections P8-9 show earlier transmission reduction in knowledge conditions.
**Break condition:** Omitting the knowledge, mis-specifying it for the environment, or the LLM failing to parse and integrate it.

### Mechanism 2: Ensemble averaging benefits depend on agent knowledge
**Claim:** Ensemble averaging of multiple independent LLM instances only improves outcomes when agents possess accurate causal knowledge; otherwise, it degrades performance.
**Mechanism:** Averaging numeric outputs across stochastic calls smooths out noise. When agents have knowledge, their individual outputs cluster around sound decisions, so averaging yields a robust policy. Without knowledge, individual choices are more arbitrary or extreme; averaging can produce a suboptimal median (e.g., insufficient restriction) that underperforms a single knowledgeable agent.
**Core assumption:** Individual LLM decision outputs are independent noisy samples around a mean that shifts with intervention quality.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports ensemble+knowledge cut cases by 50% while ensemble alone did not; Section P7-8 shows ensemble without knowledge underperforms Base; Sections P9-10 show best outcomes for ensemble with knowledge.
**Break condition:** If LLM calls are highly correlated (low diversity) or knowledge inconsistently guides agents, ensemble yields minimal benefit or harm.

### Mechanism 3: Recency-weighted memory supports adaptive policy
**Claim:** A recency-weighted dynamic memory supports adaptive policy by making recent outcomes salient, reinforcing policy inertia and allowing calibration based on recent trends.
**Mechanism:** The memory stores tuples of (week, restriction level, cases). Stochastic retrieval biased toward recency means the prompt frequently includes recent actions and outcomes. This creates a feedback loop where high cases following low restrictions (or vice versa) are recalled, adjusting future policy. Regression analysis shows strong coefficients for "last week's decision" and "avg cases in memory."
**Core assumption:** The agent uses retrieved memories to implicitly recognize patterns of policy effectiveness, akin to reinforcement learning from historical outcomes.
**Evidence anchors:** Section P5-6 describes the memory architecture with recency weighting; Section P10-11 reports regression results highlighting the influence of recent decisions and recalled severity.
**Break condition:** Rapid environmental changes (non-stationarity) could make recency bias maladaptive; noisy retrieval might fail to surface relevant patterns.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
The system comprises four interacting components: the LLM Core (GPT-5 nano) that processes prompts and outputs decisions in JSON; the Memory Store, a list of (week, restriction level, cases) tuples with recency-weighted retrieval; the Environment Interface, which encapsulates the SEIR(b) model and translates the restriction level into a transmission multiplier; and the Decision Cycle Controller, which orchestrates the weekly cycle by fetching data, constructing the prompt, invoking the LLM, applying the chosen restriction, and updating memory.

### Critical Path
Week w trigger → compute ¯C_{w-1} → sample M_m → build prompt (add knowledge if enabled) → LLMDecision() → get (G_w, ˆC0, ˆC_G, r) → set G_w → run 7-day SEIRStep loop → store (w, G_w, C_t) in M.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Fixed temperature (1.0):** Encourages output diversity for ensemble methods but sacrifices deterministic reproducibility.
- **Knowledge text brevity vs. fidelity:** Concise descriptions improve prompting efficiency but may omit important nuances like time delays or saturation effects, potentially limiting the agent's understanding.
- **Ensemble cost:** Using 10 independent LLM calls per decision week increases computational expense and API costs by an order of magnitude, trading off improved outcomes for practicality.

### Failure Signatures
- **Ensemble without knowledge outperforms Base:** If cumulative cases in the ensemble condition are lower than expected or higher than Base, it suggests that averaging amplified poor decisions; inspect individual agent outputs for high variance.
- **No reaction to rising cases:** Agent maintains G=0 despite clear increase in ¯C; likely due to prompt misconfiguration, missing guiding principle, or memory retrieval failure.
- **Chaotic G fluctuations:** Wild swings in weekly G unrelated to case trends; indicates LLM output parsing errors, inconsistent memory sampling, or prompt instability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** To what extent do the findings generalize across different LLM architectures (e.g., Claude, open-source models) beyond the tested GPT-5 nano?
**Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly state they tested only one LLM (GPT-5 nano) with fixed prompting; systematic comparison across a broader range of models remains an important direction for future research.
**Why unresolved:** Performance may vary due to differences in model training, reasoning capabilities, and response stochasticity. The observed benefits of knowledge and ensemble interventions could be model-specific.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic replication of the experimental design (2 worlds × 4 agent configurations) using multiple state-of-the-art LLMs from different developers, with consistent metrics.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How robust is the "knowledge intervention" to scenarios where the provided causal theory is incomplete or partially incorrect, mimicking real-world policy misinformation?
**Basis in paper:** The authors note as a limitation that their Knowledge intervention provides accurate information about the true causal structure; in practice, policymakers often receive conflicting or incorrect expert advice.
**Why unresolved:** The intervention's efficacy relied on a correct, simplified model of epidemic feedbacks. Real-world policymaking involves navigating ambiguous or competing expert narratives, which could degrade performance or lead to maladaptive policies.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Experimental conditions where the knowledge paragraph includes specific inaccuracies (e.g., wrong sign of a feedback loop) or omits a critical loop, measuring resulting policy decisions and epidemic outcomes against the true optimal.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can generative agent policymakers *learn* or adapt their causal understanding from repeated interaction with the environment, reducing or eliminating the need for explicit initial knowledge?
**Basis in paper:** The study used a static knowledge prompt and a recency-weighted memory that stored outcomes but not updated causal beliefs. The discussion suggests future work could test whether agents can learn to improve their causal models through extended experience.
**Why unresolved:** The memory architecture retrieved past state-action-outcome tuples but did not include a mechanism for the agent to abstract or revise its mental model of system dynamics. The observed reactive behavior may be the limit of what memory alone can achieve.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a reflective or theory-updating mechanism (e.g., prompting the agent to synthesize lessons from memory) and compare long-term performance and policy stability against the static-knowledge and base agents.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** What form of multi-agent aggregation (beyond simple averaging) best mitigates the observed risk of poor individual decisions degrading ensemble performance?
**Basis in paper:** The results show that "ensemble averaging without knowledge actually worsens outcomes by amplifying policy inertia" and that "due to stochasticity in LLM outputs, a single poor decision by one agent... can disproportionately influence the aggregate decision." They used only arithmetic mean averaging.
**Why unresolved:** Simple averaging is vulnerable to outliers. More robust aggregation methods (e.g., median, trimmed mean, weighted by forecast confidence, or a hierarchical deliberation process) might harness the wisdom of crowds without being derailed by erratic outputs.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Re-run the ensemble conditions using various aggregation rules (median, mode, confidence-weighted) and compare aggregate policy stability, epidemiological outcomes, and sensitivity to individual LLM output variance.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** How would the introduction of real-world policymaking constraints (e.g., public opinion, economic backlash, coordination failures) alter the performance gap between knowledgeable and base agents?
**Basis in paper:** The authors acknowledge that their simulation does not capture many constraints that shape real-world policymaking: political feasibility, public opinion backlash, legal and constitutional limitations... Real policymakers cannot simply choose epidemiologically optimal policies.
**Why unresolved:** The agent's sole objective function (implied by the prompt) was balancing cases and the economy without external pressures. Constraints like electoral cycles or lobbying could force suboptimal, unstable policies even with perfect causal knowledge, potentially narrowing the observed benefit of the knowledge intervention.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Augment the agent's environment and reward structure to include simulated public approval ratings, economic metrics with voter sensitivity, or mandated coordination thresholds with other jurisdictions, then re-evaluate the four agent configurations.

## Limitations
- **GPT-5 nano unavailability:** The model used is not publicly documented, preventing direct replication and raising concerns about the generality of results to other LLMs.
- **Incomplete knowledge intervention:** The exact knowledge text for World 1 is not fully provided, requiring inference and risking mis-specification in replications.
- **Undocumented LLM settings:** Besides temperature=1.0, other parameters (top-p, max tokens) and ensemble implementation details are unspecified, which could affect outcomes.
