# Autonomous Agents on Blockchains: Standards, Execution Models, and Trust Boundaries

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.04583
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04583
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Proposes Transaction Intent Schema (TIS) and Policy Decision Record (PDR) as standardized interfaces for secure agent-blockchain integration, alongside a taxonomy and threat model.

## Executive Summary
This survey addresses the critical challenge of connecting autonomous AI agents to blockchain environments, which are immutable and adversarial, in the absence of standardized, secure interfaces. Through a systematic review of 317 works, the authors develop a five-part taxonomy of integration patterns, a tailored threat model covering prompt injection to MEV exploits, and a comparative analysis of over 20 systems across 13 dimensions. The primary contribution is a research roadmap centered on two key abstractions: a Transaction Intent Schema (TIS) for portable goal specification and a Policy Decision Record (PDR) for auditable policy enforcement, plus an evaluation suite to benchmark safety and economic robustness.

## Method Summary
The study employs a systematic literature review following PRISMA guidelines, querying academic databases (IEEE, ACM, Springer), arXiv, and industry standards (EIPs). After deduplication and multi-stage screening of 3,270 records, 317 works were included. A stratified sample of 85 representative systems was coded against 13 attributes (e.g., account model, intent support, custody). Qualitative synthesis of this data produced the taxonomy and threat model, with inter-rater reliability noted but not fully disclosed.

## Key Results
- Five-part taxonomy classifying agent-blockchain integration patterns from read-only analytics to multi-agent workflows.
- Comprehensive threat model addressing classes like prompt injection, middleware manipulation, and MEV exploitation.
- Comparative analysis of 20+ systems (wallets, intent protocols, agent frameworks) across 13 security and capability dimensions.
- Proposed research roadmap emphasizing TIS for portable goal specification and PDR for verifiable authorization.
- Evaluation suite design for measuring safety, reliability, and economic robustness in agent-mediated execution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Transaction Intent Schema (TIS) as a Portable, Deterministic Abstraction
- **Claim:** Separating *what* (outcome) from *how* (execution) reduces agent cognitive load and prevents brittle, protocol-specific transaction construction, directly mitigating Class C2 (tool/data spoofing) and Class C6 (contract/dApp traps).
- **Mechanism:** Agents emit a structured `intent` object (asset, amount, constraints) instead of raw calldata. The canonical, hashed representation binds user goals to downstream checks (simulation, policy, signing), preventing "silent mutation" (Class C3) and enabling reusable, audit-friendly intents.
- **Core assumption:** High-level goals can be unambiguously expressed without protocol-specific details.
- **Break condition:** If an intent cannot express a necessary constraint for a specific DeFi primitive (e.g., concentrated liquidity range), the agent must revert to imperative calls, reintroducing brittleness.

### Mechanism 2: Policy Decision Record (PDR) as a Verifiable Authorization Artifact
- **Claim:** A signed policy attestation (PDR) creates a non-repudiable, auditable separation between *decision* and *execution*, enforcing that a transaction is both (a) approved against a declared policy and (b) unmodified after approval.
- **Mechanism:** The agent submits a TIS to a policy engine, which returns a signed JWT (`PDR`) containing `intent_hash`, `decision` (APPROVE/REJECT), and any `bound_constraints`. The signer (MPC/TEE) verifies the PDR's signature, the TIS hash match, and enforces extended constraints *before* signing. This mitigates Class C3 (middleware manipulation) and limits blast radius from a compromised agent (Class C4).
- **Core assumption:** Policy engines and signers can be trusted to verify and enforce independently; the PDR format is universally accepted.
- **Break condition:** If the PDR is forged, expired, or swapped, the signer's verification fails; if the policy engine is compromised or misconfigured, the PDR itself is malicious.

### Mechanism 3: Defense-in-Depth via Compartmentalized Pipeline (Observer→Reason→Plan→Authorize→Execute→Verify)
- **Claim:** Structuring execution as a phased pipeline with typed artifacts (TIS→PDR→signed tx) and security checkpoints (simulation, policy, custody gating) prevents single-point failures and aligns with the taxonomy's evolutionary pressure toward constrained delegation (Patterns III–V).
- **Mechanism:** Each stage produces a verifiable artifact (e.g., `simulation trace` → `TIS` → `PDR` → `on-chain receipt`). Failures halt progression; no stage can skip constraints enforced later. For example, simulation failures prevent TIS submission; PDR rejection blocks signing. This addresses the "inevitability of compromise" assumption in the threat model.
- **Core assumption:** Each component's implementation is correct and components do not share mutable global state.
- **Break condition:** A component (e.g., simulation service) is wrong but not detected; a policy logic bug (Class C3) allows unauthorized TIS to proceed; or a custody provider (MPC/TEE) is simultaneously compromised with the agent (correlated failure).

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Account Abstraction (ERC-4337)
- **Why needed here:** Enables programmable authorization, making Pattern III (Delegated Execution) feasible. Smart accounts replace all-or-nothing EOA keys with validation modules that enforce time/caps/allowlists *on-chain*, turning signing authority into a constrained capability.
- **Quick check question:** How does `validateUserOp` differ from an EOA’s `sign()` call in terms of what can be programmatically enforced?

### Concept: MEV (Maximal Extractable Value)
- **Why needed here:** Public mempools are adversarial by default. Without MEV-aware execution (private relays, intent auctions), a correctly constructed transaction can still yield a worse outcome via sandwich/front-running—a systemic risk that invalidates naive safety guarantees.
- **Quick check question:** Why is submitting a high-value intent to a public mempool fundamentally different from posting a limit order in a traditional market?

### Concept: Multi-Party Computation (MPC) Threshold Signing
- **Why needed here:** Provides "compromise-expected" custody for Pattern IV (Autonomous Signing). Agent holds one share; other shares reside in distinct, hardened locations. Single-compromise cannot yield a signature, containing blast radius.
- **Quick check question:** What is the trade-off between MPC’s 2-of-3 quorum and a single EOA key in terms of attack surface and operational latency?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Agent Runtime -> TIS -> Policy Engine -> PDR -> Custody & Signer -> Execution Venue (Bundler/Relay) -> Observability & Recovery

### Critical path
1. **Intent Formation:** Agent uses tools to observe state → constructs TIS.
2. **Policy Gate:** TIS → Policy Engine → returns PDR (APPROVE/REJECT + constraints).
3. **Signing:** Signer verifies PDR (signature, hash match, bounds) → produces signature.
4. **Submission:** Bundler/relay submits signed UserOperation/tx to chain.
5. **Verification:** Agent/agent-manager reads receipts, updates state, logs outcome.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Custody:** MPC vs. TEE vs. Smart Account. *Tradeoff:* MPC requires quorum latency; TEE centralizes trust in hardware; Smart Accounts are transparent but expensive and complex.
- **Policy:** On-chain (deterministic, costly) vs. off-chain (agile, requires trust). *Tradeoff:* Off-chain enables rich context but introduces a trust boundary; a compromised off-chain engine issues bad PDRs.
- **Execution:** Public mempool (simple) vs. private relay/solver (MEV-protected but introduces centralization/privacy tradeoffs).
- **Intent scope:** General vs. domain-specific. *Tradeoff:* General schemas reduce integration cost but may lack expressiveness for complex DeFi operations (e.g., multihop with custom routers).

### Failure signatures
- **Simulation-revert mismatch:** Simulation success but on-chain revert → likely state drift or invalid actor (Class C2/C6).
- **PDR rejected by signer:** TIS altered after policy check, or PDR expired/forged (Class C3).
- **MEV loss without approval:** Transaction executed at worse price than simulation despite PDR approval → mempool leakage or lack of private orderflow (Class C5).
- **Agent proposes same tx after PDR rejection:** Agent loop ignoring policy → may indicate prompt injection or bug (Class C1).
- **Signing quorum timeout:** MPC share offline or network partition → liveness failure, not safety, but may force fallback to unsafe channel.

### First 3 experiments
1. **TIS Robustness Test:** Generate 100 valid swapper intents; mutate one field (e.g., token address → phishing address). Verify policy engine catches and PDR is REJECT. Measures sensitivity to Class C2/C3.
2. **Policy Enforcement Stress Test:** Configure module with strict allowlist. Agent tries to call a non-whitelisted high-value function via TIS. Verify PDR REJECT and signer rejects even if PDR forged. Tests module correctness and PDR verification (Class C3).
3. **MEV Differential Under Submission Paths:** Execute identical large swap TIS via (a) public RPC, (b) Flashbots Protect, (c) intent protocol (CoW). Compare realized price vs. simulation. Quantifies effect of Class C5 mitigation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can we guarantee that non-deterministic LLM agents never violate formally specified on-chain safety constraints?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 12.1.1 identifies the "Agent-Contract Impedance Mismatch" as a core open problem.
- **Why unresolved:** The probabilistic nature of agent reasoning fundamentally conflicts with the deterministic, immutable semantics of blockchain execution; no general compositional verification framework exists to ensure safety envelopes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A formally verified end-to-end compilation pipeline from high-level intents (e.g., TIS) to on-chain transactions, together with extensive empirical evaluation on high-value DeFi workflows showing zero safety violations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What are the systemic economic risks that emerge when many autonomous, profit-seeking agents interact in blockchain markets?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 12.1.2 addresses "The Economics of Agentic Systems" and its open challenges.
- **Why unresolved:** Current models cannot capture emergent coordination, herding, or adversarial dynamics among thousands of agents; this may lead to volatility, manipulation, or cascading failures under stress.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A large-scale multi-agent simulation platform that integrates realistic blockchain dynamics, validated against mainnet data, along with stress tests that quantify systemic fragility and identify mitigation mechanisms.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Who is legally liable when an autonomous blockchain agent causes harm, and how can technical systems enforce that accountability?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 12.1.3 discusses "Governance, Regulation, and Legal Liability" as an open question.
- **Why unresolved:** Traditional legal concepts of agency break down with autonomous actors; the chain of control is diffused across developers, policy issuers, signing services, and the agent itself, making responsibility unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Judicial precedents or regulatory frameworks that recognize provenance artifacts (e.g., TIS, PDR) as admissible evidence, coupled with on-chain insurance/escrow contracts that automatically compensate on verifiable policy breaches.

## Limitations
- Full corpus of 317 works and the stratified sample of 85 systems are not publicly listed, introducing selection bias risk.
- The reproducibility package is referenced but unseen, hindering verification of screening rubrics, inter-rater reliability, and coding decisions.
- Proposed TIS and PDR abstractions lack empirical validation; their security efficacy and expressiveness in complex DeFi workflows remain theoretical.

## Confidence
- **Taxonomy & Threat Model:** High (derived from systematic review of 317 works with transparent methodology).
- **Defense-in-Depth Pipeline:** High (strong support from neighboring corpus evidence on multi-stage security layers).
- **TIS/PDR Efficacy:** Medium/Low (mechanistically sound but reliant on unvalidated assumptions; weak neighboring support).

## Next Checks
