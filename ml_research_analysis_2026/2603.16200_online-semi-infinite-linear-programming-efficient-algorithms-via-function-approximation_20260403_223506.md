# Online Semi-infinite Linear Programming: Efficient Algorithms via Function Approximation

## Quick Facts

- arXiv ID: 2603.16200  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16200  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Reduces online semi-infinite linear programming to a constant‑q dimensional problem and achieves O(q√T) regret independent of constraint cardinality.

## Executive Summary

The paper studies the Online Semi‑infinite Linear Programming (OSILP) problem, where a decision maker repeatedly chooses a finite‑dimensional action while facing a continuously large (potentially infinite) set of constraints that are revealed one by one. The key challenge is to achieve sublinear regret without having to process each constraint individually. The proposed solution approximates the infinite‑dimensional dual variable using a small set of non‑negative basis functions, thereby reducing the effective dimensionality to a constant q. Based on this approximation, a dual‑based mirror descent algorithm updates only a q‑dimensional weight vector. Under stochastic or random‑permutation input models, the algorithm attains O(q√T) regret and O(q²√T) constraint violation. With additional geometric assumptions (Global Polyhedral Growth and an error bound), a two‑stage accelerate‑then‑refine procedure further improves the regret to O(q log T + q/ε). Theoretical and empirical results demonstrate the method’s scalability as the number of constraints grows.

## Method Summary

The core idea is to replace the true dual measure u ∈ ℝ^{m}_{≥0} (m may be infinite) by an approximant u(z) ≈ Φ(z) w, where Φ is a fixed matrix of non‑negative basis functions (e.g., Gaussian radial basis functions) and w ≥ 0 is a low‑dimensional weight vector. This reduces the infinite‑dimensional dual problem to a q‑dimensional one. The primal decision x_t is derived from complementary slackness: x_t = 1 if reward r_t exceeds the predicted constraint satisfaction a_t^⊤ Φ w_t, else 0. The weight vector w_t is then updated using mirror descent on the approximate dual objective f_Φ(w). For special settings, an accelerated two‑stage algorithm first quickly drives w near the optimal w^* (stage 1, using a shrinking projection ball and virtual decisions to maintain feasibility) and then refines it with small stepsizes (stage 2). The approaches rely on boundedness assumptions for data and iterates, and the two‑stage variant further requires a Global Polyhedral Growth condition and a H"older error bound.

## Key Results

- Under a stochastic input model: O(q√T) regret and O(q²√T) constraint violation (Theorem 1).  
- Under a random permutation of the inputs: O((q + q log T)√T) regret and similar violation (Theorem 3).  
- Under Global Polyhedral Growth and an error bound (stochastic model): an accelerate‑then‑refine algorithm attains O(q log T + q/ε) regret (Theorem 6).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Dimensionality Reduction via Non‑Negative Basis Approximation
The algorithm approximates the true dual variable u (a non‑negative measure) as Φw, where Φ ∈ ℝ^{m×q}_{≥0} contains fixed basis functions and w ∈ ℝ^{q}_{≥0}. This projection reduces the effective number of constraints to a constant q, enabling sublinear regret independent of the original constraint cardinality. The approximate dual objective f_Φ(w) = d^⊤Φw + E[(r - a^⊤Φw)_+] serves as an upper bound on the true dual value f(u^*). The approximation error is controlled by the richness of Φ; the analysis assumes the optimal dual measure lies well within the span of Φ.  
*Break condition:* If the true optimal dual measure lies outside the affine span of Φ, the approximation error may be large and could dominate the regret bound.

### Mechanism 2: Dual‑Based Mirror Descent on Reduced Space
Given the q‑dimensional representation, the algorithm updates w_t via mirror descent. At each round t, a subgradient g_t = d^⊤Φ - a_t^⊤Φ x_t is computed, and w_{t+1} = argmin_{w≥0} ⟨γ_t g_t, w⟩ + D_ψ(w||w_t). The step size γ_t = 1/√T balances exploration and exploitation. Under bounded subgradients and a bounded dual iterate set (Assumptions 1 and 2), the cumulative regret from updating only q variables is O(q√T) in expectation for stochastic data and O((q+q log T)√T) under random permutations.  
*Break condition:* If the dual iterates w_t become unbounded or subgradients are not bounded, Assumptions 1–2 fail and the O(q√T) bound may not hold.

### Mechanism 3: Accelerate‑Then‑Refine for Logarithmic Regret
When the problem satisfies the Global Polyhedral Growth (GPG) assumption and a H"older error bound, a two‑stage algorithm can exploit faster convergence near the optimum. Stage 1 uses an accelerated mirror descent method with a shrinking projection ball and virtual decisions to keep the iterate within an ε‑neighborhood of w^* in O(log T) rounds. Stage 2 then applies tiny steps (γ_t ≤ 1/T) to refine the solution, incurring only O(q/ε) additional regret. The total regret is O(q log T + q/ε), which for suitable ε is asymptotically smaller than the O(√T) baseline.  
*Break condition:* The GPG assumption requires the optimal dual basis to remain stable under small RHS perturbations; it may fail near degenerate faces. The error bound must also hold, otherwise the accelerated convergence is not guaranteed.

## Foundational Learning

- **Semi‑Infinite Linear Programming (SILP):** An LP with infinitely many constraints, often indexed by a continuous parameter. The dual variable is a non‑negative measure rather than a finite vector.  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s problem is an online version of SILP, requiring understanding of the dual structure and its approximation.  
  *Quick check:* What distinguishes the dual variable of an SILP from that of a standard LP? (Answer: it is a measure, not a finite‑dimensional vector.)

- **Online Convex Optimization with Long‑Term Constraints (OCO‑wC):** A framework where an agent makes decisions sequentially while incurring convex losses and violating constraints only in a long‑term average sense.  
  *Why needed:* The OSILP problem can be viewed as an OCO‑wC instance with infinitely many constraints; the algorithm borrows virtual‑decision techniques from OCO‑wC to maintain feasibility.  
  *Quick check:* How does this paper’s constraint violation metric differ from standard OCO‑wC? (Answer: Violation is measured via a dual‑norm maximization that aggregates constraints using the same basis functions, rather than summing or taking a supremum.)

- **Mirror Descent and Bregman Divergence:** An iterative first‑order optimization method that uses a distance‑generating function to update decisions in a way that respects constraints.  
  *Why needed:* The core update on the dual weight w_t is a mirror descent step; the Bregman divergence handles the non‑negativity constraint naturally under Euclidean or entropic potentials.  
  *Quick check:* Why might mirror descent be preferred over plain projected gradient descent here? (Answer: It allows flexibility in the choice of norm/potential and leads to cleaner analysis when dealing with non‑Euclidean projections.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Observed (r_t, a_t) -> Basis Function Module (Φ) -> Reduced feature a_t^⊤Φ -> Decision Module (x_t = I(r_t > a_t^⊤Φ w_t)) -> Subgradient Module (g_t = d^⊤Φ - a_t^⊤Φ x_t) -> Dual Weight Update (Mirror Descent) -> w_{t+1}. Optional Virtual Budget Module tracks B_t for feasibility during two‑stage acceleration.

### Critical Path
1. Initialise w_1 = 0; choose basis Φ (size q) and step sizes.  
2. For t = 1 to T:  
   - Observe (r_t, a_t).  
   - Compute p_t = a_t^⊤Φ w_t.  
   - Set x_t = 1 if r_t > p_t else 0.  
   - Compute subgradient g_t = d^⊤Φ - a_t^⊤Φ x_t.  
   - Update w_{t+1} via mirror descent: w_{t+1} = argmin_{w≥0} ⟨γ_t g_t, w⟩ + D_ψ(w||w_t).  
3. (Two‑stage) In stage 1, use virtual decisions and a projected accelerated update to obtain ̃w^L near w^*; stage 2 refines from ̃w^L with smaller step sizes.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Basis Φ selection:** Larger q improves approximation of the true dual but increases per‑step computation and regret constant. Too small q may cause high constraint violation due to poor approximation.
- **Step size γ_t:** O(1/√T) yields O(√T) regret uniformly; smaller steps in the refine stage can achieve O(1/ε) locally but require a good initial point.
- **Assumption reliance:** Regret bounds of O(q√T) require bounded dual iterates (Assumption 2) and subgradients. The O(q log T) improvement additionally needs GPG and the H"older error bound, which may not hold for degenerate LPs.

### Failure Signatures
- High constraint violation despite low regret suggests Φ is too coarse; increases q or adjusts RBF widths.  
- Exploding dual weights w_t indicate poor scaling of Φ or violated boundedness assumptions; regularisation may be needed.  
- Two‑stage algorithm fails to improve rates if GPG doesn’t hold (e.g., d near a degenerate face); test by measuring sensitivity of w^* to small perturbations in d.  
- Many virtual decisions rejected in stage 1 (x_t = 0) points to over‑aggressive virtual constraint checking; adjust initial budget B_1 or scaling of Φ.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Basis function sweep:** Vary q (e.g., 5, 10, 20) on a fixed instance; plot regret and violation to observe the trade‑off between approximation quality and computational cost.  
2. **Constraint scaling:** Fix q and increase the number of true constraints m (or the complexity of the constraint index space). Compare the proposed algorithms against a baseline that updates per‑constraint dual variables; confirm that regret becomes independent of m.  
3. **Assumption stress test (GPG):** Construct an instance where d lies arbitrarily close to a degenerate face to break GPG. Run the two‑stage algorithm and check whether the O(q log T + q/ε) rate degrades to O(√T).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How does the choice and quality of non‑negative basis functions (e.g., RBF parameters) systematically affect the regret bound and approximation error in practice, beyond the theoretical O(q√T) guarantee?  
- The paper uses fixed Gaussian RBFs but provides no analysis linking basis design to approximation error or empirical performance; experiments only vary the number of constraints, not basis parameters.

### Open Question 2
Under what sufficient conditions do the Global Polyhedral Growth (GPG) and H"older Error Bound assumptions hold for general semi‑infinite LPs, and how restrictive are they in typical applications (e.g., robust optimization, resource allocation)?  
- The assumptions are stated but not verified for concrete problem classes; understanding their prevalence is crucial for the two‑stage algorithm’s applicability.

### Open Question 3
Does the proposed dual‑based constraint violation metric (weighted by basis functions) accurately reflect true feasibility of the original infinite constraints, and how does it compare to alternative feasibility measures (e.g., supremum norm over a discretization)?  
- The metric may miss violations outside the span of Φ; no comparison to a ground‑truth discretization is provided.

### Open Question 4
Does the O(q log T) regret extend to the random permutation model, or what fundamental limitation prevents a similar improvement there?  
- The two‑stage analysis is limited to the stochastic model; it is unclear whether the improvement can be achieved under adversarial orderings.

### Open Question 5
How does the algorithm perform in the general function setting (Section 7) on nonlinear problems, and do the O(q√T) and O(q log T) bounds remain tight or require different basis function constructions?  
- The paper extends the theory to concave/convex non‑linearities but offers no experiments; nonlinear g(x;θ) may demand more expressive basis sets.

## Limitations

- The dual approximation assumes the optimal measure lies in the span of the fixed non‑negative basis Φ; this is a strong structural assumption that, if violated, could lead to hidden dependence on problem difficulty.  
- The accelerated two‑stage rate relies on Global Polyhedral Growth and the H"older error bound, conditions that are non‑trivial and limit the scope where logarithmic regret is achievable.  
- The constraint violation metric is defined via a dual‑norm maximization and may not directly control pointwise constraint satisfaction; its tightness relative to actual feasibility remains unclear.

## Confidence

- Dual approximation quality: Medium  
- Accelerated rate assumptions: Low  
- Constraint violation definition: Medium
