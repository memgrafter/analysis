# Need for Speed: Zero-Shot Depth Completion with Single-Step Diffusion

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10584
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10584
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Marigold-SSD achieves a 66× speedup (0.42s vs 27.49s on RTX 4090) over Marigold-DC while improving accuracy (KITTI RMSE 1.496 vs 1.676); training requires only 4.5 GPU days.

## Executive Summary
Marigold-SSD addresses the computational bottleneck of diffusion-based depth completion by enabling single-step inference through a specialized fine-tuning paradigm. Building on the Marigold-E2E model, it introduces a late-fusion conditional decoder that injects sparse depth measurements at multiple scales, allowing the diffusion prior to be used without iterative denoising at test time. This shifts the computational burden from inference to training, resulting in a 66× speedup on an RTX 4090 (from 27.49s to 0.42s per image) while also achieving higher accuracy on zero-shot benchmarks. With only 4.5 GPU days of training on synthetic data (Hypersim and Virtual KITTI), the method narrows the efficiency gap between diffusion and discriminative models and demonstrates robust generalization across varying sparsity levels.

## Method Summary
Marigold-SSD initializes from a pre-trained Marigold-E2E depth estimator. The VAE decoder is replaced with a trainable conditional decoder that fuses features from the predicted depth latent and a sparse depth condition processed by a feature extractor (weights re-used from the VAE encoder). Fusion is performed at five decoder levels via 1×1 convolutions initialized as zero (ControlNet-style), preserving the original decoder behavior at start. During fine-tuning, the UNet and the new decoder are trained with an L1 loss on dense depth, while the VAE encoder remains frozen. For a given RGB image, the sparse depth is normalized and passed through the extractor to obtain multi-scale features. The UNet is run once with zero noise and the final diffusion timestep to directly predict the clean depth latent. This latent is decoded with the conditional decoder to produce a dense depth estimate, which is then aligned to the sparse metric measurements via least-squares scale and shift. Training uses randomly sampled sparsity densities within a range (e.g., [0.16%, 5%]) to promote robustness.

## Key Results
- 66× speedup: average inference time reduced from 27.49s to 0.42s on an RTX 4090 (no ensembling).
- Accuracy gains: on KITTI, RMSE 1.496 and MAE 0.454 vs. Marigold-DC’s 1.676 and 0.640; on average across six zero‑shot benchmarks, Marigold-SSD attains best or second‑best RMSE/MAE.
- Training efficiency: fine‑tuning requires only 4.5 GPU days on a single H100.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Shifting computational burden to fine‑tuning**  
The model leverages a pre‑trained diffusion depth prior and adapts it for single‑step inference by setting the noise to zero and the timestep to the final step. This collapses the iterative denoising process into a single forward pass, drastically reducing inference time while preserving robustness. *Break condition:* mismatched fine‑tuning data (domain or sparsity distribution) can harm zero‑shot generalization.

**Mechanism 2: Late‑fusion conditional decoder with zero‑initialization**  
The decoder mirrors the VAE decoder’s multi‑scale structure. Sparse depth is processed by a feature extractor (initialized from the VAE encoder) and fused with decoder features at each level via 1×1 convolutions initialized to zero. At initialization the condition path contributes nothing, preserving original decoder behavior; during fine‑tuning the fusion layers gradually learn to incorporate the sparse measurements effectively. *Break condition:* extremely sparse (<0.16%) or out‑of‑distribution conditions may be ignored or cause hallucinations.

**Mechanism 3: Variable sparsity training and direct L1 supervision**  
During fine‑tuning, the sparsity density of the condition is randomly sampled (e.g., [0.16%, 5%]), exposing the model to diverse patterns and promoting adaptive conditioning. The L1 loss directly penalizes absolute depth error, avoiding diffusion‑loss scale ambiguities and ensuring precise metric predictions. This yields robust performance across a wide sparsity range and outperforms iterative diffusion at very low sample counts (e.g., 500 points). *Break condition:* if the training sparsity range does not overlap with the test conditions (e.g., indoor‑only training vs. outdoor testing), performance degrades sharply.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Frozen VAE encoder (E) → latent m; Denoising UNet (from Marigold) takes x_T=0, t=T, condition m → predicts v_t → compute clean depth latent x_0; Condition feature extractor (F) processes normalized sparse depth C into multi‑scale features {f_l^F}; Conditional decoder D_C,ϕ fuses x_0’s decoder features with {f_l^F} at five levels (via zero‑init 1×1 convs) → dense depth estimate D_hat; Scale‑shift alignment (least‑squares) → final metric depth D*.

**Critical path**  
1. Encode RGB M: m = E(M) (E frozen).  
2. Set noise tensor x_T = 0; run UNet once with t=T and condition m to predict velocity v_t.  
3. Compute clean latent: x_0 = -√(1‑α_T) * v_t (since x_T=0).  
4. Normalize sparse depth C to [-1,1].  
5. Extract condition features: {f_l^F} = F(C).  
6. Decode: D_hat = D_C,ϕ(x_0, {f_l^F}) through sequential fusion blocks.  
7. Perform scale‑shift alignment: solve a,b in D* = a·D_hat + b matching sparse C*.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Late vs. early fusion:* Late fusion leverages high‑level features and preserves diffusion prior; early fusion risks interference.  
- *Zero‑initialization:* Stabilizes fine‑tuning by starting from original decoder behavior but may slow initial condition learning.  
- *Training sparsity range:* Wider range (0.16%–5%) improves generalization across densities; narrower range may specialize but harm cross‑dataset performance.  
- *Single‑step vs. multi‑step:* Single‑step sacrifices some fine details for massive speedup; trade‑off mitigated by fine‑tuning.

**Failure signatures**  
- Extreme sparsity (<0.16%): model may ignore condition and revert to monocular estimation, causing scale errors.  
- Domain mismatch (e.g., synthetic training vs. real outdoor): bias in sky or distant objects, as noted in the paper.  
- Scale ambiguity when depth range is far from training distribution; least‑squares alignment may become unstable.  
- Over‑smoothing: compared to Marigold‑DC, outputs may lack fine structures (see Figure 4).

**First 3 experiments**  
1. *Ablation on fusion strategy (Table 4):* Compare late‑fusion against early‑fusion baselines (Frozen VAE and Conditional Encoder) across datasets; late‑fusion consistently superior.  
2. *Evaluation under varying sparsity (Figures 6,7):* Test at multiple sparsity levels (150, 500, 1500, 15360 points) on indoor/outdoor datasets; at low densities (500 points), Marigold‑SSD outperforms Marigold‑DC and interpolation.  
3. *Zero‑shot benchmark comparison (Table 3):* Report MAE/RMSE on six datasets against discriminative and diffusion baselines; Marigold‑SSD achieves best or second‑best average while being 66× faster than Marigold‑DC.

## Limitations
- Missing architectural details for the conditional decoder (exact ResNet block counts, channel dimensions) hinder exact reproduction.  
- Sparse depth simulation protocol (sampling pattern, density distribution) is unspecified, affecting robustness evaluation.  
- Performance degrades under extreme sparsity (below training minimum), domain shift from synthetic training data, and scale instability in alignment.
