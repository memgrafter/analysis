# Weight Tying Biases Token Embeddings Towards the Output Space

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.26663
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26663
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: Weight tying biases shared token embeddings toward the output space due to gradient imbalance, leading to early-layer representational mismatches.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the mechanism behind weight tying in transformer language models, where the token embedding matrix is shared with the output unembedding matrix. Through a combination of alignment analysis, tuned lens evaluation, and gradient tracking, the authors demonstrate that weight tying causes the shared embedding to become biased toward the output space. This bias arises because gradients from the output pathway dominate those from the input pathway during early training, shaping the shared matrix for output prediction at the expense of input representation quality. Consequently, early transformer layers exhibit higher tuned lens KL divergence, indicating less compatibility with the final output distribution. Causal interventions that scale input gradients confirm the gradient imbalance mechanism and show that rebalancing can shift the embedding structure toward input-like space, albeit with reduced output alignment.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a comparative analysis between models with tied and untied embeddings across several architectures (OLMo, GPT-Neo/Pythia, Qwen3). They measure geometric alignment between embedding matrices using cosine similarity after orthogonal Procrustes transformation. To assess layer-wise compatibility with the output space, they train affine translators (tuned lens) for each layer and compute residual KL divergence. They track gradient norms from input and output pathways during early training to quantify gradient dominance. Finally, they perform a causal intervention by scaling input gradients during training and observe changes in alignment and tuned lens metrics.

## Key Results
- In tied models, the shared embedding matrix aligns more closely with the untied output matrix (cosine similarity ~0.72) than with the untied input matrix (~0.53) after Procrustes alignment.
- Tied models exhibit significantly higher tuned lens KL divergence in early layers (e.g., >2 bits more than untied in OLMo-1B), indicating poorer representational compatibility.
- Gradient tracking shows output-layer gradients contribute about 70% of the total gradient norm to the shared matrix in the first 1000 training steps, confirming gradient imbalance.
- Scaling input gradients during training reduces the bias toward output space, as evidenced by lower alignment with output and higher alignment with input, but at the cost of degraded output alignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** In weight-tied models, the shared embedding matrix is shaped primarily by output-layer gradients during early training.
- **Mechanism:** Gradients from the output projection pathway (directly connected to the cross-entropy loss) are stronger than those from the input embedding pathway, causing the shared parameters to optimize for output prediction at the expense of input representation.
- **Core assumption:** Gradient flows from input and output pathways accumulate independently into the shared matrix without interference.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "output gradients dominate early in training"
  - [section 5.3]: Gradient tracking on OLMo-1B shows output-layer gradients account for ~70% of total gradient norm in first 1000 steps.
  - [corpus]: Limited direct evidence; corpus paper "Headless Language Models" (arXiv:2309.08351) explores contrastive weight tying but does not address gradient imbalance mechanisms.
- **Break condition:** Scaling input gradients (e.g., 5×) during training reduces the bias, shifting embedding structure toward input-like space but at the cost of output alignment.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Tied models exhibit higher tuned-lens KL divergence in early layers, indicating that early-layer representations are less compatible with the output space.
- **Mechanism:** Because the shared matrix is optimized for output prediction, the input representations fed to early transformer layers are suboptimal for internal computations, requiring later layers to compensate for this initial mismatch.
- **Core assumption:** Tuned lens residual KL divergence measures how easily a layer's representations can be translated to the final output distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "negatively affects early-layer computations"
  - [section 4.2]: OLMo-1B tied vs. untied shows >2 bits higher KL divergence at input layer; similar patterns in Pythia/GPT-Neo and Qwen3.
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence; related work on anisotropy (e.g., Machina and Mercer, 2024) suggests untied embeddings yield more uniform representations.
- **Break condition:** Untying embeddings allows input matrix to evolve independently, reducing early-layer KL divergence as shown in model comparisons.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Tied embedding matrices geometrically align more closely with untied output matrices than with untied input matrices.
- **Mechanism:** Due to gradient dominance, the shared matrix's learned structure resembles that of an output projection matrix, which is directly optimized for token discrimination, rather than a dedicated input matrix optimized for semantic representation.
- **Core assumption:** Linear transformations (e.g., Procrustes alignment) can meaningfully compare representational spaces by accounting for basis differences.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "aligned more closely with output (unembedding) matrices"
  - [section 4.1]: For OLMo-1B, aligned cosine similarity: Output(U)→Tied (0.719) vs. Input(U)→Tied (0.525); similar trends in KNN overlap and spectral distance.
  - [corpus]: Weak; corpus lacks direct alignment studies, but cross-lingual embedding alignment literature (e.g., Conneau et al., 2020) provides methodological context.
- **Break condition:** If training dynamics were altered to balance input/output gradients equally, alignment with both spaces might become comparable, but weight tying forces a trade-off.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Key components: token embedding matrix (WE), unembedding matrix (WU), gradient hooks (for tracking/scaling), tuned lens translators (affine maps per layer), training checkpoints, and evaluation benchmarks (e.g., WikiText-2, PIQA).
- **Critical path:** The analysis proceeds as: (1) Compare geometric alignment of tied vs. untied embeddings (Section 4.1). (2) Measure tuned lens KL divergence across layers to detect early-layer mismatch (Section 4.2). (3) Track gradient norms during training to confirm output dominance (Section 5.3). (4) Causal intervention via input gradient scaling to test mechanism (Section 6).
- **Design tradeoffs:** Weight tying saves parameters (significant for small models, e.g., 73% in Pythia-70M) but introduces representational cost: the shared matrix compromises between input and output roles. At scale (>1B), parameter savings diminish while cost persists, favoring untied embeddings.
- **Failure signatures:** Elevated early-layer tuned lens KL divergence (>2 bits gap); structural alignment closer to output (cosine similarity >0.65) than input (<0.55); output gradients contributing ~70% of total gradient norm in early steps.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Embedding alignment experiment:** Compute mean cosine similarity after Procrustes alignment between tied embedding and untied input/output matrices across model families (OLMo, GPT-Neo/Pythia).
  2. **Tuned lens analysis:** Train per-layer affine translators and measure residual KL divergence; compare tied vs. untied models to detect early-layer penalties.
  3. **Gradient flow tracking:** Instrument training with gradient hooks to log L2 norms from input/output pathways; quantify relative contributions in early steps.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can targeted gradient rebalancing interventions (beyond constant scaling) resolve the trade-off between input representation quality and output prediction efficacy in weight-tied models?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "Future work could explore whether more targeted interventions, beyond simple constant scaling, can navigate this trade-off."
- **Why unresolved:** Simple gradient scaling shifts embeddings toward input-like structure but harms output alignment and shows no consistent downstream gains. It remains unknown if more sophisticated methods (e.g., adaptive scaling, phased schedules) can mitigate the representational cost without losing output performance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic evaluation of diverse intervention strategies (e.g., layer-wise scaling, curriculum-based rebalancing, auxiliary losses) on embedding alignment (cosine similarity, tuned lens KL), training dynamics (gradient norms), and downstream task performance across model scales.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent are the observed unembedding biases and early-layer penalties robust across different random seeds, training datasets, and longer training trajectories?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors note: "we test only a single training run... therefore we were not able to conduct statistical tests across multiple training runs, random seeds, etc." and that models were trained on different datasets (OLMo) or closed data (Qwen).
- **Why unresolved:** Single-run analyses may overestimate effect sizes or miss variability. The findings could be sensitive to initialization, data distribution, or training duration, limiting generalizability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication experiments with multiple seeds for tied/untied pairs on shared datasets (e.g., Pythia setup), measuring consistency of alignment metrics, tuned lens divergence, and gradient dominance patterns across runs and checkpoints.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the representational costs of weight tying generalize to architectures beyond standard autoregressive transformers, such as Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), non-autoregressive, or encoder-decoder models?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors state their analysis "may not generalize to architectures with substantially different designs, such as mixture-of-experts models or non-autoregressive language models."
- **Why unresolved:** The gradient imbalance mechanism might interact differently with sparse activations (MoE), bidirectional context (encoders), or decoding strategies. The structural role of embeddings could vary.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the same analysis pipeline (alignment, tuned lens, gradient tracking) to MoE (e.g., Mixtral), encoder-decoder (T5), and non-autoregressive models, comparing tied vs. untied variants where available or training controlled pairs.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the precise parameter-scale threshold where the representational cost of weight tying outweighs its parameter savings, and how does this interact with other efficiency techniques (e.g., grouped-query attention, KV caching)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper discusses that at >1B parameters, efficiency gains vanish while costs remain, and notes Qwen3 ties only smaller models. However, no quantitative threshold or interaction analysis is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** The trade-off depends on vocabulary size, hidden dimension, and total parameter count. Interactions with other efficiency optimizations are unexplored. The "optimal" tying decision may be more nuanced.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic scaling study across wide range of model sizes (e.g., 100M to 100B), varying vocabulary sizes, with and without other optimizations, measuring total parameters, embedding-related metrics (alignment, KL), and end-task performance to derive cost-benefit curves.

## Limitations
- Evidence is primarily from OLMo-1B; limited diversity across architectures and scales.
- Compute-intensive experiments (training from scratch, gradient tracking) hinder reproducibility.
- Tuned lens and alignment metrics rely on strong methodological assumptions (e.g., linear translatability, convergence of affine fits).
- Long-term training dynamics beyond early steps are not thoroughly investigated.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Gradient imbalance): Medium-High
- Mechanism 2 (Early-layer tuned lens KL): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (Alignment with output space): Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture gradient dominance:** Track input/output gradient norms during early training for at least three additional model families (e.g., Llama, GPT-2, Mistral) across a range of sizes (100M–10B) to confirm universality.
2. **Vocabulary size ablation:** Train tied models with fixed parameter count but varying vocabulary sizes (e.g., 10k, 50k, 100k) and measure alignment and tuned lens KL; test whether bias magnitude correlates with vocabulary size.
3. **Downstream impact with intervention:** Compare fine-tuning performance on GLUE and arithmetic reasoning tasks for tied models trained with/without input gradient scaling, to evaluate whether reducing bias improves generalization or alters failure modes.
