# MSSR: Memory-Aware Adaptive Replay for Continual LLM Fine-Tuning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09892
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09892
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: MSSR improves average accuracy by up to 1.7 percentage points and reduces forgetting by over 30% compared to prior replay methods.

## Executive Summary
Continual fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs) suffers from catastrophic forgetting, where models lose performance on previously learned tasks. Existing replay-based mitigation strategies are often heuristic or computationally expensive. This paper introduces MSSR, a memory-aware adaptive replay framework that combines a per-sample hazard-based retention estimator with an adaptive scheduler inspired by the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve. MSSR enables principled, efficient lifelong fine-tuning by dynamically determining which past samples to replay and when.

Evaluated on three model families (Qwen2.5-7B, LLaMA-3.1-8B, Gemma2-9B) across up to 11 sequential tasks, MSSR consistently outperforms prior replay baselines, achieving up to 1.7 percentage points higher average accuracy (e.g., +2.2% on GSM8K, +10.8% on ARC) and reducing forgetting by more than 30% (e.g., from 4.8% to 2.4%). Ablations confirm the cognitive-inspired scheduling yields robust long-term retention with negligible overhead (<5% compute, <6% memory). MSSR provides a practical, theory-grounded approach to continual LLM fine-tuning.

## Method Summary
MSSR addresses catastrophic forgetting through two tightly integrated components: a per-sample estimator and an adaptive scheduler. The estimator models retention using a hazard function derived from loss dynamics, identifying samples at high risk of forgetting. The scheduler, inspired by the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve, determines replay frequency (expanding intervals) and proportion (decaying ratio) based on retention signals. Together they enable memory-efficient, adaptive selection of past data for replay, avoiding heuristic or fixed schedules. The framework integrates into standard fine-tuning pipelines with minimal added cost.

## Key Results
- **Accuracy gains:** Up to 1.7 percentage points average improvement across tasks; specific gains include +2.2% on GSM8K and +10.8% on ARC.
- **Forgetting reduction:** More than 30% reduction in forgetting (e.g., forgetting drop lowered from 4.8% to 2.4%).
- **Efficiency:** Negligible overhead—less than 5% additional compute and under 6% extra memory.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MSSR’s effectiveness arises from its fine-grained, data-centric replay strategy. The hazard-based estimator quantifies each sample’s forgetting risk, allowing the system to prioritize the most vulnerable samples for replay. The adaptive scheduler then modulates replay intensity over time: as retention improves, intervals between replays expand and the replay ratio decays, mirroring the consolidation phase of human memory. This combination prevents both under-replay (leading to forgetting) and over-replay (wasting resources), achieving a superior plasticity-stability trade-off with low overhead.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Task Stream -> Hazard Estimator -> Retention Scores -> Adaptive Scheduler -> Replay Buffer -> Fine-Tuning Updates

**Critical Path:**  
1. New task data arrives; model fine-tunes on current batch.  
2. Hazard estimator updates retention probabilities for all stored samples (including current batch) based on loss changes.  
3. Adaptive scheduler uses retention scores and Ebbinghaus model to compute replay interval and ratio for the next step.  
4. Replay buffer selects a subset of past samples according to scheduler’s instructions (low retention, appropriate ratio).  
5. Model updates on combined current and replay data.  
6. Loop back to step 2 for next batch/task.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- **Memory vs. Accuracy:** Storing more samples improves retention estimation but increases memory overhead; MSSR claims <6% overhead, suggesting sparse or compressed storage.  
- **Estimation Complexity:** Hazard calculation adds compute; the paper reports <5% overhead, indicating efficient approximations.  
- **Scheduler Sensitivity:** The Ebbinghaus parameters (e.g., decay rate) may need tuning per dataset; adaptive nature reduces manual effort.  
- **Scalability:** Non-linear scaling of estimator and scheduler costs with longer sequences; needs validation.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- If hazard estimates are inaccurate (e.g., due to noisy loss signals), replay may target wrong samples, leading to increased forgetting.  
- If the Ebbinghaus model does not fit actual LLM forgetting dynamics (e.g., retention improves differently), scheduler intervals may be too aggressive or too conservative, causing either memory bloat or forgetting.  
- Overhead may spike with extremely long task sequences or large memory buffers, breaking the <5%/6% claims.

## Limitations
- **Baseline Comparison Scope:** Evaluation may not include the latest replay baselines (e.g., GeRe, FOREVER), potentially overstating gains.
- **Benchmark Realism:** Results are on academic benchmarks (GSM8K, ARC); performance on diverse, noisy, or proprietary sequential workflows is unproven.
- **Cognitive Model Translation:** The assumption that the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve directly models LLM task-specific knowledge decay lacks strong theoretical justification.
