# Antibody: Strengthening Defense Against Harmful Fine-Tuning for Large Language Models via Attenuating Harmful Gradient Influence

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00498  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00498  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Antibody achieves the lowest average harmful score (7.04%) compared to baselines like Booster (19.04%) and Lisa (15.29%), while maintaining competitive fine-tuning accuracy (63.51%), across four datasets and three model architectures.

## Executive Summary
Antibody is a two‑stage defense against harmful fine‑tuning attacks in Fine‑tuning‑as‑a‑Service (FTaaS). Malicious users may inject harmful samples into user‑submitted datasets to corrupt a model’s safety alignment. During alignment, Antibody optimizes the model into a flat loss region with respect to harmful samples, making safety harder to erase. During fine‑tuning, it dynamically down‑weights harmful samples and up‑weights benign ones using a score that compares the likelihood of the target completion versus a refusal. Across four datasets (SST2, AGNEWS, GSM8K, AlpacaEval) and three architectures (Llama‑2‑7B, Qwen‑2‑7B, Gemma‑2‑9B), Antibody reduces the average harmful score to 7.04% while preserving competitive task performance (63.51% average). The method remains robust to varying harmful ratios and learning rates, offering a practical solution for FTaaS providers.

## Method Summary
Antibody’s defense comprises two stages. **Alignment stage:** The model is trained on safety data while minimizing a sharpness term \( L_{\text{sharp}} = L_{\text{harm}}(\theta) - \min_{\phi \in B_\rho(\theta)} L_{\text{harm}}(\phi) \), which explicitly flattens the harmful loss landscape. An additional refusal loss on a one‑step perturbed model \( \theta_{\text{pert}} \) (simulating adversarial drift) further hardens the alignment. The combined objective \( L_{\text{align}} + \lambda_t L_{\text{sharp}} + \lambda_{\text{refusal}} L_{\text{refusal}} \) is optimized via a constrained update (Theorem 4.1). **Fine‑tuning stage:** For each batch, a dynamic weight \( w_i = \text{softmax}(r_i / \tau) \) is computed, where \( r_\theta(x_i,y_i) = \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_\theta(y_r|x_i)} \) compares target vs. refusal likelihood. The weighted loss \( \sum_i w_i \nabla\ell_i \) updates the model, suppressing harmful gradients while preserving benign learning. The method uses LoRA for efficiency and standard hyperparameters (alignment lr=5e‑4, fine‑tuning lr=1e‑5).

## Key Results
- Antibody achieves an average **harmful score of 7.04%**, significantly lower than Booster (19.04%) and Lisa (15.29%).
- It maintains **competitive fine‑tuning accuracy (63.51% average)**, even surpassing some baselines on certain tasks.
- The defense is **robust** to harmful sample ratios (5–50%) and learning rates up to 1e‑4, and outperforms sequential combinations of existing defenses.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Flatness Regularization in Alignment
- **Claim:** Optimizing the model into a flat loss region with respect to harmful samples during alignment makes the safety alignment more resilient to subsequent harmful fine‑tuning.
- **Mechanism:** The alignment stage minimizes \( L_{\text{align}} + \lambda_t L_{\text{sharp}} \), where \( L_{\text{sharp}} \) measures the sharpness of the harmful loss around the current parameters. The update direction derived in Theorem 4.1 ensures alignment progress while reducing sharpness, driving parameters to a region where \( \nabla L_{\text{harm}} \) is small.
- **Core assumption:** A flat harmful loss region implies small gradients for harmful samples; thus, when fine‑tuning on harmful data, the damaging updates are inherently smaller.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.1 defines \( L_{\text{sharp}} \) and the update rule; the paper explicitly states that flatness “maximally reduces the influence of harmful prompts” during SFT. Related work (e.g., “Immunization against harmful fine‑tuning attacks”) discusses robustness but does not formalize flatness as sharpness minimization.
- **Break condition:** If the harmful loss landscape lacks sufficiently flat regions that also satisfy alignment constraints, or if optimization fails to converge, harmful gradient norms remain large and the defense fails (see Appendix F.6 for validation of negation).

### Mechanism 2: Dynamic Weighting in Fine‑Tuning
- **Claim:** A dynamic weighting scheme based on a score comparing target vs. refusal completion likelihoods down‑weights harmful samples and up‑weights benign ones, attenuating harmful gradient influence while preserving task learning.
- **Mechanism:** For each sample \((x_i, y_i)\), compute \( r_\theta = \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_\theta(y_r|x_i)} \). Weights \( w_i = \frac{\exp(r_i/\tau)}{\sum_j \exp(r_j/\tau)} \) are normalized. The weighted update \( \theta \leftarrow \theta - \frac{\eta}{L} \sum_i w_i \nabla\ell_i \) amplifies benign contributions and suppresses harmful ones.
- **Core assumption:** A safety‑aligned model assigns higher probability to a refusal \( y_r \) than to a harmful target \( y_i \) (low \( r \)), and higher to the benign target than to a refusal (high \( r \)).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4.2 define \( r_\theta \) and \( w_\theta \); Proposition 4.3 analyzes the loss change; Figure 2 empirically shows score separation before/after fine‑tuning. Unlike “SafeInstr” or “Lisa”, Antibody’s weights are model‑state‑dependent and online.
- **Break condition:** If alignment degrades early such that \( r_\theta \) for harmful samples becomes comparable to benign ones, weighting fails. This can occur with many harmful samples or high learning rates (Figure 3 shows HS rising at lr=1e‑4).

### Mechanism 3: Refusal Loss on Perturbed Model
- **Claim:** Adding a refusal loss on a harmful‑perturbed model during alignment ensures that even if harmful fine‑tuning causes parameter drift, the model still assigns low probability to harmful targets relative to refusals, keeping weights effective.
- **Mechanism:** Construct \( \theta_{\text{pert}} = \theta - \rho \cdot \nabla L_{\text{harm}}(\theta) / \|\nabla L_{\text{harm}}(\theta)\| \) (one‑step adversarial move). Add \( L_{\text{refusal}}(\theta_{\text{pert}}) = \sum \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{pert}}}(y_r|x) \) over harmful prompts. This encourages robustness to the type of shift induced by harmful fine‑tuning.
- **Core assumption:** One‑step perturbation approximates multi‑step fine‑tuning, and optimizing refusal on \( \theta_{\text{pert}} \) generalizes to the actual fine‑tuned model.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.3 describes the objective; Eq. (11) includes it. Ablation (Table 4) shows it contributes to lowest HS, but isolated effect (Table 12) is mixed, indicating limited sufficiency.
- **Break condition:** If the one‑step perturbation poorly approximates cumulative changes, the refusal loss may not confer robustness. The ablation suggests it is not sufficient alone.

## Foundational Learning
- **Loss Sharpness & Flat Minima**  
  *Why needed:* Mechanism 1 hinges on minimizing sharpness of the harmful loss landscape; understanding that sharpness = loss change under perturbations explains why flat minima confer robustness.  
  *Quick check:* At a flat harmful minimum, how does the gradient magnitude of a harmful sample compare to that at a sharp minimum?

- **Weighted Mini‑batch Gradient Updates**  
  *Why needed:* Mechanism 2’s dynamic weighting directly alters the standard SFT gradient aggregation; engineers must grasp how sample weights affect batch gradient direction and magnitude to predict safety‑utility trade‑offs.  
  *Quick check:* In \( \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \frac{\eta}{L} \sum_i w_i \nabla\ell_i \), what is the effect of increasing \( w_i \) for benign samples and decreasing it for harmful samples on loss for a new benign test sample versus a new harmful one?

- **Safety Alignment Metrics (Harmful Score, Fine‑tuning Accuracy)**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s claims are quantitatively anchored on HS (percentage of harmful responses) and FA (task performance); interpreting results requires knowing their definitions, evaluation methods (BeaverTails for HS, task test sets for FA), and trade‑offs.  
  *Quick check:* Given HS=1% & FA=10% vs. HS=5% & FA=20% on GSM8K, which is better under the goal of mitigating attacks while boosting fine‑tuning performance?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Alignment Stage Module → Fine‑tuning Stage Module → (Score/Weight Calculator as subcomponent of fine‑tuning).  
The system also uses generic refusal strings and a harmless perturbation routine during alignment.

**Critical Path:**  
1. Alignment Stage Module (with \( L_{\text{align}}, L_{\text{sharp}}, L_{\text{refusal}} \)) produces aligned model \( \theta_{\text{aligned}} \).  
2. Fine‑tuning Stage Module (invoking Score/Weight Calculator) processes user dataset \( D_{\text{task}} \) with weighted updates, yielding \( \theta_{\text{fine‑tuned}} \).  
The refusal loss in alignment directly influences the weight calculator’s later efficacy.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Computational cost vs. defense strength: Adding \( L_{\text{sharp}} \) and \( L_{\text{refusal}} \) increases alignment time/memory; weighted fine‑tuning adds per‑batch scoring overhead.  
- Static alignment vs. dynamic defense: Alignment is a one‑time cost but static; adaptivity comes from online re‑weighting. If the model drifts significantly, weights may still fail.  
- Utility preservation: Weighting can slightly reduce FA gains from flatness regularization, indicating tension between suppressing harmful gradients and amplifying beneficial ones.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- High Harmful Score (> ~10%) despite Antibody, indicating flatness regularization or weighting failure.  
- Low Fine‑tuning Accuracy significantly below SFT baseline, suggesting excessive down‑weighting of benign data or refusal loss interference.  
- Weight distribution collapse: if \( r_\theta \) for harmful samples rises during fine‑tuning (e.g., due to high learning rate), their weights become comparable to benign ones (Figure 3 right).

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **Main Robustness Reproduction** – Verify Antibody achieves lowest average HS (~7%) and competitive FA (~63.5%) across SST2, AGNEWS, GSM8K, AlpacaEval on Llama‑2‑7B, and generalize to Qwen‑2‑7B/Gemma‑2‑9B (Tables 1–2).  
2. **Ablation Study** – Isolate contributions: (+ \( L_{\text{sharp}} \)) reduces HS vs. SFT but may lower FA; (+ weighted SFT) further reduces HS but may slightly lower FA from \( L_{\text{sharp}} \) alone; (+ \( L_{\text{refusal}} \)) yields final lowest HS and highest FA (Table 4).  
3. **Hyperparameter Robustness** – Test HS vs. fine‑tuning epochs and learning rates. Antibody should maintain HS < 10% across epochs (10–100) and learning rates up to 1e‑4, while Booster’s HS rises sharply (Figure 3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1: Reducing Computational Overhead in FTaaS
- **Question:** Can the computational overhead of Antibody’s two‑stage defense be reduced without compromising safety preservation, particularly for the recurring fine‑tuning stage?  
- **Basis:** Section I and Table 9 highlight high alignment and fine‑tuning costs as practical concerns.  
- **Why unresolved:** The current design imposes a one‑time alignment cost and recurring fine‑tuning overhead, which may limit scalability; no analysis explores efficiency‑accuracy trade‑offs or optimizations.  
- **Resolution evidence:** An ablation measuring the marginal contribution of each component to total compute; development of a lighter‑weight approximation; or a formal cost‑benefit analysis of when the overhead is justified by safety gains.

### Open Question 2: Hyperparameter Sensitivity
- **Question:** Why does Antibody’s defense degrade under large fine‑tuning learning rates, and can it be made more robust?  
- **Basis:** Figure 3 shows HS rises significantly beyond lr = 1e‑4, unlike at lower rates.  
- **Why unresolved:** FTaaS providers may need to accommodate users who inadvertently use larger learning rates; the underlying mechanism causing this sensitivity is unclear.  
- **Resolution evidence:** Analysis of loss landscape curvature during fine‑tuning with different learning rates; measuring the rate of harmful gradient re‑emergence; or designing an adaptive mechanism that dynamically adjusts weighting or step size.

### Open Question 3: Extension to Other Alignment Paradigms
- **Question:** Can Antibody be extended to other alignment paradigms like direct preference optimization (DPO) or reinforcement fine‑tuning?  
- **Basis:** Conclusion explicitly lists extension to other alignment techniques as future work.  
- **Why unresolved:** The core mechanisms (flatness regularization, gradient weighting) are derived for SFT objectives. DPO and RL‑based methods have different loss functions and gradient structures, so transferability is not guaranteed.  
- **Resolution evidence:** Deriving analogous flatness and weighting criteria for preference‑based losses; empirical evaluation on DPO fine‑tuning with harmful data; analysis of whether the same assumptions hold in preference contexts.

### Open Question 4: Robustness to Adaptive Poisoning Attacks
- **Question:** Does the weighting mechanism remain effective against adaptive poisoning attacks that explicitly aim to manipulate the weight assignment (e.g., by crafting harmful samples that appear benign to the scoring function)?  
- **Basis:** Paper tests out‑of‑distribution harmful data (PureBad) but not adaptive attacks where adversaries refine samples to evade weighting.  
- **Why unresolved:** In a real‑world FTaaS setting, malicious users could iteratively refine their poisoning data based on the known scoring function \( r_\theta \); the robustness of weight allocation to such strategic manipulation is unknown.  
- **Resolution evidence:** Simulating an adaptive attack loop where an adversary refines harmful samples using feedback from the model’s weight assignments; measuring attack success rates; developing a game‑theoretic analysis of the defense‑attack interaction; or incorporating adversarial training into the weighting mechanism.

## Limitations
- **Implementation ambiguities:** Exact prompt formatting for likelihood comparisons, precise inner‑minimization steps for sharpness estimation, and handling of sequence probabilities are not fully specified.  
- **Potential break conditions:** Flat regions may be unattainable for some models; weighting fails if alignment degrades early; the one‑step perturbation in \( L_{\text{refusal}} \) may not approximate multi‑step fine‑tuning.  
- **Computational overhead:** The dual‑objective alignment and per‑batch dynamic weighting increase runtime and memory, potentially hindering scalability to larger models or large‑scale FTaaS deployments.
