# Normative Equivalence in Human-AI Cooperation: Behaviour, Not Identity, Drives Cooperation in Mixed-Agent Groups

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.20487  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20487  
- **Reference count:** 12  
- **Primary result:** No significant effect of AI vs. human label on contributions in a four-player public goods game; cooperation driven by group behavior, not partner identity.

## Executive Summary
Can cooperative social norms in small groups be altered by the presence of an AI agent? To address this, researchers conducted an online experiment with 236 participants playing a ten-round, four-player Public Goods Game where three members were humans and one was a bot. The bot was labeled either as human or AI and followed three distinct strategies: unconditional cooperation, conditional cooperation, or free-riding. The primary outcome was participants’ average contribution per round.

Results revealed no significant effect of the bot’s label on contributions (β = 1.09, p = .738), and cooperation levels were statistically indistinguishable across the bot’s strategies. Follow-up one‑shot Prisoner’s Dilemma games and norm elicitation showed identical patterns across label conditions. The study concludes that cooperation in such groups is governed by *normative equivalence*: humans respond to the behavioral patterns of the group, not to the ontological status of a partner. This suggests that, in minimal anonymous interactions, cooperative heuristics are robust to the introduction of AI agents.

## Method Summary
Participants (N = 236) were recruited online and randomly assigned to groups of four (three humans, one bot). Over ten rounds of a linear Public Goods Game, each player decided how many of 100 tokens to contribute to a public pot, which was then multiplied by 1.5 and divided equally among all members. Only the total group contribution was revealed after each round; individual contributions remained anonymous. The bot was either explicitly labeled as “AI” or “human” and implemented one of three fixed strategies: always contributing 100 tokens (unconditional cooperator), matching the previous round’s group average (conditional cooperator), or contributing zero (free‑rider). After the game, participants played a one‑shot Prisoner’s Dilemma with a partner who had either cooperated or defected in the first round, and completed surveys measuring empirical and injunctive normative expectations. The primary analysis used mixed‑effects regression to model contributions based on lagged own and others’ contributions, label, and strategy.

## Key Results
- Bot label (human vs. AI) had no effect on average contributions (β = 1.09, p = .738).  
- The coefficient for the bot’s strategy was also non‑significant; contributions did not differ when the bot was unconditional, conditional, or a free‑rider.  
- Normative perceptions (empirical and injunctive expectations) and the key behavioral drivers (sensitivity to others’ contributions and individual inertia) were nearly identical across label conditions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1**  
- **Claim:** Humans default to cooperative heuristics when interacting with any agent that reciprocates, overriding categorical distinctions.  
- **Mechanism:** The Social Heuristics Hypothesis posits that cooperation is an intuitive, automated response generalized from daily social interactions. When an agent (human or AI) consistently contributes to the public good, participants intuitively reciprocate without deliberating on the agent’s ontological status.  
- **Core assumption:** Deliberate exploitation of a perceived “mindless” bot requires cognitive effort to override internalized cooperative norms, which participants in this minimal, anonymous setting lacked motivation to exert.  
- **Evidence:** The abstract states participants’ behaviour followed the same normative logic across human and AI conditions. Section 3.2.1 notes that behaviour was strongly shaped by prior contributions regardless of the bot’s label.  
- **Break condition:** If the AI explicitly violates local norms through erratic or hyper‑competitive behaviour, or if the interaction demands high‑stakes strategic calculation, the heuristic may be suppressed.

**Mechanism 2**  
- **Claim:** A human majority in the group buffers the normative influence of any single AI agent, diluting its behavioural strategy’s impact.  
- **Mechanism:** With three human participants, the aggregate behavioural signal (group total contribution) is dominated by human actions, making the specific strategy of the single AI agent less salient and preventing differential response.  
- **Core assumption:** Norm formation relies on diffuse, aggregate group signals rather than partner‑specific tracking, especially when individual contributions are hidden.  
- **Evidence:** Section 2.1 describes that participants did not see individual contributions; Section 4.1 notes that the bot’s strategy was likely diluted by the other humans.  
- **Break condition:** Increasing the proportion of AI agents (e.g., 2 AI + 2 humans) could shift the perceived social balance and make AI strategies more salient.

**Mechanism 3**  
- **Claim:** Cooperative norm adherence (compliance with empirical/injunctive expectations) operates identically in mixed and all‑human groups, even if norm enforcement may differ.  
- **Mechanism:** Participants form empirical and injunctive expectations based on observed group behaviour. These expectations guide contributions similarly regardless of partner label, as the group’s collective pattern is the primary normative cue. Enforcement (e.g., punishment) is not required for basic compliance.  
- **Core assumption:** Norms are sustained through conditional cooperation and behavioural inertia in response to group‑level signals, not through partner‑specific judgments.  
- **Evidence:** The abstract notes that normative mechanisms operated identically across conditions. Section 3.3.1 shows that normative expectation patterns mirrored contributions and were identical across treatments.  
- **Break condition:** Introducing direct communication, reputation systems, or partner‑specific punishment could make agent identity salient and alter how expectations are formed and enforced.

## Foundational Learning
- **Public Goods Game (PGG)**  
  - *Why needed:* The PGG is the core experimental platform. Understanding the tension between private gain (keeping tokens) and group benefit (contributing), the role of the multiplier (1.5), and typical patterns (initial cooperation, conditional responses, decline over rounds) is essential to interpret contribution behaviours and the impact of treatments.  
  - *Quick check:* In a linear 4‑player PGG with a 1.5 multiplier and 100‑token endowment, what is the payoff‑maximizing individual strategy if all others contribute 50 tokens? What is the group‑optimal strategy?

- **Social Norms (Bicchieri)**  
  - *Why needed:* The paper’s central thesis hinges on whether the *process* of norm‑guided behaviour (conditioning on others’ actions and perceived expectations) changes with AI presence. Distinguishing “what people do” (empirical) from “what people think should be done” (injunctive) is key to measuring normative equivalence.  
  - *Quick check:* If a participant believes others contribute 40 tokens (empirical expectation) but thinks others believe they *should* contribute 60 tokens (injunctive expectation), what type of norm conflict might this create?

- **Conditional Cooperation and Behavioural Inertia**  
  - *Why needed:* These are the two primary “normative mechanisms” identified as driving cooperation in the data. Conditional cooperation is responsiveness to others’ past contributions; inertia is persistence in one’s own past behaviour. Both are quantified via lagged variables in the regression models.  
  - *Quick check:* In the PGG regression model (Table 1), what do the coefficients for “Others’ lagged contrib.” (β = 0.14) and “Own lagged contrib.” (β = 0.28) represent? Which is a stronger predictor?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Recruitment & Randomization (Prolific) → oTree Frontend (PGG, PD, norm surveys) → Bot Controller (scripted strategies) → Data Logging → Mixed‑effects Analysis

**Critical path:**  
Recruit 4‑player groups → Randomize to condition (Human/AI label × Bot strategy) → Run 10 rounds of PGG with aggregate feedback → Run one‑shot PD with simulated cooperative partner → Elicit norms & post‑survey → Debrief & suspicion check → Export data → Model estimation (PGG mixed model, PD logistic, norm regressions) → Robustness check (exclude suspicious participants).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Ecological validity vs. control:* Anonymous, short interactions maximize control and minimize confounds (e.g., reputation, extended history) but reduce realism of human‑AI teamwork. The AI was scripted, not adaptive.  
- *Label believability vs. transparency:* The AI label needed to be credible to test identity effects, but full transparency about the bot’s simple script was withheld, raising ethical considerations and potential suspicion (18.6% expressed doubt).  
- *Signal clarity vs. dilution:* Providing only aggregate group contribution feedback increased ecological validity of collective action but diluted the impact of the single bot’s strategy, making it harder to detect strategy‑by‑label interactions.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *High suspicion rate (>25%):* Indicates the cover story was not credible, potentially biasing results if suspicious participants behave differently. The 18.6% rate here was manageable, but a sharp increase would invalidate the label manipulation.  
- *Bot strategy detection:* If participants could reliably identify the bot’s strategy (e.g., noticing the unconditional cooperator always gives 100), it would contaminate the label effect by making strategy salient.  
- *Group attrition cascade:* Because the design required all 4 members to complete, dropouts by any member terminated the session for all, leading to data loss (366 entered, 236 finished). High attrition rate (>30%) would bias the sample.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *Replication Baseline:* Reproduce the exact 2×3 design (Human/AI label × 3 bot strategies) with a new sample to confirm the primary null effect (no label or strategy differences) and the strength of inertia/conditional cooperation coefficients. Must achieve similar precision (± 4 tokens).  
2. *Adaptive AI Probe:* Replace the scripted Conditional Cooperator bot with a simple reinforcement learning agent that adapts its contribution based on the group’s average in the previous round (e.g., tit‑for‑tat with some noise). Test if adaptive behaviour interacts with label to affect normative dynamics or suspicion.  
3. *Proportion Manipulation:* Vary the number of AI agents in the group (1 AI + 3 Humans, 2 AI + 2 Humans, 3 AI + 1 Human) while keeping total group size 4. This directly tests the “human majority buffering” hypothesis and locates the threshold where “machine penalty” emerges.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
None explicitly raised; the authors focus on limitations and future validation checks.

## Limitations
- **Ecological validity:** Anonymous, short interactions with a simple scripted bot may not generalize to sustained, high‑stakes human‑AI teamwork.  
- **Label manipulation:** 18.6% of participants expressed suspicion about group composition; undisclosed doubt could subtly bias behavior.  
- **Signal dilution:** Aggregate‑only feedback obscured individual actions, potentially masking identity effects that would emerge with partner‑specific tracking.

## Confidence
- Primary null effect (label irrelevance): **High**  
- Heuristic‑driven cooperation: **Medium**  
- Human majority buffering: **Medium**  
- Normative process equivalence: **High**

## Next Checks
1. Proportion manipulation – vary the fraction of AI agents (1 to 3) to locate the threshold where any “machine penalty” appears.  
2. Adaptive AI probe – test whether a learning agent (e.g., reinforcement‑learning) that adjusts contributions interacts with label to disrupt normative equivalence.  
3. Identity salience boost – introduce partner‑specific feedback or a punishment option to make AI identity cognitively salient and assess changes in conditional cooperation or norm enforcement.
