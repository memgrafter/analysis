# Efficient Credal Prediction through Decalibration

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.08495
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08495
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Decalibration is a model‑agnostic post‑hoc method that perturbs logits to generate class‑wise probability intervals under a relative‑likelihood budget, without retraining, and matches or outperforms ensemble‑based credal methods while being orders of magnitude faster.

## Executive Summary
Credal sets provide a principled representation of epistemic uncertainty, but constructing them traditionally requires training ensembles, which is computationally expensive for large models. This paper introduces **decalibration**, a post‑hoc technique that adds a bias vector to the logits of a maximum‑likelihood trained predictor and enforces a relative‑likelihood constraint derived from the training data. The resulting feasibility set in bias space is convex; the endpoints of class‑wise probability intervals are obtained by solving convex (upper) or extreme‑point (lower) optimization problems. Decalibration is model‑agnostic, requires no retraining, and yields nested credal sets. Empirical evaluations on standard benchmarks and diverse architectures (including TabPFN and CLIP) show that it achieves coverage‑efficiency trade‑offs and out‑of‑distribution detection performance comparable to state‑of‑the‑art ensemble methods, while reducing computational cost by orders of magnitude.

## Method Summary
Given a pre‑trained classifier, decalibration considers adding a scalar bias \( t \) to the logit of a target class \( k \) while keeping others unchanged. The training log‑likelihood drop \( \Delta\ell_k(t) = \sum_n \bigl( \log p^{(n)}_{y^{(n)}}(t\cdot e_k) - \log p^{(n)}_{y^{(n)}}(0) \bigr) \) is a concave function of \( t \). For a chosen relative‑likelihood budget \( \alpha \) (e.g., 0.95), the feasible interval \( [t_k^-, t_k^+] \) consists of all \( t \) such that \( \Delta\ell_k(t) \ge \log\alpha \). The upper bound for class \( k \) on a test input \( x \) is \( p_k(x; t_k^+) \), and the lower bound is \( p_k(x; t_k^-) \). These one‑dimensional intervals are found via root‑finding (e.g., bisection) because of monotonicity, and they form an axis‑aligned box credal set. The entire procedure operates on the trained model without modification and scales to very large architectures.

## Key Results
- Decalibration achieves coverage‑efficiency and out‑of‑distribution detection (AUROC) performance comparable to ensemble‑based credal methods.
- Computational cost is orders of magnitude lower than training ensembles.
- Enables credal prediction for large models such as TabPFN and CLIP, demonstrating strong results on in‑context learning and zero‑shot classification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A fixed relative‑likelihood budget defines a convex feasibility set in bias‑vector space.
- **Mechanism:** Decalibration adds a bias vector \( c \in \mathbb{R}^K \) to all logits (train and test). The training log‑likelihood change \( \Delta\ell(c) = \sum_n \log p^{(n)}_{y^{(n)}}(c) - \log p^{(n)}_{y^{(n)}}(0) \) is a sum of log‑softmax terms and therefore concave. The feasible set \( \mathcal{F}(\alpha) = \{ c : \Delta\ell(c) \ge \log \alpha \} \) is convex and, when intersected with the identifiability hyperplane \( \mathcal{S} = \{c : \mathbf{1}^\top c = 0\} \), compact if at least two classes appear.
- **Core assumption:** Strict concavity of \( \Delta\ell \) on \( \mathcal{S} \) when training data contains at least two classes.
- **Evidence anchors:** Proposition 3.1(a) proves concavity and compactness; the abstract highlights convexity and convex‑program endpoints.
- **Break condition:** Non‑concave log‑likelihood (e.g., due to pathological interactions) could make \( \mathcal{F}(\alpha) \) non‑convex or fragmented, destroying efficient optimization and nestedness.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The upper credal bound for each class solves a convex program; the lower bound lies on the boundary of the feasibility set.
- **Mechanism:** For query \( x \), \( \overline{p}_k(x) = \sup_{c \in \mathcal{F}(\alpha)} p_k(x;c) \). Since \( \log p_k(x;c) \) is concave and \( \mathcal{F}(\alpha) \) convex, maximizing \( \log p_k \) is a convex problem with a unique solution modulo translations along \( \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\} \). The lower bound \( \underline{p}_k(x) \) minimizes a convex‑exponential over a convex set; it is generally non‑convex but attains its minimum at an extreme point of \( \mathcal{F}_S(\alpha) \) when the section is compact.
- **Core assumption:** \( c \mapsto \log p_k(x;c) \) is smooth and concave; \( \mathcal{F}(\alpha) \) is non‑empty and compact on \( \mathcal{S} \).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions interval endpoints solve convex programs; Proposition 3.1(c) and (d) formalize upper/lower bound properties.
- **Break condition:** Non‑compact \( \mathcal{F}(\alpha) \) (e.g., one class absent) may prevent attainment of lower‑bound minimum; non‑strict concavity could cause multiple optimizers.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Varying the relative‑likelihood threshold \( \alpha \) yields nested credal sets.
- **Mechanism:** For \( 0 < \alpha_2 \le \alpha_1 \le 1 \), the plausible model sets satisfy \( \mathcal{C}_{\alpha_1} \subseteq \mathcal{C}_{\alpha_2} \). Consequently, the class‑wise extrema satisfy \( \overline{p}_k(\alpha_1) \le \overline{p}_k(\alpha_2) \) and \( \underline{p}_k(\alpha_1) \ge \underline{p}_k(\alpha_2) \). This monotonicity creates the coverage–efficiency trade‑off: tighter \( \alpha \) gives smaller intervals (higher efficiency) but lower coverage.
- **Core assumption:** The relative‑likelihood score \( \gamma(h) \in [0,1] \) yields monotone \( \alpha \)‑cuts.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states nested credal sets; Proposition 2.1 proves monotonicity.
- **Break condition:** If the likelihood ordering were not total (e.g., due to multimodality), \( \mathcal{C}_\alpha \) might cease to be nested for some \( \alpha \).

## Foundational Learning

### Likelihood ratio and relative likelihood
- **Why needed:** The decalibration framework hinges on the statistical notion of relative likelihood \( \gamma(h) = L(h)/\sup_{h'} L(h') \). It defines plausibility without priors and yields interpretable \( \alpha \)-cuts.
- **Quick check:** Given the training log‑likelihood values for three models, how would you compute the relative‑likelihood region for \( \alpha = 0.8 \)?

### Convex optimization (concave maximization)
- **Why needed:** Computing the upper credal bound reduces to maximizing a concave function (log probability) over a convex set (feasible biases). This is a convex optimization problem solvable efficiently with standard tools.
- **Quick check:** If \( f(c) \) is concave and \( \mathcal{F} \) is convex, what can you say about \( \sup_{c\in\mathcal{F}} f(c) \)?
- **Answer hint:** The supremum is attained at an extreme point of \( \mathcal{F} \) if \( \mathcal{F} \) is compact; crucially, maximizing a concave function over a convex set is a convex program.

### Epistemic vs. aleatoric uncertainty
- **Why needed:** Credal sets explicitly represent epistemic uncertainty (model uncertainty). Distinguishing this from aleatoric uncertainty (data noise) is central to the motivation and evaluation (e.g., OOD detection, active learning).
- **Quick check:** In an ensemble, what source of disagreement do we typically attribute to epistemic uncertainty?
- **Answer hint:** Differences arising from uncertainty about which model is correct, as opposed to inherent noise in the data generation process.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Pre‑trained model → Bootstrap likelihood evaluator → Convex solver interface → Interval constructor

### Critical path
The workflow starts from the trained model to compute training‑time likelihood evaluations (defining the feasibility set), then solves the scalar convex programs to obtain class‑specific bias endpoints, and finally performs test‑time inference using those biases to construct the credal set. Inaccuracies in the likelihood evaluation (e.g., due to distribution shift between training and test) can mis‑calibrate the resulting intervals.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Scalar (class‑specific) bias vs. full vector:** Scalar shifts make lower‑bound optimization convex and fast but limit expressivity; a full bias vector could capture correlations but would introduce non‑convexity for lower bounds.
- **Logit perturbation vs. probability perturbation:** Operating on logits preserves the softmax structure and ensures concavity; perturbing probabilities directly would break convexity and the relative‑likelihood semantics.
- **Post‑hoc vs. retraining:** Post‑hoc avoids costly ensemble training but cannot represent uncertainty modes not reachable by logit shifts (e.g., feature‑space diversity).

### Failure signatures
- **Non‑nested intervals** when decreasing \( \alpha \) (violates Proposition 2.1).
- **Coverage below nominal** on held‑out data despite \( \alpha \) tuning.
- **Solver failure** (non‑convergence, NaNs) when concavity of \( \Delta\ell_k \) is compromised (e.g., extreme class imbalance, numerical overflow).
- **Degenerate intervals** (zero width) even for small \( \alpha \), indicating an overconfident base model.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Feasibility set sanity check:** On a synthetic two‑class problem, sample random \( c \in \mathbb{R}^2 \) and verify that \( \Delta\ell(c) \) is concave and that the level set \( \{ c : \Delta\ell(c) \ge \log\alpha \} \) is convex.
2. **Runtime vs. ensemble benchmark:** Compare wall‑clock time for computing credal sets via decalibration versus training a 5‑member ensemble on CIFAR‑10; expect >10× speedup.
3. **Coverage calibration:** On CIFAR‑10H, sweep \( \alpha \) and check that empirical coverage \( \hat{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{H}_\alpha) \) approaches the target \( \alpha \) for the box credal set, confirming the relative‑likelihood semantics translate into frequency coverage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can we develop efficient polynomial‑time approximation algorithms for the non‑convex lower credal bound maximization in fully coupled decalibration (multi‑dimensional logit shifts), with provable guarantees on approximation error?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state: “The fully coupled case remains open; upper bounds still reduce to a convex program, whereas lower bounds are non‑convex. Developing reliable relaxations, certificates, or approximation schemes… is a promising direction.”
- **Why unresolved:** The lower bounds require finding extreme points of a convex feasible set, which is combinatorially hard in high dimensions; this limits scalability and prevents direct application to large models where full‑coupled shifts might be needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An algorithm that computes an ϵ‑approximation of the lower bound via a sequence of convex programs, accompanied by theoretical guarantees on ϵ and empirical validation on benchmark datasets showing coverage properties comparable to exact (oracle) computation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How should credal sets be defined and evaluated for open‑vocabulary multimodal models (e.g., CLIP) where the label space is dynamic (determined by prompts), and what protocols can assess epistemic uncertainty quality without fixed ground‑truth distributions?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note: “Open‑vocabulary, multimodal models such as CLIP raise additional challenges. Because the label set is chosen at inference time, uncertainty should reflect not only prediction but also label selection and prompt choice. Designing credal formalisms and evaluation protocols for this setting is an important avenue for future work.”
- **Why unresolved:** Standard credal sets assume a fixed finite label space; with dynamic prompts, it is ambiguous whether the credal set should cover all possible labels or only those in the prompt, and coverage cannot be measured against a static ground‑truth distribution, making evaluation difficult.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A formal definition of prompt‑dependent credal sets along with an evaluation protocol—perhaps based on human ambiguity judgments across diverse prompts—that validates the method’s uncertainty representation on CLIP‑style models.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the approximation error incurred by restricting decalibration to class‑specific (one‑dimensional) logit shifts, and under what conditions does the resulting axis‑aligned box credal set contain or lie within the true feasible set from coupled shifts?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper adopts the one‑dimensional restriction for computational efficiency but does not analyze its relationship to the full multi‑dimensional credal set; Proposition 3.1 indicates full lower bounds are non‑convex while the restricted case yields convex sub‑problems.
- **Why unresolved:** Without understanding the approximation quality, practitioners cannot guarantee that the efficient box credal set is a meaningful outer or inner approximation, potentially leading to unreliable uncertainty estimates.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical conditions (e.g., on the Hessian of the log‑likelihood) that guarantee inclusion relationships between the axis‑aligned box and the true credal set, plus experiments comparing the bounds on synthetic problems where full optimization is tractable.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How should the relative likelihood threshold \( \alpha \) be selected in practice, and what is its relationship to standard calibration metrics (e.g., Expected Calibration Error) of the base maximum likelihood predictor?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper evaluates a range of \( \alpha \) values and discusses the resulting coverage–efficiency trade‑off, but offers no principled guidance on choosing \( \alpha \); the connection between \( \alpha \) and model calibration remains unexplored.
- **Why unresolved:** Applications require a single \( \alpha \); arbitrary selection may yield too wide or too narrow credal sets. Understanding its calibration implications would connect decalibration to familiar calibration concepts and aid practical deployment.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical studies across datasets and model architectures quantifying the correlation between \( \alpha \) and ECE, or a data‑driven procedure (e.g., based on a validation set) to select \( \alpha \) that satisfies user‑specified coverage or efficiency constraints.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Do post‑hoc uncertainty methods like decalibration retain their likelihood‑based semantics when applied to overparameterized neural networks trained with stochastic optimization, where the empirical likelihood landscape is highly non‑convex and the MLE may not be unique?
- **Basis in paper:** Decalibration relies on a well‑defined MLE and a relative‑likelihood region that is convex on an identifiability hyperplane; however, large models trained with SGD may have many near‑optimal solutions and flat likelihood regions, potentially making the feasible set ill‑behaved.
- **Why unresolved:** If the log‑likelihood at the training optimum is not sufficiently isolated, perturbations that respect a fixed relative‑likelihood budget could behave unintuitively (e.g., too permissive), undermining the interpretability of the credal sets.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical analysis of the curvature and condition number of the log‑likelihood around trained networks (using Hessian approximations), and sensitivity studies measuring how decalibration outputs vary with random seeds, optimizers, and early stopping criteria across large models.

## Limitations
- Class‑wise scalar shifts yield axis‑aligned box credal sets that may miss correlations between class probabilities, leading to conservative intervals.
- Optimization settings (tolerances, max iterations) are not fully specified, which may affect reproducibility of the bounds and runtime.
- Computing the training likelihood differences \( \Delta\ell(c) \) over the entire training set for various biases can be costly for large datasets; the paper does not discuss approximations.
