# Quantifying the Effect of Test Set Contamination on Generative Evaluations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.04301
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04301
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Test set contamination inflates generative benchmark scores via verbatim memorization, breaches irreducible error in scaling laws, and exhibits temperature-dependent breakdown.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the effect of test set contamination on generative evaluations, using the MATH benchmark as a case study. The authors pretrain small Qwen language models (34M–344M parameters) on a fixed token budget consisting of a base web corpus mixed with varying numbers of replicas of the MATH test set. They find that contamination increases Math Verify scores and lowers test loss, but these gains disappear when problems are rephrased or perturbed, confirming that improvement stems from memorization rather than reasoning. Scaling law analysis reveals that even a single replica reduces the irreducible error term below the estimated floor for uncontaminated data, challenging the fundamental assumptions of scaling laws. Additional experiments show that overtraining on fresh data dilutes contamination, supervised fine‑tuning helps low‑contamination models but harms high‑contamination ones, and high sampling temperatures or longer solution generations break memorization. A bug in the LM Evaluation Harness was fixed, raising gold scores to 100%. The work underscores the fragility of generative benchmark integrity and the need for contamination‑aware evaluation practices.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted controlled pretraining experiments with dense decoder‑only transformers (Qwen 3) of sizes 34M–344M. Each model was trained on a corpus of exactly 20 × model‑parameter tokens, composed of the FineWeb‑Edu‑Dedup dataset plus R replicas (R ∈ {0,1,3,10,32,100,316,1000,3162}) of the MATH test set (problem + solution). Training used AdamW, cosine learning‑rate decay, gradient clipping, and FlashAttention 2. Primary evaluation metrics were Math Verify (mathematical equivalence of generated solutions to gold answers) and cross‑entropy loss on gold solutions. A bug in the evaluation harness that incorrectly stripped `\boxed{}` was fixed. Additional conditions included overtraining (training on m × the base token budget), supervised fine‑tuning on the MATH train set, and temperature sweeps during generation. The loss‑compute data were fitted to scaling laws L(C,R)=E(R)+C₀(R)·C⁻ᵅ⁽ᴿ⁾, and token‑level negative log‑likelihoods were modeled as Lₜ = E + A·t⁻ᵅ to characterize memorization regimes.

## Key Results
- Contamination yields large performance gains on Math Verify and reduces test loss, but these gains vanish under rephrased or numerically perturbed problems, demonstrating that the benefit is fragile memorization rather than robust generalization.
- Scaling law fits show that even one replica of the test set lowers the irreducible error E(R) enough that the contaminated loss curve lies below the estimated E(0) of the uncontaminated corpus, indicating a breach of the theoretical limit under standard assumptions.
- The probability of successfully generating a full solution follows P(T) ≈ exp(‑E·(T‑1) ‑ (A/(1‑α))·(T^{1‑α}‑1)), leading to three regimes (Exponentially Fast Decoherence, Brittle Memorization, Deterministic Lock‑In) governed by the effective exponent α_eff = α/τ, where τ is the sampling temperature.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Test set contamination inflates generative benchmark scores primarily through fragile, verbatim memorization of solution sequences rather than acquisition of robust reasoning capabilities.
- **Mechanism:** During pretraining, repeated exposure to exact test problem-solution pairs causes the model to store high‑probability token chains. At inference, greedy or low-temperature sampling can reproduce these memorized sequences, yielding high Math Verify scores. This memorization is brittle: it collapses under paraphrased/perturbed problems (Table 1) or high sampling temperatures (Fig. 5), because the model has not learned the underlying mathematical transformations.
- **Core assumption:** Memorized sequences are recalled via deterministic or near-deterministic next-token prediction conditioned on the exact prefix. Generalization would require abstracting the problem's structure, which is not observed.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "...performance regresses to match the uncontaminated model across all model sizes and contamination levels in both conditions [rephrased, perturbed], confirming that the capabilities gained from test set contamination rely almost exclusively on verbatim memorization..."  
  [section 3, Finding #2] Table 1 shows near‑zero scores for all contaminated models when problems are rephrased or perturbed.  
  [corpus] Weak direct evidence; the corpus contains no controlled tests of paraphrasing. The conclusion is drawn from the authors' own experiments described in the paper.
- **Break condition:** Rephrasing the problem statement, perturbing numerical values, or using sampling temperatures >~0.6 breaks the memorization chain, causing performance to collapse to the uncontaminated baseline.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Even minimal contamination (a single test replica) can shift the scaling law irreducible error floor downward, allowing a model to achieve test loss lower than what infinite compute on uncontaminated data could theoretically attain.
- **Mechanism:** The contaminated test sequences become part of the training distribution. The scaling law fit (Eqn. 1) shows the irreducible error term E(R) shrinks dramatically with R. For R≥1, the predicted loss curve for contaminated models lies below the estimated E(0) of the uncontaminated scaling law. This suggests the contamination changes the "task" the model is solving on that test set from generalization to recall.
- **Core assumption:** Scaling laws (L = E + C₀·C⁻ᵅ) accurately model the relationship between compute and loss, and they extrapolate infinitely. The irreducible error E(0) represents a fundamental limit for the uncontaminated task.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "Under standard scaling law assumptions – specifically, that the scaling law extrapolates infinitely – a contaminated pretraining corpus can buy more than an 'infinite' amount of pretraining compute relative to pretraining on an uncontaminated corpus."  
  [section 3, Finding #3] Fig. 2 shows fitted scaling laws; "including even a single replica enables almost all models to achieve lower cross entropy losses than the estimated irreducible error of the uncontaminated pretraining corpus."  
  [corpus] No external evidence; this is a novel empirical finding from the authors' scaling law fits on their pretrained models.
- **Break condition:** The inference depends on the validity of the scaling law extrapolation and the assumption that E(0) is a true, unbreachable floor for the uncontaminated task. If either assumption fails, the "fundamental breach" conclusion may not hold.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The stability of memorized generation is governed by a survival process with three regimes (Exponentially Fast Decoherence, Brittle Memorization, Deterministic Lock-In), controlled by the interplay of the model's native sequence scaling exponent α, the irreducible error E, and the inference-time temperature τ.
- **Mechanism:** The probability of generating a correct full solution of length T is P(T) ≈ exp( ‑E·(T‑1) ‑ (A/(1‑α))·(T^{1‑α}‑1) ). The effective exponent becomes α_eff ≈ α/τ. Temperature therefore modulates the regime: low τ (greedy) can push a model with α<1 into pseudo‑stable Lock‑In (α_eff>1), while higher τ reveals the true brittle nature (α_eff<1). Longer sequences exponentially increase the risk of deviation from the memorized path.
- **Core assumption:** Next‑token negative log‑likelihood along a memorized sequence scales as Lₜ ≈ E + A·t⁻ᵅ (Eqn. 3). The cumulative product of token probabilities determines sequence survival.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "We mathematically describe three distinct regimes: Exponentially Fast Decoherence, Brittle Memorization, and Deterministic Lock‑In."  
  [section 5, Finding #8] "The probability of successfully generating a solution of length T can then be approximated as: P(T)≈exp(...)" and the discussion of α_eff = α/τ.  
  [section 5, Figs. 6,7,8] Empirical validation: Math Verify scores decay with length (Fig. 6), NLL scaling laws are fitted (Fig. 7), and phase diagrams show regime boundaries (Fig. 8).  
  [corpus] No external evidence; the mathematical model and regime identification are contributions of this paper based on their data.
- **Break condition:** Transitioning from deterministic (τ→0) to stochastic (τ≥1) sampling breaks the Lock‑In regime. Sequence lengths beyond a problem‑specific threshold (where P(T)≈0) cause decoherence in the Brittle regime.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Neural Scaling Laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022)**  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's core analysis (Finding #3) fits scaling laws L(C) = E + C₀·C⁻ᵅ to loss vs. compute. Understanding this form, the meaning of irreducible error E, and the assumptions of extrapolation is essential to interpret the "fundamental breach" claim.  
  - **Quick check question:** If a model's loss on a test set follows L(C)=1.0 + 100·C⁻⁰·³, what is the theoretical minimum loss achievable with infinite compute? How would adding 1 replica of the test set to training potentially change this curve?

- **Concept: Memorization vs. Generalization (Carlini et al., 2023; Hernandez et al., 2022)**  
  - **Why needed here:** Distinguishing contamination‑driven score inflation (memorization) from genuine capability improvement (generalization) is the paper's central diagnostic. The mechanisms hinge on this distinction, validated via rephrasing/perturbation tests.  
  - **Quick check question:** Design an experiment to disentangle memorization from generalization for a multiple‑choice QA benchmark. What specific changes to the test instances would you make, and what outcome patterns would indicate memorization?

- **Concept: Temperature‑Controlled Decoding & Sequence Probability**  
  - **Why needed here:** The analysis of inference‑time effects (Finding #6, #7, #8) requires understanding how sampling temperature τ scales logits and affects the probability of long, coherent sequence generation. The derived α_eff = α/τ is central.  
  - **Quick check question:** If a model has a native sequence scaling exponent α=0.7 for a memorized task, what is α_eff at τ=0.5? At τ=1.0? In which regime (Brittle vs. Lock‑In) does each operate? What does this imply for evaluating a supposedly "good" model?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
1. Pretraining Corpus Mixer: Injects R replicas of the tokenized MATH test set into a base web corpus (FineWeb‑Edu‑Dedup), maintaining constant total tokens D.  
2. Model Family: Qwen 3 decoder‑only transformers (34M to 344M params), trained with FlashAttention 2, AdamW, and cosine LR schedule.  
3. Evaluation Suite: Two metrics – (a) Math Verify (correctness of generated solutions via mathematical equivalence checking), (b) Cross‑entropy loss on gold reference solutions.  
4. Scaling Law Fitter: Fits L(C,R) = E(R) + C₀(R)·C⁻ᵅ⁽ᴿ⁾ to test loss vs. pretraining compute (C=6ND) for each R.  
5. Regime Analyzer: Fits Lₜ(N,R) ≈ E(N,R) + A(N,R)·t⁻ᵅ⁽ᴺ,ᴿ⁾ to token‑level NLL; uses this to derive P(T) and regime boundaries.  
6. Bug Patcher: Identifies and fixes the `minerva_math` scoring bug in the LM Evaluation Harness where `\boxed{}` was stripped prematurely.

**Critical path:**  
1. Define contamination levels R (0, 1, 3, 10, 32, 100, 316, 1000, 3162).  
2. For each (model size N, R), train a model to compute‑optimal Chinchilla tokens (20×N).  
3. Evaluate with greedy decoding: measure Math Verify score and test set cross‑entropy.  
4. Fit scaling laws across compute for each R.  
5. For selected (N,R) pairs, run temperature sweeps and bin by solution length to map regimes.  
6. Conduct overtraining (m>1) and SFT follow‑up experiments.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Token Budget Constancy: Keeping total pretraining tokens D constant across R isolates the dose effect of contamination from simply having more data. Strength: causal identification. Limitation: applies only to fixed compute budgets, not scenarios where contaminated data adds to the corpus.  
- Single Benchmark Focus: Using only MATH enables automatic verification and clean controlled replication. Trade‑off: limited generalizability to other generative tasks (coding, creative writing). The mechanisms (brittle memorization of long chains) may be MATH‑specific.  
- Model Scale: Models up to 344M params are tiny by frontier standards. The scaling law breach and regime transitions might shift or disappear at larger scales (10B+). The paper acknowledges this limitation.  
- Contamination Method: Injecting full input‑output pairs (problem + boxed solution) is a strong form of contamination. Real‑world contamination might be noisier (e.g., only the problem text, or an answer without solution). The effects could be weaker.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Scaling Law Fit Failure: If the NLL vs. compute data does not follow a clear power‑law+constant pattern for a given R, the irreducible error E(R) cannot be reliably estimated, breaking the "fundamental breach" analysis.  
- In‑Context Scaling Anomaly: As noted in Appendix D, NLL increasing with token index t (instead of decreasing) occurs in two regimes: (a) Small, uncontaminated models (capacity limit), (b) Large, heavily contaminated models (selection bias on long solutions). If this uptick is severe, the Lₜ ≈ E + A·t⁻ᵅ model is invalid, and the P(T) regime analysis breaks down.  
- Math Verify Bug Recurrence: If the evaluation harness bug (stripping `\boxed{}`) is not fixed, all Math Verify scores are systematically depressed and meaningless. The paper's correction is a prerequisite for any result using this metric.  
- SFT Outcome Reversal: The finding that SFT hurts high‑contamination models (Fig. 4) is theoretically plausible but counterintuitive. If replication fails (SFT always helps), it suggests the "dose‑response" and forgetting mechanism may be more fragile or dataset‑dependent than claimed.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Rephrase/Perturb Validation: For your trained (N,R) models, generate rephrased and numerically perturbed versions of 100 random MATH test problems. Run Math Verify. Expect: contaminated models' scores collapse to baseline (~0%). This verifies Mechanism 1 (memorization only).  
2. Temperature Sensitivity Sweep: For the 344M model at R=1000, run Math Verify across τ ∈ {0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.5}. Expect: near‑perfect scores at τ≤0.5, precipitous drop by τ=1.0 (Fig. 5). This verifies Mechanism 3 (temperature as truth serum).  
3. Scaling Law Refit: Using your (N,R) loss‑compute data, fit L(C,R)=E(R)+C₀(R)·C⁻ᵅ⁽ᴿ⁾. Check if E(R) decreases with R and if any R≥1 curve lies below E(0). This directly tests Mechanism 2 (breach of irreducible error). Also check for deviations in Lₜ vs. t (Appendix D) to validate regime assumptions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** How can we reconcile the finding that a single test set replica enables loss below the irreducible error with prior claims that single‑shot verbatim memorization is an illusion and that membership inference attacks are fundamentally limited?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Reconciling Memorization Thresholds: Our discovery that a single test set replica drives loss below the irreducible error contrasts with recent assertions that single‑shot verbatim memorization is an “illusion” (Huang et al., 2024) or that membership inference is fundamentally capped (Hayes et al., 2025). Future work should aim to understand this difference; one possibility is that memorization depends on the amount relative to corpus size (Schaeffer et al., 2025c; Wei et al., 2025).” (Section 6)  
- **Why unresolved:** These contradictory findings challenge the theoretical understanding of memorization and the practical limits of detection, leaving uncertainty about when and why contamination becomes effective.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying contamination dose, model scale, and corpus composition, paired with improved membership inference attacks, could identify the conditions under which memorization occurs and is detectable, bridging the gap between the two perspectives.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** Do alternative architectures such as Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoEs) or State Space Models (SSMs) exhibit different memorization patterns under test set contamination compared to dense transformers?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Architectural Susceptibility: We studied dense models, but it remains an open question whether Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoEs) or State Space Models (SSMs) exhibit different memorization patterns.” (Section 6)  
- **Why unresolved:** All current evidence comes from dense decoder‑only models; architectural differences like sparsity (MoEs) or recurrence (SSMs) could alter how models internalize and reproduce contaminated data, affecting the reliability of evaluations.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Pretraining experiments parallel to those in the paper, using MoE and SSM architectures across similar contamination doses and model sizes, would reveal whether scaling laws and memorization regimes differ.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** Does contamination efficacy vary inversely with problem compressibility, such that harder benchmarks (e.g., AIME) require higher doses of leakage for the same performance boost?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Harder Benchmarks: We hypothesize that contamination efficacy is inversely correlated with problem compressibility. Investigating whether harder benchmarks (e.g., AIME) require higher “doses” of leakage to achieve the same performance boost would be a valuable contribution to benchmark design.” (Section 6)  
- **Why unresolved:** The relationship between problem difficulty/compressibility and memorization efficiency is theoretical; it is unclear whether more complex or longer‑answer problems are more resistant to contamination, which would affect benchmark validity.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Running contamination sweeps on benchmarks with varying properties (e.g., AIME, advanced coding problems) and correlating performance gains with difficulty metrics and solution lengths would test the hypothesis and inform contamination‑resistant benchmark construction.

## Limitations
- Models capped at 344M parameters; scaling to frontier sizes (10B+) remains unknown.  
- Evaluation restricted to MATH; findings may not transfer to coding, reasoning, or creative tasks.  
- Contamination injects full problem‑solution pairs; real‑world cases often lack complete solutions.
