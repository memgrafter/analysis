# Zero-Shot Time Series Foundation Models for Annual Institutional Forecasting Under Data Sparsity

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12120
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12120
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Zero-shot TSFMs with leakage-safe covariates can match or exceed classical baselines in annual enrolment forecasting under data sparsity, but gains are model- and cohort-dependent.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the use of zero-shot Time Series Foundation Models (TSFMs) for forecasting annual student enrolments in institutional settings characterized by severe data sparsity and structural breaks. The authors benchmark TSFMs against classical persistence and ARIMA baselines, incorporating exogenous covariates such as Google Trends and an LLM-derived Institutional Operating Conditions Index (IOCI) under a rigorous leakage-safe protocol. Key findings indicate that covariate-conditioned TSFMs can achieve competitive or superior point accuracy, with performance varying significantly across model architectures and enrolment cohorts (domestic vs. international). The work contributes an auditable framework for converting narrative evidence into time-series covariates and demonstrates the potential of pre-trained temporal priors to stabilize forecasts in data-scarce environments.

## Method Summary
The study employs an expanding-window time series cross-validation framework to forecast one-year-ahead annual enrolments for domestic and international cohorts. The target series spans 19 years (2007–2025) from a single university. Covariates include Google Trends (monthly search volume aggregated and engineered via lag-1, 2-year EWMA, 3-year EWMA) and a proprietary Institutional Operating Conditions Index (IOCI) derived from historical narratives using an LLM with explicit vintage constraints to avoid look-ahead bias. All data are standardized within each training window. Zero-shot inference is applied to several TSFM families (Chronos-Bolt, Chronos-2, TimesFM, Moirai) with fixed weights, while classical baselines include persistence and ARIMAX. Performance is evaluated via point metrics (MAE, RMSE, SMAPE) and probabilistic scores (CRPS, PIT diagnostics). The experimental design isolates covariate effects through within-model comparisons across no-covariate, Google Trends, and IOCI conditions.

## Key Results
- For domestic enrolments, Chronos-Bolt models improved with IOCI (e.g., Chronos-Bolt-Tiny ΔMAE=50.22 vs. persistence baseline).
- For international enrolments, Moirai models excelled, with Moirai-Base improving when conditioned on engineered Google Trends (ΔMAE=28.3).
- Probabilistic calibration (CRPS, PIT) also improved for specific model-covariate combinations, notably IOCI for Chronos-Bolt in domestic cohorts and Google Trends for Moirai in international cohorts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Pre-trained temporal priors in TSFMs provide implicit regularisation that stabilises forecasting under severe data sparsity.
- Mechanism: TSFMs are pre-trained on large, heterogeneous time series corpora. This exposure imbues them with generic inductive biases about temporal smoothness, persistence, and regime dynamics. When applied zero-shot to a short institutional series, these priors act as a strong regulariser, preventing overfitting to noise and structural breaks that would destabilise classical models like ARIMA fitted on few observations.
- Core assumption: The target series' generating process shares enough common structure with the pre-training distribution that useful patterns can be transferred without fine-tuning.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "...pre-trained on large, heterogeneous corpora, TSFMs can provide strong zero-shot priors..."
  - [section 3.4] "We used zero-shot TSFMs to generate enrolment forecasts while keeping all model weights fixed. This strategy was well-suited to institutional contexts with short histories... because it avoided dataset-specific training."
  - [corpus] Weak direct corpus evidence; related work shows TSFMs generalise but institutional sparsity specifics are novel in this paper.
- Break condition: If the institutional series exhibits a structural break wholly absent from the pre-training distribution (e.g., a unique policy shock with no analogue in training data), the transferred priors may become misleading, leading to systematic bias.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Leakage-safe, engineered covariates inject exogenous, regime-aware signals that help TSFMs anticipate turning points missed by univariate history.
- Mechanism: Covariates like the IOCI (LLM-derived from institutional narratives) or Google Trends (demand proxies) provide information about external operating conditions or public attention. When constructed with strict vintage control (lagged to forecast origin, aggregation to annual frequency), they offer a low-dimensional summary of factors influencing enrolment but not fully captured in the short, noisy target series. TSFMs that support dynamic covariates can condition forecasts on these signals, reducing error around structural breaks.
- Core assumption: The covariate signal contains predictive information about the future path of the target series that is not already encoded in the recent autoregressive history and is robustly measured without look-ahead bias.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.1.1] "We followed a standardised extraction procedure... aggregated monthly RSV into annual summaries... and constructed leakage-safe covariates."
  - [section 3.1.2] "We specified a reproducible procedure for constructing the IOCI... explicit vintage constraints were enforced to prevent look-ahead bias."
  - [section 4.1.1] "Adding calibrated IOCI substantially improved the performance of the Chronos-Bolt family... with consistent gains across the error metrics."
- Break condition: If the covariate construction introduces subtle leakage (e.g., using future-available information in the aggregation window) or if the signal is too noisy/unstable at annual frequency, conditioning can degrade performance by adding variance or spurious correlation.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The benefit of covariates is highly contingent on architectural compatibility between the TSFM's conditioning mechanism and the covariate's statistical properties.
- Mechanism: Different TSFM architectures handle exogenous inputs differently (e.g., masked-encoder vs. decoder-only). A stable, smooth covariate like the IOCI may benefit a model with robust covariate integration (Chronos-Bolt), while a volatile, high-frequency proxy like raw Google Trends might destabilise a model with weaker conditioning logic (e.g., some TimesFM or ARIMA variants). Feature engineering (EWMA transforms) aims to smooth covariates to match the annual decision frequency and model stability requirements.
- Core assumption: Model architectures vary in their susceptibility to brittle conditioning; a covariate's value is not absolute but depends on how its statistical properties (smoothness, noise level, scaling) interact with the model's internal processing of external channels.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1.1] "For domestic enrolments, Chronos-Bolt models improved with IOCI... For international enrolments, Moirai models excelled... with Moirai-Base improving when conditioned on engineered Google Trends."
  - [section 4.1.3] "The engineered Google Trends features induced a material shift... Moirai-Base ascended to the top rank, whereas Moirai-Small ceded its lead... indicating that Google Trends acts as a selective demand-proxy signal."
  - [section 5.2] "The incremental value of covariates is heterogeneous and model-dependent... While IOCI contains a stabilising, regime-aware signal, its utility is contingent on the model’s conditioning mechanism."
- Break condition: If the covariate's transformed distribution (e.g., after EWMA) remains too volatile relative to the model's capacity to learn stable associations within the limited training window, or if the model's conditioning path is misspecified, the added channel can amplify estimation error and worsen forecasts.

## Foundational Learning
- **Time Series Cross-Validation with Strict Vintage Control**
  - Why needed here: To evaluate forecasting performance without look-ahead bias in a sparse annual setting. The expanding-window backtest must ensure that at forecast origin *t*, only data available at *t* (including lagged, vintage-aligned covariates) is used. This prevents leakage from future information, which would invalidate performance estimates.
  - Quick check question: If your covariate is a 2-year EWMA of Google Trends computed at year *t*, what is the latest data point you can use in that EWMA calculation without peeking into the future?
- **Feature Engineering for Low-Frequency Covariates**
  - Why needed here: Raw high-frequency signals (e.g., monthly Google Trends) are noisy and mismatched to the annual decision horizon. Engineering transforms (EWMA with specific spans, lag-1 features) suppress short-term volatility and create smoother, more stable predictors that are less likely to induce overfitting in small samples.
  - Quick check question: Why might a 3-year EWMA of a covariate be more appropriate than a raw monthly value for an annual enrolment forecast?
- **Probabilistic Calibration Metrics (CRPS, PIT)**
  - Why needed here: Point accuracy (MAE, RMSE) does not assess forecast uncertainty quality. TSFMs output predictive distributions; we need proper scoring rules (CRPS) and diagnostic plots (PIT) to verify that uncertainty quantification is reliable—i.e., that predicted intervals have correct coverage and are neither over- nor under-dispersed.
  - Quick check question: What does a PIT histogram that is U-shaped (more mass near 0 and 1) indicate about a forecaster's predictive distribution?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Data Ingestion & Vintage Alignment Module → Feature Engineering Pipeline → TSFM Inference Wrapper → Backtest Orchestrator → Evaluation & Diagnostics Module.

Critical path: Data Ingestion → Feature Engineering → TSFM Inference → Evaluation. The vintage alignment in the first step is non-negotiable; a single leakage event corrupts all subsequent metrics.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Model Capacity vs. Sparsity:** Larger TSFMs (Moirai-Large, Chronos-Bolt-Base) have more parameters but risk overfitting on ~15-20 annual points. Smaller variants (Chronos-Bolt-Tiny) often perform more robustly.
- **Covariate Smoothing vs. Responsiveness:** A longer EWMA span (e.g., 3-year) reduces noise but may smooth away true regime shifts. The 2-year span is a compromise.
- **Covariate Set Size:** Adding more covariates increases the risk of spurious correlation in small samples. The study uses a compact, theory-driven set (IOCI, 3 Google Trends features).

Failure signatures:
- **ARIMA with covariates:** Sharp degradation in MAE/RMSE when IOCI or Trends are added (seen in Tables 2 & 4), indicating covariate misspecification or instability in parameter estimation with few annual observations.
- **PIT histogram U-shaped or inverted:** Indicates over-dispersion (forecasts too wide) or under-dispersion (forecasts too narrow) respectively. For example, Moirai-Small no-covariate international forecasts may show slight under-dispersion.
- **Large discrepancy between no-covariate and covariate-conditioned errors for the same model:** Suggests the model's conditioning mechanism is brittle. E.g., TimesFM domestic performance collapses with IOCI.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Baseline Persistence & ARIMA:** Run the expanding-window backtest with no covariates. Record MAE, RMSE, SMAPE for Persistence and manually selected ARIMA(p,d,q). This establishes the irreducible autocorrelation benchmark.
2. **TSFM Unconditional Benchmark:** For each TSFM family (Moirai-Small/Base/Large, Chronos-Bolt-Tiny/Mini/Small/Base, Chronos-2, TimesFM-200/500), run zero-shot inference with **no covariates**. Compare point errors against Experiment 1. Identify which model families have a inherent zero-shot advantage.
3. **Covariate Sensitivity Test (IOCI for Domestic):** For the Chronos-Bolt family (Tiny, Small, Mini, Base) and for Moirai-Small/Base, run the backtest with the calibrated IOCI as the sole covariate. Compute ΔMAE relative to Experiment 1. Flag models where ΔMAE is meaningfully negative (improvement) vs. positive (degradation). This directly tests RQ2 for the domestic cohort. Repeat analogous test with engineered Google Trends for international cohort.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: To what extent do zero-shot TSFMs improve upon classical baselines for annual forecasting under severe data sparsity?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors explicitly state this as RQ1 in Section 2.6, benchmarking TSFMs against persistence and ARIMA under data sparsity.
- Why unresolved: The study finds competitive performance but is limited to a single institution with only 19 annual observations. Results vary by model capacity and cohort, and the extent of improvement may not generalize to other institutional contexts with different data characteristics or structural break patterns.
- What evidence would resolve it: Multi-institution benchmark studies across diverse universities (e.g., public/private, different countries, varying sizes) using consistent leakage-safe protocols, with meta-analysis to estimate the average treatment effect of zero-shot TSFMs relative to classical baselines under controlled sparsity levels.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Under strict leakage controls, when do external covariates improve zero-shot TSFM accuracy, and when do they destabilise conditioning?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors explicitly state this as RQ2 in Section 2.6, investigating covariate impacts under leakage-safe protocols.
- Why unresolved: The paper shows heterogeneous covariate effects: IOCI improves Chronos-Bolt for domestic enrolments but degrades TimesFM/ARIMA; Google Trends benefits Moirai for international but not all models. These findings are based on a limited set of covariates and models, and the conditions that determine beneficial versus destabilising conditioning are not fully characterized, especially under diverse sparsity regimes.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic ablation studies across a wide range of TSFM architectures, covariate types (e.g., demographic, economic, text-derived), and transformation methods, combined with institutional contexts, to identify predictors of covariate utility and instability under annual sparsity.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Which covariate constructions and feature-engineering choices yield robust and reproducible improvements in accuracy and probabilistic calibration, and which choices are brittle?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors explicitly state this as RQ3 in Section 2.6, evaluating engineered covariates like EWMA and lag-1.
- Why unresolved: The study evaluates only a few engineered features (2-year/3-year EWMA, lag-1) for Google Trends and a single LLM-derived index (IOCI) with a fixed prompt. The robustness of these constructions to alternative engineering parameters (e.g., window sizes, smoothing factors), different LLM prompts, or other covariate sources remains unknown, particularly under the severe noise of annual data.
- What evidence would resolve it: Large-scale experimental designs that vary feature engineering parameters (e.g., EWMA spans, lag choices, aggregation methods) and covariate sources, assessing stability of improvements across many bootstrap resamples or expanding-window backtests under data sparsity, with sensitivity analysis to identify brittle configurations.

## Limitations
- Single-institution dataset with only ~20 annual observations limits generalizability.
- Covariate construction (e.g., IOCI via LLM) may not be portable across different institutional contexts.
- Key implementation details for some TSFMs (TimesFM residual correction, Chronos-Bolt covariate interface) are underspecified, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- TSFM priors stabilize sparse forecasts: Medium
