# Discern Truth from Falsehood: Reducing Over-Refusal via Contrastive Refinement

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03323
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03323
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A two-stage contrastive refinement method (DCR) reduces over-refusal on benign queries (e.g., from 73% to 98% on XSTest for Qwen2.5-1.5B) while maintaining high safety defense (~81%) with minimal general QA impact.

## Executive Summary
Large language models safety-aligned to reject harmful content often over-refuse benign but lexically sensitive queries. This paper identifies the root cause as high gradient similarity between toxic and superficially toxic prompts during alignment, leading to undesired spillover. The proposed solution, Discernment via Contrastive Refinement (DCR), employs a two-stage process: first, contrastive learning on mid-layer representations separates the two prompt types; second, standard safety tuning is applied. Evaluations across five over-refusal benchmarks show DCR significantly improves compliance on benign inputs without compromising safety or general capability.

## Method Summary
DCR consists of two sequential fine-tuning stages. Stage 1 applies contrastive learning (using Circle loss) to activations at a middle model layer (e.g., layer 13 for Qwen2.5-1.5B) on a balanced batch of toxic and seemingly toxic prompts, while freezing all subsequent layers. This explicitly reduces the pairwise similarity that drives learning spillover. Stage 2 performs standard supervised safety fine-tuning (SFT) on a mixed dataset of general and toxic/refusal pairs, typically using LoRA, starting from the contrastively refined checkpoint. The disentanglement from Stage 1 prevents refusal knowledge from transferring to benign queries during Stage 2.

## Key Results
- Compliance on benign, seemingly toxic queries jumps dramatically (e.g., XSTest: 73% → 98% for Qwen2.5-1.5B).
- Defense success rate against truly toxic content remains high (~81%), comparable to standard safety tuning.
- General QA performance (MMLU, ARC) shows negligible degradation, indicating the method's specificity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Over-refusal emerges from high gradient similarity (NTK) between toxic and seemingly toxic prompts during safety alignment.
- **Evidence:** Learning dynamics analysis (Eq. 2) shows refusal probability for a prompt x′ increases with training on x proportionally to K_t(x′, x). Figure 3 demonstrates persistently high K_t between the two prompt types.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The two-stage process disentangles representations, decoupling the learning transfer.
- **Evidence:** Proposition 1 links similarity in mid-layer activations (h_x'^T Qℓ h_x) to K_t similarity. Figures 4 and 5 show DCR maintains high compliance on benign prompts during safety SFT and increases rejection only for toxic prompts, whereas a single-stage approach (STL) causes refusal to rise for both.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Circle loss is effective because it adaptively focuses on the hardest, most confusable negative pairs.
- **Evidence:** Section 5 and A.8 explain that Circle loss weights negative pairs more heavily when they are already too similar, directly targeting the pairs that contribute most to high K_t. DCR outperforms STL-aug (same data, no contrastive loss), highlighting the necessity of the adaptive objective.

## Foundational Learning
- **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK):** Quantifies how gradients from training on one prompt affect another. *Why needed:* Forms the theoretical link between representation similarity and behavioral spillover. *Quick check:* If K_t(x′, x) is high, training on x should strongly increase refusal on x′.
- **Contrastive Learning on Intermediate Representations:** Manipulates geometry of hidden states. *Why needed:* Propositions show that reducing similarity in a middle layer's activations bounds and reduces the NTK-based influence. *Quick check:* Why target a mid-layer? It balances affect on NTK and risk of catastrophic forgetting.
- **Circle Loss:** An adaptive contrastive loss that scales penalty based on how much a negative pair violates a similarity margin. *Why needed:* Focuses optimization on the "hard" toxic/seemingly-toxic pairs that are most problematic. *Quick check:* How does it differ from triplet loss? It uses a continuous, similarity-based weight instead of a fixed margin.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Base LLM -> [Contrastive Module (layer ℓ extraction + Circle loss, tail frozen)] -> Contrastive Checkpoint -> [Safety SFT Module (LoRA on full model)] -> Final Model.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Load base model (Qwen2.5-1.5B).
  2. Prepare contrastive data: 250 seemingly toxic (XSTest), 500 toxic (HH-RLHF). Extract activations at layer ℓ (e.g., 13) per prompt.
  3. Train with Circle loss + small NLL regularizer for 2–3 epochs, freezing layers > ℓ. Save checkpoint.
  4. Load checkpoint, apply LoRA adapters.
  5. Train on SFT data: 20k general (Alpaca) + 1k toxic + GPT-4o-generated refusals for 3–4 epochs.
  6. Evaluate on over-refusal, safety, and QA benchmarks.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Layer ℓ choice:** A middle layer (e.g., 13/27 for Qwen2.5) is chosen; too shallow may not influence K_t, too deep increases compute/forgetting risk.
  - **Contrastive dataset ratio:** 2:1 toxic:seemingly-toxic works best; imbalance reduces efficacy.
  - **Contrastive epochs:** 3 epochs optimal; more causes overfitting and QA drop.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - K_t similarity between toxic and seemingly toxic prompts remains high after Stage 1.
  - During Stage 2, compliance on seemingly toxic prompts plummets alongside safety improvement (STL curve in Fig 4).
  - Final model shows >5 point drop on MMLU/ARC, suggesting over-optimization of Stage 1.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Measure average normalized ||K_t||_F between toxic and seemingly toxic prompts before and after Stage 1; expect a major drop.
  2. Track refusal probability during Stage 2 for 250 seemingly toxic, 300 toxic, and 300 normal prompts; DCR curve should rise only for toxic.
  3. Vary LoRA rank in Stage 2 and plot XSTest compliance vs. average defense success; DCR's Pareto curve should dominate STL's.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to large models:** Does DCR remain effective for LLMs beyond 8B parameters? Experiments were limited to smaller scales.
- **Residual similarity to normal prompts:** DCR does not fully reduce similarity between seemingly toxic and normal prompts. The implications of this remnant coupling are unknown.
- **Preserving factual knowledge:** How can DCR be modified to avoid slight QA degradation while retaining over-refusal reduction?

## Limitations
- Replication details missing: activation pooling method, exact batch sampling strategy, and scope of NLL regularizer.
- Contrastive layer ℓ is chosen heuristically; its generalizability across architectures is unproven.
- Small, curated contrastive datasets may not capture the full diversity of toxic/manipulative text.
- Reliance on GPT-4o for some evaluations hurts reproducibility and may introduce bias.
- Long-term stability of the disentanglement through Stage 2 updates is not deeply analyzed.

## Confidence
- Gradient similarity causes over-refusal -> **Medium** (strong correlation and theory, but causal intervention evidence is indirect).
- Two-stage disentanglement prevents spillover -> **Medium** (consistent empirical gains, but mechanism not directly validated via intervention on K_t).
- Circle loss is key for hard negative separation -> **Low-Medium** (outperforms simple baselines, but comparative ablations against other contrastive losses are limited).
- DCR's benchmark improvements -> **High** (results are consistent across 5 over-refusal tests and 3 model sizes).

## Next Checks
1. **Direct NTK measurement:** Estimate K_t similarity via gradient dot-products before/after Stage 1 to confirm the targeted reduction.
2. **Layer sensitivity ablation:** Systematically vary the chosen layer ℓ across early, middle, and late positions to test robustness of the design choice.
3. **Objective comparison:** Ablate DCR's Circle loss against standard supervised contrastive or triplet loss on the same data to isolate the benefit of adaptive weighting.
