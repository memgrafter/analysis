# Measuring Reasoning Trace Legibility: Can Those Who Understand Teach?

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.20508  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20508  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Reasoning trace legibility (efficiency and transfer utility) is misaligned with final-answer accuracy; top-accuracy models are poor teachers, and a Pareto frontier exists between efficiency and transfer utility.

## Executive Summary
Reasoning language models (RLMs) produce intermediate traces that are essential for oversight, distillation, and multi‑agent collaboration. This paper introduces **Legi‑Val**, a framework for measuring trace legibility along two dimensions: **efficiency** (token length, redundancy, backtracking) and **transfer utility** (how effectively weaker student models learn from teacher traces). Evaluating 12 RLMs on 99,528 traces across MATH, GPQA, and Connections, the study finds a negative correlation between accuracy and transfer utility, a trade‑off between efficiency and transfer utility, and negligible intrinsic reward for legibility in existing reward models. The results challenge the dominant reinforcement‑learning‑with‑verifiable‑rewards (RLVR) paradigm that optimizes solely for final correctness and call for multi‑objective training of legible reasoning.

## Method Summary
Full reasoning traces are generated from 12 teacher RLMs on each dataset. Traces are segmented into atomic steps via NLTK sentence tokenization. Prefixes are built by truncating traces at regular step intervals (e.g., every three steps). Two weak student models (Phi‑3.5‑Mini and LLaMA‑3.2‑1B) are prompted to continue from each prefix; their final answers are extracted (with fallback forcing prompts) and compared to ground truth. Student accuracy as a function of prefix progress yields transfer‑utility metrics: **First‑Order Transfer Utility (FOTU)** (area under the accuracy curve), **Second‑Order Uniformity (SOTU)** (entropy of the first‑correct‑step distribution), and **Regression Rate** (frequency of accuracy drops with more steps). Efficiency metrics are computed directly from the full trace: reciprocal token length, redundancy (proportion of steps with cosine similarity > 0.8 to any prior step using MiniLM‑L6‑v2), and backtracking (proportion of steps flagged by a Gemini 2.5‑Flash judge). Model‑level aggregates enable correlation and Pareto analysis; reward model scores are correlated with legibility metrics to assess intrinsic preferences.

## Key Results
- **Negative accuracy‑transfer correlation**: Higher final accuracy strongly associates with poorer transfer utility (Spearman ρ = ‑0.47). The best model (GPT‑OSS‑120B, 82% accuracy) ranks 11th on FOTU; the worst (LLaMA‑Nano, 49%) ranks first.
- **Pareto frontier between efficiency and transfer utility**: Concise traces (e.g., Gemma‑27B) transfer poorly, while verbose, high‑transfer traces (e.g., DeepSeek‑R1) are token‑inefficient. No model excels at both.
- **Reward models ignore legibility**: When controlling for answer correctness, the correlation between reward scores and transfer metrics is near zero (ρ ≈ ‑0.05), indicating that current RMs do not reward legible steps intrinsically.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: RLVR Optimizes Terminal Outcomes, Not Pedagogy
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) optimizes a scalar reward equal to final answer correctness. Intermediate reasoning is treated instrumentally, not as a first‑class artifact to be legible. This leads to "overthinking" traces that rely on strong internal reconstruction (unhelpful to weak learners) or over‑compression that omits scaffolding steps. Evidence: GPT‑OSS‑120B (top accuracy) ranks 11th on transfer utility, while LLaMA‑Nemotron‑Nano (low accuracy) ranks first.

### Mechanism 2: Pareto Frontier Between Efficiency and Transfer Utility
Efficiency penalizes verbosity and non‑linearity; transfer utility rewards explicit, incremental, and often verbose scaffolding. Improving one dimension typically degrades the other, creating a Pareto frontier. Evidence: Gemma‑27B is efficient but transfers poorly; DeepSeek‑R1 transfers well but is inefficient. No model dominates all axes, confirming inherent multi‑objective tension.

### Mechanism 3: Reward Models Lack Legibility Signal
RMs are trained on pairwise preferences where the chosen trace yields a correct answer. They learn a shortcut: reward correlates with correctness, not with structural properties that aid learning. Consequently, when correctness is held constant, RM scores show negligible correlation with transfer utility. Evidence: At the trace level, RM score vs. FOTU correlation is ‑0.041; in the correct‑only subset, correlations vanish (SOTU shows only a weak positive). Thus RMs have no intrinsic appreciation for legibility.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)** – Trains models on final‑answer correctness; explains why intermediate steps are neglected. *Quick check*: What objective does RLVR optimize, and what does it treat as ancillary?
- **Weak‑to‑Strong Generalization** – Strong teacher’s outputs must supervise a weaker student; transfer utility operationalizes this. *Quick check*: In weak‑to‑strong, who generates the supervision signal, and who is being trained?
- **Pareto Optimality** – A point where improving one objective worsens another; underlies the efficiency‑transfer trade‑off. *Quick check*: If two objectives conflict, what does a point on the frontier imply?
- **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) and Test‑Time Compute** – Reasoning traces arise from scaling test‑time compute; this scaling dimension is central to legibility. *Quick check*: How might increasing test‑time compute (more tokens) affect legibility?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Teacher RLMs → Full Reasoning Traces → Step Segmentation (NLTK) → Prefixes (every 3 steps) → Student Models (Phi‑3.5‑Mini, LLaMA‑3.2‑1B) → Continued Answers → Grading → Accuracy Bins → Transfer Utility Metrics (FOTU, SOTU, Regression Rate). Efficiency Metrics computed independently: Token Length, Redundancy (cosine similarity > 0.8), Backtracking (Gemini judge). Datasets (MATH, GPQA, Connections) supply problems; Reward Models (Skywork, AllenAI) provide baseline scores.

**Critical Path**  
1. Generate full reasoning trace from a teacher RLM.  
2. Segment trace into steps using NLTK.  
3. Create prefix truncations at regular step intervals.  
4. Prompt student model to continue; allow up to 1,024 tokens.  
5. Extract final answer (standard parsing + forcing prompt fallback).  
6. Bin student success rates by prefix progress (2% granularity).  
7. Compute FOTU (area under curve), SOTU (entropy of first‑correct bin), and Regression Rate.  
8. Aggregate across traces; correlate with efficiency metrics and accuracy.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Student model*: Choice of Phi vs. Llama yields moderate rank correlation (~0.55) but absolute score differences.  
- *Backtracking judge*: Gemini 2.5‑Flash with JSON rubric; human agreement Kappa 0.712 ⇒ judge substitution may alter efficiency rankings.  
- *Redundancy threshold*: τ = 0.8 chosen via elbow analysis; may not generalize (e.g., Connections).  
- *Binning*: 2% bins smooth curves but can mask fine‑grained contributions; absolute step counts could be used instead.  
- *Failure signatures*:  
  - High teacher accuracy but low FOTU (top‑3 models on MATH/GPQA).  
  - SOTU unstable across tasks (overall ρ = 0.19) but stable within STEM (ρ > 0.75).  
  - Reward model correlations drop to ≈0 in correct‑only subset, confirming confound.

**First Three Experiments**  
1. **Rank correlation across datasets**: Compute Spearman’s ρ for each metric; expect token length/redundancy stable, FOTU unstable, SOTU highly stable (ρ ∈ [0.58, 0.91]).  
2. **Student model ablation**: Compare FOTU/SOTU rankings using Phi vs. Llama; expect moderate rank stability (ρ > 0.5) but absolute variations (e.g., DeepSeek‑R1 more sensitive).  
3. **Reward model correlation**: For all traces and correct‑only subset, compute Spearman between RM score and transfer metrics; near‑zero correlations (ρ ≈ 0) anticipated in correct‑only condition.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Multi‑objective RL trade‑off**: Can explicit legibility rewards in RL training break the observed efficiency‑transfer Pareto frontier, or is the trade‑off inevitable?
2. **Domain‑adaptive legibility**: Transfer utility rankings show low cross‑task agreement (ρ = 0.17); Connections diverges from STEM. How should legibility optimization adapt across diverse reasoning tasks?
3. **Human alignment**: Do automated metrics (FOTU, SOTU, redundancy, backtracking) correlate with human judgments of legibility (comprehensibility, trust, cognitive load)? Current validation is limited.
4. **In‑context vs. fine‑tuning transfer**: Does in‑context transfer utility predict success when student models are fine‑tuned on teacher traces? The paper only evaluates in‑context scaffolding.

## Limitations
- Backtracking metric relies on Gemini 2.5‑Flash; human agreement is moderate, and alternative judges could shift efficiency rankings.
- Transfer utility depends on student model; rank correlation between Phi and Llama is only moderate (~0.55), indicating sensitivity to student choice.
- NLTK step segmentation may split mathematical expressions, potentially distorting efficiency and transfer metrics.
- Redundancy threshold (cosine > 0.8) was data‑driven; may not generalize to all domains.
- Reward model analyses confirm confound by answer correctness, limiting insights into true legibility preferences.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1** (RLVR → poor legibility): *Medium* – correlational but consistent across models and tasks.  
- **Mechanism 2** (Pareto frontier): *High* – clear trade‑off observed across metrics, models, and datasets.  
- **Mechanism 3** (RMs ignore legibility): *High* – trace‑level and correct‑only analyses show near‑zero correlations.  
- **Negative accuracy‑transfer correlation**: *High* (ρ = ‑0.47).  
- **SOTU stability**: *High* within STEM domains (ρ > 0.75), *Medium* across all tasks (ρ = 0.19).

## Next Checks
1. Test transfer rankings with alternative student models (e.g., GPT‑4o‑mini, Claude‑3.5‑Haiku) to confirm robustness; expect rank correlation > 0.5.
2. Perform multi‑objective RL fine‑tuning on a base RLM with composite rewards (accuracy + Legi‑Val transfer metrics); assess whether any model can surpass the observed Pareto frontier.
3. Replace the backtracking LLM judge with a rule‑based system (e.g., phrases indicating revisions) and recompute efficiency metrics to evaluate stability of the frontier.
