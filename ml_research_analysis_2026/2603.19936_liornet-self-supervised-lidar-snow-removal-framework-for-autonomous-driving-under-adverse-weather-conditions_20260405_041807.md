# LIORNet: Self-Supervised LiDAR Snow Removal Framework for Autonomous Driving under Adverse Weather Conditions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19936
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19936
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: State-of-the-art snow removal with precision up to 0.672, recall up to 0.920 (U‑Net++ w/ post‑processing), highest F5‑score, real‑time speed (43.5 Hz), and robust validation on real snowfall data.

## Executive Summary
LiDAR point clouds degrade significantly under snowfall, introducing sparse, spurious points that threaten perception in autonomous driving. Existing remedies fall into three isolated camps: distance‑based filters, intensity‑based thresholds, or learning‑based denoisers, none of which fully exploit physical cues. LIORNet unifies these paradigms through a self‑supervised framework. It generates snow/noise pseudo‑labels by fusing multiple physical and statistical cues—range‑dependent intensity, zero reflectivity, point sparsity, and sensing range constraints—applied in a cascade. A U‑Net++ backbone is trained with an uncertainty‑weighted multi‑loss objective that balances the heterogeneous supervision signals. The approach operates on 2D range‑image representations for efficiency and includes a simple post‑processing rule to restore distant points. On the WADS and CADC datasets, LIORNet achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance (precision ≤ 0.672, recall ≤ 0.920) and runs at 43.5 Hz, meeting real‑time requirements. Its robustness is further demonstrated on real‑world snowfall data from Inje, Luleå, and Copenhagen.

## Method Summary
LIORNet begins by projecting a raw 3D LiDAR point cloud into a 2D range image with three channels: range, intensity, and reflectivity. A cascade of four rule‑based conditions then produces pseudo‑labels for snow points: (1) a range‑dependent intensity threshold isolates low‑intensity returns; (2) zero reflectivity further refines candidates; (3) an edge‑aware restoration step protects object boundaries; and (4) a density‑based object removal discards points in dense clusters. The resulting mask serves as supervision for a U‑Net++ encoder‑decoder network with residual blocks and deep supervision (auxiliary outputs at multiple scales). The loss combines five terms—intensity consistency, reflectivity discrimination, edge suppression, density sparsity, and a threshold penalty—each scaled by a learnable uncertainty weight, enabling adaptive balancing of the competing objectives. During inference, a post‑processing rule marks all points beyond the maximum sensing range (71.235 m) as non‑snow, and the final mask is applied to the original point cloud to filter out snow points.

## Key Results
- **SOTA performance**: On WADS and CADC, LIORNet (U‑Net++ + post‑processing) attains the highest F5‑score, with precision up to 0.672 and recall up to 0.920.
- **Real‑time operation**: Inference speed reaches 43.5 Hz, comfortably surpassing the 20 Hz requirement for autonomous driving.
- **Strong generalization**: The model trained on WADS generalizes qualitatively to real snowfall data collected in Inje, Luleå, and Copenhagen without retraining.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Self‑supervised pseudo‑label generation via fused physical cues
- **Claim**: LIORNet creates effective snow/noise pseudo‑labels without manual annotation by sequentially applying four rule‑based conditions to initial intensity‑thresholded points.
- **Mechanism**: The cascade first selects low‑intensity points, then refines by zero reflectivity, restores points near structural edges, and finally removes candidates that lie in dense clusters typical of objects. This multi‑criterion fusion progressively narrows down the snow mask.
- **Core assumption**: Snow points consistently exhibit low reflectivity, spatial sparsity, and avoid object edges, while valid objects show opposite patterns across diverse snowy scenes.
- **Evidence anchors**: The paper’s Algorithm 1 and Section III‑C detail the four conditions; ablation in Table I shows that removing any condition degrades performance, confirming their complementary roles.
- **Break condition**: If snow properties deviate (e.g., wet snow with high reflectivity or dense drifts mimicking objects), the pseudo‑labels become unreliable and propagate errors to the learned model.

### Mechanism 2: Uncertainty‑weighted multi‑loss optimization
- **Claim**: The self‑supervised loss uses learnable uncertainty weights to dynamically balance the five condition‑specific terms, preventing any single term from dominating training.
- **Mechanism**: The total loss (Eq. 6) sums five terms, each multiplied by \(e^{-2\sigma_i}\) (with \(\sigma_i\) learned). This scheme, inspired by Kendall et al., automatically determines the relative importance of each supervision signal during training.
- **Core assumption**: The reliability of each physical constraint varies across scenes and sensor characteristics; a fixed weighting would be suboptimal.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section III‑D introduces the uncertainty weighting; Table I (IDs 4 vs. 5‑8) demonstrates that disabling uncertainty harms performance, while Table III’s ablations confirm each term’s contribution.
- **Break condition**: Systematic biases in pseudo‑labels (e.g., consistently missing edge snow) could cause the uncertainty mechanism to down‑weight corrective signals, locking in a suboptimal solution.

### Mechanism 3: U‑Net++ backbone with deep supervision
- **Claim**: The U‑Net++ architecture with nested skip connections and deep supervision effectively translates 2D range‑intensity‑reflectivity images into precise snow masks, leveraging multi‑scale features.
- **Mechanism**: The encoder‑decoder aggregates features at multiple resolutions; deep supervision applies loss at intermediate decoder outputs, forcing the network to learn discriminative representations at different scales and preserving fine structures.
- **Core assumption**: Projecting 3D point clouds to 2D range images retains sufficient spatial and contextual information for snow segmentation, and multi‑scale supervision improves generalization to diverse snow densities.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section III‑B and Fig. 2(d) describe the architecture; Table I shows U‑Net++ (w/ DS) consistently outperforms a plain U‑Net (w/ DS) in IoU.
- **Break condition**: In scenes where snow drifts form large, object‑like clusters, the 2D projection and multi‑scale features may fail to disentangle noise from structure, leading to over‑aggressive removal or missed snow.

## Foundational Learning

### Self‑Supervised Pseudo‑Labeling
- **Why needed**: Manual point‑wise snow annotation is prohibitively expensive and subjective. Pseudo‑labels provide a scalable training signal by encoding known physical properties of snow (e.g., low reflectivity, sparsity).
- **Quick check**: If Condition 2 (zero reflectivity) were removed from pseudo‑label generation, what primary failure mode would likely emerge in the trained model?

### Uncertainty‑Weighted Multi‑Loss Optimization
- **Why needed**: The five loss terms regulate different, sometimes conflicting, aspects of the pseudo‑label rules (e.g., sparsity loss encourages fewer snow points, edge loss protects boundaries). Uncertainty weighting prevents any single term from dominating and destabilizing training.
- **Quick check**: How does the stop‑gradient strategy in the sparsity loss term (Eq. 4) prevent the network from converging to a trivial “predict no snow” solution?

### 2D Range Image Representation for 3D Tasks
- **Why needed**: Processing unordered 3D point clouds with convolutions is inefficient. Range images (range, intensity, reflectivity channels) provide a structured, dense grid amenable to fast 2D CNN operations while preserving key LiDAR measurement properties.
- **Quick check**: What critical geometric information is lost when projecting a 3D point cloud to a 2D range image, and how might LIORNet’s rules (e.g., edge detection) be affected?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: 3D point cloud → Preprocessing & projection to 2D range image (range, intensity, reflectivity) → U‑Net++ backbone (encoder‑decoder with residual blocks and nested skip connections) → Deep supervision heads (auxiliary outputs at 1/16, 1/8, 1/4 scales) → Final snow probability mask → Inference‑only post‑processing (range‑based restoration) → Mask application to original point cloud → Filtered point cloud.

- **Critical path**: Point cloud → Range image → U‑Net++ forward pass (with deep supervision) → Compute five loss terms w.r.t. pseudo‑labels → Weighted sum → Backpropagation → (Inference) Threshold mask → Apply post‑processing → Filter points.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Accuracy vs. speed: U‑Net++ with deep supervision improves segmentation quality but adds computational overhead; post‑processing is cheap yet significantly boosts precision.
  - Generality vs. simplicity: Pseudo‑label rules are hand‑crafted and sensor‑agnostic in principle, but thresholds (e.g., sensing range 71.235 m) depend on specific mounting parameters; switching LiDAR models requires re‑deriving these constants.
  - Learning capacity vs. overfitting: With only noisy pseudo‑labels, the model risks learning rule imperfections; uncertainty weighting and the diverse condition set act as regularizers.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Over‑filtering (low precision)**: Excessive removal of valid points, especially at edges or in dense clusters, when edge or sparsity constraints are under‑weighted.
  - **Under‑filtering (low recall)**: Residual snow points, particularly low‑intensity or edge snow, when intensity thresholds are too high or edge processing is too aggressive.
  - **Sensor mismatch**: Applying a model trained on Velodyne data to an Ouster sensor without adjusting range‑dependent thresholds causes systematic errors.
  - **Extreme weather breakdown**: In heavy snow where snow density approaches object density, the density‑based condition fails, leading either to missed snow or removed objects.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline comparison** (IV‑C‑2): LIORNet vs. rule‑based (DROR, LIOR, D‑LIOR) and learning‑based (LiSnowNet) methods on WADS and CADC, measuring precision, recall, F5‑score, and runtime.
  2. **Ablation study** (IV‑C‑3): Systematically disable each loss term and uncertainty weighting to validate the contribution of every physical constraint and the adaptive loss scheme.
  3. **Real‑world generalization** (IV‑C‑4): Apply the WADS‑trained model to self‑collected data from Inje, Luleå, and Copenhagen; perform visual inspection to confirm robustness across unseen sensors, snow intensities, and geographies.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Adaptation to raw 3D point clouds**  
   - *Question*: How can LIORNet be adapted to process raw 3D point clouds directly without projecting to 2D range images, and would this improve preservation of fine geometric structures?  
   - *Basis*: The paper explicitly states this as future work (“extend LIORNet by directly leveraging raw 3D point clouds instead of 2D projections”).  
   - *Why unresolved*: The current 2D projection may lose fine‑grained 3D details (e.g., thin objects, small clusters), and no 3D‑native architectures have been explored.

2. **Incorporating temporal information**  
   - *Question*: Does incorporating temporal information from consecutive LiDAR frames improve both denoising accuracy and computational efficiency, and what architectural changes are optimal?  
   - *Basis*: The paper mentions “incorporating temporal information to improve both efficiency and accuracy” as a direction.  
   - *Why unresolved*: The present model processes frames independently, missing potential temporal redundancies that could stabilize predictions and reduce per‑frame computation; no temporal module is currently explored.

3. **Generalization to rain and fog**  
   - *Question*: Can the self‑supervised pseudo‑label generation strategy be generalized to rain and fog, which have noise characteristics distinct from snow?  
   - *Basis*: The paper aims to extend beyond snow to other adverse weather conditions.  
   - *Why unresolved*: Pseudo‑label rules (e.g., zero reflectivity for snow) are calibrated for snow; rain and fog produce noise with different physical properties, requiring a redesign of the rule cascade.

## Limitations
- Pseudo‑label rules rely on strict physical properties (low reflectivity, sparsity) that may not hold for wet snow or dense drifts, leading to noisy supervision.
- Hand‑tuned thresholds (intensity range dependence, edge detection) are sensor‑specific; adapting to other LiDAR models requires re‑deriving physics constants.
- The 2D range‑image projection discards fine 3D context, potentially merging thin structures or complex snow geometries into the background.
- Real‑world validation is primarily qualitative; quantitative metrics on external snowfall datasets are lacking, limiting confidence in generalization.
- Post‑processing cutoff at 71.235 m is tied to a specific sensor mounting; its robustness to other configurations is unknown.
