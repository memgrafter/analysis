# Reducing Estimation Uncertainty Using Normalizing Flows and Stratification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10706
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10706
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Stratified normalizing flows consistently reduce estimation variance and improve accuracy over crude Monte Carlo and Gaussian mixture models on synthetic and real high-dimensional datasets.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the fundamental problem of estimating the expectation of a function under an unknown data distribution when only limited samples are available. The authors propose a novel integration of normalizing flows with stratified sampling: a continuous normalizing flow (CNF) is trained to model the unknown distribution by transforming a Gaussian base; stratification is then applied in the latent Gaussian space using Cartesian or spherical splits, and samples are allocated to strata either proportionally or optimally based on a pilot run. This approach retains the flexibility of non-parametric density modeling while gaining the variance reduction benefits of stratified sampling.

Experiments on 2D, 30D, and 128D synthetic datasets and a real wind measurement dataset demonstrate that the method achieves lower standard deviations and higher accuracy (more correct digits) compared to crude Monte Carlo and Gaussian mixture model baselines. For instance, in a 2D task with 16 strata, the stratified flow estimator yields an accuracy of about 2.22 (correct digits) versus 1.68 for crude Monte Carlo. Training requires only moderate sample sizes (500–1000), and the stratification strategies scale to high dimensions via radial or selective coordinate splitting. The results indicate that the method reliably reduces uncertainty without needing to model heavy‑tailed distributions explicitly.

## Method Summary
The method comprises four main stages. First, a continuous normalizing flow (CNF)—based on architectures like FFJORD—is trained by maximum likelihood on the observed samples to learn an invertible transformation from a standard Gaussian base distribution to an approximation of the unknown data distribution. Second, the latent Gaussian space is partitioned into \(m\) equiprobable strata using either Cartesian splits (intervals along each coordinate) or spherical splits (radius and angular sectors). For high dimensions (\(d >\) few), radial‑only (Mrad) or three‑coordinate selections (MHigh3/MRand3) are used to avoid the exponential blow‑up. Third, sample counts \(R_j\) per stratum are assigned either proportionally to stratum probability \(p_j\) or optimally by \(R_j \propto p_j \sigma_j\), where \(\sigma_j\) is an estimate of the within‑stratum standard deviation of \(f\); the latter uses a pilot simulation (e.g., \(R' = R/8\) samples drawn proportionally) to estimate \(\sigma_j\). Fourth, for each stratum, \(R_j\) latent points are drawn, transformed to data space via the CNF inverse, and the target function \(f\) is evaluated. The final expectation estimate is the weighted average of stratum means. This pipeline can be repeated with different random seeds to assess estimator variability.

## Key Results
- In a 2D synthetic example, stratified flow with 16 Cartesian strata achieved an accuracy (AC) of approximately 2.22, while crude Monte Carlo attained about 1.68, and the 95% confidence interval was notably narrower.
- For 30D and 128D tasks involving small probabilities (e.g., \(I \approx 0.0019\)), radial and selective‑coordinate stratification (Mrad, MHigh3) significantly reduced standard deviation and improved accuracy compared to crude Monte Carlo, even with limited training data (500 points).
- On a real‑world AmeriGEOSS wind dataset, spherical stratification with optimal allocation yielded the smallest standard deviations for several functionals (e.g., SD=0.010 vs 0.026 for crude MC), confirming the method’s practical utility.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Flexible Distribution Approximation via Normalizing Flows
- **Claim**: The method approximates the unknown data distribution using a flexible normalizing flow model, enabling sampling from a quasi‑true distribution.
- **Mechanism**: A continuous normalizing flow (CNF) is trained via maximum likelihood on observed data. The CNF transforms a base Gaussian distribution into an approximation of the data density via a learned invertible transformation. This allows generating arbitrary samples from the learned distribution, bypassing parametric assumptions.
- **Core assumption**: The observed samples are representative, and the flow has sufficient capacity to approximate the true distribution.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states the flow‑based model offers greater flexibility; Section 2.1 introduces CNFs; Section 3.4 details training and sampling. Neighboring works on normalizing flows for density estimation support this.
- **Break condition**: If the flow fails to capture multi‑modality or heavy tails, generated samples are biased, leading to inaccurate estimation.

### Mechanism 2: Latent‑Space Stratification for Variance Reduction
- **Claim**: Stratification in the latent Gaussian space reduces estimator variance by facilitating efficient sampling across regions of differing variance.
- **Mechanism**: Instead of stratifying directly in data space (intractable), the method applies stratified sampling to the Gaussian base of the flow. Two strategies: Cartesian (split each latent dimension into intervals) and spherical (split radius and angles). These produce equiprobable strata; samples drawn from each stratum are transformed via the flow to data space.
- **Core assumption**: The stratification in latent space translates effectively to data space because the flow is a diffeomorphism; the variance reduction properties of stratified sampling hold after transformation.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 3.1 describes the two stratification methods; Section 3.4 integrates them with the flow; theoretical guarantees for stratified sampling variance reduction are classical.
- **Break condition**: In high dimensions, Cartesian stratification becomes exponential; spherical may misalign with data structure, causing poor variance reduction if the function correlates poorly with the chosen latent coordinates.

### Mechanism 3: Optimal Allocation for Minimal Variance
- **Claim**: Optimal allocation of sample counts across strata minimizes variance given a fixed total number of samples, further enhancing precision over proportional allocation.
- **Mechanism**: After defining strata, the method can allocate samples proportionally to stratum probabilities or optimally based on estimated within‑stratum variances. Optimal allocation uses a pilot run to estimate \(\sigma_j^2\) and assigns \(R_j \propto p_j \sigma_j\), which minimizes the stratified estimator variance.
- **Core assumption**: The pilot estimates accurately reflect true stratum variances; the classical allocation formula remains valid after flow transformation.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 2.3 derives the optimal split; Section 3.2 describes the pilot simulation procedure.
- **Break condition**: If the pilot run is too small, variance estimates become noisy, leading to suboptimal allocation that may increase variance.

## Foundational Learning

**Normalizing Flows**
- **Why needed here**: They provide a flexible, differentiable way to learn complex densities from data without parametric assumptions, essential for modeling unknown distributions.
- **Quick check**: How does a continuous normalizing flow (CNF) define the density of a data point \(x\) given a base Gaussian \(z\)? (Answer: via the change‑of‑variable formula with the Jacobian determinant of the transformation; see Eq. (2).)

**Stratified Sampling**
- **Why needed here**: It is a classical variance reduction technique that exploits heterogeneity by dividing the population into homogeneous subgroups and sampling each, thereby improving estimation precision.
- **Quick check**: What is the formula for the optimal allocation of samples across strata to minimize variance? (Answer: \(R_j \propto p_j \sigma_j\).)

**Expectation Estimation (Monte Carlo)**
- **Why needed here**: The ultimate goal is to estimate \(I = \mathbb{E}[f(X)]\) from limited samples; this underpins the entire problem.
- **Quick check**: What is the effect of stratified sampling on the variance of the sample mean compared to crude Monte Carlo? (Answer: \(\text{Var}(\hat{Y}_{strat}) = \frac{1}{R}\sum p_j \sigma_j^2 \le \frac{1}{R}\text{Var}(Y) = \text{Var}(\hat{Y}_{CMC})\).)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**
CNF Model -> Stratification Module -> Allocation Optimizer -> Sampler -> Estimator

**Critical path**
1. Train a CNF on the observed data to obtain flow parameters \(\beta\).
2. Choose a stratification method (Cartesian, spherical, or high‑dimensional variant) and the number of strata \(m\).
3. If using optimal allocation, run a pilot simulation with \(R'\) samples (typically drawn proportionally) to estimate within‑stratum variances \(s_j'^2\).
4. Compute the allocation \(R_j\) (proportional or optimal) for each stratum.
5. For each stratum \(j\), draw \(R_j\) latent samples \(Z_j\) from the Gaussian restricted to that stratum.
6. Transform the latent samples to data space via the trained CNF inverse: \(x_j = h_\beta(Z_j)\).
7. Evaluate the target function \(f\) on all transformed samples and compute the stratum mean \(\hat{Y}_j\).
8. Combine the stratum means using a weighted average: \(\hat{Y} = \sum p_j \hat{Y}_j\) (with \(p_j = 1/m\) unless unequal probabilities were used).

**Design tradeoffs**
- *Stratification method*: Cartesian is straightforward but leads to an exponential number of strata in the number of dimensions; spherical scales better but may not align with anisotropic data structures.
- *Number of strata \(m\)*: More strata can reduce variance but increase pilot cost and risk empty strata; typical values are powers of 2 or 3 (e.g., 4, 8, 16, 27).
- *Allocation*: Optimal allocation requires accurate pilot estimates of \(\sigma_j\); too small a pilot yields noisy estimates and may underperform proportional allocation.
- *Flow capacity*: Deeper CNFs and larger hidden dimensions capture more complex distributions but risk overfitting and increase training time; the paper uses widths of 64–256 depending on dimensionality.

**Failure signatures**
- *Flow not converged*: Training loss plateaus at a high value; check negative log‑likelihood on held‑out data or compare flow‑based CMC to ground truth.
- *Poor stratification fit*: Transformed samples exhibit high variance within strata; consider alternative stratification method or apply the Appendix’s rotation heuristic.
- *Allocation misestimation*: Optimal allocation yields higher variance than proportional; increase pilot size \(R'\) or use a more stable variance estimator.
- *High‑dimensional waste*: Cartesian stratification in \(d>10\) creates too many strata; switch to Mrad or coordinate‑selective methods.

**First 3 experiments**
1. *Example 1 (2D synthetic)*: Trained on 1,000 points from a non‑Gaussian density \(g(x_1,x_2) = x_1 \exp(-x_1(x_2+1))\); estimated \(I = P(X_1>1.2, X_2>1.2)\). With \(m=16\) Cartesian strata, stratified flow achieved AC ~2.22 vs ~1.68 for crude MC, and confidence intervals were narrower.
2. *Example 5 (30D synthetic)*: Flow trained on 500 points from a mixed distribution; estimated probabilities of all coordinates exceeding a threshold \(t\) (tiny \(I\approx 0.0019\)). Using Mrad (\(m=7\)) and MHigh3 (\(m=27\)), stratified methods outperformed crude MC in accuracy and stability.
3. *Example 4 (real‑world AmeriGEOSS)*: Trained on 3,000 wind measurements; estimated expectations of several functions (e.g., indicator of pressure difference). Spherical stratification with \(m=4\times4\) and optimal allocation produced the smallest standard deviations (e.g., \(g2\) SD=0.010 vs 0.026 for CMC).

## Limitations
- **Latent‑space stratification validity**: The assumption that equiprobable strata in the Gaussian base translate to effective strata in data space may fail for anisotropic structures, especially with spherical splits, reducing variance gains.
- **Flow approximation bias**: The entire pipeline depends on the CNF accurately capturing the unknown distribution; capacity limits or insufficient training may lead to biased estimates, particularly for heavy‑tailed or highly multi‑modal data.
- **Optimal allocation sensitivity**: The pilot‑based variance estimates for optimal allocation can be noisy, especially with few pilot samples or many strata, potentially degrading performance relative to proportional allocation.

## Confidence
- Normalizing flow approximation: **High**
- Stratification variance reduction: **Medium**
- Optimal allocation improvement: **Low to Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Stratum homogeneity audit**: For a held‑out dataset, transform latent strata boundaries to data space and compute the coefficient of variation (CV) of \(f(x)\) within each stratum. Low CV indicates effective stratification; high CV signals misalignment.
2. **Flow bias sensitivity test**: Deliberately underfit the CNF (e.g., halve network width or train for fewer epochs) and re‑run the stratified estimation. If variance reduction diminishes or bias increases, the method’s robustness to flow quality is confirmed.
3. **Pilot size robustness**: Vary the pilot sample size \(R'\) (e.g., \(R/4\), \(R/8\), \(R/16\)) while keeping total \(R\) fixed, and measure the resulting estimator variance. A strong dependence on \(R'\) would highlight the need for careful pilot tuning.
