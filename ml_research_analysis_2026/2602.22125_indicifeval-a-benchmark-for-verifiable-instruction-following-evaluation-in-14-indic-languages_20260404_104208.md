# IndicIFEval: A Benchmark for Verifiable Instruction-Following Evaluation in 14 Indic Languages

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.22125
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22125
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Primary result:** IndicIFEval provides ~800 human-verified examples per language across two subsets, revealing a persistent performance gap between English and Indic languages in instruction-following, especially on lexical and cross-lingual constraints.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the lack of verifiable instruction-following benchmarks for Indic languages by introducing IndicIFEval, a benchmark with approximately 800 human-verified examples per language across two complementary subsets: IndicIFEval-Trans (localized translations of English prompts) and IndicIFEval-Ground (synthetically generated prompts grounded in native content). Evaluation across a wide range of models (0.5B to 70B+ parameters) shows a consistent performance gap between English and Indic languages. Models excel at format-based constraints but struggle significantly with lexical inclusion/exclusion and cross-lingual tasks (e.g., responding in English when prompted in Indic). Hindi consistently shows the smallest gap, while Sanskrit lags the most. Proprietary models maintain an advantage, and larger parameters or thinking modes reduce but do not eliminate the disparity. The benchmark and evaluation scripts are publicly released to spur progress in multilingual instruction following.

## Method Summary
IndicIFEval comprises two subsets per language: IndicIFEval-Trans localizes English IFEval prompts via preprocessing, keyword-aware translation, and human verification, enabling direct cross-lingual comparison. IndicIFEval-Ground uses native Indic content from the Sangraha corpus to generate context-grounded prompts: constraints are defined, relevant contexts are mined via TF-IDF, LLM-based prompt generation occurs, and human verification ensures naturalness and constraint clarity. Evaluation uses the Language Model Evaluation Harness with greedy decoding. Custom verification integrates the Indic NLP Library for language-specific tokenization and sentence splitting, applying rule-based checks (regex, counts) for 25 fine-grained constraints aggregated into five semantic categories (FORMAT, KEYWORD, LENGTH, LANGUAGE, ENGLISH). The primary metric is prompt-level loose accuracy; disparity (Δ) between Indic and English scores is computed to diagnose cross-lingual gaps.

## Key Results
- Models exhibit persistent performance gaps on Indic languages, with Hindi closest to English and Sanskrit lagging the most.
- Format constraints are adhered to strongly, but lexical (keyword) and cross-lingual (ENGLISH) tasks see significant degradation.
- Proprietary models consistently outperform open-weight models; larger parameter counts and thinking modes narrow the gap, though not eliminating it.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Dual-Pipeline Construction Ensures Validity and Localization
The benchmark’s validity stems from two complementary pipelines. **IndicIFEval-Trans** creates a parallel corpus for clean cross-lingual comparison via localized translations (preprocessing, keyword-aware translation, verification). **IndicIFEval-Ground** uses native content and context-grounded generation to capture culturally embedded constraints that translation alone would miss. The core assumption is that automatically verifiable constraints, combined with Indic NLP Library tokenization and human verification, yield low-bias evaluation across scripts. Evidence: detailed pipelines in Section 3 and comparison with prior benchmarks (e.g., M-IFEval) that lack localization. Break condition: if human verification reveals systemic prompt naturalness or constraint clarity issues for a language, dataset validity is compromised.

### Mechanism 2: Constraint Categorization and Disparity Metric Isolates Deficits
The framework isolates specific deficits by aggregating 25 IFEval constraints into five semantic categories (FORMAT, KEYWORD, LENGTH, LANGUAGE, ENGLISH) and computing performance gap (Δ) relative to English. This identifies which constraint types and languages suffer the most. The core assumption is that Δ meaningfully quantifies cross-lingual transfer brittleness independent of absolute scores. Evidence: Section 5.1 and Figures 4–5 show strong FORMAT adherence but severe drops for KEYWORD and ENGLISH constraints; Hindi has minimal Δ, Sanskrit and cross-lingual tasks have maximal Δ. Break condition: if constraint categorization is semantically incoherent or Δ does not correlate with other multilingual metrics, diagnostic utility fails.

### Mechanism 3: Scale and Thinking Mechanisms Reduce but Do Not Eliminate Gaps
Larger model parameters enhance baseline capacity, enabling better use of multilingual pretraining data. Thinking modes (e.g., chain-of-thought) provide computational space for cross-lingual mapping and constraint planning. Both consistently narrow the gap across scales, though the underlying lag remains. The core assumption is that improvements are causally linked to enhanced multilingual representation and planning. Evidence: Section 5.1 shows performance plateaus around 12B–14B parameters; Figure 7 shows thinking modes narrow disparity constantly, independent of parameter count; proprietary models maintain lower Δ. Break condition: if thinking benefits are identical for monolingual vs. multilingual tasks, or if scale plateaus differ drastically across families, the mechanism would be invalidated.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Constrained Generation & Automatic Verification**  
   - *Why needed:* The benchmark relies on generating text that adheres to syntactically/semantically verifiable rules (e.g., keyword count, JSON format). Understanding mechanical checking (regex, token counts) is essential for valid prompt design and interpreting failures.  
   - *Quick check:* If a prompt requires "exactly 3 sentences ending with a question mark," how would you write a verification script for Hindi, considering sentence segmentation rules differ from English?

2. **Localization vs. Translation in Multilingual NLP**  
   - *Why needed:* IndicIFEval-Ground’s core innovation is moving beyond translation to *localization*—using native content to create natural prompts. This distinction explains why translated benchmarks may underestimate true capability.  
   - *Quick check:* Give an example from the paper (e.g., keyword frequency) where a direct translation of an English prompt would yield an unnatural or invalid constraint in Tamil, and how the Ground pipeline fixes this.

3. **Cross-lingual Transfer Brittleness**  
   - *Why needed:* The key finding is not just lower scores for Indic languages but disproportionate failure on specific constraint types (keyword inclusion, cross-lingual output), indicating brittle knowledge transfer rather than mere resource scarcity.  
   - *Quick check:* Based on the paper’s analysis, why might a model successfully avoid a forbidden keyword in Hindi but fail to include a required keyword, even if both use the same lexical item?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Raw IFEval prompts → [Trans Pipeline: Preprocessing → Keyword Extraction/Translation → Full Translation → Verification] → IndicIFEval-Trans prompts  
Raw content + constraints → [Ground Pipeline: Constraint Definition → Context Mining (TF-IDF) → LLM Prompt Generation → Verification] → IndicIFEval-Ground prompts  
IndicIFEval prompts (Trans & Ground) → Evaluation Harness (Model Inference + Indic NLP Library + Rule-Based Verification) → Raw results → Analysis Framework (Constraint aggregation → Accuracy calculation → Δ computation)

### Critical Path
Raw IFEval Prompts → IndicIFEval-Trans Pipeline → Human-Verified Trans Prompts & IndicIFEval-Ground Pipeline → Human-Verified Ground Prompts → Model Inference (Greedy) → Rule-Based Verification → Accuracy Metrics (Prompt-level, Category-level) → Gap Analysis (Δ)

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Translation Parallelism vs. Naturalness:** Trans offers clean cross-language comparison but risks unnatural constraints; Ground offers ecological validity but makes direct comparison across languages difficult.
- **Human Verification Cost vs. Scale:** Single-annotator verification keeps the project feasible but limits reliability; multiple annotators would improve quality but increase cost.
- **Constraint Coverage:** Focusing on six constraint types for Ground simplifies generation but excludes potentially important linguistic constraints (e.g., numeral formats, romanization).

### Failure Signatures
- **Data Pipeline:** High keyword translation collapse (identical translations across languages); many prompts rejected by annotators (>40% failure in early stages for some languages).
- **Model Performance:** Abysmal scores (<30%) on small models for low-resource languages; extremely high Δ (>0.5) for Aya family on LANGUAGE/ENGLISH constraints; Gemma family shows negative Δ on ENGLISH constraint (worse when prompting in English).
- **Evaluation:** Incorrect sentence segmentation due to Indic NLP Library limitations; regex checks failing for script-specific formatting (e.g., different quote marks).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What additional constraint types should be incorporated?**  
   *Basis:* The authors note in Section 7 that their grounded benchmark explores only a subset of possible constraints and suggest adding romanization requirements, numeral format specifications, and unit system constraints.  
   *Why unresolved:* Current constraints (six types) may not capture all linguistically relevant challenges in Indic languages, limiting diagnostic comprehensiveness.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Extend IndicIFEval with new constraint types based on linguistic analysis, then evaluate diverse models to identify performance patterns on these added constraints.

2. **How does single-annotator verification affect reliability?**  
   *Basis:* The paper acknowledges (Section 7) that annotations were produced by a single annotator due to resource constraints, with no inter-annotator agreement (IAA) reported.  
   *Why unresolved:* Without multiple annotators, there is no quantitative measure of consistency, risking subjective biases, especially for low-resource languages.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Re-annotate a statistically significant subset per language with multiple native speakers, calculate IAA metrics (e.g., Cohen’s kappa), and analyze disagreements to refine guidelines.

3. **Do thinking mechanisms generalize beyond Qwen?**  
   *Basis:* The study evaluated only Qwen models with thinking modes (Section 7), leaving other families untested.  
   *Why unresolved:* It is unclear whether the observed gap-narrowing effect of thinking is universal or model-specific, limiting understanding of thinking as a solution.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Evaluate a diverse set of proprietary and open-weight models that offer thinking or reasoning modes (e.g., GPT-o1, Claude extended thinking, Llama with CoT) on IndicIFEval and compare gap reduction across families.

4. **Why do Gemma models show negative Δ on ENGLISH constraints?**  
   *Basis:* The paper infers from observed behavior (Page 6) that Gemma models often add translations/transliterations in parentheses when the prompt is in English, violating strict language constraints.  
   *Why unresolved:* The underlying mechanism driving this prioritized readability over constraint adherence is not explained.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Conduct qualitative error analysis of Gemma outputs on ENGLISH-constraint prompts, probe model attention and generation probabilities, and run controlled fine-tuning experiments to test if such behavior can be suppressed without harming overall performance.

## Limitations
- Single-annotator human verification without inter-annotator agreement may introduce bias and limit dataset reliability.
- The ecological validity of the Ground subset versus real-world instruction following remains unclear.
- Findings are constrained to automatically verifiable, rule-based instructions and may not generalize to open-ended tasks.

## Confidence
- **Benchmark Validity:** High
- **Diagnostic Framework:** Medium
- **Scale & Thinking Benefits:** Medium
