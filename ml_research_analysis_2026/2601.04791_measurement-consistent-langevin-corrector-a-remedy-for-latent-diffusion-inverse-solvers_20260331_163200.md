# Measurement-Consistent Langevin Corrector: A Remedy for Latent Diffusion Inverse Solvers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.04791  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04791  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: MCLC stabilizes latent diffusion inverse solvers by reducing KL divergence to the target distribution while preserving measurement fidelity, consistently improving reconstruction quality across multiple solvers and tasks with minimal overhead.

## Executive Summary
Latent diffusion inverse solvers often suffer from instability because the measurement-consistency step perturbs the latent variable away from the distribution learned by the diffusion model. This paper introduces the Measurement-Consistent Langevin Corrector (MCLC), a theoretically-grounded plug-and-play module that applies Langevin updates constrained to the orthogonal complement of the measurement gradient. By doing so, MCLC reduces the KL divergence to the target time-marginal distribution while maintaining data fidelity. Experiments on FFHQ and ImageNet across diverse solvers and inverse problems show consistent quantitative improvements and artifact removal, with only a ~3% increase in runtime.

## Method Summary
MCLC is inserted immediately after the measurement-consistency update in existing latent diffusion inverse solvers (e.g., LDPS, PSLD). It performs multiple iterations of a Langevin step using the diffusion model’s score function \(s_\theta(z,t)\), but projects the update direction onto the subspace orthogonal to the measurement gradient \(g_t = \nabla_z r(z_t)\) (where \(r\) is the measurement residual). The projection ensures the first-order change in the residual is zero, preserving measurement fidelity, while the Langevin drift continues to reduce the KL divergence to the target distribution \(p_t\). The step size \(\eta_t\) is adaptively scaled by a hyperparameter \(\lambda\) to control the expected squared perturbation magnitude, which bounds the expected residual drift (Theorem 3.4). MCLC is applied every \(k\) timesteps with \(N_c\) iterations per invocation, adding negligible memory overhead.

## Key Results
- For Gaussian deblur with PSLD, MCLC improves PSNR by 0.13 dB (27.84→27.97), reduces FID by 23.9 (89.18→66.28), and lowers LPIPS by 0.028 (0.314→0.286).
- MCLC stabilizes solvers and removes artifacts compared to prior correction methods like DiffStateGrad.
- Gains are consistent across solvers (LDPS, PSLD, ReSample, LatentDAPS) and tasks (deblur, super-resolution, inpainting, HDR, nonlinear deblur), and it extends to flow-based solvers and different LDM priors, with only ~3% additional runtime.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Distributional Gap Causes Instability**  
Instability arises from a positive KL divergence \(D_{KL}(q_t \| p_t) > 0\) between the solver’s distribution \(q_t\) and the target time‑marginal \(p_t\) at each timestep \(t\). The measurement‑consistency update perturbs the latent away from \(p_t\), creating this gap. The paper formalizes this as Assumption 3.1 and empirically visualizes it in Figure 2.

**Mechanism 2: Langevin Dynamics Reduce KL Divergence**  
Unconstrained Langevin updates using the diffusion score \(s_\theta\) monotonically decrease KL in continuous time (Proposition 3.2). The discrete Euler–Maruyama approximation preserves this decreasing property for sufficiently small step sizes \(\eta_t\), pulling the distribution back toward \(p_t\).

**Mechanism 3: Projection Preserves Fidelity While Reducing KL**  
Projecting the Langevin update onto \(g_t^\perp\) (i.e., \(P_{\perp g} = I - g_t g_t^\top\)) ensures the first‑order change in the measurement residual is zero: \(\nabla r^\top \Delta z = 0\). Theorem 3.4 bounds the expected residual change by \(C \cdot \mathbb{E}[\|\Delta z_t\|^2] + O(\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta z_t\|^3])\). Since \(\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta z_t\|^2]\) is controlled by \(\lambda\), expected drift is bounded, preserving fidelity. The projection does not block the score component orthogonal to \(g_t\), so KL still decreases.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Pretrained LDM (VAE encoder/decoder, score network \(s_\theta\)) → Measurement operator \(A\) and observation \(y\) → Base inverse solver (e.g., LDPS, PSLD) → MCLC module → Corrected latent → Reverse sampler (e.g., DDIM) → Final latent \(z_0\) → Decoded image.

**Critical path**  
1. Base solver computes measurement‑consistent gradient and updates \(z_t \to z_t^\#\).  
2. Every \(k\) steps, invoke MCLC: for \(c=1..N_c\), apply projected Langevin update using \(s_\theta(z,t)\) and measurement gradient \(g_t\).  
3. Proceed with reverse sampling (e.g., compute \(z_{t-1}\) from corrected \(z_t^\#\)).  
4. Repeat until \(t=0\); decode \(z_0\) to obtain reconstruction.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Correction frequency \(k\): smaller \(k\) (more frequent) stabilizes more but increases runtime (~3% overhead per step).  
- Number of corrector iterations \(N_c\): more iterations improve KL reduction but with diminishing returns; defaults (1–3) often suffice.  
- Step size \(\lambda\): controls \(\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta z_t\|^2]\); too large breaks consistency, too small under‑corrects. A single default may work across tasks.  
- Memory: negligible; only forward passes of score network.

**Failure signatures**  
- Zero measurement gradient (\(\|g_t\| \approx 0\)): projection degenerates to identity; unconstrained Langevin may harm fidelity. Skip correction when \(\|g_t\|\) is small.  
- Large \(\lambda\) causing blob artifacts: amplified by decoder Jacobian; reduce \(\lambda\) or increase \(N_c\).  
- Poor score quality at low noise (large \(t\)): consider restricting MCLC to mid‑to‑early timesteps.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Reproduce KL gap: Compute \(D_{KL}(q_t \| p_t)\) across timesteps with and without MCLC (using GMMs) to verify gap reduction.  
2. Ablate projection: Compare MCLC (with projection) against vanilla Langevin corrector (without) on a linear task (e.g., Gaussian deblur); expect MCLC to preserve y‑PSNR while vanilla may degrade it.  
3. Cross‑solver compatibility: Integrate MCLC into at least two base solvers (e.g., LDPS and PSLD) on FFHQ 4× SR; expect consistent PSNR/LPIPS/FID gains and reduced artifacts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What architectural or training modifications to latent diffusion models can produce latent spaces without scaled‑outliers, thereby preventing blob artifacts at the source?**  
   *Basis*: Appendix B.7 notes that SD v1.5 latents contain outliers amplified by the decoder, while SDXL latents do not, but the cause is unexplored.
2. **Can MCLC be effectively adapted for inverse solvers that do not follow reverse diffusion dynamics (e.g., LatentDAPS with decoupled steps and re‑initialization)?**  
   *Basis*: Section B.1 discusses that LatentDAPS, which repeatedly resets from annealed noise, shows limited benefit because its dynamics break the continuous reverse trajectory assumption.
3. **How robust is MCLC to errors in the learned diffusion score network, and does imperfect scoring invalidate the theoretical guarantee of monotonic KL reduction?**  
   *Basis*: Theoretical results assume access to the true score; learned \(s_\theta\) is approximate, but the impact of this approximation is not analyzed.
4. **Does incorporating curvature information (e.g., Hessian of measurement loss) into the measurement‑consistent projection improve the trade‑off between data fidelity and stability beyond the current gradient‑based orthogonal projection?**  
   *Basis*: Theorem 3.4’s bound uses a first‑order Taylor expansion; higher‑order terms are treated as \(O(k)\). For strongly nonlinear measurements, a second‑order projection could better navigate the local geometry.

## Limitations
- Theoretical approximations assume infinitesimal steps and a perfect score; in practice, step size trade‑offs and score errors at low noise may violate monotonicity.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (e.g., \(\lambda\), \(k\), \(N_c\)) is not fully characterized across diverse tasks, noise levels, and LDM architectures; the bound in Theorem 3.4 depends on controlling \(\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta z\|^2]\), which may be fragile.
- Reproducibility gaps: critical hyperparameters (e.g., measurement gradient scaling \(\zeta\) per solver, exact \(\alpha\) schedule, Patch‑FID evaluation details) are omitted or inconsistently reported, risking implementation mismatch.

## Confidence
- **Core claim** (instability from \(q_t\) vs. \(p_t\) gap): **High**  
- **Langevin correction reduces KL divergence**: **Medium** (continuous case solid; discrete may deviate)  
- **Projection preserves measurement fidelity**: **Medium** (bound under smoothness assumptions; fails when \(\nabla r \approx 0\))  
- **Cross‑solver consistent improvement**: **Medium‑High** (results across solvers/tasks convincing but limited to SD v1.5)
