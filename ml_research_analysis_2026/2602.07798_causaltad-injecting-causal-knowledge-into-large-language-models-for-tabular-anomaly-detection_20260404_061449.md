# CausalTAD: Injecting Causal Knowledge into Large Language Models for Tabular Anomaly Detection
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07798
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07798
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Achieves average AUC-ROC of 0.835 on ODDS datasets and 0.834 on mixed-type datasets using SmolLM-135M, with up to 9.1% relative improvement on text-rich datasets.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as promising tools for tabular anomaly detection, yet existing approaches serialize columns in arbitrary order, overlooking causal relationships that govern normal data generation. CausalTAD bridges this gap by injecting causal knowledge into the LLM pipeline. It first discovers high-level factors and their causal graph from training data, then projects this structure onto a column-level preference matrix and solves a linear ordering problem to obtain column permutations that respect causal precedence. Additionally, it reweights column-wise anomaly contributions based on the number of factors each column participates in, emphasizing causally central columns. Extensive experiments on over 30 datasets demonstrate that CausalTAD consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, including AnoLLM, with statistically significant gains. The method is robust across different causal discovery algorithms and model sizes, offering a new direction for LLM-based anomaly detection.

## Method Summary
CausalTAD processes tabular data in three main stages: (1) factor extraction, (2) causal-driven ordering, and (3) LLM fine-tuning with reweighted scoring. In stage 1, each training sample is serialized into a textual description; an LLM is prompted to propose high-level factors (e.g., "Compensation") and define annotation criteria. The LLM then assigns discrete factor values to each sample, forming a factor-value matrix. In stage 2, a standard causal discovery algorithm (PC, LiNGAM, or FCI) is applied to this matrix to learn a directed factor graph \(G_F\). A projection maps the factor-level edges to a column-level preference matrix \(W\) based on the factor-to-column mapping. A Linear Ordering Problem (LOP) solver enumerates column permutations that maximize precedence satisfaction; the top-\(K\) orderings (typically \(K=10\)) are retained. In stage 3, an LLM backbone (e.g., SmolLM-135M) is fine-tuned on normal training samples serialized using randomly selected orderings via the standard autoregressive loss. At inference, for each test sample, column-wise negative log-likelihoods are computed under each of the \(K\) orderings, weighted by the number of factors each column maps to (\(\alpha_j\)), and averaged to produce the final anomaly score.

## Key Results
- **AUC-ROC on ODDS:** CausalTAD (SmolLM-135M) reaches 0.835 average, a 6.0% relative improvement over AnoLLM (0.788).
- **AUC-ROC on mixed-type datasets:** 0.834 vs. 0.803 for AnoLLM.
- **Notable gain on text-rich data:** 9.1% relative improvement on the Fake job posts dataset.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CausalTAD’s effectiveness stems from three tightly coupled mechanisms:

**1. Factor-based Causal Structure Discovery**  
Instead of inferring causality directly from raw columns, which may be noisy or low-level, the method abstracts the data into a small set of high-level factors (e.g., "Compensation", "Experience"). An LLM proposes these factors and annotates their discrete values for every sample. The resulting factor matrix is fed to a causal discovery algorithm (PC, LiNGAM, FCI) to learn a weighted directed graph \(G_F\). This higher-level representation captures semantically meaningful and causally coherent relationships, forming a more reliable basis for ordering.

**2. Causal-Driven Column Ordering via Linear Ordering Problem**  
The factor-to-column mapping \(M\) is used to project the factor graph’s edge strengths onto a column-level preference matrix \(W\). An ideal column permutation should place causally influential columns before their effects. Formally, the goal is to maximize \(\sum_{i,j} W_{ij} \cdot I[\pi(c_i) < \pi(c_j)]\), which is a Linear Ordering Problem (LOP). An integer programming solver finds the optimal objective \(O^*\), and all permutations achieving at least \(0.9 \times O^*\) are enumerated to select a diverse set \(K\). By conditioning the LLM on these causally consistent orders, the model learns more accurate conditional distributions for normal data; anomalies then exhibit higher losses when the context (causes) is violated.

**3. Causal-Influence Reweighting**  
Not all columns contribute equally to the causal structure. A column that participates in many factors has a broader impact on the data distribution and on the conditional probabilities of other columns. CausalTAD assigns each column a weight \(\alpha_j = |\mathcal{M}^{-1}(c_j)|\) (the number of factors that map to it). The final anomaly score averages weighted column losses across the \(K\) orderings. This reweighting emphasizes anomalies that upset causally central columns, further improving detection performance. Ablation studies confirm that factor-count weighting consistently beats uniform weighting across different causal discovery backends.

## Foundational Learning
- **Causal Discovery Algorithms (PC, LiNGAM, FCI)**  
  *Why needed:* They infer the directed acyclic graph \(G_F\) among high-level factors from the factor matrix, providing the causal relationships that will guide column ordering.  
  *Quick check:* What is a key assumption of LiNGAM that distinguishes it from PC/FCI, and why might that matter for tabular data with mixed types?

- **Linear Ordering Problem (LOP)**  
  *Why needed:* The column preference matrix \(W\) may contain cycles; LOP finds a permutation that maximizes satisfied precedence constraints, balancing conflicting influences.  
  *Quick check:* Why can't a simple topological sort on a directed graph derived from \(W\) suffice, and what does solving the LOP (vs. finding a Hamiltonian path) buy you?

- **Autoregressive Language Modeling Loss**  
  *Why needed:* The LLM is fine-tuned with the standard next-token prediction objective. The column order determines the conditioning context; causal ordering aims to make this context more semantically sound, thereby improving the model’s normal distribution estimate.  
  *Quick check:* How does the final scoring formula \(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{z=1}^K \sum_{j=1}^d \alpha_j \cdot \ell_j(X, \pi_z)\) differ from AnoLLM’s original averaging, and why is reweighting applied after computing per-column losses?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input Serialization → Factor Extraction Module → Causal Discovery Engine → Column Preference Projector → Linear Ordering Solver → LLM Training Pipeline → Anomaly Scoring Engine.

**Critical path:** The main workflow runs from data serialization through factor extraction, causal graph learning, column ordering, LLM fine-tuning, and finally scoring. The two main bottlenecks are (i) factor extraction and annotation, which relies on external LLM APIs and can be costly, and (ii) the LOP enumeration, which grows exponentially with the number of columns but is mitigated by the 90% threshold and typically works for tens of columns.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Factor extraction quality vs. cost:* Using a high-capacity proprietary LLM (GPT-4) yields reliable factors but introduces dependency and expense; switching to a smaller open-source model reduces cost but may lower factor diversity and coherence.
- *LOP solution:* Exact enumeration guarantees that all permutations within 90% of the optimum are considered, but the number of feasible permutations can explode. For datasets with >15 columns, one may need to switch to heuristic search, potentially sacrificing some ordering quality.
- *Number of orderings \(K\):* Increasing \(K\) captures more of the causal ordering space and reduces variance in scoring, but multiplies test-time inference cost. Empirically, \(K=10\) is sufficient for the evaluated datasets.

**Failure signatures:**
- Factor matrix has very low entropy (e.g., >95% of values identical across samples) → causal graph becomes empty or trivial → ordering degenerates to random.
- Column preference matrix \(W\) is nearly uniform or highly cyclic → LOP solutions are indistinguishable from random permutations, and performance approaches random ordering baseline.
- Reweighting factor counts \(\alpha_j\) are all 1 (or nearly equal) despite varied factor-to-column mapping → indicates mapping \(M\) is degenerate (e.g., each column maps to at most one factor) and reweighting cannot help.
- Test-time anomaly scores show high variance across the \(K\) orderings → suggests the orderings are unstable or the model is overly sensitive; may require more \(K\) or improved factor graph.

**First 3 experiments for onboarding:**
1. **Factor extraction sanity check:** On the Fake job posts dataset, run the provided prompts with an LLM (e.g., GPT-4). Verify that the extracted factors are abstract (not just column names) and that each factor has at least three distinct values across the training set. Check that the factor-to-column mapping \(M\) makes intuitive sense (e.g., both "salary" and "benefits" map to a "Compensation" factor).
2. **LOP ordering validation:** Construct a synthetic dataset with known causality: column A → B → C. Generate normal data accordingly. Compute the preference matrix \(W\) and solve LOP. Confirm that the top ordering places A before B before C. Then train a baseline AnoLLM with random ordering and compare AUC on a held-out normal+anomaly test set; causal ordering should yield higher AUC.
3. **Reweighting ablation:** On a mixed-type dataset such as Fraud e-commerce, train two CausalTAD models: one with \(\alpha_j = |\mathcal{M}^{-1}(c_j)|\) as proposed, and one with uniform \(\alpha_j = 1\). Compare AUC-ROC and F1 scores. A significant drop for uniform weighting confirms the benefit of factor-count reweighting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability to high-dimensional tables:** How does CausalTAD handle datasets with hundreds of columns, and can the LOP step be approximated efficiently for such widths without losing causal fidelity?
2. **Robustness to noisy factor extraction:** The pipeline depends on LLM-generated factors and annotations. How sensitive is performance to errors (e.g., incorrect factor proposals, inconsistent annotations)? Can the method be made robust to such imperfections?
3. **Sensitivity to causal discovery errors:** Real-world data may violate assumptions of PC/LiNGAM/FCI (e.g., hidden confounders, cycles). Under what conditions does an inaccurate factor graph lead to ordering that harms performance relative to random ordering?
4. **Optimality of factor-count reweighting:** Is counting factors the best proxy for column importance? Could learned or data-driven weights further improve detection?
5. **Cross-domain generalization:** The factor discovery prompts are tailored to certain datasets. How well does CausalTAD transfer to domains like healthcare or finance where factors may be less interpretable and column types more heterogeneous?

## Limitations
- The factor extraction stage relies on proprietary LLMs (GPT-4, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B) with undocumented prompting and sampling details, compromising reproducibility and cost transparency.
- Causal discovery hyperparameters (e.g., significance level for PC, regression type for LiNGAM) are not specified, making the learned graphs potentially unstable across runs.
- Linear Ordering enumeration scales exponentially with column count; for datasets with >15 columns the paper does not provide a reliable heuristic fallback, limiting applicability to very wide tables.
- Fine-tuning details such as batch size, optimizer parameters, and tokenization schemes for mixed-type data are under-specified, hindering exact replication.
- Evaluation is restricted to datasets with at most 50 columns and a limited number of domains, leaving open questions about performance on ultra-wide or highly sparse real-world tables.

## Confidence
- **Performance superiority over baselines:** High – consistent gains observed across more than 30 datasets.
- **Robustness to choice of causal discovery algorithm:** Medium – tested with three algorithms but without exhaustive hyperparameter sweeps.
- **Effectiveness of factor-count reweighting:** High – ablations show steady improvement over uniform weighting.
- **Overall claim that causal ordering improves LLM-based anomaly detection:** High, based on strong empirical evidence and ablations.
