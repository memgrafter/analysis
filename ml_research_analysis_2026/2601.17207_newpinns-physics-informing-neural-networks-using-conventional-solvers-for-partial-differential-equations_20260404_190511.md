# NewPINNs: Physics-Informing Neural Networks Using Conventional Solvers for Partial Differential Equations

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.17207  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17207  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Achieves low steady-state L² errors (e.g., <5×10⁻³ for Fokker‑Planck, ~10⁻³ for lid‑driven cavity) and robust parameter recovery (within ±0.1% for Allen‑Cahn under 10% noise) without manual loss weighting, by coupling neural networks with conventional solvers.

## Executive Summary
NewPINNs introduces a framework that replaces the PDE residual loss in standard physics‑informed neural networks (PINNs) with a solver‑consistency objective. A neural network proposes solution states, a conventional numerical solver (e.g., finite volume, finite element, spectral) evolves those states, and training minimizes the discrepancy between the network’s next‑step prediction and the solver‑evolved state. This delegates physics enforcement and stability to established solvers, eliminating manual loss balancing and optimization pathologies. Experiments on forward and inverse problems show accurate solutions in stiff, nonlinear, and chaotic regimes where PINNs fail, yielding low steady‑state errors and robust parameter recovery. The approach is solver‑agnostic and inherits the robustness of classical discretizations.

## Method Summary
Given a PDE with parameters, a neural network \(f_\theta\) maps inputs (spatial coordinates, time, parameters) to a candidate solution field. Instead of computing PDE residuals via automatic differentiation, NewPINNs uses a black‑box numerical solver as a consistency operator. For a batch of inputs, the network’s current prediction is fed to the solver, which advances it for \(N_s\) iterations (or to steady‑state) to produce a solver‑evolved state. The network also predicts the state at a subsequent time or parameter setting. The loss compares the network’s next‑step prediction to the solver‑evolved state, supplemented by initial condition or data misfit terms for transient or inverse problems. Only the network parameters are updated via gradient descent; the solver remains non‑differentiable and detached from the computational graph. This shifts the burden of physics enforcement from a manually weighted multi‑term loss to the solver’s inherent stability and conservation properties.

## Key Results
- Low steady‑state L² errors: <5×10⁻³ for Fokker‑Planck, ~10⁻³ for lid‑driven cavity velocity fields.
- Robust parameter recovery: within ±0.1% for Allen‑Cahn diffusion coefficient under 10% noise.
- Solver‑agnostic operation: works with finite volume, finite element, and spectral solvers; eliminates manual loss weighting and improves performance in stiff, nonlinear, and chaotic regimes compared to standard PINNs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Solver‑consistency replaces residual loss**  
Standard PINNs combine PDE, boundary, and initial condition residuals, creating a highly non‑convex loss landscape with competing gradients. NewPINNs replaces these with a single solver‑consistency term: \(\|f_\theta(t_{n+1}) - S[f_\theta(t_n)]\|\), where \(S\) is the numerical solver. This eliminates conflicting gradient signals because the solver itself enforces boundary conditions and numerical stability. The core assumption is that the solver’s update operator provides a physically consistent evolution. Evidence includes the abstract’s claim that this mitigates optimization pathologies and sensitivity to loss weighting, and the formal loss definition in Section 2.2.1.

**Mechanism 2: Black‑box solver leverages mature numerical methods**  
Unlike differentiable physics approaches, NewPINNs treats the solver as a black box, enabling the use of any established, highly optimized commercial or open‑source solver (e.g., COMSOL, Ansys). The network only needs to produce solver‑admissible states; the solver’s internal computations are not differentiated. This inherits the robustness of classical discretizations for complex boundary conditions and high‑order operators. The core assumption is that the solver is sufficiently robust to handle the discretized physics for any reasonable initial state. Evidence is the explicit contrast with PINNs in the abstract and Section 2.1, and the use of multiple solver types (FVM, FEM, spectral) in experiments.

**Mechanism 3: Closed‑loop consistency learns solver‑invariant solutions**  
The training loop creates a feedback where the network’s predictions are validated by the solver’s evolution. This encourages the network to learn a solution that lies on or near the solver’s invariant sets (e.g., fixed points for steady‑state, stable trajectories for transient). The network is not trained on static solver data but on a state‑dependent consistency error that depends on its own outputs. Propositions 1 and 2 in Section 2.4 provide a posteriori bounds linking the solver‑consistency residual to the distance from a solver‑consistent equilibrium/trajectory, assuming contractivity or Lipschitz continuity of the solver operator.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Neural Network (produces current state) → Solver Interface (applies numerical solver for \(N_s\) iterations) → Loss Computation (compares solver‑evolved state to network’s next‑step prediction, plus IC/data loss) → Optimizer (updates network parameters; optionally learns physical parameters in inverse problems).

**Critical path**:
1. Initialize the neural network with weights that yield bounded, physically plausible outputs (e.g., positive densities) to avoid solver instability.
2. Sample a batch of parameters (and time for transient problems).
3. For each sample, use the network to predict the current state \(u_\text{hat}\).
4. Pass \(u_\text{hat}\) (with boundary conditions and parameters) to the solver and run \(N_s\) iterations to obtain \(u_\text{solver}\).
5. Use the network to predict the next state \(\hat{u}_{n+1}\) at the same input conditions.
6. Compute the total loss: \(\|\hat{u}_{n+1} - u_\text{solver}\|^2\) plus initial condition loss (for transient) or data misfit (for inverse).
7. Backpropagate the loss through the network only (solver is detached) and update parameters via gradient descent (e.g., Adam).
8. Repeat.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Solver iteration count \(N_s\):* Larger \(N_s\) enforces stronger solver consistency but increases cost and may cause divergence from poor initializations. Small \(N_s\) (1–10) often suffices once the network is near a solver fixed point.
- *Network architecture vs. solver grid:* For grid‑based solvers, network output should match the solver’s discretization or be interpolable. Mesh‑based solvers require careful mapping (e.g., interpolation) which can introduce errors.
- *Initialization:* Default initializations may produce out‑of‑range values (e.g., negative densities) that destabilize the solver. Use smaller gain or warm‑start from a coarse solver solution.

**Failure signatures**:
- *Solver crash:* Loss becomes NaN/Inf because the solver failed (non‑convergence, blow‑up) on network‑generated states. Check solver logs.
- *Stagnant loss:* Loss plateaus at a high value. May indicate insufficient network capacity or that the solver‑consistent set is not reachable from current initialization.
- *Discrepancy in inverse problems:* Recovered parameters are biased or high‑variance. Suggests the forward network has not learned solver‑consistent dynamics well.
- *Chaotic explosion:* For chaotic systems (e.g., KS, Lorenz), validation error grows rapidly with time even if short‑term predictions are good.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **1D linear ODE** (e.g., \(dy/dt = -\alpha y\)) with explicit Euler solver. Verifies the pipeline: network learns decay rate \(\alpha\), loss decreases, predictions match analytical solution. Controls for solver complexity.
2. **1D Burgers’ equation** with FTCS finite difference solver. Introduces nonlinear advection and diffusion, requiring CFL stability. Tests sensitivity to \(\Delta t\), \(\Delta x\), and network depth (Appendix B.1).
3. **1D Allen‑Cahn** with spectral (Chebfun) solver. Involves stiff nonlinear reaction term, smooth transients, and periodicity. Validates solver‑consistency for high‑accuracy solvers and stiff problems (Section 3.3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **How can NewPINNs be extended to achieve accurate long‑term prediction in strongly chaotic systems?**  
   *Basis:* The paper explicitly states in Section 4 that capturing long‑term behavior in strongly chaotic and high‑dimensional systems remains challenging and observes degradation in chaotic regimes (e.g., Kuramoto–Sivashinsky, Lorenz) due to intrinsic sensitivity.  
   *Why unresolved:* The method relies on a neural network to approximate solver‑consistent trajectories, but finite‑capacity architectures may fail to capture exponential divergence and fine‑scale structures over long horizons. The paper hypothesizes this is representational but does not test architectural modifications or training strategies.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Systematic experiments with increased network capacity (depth/width), multiscale architectures, or adaptive temporal resolution on benchmark chaotic PDEs/ODEs, measuring error growth over extended time horizons compared to standard NewPINNs.

2. **What are the theoretical stability and convergence guarantees for NewPINNs when the underlying dynamics are chaotic or stiff?**  
   *Basis:* Section 2.4 provides a posteriori error bounds for steady‑state and transient NewPINNs but only under assumptions of local contractivity (steady‑state) or Lipschitz continuity (transient). The paper acknowledges these are “local” and do not cover chaotic regimes where Lyapunov exponents are positive or solver stability breaks down.  
   *Why unresolved:* Without theoretical guarantees for chaotic/stiff systems, training may diverge or converge to spurious fixed points, and there is no principled way to choose hyperparameters (e.g., solver iteration count \(N_s\), initialization scales). The analysis stops short of addressing systems with sensitive dependence on initial conditions.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Development of operator‑theoretic stability analysis (e.g., using probabilistic or mean‑square bounds) for the coupled network‑solver system under chaotic dynamics, validated on equations like the KS equation with varying \(\alpha\). This could involve measuring attractor dimensions or shadowing properties of the learned surrogate.

3. **Does NewPINNs inherit and amplify numerical errors from the underlying solver, and can hybrid error‑correction mechanisms improve fidelity?**  
   *Basis:* The paper states in Section 4: “NewPINNs fundamentally inherits the accuracy and limitations of the underlying solver… solver errors may propagate into the model predictions.” This is observed in experiments (e.g., grid‑to‑mesh conversion error in FEM).  
   *Why unresolved:* The framework treats the solver as a black box but does not incorporate any mechanism to correct or compensate for solver discretization errors. The surrogate merely mimics the solver, so any bias or instability in the numerical method (e.g., artificial dissipation, dispersion) is directly reflected in the learned model. This limits accuracy even if network capacity is high.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Comparative studies using multiple solvers (e.g., different FVM schemes, mesh resolutions) for the same PDE, measuring whether solver‑specific errors persist in the NewPINNs surrogate. Then augment the training loop with a correction network or residual loss that enforces additional consistency (e.g., conservation laws) beyond solver‑consistency.

4. **What initialization and curriculum strategies ensure stable training of NewPINNs in high‑dimensional, stiff, or multi‑physics PDEs?**  
   *Basis:* Section 4 notes that “poorly scaled or unstable network outputs can adversely affect solver convergence, particularly in stiff or highly nonlinear regimes,” and that “initialization effects can have more severe consequences” in high dimensions. The paper uses standard Kaiming initialization but does not explore alternatives.  
   *Why unresolved:* The solver’s stability criteria (e.g., CFL conditions, positivity constraints) may be violated by random network outputs, causing NaNs/Infs and training collapse. This problem is expected to worsen with dimensionality and stiffness. The paper does not provide guidelines for initialization scales, warm‑starting, or curriculum scheduling to keep predictions within solver‑admissible regions.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Design curriculum schemes that gradually increase solver iteration count or problem stiffness (e.g., start with low Reynolds numbers, small \(N_s\)) and analyze success rates. Explore physics‑informed initialization (e.g., pre‑train on coarse solver data, enforce positivity). Test on high‑dimensional benchmarks (e.g., 3D Navier‑Stokes) to quantify failure modes.

5. **Can NewPINNs be extended to problems with moving boundaries, adaptive mesh refinement, or discontinuous solutions without sacrificing robustness?**  
   *Basis:* The paper demonstrates NewPINNs on fixed‑geometry domains with smooth solutions (Fokker–Planck, lid‑cavity, Allen–Cahn, KS). It uses solvers with static meshes/grids. The conclusion mentions “complex boundary conditions” but does not address moving boundaries or discontinuities (e.g., shocks, phase interfaces).  
   *Why unresolved:* Many engineering problems involve moving boundaries (fluid‑structure interaction) or discontinuous solutions (shock‑capturing). Standard solvers for such cases often use adaptive meshes or specialized limiters. Feeding network outputs into such solvers may break if the network produces non‑admissible states (e.g., negative densities, oscillations). The framework’s robustness in these contexts is unexplored.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Implement NewPINNs with solvers that support moving meshes (e.g., FEM with ALE formulation) or shock‑capturing (e.g., FVM with flux limiters). Test on problems like shock‑tube or fluid‑structure interaction, monitoring solver convergence and solution accuracy compared to direct numerical simulation.

## Limitations
- The solver‑consistency theory assumes the evolution operator is contractive or Lipschitz; this may fail for stiff or under‑resolved discretizations, leaving theoretical gaps.
- The black‑box solver can produce NaNs/Infs for unstable network outputs; the paper offers no systematic fix beyond careful initialization.
- Essential experimental details (solver mesh, time step, convergence tolerances, network architecture) are omitted, hindering exact replication.
- Evaluation is limited to 1D/2D problems; performance on 3D or high‑dimensional chaotic flows remains unknown.
- Multiple solver‑consistent equilibria may trap training in spurious states; no global convergence theory is provided.
