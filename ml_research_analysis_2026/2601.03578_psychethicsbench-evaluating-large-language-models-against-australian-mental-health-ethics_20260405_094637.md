# PsychEthicsBench: Evaluating Large Language Models Against Australian Mental Health Ethics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03578
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03578
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Refusal-based safety metrics are inadequate for mental health ethics; domain-specific fine-tuning can degrade ethical robustness.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces PsychEthicsBench, a novel benchmark for evaluating large language models (LLMs) in mental health contexts, grounded in 392 Australian psychology and psychiatry ethical principles. The benchmark comprises 1,377 multiple-choice questions (MCQs) testing knowledge and 2,612 open-ended questions (OEQs) assessing behavioral ethics, each with fine-grained annotations across eight violation categories. Evaluations of 14 models reveal that simple refusal rates are poor proxies for ethical behavior. Medical-domain LLMs consistently outperform both their base models and dedicated mental health LLMs—which frequently underperform their bases—highlighting risks from domain-specific fine-tuning. Furthermore, all models exhibit persistent U.S.-centric jurisdictional bias despite prompts specifying the Australian context, underscoring the need for locality-aware alignment.

## Method Summary
PsychEthicsBench was constructed by extracting 392 ethical principles from Australian professional bodies (APS, Ahpra, RANZCP). Using four prompt templates tailored to psychology/psychiatry and MCQ/OEQ formats, LLM generators (GPT-5, Claude-Sonnet-4.5) produced candidate questions. A three-stage pipeline involving cross-model scoring and clinical expert review ensured fidelity: experts iteratively refined prompts and validated outputs against strict rubrics (MCQ ≥23/26, OEQ ≥9/10). The final dataset includes MCQs for principle knowledge and OEQs simulating scoped ethical dilemmas, with responses annotated for eight violation types and a non-ethical quality gate. Models were evaluated on MCQ exact match/precision and OEQ metrics: general/jurisdictional refusal rates, quality pass rate, and overall/clinical ethicality rates.

## Key Results
- Refusal rates (GRR/JRR) show near-zero or negative correlation with ethical behavior (OER/CER), proving they are poor indicators of ethicality in mental health scenarios.
- Medical-domain LLMs (e.g., Meditron3) consistently exceed both their base models and mental health-specialized LLMs on knowledge (MCQ EM/PC) and behavioral (OER/CER) metrics.
- Mental health-specialized LLMs frequently underperform their base counterparts, demonstrating that domain-specific fine-tuning can degrade ethical robustness.
- All models exhibit pervasive U.S.-centric jurisdictional bias, with >5% U.S. keyword prevalence even under Australian-context prompts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Grounding evaluation in jurisdiction-specific ethical principles (Australian) yields more accurate alignment assessment than generic or U.S.-centric benchmarks.  
**Mechanism:** By sourcing principles from Australian professional bodies and constructing questions with one-to-one mappings, the benchmark exposes whether models can suppress irrelevant jurisdictional priors (e.g., U.S. laws) and apply locally correct reasoning.  
**Core assumption:** Ethical mental health practices vary significantly across jurisdictions (e.g., involuntary commitment criteria), so alignment must be measured against the target regulatory context.  
**Evidence anchors:** Performance gaps between "Aussie" and "Global" modes; quantification of U.S. keyword frequency despite Australian prompts.  
**Break condition:** If models perform equally across modes and show no U.S. keyword bias, the jurisdictional grounding loses discriminatory power.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Evaluating ethical *behavior* via multi-dimensional violation annotations (beyond refusals) better predicts clinically appropriate responses than refusal rates alone.  
**Mechanism:** The framework decouples refusal detection from ethical quality: responses must pass a non-ethical quality gate first, then avoid eight rule-break categories (e.g., Credentials, Jurisdictional). This captures nuances like empathetic refusals vs. harmful compliance.  
**Core assumption:** Ethically acceptable behavior includes non-refusal actions; refusals can be unethical (e.g., unempathetic, misleading).  
**Evidence anchors:** Low correlation between GRR/JRR and OER/CER; mental health LLMs with high GRR have low CER.  
**Break condition:** If OER/CER metrics correlate perfectly with refusal rates across models, the multi-dimensional mechanism is redundant.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Expert-in-the-loop prompt optimization and cross-model quality control ensures questions remain faithful to principles while being challenging for LLMs.  
**Mechanism:** Clinical psychologists/psychiatrists iteratively refine prompts, then cross-evaluate GPT-5/Claude outputs with rubrics (MCQ score ≥23/26, OEQ ≥9/10), filtering out low-fidelity items.  
**Core assumption:** Raw LLM-generated questions drift from precise principle interpretation; expert oversight is necessary for validity in high-stakes domains.  
**Evidence anchors:** High expert validation scores (average 23.2/26, 8.9/10); significant dataset reduction from initial generation to final set.  
**Break condition:** If expert scores drop below thresholds during validation, the quality control fails to ensure benchmark integrity.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Guideline Collection → Prompt Templates → LLM Generators → Cross-Evaluation Module → Expert Review Layer → Annotation Framework → Evaluation Metrics

**Critical path:**  
Principle extraction → Prompt design (expert-in-the-loop) → LLM generation → Cross-scoring → Expert QC → Final dataset → Model evaluation (MCQ/OEQ) → Violation analysis

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Breadth vs. Depth:* 392 principles ensure coverage but limit scenario diversity per principle.  
- *Automation vs. Expert cost:* LLM generation scales but requires experts for prompt iteration and QC.  
- *Generalizability vs. Specificity:* Australian context ensures clean jurisdiction tests but reduces global applicability.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High GRR but low CER: Model over-refuses but still produces unethical content when it engages.  
- >5% U.S. keyword frequency in "Aussie" mode: Jurisdictional bias persists.  
- Specialized model underperforms its base on MCQ PC/OEQ CER: Domain fine-tuning degraded ethical knowledge/behavior.  
- OEQ quality pass rate <90%: Model outputs are incoherent or trivial.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline jurisdiction test:** Run all models in "Aussie" vs. "Global" modes on SMCQs; measure performance gap and U.S. keyword rate.  
2. **Refusal-ethics dissociation:** For each model, compute correlation between GRR/JRR and CER; expect near-zero or negative correlation.  
3. **Specialization degradation check:** Compare each mental health LLM to its base model on MCQ PC and OEQ CER; expect underperformance in specialized variants.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can we develop accurate, lightweight classifiers to replace or supplement LLM-as-judge systems for ethicality annotation in mental health benchmarks?  
**Basis:** The authors state in Limitations: "the current ethicality annotation framework relies on an LLM as a judge. Future work could develop lightweight, task-specific classifiers to complement LLM-based judges."  
**Why unresolved:** LLM-as-judge may introduce biases, scalability issues, and opacity; reliable, efficient alternatives are needed.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Compare performance and cost of proposed classifiers against human expert annotations and LLM-as-judge baselines on the PsychEthicsBench dataset, measuring agreement and consistency.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** To what extent do ethical principles from different jurisdictions (e.g., Australia vs. U.S. vs. U.K.) conflict in mental health LLM scenarios, and how can benchmarks systematically incorporate this variation?  
**Basis:** The paper grounds PsychEthicsBench in Australian context, noting jurisdictional differences (e.g., involuntary commitment criteria) and that benchmarks like MedEthicsQA "overlook jurisdictional variation." The limitation notes the benchmark "is grounded in the Australian regulatory context and is therefore not universal."  
**Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates U.S.-centric bias but does not quantify principle conflicts across jurisdictions or provide a method for multi-jurisdictional evaluation.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Map overlapping principles across 3–5 key jurisdictions, create parallel question sets, and measure model performance variance to identify high-conflict versus harmonized principles.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What specific mechanisms cause domain-specific fine-tuning (e.g., on mental health dialogues) to degrade ethical robustness compared to broader medical or general fine-tuning?  
**Basis:** Results show "mental health-specialized LLMs frequently underperform their base counterparts" and "domain-specific fine-tuning can degrade ethical robustness." The discussion speculates narrow-task fine-tuning may be the cause.  
**Why unresolved:** The phenomenon is identified but causal factors (e.g., data quality, loss of general capabilities, misaligned objectives) are not isolated.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled fine-tuning experiments varying data sources (pure dialogue vs. mixed ethics texts), objectives (SFT vs. DPO), and model scales, measuring shifts in ethical knowledge (MCQs) and behavior (OEQs) against base models.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How does the demographic and contextual diversity of scenario representations in ethical benchmarks affect model ethical alignment across different user groups?  
**Basis:** The limitation states: "by focusing on one-to-one mappings... the current benchmark does not explicitly account for the diversity of populations represented in scenario design. Future work could expand the scenario coverage to include more varied demographic and contextual settings."  
**Why unresolved:** Scenarios may implicitly center on majority identities; unknown if models exhibit disparate ethical performance for underrepresented groups.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically augment PsychEthicsBench with varied demographics (e.g., Indigenous, LGBTQ+, diverse socioeconomic backgrounds), then evaluate model ethicality metrics (OER/CER) across these subgroups to detect disparities.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** Can targeted safety alignment during medical domain fine-tuning (as seen in models like Meditron3) be replicated in mental health fine-tuning to prevent ethical degradation?  
**Basis:** Medical models "consistently outperform their base models" and "explicitly document safety alignment, a practice largely absent in current mental health LLMs." This suggests alignment practices may explain performance differences.  
**Why unresolved:** It is unclear which alignment techniques (e.g., RLHF, constitutional AI, rule-based filtering) are effective and transferable to mental health ethics, given its nuance beyond refusals.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Apply documented safety alignment pipelines from medical LLMs to mental health fine-tuning, evaluate on PsychEthicsBench, and compare ethical metrics against non-aligned mental health models and base models.

## Limitations
- **Jurisdictional specificity:** Findings may not generalize beyond Australian ethics, though the mechanism for exposing jurisdictional bias is likely transferable.
- **Benchmark format constraints:** Multiple-choice questions test knowledge but not dynamic ethical reasoning in live interactions; open-ended responses are single-turn, lacking conversational context.
- **Principle coverage:** 392 principles provide breadth but may miss rare or emerging ethical dilemmas in mental health practice.
