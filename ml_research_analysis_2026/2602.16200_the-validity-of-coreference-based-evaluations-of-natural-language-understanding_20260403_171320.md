# The Validity of Coreference-based Evaluations of Natural Language Understanding

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.16200
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16200
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Coreference resolution benchmarks suffer from validity issues; LLMs exhibit conceptual fragility in event plausibility judgments despite high scores.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the measurement validity of coreference resolution (CR) evaluations, arguing that contestable construct definitions and poor convergent validity lead to non‑generalizable conclusions about model performance. The authors analyze seven English CR benchmarks to show how annotation choices shape metrics, compare prompted large language models (LLMs) with supervised systems while controlling for model size, and introduce a novel framework that probes consistency of event plausibility judgments across a lexical hierarchy. Results reveal that standard metrics reflect dataset‑specific biases rather than a unified construct, LLMs paradoxically excel on synthetic Winograd sets but falter on real pronouns, and much reported architectural progress disappears when scaling is accounted for. Moreover, LLMs display severe conceptual inconsistencies (e.g., rating “person breathe” plausible but “dentist breathe” implausible); a post‑hoc consistency method improves human alignment. The study concludes that coreference‑based evaluations are design‑limited and that even state‑of‑the‑art LLMs remain fragile and lack robust form‑generalization.

## Method Summary
The authors first conducted a meta‑analysis of seven widely used English coreference resolution datasets to quantify how differences in annotation guidelines and operational definitions affect evaluation metrics. They then compared recent large language models assessed via prompting with traditional supervised coreference systems, carefully controlling for the size of the underlying language model to isolate architectural contributions. Finally, they developed a tightly controlled evaluation framework that tests consistency of event plausibility judgments across a lexical hierarchy, providing a more construct‑valid probe of coreference understanding.

## Key Results
- Standard coreference metrics are strongly correlated with dataset‑specific annotation choices, undermining the notion that they measure a single unified construct.
- LLMs exhibit a performance paradox: they excel on Winograd‑style challenge sets yet underperform supervised systems on attested pronouns, and they show severe conceptual inconsistency in event plausibility judgments, which can be partially mitigated by post‑hoc hierarchical consistency methods.
- When controlling for language model size, older encoder‑based architectures (e.g., C2F) match newer designs, suggesting that much of the reported progress in coreference resolution is due to scaling rather than architectural innovation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The plausibility‑consistency framework works by exploiting lexical hierarchical relations (e.g., hypernymy) to create minimal pairs of event descriptions that share a verb but differ only in the specificity of an argument. If a model truly understands the event, it must assign consistent judgments: if a specific instance (e.g., “dentist breathe”) is implausible, then its more general class (“person breathe”) must also be implausible, because the general class encompasses the specific. Violations indicate fragmented knowledge or reliance on superficial lexical associations rather than coherent conceptual representation. By focusing on consistency across the hierarchy, the evaluation bypasses dataset‑specific artifacts and directly probes the model’s internal world knowledge, offering a more valid measure of coreference capabilities.

## Foundational Learning
- **Coreference Resolution**: The task of linking mentions to the same entity; needed to understand the phenomenon under evaluation.  
  *Quick check*: Can you identify which noun phrases refer to the same entity in a given text?

- **Construct Validity**: The degree to which an evaluation measures the intended theoretical construct; needed to assess whether coreference metrics truly capture understanding.  
  *Quick check*: Does the metric correlate with human judgments of understanding across diverse datasets?

- **Convergent Validity**: The extent to which different operationalizations of the same construct yield similar results; needed to claim a unified notion of coreference.  
  *Quick check*: Do scores on different coreference datasets correlate with each other?

- **Lexical Hierarchy**: Organization of lexical items from general to specific (e.g., person → dentist); needed to create minimal pairs for consistency testing.  
  *Quick check*: Given a hypernym and hyponym, can you list properties that should hold for both?

- **Event Plausibility**: Assessment of whether an event description is realistic or possible in the world; needed to evaluate world knowledge in models.  
  *Quick check*: Is the sentence “The chef baked a cake” plausible? Is “The chef baked a theorem” implausible?

- **Prompting vs Supervised Evaluation**: Different paradigms for assessing model performance; needed to compare LLMs with traditional systems.  
  *Quick check*: Does providing a model with a natural language instruction yield the same performance as fine‑tuning on labeled data?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Lexical Hierarchy → Event Pair Generator → Model Evaluator → Consistency Scorer  

**Critical path**:  
1. Construct lexical hierarchy (e.g., from WordNet) of nouns and verbs.  
2. Generate event triples (subject, verb, object) at varying levels of specificity.  
3. Query the target model (via prompting or scoring) for plausibility judgments.  
4. Compute consistency metrics by comparing responses for hierarchical pairs.  
5. Interpret violations as indicators of conceptual fragility.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Coverage vs. annotation effort*: Including many verbs and nouns improves generality but increases manual effort for hierarchy curation.  
- *Lexical association control*: Using minimal pairs reduces confounds but may still suffer from residual biases in the hierarchy.  
- *Prompt variability*: Fixed prompts ensure consistency when evaluating LLMs, but may not reflect real‑world usage or prompt sensitivity.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Hierarchical violations where a specific event is judged plausible while its general hypernym is judged implausible.  
- High sensitivity to minor lexical changes, indicating reliance on surface patterns.  
- Disparities between performance on Winograd‑style synthetic tests and real‑world coreference, suggesting overfitting to benchmark artifacts.

**First experiments**:  
1. Pilot replication of the consistency metric on a small, manually verified subset to confirm the framework’s reliability.  
2. Evaluate a suite of LLMs of varying sizes to test whether consistency improves with scale.  
3. Apply a post‑hoc hierarchical consistency method to model outputs and measure its impact on correlation with human plausibility judgments.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*Assumption: The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions; the following are inferred from its discussion and limitations.*

- How can coreference evaluation benchmarks be redesigned to ensure better construct validity across diverse annotation guidelines?
- To what extent do conceptual inconsistencies in event plausibility judgments reflect fundamental limitations in LLM training objectives, and can they be mitigated through architectural changes rather than post‑hoc consistency methods?
- Do the validity issues observed in English coreference resolution extend to other languages and language families, and how might typological differences affect the findings?
- Can future work develop unified metrics that capture a single notion of coreference, perhaps by incorporating world knowledge more directly?
- How does the observed scaling effect interact with data diversity—would larger models trained on more varied data show improved consistency?

## Limitations
- Scope: Analysis restricted to English coreference; cross‑lingual generalizability is unknown.  
- Plausibility task design: The manually curated lexical hierarchy may not capture all facets of event knowledge and could introduce bias.  
- Architecture isolation: Holding LM size constant still conflates differences in pretraining data, objectives, and decoding strategies, leaving architectural conclusions tentative.

## Confidence
- Dataset‑specific metrics reflect annotation choices, not unified construct: **High**
