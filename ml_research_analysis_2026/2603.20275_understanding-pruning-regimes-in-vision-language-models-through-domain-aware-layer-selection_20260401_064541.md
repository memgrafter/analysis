# Understanding Pruning Regimes in Vision-Language Models Through Domain-Aware Layer Selection

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.20275
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20275
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Domain-aware decoder layer pruning in VLMs preserves mathematical reasoning and general multimodal capabilities through three distinct pruning regimes.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method for structured pruning of decoder layers in vision-language models (VLMs) to maintain both mathematical reasoning and general multimodal capabilities under fixed budget constraints. The core approach ranks layers based on domain-specific input-output activation similarity, where high cosine similarity indicates redundancy for a given domain. Using math and non-math probing prompts, they derive three ranking strategies—math-aware, non-math-aware, and mixed—and prune mid-layers while protecting first and last layers, followed by brief supervised fine-tuning. Across Qwen3-VL-2B and Qwen3-VL-4B models, they identify three consistent pruning regimes: at low budgets, performance is highly sensitive to which layers are removed; at moderate budgets, methods converge as damage accumulates; at high budgets, structural continuity (e.g., enforced layer spacing) becomes critical. Domain-aware rankings achieve a Pareto frontier in the math–general trade-off, outperforming random and CKA-based baselines.

## Method Summary
The study targets structured decoder layer pruning in VLMs under fixed budgets (10%, 25%, 40%). Using Qwen3-VL-2B and -4B models, they compute per-layer redundancy via domain-specific activation similarity: for math and non-math probing sets (5k and 4k samples respectively), they measure token-wise cosine similarity between pre- and post-layer hidden states, average per domain, and z-normalize across pruneable layers. Rankings are generated for math-aware, non-math-aware, and mixed (α=0.7) strategies. Pruning removes top-ranked mid-layers while preserving first/last decoder layers. Post-pruning, all models undergo identical LoRA-based supervised fine-tuning (200k mixed data: 100k Snapask + 100k LLaVA-OneVision) for one epoch. Evaluation spans math (Snapask, NuminaMath) and general VL benchmarks (LLaVA-OneVision, ChartQA, RealWorldQA, ScienceQA, VStar), with correctness judged by GPT-5.

## Key Results
- Three consistent pruning regimes: at low budgets (e.g., 10%), performance is highly sensitive to layer choice; at moderate budgets (25%), methods converge; at high budgets (40%), structural continuity (e.g., Interlace spacing) dominates.
- Domain-aware rankings (math-aware, non-math-aware, mixed) achieve the highest stability and accuracy in their respective domains at low budgets, tracing a Pareto frontier between math and general capabilities.
- Random and CKA-based pruning consistently underperform, especially in the ranking-sensitive regime.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Layers that exhibit high cosine similarity between their input and output activations for a given domain are functionally redundant for that domain.
- **Mechanism:** For each decoder layer, compute token-wise cosine similarity between pre- and post-layer activations on domain-specific probing prompts. Aggregate across samples and tasks, then normalize across layers. Layers with higher normalized similarity are ranked as more redundant and pruned first.
- **Core assumption:** The magnitude of representation transformation (captured by cosine similarity) correlates with a layer's contribution to domain-specific computation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "rank decoder layers by domain-specific input–output activation similarity: high cosine similarity between a layer’s input and output indicates redundancy for that domain." [section 3.3] Formal definition of `sim(i)_ℓ` (Eq. 6) and domain aggregation `S(d)_ℓ` (Eq. 7). [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., HiPP-Prune, Interlace) use different redundancy signals; none use input–output cosine similarity, so evidence is indirect.
- **Break condition:** If a layer performs critical operations that preserve directional similarity (e.g., refining representations without large norm changes), high cosine similarity may not indicate redundancy.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Domain-aware layer ranking preserves domain-critical computation more effectively than domain-agnostic methods at low pruning budgets.
- **Mechanism:** By computing redundancy scores separately on math and non-math probing sets, the method identifies layers that are less transformative for a specific domain. Pruning these layers first retains layers that strongly transform representations for that domain.
- **Core assumption:** Different domains (math vs. general vision-language) rely on partially disjoint subsets of decoder layers; hence, a layer redundant for one domain may be critical for another.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "domain-aware rankings achieve the highest stability and accuracy in math benchmarks" and trace a "Pareto frontier in the math–general vision-language trade-off." [section 5.1] Tables 2–3 show that at 10% budget, math-aware ranking excels on Snapask/NuminaMath while non-math-aware excels on general benchmarks; mixed ranking balances both. [corpus] HiPP-Prune conditions pruning on user preferences, supporting the idea that task-specific rankings can improve preservation, though the conditioning signal differs.
- **Break condition:** If the probing tasks do not fully represent the target domain’s computational demands, or if layer functionality is highly shared across domains, domain-aware ranking may not outperform a well-tuned domain-agnostic method.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Pruning performance exhibits three regimes: (1) ranking-sensitive (low budgets), where choice of layers matters; (2) transition (moderate budgets), where methods converge as damage accumulates; (3) structure-dominated (high budgets), where layer spacing becomes critical.
- **Mechanism:** At low budgets, enough capacity remains that removing non-redundant layers causes disproportionate damage, making ranking crucial. At moderate budgets, widespread removal means all methods suffer similar capacity loss. At high budgets, contiguous gaps disrupt residual flow and attention, so strategies that enforce spacing (e.g., Interlace) become favorable.
- **Core assumption:** Redundancy is non-uniformly distributed across depth; early/mid layers may be more critical for multimodal fusion, while late layers handle output prediction. Extreme removal creates structural holes that harm stability beyond simple capacity loss.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "three consistent pruning regimes: at low pruning budgets, performance is highly sensitive to which layers are removed; at moderate budgets, methods converge as structural damage accumulates; and at high budgets, structural continuity becomes the limiting factor." [section 5.3] Figure 4 shows removal patterns: domain-aware methods cluster deletions; Interlace spaces them; random is unstructured. Figure 2 visualizes regime transitions. [corpus] Interlace [20] (neighbor) explicitly addresses high-budget spacing, providing external validation that structure matters at aggressive sparsity.
- **Break condition:** If the model has极度 uniform redundancy or extremely robust residual connections, the regime boundaries may blur, and spacing may never dominate.

## Foundational Learning
### Autoregressive Transformer Decoders in VLMs
- **Why needed:** The paper prunes decoder layers of a transformer-based VLM (e.g., Qwen3-VL), where the decoder autoregressively processes concatenated visual and textual tokens. Understanding the decoder’s role in multimodal fusion and token generation is essential to interpret layer functions.
- **Quick check:** In a VLM, why are the first and last decoder layers typically protected from pruning?

### Activation Similarity as a Redundancy Signal
- **Why needed:** The core method uses input–output cosine similarity to quantify how much a layer transforms representations. This requires understanding why high similarity might indicate redundancy versus a layer performing subtle but essential modulation.
- **Quick check:** If a layer’s input and output have high cosine similarity, does that necessarily mean the layer is unimportant? What alternative explanations exist?

### Domain-Specific Probing and Aggregation
- **Why needed:** The method derives domain-aware scores by probing with multiple subtasks per domain (e.g., Math-CoT, Math-Direct). This design assumes that diverse prompts reveal a layer’s role across a domain’s computational spectrum.
- **Quick check:** Why does the paper use five math and four non-math subtasks instead of a single prompt per domain? What risk does this mitigate?

### Post-Pruning Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
- **Why needed:** Pruning structurally alters the network, potentially destabilizing generation. A brief SFT stage restores functionality, ensuring fair comparison across pruning strategies.
- **Quick check:** Why is it necessary to apply the same SFT mixture to both pruned and unpruned baselines? What would happen if SFT data were tailored per method?

## Architecture Onboarding
VLM backbone -> Activation logger -> Redundancy scorer -> Ranking engine -> Pruner -> Stabilizer

1. **Setup:** Load frozen VLM, register forward hooks on all decoder layers.
2. **Probing:** Run forward passes on math and non-math prompt sets (5k and 4k samples respectively), capturing mean-pooled input/output vectors per layer.
3. **Scoring:** For each layer, compute cosine similarity per sample, average across domain samples, then z-normalize across pruneable layers.
4. **Ranking:** Sort layers by domain-normalized score (descending = more redundant). For mixed, combine with α=0.7.
5. **Pruning:** Remove top-K ranked mid-layers (budget-dependent), keep first/last decoder layers.
6. **SFT:** Apply identical LoRA fine-tuning (r=32, α=64) to pruned model using 200k mixed data (100k Snapask + 100k LLaVA-OneVision) for 1 epoch.

- **Budget sensitivity:** Low budgets favor precise ranking; high budgets favor spacing (Interlace). Mixed ranking (α=0.7) balances math/general trade-off but may not be optimal for extreme specialization.
- **Domain definition:** Math vs. non-math split is coarse; finer-grained domains (e.g., diagram reasoning) might require separate probes.
- **Endpoint protection:** First layer (multimodal fusion) and last layer (output prediction) are fixed. Removing them could cause catastrophic collapse, but might be safe if redundancy exists.
- **SFT mixture:** Uses general + math data; omitting either could degrade the opposite capability. ChartQA results suggest SFT distribution shift can dominate pruning effects.

- **Ranking-sensitive regime failure:** If domain-aware methods do not outperform random at 10%, probes may be unrepresentative or similarity metric ineffective.
- **Transition regime anomalies:** If methods do not converge by 25%, the model may have atypical depth redundancy distribution.
- **Structure-dominated regime breakdown:** If Interlace does not dominate at 40%, either the model is robust to gaps (e.g., strong residual connections) or spacing heuristic is suboptimal.
- **General red flag:** All methods degrade sharply beyond 25% for 2B model (Table 2), indicating limited depth redundancy; further pruning may require architectural changes, not just layer removal.

1. **Reproduce three-regime pattern:** For Qwen3-VL-2B, prune at 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 25%, 30%, 35%, 40% using mixed ranking. Plot math (Snapask) and general (LLaVA-OneVision) accuracy vs. budget. Verify ranking-sensitive (0–15%), transition (15–32%), structure-dominated (32–40%) regimes as in Figure 2.

3. **Test endpoint protection:** Run 10% pruning without protecting first/last layers (i.e., allow any mid-layers to be removed). Compare to protected version on all benchmarks. Unprotected should show severe degradation, especially on LLaVA-OneVision and Snapask, confirming endpoint criticality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Are the three-pruning-regime findings (ranking-sensitive, transition, structure-dominated) generalizable to larger vision-language models (>10B parameters) and architectures beyond decoder-only (e.g., encoder-decoder)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study only evaluates two Qwen3-VL models (2B and 4B) and does not test other scales or architectures.
- **Why unresolved:** Model size and architecture may alter the distribution of functional roles across layers, potentially changing regime boundaries or eliminating them.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic pruning experiments at various budgets across a diverse suite of VLMs of different sizes and architectures, measuring accuracy to see if the three-regime pattern persists or evolves.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How robust are the domain-aware layer rankings to variations in the probing prompts used to compute activation similarity?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method relies on five math and four non-math prompts, claiming diversity is essential, but no analysis of prompt set sensitivity is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** Different prompt choices may activate different computational pathways, leading to different layer redundancy scores and pruning decisions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies using alternative or reduced sets of math/non-math prompts; compare resulting rankings and final model performance to assess consistency.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the mixed ranking’s fixed weighting (α=0.7) be improved by adapting α to the desired math vs. general vision-language trade-off, or by learning it from data?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper sets α=0.7 based on empirical trade-off performance but does not explore whether other α values might yield better Pareto frontiers for different target distributions.
- **Why unresolved:** Deployment scenarios may prioritize math or general capabilities differently; a single fixed α may be suboptimal.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate a spectrum of α values (0 to 1) for each pruning budget, then plot the resulting math and general VL accuracy. Identify if the optimal α shifts with budget or target preference.

## Limitations
- Probing datasets (math/non-math) are not publicly released; exact domain-aware scores cannot be reproduced.
- Evaluation relies on GPT-5; alternative metrics may yield different relative rankings.
- Study limited to Qwen3-VL-2B/4B; generalizability to other VLM families is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Redundancy hypothesis:** Medium. Correlation shown but alternative mechanisms not excluded.
- **Domain-aware superiority:** High. Consistent low-budget improvements across math/general benchmarks.
- **Three-regime pattern:** High. Robust across two model scales, supported by removal pattern analysis.
