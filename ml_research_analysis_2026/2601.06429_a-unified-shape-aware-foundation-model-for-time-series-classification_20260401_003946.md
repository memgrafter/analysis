# A Unified Shape-Aware Foundation Model for Time Series Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06429
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06429
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** Pre-trained on 1.89 M samples, fine‑tuned on 128 UCR datasets achieves 87.08% average accuracy (rank 2.71), significantly outperforming 16 baselines (p < 0.05).

## Executive Summary
Time series classification (TSC) foundation models have been largely focused on forecasting, leaving a gap in modeling interpretable shapelets—discriminative subsequences—crucial for TSC. UniShape addresses this by introducing a shape‑aware adapter that splits input series into multiscale subsequences, converts them into shape tokens, and aggregates them into class tokens via attention‑based pooling, automatically selecting discriminative scales. Its prototype‑based pretraining jointly optimizes instance‑ and shape‑level contrastive losses against learnable class prototypes, enabling transferable shapelet pattern learning from weakly labeled large‑scale data. Pre‑trained on 1.89 M samples across domains, UniShape fine‑tuned on 128 UCR datasets achieves 87.08% average accuracy, significantly beating 16 baselines. In zero‑shot evaluation on 30 unseen datasets it reaches 72.62% accuracy, surpassing other foundation models. Ablations confirm the adapter’s multiscale design and dual‑level pretraining are critical.

## Method Summary
UniShape’s architecture consists of a shape‑aware adapter and a Transformer backbone. The adapter first segments a raw time series into overlapping windows at predefined lengths (e.g., 64, 32, 16, 8, 4). Each subsequence is normalized and encoded by a small 1D CNN into a shape token. An attention‑pooling head then aggregates the shape tokens of each scale into a scale‑specific class token. Class tokens from all scales are fused hierarchically into a final class token, while the top‑attended shape tokens are retained. During pretraining, two augmented views of each series are processed. A self‑supervised MoCo v3 loss enforces consistency between a query and its momentum key. Additionally, a bank of learnable class prototypes (updated by exponential moving average) is used: the instance‑prototype loss aligns the class token with its prototype, and the shape‑prototype loss aligns the selected shape tokens with the same prototype. The total loss combines these three terms. For fine‑tuning, the pretrained weights are frozen or lightly updated, and a linear classification head is trained on the final class token.

## Key Results
- UniShape fine‑tuned on 128 UCR datasets: average accuracy 87.08% (rank 2.71), significantly better than 16 baselines (p < 0.05).
- Zero‑shot feature extraction on 30 unseen datasets: 72.62% accuracy, outperforming other foundation models.
- Ablations show that removing the shape‑aware adapter, prototype losses, or the Transformer encoder all cause large performance drops, confirming each component’s importance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Multiscale Shapelet Selection via Attention Pooling
- **Claim:** The shape‑aware adapter automatically selects discriminative multiscale subsequences (shapelets) and aggregates them into class tokens, improving accuracy and interpretability.
- **Mechanism:** The adapter segments the input series into overlapping windows at multiple lengths. Each window is normalized and encoded by a small CNN into a shape token. For each scale, an attention‑pooling head computes weights over the shape tokens and produces a weighted sum as the scale‑specific class token. The class tokens from all scales are fused hierarchically. The attention weights indicate which subsequences are most relevant.
- **Core assumption:** Discriminative shapelets exist across various lengths and an attention mechanism can assign higher weights to the most relevant ones.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “shape‑aware adapter that splits input series into multiscale subsequences, converts them to shape tokens, and aggregates them into class tokens via attention‑based pooling, automatically selecting the most discriminative scales.”
  - Section “Shape‑Aware Adapter” (Pages 3‑4) details segmentation, token construction, and attention pooling.
  - Nearby works (e.g., UniCL, Abstracted Shapes as Tokens) consider multiscale representations but not the exact adapter; direct corpus support is weak.
- **Break condition:** If none of the predefined window lengths matches a true discriminative scale, the adapter cannot capture key shapelets. Attention may also fail to concentrate on informative subsequences when training data are scarce or augmentations overly destructive.

### Mechanism 2: Prototype‑Based Dual‑Level Contrastive Pretraining
- **Claim:** Prototype‑based pretraining with instance‑ and shape‑level contrastive losses learns transferable shape patterns even with partially labeled source data.
- **Mechanism:** A bank of learnable class prototypes is maintained and updated via exponential moving average of class tokens. For each input, the instance‑prototype loss aligns the class token with its prototype (contrastive formulation). The shape‑prototype loss uses the highest‑attention shape tokens (selected by the adapter) to align local shape patterns with the same prototype. Both losses encourage clustering of same‑class instances and shapes while separating different classes. During pretraining, prototypes serve as anchors for weak supervision.
- **Core assumption:** Aligning both whole‑series and local‑shape representations to class prototypes captures global semantics and local discriminative patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “prototype‑based pretraining module jointly optimizes instance‑level (class token) and shape‑level (shape token) contrastive losses against learnable class prototypes, enabling transferable shapelet pattern learning.”
  - Section “Prototype‑based Pretraining” (Pages 4‑5) defines prototype updates and loss terms mathematically.
  - Other foundation models (MOMENT, Mantis) use contrastive losses only at instance level; the shape‑level extension is novel, so direct corpus validation is absent.
- **Break condition:** If the labeling ratio is too low (e.g., only pseudo‑labels) or the momentum β is poorly set, prototypes may drift or collapse. An inappropriate shape‑token selection ratio ε may exclude critical shapelets (too low) or introduce noise (too high).

### Mechanism 3: Combining Self‑Supervised Multiview Contrastive Learning (MoCo v3) with Prototype Losses
- **Claim:** Adding MoCo v3 self‑supervision to prototype losses yields stable pretraining and diverse representations that generalize to new domains, both in fine‑tuned and zero‑shot settings.
- **Mechanism:** For each series, two augmented views are created and encoded. MoCo v3 enforces consistency between a query and its momentum key, increasing sample diversity and preventing collapse. This self‑supervised signal is added to the prototype losses (instance and shape) to inject class semantics. The combined objective is optimized during large‑scale pretraining.
- **Core assumption:** Multiview augmentations preserve class‑relevant shapelets while providing varied contexts; the joint loss balances representation quality with semantic structure.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Section “The Overall Training Loss Function” (Page 5) introduces L_self and its summation with L_proto.
  - Section “Pretraining Analysis” (Page 6) shows performance gains with higher labeling ratios, indirectly supporting the benefit of adding supervised signal to self‑supervision.
  - Forecasting‑oriented FMs (Chronos, Moirai) rely solely on self‑supervision and exhibit poor zero‑shot classification (Table 3), implying that classification‑specific objectives are necessary; however, the exact combination with prototypes is not directly tested elsewhere.
- **Break condition:** If augmentations (random cropping) remove or distort critical shapelet segments, the self‑supervised signal may become misaligned with the classification task, reducing transferability. Over‑weighting prototype losses relative to L_self early in training could also cause overfitting to noisy prototypes.

## Foundational Learning
### Prototype Learning
- **Why needed:** Provides an efficient, class‑level memory that can be updated from streaming data and supports learning under weak supervision, enabling the model to capture global class patterns without full labels.
- **Quick check:** How are class prototypes updated in UniShape, and what effect does the momentum coefficient β have?

### Contrastive Learning
- **Why needed:** Encourages the model to group together representations of the same class or shape and separate different ones, which is essential for clustering and discrimination in a foundation model pretrained on diverse sources.
- **Quick check:** What are the two levels of contrastive losses in UniShape and what specific tokens do they operate on?

- **Why needed:** Discriminative temporal patterns can span different lengths; modeling shapelets at multiple scales increases robustness and interpretability.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Raw time series → Multiscale segmenter → Shape‑aware adapter (subsequence normalization, CNN encoding, attention pooling) → Class tokens (per scale) & Shape tokens → Hierarchical fusion of class tokens → Final class token + selected shape tokens → Transformer encoder → [For pretraining: Prototype bank (EMA updates), Instance‑prototype loss, Shape‑prototype loss, MoCo v3 self‑supervised loss] → [For fine‑tuning: Linear classification head].

### Critical Path
1. Input series is split into subsequences at multiple fixed window lengths.
2. Each subsequence is normalized and passed through a small CNN to produce a shape token.
3. Attention pooling over shape tokens of a given scale yields a scale‑specific class token.
4. Scale‑specific class tokens are fused hierarchically into a final class token; the top‑ε% shape tokens (by attention) are selected.
5. The class token and shape tokens are fed to a Transformer encoder, which outputs refined representations.
6. During pretraining, class tokens are aligned with class prototypes via instance‑prototype loss; selected shape tokens are aligned with the same prototypes via shape‑prototype loss. A separate momentum encoder provides a query‑key pair for MoCo v3 loss. Prototypes are updated by exponential moving average of class tokens.
7. For fine‑tuning, the pretrained encoder is frozen or lightly tuned, and a linear head trained on the final class token for classification.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Adapter parallelism:** Three parallel 1D CNNs (kernel sizes 8, 4, 1) capture multi‑resolution patterns efficiently but add parameters; a single CNN might not cover all scales.
- **Number of scales Q:** More scales increase coverage of potential shapelet lengths but linearly increase subsequence count and memory usage.
- **Backbone choice:** Transformer encoder yields better global context than CNN or MLP (ablation) but at higher computational cost; MLP fails to learn generalizable patterns.
- **Labeling ratio:** Using only 10% labeled data for prototype updates balances performance and annotation cost; using more yields marginal gains.
- **Shape‑token selection ratio ε:** 60% is a sweet spot; too low loses shape diversity, too high introduces noise.

### Failure Signatures
- Diffuse attention weights across subsequences (no clear peaks) indicating the adapter fails to focus on discriminative regions, possibly due to inadequate pretraining or excessive augmentation.
- Prototype collapse (all prototypes similar) suggests instance‑ and shape‑level losses are unbalanced or β is too high.
- Large performance drop when removing any pretraining component (Table 4) indicates a brittle design; if fine‑tuning accuracy is near random, verify that pretrained weights were loaded correctly.
- Poor zero‑shot results when L_self dominates imply the model did not incorporate class semantics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can UniShape be effectively extended to multivariate time series while modeling cross‑channel dependencies?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states: "In the future, we aim to extend UniShape toward modeling multivariate dependencies for more generalizable foundation models."
- **Why unresolved:** The current design processes each channel independently via channel‑independent transformation, ignoring inter‑channel correlations that may contain discriminative signals for classification.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a multi‑channel shape‑aware adapter that jointly aggregates subsequences across channels, and validate on multivariate benchmarks (e.g., UEA archive) with statistical comparisons to channel‑independent variants.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What labeling ratio during source pretraining optimally balances annotation cost and classification performance for prototype‑based learning?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 2 shows marginal gains beyond 10% labeling (p>0.05), and authors adopt 10% due to computational limits, but finer‑grained ratios (e.g., 15%, 20%) or domain‑specific optima remain unexplored.
- **Why unresolved:** The plateau could indicate model saturation, but the true optimum may vary by dataset characteristics; coarse sampling leaves gaps in understanding the cost‑performance curve.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct large‑scale experiments with labeling ratios in 5% increments across diverse source domains, plotting accuracy vs. annotation cost to identify universal or adaptive optimal thresholds.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are the fixed multiscale window lengths (64,32,16,8,4) universally optimal, or should scale configurations be dynamically adapted to domain‑specific shapelet distributions?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The shape‑aware adapter uses pre‑defined scales without justification or sensitivity analysis; the paper notes shapelets have "multiscale properties" but employs static lengths.
- **Why unresolved:** Different domains (e.g., medical vs. motion) exhibit varying discriminative subsequence lengths; fixed scales may miss critical patterns or include irrelevant ones.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablate scale sets (exponential, logarithmic, data‑driven ranges) on datasets with known shapelet length distributions, measuring impact on attention alignment with ground‑truth shapelets and classification accuracy.

## Limitations
- **Fixed scale set:** Adapter uses predefined window lengths; may miss shapelets at unrepresented scales. No analysis of robustness to scale choice.
- **Prototype stability:** Behavior under very weak supervision (e.g., <1% labels) is unexplored; β sensitivity not reported.
- **Zero‑shot evaluation:** Uses frozen features + Random Forest; not a true end‑to‑end foundation model test.
- **Interpretability:** No quantitative validation that high‑attention shape tokens match ground‑truth shapelets.

## Confidence
- **Multiscale shape‑aware adapter:** Medium (ablation confirms necessity but scale selection and interpretability lack direct evidence).
- **Prototype‑based dual‑level pretraining:** Medium‑High (gains with labeling ratio shown; hyperparameter robustness uncertain).
- **Fine‑tuned SOTA performance:** High (statistically significant improvements across 128 UCR datasets).
- **Zero‑shot generalization:** Medium (outperforms peers in feature extraction, but evaluation protocol may understate capability).
