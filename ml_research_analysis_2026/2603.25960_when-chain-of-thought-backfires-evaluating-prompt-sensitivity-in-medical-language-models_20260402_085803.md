# When Chain-of-Thought Backfires: Evaluating Prompt Sensitivity in Medical Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25960
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25960
- **Reference count:** 33
- **Primary result:** MedGemma shows severe prompt sensitivity: CoT reduces accuracy by 5.7%, few-shot degrades by 11.9%, shuffling options flips 59.1% of predictions, and cloze scoring (log‑prob) achieves 64.5% (27B), outperforming all generative methods.

## Executive Summary
This study systematically evaluates the robustness of MedGemma, a medical language model, to variations in prompting strategies, answer option ordering, and context truncation on two medical QA benchmarks (MedMCQA and PubMedQA). Ten prompting strategies are tested, revealing that widely used techniques such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and few‑shot exemplars can significantly degrade performance. The model exhibits extreme sensitivity to the ordering of multiple‑choice options, with nearly 60% of predictions changing when options are shuffled. Context truncation direction matters: removing the first half of an abstract harms accuracy more than removing the second half. Crucially, a simple cloze‑scoring approach—selecting the option with the highest log‑probability—consistently yields the highest accuracy, uncovering a substantial gap between the model’s internal knowledge and its generated output.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate MedGemma‑4B and MedGemma‑27B in a frozen inference setup on MedMCQA (4,183 validation questions) and a labeled subset of PubMedQA (1,000 questions). Input prompts are constructed according to ten strategies (e.g., zero‑shot direct, zero‑shot CoT, few‑shot variants, answer‑only). Perturbations include shuffling answer options, rotating positions, and truncating context either from the front, back, or by extracting salient sentences. Performance is measured via accuracy, position bias, flip rate (proportion of changed predictions under shuffling), and per‑position accuracy. For each question, the model either generates a textual response (greedy decoding for direct/few‑shot; temperature‑0.7 sampling for CoT self‑consistency) or computes token log‑probabilities for cloze scoring without generation. Robustness techniques such as permutation voting (majority vote over four orderings) and CoT self‑consistency are also tested. All experiments are repeated over multiple random seeds.

## Key Results
- Prompt variation causes large accuracy swings: zero‑shot CoT lowers accuracy by 5.7 percentage points relative to direct answering; few‑shot exemplars degrade accuracy by 11.9 points and triple position bias.
- Answer option shuffling induces a 59.1% mean flip rate and up to 27.4‑point accuracy drop; the model overweights option “A” irrespective of content.
- Context truncation is highly asymmetric: front‑truncation to 50% of an abstract yields accuracy below the no‑context baseline, while back‑truncation retains 97% of full‑context performance.
- Cloze scoring (log‑prob selection) achieves the highest accuracy—51.8% (4B) and 64.5% (27B)—surpassing all generative methods by up to 17 points and exhibiting near‑zero position bias.
- Permutation voting (majority over four orderings) recovers ~4 percentage points over single‑ordering inference.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Chain‑of‑thought prompting reduces accuracy on medical QA for MedGemma because it disrupts the model’s internalized domain‑specific reasoning patterns, leading to overthinking and self‑contradiction.  
**Mechanism:** The model, pre‑trained on extensive medical text, likely encodes structured clinical reasoning in its parameters. CoT prompts force an explicit step‑by‑step generation that overrides these latent patterns with a more deliberative but less reliable chain, creating opportunities for errors to compound.  
**Core assumption:** MedGemma’s training data included coherent medical reasoning traces that the model internalized; the CoT prompt triggers a different reasoning mode that is not as well calibrated.  
**Evidence anchors:** CoT reduces accuracy by 5.7% (abstract); Table 4 shows CoT accuracy 41.9% vs. direct 47.6% (Section 4.1); CoT hurts 750 questions vs. helped 512, with verbose reasoning (90.7% >500 chars) and self‑contradiction (25.6%) (Section 5.1).

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** The model’s multiple‑choice answers are strongly driven by option position rather than content, causing high flip rates under shuffling and amplified position bias with few‑shot prompting.  
**Mechanism:** During training on multiple‑choice questions, the model may have learned statistical associations between option letters (A, B, C, D) and correctness due to imbalanced answer distributions. Autoregressive decoding tokens are influenced by preceding prompt tokens, so changing the position of the correct answer changes subsequent token probabilities. Few‑shot examples further bias the model by priming it with patterns that over‑represent certain positions.  
**Core assumption:** The model does not maintain a position‑invariant representation of the correct answer; it treats the option text as conditioned on its positional token (e.g., “A.” vs. “B.”).  
**Evidence anchors:** Shuffling answer options changes predictions 59.1% of the time (abstract); mean flip rate 59.1% and rotation perturbations cause up to 27.4% drop (Table 6, Section 4.3); few‑shot direct increased position bias from 0.137 to 0.472 (Section 4.1); Table 11 shows overweighting of position A (76% predicted vs. 32.2% true).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Context truncation direction critically impacts accuracy because the model relies on the beginning of the context to establish topic and orient its reasoning; front‑truncation removes this orienting information, while back‑truncation preserves it.  
**Mechanism:** The model processes context sequentially and builds an internal state; initial tokens likely serve as a “setup” that frames subsequent information. Removing the beginning deprives the model of this framing, leading to misinterpretation. Back‑truncation removes only concluding sentences, which may be less critical for orientation. “Results‑only” context performing near full‑context supports that conclusions are high‑value but not essential for the initial frame.  
**Core assumption:** The model’s attention or state‑building process is order‑sensitive and early context has an outsized influence on interpretation.  
**Evidence anchors:** Front‑truncating context to 50% causes accuracy to plummet below the no‑context baseline, while back‑truncation retains 97% of full‑context accuracy (abstract); Table 7: front‑trunc 50% yields 13.8% vs. question‑only 34.5%; back‑trunc 50% yields 44.5% vs. full 45.8% (Section 4.4); Appendix E notes a 30.7 pp gap between back and front truncation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Prompt Sensitivity in LLMs**  
  *Why needed here:* The entire study revolves around how small changes in prompt format (CoT, few‑shot, option order) drastically affect model outputs. Understanding that LLMs are not robust to such variations is key to interpreting benchmark results and deploying safely.  
  *Quick check question:* If you shuffle the order of answer options in a multiple‑choice question, should a robust model change its prediction? Why or why not?

- **Concept: Chain‑of‑Thought Prompting**  
  *Why needed here:* CoT is a widely adopted technique intended to improve reasoning. This paper shows it can backfire in medical QA. Grasping its mechanism and limitations helps in deciding when to use it.  
  *Quick check question:* What are two potential failure modes of CoT prompting observed in the paper (e.g., overthinking, self‑contradiction)?

- **Concept: Cloze Scoring vs. Generative Decoding**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper demonstrates that cloze scoring (selecting highest log‑prob option) outperforms all generative methods. This reveals a disconnect between the model’s internal knowledge and its generated output, with implications for evaluation and deployment.  
  *Quick check question:* Why might a model’s accuracy be higher when using log‑probabilities to choose an answer than when generating a textual response?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input → Prompt constructor (applies strategy, formats options, truncates context) → Model inference engine (autoregressive generation or log‑prob computation) → Output parser (regex extraction) → Scorer (compare to gold) → Metrics calculator (accuracy, bias, flip rate). Experiment orchestrator coordinates multiple runs.
- **Critical path:** (1) Construct prompt with chosen strategy and context. (2) Run model inference (generate tokens or compute option log‑probs). (3) Parse output to obtain answer. (4) Compare answer to ground truth. (5) Record result for aggregation.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Greedy decoding ensures reproducibility but CoT self‑consistency requires sampling; diversity trades off with stability.  
  - Few‑shot examples may improve format adherence but increase token cost and position bias.  
  - Context truncation: back‑truncation is safer than front; salient‑sentence extraction balances retention and length.  
  - Cloze scoring bypasses generation errors but provides no reasoning trace; may be unacceptable for explainability.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Accuracy drop ≥5.7% when using CoT vs. zero‑shot direct.  
  - Position bias: model predicts “A” far more often than its true frequency; flip rate >50% under option shuffling.  
  - Front‑truncation causing accuracy below no‑context baseline.  
  - Cloze scoring consistently outperforming generative methods by multiple points.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Prompt Ablation** – Compare zero‑shot direct, zero‑shot CoT, few‑shot direct, few‑shot CoT, and answer‑only on MedMCQA.  
  2. **Option Order Sensitivity** – Shuffle/reorder answer options and measure flip rates and accuracy changes.  
  3. **Evidence Conditioning** – Vary context presentation (truncation strategies, background/results only) on PubMedQA.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Do the observed “backfire” effects—such as CoT degradation, extreme option‑order sensitivity, and front‑truncation harms—generalize to other domain‑specific medical LLMs (e.g., BioMistral, clinical Llama adaptations) and to non‑multiple‑choice clinical tasks like free‑text differential diagnosis?  
  *Basis in paper:* Whether these generalize to other models and task formats remains open; the mechanisms may persist but manifest differently.  
- **Open Question 2:** What underlying mechanism causes the cloze‑scoring (log‑probability selection) to consistently outperform generative prompting by up to 17 percentage points, revealing a large knowledge‑expression gap?  
  *Basis in paper:* The paper speculates on generation noise and parsing failures but does not isolate whether the gap is due to decoding constraints, parsing brittleness, or representational discrepancies.  
- **Open Question 3:** Why does front‑truncation of context catastrophically harm accuracy while back‑truncation nearly preserves full‑context performance?  
  *Basis in paper:* The model “relies on opening context for orientation,” but the exact cause—training data structure, attention biases, or learned heuristics—is unknown.  
- **Open Question 4:** What causes few‑shot learning to dramatically amplify position bias and degrade accuracy in medical LLMs, given that in‑context examples are curated to be balanced and subject‑matched?  
  *Basis in paper:* The model picks up spurious patterns from in‑context examples; the paper does not analyze whether the examples themselves introduce positional cues or whether the model misinterprets the task format.

## Limitations
- Few‑shot example curation (source, selection criteria) is undocumented, preventing exact replication.
- Truncation strategies (sentence segmentation method, token vs. word truncation) are unspecified.
- Results are model‑specific; MedGemma’s instruction tuning may drive observed sensitivities, with unknown transfer to other LLMs.

## Confidence
- **Prompt sensitivity** (CoT degradation, option shuffling flip rates, truncation direction effects): **High**  
- **Cloze scoring superiority**: **High**  
- **Proposed mechanisms** (e.g., CoT overthinking, position bias from training): **Medium**
