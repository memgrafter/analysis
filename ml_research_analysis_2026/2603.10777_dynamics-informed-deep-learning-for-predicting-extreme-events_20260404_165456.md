# Dynamics-Informed Deep Learning for Predicting Extreme Events
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10777
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10777
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Predicts extreme dissipation bursts in Kolmogorov flow with extended horizon (τ up to 15) using FTLE-based precursors from OTD modes and a Transformer, outperforming Fourier-mode baseline.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents a framework for predicting extreme events in high-dimensional chaotic systems by combining reduced-order instability diagnostics with deep learning. Finite-time Lyapunov exponents (FTLEs) are computed in a dynamically adaptive subspace spanned by Optimal Time-Dependent (OTD) modes, directly from state snapshots without requiring explicit equations. These interpretable precursors, together with their derivatives, are fed to a Transformer model (Informer) to forecast extreme energy dissipation bursts in 2D Kolmogorov flow. Across lead times τ=2–15, the FTLE-informed predictor consistently achieves higher precision, recall, F1-score, and adjusted AUC compared to a Fourier-mode baseline. Notably at τ=10, the FTLE approach remains near-optimal while the baseline degrades sharply, demonstrating that encoding transient instability mechanisms significantly extends the prediction horizon.

## Method Summary
The method comprises three main stages. First, a data-driven dynamics surrogate (e.g., FNO, ResUNet++, or ResCNN) is trained on snapshot-derivative pairs to approximate the system’s right-hand side. Second, using the surrogate, the leading finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE) is computed in a low-dimensional subspace spanned by OTD modes. Specifically, the Jacobian-vector products of the surrogate are used to evolve r=6 OTD modes via an orthonormalization procedure; the reduced linear operator is integrated over a finite horizon T=5 seconds to obtain the Cauchy–Green tensor, whose eigenvalues yield the FTLEs. The leading FTLE Γ1 and its time derivative form the precursor vector. Third, a Transformer (Informer) with probabilistic sparse self‑attention maps a lookback window of length Δ=4τ of this precursor sequence to the future energy dissipation D(t+τ). The model is trained with an output‑weighted MAE loss that up‑weights rare extreme samples by scaling each error inversely by a kernel density estimate of the target distribution. Evaluation uses metrics such as F1, AUC, adjusted AUC α*, and extreme event count accuracy, with extreme defined as D > mean(D) + 2·std(D). The pipeline is applied to 2D Kolmogorov flow at Re=40 on a 64×64 grid, using 70% of 300 k snapshots for training and 30% for testing.

## Key Results
- FTLE‑based Transformer outperforms the Fourier‑mode baseline in F1, AUC, α*, and event‑count accuracy for all tested lead times τ∈{2,5,7,10,12,15}.
- At τ=10, the FTLE predictor remains near‑optimal while the baseline’s performance drops sharply, indicating a longer effective prediction horizon.
- The FTLE approach accurately captures the tail of the dissipation distribution up to τ≈12, as measured by discrepancy metric D.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The framework constructs interpretable precursors by computing the leading finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE) in an adaptively evolving low‑dimensional subspace spanned by Optimal Time‑Dependent (OTD) modes, directly from state snapshots without requiring explicit governing equations.
- **Mechanism:** A data-driven approximation of the system dynamics (e.g., a neural operator) is learned from snapshots. Its Jacobian‑vector products are used via finite differences to integrate the OTD mode equations, which form an orthonormal basis aligned with directions of maximal transient growth. The linearized operator is projected onto this subspace to obtain a reduced matrix, whose fundamental solution is integrated over a finite horizon *T*. The resulting reduced Cauchy–Green tensor yields FTLE values; the leading FTLE \(\hat{\Gamma}_1(t)\) serves as the instability precursor.
- **Core assumption:** (i) The transient instabilities that trigger extreme events are confined to a low‑dimensional subspace of the tangent dynamics; (ii) The leading FTLE over an appropriately chosen horizon *T* is sensitive to the impending extreme; (iii) The learned dynamics \(\hat{F}\) is accurate enough for reliable Jacobian‑vector evaluations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “computing finite-time Lyapunov exponents (FTLEs) in a reduced-order subspace spanned by dynamically adaptive Optimal Time‑Dependent (OTD) modes, solely from state snapshots.”
  - [section 2.3.1]: Details the reduced‑order FTLE computation; notes that OTD modes converge to the dominant eigendirections of the Cauchy–Green tensor.
  - [section 5.1]: Empirical study showing convergence of \(\hat{\Gamma}_1\) for \(r \geq 6\) modes and optimal horizon \(T=5\,\)s; Figure 5d shows peaks of \(\hat{\Gamma}_1\) preceding dissipation bursts.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor “XAI4Extremes” also seeks interpretable precursors but does not employ OTD‑FTLE; the present approach is distinctive in this corpus.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the OTD subspace is too low‑dimensional (e.g., \(r=2\) underestimates growth – Fig 5a), if the horizon *T* is too long (over‑smoothing and lag – Fig 5b), or if the learned dynamics is inaccurate (e.g., ResCNN yields high Sobolev errors – Table 1, Appendix B.1). In such cases the FTLE no longer reliably anticipates extremes.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A Transformer‑based sequence‑to‑sequence model (Informer) maps the recent history of FTLE‑based precursors to the future observable (energy dissipation) over a lead time \(\tau\), enabling long‑horizon extreme‑event forecasts.
- **Mechanism:** The precursor vector \(\pi(t) = [\hat{\Gamma}_1(t), \hat{\Gamma}'_1(t)]\) over a lookback window \(\Delta\) (partitioned into \(n_\Delta\) steps) is embedded and fed to an encoder with probabilistic sparse self‑attention, building a context‑aware representation. A decoder with masked self‑attention and cross‑attention generates the forecast sequence over \(\tau\) (\(n_\tau\) steps). The terminal prediction \(\hat{z}(t+\tau)\) is used to classify extreme events. Training minimizes an output‑weighted MAE loss that upweights rare high‑dissipation samples.
- **Core assumption:** (i) The temporal pattern of FTLE and its derivative contains sufficient information to predict dissipation \(\tau\) steps ahead; (ii) Attention can capture long‑range dependencies beyond Markovian models; (iii) Output‑weighting improves detection of rare events without excessive false positives.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “These precursors are then provided as input to a Transformer‑based model, enabling forecast of extreme‑event observables.”
  - [section 3.2.1]: Formal description of the Transformer (Informer) architecture, the input channels, and the output‑weighted loss (Eq 24).
  - [section 5.2]: Across lead times \(\tau \in \{2,\dots,15\}\) the FTLE‑based Transformer consistently outperforms the Fourier‑mode baseline in F1, AUC, \(\alpha^*\), and event‑count accuracy (Fig 6); it maintains near‑optimal performance at \(\tau=10\) where the baseline degrades sharply (Fig 7).
  - [corpus]: Papers like “Extreme Event Aware (\(\eta\)-) Learning” and “Identifying Key Drivers of Heatwaves” also use deep learning for extremes but without dynamics‑aware precursors; the present work demonstrates extended predictability via mechanism‑informed features.
- **Break condition:** If the lead time \(\tau\) exceeds the intrinsic predictability horizon (for the Kolmogorov flow \(\tau > 15\) – Fig 6), both FTLE and baseline degrade. A too‑short lookback \(\Delta\) deprives the model of context; the paper found \(\Delta=4\tau\) optimal. Over‑fitting (e.g., insufficient dropout) or numerical instability from aggressive output‑weighting (poor KDE) can also break the mechanism; no ablation of the loss is provided, leaving this as a potential vulnerability.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** An output‑weighted loss function, where each sample’s absolute error is scaled inversely by the estimated probability density of the target observable, forces the model to prioritize rare extreme events and improves their detection.
- **Mechanism:** During training, the loss \(L_{\text{OW}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j} \frac{|\hat{z}_j - z_j|}{p_z(z_j)}\). The density \(p_z(z)\) is estimated via kernel density estimation (KDE) from the training data. This amplifies the penalty for errors on low‑density (extreme) outcomes while down‑weighting frequent small fluctuations.
- **Core assumption:** (i) The KDE provides a reasonably accurate estimate of \(p_z(z)\) across the support, including the tail; (ii) The extreme class is sufficiently represented to avoid degenerate solutions; (iii) Upweighting does not destabilize optimization or cause excessive false positives.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2.1]: Equation (24) defines the output‑weighted MAE loss and explains its purpose.
  - [section 5.2]: The FTLE‑based predictor (which uses this loss) achieves higher precision‑recall than the Fourier baseline; tail discrepancy metric \(D\) (Eq 27) shows better preservation of extreme‑event statistics up to \(\tau\approx12\) (Fig 8).
  - [corpus]: “Extreme Event Aware (\(\eta\)-) Learning” (neighbor) introduces a related idea of event‑aware learning; the present loss is a concrete instance of that paradigm.
- **Break condition:** If the training data are scarce, the KDE in the extreme tail becomes noisy, leading to unstable weights and potentially gradient explosion. With extremely rare events (e.g., < 0.1% occurrence), the weighting may dominate the loss, pushing the model toward always predicting extremes (high false positives). The paper does not explore data‑scarcity limits, so this remains a potential break condition.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Finite‑Time Lyapunov Exponent (FTLE)**
  - *Why needed here:* The leading FTLE \(\hat{\Gamma}_1\) is the core precursor that quantifies the cumulative exponential growth of perturbations over a finite horizon, directly linking to the onset of extreme dissipation bursts.
  - *Quick check:* “According to Figure 5b, what happens to the FTLE’s ability to anticipate an extreme when the integration horizon *T* is increased from 5 s to 20 s? What does that imply about the choice of *T*?”
- **Concept: Optimal Time‑Dependent (OTD) modes**
  - *Why needed here:* OTD modes provide an adaptive, flow‑dependent orthonormal basis that aligns with the most unstable directions, enabling a reduced‑order computation of FTLE without the prohibitive cost of full‑order Lyapunov analysis.
  - *Quick check:* “How do OTD modes differ from proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) modes in capturing transient instabilities? (Hint: See Section 2.2.)”
- **Concept: Transformer (Informer) with attention**
  - *Why needed here:* The Informer processes variable‑length FTLE histories to forecast dissipation at long lead times, leveraging self‑attention to capture both short‑ and long‑range temporal dependencies that traditional RNNs might miss.
  - *Quick check:* “Why does the Informer replace full self‑attention with probabilistic sparse attention, and how does this relate to the lookback window length \(\Delta\)? (See Appendix A.2.1.)”

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Dynamics estimator (surrogate) → OTD integrator (evolve OTD modes) → Reduced linear operator (project Jacobian) → Fundamental matrix integrator (integrate reduced ODE) → FTLE calculator (Cauchy–Green eigenvalues) → Derivative estimator (finite difference) → Transformer predictor (Informer) → Output‑weighted loss.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Train \(\hat{F}\) on snapshot‑derivative pairs.
  2. Along test trajectory, use \(\hat{F}\) to compute OTD modes and \(\hat{\Gamma}_1\) history.
  3. Train Transformer on \((\pi(\cdot), z(t+\tau))\) pairs.
  4. At inference, for current time \(t\): (a) compute \(\pi(t)\) from recent OTD/FTLE steps, (b) feed to Transformer to obtain \(\hat{z}(t+\tau)\), (c) classify extreme if \(\hat{z}(t+\tau) \geq \hat{z}^\star\).
  The quality of step 1 directly impacts step 2; the accuracy of \(\hat{\Gamma}_1\) and the choice of hyperparameters (e.g., \(r, T, \Delta\)) determine the reliability of step 3.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Dynamics estimator*: FNO yields lowest Sobolev errors but larger memory; ResUNet++ is a compromise; ResCNN is fastest but may omit multiscale interactions.
  - *OTD subspace dimension* \(r\): Larger \(r\) captures more instability directions but increases computational load; the paper found \(r=6\) sufficient for convergence.
  - *FTLE horizon* \(T\): Short \(T\) (e.g., 5 s) gives sharp, early peaks; long \(T\) (e.g., 20 s) smooths and lags (Fig 5b). Tradeoff: sensitivity vs robustness.
  - *Lookback window* \(\Delta\): Longer \(\Delta\) provides more context but raises model complexity; \(\Delta=4\tau\) was optimal in experiments.
  - *Transformer depth/width*: More layers/heads increase capacity but risk overfitting on limited extreme examples; dropout mitigates this.
  - *Loss weighting*: Output‑weighted loss improves tail recall but may inflate false positives if KDE bandwidth is poorly chosen.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Poor dynamics approximation (high H0–H2 errors) leads to inaccurate OTD modes → flat or mistimed FTLE peaks.
  - Setting \(r=2\) yields FTLE that underestimates growth (Fig 5a), missing early warning.
  - Using \(T=20\,\)s produces delayed FTLE, misaligned with dissipation bursts (Fig 5b).
  - Transformer fails to converge (loss plateaus, predictions constant) – often due to inadequate capacity, missing positional encoding, or unstable loss weighting.
  - High \(|\Delta N_{\text{EE}}|\) (Fig 6d) indicates systematic bias in event frequency, possibly from threshold mismatch or excessive false alarms.
  - Generalization failure when test conditions (e.g., Reynolds number) deviate from training dynamics.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reduced‑order FTLE validation:** Vary \(r\) and \(T\), compare data‑driven \(\hat{\Gamma}_1\) with equation‑based reference (where available). Demonstrates convergence at \(r\ge6\) and optimal sensitivity at \(T=5\,\)s (Fig 5). Establishes applicability of the precursor.
  2. **End‑to‑end prediction comparison:** Train Transformer with FTLE precursor vs. Fourier‑mode precursor across \(\tau \in \{2,5,7,10,12,15\}\). FTLE consistently yields higher F1, AUC, \(\alpha^*\) and lower \(|\Delta N_{\text{EE}}|\) (Fig 6). Visual inspection (Fig 7) and tail PDFs (Fig 8) confirm improved extreme‑event capture, especially at \(\tau=10\)–12.
  3. **Hyperparameter tuning for Transformer:** Systematically explore lookback \(\Delta\) (2τ, 3τ, 4τ, 5τ), embedding dimension, number of layers, etc. Identify \(\Delta=4\tau\) as best under AUC criterion (Appendix A.2). This guides practical deployment for different lead times.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How well does the FTLE-informed Transformer framework generalize to other high-dimensional chaotic systems with different dynamics or extreme event mechanisms?
2. What is the optimal dimensionality r for the OTD subspace when computing FTLEs, and how does it scale with system complexity or Reynolds number?
3. How sensitive are FTLE-based precursors and extreme event forecasts to errors in the data-driven approximation of the dynamical operator \(\hat{F}\)?
4. Does the output-weighted loss function's dependence on kernel density estimation (KDE) of the observable distribution introduce biases in tail prediction for rare events?

## Limitations
- The pipeline's robustness depends on accurate surrogate dynamics; surrogate errors may propagate to FTLE and degrade forecasts, but no error analysis is provided.
- All experiments are on a single configuration (Kolmogorov flow at Re=40, n=4); generalizability to other flows or parameter regimes remains untested.
- The output-weighted loss relies on KDE, which can be unreliable in the extreme tail and may lead to unstable training or biased performance.

## Confidence
- OTD‑FTLE precursor: Medium (empirically validated for r≥6 and T=5 s, but sensitivity to surrogate accuracy not quantified).
- Transformer predictor: Medium (consistent gains across τ, but training details missing and risk of overfitting on rare events).
- Output‑weighted loss: Low (no ablation, potential instability from KDE tail estimation).
