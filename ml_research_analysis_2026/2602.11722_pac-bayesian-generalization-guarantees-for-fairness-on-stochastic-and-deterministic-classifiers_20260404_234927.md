# PAC-Bayesian Generalization Guarantees for Fairness on Stochastic and Deterministic Classifiers

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.11722  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11722  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A PAC-Bayesian framework that provides computable generalization bounds for fairness measures expressible as risk discrepancies, enabling self-bounding optimization of prediction risk and fairness for both stochastic and deterministic classifiers.

## Executive Summary
Classical PAC-Bayesian bounds focus solely on predictive risk, leaving fairness unguaranteed. This paper extends the PAC-Bayesian paradigm to fairness by deriving bounds for measures that can be written as absolute differences of conditional risks over sensitive groups (e.g., Demographic Parity, Equalized Odds, Equal Opportunity). For stochastic Gibbs classifiers, the bounds follow from a union of PAC-Bayes intervals applied to each subgroup. For deterministic majority votes, a risk decomposition technique translates the Gibbs bound into a deterministic counterpart. These bounds are used within a self-bounding algorithm that directly optimizes a trade-off between prediction risk and fairness during training. Theoretical results establish tight bounds for both settings, and experiments on five datasets confirm that the bounds are close to actual test errors and that the algorithm achieves favorable risk-fairness trade-offs, outperforming prior work limited to stochastic classifiers or specific fairness notions.

## Method Summary
The method begins by defining fairness risk as \(R_{FD}(h) = |R_{D|a}(h) - R_{D|b}(h)|\), where \(R_{D|a}\) is the conditional risk on the privileged group and \(R_{D|b}\) on the unprivileged group. For a stochastic Gibbs classifier \(G\rho\) (drawn from a posterior \(\rho\) over a finite hypothesis set), standard PAC-Bayesian bounds provide high-probability upper and lower estimates for each conditional risk; combining them yields an upper bound on \(R_{FD}(G\rho)\). To extend to deterministic majority votes \(H_\rho\), the authors use a recent decomposition that expresses the true risk as a function of the Gibbs risk and two conditional expectations, \(0^\rho_D(h)\) and \(1^\rho_D(h)\), which are bounded via a partition problem on the posterior weight vector. The final bounds are plugged into a composite objective \((1-\lambda)R_D + \lambda R_{FD}\) that is minimized directly. For stochastic models, gradient-based optimization (Adam) adjusts the posterior parameters. For deterministic majority votes, the posterior is first optimized stochastically, then a suite of heuristics (clipping small weights, coordinate descent, multiplicative scaling) iteratively refines the parameters to tighten the deterministic bound. The hypothesis class consists of weighted majority votes over axis-aligned decision stumps, with posteriors including categorical, Dirichlet, and Gaussian families. Evaluation on Adult, COMPAS, German Credit, and MEPS datasets demonstrates tight bounds and effective risk-fairness trade-offs.

## Key Results
- PAC-Bayesian bounds for fairness are derived for both stochastic Gibbs and deterministic majority‑vote classifiers, covering any measure reducible to a risk discrepancy (e.g., DP, EO, EOP).
- Empirical results show the theoretical fairness bounds are tight, consistently close to the true test‑time fairness error across datasets and posterior families.
- The self‑bounding algorithm yields models that efficiently trade off prediction risk and fairness, surpassing prior PAC‑Bayesian fairness methods limited to stochastic classifiers or specific fairness criteria.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1**  
- **Claim:** Any fairness measure expressible as an absolute difference between two conditional risks (e.g., Demographic Parity, Equal Opportunity) can be bounded using a PAC‑Bayesian framework that separately controls the generalization gap of each conditional risk.  
- **Mechanism:** The true fairness risk is defined as \(R_{FD}(h) = |R_{D|a}(h) - R_{D|b}(h)|\). A PAC‑Bayesian bound on the Gibbs risk for each subgroup (using a union bound) yields a bound on the absolute difference. This is achieved by applying an existing PAC‑Bayes theorem (e.g., Theorem 2.1) to provide both upper and lower bounds on \(R_{D|a}(G\rho)\) and \(R_{D|b}(G\rho)\), then combining them to bound the difference.  
- **Core assumption:** The fairness criterion must be decomposable into a difference of two population risks over disjoint data subgroups (defined by the sensitive attribute). The PAC‑Bayes bound used must provide symmetric upper and lower confidence intervals.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: “covers… fairness measures that can be expressed as a risk discrepancy (e.g., Demographic Parity, Equalized Odds, Equal Opportunity).”  
  - [section]: Section 3.1 formally defines \(R_{FD}(h)\) and instantiates it for DP, EO, EOP.  
  - [corpus]: Prior work (Oneto et al., 2020) is restricted to Equal Opportunity; this work generalizes to any risk discrepancy.  
- **Break condition:** The fairness measure cannot be written as \(|R_A - R_B|\) for two distinct subgroup risks (e.g., measures requiring third‑order interactions).

**Mechanism 2**  
- **Claim:** Deterministic majority vote classifiers \(H_\rho\) inherit generalization bounds on their risk from the stochastic Gibbs classifier \(G\rho\) via a risk decomposition that introduces computable correction terms based on the posterior distribution.  
- **Mechanism:** For a deterministic \(h \in H\), Proposition 4.3 decomposes its true risk as \(R_D(h) = R_D(G\rho) - \frac{0^\rho_D(h)}{1^\rho_D(h) - 0^\rho_D(h)}\), where \(0^\rho_D(h)\) (resp. \(1^\rho_D(h)\)) is the conditional expectation of the Gibbs loss given that \(h\) predicts correctly (resp. incorrectly). Upper and lower bounds on \(R_D(h)\) are derived by combining a PAC‑Bayes bound on \(R_D(G\rho)\) with bounds on these conditional expectations.  
- **Core assumption:** The hypothesis class \(\mathcal{H}\) consists of weighted majority votes over a finite set of base classifiers (Eq. 11), enabling the calculation of tight bounds on \(0^\rho_D(h)\) and \(1^\rho_D(h)\) via a partition problem on the posterior weight vector.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: “for deterministic classifiers… we leverage a recent advance in PAC‑Bayes to extend the fairness bound beyond the stochastic setting.”  
  - [section]: Section 4.2 introduces Prop 4.3 (risk decomposition) and Lemma 4.4 (bounding \(R_D(h)\)).  
  - [corpus]: Neighbor “A Framework for Bounding Deterministic Risk with PAC‑Bayes: Applications to Majority Votes” (paper_id 70003) is the cited recent advance.  
- **Break condition:** The decomposition terms \(0^\rho_D(h)\) and \(1^\rho_D(h)\) become numerically unstable or intractable to bound for complex posteriors or high‑dimensional weight vectors, as hinted by poor performance on MEPS.

**Mechanism 3**  
- **Claim:** The learning algorithm is “self‑bounding”: it directly minimizes a computable upper bound on the trade‑off \((1-\lambda) R_D(\cdot) + \lambda R_{FD}(\cdot)\), yielding a model whose theoretical fairness and risk guarantees are optimized during training.  
- **Mechanism:** For a stochastic Gibbs classifier, the objective \(GS(\rho)\) combines a PAC‑Bayes bound on \(R_D(G\rho)\) (Theorem 2.1) and the fairness bound from Mechanism 1 (Theorem 4.1). For a deterministic majority vote, the objective \(HS(\rho)\) replaces the Gibbs risk bound with the deterministic risk bound from Mechanism 2 (Lemma 4.4). The posterior parameters \(\alpha\) are optimized via gradient descent (for \(GS\)) or heuristics (for \(HS\)) to minimize this composite bound.  
- **Core assumption:** The PAC‑Bayes bounds used are sufficiently tight to make the optimization landscape meaningful; the prior \(\pi\) is chosen to be in the same family as the posterior to avoid intractable KL divergences.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: “leads to a self‑bounding algorithm in which the learning procedure directly optimizes a trade‑off between generalization bounds on the prediction risk and on the fairness.”  
  - [section]: Algorithm 1 and Equations (13)–(14) define the learning objectives. Section 5 describes the two‑phase optimization for majority votes.  
  - [corpus]: The concept of self‑bounding algorithms originates from Freund (1998), revived in recent PAC‑Bayes work (e.g., Rivasplata 2022, Viallard 2023).  
- **Break condition:** If the fairness bound is excessively loose compared to the true fairness risk, minimizing it may not correlate with improving actual fairness, leading to poor empirical trade‑offs.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept 1: PAC-Bayesian Generalization Bounds (e.g., Theorem 2.1)**  
*Why needed here:* The entire framework relies on providing a data‑dependent upper bound on the Gibbs risk \(R_D(G\rho)\) that is computable from the training sample and optimizable. This bound is the building block for both stochastic and deterministic fairness guarantees.  
*Quick check:* Given a prior \(\pi\), posterior \(\rho\), empirical Gibbs risk \(\hat{R}_S(G\rho)\), and sample size \(m\), how does the KL divergence \(\text{KL}(\rho\|\pi)\) affect the tightness of the PAC‑Bayes bound? When would it become vacuous?

**Concept 2: Risk Decomposition for Deterministic Classifiers (Proposition 4.3)**  
*Why needed here:* Classical PAC‑Bayes bounds apply only to stochastic predictors (\(G\rho\)). To extend guarantees to a deterministic model \(h\), we must relate \(R_D(h)\) to \(R_D(G\rho)\) via conditional expectations that depend on \(\rho\). This decomposition is the key technical tool.  
*Quick check:* If \(1^\rho_D(h) = 0^\rho_D(h)\), what happens to the decomposition? Why is this case pathological?

**Concept 3: Group Fairness as Risk Discrepancy (Section 3.1)**  
*Why needed here:* The paper's fairness guarantees apply to any measure that can be written as an absolute difference between conditional risks over sensitive groups. This unifies DP, EOP, and EO under one bound.  
*Quick check:* How would you express Equalized Odds in the form \(|R_{D|a}(h) - R_{D|b}(h)|\)? What are the two conditional distributions involved?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Input (training set, sensitive attribute, fairness measure, λ, prior π, confidence δ) → Base Learners (axis‑aligned decision stumps) → Posterior Family (categorical/Dirichlet/Gaussian) → Partition Problem (computes ᾱ, ᾱ from α) → Bound Computation (GS for stochastic or HS for deterministic) → Optimization Loop (Adam for GS; heuristics for HS) → Output (posterior ρ or deterministic majority vote h_α).

**Critical path:**  
The tightness of the final fairness bound for a deterministic majority vote critically depends on the partition problem solution (Step 4). A poor partition (suboptimal ᾱ, ᾱ) leads to loose bounds on \(0^\rho_D\) and \(1^\rho_D\), which then inflate the HS(ρ) objective and may misguide optimization.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Stochastic vs. Deterministic:** Stochastic (Gρ) has a simpler, directly optimizable objective (GS) but the fairness measure RFD(Gρ) may not reflect individual classifier fairness. Deterministic (h_α) yields a model with inherent fairness guarantees but requires discrete optimization heuristics, which are less stable (evidenced by worse results on high‑dimensional MEPS).  
- **Posterior Family:** Categorical yields the simplest bounds (Prop 4.6.1) but may be less expressive. Dirichlet and Gaussian provide more nuanced weight distributions but involve special functions (Beta incomplete, error function) in their bounds.  
- **Bound Tightness vs. Computational Cost:** The partition problem is NP‑hard (Mertens, 2005). The heuristics (Section 5) are approximations; in high dimensions, they may fail to find a tight partition, causing the deterministic bound to be loose.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. **MEPS Dataset:** Deterministic bounds become very loose (Figs 1d, 3d, Table 4/5). Indicator: High‑dimensional feature space (d=41) likely makes the partition heuristic ineffective.  
2. **Gaussian Posterior:** May produce underestimates of \(1^\rho_D(h_α)\) (Prop 4.6.3) if the Gaussian’s variance is not well‑controlled, causing the lower risk bound \(R^\downarrow_S\) to be too high.  
3. **Heuristic Saturation:** If applying Heuristics 5.1–5.3 no longer decreases HS(ρ), it suggests the current α is near a local optimum of the bound, but not necessarily of the true risk‑fairness trade‑off.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Bound Tightness Validation:** For a fixed λ and posterior family, train models (stochastic and deterministic) on a dataset (e.g., ADULT‑GENDER). Plot the computed upper bound RFD (from Theorem 4.1/4.5) against the test‑set RFD for the learned model across random seeds. A tight bound will lie close to the test error line.  
2. **Trade‑off Curve:** Sweep λ ∈ {0,0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9,1} for a specific fairness measure (e.g., DP) and posterior (Categorical). Plot test error vs. test DP for all models. Compare the Pareto frontier against the baseline O1 (Oneto et al.’s stochastic bound) to see if optimization directly on the bound yields a better trade‑off.  
3. **Deterministic Heuristic Impact:** Train a deterministic majority vote on COMPAS‑GENDER with Dirichlet posterior. Compare HS(ρ) value and test performance **with** and **without** the heuristics (Lines 10–12 in Algorithm 1). Expected: Heuristics should lower HS(ρ) and potentially improve test DP at the cost of risk, demonstrating their role in tightening bounds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to multi‑class and multi‑group settings:** How can the PAC‑Bayesian fairness framework be extended to multi‑class classification problems and sensitive attributes with more than two groups? The current derivations assume binary classification and binary sensitive groups.  
2. **Efficient partition approximation:** What efficient approximation algorithms can be employed for the NP‑hard partition problem in Proposition 4.6 to ensure computability of deterministic classifier bounds for large numbers of base classifiers?  
3. **Guaranteed optimization for deterministic bounds:** Can the ad‑hoc heuristics in Algorithm 1 for optimizing the deterministic classifier’s bound be replaced with optimization methods that have convergence guarantees?  
4. **Incorporating non‑risk‑discrepancy fairness:** To what extent can fairness measures not expressible as risk discrepancies (e.g., individual fairness, calibration) be incorporated into the PAC‑Bayesian framework?  
5. **Assumption \(1^\rho_D(h) \neq 0^\rho_D(h)\):** Under what conditions does the assumption in Proposition 4.3 hold in practice, and how do violations affect the validity of the derived bounds?

## Limitations
- **Deterministic bound tightness on high‑dimensional data:** The partition heuristic may produce loose bounds when many base learners are used, as observed on MEPS, limiting practical guarantees for deterministic models.  
- **Optimization details omitted:** The paper does not specify the exact partition algorithm or how gradients flow through the inverse KL term, raising reproducibility concerns and possibly affecting training stability.  
- **Fairness measure scope:** The framework applies only to fairness criteria expressible as absolute differences of conditional risks (e.g., Demographic Parity, Equalized Odds, Equal Opportunity). Measures like Predictive Parity or Treatment Equality fall outside this class.
