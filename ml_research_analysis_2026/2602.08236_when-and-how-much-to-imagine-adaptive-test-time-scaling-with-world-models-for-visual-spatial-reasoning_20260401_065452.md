# When and How Much to Imagine: Adaptive Test-Time Scaling with World Models for Visual Spatial Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08236  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08236  
- **Reference count:** 31  
- **Primary result:** AVIC attains state-of-the-art or competitive accuracy on spatial reasoning (SAT, MMSI) and embodied navigation (R2R) benchmarks while drastically reducing computation—e.g., on SAT-Real with GPT-4.1 it achieves 79.3% accuracy with only 0.73 average world‑model calls per instance, compared to 77.3% using a fixed imagination strategy with 12.34 calls.

## Executive Summary
AVIC is an adaptive test‑time framework that decides *when* to invoke a visual world model for imagination and *how many* actions to simulate. A policy model evaluates the sufficiency of the current observation; if inadequate, it proposes a short action plan, triggering the world model to render a trajectory of imagined views. A verifier scores each full trajectory, selecting the most informative one before the QA model produces the final answer. Across SAT‑Real, MMSI‑Bench, and R2R, AVIC matches or surpasses prior accuracy while cutting world‑model calls by over 90%. The work demonstrates that selective, instance‑adaptive scaling—not indiscriminate imagination—is crucial for efficient visual spatial reasoning, with performance saturating after just 1–2 novel views.

## Method Summary
No training is required. At inference, a policy model (the same base MLLM) is prompted with the initial observation, question, and answer choices. It outputs a binary decision (skip or call world model) and, if calling, a short discrete action plan (e.g., “turn‑left 9°, move‑forward 0.25m”). The policy is sampled *M* times (default *M*=5) and majority voting determines the gating decision. When “call” wins, each proposed action sequence is executed by a frozen visual world model (Stable Virtual Camera) to generate an imagined trajectory of views. A verifier model scores each full trajectory; the highest‑scoring one is retained. Finally, the QA model reasons over the original observation and the selected imagined views to predict the answer. Efficiency metrics (world‑model calls, token usage, wall‑clock time) are measured and compared against fixed‑imagination baselines.

## Key Results
- AVIC delivers SOTA or competitive accuracy on SAT‑Real, MMSI‑Bench, and R2R while reducing average world‑model calls by >90% compared to fixed imagination strategies.  
- On SAT‑Real (GPT‑4.1), AVIC reaches **79.3%** accuracy with **0.73** world‑model calls per instance, outperforming the always‑on baseline (77.3% with 12.34 calls).  
- Imagination provides the largest boost for **action‑conditioned reasoning** errors (+57.1% gain), and accuracy saturates after **1–2** imagined views, highlighting the value of minimal, targeted simulation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Selective invocation**  
- *Claim:* Selective invocation prevents wasted computation and avoids misleading spatial evidence.  
- *Mechanism:* The policy model assesses whether the current visual observation is sufficient for the question; if insufficient, it calls the world model; otherwise it skips imagination entirely.  
- *Core assumption:* The policy can reliably infer necessity from the question and observation.  
- *Evidence anchors:* Abstract: “explicitly reasons about the sufficiency of current visual evidence before selectively invoking…”; Section 4.2 describes gating with decision d and the skip path.  
- *Break condition:* If the policy’s sufficiency judgment is consistently misaligned with actual error types (e.g., skipping when action‑conditioned reasoning is needed), the benefits of selectivity diminish.

**Mechanism 2: Dynamic‑length action planning**  
- *Claim:* Planning a targeted action sequence focuses imagination on potentially informative viewpoints rather than exhaustive or random views.  
- *Mechanism:* When imagination is triggered, the policy emits a short discrete action plan π that guides the world model’s simulation, limiting the number of calls and steering the exploration.  
- *Core assumption:* A small set of policy‑proposed actions is more likely to yield spatially relevant views than fixed or random sets.  
- *Evidence anchors:* Abstract: “plan a dynamic‑length action sequence for targeted world‑model invocation”; Section 4.2 defines π and its sampling.  
- *Break condition:* If policy‑proposed actions consistently misdirect (e.g., turning away from relevant regions), planning offers no benefit over random exploration and adds planning overhead.

**Mechanism 3: Trajectory‑level verification**  
- *Claim:* Scoring entire imagined trajectories as coherent units selects useful, noise‑filtered sequences before final reasoning.  
- *Mechanism:* Multiple action plans may be sampled; the verifier evaluates each resulting full trajectory (sequence of views) and retains only the top‑ranked one.  
- *Core assumption:* Evaluating the whole trajectory (geometric/temporal consistency) is more effective than selecting isolated keyframes.  
- *Evidence anchors:* Section 4.3: “our verifier evaluates the entire imagined trajectory as a coherent unit… Only the top‑ranked imagined trajectory is retained.”  
- *Break condition:* If the verifier’s scoring favors longer or redundant trajectories irrespective of informativeness, it may consistently select unhelpful sequences.

## Foundational Learning
- **World Models as Action‑Conditioned Simulators**  
  *Why needed:* AVIC requires a world model that can render photorealistic novel views conditioned on an initial observation and an egocentric action (e.g., “turn‑left 9°”).  
  *Quick check:* Is the world model’s action space aligned with the policy’s discrete actions? Mismatches (continuous vs. discrete) break the execution loop.

- **Test‑Time Scaling (TTS) vs. Training‑Time Scaling**  
  *Why needed:* AVIC is a TTS method; it allocates extra compute (world‑model calls, policy sampling) at inference without retraining the base MLLM.  
  *Quick check:* Does adding more test‑time compute (e.g., longer action plans, more policy samples) always improve accuracy? The paper shows non‑monotonic gains and saturation.

- **Policy Model for Tool‑Use Gating**  
  *Why needed:* The policy acts as a general MLLM used in a *tool‑use* role—deciding if and how to call an external tool (the world model).  
  *Quick check:* What signals in the question/observation influence the binary gating decision? (e.g., keywords like “turn”, “occluded”, ambiguity in answer choices.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input (I, q, A) -> Policy Model θ -> Decision d (skip/call_wm) & Action plan π (if call) -> [if d=skip] -> QA Model ϕ -> â  
[if d=call_wm] -> World Model W (render I_π) -> Verifier V (score trajectory) -> Trajectory Selector (choose π*) -> QA Model ϕ -> â

**Critical path:** Policy gating → Conditional world model execution → Trajectory verification → Final QA. The skip path bypasses world model, verifier, and selector.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Policy strength vs. cost: Stronger policy models may gate more accurately but increase latency.  
- Action plan length vs. informativeness: Longer plans may reach relevant viewpoints but risk compounding errors and higher computation.  
- Verifier specificity: General verifier may misalign with QA’s needs; task‑specific verifier could help but likely requires training.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High WM calls + low accuracy gain → policy over‑calling (low precision).  
- Low WM calls + missed improvements → policy under‑calling (low recall), e.g., failing on action‑conditioned tasks.  
- Saturation after 1–2 views → verifier fails to suppress redundant/long trajectories.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Ablation: Gating Effectiveness** – Compare AVIC against (a) always‑on (fixed 12 views), (b) random gating (randomly skip 50% of calls), and (c) oracle gating (oracle knowledge of helpfulness). Measure accuracy vs. average WM calls.  
2. **Analysis: Action Plan Utility** – For calls that trigger WM, vary maximum plan length (1, 2, 4, 6 actions) and plot accuracy saturation curve. Expect sharp rise then plateau.  
3. **Error Type Correlation** – Manually categorize validation instances by error type (LO, VD, AC, DU). Compute (a) fraction of instances where AVIC called WM per type, and (b) accuracy improvement of AVIC within each type. Expect high call rate & big gain for AC, lower for VD.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** Under what specific spatial reasoning conditions (e.g., error types like viewpoint dependence or action‑conditioned reasoning) should a world model be invoked, and how does this vary across instances?  
*Basis:* WM helps most for action‑conditioned reasoning (+57.1% gain) but policy has low recall/precision for these cases (Sec 5.4, Tab 7).  
*Why unresolved:* Policy gating is misaligned; it calls WM frequently for EgoM errors but under‑utilizes for VD errors, a major failure mode.  
*Evidence needed:* Error‑aware analysis correlating policy confidence, question embeddings, or visual features with per‑instance WM utility; ablation over policy prompts/models to identify missing cues.

**Open Question 2:** What is the minimal necessary amount of imagination (number and type of action sequences) for different spatial reasoning tasks, and does a universal threshold exist?  
*Basis:* Accuracy saturates after 1–2 imagined views (Fig 4c); AVIC uses only 0.88 views on average.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper tests fixed amounts but does not analyze whether optimal amount depends on error type or question category; per‑instance planning (1–6 actions) not broken down by task.  
*Evidence needed:* Fine‑grained analysis of accuracy vs. imagined views separately for each error type (LO, VD, AC, DU) and task category; testing longer plan lengths to identify task‑specific saturation points.

**Open Question 3:** What are the root causes of misleading or low‑quality imagination from world models, and how can they be detected or mitigated before affecting reasoning?  
*Basis:* “Case 2: Imagination Misleading” accounts for ~9% of SAT‑Real failures (Fig 1); paper notes but does not analyze why SVC fails or how to filter.  
*Why unresolved:* The verifier scores trajectories but is not used to diagnose failure modes; no investigation into whether misleading views stem from action space limits, world‑model inaccuracies, or poor policy proposals.  
*Evidence needed:* Controlled human evaluation or automated metrics (e.g., object‑level consistency) on failed imagined trajectories to classify error sources; integration of a pre‑render quality check or refined action planning.

**Open Question 4:** Can the adaptive policy model be improved to better align WM invocation with underlying reasoning needs, and what architectural or training enhancements are necessary?  
*Basis:* Policy shows low recall (43.90%) and precision (27.14%) for error cases needing WM (Tab 7). The paper states “substantial room for improvement.”  
*Why unresolved:* The policy is a simple zero‑shot MLLM with fixed prompts (Table 9). No exploration of model size, fine‑tuning on error‑prone examples, uncertainty estimates, or verifier feedback.  
*Evidence needed:* Comparison of different policy architectures (small fine‑tuned vs. large zero‑shot) and training objectives (e.g., RL with verifier reward); probing what input features correlate with correct WM invocation.

**Open Question 5:** How does adaptive visual imagination transfer to open‑world, long‑horizon embodied tasks beyond constrained benchmarks?  
*Basis:* Evaluation on R2R uses MapGPT integration and static scenes; impact statement mentions broader applicability, but no tests in dynamic environments or real‑world deployment.  
*Why unresolved:* R2R uses pre‑mapped, static scenes; unknown if AVIC handles continuous interaction, partial observability over long trajectories, or cross‑domain generalization (outdoor, aerial). The fixed action space may not scale to complex navigation.  
*Evidence needed:* Evaluation on more challenging benchmarks (e.g., RxR with longer instructions, Habitat with physics) or real robot experiments; measuring WM utility over many steps; ablations over longer action plans and diverse environment densities.

## Limitations
- Exact policy decoding parameters (temperature, top‑p) and the SVC world model’s API/action execution details are unspecified, hindering faithful reproduction.  
- Token counting and latency measurement methodology are not fully described, making efficiency comparisons difficult.  
- Generalization to new tasks or domains remains untested; results are confined to static benchmarks (SAT, MMSI, R2R).  

## Confidence
- **Adaptive test‑time scaling** (ability to drastically reduce compute while maintaining accuracy): **High**  
- **Mechanistic contributors** (selective gating, dynamic planning, trajectory verification): **Medium**  
- **Generalization to new tasks** (beyond evaluated benchmarks): **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Policy gating audit** – Human‑annotate a validation subset for true need of imagination; compute policy precision/recall and correlate with instance‑level correctness; identify consistent failure patterns (e.g., missing action‑conditioned reasoning).  
2. **Planning benefit isolation** – Compare AVIC’s adaptive plans against controlled baselines: (a) random fixed‑length action sequences, (b) oracle shortest‑path actions (using ground‑truth scene graphs), and (c) single‑step imagination, while holding world‑model calls constant to attribute gains to planning.  
3. **World model robustness test** – Degrade SVC output quality (e.g., blur, object disappearance) in a controlled manner; evaluate AVIC’s accuracy and policy gating behavior to determine if the policy adapts to unreliable imagination and to quantify performance decay thresholds.
