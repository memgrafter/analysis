# Quantifying Hallucinations in Language Language Models on Medical Textbooks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09986
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09986
- **Reference count:** 8
- **Primary result:** LLaMA-70B-Instruct hallucinated in 19.7% (95% CI 18.6–20.7%) of cases on a textbook-grounded medical QA benchmark.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel benchmark for measuring hallucination in medical LLMs using textbook-grounded question-answer pairs. The pipeline extracts passages from public-domain medical textbooks, generates diverse QA formats (including inverse variants), and validates items through medically trained annotators. Evaluating multiple models, the study finds that hallucination rates decrease with model scale but increase for inverse and list-type questions. Despite scaling, all models hallucinate substantially, and higher hallucination correlates with lower clinician-rated usefulness. The results indicate current LLMs are unfit for unsupervised clinical deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors built an end-to-end pipeline to create a gold-standard medical QA benchmark. First, they filtered public-domain textbook passages for quality using heuristics like entity density and semantic coherence. Next, they prompted LLaMA-70B-Instruct to generate QA pairs across seven formats (including true/false, list, multiple-choice, short answer, multi-hop, and two inverse types). Generated pairs underwent structured verification by clinicians in Portal 1, who rated relevance, flagged issues, and adjudicated disputes to establish "ideal" QA pairs. The verified benchmark was then used to evaluate multiple LLMs zero-shot. In Portal 2, clinicians blindly ranked anonymized model outputs and labeled each as Good/Okay/Bad based on factual correctness and clinical utility. Hallucination was defined as any content not supported by the source passage. Inter-annotator agreement was measured via quadratic-weighted Cohen's κ and Kendall's τb.

## Key Results
- LLaMA-70B-Instruct hallucinated in 19.7% (95% CI 18.6–20.7%) of cases, with plausibility near 98.8%.
- Hallucination rates decreased with model scale: from 27.1% (1B) to 9.3% (70B+).
- Inverse QA formats increased hallucinations by 6–9% compared to forward formats.
- Clinician usefulness scores negatively correlated with hallucination rates (ρ = −0.71, p = 0.058).
- High annotator agreement was achieved (quadratic weighted κ = 0.92 in Experiment 1; κ = 0.57–0.61 for Likert labels in Experiment 2).
- All evaluated models hallucinated; none suitable for unsupervised clinical use.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** A textbook-grounded, automatically generated QA benchmark can isolate and measure hallucination prevalence in medical LLMs.  
**Mechanism:** The pipeline extracts dense, boilerplate-free paragraphs from public-domain medical textbooks, uses an LLM (LLaMA-70B-Instruct) to generate diverse QA pairs (including inverse formats), and subjects the pairs to structured verification by medically trained annotators. Hallucination is scored as any model output content not supported by the source passage.  
**Core assumption:** Model outputs can be reliably judged against a fixed, authoritative source passage for faithfulness, and human annotators can consistently identify unsupported content.  
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract describes the pipeline; Section 3.3 details corpus filtering heuristics and QA templates; neighboring papers confirm the active area but note the novel benchmark creation and hallucination-focused annotation protocol.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Clinician annotation with structured quality tags can yield high agreement on hallucination severity and overall clinical usefulness in LLM responses.  
**Mechanism:** Clinicians review model outputs blind to model identity, rank them, and label each as Good/Okay/Bad based on factual correctness and clinical utility. Annotator agreement is measured via quadratic-weighted Cohen's κ for labels and Kendall's τb for rankings, with dual verification and adjudication.  
**Core assumption:** Clinicians share a stable, interpretable standard for "useful" and "factually correct" medical text that can be operationalized in a labeling task.  
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract reports high annotator agreement (κ=0.92); Section 4 details the annotation portals, dual verification, and adjudication process; neighboring work (e.g., MedHalu) does not report comparable clinician agreement metrics.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Hallucination rate is a negative predictor of clinician-rated usefulness, and hallucination propensity scales with model size but is exacerbated by certain QA formats (inverse, list).  
**Mechanism:** By applying the same benchmark to models of varying scale (1B to 70B+ parameters), the study correlates computed hallucination rates with clinician-assigned usefulness scores. Format-specific effects are analyzed by comparing inverse/non-inverse and list/short-answer item performance.  
**Core assumption:** The benchmark's QA format diversity reliably stresses models to reveal hallucination tendencies correlated with real-world clinical utility.  
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract reports the correlation (ρ=−0.71), scale trend, and inverse effect; Section 5.2 provides examples; neighboring work confirms scaling generally reduces errors but this paper provides specific medical-textbook-grounded quantification and the inverse QA stress-test finding.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Textbook-Grounded Evaluation**  
  **Why needed here:** The paper's core innovation is moving beyond contested exam-style benchmarks to a setting where every answer must be traceable to a fixed source paragraph, making "hallucination" a clear, text-faithfulness concept.  
  **Quick check question:** Why is using a single, fixed source passage per QA pair critical for measuring hallucination, and how does this differ from evaluating on multiple-choice medical exams?

- **Concept: Hallucination Taxonomy in Medical Context**  
  **Why needed here:** The paper adopts a broad definition (plausible yet nonfactual, false, irrelevant, unfaithful). Understanding the distinction between *intrinsic* (contradicts source) and *extrinsic* (can't be verified from source) hallucinations from cited surveys is key to interpreting their annotation scheme.  
  **Quick check question:** How does the paper's definition of hallucination as "content not supported by the source paragraph" map onto the intrinsic/extrinsic hallucination taxonomy?

- **Concept: Human-in-the-Loop Benchmark Validation**  
  **Why needed here:** The benchmark's validity rests entirely on clinician annotation quality and agreement. The process of dual annotation, adjudication, and measuring κ/τ is the engine that turns raw model outputs into a trustworthy dataset.  
  **Quick check question:** What operational steps (e.g., blinding, duplication, adjudication) are necessary to turn subjective clinical judgment into a reliable benchmark label, and what metrics signal success or failure?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Corpus Processor -> QA Synthesizer -> Verification Portal 1 (Benchmark Build) -> Evaluation Engine -> Verification Portal 2 (Preference Study) -> Metrics Calculator.
- **Critical path:** Corpus Processor → QA Synthesizer → Portal 1 → Evaluation Engine → Portal 2 → Metrics. Portal 1 output (verified benchmark) is the central artifact; Portal 2 inputs come from Evaluation Engine outputs.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Cost vs. Coverage:** Human verification costs ~$3.33/QA; full coverage infeasible, so ~45% double-annotation is used for reliability estimates.  
  - **Automation vs. Ground Truth:** Fully automatic generation is cheap but yields low acceptance (RealMedQA: 19%); this pipeline uses LLM generation but mandates human verification, trading scale for gold-standard data.  
  - **Format Diversity:** Adding inverse/list QA types increases hallucination rates (better stress-test) but also increases annotator disputes (list & MC have high dispute counts).
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Low IAA:** Quadratic-weighted κ < 0.8 for validity judgments in Portal 1 signals ambiguous instructions or poor training.  
  - **Drop in Ideal-Pair Yield:** If <80% of QA pairs achieve max GP relevance *and* zero disputes, corpus filtering or generation is producing low-quality items.  
  - **Inverse QA Monoculture:** If only inverse questions show hallucination, models may have a specific negation weakness, but the benchmark may not gauge general medical knowledge fidelity.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Pipeline Sanity Check:** Generate 100 QA pairs from a small, known textbook chapter. Have 2 clinicians annotate all in Portal 1. Target: >90% κ for validity, >85% ideal-pair yield. Fail if κ < 0.7.  
  2. **Baseline Replication:** Run LLaMA-70B-Instruct on the full benchmark via Evaluation Engine. Compare hallucination rate to paper's 19.7% (95% CI 18.6–20.7%). Fail if rate is outside CI or if plausibility ≠ ~98.8%.  
  3. **Cross-Model Stress Test:** Select 3 models (e.g., 1B, 8B, 70B). Run through full pipeline, collect Portal 2 rankings/Likerts. Verify: (a) hallucination rate decreases with scale, (b) inverse QA shows +6–9% hallucination vs. forward, (c) Kendall's τb for rankings > 0.6 among prolific annotators.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Why do inverse and list-type QA formats significantly increase hallucination rates compared to forward formats, despite model scaling?  
  **Basis:** The paper observes that inverse prompts increased hallucinations by 6–9% and list questions were particularly challenging, but does not explain the underlying mechanism.  
  **Why unresolved:** Understanding this could inform benchmark design and model training to target these failure modes and improve reliability in critical scenarios.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments isolating linguistic features of inverse/list questions, or analysis of model attention and reasoning pathways on such prompts across scales.

- **Question:** Can automated hallucination detection methods achieve reliability comparable to expert clinicians to reduce the prohibitive cost of verification?  
  **Basis:** The paper states that human verification dominates costs and that current LLMs are unfit for unsupervised deployment due to hallucinations, implying a need for automated solutions.  
  **Why unresolved:** Without reliable automation, high-risk domains like medicine cannot safely deploy LLMs at scale, as verification by clinicians is not scalable.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and validation of automated metrics or models that correlate highly (e.g., κ > 0.9) with expert judgments on diverse, challenging medical QA tasks, including inverse and list formats.

- **Question:** How do hallucination rates on textbook-grounded QA generalize to real-world clinical text, such as electronic health records or recent medical literature?  
  **Basis:** The study uses public-domain medical textbooks as the sole knowledge source, which may not represent the complexity, noise, and temporal dynamics of live clinical documents.  
  **Why unresolved:** Textbook content is static and curated, whereas clinical settings involve dynamic, patient-specific, and potentially outdated or conflicting information.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication of the benchmark using real clinical notes or up-to-date medical literature, comparing hallucination patterns and severity to those found with textbooks.

- **Question:** What explains the disconnect between high plausibility scores (98.8%) and substantial hallucination rates (19.7%), indicating that fluent, professional responses can still be factually unsupported?  
  **Basis:** The paper explicitly reports that nearly all responses received maximal plausibility yet 19.7% were hallucinated, showing no significant correlation between plausibility and hallucination.  
  **Why unresolved:** This gap challenges the reliance on plausibility or fluency as proxies for safety and suggests current evaluation methods miss subtle factual errors in medically coherent text.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Fine-grained linguistic and semantic analysis of hallucinated vs. non-hallucinated responses to identify markers (e.g., hedging language, citation patterns, entity consistency) that distinguish them beyond surface plausibility.

## Limitations
- **Benchmark access/representativeness:** Full dataset gated; sample may not capture full QA type/hallucination distribution (HIGH impact on replication).
- **Corpus & filtering details:** Exact textbook sources & heuristic thresholds (e.g., semantic entropy) unspecified; proxy corpus may yield different item difficulty/plausibility.
- **Internal model specs:** Two evaluated models anonymized, preventing exact scale/usefulness replication.
- **Generalizability:** Findings may not transfer to non-textbook medical QA or other clinical sub-domains.

## Confidence
- **Benchmark validity via high IAA:** High (κ=0.92 in Experiment 1, robust dual-annotation protocol).
- **Scale-hallucination correlation:** Medium (observed trend clear but limited model diversity; ρ=−0.71, p=0.058).
- **Inverse QA stress effect:** Medium-Low (6–9% increase noted, but format-specific; may not generalize).
- **Clinical unsuitability conclusion:** High (all models hallucinated; conclusion robust to minor variations).

## Next Checks
1. **Corpus robustness test:** Build parallel benchmark from different public medical textbooks (e.g., OpenStax); compare hallucination rates & plausibility distributions to reported values.
2. **Model generalization check:** Evaluate hallucination scale trend on 3–4 additional open-source models (e.g., Meditron, BioMistral) not in original study; confirm monotonic decrease.
3. **Annotation stability audit:** Recruit new clinician cohort to annotate 20% of benchmark items blind; compute quadratic-weighted κ against original labels; accept if κ ≥ 0.85.
