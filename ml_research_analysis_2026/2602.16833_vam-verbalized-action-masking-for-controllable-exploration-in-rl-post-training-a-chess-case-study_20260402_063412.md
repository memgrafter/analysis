# VAM: Verbalized Action Masking for Controllable Exploration in RL Post-Training -- A Chess Case Study

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16833
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16833
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** Verbalized Action Masking (VAM) with iterative action-space pruning significantly improves both puzzle accuracy and full-game play quality in chess for RL post-trained LLMs, outperforming strong GRPO baselines.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement Learning (RL) post-training of large language models (LLMs) on tasks with large action spaces often suffers from premature convergence to repetitive behaviors due to poor exploration. This paper introduces Verbalized Action Masking (VAM) for the domain of chess. VAM works by (1) verbalizing the set of legal moves (the admissible action set) directly into the prompt and (2) applying a deterministic penalty from a verifier (Stockfish) for any output not in that set. To further encourage exploration, it employs iterative action-space pruning: across multiple samples for the same position, previously valid actions are removed from the candidate list, forcing the model to propose distinct moves. Evaluated on Qwen2.5-3B and -7B models under two training regimes (fixed dataset and engine-play), VAM consistently improves one-step puzzle accuracy (pass@1) and reduces average centipawn loss (ACPL) in full games compared to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) baselines using various engine-derived rewards. The expected-score reward design proved strongest among GRPO variants, but VAM delivered gains regardless.

## Method Summary
The core method, Verbalized Action Masking (VAM), augments the standard RL post-training framework (specifically GRPO) with two exploration-promoting mechanisms. First, the prompt for each state is extended to include a list of all legal actions (e.g., "Legal moves: e2e4, g1f3, ..."). During training, if the model's sampled output does not match any item in this verbalized list, a deterministic penalty (a fixed negative value) is applied via the reward function. This turns the binary problem of action validity into a dense, verifiable learning signal. Second, to combat mode collapse within groups of sampled actions for the same state, an iterative pruning procedure is used: across multiple generation steps for a position, any action that has already been sampled and verified as legal is removed from the verbalized list for subsequent samples in that same "round." This forces the model to explore a broader range of valid actions within its sample group. The method is agnostic to the specific RL algorithm but is coupled with GRPO in the evaluations.

## Key Results
- VAM substantially improves **one-step puzzle accuracy (pass@1)** over GRPO baselines with various engine-derived rewards (expected score, win rate, ranking) for both 3B and 7B model scales.
- VAM reduces **full-game Average Centipawn Loss (ACPL)** against Stockfish, demonstrating better practical play quality.
- The **3B model trained with VAM surpasses the 7B model trained with standard GRPO** on puzzle accuracy, highlighting VAM's effectiveness in scaling.
- **Expected-score reward** (probability-weighted outcome from engine WDL probabilities) is the strongest GRPO baseline, but VAM provides consistent gains on top of it.
- Gains are observed under both **fixed-dataset training** (higher puzzle accuracy) and **on-policy engine-play position generation** (lower full-game ACPL).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
VAM addresses the core failure mode of RL post-training in large-discrete spaces: the policy quickly collapses to a small, high-reward subset of actions because sparse, scalar rewards provide no gradient for *why* an invalid action failed. By verbalizing the admissible set, VAM makes the validity of every candidate action explicit in the context. The deterministic verifier penalty converts "output is illegal" into a clear, learnable signal, separating the problem of "learning to propose only legal moves" from "learning which legal move is best." This prevents the policy from wasting capacity on modeling invalid output patterns. The iterative pruning mechanism then tackles *exploration within the legal set*. By dynamically shrinking the candidate list after each valid sample, it forces the model to consider a wider variety of legal moves within its group, reducing the dominance of a single preferred action and promoting a more diverse and robust policy over the training course.

## Architecture Onboarding
Here is a conceptual map of the VAM-enhanced training system.

**Component Map (Plain Text):**
Environment (Chess Position) -> Action Mask Generator (Legal Move Calculator) -> Prompt Builder (State + Verbalized Legal Moves) -> LLM Policy (Qwen2.5 GRPO) -> Sampled Actions -> Verifier (Stockfish: Validity & Evaluation) -> Reward Shaping (Validity Penalty + Game Outcome/Score Reward) -> GRPO Update -> LLM Policy

**Critical Path:** For a given training batch: a chess position is generated (from dataset or self-play) -> its list of legal moves is computed -> this list is formatted into a string and appended to the prompt -> the policy (LLM) generates multiple candidate moves from this prompt -> each move is sent to the verifier (Stockfish) to check legality and/or compute a game-based reward (e.g., expected score) -> moves deemed illegal incur a fixed penalty -> for moves within the same generation group for that state, any legal move that has been sampled is marked and *removed from the verbalized list* for subsequent samples in that group's lifecycle -> the final per-action rewards are grouped and normalized (GRPO) to compute policy gradient updates.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Verifier Dependency vs. Generality:** The method's strength relies on a fast, deterministic verifier. This is a double-edged sword: it provides a clean signal but limits application to domains lacking such an oracle.
- **Prompt Length vs. Action Space Size:** Verbalizing all legal moves increases context length. For chess (~30-50 moves), this is manageable. For vastly larger action spaces, this could become inefficient or exceed context windows.
- **Computational Cost of Pruning:** The iterative pruning requires re-running generation for the same state multiple times with an updated mask, increasing inference cost per training step compared to single-sample baselines.

**Failure Signatures:**
- **High Repetition Despite Masking:** If the model continues to output the same legal move repeatedly across pruned rounds, the pruning mechanism is not successfully encouraging diversity—potentially due to insufficient penalty weight or a flawed reward signal that overly favors one move.
- **Verifier Bottleneck/Errors:** If the deterministic verifier is slow or produces inconsistent legality judgments (e.g., due to a bug), training stability collapses as the validity signal becomes noisy.
- **Prompt Format Sensitivity:** Poor formatting of the verbalized legal moves (e.g., unclear delimiters) could cause the model to fail to parse the mask, rendering it ineffective and leading to high invalid action rates.

**First 3 Experiments to Onboard:**
1. **Ablation: Masking vs. Pruning:** Train with GRPO + VAM but disable the iterative pruning (only use the initial full legal move list). Compare diversity (number of unique legal moves sampled per state) and final ACPL/puzzle accuracy against the full VAM setup to isolate the contribution of pruning.
2. **Hyperparameter Sweep - Penalty Value:** Vary the out-of-mask penalty (e.g., -1.0, -10.0, -100.0) while keeping other VAM components fixed. Monitor the rate of illegal actions early in training and the final performance to find a robust range.
3. **Cross-Domain Dry Run (Non-Verifiable Actions):** Attempt to apply the same VAM procedure to a text-based environment with a *learned* classifier as a verifier (instead of a deterministic engine). Compare the stability of training and final performance to the chess setup to diagnose the sensitivity to verifier quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can VAM be adapted to domains with non-enumerable action spaces or without deterministic verifiers?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Many structured decision problems do not offer these properties, so applying VAM in domains with very large action spaces or without strong deterministic evaluators will require dynamic candidate generation and approximate verifiers." (Limitations and future work)
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates VAM in chess, where legal moves are finite and engine evaluations are exact. The suggested mechanisms (dynamic candidate generation, approximate verifiers) are not implemented or tested, leaving their feasibility and design unproven.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Deploy VAM in a new domain (e.g., open-vocabulary text generation) where actions are proposed by a secondary policy or retrieval system and rewards are computed by a learned judge. Compare exploration diversity and final performance against baselines without masking.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does the expected-score reward derived from engine WDL probabilities outperform other engine-based rewards (win-rate, rank-based) in GRPO training for chess?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Figure 4 (left) shows expected-score achieving the highest puzzle pass@1, but the paper offers no analysis of this performance gap.
- **Why unresolved:** Reward design is crucial for RL performance, but without understanding why expected-score works best, we cannot generalize to other reward structures or domains. The authors do not hypothesize about the underlying cause.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablate properties of the reward signals (e.g., dynamic range, discrimination between moves) and correlate with GRPO learning stability. Alternatively, equalize these properties across rewards to isolate whether expected-score's specific formulation provides a better learning gradient.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What factors cause the trade-off between fixed-dataset training (higher puzzle accuracy) and engine-play position generation (lower full-game ACPL), and can a hybrid data pipeline achieve both?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Fixed-dataset training yields stronger puzzle accuracy, while engine-play position generation yields better full-game ACPL" (RQ3 results and Figure 6). The authors also note differences in response length and KL loss across regimes.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not investigate the root cause (e.g., position distribution differences, on-policy vs. off-policy effects), so we cannot predict which regime to use for a given objective or how to combine them.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze the state distributions (e.g., game phase, tactical complexity) under both regimes and their correlation with learning outcomes. Experiment with mixed-data curricula—progressively increasing engine-play data—to see if both puzzle accuracy and ACPL can be improved simultaneously.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive is VAM to its hyperparameters (out-of-mask penalty, max pruning rounds Rmax, group size G), and what principles should guide their selection for new tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper uses fixed values (e.g., penalty = 0, G = 8, Rmax = 4) and does not report any sensitivity analysis or ablations for these knobs.
- **Why unresolved:** These parameters control the strength of the exploration constraint and the computational budget per state. Without guidance, practitioners may set them sub-optimally when scaling to different action space sizes or domains.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform systematic sweeps over these parameters on chess puzzles and full-game play, plotting metrics like intra-group action diversity, convergence speed, and final performance. Derive scaling rules (e.g., Rmax ∝ log|A(s)|) or robustness thresholds.

## Limitations
- **Domain Specificity:** VAM's reliance on a deterministic verifier (Stockfish) and a finite, enumerable action set makes its direct applicability to open-ended domains (e.g., creative writing) uncertain.
- **Computational Cost:** The iterative pruning mechanism increases the number of forward passes per training state, an overhead not quantified in the paper and may not scale to very large action spaces or long-horizon tasks.
- **Evaluation Rigor:** Experimental results lack statistical reporting (e.g., means and standard deviations over multiple random seeds), making it difficult to assess the robustness and significance of the observed improvements.

## Confidence
- **Performance superiority over GRPO on chess:** Medium (Empirical support on two model sizes, but limited to one domain and without statistical tests).
- **Controllable exploration mechanism (masking + pruning) prevents collapse:** Medium (The design is plausible and results are positive, but a direct ablation isolating each component's contribution is missing).
- **Robustness across model scales (3B->7B) and training regimes (fixed vs. engine-play):** Medium (Tested on two scales and two regimes, but constitutes a small configuration sample).
- **Generalization to other domains (e.g., Go, text games):** Low (Evaluated only on chess; the core assumption of a deterministic, fast verifier may not hold elsewhere).
