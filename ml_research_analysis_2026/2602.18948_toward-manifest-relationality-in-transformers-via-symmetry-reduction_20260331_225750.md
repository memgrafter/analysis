# Toward Manifest Relationality in Transformers via Symmetry Reduction
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.18948
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18948
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Proposes a symmetry reduction framework for transformers using invariant relational representations (Gram matrices for model space, composites for head space) to eliminate parameter redundancy and enable optimization on quotient spaces.

## Executive Summary
Transformer architectures exhibit internal redundancy due to continuous symmetries in both the representation space (model space) and the linear layers of attention heads. These redundancies create flat directions in the loss landscape and introduce conserved quantities that could impede optimization. This paper identifies the exact continuous symmetries: global O(d) rotations of hidden states, O(dₕ) simultaneous orthogonal transformations on query and key matrices, and GL(dₕ, ℝ) affine transformations on value-output pairs. It then develops a geometric framework that reformulates the forward pass and optimization in terms of invariant composites—the Gram matrix XX⊤ for model space, G_QK = W_Q⊤W_K for query-key, and G_VO = W_O W_V for value-output. Three schemes are proposed to ensure learning occurs only along functionally relevant directions: invariant reparameterization directly on these composites, gradient projection onto the transverse subspace of symmetry orbits, and dressing maps that periodically remove accumulated gauge motion. The approach theoretically eliminates unnecessary degrees of freedom and yields a cleaner optimization dynamic. The work is purely theoretical; empirical validation is reserved for future research.

## Method Summary
The method eliminates parameter redundancy by identifying continuous symmetries in transformer blocks and expressing the model in terms of invariants. For model-space O(d) symmetry, token representations are replaced by the Gram matrix G = XX⊤, and attention scores become a function of entries of G, preserving relational structure. For attention heads, the relevant parameters are the composites G_QK = W_Q⊤W_K (governing scores) and G_VO = W_O W_V (governing value-output). The individual weight matrices are either omitted (invariant reparameterization) or updated with gradient projection to remain orthogonal to symmetry directions. Optimization thus operates on the reduced quotient space Θ/G. Practical implementations include: (i) optimizing the composites directly under constraints (e.g., rank(G_VO) ≤ dₕ via low‑rank factorization), (ii) projected gradient descent where updates are orthogonalized against the orbit’s tangent space, or (iii) dressing maps that periodically return parameters to a chosen gauge. A minimal reproduction builds a transformer block without LayerNorm or biases (to preserve exact symmetries), implements the reduced forward pass, and compares convergence with standard training.

## Key Results
- **Identified symmetries**: O(d) global rotations of hidden states; O(dₕ) simultaneous orthogonal transformations on W_Q and W_K; GL(dₕ,ℝ) affine transformations on (W_V, W_O) preserving the product.
- **Invariant composites**: Gram matrix G = XX⊤ for model space; G_QK = W_Q⊤W_K for query‑key; G_VO = W_O W_V for value‑output (subject to rank ≤ dₕ).
- **Optimization schemes**: Three ways to operate on reduced spaces: (1) reparameterize directly on invariants, (2) project gradients onto the transverse subspace to nullify orbit‑direction updates, (3) use dressing maps to periodically remove accumulated gauge motion. This framework removes flat directions and the associated conserved quantities (Noether charges).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Model-space O(d) symmetry and Gram reduction
Idealized transformer blocks (without LayerNorm, biases, or nonlinear MLPs) are invariant under global rotations of the hidden dimension: X → XU⊤ for any U ∈ O(d). This renders individual token vector coordinates redundant. By replacing X with the Gram matrix G = XX⊤, which is O(d)-invariant, the relational structure among tokens is retained. Attention scores become a function f(G_{ij}, G_{ii}, G_{jj}), and updates can be defined equivariantly on vectors or invariantly on G. If G loses rank (e.g., token vectors become linearly dependent), the SVD‑based dressing becomes degenerate, revealing a residual symmetry of the second kind.

### Mechanism 2: Head-space symmetries and invariant composites
The attention score between tokens i and j is q_i^⊤k_j with q_i = W_Qx_i, k_j = W_Kx_j; this equals x_i^⊤G_QKx_j where G_QK = W_Q^⊤W_K. Thus the functional effect of (W_Q, W_K) depends only on the composite G_QK. The matrices W_Q and W_K are individually redundant because they can be transformed simultaneously by any orthogonal matrix R ∈ O(dₕ): (W_Q, W_K) → (RW_Q, RW_K) leaves G_QK unchanged (since R^⊤R = I). Similarly, the value‑output contribution is G_VOx_i with G_VO = W_O W_V. The pair (W_V, W_O) admits a GL(dₕ, ℝ) symmetry: (W_V, W_O) → (S^{-1}W_V, W_O S) for any S ∈ GL(dₕ, ℝ) preserves G_VO. Both composites must satisfy rank ≤ dₕ to be factorable; this constraint must be respected during optimization.

### Mechanism 3: Optimization on reduced quotient spaces
Standard gradient descent updates parameters along all gradient directions, including those tangential to symmetry orbits, which correspond to reparameterizations that do not change the model’s function. To avoid this waste, optimization is reformulated on the quotient space Θ/G. Three practical schemes achieve this: (i) invariant reparameterization: express the model solely in terms of the invariant composites (e.g., use G_QK, G_VO as direct parameters); (ii) projected gradient descent: after a standard update, project the parameter point onto the transverse subspace of the orbit (i.e., remove components along the infinitesimal generators of the group action); (iii) dressing maps: intermittently remove accumulated motion along orbits by moving to a chosen gauge (e.g., via SVD‑based polar decompositions). These approaches eliminate flat directions and the associated conserved quantities (Noether charges) that would otherwise appear in idealized Hamiltonian learning dynamics.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Relational Representation Module -> Invariant Attention Scorer -> Equivariant/Invariant Propagation -> Head Symmetry Reduction -> Symmetry‑Reduced Optimizer

**Critical path**  
1. Identify the symmetry group for the component being targeted (O(d) for model space; O(dₕ) or GL(dₕ, ℝ) for a specific head sector).  
2. Choose the invariant composite (e.g., G = XX^⊤, G_QK, G_VO).  
3. Re‑implement the forward pass to depend *only* on the chosen invariant(s). For model space, this means attention scores from G; for heads, the linear contribution from G_QK or G_VO.  
4. Decide on a parameterization for the invariant that respects any necessary constraints (e.g., low‑rank for G_VO).  
5. Implement the backward pass, ensuring gradients flow correctly through the composite operations (e.g., for G_VO = AB, use product rule).  
6. (Optional) Implement a gradient projection step if maintaining a redundant parameterization for efficiency but wanting to nullify tangent components.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Expressivity vs. Constraint**: Enforcing rank(G_VO) ≤ dₕ strictly via low‑rank factorization (AB) restricts the model class compared to an unconstrained d×d matrix. Using an unconstrained G_VO with projection after each step approximates the constraint but may introduce numerical drift or projection error.  
- **Computational Cost**: Gram matrix computation is O(n²d). Storing and manipulating d×d composites (G_QK, G_VO) for many heads adds O(H d²) parameters vs. O(H dₕ d). This is favorable only if dₕ is not much smaller than d.  
- **Compatibility with Standard Components**: LayerNorm and biases break O(d) symmetry. The relational Gram‑based attention must be applied *before* LayerNorm, or LayerNorm must be reformulated in a relational way (non‑trivial). The approach is thus most natural for a "clean" attention submodule analysis.  
- **Residual Symmetry**: Even after reduction, residual symmetries (of the second kind) remain, e.g., in the SVD dressing or the GL(dₕ) action on a factorized (A, B) pair. These do not cause optimization flatness but require conventions for a unique representative.

**Failure signatures**  
- **Training Divergence/Instability**: If the invariant parameterization (G_QK, G_VO) is not updated correctly (e.g., gradients not flowing through the composite construction), or if the rank constraint is violated without correction, training may become unstable.  
- **Performance Drop vs. Baseline**: A significant drop in validation accuracy suggests the reduced parameter space may be *too* constrained, possibly because the baseline model relied on symmetry‑breaking components (LayerNorm, biases) that are not properly accounted for in the reduced formulation.  
- **Rank Collapse**: If G = XX^⊤ becomes low‑rank (e.g., all token vectors become parallel), the relational information is lost, and the model may struggle to represent diverse token relationships.  
- **No Improvement in Optimization**: If projected gradient or invariant training does not lead to faster convergence or lower loss, it may indicate that in practice, with modern optimizers (AdamW) and architectural details (LayerNorm), the symmetry‑induced degeneracies are already navigated effectively, and the overhead of reduction isn’t worth it.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Isolated Gram Attention**: Replace the score computation in a single attention head of a small transformer (e.g., on a synthetic sequence matching task) with the Gram‑based f(G_{ij}, G_{ii}, G_{jj}). Train with and without this change. Compare final accuracy, training stability, and the spectrum of W_Q^⊤ W_K vs. the learned f’s effective kernel. *Hypothesis*: Task performance should be similar if the task is relational, but optimization may be more stable if symmetry was causing flat directions.  
2. **Invariant Composite Optimization (Value‑Output)**: For a single‑head model, replace (W_V, W_O) with a low‑rank parameterization G_VO = AB (with A∈ℝ^{d×dₕ}, B∈ℝ^{dₕ×d}). Train the model using gradients computed via AB. Compare convergence speed and final loss to a baseline where (W_V, W_O) are trained freely. Monitor the Frobenius norm of (W_V, W_O) differences along orbits. *Hypothesis*: The AB model should converge to a similar minimum but may do so without lingering motion along GL(dₕ) orbits.  
3. **Symmetry Breaking Perturbation Test**: Train a standard transformer with full (W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O) and also a version where, after every update, the (W_Q, W_K) pair is explicitly rotated to a canonical form (e.g., via SVD of W_Q). This "gauge fixing" should not change the model function but might affect optimization trajectories. Compare to the projected gradient approach where updates are made orthogonal to the O(dₕ) symmetry generators (estimated via finite differences). *Hypothesis*: Both interventions should yield similar trajectories if the projection is accurate, confirming the tangent directions identified.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Empirical impact of symmetry reduction**  
   *Question*: What is the empirical impact of symmetry‑reduced training on convergence, generalization, and optimization landscape compared to standard transformer training?  
   *Basis in paper*: The conclusions state that empirical evaluation is future work, proposing metrics such as training loss curves, sensitivity to initialization, effective rank of internal matrices, and qualitative attention patterns.  
   *Why unresolved*: The paper presents only a theoretical framework without experiments; it is unknown whether eliminating redundant directions improves optimization efficiency or model performance in practice.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Systematic benchmarks comparing standard vs. symmetry‑reduced transformers across tasks, measuring convergence speed, final accuracy, loss curvature (e.g., sharpness), and robustness to random seeds.

2. **Effect of common architectural components on symmetries**  
   *Question*: How do common architectural components (e.g., LayerNorm, biases) affect the continuous symmetries (O(d), O(dₕ), GL(dₕ,ℝ)) assumed in the idealized model, and does symmetry reduction remain beneficial under these symmetry‑breaking perturbations?  
   *Basis in paper*: Section III A 1 acknowledges that LayerNorm and biases restrict the O(d) symmetry and treats them as symmetry‑breaking perturbations, but does not quantify their impact.  
   *Why unresolved*: The analysis assumes an idealized block; real transformers include these components, which may partially or fully break the symmetries, potentially invalidating the reduction or altering its benefits.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Empirical measurement of residual symmetry in real models (e.g., via orbit metrics) and evaluation of symmetry‑reduced training’s effectiveness when applied to architectures with LayerNorm/biases.

3. **Enforcing rank constraints**  
   *Question*: How can the rank constraints (rank(G_{QK}) ≤ dₕ, rank(G_{VO}) ≤ dₕ) on the invariant composites be effectively enforced during training without compromising model expressivity or numerical stability?  
   *Basis in paper*: Section V and Appendix A discuss that G_{QK} and G_{VO} must satisfy rank ≤ dₕ, proposing low‑rank parameterization or projection, but note potential issues with expressivity and optimization.  
   *Why unresolved*: Unrestricted updates to G matrices may leave the representable set; enforcing the constraint could bias optimization or limit what the model can learn, especially if true functions require higher effective rank.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Comparative studies of constraint enforcement methods (e.g., low‑rank factorization vs. SVD projection) on learning dynamics, final rank of learned composites, and task performance, possibly with diagnostics for constraint violations.

4. **Comparative analysis of reduction schemes**  
   *Question*: Among the three proposed symmetry‑reduced optimization schemes (invariant reparameterization, gradient projection, dressing maps), which is most scalable, stable, and computationally efficient for large‑scale transformer training?  
   *Basis in paper*: Section V C outlines all three schemes as practical implementations but provides no comparison or empirical analysis of their trade‑offs.  
   *Why unresolved*: Each scheme has different computational characteristics (e.g., storage, projection cost, forward‑pass changes), but their relative efficiency and impact on convergence for realistic model sizes and sequence lengths are unknown.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Scalability benchmarks measuring memory usage, training time per step, and convergence across model scales (e.g., varying d, dₕ, n) for each scheme, including stability under different optimizers (SGD, Adam).

## Limitations
- The framework assumes idealized transformer blocks without LayerNorm, biases, or nonlinear MLPs; real‑world applicability is uncertain because these components break the exact symmetries.
- Enforcing rank constraints on the invariant composites (rank ≤ dₕ) may restrict model expressivity, particularly when head dimension dₕ is small or higher‑rank interactions are required.
- The method incurs significant computational overhead: Gram matrices require O(n²d) operations, and handling d×d composite matrices for each head adds O(H d²) parameters, which could be prohibitive for large hidden dimensions.
