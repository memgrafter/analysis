# Tree of Preferences for Diversified Recommendation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.02386
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02386
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ToP-Rec outperforms nine competitive baselines in both diversity (Category-Entropy@k) and relevance (Recall@k), achieving the best diversity-relevance trade-off with reasonable inference latency.

## Executive Summary
Diversified recommendation aims to provide a varied set of items while maintaining relevance. However, recommendation data suffers from exposure and selection bias, which obscures users' underexplored preferences and leads to homogeneous results. This paper introduces ToP-Rec, a method that leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to construct a hierarchical Tree of Preferences (ToP) that systematically reasons over a user's historical interactions to uncover latent interests. From this tree, synthetic interactions are generated for selected users and used to augment the training data, debiasing the learning process. An efficiency mechanism based on gradient alignment dynamically chooses influential users for augmentation, keeping computational costs manageable. Evaluated on Twitter, Weibo, and Amazon datasets, ToP-Rec surpasses nine strong baselines on both diversity and relevance metrics, delivering the best trade-off without incurring prohibitive latency.

## Method Summary
ToP-Rec operates in two phases. **Offline**, it constructs a global Tree of Preferences (ToP) by prompting an LLM on a sampled, text‑rich subset of items; the tree structures preferences from coarse to fine. Every item in the catalog is then assigned to a leaf node via LLM prompting, with load‑balancing refinement to ensure reasonable distribution.

**Online**, during recommender training (using LightGCN with BPR loss), the method periodically selects a small set of "influential" users by computing each user's k‑step influence (gradient alignment with the parameter update trajectory). For each selected user, the LLM traverses the global ToP breadth‑first to identify a set of leaf nodes representing both observed and inferred latent preferences. Items belonging to those leaves are scored by a combination of semantic relevance (cosine similarity of user and item texts) and diversity (inverse frequency of the leaf in the user's history). The top‑scoring items are added as synthetic positives to the user's interaction history. The augmented data is then used to continue training. This cycle repeats until convergence, offering a scalable way to inject debiased, diverse signals into the model.

## Key Results
- ToP-Rec achieves superior performance on both **Recall@k** (relevance) and **Category‑Entropy@k** (diversity) compared to nine competitive baselines across three datasets.
- It yields the **best diversity‑relevance trade‑off**, demonstrating that diversity gains do not come at a severe cost to relevance.
- The approach maintains **reasonable inference latency** and scales efficiently via the dynamic user selection mechanism.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Hierarchical Preference Reasoning via ToP**  
The core innovation is a globally shared Tree of Preferences, built by an LLM from item attributes. This tree provides a structured, multi‑level space where each leaf represents a fine‑grained preference. For a given user, the LLM performs a breadth‑first traversal, selecting paths that best explain the user's interaction history and explicitly checking for unobserved preferences. This systematic reasoning goes beyond simple similarity matching, allowing the model to infer latent interests that are under‑represented due to data bias. The paper details the construction prompt and BFS algorithm in Section 3.2 and assumes LLMs can reliably produce a semantically coherent hierarchy and map user behavior onto it. A failure condition would be an incoherent tree (e.g., flat, inconsistent branching, or non‑mutually exclusive leaves) that yields meaningless user paths.

**Mechanism 2: Synthetic Data Augmentation for Debiasing**  
From the user's inferred leaf nodes, the method scores candidate items by combining relevance (semantic alignment) and diversity (inverse historical frequency of the leaf). High‑scoring items are treated as synthetic positive interactions and added to the user's history. This creates a debiased training set that "fills in" the missing parts of the user's preference distribution caused by exposure/selection bias. The approach directly intervenes at the data level rather than only adjusting the model. The scoring function (Equation in Section 3.3) balances the two objectives; the diversity term is crucial for exploring underexplored areas. Potential failure modes include noisy item‑to‑leaf assignments or an overly strong diversity term that introduces irrelevant items.

**Mechanism 3: Gradient‑Aligned Influential User Selection**  
Generating synthetic interactions for every user via LLM prompts would be prohibitively expensive. To scale, ToP‑Rec dynamically identifies the most "influential" users during training using a metric called k‑step influence, which measures the alignment between a user's local gradient and the actual parameter update step. Users with the highest influence are selected for augmentation. This ensures that the costly LLM calls are spent on users whose synthetic data most benefits the model's global progress. The method adapts ideas from influence functions and gradient tracing (cited in Section 5) to the context of diversified recommendation. If the influence score poorly predicts benefit for diversity, or if the selection frequency is mis‑tuned, the efficiency gains may vanish or even harm performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  ToP Constructor → Item Assigner → (global tree T, item‑to‑leaf mapping)  
  Initial recommender training on original data R  
  Loop: Influential User Selector → (top‑K users) → User Preference Reasoner → Synthetic Interaction Generator → augment training data → continue Recommender Trainer training (periodically recompute influence)

- **Critical path**: Offline modules (ToP Constructor, Item Assigner) produce a shared tree and item mapping. The online training begins with the recommender on original interactions. The system then enters an iterative loop where the Influential User Selector identifies users, the Preference Reasoner and Synthetic Generator produce latent‑preference items, and the trainer consumes the augmented data. This cycle repeats until convergence.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Global vs. User-Specific ToP*: A single global tree ensures consistency and efficient item assignment but may not capture ultra‑niche groups; per‑user trees would be more personalized but vastly more costly.
  - *λ in s(u,i)*: Directly controls the diversity‑relevance trade‑off; higher λ increases diversity risk.
  - *Tree Granularity*: Deeper trees enable finer discrimination (better diversity potential) but increase LLM complexity and risk semantic fragmentation.
  - *Influence Window k*: Smaller k is cheaper and more responsive; larger k is more stable but computationally heavier and may lag behind parameter changes.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - ToP Construction: LLM produces flat trees, inconsistent branching, or leaves that are not mutually exclusive/collectively exhaustive.
  - Item Assignment: Severe load imbalance after refinement (many empty leaves, few overloaded).
  - Synthetic Data: High overlap with the user's original interactions or low semantic similarity scores for selected items.
  - User Selection: No performance gain or performance degradation after augmentation, suggesting poor influence estimation.

- **First three experiments**:
  1. *ToP Structure Ablation*: Vary prompt instructions to generate trees of different average depth/branching factors. Measure the impact on R@100 and CE@100 to determine optimal granularity.
  2. *Component Ablation*: Remove synthetic interaction generation (w/o Div, w/o Rel), influential user selection (w/o US), and preference reasoning (w/o PR) to quantify each component's contribution.
  3. *Hyperparameter Sensitivity*: Systematically vary the number of synthetic interactions per user (augmentation count N) and the diversity weight λ. Map the Pareto frontier of diversity vs. relevance and identify stable operating regions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1: How can ToP‑Rec be extended to simultaneously optimize for diversity, relevance, and novelty?**  
*Basis*: The paper explicitly states that ToP‑Rec does not focus on enhancing recommendation novelty and that future work will integrate novelty‑aware objectives.  
*Why unresolved*: The current method only considers diversity and relevance, omitting novelty, which is another important aspect of recommendation quality.  
*Evidence needed*: Experiments that incorporate novelty metrics (e.g., long‑tail item coverage) into the item selection score, comparisons against baselines on novelty, and user studies assessing perceived novelty.

**Open Question 2: How can ToP‑Rec be adapted for platforms with minimal or low‑quality textual user/item attributes?**  
*Basis*: The paper acknowledges as a limitation that the quality of textual attributes affects effectiveness; platforms dominated by images or videos may suffer.  
*Why unresolved*: The method relies heavily on textual descriptions for ToP construction, item matching, and semantic alignment; without high‑quality text, the LLM's reasoning and matching would be impaired.  
*Evidence needed*: Development of multimodal extensions that incorporate visual or audio features; evaluation on datasets with sparse text; analysis of performance degradation as text quality decreases.

**Open Question 3: To what extent do LLM‑generated preference trees accurately reflect users' true underexplored interests, and how does this affect recommendation performance?**  
*Basis*: The paper assumes LLMs can reliably reason about user preferences via ToP, but does not validate the intermediate preference outputs against ground truth or user feedback.  
*Why unresolved*: The method's effectiveness hinges on the quality of the extracted preferences; LLM hallucinations or biases could lead to incorrect latent preference inference, potentially harming rather than helping.  
*Evidence needed*: Human evaluation of ToP paths for a sample of users; comparisons of different LLMs' ToP structures; correlation between preference confidence scores and recommendation outcomes; ablation studies using gold‑standard preference annotations.

**Open Question 4: How does the dynamic user selection mechanism based on gradient alignment compare to alternative data selection strategies in terms of cost‑effectiveness and recommendation quality?**  
*Basis*: The paper proposes a custom influence measure but only compares it against random selection; the theoretical justification is provided but empirical comparison to other methods is lacking.  
*Why unresolved*: The claimed efficiency gain might be suboptimal; other selection criteria (loss magnitude, preference diversity, representativeness) could be more effective.  
*Evidence needed*: Systematic comparison of multiple selection strategies (random, loss‑based, gradient alignment, influence functions, clustering‑based) across varying computational budgets; analysis of correlation between influence scores and actual performance gains; case studies on selected user profiles.

## Limitations
- The paper does not specify several key reproduction parameters (LLM temperature, random seeds, ToP sampling details, augmentation count N, λ, dynamic selection settings), which may cause implementation divergence and hinder reproducibility.
- The method depends heavily on the quality of textual user and item attributes; it may perform poorly on platforms with minimal or low‑quality text, such as those dominated by visual content.
- LLM‑based components (tree construction, item assignment, preference reasoning) are sensitive to prompt design and model choice, raising reproducibility risks and potentially affecting the robustness of the approach.
