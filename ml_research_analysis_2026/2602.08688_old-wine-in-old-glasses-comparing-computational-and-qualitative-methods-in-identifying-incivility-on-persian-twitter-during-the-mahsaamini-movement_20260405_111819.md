# Old wine in old glasses: Comparing computational and qualitative methods in identifying incivility on Persian Twitter during the #MahsaAmini movement

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08688  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08688  
- **Reference count:** 21  
- **Primary result:** ParsBERT (weighted F1=0.78) substantially outperformed all tested ChatGPT models (best F1=0.57) on detecting incivility in Persian tweets from the #MahsaAmini movement, with ChatGPT failing on both explicit and subtle forms irrespective of prompt language.

## Executive Summary
This study benchmarks human qualitative coding, a domain-adapted Persian BERT model (ParsBERT), and seven ChatGPT variants for incivility detection (hate speech, pejorative speech, threats) in 47,278 Persian tweets from the #MahsaAmini protests. ParsBERT, fine-tuned on a balanced dataset augmented via weak labeling, achieved a weighted F1-score of 0.78. The best ChatGPT model (gpt-4.1) reached only 0.57, consistently misclassifying subtle, context-dependent incivility. Prompt language (English vs. Persian) did not significantly affect performance. The authors conclude that for Persian hate speech, established supervised models remain more reliable than current large language models.

## Method Summary
The research collection used the Twitter Academic API to gather 47,278 Persian tweets from September 15 to November 15, 2022. Five human coders annotated tweets for incivility type (Pejorative Speech, Insult, Threatening Message, Neutral) and implicitness level (0–3) using a Social Media Critical Discourse Studies (SM-CDS) approach, achieving intercoder F1-scores of 0.83 (type) and 0.86 (level). For ParsBERT, an initial imbalanced training set was built from all uncivil tweets plus 6,000 random neutrals (“sample A”). Weak labeling then used the initial ParsBERT to pseudo-label 100,000 additional tweets; manually verified threatening messages (4,085) were added, creating a balanced augmented set (“sample B”). ParsBERT was fine-tuned on both sets and evaluated on held-out test sets. For ChatGPT, seven models were prompted in English and Persian on a 10% quota sample (2,086 tweets) from sample A, with structured responses parsed for evaluation. Performance was compared via weighted F1-scores against human ground truth.

## Key Results
- ParsBERT (augmented) achieved a weighted F1-score of **0.78**, with threatening message recall improving from 0.40 to 0.71 after weak labeling augmentation.
- The best ChatGPT model (**gpt-4.1**) achieved a weighted F1-score of only **0.57**; no model exceeded 0.60.
- ChatGPT systematically misclassified **Pejorative Speech** as Neutral (and vice versa), failing on implicit, rhetorical, and multimodal-dependent incivility; prompt language had negligible effect on performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Monolingual Domain Adaptation
**Claim:** A monolingual, domain-adapted model (ParsBERT) outperforms general-purpose multilingual LLMs (ChatGPT) on Persian hate speech detection.  
**Mechanism:** ParsBERT’s pre-training on 3.9 million Persian documents captures language-specific tokenization, syntax, and semantic nuances essential for distinguishing subtle, culturally-contextual incivility (e.g., rhetorical pejorative speech). General LLMs, trained predominantly on English-centric web corpora, lack this fine-grained Persian linguistic grounding.  
**Evidence:** ParsBERT’s weighted F1 of 0.78 versus best ChatGPT’s 0.57. ParsBERT’s design as a monolingual Persian BERT [section 3.1].

### Mechanism 2: Weak Labeling for Class Imbalance
**Claim:** Addressing severe class imbalance (68.5% neutral) via weak labeling significantly improves a supervised model’s recall for minority incivility classes.  
**Mechanism:** The initial human-coded dataset was highly imbalanced. By pseudo-labeling a large unset and manually verifying predicted threatening messages, minority class samples were augmented (threatening messages from 1,675 to 5,760). This reduces bias toward the majority class and forces the model to learn discriminative features for rare hate types.  
**Evidence:** After augmentation, ParsBERT’s F1 increased from 0.69 to 0.78; threatening message F1 rose from 0.40 to 0.71 [section 4.3, Tables 3 & 4].

### Mechanism 3: LLMs Lack Contextual Understanding of Implicitness
**Claim:** Current LLMs fail on Persian hate speech not due to language barriers but because their general training lacks fine-grained, culturally-situated understanding of implicit, discursive forms in political discourse.  
**Mechanism:** LLMs optimize for statistical likelihood of general concepts, not deep discourse-level interpretation. Human coders using SM-CDS integrated user metadata, visual context (e.g., images), and intertextual knowledge to decode indirect hostility—capabilities absent in text-only LLM inference.  
**Evidence:** ChatGPT consistently failed on implicit Pejorative Speech; confusion analysis showed main errors in coding Pejorative Speech as Neutral. Example: a tweet with a regime-critical image was coded Neutral by ChatGPT but uncivil by humans [section 4.4]. Prompt language (English vs. Persian) had no significant effect.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Supervised Fine-Tuning vs. Zero/Few-Shot Prompting**  
   *Why needed:* Core methodological divergence explains performance gap. ParsBERT adapts weights via fine-tuning; ChatGPT relies on in-context learning without weight updates.  
   *Quick check:* Given 5,000 labeled Persian hate speech tweets, would you fine-tune ParsBERT or prompt ChatGPT? Trade-offs: fine-tuning yields higher accuracy but needs labeled data; prompting is flexible but less accurate and costly at scale.

2. **Class Imbalance and Weak Supervision**  
   *Why needed:* The dataset was 68.5% neutral; naive models would trivially predict “neutral.” Weak labeling augmented minority classes, lifting overall F1 by 0.09.  
   *Quick check:* How does oversampling minorities (via weak labeling) change precision and recall? Why might it harm majority-class performance? (Hint: model may overfit to minority patterns.)

3. **Implicitness as a Multi-Dimensional Hate Speech Property**  
   *Why needed:* Incivility exists on an explicitness spectrum (0–3) independent of type, making detection a complex, nuanced judgment harder for machines.  
   *Quick check:* Provide one example of an *explicitly* insulting Persian tweet and one that is *implicitly* pejorative. What linguistic or contextual features distinguish them? (e.g., direct slurs vs. metaphor/sarcasm.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Ingestion -> Human Annotation -> Training Data Construction -> Supervised Model (ParsBERT) -> LLM Inference -> Evaluation Engine

**Critical path:** Data collection (Twitter API) → Human coding (SM-CDS, consensus) → Training data prep (balance via weak labeling) → ParsBERT train/test → ChatGPT prompting/test → Comparative analysis (F1, confusion matrices) → Qualitative error inspection.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **ParsBERT:** High accuracy (F1 0.78) but requires expensive human-labeled data; sensitive to class imbalance; text-only.
- **ChatGPT:** No training data needed; multimodal potential; poor accuracy (F1 ≤0.57); black-box; high token cost for large sets.
- **Human coding:** Gold-standard nuance (SM-CDS) but slow, expensive, non-scalable.

**Failure signatures:**
- **ParsBERT:** Low recall on rare classes (e.g., threatening messages at F1 0.40 in imbalanced set). Indicates inadequate minority representation.
- **ChatGPT:** Systematic misclassification of Pejorative Speech as Neutral, especially when implicitness requires contextual inference (metaphor, image-text synergy); occasional failure on explicit hate words.
- **General:** Any text-only model underperforms on tweets containing images/videos, as humans used multimodal context.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replicate ParsBERT baseline:** Fine-tune on imbalanced Sample A (all uncivil + 6k neutrals). Expect weighted F1 ≈0.69.
2. **Weak labeling pipeline:** Use baseline model to pseudo-label 100k unset tweets; manually verify predicted TMs; augment training; retrain. Expect F1 rise to ≈0.78.
3. **LLM prompt sensitivity:** Run gpt-4.1 on 2,086-tweet subset with English and Persian prompts. Expect F1 difference <0.01; confirm Pejorative Speech → Neutral confusion dominates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why was ParsBERT not evaluated for Level of Implicity (LoI) detection, and how would its performance compare to ChatGPT on this fine-grained task?
2. To what extent are the performance gaps generalizable beyond Persian and the #MahsaAmini context to other low-resource languages and movements?
3. What specific linguistic, cultural, or multimodal features primarily cause ChatGPT’s failures (e.g., metaphors, sarcasm, images)?
4. Does incorporating non-textual context (images, videos) into LLM inputs meaningfully improve Persian hate speech detection, and is there a modality-specific performance ceiling?
5. How rapidly do LLM capabilities for Persian hate speech advance, and is the ParsBERT advantage persistent against newest architectures (post-2025)?

## Limitations
- Missing critical details: exact ChatGPT prompts, train/test split seeds, ParsBERT hyperparameters, weak-labeling verification process.
- Generalizability limited to Persian political discourse during one protest movement; may not hold for other languages/contexts.
- Assumption that manual verification sufficiently controlled weak-label noise lacks reported inter-coder agreement for that step.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (monolingual advantage):** High. Strong empirical gap aligns with NLP patterns for low-resource languages.
- **Mechanism 2 (weak labeling effectiveness):** Medium. Improvement clear, but incomplete pipeline documentation and noise quantification reduce certainty.
- **Mechanism 3 (LLM failure due to implicitness):** Medium. Prompt language effects negligible, but only 14 model-language combinations tested; future LLMs could alter conclusion.
