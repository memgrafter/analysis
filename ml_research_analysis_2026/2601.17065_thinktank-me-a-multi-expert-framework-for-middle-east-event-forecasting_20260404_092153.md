# ThinkTank-ME: A Multi-Expert Framework for Middle East Event Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17065  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17065  
- **Reference count:** 25  
- **Primary result:** Elite Ensemble (Weighted Best-of-N) achieves state-of-the-art accuracy (24.6% micro, 25.0% macro), improving 8.4–10.6% over All-data Training and surpassing GPT-4o.

## Executive Summary
ThinkTank-ME addresses the limitation of single-LLM predictors in capturing diverse geopolitical nuances for Middle East event forecasting. It introduces a framework with multiple country-specific expert LLMs, fine-tuned on a new benchmark POLECAT-FOR-ME, and coordinated by a leader model that aggregates predictions. The Elite Ensemble strategy—using a trained router to rank experts and aggregate the top‑k—optimizes the accuracy‑efficiency trade‑off and achieves state-of-the‑art performance, demonstrating the value of specialized, ensemble-based forecasting in complex regional contexts.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with POLECAT-FOR-ME construction: the POLECAT dataset is cleaned, formed into historical event sequences, and partitioned by country to yield 35 country‑specific subsets. For each country, a specialist expert model is created via parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (LoRA) of a base LLM (Llama‑3.1‑8B). A router model is trained on historical samples to predict which expert would be correct for a given query. During inference, the router ranks experts; the Elite Ensemble then selects the top‑k and applies weighted best‑of‑N aggregation. Three strategies are evaluated: (a) routing to a single expert, (b) aggregating all experts (wisdom of the crowd), and (c) Elite Ensemble. Performance is measured on a held‑out temporal test set.

## Key Results
- Elite Ensemble (Weighted Best-of-N) achieves 24.6% micro‑average and 25.0% macro‑average accuracy.
- Represents an 8.4% (micro) and 10.6% (macro) improvement over the All‑data Training baseline.
- Surpasses proprietary models including GPT‑4o.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Multi‑expert collaboration captures diverse geopolitical perspectives that a single‑LLM predictor misses.
- **Mechanism:** Specialized expert models are fine‑tuned on country‑specific subsets of POLECAT‑FOR‑ME. Each expert internalizes unique regional historical dynamics, actor relationships, and cultural contexts. The leader model then synthesizes these divergent predictions.
- **Core assumption:** Geopolitical event forecasting necessitates modeling multiple, often conflicting, national or regional viewpoints; a single model’s weights encode a dominant, averaged perspective that obscures critical nuance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "single‑LLM predictors… generate predictions along a singular explicit trajectory, constraining their ability to capture diverse geopolitical nuances."
  - [section 3.2]: Expert models are built via "parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning on country‑level event datasets" to "capture unique regional event patterns."
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers on "Geopolitical Event Forecasting" and "LLM Persona Generation in Polarised Contexts" suggest this is an active research pain point, though direct evidence for *this mechanism* is weak.
- **Break condition:** If country‑specific training data is extremely sparse or non‑diverse, fine‑tuning fails to instill distinct expertise, causing experts to converge toward similar, generic predictions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The Elite Ensemble strategy (trained router + top‑k weighted aggregation) optimally balances prediction accuracy and computational efficiency.
- **Mechanism:** A router model (trained on historical routing correctness) ranks experts by predicted competence for a given query. Only the top‑k experts (e.g., k=5) are aggregated via Weighted Best‑of‑N. This filters out low‑value expert contributions while retaining collective wisdom.
- **Core assumption:** Expert performance is query‑dependent; a subset of "elite" experts can produce a more accurate consensus than all experts or a single routed expert. The router learns to identify this subset.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3.3]: Elite Ensemble "strategically combines expert routing with the principle of 'wisdom of the crowd'" and "reuses the routing model to rank experts… and selects the top‑𝑘 experts for selective aggregation."
  - [section 4, RQ3]: Figure 3(b) shows a trained router yields a peaked accuracy curve (improves then degrades as k increases), while an untrained router does not, demonstrating effective elite selection.
  - [table 1]: Elite Ensemble (Weighted Best‑of‑N) achieves the highest accuracy (24.6% Mi) among all aggregation strategies, surpassing full Wisdom Aggregation (23.9% Mi).
- **Break condition:** If the routing model’s training signal (historical correct expert predictions) is noisy or biased, it learns poor rankings, either missing capable experts or selecting irrelevant ones, degrading the ensemble.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The POLECAT‑FOR‑ME dataset’s construction (finer granularity, Middle East focus) is a necessary precondition for effective expert specialization and rigorous evaluation.
- **Mechanism:** Starting from POLECAT, the authors perform data cleaning, form historical event sequences, and partition by country attribute. This yields country‑specific datasets with sufficient events and modern ontology (vs. CAMEO) to train distinct expert models and test regional nuance.
- **Core assumption:** Generic, global event datasets (e.g., ICEWS, GDELT) lack the temporal‑spatial granularity and regional event coverage needed to train models that capture specific Middle Eastern geopolitical dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1]: Explicitly states standard datasets' "2009 schema struggles to reflect modern geopolitical complexity" and POLECAT provides "finer temporal–spatial granularity and broader event coverage."
  - [section 4, RQ2]: Figure 3(a) shows most country‑specific experts outperform the "No Training" baseline on their *own* country’s test set, implying the dataset successfully isolates learnable expertise.
  - [corpus]: Strong signal from neighbor papers on "Spatio‑Temporal… Forecasting" and "Temporal Structures for Geopolitical Events" that dataset design is a recognized bottleneck.
- **Break condition:** If the data cleaning or sequence formation is flawed (e.g., incorrect entity resolution, broken temporal chains), the resulting country datasets contain noise or lose critical context, preventing experts from learning coherent regional patterns.

## Foundational Learning

  *Why needed here:* Understands the core principle of the router in Elite Ensemble—learning a gating function to assign inputs to specialized sub‑models.  
  *Quick check question:* How does a trained routing model’s objective differ from a standard MoE router, given its supervision comes from historical "correct expert" labels rather than loss gradients?
- **Concept: Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning (e.g., LoRA)**  
  *Why needed here:* Expert models are built by fine‑tuning base LLMs (Llama‑3.1‑8B) on country data. LoRA is mentioned as the implementation method, crucial for adapting large models to narrow domains without catastrophic forgetting or excessive cost.  
  *Quick check question:* Why is full fine‑tuning on a single country’s data potentially problematic compared to a method like LoRA?
- **Concept: Wisdom of the Crowd / Ensemble Methods**  
  *Why needed here:* The leader model strategies (Majority Voting, Best‑of‑N) directly apply ensemble theory. Understanding the "wisdom of crowds" principle and its limitations (diversity, independence) explains why naive aggregation sometimes fails and why "elite" selection is proposed.  
  *Quick check question:* What is the hypothesized failure mode of full Wisdom Aggregation (all experts) that Elite Ensemble aims to solve?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Dataset Pipeline → Expert Model Factory → Router Trainer → Leader/Ensembler → Evaluator  
  (Data cleaner → Sequence builder → Country data splitter produces 35 datasets; each expert fine‑tuned on one country dataset; router trained on all expert predictions over training queries; leader implements routing, full aggregation, or elite ensemble; evaluator computes accuracy on test set.)
- **Critical path:** Dataset construction → Expert training → Router training → Ensemble strategy implementation → End‑to‑end evaluation on held‑out temporal test set.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Accuracy vs. Compute:* Expert Routing is cheapest but lowest accuracy. Full Wisdom Aggregation (all experts) is accurate but costly. Elite Ensemble is a tunable trade‑off via `k`.  
  - *Router Complexity:* A more complex router might better rank experts but risks overfitting on the noisy "correct expert" training signal.  
  - *Dataset Granularity:* Finer country splits increase expert specialization potential but reduce per‑expert training data, risking underfitting.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Router outputs nearly uniform rankings (low entropy), indicating it hasn’t learned to discriminate expert quality.  
  - Elite Ensemble performance degrades as `k` increases *from the very first step*, suggesting the router’s top‑ranked experts are consistently poor.  
  - Country‑specific expert performance is no better than the "No Training" baseline on its own test set, indicating failed specialization.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Sanity Check Experts:** For a few high‑data countries (e.g., USA, IRN), train a single expert. Evaluate on that country’s test set. Expect > baseline "No Training" accuracy. If not, debug data pipeline or fine‑tuning setup.  
  2. **Validate Router Training Signal:** Sample 100 training queries. For each, record which expert(s) got the correct answer. Check distribution: is there a *clear* correct expert for most queries, or is it ambiguous? High ambiguity suggests the router’s supervision is too noisy.  
  3. **Tune Elite Ensemble `k`:** On a validation slice, run Elite Ensemble with `k` = {1, 3, 5, 10, 15, all}. Plot accuracy vs. `k`. Expect a peak. If accuracy monotonically *decreases* with more experts, either the router is ranking poorly or the "elite" assumption fails (all experts are similarly bad/good). If accuracy monotonically *increases*, try `k`=all (i.e., full Wisdom Aggregation) as it may be optimal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the reasoning capabilities of LLMs be better leveraged within ThinkTank‑ME to improve forecasting accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states: "Future work involves leveraging the reasoning capabilities of LLMs to improve accuracy..."
- **Why unresolved:** The current framework relies on fine‑tuning and aggregation but does not deeply integrate advanced reasoning techniques (e.g., chain‑of‑thought, latent reasoning) that could enhance predictive depth for complex geopolitical events.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments comparing the baseline ThinkTank‑ME with variants that incorporate explicit reasoning prompts or fine‑tuning for reasoning, using POLECAT‑FOR‑ME to measure accuracy changes and identify optimal reasoning integration strategies.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does ThinkTank‑ME generalize to other geographical regions beyond the Middle East?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion notes: "scaling the framework to broader geographical regions to validate its generalizability."
- **Why unresolved:** The framework is evaluated solely on Middle Eastern events; regional variations in geopolitical dynamics, data availability, and event patterns may affect the effectiveness of country‑specific expert modeling and aggregation strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the framework on a different region (e.g., East Asia or Africa) using a similarly constructed benchmark, then comparing performance gains against single‑model baselines to assess transferability.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does ThinkTank‑ME's performance depend on the underlying LLM architecture and scale?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] All experiments use Llama‑3.1‑8B; no ablation study explores different model families or sizes.
- **Why unresolved:** Different LLMs exhibit varying capacities for geopolitical knowledge and reasoning; the observed gains may not hold for smaller, larger, or architecturally distinct models.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Running the full ThinkTank‑ME pipeline (expert fine‑tuning, routing, ensemble) with multiple LLMs (e.g., decoder‑only models of different scales, encoder‑decoder models) and reporting accuracy variations to determine model dependency.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How robust is the Elite Ensemble strategy to the presence of low‑performing or incompetent expert models?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The evaluation assumes reasonably competent experts; no experiments simulate expert failure or degradation.
- **Why unresolved:** Real‑world expert panels may include members with poor predictive ability; the router's ability to filter out such experts and the ensemble's resilience to noise remain untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Introducing controlled degradation (e.g., random predictions, fine‑tuning on irrelevant data) into a subset of experts, then measuring Elite Ensemble's accuracy compared to other aggregation methods and analyzing router selection fidelity.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can ThinkTank‑ME be adapted to mitigate data imbalance between high‑profile and low‑resource countries?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper notes proprietary models like GPT‑4o outperform on high‑profile countries (e.g., Israel, Egypt) due to "long‑tail" data distribution, but does not address how the multi‑expert approach handles such imbalance.
- **Why unresolved:** Country‑specific datasets likely vary significantly in size and coverage; experts for low‑data countries may be underfit, limiting overall framework fairness and performance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying data augmentation, oversampling, or weighted loss functions for low‑resource country experts, then evaluating changes in country‑specific accuracy (especially for underrepresented nations) and macro‑average performance.

## Limitations
- **Expert Specialization Validity:** The paper shows country experts outperform "No Training" baselines, but it's unclear if gains reflect genuine geopolitical nuance capture versus spurious correlations (e.g., memorizing country names in prompts).  
- **Router Signal Quality:** The router’s training uses historical "correct expert" labels, which may be ambiguous if multiple experts often predicted correctly. Noisy supervision could yield arbitrary rankings.  
- **Dataset Generalization:** POLECAT‑FOR‑ME’s construction is praised, but without ablations on alternative data partitions or synthetic noise, its role as a *necessary* precondition remains assumed.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Multi‑expert capture):** *Medium* – evidence of expert specialization exists (Fig 3a), but causality to "nuance" is not isolated.  
- **Mechanism 2 (Elite Ensemble efficiency):** *High* – clear ablation curves (Fig 3b) show trained router improves accuracy‑efficiency trade‑off vs. untrained router.  
- **Mechanism 3 (Dataset necessity):** *Medium* – paper contrasts with older schemas but lacks controlled comparisons against other Middle East–focused datasets.

## Next Checks
1. **Expert Ablation on Queries:** For high‑data countries, run experts on *counterfactual queries* where country references are removed but regional context preserved. If performance drops sharply, specialists rely on superficial cues.
2. **Router Ambiguity Metric:** Compute the entropy of "correct expert" distribution across router training samples. High average entropy indicates a noisy supervision signal, undermining router utility.
3. **Dataset Perturbation Test:** Rebuild POLECAT‑FOR‑ME with a *different* country partition (e.g., by region instead of nation) and re‑train experts. If macro‑accuracy degrades significantly, results are brittle to partition choices.
