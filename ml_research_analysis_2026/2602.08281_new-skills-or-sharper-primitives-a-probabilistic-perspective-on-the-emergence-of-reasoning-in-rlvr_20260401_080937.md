# New Skills or Sharper Primitives? A Probabilistic Perspective on the Emergence of Reasoning in RLVR

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08281  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08281  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) creates new reasoning abilities by sharpening atomic step probabilities, leading to a phase transition where previously impossible tasks become feasible, though this process may cause capability erosion in some atomic skills.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has been observed to elicit complex reasoning from language models, but it remains debated whether this constitutes truly new capabilities or merely surfacing pre-existing knowledge. This paper frames the question probabilistically, defining capability at the instance level and identifying a "multiplicative barrier" where multi-step success decays exponentially with the number of atomic steps. Using a controlled algebraic framework (Algebrarium), models are trained only on single-step operations and evaluated on unseen multi-step problems. Results show that RLVR amplifies the probabilities of correct atomic steps, shifting 22.6% of tasks from an "impossible" Null set into a "feasible" set with mean accuracy 0.60. Composite performance strongly correlates with the product of atomic accuracies (Pearson ρ ∈ [0.69,0.96]). However, training also induces capability erosion: some atomic skills regress even as overall reward improves. The findings support that RLVR gives rise to new abilities not by learning composite logic but by sharpening primitive skills.

## Method Summary
The authors introduce a formal definition of per-instance solvability, \(P_{\theta}(q)\), and prove that for chains of \(M\) independent atomic steps, the composite success probability is approximately the product of the atomic step probabilities, \(P_{\theta}(q) \approx \prod_{j=1}^M P_{\theta}(s_j)\). This multiplicative barrier explains why complex reasoning is often infeasible. To test the emergence mechanism, they use the Algebrarium framework, which generates infinite algebraic tasks with verifiable labels. Models are pre-trained on general data, then fine-tuned with RLVR (specifically GRPO) using a binary reward that only checks final correctness, while the training set consists solely of atomic (single-operation) problems. The test set comprises multi-step problems requiring composition of 2–5 atomic operations. After RLVR, the same composite test set is re-evaluated, and the change in per-instance probabilities is analyzed, including decomposition into atomic steps, classification into Null (impossible) and Feasible (reliable) sets, and correlation of joint atomic accuracy with composite outcomes.

## Key Results
- RLVR amplifies atomic step probabilities, moving 22.6% of previously unsolvable tasks (Null set) into the feasible set with a mean accuracy of 0.60 on those tasks.
- Composite performance is highly correlated with the product of atomic step accuracies across model families (Pearson ρ ∈ [0.69, 0.96]).
- Training induces capability erosion: some atomic skills exhibit negative performance change while overall reward increases, indicating a trade-off between aggregate and instance-level performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: The Multiplicative Barrier
**Claim:** Multi-step reasoning success decays multiplicatively due to the joint probability of sequential atomic steps, creating an exponential barrier that renders complex tasks practically unsolvable even with moderately accurate atomic skills.  
**Mechanism:** For a chain of \(M\) atomic steps, the composite success probability is modeled as \(P(q) \approx \prod_{j=1}^M P(s_j)\). This product decays exponentially with \(M\) because each \(P(s_j) < 1\), causing performance to collapse below a solvability threshold (\(\epsilon \approx 0.023\)) for deep chains.  
**Core assumption:** Atomic step correctness is independent; the model's per-step accuracy is stable and stationary across compositions.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the multiplicative barrier; Section 5.1 presents the product formula and decay example (e.g., \(0.3^5 \approx 0.0024\)); related literature on compositional barriers provides context.  
**Break condition:** If atomic steps are not independent (e.g., due to error propagation or shared representations), the product model fails and the barrier may be less severe.

### Mechanism 2: Emergence via Sharpening Primitives
**Claim:** RLVR emergence occurs not by learning new composite logic, but by "sharpening" the probability of correct atomic steps, pushing the joint product above a feasibility threshold (\(\delta = 0.125\)) and causing a phase transition from the Null to Feasible set.  
**Mechanism:** RLVR optimizes a policy to maximize expected reward on the training distribution of solvable atomic subtasks. Gradient updates increase \(P(s_j)\) for steps that appear in high-reward trajectories. When the product \(\prod P(s_j)\) for a given composite chain exceeds \(\delta\), the task transitions from impossible (Avg@128 = 0) to reliably solvable (Avg@128 \(\ge \delta\)).  
**Core assumption:** RLVR's updates preferentially boost atomic probabilities without needing explicit supervision on the composite task.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract asserts that RLVR creates new abilities by sharpening primitives; Section 5.2 formalizes emergence as crossing the feasibility boundary (e.g., \(0.35^5\) Null vs. \(0.75^5\) Feasible); Section 7.2 reports that 22.6% of Null tasks become Feasible after RL with mean accuracy 0.60.  
**Break condition:** If RL fails to increase atomic probabilities (due to poor exploration, reward hacking, or catastrophic forgetting), the product may not cross the threshold and no phase transition occurs.

### Mechanism 3: Capability Erosion as a Trade-off
**Claim:** RLVR induces capability erosion: optimizing global expected reward can degrade performance on specific atomic or composite tasks, representing a redistributive trade-off rather than uniform improvement.  
**Mechanism:** The RL objective \(J(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum \mathbb{E}[R]\) optimizes the mean across the task distribution. Gradient updates may overwrite parameters critical for minority or previously mastered skills to benefit majority or high-reward tasks, causing localized regression.  
**Core assumption:** The model has finite capacity or shared representations where improving one skill's distribution can adversely affect another.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes that RLVR, as a global optimizer, can sacrifice specific skills for aggregate reward; Section 5.3 describes that optimizing expected reward can improve aggregate performance while degrading specific instances; Section 7.4 and Figure 6 show negative \(\Delta\) (performance loss) for some atomic skills, especially in Llama/Gemma, correlating with initial high accuracy.  
**Break condition:** If the model has sufficient capacity (e.g., parameter isolation, modular addition) or the task distribution is perfectly aligned with all skills, erosion may be minimal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Instance-Level Probabilistic Solvability (\(P_{\theta}(q)\))**  
  *Why needed:* Disentangles aggregate Pass@k from the true driver of performance. Enables defining Null/Feasible sets and analyzing the multiplicative barrier at the level of individual problems.  
  *Quick check:* If a model's Pass@1 on a specific problem is 0 but Pass@128 is 0.1, what does that imply about its true \(P_{\theta}(q)\) and its classification (Null/Feasible)?  

- **The Multiplicative Barrier & Joint Probability**  
  *Why needed:* Explains why complex reasoning is exponentially harder than atomic steps, forming the core theoretical bottleneck that RLVR must overcome.  
  *Quick check:* If all atomic steps have \(P(s_j)=0.6\), what is the approximate composite \(P(q)\) for a 4-step chain? Is this likely above or below \(\delta=0.125\)?  

- **Controlled Decomposition (Atomic-Composite Split)**  
  *Why needed:* The experimental design cornerstone. Training *only* on atomic operations while testing on composites isolates the mechanism of emergence as "sharpening" rather than "learning composite logic."  
  *Quick check:* Why is training on multi-step problems a confounder for testing the "sharpening primitives" hypothesis?  

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Algebrarium Generator → Atomic/Composite Task Sets → Base Model → Pre-evaluation (Compute \(P_{\theta}(q)\), classify Null/Feasible) → RLVR Trainer (GRPO) → Post-evaluation (Same composite set) → Analysis (Transition rate, correlation, erosion scatter)

**Critical path:**  
1. Define algebraic domain via Algebrarium.  
2. Generate atomic-only training set (1-op problems) and composite test set (2-5 ops).  
3. Pre-evaluate base model: compute Avg@128 for all test instances → derive \(P_{\theta}(q)\) and classify Null (\(<\epsilon\)) vs Feasible (\(\ge\delta\)).  
4. Run RLVR training on atomic set.  
5. Post-evaluate model on identical composite test set.  
6. Analyze: (a) percentage of Null→Feasible transitions, (b) correlation between \(\prod P(s_j)\) and \(P(q)\), (c) scatter of \(\Delta\) vs initial \(P(s_j)\).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Threshold selection (\(\epsilon, \delta\)):** \(\epsilon\) based on statistical rule of three (3/\(K_{\text{large}}\)); \(\delta\) tied to compute budget (1/\(K_{\text{min}}\)). Choices define "impossible" vs "reliable."  
- **Atomic step decomposition:** Requires ground-truth canonicalization (e.g., Free Group reduction rules). Imperfect decomposition adds noise to \(P(s_j)\) estimates.  
- **GRPO hyperparameters (group size \(G\), KL penalty):** Affect exploration/exploitation balance. Larger \(G\) improves reward estimation but reduces sample efficiency.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. **No Null→Feasible transition:** Post-RL Avg@128 remains ~0 for former Null tasks → atomic sharpening insufficient.  
2. **Low \(\rho\) (joint vs outcome):** Pearson correlation <0.5 → composite success not driven by atomic product; possibly due to non-linear composition, error recovery, or poor atomic labeling.  
3. **Universal atomic regression:** Most \(\Delta\) (RL - Base) for atomic skills negative → severe capability erosion; RL may be learning a different policy.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Validate Pass@k Theory Fit:** Plot empirical Pass@k curves (k=1..128) vs theoretical curves computed from Avg@128 estimates; check MSE < 1e-4 as in Fig 2.  
2. **Quantify Phase Transition:** For base model, identify tasks with Avg@128 < \(\epsilon\) (Null). After RL, compute % of these moving to Avg@128 \(\ge \delta\) (Feasible); expect ~20% (Fig 3).  
3. **Verify Exponential Decay:** For test set, group by operation count (2-5 ops). Plot accuracy vs depth on log-linear scale; expect linear decay (y ≈ p^x) and compute fitted p. Check that p matches average atomic \(P(s_j)\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** To what extent do the findings on the multiplicative barrier and RLVR-induced emergence generalize from synthetic algebraic tasks to natural language reasoning tasks?  
  *Basis:* Experiments exclusively use Algebrarium synthetic tasks; no evaluation on standard NLP benchmarks.  
  *Why unresolved:* Conclusions are limited to artificial domains; generalizability to real-world noisy reasoning is unknown.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate the methodology on diverse natural language reasoning benchmarks (e.g., math word problems, logical deduction, coding) and test if the multiplicative barrier and high correlation between joint atomic accuracy and composite performance hold.

- **Question:** How does statistical dependence between atomic reasoning steps affect the multiplicative barrier hypothesis and the observed correlation between joint step accuracy and outcome success?  
  *Basis:* The theoretical model assumes independence of atomic step correctness; the paper acknowledges this simplification but does not explore dependencies.  
  *Why unresolved:* Analysis relies on independence; impact of dependencies on the exponential decay and correlation predictions remains unaddressed.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Design tasks with controlled conditional dependencies between steps (e.g., later steps depend on previous correctness) and measure whether the product of marginal probabilities still predicts composite performance, or extend the theory to incorporate conditional probabilities.

- **Question:** What specific model properties (e.g., architecture, pre-training distribution, or RLVR algorithm characteristics) explain the observed robustness of Qwen models to capability erosion compared to Llama and Gemma?  
  *Basis:* Section 7.4 documents that Qwen models show minimal atomic regression during RLVR, whereas Llama/Gemma exhibit significant erosion; the authors speculate about "stronger logical consistency" but do not investigate.  
  *Why unresolved:* No analysis of underlying causes; factors enabling stable optimization are unknown.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Comparative studies of internal representations, gradient dynamics, or pre-training data distributions across these families; or introduce consistency-promoting mechanisms into erosion-prone models to see if degradation is mitigated.

- **Question:** Can RLVR objectives be redesigned to reduce capability erosion while still enabling the emergence of complex reasoning capabilities?  
  *Basis:* Section 5.3 describes erosion as an inherent outcome of global reward maximization; the paper uses standard GRPO without exploring alternatives.  
  *Why unresolved:* No attempts to modify the RLVR algorithm to address the trade-off; practical methods for balancing emergence with stability are undeveloped.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement and test RLVR variants (e.g., adding constraints to maintain atomic skill performance, multi-objective optimization, curriculum-based approaches) and evaluate whether they reduce erosion without compromising composite task solvability.

## Limitations
- The core multiplicative barrier model assumes independence between atomic step correctness; this assumption may not hold for natural reasoning where steps are often correlated.
- The experimental evaluation is confined to synthetic algebraic tasks (Algebrarium), leaving open whether results generalize to more realistic, noisy, or language-based reasoning scenarios.
- The causal inference that RLVR solely sharpens primitives relies on correlation; alternative explanations (e.g., latent structure learning, trajectory stitching) are not fully ruled out.

## Confidence
- **Multiplicative barrier existence:** High  
- **Phase transition via atomic sharpening:** Medium  
- **Capability erosion as a trade-off:** Medium  
- **General conclusion ("sharper primitives, not new skills"):** Low–Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Test atomic step independence:** Train models on deliberately correlated atomic tasks (e.g., steps sharing variables) and measure deviation of empirical \(P(q)\) from \(\prod P(s_j)\). A systematic gap would refute the independence premise.
2. **Vary solvability thresholds:** Re-run classification with \(\epsilon \in [0.01, 0.05]\) and \(\delta \in [0.08, 0.2]\). If the Null→Feasible transition rate remains stable, the phase transition is robust to threshold selection.
3. **Cross-domain replication:** Apply identical methodology to a non-algebraic RLVR benchmark (e.g., code generation or logical puzzles). If the same multiplicative correlation and erosion patterns hold, generalizability is supported.
