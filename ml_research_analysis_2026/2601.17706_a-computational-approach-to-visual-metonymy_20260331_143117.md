# A Computational Approach to Visual Metonymy

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.17706
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17706
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introduces ViMET, a 2,000‑question benchmark for visual metonymy; humans achieve 86.9% accuracy while state‑of‑the‑art VLMs peak at 65.9%, a 21% gap.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the first computational study of visual metonymy—the phenomenon where an image indirectly evokes a concept through associated visual cues. The authors design a semiotic‑grounded pipeline that leverages large language models (LLMs) to generate concrete associated objects (representamens) and chain‑of‑thought (CoT) visual descriptions, which are then rendered by a text‑to‑image model. The pipeline filters candidate concepts by concreteness and WordNet supersense categories to ensure metonymic potential. From this process they derive ViMET, a benchmark of 2,000 multiple‑choice questions (1,000 concepts, two images each) that tests a model’s ability to infer the evoked concept. Human evaluators achieve 86.9% accuracy; the best VLM scores 65.9%, revealing a substantial performance gap. The pipeline produces metonymic images in 84.3% of cases—far above a simple‑prompt baseline (41.2%). Analysis shows VLMs perform best on symbolic associations (76.3%) and worst on contextual ones (54.5%), highlighting the challenge of ambiguous, context‑dependent metonymy.

## Method Summary
The work constructs a fully automated pipeline for generating and evaluating visual metonymy. Concept selection starts from all WordNet nouns; only those with a concreteness score below 3.5 and belonging to one of 13 WordNet supersenses with historically >60% human‑judged metonymic success are retained, yielding approximately 2,000 target concepts. For each concept, an LLM (Llama 3.1‑70B‑Instruct) produces 5–6 concrete, visually depictable representamens—objects strongly associated with the concept. A second LLM then crafts a short visual description using CoT prompting: it must integrate only a sufficient subset of the representamens, avoid explicitly naming the target concept, and specify compositional elements (lighting, mood) for either a naturalistic or a stylistic image. The description is fed into Stable Diffusion 3.5‑Large to generate the image. Human annotation on 1,000 generated images confirms the pipeline yields metonymic content in 84.3% of cases, versus 41.2% for a naive prompt. The ViMET benchmark is built by pairing each of 1,000 sampled concepts with two images (naturalistic and stylistic) and constructing four‑option multiple‑choice questions. Distractors are created via CLIP image similarity and ConceptNet relational filtering, with additional checks to avoid synonyms and overly similar distractors. Finally, eight vision‑language models are evaluated on the full 2,000‑question set.

## Key Results
- Human accuracy on ViMET: 86.9%; best VLM accuracy: 65.9% (21% gap).
- Pipeline generates metonymic images at 84.3% success vs. 41.2% for a simple prompt.
- VLM performance varies by association type: symbolic (76.3%) significantly higher than contextual (54.5%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: LLM‑generated representamens provide visual cues.
- **Claim**: Leveraging LLMs to produce a set of concrete, associated objects (representamens) effectively supplies visual cues that can later be integrated into images to evoke a target concept metonymically.
- **Mechanism**: Given a target concept and few‑shot examples, the LLM (e.g., Llama 3.1‑70B‑Instruct) outputs 5–6 concrete, visually depictable items that commonly co‑occur or are culturally linked to the concept. These serve as seeds for scene construction.
- **Core assumption**: The LLM has absorbed widespread human associations from its training data, so its suggested objects are recognizable and reliably trigger the intended concept in most observers.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “A novel semiotic‑grounded pipeline uses LLMs to generate representamens (associated objects) and chain‑of‑thought visual descriptions”; [section] “Section 3.1: Generating Representamens… We provide Llama 3.1‑70B‑Instruct with a few illustrative examples and prompt it to produce a set of objects that share a strong cognitive connection to the given noun.”
- **Break condition**: If the LLM produces abstract or non‑visual associations (e.g., emotions, actions), the subsequent image generation will likely fail to produce a metonymic depiction because the cues cannot be rendered concretely.

### Mechanism 2: Chain‑of‑thought (CoT) with constraints yields metonymic descriptions.
- **Claim**: CoT prompting with explicit constraints (avoid mentioning the target concept, select a subset of representamens, incorporate tone/color) guides the LLM to create scene descriptions that yield genuinely metonymic images rather than literal ones.
- **Mechanism**: After obtaining representamens, a second LLM is prompted to craft a short visual description. CoT steps ensure the description integrates only the necessary objects, excludes the target word, and adds compositional details (lighting, mood). A fail‑safe re‑generates if the target word appears.
- **Core assumption**: The LLM can be steered by natural language instructions to suppress explicit concept mention and to prioritize associative composition, thus maintaining the semiotic gap between representamen and object.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “chain‑of‑thought visual descriptions”; [section] “Section 3.1: Visual Description using Chain‑of‑Thought… instructing the model to: a) construct a coherent and natural visual description that integrates only those representamens that are sufficient to evoke the concept… b) avoid explicitly naming the concept word… c) incorporate visual compositional elements…”.
- **Break condition**: Failure of the LLM to omit the target concept word (due to prompt misalignment) results in a literal description and consequently a non‑metonymic image.

### Mechanism 3: Concept filtering by concreteness and WordNet categories improves yield.
- **Claim**: Filtering candidate concepts by moderate abstraction (concreteness < 3.5) and by WordNet supersense categories that historically yield metonymic images (>60% success) significantly improves pipeline efficiency and output quality.
- **Mechanism**: Starting from all WordNet nouns, concepts with high concreteness (≥3.5) and those in supersenses (e.g., animal, food, object) where human annotation showed <60% metonymic success are removed. This leaves a subset of ~2,000 concepts that are more amenable to metonymic representation.
- **Core assumption**: Concepts that are neither too concrete nor too abstract, and that belong to certain semantic domains, have richer sets of established visual associations that can be captured by representamens.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “filtered by concreteness and WordNet categories”; [section] “Section 3.2: Human Feedback Based Concept Filtering… we introduce two criteria to filter out concepts that have a lower likelihood of producing successful metonymic images…”.
- **Break condition**: If a concept falls outside the filtered set but someone attempts to generate for it, the pipeline tends to produce non‑metonymic images, as seen for highly concrete items like ‘table’ or ‘chair’.

## Foundational Learning
**Visual metonymy**  
*Why needed*: Defines the core phenomenon the pipeline aims to generate and evaluate. Without understanding that an image can evoke a concept indirectly via associated cues, the rationale for representamens and the ViMET task is unclear.  
*Quick check*: Explain the difference between visual metonymy and visual literal depiction. Provide your own example of a metonymic image for the concept “celebration”.

**Semiotic triad (Peirce)**  
*Why needed*: The pipeline is explicitly grounded in Peirce’s sign theory; the object‑representamen‑interpretant mapping structures every step from concept to representamen to image interpretation.  
*Quick check*: Apply the semiotic triad to the concept “writer”: what is the object, possible representamens, and the intended interpretant?

**Chain‑of‑thought (CoT) prompting**  
*Why needed*: CoT is the key technique that steers the LLM to produce descriptions that avoid literal depiction and instead incorporate compositional and tonal cues, which is essential for generating metonymic images.  
*Quick check*: Why might standard prompting (e.g., “Describe an image for X”) lead to literal depictions, whereas CoT with specific constraints leads to metonymic ones?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Concept Filter -> Representamen Generator (LLM1) -> Visual Description Generator (LLM2) -> Text‑to‑Image Generator -> Human Evaluation (for validation)

**Critical path**: Concept → Concept Filter → LLM1 (representamens) → LLM2 (description) → T2I model → (optional human eval). This flow ensures each step builds on the previous to embed metonymic association.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- LLM vs. human curation of representamens: scalable but model‑dependent; may miss culturally specific associations.
- Two visual styles (naturalistic/stylistic): increases diversity and tests robustness, but doubles generation cost and may affect VLM performance.
- Strict filtering reduces concept space (~2,000 from ~19,000), improving yield but excluding many interesting abstract concepts.
- CoT constraints vs. creative freedom: overly rigid instructions may produce unnatural scenes; the chosen balance (temperature 0.9, fail‑safe) was empirically effective.

**Failure signatures**:
- Representamen list contains non‑visual terms → vague/irrelevant description.
- Visual description includes target concept word → literal image (e.g., text in image, explicit object).
- Filtered concept too concrete (e.g., ‘cup’) → annotators rate as non‑metonymic.
- T2I disregards prompt details → image does not match described representamens.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Pipeline vs. baseline**: Human annotation of 1,000 images showed 84.3% metonymic for the pipeline vs. 41.2% for a simple prompt (Table 1, Section 3.3).
2. **Generalization across models**: Tested 5 LLMs × 4 T2I models on 100 concepts; all combinations yielded >75% metonymic rates, confirming framework robustness (Table 3, Section 5).
3. **ViMET benchmark evaluation**: Ran 8 VLMs on 2,000 multiple‑choice questions; best accuracy 65.9% vs. human 86.9%, revealing a 21% performance gap (Table 2, Section 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Component contribution analysis** – Each pipeline step’s individual impact is not isolated; future work needs ablation studies and step‑wise human evaluation.
2. **Automated metrics for representamen/description quality** – Current reliance on human evaluation is costly; embedding‑based similarity or multimodal consistency metrics validated against humans are needed.
3. **VLM performance on contextual associations** – VLMs score 54.5% on contextual vs. 76.3% on symbolic; error analysis and training experiments are required to determine whether data bias, reasoning depth, or representation limitations cause this gap.
4. **Bias in visual metonymy generation** – LLM‑generated associations may reinforce cultural stereotypes; frameworks to systematically detect, quantify, and mitigate representational harms are needed.
5. **Benchmark ambiguity handling** – Single‑answer accuracy may penalize valid alternative interpretations; multi‑annotator plausibility scores and similarity‑based metrics should be explored to better align evaluation with inherent ambiguity.

## Limitations
- ViMET benchmark’s exact concept list and image set are not publicly released, hindering exact replication.
- Distractor generation protocol (CLIP model variant, BERT similarity threshold, ConceptNet filtering steps) remains underspecified, making it difficult to assess question difficulty and validity.
- The pipeline relies on LLM‑generated associations, which may reflect Western/English‑centric biases; no systematic bias evaluation was conducted.

## Confidence
- Pipeline generates metonymic images (84.3% vs 41.2% baseline): **High**.
- VLM performance gap (21%): **High**.
- Robustness across LLM/T2I models: **Medium**.
- Semiotic‑grounded design rationale (necessity of each constraint): **Medium**.
- Cultural generalizability of associations: **Low** (due to LLM bias and lack of cross‑cultural evaluation).

## Next Checks
1. **Representamen Diversity Audit**: Sample 200 generated representamen lists; have human annotators rate each object’s visual concreteness and cultural specificity; quantify the proportion of non‑visual or culturally niche items.
2. **Distractor Challenge Test**: Re‑administer ViMET multiple‑choice questions to a new cohort of humans with an added “none of the above” option; a high selection rate would indicate insufficiently challenging distractors or unreliable concept evocation.
3. **Cross‑Cultural Pipeline Validation**: For a 100‑concept subset, use a non‑English LLM (e.g., Llama 3 in Spanish) and evaluate metonymic image success rate with annotators from that language/culture; tests the cultural dependency of LLM association knowledge.
