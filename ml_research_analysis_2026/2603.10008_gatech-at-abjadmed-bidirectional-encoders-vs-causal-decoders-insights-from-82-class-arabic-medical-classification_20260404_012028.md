# GATech at AbjadMed: Bidirectional Encoders vs. Causal Decoders: Insights from 82-Class Arabic Medical Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10008
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10008
- **Reference count:** 3
- **Primary result:** Fine-tuning AraBERTv2 with hybrid pooling and multi-sample dropout achieves a Macro-F1 of 0.3934 on the 82-class Arabic medical test set, significantly outperforming feature extraction from the Qwen 3B decoder (0.1278) and a Llama 3.3 70B re-ranking pipeline (0.3035).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of classifying Arabic medical queries into 82 fine-grained categories, a task complicated by extreme class imbalance and label noise. The proposed solution fine‑tunes the AraBERTv2 encoder with a hybrid pooling strategy (mean + attention) and multi‑sample dropout for regularization. The model achieves a Macro‑F1 score of 0.3934 on the test set, substantially beating alternative multilingual encoders and causal decoder‑based methods. The key insight is that bidirectional encoders, which capture global context, provide superior semantic compression for this high‑cardinality medical categorization task compared to larger causal decoders, which are biased toward sequential prediction and fail to integrate the full context needed for precise distinction among many classes.

## Method Summary
The method addresses an 82‑class Arabic medical text classification problem using a specialized encoder. The base model is `bert-base-arabertv02` (AraBERTv2), which is fine‑tuned. The architecture adds a **hybrid pooling layer** that concatenates mean‑pooled and attention‑pooled token vectors from the encoder’s last hidden state. This pooled representation is fed to a classification head with LayerNorm and GELU. For regularization, **Multi‑Sample Dropout (MSD)** applies five independent dropout masks (rates 0.1–0.3) to the pooled vector before the final dense layer; the resulting logits are averaged. Training uses label smoothing (ε=0.1) and the AdamW optimizer with a cosine learning rate scheduler and Layer‑wise Learning Rate Decay (factor 0.95). Inputs are tokenized with the AraBERTv2 WordPiece tokenizer and dynamically padded per batch.

## Key Results
- AraBERTv2 + hybrid pooling + MSD yields Macro‑F1 **0.3934** on the test set.
- Feature extraction from the Qwen 3B decoder (using last‑token embedding) achieves a much lower Macro‑F1 of **0.1278**.
- A hybrid pipeline that re‑ranks AraBERTv2 predictions with Llama 3.3 70B degrades performance to **0.3035**.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The hybrid pooling strategy (mean + attention) provides superior semantic representation for medical query classification compared to using only the [CLS] token or a single pooling method.
- **Mechanism:** The encoder outputs a sequence of token embeddings. Mean pooling averages all non‑padding tokens to capture global thematic content. Attention pooling uses a learned vector to compute attention weights over tokens, highlighting salient medical terms. Concatenating these two vectors yields a richer fixed‑size representation that combines overall topic with keyword focus, which is then fed to the classification head.
- **Core assumption:** Medical queries often contain dispersed diagnostic clues; a single [CLS] token may not reliably summarize all relevant information.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "enhanced with a hybrid pooling strategies, combining attention and mean representations".
  - [section] Section 3.2 "Hybrid Pooling Strategy" details the implementation and rationale.
  - [corpus] No direct counterpart in the retrieved corpus; the hybrid approach appears novel for Arabic medical classification.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Multi‑Sample Dropout (MSD) improves model robustness and generalization, especially for minority classes in a highly imbalanced dataset with label noise.
- **Mechanism:** During training, MSD applies several independent dropout masks (rates 0.1–0.3) to the pooled representation before the classification layer. Each masked version produces its own logits; the logits are averaged to compute loss. This acts as an internal ensemble of thinned networks, reducing variance and preventing over‑confidence on potentially mislabeled or majority‑class examples.
- **Core assumption:** Extreme class imbalance and label inconsistency cause models to overfit to majority patterns; regularizing via stochastic multi‑path inference smooths decision boundaries and stabilizes minority class learning.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "and multi‑sample dropout for robust regularization".
  - [section] Section 3.3 outlines the five parallel dropout paths and their rates.
  - [corpus] The corpus does not highlight MSD; it is a known technique from prior work (Inoue, 2020) and not a focus of neighboring papers.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Fine‑tuned bidirectional encoders (AraBERTv2) significantly outperform large causal decoders (Qwen, Llama) for fine‑grained Arabic medical text classification because they capture global context.
- **Mechanism:** Bidirectional encoders use self‑attention where each token can attend to all other tokens in both directions, yielding representations that integrate the entire sequence simultaneously. This holistic compression is ideal for discriminative tasks where sensitivity to subtle semantic boundaries matters. Causal decoders attend only to preceding tokens (autoregressive), so their final token embedding reflects a left‑to‑right history and lacks future context, making them less effective at summarizing a full query for classification.
- **Core assumption:** For high‑cardinality categorization with nuanced class definitions, bidirectional attention provides more precise semantic boundaries than the generative knowledge or parameter scale of causal decoders.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "Our findings demonstrate that specialized bidirectional encoders significantly outperform causal decoders in capturing the precise semantic boundaries required for fine‑grained medical text classification."
  - [section] Section 4.2 "The Semantic Limitations of Causal Decoders" explains the sequence bias and reports Macro‑F1 of Qwen 3B feature extraction (0.1278).
  - [corpus] Neighbor paper "Sequence Repetition Enhances Token Embeddings and Improves Sequence Labeling with Decoder‑only Language Models" underscores the difficulty of using decoder‑only models for tasks requiring bidirectional context, aligning with our conclusion.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Tokenizer (AraBERTv2 WordPiece) -> Encoder (AraBERTv2 base, 12‑layer Transformer) -> Hybrid Pooling (mean + attention) -> Classification Head (LayerNorm -> GELU -> Dense)
  -> Multi‑Sample Dropout (training only: 5 parallel masks) -> Loss (cross‑entropy with label smoothing ε=0.1)
  Optimizer: AdamW with LLRD decay=0.95, cosine LR scheduler, initial LR=2e‑5

- **Critical path (training):**
  1. Tokenize batch → input IDs, attention mask.
  2. Encoder forward → last hidden states (batch, seq_len, hidden_dim).
  3. Mask padding in token embeddings.
  4. Mean pooling: sum(non‑pad embeddings) / seq_len.
  5. Attention pooling: dot product with a learned vector → softmax over tokens → weighted sum.
  6. Concatenate mean and attention vectors → pooled representation (batch, 2*hidden_dim).
  7. For k=5: apply dropout mask to pooled representation → LayerNorm → GELU → dense → logits_k.
  8. Average logits across k samples.
  9. Compute smoothed cross‑entropy loss.
  10. Backpropagation with AdamW (LLRD) and cosine LR scheduling.
  Inference: steps 1–6 → single pass through head (no MSD) → logits.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Hybrid pooling doubles head input size, increasing parameters and risk of overfitting; mitigated by MSD and label smoothing.
  - MSD multiplies forward cost through the head by k (5×) but yields regularization comparable to ensembling at no inference overhead.
  - AraBERTv2 offers Arabic‑specific linguistic knowledge but may have limited medical lexicon; a multilingual encoder could broaden coverage but typically lowers performance as observed.
  - Label smoothing reduces over‑confidence on noisy labels but may slightly lower clean accuracy; ε=0.1 balances robustness and discriminative power.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Low minority‑class F1:** Macro‑F1 dragged down by a few classes; indicates insufficient regularization or attention ignoring rare cues. Remedy: increase dropout or consider class‑balanced resampling.
  - **Stuck training loss:** Learning rate may be too low or LLRD decay too aggressive; adjust base LR or decay factor.
  - **Overfitting** (train loss →0, dev loss ↑): Suggestive of memorizing label noise; increase MSD sample count or label smoothing ε.
  - **Attention weights uniform or degenerate:** The attention pooling fails to learn token importance; may stem from tiny dataset or poor initialization—check gradient norms.
  - **Decoder feature performance ~0.13:** As in Table 1; signals that raw decoder states are inadequate without task‑specific adaptation.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline:** Fine‑tune AraBERTv2 using only the [CLS] token, no MSD, no label smoothing. Record Macro‑F1 and per‑class scores.
  2. **Add hybrid pooling:** Replace [CLS] with concatenated mean+attention pooling. Compare improvement, especially on tail classes.
  3. **Add regularization:** Introduce Multi‑Sample Dropout (k=5) and label smoothing (ε=0.1). Expect further Macro‑F1 gain and more balanced per‑class performance; monitor training stability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the isolated contribution of each component (hybrid pooling, multi‑sample dropout, label smoothing) to the overall Macro‑F1 score, particularly for minority classes?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly state in Section 6.3 (Future Work) that "a more fine‑grained analysis remains valuable. Future work should include ablation studies that evaluate the individual impact of hybrid pooling, multi‑sample dropout, and label smoothing."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper presents a system combining all three techniques but does not disentangle their effects. Without ablation, it is impossible to determine which component is most responsible for the reported performance gain or how each affects robustness to class imbalance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled experimental evaluation where each technique (hybrid pooling, multi‑sample dropout, label smoothing) is systematically removed or replaced with a baseline (e.g., standard [CLS] pooling, single dropout, no label smoothing), with Macro‑F1 and per‑class F1 scores reported for the test set.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Which specific category pairs or groups exhibit the highest label noise and ambiguity, and how does this noise correlate with the model's error patterns (e.g., confusion matrix)?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors identify label noise qualitatively in Section 2.2 (e.g., "General Medicine" vs. "Dermatological diseases", "Sexual Health" vs. "Medicinal herbs") and use label smoothing to mitigate it. However, they do not quantify which categories are most affected or analyze the resultant confusion.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper mentions label noise as a challenge but provides no systematic analysis of its distribution or impact. Understanding the specific ambiguous boundaries is critical for diagnosing errors and improving label quality or model calibration.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed error analysis of the model's predictions on a validation set, including: (a) a pairwise confusion matrix highlighting frequently misclassed categories, (b) manual review of misclassified queries to categorize noise types (e.g., annotator inconsistency, semantic overlap), and (c) correlation between noise‑related errors and training sample frequency.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can causal decoders (e.g., Qwen, Llama) be effectively fine‑tuned for this high‑cardinality Arabic medical classification task, or are they fundamentally limited by their generative pre‑training objectives?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper only evaluates causal decoders via feature extraction (Qwen) or zero‑shot re‑ranking (Llama), both of which underperform. It attributes this to "sequence bias" and poor "semantic compression" (Section 4.2, 4.3) but never tests fine‑tuning the decoder's entire model on the task.
- **Why unresolved:** The conclusion that causal decoders are inferior is based on non‑fine‑tuned usage. It remains unknown whether task‑specific fine‑tuning could adapt their internal representations to capture global context for discrimination, potentially closing the performance gap with encoders.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An experiment where the Qwen 3B model is fully fine‑tuned (using the same training procedure as AraBERTv2) on the AbjadMed training set, with Macro‑F1 compared directly to the feature extraction baseline and the AraBERTv2 result. Additional probing of attention patterns could test if fine‑tuning mitigates the "sequence bias."

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does each element of the hybrid pooling strategy (mean vs. attention pooling) differentially impact performance across queries of varying length and medical specificity?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors concatenate mean and attention pooling (Section 3.2) but do not isolate their contributions. They hypothesize that mean pooling captures "global thematic summary" while attention pooling focuses on salient medical keywords, yet this is not empirically validated.
- **Why unresolved:** Without separating the two representations, it is unclear whether one dominates performance, if they are complementary, or if their effectiveness varies with query characteristics (e.g., short keyword‑heavy vs. long conversational queries). This limits interpretability and potential simplification.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation experiments using (1) only mean pooling, (2) only attention pooling, and (3) their concatenation, with results stratified by query length bins and by whether queries contain explicit medical terminology. Analysis of attention weights could also reveal if the model attends to clinically relevant terms.

## Limitations
- Hybrid pooling implementation details and critical hyperparameters (batch size, epochs, exact LLDR scheme) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- Decoder baselines were not fine‑tuned; only feature extraction or re‑ranking was used, so the conclusion that encoders are superior may not hold for fully adapted decoders.
- No ablation studies isolate the contribution of hybrid pooling, multi‑sample dropout, or label smoothing; the claimed mechanisms remain theoretical.
- The paper does not quantify the level of label noise or provide a detailed class distribution, making it hard to assess robustness claims fully.
- No statistical significance testing is reported for the Macro‑F1 differences between models.

## Confidence
- **Bidirectional encoders > causal decoders:** Medium (evidence from non‑fine‑tuned decoders; fine‑tuned comparison missing).
- **Hybrid pooling improves representation:** Medium (plausible and described, but unvalidated by ablation).
- **MSD enhances robustness:** Medium (known technique, not empirically shown here to be the cause of improvement).
- **Overall reported Macro‑F1 (0.3934):** Medium (reproducible in principle but with unspecified hyperparameters and dataset details).
