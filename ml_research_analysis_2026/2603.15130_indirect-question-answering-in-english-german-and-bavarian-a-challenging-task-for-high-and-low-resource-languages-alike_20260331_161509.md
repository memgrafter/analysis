# Indirect Question Answering in English, German and Bavarian: A Challenging Task for High- and Low-Resource Languages Alike

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15130
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15130
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art multilingual transformers fail to surpass majority class baselines on IQA, with severe overfitting and low macro-F1 scores across English, German, and Bavarian.

## Executive Summary
Indirect Question Answering (IQA) is a pragmatically complex task that involves classifying the polarity (e.g., yes, no, conditional) of an indirect response to a polar question. This study investigates IQA across a high-resource language (English), a standard mid-resource language (German), and a low-resource dialect (Bavarian). The authors create two datasets: a high-quality, hand-annotated test set (InQA+, 438 instances per language) and a larger, LLM-generated training set (GenIQA, 1,500 instances per language). Fine-tuning standard multilingual models (mBERT, XLM-R, mDeBERTa) yields uniformly poor performance, with no model exceeding the majority class baseline on the test set. Results highlight the profound difficulty of modeling pragmatic ambiguity, the challenges of low-resource dialect data, and the limitations of current encoder-only transformers for tasks requiring deep contextual inference.

## Method Summary
The study uses a fine-tuning paradigm on encoder-only multilingual transformers. Two primary datasets are employed: InQA+ (gold-standard, parallel test set) and GenIQA (synthetic training data generated via GPT-4o-mini with few-shot prompting). Models are evaluated on classification accuracy and macro-F1 score across three languages (EN, DE, BAR) and three label set granularities (6-class, 4-class remapped, 2-class yes/no). A hyperparameter search is conducted over learning rate, batch size, dropout, weight decay, and label smoothing, with model selection based on dev set accuracy and early stopping to combat overfitting.

## Key Results
- All models fail to beat the majority class baseline (40.6% accuracy) on the hand-annotated InQA+ test set, with best macro-F1 at only ~21.4% (XLM-R on English).
- Larger LLM-generated training data (GenIQA) improves macro-F1 over smaller high-quality data but does not improve test accuracy, and the Bavarian GenIQA data is of very low quality (native speaker dialectness rating: 1.1/3).
- Simplifying the label set to a binary yes/no task significantly boosts macro-F1 (up to 61.7%) but not accuracy, indicating models can learn a simpler signal but still struggle with the full pragmatic scope.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Human annotator disagreement directly introduces noise into the training data, impairing model learning.
- **Mechanism:** The moderate inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ = 0.53) for the full label set reflects the inherent perspectivism of pragmatic interpretation. Models trained on such data cannot learn clear decision boundaries, as the "ground truth" is not stable.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states no model surpassed baseline and cites κ=0.53; Section 3.2 reports low agreement for ambiguous labels; InQA+ includes context-dependent labels like *Lacking Context*.
- **Break condition:** A label set with unambiguous, context-independent definitions achieving high human agreement (κ > 0.8).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Standard multilingual transformers are fundamentally misaligned with the pragmatic reasoning required for IQA.
- **Mechanism:** These models, optimized for semantic and syntactic patterns via masked language modeling, lack mechanisms for pragmatic inference (implicature, speaker intent). They default to exploiting superficial cues or majority biases, failing to generalize.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes severe overfitting and low performance; Section 6.1 shows all scores below majority baseline; Section 7 cites related work where only very large decoder models showed promise.
- **Break condition:** Models explicitly designed/trained with pragmatic reasoning objectives or significantly larger decoder models with in-context learning.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Data constraints—small dataset size and low-quality LLM-generated dialect data—prevent models from acquiring sufficient patterns.
- **Mechanism:** IQA requires exposure to diverse pragmatic constructions. InQA+ (438) is too small. GenIQA (1,500) provides more data but has low labeling accuracy (65% EN, 55% BAR) and, for Bavarian, extremely poor dialect authenticity (rating 1.1/3). This noise prevents learning.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes GenIQA improved F1 but not accuracy and reports low BAR dialectness; Section 4.1 describes GenIQA BAR resembling colloquial German; Section 4.2 reports labeling accuracies; Section 6.2 states 1,500 is still too small.
- **Break condition:** Availability of large-scale (10k+), high-quality, manually annotated IQA datasets for multiple dialects, or LLM generation techniques guaranteeing pragmatic and dialectal authenticity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Pragmatic Ambiguity & Indirect Speech Acts**
  - **Why needed here:** IQA is about inferred speaker meaning, not literal truth. A single answer string (e.g., "I have plans") can map to different labels depending on unstated context.
  - **Quick check:** Can you annotate the polarity for "Is it raining? – My hair is a mess."? What context would change your label?
- **Concept: Human Label Variation**
  - **Why needed here:** The moderate IAA (κ=0.53) shows labels are perspectival, not objective. Training on such data means models mimic a noisy consensus.
  - **Quick check:** Why might annotators disagree on "You're not coming? – Do I have to be?" (Conditional Yes vs. Neither Yes nor No)?
- **Concept: Low-Resource Dialect Modeling**
  - **Why needed here:** Bavarian lacks standard orthography and digital presence. Models trained on Standard German may not transfer, and LLMs generate pseudo-dialect, compounding the challenge.
  - **Quick check:** What specific linguistic features differentiate authentic Bavarian from Standard German, using examples from Appendix D?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Raw Text (question, answer) -> Tokenizer (language-specific) -> Encoder Model (mBERT/XLM-R/mDeBERTa) -> Classification Head -> Probability Distribution over Labels -> Loss Calculation (Cross-Entropy) -> Parameter Update -> Evaluation Metrics (Accuracy, Macro-F1) on Fixed Test Set.
- **Critical path:**
  1. **Data Preparation:** Pre-filter OpenSubtitles, manual annotation (InQA+), LLM generation with few-shot prompt (GenIQA), dialect quality survey.
  2. **Model Configuration:** Select base model, define label set version, set hyperparameter search spaces.
  3. **Training & Validation:** Fine-tune on train split, monitor dev loss, select best checkpoint via dev accuracy.
  4. **Evaluation:** Test on InQA+, compute Accuracy/Macro-F1, analyze per-label performance and confusion matrices.
  5. **Analysis:** Compare across data sources, label sets, languages; investigate overfitting via train/dev scores.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Quality vs. Quantity:** Larger GenIQA improves F1 over smaller high-quality IndirectQA, but both fail to beat accuracy baseline. Quality seems key for avoiding majority collapse.
  - **Label Granularity:** Full 6-label set captures nuance but is ambiguous. *yesno* removes ambiguous cases, boosting F1 but losing task scope.
  - **Model Size vs. Resources:** Larger models (e.g., flan-t5-xxl) may help but are computationally prohibitive; base-sized models used for feasibility.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Symptom:** Test accuracy ≤ majority baseline (40.6%). **Cause:** Model collapsed to predicting majority class due to noisy labels or insufficient signal.
  - **Symptom:** Large train-dev accuracy gap (>5 p.p.). **Cause:** Severe overfitting to spurious patterns in small/noisy training data.
  - **Symptom:** High Macro-F1 but low Accuracy. **Cause:** Model recalls minority classes but still mostly predicts majority (check confusion matrix).
  - **Symptom:** Performance drops EN → DE → BAR. **Cause:** Cross-lingual transfer failure compounded by BAR data quality issues.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Primary data quality/quantity comparison:** Fine-tune mBERT on IndirectQA EN (small, high-quality) vs. GenIQA EN (large, low-quality). Evaluate on InQA+ EN. Expect: GenIQA higher F1, both fail to beat majority baseline.
  2. **Label set simplification:** Train on *yesno* versions of GenIQA. Evaluate on *yesno* InQA+. Expect: Macro-F1 jumps (e.g., ~61% for BAR), Accuracy near majority baseline (~64.6%).
  3. **Bavarian data sanity check:** Sample 100 GenIQA BAR instances, have native speaker re-annotate labels and rate dialect authenticity. Expect: Label accuracy ~55%, dialectness ~1.1/3, confirming data is too noisy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What is the minimum dataset size required to train effective IQA models for both high-resource and low-resource languages, and how does data quality interact with size?
  - **Basis in paper:** The paper compares small high-quality data (615 instances) with larger low-quality data (1,500 instances) and finds larger data improves F1 but not accuracy, citing related work suggesting 6,000–35,000 instances might be necessary.
  - **Why unresolved:** Exact threshold and quality-size interaction are unknown; no controlled experiments varying both.
- **Open Question 2:** How can we improve the generation of authentic and natural-sounding IQA data for low-resource dialects like Bavarian using LLMs?
  - **Basis in paper:** GenIQA BAR received low dialectness (1.1/3) and authenticity (1.4/3) ratings; many instances resemble colloquial German more than genuine Bavarian.
  - **Why unresolved:** Reasons for poor dialect generation not systematically analyzed; no solutions proposed.
- **Open Question 3:** Do larger decoder-only language models inherently possess better pragmatic understanding for IQA compared to smaller encoder models, and can they be fine-tuned to overcome the performance deficits seen with encoder models?
  - **Basis in paper:** Authors use small encoder models and observe poor performance; they cite Sravanthi et al. (2024) who achieved better results with very large models (flan-t5-xxl, llama-2-70b-chat) on a related task.
  - **Why unresolved:** No direct comparison with large decoder models in this study; unclear if scale alone solves IQA challenges.
- **Open Question 4:** Can refining the IQA label set and annotation guidelines to better capture pragmatic nuances reduce label ambiguity and improve model performance in multilingual settings?
  - **Basis in paper:** Simplified label sets (yesno) increased human IAA (κ=0.70 vs. 0.53) and model macro-F1 but not accuracy; the optimal balance of clarity vs. informativeness is unknown.
  - **Why unresolved:** No systematic creation/testing of intermediate label sets; impact on generalizability across languages unclear.

## Limitations
- **Human disagreement ambiguity:** Moderate IAA (κ=0.53) may stem from task ambiguity or label guidelines; unclear if noise or legitimate perspectivism.
- **Data constraints:** InQA+ (438/ln) is small; GenIQA (1500/ln) suffers low quality (label accuracy ~65% EN, ~55% BAR; BAR dialectness 1.1/3).
- **Methodological gaps:** Training details (epochs, optimizer, early stopping) only in unprovided Appendix E; BAR tokenization specifics not described.

## Confidence
- **Human disagreement → noise impairs learning:** **Medium**. Evidence from IAA and model failure, but cannot exclude models learning annotator bias instead of failing due to noise.
- **Standard transformers misaligned for pragmatics:** **Medium**. Uniformly low scores, but confounded by data quality/size; larger models may help.
- **Data constraints primary bottleneck:** **High**. Direct quality metrics and GenIQA’s F1 improvement over smaller high-quality data strongly support this.
