# Frequency-aware Adaptive Contrastive Learning for Sequential Recommendation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2601.17057
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17057
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: FACL consistently outperforms state-of-the-art contrastive methods, achieving up to 3.8% higher HR@10 and NDCG@10 while reducing performance disparity for low-frequency items and sparse user behaviors.

## Executive Summary
Contrastive learning for sequential recommendation often suffers from data augmentation bias, where low-frequency items and sparse user sequences are over-perturbed, degrading long-tail performance. This paper introduces Frequency-aware Adaptive Contrastive Learning (FACL), a dual-level framework that adaptively adjusts augmentation at both the item (micro) and sequence (macro) levels. Micro-level adaptive perturbation scales augmentation probability by item frequency, protecting rare items. Macro-level reweighting assigns higher training weights to sequences containing many rare items or having short lengths, countering sparsity. Integrated with a backbone sequential recommender (e.g., SASRec), FACL combines next-item prediction loss with a contrastive loss weighted by these adaptive coefficients. Evaluated on five benchmarks (Beauty, Sports, ML-1M, ML-20M, Yelp), FACL consistently surpasses state-of-the-art data and model augmentation methods, achieving significant gains in HR@10 and NDCG@10 (up to 3.8%) and notably narrowing the performance gap for long-tail items and users.

## Method Summary
FACL augments user sequences before feeding them into a sequential recommendation model (typically SASRec). The augmentation process operates in two adaptive stages. First, at the micro level, each item’s probability of being dropped, substituted, or inserted is scaled by the ratio of its global frequency to the sequence’s average frequency, thereby reducing alterations for rare items. For subsequence-level operations (crop, reorder), a subsequence is accepted with probability proportional to the ratio of its minimum item frequency to the global average, ensuring rare items are retained. Second, at the macro level, each sequence receives a weight λ(Su) based on its average item frequency and length: sequences with rarer items or shorter histories are up-weighted. During training, the total loss for a user sequence combines the next-item prediction loss and the InfoNCE contrastive loss (between two augmented views), both multiplied by λ(Su). This design preserves subtle signals from long-tail items and sparse behaviors while maintaining augmentation diversity. Hyperparameters γ (target augmentation strength), η (subsequence length ratio), β (reweighting exponent), and τ (temperature) are tuned on validation data.

## Key Results
- FACL achieves state-of-the-art performance on five datasets, with relative improvements of up to 3.8% in HR@10 and NDCG@10 over strong baselines like CL4SRec, RCL, and CoSeRec.
- The method dramatically reduces the performance gap between high- and low-frequency items and users, confirming robust handling of long-tail scenarios.
- Ablation experiments show that removing either the micro-level adaptive perturbation or the macro-level reweighting leads to significant performance degradation, underscoring the synergy of the dual-level design.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Micro-level adaptive perturbation** protects low-frequency items by scaling augmentation probabilities inversely with item frequency. For a sequence Su, each item v has perturbation probability ρ(v)=γ·f(v)/¯f(Su), where f(v) is global frequency and ¯f(Su) the sequence’s average frequency. rarer items thus have lower probability of being altered. This is motivated by the assumption that rare items have fragile co-occurrence patterns easily destroyed by random perturbations. Evidence includes frequency-binned HR improvements in experiments and the formulation in Section 3.1. The mechanism fails if γ is too high (insufficient protection) or if frequency distribution becomes uniform.

**Macro-level reweighting** amplifies the contribution of sequences that are either rich in rare items or short. Weight λ(Su) = (¯f/¯f(Su) · ¯l/|Su|)^β, where ¯f and ¯l are global averages. Sequences with low average frequency or short length receive higher weight, countering their underrepresentation. The core assumption is that such sequences carry informative long-tail preferences. Ablation studies and the loss integration in Section 3.3 confirm its benefit. Break conditions include excessive β causing overemphasis on noisy short sequences or shifts in length distribution.

**Dual-level synergy** arises because micro-level adjustments prevent corruption of rare items within sequences, while macro-level weighting ensures those sequences are not drowned out by frequent-item sequences during optimization. The combined loss L = Σ_u λ(Su)(L_rec + τ L_cl) integrates both effects. The paper’s ablation (Section 4.3) shows that removing either module hurts performance, validating their complementary roles. If one module is misconfigured (e.g., γ too low, β too high), the synergy may turn antagonistic, leading to instability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Contrastive Learning for Sequential Recommendation**: FACL builds on contrastive learning to learn robust sequence representations by creating positive pairs from augmented views of the same sequence and contrasting them with negatives using InfoNCE loss. *Why needed*: Understanding how augmentation and contrastive losses improve Next-Item Prediction beyond supervised learning is prerequisite. *Quick check*: How does creating two augmented views of the same sequence help the model learn user intent beyond next-item prediction?
- **Long-Tail Distribution in Recommendation**: Recommendation data often follows a power-law, where many items have few interactions. Low-frequency items suffer more from random augmentation because their sparse co-occurrence patterns are easily destroyed. *Why needed*: Grasping the impact of long-tail bias on augmentation informs FACL’s frequency-aware adjustments. *Quick check*: Why do low-frequency items suffer more from random data augmentation than high-frequency items?
- **Sequence Representation Learning (e.g., SASRec)**: FACL augments sequences before encoding via a backbone model like SASRec, which uses embedding layers, positional encoding, and a Transformer encoder to capture sequential dependencies. *Why needed*: Knowing where augmentation integrates and how self-attention works is essential to understand how perturbations affect learned representations. *Quick check*: In a Transformer-based SR model, how does the self-attention mechanism capture sequential dependencies, and where could augmentation disrupt this?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: User sequence Su → Frequency statistics (global f(v), ¯l) → Micro-augmentation module (adaptive drop/substitute/insert/crop/reorder) → Two augmented views S(1)u, S(2)u → Backbone SR encoder (SASRec: embedding + Transformer) → Representations h(1)u, h(2)u, hu → Loss module (L_rec on original Su, L_cl on views) → Macro-reweighting (λ(Su)) applied to loss → Weighted loss Σ_u λ(Su)(L_rec+τL_cl) → Gradient update.

**Critical path**: Sequence → micro-augmentation (frequency-aware) → encode → contrastive loss + rec loss → macro-weight application → gradient update. Frequency stats are looked up externally.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Micro*: Stronger protection (smaller γ) preserves rare items but may reduce augmentation diversity; γ too large reintroduces bias.
- *Macro*: Higher β amplifies sparse sequences but risks overfitting to noise in very short sequences; β=0 disables reweighting.
- *Computational*: Adaptive probabilities add O(sequence length) overhead, negligible compared to contrastive loss O(batch²).

**Failure signatures**:
- Low-frequency item HR@10 drops relative to high-frequency (micro failing).
- Overall performance degrades especially on sparse datasets (macro mis-weighting).
- Training loss becomes unstable if λ(Su) varies extremely (e.g., β too high).
- Contrastive loss collapses (all similarities → 1) if views too similar or τ mis-set.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Overall comparison (RQ1): Run FACL vs. baselines (CL4SRec, RCL, CoSeRec, etc.) on all five datasets; expect FACL to achieve highest HR@10/NDCG@10, with largest margins on sparse datasets like Beauty and Yelp.
2. Ablation study (RQ2): Remove micro-level adaptation (w/o adaptive aug.) or macro-level reweighting (w/o λ); expect significant performance drop (~1-2% absolute) versus full FACL.
3. Frequency bias analysis (RQ4): Bin test items/users by frequency; plot HR@10 per bin. Expect FACL to show the smallest gap between low- and high-frequency bins compared to baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can item semantic content be integrated into FACL's micro-level adaptive perturbation to better preserve truly informative rare items, rather than relying solely on frequency? *Basis*: The authors' LLM-based augmentation variant underperformed frequency-based adjustment (Table 4), but only one prompting strategy was tested; other semantic features (e.g., pre-trained embeddings) were not explored.
- **Open Question 2**: Can the benefits of macro-level reweighting be achieved by simply oversampling such sequences during training, or does loss reweighting provide unique advantages? *Basis*: FACL uses a loss-level coefficient λ(Su) but does not compare with oversampling; the two approaches may differ in optimization dynamics and interaction with contrastive learning.
- **Open Question 3**: Does combining FACL's adaptive data augmentation with model augmentation methods (e.g., DuoRec’s same-target pairs) yield additive improvements, or do the two approaches conflict? *Basis*: FACL is compared with model augmentation baselines but no combination is explored; the complementary effects are unknown.
- **Open Question 4**: What is the theoretical foundation for FACL's frequency-adaptive perturbation? Does it minimize an upper bound on contrastive learning error or maximize a frequency-aware mutual information objective? *Basis*: The design is motivated empirically without theoretical analysis; a generalization bound linking augmentation probabilities to frequency would clarify when and why it works.

## Limitations
- **Hybrid correlation score undefined**: The exact computation for substitute/insert operators (referred to as "hybrid correlation score") is not detailed; the authors likely adapted CoSeRec, but implementation details may vary, affecting reproducibility and exact behavior.
- **Evaluation protocol ambiguity**: The precise train/validation/test splits and negative sampling numbers are not specified; assuming standard leave-one-out may not match the authors' setup, risking unfair comparisons.
- **Static frequency statistics**: Global item frequencies are precomputed and fixed, which may not handle dataset drift (e.g., new items) and could become stale in streaming scenarios.

## Confidence
- **Micro-level adaptive perturbation**: Medium (empirical evidence supports protection of rare items, but core assumption not isolated causally).
- **Macro-level reweighting**: Medium (ablation shows benefit, but weighting formula is heuristic and may be suboptimal).
- **Dual-level synergy**: High (consistent gains across five datasets and ablations confirm necessity of both components).
- **Overall superiority over SOTA**: High (rigorous experiments with multiple benchmarks support the claim).
