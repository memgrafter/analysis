# Modeling and Benchmarking Spoken Dialogue Rewards with Modality and Colloquialness

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.14889  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14889  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: SDiaReward-7B achieves state-of-the-art pairwise preference accuracy (96.61% modality, 97.20% colloquialness, 96.70% macro) on ESDR-Bench.

## Executive Summary
Spoken dialogue systems often struggle with two key evaluation gaps: modality (prosody and emotion) and colloquialness (natural speech vs. written scripts). This paper introduces SDiaReward, an end-to-end multi-turn reward model trained on SDiaReward-Dataset, a novel collection of 13,356 episode-level preference pairs explicitly designed to address these gaps. Using a multimodal LLM backbone (Qwen2.5-Omni) with mean pooling and center loss regularization, SDiaReward-7B attains near-perfect pairwise preference accuracy on the dedicated ESDR-Bench benchmark: 96.61% on modality and 97.20% on colloquialness, with an overall macro accuracy of 96.70%. These results far surpass general-purpose audio LLMs (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro at 72.63% modality micro accuracy). Human evaluation confirms 83.5% agreement with model predictions, and the model demonstrates robust generalization across domains (94.91% modality macro).

## Method Summary
SDiaReward is a reward model for end-to-end spoken dialogue systems. It processes multi-turn episodes composed of interleaved audio and transcript inputs via the Qwen2.5-Omni multimodal LLM backbone. The model extracts mean-pooled representations from the final hidden states and passes them through a linear head to produce a scalar reward. Training uses pairwise preference data from SDiaReward-Dataset (11,630 training pairs), constructed to isolate modality (human vs. TTS audio with matched text) and colloquialness (written-script vs. spontaneous rewrites). The loss combines a Bradley-Terry pairwise sigmoid loss with a center loss term (λ=1e-2) to stabilize reward scales across heterogeneous domains. Optimization employs AdamW (lr=2e-5, weight decay 0.05), bf16 mixed precision, FlashAttention-2, and DeepSpeed ZeRO-2. Evaluation is performed on ESDR-Bench, a stratified benchmark covering wild, semi-wild, scripted, and colloquial subsets, reporting both micro-weighted and macro-averaged pairwise preference accuracy.

## Key Results
- Highest pairwise accuracy: 96.61% (modality), 97.20% (colloquialness), 96.70% macro on ESDR-Bench.  
- Outperforms strong baselines by large margins (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro: 72.63% modality micro).  
- Human-model alignment: 83.5% agreement on a sample of 75 dialogues.  
- Robust cross-domain performance: 94.91% modality macro, with 7B model showing minimal overfitting.  
- The model implicitly calibrates rewards across domains, capturing relative conversational expressiveness rather than merely detecting synthesis artifacts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Learning Colloquialness from Multi-Turn Context
Multi-turn episodic pairwise supervision allows the model to learn colloquialness preferences that generalize across stylistic cues. Preference pairs differ only in style (written vs. spoken rewrites) while acoustics are matched via identical TTS. The full dialogue context provides discourse-level cues (e.g., turn-taking patterns, pragmatic markers) that are associated with higher rewards for colloquial flow. High accuracy on the colloquialness subset (97.20%) despite controlled acoustics confirms that the model exploits contextual stylistic features rather than acoustic artifacts. The core assumption is that colloquialness is a distributed property best captured at the episode level. Break condition would be a significant drop in colloquialness accuracy under acoustic matching; the observed success indicates the mechanism holds.

### Mechanism 2: Discriminating Modality via Content-Matched Audio
By pairing authentic human speech with high-quality TTS outputs that preserve lexical content and speaker coherence, the model is forced to discriminate paralinguistic naturalness (prosody, emotion) beyond semantic content. The identical text ensures that the primary difference lies in acoustic realization. General-purpose audio LLMs perform near chance on this task, whereas SDiaReward achieves 96.61% accuracy, indicating it has learned to recognize subtle prosodic variations, breathiness, micro-pauses, and emotional expressivity. A break condition would be if accuracy correlated with noise/cleanliness metrics rather than human naturalness judgments; the high accuracy suggests true paralinguistic understanding.

### Mechanism 3: Center Loss Calibrates Reward Scales Across Domains
Heterogeneous recording conditions (wild podcasts vs. scripted studio sessions) cause absolute reward scales to drift, making cross-domain comparisons unreliable. The center loss term (L_center) penalizes the squared mean of chosen/rejected rewards, pulling the overall distribution toward zero. This stabilizes the scale without altering relative orderings within a domain. Without center loss, the mean reward drifts above 5.0; with it, the mean settles around 0.32. Domain-specific medians may shift (e.g., scripted data yields lower absolute scores), but separation between chosen and rejected responses remains. A break condition would be reduced pairwise accuracy or failure to eliminate domain offsets; slight accuracy drop occurs for the 3B model but the 7B model maintains both stability and performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Qwen2.5-Omni backbone → mean pooling → linear head → scalar reward (+ center loss regularization).  
**Critical path:** Raw multi-turn audio → LLM forward pass → mean pooling over sequence → MLP → scalar reward. Turn order and speaker context must be preserved.  
**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Mean pooling provides stable, holistic representations; attention pooling may give higher peak accuracy but is more distribution‑dependent. Last‑token pooling failed.  
- Model capacity: 7B outperforms 3B on semi‑wild data, indicating need for larger capacity to handle acoustic variability.  
- Center loss (λ=1e‑2) stabilizes reward scale but may slightly reduce accuracy on some subsets.  
**Failure signatures:**  
- Score drift without center loss (mean reward >5.0).  
- Large micro–macro accuracy gap (e.g., 3B: 88.62% vs. 79.20%) signals overfitting to dominant “Wild” features.  
- Baselines with near‑chance modality accuracy (50–72%) indicate modality blindness.  
**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Ablate pooling strategy: compare mean, last-token, attention pooling; mean should yield lowest variance and competitive accuracy.  
2. Domain generalization test: evaluate on ESDR‑Bench stratified splits; a significant micro‑macro drop indicates overfitting.  
3. Score distribution analysis: plot chosen/rejected rewards per source (“Wild”, “Scripted”, etc.); expect clear separation but potential domain‑specific medians; overlap suggests failure to learn relative ranking.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Enhancing robustness with diverse speech and synthesis engines** – The current dataset relies heavily on in‑the‑wild recordings and a single dialogue‑TTS system. Incorporating broader acted corpora and multiple TTS engines could improve cross‑domain generalization and human‑judgment correlation.
2. **Capturing fine‑grained subjective preferences at scale** – Human evaluation was limited to 75 samples, leaving many nuanced aspects (emotional authenticity, turn‑taking) unexplored. Large‑scale multi‑annotator studies are needed to elicit detailed preference dimensions and incorporate them into reward training.
3. **Developing domain‑invariant reward modeling objectives** – The model exhibits domain‑specific score biases (e.g., lower absolute scores for scripted data). Techniques such as domain adversarial training or risk pooling could yield consistent, well‑calibrated scores across heterogeneous sources.
4. **Mitigating reward hacking in RL optimization** – Learned rewards may be exploited via shortcuts (e.g., focusing on spurious acoustic cues). Robust RL algorithms with uncertainty estimation and human‑in‑the‑loop validation are necessary to ensure stable optimization of end‑to‑end spoken dialogue systems.

## Limitations
- Access to the SDiaReward-Dataset and ESDR-Bench splits is not guaranteed, and exact implementation details (e.g., reward head architecture, tokenization) require code release, hindering full reproduction.  
- Potential overfitting to the dominant “Wild” subset and limited human evaluation (75 samples) introduce uncertainties about generalization and alignment.  
- Long‑term stability of center‑loss scaling across truly unseen domains remains untested.

## Confidence
- **Performance accuracy:** High – hyperparameters fully specified, strong baselines, human agreement reported.  
- **Mechanism (multi‑turn stylistic learning):** Medium – controlled data construction supports claim, but direct probing of contextual cues is absent.  
- **Generalization across domains:** Medium – macro scores are decent but stratification details and low‑resource performance opaque.  
- **Center loss stabilization:** High – clear ablations and training stability metrics demonstrate its effect.
