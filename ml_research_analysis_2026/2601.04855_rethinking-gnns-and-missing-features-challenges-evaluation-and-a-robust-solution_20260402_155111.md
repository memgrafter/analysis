# Rethinking GNNs and Missing Features: Challenges, Evaluation and a Robust Solution
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04855
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04855
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A simple method (GNNmim) that concatenates a missingness mask to zero-padded features achieves competitive or superior robustness across datasets, missingness types, and distribution shifts, revealing that lightweight assumption-free approaches can effectively address missing features when evaluated on appropriate dense benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Graph neural networks (GNNs) handling missing node features are often evaluated on sparse benchmarks (e.g., CORA) where missingness causes negligible information loss, creating an illusion of robustness. This paper shows that high sparsity bounds the mutual information loss, delaying performance degradation until extreme missingness (>90%). To expose weaknesses, the authors introduce dense datasets (SYNTHETIC, AIR, ELECTRIC, TADPOLE) and realistic missingness mechanisms (label-dependent MCAR, feature- and class-dependent MNAR, and distribution shifts). Their solution, GNNmim, simply concatenates a binary missingness mask to zero-padded features and processes them with a standard GNN. Experiments demonstrate that on dense data, performance degrades earlier and GNNmim remains competitive or best across all scenarios, highlighting the need for proper evaluation and the effectiveness of a lightweight, assumption-free design.

## Method Summary
The proposed method, GNNmim, operates as follows: for a graph with node feature matrix **X** (with missing entries) and adjacency **A**, first generate a binary mask **M** where *M<sub>ij</sub>=1* if *X<sub>ij</sub>* is missing and *0* otherwise. Replace missing entries in **X** with zeros to obtain **X<sub>zero</sub>**. Then concatenate **X<sub>zero</sub>** and **M** along the feature dimension to form augmented features **X<sub>aug</sub>**. These are fed into any standard GNN layer (e.g., GCN, GraphSAGE, GIN). The model is trained transductively (except inductive tests) using cross-entropy loss, with hyperparameters (layer type, depth, learning rate, weight decay) selected via grid search on a validation set. No imputation is performed; the mask explicitly informs the network which values are missing, allowing it to ignore arbitrary placeholders without assuming any missingness distribution.

## Key Results
- On traditional sparse benchmarks (CORA, CITESEER, PUBMED), all models—including simple zero‑padding—remain robust until missingness exceeds 90%, confirming the sparsity illusion.
- On newly introduced dense datasets, performance degrades at much lower missingness rates (≈30–50%), revealing weaknesses in many existing methods under realistic mechanisms (LD‑MCAR, FD‑MNAR, CD‑MNAR) and distribution shifts.
- GNNmim achieves competitive or superior F1 scores across all evaluated scenarios, often outperforming more complex imputation‑based baselines (e.g., GSPN, GOODIE, PCFI) without relying on MAR assumptions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Sparsity bounds information loss
High feature sparsity in traditional benchmarks severely limits the information loss caused by missing features, making all models appear robust and preventing meaningful comparison. Theorem 2 provides a mutual information bound: Δ = I(Y; ˜X) - I(Y;X) ≤ 0 and Δ ≥ -n d μ h²(E[s(X)]). When sparsity s(X) is high (e.g., BoW/TF‑IDF with >90% zeros), h²(E[s(X)]) is tiny, so Δ ≈ 0 unless missingness rate μ is extreme. Thus performance degrades only for μ > 90%, creating an illusion of robustness.

### Mechanism 2: New missingness mechanisms expose weaknesses
Evaluating GNNs only under MCAR missingness fails to expose weaknesses under realistic feature‑ or label‑dependent missingness (MNAR). The paper defines four mechanisms: LD‑MCAR (missingness probability proportional to feature‑label mutual information), FD‑MNAR (extreme feature values more likely missing), CD‑MNAR (features that strongly predict a class more likely missing), and S‑MCAR (entire nodes masked). These create correlations between missingness and data, violating MAR assumptions and testing model adaptability. Results show that many methods degrade earlier on dense data under these mechanisms.

### Mechanism 3: GNNmim’s mask encoding avoids MAR assumptions
GNNmim achieves robustness because it explicitly encodes missingness via a binary mask concatenated to zero-padded features, allowing the GNN to learn P(Y|X<sub>obs</sub>, M) directly without imputation. Zero values are arbitrary placeholders; the mask tells the network which entries are missing, so it can ignore them during message passing. No assumption about the missingness distribution is required, making it effective under both MAR and MNAR. Simplicity avoids misspecification risks of imputation models.

## Foundational Learning
- **Missing Data Mechanisms (MCAR, MAR, MNAR)**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s theoretical analysis and evaluation hinge on these definitions; understanding them explains why assumptions matter and what GNNmim avoids.  
  *Quick check:* If a model assumes MCAR during training but the true test mechanism is FD‑MNAR, what type of error is likely? (Answer: The model’s imputation or marginalization will be biased because missingness depends on unobserved feature values.)

- **Graph Neural Networks (Message Passing)**  
  *Why needed:* All baselines and GNNmim are built on GNNs. Knowing how node features are aggregated (e.g., sum, mean, attention) explains why missing features disrupt learning and how the mask can be integrated.  
  *Quick check:* In a GraphSAGE layer, how would a zero‑padded feature (with mask=1) affect the aggregated representation of a neighbor? (Answer: Without the mask, the zero contributes to the sum/mean; with the mask, the network can learn to treat it as “ignore”.)

- **Mutual Information and Binary Entropy**  
  *Why needed:* Theorem 2 uses the binary entropy h²(u) to bound information loss; this connection justifies the focus on feature sparsity.  
  *Quick check:* If a binary feature column has sparsity 0.95, what is h²(0.95) and what does it imply about the potential loss when that column becomes missing? (Answer: h²(0.95) ≈ 0.286 bits, very small; thus losing that column yields little information loss.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input features X (with missing entries) -> Generate binary mask M -> Zero-pad X to get X_zero -> Concatenate [X_zero, M] -> GNN encoder (e.g., GCN/GraphSAGE/GIN) -> Node embeddings -> Linear classifier -> Predictions.

- **Critical path:**  
  Correct alignment of M with X_zero; mismatches lead to conditioning on wrong bits. Consistency of preprocessing between training and inference (same mask creation). GNN architecture selection (the paper finds dataset-dependent best encoder). Proper hyperparameter tuning (depth 1–3 layers) to avoid over-smoothing.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  *Zero vs other defaults:* Zero is parameter‑free and works well with mask; but if features are normalized, zeros may be far from the distribution—consider normalizing after padding. *Mask placement:* Input‑level concatenation is simplest; alternative (e.g., modifying aggregation to weight by mask) may add capacity but also complexity. *Depth:* The paper uses 1–3 layers; deeper networks may over‑propagate zero placeholders.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  Performance similar to plain zero‑imputation (no mask) → the network ignores the mask (check gradients, training accuracy). Collapse on FD‑MNAR/CD‑MNAR while others drop less → may indicate the GNN struggles to use mask in MNAR contexts. High variance across seeds → adjust dropout or learning rate. No weakness on sparse benchmarks → likely evaluating on unsuitable data; switch to a dense dataset.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline replication:** On SYNTHETIC, vary μ under S‑MCAR and plot F1 curves for GNNmim vs. GNNmi, GNNzero, PCFI, etc. Replicate Figure 1.  
  2. **Mechanism sensitivity:** Train each model under LD‑MCAR, FD‑MNAR, CD‑MNAR (μ≈0.5). Compute AUC over μ range (like Figure 2). Expect GNNmim to show balanced robustness.  
  3. **Distribution shift (R2):** Train on FD‑MNAR with μ_tr=0.5, test under U‑MCAR with μ_te∈{0,0.25,0.5}. Compare F1 drop to i.i.d. (R1) baseline. Repeat with CD‑MNAR training.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What design principles should guide the creation of large-scale graph benchmarks for missing feature robustness evaluation, beyond current criteria of low sparsity and feature-structure complementarity?  
  *Basis:* The paper identifies limitations of existing benchmarks and introduces criteria for suitable datasets (low sparsity, feature-structure separability and complementarity), but notes that "no existing large-scale graph datasets simultaneously meet all these criteria." This points to an open challenge in benchmark design.  
  *Why unresolved:* The new datasets introduced are small (hundreds to thousands of nodes), limiting scalability studies and generalizability. Larger benchmarks are needed to assess whether robustness trends hold at scale and to serve as standard evaluation platforms.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Development of a suite of large-scale (>10,000 nodes), dense-feature graph datasets with documented missingness mechanisms and validated feature-structure relationships, similar to how ogbn-arxiv or ogbn-products serve as standards for other graph tasks.

- **Open Question 2:** How can missingness mechanisms incorporate graph structural dependencies, and what is the impact of such MNAR processes on the relative performance of GNN architectures for missing features?  
  *Basis:* The authors frame future work: "Another promising direction concerns the development of more realistic MNAR mechanisms, potentially incorporating graph-specific dependencies where missingness is influenced by structural properties of the graph itself." Their current FD‑MNAR and CD‑MNAR only depend on feature values/labels.  
  *Why unresolved:* All current evaluation mechanisms treat missingness as independent of graph topology. However, in real-world graphs (e.g., social networks, sensor networks), missingness might correlate with node degree, community structure, or network dynamics. Understanding such effects is crucial for robust deployment.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Empirical evaluation of models under MNAR mechanisms where missingness probabilities are functions of structural attributes (e.g., higher missingness for peripheral nodes, or within certain communities), combined with theoretical analysis of identifiability and learning bounds under such mechanisms.

- **Open Question 3:** Why does the Missing Indicator Method (MIM), theoretically unnecessary under MAR assumptions, consistently outperform more complex models that explicitly model feature distributions across diverse missingness types?  
  *Basis:* Theorem 1 shows that under feature-MAR and label-MAR, the missingness mask provides no theoretical advantage. Yet experiments show GNNmim (MIM) achieving competitive or superior F1 scores across all datasets, missingness types, and shifts, while models like GCNmf and GSPN that model Pθ often underperform.  
  *Why unresolved:* The empirical success of MIM contradicts the theoretical ideal. Possible explanations include: (a) real missingness mechanisms violate MAR; (b) MIM provides implicit regularization; (c) complex models overfit or suffer from optimization difficulties; or (d) evaluation metrics may not fully capture all aspects of robustness. The paper does not investigate these hypotheses.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments with synthetic data where MAR holds exactly, comparing MIM and other methods; ablation studies isolating the effect of the mask; and theoretical analysis of how deviations from MAR affect the relative risks of different modeling strategies.

- **Open Question 4:** What architectural or training modifications can improve GNN robustness to missingness distribution shifts between training and test data, a scenario common in real deployments?  
  *Basis:* Section 4 introduces R2 regime for missingness distribution shift and shows that all models degrade when train and test missingness mechanisms differ, even when test missingness is milder. GNNmim is relatively better but still affected.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper demonstrates the problem but does not provide solutions beyond using GNNmim. Real-world applications often experience such shifts (e.g., historical incomplete data vs. modern complete sensors), so robustness to shifts is essential.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Proposals of new training objectives (e.g., invariant risk minimization, domain adaptation), augmentation strategies that simulate diverse missingness during training, or architectural changes that make the model's reliance on the mask more adaptable. Evaluation would involve systematically measuring performance across shift strengths.

- **Open Question 5:** Do the paper's conclusions regarding the superiority of simple MIM-based approaches generalize to other graph learning tasks beyond node classification, such as link prediction or graph classification?  
  *Basis:* The study focuses exclusively on node classification on attributed graphs. The problem of missing features may manifest differently in tasks where the prediction target involves relationships between nodes or the entire graph.  
  *Why unresolved:* Different tasks may have different sensitivity to missing features. For example, link prediction might rely more on local neighborhoods, while graph classification aggregates over the whole graph. The theoretical properties of MIM under different missingness assumptions may also vary.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic replication of the experimental protocol on standard benchmarks for link prediction (e.g., FB15k-237 with missing entity features) and graph classification (e.g., PROTEINS with missing node attributes), comparing MIM-based and other methods under the same missingness mechanisms and shifts.

## Limitations
- The theoretical bound (Theorem 2) assumes binary features and MCAR/MAR; its applicability to non‑binary or other missingness mechanisms is less certain.
- The new missingness mechanisms (LD‑MCAR, FD‑MNAR, CD‑MNAR) are parameterized constructs; their coverage of real‑world missingness is unverified, and sensitivity to parameter choices is not explored.
- The introduced dense datasets are small (hundreds to thousands of nodes), limiting scalability studies and generalizability of conclusions to large graphs.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Sparsity illusion):** High for MCAR/MAR settings with binary‑like features; Medium for generalizing to all sparse benchmarks and non‑binary data.
- **Claim 2 (New mechanisms expose weaknesses):** Medium—empirically supported on evaluated dense datasets, but realism and sensitivity to mechanism parameters remain open.
- **Claim 3 (GNNmim robustness):** High for the evaluated scenarios (dense data, various mechanisms/shifts); Medium for universal claims beyond these settings.
