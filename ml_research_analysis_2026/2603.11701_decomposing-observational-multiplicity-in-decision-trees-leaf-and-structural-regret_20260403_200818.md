# Decomposing Observational Multiplicity in Decision Trees: Leaf and Structural Regret

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11701  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11701  
- **Reference count:** 21  
- **Primary result:** Decomposition of observational multiplicity into leaf and structural regret, with structural dominating in credit scoring, enabling selective prediction that improves recall to 100% on stable sub-populations.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates predictive multiplicity arising from stochastic label realizations (observational multiplicity) in decision trees. It formally decomposes total variability into leaf regret (within-leaf finite-sample noise) and structural regret (due to unstable tree structures). Theoretically, leaf regret is bounded by 1/(4n_L) and vanishes as leaf size grows. Empirically on binary credit scoring datasets, structural regret is 6–15× larger than leaf regret, and the decomposition is validated by near-perfect alignment between estimated and simulated total regret. Moreover, using the total regret score for selective prediction abstention can raise recall from 92% to 100% on the most stable subpopulations, offering a practical safety tool.

## Method Summary
The method relies on resampling to estimate regret components. For decomposition validation, conditional probabilities (oracle) are first estimated—typically via logistic regression on the full dataset. Then, for \(B\) iterations, new labels are drawn from Bernoulli(\(\hat{p}_i\)) and a CART tree is trained on \((X, \tilde{Y}^{(b)})\). For each instance, total regret is the variance of predictions \(\hat{p}^{(b)}(x)\) across the \(B\) trees. Leaf regret for a leaf is computed as \(\hat{p}_L(1-\hat{p}_L)/n_L\); the expected leaf regret for an instance averages the leaf regret over the \(B\) trees where that instance falls. Structural regret equals total minus leaf regret (clipped to zero). For selective prediction, bootstrap resampling of the original training data (with observed labels) generates \(B\) trees; predictions are averaged, and classification uses a 0.5 threshold. Instances are ranked by estimated total or component regret; for varying coverage levels, recall is computed only on the selected low‑regret subset.

## Key Results
- Theoretical bound: leaf regret ≤ 1/(4 n_L) and vanishes as leaf size grows.
- Empirical finding: Across six credit scoring datasets, structural regret dominates leaf regret by factors of 6 to 15.
- Decomposition validation: Correlation between estimated (leaf + structural) and simulated total regret is near 1.0 (Figure 1).
- Selective prediction: Ranking by structural or total regret yields monotonic recall improvements; e.g., on German Credit, recall increases from 92% (no abstention) to 100% when covering only the 5% most stable instances.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Within a fixed decision tree leaf, prediction variability arises from finite‑sample noise in label realizations (aleatoric uncertainty), quantified as leaf regret.  
- **Mechanism:** For a leaf with empirical class probability estimate \(\hat{p}_L\) based on \(n_L\) samples, the variance of \(\hat{p}_L\) given the leaf is \(p^*_L(1-p^*_L)/n_L\). This variance is the unavoidable instability in the leaf’s constant prediction due to the stochasticity of \(Y|X\).  
- **Core assumption:** The leaf’s conditional label distribution is Bernoulli(\(p^*_L\)), and the tree structure is held fixed.  
- **Evidence anchors:** abstract, section 3.2 (Lemma 1), and the mechanism is a novel contribution.  
- **Break condition:** The bound fails if the Bernoulli assumption is violated (e.g., non‑binary labels, heteroscedastic noise) or the leaf is not homogeneous under the true conditional distribution.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Additional prediction variability arises from the instability of the tree’s structure itself (epistemic uncertainty) across different valid training sets.  
- **Mechanism:** For a fixed input \(x\), the variance of its predicted probability across trees trained on different label realizations captures how splits and leaf assignments change. This reflects the algorithm’s sensitivity to sample noise.  
- **Core assumption:** The tree induction algorithm (e.g., CART) is deterministic given a dataset, and different datasets arise from the same underlying \(P(Y|X)\).  
- **Evidence anchors:** abstract, section 3.6 (definition of structural regret), and related work on tree stability.  
- **Break condition:** The Monte Carlo estimate becomes misleading if bootstrapped datasets are not representative or if the algorithm includes strong randomization (e.g., random feature subsets).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Total individual‑level predictive variability from observational multiplicity decomposes exactly into the sum of expected leaf regret (within‑structure) and structural regret (between‑structure).  
- **Mechanism:** \(\text{Var}(\hat{p}(x)) = \mathbb{E}_T[R_{\text{leaf}}(x;T)] + R_{\text{struct}}(x)\). The first term averages leaf‑level noise over the random tree structure; the second is the variance of the mean prediction across structures.  
- **Core assumption:** Tree structure \(T\) and conditional label distribution within each leaf are well‑defined; relies on law of total variance.  
- **Evidence anchors:** abstract, section 3.6 (Lemma 6), section 4.1 (near‑perfect alignment in Figure 1).  
- **Break condition:** Identity fails if leaf regret is not computed conditional on the specific leaf assignment for a given tree, or if structural regret estimation does not properly account for the joint distribution of tree structures.

## Foundational Learning
- **Probabilistic Classification & Observational Multiplicity**  
  - Why needed: The framework assumes \(Y\) is a stochastic realization of \(P(Y=1|X)\); understanding that a single training label is one draw from this distribution is prerequisite.  
  - Quick check: If you redraw the training labels from the same \(P(Y|X)\), how might a decision tree’s predictions for a fixed individual change, and why?

- **Decision Tree Induction & Stability**  
  - Why needed: Leaf regret exists under a fixed tree; structural regret exists because tree structures vary. Knowing how CART splits (e.g., Gini impurity) and its known instability to data perturbations is essential.  
  - Quick check: What property of a leaf’s node (e.g., sample size, class proportion) directly bounds its leaf regret, and what tree parameter controls this?

- **Monte Carlo Resampling & Variance Estimation**  
  - Why needed: Both regret components are estimated via resampling: leaf regret via parametric Bernoulli draws given \(\hat{p}_L\), structural regret via bootstrap trees. Understanding the difference between estimating variance *within* a model and *across* models is key.  
  - Quick check: When estimating structural regret for instance \(x\), what is the source of randomness in the \(B\) predictions \(\hat{p}^{(b)}(x)\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Base Tree (CART) → Leaf Regret Estimator (per‑leaf \(bR_{\text{leaf}}\))  
Bootstrap → Tree Trainer (B trees) → Structural Regret Estimator (instance‑level \(bR_{\text{struct}}\))  
Leaf Regret Estimator (averaged over trees) + Structural Regret Estimator → Decomposition Engine (validates Lemma 6)  
Decomposition Engine → Selective Prediction Module (ranking & abstention)

**Critical path**  
1. Train a baseline CART tree on \(D\).  
2. For every leaf, compute \(bR_{\text{leaf}} = \hat{p}_L(1-\hat{p}_L)/n_L\).  
3. For \(b = 1,\dots,B\): bootstrap \(D\) → train tree \(T^{(b)}\) → for each validation instance \(x\), store \(\hat{p}^{(b)}(x)\).  
4. For each \(x\): compute \(bR_{\text{struct},B}(x) = \text{Var}_b(\hat{p}^{(b)}(x))\). Also compute expected leaf regret: average the leaf’s \(bR_{\text{leaf}}\) over the \(B\) trees (i.e., for each tree, find \(x\)’s leaf, retrieve its \(bR_{\text{leaf}}\), then average).  
5. Verify decomposition: total MC variance ≈ avg leaf + struct.  
6. Use the sum as an instability score for selective prediction.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Computational cost: structural regret requires \(B\) tree trainings (\(O(B\cdot \text{CART cost})\)).  
- Estimation bias: using a single reference tree’s leaf regrets is an approximation; nested MC (recomputing leaf regret for each bootstrapped tree) is more accurate but costlier.  
- Granularity: leaf regret is leaf‑specific; structural regret is instance‑specific; the “expected leaf regret” for an instance averages over random leaf assignments, potentially smoothing local noise.

**Failure signatures**  
- Decomposition mismatch: estimated total (leaf + struct) diverges from directly simulated total variance.  
- Structural regret ≈ 0: may indicate overly regularized tree (low depth, high min_samples_leaf).  
- Leaf regret dominates: suggests extremely small leaf sizes (e.g., min_samples_leaf=1).  
- Selective prediction recall does not improve with abstention: regret ranking may not correlate with actual error.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Decomposition Validation Replication** – On dataset `hmeq`, run nested MC (200 label realizations, B trees each). Plot estimated (E[leaf]+struct) vs. simulated total variance; expect \(R^2 \approx 1.0\) as in Figure 1.  
2. **Leaf Size Impact** – Vary `min_samples_leaf` (5–200). Compute average leaf regret and structural regret; confirm leaf regret decreases with \(n_L\) (Lemma 2) and observe trend of structural regret (may increase slightly with smaller leaves or decrease with reduced sensitivity).  
3. **Selective Prediction Efficacy** – On `german_credit`, train CART with `min_samples_leaf=100`. For test set, compute total regret. Sort by regret; for coverage thresholds (100%, 95%, …, 5%), compute recall among the lowest‑regret instances. Expect monotonic increase, reaching 100% at very low coverage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can the decomposition of observational multiplicity into leaf and structural regret be extended to multiclass classification and ensemble tree methods? The paper’s conclusion explicitly mentions this as future work.  
- **Open Question 2:** Can structural regret be theoretically bounded or characterized beyond algorithmic stability arguments, especially for finite samples? The paper acknowledges structural regret is “intractable and algorithm‑dependent” and provides only Monte Carlo estimation and asymptotic guarantees.  
- **Open Question 3:** How does the relative dominance of structural versus leaf regret vary across non‑financial high‑stakes domains (e.g., healthcare diagnostics, criminal risk assessment)? All experiments are on credit scoring datasets.  
- **Open Question 4:** How do regret‑based abstention mechanisms for selective prediction compare to alternative uncertainty quantification methods (e.g., conformal prediction sets, Bayesian credibility intervals) in coverage–recall trade‑offs and computational efficiency? The paper does not benchmark against other approaches.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter details (max depth, min_samples_split, number of bootstraps) are missing, preventing exact replication.  
- Oracle conditional probabilities estimated via logistic regression lack specification of regularization or validation, risking overfitting and biased leaf regret.  
- Core assumptions (Bernoulli within‑leaf labels, deterministic CART, bootstrap representativeness) remain untested; violations could invalidate the decomposition and estimation.
