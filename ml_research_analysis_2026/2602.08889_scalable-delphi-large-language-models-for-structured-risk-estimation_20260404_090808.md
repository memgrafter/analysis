# Scalable Delphi: Large Language Models for Structured Risk Estimation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08889
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08889
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLM-based Scalable Delphi produces calibrated risk estimates in minutes, matching expert agreement levels.

## Executive Summary
Structured risk assessment via the Delphi method traditionally requires months of coordinated effort from human experts. This paper introduces Scalable Delphi, an automated approach that uses LLM agents with diverse personas to estimate latent probabilities. The protocol involves independent probability estimation, rationale sharing, mediated feedback, and iterative refinement, reducing the time to minutes. Evaluated on AI-augmented cybersecurity risk across three benchmarks (BountyBench, Cybench, CyberGym) and human studies, LLM panels demonstrate strong calibration (Pearson r=0.87–0.95), systematic improvement with added evidence, and alignment with human experts. Notably, the disagreement between an LLM panel and a human panel (5.0 percentage points) is lower than the disagreement between two human panels (16.6 points), indicating that LLMs can achieve expert-level consistency. These findings suggest that LLM-based elicitation can scale structured risk modeling to new domains.

## Method Summary
Scalable Delphi adapts the classic Delphi method to LLM agents. A panel of five LLM experts, each assigned a distinct cybersecurity persona (defensive specialist, malware reverse engineer, AI/ML security researcher, threat intel analyst, compliance officer), independently estimates probabilities for a given query. The query includes a structured evidence package containing descriptions of relevant benchmarks and agent capabilities. Each expert provides a point estimate (or distribution) along with a written rationale. A mediator agent then synthesizes the round‑1 responses into an anonymized summary that emphasizes areas of agreement and disagreement. Experts receive this feedback and submit revised estimates in a second round. The final panel estimate is the mean of the round‑2 estimates, with uncertainty captured by a 95% confidence interval across panelists.

The method is evaluated on AI‑augmented cybersecurity risk. Using a leave‑one‑out protocol, the authors predict success rates for agents on held‑out benchmark tasks. Calibration is measured by Pearson correlation between LLM estimates and actual success rates. Evidence sensitivity is tested by varying the amount of provided context (none, benchmark, agents, full). Human alignment is assessed by comparing LLM panel estimates to those from two human expert panels on the same set of tasks. The models used are GPT‑5.1 and Claude Opus 4.1.

## Key Results
- LLM panels achieve strong calibration on three benchmarks (Pearson r = 0.87–0.95) and show systematic improvement when richer evidence is provided.
- The LLM panel’s estimates differ from a human expert panel by only 5.0 percentage points on average, while two human panels differ by 16.6 points, indicating comparable agreement levels.
- The entire elicitation process is automated, reducing the time from months to minutes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
#### Mechanism 1: Diverse Personas Capture Expert Judgment Distribution
- **Claim:** Diverse personas enable the LLM panel to capture a distribution of expert judgments, improving calibration and reducing systematic bias.
- **Mechanism:** Each LLM agent is instantiated with a distinct cybersecurity persona (e.g., defensive specialist, malware reverse engineer). This prompts the model to adopt different reasoning styles, priors, and evidence weighting, mimicking heterogeneous human expert panels. The final estimate aggregates these perspectives, averaging out individual biases.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM can reliably simulate the assigned personas, and persona diversity maps onto genuine expert disagreement.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract mentions "LLM agents with diverse personas"; Section 2.1 states that diversity mirrors human Delphi panels; neighboring papers explore hybrid Delphi but do not directly test persona diversity effects.
- **Break condition:** If personas produce near-identical reasoning due to model mode collapse, panel variance underestimates true uncertainty, leading to overconfident estimates.

#### Mechanism 2: Iterative Refinement with Mediated Feedback
- **Claim:** Iterative refinement with mediated feedback allows experts to correct individual errors and converge toward more accurate estimates.
- **Mechanism:** After the first round, a mediator anonymizes and summarizes the panel's estimates and rationales, highlighting areas of agreement and disagreement. Each expert revises their estimate in light of this group input, potentially adjusting extreme values and incorporating new arguments from peers.
- **Core assumption:** Experts meaningfully update their estimates based on the feedback rather than ignoring it or anchoring excessively.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract mentions "iteratively refine"; Section 2.2 describes the feedback loop; Section 4.5 shows two-round Delphi yields estimates aligned with human panels.
- **Break condition:** If feedback fails to surface relevant disagreements or experts are overconfident and ignore feedback, iterations may not improve estimates.

#### Mechanism 3: Rationale Sharing Enables Transparent, Auditable Reasoning
- **Claim:** Rationale sharing promotes transparent, evidence-based reasoning and enables auditability, improving estimate quality.
- **Mechanism:** Experts must provide written rationales alongside their probability estimates. The mediator uses these to create a structured summary of the group's reasoning. This forces experts to articulate their thought process and helps them identify gaps or biases.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can generate coherent, relevant rationales that reflect their internal reasoning, and the mediator can accurately distill them.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "share rationales through a mediator"; Section 2.2 describes the process; Section 4.4 shows reasoning changes when evidence is provided.
- **Break condition:** If rationales are post-hoc justifications not reflecting actual estimation, the feedback may be misleading.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Evidence Package & Prompt Templates -> Expert Agents -> Mediator Agent -> Aggregation Module

**Critical path:**
1. Load personas and evidence.
2. For each of the 5 expert agents, invoke the LLM with the expert prompt to obtain a probability estimate and rationale (Round 1).
3. The mediator agent collects all Round‑1 outputs and generates an anonymized feedback summary.
4. Each expert agent is re‑prompted with the feedback summary, producing revised estimates and rationales (Round 2).
5. After the final round, the aggregation module computes the panel’s final estimate (mean) and 95% confidence interval.
6. The results are returned.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Rounds:* More rounds may improve calibration but increase cost/time; paper uses 2 rounds.
- *Persona count:* More personas increase diversity but linearly increase API calls; 5 used.
- *Aggregation:* Simple mean vs. performance-weighted; tradeoff between simplicity and robustness.
- *Independence:* Query each quantity independently vs. sequentially (preserving history); affects consistency and anchoring.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low correlation (r < 0.7) with benchmark ground truth.
- No improvement when adding relevant evidence (evidence sensitivity fails).
- Rationales that are generic, off-topic, or not grounded in provided evidence.
- High inter-round revisions without convergence (estimates keep jumping).
- Personas produce nearly identical estimates (low variance), suggesting mode collapse.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Calibration test: Leave-one-out prediction on benchmark matrices to measure correlation between LLM estimates and actual agent success rates.
2. Evidence sensitivity: Repeat calibration under varying information conditions (none, benchmark, agents, full) to test if estimates improve with relevant context.
3. Expert alignment: Replicate human elicitation from Murray et al. (2025) and compare LLM panel estimates to human expert panels using mean absolute difference.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How can the validity of LLM-based risk estimates for fundamentally unobservable quantities be directly validated beyond necessary conditions like calibration on proxies?  
*Basis:* The authors state that ground truth is unavailable, and evaluation relies on necessary conditions and corroborating evidence.  
*Why unresolved:* The core quantities cannot be measured, so indirect evaluations leave a fundamental validation gap.  
*Resolving evidence:* Longitudinal observational studies correlating estimates with later incident reports, or controlled red-team exercises with known outcomes.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent do the observed results generalize beyond AI-augmented cybersecurity risk to other high-stakes domains?  
*Basis:* The authors list cross-domain validation as future work and note their evaluation is limited to two frontier model families on cybersecurity.  
*Why unresolved:* Other domains may have less structured evidence, nebulous definitions of success, or different uncertainties that could break the patterns.  
*Resolving evidence:* Replication studies applying the identical protocol to distinct risk domains (e.g., pandemic projection, financial market crashes) with domain-specific evidence and human baselines.

**Open Question 3:** What is the causal mechanism behind the strong calibration observed? Is it primarily the iterative Delphi protocol, the inherent knowledge of frontier LLMs, or a specific combination?  
*Basis:* The paper's persona ablation shows estimates differ by only ~2-3 pp MAD between diverse and uniform personas, suggesting the protocol structure may dominate.  
*Why unresolved:* The evaluation does not disentangle the contribution of the protocol from base model capabilities, as there is no systematic ablation.  
*Resolving evidence:* Controlled experiments systematically removing protocol elements (single-expert single-round, multi-expert single-round without mediation, etc.) while keeping the base model constant.

**Open Question 4:** Why do LLM panels sometimes diverge significantly from human expert panels (e.g., on the "Unbreakable" task), and what does this divergence indicate about reliability in edge cases?  
*Basis:* The largest divergence occurs on "Unbreakable": LLMs estimate 51–61% vs. humans 30–39%. Human experts flagged this task as having inflated first-solve time relative to technical difficulty.  
*Why unresolved:* Without deeper analysis of rationales or testing perturbations, the reason for divergence and whether it reflects model bias or a different valid perspective is unclear.  
*Resolving evidence:* Targeted follow-up: qualitative comparison of rationales, perturbing evidence to test adjustment when told about inflation, and comparing to additional human panels.

## Limitations
- **Prompt and persona unknowns:** Exact prompts, persona definitions, and mediator logic are not provided, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Persona mode collapse risk:** LLMs may simulate personas too similarly, leading to low variance and overconfident estimates.
- **Rationale grounding risk:** Rationales might be post‑hoc justifications that do not reflect the actual estimation process, potentially misleading feedback.
