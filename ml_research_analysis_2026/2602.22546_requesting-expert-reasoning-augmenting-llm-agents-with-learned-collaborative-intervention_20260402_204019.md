# Requesting Expert Reasoning: Augmenting LLM Agents with Learned Collaborative Intervention

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.22546
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22546
- **Reference Count:** 32
- **Primary Result:** Introduces AHCE, a framework where agents learn to request expert reasoning via a Human Feedback Module (HFM), boosting Minecraft task success by 32% (normal) and nearly 70% (hard) while cutting human interaction time by ~75%.

## Executive Summary
LLM agents often fail at specialized, long-tail tasks where autonomously acquired knowledge is insufficient. Raw human guidance, while valuable, is typically unstructured and inconsistently applied. This paper presents AHCE (Augmented Human-Collaborative Execution), a framework that teaches agents to actively and efficiently solicit human expertise. A Human Feedback Module (HFM) learns via reinforcement learning to treat the human as an interactive reasoning tool, synthesizing unstructured feedback into robust, executable plans. Evaluated in the open-world Minecraft environment, AHCE significantly improves task success rates across difficulty levels and dramatically reduces the human burden compared to naive help-seeking, demonstrating that learning how to collaborate is key to effective human-AI augmentation.

## Method Summary
AHCE integrates three learned components into a base LLM agent (MP5-core). First, a Problem Identification Module (PIM) monitors execution and triggers help after a threshold of consecutive sub-task failures. Second, the Human Feedback Module (HFM), an LLM fine-tuned with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), engages in an iterative dialogue with a human expert using structured tags (`<search>`, `<result>`, `<Answer>`), actively querying to build a corrective plan. Third, a Query Execution Module (QEM) translates the HFM's output into two forms of intervention: injecting advice into the planner's system prompt (high-level) and inserting procedural escape maneuvers into the performer's action sequence (low-level). The HFM is trained exclusively on the MuSiQue multi-hop QA dataset to learn interactive reasoning without memorizing Minecraft-specific knowledge.

## Key Results
- In Minecraft, AHCE (with a 32B HFM) increased task success rates by **32%** for normal difficulty and **nearly 70%** for highly difficult tasks compared to a fully autonomous baseline.
- The learned HFM reduced **human interaction time by ~75%** over a naive help-seeking baseline (AHCE-log) that directly presented execution logs.
- The framework maintained high autonomy, with human interventions needed in fewer than 20% of sub-tasks on average.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### 1. Autonomous and Timely Intervention
The Problem Identification Module (PIM) monitors consecutive sub-task failures (timeouts) and only triggers the HFM when the count exceeds a threshold `n_max` (set to 3). This ensures the agent remains autonomous and seeks help only at critical junctures where self-correction is unlikely, avoiding unnecessary human burden. The mechanism breaks if the threshold is misconfigured: too low increases human load; too high risks irrecoverable failure, as shown in ablation studies where success drops sharply for complex tasks when `n_max > 3`.

### 2. Learned Interactive Tool Use
The HFM employs a learned policy (via GRPO) to treat the human as an interactive tool, not a passive oracle. It generates reasoning steps and issues targeted `<search>` queries; the human's responses are incorporated, and the LLM iterates until producing a final plan in `<Answer>` tags. The GRPO objective optimizes for effective synthesis using group-relative advantages and a KL penalty to a reference policy. This interactive loop allows the agent to actively fill knowledge gaps. The mechanism fails if human responses are low-quality or queries are ineffective, leading to incomplete or erroneous plans.

### 3. Multi-Level Guidance Execution
The Query Execution Module (QEM) bridges the gap between abstract advice and executable actions. It injects the HFM's plan into the planner's system prompt for high-level re-orientation and maps specific maneuvers (e.g., "dig down") into low-level primitive actions (e.g., `move_down_continuous(5s)`) for the performer to execute immediately. This dual-channel integration lets the agent recover from both planning and execution failures. The mechanism can break if advice is too abstract for the performer or if the planner misinterprets the injected context.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
Planner (LLM) -> Performer (vision controller) & PIM (monitor) -> HFM (LLM + GRPO) -> QEM -> Planner (prompt injection) + Performer (maneuver injection) -> Human Expert (responds to queries)

**Critical Path:**
1. Task input → Planner decomposes into sub-tasks.
2. Performer executes; PIM tracks consecutive sub-task failures.
3. If failures > `n_max`, PIM activates HFM.
4. HFM enters iterative` → `<search>` (query) → human `<result>` → repeat until `<Answer>` (final plan).
5. QEM parses plan: (a) updates Planner's system prompt; (b) translates maneuvers into Performer's action sequence.
6. Planner replans with new context; Performer executes corrected actions.
7. Cycle continues until success or final failure.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Autonomy vs. Human burden:** Lower `n_max` increases human calls but may prevent failures; higher `n_max` reduces burden but risks failure on hard tasks (ablation shows optimal `n_max=3`).
- **Model capacity:** Larger HFM (32B vs. 7B) improves success and efficiency but at higher computational cost.
- **Training data:** HFM trained on MuSiQue (generic multi-hop QA) to learn interactive reasoning without Minecraft memorization; avoids overfitting but may limit domain-specific nuance capture.
- **QEM granularity:** Planner prompt injection is flexible but prone to misinterpretation; low-level maneuvers are reliable but limited to a predefined set.

**Failure Signatures:**
- **PIM:** Help triggered too early/late; diagnose via histogram of `n_fail` at intervention.
- **HFM:** Repetitive/irrelevant `<search>` queries, failure to emit `<Answer>`, ignoring human responses.
- **QEM:** Correct HFM advice not reflected in Planner's output; maneuvers blocked or ineffective in the environment.
- **System:** Low success despite human help indicates poor synthesis or execution breakdown.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **PIM validation:** Vary `n_max` (1,3,5,∞) on a fixed task set; measure success rate and human call frequency to identify optimal threshold.
2. **HFM fixed-policy baseline:** Replace learned HFM with a rule-based policy that always requests a full solution; compare to AHCE to isolate value of interactive synthesis.
3. **Full system ablation:** Compare AHCE (7B/32B) against (a) MP5-core (no human), (b) AHCE-log (direct log presentation), (c) AHCE with random HFM; assess impact of learned policy and model scale.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### 1. Generalization Beyond Minecraft
The paper only evaluates in Minecraft; it is unclear if AHCE transfers to other domains (e.g., robotics, software engineering) with different dynamics and knowledge requirements.

### 2. Robustness to Noisy Human Feedback
The study aggregates over 10 participants but does not examine variability or the impact of erroneous/inconsistent advice. Real-world applications will face unreliable humans.

### 3. Transfer from MuSiQue Training
HFM is trained exclusively on MuSiQue to avoid Minecraft memorization. It is unknown whether this generic multi-hop QA training effectively transfers to interactive, context-sensitive querying in specialized domains.

### 4. Learned vs. Fixed Impasse Detection
PIM uses a static failure threshold. A dynamic, learned policy might better adapt to varying task complexities and balance autonomy with intervention.

## Limitations
- **HFM Training Opacity:** Critical GRPO training details (reward shaping, group size G, human simulation during RL) are missing, hindering replication of the learned policy. Transfer from MuSiQue to Minecraft is asserted but not evidenced.
- **Task/PIM Specification Gaps:** Full task list, success conditions, and sub-task boundary definitions are not provided. The sub-task timeout threshold for PIM is ambiguous.
- **QEM Mapping Underspecified:** The lexicon and rules for translating natural language advice into low-level performer maneuvers are undocumented.

## Confidence
**PIM Autonomy:** Medium – Supported by ablation showing sensitivity to `n_max`, but threshold optimality is task-dependent and not generalized.
**HFM Learned Collaboration:** Low – GRPO mechanism described, but without training specifics and human simulation, the claim of effective learning is unverified.
**QEM Multi-Level Integration:** Medium-Low – Design is clear, but no direct evidence validates correct parsing and application across diverse scenarios.

## Next Checks
1.  **HFM Query Relevance Audit:** Log all `<search>` queries and human responses during evaluation. Annotate (e.g., by a third party) whether each query led to actionable advice. A low precision score would indicate poor reward specification or policy failure.
2.  **PIM Sensitivity Analysis:** Systematically vary `n_max` (1–10) and sub-task failure definitions (step limits, reward plateaus) on the hardest tasks. Plot success rate vs. human burden to find robust operating ranges and confirm the reported `n_max=3` optimality.
3.  **QEM Parsing Fidelity Test:** Build a test suite of 50 diverse HFM `<Answer>` statements. Manually verify each is correctly parsed into both planner prompt injections *and* corresponding performer primitive sequences. Track failure modes (misparsing, missing mappings).
