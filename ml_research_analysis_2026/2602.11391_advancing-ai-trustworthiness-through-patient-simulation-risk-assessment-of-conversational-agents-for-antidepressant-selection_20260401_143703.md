# Advancing AI Trustworthiness Through Patient Simulation: Risk Assessment of Conversational Agents for Antidepressant Selection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11391  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11391  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Health literacy creates a monotonic performance gradient in clinical conversational AI, with Rank-1 concept retrieval dropping from 81.9% (proficient) to 47.6% (limited).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a scalable patient simulator grounded in the NIST AI Risk Management Framework to systematically evaluate healthcare conversational agents across diverse patient communication styles. By integrating medical profiles derived from real EHR data, linguistic profiles modeling health literacy, and behavioral profiles representing engagement patterns, the simulator stress-tests an AI decision aid for antidepressant selection. Across 500 simulated conversations, the study reveals a significant performance degradation tied directly to patient health literacy, identifying it as a primary measurable risk factor for equitable AI deployment in clinical settings. The work provides a methodology for risk identification and assessment in AI-driven healthcare tools.

## Method Summary
The simulator generates synthetic patients using the MAGI algorithm, which constructs clinically coherent medical profiles from All of Us EHR data via risk-ratio gating and independence screening. Linguistic profiles (Limited, Functional, Proficient health literacy; condition-specific styles) and behavioral profiles (Structured, Distracted, Adversarial) are applied to these medical facts. A GPT-4.1 agent then conducts full conversational intake, producing traced JSON responses. The AI decision aid processes these simulated inputs, and performance is evaluated through human annotation, an LLM judge, and automated metrics. This approach operationalizes the NIST AI RMF’s MAP and MEASURE functions to expose risks across medical accuracy, communication, and interaction dimensions.

## Key Results
- Rank-1 concept retrieval degraded monotonically with health literacy: 81.9% (proficient) → 47.6% (limited)
- Medical concept fidelity remained high at 96.6% accuracy for simulator output
- Behavioral profiles were reliably distinguished (κ=0.93); linguistic profiles showed moderate agreement (κ=0.61)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Health literacy creates a monotonic performance gradient in clinical conversational AI because systems rely on precise, canonical medical terminology.
- Mechanism: Patients with limited health literacy use vague, colloquial, or fragmented language (“my morning pill for my nerves”) that creates a larger semantic distance from the system’s normalized concept vocabulary compared to proficient patients (“20 mg of fluoxetine for generalized anxiety disorder”). The RAG-based retrieval component fails to bridge this gap, causing lower Rank-1 accuracy and downstream recommendation errors.
- Core assumption: The AI Decision Aid’s concept normalization and retrieval are optimized for lexically precise, structured input.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] “Rank-1 concept retrieval dropped from 81.9% (proficient) to 47.6% (limited).”
  - [section 4.6] “Retrieval accuracy increases with health literacy level… This gradient reflects the decision aid’s reliance on precise terminology.”
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., PSYCHE, MATRIX) also identify linguistic variation as a key evaluation dimension but do not isolate health literacy as a monotonic risk factor with quantifiable retrieval degradation.
- Break condition: If the retrieval system robustly handles paraphrastic or vernacular expressions (e.g., via contextual embeddings or clarification dialogs), the literacy-performance gradient would diminish or disappear.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The MAGI algorithm generates clinically coherent and outcome-relevant medical profiles by combining risk-ratio gating with independence screening, avoiding implausible feature combinations while preserving diversity.
- Mechanism: Stage 1 filters to the Top-K predictors of antidepressant response. Stage 3 adds features only if their risk ratio with *all* already-selected features falls within a symmetric band (1/1.5 < RR ≤ 7), enforcing approximate statistical independence and clinical coherence. Stage 4 adds residual features correlated (>1.5 RR) with at least one selected feature, injecting controlled heterogeneity. This balances relevance, coherence, and variability.
- Core assumption: Risk ratios derived from population EHR data can approximate clinical plausibility for individual patient profiles, and symmetric RR bands prevent contradictory comorbidity pairings.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.3.2] “Features are added iteratively, with each candidate v screened for statistical independence… This symmetric band around unity excludes near-deterministic couplings and strong anticorrelations.”
  - [section 4.1] “Coherence was reflected in an average risk ratio of 2.64 among outcome-related features… Variability was maintained through concept diversity, with profiles containing 292 unique concept codes.”
  - [corpus] Related work (e.g., SimSUM, Synthea) uses generative or Bayesian methods; this mechanism’s explicit RR gating is distinctive for auditable profile construction.
- Break condition: If the risk-ratio thresholds (1/1.5, 7) are mismatched to the underlying comorbidity distributions, the algorithm may either over-prune valid clinical combinations (e.g., diabetes with neuropathy) or include implausible ones, reducing profile realism.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: An LLM judge can reliably automate large-scale evaluation of simulator and system performance, matching human agreement on standard tasks but diverging on subtle semantic perturbations.
- Mechanism: A separate LLM (Claude Opus 4.6) is prompted with the same annotation schema used by human experts. It evaluates medical concept fidelity, linguistic profile classification, and behavioral profile classification. Its strong overall agreement (κ=0.78, F1=0.93) supports its use for screening, while lower agreement on perturbed concepts (κ=0.24) mirrors human difficulty, indicating it captures the same edge cases.
- Core assumption: An LLM’s judgment alignment with domain experts generalizes across the evaluation dimensions and can be calibrated via prompt engineering on a held-out set.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.6.5] “The LLM judge align[ed] strongly with human annotators overall (0.93 F1) but showed lower agreement on perturbed concepts (0.77 F1), mirroring divergence among human annotators.”
  - [section 4.2] Table 5 shows human-LLM κ=0.78 full, 0.24 perturbed; human-human κ=0.73 full, 0.30 perturbed.
  - [corpus] Other studies (e.g., LLMaAA) explore LLM-as-annotator; this paper provides empirical validation in a clinical simulation context with perturbation-based stress testing.
- Break condition: If the LLM judge exhibits systematic biases (e.g., over-tolerance for “plausible” but unsupported content) that do not match human experts on a larger, diverse validation set, its scores would misrepresent true system performance, especially for nuanced clinical inaccuracies.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)**
  - Why needed here: The entire simulator architecture is explicitly designed to operationalize the MAP (risk identification) and MEASURE (risk assessment) functions of the NIST AI RMF. Understanding these functions is necessary to see why the three profile dimensions are structured as they are—each targets a distinct risk category (medical accuracy, communication, interaction).
  - Quick check question: Which NIST AI RMF functions do the medical, linguistic, and behavioral profiles primarily map to, and what specific risk category does each address?
- **Concept: Health Literacy as a Gradient**
  - Why needed here: The core finding is a monotonic performance drop across a *gradient* of health literacy (Limited → Functional → Proficient). It’s not a binary “high/low” but a continuous spectrum affecting lexical precision, sentence structure, and medical term density.
  - Quick check question: How do the linguistic attributes of the “Limited” profile (e.g., vague quantities, fragmented sentences) differ systematically from the “Proficient” profile, and why would this affect RAG retrieval?
- **Concept: Risk-Ratio Gating for Feature Selection**
  - Why needed here: This is the core technical innovation in the MAGI algorithm for medical profile generation. It uses population-level risk ratios to enforce statistical independence and clinical coherence when selecting features from high-dimensional EHR data.
  - Quick check question: What is the purpose of the symmetric acceptance band (1/1.5 < RR ≤ 7) in Stage 3, and what would happen if only a simple “RR > 1” threshold were used?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Data Source (All of Us Registered Tier v8 EHR) -> Medical Profile Generator (MAGI) -> Linguistic Profile Spec & Behavioral Profile Spec -> Patient Simulator Agent -> AI Decision Aid -> Evaluation Suite (Human annotators, LLM judge, automated metrics) -> Controlled Perturbation Engine

Critical path:
1. Cohort Extraction: From All of Us, identify MDD patients and antidepressant trials.
2. Medical Profile Generation: Run MAGI for N patients per antidepressant, using risk-ratio gating and binomial band sampling.
3. Profile Combination: For each experimental setting (linguistic-only, behavioral-only, intersectional), assign specific linguistic and behavioral specs to each medical profile.
4. Simulation: For each (medical, linguistic, behavioral) triple, run full conversational intake. Patient Simulator produces traced JSON responses.
5. System Evaluation: AI Decision Aid processes simulator output. Extract retrieved concepts, recommendation probabilities.
6. Annotation & Metrics: Human/LLM annotate simulator output fidelity and profile adherence. Compute recall, accuracy, F1, clustering metrics.
7. Risk Analysis: Correlate performance metrics (Rank-1 accuracy, F1) with profile dimensions (health literacy level).

Design tradeoffs:
- Medical Profile Realism vs. Efficiency: MAGI uses ~8 features/profile vs. ~99 in raw EHR. This improves evaluation scalability but may under-represent complex, multi-morbid patients.
- Linguistic Gradient vs. Discrete Profiles: Health literacy is modeled as three discrete buckets (Limited/Functional/Proficient) for可控 evaluation, but real-world literacy is continuous; overlap between Functional/Proficient in t-SNE is expected.
- Behavioral Simplification: 13 documented behaviors mapped to 4–5 profiles. This captures key dynamics but loses nuance (e.g., “unreliable historian” vs. “difficulty planning ahead” both map to Distracted).

Failure signatures:
- Profile Leakage: Behavioral or linguistic characteristics influencing medical concept expression beyond intended style-transfer (e.g., Distracted profile causing more *Unsupported* medical concepts).
- Retrieval System Brittleness: High dependence on exact match or synonym lookup; large performance delta between Proficient and Limited inputs.
- Simulator Incoherence: Medical facts contradicting each other (e.g., prescribed incompatible drugs) or violating temporal logic.
- Perturbation Detection Failure: LLM judge or humans failing to label subtly perturbed concepts (e.g., “prehypertension” instead of “hypertension”) as Inaccurate.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Medical Profile Coherence Stress Test**: Generate 100 MAGI profiles. Have a clinician review for implausible combinations (e.g., contraindicated meds). Compute average risk ratio among selected features (target ~2.64 per paper). Verify no profile exceeds 9–10 features (efficiency).
2. **Linguistic Profile Distinguishability**: Simulate 50 conversations per linguistic profile with Structured & Cooperative behavior. Compute FKGL, medical term density, response length. Run t-SNE on response embeddings; expect distinct clusters with Functional/Proficient overlap. Have annotators classify 100 random turns; target κ ≥ 0.6.
3. **Behavioral Profile Toxicity & Drift**: Simulate 50 conversations per behavioral profile with Functional Health Literacy. Compute mean toxicity score per profile; expect Adversarial >> others. Compute on-topic cosine similarity; expect Distracted lower than Structured. Have annotators classify behavior; target κ ≥ 0.9.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How ecologically valid are simulated conversations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Authors state: "ecological validity remains untested."
- Why unresolved: Unclear if simulator captures real patient communication patterns and AI failure modes.
- What evidence: Comparison with real patient-AI dialogues via human and automated metrics.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Do health literacy performance gaps generalize to other clinical domains?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Study was limited to depression management; authors note this as a limitation.
- Why unresolved: The risk factor might be specific to depression or the evaluated AI; broader applicability is unknown.
- What evidence: Repeating the simulation in multiple clinical areas (e.g., diabetes, cardiology) with varied AI systems.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Would incorporating unstructured clinical notes improve medical profile realism?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Limitation: "medical profiles relied exclusively on structured EHR data, excluding unstructured narratives."
- Why unresolved: Unstructured notes may contain essential context missing from structured data, affecting communication and outcomes.
- What evidence: Extend MAGI to use unstructured notes and evaluate changes in profile diversity and AI performance.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Does the independence screening criterion bias comorbidity representation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "the independence screening criterion may exclude valid coupled comorbidities... underrepresenting patients with strongly correlated conditions."
- Why unresolved: Underrepresentation of comorbid patients could skew risk assessment for a large patient subgroup.
- What evidence: Compare profiles and AI outcomes with and without the screening, focusing on comorbidity prevalence.

## Limitations
- Data & algorithm access: Reproduction requires All of Us EHR data and exact MAGI implementation (`PredictResp(S, e0)`), which are unavailable.
- System opacity: AI Decision Aid’s RAG pipeline details (index construction, normalization, thresholds) are omitted, preventing direct replication of retrieval metrics.
- Evaluation artifacts: LLM judge prompt, perturbation engine specifics, and full annotation schema are not provided, limiting independent verification of reliability metrics.

## Confidence
- Health literacy gradient effect (Rank-1 drop 81.9%→47.6%): Medium
- MAGI profile coherence (RR≈2.64, κ=0.93): Medium
- LLM judge utility (κ=0.78 full, 0.24 perturbed): Medium

## Next Checks
1.  **MAGI Approximation Stress Test**: Re-implement MAGI's four-stage process using a publicly available comorbidity dataset (e.g., MIMIC-III). Generate 500 profiles and compute pairwise risk-ratios. **Success criterion**: >90% of intra-profile feature pairs fall within the 1/1.5–7 band; no >10% of profiles contain known contraindicated pairs (e.g., SSRIs + MAOIs).
2.  **Linguistic Gradient Ablation on Retrieval**: Using a fixed set of 50 medical profiles, generate "Limited" and "Proficient" utterances via prompt-based style transfer (without the simulator). Feed these to a *standardized* RAG system (e.g., PubMedBERT + FAISS). **Success criterion**: Replicate a >30% absolute Rank-1 accuracy gap between profile styles, confirming the gradient is robust to simulator-specific design.
3.  **LLM Judge Edge-Case Audit**: Using the paper's perturbed concept examples (e.g., "prehypertension" vs. "hypertension"), run the LLM judge (Claude Opus) on 200 such cases with a recreated prompt. **Success criterion**: Achieve κ >0.6 against a new human annotator pair on *only* perturbed items, indicating the reported low agreement (κ=0.24) is not an artifact of a specific prompt version.
