# Deepfake Word Detection by Next-token Prediction using Fine-tuned Whisper

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22658  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22658  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Fine-tuned Whisper detects synthetic words with low error (FAR ~1.38%, FRR ~1.79%) when test data matches training domain/synthesizer, but degrades severely on out-of-domain data (FAR up to 78.60%, FRR up to 88.75%); transcription WER remains low in-domain (~2.2%) but worsens OOD.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a method for partial deepfake speech detection by fine-tuning OpenAI’s Whisper ASR model to predict synthetic word boundaries via special tokens inserted into transcription targets. The approach requires no architectural changes, repurposing Whisper’s next-token prediction to simultaneously transcribe and flag synthetic words. Training uses utterances with one or more replaced words, either from real TTS systems or vocoded simulations. In-domain evaluation shows strong detection and transcription accuracy, but performance collapses on out-of-domain test sets with unseen synthesis methods or acoustic conditions, highlighting a critical domain shift problem. Vocoded training data does not consistently improve generalization.

## Method Summary
The method modifies the ASR training target by injecting two special tokens (`'!!!!!!'` as `<TOF>`, `'∼∼∼'` as `<EOF>`) immediately before and after each synthetic word’s tokens in the reference transcription. Whisper (large-v3) is then fine-tuned end-to-end on this sequence-to-sequence task. Training data comprises two curated sets: `Ft.Voc` (simulated synthetic words from vocoding real speech) and `Ft.TTS` (real synthetic words from six TTS systems), plus a mixed set `Ft.V+T`. Word-level alignments from WhisperX guide token insertion. At inference, any tokens bracketed by `<TOF>` and `<EOF>` are labeled synthetic. Detection metrics are word-level False Acceptance Rate (FAR) and False Rejection Rate (FRR); transcription quality uses WER (after removing special tokens). A ResNet152 frame-level detector serves as a baseline.

## Key Results
- In-domain detection is strong: on `E.TTS`, FAR ~1.38% and FRR ~1.79%; on `E.Voc`, FAR ~1.52% and FRR ~2.19%.
- Transcription remains accurate in-domain (WER ~2.20% on `E.TTS`).
- Out-of-domain performance degrades sharply: on `E.PE` (studio, VoiceCraft/SSR-speech), FAR reaches 78.60% and FRR 88.75%; on `E.AV1M` (YouTube, YourTTS/VITS), FAR 39.98% and FRR 52.17%.
- Vocoded training (`Ft.Voc`) does not reliably improve OOD detection and sometimes worsens it compared to `Ft.TTS`.
- The ResNet baseline shows similar OOD collapse, suggesting the limitation is not architecture-specific.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by integrating detection into the established next-token prediction framework of Whisper, which already encodes robust acoustic-linguistic features. Inserting special tokens around synthetic words teaches the model to associate certain acoustic patterns with these markers, effectively using its decoder to localize artifacts without separate modules. In-domain, the model learns to recognize the specific spectral and prosodic distortions introduced by the training synthesizers/vocoders. However, the mechanism relies heavily on training-data distribution; out-of-domain synthesizers produce artifacts not seen during fine-tuning, and acoustic mismatches (e.g., noise, room acoustics) shift the feature space, causing the learned detection cues to fail. The paper does not analyze *why* the tokens succeed in-domain or *which* features are leveraged, leaving the underlying mechanism speculative.

## Foundational Learning
- **Fine-tuning a pre-trained ASR model:** Adapts Whisper’s general speech-to-text knowledge to a specialized detection task with minimal architectural change. *Why needed:* Provides a strong acoustic and language foundation; training from scratch would require massive data. *Quick check:* Verify that fine-tuning reduces WER on in-domain test vs. base Whisper.
- **Next-token prediction with special tokens:** Repurposes the language modeling objective to signal synthetic segments via boundary markers. *Why needed:* Enables word-level detection within seq2seq without extra heads or losses. *Quick check:* Inspect generated token sequences to ensure `<TOF>/<EOF>` appear only around synthetic words.
- **Word-level forced alignment:** Uses WhisperX to obtain timestamps for precise token insertion. *Why needed:* Ensures synthetic word boundaries in transcription match audio. *Quick check:* Visualize alignments for 10 samples; check that synthetic segment durations align with word edges.
- **Vocoding for simulated deepfakes:** Replaces real speech segments with vocoded versions to create cheap synthetic data. *Why needed:* Augments training with diverse artifacts without requiring multiple TTS systems. *Quick check:* Compare spectrograms of vocoded vs. TTS-generated words for artifact similarity.
- **Domain mismatch in deepfake detection:** Performance drops when test data differs from training in acoustics or synthesizer. *Why needed:* Highlights that detection models are brittle to distribution shift. *Quick check:* Train on audiobook speech, test on YouTube; expect FAR/FRR to rise sharply.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Audio waveform -> Whisper Encoder (feature extraction) -> Whisper Decoder (autoregressive token prediction) -> Token sequence containing `<TOF>`/`<EOF>` markers -> Post-processing: extract tokens between markers as synthetic word predictions.
- **Critical path:** The decoder’s next-token probability distribution is conditioned on both encoder features and previously generated tokens. During training, the loss pushes the model to emit `<TOF>` when entering a synthetic segment and `<EOF>` when exiting, effectively learning to detect anomalies within the transcription stream.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  1. *Minimal intervention* (reusing existing tokens) vs. *optimal signaling* (dedicated tokens might be clearer).  
  2. *Joint transcription and detection* vs. *specialization* (separate models may excel at each).  
  3. *Training efficiency* (single model) vs. *generalization* (overfitting to in-domain artifacts).
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - `<TOF>/<EOF>` appear at wrong boundaries or not at all on OOD data.  
  - WER spikes on OOD test sets, indicating encoder feature mismatch.  
  - Detection FAR/FRR approach 50% (random) on worst OOD cases.
- **Exactly 3 first experiments:**  
  1. **In-domain sanity check:** Fine-tune on `Ft.TTS`, evaluate on `E.TTS`; expect FAR/FRR <5%, WER ~2%.  
  2. **OOD stress test:** Use the same model on `E.PE`; expect FAR/FRR >40% and WER increase.  
  3. **Token insertion ablation:** Train with *no* special tokens (standard Whisper fine-tuning) and measure detection performance by heuristics (e.g., low confidence words); expect near-random detection, confirming tokens are essential.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Which specific domain mismatches (acoustic conditions, speaker characteristics, synthesis artifact distributions) between the audiobook training data and out-of-domain test sets cause the severe degradation in synthetic word detection, and how can these mismatches be quantified to guide domain adaptation?
2. Why does fine-tuning with vocoded synthetic words fail to consistently improve out-of-domain detection performance, and what improvements to the vocoding process (e.g., incorporating artifacts from target-domain synthesizers, using a more diverse set of vocoders) could make simulated data more effective for generalizing to unseen deepfake generation methods?
3. Is the similar out-of-domain performance of fine-tuned Whisper (next-token prediction) and the dedicated ResNet detector due to a fundamental limitation of sequence-to-sequence approaches for partial deepfake detection, or do both models suffer equally from domain shift, implying that architectural changes alone will not suffice without more diverse training data?

## Limitations
- **Alignment uncertainty:** WhisperX version and parameters are unspecified; misalignments could corrupt token insertion and evaluation.
- **Data opacity:** Exact corpus splits, TTS/vocoder checkpoints, and preprocessing details are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
- **Generalization scope:** OOD tests cover only two domains (YouTube, studio); performance on other domains (e.g., noisy environments, non-English) is unknown.
- **Validation ambiguity:** Checkpoint selection metric (WER vs. detection error) is not stated, potentially biasing results.

## Confidence
- **In-domain detection and transcription accuracy:** High – Consistent low error rates across matching train/test pairs.
- **Out-of-domain generalization failure:** High – Reproducible large error increases on two distinct OOD datasets.
- **Vocoded training consistency:** Medium – Claim that vocoded data doesn’t reliably help OOD is stated but lacks detailed ablations to explain conditions under which it might succeed.

## Next Checks
1. **Quantify alignment accuracy:** Run forced alignment (e.g., Montreal Forced Aligner) on 100 in-domain utterances and compare word boundaries with WhisperX outputs; report mean absolute error (ms) and synthetic word mis-encapsulation rate.
2. **Expand OOD evaluation:** Test fine-tuned models on at least two additional datasets (e.g., LibriSpeech test-clean for read speech, VoxCeleb for speaker diversity); measure FAR/FRR and WER to map generalization boundaries.
3. **Ablate validation metric selection:** Fine-tune models per training set, selecting checkpoints by (a) lowest WER, (b) lowest detection error, and (c) a weighted sum; compare OOD performance to determine if checkpoint strategy influences reported degradation.
