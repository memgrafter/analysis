# SCENEBench: An Audio Understanding Benchmark Grounded in Assistive and Industrial Use Cases
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09853
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09853
- **Reference count:** 37
- **Primary result:** SCENEBench reveals that state-of-the-art Large Audio Language Models (LALMs) exhibit a severe bias toward speech transcription, failing to understand full-scene audio (e.g., background sounds, motion cues) without explicit prompting, with free-response accuracy often below 30%.

## Executive Summary
SCENEBench introduces a benchmark to evaluate Large Audio Language Models (LALMs) on four real-world audio understanding tasks beyond automatic speech recognition (ASR): background sound identification, noise localization (motion detection), cross-lingual transcription, and vocal characterizer recognition. Using synthetically constructed and natural audio, the benchmark measures accuracy and latency. Evaluations on five SOTA LALMs show critical gaps: models rarely volunteer non-speech events in free responses (e.g., 6.5–29.3% accuracy), improve with direct questioning, but still struggle with temporal motion cues and multilingual preservation. The findings expose a systemic bias toward foreground speech, limiting utility in assistive and industrial scenarios requiring full acoustic scene comprehension.

## Method Summary
The benchmark constructs task-specific synthetic audio by modifying existing datasets: overlaying ESC-50 environmental sounds on DailyTalk speech for background identification; applying amplitude modulation to ESC-50 sounds for motion detection; inserting non-English spans (Mandarin, Spanish, Hindi, Portuguese) into DailyTalk utterances via TTS for cross-lingual transcription; and aggregating public vocal sound datasets for characterizer recognition. A small natural validation set (~20 clips/task) from sources like AudioSet is also used. Five LALMs (GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5, Qwen2-Audio-7B, Audio-Flamingo-3, DeSTA2-8B) are evaluated with a fixed prompt sequence: free-response 1 (FR1, general description) → if omitted, free-response 2 (FR2, targeted) → multiple-choice 1 (MC1). Responses are scored via keyword/synonym matching or sentence similarity. Inference latency is measured for open-weight models.

## Key Results
- **Background sound identification:** Free-response accuracy is very low (FR1: 6.5–29.3%; FR2: 8.3–32.8%), but multiple-choice improves (8.0–74.2%), indicating models can recognize sounds when cued but do not spontaneously report them.
- **Noise localization (motion):** FR1 accuracy is near floor (6–16%), while FR2 shows substantial gains (e.g., Qwen2 57.2%), revealing a gap in spontaneous temporal reasoning.
- **Cross-lingual transcription:** GPT-4o and Gemini lead on Spanish (93.9%) but lag on Mandarin (84.7%); errors are dominated by monolingual normalization (64%).
- **Vocal characterizers:** All models exceed chance (20%) but performance varies widely; Gemini is weak on sneeze/yawn (14.0%, 7.5%), and Flamingo fails completely on yawn (0%).
- **Latency:** Open-weight models range from 0.80s (Flamingo) to 14.53s (Desta), with high variance for some (p90 ~72s for Desta).
- **Natural vs. synthetic:** Natural validation clips show preserved model rankings but lower absolute scores, highlighting synthetic data limitations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Current LALMs are biased toward foreground speech transcription, causing them to omit salient background sounds in free-response descriptions.
- **Mechanism:** Models are predominantly trained on clean, single-source speech corpora (e.g., LibriSpeech) and evaluated on ASR-centric benchmarks. This optimization leads to audio representations that prioritize linguistic content over environmental context, resulting in "noise override" errors where the model's output focuses exclusively on speech while ignoring concurrent background events.
- **Core assumption:** Training data distribution shapes model attention; if background sounds are rarely present or labeled in training, the model learns not to allocate representational capacity to them.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "expose LALMs' bias toward speech transcription over full scene audio comprehension." Section 4.1: "noise override: in 75% of wrong free responses, models transcribe the speech but never mention the background event." Corpus: Neighbor papers (ISA-Bench, PARSA-Bench) indicate this is a systemic issue across LALMs, but direct evidence is from SCENEBench's synthetic construction isolating background sounds.
- **Break condition:** If FR1 accuracy for background sound identification rises consistently above 50% across models without explicit prompting, indicating learned salience for non-speech events.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** LALMs lack robust temporal integration for motion perception, failing to spontaneously describe amplitude-based spatial cues (approach/recede/oscillate) but can answer when explicitly asked.
- **Mechanism:** Audio encoders likely process short-term frames without explicit temporal modeling objectives for motion. The jump from FR1 (6–16%) to FR2 (up to 57.2%) shows models can retrieve motion patterns when cued but do not spontaneously track them, suggesting a failure in end-to-end temporal reasoning rather than complete inability.
- **Core assumption:** Motion perception requires integrating amplitude envelopes over time; models are trained on static classification or transcription, not dynamic spatial reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.2: "FR1 remains challenging... Oscillating remains near floor... Asking directly (FR2) helps: ∆(FR2–FR1)= +38.0 pts (GPT-4o)." Section 4.6: "omission (43%; no motion described), oscillation collapse (25%; periodic loud–soft patterns reduced to 'volume changes')." Corpus: Weak; neighboring papers focus on static audio understanding, not motion.
- **Break condition:** FR1 accuracy for oscillating motion exceeds 40% across models, showing spontaneous temporal pattern recognition.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Cross-lingual transcription failures stem from monolingual normalization, where models drop or translate non-English spans instead of preserving code-mixing.
- **Mechanism:** Training on predominantly monolingual English speech (e.g., LibriSpeech) or balanced multilingual but not code-switched data encourages models to "correct" mixed input toward the dominant language. The 64% "dropping language" error shows a prior for linguistic homogeneity.
- **Core assumption:** Models lack exposure to natural code-switching patterns during pre-training or fine-tuning, so they treat non-English tokens as noise to be normalized.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.3: "64% of errors are monolingual normalization, where non-English spans are dropped." Section 3.1.3: Synthetic construction deliberately inserts non-English spans with back-translation filtering to ensure they are present and meaningful. Corpus: PARSA-Bench (Persian audio benchmark) and ISA-Bench (instruction sensitivity) suggest multilingual robustness is an open challenge.
- **Break condition:** FR1 transcription similarity for multilingual clips remains within 5% of monolingual baselines without translation of embedded spans.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Audio Source Separation Fundamentals**
  - *Why needed here:* SCENEBench constructs background sound tasks by overlaying environmental noise on speech. Understanding superposition and signal-to-noise ratios is key to interpreting why models fail on mixed audio.
  - *Quick check question:* If a model attends only to the louder source in a mixed clip, what does this imply about its learned attention mechanisms?

- **Concept: Paralinguistics vs. Linguistic Content**
  - *Why needed here:* The vocal characterizer task explicitly separates non-lexical cues (cough, laugh) from speech. Models must ignore semantic content to recognize acoustic forms—a shift from typical ASR objectives.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might a model high in ASR accuracy still fail at yawn recognition? What representational distinction is missing?

- **Concept: Code-Switching and Span Preservation**
  - *Why needed here:* Cross-lingual task tests whether models preserve non-English inserts. This requires recognizing language boundaries and resisting default translation—a skill undertrained in current LALMs.
  - *Quick check question:* If a model transcribes "I have a fifteen-day vacation 我想拥有一个 trip to England" as "I have a fifteen-day vacation I want to have a trip to England," what error category does this represent and why?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Data Pipeline (synthetic generator → natural sampler → prompt formatter) -> Model Interface (audio+text input -> text output) -> Scoring Engine (FR/MC scoring -> latency logger) -> Analysis Module (error taxonomy -> ranking check -> statistical testing).

- **Critical path:**
  Synthetic data generation -> Model inference (all prompts) -> Automated scoring -> Error analysis -> Latency reporting. Natural sample evaluation runs in parallel.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Synthetic vs. natural:* Synthetic clips offer control and scale but lack acoustic realism; natural clips provide ecological validity but are scarce and noisy. The paper uses both but notes natural scores are lower.
  - *Free-response vs. multiple-choice:* FR measures open-ended competence but is harder to grade reliably; MC gives stable comparisons but may overestimate capability by cueing the answer.
  - *Latency measurement:* Only feasible for open-weight models; cloud APIs add network variance, so they are excluded from timing comparisons.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Omission:** Background sound not mentioned in FR1 (75% of background errors).
  - **Normalization:** Non-English spans dropped or translated (64% of cross-lingual errors).
  - **Misattribution:** Wrong vocal class chosen (94% of vocal char errors).
  - **Short-horizon listening:** Motion inferred from last seconds only (implied by oscillation collapse).

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline FR1 sweep:** Run all models on FR1 prompts for each task to measure spontaneous competence. Expect very low scores on background sound (6.5–29.3%) and noise localization (6–16%).
  2. **FR2 after FR1 failure:** For clips where FR1 omitted the target, issue FR2 follow-up. Measure jump in accuracy; large deltas (e.g., +49.8 pts for Qwen2 on motion) indicate retrieval vs. reasoning gaps.
  3. **MC1 control test:** Administer multiple-choice to all clips regardless of FR performance. High MC1 but low FR1 (e.g., Flamingo 74.2% MC vs 20.6% FR2) suggests models can discriminately recognize features when cued but do not spontaneously generate them.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** To what extent do LALMs' capabilities on SCENEBench synthetic tasks translate to performance on large-scale, naturally occurring audio scenes with unscripted background noises and spatial dynamics?
  - *Basis in paper:* The authors validate SCENEBench with 20 natural audio clips per task, finding preserved relative model rankings but substantially lower absolute accuracy, especially for noise localization and cross-lingual tasks. They attribute differences to subtlety of real cues and speech prioritization.
  - *Why unresolved:* The natural validation set is small (N=20 per task) and manually curated to match synthetic criteria, limiting generalizability to the full variability of real-world audio.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Evaluation on a large, diverse corpus of human-recorded audio (e.g., from accessibility datasets, field recordings) with ground-truth annotations for background sounds, motion, and multilingual spans, directly comparing performance distributions to synthetic benchmarks.

- **Open Question 2:** Can LALMs robustly handle acoustic conditions with varying signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) and realistic spatial audio cues (e.g., Doppler effects, occlusion), beyond the controlled amplitude-modulation and equal-loudness overlays used in SCENEBench?
  - *Basis in paper:* The limitations section explicitly states that equal-level mixing "may not reflect signal-to-noise ratio distributions encountered in the wild" and that future work should "sweep speech–background SNRs" and use "field recordings with moving sources."
  - *Why unresolved:* SCENEBench uses fixed SNR (0 dB for overlays) and synthetic amplitude envelopes for motion, which do not capture complex acoustic interactions in real environments.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Extending SCENEBench with parametrically varied SNRs (e.g., -10 to +10 dB) and real spatial audio recordings (e.g., multi-channel moving sources), then measuring LALM performance trends across these conditions.

- **Open Question 3:** What architectural or training modifications are necessary to mitigate LALMs' bias toward foreground speech transcription at the expense of background sound understanding and paralinguistic cues?
  - *Basis in paper:* Error analysis reveals "noise override" (75% of background sound errors) and "dropping language" (64% of cross-lingual errors). Discussion suggests training on speech–noise mixtures with explicit background labels and span-annotated transcripts.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper proposes training strategies but does not implement or compare them; it remains unclear whether architectural changes or data augmentation can effectively rebalance attention without degrading ASR performance.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments fine-tuning LALMs on targeted datasets (e.g., mixtures with background salience supervision, code-switched transcripts) and measuring trade-offs between ASR accuracy and SCENEBench task performance, ideally with ablation studies of loss functions and input representations.

## Limitations
- **Synthetic data limitations:** Synthetic construction likely oversimplifies real acoustic complexity; natural validation shows consistently lower absolute scores, indicating potential overestimation of model competence on real-world scenes.
- **Prompt and parsing dependencies:** Exact prompt phrasing, tokenization, and response-scoring logic are repository-dependent; deviations from the released code could alter results.
- **Evaluation order bias:** The FR1→FR2→MC1 sequence may prime models, affecting FR2 performance compared to independent testing.

## Confidence
- **Speech transcription bias & omission failures (FR1 Background Sound):** High. Supported by overwhelming error analysis ("noise override" in 75% of errors) and consistent low FR1 scores across models.
- **Temporal motion perception gap (FR1 vs FR2 Noise Localization):** Medium. The large FR1→FR2 delta suggests a retrieval/reasoning split, but the mechanism (temporal integration) is inferred, not directly probed.
- **Multilingual normalization in transcription:** High. The 64% "dropping language" error rate is a strong, direct statistical signal from systematic testing.

## Next Checks
1. **Acoustic realism stress test:** Re-run SCENEBench’s natural validation set with *additional* diverse in-the-wild recordings (e.g., crowdsourced environmental audio, multi-speaker meetings) to quantify the synthetic-to-natural performance drop and identify failure modes unique to uncontrolled acoustics.
2. **Prompt engineering ablation:** Systematically vary FR1 prompt phrasing (e.g., "describe all sounds" vs. "list non-speech events") on a subset of clips to measure the sensitivity of "spontaneous" reporting to instruction wording, isolating true capability from prompt artifacts.
3. **Replication integrity audit:** Independent teams must reproduce the benchmark using *only* the public repository (no author coordination), documenting any discrepancies in data generation, model API calls, or scoring logic to establish the benchmark’s reproducibility robustness.
