# Contractual Deepfakes: Can Large Language Models Generate Contracts?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09384
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09384
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** LLMs can generate superficially plausible but fundamentally flawed "contractual deepfakes" that are either unenforceable or commercially unsuitable, with the cost of review/repair exceeding traditional drafting.

## Executive Summary
This paper argues that Large Language Models (LLMs) are incapable of generating legally viable contracts for real transactions. While they can produce text that mimics the form of a contract, they lack the substantive understanding, reasoning, and contextual awareness required for effective legal drafting. The core thesis is that LLM output, based on probabilistic word prediction, results in documents riddled with inconsistencies, hallucinations, and misalignments with the specific transaction and governing law. Consequently, the effort needed to verify and correct such drafts makes LLMs an inefficient and unreliable tool compared to customizing a trusted template or drafting manually.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a conceptual critique rather than an empirical study. It contrasts the fundamental technical operation of LLMs—autoregressive, token-by-token prediction based on static statistical patterns from training data—with the substantive requirements of contract law, which demand transaction-specific reasoning, dynamic legal knowledge, and coherent document-level planning. The argument is built by analyzing inherent model limitations (e.g., lack of world model, frozen knowledge, incoherent long-range dependencies) and mapping these to common failure modes in contract generation. No experimental data is presented; the conclusions are derived from first principles of how LLMs function versus what contract drafting requires.

## Key Results
- LLM-generated contracts are "contractual deepfakes": superficially plausible but often contain fatal legal flaws or commercial mismatches.
- Such outputs are typically either legally unenforceable due to inconsistencies/hallucinations or are enforceable but unfit for the specific transaction's purpose.
- The resources required to review and repair an LLM-generated draft exceed those of traditional drafting or template customization, negating any efficiency gain from automatic generation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Probabilistic Word Prediction vs. Transaction-Specific Legal Reasoning
LLMs generate text by predicting the next statistically probable token (`P(wn | w1...wn−1)`), not by reasoning about the specific facts, parties, or legal jurisdiction of a transaction. This produces text that replicates high-frequency legal phrases without ensuring those phrases are substantively appropriate or coherent in context. Contract drafting requires selecting provisions based on unique transactional objectives and applicable law—a process reliant on contextual reasoning, not pattern replication. The objective theory of contract makes these "plausible" but flawed outputs legally dangerous.

### Mechanism 2: Static Parametric Knowledge vs. Dynamic Legal & Factual Context
An LLM's knowledge is frozen at its training data cut-off and is stored as weighted connections between tokens, not as grounded facts. It cannot access or integrate current law, the specific factual matrix of a transaction, or evolving commercial standards at inference time. While RAG can *present* external information, the model cannot *meaningfully reason* over it to apply legal principles to novel facts due to the symbol grounding problem. Viable contracts must align with the precise factual and legal context at the time of drafting, which is external to the model's static, text-only training.

### Mechanism 3: Incoherent Long-Range Dependencies & Unexplainable Output
Autoregressive generation proceeds token-by-token without a holistic document plan. Early decisions cannot be revised, leading to accumulated contradictions, undefined terms, and misaligned cross-references across a multi-page contract. Attention mechanisms support local but not document-level coherence. Furthermore, the output is unexplainable; there is no reliable trace of *why* a particular clause was included, making post-generation verification a full, costly human read-through. Contract viability depends on internal consistency and alignment with an unstated transactional logic, which this mechanism prevents.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Parametric Knowledge**
  *Why needed here:* To understand that an LLM’s "knowledge" is a statistical map of word co-occurrences in its training corpus, not a database of facts or rules. It memorizes patterns, not meanings.
  *Quick check question:* If an LLM memorizes the phrase “shipment shall be FOB destination,” does it understand the legal and logistical implications of FOB terms? Explain why or why not based on parametric knowledge.

- **Concept: Symbol Grounding Problem**
  *Why needed here:* This explains why text-only training cannot confer true understanding. Words like "delivery" or "risk" require connection to real-world referents and experiences, which an LLM lacks.
  *Quick check question:* Can an LLM trained on all contract law texts ever truly understand the concept of “impossibility of performance”? Why does the octopus analogy illustrate this?

- **Concept: Objective Theory of Contract**
  *Why needed here:* The paper’s risk analysis hinges on this doctrine: a signed document is the contract, regardless of the parties’ subjective intent or whether an AI drafted it. Superficial plausibility is legally binding and dangerous.
  *Quick check question:* Under the objective theory, if both parties sign an LLM-generated contract with a glaring error, can they later avoid it by saying “we didn’t mean that”? What legal mechanism might *potentially* fix it, and why is it uncertain?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:
Prompt Input -> LLM Core -> Output -> Human-in-the-Loop
(Optional RAG Module can feed into LLM Core)

Critical path: Prompt Engineering -> LLM Generation -> Line-by-Line Human Legal Review -> Manual Rewrite/Amend -> Validation (repeat as needed). The review/rewrite cycle dominates total time; generation is cheap.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Control vs. Efficiency:** Templates offer high control and known provenance. LLM generation offers low control and no provenance. Any speed gain from generation is likely offset by review cost.
- **Template Customization vs. Generation:** Customizing a known template is almost always more efficient than generating and then verifying a novel document. Generation only makes sense if no suitable template exists *and* the transaction is so novel that template customization is impossible (rare).
- **Prompt Complexity vs. Model Ignorance:** Adding transactional detail to the prompt often fails because the LLM cannot integrate it meaningfully. Detailed prompts may cause "context rot" or be ignored in favor of high-probability generic phrases.

Failure signatures:
- **Semantic Hallucination:** Provision uses correct terminology in a nonsensical/commercially absurd way (e.g., “delivery shall occur before shipment”).
- **Contextual Inconsistency:** A defined term (“Goods”) is used differently in separate clauses; payment terms conflict with delivery terms.
- **Jurisdictional Mismatch:** Mixes principles from incompatible legal systems (e.g., U.S. arbitration clauses in an EU consumer contract).
- **Obsolete Law:** Relies on overruled precedent or repealed statutes due to static training data.
- **Missing Essential Term:** Fails to include a jurisdiction-specific mandatory clause (e.g., a construction payment mechanism under the UK’s HGCRA 1996).
- **Unexplainable Output:** Cannot trace why a specific phrase was generated; no reliable "reasoning trace" exists.

First 3 experiments:
1.  **Baseline Enforceability Test:** Prompt an LLM to generate a simple contract of sale for a common item (e.g., a used car). Have a contract lawyer annotate: (a) legally unenforceable provisions in a target jurisdiction, (b) internal contradictions, (c) missing standard terms. Measure % of document requiring amendment.
2.  **Context Sensitivity Test:** Generate two contracts with nearly identical prompts, differing only in one key transactional fact (e.g., “seller is a consumer” vs. “seller is a business”). Analyze output for legally relevant differences. Expect minimal/no change, demonstrating lack of contextual reasoning.
3.  **RAG Efficacy Test:** Provide the LLM with a perfect, jurisdiction-specific template via RAG. Prompt it to customize that template for a slightly modified transaction. Compare output to original template: quantify changes. Manually verify if modifications are legally sound and transaction-appropriate. Expect many changes to be superficial or harmful.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
What constitutes the minimum substantive quality threshold for a "viable contract" when generated by an LLM, and how does this threshold vary across transaction types and jurisdictions? The paper defines viability as a contract "adequate in light of the parties’ transaction and compliant with the jurisdiction-specific legal requirements" but does not operationalize this, making the core claim empirically untestable.

### Open Question 2
To what extent can domain-specific training on curated, high-quality legal corpora mitigate the "symbol grounding problem" and improve an LLM's ability to generate context-appropriate contractual provisions? The paper dismisses text-only training as insufficient for understanding but does not test the boundaries of what specialized, structured legal data might achieve.

### Open Question 3
Can advanced prompting techniques (e.g., chain-of-thought) or Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) provide LLMs with sufficient *processable* transactional and legal context to reliably avoid inconsistencies and incoherence? The paper is skeptical based on inherent LLM limitations but does not empirically test the upper limits of current prompting and RAG architectures.

### Open Question 4
What is the comparative *total resource cost*—including prompt engineering, lawyer review, and potential dispute resolution—of using an LLM versus customizing an existing template? The paper claims LLM "repair" costs exceed traditional drafting but provides no empirical cost model or case study.

## Limitations
- The core concept of "viability" is context-dependent and subjective, lacking a standardized, operational definition for empirical testing.
- The argument is theoretical/conceptual, with no empirical data on LLM output quality, comparative drafting costs, or systematic error rates.
- The analysis assumes current LLM architectures; it does not quantify whether specialized legal fine-tuning or future architectural changes could alter the conclusions.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Probabilistic prediction vs. reasoning):** High – well-aligned with established LLM technical limits.
- **Mechanism 2 (Static knowledge vs. dynamic context):** Medium – holds for current models but may be challenged by future RAG/real-time integration.
- **Mechanism 3 (Incoherent long-range dependencies):** High – inherent to autoregressive generation without document-level planning.
