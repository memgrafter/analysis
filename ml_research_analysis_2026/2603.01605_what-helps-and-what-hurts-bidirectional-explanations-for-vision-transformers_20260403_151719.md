# What Helps---and What Hurts: Bidirectional Explanations for Vision Transformers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01605
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01605
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: BiCAM yields superior explanation quality (IoU 0.5419, F1 0.6624 on ImageNet) and adversarial detection (AUROC up to 0.842) while being efficient (16 ms/img) and generalizable to DeiT/Swin without retraining.

## Executive Summary
Vision Transformers (ViTs) are powerful but lack interpretability because traditional CAM methods discard negative (suppressive) signals. This paper introduces BiCAM, a bidirectional attribution method that preserves both supportive and suppressive contributions by strategically aggregating deeper transformer layers. BiCAM produces contrastive heatmaps, enables a Positive-to-Negative Ratio (PNR) for lightweight adversarial detection, and achieves state-of-the-art localization (ImageNet IoU 0.5419, F1 0.6624) and faithfulness across datasets. The approach is efficient (16 ms/image) and generalizes to DeiT and Swin Transformers without retraining, offering a practical tool for ViT interpretability.

## Method Summary
BiCAM builds on Grad-CAM but adapts it to the transformer architecture. For a target class, it extracts attention matrices and value projections from the last ℓ (2L/3) blocks. After a forward pass to compute the class score, a backward pass yields gradients with respect to each block’s [CLS] output. For each layer, it computes an attribution mask as the element-wise product of the value-gradient combination and the temperature-scaled attention weights (no ReLU), preserving signed contributions. The masks from all selected layers are summed, the [CLS] token is discarded, and the resulting spatial map is upsampled to the input resolution. The final signed heatmap highlights regions that support (positive) or suppress (negative) the class. Additionally, the Positive-to-Negative Ratio (PNR) summarizes the balance of signed attributions; adversarial examples typically skew PNR, enabling detection without retraining.

## Key Results
- Localization: Achieves IoU 0.5419 and F1 0.6624 on ImageNet-1k, outperforming prior CAM methods.
- Faithfulness: Attains a faithfulness score (AUC difference) of ≥0.46 on VOC/COCO, indicating explanations align with model reasoning.
- Adversarial detection: PNR-based detection yields AUROC up to 0.842 on VOC for various attacks (PGD, C&W, MI-FGSM).
- Efficiency & generalization: Runs in ~16 ms/image; extends to DeiT and Swin Transformers without retraining.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Strategic aggregation of deeper layers improves localization by focusing on class-discriminative signals and filtering low-level noise.  
**Mechanism:** BiCAM selects the last ℓ transformer blocks (ℓ = 2L/3) and computes per-layer attribution masks via attention–gradient fusion, then sums them. This avoids recursive multiplication and reduces early-layer interference.  
**Core assumption:** Class-relevant information concentrates in later ViT layers.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.1; no direct corpus evidence but aligns with Raghu et al. [20] on layer specialization.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Preserving signed (positive/suppressive) contributions yields contrastive explanations and enables PNR-based adversarial detection.  
**Mechanism:** No ReLU or clipping is applied during mask computation; thus both supportive (positive) and suppressive (negative) values are retained. Positive values highlight regions that increase the class score, negative values indicate regions that decrease it.  
**Core assumption:** Suppressive signals are informative for understanding model decisions and their disruption signals adversarial attacks.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.2; concept of signed attributions exists in LRP but not in CAM-style ViT methods.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The Positive-to-Negative Ratio (PNR) summarizes attribution balance and provides a lightweight adversarial detector without retraining.  
**Mechanism:** PNR = (∑ positive attributions) / (∑ |negative| + ε). Adversarial perturbations distort spatial structure, inflating PNR; ΔPNR = PNR_adv – PNR_clean indicates attack.  
**Core assumption:** Clean samples exhibit spatially structured positive–negative balance aligned with semantic regions; adversarial examples cause dispersed or exaggerated responses.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract, Section 3.3, and experimental results (Table 3) showing AUROC up to 0.842 on VOC.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input image -> ViT model (with forward/backward hooks on last ℓ blocks) -> Extract attention A(l) and values V(l) -> Compute gradients w_c^(l) -> Per-layer mask(l) = Σ_h (V(l)_h · w_c^(l)) ⊙ α(l)_h -> Sum masks over layers -> Remove [CLS] token -> Reshape to spatial grid -> Upsample to input resolution -> Signed heatmap (and optional PNR).

Critical path:
1. Register forward hooks on target layers (last ℓ) to capture attention matrices A^(l) (pre-softmax for CLS token) and value projections V^(l).
2. Forward pass: obtain class score y_c; compute temperature-scaled attention α^(l) = softmax(A^(l) / T).
3. Backward pass: compute gradient w_c^(l) = ∂y_c/∂o_cls^(l).
4. For each selected layer: compute val_grad = V^(l) · w_c^(l); mask^(l) = sum_h (val_grad ⊙ α^(l)_h).
5. Aggregate: mask = Σ_l mask^(l); discard [CLS]; reshape to (H_patch, W_patch); upsample.
6. (Optional) Compute PNR from final signed mask.

Design tradeoffs:
- Layer window ℓ: Larger ℓ includes more cues but risks early-layer noise; smaller ℓ loses mid-level signals. Ablation suggests ℓ = 2L/3.
- Temperature T: Lower T yields peaky attention (unstable); higher T over-smooths. T=2 empirically balanced.
- Summation vs. weighted: Simple sum avoids optimization and works well; weighted combos add complexity without clear gain.
- Signed vs. magnitude-only: Signed gives contrastive maps but may include less reliable negative noise; magnitude-only simpler but loses suppressive info.

Failure signatures:
- Degraded localization if ℓ is too small or too large (see Table 5 ablation).
- Noisy or oversmooth heatmaps with extreme T values (e.g., T=1 or T=3).
- Gradient instability in non-smooth models; verify gradient magnitudes and consider smoothing.
- PNR detection failure if threshold not calibrated or under adaptive attacks.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Localization replication:** On ImageNet-1k validation set, compute IoU and F1 for BiCAM on ViT-B/16; expect IoU ≥ 0.5419, F1 ≥ 0.6624 (Table 1).
2. **Faithfulness test:** On VOC/COCO, perform iterative patch removal (MIF/LIF) and compute Faithfulness = AUC_LIF – AUC_MIF; expect ≥ 0.46 (Table 2).
3. **Adversarial detection:** Generate PGD, C&W, and MI-FGSM attacks on VOC multi-object scenes; compute ΔPNR and threshold via Youden’s J; expect AUROC ≥ 0.78 (Table 3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can we establish reliable, ground-truth-based evaluation for attribution methods like BiCAM? The absence of ground-truth attributions limits validation; creating benchmarks with human-annotated supportive/suppressive regions could resolve this.
- **Open Question 2:** How can BiCAM reduce its reliance on potentially noisy gradients while preserving signed attributions? Hybrid approaches with stable gradient estimators or alternative attribution mechanisms are needed.
- **Open Question 3:** How robust is PNR-based adversarial detection against adaptive adversaries and compared to dedicated detectors? Benchmarking under adaptive attacks is required.
- **Open Question 4:** Can PNR generalize to out-of-distribution detection? Systematic evaluation on OOD benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet-O) is needed.

## Limitations
- Implementation details for Swin Transformer adaptation are vague, hindering exact replication.
- Temperature scaling (T=2) was chosen via limited ablation; its optimality across models and datasets is uncertain.
- Adversarial detection robustness against adaptive attacks remains unevaluated, and PNR threshold may not generalize across datasets.
