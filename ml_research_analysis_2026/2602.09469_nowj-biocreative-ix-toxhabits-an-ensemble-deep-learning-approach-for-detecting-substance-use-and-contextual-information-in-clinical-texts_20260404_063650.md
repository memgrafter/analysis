# NOWJ @BioCreative IX ToxHabits: An Ensemble Deep Learning Approach for Detecting Substance Use and Contextual Information in Clinical Texts
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.09469
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09469
- **Reference count**: 10
- **Primary result**: Achieved 0.94 F1-score (0.97 precision) for Trigger detection and 0.91 F1-score for Argument detection on the ToxHabits test set.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces NOWJ, an ensemble deep learning system for extracting substance use mentions (triggers) and their contextual attributes (arguments) from Spanish clinical case reports in the BioCreative IX ToxHabits shared task. The core approach frames both extraction tasks as a unified BIO sequence labeling problem, using a shared BETO (Spanish BERT) encoder with separate CRF decoders for each task. A pre-inference sentence filter based on a binary BERT classifier removes non-relevant sentences to boost precision, and final predictions are derived by majority voting across models trained with diverse class imbalance mitigation strategies. On the official test set, the system obtained a micro-F1 of 0.94 (precision 0.97) for trigger detection and 0.91 for argument detection, demonstrating the effectiveness of the multi-output ensemble design in a low-resource Spanish clinical domain.

## Method Summary
NOWJ processes each document by first segmenting it into sentences. A binary BETO classifier then filters out sentences unlikely to contain any trigger or argument, reducing noise and computation. The retained sentences are tokenized with the BETO tokenizer and fed into a multi-output BERT-CRF model: the shared BETO encoder generates contextual embeddings, which pass through dropout to two parallel branches, each consisting of a linear layer and a conditional random field (CRF) decoder. One branch predicts trigger labels (substance use entities: Tobacco, Cannabis, Alcohol, Drug), and the other predicts argument labels (contextual attributes: Type, Method, Amount, Frequency, Duration, History). The model is trained with a joint loss equal to the sum of the CRF negative log-likelihoods from both tasks. To improve robustness over the small training set, an ensemble of models is constructed by varying training strategies (label-weighted loss, data oversampling, weighted random sampling) and/or data subsets. At inference, subword-level predictions are detokenized and normalized, and final spans are obtained via majority voting across the ensemble members.

## Key Results
- **Trigger detection**: micro-F1 = 0.94, precision = 0.97.
- **Argument detection**: micro-F1 = 0.91.
- The system's top official run placed it among the best performers in the ToxHabits shared task.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Unified BIO Sequence Labeling
- **Claim**: Framing both trigger and argument extraction as a unified BIO sequence labeling task improves performance by modeling shared contextual dependencies.
- **Mechanism**: A shared BETO encoder generates contextual embeddings for each token. Two task-specific fully connected layers produce emission scores for the Trigger and Argument label spaces. A CRF decoder for each branch enforces valid BIO transition constraints (e.g., I-tags must follow B-tags), ensuring globally optimal span predictions for both tasks in a single forward pass.
- **Core assumption**: Trigger and Argument mentions in Spanish clinical text exhibit strong lexical and syntactic co-occurrence patterns within sentence boundaries that a shared encoder can capture jointly.
- **Evidence anchors**: The abstract states the approach "frames both named entity recognition (Trigger detection) and attribute extraction (Argument detection) as a unified BIO sequence labeling task." Section 2.1.2 details the shared encoder and separate CRF layers. Indirect support comes from related multi-task works in other domains (e.g., COLIEE 2023 entry).
- **Break condition**: If Triggers and Arguments frequently occur in disjoint syntactic contexts or with long-range dependencies beyond the model's context window, joint labeling may force incorrect shared representations, harming individual task performance.

### Mechanism 2: Sentence Filtering for Precision
- **Claim**: Pre-inference sentence filtering via a binary BERT classifier increases overall precision by removing non-relevant sentences that generate false positive spans.
- **Mechanism**: A separate BETO-based binary classifier, trained to predict if a sentence contains any trigger/argument, screens input documents. Only positively classified sentences proceed to the multi-output BERT-CRF model, reducing the volume of text processed and minimizing predictions in irrelevant contexts.
- **Core assumption**: A significant portion of sentences in Spanish clinical case reports (e.g., administrative headers, differential diagnoses) contain no substance use mentions, and processing them produces spurious token labels that lower precision.
- **Evidence anchors**: The abstract mentions "employs... sentence filtering to boost precision." Section 2.1.1 details the training of the independent BETO binary classifier. The corpus itself contains many such irrelevant sentences, though the exact proportion is not quantified.
- **Break condition**: If the binary classifier's precision is low (high false negative rate), it will incorrectly discard sentences containing valid triggers/arguments, causing an irrecoverable recall loss that majority voting cannot fix.

### Mechanism 3: Ensembling Diverse Training Strategies
- **Claim**: Ensembling models trained with diverse strategies (resampled datasets, loss weighting, oversampling) reduces variance and mitigates overfitting on the low-resource Spanish clinical corpus.
- **Mechanism**: Multiple BERT-CRF models are trained on different subsets of the training data using varied class imbalance mitigation techniques (Label-Weighted Loss, Data Oversampling, Weighted Random Sampling). At inference, final spans are derived via majority voting across model outputs, which suppresses idiosyncratic errors from any single model.
- **Core assumption**: No single training configuration will yield a model with optimal generalization on the small, domain-specific training set; aggregating diverse, imperfect learners produces a more robust predictor.
- **Evidence anchors**: The abstract notes "aggregates diverse model variants via majority voting." Section 2.2 explicitly lists the three training strategies and the ensemble construction from resampled datasets. Section 3.4 shows performance gains (F1 increase from 0.93 to 0.94 for Triggers) when using the ensemble with sentence filtering vs. a base ensemble.
- **Break condition**: If all ensemble members share a common systematic bias (e.g., all fail on a rare entity type due to data split), majority voting will perpetuate that error. Diversification must target independent failure modes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Conditional Random Fields (CRFs)**: CRFs are needed to enforce valid BIO tag sequences by modeling label transition probabilities, which a token-level classifier alone cannot guarantee. They ensure that, e.g., an I-tag follows a B-tag and prevent invalid transitions. *Quick check*: In the CRF equation P(y|E), what does the partition function Z(E) computationally represent, and why is it necessary for training?
- **Transfer Learning with BETO**: BETO, a Spanish BERT model pre-trained on a large Spanish corpus, provides a strong initialization that captures general Spanish morphology and syntax. Fine-tuning this domain-general model on the small ToxHabits dataset yields better performance than training from scratch or using a multilingual model because BETO's vocabulary and representations are better aligned to Spanish clinical language. *Quick check*: Why might BETO outperform a multilingual BERT (mBERT) for this Spanish clinical NER task, even if mBERT was trained on larger multilingual data?
- **Handling Class Imbalance in NER**: NER datasets are highly imbalanced, with many O-tokens and rare entity types. Without mitigation, the model biases toward predicting O, hurting recall. Techniques like label-weighted loss (higher loss weight for minority classes) or weighted random sampling (oversampling minority examples) address this. *Quick check*: What is a key difference in effect between using "Label-Weighted Loss" and "Weighted Random Sampling" for addressing class imbalance?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Sentence segmentation → Binary filtering (BETO classifier) → Tokenization (BETO tokenizer) → Shared BETO encoder → Dropout → [Trigger branch: FC + CRF] and [Argument branch: FC + CRF] → Detokenization → BIO normalization → Majority voting across ensemble members

### Critical path
The critical path runs: sentence segmentation → binary filtering → tokenization & BETO encoding → dual CRF decoding → detokenization → majority voting. The filtering step is a gating critical path; if it fails, downstream computation is wasted or precision drops.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Precision vs. Recall**: Sentence filtering and majority voting both aim to increase precision by reducing false positives but risk lowering recall if too aggressive.
- **Complexity vs. Resource Use**: Multi-output BERT-CRF shares encoder computation (efficient) but requires maintaining two CRF transition matrices. Ensemble inference multiplies inference cost by ensemble size (e.g., 6 or 19 models).
- **Joint vs. Separate Tasks**: Joint multi-output learning shares representations but may cause negative transfer if Triggers and Arguments have conflicting optimal representations. The equal loss weighting (α=β=1) assumes equal task importance; adjusting these could optimize for subtask-specific metrics.

### Failure signatures
- **Low Precision, High Recall**: Overly permissive sentence filtering (few sentences filtered) or ensemble voting threshold too low (e.g., 2/19 instead of majority).
- **Low Recall, High Precision**: Overly aggressive sentence filtering (false negatives from binary classifier) or CRF transition constraints too strict, rejecting valid I-tags.
- **Poor Argument F1 despite good Trigger F1**: Multi-output training might be Triggers-dominated; consider decoupled fine-tuning or tuned loss weights (α≠β).
- **High variance across ensemble members**: Training strategies not sufficiently diverse; all models make same errors on certain entity types.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Sentence Filter Threshold Sweep**: Vary the classification probability threshold (default 0.5) for the binary sentence filter. Measure impact on end-to-end precision/recall to find optimal operating point for your precision/recall priority.
2. **Single- vs. Multi-output Ablation**: Train a single-output BERT-CRF for Triggers and another for Arguments (separate models, no shared encoder). Compare F1 to the paper's multi-output approach to quantify joint learning benefit on this dataset.
3. **Ensemble Size & Diversity Test**: Train a small ensemble (e.g., 3 models) using only one diversification strategy (e.g., only 5-fold resampling). Compare to a diverse ensemble (mixing oversampling, weighting, sampling). Measure if diversity in training strategy correlates with lower variance and higher F1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: How can Large Language Models (LLMs) be effectively integrated into the proposed multi-output ensemble system to improve generalization and contextual understanding in Spanish clinical text extraction, while addressing concerns over trust, control, and efficiency?  
*Basis*: The discussion states that the current system does not utilize LLMs but future work will explore their integration.  
*Why unresolved*: LLMs are explicitly avoided due to trust, control, and efficiency issues in clinical settings; their potential benefits and practical integration methods for this low-resource Spanish task remain unexplored.  
*Evidence needed*: Comparative experiments on the ToxHabits dataset using LLM-augmented variants (e.g., LLM as encoder or in ensemble) versus the current BETO-CRF system, with metrics on performance, computational cost, and interpretability.

**Open Question 2**: What is the optimal weighting strategy (α and β) for the joint loss function when the frequencies of triggers and arguments differ, and can dynamic weighting based on class distribution or task difficulty improve the micro-F1 scores?  
*Basis*: The paper notes that adjusting weights could be an option when trigger and argument frequencies differ substantially, but uses equal weights (α=β=1) in experiments.  
*Why unresolved*: No experiments with alternative weightings are reported; the impact of weighting on balancing task learning is unknown despite observed differences in average counts (triggers 6.33/doc, arguments 7.95/doc).  
*Evidence needed*: Validation set experiments with varying weight schemes (e.g., inverse frequency, learned weights) and analysis of per-task F1 changes to identify optimal configurations for imbalanced data.

**Open Question 3**: Why does sentence filtering, while increasing precision, sometimes reduce recall for argument detection (as seen in Table 3.4.2), and how can the binary filter be optimized to minimize this trade-off?  
*Basis*: Results show filtering raised precision for arguments from 0.91 to 0.92 but lowered recall from 0.90 to 0.88, decreasing F1 from 0.91 to 0.90; the paper attributes filtering to precision boosts but does not explain the recall drop.  
*Why unresolved*: No error analysis or investigation into which argument spans are lost during filtering is provided; the binary classifier's threshold or design might inadvertently exclude relevant sentences.  
*Evidence needed*: Qualitative review of false-negative arguments in filtered sentences, experimentation with filter confidence thresholds, and alternative filtering approaches (e.g., multitask filtering) to find a better precision-recall balance.

**Open Question 4**: Could more sophisticated ensemble aggregation methods (e.g., confidence-weighted voting or stacking) outperform simple majority voting for this multi-output BIO labeling task, and what ensemble size or diversity maximizes robustness?  
*Basis*: The paper uses majority voting across resampled model variants but does not justify this choice or compare it to other aggregation techniques; ensemble configurations (6 vs. 19 models) are chosen ad hoc without analysis of optimality.  
*Why unresolved*: No ablation study on aggregation strategies or ensemble size is conducted; the assumption that majority voting suffices remains untested.  
*Evidence needed*: Controlled experiments on the same model pool comparing majority voting with probabilistic averaging, weighted voting based on individual model validation F1, or meta-learner stacking, measuring gains in micro-F1 and variance reduction.

## Limitations
- **Corpus representativeness**: The ToxHabits dataset size (1,499 documents), annotation consistency, and domain specifics (e.g., hospital vs. primary care) are not detailed, limiting claims about generalizability to Spanish clinical texts.
- **Sentence filtering trade-offs**: The binary classifier's threshold (default 0.5) and its impact on recall loss are not quantified; optimal operating point for precision/recall balance is unknown.
- **Ensemble diversity justification**: The paper claims diversity from training strategies but does not measure error correlation among models or identify which strategies contributed most to the ensemble gain.
