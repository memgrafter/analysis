# Physics-informed fine-tuning of foundation models for partial differential equations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.15431
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15431
- **Reference Count**: 12
- **Primary Result**: Physics-informed fine-tuning adapts PDE foundation models to new tasks by incorporating PDE residuals and boundary conditions, achieving competitive accuracy without ground truth and superior OOD generalization with minimal data.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a physics-informed fine-tuning approach for foundation models applied to partial differential equations (PDEs). By integrating PDE residuals and boundary conditions into the fine-tuning objective, the method effectively adapts pre-trained models to new PDE tasks, particularly in scenarios with scarce data or distribution shifts. Experiments on Poisson and Helmholtz benchmarks demonstrate that purely physics-informed fine-tuning yields competitive results without any ground-truth solutions, while a hybrid strategy combining physics and data losses achieves the best out-of-distribution generalization when only limited training data is available.

## Method Summary
The proposed fine-tuning methodology extends conventional fine-tuning by augmenting the loss function with physics-informed terms. Specifically, for a pre-trained PDE foundation model, the fine-tuning objective combines a data-driven loss (if ground-truth solutions are available) and a physics-informed loss computed from the PDE residual and boundary condition violations at sampled collocation points. This dual-objective allows the model to respect the underlying physics of the target problem while adapting to new tasks. The approach is evaluated on linear elliptic PDEs (Poisson and Helmholtz) under varying data availability, showing that even without ground truth, the physics-only loss can produce accurate solutions, and with minimal data, the hybrid loss further improves generalization to out-of-distribution scenarios.

## Key Results
- Physics-informed fine-tuning alone achieves competitive accuracy on Poisson and Helmholtz PDEs without requiring ground-truth solutions.
- A hybrid loss combining physics residuals and sparse data yields superior out-of-distribution generalization in low-data regimes.
- The method reduces relative L1 errors and improves physical consistency compared to standard fine-tuning baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Physics-informed fine-tuning works by directly embedding the governing PDE into the optimization objective. During fine-tuning, the model is trained to minimize not only the mismatch with any available data but also the residual of the PDE and violations of boundary conditions at a set of collocation points. This dual constraint steers the model toward solutions that satisfy the physical laws, effectively regularizing the adaptation process. Even in the absence of ground-truth data, minimizing the physics loss alone can produce valid solutions because the PDE itself defines a feasible solution space. When sparse data is available, the hybrid loss combines the strengths of data-driven fitting and physics-based regularization, leading to better generalization, especially under distribution shifts where the data might be non-representative.

## Foundational Learning
- **PDE Foundation Models**: Pre-trained neural networks that approximate solutions to a broad class of PDEs, providing a reusable representation. *Why needed*: They capture generalizable features across PDEs, enabling transfer to new tasks with limited data. *Quick check*: Can the model solve a PDE it wasn't explicitly trained on after fine-tuning?
- **Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)**: A framework that incorporates PDE residuals into the loss function of neural network training. *Why needed*: Ensures model predictions satisfy physical laws without requiring explicit solution data. *Quick check*: Does minimizing the residual improve solution accuracy on unseen domains?
- **Fine-tuning**: Adapting a pre-trained model to a new task via additional training, often with a smaller dataset. *Why needed*: Leverages prior knowledge to reduce sample complexity for the target task. *Quick check*: Does fine-tuning from a pre-trained model outperform training from scratch with limited data?
- **Loss Weighting**: Balancing multiple loss components (e.g., data vs. physics) via hyperparameters. *Why needed*: Prevents one loss term from dominating and ensures both data fidelity and physical compliance. *Quick check*: How sensitive is performance to the physics weight hyperparameter?
- **Collocation Points**: Points in the domain where the PDE residual is evaluated during training. *Why needed*: Provide a means to enforce the physics constraint continuously across the domain. *Quick check*: Does the number and distribution of collocation points affect solution quality?
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization**: Model performance on tasks or conditions different from the training distribution. *Why needed*: Real-world applications often encounter scenarios not covered by the training data. *Quick check*: Does the model maintain accuracy when the PDE coefficient or boundary conditions change?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Pre-trained PDE Solver -> Collocation Point Sampler -> Residual Calculator -> Loss Aggregator -> Optimizer  
**Critical Path**: During each fine-tuning iteration: (1) Sample collocation points (and any available data points), (2) Compute model predictions at these points, (3) Evaluate PDE residuals and boundary conditions, (4) Aggregate data loss and physics loss with weighting, (5) Backpropagate total loss to update model weights.  
**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *Physics vs. Data Loss Weighting*: A higher physics weight enforces stronger physical consistency but may conflict with sparse data; a lower weight risks ignoring physics.  
- *Collocation Point Selection*: Uniform sampling ensures coverage but may miss critical regions; adaptive sampling focuses on high-error areas but adds complexity.  
- *Computational Overhead*: Computing residuals and their gradients (via automatic differentiation) increases per-iteration cost compared to standard fine-tuning.  
**Failure Signatures**:  
- Divergence when physics weight is too high, leading to oscillations or saturation.  
- Poor OOD generalization if the model overfits to the specific collocation points or data from the source distribution.  
- High variance in residuals if collocation points are too few, causing noisy gradients.  
**First Three Experiments**:  
1. Perform a sensitivity analysis on the physics loss weight across a range of values on a held-out validation set, measuring both data error (e.g., relative L1) and physical consistency (e.g., maximum residual).  
2. Evaluate pure physics-informed fine-tuning (no data) on at least two different PDE families (e.g., Poisson and a nonlinear reaction-diffusion equation) to test versatility.  
3. Compare the hybrid approach against standard fine-tuning (data only) and training from scratch under low-data regimes (e.g., 1%, 5%, 10% of training data) on an OOD task (changed coefficient or domain geometry).

## Limitations
- Experiments are limited to simple linear PDEs (Poisson, Helmholtz); scalability to high-dimensional or nonlinear problems is untested.  
- No theoretical analysis of stability, convergence, or optimal loss weighting strategies.  
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (e.g., physics weight, collocation point count) is not systematically investigated.

## Confidence
- Claim: Physics-informed fine-tuning effectively adapts PDE foundation models to new tasks. **Confidence: Medium** (supported by two linear PDE experiments, but limited scope).  
- Claim: Purely physics-informed fine-tuning achieves competitive accuracy without ground-truth solutions. **Confidence: Medium-Low** (performance likely depends on PDE type, initialization, and hyperparameters; not universally guaranteed).  
- Claim: Hybrid loss yields superior OOD generalization under low-data regimes. **Confidence: Medium** (validated on Poisson and Helmholtz with minimal data, but only two benchmarks).
