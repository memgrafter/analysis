# CaseFacts: A Benchmark for Legal Fact-Checking and Precedent Retrieval
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17230
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17230
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: State-of-the-art LLMs struggle on the benchmark; fine-tuned embeddings gain +19.6 Recall@1.

## Executive Summary
High-stakes legal fact-checking requires verifying colloquial claims against formal, evolving jurisprudence. This paper introduces CaseFacts, a benchmark of 6,294 claims (Supported, Refuted, Overruled) derived from SCOTUS case summaries via a multi-stage LLM pipeline. The pipeline synthesizes natural language claims, enforces stylistic constraints, and uses a semantic similarity heuristic to detect cross-case contradictions. Experiments show that current LLMs perform modestly in a closed-book setting (Verdict Score 0.260) and that unrestricted web search harms performance (0.228) due to noisy retrieval. However, fine-tuning embedding models on the training set substantially improves legal precedent retrieval (+19.6 Recall@1 for Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B), highlighting the importance of domain-specific embeddings.

## Method Summary
The CaseFacts benchmark construction proceeds in three stages. First, expert-authored SCOTUS case summaries are fed to an LLM to generate claims that a layperson might make about the case; these claims are naturally either supported by the case text, refuted by it, or overruled by a later case. Second, stylistic constraints are imposed to ensure the claims resemble colloquial speech rather than legal jargon. Third, a novel semantic similarity heuristic pairs claims with potential contradictory cases to label the Overruled class. The resulting dataset covers three semantic relations and is used to evaluate fact-checking and precedent retrieval systems.

## Key Results
- Closed-book LLMs achieve a Verdict Score of 0.260, while web-augmented retrieval drops to 0.228, indicating that noisy retrieval harms performance.
- Fine-tuning embedding models on the CaseFacts training set yields large gains; Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B improves Recall@1 by 19.6 points.
- The final benchmark contains 6,294 claims across Supported, Refuted, and Overruled classes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark's effectiveness stems from its ability to bridge the semantic gap between informal legal claims and formal jurisprudence. LLM-driven claim generation, guided by stylistic constraints, produces naturalistic statements that mirror real-world misinformation. The semantic similarity heuristic captures overruling relationships by measuring textual opposition across cases, avoiding the need for explicit citation links. Together, these components create a challenging yet realistic testbed for legal fact-checking and precedent retrieval.

## Foundational Learning
- Legal fact-checking: Why needed? High-stakes decisions depend on accurate claim verification. Quick check: Does the system correctly classify a claim as supported or refuted by a case?
- Precedent retrieval: Why needed? Identifying relevant prior cases is fundamental to legal reasoning. Quick check: Are retrieved cases truly pertinent to the claim's legal context?
- Semantic similarity for contradiction detection: Why needed? Overruled relationships are often implicit; similarity must detect semantic conflict. Quick check: Does the similarity score correctly flag a later case that contradicts an earlier precedent?
- LLM-based claim synthesis: Why needed? Scalable generation of colloquial claims from formal texts. Quick check: Are generated claims natural and factually anchored to the source summary?
- Embedding fine-tuning: Why needed? General embeddings lack legal domain nuance. Quick check: Does fine-tuning improve retrieval of legally relevant documents?
- Stylistic constraint enforcement: Why needed? To ensure claims mimic layperson language, bridging the informal-formal gap. Quick check: Do constrained claims sound less technical and more conversational?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
SCOTUS Summaries -> Claim Synthesis (LLM) -> Stylistic Constraint Enforcer -> Claim-Document Pair Builder -> Semantic Similarity Scorer -> Labeled Dataset

**Critical Path:** The pipeline starts with case summaries, transforms them into natural claims, applies style rules, then uses similarity to discover contradictions, finally producing labeled claim-case pairs.

**Design Tradeoffs:** Using an LLM for claim generation offers flexibility but risks synthetic artifacts; stylistic constraints improve realism at the cost of possibly over-constraining diversity. Semantic similarity is lightweight but may miss subtle legal reasoning; exact match would be too brittle.

**Failure Signatures:** Noisy web retrieval degrades performance, indicating sensitivity to irrelevant context. Lack of human validation could hide misalignments between claims and labels. Overfitting of embedding models to the single SCOTUS split may reduce generalizability.

## Limitations
- Benchmark limited to SCOTUS cases; may not generalize to other jurisdictions or trial-level opinions.
- Claims are LLM-generated from summaries; could miss colloquial nuances or introduce synthetic artifacts.
- No reported human validation of claim-label alignment; quality remains uncertain.

## Confidence
