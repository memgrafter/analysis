# Evaluating Human and Machine Confidence in Phishing Email Detection: A Comparative Study

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04610
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04610
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** Interpretable ML models (LR, DT, RF) achieved F1-scores up to 0.73 for phishing detection using TF-IDF features, while humans maintained stable confidence (60-80%) and identified a broader range of linguistic cues.

## Executive Summary
This study compares human and machine cognition in phishing email detection. Interpretable machine learning models (Logistic Regression, Decision Trees, Random Forests) were trained on TF-IDF and semantic embeddings, with TF-IDF features yielding superior performance (F1 up to 0.73). Human annotators exhibited consistently stable confidence ratings (60-80% average) and reported a more diverse set of linguistic cues than the models. While certain models matched human accuracy, their confidence levels varied widely. Demographic analysis revealed a peak detection rate of 78% for ages 36-45, with no significant accuracy difference between native and non-native English speakers (~71%).

## Method Summary
The study used the Enron dataset for training and validation (70/30 split, random_state=42). Assumption: The dataset is labeled for phishing, though the exact labeling method is unclear. Preprocessing involved email/URL removal, HTML stripping, normalization, and tokenization/lemmatization. Two feature extraction methods were applied: TF-IDF (max_features=5000, unigram+bigram) producing sparse vectors, and Sentence-BERT embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 384-dim). Three models (Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest) were trained separately on each feature set. A test set of 20 emails (10 phishing, 10 legitimate) generated via ChatGPT was evaluated alongside human annotations collected via questionnaire. Unknown: The synthetic test set may limit ecological validity. Human responses provided labels, confidence (0-100%), linguistic cues, and emotion. Performance was assessed using F1-score; model confidence came from `predict_proba`; feature importance from TF-IDF models extracted per-email influential words for comparison with human cues. Assumption: For Logistic Regression, feature importance is based on coefficients; the extraction method per email is ambiguous.

## Key Results
- TF-IDF-based models outperformed embedding-based counterparts: LR F1 0.72 vs 0.67, DT F1 0.73 vs 0.59.
- Human annotators maintained consistent confidence (60-80% average) while machine confidence varied significantly.
- Humans identified a more diverse set of linguistic cues than models, referencing a broader vocabulary.
- Age affected detection performance, peaking at 78% for ages 36-45; language proficiency showed no significant difference (both ~71%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** TF-IDF feature extraction outperforms semantic embeddings for phishing detection in interpretable linear and tree-based models because it emphasizes discriminative lexical tokens.  
**Mechanism:** TF-IDF increases weight for words frequent in phishing emails but rare in the general corpus, providing a sparse signal that directly correlates with common phishing vocabulary (e.g., "account", "verify", "payment"). Semantic embeddings map text to dense vectors capturing broader context, which may dilute focus on these specific high-signal tokens.  
**Core assumption:** Phishing emails rely on predictable, repeated lexical triggers more than nuanced semantic context, and interpretable models benefit more from explicit token importance than abstract vector similarity.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "models trained using TF-IDF features generally outperform their embedding-based counterparts in phishing detection"  
- [section IV, Results] Table I shows LR with TF-IDF achieves F1 0.72 for phishing vs 0.67 with embeddings; DT with TF-IDF achieves 0.73 vs 0.59 with embeddings.  
- [corpus] Evidence is weak; neighboring papers focus on newer models or datasets, not directly comparing TF-IDF vs embeddings for interpretable models.  
**Break condition:** If phishing tactics shift toward more sophisticated, semantically varied language (e.g., LLM-generated emails), the advantage of TF-IDF may diminish, and embedding-based models could surpass.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Human confidence in phishing judgments is more stable and better calibrated to actual performance than machine confidence, which varies significantly, because humans integrate a broader, more consistent set of contextual linguistic cues.  
**Mechanism:** Humans draw on diverse linguistic patterns (e.g., urgency, emotional tone, structural anomalies) and real-world experience, leading to a steadier internal certainty scale. Machine confidence (based on predicted probabilities) fluctuates with feature representation sparsity and model architecture, especially when encountering edge cases or out-of-distribution patterns.  
**Core assumption:** Human cognitive processes incorporate multiple heuristic signals beyond keyword frequency, resulting in more consistent metacognition; machine confidence scores from probabilistic models are sensitive to feature space geometry and may not reflect true "certainty" in a human-like sense.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "human evaluators... retain more consistent confidence" and "machine learning models provide good accuracy rates, but their confidence levels vary significantly."  
- [section IV, Results] "humans showed consistent confidence in 60-80% on average" while models "lacked confidence in their detection."  
- [section V, Discussion C] "human confidence is better calibrated with their actual performance, whereas machine confidence may fluctuate more widely."  
Unknown: Formal calibration metrics like Expected Calibration Error are not computed, so calibration claims are qualitative.  
**Break condition:** If machine confidence calibration is explicitly optimized (e.g., through temperature scaling or Bayesian methods) and humans are placed under cognitive load or time pressure, machine confidence stability could match or exceed human consistency.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Humans identify a more diverse set of linguistic cues for phishing detection than interpretable ML models because human reasoning incorporates pragmatic, emotional, and contextual inferences beyond lexical frequency.  
**Mechanism:** Human annotators listed a wider variety of "important words" (e.g., "link", "hours", "help", "funds") across emails, suggesting they weigh urgency, social engineering, and semantic relationships. ML models (especially TF-IDF based) consistently highlight a narrow set of high-frequency tokens (e.g., repetitive "account", "payment") aligned with statistical patterns in training data.  
**Core assumption:** Human language comprehension integrates world knowledge and deception schemas, allowing detection based on atypical combinations or implied meaning; interpretable models are constrained to explicit feature weights and cannot easily capture non-linear or interaction effects without explicit feature engineering.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "Human annotators... identified a broader range of linguistic cues than models."  
- [section IV, Results] Tables II & III show human-selected words have higher frequency counts and greater lexical variety (e.g., "email", "verify", "update", "link", "help") compared to model-selected words (e.g., repetitive "account", "desk", "support", "revised").  
Assumption: The extraction of model cues is valid, though for Logistic Regression, the method may be incorrect as per limitations.  
- [section V, Discussion D] "Humans demonstrated a broader range of important words... reflecting richer reasoning strategies."  
**Break condition:** If models are trained on features engineered to capture pragmatic markers (e.g., sentiment scores, urgency lexicons, named entity patterns) or if more complex models like transformers are made interpretable (e.g., via SHAP), the diversity of model-highlighted cues could approach human breadth.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
1. Data Layer: Enron dataset (training) + ChatGPT-generated emails (test)
2. Preprocessing Pipeline: Email/URL removal → HTML stripping → normalization → tokenization/lemmatization
3. Feature Extraction: Two parallel branches: (a) TF-IDF vectorizer (max_features=5000, unigram+bigram) → sparse matrix; (b) Sentence-BERT embedder (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) → 384-d dense vector
4. Model Zoo: Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest (each trained separately on TF-IDF and embedding features)
5. Human Annotation Interface: Questionnaire capturing label, confidence (0-100%), linguistic cues, emotion
6. Evaluation Engine: Computes F1-score (primary), accuracy; extracts model confidence via predict_proba(); compares human vs. model predictions/confidence; analyzes top-N important words per email (TF-IDF only)
7. Demographics Analyzer: Groups human performance by age and language background

Critical path: Data Collection → Preprocessing → Feature Extraction (TF-IDF & Embeddings) → Model Training → Human Annotation → Parallel Evaluation (same test set) → Comparison of Metrics (F1, confidence, cue diversity) → Demographic Analysis → Synthesis.

Design tradeoffs:
- Interpretability vs. Accuracy: Chose LR/DT/RF over BERT for tractable feature analysis, likely ceding some accuracy for transparency.
- Dataset Control vs. Realism: Used small, synthetic ChatGPT emails for human-test alignment, trading ecological validity for controlled comparison.
- Feature-Type Tradeoff: TF-IDF provides interpretable word-level signals; embeddings provide semantic richness but lack per-word importance extraction, limiting direct cue comparison.

Failure signatures:
- Models show near-random F1-scores (<0.5) on phishing class, indicating feature extraction failed to capture discriminative signals.
- Human confidence shows no correlation with accuracy (e.g., high confidence but low accuracy), suggesting annotation protocol or participant pool issues.
- Per-email important word lists from models are empty or uniform across emails, indicating model failure to learn meaningful features.
- Demographic analysis yields no variance (e.g., all age groups identical), suggesting insufficient sample size or poor grouping.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline Model Comparison: Train all three models on TF-IDF and embeddings; report F1-scores per class; verify which feature-model pair performs best.
2. Human-Machine Alignment Test: On the shared test set, compute (a) agreement rate between majority-vote human labels and each model's prediction; (b) distribution of confidence scores (model probability vs. human self-rating); (c) statistical test (e.g., t-test) on confidence stability (variance).
3. Linguistic Cue Diversity Analysis: For each email, extract top 5 important words from TF-IDF-based models; compare with the set of words humans self-reported as cues; compute Jaccard similarity or average overlap; test if human sets are significantly more diverse (e.g., higher entropy).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can interpretable ML models be engineered to incorporate the broader, more diverse set of linguistic cues that humans naturally use for phishing detection?
2. What specific algorithmic or post-processing techniques can most effectively calibrate the confidence scores of interpretable phishing detectors to match the stable, well-calibrated confidence levels observed in human annotators?
3. What underlying cognitive or perceptual factors (e.g., working memory, susceptibility to urgency cues, digital literacy) explain the observed peak detection accuracy in the 36-45 age group, rather than a simple linear decline with age?
4. To what extent do the study's conclusions—particularly on linguistic cue diversity and demographic effects—generalize when human and model evaluations are performed on large-scale, authentic, and evolving phishing datasets (e.g., including recent tactics, multilingual emails, and header/URL metadata)?

## Limitations
- Dataset provenance ambiguity: Exact Enron labeling method and source unclear; standard Enron isn't phishing-labeled, blocking replication of training distribution.
- Test set non-generality: 20 synthetic ChatGPT emails are tiny and artificial; risks overfitting to generation style and limits ecological validity.
- Confidence calibration unverified: Claims about human vs. machine confidence stability lack formal calibration metrics (e.g., Expected Calibration Error); self-rated human confidence may not reflect true certainty.
- Feature importance extraction flaw: Paper cites `feature_importances_` for Logistic Regression (incorrect; LR uses coefficients); method for per-email cue extraction is ambiguous.
- Model scope narrow: Only interpretable models tested; no comparison to modern phishing detectors (BERT, LLMs), so performance ceiling unknown.
- Human cue analysis limited: Model cue comparison restricted to TF-IDF models; embedding-based models' cues not analyzed, biasing diversity conclusions.
