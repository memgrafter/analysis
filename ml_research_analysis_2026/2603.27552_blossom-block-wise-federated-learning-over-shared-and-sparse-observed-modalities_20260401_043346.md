# BLOSSOM: Block-wise Federated Learning Over Shared and Sparse Observed Modalities

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27552
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27552
- **Reference Count:** 30
- **Primary Result:** Under modality-incomplete settings, BLOSSOM achieves an average 18.7% performance gain over full-model aggregation; under modality-exclusive settings, the gain increases to 37.7%.

## Executive Summary
BLOSSOM is a federated learning framework for multimodal systems where clients possess arbitrary, sparse, or disjoint subsets of data modalities. It addresses the fundamental challenge that standard federated averaging fails when client models are structurally heterogeneous due to missing modalities. The method decomposes the model into three modular blocks—modality encoders, a fusion module, and a task head—and performs selective, block-wise parameter aggregation. Only updates for shared modality encoders are averaged across clients that possess them, while task-specific components (fusion and/or head) can be kept private for personalization. This design enables robust collaboration despite extreme modality sparsity and disjoint client populations, yielding significant performance improvements over state-of-the-art multimodal federated learning benchmarks.

## Method Summary
BLOSSOM operates on a late-fusion architecture comprising separate modality encoders, a fusion block, and a task head. During training, the server maintains global parameters for these blocks but modifies the aggregation step: for each modality encoder, it averages updates only from clients that locally have that modality. Clients without a modality neither train nor receive its encoder parameters. For the fusion and head blocks, the system supports three personalization modes: Federated Merging (FM, all blocks shared), Partial personalization of the Head (PH, fusion shared, head private), and personalization of both Fusion and Head (PHF, only shared encoders). This block-wise scheme prevents the corruption of global models by incompatible gradients from clients with missing modalities, while local private blocks allow adaptation to client-specific modality combinations and label distributions.

## Key Results
- BLOSSOM's block-wise aggregation with personalization (PH/PHF) substantially outperforms full-model aggregation under sparse modality conditions, with gains of 18.7% (incomplete) and 37.7% (exclusive) on average.
- On modality-insufficient tasks (e.g., KU-HAR, PTB-XL), where no single modality is sufficient, personalization (especially PHF) yields extreme improvements (up to 80.12%) by allowing unimodal clients to effectively leverage globally-aggregated unimodal representations.
- Compared to the FedMultimodal benchmark, BLOSSOM exhibits lower relative performance degradation under structural missingness, demonstrating greater robustness to realistic client heterogeneity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Selective Encoder Aggregation
Block-wise aggregation prevents collaboration breakdown by ensuring modality encoders are only averaged among clients that possess the corresponding modality. This avoids averaging gradients from clients with missing data, which would otherwise introduce noise or zero-valued updates that corrupt the global encoder for that modality. The mechanism relies on a modular architecture and the assumption that each modality is observed by some subset of clients over time.

### Mechanism 2: Partial Personalization for Local Adaptation
Keeping the task head (PH) or both fusion and head (PHF) private allows clients to adapt the shared multimodal representation to their specific available modalities and local label distribution. This mitigates performance loss when client modality sets are sparse or highly imbalanced. A globally-aggregated encoder provides a strong, general representation, while the private blocks learn client-specific decision boundaries and fusion strategies.

### Mechanism 3: Compensation for Modality-Ins insufficient Tasks
In tasks where no single modality carries enough signal, clients with only one modality would perform poorly alone. BLOSSOM allows their unimodal encoder updates to contribute to a global, stronger unimodal encoder. Personalization (PHF) then enables these clients to learn a fusion/head that optimally uses their single (now improved) encoder, compensating for the absence of cross-modal cues they can never observe locally.

## Foundational Learning
- **Federated Averaging (FedAvg):** The core primitive for distributed model training. BLOSSOM extends FedAvg by modifying the server's aggregation rule to be component-specific. *Quick check:* In standard FedAvg, what parameters are averaged, and what is the assumed relationship between global and local models? **Answer:** All parameters of the model are averaged across participating clients. The global model is the weighted average of locally trained models, assuming local models are initialized from the global model, share the same architecture, and that local updates are comparable.
- **Late-Fusion Multimodal Architectures:** Model structure where modality-specific encoders produce features that are combined before the final task head. This allows missing modalities to be handled by simply omitting an encoder's output. *Quick check:* How does a late-fusion model handle a missing modality during forward pass versus an early-fusion model? **Answer:** In late-fusion, missing modalities are omitted; the fusion module processes only available modality features. In early-fusion, features are concatenated before fusion, so missing modalities would require imputation or special handling at the input level.
- **Client Heterogeneity in FL (Statistical vs. Structural):** Statistical heterogeneity refers to differing data distributions (Non-IID labels). Structural heterogeneity refers to differences in model capability or input space (e.g., missing modalities). BLOSSOM addresses both via personalization and block-wise updates. *Quick check:* What is the key difference between these two heterogeneity types in multimodal FL? **Answer:** Statistical heterogeneity concerns variation in data distributions (e.g., label skew) among clients, while structural heterogeneity concerns variation in model structure or input space, such as clients having different subsets of modalities.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Modality Encoders -> Fusion Block -> Task Head
- **Critical path:** Server initializes global blocks -> sends each client the global encoders for its available modalities (plus global fusion/head if not personalized) -> client trains all received blocks locally -> client sends back updates only for blocks it trained -> server aggregates: (1) each encoder from only clients that have that modality; (2) fusion/head according to chosen personalization mode -> repeat.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - PH vs PHF: PHF (private fusion+head) yields larger gains with learnable fusion (AttentionFusion) but reduces cross-client fusion knowledge sharing. PH is simpler and often sufficient with fixed fusion (ConcatFusion).
  - Aggregation mode: More privatization improves robustness to sparsity but risks slower convergence for shared encoders if too few clients contribute to a given modality.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Unimodal clients consistently underperforming: Check if any clients are contributing to that modality's global encoder.
  - Performance worse than FedMultimodal: Likely incorrect block-wise aggregation logic (e.g., aggregating fusion from clients with mismatched modality sets).
  - Instability with PHF and AttentionFusion: May be due to high divergence in local fusion modules; consider more local epochs or adjusted learning rates for private blocks.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Run FM mode on a modality-complete, IID split (e.g., KU-HAR 0–0–10). Performance should match a standard FedAvg baseline closely.
  2. Validate block-wise logic: Run a modality-exclusive split (e.g., 5–5–0) with PH. Verify that for each encoder, only the 5 clients that possess that modality send updates for it.
  3. Compare PH vs PHF: On a modality-insufficient dataset (PTB-XL) with AttentionFusion and a sparse split (3–3–4 NIID), confirm PHF significantly outperforms PH, especially for unimodal client groups.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions or future research directions. However, the limitations and discussion suggest several unresolved issues that could be considered open questions:
- How does BLOSSOM generalize to vision-centric or highly temporal multimodal tasks (e.g., video+audio)?
- What happens when a modality has zero contributors in a round or in truly disjoint populations (e.g., 10–0–0)?
- How can implementation fidelity be improved with more detailed hyperparameter reporting and deterministic client assignment?

## Limitations
- **Implementation fidelity:** Exact per-dataset hyperparameters (learning rate, optimizer) and the deterministic algorithm for client modality assignment are unreported, risking replication drift.
- **Generalization scope:** Validation is limited to 6 datasets (primarily sensor/audio/text). Applicability to vision-centric or highly temporal multimodal tasks (e.g., video+audio) is unknown.
- **Break condition under-studied:** Performance in scenarios where a modality has zero contributors in a round or in truly disjoint populations (e.g., 10–0–0) is not reported.

## Confidence
- **Block-wise aggregation prevents gradient corruption:** High. Directly supported by algorithmic description and ablations showing FM failure under sparsity.
- **Partial personalization (PH/PHF) improves robustness:** High. Consistently validated across experiments with large effect sizes under sparsity and NIID conditions.
- **Extreme gains on modality-insufficient tasks:** Medium. Strong empirical evidence on two defined datasets, but the "insufficiency" property is post-hoc inferred and not formally quantified; lacks external corroboration.
