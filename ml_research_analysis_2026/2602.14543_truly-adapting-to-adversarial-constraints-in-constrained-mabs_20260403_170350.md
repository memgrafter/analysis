# Truly Adapting to Adversarial Constraints in Constrained MABs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14543
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14543
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Achieves Õ(√T + C) regret and violation for full feedback and bandit loss feedback; Õ(√T + C√T) regret for bandit constraint feedback.

## Executive Summary
Constrained multi-armed bandits (CMABs) extend classic bandit problems by imposing safety or budget constraints that must be respected over time. This paper studies a setting where constraint distributions can change arbitrarily over time, quantified by a corruption level C (applying only to constraints), while losses may be fully adversarial. The authors present three algorithmic variants tailored to different feedback models: (i) full feedback on both losses and constraints, (ii) bandit feedback on losses but full constraint observations, and (iii) bandit feedback on constraints. Across these settings, they achieve regret and constraint violation that degrade gracefully with the corruption level, matching known optimal rates when C=0 (stochastic constraints) while maintaining robustness to adversarial constraint changes. The core techniques involve optimism-based feasible set construction, online mirror descent (OMD) with a fixed-share update to track changing constraints, implicit exploration for partial loss feedback, and a forced exploration phase for partial constraint feedback. The results contribute to the understanding of adaptation in non‑stationary constrained bandit environments.

## Method Summary
The paper develops three algorithms corresponding to different levels of feedback. In the full‑feedback setting, the learner observes the entire loss vector and the full constraint outcome each round. The algorithm first constructs a per‑round feasible set of actions that are likely to satisfy the constraints using an optimistic estimate derived from a fixed‑share OMD update that accumulates historical constraint information and down‑weights corrupted rounds. Actions are then selected by solving an OMD update with this feasible set, ensuring low regret while maintaining feasibility. For the setting where only the loss of the chosen arm is observed (bandit feedback on losses) but constraints are fully visible, the authors incorporate an implicit exploration technique into the OMD framework to efficiently trade off exploration and exploitation. Finally, when both losses and constraints are only partially observed (bandit feedback on constraints), the algorithm begins with a forced exploration phase of Θ(T^{β}) rounds (with β=1/2) to gather sufficient constraint samples for accurate estimation, after which it employs the same OMD‑based decision rule as in the full‑feedback case. The analysis shows that the proposed methods achieve regret and violation bounds that grow with √T and linearly with the corruption level C, with a slight degradation in the bandit‑constraints case due to the exploration overhead.

## Key Results
- **Full feedback & bandit losses:** Regret and constraint violation both scale as Õ(√T + C).
- **Bandit constraints:** Regret scales as Õ(√T + C√T) and violation as Õ(√T + C).
- The rates recover optimal Õ(√T) bounds when constraint corruption is absent (C=0).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The algorithms succeed by combining several principled ideas. First, the use of optimism to construct feasible sets ensures that actions selected are likely to satisfy constraints even when past constraint data is corrupted. The fixed‑share OMD update acts as a robust estimator that discounts the influence of potentially adversarial corruption by maintaining a weighted combination of past decisions, effectively tracking the changing constraint distributions while limiting worst‑case errors. In bandit settings, implicit exploration injects a small amount of forced exploration into the mirror descent update, enabling efficient learning of arm losses without requiring explicit separate exploration phases. For bandit constraints, a dedicated forced exploration phase at the start gathers enough samples to form reliable constraint estimates before the main learning proceeds, at the cost of an additive √T term in regret. The overall framework leverages the power of online convex optimization to balance regret minimization and constraint adherence while adapting to non‑stationarity via the corruption‑sensitive updates.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Constraint Estimation (Fixed‑Share/Forced Exploration) → Feasible Set Construction (Optimism) → Decision Module (OMD) → Action Selection → Feedback (Losses/Constraints)

**Critical Path**
1. Initialize OMD state and constraint estimator.  
2. For each round: construct optimistic feasible set based on current constraint estimates; solve OMD update within that set; play chosen arm; observe feedback; update constraint estimator with fixed‑share (or forced exploration during initial phase).  
3. Repeat until horizon T.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- *Exploration vs. Regret:* Forced exploration adds a √T penalty but is necessary for accurate constraint estimation under bandit feedback.  
- *Conservatism of Feasible Sets:* Greater optimism improves feasibility but may exclude optimal actions, affecting regret.  
- *Fixed‑share parameter:* Balances responsiveness to changes against noise amplification; too high slows adaptation, too low increases sensitivity to corruption.

**Failure Signatures**
- Sudden spikes in constraint violation indicate that feasible set construction became too optimistic.  
- Bounded regret degradation relative to √T and C as predicted; if violation grows super‑linearly, the fixed‑share or exploration mechanisms may be mis‑calibrated.  
- Numerical instability in OMD updates (e.g., exploding dual variables) suggests poor choice of step size or regularizer.

**First Experiments**
1. *Stochastic baseline:* Set C=0 and compare against known optimal constrained bandit algorithms (e.g., C‑UCB) to verify the Õ(√T) rates.  
2. *Corruption sensitivity:* Vary C from small to large and measure how regret and violation increase linearly (or √T in bandit‑constraint case).  
3. *Bandit feedback comparison:* Evaluate the three feedback settings (full, bandit losses, bandit constraints) on a simple linear constraint environment to observe the predicted Õ(√T) vs. Õ(√T + C√T) distinction.

## Limitations
- The algorithms require knowledge of the corruption level C as a parameter; parameter‑free variants are not discussed.  
- The regret bound for bandit feedback on constraints (Õ(√T + C√T)) may be suboptimal; its tightness is uncertain.  
- No empirical validation is provided; practical performance, computational cost, and comparison to existing baselines remain unknown.
