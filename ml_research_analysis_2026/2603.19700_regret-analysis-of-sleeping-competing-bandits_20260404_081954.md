# Regret Analysis of Sleeping Competing Bandits
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19700
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19700
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Introduces Sleeping Competing Bandits; defines player-optimal and player-pessimal stable regret; proposes AC-UCB (O(NK log T_i/Δ^2)) and AC-ETGS (O(NK^2 log T_i/Δ^2)); proves lower bound Ω(N(K-N+1) log T_i/Δ^2); shows AC-UCB is asymptotically optimal when K ≫ N.

## Executive Summary
The paper extends the competing bandits framework to a setting where both players and arms may be unavailable (sleep) in each round. This model better captures real-world resource allocation problems with dynamic participation. The authors define two performance metrics: player-optimal and player-pessimal stable regret, which measure cumulative loss relative to the best stable matching from a player’s perspective. They propose two centralized algorithms: Awake Centralized UCB (AC-UCB) and Awake Centralized Explore‑Then‑Gale–Shapley (AC‑ETGS). AC‑UCB interleaves exploration and exploitation using UCB confidence bounds and selects a stable matching via a max‑weight matching, achieving a player‑pessimal regret bound of O(NK log T_i / Δ^2). AC‑ETGS uses a separate exploration phase to collect accurate reward estimates, then applies Gale‑Shapley to compute the player‑optimal stable matching; its regret bound is O(NK^2 log T_i / Δ^2). A lower bound of Ω(N(K−N+1) log T_i / Δ^2) is proved, showing that AC‑UCB is asymptotically optimal when the number of arms K is much larger than the number of players N. These results advance the theory of multi‑agent bandits and open new directions for fair and robust resource allocation under uncertainty.

## Method Summary
The authors propose two centralized algorithms for the Sleeping Competing Bandits problem. The first, Awake Centralized UCB (AC‑UCB), maintains UCB upper confidence bounds for all player‑arm mean rewards. In every round, given the currently awake players and arms, AC‑UCB constructs a bipartite graph with edges only for awake pairs, weights each edge by its UCB index, and computes a maximum‑weight matching. This matching is provably stable with respect to the optimistic ordering and, after sufficient exploration, converges to the player‑pessimal stable matching under the true means. AC‑UCB achieves a player‑pessimal stable regret of O(NK log T_i / Δ^2). The second algorithm, Awake Centralized Explore‑Then‑Gale–Shapley (AC‑ETGS), separates exploration and exploitation. It first ensures that each player‑arm pair is sampled Ω(log T_i / Δ^2) times when both are awake, yielding accurate empirical means. Then, using these means as preferences, it runs the Gale‑Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm (with players proposing) to obtain the player‑optimal stable matching, which is repeatedly played during exploitation. AC‑ETGS attains a player‑optimal regret of O(NK^2 log T_i / Δ^2). The extra factor of K arises because the exploration cost multiplies by the number of arms in the worst case due to sleeping. The analysis relies on standard UCB concentration, properties of stable matchings, and careful handling of the effective horizon T_i (the number of rounds each player is awake). A matching lower bound of Ω(N(K−N+1) log T_i / Δ^2) is proved by embedding a standard competing bandits instance, establishing the asymptotic optimality of AC‑UCB for K ≫ N.

## Key Results
- Introduces the Sleeping Competing Bandits model and defines player‑optimal and player‑pessimal stable regret metrics.
- AC‑UCB achieves a player‑pessimal stable regret upper bound of O(NK log T_i / Δ^2).
- AC‑ETGS achieves a player‑optimal stable regret upper bound of O(NK^2 log T_i / Δ^2), and the lower bound Ω(N(K−N+1) log T_i / Δ^2) shows AC‑UCB is asymptotically optimal when K ≫ N.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
AC‑UCB’s regret bound relies on optimism: each player‑arm pair has a UCB index that with high probability exceeds the true mean. By solving a maximum‑weight matching on the awake bipartite graph using these indices, the algorithm selects a matching stable under the optimistic ordering. Once all suboptimal pairs have been sampled enough, the UCBs concentrate, and the matching converges to the player‑pessimal stable matching under the true means. The number of times a suboptimal pair can be chosen before its UCB drops below the optimal values is bounded by O(log T_i / Δ^2). Summing over all N players and at most K arms yields O(NK log T_i / Δ^2). Sleeping only reduces the effective horizon per player to T_i but does not affect the logarithmic factor.

AC‑ETGS decouples exploration and exploitation. It first guarantees that every player‑arm pair is observed Ω(log T_i / Δ^2) times during an exploration phase (whenever both are awake). This yields empirical means that are very close to the true means. Afterwards, Gale‑Shapley (with players proposing) computes the true player‑optimal stable matching with high probability, and the exploitation phase repeatedly plays it. The total regret is dominated by exploration: each of the N players must explore up to K arms, and due to sleeping the exploration may need to be extended, leading to O(NK^2 log T_i / Δ^2). The extra K factor stems from the need to learn each arm’s value for every player, exacerbated by the sleeping constraints.

The lower bound follows by reduction: if all players and arms are always awake (a special case of sleeping with probability 1), the model reduces to standard competing bandits, for which a lower bound of Ω(N(K−N+1) log T / Δ^2) is known. Since T_i is proportional to T under uniform availability, the same bound holds, matching AC‑UCB’s upper bound up to constants when K ≫ N.

## Foundational Learning
- **Sleeping Bandits**: Variant of multi‑armed bandits where arms can be unavailable (asleep) in some rounds. *Why needed*: Models real‑world scenarios with stochastic resource availability (e.g., users in recommender systems, channels in spectrum access). *Quick check*: How does the algorithm account for an arm being unavailable when selected?
- **Competing Bandits**: Multi‑player extension where each player chooses an arm, with the constraint that an arm can serve at most one player per round. *Why needed*: Captures competition for limited resources among self‑interested agents. *Quick check*: What happens if two players attempt to pull the same arm?
- **Stable Matching**: A matching where no player‑arm pair would both prefer each other over their current partners; two canonical solutions are player‑optimal and player‑pessimal stable matchings. *Why needed*: Provides an equilibrium concept; defines the benchmark for regret. *Quick check*: How many stable matchings can exist, and which one is typically better for players?
- **Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)**: Balances exploration and exploitation by maintaining upper confidence bounds on mean rewards. *Why needed*: Guarantees near‑optimal regret in stochastic bandits. *Quick check*: What is the typical form of a UCB index and how does it change over time?
- **Gale–Shapley Algorithm**: Deferred acceptance procedure that computes a stable matching given preferences. *Why needed*: Used in AC‑ETGS to produce the player‑optimal stable matching after exploration. *Quick check*: What is the computational complexity of Gale‑Shapley for N players and K arms?

## Architecture Onboarding
Observation Module -> UCB Estimator -> Matching Engine -> Action Executor -> Reward Feedback -> UCB Estimator (cyclic)

**Critical Path**: Each round t proceeds as follows:
1. Observe awake players \(A_t\) and awake arms \(B_t\).
2. For each awake pair, retrieve current UCB estimate (or empirical mean for AC‑ETGS exploitation).
3. Compute a stable matching: AC‑UCB solves max‑weight matching with UCB weights; AC‑ETGS uses Gale‑Shapley with empirical means (post‑exploration) or explores uniformly during exploration.
4. Assign awake players to matched arms.
5. Observe stochastic rewards for the pulled pairs.
6. Update UCB statistics for each played pair.
7. Increment awake counters \(T_i\) for participating players.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- Sample complexity vs. optimality: AC‑UCB (O(NK)) provides a player‑pessimal guarantee; AC‑ETGS (O(NK^2)) achieves player‑optimal at higher cost.
- Computation: AC‑UCB requires a max‑weight bipartite matching (e.g., Hungarian algorithm) each round; AC‑ETGS uses Gale‑Shapley (cheaper) after exploration.
- Sleeping dynamics: Both algorithms adapt to random availabilities using per‑player T_i; however, highly imbalanced availabilities can hurt performance for rarely‑awake players.
- Exploration strategy: AC‑ETGS performs explicit uniform exploration of all pairs, which may be inefficient if some arms are rarely available; AC‑UCB’s adaptive UCB naturally balances exploration.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Instability: post‑matching check reveals a blocking player‑arm pair, indicating a flaw in the matching computation.
- Super‑logarithmic regret growth: suggests mis‑calibrated confidence bounds, violation of assumptions (e.g., non‑uniform gaps, dependent rewards), or poor handling of sleeping.
- Excessive runtime: especially for AC‑UCB when K is large due to O(N^3) matching; profiling may reveal need for approximation.
- Poor performance for low‑availability players: these players may suffer high regret if not sufficiently explored; an adaptive mechanism might be needed.

**First Experiments**:
1. Compare AC‑UCB and AC‑ETGS against baselines (random matching, greedy UCB without stability, oracle stable matching) on synthetic data; plot cumulative regret vs. time, measure asymptotic slopes.
2. Test sensitivity to gap Δ and availability probability p: vary Δ ∈ {0.1,0.2,0.5} and p ∈ {0.3,0.5,0.8}; verify 1/Δ^2 scaling and effect of p on regret.
3. Scalability stress test: set N=200, K=2000; measure average per‑round computation time for AC‑UCB (Hungarian) and AC‑ETGS (Gale‑Shapley); identify breaking points.

## Limitations
- The precise sleeping model (generation and revelation of availabilities) is not fully specified, hindering assessment of algorithm robustness to different patterns.
- Key assumptions (bounded rewards, uniform gap Δ, independence, stationarity) are not clearly stated, limiting generalizability.
- No experimental validation is provided; practical performance, sensitivity to Δ, computational overhead, and robustness to model misspecification remain untested.
