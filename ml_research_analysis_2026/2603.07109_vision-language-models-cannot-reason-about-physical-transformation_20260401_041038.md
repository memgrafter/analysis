# Vision Language Models Cannot Reason About Physical Transformation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07109
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07109
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Vision Language Models (VLMs) show near-chance accuracy (20–69%) on a physical conservation benchmark, revealing a systematic invariance bias and a failure to model transformation-invariant physical properties, whereas humans achieve 98%.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ConservationBench, a rigorous benchmark with 192 synthetic videos testing physical conservation (invariance of quantity under transformation) across four properties. Evaluating 112 VLMs under 23,040 trials, the study finds that models perform near the 33% chance level, in stark contrast to human performance (98%). A robust negative correlation between conservation and non-conserving control trials reveals a deep-seated bias toward predicting invariance. Interventions such as increased frame count, improved prompting, or curated frame sampling fail to yield significant gains, indicating a fundamental limitation in current VLM architectures: they lack transformation-invariant representations of physical quantities, hindering embodied reasoning.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed ConservationBench using 192 short videos depicting conserving and matched non-conserving transformations for number, length, volume, and size. Videos were synthesized to control for confounding factors. Each video was evaluated under 60 different experimental conditions—varying the number of input frames, frame sampling strategies, and prompting templates—yielding 11,520 test cases for conserving and 11,520 for non-conserving scenarios. A total of 112 publicly available VLMs were queried with a binary yes/no question (“Is X conserved?”). Performance was compared to a text-only baseline (descriptions only) and human benchmarks. The primary analysis measured accuracy and the correlation between model performance on conserving vs. non-conserving control trials.

## Key Results
- **Near-chance accuracy:** All 112 VLMs scored between 20% and 69% on conservation questions, clustering near the 33% random baseline.
- **Invariance bias:** A strong negative correlation (r = -0.51) between accuracy on conserving and non-conserving trials shows models systematically default to “yes, invariant.”
- **Visual impairment:** Text-only baselines (no video) often outperformed full video models, suggesting visual input degrades physical reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper finds no evidence that VLMs internally represent transformation-invariant physical quantities. The dominant invariance bias suggests models rely on superficial textual or statistical priors (e.g., “quantities usually stay the same”) rather than building causal, physics-aware representations. The failure of visual information to improve—and often harm—performance indicates that current multimodal fusion mechanisms may not properly integrate temporal visual changes into stable property estimates. The root cause is likely a combination of training objectives (next-token prediction) that do not enforce invariance and architectures that lack explicit mechanisms for tracking object permanence and continuous change.

## Foundational Learning
- **Conservation Principle:** The core concept that certain measurable properties (e.g., number, volume) remain invariant despite superficial visual changes. *Why needed:* The benchmark directly tests this foundational physical intuition. *Quick check:* Can a model recognize that pouring water from a short wide glass into a tall narrow glass conserves volume?
- **Transformation Invariance:** The ability to recognize that a property persists across a spatial or temporal transformation. *Why needed:* It is the precise cognitive skill being evaluated. *Quick check:* Does a model treat a scattered pile of coins as having the same number as when they were grouped?
- **Temporal Resolution:** The granularity of video frames provided as input. *Why needed:* The paper varied frame count to test if more temporal data aids reasoning. *Quick check:* Does seeing intermediate frames during a transformation improve conservation judgment?
- **Textual Prior:** A model’s learned tendency from language data to assume stability or invariance in described scenarios. *Why needed:* Explains the strong invariance bias observed in results. *Quick check:* Without visual input, does a model still answer “conserved” to most questions?
- **Multimodal Fusion:** The architectural mechanism for combining visual and textual streams. *Why needed:* Failure here could explain why visual data harms performance. *Quick check:* Are features from late frames properly integrated with the initial state representation?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map:** Input Video Frames -> Visual Encoder (e.g., ViT) -> Multimodal Fusion Module (e.g., cross-attention) -> Large Language Model (Decoder) -> Yes/No Token Output.
- **Critical Path:** The model must (1) encode a pre-transformation state, (2) encode a post-transformation state, (3) fuse these with the textual question, and (4) generate a binary answer based on a comparison of the relevant property. The failure likely occurs at step 3 or 4, where no explicit comparison operation exists.
- **Design Tradeoffs:** Using fewer frames reduces computational cost but may miss critical transformation details; using more frames increases noise and demands stronger temporal modeling. Prompt engineering tries to force explicit comparison but cannot alter the model’s internal lack of invariant representation.
- **Failure Signatures:** 1) Consistently high “invariant” predictions across non-conserving trials, 2) Performance drop when visual input is added to a text-only query, 3) No performance gain from increased temporal resolution or better prompts.
- **First Three Experiments:** 1) Ablate the visual encoder (use text-only) to measure pure textual prior strength. 2) Incrementally increase frame count from 1 to 16 to plot accuracy vs. temporal resolution curve. 3) Compare “descriptive” prompts (“What changed?”) vs. “comparative” prompts (“Is the number the same?”) to test prompt sensitivity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list separate open questions beyond the limitations section.

## Limitations
- Generalization is limited to four conservation properties; mass, energy, or momentum remain untested.
- All evaluations are zero-shot; it is unknown whether task-specific fine-tuning could overcome the failure.
- Stimuli are clean synthetic videos; real-world noise, occlusion, and complexity may exacerbate the problem.
- No probing of internal model representations to identify where the invariance bias originates.

## Confidence
- **VLMs fail physical conservation:** **High** (112 models, 23k trials, near-chance vs. 98% human).
- **Systematic invariance bias exists:** **High** (strong negative correlation r=-0.51 across models).
- **Visual input impairs performance relative to text-only:** **Medium** (consistent trend but exact contribution of visual noise is unclear).
- **Increasing frames/sampling/prompting does not help:** **Medium** (statistically insignificant gains across many conditions).
- **Model size does not predict conservation ability:** **Medium** (correlation analysis across parameter counts).

## Next Checks
1. **Representation probing:** Train linear probes on intermediate VLM layers to predict conservation labels and identify if any layer encodes transformation-invariant information.
2. **Real-world video evaluation:** Port ConservationBench logic to unscripted videos of natural physical interactions (e.g., pouring, breaking) to test robustness to real-world variability.
3. **Targeted fine-tuning:** Fine-tune a VLM with a contrastive objective that explicitly rewards similar embeddings for pre- and post-transformation states of the same quantity, then re-evaluate on ConservationBench.
