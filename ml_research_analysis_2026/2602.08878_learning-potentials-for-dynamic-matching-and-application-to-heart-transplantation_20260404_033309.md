# Learning Potentials for Dynamic Matching and Application to Heart Transplantation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08878
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08878
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A potential-based policy trained via imitation learning achieves 3,302.1 average monthly population life years gained (PLYG), a 22 PLYG improvement over greedy matching, and attains 95% of the theoretical omniscient upper bound on real UNOS data.

## Executive Summary
Heart transplant allocation suffers from inefficient static policies that cannot adapt to dynamic organ arrivals and a changing waitlist. This paper introduces a framework for non-myopic policy optimization using learned "potentials"—values that penalize matching a patient to preserve their long-term strategic value. Potentials are trained via self-supervised imitation learning, where a neural network mimics decisions from an omniscient algorithm with perfect foresight. Evaluated on historical UNOS data, the approach significantly outperforms the current US policy, the proposed CAS framework, and a myopic utility maximizer, achieving near-optimal performance.

## Method Summary
The method transforms dynamic heart allocation into a series of online matching decisions. For each arriving donor, the policy selects the compatible patient maximizing `Q(d,p,P) = U(d,p) - Pθ(d,p,P)`, where `U` is an immediate utility (predicted life years gained) and `Pθ` is a learned potential function that penalizes removing a patient with high future strategic value. The potential network is trained offline to imitate an omniscient oracle that computes optimal allocations over historical horizons. To mitigate overfitting, a data augmentation pipeline generates diverse semi-synthetic training trajectories from historical data by resampling arrival volumes and times while preserving patient progression statistics. The trained policy is then evaluated by simulation on held-out real trajectories.

## Key Results
- The best potential-based model (34-feature neural network) achieves an average of 3,302.1 PLYG per month.
- This represents a 22 PLYG improvement over a greedy (myopic) utility maximizer.
- The policy attains 95% of the theoretical upper bound from an omniscient matching algorithm with perfect foresight.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A learned potential function augments immediate utility to induce non-myopic decisions in an otherwise greedy online matching policy.
- **Mechanism:** The policy selects the patient maximizing `Q(d,p,P) = U(d,p) - Pθ(d,p,P)`. The potential `Pθ` acts as a penalty, making the policy avoid matching patients with high future strategic value (low penalty) unless the immediate gain is sufficiently large. By subtracting the potential, the policy implicitly reserves "high-value" patients for potentially better future donors.
- **Core assumption:** The potential function can be learned such that subtracting it from immediate utilities yields decisions that mimic an omniscient, long-term optimal policy.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "relying on potentials... The augmented edge weight is defined as a combination of the actual edge weight and a parameterized potential function." [section 3.1] "The potential function `Pθ` acts as a penalty for removing a patient from the waitlist."
- **Break condition:** If the utility model `U(d,p)` is severely misspecified, penalizing based on `Pθ` will compound errors. The mechanism also breaks down if the waitlist state representation `P(t)` fails to capture critical future matching opportunities (e.g., rare blood types not explicitly tracked).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Self-supervised imitation learning from an omniscient oracle efficiently trains expressive potential functions by converting the online matching problem into a supervised ranking task.
- **Mechanism:** An omniscient algorithm computes the optimal allocation over a historical horizon with full foresight. Its decisions generate a training dataset of tuples `(d, p*, P(t))`. The potential network is then trained via ranking losses (pairwise or listwise) to score the chosen patient `p*` higher than all other compatible patients for the same donor and state.
- **Core assumption:** The optimal decisions from the omniscient oracle on historical data provide a sufficiently rich and correct training signal for learning the long-term strategic trade-offs needed for online deployment.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Our approach is a form of self-supervised imitation learning: the potentials are trained to mimic an omniscient algorithm that has perfect foresight." [section 4.2.2] "We compile a dataset of optimal decisions from the omniscient algorithm... the policy learns to mimic the omniscient algorithm."
- **Break condition:** Covariate shift: the online policy may encounter waitlist states not present in the omniscient oracle's training distribution. If the historical horizon is too short or unrepresentative, the learned potentials may overfit to specific arrival sequences rather than general matching logic.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Data augmentation with semi-synthetic trajectories mitigates overfitting and covariate shift by exposing the learner to a diverse distribution of waitlist states while preserving key statistical properties.
- **Mechanism:** From a single historical trajectory, multiple synthetic trajectories are generated by (1) resampling donor and patient volumes within observed historical bounds, (2) randomly reassigning arrival times within a new horizon, and (3) preserving individual patient progression timelines relative to their new arrival time. This creates varied concurrent waitlist compositions without altering underlying feature distributions or progression hazards.
- **Core assumption:** The underlying distributions of patient features, donor features, and patient progression are stationary over time, so randomizing arrival order does not distort the learning problem.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "We introduce a data augmentation pipeline that generates diverse, medically realistic training scenarios from historical trajectories." [section 5] "Our algorithm for generating such semi-synthetic trajectories... preserves the realistic structure of the waitlist while slightly altering the starting state."
- **Break condition:** If patient progression dynamics are non-stationary (e.g., due to medical advances or changing demographics), the synthetic trajectories may misrepresent true waitlist evolution. Also, if the historical window is too short, the resampling bounds may not capture true variability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Potentials in online matching (from kidney exchange).
  - *Why needed here:* The paper adapts potentials from kidney exchange (which involves cycles and chains) to heart transplantation (immediate, irrevocable matches). Understanding the original concept is key to seeing how they modify greedy policies for long-term efficiency.
  - *Quick check:* How does the role of potentials differ between a setting with altruist donor chains (kidney) versus one with only deceased donors (heart)?
- **Concept:** Imitation learning / behavioral cloning.
  - *Why needed here:* The core training method is offline imitation learning from an omniscient oracle, avoiding the high variance and sample inefficiency of reinforcement learning in this sparse-reward, combinatorial domain.
  - *Quick check:* Why is imitation learning preferred over reinforcement learning for this policy optimization problem?
- **Concept:** Learning-to-rank (pairwise/listwise losses).
  - *Why needed here:* The potential network is trained to rank the omniscient-chosen patient above all others for a given donor and waitlist state. This requires losses that operate on variable-sized preference lists, not standard classification.
  - *Quick check:* What is the difference between pairwise hinge loss (SVM-style) and listwise KL loss in training the potential network, and why might listwise be better for high-dimensional features?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Preprocessor -> Omniscient Oracle -> Data Augmenter -> Potential Network -> Online Policy -> Evaluator
- **Critical path:** Data preprocessing → oracle labeling → data augmentation → network training → deployment simulation. The most computationally heavy step is oracle labeling (solving many LPs), but it is done offline.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Linear vs. neural potentials:* Linear (e.g., 4 or 13 features) is interpretable and scalable but may underfit. Neural (34 features) captures interactions but risks overfitting without augmentation.
  - *Loss function:* Pairwise (SVM/LR) is simpler; listwise (KL) better aligns with global ranking objective. Paper finds listwise slightly better for 34-feature model.
  - *Feature set:* Including waitlist state features (e.g., blood type distribution) improves performance (34-feature > 4-feature) but adds complexity.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - *Performance collapse on test period:* Indicates overfitting to training trajectories or covariate shift. Augmentation should mitigate.
  - *High variance across blood types:* Potential-based policies still show disparity (Fig 5-6); if fairness is a goal, the current `Pθ` does not explicitly penalize inequity.
  - *SMAC fails to converge for high-dim models:* Black-box optimization unscalable; switch to imitation learning.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate Table 2: Train linear potentials (LR) with synthetic data vs. real-only data. Expect ~5 PLYG/month improvement with augmentation.
  2. Ablate feature richness: Compare 4-feature neural potential (blood type only) vs. 34-feature (includes waitlist state). Expect 34-feature to achieve higher mean PLYG.
  3. Test CAS optimization: Learn CAS weights via imitation learning (LR/SVM) vs. SMAC. Expect imitation learning to yield ~665 PLYG/month higher than SMAC.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Can the remaining 5% gap to the omniscient oracle be closed without knowledge of future donor arrivals?
  - *Basis:* "Our best potential-based model achieves a competitive ratio of around 95%... It is unknown whether closing the remaining 5% gap is possible without knowledge of the future."
  - *Why unresolved:* Theoretical limits of no-foresight online matching for heart transplantation are not characterized; unclear if gap is fundamental or due to the learning approach.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* A provable competitive ratio lower bound showing 95% is optimal for any no-foresight policy, or a new algorithm that consistently achieves >95% without future information.
- **Question:** How can potentials be designed to explicitly penalize inequality across demographic groups while maintaining high efficiency?
  - *Basis:* "Using our methodology, designing potentials that explicitly penalize inequality is a feasible direction for future work." and "future work could explore policies that explicitly balance efficiency with equity."
  - *Why unresolved:* Current policy optimizes solely for PLYG, leading to disparities (e.g., blood type O patients face higher mortality). Framework lacks mechanisms to incorporate fairness constraints.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* An extension where the omniscient oracle is constrained to satisfy fairness metrics, or where the loss function directly penalizes inequity, with empirical demonstration of improved fairness and limited efficiency loss.
- **Question:** What incentive misalignments arise from deploying learned potentials, and how can allocation policies be made robust to strategic behavior?
  - *Basis:* "Studying the myriad of incentive issues in the allocation of other organs such as hearts is an important direction for future work." Also cited: "Recent work highlights widespread incentive misalignment in the US heart transplant allocation system."
  - *Why unresolved:* Framework assumes truthful reporting; strategic actions (e.g., listing at multiple centers, refusing offers) could distort assumptions and outcomes.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Game-theoretic analysis of the strategic environment under potential-based policies, identification of dominant manipulation strategies, and design of modified mechanisms that are strategy-proof or robust.
- **Question:** Can interpretable potential models (e.g., shallow decision trees) achieve performance comparable to neural networks while providing clinically transparent logic?
  - *Basis:* "One can learn more interpretable potential functions, such as decision trees, or higher-dimensional functions, including deeper neural networks..."
  - *Why unresolved:* Neural networks yield best empirical results but are black boxes; clinicians and regulators require understandable prioritization rationales. Trade-off between interpretability and performance remains unexplored.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Training decision tree potentials with the same imitation learning framework, then comparing PLYG and fairness metrics against neural networks, supplemented by clinician studies assessing the trees' logical coherence.

## Limitations
- The data augmentation pipeline assumes stationary patient progression dynamics; medical advances or shifting demographics could make synthetic trajectories misrepresent real waitlist evolution.
- The potential function optimizes for aggregate life years (PLYG) without explicit fairness constraints, leading to persistent disparities across blood types (Fig 5–6).
- Evaluation relies on historical simulation using the learned PLYG utility model; if `U(d,p)` is misspecified (e.g., fails to capture post-transplant quality of life), real-world performance may degrade.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Potentials enable non-myopic matching):** Medium. Theoretically sound and supported by imitation from an omniscient oracle, but hinges on accurate waitlist state representation `P(t)` and a well-specified `U(d,p)`. Break conditions are plausible.
- **Claim 2 (Imitation learning + augmentation succeeds):** Medium. Empirical gains over greedy/SMAC are clear, but covariate shift risks remain despite augmentation. Oracle's optimality is assumed, not proven for the true objective.
- **Claim 3 (Achieves 95% of omniscient bound):** Medium-High. Robust within the simulation framework, but depends entirely on the fidelity of the PLYG model and the historical data’s representativeness of future arrivals.

## Next Checks
1. **Stress-test augmentation under non-stationarity:** Simulate trajectories with time-varying patient hazards (e.g., improving survival over years) and measure policy performance decay versus augmentation that accounts for trend shifts.
2. **Audit potential function for disparate impact:** Compute match rates and PLYG gains by demographic/clinical subgroups (blood type, age, sensitization). Test if adding a fairness regularization term to the potential loss reduces disparity at minimal aggregate cost.
3. **Validate PLYG model externally:** Compare predicted life years from `U(d,p)` against actual post-transplant survival in a holdout UNOS cohort, using competing-risks models to account for waitlist mortality.
