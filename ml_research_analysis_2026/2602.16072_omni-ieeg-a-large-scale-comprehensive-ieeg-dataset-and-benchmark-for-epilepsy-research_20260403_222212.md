# Omni-iEEG: A Large-Scale, Comprehensive iEEG Dataset and Benchmark for Epilepsy Research

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16072  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16072  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: PyHFO-Omni achieves macro-F1 0.806 and AUC 0.939 on HFO classification; TimeConv-CNN achieves channel AUC 0.806 and outcome AUC 0.738.

## Executive Summary
Existing iEEG datasets for epilepsy are fragmented, with inconsistent formats and lacking standardized benchmarks and pathological annotations, which hinders reproducibility and clinical translation. Omni-iEEG addresses this by harmonizing 302 patients (178 hours) across eight centers into a unified BIDS schema, providing expert-validated metadata and over 36,000 high-frequency oscillation (HFO) annotations. It defines clinically meaningful tasks with unified metrics: pathological event classification and pathological brain region identification. Benchmarks show that a clinically grounded HFO classifier (PyHFO-Omni) leads in event classification, while an end-to-end model (TimeConv-CNN) excels at channel-level and outcome prediction. Cross-domain transfer from audio-pretrained models (CLAP) also yields competitive performance, demonstrating broad utility.

## Method Summary
The authors aggregated iEEG recordings and clinical data from four public and four private sources, totaling 302 patients. They applied a standardized preprocessing pipeline (resampling to 1000 Hz, bipolar montage where appropriate) and harmonized metadata (seizure onset zones, resections, outcomes) through expert review into a BIDS-compatible structure. For annotations, three HFO detection algorithms generated candidates, which were manually reviewed by four board-certified epileptologists, yielding 36,000+ expert-validated events with high inter-rater agreement (Fleiss' κ=0.92). Two primary benchmark tasks are defined: (1) event-level classification of HFOs (spkHFO, non-spkHFO, artifact) using 570 ms windows; (2) channel-level identification of pathological regions and prediction of surgical outcome from resection ratios. Inputs include raw signals and time-frequency spectrograms (Morlet wavelet, 10–300 Hz). Baselines include PyHFO-Omni for events and TimeConv-CNN for channels, trained with Adam (lr=3e-4, batch size 32) using weighted sampling for class imbalance. Evaluation uses macro-F1, AUC, and outcome AUC, with subject-level splits stratified by data source and outcome.

## Key Results
- PyHFO-Omni achieves macro-F1 of 0.806 and AUC of 0.939 on HFO event classification.
- TimeConv-CNN achieves channel-level AUC of 0.806 and outcome AUC of 0.738 for pathological brain region identification and surgical outcome prediction.
- Audio-pretrained model CLAP transfers effectively to iEEG, demonstrating cross-domain generalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Cross-Center Data Harmonization
Standardizing heterogeneous iEEG recordings and metadata from multiple institutions into a unified BIDS schema eliminates format inconsistencies and enables multi-center machine learning. The authors aggregated data from sources with different sampling rates, channel labels, and clinical annotations, applied a standardized preprocessing pipeline (resampling to 1000 Hz, bipolar montage where appropriate), and harmonized metadata through expert review to ensure consistent definitions of SOZ, resection, and outcomes. The core assumption is that physiological signals and clinical concepts across centers are sufficiently compatible to be aligned without loss of meaning. Evidence includes the harmonization of 302 patients across eight centers with expert-verified metadata and the detailed preprocessing described in Section 3. A break condition occurs if a contributing dataset lacks essential metadata (e.g., SOZ or resection labels), forcing exclusion and reducing cohort diversity.

### Expert-Annotated Pathological Events
A large, multi-rater validated set of HFO annotations provides a reliable supervisory signal for learning pathological biomarkers. Candidate HFOs detected by three algorithms were manually reviewed by four board-certified epileptologists across institutions, achieving high inter-rater agreement (Fleiss' κ=0.92); the annotation protocol is publicly released. The core assumption is that expert consensus approximates a gold-standard definition of pathological HFOs despite inherent subjectivity. Evidence includes over 36,000 expert-validated annotations and the reported κ values in Appendix B. A break condition arises if annotator disagreement rises (κ<0.7), making labels too noisy for supervised training.

### Clinically Grounded Task Design
Defining evaluation tasks and metrics based on clinical priors ensures that model performance is meaningful for surgical planning. The benchmark includes two clinically relevant objectives: (1) distinguishing pathological channels (SOZ vs. preserved in seizure-free patients) and (2) predicting postoperative seizure freedom from resection ratios derived from model scores. Metrics like specificity, recall, and outcome AUC are emphasized. The core assumption is that the clinical surrogates (SOZ channels, resection ratio) are valid proxies for the true epileptogenic zone and therapeutic success. Evidence is found in Section 4.2 and the abstract’s mention of “clinically meaningful tasks with unified evaluation metrics grounded in clinical priors.” A break condition occurs if these priors fail to generalize to new populations (e.g., different epilepsy syndromes), reducing correlation with real-world surgical outcomes.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Data harmonization module (standardizes multi-center data) -> Annotation pipeline (HFO detection and expert review) -> Task-specific data loaders (event vs. segment extraction) -> Model zoo (baseline architectures with pretrained checkpoints) -> Evaluation harness (computes metrics).

**Critical path**:
1. Acquire dataset from project page (Hugging Face).
2. Set environment with provided `omni-ieeg` library (or use MNE).
3. Load a subject’s iEEG and clinical metadata to verify integrity.
4. For Task 1: extract HFO events with annotations, compute spectrograms, train classifier.
5. For Task 2: generate channel-level segment features, train segment model, compute resection ratios, evaluate outcome AUC.
6. Compare against published baselines.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Resampling to 1000 Hz vs. preserving native rates: 1000 Hz simplifies input but discards ultra-high-frequency content >500 Hz; original full-band data remain accessible.
- Event-centric vs. segment-centric models: Event models focus on fine-grained HFO morphology; segment models capture broader context but may be less transparent.
- Segment length: Default 1-minute balances context and memory; shorter clips reduce context, longer increase GPU demands and may dilute patterns.

**Failure signatures**:
- Many “bad” channels remain after harmonization → check channel naming consistency and quality flags.
- Low inter-rater agreement in annotations → verify annotator training and exclude ambiguous subsets.
- Outcome AUC below 0.55 → model scores may not reflect resection completeness; investigate resection ratio calculation or label noise.
- Performance collapse on leave-one-dataset-out → potential source-specific bias; consider domain adaptation or augmentation.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Data Loading Test: Load a known patient (e.g., `sub-openieegUCLA01`) and confirm total channels, duration, and SOZ label match Table 1/14.
2. Baseline Reproduction: Train PyHFO-Omni on provided HFO training split; achieve macro-F1 ≥0.75 and AUC ≥0.90 within 20 epochs.
3. Cross-Center Generalization: Train TimeConv-CNN on all centers except Zurich, evaluate Zurich test set; expect channel AUC >0.6. If performance degrades severely, investigate dataset shift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **How can explainable AI (XAI) methods be effectively integrated into iEEG analysis to provide clinically interpretable insights for epilepsy surgery planning?**  
   The paper identifies XAI development as a future direction, noting that clinical deployment requires models that are interpretable. It remains unresolved because current deep learning models are black boxes; without understanding which features drive predictions, clinicians cannot trust outputs. Evidence to resolve would include comparative studies of XAI methods showing alignment with clinical reasoning, user studies demonstrating improved decision-making with explanations, or novel model architectures that produce clinically meaningful attributions validated by experts.

2. **To what extent do commonly used clinical labels (seizure onset zones, surgical outcomes) introduce noise in model training and evaluation due to their imperfect correlation with the true epileptogenic zone?**  
   The paper uses SOZ channels as positives and preserved channels in seizure-free patients as negatives, acknowledging ambiguity. This is inferred from limitations. The issue is that surgical success depends on many factors beyond EZ resection, and SOZ may not capture the full epileptogenic network, leading to label noise that can mislead learning and affect performance estimates. Resolution would require sensitivity analyses varying label definitions, comparisons against alternative gold standards (e.g., long-term seizure freedom, neurostimulation response), or modeling approaches that account for label uncertainty.

3. **What is the optimal temporal context length for iEEG-based seizure localization, and how should models be designed to effectively leverage long-range dependencies in minute-scale recordings?**  
   The paper introduces TimeConv-CNN for minute-long segments but notes difficulty in modeling such long sequences; an ablation shows performance varies with segment length, indicating the optimal window is unknown. iEEG dynamics are multi-scale; too little context misses patterns, too much includes noise. Current architectures struggle with very long sequences due to computational costs. Evidence to resolve would include systematic experiments across tasks with varied segment lengths, analysis of temporal patterns at different scales, and development of efficient architectures (e.g., temporal convolutions, attention) that scale to long sequences.

4. **Why do models pretrained on non-neurophysiological data (e.g., audio) transfer effectively to iEEG classification, and which specific representations or invariances from the source domain are most beneficial?**  
   The authors explicitly highlight that CLAP, an audio-pretrained model, achieves competitive performance, showing cross-domain generalization. This is surprising given different biophysical origins; the specific features enabling transfer are unknown, limiting better pretraining strategies. Resolution would involve representation analysis (probing, dimensionality reduction) comparing frozen vs. fine-tuned layers, ablation of pretraining objectives, or controlled experiments with synthetic signals to isolate transferable features.

## Limitations
- Incomplete metadata: Only patients with full SOZ, resection, and outcome data are included, causing selection bias.
- HFO annotation limits: Manual review is subjective and covers only a fraction of events; despite high κ, generalizability is uncertain.
- Clinical task assumptions: SOZ channels and resection ratio may not be perfect proxies for epileptogenic zone and seizure freedom, limiting external validity.
