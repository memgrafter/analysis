# Leverage-Weighted Conformal Prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12693
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12693
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** LWCP adapts prediction-interval width to heteroscedasticity by weighting nonconformity scores with statistical leverage, achieving improved conditional coverage without auxiliary models and recovering classical Gaussian intervals while retaining distribution-free marginal coverage.

## Executive Summary
Leverage-Weighted Conformal Prediction (LWCP) addresses a key limitation of standard split conformal prediction: its production of constant-width intervals that over-cover in low-variance regions and under-cover in high-variance regions. Instead of training auxiliary models to adapt widths, LWCP uses the geometry of the design matrix—specifically, the statistical leverage (diagonal of the hat matrix)—to weight nonconformity scores. This simple modification provably preserves finite-sample marginal coverage for any weight function. Under the common scenario where conditional variance factors through leverage, LWCP achieves asymptotically optimal conditional coverage with negligible width penalty. It even recovers the exact form and width of classical Gaussian prediction intervals while maintaining distribution-free guarantees. Experiments across synthetic and real datasets confirm substantial reductions in conditional coverage disparity with minimal computational overhead beyond vanilla conformal prediction.

## Method Summary
LWCP modifies split conformal prediction by introducing a weight function \(w(\cdot)\) applied to nonconformity scores. The procedure: (1) split data into training (\(D_1\)) and calibration (\(D_2\)); (2) train a predictor \(\hat{f}\) on \(D_1\); (3) standardize features using \(D_1\) statistics; (4) compute statistical leverages \(h(x)\) from the standardized design matrix \(X_1\) (via SVD); (5) form weighted scores \(R_i^{(w)} = |Y_i - \hat{f}(X_i)| \cdot w(h(X_i))\) for calibration points; (6) take the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile \(\hat{q}_w\) of these scores; (7) for a test point \(x_{\text{new}}\), construct the interval \(\hat{f}(x_{\text{new}}) \pm \hat{q}_w / w(h(x_{\text{new}}))\). The default weight is \(w(h) = (1+h)^{-1/2}\). Variants include LWCP+ which also learns a scale estimator \(\hat{\sigma}(x)\) to handle heteroscedasticity orthogonal to leverage.

## Key Results
- Vanilla split conformal yields constant-width intervals, causing conditional coverage disparity: over-coverage in low-variance regions and under-coverage in high-variance regions.
- LWCP uses leverage-based weighting to adapt interval widths, substantially reducing conditional coverage gap when heteroscedasticity correlates with leverage.
- LWCP preserves finite-sample marginal coverage exactly (up to \(O(1/n_2)\)) for any weight function and predictor under exchangeability.
- Under a linear model with leverage-dependent heteroscedasticity, the default weight \(w(h)=(1+h)^{-1/2}\) achieves asymptotically optimal conditional coverage with negligible extra width.
- LWCP recovers the precise form and width of classical Gaussian prediction intervals \(\hat{f}(x) \pm \sigma z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{1+h(x)}\) while retaining distribution-free guarantees.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Leverage-Weighted Adaptivity
- **Claim:** Weighting nonconformity scores by a decreasing function of statistical leverage (e.g., \(w(h) = (1+h)^{-1/2}\)) adapts interval width to heteroscedasticity, improving conditional coverage without auxiliary models.
- **Mechanism:** Leverage scores \(h(x)\) measure a point’s influence on the fitted model and directly govern prediction variance. Scaling residuals by \(w(h(x))\) stabilizes the variance of nonconformity scores across the feature space. The conformal quantile then yields intervals that widen for high-leverage (high-variance) points and narrow for low-leverage points.
- **Core Assumption:** Heteroscedasticity factors through leverage: \(\text{Var}(Y|X=x) \propto g(h(x))\). When this holds, \(w^*(h)=1/\sqrt{g(h)}\) achieves asymptotic conditional coverage.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract states derivation “from the design matrix geometry”; Section 2 defines weighted score \(V_w(x,y)=|y-\hat f(x)|\cdot w(h(x))\); Theorem 3.4 proves asymptotic optimality under leverage‑dependent heteroscedasticity.
- **Break Condition:** If \(\eta_{\text{lev}} \approx 0\) (heteroscedasticity orthogonal to leverage), LWCP reduces to vanilla CP. For non‑linear \(\hat{f}\) where input‑space leverage poorly tracks prediction variance, gains diminish.

### Mechanism 2: Finite‑Sample Marginal Coverage via Exchangeability
- **Claim:** For any predictor \(\hat{f}\) and any weight function \(w\), LWCP preserves finite‑sample marginal coverage \(\ge 1-\alpha\) (tight to \(O(1/n_2)\)).
- **Mechanism:** Conditional on training set \(D_1\), the weight \(w(h(\cdot))\) is \(D_1\)-measurable (depends only on \(X_1\)). Weighted nonconformity scores for calibration and test points remain exchangeable. The standard conformal uniformity of ranks argument then gives coverage.
- **Core Assumption:** Data are exchangeable (e.g., i.i.d.). No assumptions on distribution, predictor, or weight.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract: “preserves finite‑sample marginal validity for any weight function”; Theorem 3.1 formal proof using exchangeability.
- **Break Condition:** Exchangeability violation (covariate shift, temporal dependence) breaks marginal coverage. Importance weighting can restore validity under known density ratios.

### Mechanism 3: Recovery of Classical Gaussian Intervals
- **Claim:** Under homoscedastic Gaussian errors with OLS, LWCP recovers the classical prediction‑interval form \(\hat f(x) \pm \sigma z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{1+h(x)}\) while retaining distribution‑free guarantees.
- **Mechanism:** In the Gaussian linear model, prediction variance at \(x\) is \(\sigma^2(1+h(x))\). Setting \(w(h)=(1+h)^{-1/2}\) stabilizes nonconformity scores to i.i.d. half‑normal, yielding quantile \(\hat q_w \to \sigma z_{1-\alpha/2}\). Interval width becomes \(2\hat q_w/w(h(x)) = 2\sigma z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{1+h(x)}\). Coverage remains distribution‑free because the finite‑sample proof (Theorem 3.1) requires no Gaussianity or linearity.
- **Core Assumption:** For exact recovery: linear model, OLS, homoscedastic Gaussian errors. For distribution‑free coverage: only exchangeability and \(D_1\)‑measurability of \(w\).
- **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract: “recovers the form and width of classical prediction intervals under Gaussian assumptions”; Theorem 3.9 formal statement and proof.
- **Break Condition:** Model misspecification breaks classical recovery but preserves marginal coverage. Misaligned \(w\) (e.g., \(w\propto (1+h)^{-1}\) in homoscedastic case) degrades conditional coverage (Theorem 3.14).

## Foundational Learning
- **Statistical leverage (hat matrix diagonal)**
  - Why needed: Leverage scores are the key geometric quantity LWCP uses to adapt intervals; they capture how far \(x\) lies from the training centroid and govern prediction variance.
  - Quick check: For a linear model with design matrix \(X\), leverage of observation \(i\) is \(h_i = X_i (X^\top X)^{-1} X_i^\top\). What does a high leverage value indicate about the observation’s influence?

- **Conformal prediction (split conformal)**
  - Why needed: LWCP is a variant of split conformal; understanding the baseline construction is necessary to see the modification.
  - Quick check: In vanilla split conformal, what two datasets are used and how is the interval derived?

- **Heteroscedasticity and conditional variance**
  - Why needed: LWCP addresses the limitation that vanilla CP produces constant‑width intervals, which over‑cover in low‑variance regions and under‑cover in high‑variance regions.
  - Quick check: Define marginal coverage vs. conditional coverage. Why is conditional coverage harder to achieve?

- **Hat matrix and prediction variance**
  - Why needed: The theoretical analysis ties prediction variance to \(1+h(x)\); this underpins the default weight choice and recovery of classical intervals.
  - Quick check: For OLS, what is the formula for the variance of the predicted mean \(\hat{f}(x)\) and for a new observation \(Y_{\text{new}}\)?

- **Exchangeability**
  - Why needed: LWCP’s finite‑sample marginal coverage proof relies on exchangeability of weighted scores.
  - Quick check: Why does exchangeability guarantee uniformity of ranks in conformal prediction?

- **Scale‑family model**
  - Why needed: The optimality result assumes conditional distribution is a scale family given leverage: \(Y|X=x \sim \text{scale} \cdot \text{base distribution}\).
  - Quick check: If \(\text{Var}(Y|X)=g(h(x))\sigma^2\) and errors are Gaussian, what is the distribution of \(|Y-\hat f(x)|\) after multiplying by \(w(h)=1/\sqrt{g(h)}\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  - Data split (\(D_1\), \(D_2\)) -> Predictor training (\(\hat f\) on \(D_1\)) -> Feature standardization (using \(D_1\) stats) -> Leverage computation (SVD of \(X_1\)) -> Nonconformity scoring (\(R_i^{(w)}\)) -> Quantile estimation (\(\hat q_w\) on \(D_2\)) -> Interval construction (\(\hat f(x_{\text{new}}) \pm \hat q_w / w(h(x_{\text{new}})\)). For LWCP+: add scale estimator \(\hat\sigma\) training on \(D_1\).

- **Critical path**:
  - Train \(\hat f\) → Compute SVD of \(X_1\) → Compute \(h\) for calibration and test → Score calibration points → Compute \(\hat q_w\) → Form interval.
  - Dependencies: Leverage requires \(X_1\); \(\hat f\) must be trained before scoring. Randomized SVD can replace full SVD.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Weight function: Fixed default \(w(h)=(1+h)^{-1/2}\) is safe (Theorem D.13) and near‑optimal when heteroscedasticity aligns with leverage. Data‑driven selection via validation set improves fit but needs extra split.
  - Leverage accuracy: Exact SVD vs. randomized approximation. Approximation preserves coverage exactly (Theorem 3.10) but may degrade conditional coverage if truncation is aggressive (recommend \(k\ge p/2\)).
  - High dimensions (\(p\ge n\)): Use ridge leverage with \(\lambda>0\) (Theorem 3.13) to avoid ill‑conditioned hat matrix; \(\lambda\) can be set by cross‑validation on the prediction task.
  - LWCP+: Includes an auxiliary model (e.g., random forest) for \(\hat\sigma\) to handle feature‑dependent heteroscedasticity beyond leverage. Adds computation (10–100× slower than pure LWCP) but often outperforms studentized CP at lower budget.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Marginal coverage < nominal level: Check exchangeability (time series? covariate shift?) or ensure \(w\) is computed purely from \(D_1\) (no leakage).
  - Conditional gap unchanged vs. vanilla CP: Compute diagnostic \(\hat\eta = \text{std}(h)/\text{mean}(h)\); if \(\hat\eta < 0.5\), leverage heterogeneity is low and LWCP offers little gain.
  - Extremely wide intervals: Points with \(h(x)\) far above calibration maximum indicate extrapolation; flag or clip weight (e.g., \(w_{\text{clip}}(h)=w(\min(h, h_{\max}))\)).
  - Instability under collinearity: If high condition number \(\kappa\), ridge leverage stabilizes (Proposition D.15). Monitor leverage sensitivity to small perturbations in \(X_1\).

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Marginal coverage validation**: Generate i.i.d. data from a linear model (any noise). Run LWCP with \(w\equiv1\) (vanilla) and \(w(h)=(1+h)^{-1/2}\). Over 1,000 replications, check both achieve coverage within binomial error of nominal 90%. Confirms Theorem 3.1.
  2. **Conditional coverage by leverage**: Use a heteroscedastic DGP where \(\text{Var}(Y|X) \propto (1+h(x))\). Split data into leverage deciles. Plot conditional coverage for vanilla CP vs. LWCP. Expect LWCP to be nearly flat across deciles while vanilla undercovers at high leverage.
  3. **Efficiency vs. adaptive baselines**: On a synthetic DGP with leverage‑dependent heteroscedasticity, compute MSCE (mean squared coverage error) for LWCP, CQR, studentized CP, and localized CP. Expect LWCP to achieve similar or better conditional coverage with lower runtime.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Estimating leverage‑explained variance fraction \(\eta_{\text{lev}}\):** Can we robustly and consistently estimate the population \(\eta_{\text{lev}}\) (fraction of conditional variance heterogeneity captured by leverage) from data to decide whether to use LWCP? The heuristic \(\hat\eta = \text{std}(h)/\text{mean}(h)\) is suggested but its relationship to \(\eta_{\text{lev}}\) is not formalized. What threshold on \(\hat\eta\) guarantees LWCP’s gap improvement? A non‑asymptotic bound linking the gap to an estimator of \(\eta_{\text{lev}}\) would resolve this.

2. **Leverage for non‑linear predictors:** What is the appropriate definition of leverage for feature spaces of non‑linear models (e.g., neural networks), and does the default weight \(w(h)=(1+h)^{-1/2}\) remain provably safe and near‑optimal? Theory exists for input‑space leverage under linear models, but for non‑linear \(\hat{f}\) the efficiency guarantees rely on a qualitative mismatch coefficient \(\eta_{\text{lev}}\). A theorem analogous to Theorem 3.4 for a linearized neural network regime is needed to justify the default weight.

3. **Finite‑sample analysis of LWCP+:** What is the precise statistical efficiency (conditional coverage gap, expected width) of LWCP+ when the scale estimator \(\hat\sigma(x)\) is learned from finite data (e.g., random forest with finite trees), and how should one balance the complexity of \(\hat\sigma\) against potential improvements? The current analysis assumes perfect \(\hat\sigma\); a decomposition of error into residual heteroscedasticity and \(\hat\sigma\) estimation error, with rates, would guide hyperparameter choices.

4. **Group‑conditional coverage:** Can we design a theoretically grounded and computationally efficient variant of LWCP that provides distribution‑free conditional coverage guarantees for *subpopulations* or *groups* (e.g., fairness‑aware coverage), rather than only for the continuous leverage score? Mondrian LWCP (separate quantile per group) is proposed but loses power in small groups. Analysis of the trade‑off and empirical evaluation on fairness benchmarks is needed.

5. **Diagnostics for misalignment:** For LWCP+ or any LWCP variant with learned \(\hat\sigma\), can we construct finite‑sample, distribution‑free diagnostics or tests to detect when the leverage‑based correction has been misapplied—e.g., when heteroscedasticity is “anti‑aligned” with leverage (\(g_{\text{adv}}(h) \propto 1/(1+h)\)) or when \(\hat\sigma\) is severely misestimated? A slope‑based test from regressing squared residuals on leverages could flag negative correlation and trigger a fallback to vanilla CP.

## Limitations
- LWCP’s adaptivity requires heteroscedasticity to correlate with statistical leverage. If variance is orthogonal to leverage, LWCP offers no improvement over vanilla CP and the simple diagnostic \(\hat\eta\) may not reliably detect weak dependence.
- Theoretical conditional coverage results are asymptotic and rely on exact knowledge of the variance function \(g(h)\) for the optimal weight. Finite‑sample misspecification or small calibration sizes can degrade performance; the default weight is near‑optimal only when \(g(h)\propto(1+h)\).
- High‑dimensional or ill‑conditioned designs (\(p\ge n\)) destabilize the ordinary hat matrix. Ridge leverage is proposed but the choice of \(\lambda\) is not fully addressed and may affect both coverage and efficiency.
- LWCP+ adds an auxiliary model (e.g., random forest) to estimate \(\sigma(x)\), increasing runtime 10–100× and risking overfitting if not carefully regularized, partially negating the original efficiency promise.
- The “negligible computational overhead” claim holds primarily for linear predictors; with non‑linear predictors, computing leverage via SVD of \(X_1\) can dominate cost for large \(n\) or \(p\).
- Marginal coverage requires exchangeability; under distribution shift (temporal dependence, covariate shift) coverage may fail unless importance weighting is incorporated, which adds complexity.

## Confidence
- **Marginal coverage guarantee** (Theorem 3.1): LWCP preserves finite‑sample marginal coverage \(\ge 1-\alpha\) for any weight function under exchangeability. **Confidence: High** (rigorous proof).
- **Asymptotic conditional coverage** (Theorem 3.4): If \(\operatorname{Var}(Y|X)\propto g(h)\) and \(w=1/\sqrt{g(h)}\), LWCP attains asymptotically optimal conditional coverage with minimal width cost. **Confidence: Medium** (asymptotic, assumes perfect knowledge of \(g\)).
- **Empirical reduction in conditional coverage disparity**: Synthetic results show large improvements; real‑data improvements are consistent but rely on unverified heteroscedastic structure. **Confidence: Medium**.

## Next Checks
1. **Orthogonal heteroscedasticity test:** Simulate a DGP where \(\operatorname{Var}(Y|X)\) depends on features orthogonal to leverage (e.g., rotate \(X\)). Verify LWCP’s conditional coverage gap matches vanilla CP and that \(\hat\eta\approx0\), confirming the break condition.
2. **High‑dimensional stability assessment:** Set \(p\gg n\) (e.g., \(p=1000, n_1=200\)) with strong collinearity. Compare exact SVD, randomized SVD (varying rank), and ridge leverage (\(\lambda\) via CV). Check marginal coverage remains \(\ge1-\alpha\) and quantify any conditional coverage loss versus low‑dimensional regimes.
3. **Finite‑sample asymptotic convergence:** For a leverage‑dependent DGP (\(\operatorname{Var}(Y|X)=\sigma^2(1+h(x))\)), vary calibration size \(n_2\in\{100,500,2000\}\). Measure maximum conditional coverage error and average width inflation. Confirm error decays as \(O(1/\sqrt{n_2})\) and width inflation stays bounded.
