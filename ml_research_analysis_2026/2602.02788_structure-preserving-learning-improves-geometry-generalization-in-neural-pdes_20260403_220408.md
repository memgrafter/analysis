# Structure-Preserving Learning Improves Geometry Generalization in Neural PDEs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.02788
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02788
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: Geo-NeW achieves state-of-the-art out-of-distribution geometry generalization (e.g., normalized L2 error 0.033 vs 8.90 on Poly-Poisson and 0.112 vs 0.38 on Pipe flow) while exactly enforcing Dirichlet boundary conditions.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the limited geometry generalization of neural PDE solvers by proposing Geo-NeW, a data-driven finite element method that learns a differential operator and compatible reduced Whitney form spaces conditioned on mesh geometry. Geometry is encoded through transformer‑based embeddings of intrinsic descriptors (HKS, HC, SDF), enabling adaptation to unseen meshes. By building on finite element exterior calculus, Geo-NeW exactly preserves physical conservation laws and Dirichlet boundary conditions. Experiments demonstrate substantial out‑of‑distribution improvements on challenging benchmarks, with near‑perfect boundary compliance and competitive in‑distribution performance.

## Method Summary
Geo-NeW reformulates PDE solving as learning an implicit operator \(G_\theta(u,\alpha)=0\), where \(u\) is the solution field and \(\alpha\) encodes the geometry. The operator is constructed from reduced‑order finite element spaces (Whitney forms) that are conditioned on a geometry context \(z\) obtained via a transformer processing intrinsic mesh features. Boundary conditions are exactly enforced by eliminating constrained degrees of freedom. During training, the model is optimized using PDE‑constrained loss with gradients computed via implicit differentiation (adjoint method) through Newton solves. At inference, the learned operator is solved on any new mesh, yielding a solution that respects the mesh topology and metrics.

## Key Results
- Poly-Poisson OOD test: normalized L2 error 0.033 vs 8.90 for the strongest baseline.
- Pipe flow benchmark: 0.112 vs 0.38.
- Exact (zero error) Dirichlet boundary enforcement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Learning a differential operator \(G_\theta(u,\alpha)\) instead of the solution map \(S(\alpha)\) decouples learned physics from mesh‑specific discretizations, enabling generalization to new geometries.  
**Mechanism:** The model is trained to find \(u\) such that \(G_\theta(u,\alpha)=0\) and \(B_\theta(u)=u_b\). At inference, the same operator is solved implicitly (e.g., Newton) on any new mesh \(M_g\), producing \(u\). The operator is built from reduced finite element spaces conditioned on geometry, so it respects the new mesh’s topology and metric data.  
**Evidence:** [abstract] “we instead pose the learning problem as identifying a reduced‑order finite element model which links coordinate‑free representations of both physics and geometry through strongly imposed boundary conditions.” [section 2.1] “We instead construct approximate operators…such that \(S\) is defined implicitly.”  
**Break condition:** If \(G_\theta\) is not coercive or Lipschitz‑bounded on a new geometry, the implicit solve may diverge.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Structure preservation via Finite Element Exterior Calculus (FEEC) and Whitney forms guarantees exact enforcement of conservation laws and Dirichlet boundary conditions across geometries, providing a strong inductive bias.  
**Mechanism:** The reduced spaces \(W^0_g\) and \(W^1_g\) preserve the exact sequence property (\(\nabla W^0_g \subseteq W^1_g\)), ensuring discrete compatibility (div‑grad‑curl). The discrete system inherits mesh topology via incidence \(\delta\) and metrics via mass matrices \(M_0, M_1\). Boundary conditions are enforced exactly by eliminating constrained degrees of freedom, yielding zero Dirichlet error.  
**Evidence:** [section 2.2] “These spaces yield linear algebra building blocks sufficient for discretizing H(div) conservation laws…” [section B] describes the discrete de Rham complex and compatibility. [section 3.5] explains exact Dirichlet BC enforcement.  
**Break condition:** If the reduced spaces fail to preserve the exact sequence (e.g., due to poor conditioning), discrete conservation may be violated.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Geometry encoding via HKS, HC, SDF and a transformer‑based mesh encoder provides coordinate‑free, discretization‑invariant context that conditions the reduced FE spaces and flux, enabling adaptation to unseen meshes.  
**Mechanism:** Intrinsic descriptors (HKS: spectral signature; HC: boundary‑sensitive coordinates; SDF: distance to boundaries) are processed by an inducing‑point transformer to produce per‑node embeddings \(z\). These embeddings cross‑attention pool to condition the neural field \(W(x,z)\) defining the PoU basis and the flux \(F_\theta(u,z)\). The intrinsic features and anchor attention yield invariance to mesh discretization and rigid transforms.  
**Evidence:** [section 3] “Together, these features provide a translationally‑ and rotationally‑invariant representation…” [section C] describes harmonic extensions and invariance. [abstract] “Geometry enters the model as a discretized mesh…”  
**Break condition:** If descriptors are not discriminative enough (e.g., isospectral shapes) or transformer lacks capacity, conditioning fails.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Eθ (geometry encoder) → z → (Wθ reduced basis, Fθ flux) → Gθ(u,z)=0 → Newton solve → u → Loss → adjoint → θ update.
- **Critical path**: Geometry (mesh + features) → Encoder Eθ → context z → (Wθ reduced basis + Fθ flux) → Assemble Gθ → Implicit Newton solve for u → Reconstruction loss + PDE residual → Backward via adjoint → Update parameters. Forward solve convergence is essential.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Reduced dimension P: Larger P increases expressivity but raises solve cost (O(P³)) and memory (P²).
  - Lipschitz bound ζ: Tighter ζ ensures well‑posedness but may restrict flux nonlinearity, hurting accuracy.
  - Encoder capacity vs invariance: More layers/heads improve geometric context but risk overfitting to training discretizations; anchor attention balances cost and global aggregation.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Non‑converging Newton solves: caused by high Lipschitz constant, poor initial guess, or ill‑conditioned stiffness matrix.
  - Boundary leakage: non‑zero Dirichlet error indicates basis Wθ did not properly incorporate boundary partitions.
  - OOD collapse: sudden error increase on geometrically extrapolated tests; likely due to insufficiently discriminative geometric descriptors.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Standard benchmarks (Pipe, Elasticity, NACA) to verify in‑distribution competence against baselines like Transolver.
  2. Poly‑Poisson OOD test (train on polygons with <5 sides, test on >5) to demonstrate geometry generalization.
  3. Ablation comparing mesh‑based encoding vs point‑cloud input to isolate the benefit of mesh‑aware processing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to time‑dependent PDEs**: The paper only considers steady‑state problems; handling temporal dynamics would require new formulations (e.g., space‑time FEEC or time‑marching) and stability guarantees.
2. **Exact enforcement of incompressibility in Navier‑Stokes**: The treatment of the divergence‑free constraint is not described; exact enforcement via mixed H(div)‑L² elements would be needed for mass conservation across geometries.
3. **3D scalability and performance**: The framework is evaluated only in 2D; its computational cost and memory usage on large 3D meshes (e.g., up to 10⁶ elements) remain unknown.

## Limitations
- No theoretical guarantee that the learned operator \(G_\theta\) remains coercive and Lipschitz‑bounded on arbitrary unseen geometries.
- Geometry encoding relies on intrinsic descriptors (HKS, HC, SDF) that may fail to distinguish shapes with similar spectra or extreme anisotropy.
- Inference requires solving an implicit Newton system; cubic cost in reduced dimension \(P\) and lack of 3D scalability studies hinder practical deployment.
