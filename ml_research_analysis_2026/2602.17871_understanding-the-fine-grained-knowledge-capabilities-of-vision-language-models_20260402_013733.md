# Understanding the Fine-Grained Knowledge Capabilities of Vision-Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17871
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17871
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: Fine-grained classification is a distinct dimension of visual intelligence; upgrading the vision encoder and unfreezing the LLM during pretraining significantly improve fine-grained accuracy; a ~12 percentage point gap to state-of-the-art remains due to larger pretraining scale.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why vision-language models (VLMs) often underperform on fine-grained image classification despite excelling at general visual question answering. Through systematic evaluation of 15 VLMs on four fine-grained benchmarks and eight general VQA benchmarks, along with 22 ablation experiments, the authors reveal that fine-grained performance is not correlated with general VQA scores, confirming it as a separate capability. Key findings show that enhancing the vision encoder (e.g., using DFN‑CLIP) and unfreezing the language model during pretraining yield substantial gains in fine-grained accuracy (+4.5pp and +5.5pp respectively) without compromising general VQA. However, a performance gap of approximately 12 percentage points remains compared to state-of-the-art models like Qwen2‑VL, which the authors attribute to its massive pretraining scale of 1.4 trillion tokens. The study underscores the importance of vision encoder quality, training strategy, and data scale for fine-grained visual understanding.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluated 15 vision-language models on four fine-grained classification benchmarks (ImageNet‑1K, Flowers, Pets, Food) and eight general VQA benchmarks. They conducted 22 ablation experiments to isolate the effects of four key factors: language model choice, vision encoder, pretraining data (scale and quality), and weight updating strategies (connector‑only vs. LLM+connector). Models followed a standard LLaVA‑style architecture with a vision encoder, a connector (MLP), and a large language model. Two‑stage training was used: first, pretraining on image‑caption pairs to align visual and textual representations; second, instruction tuning on multimodal dialogue data. The ablations systematically varied components (e.g., CLIP vs. DFN‑CLIP, Vicuna vs. Qwen2, LLaVA CC‑3M vs. Molmo PixMo‑Cap) and updated either only the connector or both connector and LLM during pretraining. Performance was measured as average accuracy across the benchmarks.

## Key Results
- Fine-grained classification performance varies widely among VLMs with similar general VQA scores, indicating it is a distinct dimension of visual intelligence.
- Upgrading the vision encoder to DFN‑CLIP yields a +4.5 percentage point improvement in fine-grained accuracy when combined with sufficient pretraining; unfreezing the LLM during pretraining adds a further +5.5pp without harming general VQA.
- A remaining gap of approximately 12 percentage points to top models (e.g., Qwen2‑VL at 87.9%) is attributed to their much larger pretraining scale (1.4 trillion tokens).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Vision Encoder Upgrade
The claim is that upgrading the vision encoder to a stronger model like DFN‑CLIP disproportionately improves fine-grained classification compared to general VQA. The mechanism is that DFN‑CLIP is trained on larger‑scale, higher‑quality data with improved filtering, producing more discriminative visual features essential for distinguishing fine‑grained categories. These enhanced features transfer directly to classification tasks but have limited impact on general VQA, which relies more on reasoning and multimodal alignment. The core assumption is that fine‑grained classification depends primarily on the quality of visual representations from the vision encoder, while general VQA depends more on the LLM’s reasoning and connector alignment. Evidence: Switching to DFN‑CLIP improves fine‑grained performance by +4.5pp and general performance by only +1.2pp given enough pretraining (Section 4.2.2). However, without prior pretraining, a stronger vision encoder can degrade general VQA (‑2.5pp) and provide minimal fine‑grained gain (+2.8pp), indicating that improper integration negates the benefits.

### LLM Unfreezing During Pretraining
Unfreezing the LLM during the pretraining stage substantially boosts fine‑grained classification without harming general VQA. The mechanism is that allowing the LLM weights to update during pretraining (after a connector warmup) enables the language model to align its textual knowledge and reasoning patterns with the specific visual features produced by the connector and vision encoder, creating a more cohesive representation for classification. This adaptation is particularly beneficial for tasks requiring precise visual discrimination. The core assumption is that the LLM must actively learn to interpret the visual representations; freezing it limits this adaptation. Evidence: Pretraining both LLM and connector (instead of only connector) significantly enhances fine‑grained classification (+5.5pp) without compromising general VQA scores (abstract, Section 4.3.1). A break condition: if the LLM is very small or poorly pretrained, unfreezing may not yield gains or could destabilize training; the 20% connector‑only warmup appears necessary for stability.

### Pretraining Scale
The remaining performance gap (~12 percentage points) between the authors’ ablated models and top models like Qwen2‑VL is primarily due to massive pretraining data scale (1.4T tokens vs. ~0.2T tokens in their experiments). The mechanism is that extremely large‑scale pretraining exposes the model to a vast diversity of fine‑grained visual concepts and image‑caption pairs, building robust, generalizable visual knowledge that smaller‑scale pretraining cannot capture. This scaling effect is especially pronounced for fine‑grained tasks that require recognizing subtle distinctions across many categories. The core assumption is that fine‑grained knowledge acquisition follows stronger data scaling laws than general VQA abilities; reaching high performance requires orders of magnitude more pretraining tokens. Evidence: Their experiments involved relatively small datasets (~200M–400M tokens), whereas Qwen2‑VL used 1.4T tokens (Section 4.4). However, this attribution is correlational; architectural differences may also contribute.

## Foundational Learning
**VLM Architecture**: The standard LLaVA‑style design comprises three components: a vision encoder (extracts visual features), a connector (e.g., MLP that maps vision features to the LLM’s input space), and a large language model (processes combined multimodal tokens). This architecture is fundamental because the paper’s ablations manipulate each component separately to assess their individual contributions to fine‑grained versus general performance.
- *Why needed here*: To interpret how changes in one part (e.g., vision encoder) affect overall performance, one must understand the role and data flow of each component.
- *Quick check*: In the LLaVA architecture, what are the three core components and what does each do?

**Two‑Stage Training**: Vision‑language models typically undergo two training phases: (1) *Pretraining* on image‑caption pairs to align visual and textual representations without explicit task supervision; (2) *Instruction tuning* on task‑specific multimodal data (e.g., VQA, dialogue) to teach the model to follow instructions. The stages serve distinct purposes: pretraining builds general multimodal knowledge, while instruction tuning adapts the model to downstream tasks.
- *Why needed here*: The paper isolates the effects of pretraining (e.g., data scale, weight updating) versus instruction tuning; confusing these stages would misinterpret the results.
- *Quick check*: What is the primary objective of the pretraining stage versus the instruction tuning stage in VLMs?

**Benchmark Types**: Fine‑grained classification benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet‑1K, Flowers, Pets, Food) require distinguishing between visually similar subcategories, testing nuanced visual recognition. General VQA benchmarks (e.g., MMMU, MMBench, MMStar) assess broader multimodal reasoning, world knowledge, and contextual understanding.
- *Why needed here*: The central finding is that performance on these two benchmark types is decoupled; a model can excel on general VQA yet lag in fine‑grained classification. Understanding the differing demands of the benchmarks is essential to grasp the paper’s conclusions.
- *Quick check*: Why might a model score similarly on MMMU but very differently on ImageNet‑1K?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Vision Encoder -> Connector -> LLM

**Critical Path**: The experimental progression isolates the impact of each design choice:
1. Establish a baseline: Train a standard LLaVA‑1.5 model (Vicuna‑7B + CLIP ViT‑L/14) with connector‑only pretraining; expect fine‑grained accuracy ~52–59% and general VQA ~39–42%.
2. Upgrade the vision encoder: Replace CLIP ViT‑L/14 with DFN‑CLIP ViT‑H/14, keeping all else identical. Without pretraining, expect minimal fine‑grained gain (+2.8pp) and possible drop in general VQA (‑2.5pp); with connector pretraining, expect a larger fine‑grained gain (+4.5pp).
3. Unfreeze the LLM during pretraining: On the best setup from step 2 (DFN‑CLIP + Qwen2‑7B with connector pretraining), switch from connector‑only to LLM+connector pretraining. Expect a significant fine‑grained boost (+5.5pp) while maintaining general VQA scores.
4. For state‑of‑the‑art performance: Combine all above (DFN‑CLIP, Qwen2, LLM+connector pretraining) with massive pretraining scale (>1T tokens).

**Design Tradeoffs**
- *Vision encoder strength vs. pretraining*: A stronger encoder (DFN‑CLIP) only yields net benefits if the connector is properly pretrained first; otherwise it can harm general VQA.
- *LLM unfreezing*: Increases computational cost and risk of catastrophic forgetting; a 20% connector‑only warmup is necessary for stable training.
- *Data quality vs. scale*: High‑quality captions (Molmo PixMo‑Cap) show minimal benefit over web‑scraped captions (LLaVA CC‑3M) when the LLM remains frozen during pretraining, suggesting that scale may be more important than caption quality in that regime.

**Failure Signatures**
- Model achieves high general VQA (>50%) but low fine‑grained (<60%): Likely missing pretraining or using a weaker vision encoder without DFN‑CLIP.
- DFN‑CLIP model without pretraining shows degraded general VQA: Indicates the connector is not aligned to the new visual feature distribution.
- Unfreezing the LLM leads to unstable training or formatting errors: Insufficient connector warmup or too high a learning rate for the LLM.
- No improvement from switching to PixMo‑Cap data: Expected if the LLM remains frozen during pretraining.

**First 3 Experiments**
1. Baseline audit: Train LLaVA‑1.5 (Vicuna‑7B + CLIP ViT‑L/14, connector‑only pretraining) and evaluate on all fine‑grained and general VQA benchmarks to establish the performance gap.
2. Vision encoder ablation: Swap CLIP for DFN‑CLIP ViT‑H/14, holding everything else constant. Compare results with and without connector pretraining to isolate the encoder’s differential impact.
3. LLM updating ablation: Using the best configuration from experiment 2 (DFN‑CLIP + Qwen2‑7B with connector pretraining), compare connector‑only vs. LLM+connector pretraining to measure the fine‑grained gain and monitor general VQA stability.

## Limitations
- The attribution of the ~12pp performance gap to pretraining scale is correlational; Qwen2‑VL may have undisclosed architectural advantages (e.g., connector design, context length) that also contribute.
- Data quality conclusions are based on only two caption datasets (LLaVA CC‑3M vs. Molmo PixMo‑Cap), limiting generalizability to other notions of quality.
- External validation is weak: neighboring related work averages 0.0 citations, indicating little independent replication of these findings.

## Confidence
- Vision encoder upgrade (Mechanism 1): High
- LLM unfreezing during pretraining (Mechanism 2): High
- Pretraining scale explanation (Mechanism 3): Medium

## Next Checks
1. Generalize vision encoder benefit: Integrate DFN‑CLIP into at least two other VLM architectures (different connector designs) and repeat the ablation to confirm the disproportionate fine‑grained gain.
2. Direct scaling test: In the best‑performing setup, incrementally increase pretraining tokens from ~200M to 1.4T while holding architecture fixed, to quantify scaling curves for fine‑grained vs. general performance.
3. Isolate architectural confounders: Replicate Qwen2‑VL’s reported architectural details (e.g., connector depth, vision token sampling) within the authors’ framework; if the gap closes, architecture plays a major role; if it persists, scale remains primary.
