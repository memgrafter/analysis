# Simplify-This: A Comparative Analysis of Prompt-Based and Fine-Tuned LLMs

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2601.05794
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05794
*   **Reference count:** 39
*   **Primary result:** Fine-tuned encoder-decoder models achieve more genuine text simplification than prompting, as measured by human preference and structural metrics like SARI.

## Executive Summary
This study systematically compares two primary paradigms for applying large language models (LLMs) to text simplification: supervised fine-tuning and prompt engineering. Using encoder-decoder models (BART, T5, Pegasus, ProphetNet) fine-tuned on a cleaned WikiLarge dataset and prompted with ten diverse templates, the authors evaluate on three English simplification benchmarks (ASSET, Med-EASi, OneStopEnglish). Results from automatic metrics (SARI, FKGL, BERTScore, LENS) and a human preference study (260 judgments) consistently show that fine-tuning produces outputs with greater structural simplification. Prompt-based approaches, while sometimes scoring highly on similarity metrics, frequently result in verbatim copying of the input. Human evaluators significantly preferred fine-tuned simplifications. The work establishes fine-tuning as the more reliable method for achieving meaningful simplification, cautioning that prompt-based approaches can yield superficial results.

## Method Summary
The study employs a comparative experimental design. For the fine-tuning paradigm, four encoder-decoder models are supervised fine-tuned on a cleaned version of the WikiLarge dataset (124k sentence pairs). For the prompt-based paradigm, the same four models are used zero-shot with ten manually crafted prompt templates designed to elicit simplification. Model outputs are evaluated automatically using SARI (for lexical/structural changes), FKGL (for readability), BERTScore and LENS (for semantic similarity), and by measuring the rate of identical output-input copying. A final human evaluation, where annotators choose the better simplification between paired model outputs, provides a preference judgment.

## Key Results
*   Fine-tuned models significantly outperform prompted models on SARI, indicating they apply more substantial lexical and syntactic changes.
*   Prompted models exhibit a high rate of identical output-input copying, suggesting they often fail to simplify beyond surface-level adjustments or direct replication.
*   In a human preference study, simplifications from fine-tuned models were chosen significantly more often (46.9%) than those from prompts (31.5%), with a p-value of 0.0062.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Fine-tuning adapts the model's internal weights specifically for the simplification task, teaching it to transform complex input representations into simplified output representations. This weight adjustment embeds the simplification "policy" into the model's parameters, leading to consistent, non-trivial transformations. In contrast, prompting relies on the model's pre-trained knowledge and in-context learning to follow an instruction. Without dedicated weight updates, the model's default behavior often prioritizes fluency and semantic preservation (measured by BERTScore/LENS) over active simplification, leading to high copying rates as a safe, high-similarity strategy that avoids potential meaning alteration.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Text Simplification:** The core NLP task of reducing linguistic complexity while preserving meaning. *Why needed:* Defines the objective and success criteria. *Quick check:* Does a simplified sentence use simpler vocabulary/syntax without losing key information?
*   **SARI (System Output Against References and Input):** An automatic metric that rewards n-gram changes present in reference simplifications but absent in the input. *Why needed:* Primary metric for evaluating the degree of structural/lexical simplification. *Quick check:* Why does SARI penalize copying and reward novel n-grams found in references?
*   **Fine-Tuning vs. Prompting:** Two paradigms for adapting LLMs. Fine-tuning updates model weights on task-specific data; prompting uses frozen weights with natural language instructions. *Why needed:* Central comparison of the paper. *Quick check:* What are the major cost, data, and flexibility trade-offs between these approaches?
*   **Encoder-Decoder Architecture:** Model structure (e.g., BART, T5) where an encoder processes input and a decoder generates output, commonly used for sequence-to-sequence tasks. *Why needed:* The specific model family studied. *Quick check:* How does the separate encoder/decoder design differ from a decoder-only model (like GPT) for text generation?
*   **Human Evaluation for Text Quality:** Direct assessment by people to judge attributes like adequacy, fluency, and simplicity. *Why needed:* Provides a ground truth for what constitutes a "good" simplification, validating automatic metrics. *Quick check:* What specific criteria might human evaluators use to judge if a simplification is successful?
*   **BERTScore & LENS:** Semantic similarity metrics based on contextual embeddings (BERTScore) or learned evaluation models (LENS). *Why needed:* Used to assess meaning preservation; often favor outputs similar to the input. *Quick check:* Why might high BERTScore/LENS correlate with verbatim copying rather than genuine simplification?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Input Text -> [Model: BART/T5/Pegasus/ProphetNet] -> Simplified Output; Split into two paths: (1) Fine-Tuned Weights <- Cleaned WikiLarge Data, (2) Frozen Weights + Prompt Template.

**Critical Path:** The input sentence is processed by the model's encoder. The decoder then generates tokens autoregressively. The critical difference lies in the decoder's generation behavior: fine-tuned models have learned a direct mapping to a simplified latent space, while prompted models must infer the simplification task from the prompt's instruction within their general pre-trained distribution.

**Design Tradeoffs:** Fine-tuning offers higher task-specific performance and genuine simplification but requires labeled data, computational resources for training, and a fixed model. Prompting is flexible, requires no training data, and can be applied to any pre-trained model, but performance is highly sensitive to prompt design and often defaults to conservative, copying outputs due to the model's training objective (next-token prediction on diverse data).

**Failure Signatures:** For prompting: a) High "identical ratio" (output equals input), b) Low SARI scores despite high BERTScore/LENS. For fine-tuning: a) Over-simplification or meaning drift (lower BERTScore), b) Poor generalization to out-of-domain texts if training data is narrow.

**First Three Experiments:**
1.  **Replicate core finding with decoder-only models:** Run the same comparison (fine-tuning vs. prompting) on instruction-tuned decoder-only models (e.g., Llama-3-Instruct) to test if the paradigm difference holds outside encoder-decoder architectures.
2.  **Prompt ablation and decoding parameter sweep:** Systematically vary prompt templates, few-shot examples, and decoding parameters (temperature, top-p) for prompting to determine if the high copying rate is an artifact of the specific template set or decoding strategy used.
3.  **Cross-domain fine-tuning evaluation:** Fine-tune a model on WikiLarge and evaluate its performance on a completely out-of-domain simplification dataset (e.g., legal or biomedical text) to measure robustness and the cost of domain mismatch.

## Limitations
*   **Model Scope:** Findings are restricted to traditional encoder-decoder models; they may not generalize to modern decoder-only or multi-modal LLMs.
*   **Human Evaluation:** The preference study, while statistically significant, involved a limited number of judgments (260) without detailed demographic or expertise breakdown of annotators.
*   **Dataset & Metric Biases:** Fine-tuning used WikiLarge, which may not represent all simplification domains; automatic metrics like SARI may over-value lexical changes, while LENS/BERTScore may inadvertently reward the copying seen in prompt outputs.

## Confidence
*   *Fine-tuning yields more genuine simplification than prompting (for studied models/datasets):* **High**
*   *Prompt-based simplification often results in superficial copying:* **Medium-High**
*   *Results generalize to all LLM architectures and simplification domains:* **Low**

## Next Checks
1.  Replicate the study using recent instruction-tuned decoder-only models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, Llama-3) via both prompting and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA).
2.  Evaluate on non-English simplification corpora and highly specialized domains (e.g., clinical notes, legal contracts) to test cross-lingual and domain robustness.
3.  Conduct a larger-scale human evaluation with diverse annotators (e.g., target end-users like language learners or subject matter experts) using explicit rubrics for simplicity, fluency, and meaning preservation.
