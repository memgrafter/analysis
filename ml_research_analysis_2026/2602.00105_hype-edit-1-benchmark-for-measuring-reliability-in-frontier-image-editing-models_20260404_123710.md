# HYPE-EDIT-1: Benchmark for Measuring Reliability in Frontier Image Editing Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.00105
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00105
- **Reference count:** 2
- **Primary result:** Models with lower per-image pricing can exhibit higher effective cost in practice due to greater retry requirements, undermining simple cost-per-image comparisons.

## Executive Summary
HYPE-EDIT-1 introduces a benchmark to measure the real-world reliability and effective operational cost of frontier image editing models, addressing the gap between demo-quality outputs and consistent performance in production workflows. The benchmark evaluates 100 reference-based marketing and design tasks by generating 10 independent outputs per task per model, using human majority voting to determine pass/fail labels. Results across seven models reveal substantial variability in per-attempt success rates (34%–83%) and highlight that effective cost per successful edit—factoring in both model pricing and human review time—ranges from $0.66 to $1.42. The core finding is that models with cheaper per-image pricing often require more retries, leading to higher total operational costs and challenging simplistic cost-per-image evaluations.

## Method Summary
The benchmark employs a repeated-trial protocol: for each of 100 tasks (50 public, 50 private), a model generates 10 independent outputs given an instruction and reference image(s). Each output is judged PASS/FAIL by a panel of 5 human raters (majority vote), with a VLM judge (Gemini 3 Flash) serving as a scalable consistency check. From these labels, per-task success probability \(p_t\) is estimated. Aggregated metrics include per-attempt pass rate (P@1), pass@10 (probability of ≥1 success in 10 tries), pass@4 (success within 4 retries), expected attempts under a retry cap \(A=4\), and effective cost \(C_{\text{eff}}\). \(C_{\text{eff}}\) combines model cost per candidate, a fixed human review cost ($0.278 per candidate, based on $50/hr and 20s review), and the expected number of attempts per successful edit, normalized by pass@4. A held-out private task split is used for server-side leaderboards to mitigate contamination.

## Key Results
- Per-attempt pass rates (P@1) across seven models range from 34% to 83%.
- Pass@4 (success within four tries) ranges from 57% to 90%.
- Effective cost per successful edit varies from $0.66 to $1.42.
- Models with lower per-image costs often have higher effective costs due to lower reliability and increased retry burden.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Repeated trials transform stochastic model behavior into a measurable reliability distribution. By sampling 10 independent outputs per task, the benchmark estimates a per-task success probability \(p_t\), enabling calculation of Pass@k metrics that model user retry behavior. This assumes outputs are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) across tries, approximating real-world retries. Evidence includes the benchmark’s generation of \(K=10\) candidates per task and analogous use of pass@k in related work like "Cost-of-Pass."

Effective cost modeling integrates monetary and human overhead. The metric \(C_{\text{eff}} = (E \cdot C_{\text{attempt}})/p@4\) accounts for model cost per candidate \(C_{\text{model}}\), fixed human review cost \(C_{\text{review}}\), and expected attempts \(E\) under a retry cap. This reveals that low per-image pricing can be misleading if retries are frequent. The approach is grounded in economic evaluation frameworks from "Cost-of-Pass" and explicitly defines \(C_{\text{review}}\) and a practical retry limit \(A=4\).

A private held-out task split preserves benchmark longevity. By reserving 50 tasks unreleased to the public and using them for official server-side evaluation, the benchmark reduces the risk of model overfitting to the public set. This design follows standard ML evaluation practices to ensure generalizable reliability measurement.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Pass@k Reliability Metric**  
Why needed here: HYPE-EDIT-1 focuses on reliability over peak quality. Pass@k (probability of ≥1 success in k tries) captures the practical user experience of retrying and enables calculation of expected attempts. Quick check: If P@1 = 40%, what is approximate Pass@4? (Answer: \(1 - (0.6)^4 \approx 0.87\), though the paper’s cap-adjusted formula gives a precise expected attempts value).

**Concept: Effective Cost Modeling**  
Why needed here: The paper’s key conclusion is that cheaper per-image models can be more expensive in practice. This requires combining stochastic success rates, model pricing, and human review time into an operational cost metric \(C_{\text{eff}}\). Quick check: Model A costs $0.05/image with P@1=80%, Model B costs $0.01/image with P@1=30%. Roughly, which has lower effective cost per first success ignoring review and caps? (Answer: A: $0.05/0.8 = $0.0625; B: $0.01/0.3 ≈ $0.033. B appears cheaper initially, but incorporating review and retries may invert rankings).

**Concept: Reference-Based vs. Reference-Free Editing Evaluation**  
Why needed here: HYPE-EDIT-1 uses reference images and precise instructions (“Keep everything else the same”), requiring judges to verify both edit execution and content preservation. This differs from open-ended text-to-image generation benchmarks. Quick check: Why is human majority vote the gold standard, and what is the VLM judge’s role? (Answer: Human majority defines the binary ground truth; the VLM judge (Gemini 3 Flash) provides scalable, low-cost scoring with ~80% agreement but is more critical, serving as a secondary consistency check).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Task Loader -> Model Inference Client -> Judging Subsystem -> Scoring Engine -> Results Aggregator  
(Data Store underlies all components; Human Judge UI and VLM Judge are parallel paths within Judging Subsystem)

**Critical path:**  
1. Load tasks from public/private JSON files.  
2. For each task, generate K=10 independent outputs via Model Inference Client.  
3. Judge each output via Human majority (5 raters) or VLM check.  
4. Compute per-task \(p_t\) and aggregate metrics (P@1, Pass@10, Pass@4, \(E_t\), \(C_{\text{eff}}\)).  
5. Combine across tasks for final leaderboard results.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Scale vs. depth: 100 tasks is small vs. I2EBench’s 2000, but enables expensive human judging and repeated trials focused on reliability diagnostics.  
- Judge scalability: Human judging is gold-standard but slow; VLM judge is fast but less aligned. The design uses human majority as truth and VLM as a consistency check, accepting ~20% disagreement.  
- Cost model simplicity: Fixed \(C_{\text{review}}\) and retry cap \(A=4\) are practical but may not reflect variable real-world workflows or best-of-N selection strategies.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High Hype Gap (Pass@10 − P@1): Indicates high output variance; best-of-10 much better than typical output.  
- Low Pass@4 (<60%) with high Expected Attempts (>2): Retries become costly, inflating \(C_{\text{eff}}\).  
- High VLM/Human disagreement (>25%): Suggests ambiguous judging criteria or mis-specified VLM prompt.  
- Private vs. Public performance drop: Model is overfitted to public tasks, indicating contamination mitigation failure.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Sanity Check:** Run `seedream-4.0` on all 50 public tasks. Verify 10 outputs per task are saved. Confirm judging UI and VLM script produce aligned binary labels for a sample. Compare computed P@1 to published ~35%.  
2. **Cost Sensitivity:** For the same run, vary retry cap \(A\) (1,4,10) in scoring. Plot \(C_{\text{eff}}\) vs. \(A\). Validate diminishing returns and that p@4 lies between P@1 and Pass@10.  
3. **Judge Correlation:** Sample 100 outputs from full run. Obtain labels from two additional independent human raters. Compute inter-rater agreement (e.g., Cohen’s κ) among all pairs and against majority vote to test robustness of human ground truth.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How does using multiple VLM judges affect the consistency and bias of automated evaluation in HYPE-EDIT-1?  
*Basis:* Future Work calls for evaluating with multiple independent VLM judges to measure judge correlation and bias, as the current protocol uses a single VLM judge (Gemini 3 Flash) with ~80% human agreement.  
*Why unresolved:* One VLM judge may introduce systematic bias; varying judge criticality could alter pass/fail labels and skew reliability metrics.  
*Resolving evidence:* Re-evaluate a subset of tasks with diverse VLM judges, compute inter-rater reliability (e.g., Fleiss’ κ), and analyze how disagreement affects pass rates and effective cost.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent do variations in human review time, labor rates, and retry caps change the effective cost rankings of image editing models?  
*Basis:* Future Work explicitly requests reporting effective-cost sensitivity to review time, labor rate, and retry-cap budgets, as the current model fixes these ($50/hour, 20s review, \(A=4\)).  
*Why unresolved:* Real-world operational parameters vary widely; fixed assumptions may misrepresent cost-effectiveness and invalidate conclusions about model affordability.  
*Resolving evidence:* Conduct sensitivity analysis by varying each parameter across plausible ranges (e.g., review time 5–60 s, labor rate $30–$100/hr, retry cap 2–10) and map how \(C_{\text{eff}}\) and model rankings shift, identifying regimes where rankings invert.

**Open Question 3:** Does the independence assumption in HYPE-EDIT-1’s repeated-trial protocol accurately reflect user retry patterns, and what is the impact on estimated reliability metrics if attempts are correlated?  
*Basis:* Limitations state: “The repeated-trial protocol assumes that attempts are independent, which may not perfectly model real-world conditions,” implying potential bias in pass probability estimates.  
*Why unresolved:* User retries are likely correlated (e.g., prompt adjustments after failures); modeling attempts as independent Bernoulli trials could misestimate per-attempt pass rates, expected attempts, and effective cost.  
*Resolving evidence:* Collect empirical data from user studies or log analysis of sequential retry behaviors, fit models of correlated attempts (e.g., Markov processes), and simulate benchmark metrics under correlated vs. independent assumptions to quantify bias in Pass@k and cost.

## Limitations
- Human judging uses 5 raters per output without reported inter-rater agreement; ambiguous tasks may introduce systematic bias.
- The VLM judge aligns with humans at ~80% and is “more critical,” potentially skewing results if used for filtering or calibration.
- The i.i.d. assumption for repeated attempts is untested; correlated outputs would invalidate Pass@k as a reliability metric.
- Cost model fixes human review time (20 s) and retry cap \(A=4\); real workflows vary, limiting generalizability of \(C_{\text{eff}}\) figures.
- Task set is small (100) and domain-specific (marketing/design); findings may not extrapolate to other editing scenarios.

## Confidence
- **High:** Cheaper per-image models can incur higher effective cost due to retries (directly measured across 7 models).
- **Medium:** Validity of Pass@k and \(C_{\text{eff}}\) as general metrics (theoretically sound but sensitive to assumptions like i.i.d. attempts and fixed review cost).
- **Low:** Generalization to all frontier image editing models (limited model and task diversity).

## Next Checks
1. **Quantify human rater consistency:** Compute Cohen’s κ across the 5 raters on a common subset; if low, revise judging protocol or discard ambiguous tasks.
2. **Verify output independence:** For 10 tasks, generate 100 outputs each; test distribution against binomial variance and check autocorrelation between successive generations.
3. **Stress-test cost assumptions:** Re-calculate \(C_{\text{eff}}\) using review times (5–60 s) and retry caps (2–10) from practitioner surveys; assess whether model cost rankings remain stable.
