# Divine Benevolence is an $x^2$: GLUs scale asymptotically faster than MLPs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14495  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14495  
- **Reference count:** 17  
- **Primary result:** GLU architectures achieve a steeper asymptotic error decay ($L(P) \propto P^{-3}$) compared to MLPs ($L(P) \propto P^{-2}$) for function approximation tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper theoretically and empirically investigates the scaling behavior of Gated Linear Unit (GLU) architectures versus Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). By modeling MLPs as piecewise linear splines and GLUs as piecewise quadratic splines, it derives that the approximation error $L(P)$ scales as $P^{-2}$ for MLPs and $P^{-3}$ for GLUs under a constructive parameterization. This predicts an asymptotic advantage for GLUs. The authors validate these predictions on 1D function approximation, observing empirical scaling exponents of approximately -2.18 for MLPs and -3.12 for GLUs with respect to parameter count. Additional experiments on higher-dimensional and real-world datasets show consistent, though dimension-dependent, steeper scaling for GLUs. The work suggests that architectural inductive biases can fundamentally alter scaling laws, with implications for efficient model design.

## Method Summary
The study verifies asymptotic scaling slopes for shallow networks on 1D function approximation. The target function is $f(x) = (1 + \cos^2(\pi x))^{-1}$ over $[-1,1]$, with inputs uniformly sampled. Models (single hidden layer MLP and GLU) are trained using a custom Newton optimizer that performs full-batch, layer-wise updates with Jacobi preconditioning and line search. A specific spline-based initialization is used (alternating signs for gates and linear spacings for hidden units) to position models near spline-optimal configurations. Parameter counts are related to neuron count $n$ via $P_{\text{MLP}} = 3n+1$ and $P_{\text{GLU}} = 5n+1$ for 1D. The metric is RMSE on a test set, and scaling exponents are extracted via linear regression on log-log plots of error vs. parameter count across a range of $n$ up to 50+.

## Key Results
- Theoretical scaling: $L(P) \propto P^{-2}$ for MLPs, $L(P) \propto P^{-3}$ for GLUs under constructive parameterization.
- Empirical 1D slopes: approximately -2.18 (MLP) and -3.12 (GLU) vs. parameter count, confirming faster decay for GLUs.
- Gated Quadratic Unit (GQU) shows an even steeper empirical slope of -3.55.
- In higher dimensions and real regression datasets, GLUs consistently yield steeper scaling than MLPs, though absolute slopes decrease with input dimension.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MLPs with ReLU activations produce piecewise linear splines; their approximation error for smooth functions decays as $P^{-2}$ under optimal parameterization. GLUs introduce multiplicative gating, enabling piecewise quadratic splines with higher approximation power, yielding $P^{-3}$ error decay. The constructive parameterization explicitly sets network parameters to match spline knots and coefficients, realizing these rates given sufficient width and appropriate optimization. The faster scaling stems from the increased effective polynomial order of the piecewise approximation.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**: Input x → Hidden Layer (GLU: for each neuron, two linear branches (value and gate) → sigmoid → elementwise product) → Output Layer (linear aggregation). MLP: Input → Hidden Layer (ReLU) → Output Layer (linear).
- **Critical Path**: For a GLU with n neurons and 1D input: compute n value pre-activations (a_i), n gate pre-activations (g_i), apply sigmoid to gates, multiply elementwise, then linearly combine with output weights u_i plus bias d. Parameter count: 4n (hidden weights/biases) + n (output weights) + 1 (output bias) = 5n+1.
- **Design Tradeoffs**: GLU increases parameters per neuron (4 vs 2 for MLP) but gains quadratic approximation power. Gating introduces multiplicative interactions that can model complex functions but risks saturation/dead neurons. The spline construction requires careful initialization (alternating gate signs, linear spacings) to avoid overlapping knots. The Newton optimizer (full-batch, Jacobian-based) is expensive but needed to reach spline-optimal regimes; standard optimizers may not achieve theoretical exponents.
- **Failure Signatures**: 
  - Shallow scaling exponent (e.g., > -2 for MLP, > -3 for GLU) indicates failure to reach asymptotic regime or stalled optimization.
  - Dead neurons: many hidden units produce zero (ReLU) or constant (GLU with saturated gates) output, reducing effective width.
  - Errors significantly above analytical construction bounds suggest misconfigured initialization or optimizer.
  - Parameter count mismatches (e.g., biases counted differently) cause horizontal shifts but not slope changes; if slopes change, mapping may be incorrect.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do the asymptotic scaling exponents (MLP: $P^{-2}$, GLU: $P^{-3}$) derived for shallow networks hold for large, deep models like frontier LLMs?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Follow-up work should validate convergence rates on real-world datasets."
- **Why unresolved:** Experiments were on shallow models and small datasets; scaling in deep, massive models is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Measure loss vs parameters for deep models with MLP/GLU activations on large data, or re-analyze existing scaling law data to extract exponents.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why do some high-dimensional datasets (e.g., California Housing) show similar scaling exponents for MLP and GLU, contrary to the expected steeper GLU slope?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "not every problem matches the expected slope."
- **Why unresolved:** Theory predicts a reduction by factor 1/d, but empirical deviations (e.g., dim=8 yields both -0.09) suggest data smoothness, sample complexity, or curse of dimensionality effects.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments with synthetic d-dimensional target functions of known smoothness, measuring exponents across dimensions and analyzing how data distribution and noise affect slopes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does the Gated Quadratic Unit (GQU) achieve an empirical scaling slope of -3.5 instead of the theoretically expected -4?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "This is lower than the gut instinct hypothesis."
- **Why unresolved:** The GQU's cubic terms may not fully cancel truncation errors due to parameter coupling, optimization constraints, or insufficient width to realize the asymptotic regime.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive the truncation error for GQU under the spline construction to determine the achievable order; empirically push width and precision further, test alternative parameterizations, or design a construction that cancels more terms.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can standard deep learning optimizers (e.g., SGD, Adam) converge to spline-optimal parameter configurations that achieve the theoretical scaling rates, or do optimization dynamics impose a ceiling?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "The extra nonlinearities can also change optimization dynamics, preventing convergence to spline-optimal minima."
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical construction assumes a specific parameter setting; practical training might get stuck in suboptimal minima or suffer inductive biases that limit error decay.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare scaling exponents achieved with standard optimizers (Adam, SGD) vs specialized ones (Newton) on identical architectures and data; analyze optimization trajectories, loss landscapes, and initialization sensitivity; possibly design training techniques that encourage convergence to spline-optimal basins.

## Limitations
- Theoretical derivation relies on spline approximation assumptions; real-world data may not meet smoothness conditions.
- Empirical validation depends on unspecified optimizer details (Newton parameters) and exact initialization; deviations could affect achieved slopes.
- Scaling exponents in higher dimensions are observed to decrease with input dimension, but the exact relationship and its dependence on data smoothness are unclear.

## Confidence
- Theoretical scaling exponents (α=2 MLP, α=3 GLU): **High confidence** (derivation under spline model assumptions is sound).
- Empirical replication of slopes (-2.18, -3.12): **Medium confidence** (hinges on correct optimizer implementation and sufficient neuron count, n≥50).
- Generalization to higher dimensions/real datasets: **Low confidence** (paper notes absolute slopes decrease with dimension, but exact rate unspecified).
