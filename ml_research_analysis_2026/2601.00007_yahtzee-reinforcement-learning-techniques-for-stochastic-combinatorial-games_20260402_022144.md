# Yahtzee: Reinforcement Learning Techniques for Stochastic Combinatorial Games

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.00007
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00007
- **Reference count**: 11
- **Primary result**: An A2C agent trained on 1 million games achieved a median score of 241.78, within 5% of the dynamic programming optimal score of 254.59, but struggled with the upper section bonus strategy.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether deep reinforcement learning can approach optimal performance in solitaire Yahtzee, a stochastic combinatorial game. The authors formulate Yahtzee as a Markov Decision Process and train agents using REINFORCE, A2C, and PPO with a shared-trunk neural network. Under a fixed training budget, A2C emerges as the most robust algorithm, with its best agent reaching a median score of 241.78—within 5% of the dynamic programming optimum. However, all models exhibited persistent difficulty in learning the long-horizon strategy required for the upper section bonus, highlighting ongoing challenges in credit assignment and exploration for stochastic games with delayed rewards.

## Method Summary
The Yahtzee environment is implemented as a custom Gym simulator with states comprising the five dice, 13-category scorecard, roll index, and turn index. The state is encoded using a combination of one-hot dice representations and count-based features, along with normalized bonus progress, roll indicators, and lock-in flags. A shared neural network trunk (three 600-unit fully connected layers with Swish activations, LayerNorm, and dropout) feeds three heads: a rolling head for keep actions (categorical over 32 possibilities), a scoring head for category selection (categorical over 13 masked categories), and a value head. Agents are trained with on-policy algorithms (REINFORCE, A2C with TD(0), PPO with GAE) using parallel environment batches. The loss combines policy gradient, value error (λ_V=0.5), entropy regularization (with scheduled annealing), and optional reward shaping via a potential-based bonus predicting the upper section. Adam optimization employs a warmup-plateau-decay learning rate schedule. Performance is evaluated over 100,000 deterministic games, reporting median scores, bonus rates (upper sum ≥63), and Yahtzee rates.

## Key Results
- The best A2C agent achieved a median score of 241.78 over 100,000 evaluation games, compared to the DP-optimal score of 254.59 (a 4.9% gap).
- The A2C agent attained the upper section bonus in only 24.9% of games (vs. 68.1% for DP-optimal) and a Yahtzee in 34.1% (vs. 33.7% for DP-optimal).
- Under identical hyperparameters and training budget, A2C learned stably and outperformed REINFORCE and PPO, which were highly sensitive and failed to reach near-optimal scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: A2C with TD(0) targets yields more stable and efficient learning than Monte Carlo returns (REINFORCE) in this Yahtzee study.
- **Mechanism**: The critic provides a baseline that reduces variance of policy gradient estimates. TD(0) bootstraps from the value estimate, yielding lower-variance advantage estimates than full Monte Carlo returns and enabling online updates.
- **Core assumption**: The value function can be learned accurately from the data, and the environment’s Markov property holds so that bootstrapping is valid.
- **Evidence anchors**: The abstract notes REINFORCE and PPO are sensitive and fail to reach near-optimal performance, whereas A2C trains robustly. Section 2.1.2 explains TD(0) variance reduction; Section 4.6.3 defines the TD error; Figure 6 shows A2C achieves higher scores more smoothly than REINFORCE.
- **Break condition**: If the value function is poorly initialized or the environment has high non-stationarity, TD(0) bias may hinder learning. For extremely long horizons, credit assignment may remain insufficient.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Combining one-hot dice encodings with count features (n(d)) improves state representation by capturing both individual die faces and global frequency statistics, which are critical for evaluating Yahtzee hands.
- **Mechanism**: One-hot encoding (after sorting dice) retains per-die information, while count features provide a permutation-invariant summary of dice frequencies. This explicit combination allows the network to more easily assess categories like full house, straights, and the upper bonus, which depend on counts rather than specific positions.
- **Core assumption**: That the optimal policy relies on both types of information and that the network cannot efficiently learn counts from one-hot alone within the given architecture and training budget.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 4.2.1 defines the feature sets; Section 5.2.2 reports that using both improved reliability; Figure 8 shows the combined representation leads to a higher upper bonus rate compared to ablations.
- **Break condition**: If dice are always sorted, one-hot may be redundant with counts; or with very high network capacity, explicit count features might become unnecessary.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Entropy regularization prevents premature policy collapse and maintains exploration, which is essential for discovering high-reward long-horizon strategies like the upper section bonus.
- **Mechanism**: An entropy bonus is added to the loss, encouraging the policy to maintain stochasticity. This delays convergence to deterministic suboptimal policies, allowing more diverse trajectory exploration and potentially finding rare strategies (e.g., securing the 35-point bonus).
- **Core assumption**: Without explicit regularization, policy gradient methods quickly converge to local optima with low entropy, especially in environments with sparse/delayed rewards.
- **Evidence anchors**: The methodology mentions entropy regularization; the discussion notes models struggled with the upper bonus, highlighting exploration challenges. Section 4.6 details the entropy bonus; Section 5.2.5 shows removing entropy causes early collapse and poor performance (Figure 16), while tuned entropy achieves higher scores.
- **Break condition**: If the entropy coefficient is too high, learning is prohibitively slow; if too low, collapse occurs. The mechanism also depends on proper scheduling (annealing).

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Environment -> State Encoder (ϕ(s)) -> Shared Trunk Network -> [Rolling Head, Scoring Head, Value Head] -> RL Algorithm (A2C/REINFORCE/PPO) -> Training Loop

### Critical path
1. Encode state s → feature vector x via ϕ(s).
2. Pass x through shared trunk to obtain hidden representation.
3. During rolls 0–1: use rolling head to sample keep actions; during roll 2: use scoring head to choose category.
4. Execute actions in environment, observe reward r and next state s′.
5. Store transition (s,a,r,s′) in buffer.
6. At epoch end: compute TD(0) or GAE advantages using stored rewards and value estimates.
7. Compute total loss: L = policy_loss + λ_V * value_loss + entropy_bonus (+ shaping_loss if used).
8. Backpropagate, clip gradients, update parameters.
9. Periodically evaluate on deterministic policy to monitor progress.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Shared trunk vs separate networks**: Sharing parameters reduces total size and promotes transfer between rolling and scoring tasks but risks interference if task gradients conflict. The paper found sharing effective.
- **Action representation**: Bernoulli (5 independent binaries) vs Categorical (32-way). Bernoulli is more expressive per die but cannot capture joint dependencies; categorical simplifies learning but limits granularity. Categorical outperformed.
- **Feature engineering**: Adding computed features (e.g., normalized upper sum, lock-in flags) reduces burden on the network to discover key statistics but may bias the policy if features are incomplete or misleading.
- **Entropy schedule**: Starting with high entropy encourages exploration, then annealing allows exploitation. Tuning the decay schedule is critical; too fast causes collapse, too slow slows convergence.

### Failure signatures
- **Policy collapse**: Entropy drops to near zero early, action distribution becomes deterministic, performance plateaus low (e.g., "None" entropy regime in Figure 16).
- **Poor value predictions**: Explained variance near 0 indicates the critic is not learning useful value estimates, leading to noisy advantages and unstable updates.
- **Gradient explosion**: High gradient norms and frequent clipping signal unstable updates; may result from excessive learning rate or insufficient normalization.
- **Strategy fixation**: Agent over-optimizes immediate rewards (e.g., four-of-a-kind) and fails to learn the upper bonus, shown by low bonus achievement rates despite decent Yahtzee rates.
- **Single-turn vs full-game divergence**: High single-turn scores but low full-game scores indicate myopic behavior without long-term planning.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Dice representation ablation**: Train agents with one-hot-only, count-only, and combined dice encodings; keep all else equal. Evaluate median full-game score and upper bonus rate over 100k games. Hypothesis: combined yields best performance.
2. **Algorithm comparison under fixed budget**: Train REINFORCE, A2C, and PPO with identical architectures/hyperparameters (except algorithm-specific ones) for 250k games. Compare learning stability (gradient norms, entropy curves) and final scores. Expect A2C to be most robust and highest.
3. **Entropy coefficient sweep**: Vary β_roll and β_score across low, baseline, high regimes (as in Table 1). Monitor policy entropy over time and final performance. Identify regime that prevents collapse while enabling convergence near 240 points.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter opacity: Exact configuration (especially reward shaping weights, entropy schedule) for the final 1M-game A2C model is unspecified; reported results likely used settings not detailed in the main tables.
- Training protocol ambiguity: Precise definition of "epoch," number of gradient updates per batch, and whether A2C used multiple inner epochs (like PPO) are unclear, hindering exact replication.
- Evaluation variance: Final metrics (mean/median score, bonus rates) are from a single evaluation seed due to compute limits; true performance distribution and sensitivity to random seeds are unknown.

## Confidence
- **Core result (A2C near DP-optimal)**: High – Direct empirical comparison with DP baseline on 100k evaluation games shows <5% gap.
- **Algorithm robustness (A2C vs. REINFORCE/PPO)**: Medium – Supported by training curves and stability metrics, but exact sensitivity to hyperparameters is not exhaustively mapped.
- **Representation benefit (combined dice encoding)**: Medium – Ablation in Figure 8 shows improvement, but statistical significance and interaction with other factors (e.g., network size) are not quantified.
- **Exploration/credit assignment challenges**: Low – Inferred from bonus/Yahtzee rate discrepancies and entropy ablation; no direct diagnostic (e.g., trajectory analysis) confirms mechanism.
