# Semantic Invariance in Agentic AI
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.13173  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13173  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Smaller models (e.g., Qwen3-30B-A3B) exhibit greater semantic invariance than larger counterparts under metamorphic testing, contradicting scale‑robustness assumptions; contrastive transformations universally degrade performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the reliability of LLM‑based reasoning agents when faced with semantically equivalent input variations—a property termed *semantic invariance*. Standard benchmarks fail to measure this, so the authors introduce a metamorphic testing framework that applies eight semantic‑preserving transformations to 19 multi‑step scientific problems. Evaluating seven foundation models reveals a *scale‑robustness inversion*: the smaller Qwen3‑30B‑A3B achieves the highest stability (79.6% invariant responses, semantic similarity 0.91), while larger models are more fragile. All models are vulnerable to contrastive transformations, and distinct model‑family sensitivity patterns emerge based on architecture and training.

## Method Summary
The study constructs a corpus of 19 multi‑step scientific problems across eight domains. Eight semantic‑preserving transformations (identity, paraphrase, fact reordering, expansion, contraction, academic context, business context, contrastive) are applied to each problem. Models are prompted to solve each transformed version step‑by‑step using fixed inference parameters (temperature 0.7, top‑p 0.95, max tokens 1024). Outputs are compared to reference solutions via semantic similarity (Sentence‑BERT `all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2`). Primary metrics are Mean Absolute Delta (MAD) of solution quality scores and Stability Rate (proportion of transformations with |Δ| < 0.05). Statistical validation uses Mann‑Whitney U and Kruskal‑Wallis tests.

## Key Results
- **Scale‑robustness inversion**: Parameter count does not predict robustness; Qwen3‑30B‑A3B (3B active) outperforms larger siblings and competitors.
- **Family‑specific signatures**: Hermes models show extreme contrastive fragility; DeepSeek‑R1 is sensitive to structural changes; gpt‑oss suffers from reordering and contrastive instability.
- **Universal contrastive vulnerability**: All seven models degrade under contrastive framing, indicating poor filtering of misleading but semantically plausible context.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Scale‑Robustness Inversion
Larger models may overfit to spurious surface patterns or dataset artifacts, making them sensitive to superficial reformulations. Smaller models might generalize more conservatively, with representations less entangled with specific phrasing. Evidence: Qwen3‑30B‑A3B’s superior stability (79.6% invariant) versus larger models’ fragility.

### Mechanism 2: Family Vulnerability Signatures
Architectural choices (dense vs. MoE, reasoning‑optimized training) shape how models integrate information, creating characteristic failure patterns. For example, Qwen3’s MoE design supports balanced robustness, while DeepSeek‑R1’s reasoning‑focused training leads to structural sensitivity.

### Mechanism 3: Contrastive Vulnerability
Transformers process all tokens unconditionally; explicit contrasts with alternative scenarios act as distractors, causing attention drift or reasoning path corruption even when the semantic core is preserved. This reveals a lack of robust context‑gating mechanisms.

## Foundational Learning
- **Metamorphic Testing**: Enables evaluation without ground‑truth outputs for every transformation by checking relations among outputs. *Why needed*: Essential for measuring semantic invariance where traditional oracles fail. *Quick check*: How does a metamorphic relation differ from an oracle, and why is this critical for LLM agent reliability?
- **Semantic Invariance**: The expectation that semantically equivalent inputs yield equivalent reasoning outputs. *Why needed*: Defines a prerequisite for trustworthy agent deployment. *Quick check*: Can a model score high on a benchmark yet fail semantic invariance? Illustrate using the paper’s transformations.
- **Reasoning Trace Analysis**: Examines intermediate steps, not just final answers, to detect fragility in the reasoning process itself. *Why needed*: Reveals whether robustness issues originate in final answers or deeper reasoning coherence. *Quick check*: What does high trace similarity under transformations indicate about model robustness, and how might it differ from high solution‑level scores?

## Architecture Onboarding
Transformation Engine -> Model Inference Layer -> Evaluation Module -> Statistical Analyzer

Critical path: Problem corpus -> transformation generation -> model inference (multiple transformations × problems) -> embedding calculation -> metric aggregation -> statistical validation -> signature identification.

Design tradeoffs: Single‑inference vs. multiple sampling (realism vs. variance capture); LLM‑assisted vs. rule‑based transformations (flexibility vs. control); semantic embedding choice (speed vs. domain specificity).

Failure signatures: High MAD and low stability rate indicate global fragility; specific metamorphic relations with large negative Δ pinpoint family weaknesses; wide variance in score deltas for a single metamorphic relation suggests inconsistent internal processing.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Baseline Invariance Check**: Run identity transformation (τ_id) across all models; stability rates should be near 100% (sampling noise floor). Any model below 95% suggests experimental artifact.
2. **Family Signature Isolation**: Compute mean Δ per model family per metamorphic relation; produce a heatmap (like Figure 4) to confirm patterns (e.g., Hermes contrastive fragility, Qwen3 uniformity).
3. **Scale‑Robustness Inversion Validation**: Within Qwen3, gpt‑oss, and Hermes families, perform pairwise Mann‑Whitney U tests on MAD distributions; expect significant p‑values showing the smaller member is more robust.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Fine‑ing for invariance**: How to develop robustness‑aware fine‑tuning objectives? No such fine‑tuning was evaluated.
- **Ensemble design**: Can ensembles combine models with complementary vulnerability profiles to improve overall robustness? Ensembles were not tested.
- **Multi‑agent extension**: How do metamorphic transformations affect multi‑agent collaborative reasoning? Only single agents were studied.
- **Generalization**: Do the robustness patterns hold for a larger, more diverse problem set and with repeated sampling? Only 19 problems and one sample per transformation were used.

## Limitations
- Exact 19‑problem corpus and reference solutions are not provided; source [45] may be inaccessible.
- Precise prompts for LLM‑assisted transformations and step‑alignment logic are underspecified.
- Model access details (identifiers, platforms) are ambiguous for some families.
- Single‑run inference ignores stochastic variance; transformation implementation drift is a risk.

## Confidence
- **Scale‑robustness inversion**: Medium (strong within‑study evidence but limited to evaluated families; may not generalize to newer architectures).
- **Family vulnerability signatures**: Medium (consistent patterns observed, but within‑family variability and training differences not fully isolated).
- **Universal contrastive vulnerability**: High (robust across all 7 models; less likely to be artifact).

## Next Checks
1. **Independent replication** with a sourced/crowdsourced problem set and documented transformation prompts to verify MAD and stability rankings.
2. **Cross‑family scale test** on contemporary models (e.g., GPT‑4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 405B) to confirm whether the inversion persists beyond the studied families.
3. **Semantic drift audit**: Human‑validate 10% of transformed problems and compute embedding‑based drift metrics (e.g., BERTScore) to correlate transformation noise with robustness drops.
