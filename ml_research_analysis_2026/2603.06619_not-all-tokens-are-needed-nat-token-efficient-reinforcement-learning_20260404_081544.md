# Not all tokens are needed(NAT): token efficient reinforcement learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06619
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06619
- **Reference Count:** 25
- **Primary Result:** NAT matches full-token GRPO performance using as few as 50% of tokens; RPC reduces peak GPU memory by 18% and forward/backward training time by 29% for Qwen3-8B.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs) often requires processing long chain-of-thought (CoT) trajectories, incurring high computational costs due to backpropagation through every token. This paper introduces Not All Tokens Are Needed (NAT), a framework that updates the policy using only a selected subset of tokens while preserving the full-sequence learning signal. NAT employs Horvitz-Thompson reweighting to obtain an unbiased policy-gradient estimator under token subsampling, instantiated via two simple schemes: Uniform Random Sampling (URS) and Random Prefix Cutting (RPC). Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH) show that NAT achieves performance comparable to full-token GRPO with only half the tokens, and RPC yields substantial memory and time savings for Qwen3-8B.

## Method Summary
NAT modifies the standard policy gradient algorithm by subsampling tokens during the update phase. Given a trajectory, each token is included with a certain probability (e.g., 50%). The policy gradient estimator reweights the contribution of each included token by the inverse of its inclusion probability (Horvitz-Thompson estimator), ensuring the expected gradient equals that of the full sequence. Two subsampling strategies are proposed: URS picks tokens uniformly at random, while RPC randomly selects a prefix of the sequence and discards all suffix tokens. By processing only the selected subset during the forward/backward pass, NAT reduces the computational burden while maintaining unbiasedness.

## Key Results
- NAT (both URS and RPC) matches the performance of full-token GRPO on GSM8K and MATH while using only 50% of tokens.
- RPC reduces peak GPU memory consumption by 18% and forward/backward training time by 29% on Qwen3-8B.
- The Horvitz-Thompson reweighting yields an unbiased policy gradient estimator even with aggressive token subsampling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Horvitz-Thompson estimator corrects the bias introduced by subsampling. Each token’s gradient contribution is multiplied by the reciprocal of its probability of being selected. Because the expectation of this weight equals one for every token, the overall estimator remains unbiased. This allows the policy to learn from a random subset of tokens without altering the target gradient that would have been obtained from the full sequence.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Trajectory -> TokenSelector -> SelectedTokenForward -> ImportanceWeight -> GradientComputation -> PolicyUpdate  

**Critical Path**  
1. Sample a trajectory from the current policy.  
2. Compute per‑token advantages (or reward‑to‑go).  
3. Apply token selection (URS/RPC) to obtain a binary mask and inclusion probabilities.  
4. Run a forward pass *only* on the selected tokens, respecting causal context (e.g., for prefix cuts, stop after the selected prefix).  
5. Extract log‑probabilities for the selected positions.  
6. Multiply each log‑prob by its importance weight (1/p).  
7. Form the policy gradient as the weighted sum of log‑prob gradients times advantages.  
8. Backpropagate and update the policy via an optimizer.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *URS* vs *RPC*: URS allows fine‑grained dropping but may require careful handling of causality; RPC preserves a valid prefix, making it easy to truncate the forward pass and typically yielding lower variance.  
- *Subsampling rate*: Higher dropout increases memory/time savings but also raises gradient variance, potentially harming stability.  
- *Computational savings*: RPC’s prefix cut directly shortens the forward pass, while URS may still need to compute hidden states for unselected tokens if causality is to be maintained, limiting its efficiency.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Exploding loss or high variance in training metrics indicates over‑aggressive subsampling.  
- Performance collapse when the selection probability of critical tokens becomes too low.  
- Memory savings not realized if the implementation inadvertently retains full‑sequence activations (e.g., due to improper masking).

**First Experiments**  
1. On a single dataset (e.g., GSM8K), compare full‑token GRPO, NAT‑URS, and NAT‑RPC at 50% subsampling to verify performance parity and measure memory/time.  
2. Sweep the subsampling rate (e.g., 25%, 50%, 75%) to characterize the efficiency–performance tradeoff.  
3. Monitor gradient variance, KL divergence, and reward correlation across training for NAT vs. full‑token to assess stability.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is limited to mathematical reasoning benchmarks; applicability to other long‑horizon domains (coding, dialogue, agent tasks) is untested.  
- Empirical results are shown only for Qwen3‑8B; gains may not generalize to other architectures or scales.  
- Potential increase in gradient variance due to subsampling is not thoroughly analyzed; stability at high dropout rates remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- Core methodological claim (unbiased estimator via Horvitz‑Thompson): **High Confidence**  
- Empirical claim of matching full‑token GRPO on math benchmarks with ~50% tokens: **Medium Confidence** (supported by results but domain robustness unclear)  
- Broad claim of universal efficiency gains for RL on LLMs: **Low Confidence** (evaluation too narrow)

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑Domain Evaluation** – Test NAT on at least two additional long‑horizon RL tasks (e.g., code generation, interactive web agents) to verify performance retention.  
2. **Architectural Scalability Test** – Apply NAT to other model families (e.g., Llama‑3, MoE models) across scales (1B, 70B) to validate memory/time savings and identify scaling limits.  
3. **Gradient Stability Analysis** – Measure and compare the variance of policy gradients, KL divergence, and reward correlation between full‑token GRPO and NAT (especially RPC at 50%) throughout training to diagnose potential instability from subsampling.
