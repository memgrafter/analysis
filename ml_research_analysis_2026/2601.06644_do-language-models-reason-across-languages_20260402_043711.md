# Do Language Models Reason Across Languages?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06644
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06644
- **Reference Count:** 24
- **Primary Result:** Language models exhibit asymmetric sensitivity to cross‑lingual changes in multi‑hop QA (Hop‑2 F1 drop 13.96% vs. Hop‑1 2.27%) and suffer from unfaithful reasoning (up to 33% correct overall but wrong first sub‑question); SUBQ prompting mitigates compositional failure (accuracy from 10.1%→66.56%).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models can reason across languages in a multi‑hop question‑answering setting. The authors construct a benchmark by extending decomposed HotpotQA and translating it into five languages (English, French, Russian, Arabic, Chinese). Evaluating three instruction‑tuned models (Gemma‑3‑27B, Qwen‑3‑8B, Llama‑3.1‑8B), they find that performance degrades significantly more when the second‑hop (answer‑span) document is in a different language than the query compared to when the first‑hop (bridging) document changes language. Moreover, a substantial portion of correct final answers are produced without correctly identifying the bridge entity (unfaithfulness), and even when both sub‑questions are answered correctly, the final answer often remains wrong (compositional failure). To address this, the authors propose SUBQ prompting, a three‑stage decomposition that explicitly extracts and carries the bridge entity. This method dramatically improves accuracy on the compositional‑failure subset, highlighting the bottleneck in integrating intermediate results.

## Method Summary
The study builds on the decomposed HotpotQA dataset, initially comprising 1,000 examples. To force genuine multi‑hop reasoning, the authors filter out any instance that a model can answer correctly using only the first or only the second document (or neither), yielding a final set of 182 examples. All textual components (questions, documents, sub‑questions) are translated into four additional languages using GPT‑4o‑mini, with quality checks by native speakers. The models are evaluated under three prompting conditions: (1) a default prompt that asks for the final answer directly, (2) zero‑shot chain‑of‑thought (“Think step‑by‑step”), and (3) a novel three‑stage SUBQ prompting that sequentially asks for the bridge entity, then the answer given the bridge, and finally the combined answer. Performance is measured with token‑level F1 against gold answers. Additional analyses compute unfaithfulness (correct final answer but incorrect first sub‑question) and compositional failure (both sub‑questions correct but final wrong), and employ input‑erasure attribution to assess each document's contribution.

## Key Results
- Asymmetric cross‑lingual impact: When the Hop‑2 document language differs from the query, average F1 drops by 13.96%, versus a drop of only 2.27% for Hop‑1 language changes (consistent across models and query languages).
- Unfaithfulness: Up to 33% of correctly answered two‑hop questions have an incorrect first sub‑question, indicating the model often bypasses explicit bridge identification.
- Compositional failure: Up to 18% of cases show both sub‑questions answered correctly yet the final answer is wrong. SUBQ prompting raises accuracy on this subset from 10.1% to 66.56%, while zero‑shot CoT yields smaller gains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Asymmetric Language Sensitivity
- **Claim:** Language models exhibit greater performance degradation when the answer‑span (Hop‑2) document language differs from the query compared to when the bridging (Hop‑1) document language differs.
- **Mechanism:** The two reasoning steps likely rely on distinct sub‑processes; Hop‑1 (bridge entity extraction) can be solved via language‑agnostic pattern matching or internal knowledge, whereas Hop‑2 requires precise alignment of the query’s intent with a foreign‑language document, making it more fragile.
- **Core assumption:** Hop‑1 and Hop‑2 are disentangled; Hop‑2 is the bottleneck under cross‑lingual variation.
- **Evidence:** Across models and query languages, F1 drops for Hop‑2 language changes are consistently larger than for Hop‑1 changes (Table 1), averaging 13.96% vs. 2.27%.
- **Break condition:** If future work finds equal degradation for both hops across diverse models and datasets, the asymmetry claim is falsified.

### Unfaithful Reasoning
- **Claim:** Models often answer the overall two‑hop question correctly while failing the first sub‑question (bridge entity extraction), indicating they bypass explicit decomposition.
- **Mechanism:** The model may directly associate the query with answer patterns in Hop‑2 using latent knowledge or superficial cues, implicitly leveraging Hop‑1 without explicitly identifying the bridge.
- **Core assumption:** High confidence in a final answer does not guarantee faithful stepwise reasoning.
- **Evidence:** Up to 33% of correctly answered two‑hop instances have incorrect SubQ1 but correct SubQ2 (Table 3a). Context attribution shows Hop‑1 still contributes to answers in unfaithful cases (Figure 2).
- **Break condition:** If sub‑question evaluation consistently shows near‑zero unfaithfulness (correct two‑hop always implies correct SubQ1), the prevalence of non‑faithful reasoning is disproven.

### Compositional Failure & SUBQ Mitigation
- **Claim:** Compositional failure (both sub‑questions correct yet final answer wrong) occurs in up to 18% of cases; SUBQ prompting, which enforces explicit stepwise information passing, dramatically reduces this failure.
- **Mechanism:** Standard autoregressive decoding lacks a reliable mechanism to combine intermediate results. SUBQ prompting breaks the task into three discrete stages, explicitly injecting the bridge entity into the second stage, thus addressing the composition bottleneck.
- **Core assumption:** The composition operation is the weak link, not sub‑question competency.
- **Evidence:** SUBQ prompting boosts accuracy on the compositional‑failure subset from 10.1% to 66.56% (Figures 5, Table 3b). Zero‑shot CoT yields smaller improvements, underscoring the need for structured decomposition.
- **Break condition:** If an alternative prompting method (e.g., improved zero‑shot CoT) achieves similar gains without explicit stages, the necessity of the SUBQ structure is questioned.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multilingual Semantic Alignment**  
  *Why needed:* To understand why cross‑lingual variation impacts performance, one must recognize that LLMs embed concepts in a language‑agnostic latent space but the quality of alignment varies by language pair and document role.  
  *Quick check:* If embeddings for “president” in English and “président” in French are highly similar, why might a query in Chinese still struggle with a French Hop‑2 document?

- **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Prompting**  
  *Why needed:* The paper contrasts standard CoT with SUBQ prompting; understanding CoT’s limitations (unfaithfulness, partial help) explains why stricter decomposition is proposed.  
  *Quick check:* Why might asking a model to “think step‑by‑step” still result in unfaithful reasoning traces?

- **Compositional Generalization**  
  *Why needed:* Multi‑hop QA tests the ability to combine known facts from different hops; failures reveal core limitations in LLMs’ inferential architecture.  
  *Quick check:* If a model knows “A is B” and “B is C”, why might it still fail to infer “A is C” when the statements are in different languages?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
- **Input Encoder:** Multilingual transformer that processes the concatenated query and two documents.  
- **Implicit Reasoning Module:** Autoregressive decoder attending over all tokens to produce the answer (baseline).  
- **Decomposition Controller (for SUBQ):** Prompt parser that splits the task into three sequential calls to the decoder, managing state (bridge entity) between calls.  
- **Attribution Analyzer:** Token‑erasure utility to measure Hop‑1/Hop‑2 contribution (used in analysis, not inference).

### Critical Path
The primary bottleneck is the bridge entity extraction and its integration into Hop‑2 reasoning. For Hop‑2, the model must align the query’s intent with a foreign‑language document and anchor it to the correct entity from Hop‑1. SUBQ prompting explicitly isolates this step, ensuring reliable state transfer.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Explicit vs. Implicit Reasoning:** SUBQ uses explicit decomposition (higher latency, better composition) vs. implicit single‑pass generation (lower latency, prone to failure).  
- **Prompt Engineering vs. Fine‑Tuning:** The proposed solution is prompt‑based; fine‑tuning on decomposed data could yield more robust integration but requires labeled training data.  
- **Gold Documents vs. Retrieval:** The study uses provided gold documents; real‑world systems with retrieval add noise that could amplify observed failures.

### Failure Signatures
- **Asymmetric Language Drop:** >10% performance gap when Hop‑2 language differs vs. <3% for Hop‑1.  
- **High Unfaithfulness Ratio:** >25% of correct two‑hop answers have incorrect SubQ1.  
- **Compositional Failure Gap:** >15% of cases have correct sub‑answers but wrong final answer, and SUBQ prompting does not fully close this gap (still 33.44% error on that subset).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate Asymmetry:** Run baseline evaluation on the provided dataset (182 filtered examples) across Gemma‑3, Qwen‑3, Llama‑3.1 for all query/document language combinations; confirm the larger performance drop for Hop‑2 language changes.  
2. **Measure Unfaithfulness:** For all correctly answered two‑hop instances, separately score SubQ1 and SubQ2. Compute the unfaithfulness ratio (✓2hop, ✗SubQ1, ✓SubQ2). Perform context attribution on these unfaithful cases to quantify Hop‑1’s implicit contribution.  
3. **Test SUBQ Prompting:** On the subset with compositional failure (✓SubQ1, ✓SubQ2, ✗2hop), apply the three‑stage SUBQ prompt. Compare accuracy with zero‑shot CoT and baseline; verify that the gain approaches the performance ceiling set by the ground‑truth sub‑answers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What causes the larger performance drop when Hop‑2 vs. Hop‑1 language varies?  
- **Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly raise this discrepancy on page 2, questioning whether the first step is intrinsically easier or models bypass faithful reasoning.  
- **Why unresolved:** It remains unknown whether Hop‑2 reasoning is inherently harder or models rely on shortcuts that avoid explicit bridge extraction.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments that independently vary linguistic distance of each hop while keeping content constant, measuring step‑level accuracy and attention patterns.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do models answer correctly overall yet fail the first sub‑question (unfaithfulness)?  
- **Basis in paper:** Context attribution (Section 3.2.1) shows Hop‑1 contributes substantially despite missing explicit bridge inference, indicating a “nuanced intrinsic reasoning mechanism”.  
- **Why unresolved:** The precise internal process enabling latent integration without explicit sub‑question answering is not identified.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Probe model internals (e.g., attention heads, MLP activations) on unfaithful‑correct versus faithful‑correct cases to reveal alternative reasoning pathways.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the observed patterns generalize to low‑resource languages?  
- **Basis in paper:** Limitation (page 9): “our translated dataset focuses on four high‑resource languages and does not include low‑resource languages.”  
- **Why unresolved:** Language resource levels can affect model performance; the behavior might differ for languages with limited training data.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend the multilingual two‑hop benchmark to low‑resource languages (e.g., Swahili, Tamil) and replicate the analysis to compare asymmetry and unfaithfulness ratios.

## Limitations
- **Dataset specifics:** The exact 182‑example filtered set and its contamination‑avoidance criteria are unreleased, limiting exact replication.  
- **Prompt templates:** Full multilingual prompt templates (French, Russian, Arabic) are not provided; inferred versions may introduce subtle linguistic mismatches.  
- **Model configuration details:** Precise instruction‑tuning settings, tokenizers, and post‑processing steps are unspecified, potentially causing small deviations in results.

## Confidence
- **Asymmetric language sensitivity (Hop‑1 vs. Hop‑2):** Medium  
- **High unfaithfulness in stepwise reasoning:** High  
- **Compositional failure & SUBQ mitigation:** Medium  

## Next Checks
- **Replicate dataset construction:** Implement the filtering pipeline on the raw decomposed HotpotQA (1k examples) using the described accuracy thresholds; verify that the resulting example count and language distribution match the reported trends.
- **Prompt template reconstruction:** Translate the English prompt into French, Russian, and Arabic using professional translators; run a small human evaluation (n = 20 per language) to confirm semantic parity with the original.
- **SUBQ robustness test:** Apply SUBQ prompting to the compositional‑failure subset under two variations: (a) swapping the order of the two sub‑questions, and (b) using a different LLM (e.g., GPT‑4); measure any accuracy drop to assess dependency on exact prompt structure and model family.
