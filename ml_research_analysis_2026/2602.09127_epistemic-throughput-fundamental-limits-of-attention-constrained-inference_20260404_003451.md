# Epistemic Throughput: Fundamental Limits of Attention-Constrained Inference

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.09127
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09127
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The JaKoB scaling law shows epistemic throughput (information gain) is upper bounded by \( I_{\text{ver}} (Bp + c\sqrt{J K B}) \), revealing a square-root amplification from screening volume \(K\); sparse verification (\(B \ll K\)) yields polynomial leverage only with heavy-tailed screening scores, otherwise logarithmic.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the bottleneck in attention-constrained AI systems where many candidate records are generated cheaply but only a few can be verified due to limited attention. It formalizes Attention-Constrained Inference (ACI) as a two-stage process: a large volume \(K\) of records is screened using a noisy heuristic, and then up to \(B\) records are verified with high fidelity. The key performance metric is *epistemic throughput*—the expected reduction in Bayesian log-loss per window. The authors derive the JaKoB scaling law, proving that throughput cannot exceed \( I_{\text{ver}} (Bp + c\sqrt{J K B}) \), where \(I_{\text{ver}}\) is information per verification, \(p\) is prevalence of informative records, \(J\) is mutual information between screening and informativeness, and \(c\) is a universal constant. The square-root term captures a fundamental amplification from screening volume. In the sparse-verification limit (\(B \ll K\)), the benefit of larger \(K\) depends entirely on the tail of the screening score distribution: heavy-tailed scores (e.g., Pareto) yield polynomial leverage, while light-tailed scores (e.g., Gaussian) yield only logarithmic leverage. Furthermore, a simple score-based top-\(B\) policy achieves this scaling asymptotically in a weak-screening regime, proving the converse bound is tight. These results characterize fundamental limits for retrieval-augmented generation, tool use, and other systems where verification is scarce relative to candidate generation.

## Method Summary
The authors adopt an information-theoretic framework. They model records as i.i.d. draws with a latent informative type \(T\) and a target state \(\Theta\). A screening channel produces a noisy statistic \(Z\) for each record, from which a score \(\eta(Z) = P(T=1|Z)\) is derived. A selection rule chooses at most \(B\) records for verification, where a high-fidelity channel reveals \(\Theta\) for informative records. The throughput is the mutual information \(I(\Theta; \text{verified artifacts})\). They derive a universal upper bound via a reduction to a pointwise maximum likelihood problem and an enrichment argument (Lemma 4). Achievability is analyzed by considering the expected sum of top-\(B\) scores and using extreme value theory to characterize order statistics under a weak-screening local model (Assumption 8). The finite-\(K\) regime is explored via simulations using a logistic screening model and compared to the theoretical benchmark (Theorem 17) and the weak-screening bound. The analysis reveals the crucial role of the tail mean \(m_G(\alpha)\) of a standardized log-odds variable, leading to the dichotomy between heavy-tailed and light-tailed score distributions.

## Key Results
- **JaKoB upper bound**: Epistemic throughput \( \le I_{\text{ver}} \left( Bp + \frac{\sqrt{\ln 2}}{2} \sqrt{J K B} \right) \) (Theorem 6).
- **Sparse verification dichotomy**: When \(B \ll K\), the leverage from increasing \(K\) is governed by the tail of the screening score distribution; heavy tails (e.g., Pareto) give polynomial increase in throughput, light tails (e.g., Gaussian) give only logarithmic increase (Propositions 12, 13).
- **Achievability**: The top-\(B\) verification policy attains the JaKoB scaling (up to constants) in the weak-screening limit, establishing tightness of the bound (Corollary 11).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Square-root amplification via oversampling
- **Claim**: Screening a large volume \(K\) nonlinearly amplifies scarce verification capacity \(B\) via a \(\sqrt{JK/B}\) term, even with weak screening.
- **Mechanism**: Oversampling (high \(K/B\)) allows cherry-picking informative records. The enrichment bound (Lemma 4) limits the maximal increase in hit rate among verified records to \(p + c\sqrt{JK/B}\). Multiplying by per-verification gain \(I_{\text{ver}}\) and scaling by \(B\) yields the amplification term.
- **Break condition**: Amplification saturates quickly if screening scores have light tails; leverage becomes only logarithmic in \(K/B\).

### Mechanism 2: Tail leverage dichotomy in sparse regime
- **Claim**: In the limit \(B \ll K\), the throughput gain from larger \(K\) depends entirely on the upper tail behavior of the screening score distribution.
- **Mechanism**: Under the weak-screening local model (Assumption 8), the key quantity is the tail mean \(m_G(\alpha)\) of the standardized log-odds score \(G\) with \(\alpha = B/K\). Heavy-tailed distributions (e.g., Pareto) make \(m_G(\alpha)\) decay polynomially as \(\alpha \to 0\), while light-tailed (e.g., Gaussian) give \(m_G(\alpha) \sim \sqrt{2\ln(1/\alpha)}\). This directly affects the constant in the scaling law.
- **Break condition**: Heavy-tail polynomial leverage requires a sufficiently heavy tail (e.g., Pareto exponent \(\nu > 3\)). Light-tailed or sub-Gaussian scores yield at best logarithmic leverage.

### Mechanism 3: Tightness via top-B policy
- **Claim**: A simple score-based (top-\(B\) by \(\eta(Z)\)) verification policy achieves the JaKoB scaling asymptotically in the weak-screening regime, proving the converse bound is tight.
- **Mechanism**: Under the decoupled-claim specialization (Assumption 9), the problem reduces to selecting records to maximize the sum of their scores. The top-\(B\) rule maximizes the expected sum. In the weak-screening limit (\(\varepsilon \to 0\)), this expected sum matches the enrichment bound from Lemma 4 up to the factor \(m_G(\alpha)\).
- **Break condition**: Tightness is proved only for \(\varepsilon \to 0\) (weak screening). For stronger screening, the gap between achievability and converse may be larger, and the weak-screening approximation becomes conservative.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Mutual Information \(J = I(T;Z)\)**
  - *Why needed*: Quantifies screening quality; appears as the coefficient of the \(\sqrt{JK/B}\) amplification term in the throughput bound.
  - *Quick check*: If \(J\) doubles while \(p, B, K\) are fixed, the upper bound on throughput increases by a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\) in the amplification term.

- **Concept: Bayes Log-Loss and Conditional Entropy**
  - *Why needed*: Epistemic throughput is defined as the reduction in expected Bayes log-loss, which equals the conditional entropy \(H(\Theta|\text{artifacts})\). This connects the performance metric to information gain.
  - *Quick check*: The Bayes-optimal predictor's log-loss equals \(H(\Theta|I_j)\) for decoder \(j\); this implies that minimizing log-loss is equivalent to maximizing mutual information between \(\Theta\) and the decoder's information.

- **Concept: Extreme Value Theory for Order Statistics**
  - *Why needed*: The expected sum of the top-\(B\) screening scores determines the achievable throughput. In the large-\(K\) limit, this sum converges to an integral over the score quantile function, and the tail mean \(m_G(\alpha)\) emerges naturally.
  - *Quick check*: In the benchmark (Theorem 17), the minimum achievable risk \(\bar{D}^\star(K,B)\) is characterized by a single-letter functional of the score distribution \(\eta(Z)\).

- **Concept: Weak-Screening Local Approximation (Assumption 8)**
  - *Why needed*: Transforms the discrete score \(\eta(Z)\) into a continuous, locally linear function of a standardized log-odds variable \(G\). This asymptotic approximation enables derivation of the constant \(c_G(p,\alpha)\) and the tail mean \(m_G(\alpha)\).
  - *Quick check*: The parameter \(\varepsilon\) measures screening strength; \(J \approx p(1-p)\varepsilon^2/(2\ln 2)\) in the limit \(\varepsilon \to 0\).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Screening Module → computes noisy statistics \(Z_i\) for each of \(K\) records → Verification Selector → scores each record \(\eta_i = \eta(Z_i)\) and selects top \(B\) → Verification Channel → performs expensive verification on selected records, producing high-fidelity artifacts \((T_i, V_i)\) → Aggregator/Decoder → combines verified artifacts to infer \(\Theta\).
- **Critical path**: Inspect \(K\) → Compute \(Z_i\) → Score \(\eta_i\) → Select Top-\(B\) → Verify \(B\) → Publish Artifacts → Aggregate.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Screening quality vs. volume: Higher mutual information \(J\) reduces the needed \(B\) for a given gain, but achieving high \(J\) may limit feasible \(K\) due to computational constraints. The \(\sqrt{JK}\) term suggests increasing \(K\) yields better returns after a point.
  - Score distribution engineering: To maximize leverage, design screening to produce occasionally very high scores (heavy-tailed) rather than merely increasing the mean. This may involve ensembles, diverse retrieval, or extreme-preference heuristics.
  - Verification artifact design: Standardized, attestable formats increase reusability across decoders, effectively raising usable \(B\) per verification action.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Gaussian trap: Gain scales only as \(\sqrt{\log(K/B)}\) when scores are light-tailed; suspect sub-Gaussian tails.
  - Verification saturation: Gain plateaus at \(H(\Theta)\) well before the theoretical limit, indicating low \(J\), insufficient \(K\), or suboptimal policy.
  - Selection corruption: Adversarial or noisy scores cause top-\(B\) to pick uninformative records; monitor precision of the selected set.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Validate JaKoB Scaling**: Vary \(K\) and \(B\) under fixed screening model (e.g., logistic with known \(d\)); compare empirical gain to the upper bound (Theorem 6) and the weak-screening achievability.
  2. **Test Tail Leverage**: Construct two screening models with identical \(J\) but different tail behaviors (e.g., Gaussian vs. Pareto scores); plot gain vs. \(K/B\) on log-log scale to show polynomial vs. logarithmic growth.
  3. **Benchmark Score Policy vs. Oracle**: Compare top-\(B\) policy's gain to the theoretical benchmark (Theorem 17) and the finite-pool oracle; observe gap closing as screening weakens (\(\varepsilon \to 0\)).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How does epistemic throughput scale in sequential settings?** The current window-based i.i.d. model ignores feedback loops; extending to active learning or adaptive experimentation could yield new scaling laws.
2. **What are the limits under alternative loss functions?** Focusing on Bayes log-loss may obscure tradeoffs for other objectives like decision regret, FDR, or calibration; different losses could change the throughput-inspection tradeoff.
3. **How do heterogeneous verification costs and correlated evidence affect scaling?** Real systems have tiered verification costs and dependent outputs (e.g., tool chains); a richer model is needed to incorporate these factors.
4. **How can screening be engineered to produce heavy-tailed, robust score distributions?** While heavy tails enable polynomial leverage, practical designs that remain robust to adversarial manipulation are unknown.
5. **What is the optimal screening allocation when records have heterogeneous \(J\)?** The theory assumes homogeneous screening quality; in practice, screening efficacy may vary across records, calling for adaptive screening strategies.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on the weak-screening limit (\(\varepsilon \to 0\)) and the decoupled-claim model; these may not hold for strong screening or correlated records.
- The tail-leverage dichotomy is asymptotic (\(B \ll K\)); finite-\(K\) effects could blur the distinction between heavy-tailed and light-tailed behaviors.
- The universal constant \(c = \sqrt{\ln 2}/2\) emerges from specific proof steps; its generality across all screening models has not been tested.
- Verification is modeled as providing constant, independent information \(I_{\text{ver}}\) per informative record, ignoring potential variation in verification quality.
- Screening quality is captured solely by mutual information \(I(T;Z)\), possibly overlooking calibration, adversarial robustness, and other signal properties.

## Confidence
- [High] JaKoB scaling law upper bound (Theorem 6) – rigorous within ACI framework.
- [High] Tail leverage dichotomy (Propositions 12, 13) – mathematically established for specified distributions under large \(K/B\).
- [Medium] Tightness of top-\(B\) policy (Corollary 11) – proved only for \(\varepsilon \to 0\) and continuous scores; extension to practical settings remains conjectural.
