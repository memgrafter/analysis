# When Fine-Tuning Fails and when it Generalises: Role of Data Diversity and Mixed Training in LLM-based TTS

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10904
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10904
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** LoRA fine-tuning of the Qwen-0.5B backbone for voice cloning improves DNS-MOS by up to +0.42 and speaker similarity for all speakers when training data has high acoustic energy variability; on low-variability data perceptual quality degrades despite loss reduction.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates fine-tuning a frozen LLM backbone (Qwen-0.5B) in an LLM-based TTS system (NeuTTS) using LoRA for speaker-specific voice cloning. The authors find that fine-tuning outcomes hinge on the acoustic diversity of the training data, measured by energy standard deviation. For speakers with high variability (>13 dB), LoRA yields significant gains in perceptual quality (DNS-MOS) and speaker similarity, and multi-speaker mixed training enables zero-shot improvements on unseen speakers with minimal per-speaker data. Conversely, training on acoustically homogeneous data (<10 dB energy std) causes a loss-quality divergence where validation loss keeps improving but perceptual quality drops. Quantized LoRA (GGUF Q8) reduces inference latency 4-5× with negligible overhead. The work underscores that data diversity, not just parameter-efficient optimization, governs success.

## Method Summary
The authors employ LoRA on the Qwen-0.5B LLM backbone of NeuTTS, injecting low-rank adapters into the query, key, and value projection layers while keeping the backbone frozen. The system conditions on a reference audio embedding and predicts sequences of discrete acoustic tokens, which are decoded into waveforms by a neural codec. Training uses Adam (learning rate unspecified), batch size 4 with gradient accumulation to an effective batch of 8, for up to 5 epochs. LoRA rank `r=8`, alpha `α=16`. Evaluation metrics include DNS-MOS (OVRL), cosine speaker similarity, and WADA-SNR. Experiments compare per-speaker LoRA adapters on HiFi-TTS and LibriHeavy-HQ speakers, analyze energy standard deviation of training corpora, test mixed-speaker training with proportional data slicing, and assess quantization (GGUF Q8) latency gains.

## Key Results
- For speakers with training data energy std >13 dB, LoRA improves DNS-MOS by up to +0.42 and increases speaker similarity for all speakers.
- Training on low-variability data (energy std <10 dB) leads to perceptual degradation despite continued loss reduction, evidencing a loss-quality divergence.
- Multi-speaker LoRA training yields zero-shot MOS gains of +0.29 on unseen speakers and, with mixed training using only 11-22% of per-speaker data, matches dedicated model similarity within 5-9% of the full-data baseline.
- Quantized LoRA (GGUF Q8) reduces inference latency by 4-5× with negligible quality overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: LoRA Adapts Speaker Identity via Low-Rank Updates
**Claim:** LoRA fine-tuning on the LLM backbone enables adaptation to speaker-specific acoustic and perceptual characteristics without degrading linguistic modeling, as measured by consistent increases in voice similarity across all speakers.  
**Mechanism:** LoRA injects low-rank updates into the query, key, and value projection layers of the frozen Qwen-0.5B backbone. These updates act as structured perturbations within the pretrained acoustic token likelihood landscape, allowing the model to shift its output distribution toward speaker-specific acoustic manifolds present in the fine-tuning data. The frozen backbone's strong prior provides implicit regularization, preventing sustained amplification of artifacts.  
**Core assumption:** The pretrained LLM backbone encodes a generalizable acoustic representation space, and speaker identity can be captured by a low-rank subspace adjustment rather than wholesale overwriting of weights.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "LoRA fine-tuning consistently outperforms the non–fine-tuned base Qwen-0.5B model... speaker fidelity improves for all evaluated speakers, with consistent increases in voice similarity"  
- [section 3.2] "LoRA effectively adapts speaker identity representations without degrading linguistic modeling"  
- [section 4.4.8] "LoRA fine-tuning consistently improves speaker similarity across all evaluated speakers"  
- [corpus] Corpus analysis did not return directly related work on LLM-backbone LoRA for TTS, supporting the paper's claim of an unexplored area.  
**Break condition:** When training data is acoustically homogeneous (energy std <10 dB), LoRA faithfully clones narrow acoustic manifolds, amplifying noise and artifacts, leading to perceptual degradation despite identity capture.

### Mechanism 2: Data Diversity Governs Success via Robust Speaker Representations
**Claim:** Success of LLM-backbone LoRA adaptation is primarily governed by the acoustic diversity (proxied by energy standard deviation) of the speaker's training data, not merely the parameter-efficient optimization technique itself.  
**Mechanism:** High acoustic energy variability (>13 dB std) in training data ensures exposure to a broad range of recording conditions, speaking styles, and signal qualities. This diversity forces the LoRA updates to learn a more robust, generalizable speaker representation that disentangles identity from transient artifacts. Low variability data causes overfitting to a narrow manifold, where LoRA updates amplify the limited, potentially low-quality modes.  
**Core assumption:** Energy standard deviation is an effective, computationally cheap proxy for overall acoustic diversity in a speaker's corpus, reflecting variations in environment, microphone distance, and prosody.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "These improvements are strongly governed by the characteristics of the training data... Speakers with high variability... achieve simultaneous gains"  
- [section 4.4] Table 9 and analysis: "higher energy variability... strongly correlates with increased DNS-MOS variability and improved perceptual gains... Speakers with energy standard deviation exceeding approximately 13 dB achieve the largest perceptual improvements, whereas... below 10 dB experience pronounced degradation."  
- [section 4.4] "Acoustically homogeneous data causes the model to overfit narrow acoustic manifolds"  
**Break condition:** Energy standard deviation below ~10 dB in the training corpus predicts perceptual collapse (MOS drop) despite continued loss reduction.

### Mechanism 3: Validation Loss Is Unreliable for Perceptual Quality (Loss-Quality Divergence)
**Claim:** In LLM-based TTS, validation loss is an unreliable proxy for perceptual quality (DNS-MOS); a "loss-quality divergence" occurs where loss improves monotonically while perceptual quality degrades for low-variability speakers.  
**Mechanism:** The cross-entropy loss on acoustic tokens measures improvement in predicting the specific token sequences in the training data. For low-variability data, this corresponds to mastering a narrow, possibly low-quality distribution. Perceptual quality (human-like naturalness) requires generating from a broader, higher-quality distribution. The frozen LLM backbone's strong prior exerts a regularizing pull; early LoRA updates can overspecialize to training artifacts, harming MOS. Later training sees the backbone's prior reassert itself, allowing partial MOS recovery.  
**Core assumption:** The perceptual quality of generated speech (MOS) is not perfectly correlated with token-level likelihood on a narrow, potentially artifact-heavy training set.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "revealing a loss–quality divergence phenomenon... training and validation loss continue to improve during fine-tuning while perceptual quality degrades"  
- [section 4.2] "the loss curves... exhibit smooth and monotonic convergence... however, the corresponding DNS-MOS... show pronounced non-monotonic behavior... For speakers... with lower perceptual quality... rapid early loss reduction is accompanied by a marked drop in DNS-MOS."  
**Break condition:** Using validation loss as the sole criterion for checkpoint selection will select models that have overfitted to narrow, low-quality manifolds, resulting in degraded perceptual output.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) & LoRA**  
- *Why needed here:* The paper's core method is LoRA applied to an LLM backbone. Understanding that LoRA learns low-rank adaptations (ΔW = BA) to a frozen model is essential to grasp why it can adapt speaker identity with minimal parameters and why its updates are bounded perturbations.  
- *Quick check:* Why might a low-rank update to an LLM's attention layers be sufficient for capturing speaker identity, and how does this differ from full fine-tuning's risk?

**Concept: Acoustic Token-based TTS (e.g., NeuTTS)**  
- *Why needed here:* The system uses an LLM to predict sequences of discrete acoustic tokens (from a neural codec). This architecture collapses linguistic, prosodic, and acoustic modeling into one autoregressive backbone. This is why loss-quality divergence is possible—token prediction loss doesn't directly map to waveform naturalness.  
- *Quick check:* In a token-based TTS system, what does a decrease in cross-entropy loss on the acoustic token sequence actually measure, and why might it not correlate with human-perceived quality?

**Concept: Speaker Identity vs. Perceptual Quality in Voice Cloning**  
- *Why needed here:* The paper separates metrics: *Voice Similarity* (cosine similarity of speaker embeddings) measures identity cloning; *DNS-MOS* measures overall perceptual naturalness. The key finding is that LoRA improves similarity always, but MOS improves only with diverse data. These are distinct goals.  
- *Quick check:* Can a TTS system achieve high speaker similarity (it sounds like the target person) while having a low MOS (it sounds bad/noisy)? According to this paper, under what conditions does this happen?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Reference audio encoding → Text tokens → LLM backbone (Qwen-0.5B with LoRA adapters) → Acoustic token sequence → Neural codec decoder → Waveform

**Critical path:** Text → [Tokenizer] → Text tokens → **LLM (with LoRA)** → Acoustic token sequence → [Codec Decoder] → Waveform

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **LoRA Rank/Alpha vs. Capacity:** Higher rank increases adaptation capacity but also risk of overfitting to artifacts. Paper uses rank=8, alpha=16.  
- **Data Diversity vs. Quantity:** 1–2 hours of *diverse* data (high energy std) per speaker can outperform much larger *homogeneous* datasets. Mixed-speaker training with minimal per-speaker data (11–22%) can approach dedicated model performance.  
- **Inference Temperature:** Must be tuned based on training data variability. Conservative (low T) helps low-variability speakers suppress artifacts; high T is needed for high-variability speakers to maintain expressiveness.  
- **Quantization (GGUF Q8):** Accepts ~5× latency reduction with negligible quality loss for practical deployment. LoRA adapters add minimal overhead on top of quantized base.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Perceptual degradation despite loss decrease:** Monitor DNS-MOS, not just loss, especially early in training. If MOS drops while loss falls, you have "loss-quality divergence" likely due to low-variability data.  
- **Low speaker similarity:** Check if LoRA was applied correctly to attention layers. Also, insufficient or low-quality reference audio.  
- **Excessive noise/artifacts in output:** Likely caused by fine-tuning on data with low acoustic energy variability (std <10 dB) or low reference audio SNR. The model is amplifying the narrow, noisy manifold.  
- **Poor zero-shot generalization:** If a multi-speaker model fails on new voices, it may have been trained on insufficiently diverse data overall.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Controlled Variability Test:** Fine-tune identical LoRA setups on two matched-duration speaker datasets: one with high energy std (>13 dB) and one with low energy std (<10 dB). Measure DNS-MOS, similarity, SNR. Expect: high-variability speaker shows MOS gain; low-variability shows MOS drop.  
2. **Loss-MOS Decoupling Check:** During fine-tuning, plot training/validation loss and DNS-MOS (via periodic evaluation on a fixed validation set) on the same axes. Confirm non-monotonic MOS vs. monotonic loss for low-variability speakers.  
3. **Mixed-Speaker Data Efficiency:** Train three models: (a) dedicated 2-hour model for a speaker, (b) a model trained on all 6 speakers with 2/9th data per HiFi-TTS speaker and 1/9th per LibriHeavy speaker, (c) a model trained only on the other 3 HiFi-TTS speakers (2 hrs each). Evaluate all on all 6 speakers. Expect: (b) achieves similarity within 5-9% of (a) and provides +0.1-0.3 MOS gain on unseen LibriHeavy speakers vs. their dedicated models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the mechanistic cause of the "loss–quality divergence" observed in LLM-based TTS, where validation loss continues to improve while perceptual quality (DNS-MOS) degrades for low-variability speakers?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4.2 explicitly identifies and names the phenomenon: "monotonic convergence" of loss curves versus "pronounced non-monotonic behavior" in DNS-MOS, particularly for speakers with low acoustic energy variability (e.g., Speaker 1212 and 1401). The paper hypothesizes the frozen backbone's pretrained acoustic prior reasserts itself but does not provide a mechanistic analysis of *why* likelihood improvements diverge from perceptual metrics in this architecture.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper establishes the empirical pattern but does not decompose whether the divergence stems from (a) the autoregressive token-level prediction objective being insensitive to certain perceptual artifacts, (b) the LoRA updates amplifying narrow-manifold noise in ways not penalized by cross-entropy loss, or (c) a fundamental mismatch between token-likelihood and human perception in LLM-acoustic-token spaces. Understanding this is critical for developing reliable early-stopping criteria.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A targeted ablation study that (1) analyzes the distribution of generated acoustic token sequences (e.g., token entropy, rare-token frequency) alongside perceptual metrics, (2) measures the perceptual impact of deliberately injecting training-set artifacts (e.g., specific noise profiles) into low-variability fine-tuning, and (3) compares the divergence against a non-LLM TTS backbone (e.g., a diffusion or encoder-decoder model) trained on the same data to isolate the effect of the LLM's pretrained prior and autoregressive decoding.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the precise acoustic or information-theoretic threshold (beyond simple energy standard deviation) that defines "sufficient data diversity" for successful LoRA adaptation of an LLM TTS backbone?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4.4 and the conclusion strongly emphasize energy standard deviation as a predictor, noting a "monotonic trend" where energy std >~13 dB correlates with success and <10 dB with failure. However, the paper states this is a "proxy for acoustic diversity" and acknowledges energy mean is not predictive.
- **Why unresolved:** The 13 dB threshold is derived from a small, specific set of speakers in two datasets (HiFi-TTS, LibriHeavy-HQ). It is unclear if this threshold generalizes to other recording conditions (e.g., conversational vs. read speech, different microphone setups), languages, or acoustic features (e.g., spectral flatness, prosodic contour variance). The paper suggests "enforcing minimum variability thresholds," but a single scalar threshold may be insufficient.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A large-scale, multi-dataset correlation study measuring a suite of acoustic diversity metrics (energy std, spectral flux, pitch std, speaking rate variance, room impulse response diversity) against fine-tuning outcomes (ΔMOS, Δsimilarity). This would identify a minimal, robust set of features that define the "acoustic diversity manifold" required for adaptation. Additionally, controlled data augmentation experiments (e.g., artificially increasing the energy std of a homogeneous dataset via volume normalization or noise addition) could test causality.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does multi-speaker LoRA fine-tuning enable zero-shot perceptual quality improvements on speakers with inherently low-quality reference audio (e.g., LibriHeavy-HQ speakers), and what specific acoustic representations are being transferred?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4.6 shows that models trained *only* on HiFi-TTS speakers (2+2+2 FT, 1+1+1 FT) generalize to improve MOS on unseen LibriHeavy speakers (gain +0.11 to +0.29) despite never seeing them. The explanation given is that LibriHeavy speakers have "narrow acoustic distributions" (low energy std), so a model overfitted to that narrow manifold amplifies artifacts, while a multi-speaker model exposed to "broader acoustic diversity" generalizes better.
- **Why unresolved:** The claim that "broader acoustic diversity" in the training set is the key factor is plausible but not proven. It is unclear what in the shared LoRA weights is transferable: Is it (a) a general "artifact suppression" prior learned from diverse clean data, (b) a more robust representation of speaker-independent phonetic and prosodic content, or (c) a better calibration of the decoder's sampling distribution? The zero-shot benefit on *quality* (MOS) but not *similarity* (which drops for zero-shot speakers) suggests the adaptation learns general acoustic robustness rather than speaker identity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An analysis of the *type* of errors reduced in the zero-shot setting. For example, do the multi-speaker models generate less noisy, more normalized speech for low-quality references? A representational similarity analysis (RSA) or probing study on the hidden states of the LLM backbone when processing text from low-quality vs. high-quality speakers could reveal if multi-speaker training induces a representation that is less sensitive to the acoustic artifacts present in the narrow test distribution.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How exactly does decoding-time hyperparameter optimization (temperature, top-k) interact with the state of LoRA adaptation to partially mitigate perceptual degradation for low-variability speakers, and what does this reveal about the learned distribution?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4.2 and the conclusion note that constrained decoding (T=0.8, k=40) yields "substantial MOS improvements" for low-quality speakers (e.g., 1212, 1401) while slightly degrading high-quality ones. The paper states this "suppress[es] low-likelihood acoustic artifacts," implying the LoRA-adapted model for low-variability data has a distribution with high-probability regions containing artifacts.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze *what* in the token distribution is being suppressed. Does low temperature truncate the tail where noise tokens appear? Does top-k filtering remove specific rare acoustic tokens that correspond to overfit background noise or room resonance? Understanding this interaction is key to designing training objectives that make the model's distribution inherently more robust, reducing the need for post-hoc decoding intervention.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed analysis of the token probability distributions at each decoding step for low-variability vs. high-variability LoRA models. Specifically, comparing the entropy, the mass in the top-k vs. tail, and the acoustic characteristics (via the codec's decoder) of tokens pruned by top-k filtering for each model type. Correlating these statistics with perceptual degradation would clarify the decoding mitigation strategy.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** To what extent are the findings regarding data diversity and LoRA success specific to small LLM backbones (Qwen-0.5B) versus larger or differently pretrained language models?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The "Limitations" section explicitly states: "This work has explored fine tuning on Small language models like Qwen 0.5 billion, the insights may or may not apply to Large Language models or medium language models." The entire experimental foundation is the Qwen-0.5B backbone within the NeuTTS framework.
- **Why unresolved:** The core thesis—that data diversity governs adaptation success—is compelling but is demonstrated only on a single, relatively small model. Larger LLMs have vastly different pretrained distributions and representational capacities. A larger model might (a) require less data diversity to adapt successfully due to stronger priors, (b) be more susceptible to overfitting on narrow data due to higher capacity, or (c) exhibit a different loss-quality relationship. The paper's key practical guidance (e.g., energy std threshold) cannot be safely generalized without testing across model scales.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the core experiments (single-speaker LoRA on homogeneous vs. heterogeneous data, multi-speaker generalization) using LLM backbones of significantly different sizes (e.g., 1B, 7B, 70B parameters) and pretraining objectives (e.g., purely text-trained vs. speech-text multimodal). A systematic study comparing the loss-quality divergence pattern, the data diversity thresholds, and the effectiveness of multi-speaker training across these model families would determine the scope of the paper's conclusions.

## Limitations
- Exact dataset splits and filtering criteria (utterance continuity, SNR thresholds) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Full training hyperparameters (learning rate, scheduler, warmup steps) and evaluation reference audio details are omitted.
- Implementation specifics of the NeuTTS pipeline (codec, prompt formatting) require reverse‑engineering from descriptions.
- The energy standard deviation proxy for acoustic diversity is correlational, not causally established.

## Confidence
- **Loss-quality divergence claim:** High  
- **Data diversity threshold effect (energy std >13 dB vs <10 dB):** Medium  
- **LoRA’s sufficiency for speaker adaptation (low-rank subspace hypothesis):** Medium  
- **Quantitative mixed-training efficiency gains (5-9% similarity match):** Low  

## Next Checks
1. **Energy Std Threshold Generalization:** Train LoRA adapters on 10+ additional speakers spanning a broader energy std range (5–20 dB) from diverse corpora (e.g., VoxCeleb, LibriTTS). Re-evaluate the 13 dB/10 dB breakpoints for DNS-MOS and similarity to test robustness of the proxy.
2. **LoRA Rank/Ablation Study:** Systematically vary LoRA rank (r=2,4,8,16) and target modules (Q/K/V vs. all linear) on a high-variability and a low-variability speaker. Measure speaker similarity, DNS-MOS, and artifact prevalence to confirm that low-rank updates capture identity without amplifying narrow-manifold noise.
3. **Multi-Speaker Adapter Merging:** For mixed-trained models, test adapter merging techniques (e.g., TIES-Merging, DARE) on zero-shot speakers. Quantify similarity retention vs. dedicated single-speaker models to determine if the reported 5-9% gap can be closed without per-speaker data scaling.
