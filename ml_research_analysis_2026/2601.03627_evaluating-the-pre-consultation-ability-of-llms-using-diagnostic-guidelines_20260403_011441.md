# Evaluating the Pre-Consultation Ability of LLMs using Diagnostic Guidelines

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03627
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03627
- **Reference Count:** 33
- **Primary Result:** Fine-tuned small open-source models (e.g., Qwen2.5-32B) can match or outperform frontier models like GPT-4.1 in gathering clinically relevant History of Present Illness (HPI) and diagnosis, even exceeding a human expert baseline.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the EPAG benchmark to evaluate Large Language Models (LLMs) in the critical clinical task of pre-consultation: gathering a structured History of Present Illness (HPI) using disease-specific diagnostic guidelines. The framework simulates doctor-patient dialogues where the LLM acts as the doctor, generating constrained multiple-choice questions to elicit information. The collected HPI is assessed through two methods: a direct, guideline-weighted comparison and an indirect evaluation via a separate diagnostic model. Key findings demonstrate that targeted supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a relatively small, curated dataset (3k dialogues) enables a 32B parameter model to surpass both a larger frontier model (GPT-4.1) and a human expert baseline on these metrics. The study also reveals that more HPI does not guarantee better diagnosis and that the dialogue language (Korean vs. English) systematically influences the agent's information-gathering strategy and diagnostic outcomes.

## Method Summary
The core method is the EPAG (Eliciting Patient history for Assistive Guidance) benchmark. It simulates a multi-turn dialogue where an LLM doctor-agent generates `[Question, Options]` pairs for a deterministic patient-agent (with a known ground-truth disease profile). The dialogue history is processed by an "Organizer" into discrete "atomic" HPI units (e.g., "Patient reports chest pain"). These units are evaluated in two ways: 1) A "Comparer" matches each unit to a pre-defined, clinically weighted list of key symptoms/history for the true disease (the guideline), computing a raw and weighted score. 2) A fixed "Diagnostician" model (GPT-4o-mini) receives all organized HPI units and outputs a ranked disease list, which is compared to the ground-truth for accuracy (Top-1, Top-k). The primary variable of interest is the doctor-agent model, with experiments comparing base models, fine-tuned variants, and human performance.

## Key Results
*   Fine-tuned Qwen2.5-32B achieved a weighted HPI score of 8.12 and Top-1 diagnosis accuracy of 74.56%, exceeding the human expert baseline (7.29, 68.24%) and rivaling GPT-4.1.
*   Increasing the number of dialogue turns (and thus raw HPI units collected) did not improve, and often degraded, diagnostic accuracy.
*   The language of the dialogue (Korean vs. English) created a trade-off: Korean dialogues yielded higher HPI-guideline match scores but lower diagnostic accuracy, while English showed the inverse pattern.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Task-Specific SFT Drives Performance
- **Claim:** Targeted supervised fine-tuning on a curated, task-specific dialogue dataset enables smaller open-source models to match or exceed larger frontier models in clinical pre-consultation.
- **Mechanism:** The fine-tuning adapts the model's generative policy to the precise structure of the task—producing effective `[Question, Options]` pairs that efficiently elicit guideline-relevant HPI. This specialized training on the narrow conversational format is more impactful than general medical knowledge or sheer model scale for this specific benchmark.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Results show fine-tuned Qwen2.5-32B outperforming GPT-4.1 and human experts. The underperformance of Medgemma-4B (a medically fine-tuned model) suggests that *task-specific* adaptation, not just domain knowledge, is crucial.

### Mechanism 2: HPI Relevance Trumps Quantity
- **Claim:** Diagnostic accuracy is not positively correlated with the volume of collected HPI; irrelevant, redundant, or misleading information can harm performance.
- **Mechanism:** Diagnosis relies on a weighted subset of clinically significant features. Adding low-significance HPI units dilutes the signal from high-significance ones or may trigger spurious diagnostic heuristics (e.g., keyword fixation). The guideline weighting system operationalizes this principle.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Experiments show flat/declining diagnosis accuracy with more dialogue turns. A case study (Acute Kidney Injury vs. Renal Colic) demonstrates a single additional, plausible but less discriminative HPI unit altering the diagnosis.

### Mechanism 3: Language Shapes Conversational Strategy
- **Claim:** The dialogue language (Korean vs. English) systematically influences the doctor-agent's questioning strategy, creating a trade-off between breadth (coverage of symptoms) and depth (repetitive follow-ups).
- **Mechanism:** The model's language-specific training biases affect its probabilistic generation, leading to divergent exploration (Korean: wider net of distinct symptoms) vs. exploitation (English: deep follow-ups on few symptoms) strategies in the conversational search space.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Empirical results show an inverse relationship between language and metrics (Korean: higher HPI score, lower diagnosis accuracy; English: lower HPI score, higher diagnosis accuracy). Qualitative analysis describes the "wider net" vs. "deep, repetitive follow-ups" behavior.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Diagnostic Guideline Structuring**
  - **Why needed:** The entire HPI evaluation relies on pre-defined lists of key symptoms/history for each disease, weighted by clinical significance (high/medium/low). Understanding this structure is essential to interpret the weighted HPI score.
  - **Quick check:** If a high-significance guideline item is elicited, what is its expected impact on the final *weighted* HPI score compared to a low-significance item?
- **Concept: History of Present Illness (HPI) Atomic Unit Decomposition**
  - **Why needed:** The "Organizer" converts dialogue turns into discrete, patient-perspective statements (e.g., "Patient has headache"). This granular decomposition is critical for precise, unambiguous comparison against guideline items and for properly counting negations.
  - **Quick check:** If a patient answers "No" to a list of 4 symptom options in one turn, how many atomic HPI units should be generated, and what should their polarity (presence/absence) be?
- **Concept: Task-Specific Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for Conversational Agents**
  - **Why needed:** The central finding is that SFT on the specific pre-consultation task format (`[Question, Options]` generation) is key. Understanding how SFT modifies a base model's behavior for a narrow, structured task is prerequisite.
  - **Quick check:** Why might a model fine-tuned on a general medical corpus (e.g., Medgemma) underperform on this benchmark compared to a base model fine-tuned on a smaller, task-specific dataset?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Patient-Agent (deterministic, holds ground truth) <-> Doctor-Agent (LLM under evaluation) -> Organizer -> (HPI units) -> Comparer -> (HPI score) AND -> Diagnostician -> (Diagnosis) -> Evaluator.
- **Critical path:** 1) Patient & Doctor agents interact for N turns → 2) Organizer converts full dialogue into atomic HPI units → 3) Comparer matches each unit to guidelines for the HPI score → 4) Diagnostician consumes all HPI units to predict disease → 5) Evaluator compares prediction to ground truth for accuracy.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Constrained Patient Responses:** Using a deterministic Patient-Agent with predefined options ensures clean, structured data for the Organizer but sacrifices the open-ended, potentially ambiguous responses of real patients.
  - **Separate Diagnostician:** Using a fixed, external diagnostician (GPT-4o-mini) isolates the *pre-consultation quality* (HPI gathering) from the diagnostic model's own capability. Tradeoff: introduces dependency on another model's prompt design and performance.
  - **Guideline Weighting:** Assigning significance weights (high/medium/low) adds clinical nuance but introduces subjectivity from the clinicians curating the guidelines and may not perfectly reflect real-world diagnostic weighting.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Organizer:** Fails to create separate atomic units for negated symptoms; includes meta-comments or doctor-perspective statements; duplicates information.
  - **Comparer:** False negatives (failing to match a relevant HPI unit to its guideline item); false positives (matching irrelevant HPI); ignores significance weights.
  - **Doctor-Agent:** Generates repetitive questions; produces options that do not align with guideline features; fixates on an early diagnostic hypothesis (see Appendix E case); fails to cover key high-significance guideline items.
  - **Systemic:** Language-specific strategy divergence makes cross-lingual performance comparisons indirect.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Fine-tuning Impact Replication:** Fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B/32B/72B on the provided 3k dataset. Measure changes in all four metrics (unweighted/weighted HPI score, Top-1/Top-k diagnosis). Expect peak performance at 32B parameters.
  2. **HPI Quantity vs. Quality:** For a fixed doctor-agent, run dialogues with varying turn counts (1-9). Plot (a) total atomic HPI units and (b) Top-1 diagnosis accuracy. Expect a non-monotonic or flat accuracy curve. Analyze per-turn contributions: does each new unit match a "high" significance guideline?
  3. **Language Strategy Probe:** Using the same doctor-agent (e.g., Qwen2.5-32B), generate 50 Korean and 50 English dialogues (5 turns). Measure (a) average unique guideline-matching HPI units, (b) average number of *distinct* symptom types queried, (c) diagnostic accuracy. Expect Korean to have higher (b) but lower (c), English to have higher (c) but lower (b).

## Limitations
- The simulated environment with a deterministic Patient-Agent and constrained options does not capture the complexity and ambiguity of real doctor-patient conversations.
- Guideline weighting and content are subject to clinician curation bias and may not generalize across all medical contexts or cultural settings.
- The training dataset is small (3k dialogues) and focused on specific diseases, limiting conclusions about generalizability to other medical domains.
- The performance evaluation depends on a fixed, external Diagnostician model (GPT-4o-mini), introducing a confounding variable in the accuracy measurements.
- The language comparison is limited to only Korean and English, preventing broader conclusions about linguistic or cultural influences.

## Confidence
- **Task-specific SFT superiority over larger frontier models:** High Confidence. Robust empirical results across multiple metrics support this claim.
- **No positive correlation between HPI volume and diagnostic accuracy:** High Confidence. Clear evidence from controlled experiments and illustrative case studies.
- **Language influences HPI/diagnosis trade-off:** Medium Confidence. Observed pattern is strong in two languages but not causally isolated; qualitative inference may not hold for other language pairs.
- **Exceeding human expert baseline:** Medium Confidence. Baseline is from a single clinician on a limited set; broader human validation is needed.

## Next Checks
1. **Real-world validation:** Deploy the doctor-agent in actual telemedicine or online consultation settings, comparing elicited HPI against clinician-validated guidelines and final diagnoses from human experts.
2. **Dataset scaling and disease diversity analysis:** Systematically vary the fine-tuning dataset size (1k to 10k+ dialogues) and the number/variety of included diseases to determine the boundaries of the SFT advantage and its generalizability.
3. **Cross-lingual replication with cultural adaptation:** Replicate the language comparison experiment in 3+ additional languages (e.g., Spanish, Mandarin, French) with native-speaking clinician involvement for guideline creation and validation to isolate language/culture effects from task structure.
