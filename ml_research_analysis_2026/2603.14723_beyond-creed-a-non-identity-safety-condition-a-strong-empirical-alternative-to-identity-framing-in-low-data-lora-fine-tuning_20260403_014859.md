# Beyond Creed: A Non-Identity Safety Condition A Strong Empirical Alternative to Identity Framing in Low-Data LoRA Fine-Tuning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14723
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14723
- **Reference count:** 9
- **Primary result:** A non-identity safety framing (Condition D) achieves the highest refusal rates on HarmBench, outperforming creed-style identity framing (Condition B) by an average of 12.4 percentage points across three model families without capability loss.

## Executive Summary
This study empirically tests whether explicit identity framing (e.g., creed-style prompts) is necessary for effective safety fine-tuning under low-data LoRA constraints. Comparing four supervision formats that share identical core safety rules but differ in rhetorical framing—constitutional rules (A), creed-style identity (B), creed with identity-maintenance tail (C), and a matched non-identity condition (D)—the research finds that Condition D consistently yields the strongest safety gains on HarmBench across three independent model families (Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, Gemma 3 4B). The non-identity formulation also preserves general capabilities measured by MMLU and ARC-Challenge. These results challenge a strong identity-framing hypothesis, demonstrating that supervision wording and style are critical, independent levers in low-resource safety alignment.

## Method Summary
The experiment fine-tuned three mid-sized models using LoRA with a strict low-data regime (16 examples per safety rule). Four conditions varied only in the rhetorical framing of identical safety rules: (A) constitutional-style, (B) creed-style identity, (C) creed with identity-maintenance tail, and (D) a matched non-identity formulation. Safety performance was evaluated on HarmBench via a reconciled dual-judge pipeline, while general capability was assessed on MMLU and ARC-Challenge. All models, data, and evaluation protocols were held constant except for the supervision format.

## Key Results
- Condition D (non-identity) achieves the highest refusal rates: Llama 74.4%, Gemma 76.9%, Qwen 74.1%.
- D’s mean improvement over baseline (+19.1 pp) far exceeds creed (B)’s (+6.7 pp), with statistically significant margins (p<0.05) on all models.
- Capability benchmarks show no meaningful degradation, with maximum spread of 0.94 pp across conditions, indicating preserved general performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The superior performance of the non-identity condition suggests that direct, rule-based supervision provides a clearer and less noisy learning signal for safety alignment in low-data regimes. Identity framing may introduce anthropomorphic assumptions or overfitting that impede the model’s ability to generalize safety boundaries, whereas explicit rules facilitate more robust refusal behavior without sacrificing capability.

## Foundational Learning
1. **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)**: Why needed: Enables parameter-efficient fine-tuning by updating only low-rank matrices, making it feasible to adapt large models with minimal data. Quick check: Adapter rank (e.g., r=8) is orders of magnitude smaller than model dimension.
2. **Identity Framing in AI Safety**: Why needed: Uses anthropomorphic prompts (e.g., “you are a helpful assistant”) to instill persistent behavioral traits. Quick check: Creed-style conditions embed model identity within safety rules.
3. **Low-Data Fine-Tuning Regime**: Why needed: Simulates real-world scenarios where labeled safety data is scarce. Quick check: This study uses only 16 examples per rule.
4. **Safety Benchmarking (HarmBench)**: Why needed: Provides a standardized suite of adversarial prompts to evaluate model refusal behaviors across harm categories. Quick check: Employs automatic judging to scale evaluation.
5. **Dual-Judge Pipeline**: Why needed: Improves reliability of automated safety evaluation by reconciling multiple judge outputs. Quick check: Two independent judges with a tie-breaking mechanism.
6. **Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)**: Why needed: Reduces computational and storage costs compared to full fine-tuning. Quick check: LoRA freezes pretrained weights and trains adapters only.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Pretrained Model -> LoRA Adapter (trained on condition-specific prompts) -> Response Generator -> Dual-Judge Pipeline -> Safety Score (refusal rate)

Critical path: Rule formulation (varying rhetorical framing) -> Adapter training (16 examples/rule, LoRA) -> HarmBench evaluation (adversarial prompts) -> Dual-judge scoring -> Statistical comparison of refusal rates.

Design tradeoffs: Rhetorical framing (identity vs. non-identity) vs. data efficiency; automated judging scalability vs. nuanced harm detection; low-data adaptation speed vs. generalization robustness.

Failure signatures: Condition D shows lower than expected refusal rates; high disagreement between dual judges; capability drops on MMLU/ARC indicating interference; inconsistent performance across model families.

First experiments:
1. Replicate the full four-condition comparison on Llama 3.1 8B to verify the non-identity advantage.
2. Compare dual-judge pipeline consistency against a single-judge baseline on a stratified sample of responses.
3. Test with 8 vs. 16 examples per rule to assess data sensitivity and potential for further reduction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does Condition D's performance generalize to other safety benchmarks (e.g., TruthfulQA, ToxicChat, custom red-team datasets)? The study only evaluated on HarmBench.
- Does the advantage of non-identity framing hold for larger models (e.g., >20B parameters) or different architectures such as Mixture-of-Experts? The current results are limited to three mid-sized models.
- What is the sensitivity of the non-identity effect to the amount of training data? The study used 16 examples per rule; would fewer examples still yield similar gains, or would the gap narrow with more data?
- Could combining non-identity supervision with other alignment methods (e.g., RLHF, DPO) further improve safety without capability trade-offs?
- How does the dual-judge pipeline's reliability vary across harm categories, and would human evaluation confirm the automated scores, especially for edge cases?

## Limitations
- Evaluation is restricted to HarmBench; performance on other safety benchmarks (e.g., TruthfulQA, custom red-teaming suites) remains unknown.
- Safety assessments rely on an automated dual-judge pipeline, which may not fully capture nuanced harms or align perfectly with human judgment, especially for edge cases.
- Results are demonstrated on three specific mid-sized model families; generalizability to larger models, different architectures (e.g., MoE), or varied pretraining distributions is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Primary claim (Condition D outperforms B/C/A on HarmBench): **High**
- Capability preservation claim (no meaningful MMLU/ARC degradation): **High**
- Challenge to strong identity-framing hypothesis: **Medium**

## Next Checks
