# Stroke outcome and evolution prediction from CT brain using a spatiotemporal diffusion autoencoder
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00756
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00756
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: Achieves AUC of 0.669 for predicting 24-hour NIHSS improvement (≥4 points) and 0.788 for dichotomized discharge mRS (<3 independent), significantly outperforming baseline models.

## Executive Summary
This paper proposes a self-supervised spatiotemporal diffusion autoencoder to predict stroke outcomes from early non-contrast CT scans using minimal labeled data. The method encodes an early CT slice into a semantic latent representation and trains a conditional diffusion model to denoise a later slice, conditioned on time since stroke onset. By leveraging unlabeled longitudinal CT pairs, it learns features predictive of both short-term (24-hour NIHSS) and discharge (mRS) outcomes. Evaluated on 5,824 CT slices from 3,573 patients, the approach achieves superior performance compared to CNN, VICReg, VAE, and spatial diffusion baselines.

## Method Summary
The core architecture uses a ResNet-50 encoder to map an early CT slice \(x_a\) to a 512-dimensional latent vector \(z\). A conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) with a U-Net backbone is trained to reverse a noising process on a later slice \(x_b\), conditioned on \(z\) and the time since onset \(n\). Conditioning is implemented via Adaptive Spatial Group Normalization (AdaSpaGN) and Adaptive Temporal Group Normalization (AdaTempGN), which modulate the U-Net's features using embeddings of \(z\) and \(\log(n)\). The pretext task uses all available longitudinal pairs (earliest and later scans), requiring only minimal outcome labels for downstream fine-tuning. After pre-training for 1 million steps with augmentations (flips, scaling, translation, rotation), a linear classification head is attached to the (partially frozen) encoder and fine-tuned for 100k steps on each outcome prediction task.

## Key Results
- **24-hour NIHSS improvement** (≥4 points): AUC = 0.669.
- **Discharge mRS** (dichotomized <3 independent): AUC = 0.788.
- Performance significantly (p≤0.05) exceeds baseline CNN, VICReg, variational autoencoder, and spatial diffusion autoencoder models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Semantic Encoding via Diffusion Denoising
The diffusion autoencoder forces the model to denoise a target CT slice \(x_b\) using a latent code \(z\) derived from an early slice \(x_a\). Successful denoising requires \(z\) to capture stroke pathology semantics invariant to low-level variations (e.g., view, noise). Joint optimization of the encoder and DDPM with the simplified diffusion loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}\) encourages \(z\) to encode clinically relevant features rather than pixel-level statistics. Conditioning is achieved through AdaSpaGN, which injects \(z\) into the U-Net's normalization layers.

### Mechanism 2: Temporal Conditioning for Evolution Dynamics
By conditioning the diffusion process on both \(z\) and the absolute time \(n\) (log-transformed) of \(x_b\) via AdaTempGN, the model learns representations that are invariant to the snapshot time but sensitive to the stroke's temporal trajectory. This explicit modeling of CT changes over time (e.g., edema progression) is hypothesized to improve prognosis prediction. The spatiotemporal variant significantly outperforms the spatial-only model in discharge mRS prediction (AUC 0.788 vs. lower), supporting the benefit of temporal information.

### Mechanism 3: Self-Supervision from Longitudinal Pairs
The pretext task leverages all patients with at least two CT scans (≈40% lack outcome labels), circumventing label scarcity. Only the final linear layer of the semantic encoder is fine-tuned on the small labeled subset for each specific outcome. The assumption is that predicting a future slice from an earlier one transfers to predicting clinical outcomes, despite differing objectives. Superior performance over direct CNN training (using only labeled data) validates this transfer.

## Foundational Learning
- **Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)**: Why needed: The entire method hinges on a conditional DDPM as a trainable autoencoder; understanding the forward noising and reverse denoising processes, and the variational lower bound simplified to \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}\), is key to grasping how \(z\) is integrated. Quick check: What is the role of the noise schedule \(\beta_t\) and why is predicting the noise \(\epsilon\) instead of \(x_0\) a common and stable training objective?
- **Self-Supervised Learning via Pretext Tasks**: Why needed: The innovation lies in using a pretext task (future slice prediction) to learn a representation that transfers to downstream clinical prediction with minimal labels. Quick check: How does the pretext task (predicting \(x_b\) from \(x_a\) and time) differ from standard contrastive SSL, and why might it be more suitable for capturing longitudinal stroke changes?
- **Medical Image Preprocessing & Augmentation**: Why needed: Proper handling of 2D slice extraction from 3D CT volumes, normalization, and augmentation is critical for generalization given scanner variability and to prevent overfitting. Quick check: Why are isotropic scaling (±5%) and axial rotation (±0.5 rad) specifically chosen for CT augmentation, and what might be the risk of over-augmentation in a medical context?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Pipeline (CT loading, lesion slice selection via external model, 2D extraction, resizing to 512×512, clipping and Z-score normalization) → Semantic Encoder (ResNet-50, outputs 512-D latent z) → Diffusion U-Net (conditional DDPM with AdaSpaGN/AdaTempGN, GroupNorm skip connections, BigGAN-style residual blocks, attention at 16×16 resolution) → Time Embeddings (sinusoidal encoding for diffusion step t and log-transformed clinical time log(n), combined via MLP with z to produce modulation parameters) → Downstream Head (single linear layer for fine-tuning on outcomes).

**Critical path:**  
1. Preprocess all CT volumes to uniform 2D slices.  
2. For each training step: sample patient, sample pair (\(x_a\)=earliest, \(x_b\)=later) with time delta, augment both, compute \(z = \text{Enc}(x_a)\).  
3. Sample diffusion timestep \(t\), add noise to \(x_b\) to get \(x_b^t\).  
4. Pass (\(x_b^t, t, z\)) (and \(\log(n)\) for spatiotemporal) through U-Net to predict noise \(\epsilon_\theta\).  
5. Compute \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \| \epsilon_\theta - \epsilon \|^2\); update Enc and U-Net.  
6. After pre-training (1M steps), freeze Enc and U-Net, attach linear head, fine-tune on labeled outcome data (100k steps, with encoder partially unfrozen initially).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Spatial vs. Spatiotemporal:** Adding time conditioning improves outcome AUC but adds complexity and assumes ordered scans.  
- **2D Slices vs. 3D Volumes:** 2D simplifies data handling and increases sample count but loses inter-slice context; 3D would be more realistic but computationally expensive.  
- **Diffusion vs. Other SSL:** Diffusion yields superior reconstruction (lower FID) but slower training than contrastive methods (VICReg); tradeoff favors representation quality over speed.  
- **Minimal Labels:** Leverages unlabeled data but relies on the assumption that evolution pretext aligns with outcome prediction.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Poor Reconstruction:** High FID/MSE indicates U-Net/conditioning failure; check noise prediction loss convergence.  
- **No Gain over Baselines:** If AUC ≈ CNN direct training, representation may not transfer; try fine-tuning entire encoder.  
- **Instability During Pre-training:** Loss diverges; likely due to aggressive augmentation, incorrect AdaGN scaling, or \(z\) magnitude issues. Monitor \(z\) norms.  
- **Time Conditioning Harmful:** Spatiotemporal model underperforms spatial; verify log(time) computation and time gap distribution.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Reconstruction sanity check:** Train on a small subset; visualize \(x_b\) vs. predicted \(x_0\) for various \(t\) to ensure semantic plausibility.  
2. **Ablation: Time Conditioning:** Pre-train spatial and spatiotemporal models identically; compare FID/MSE and downstream discharge mRS AUC to isolate time contribution.  
3. **Pair Ordering Ablation:** As in Table 3, compare "Any forward pair" vs. "Any pair"; if performance drops without earliest-scan constraint, temporal inductive bias is key.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Integration of Clinical Information** – The method uses only CT scans, omitting age, treatment, etc., which may limit real-world applicability. Future work should fuse these modalities.
2. **Validation on Long-Term Outcomes** – Evaluation used discharge mRS and 24-hour NIHSS; prospective studies with 90-day mRS are needed to assess temporal generalizability.
3. **Reproducibility on Public Datasets** – Results are on a private dataset; without public benchmarks, it is unclear if performance generalizes. Release of code/weights and testing on public stroke CT datasets (e.g., ISLES) is required.

## Limitations
- Reliance on a single-center dataset and an external model for lesion slice selection may introduce bias.
- Analysis of 2D slices discards 3D contextual information present in full CT volumes.
- The requirement for an earliest scan may constrain deployment in settings with missing or delayed imaging.
- The labeled fine-tuning set is modest (∼60% of patients lack outcomes), risking overfitting despite self-supervision.

## Confidence
- **Meaningful representation from diffusion autoencoder**: Medium (supported by downstream gains but no direct latent interpretation).
- **Temporal conditioning adds prognostic value**: Medium (spatiotemporal model outperforms but gain is marginal; ablation needed).
- **Self-supervision reduces label need**: Medium (beats CNN on same labels but lacks label-efficiency curve).

## Next Checks
1. **Label-efficiency test**: Train encoders from scratch on 10%, 25%, 50% of labeled data and compare with pre-trained approach to quantify label savings.
2. **External validation**: Evaluate the trained model on an independent multi-center stroke CT dataset to assess generalizability across institutions and scanners.
3. **Interpretability analysis**: Use linear probes to predict clinical markers (e.g., ASPECTS, hemorrhage volume) and generate saliency maps to verify focus on pathological regions.
