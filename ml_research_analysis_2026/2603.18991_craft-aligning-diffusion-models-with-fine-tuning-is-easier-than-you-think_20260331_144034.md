# CRAFT: Aligning Diffusion Models with Fine-Tuning Is Easier Than You Think

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.18991
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18991
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** CRAFT fine-tuning of SDXL and SD1.5 achieves 11–220× faster convergence than SOTA preference optimization methods while attaining superior performance on human preference (HPSv2.1, AES, ImageReward, MPS) and compositional (GenEval) benchmarks using only ~100 training samples.

## Executive Summary
CRAFT introduces a novel approach to aligning diffusion models with human preferences that drastically reduces data and computational requirements. The method consists of two key components: Composite Reward Filtering (CRF) to curate a high-quality, consistent training set from just 100 samples, and an enhanced supervised fine-tuning (SFT) procedure that uses a weighted loss derived from group advantages. Theoretical analysis shows that CRAFT optimizes a lower bound of a group-based reinforcement learning objective, thereby connecting SFT with RL.

Empirically, fine-tuning Stable Diffusion XL and 1.5 with CRAFT yields 11–220× faster convergence compared to state-of-the-art preference optimization methods like SPO and SmPO, while achieving superior results on human preference benchmarks (HPSv2.1, AES, ImageReward, MPS) and compositional evaluations (GenEval). These results demonstrate that efficient alignment is possible with minimal data and compute.

## Method Summary
CRAFT operates in two stages. First, it constructs a high-quality training set using Composite Reward Filtering (CRF): each prompt from the HPDv2 training set is refined into four variants via an LLM; images are generated for all prompts using the base diffusion model; three reward models (HPS v2.1, PickScore, AES) score each image; a strict filter (I_hpa) retains refined images that surpass the original prompt's image on all three rewards; finally, the top 100 images by a weighted composite score (0.4*HPS + 0.4*PickScore + 0.2*AES) are selected.

Second, the diffusion model's UNet is fine-tuned with a weighted loss: for each retained sample, its advantage is computed as the normalized composite reward within its original prompt group; the standard diffusion noise prediction loss is multiplied by this advantage, thus up-weighting high-reward samples and down-weighting low-reward ones. Training uses AdamW with a learning rate of 5e-5 and a small number of steps (120 for SD1.5, 200 for SDXL).

## Key Results
- 11–220× faster convergence than SOTA preference optimization (SPO, SmPO).
- Superior performance on human preference benchmarks (HPSv2.1, AES, ImageReward, MPS) and compositional tasks (GenEval).
- Achieves these gains using only ~100 training samples (<0.5% of baseline data) and minimal compute.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Composite Reward Filtering (CRF) for High‑Quality Data
- **Claim:** CRF constructs a high‑quality, consistent training set from minimal samples by leveraging multiple complementary reward models to filter out low‑quality generations relative to an original prompt.
- **Mechanism:** Generate multiple images per original prompt using refined prompts. For each image, compute rewards from HPS v2.1, PickScore, and AES. Retain only images where all selected reward metrics exceed the reward of the image from the original prompt. This selects samples that are demonstrably better across key dimensions (human preference, text‑image alignment, aesthetics) compared to the baseline generation for that prompt.
- **Core assumption:** A small set of high‑quality, distribution‑consistent (prompt‑wise) examples is more effective for supervised fine‑tuning than a larger set of noisier examples. Refined prompts increase diversity without altering the underlying prompt identity for reward comparison.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states CRF constructs a high‑quality and consistent dataset; Section 3.2 details the filtering sets Iξ, Iha, Ipa, Ihpa based on comparisons to the original prompt's image reward; the paper's experiments show CRF selects samples with higher rewards across all three metrics.
- **Break condition:** The filtering fails if the chosen reward models are poorly correlated with true human preference, if the original prompt's baseline generation is anomalously high‑quality (leaving no valid "better" samples), or if the refinement step introduces semantic drift not caught by reward comparison.

### Mechanism 2: Weighted SFT with Group Advantages
- **Claim:** Weighted SFT with group‑based advantages (normalized rewards) steers the diffusion model to increase the likelihood of high‑reward samples while decreasing the likelihood of low‑reward samples within a prompt group, effectively implementing a form of reward maximization.
- **Mechanism:** For a batch of N images generated from one original prompt (via refined prompts), compute the normalized advantage Â(i,j) for each image based on its total composite reward relative to the group's mean/std. The fine‑tuning loss is the standard diffusion noise prediction loss weighted by Â(i,j). Positive advantages increase gradient magnitude for that sample; negative advantages decrease it (or implicitly reduce its contribution if only filtered positive samples are used, as in Ihpa).
- **Core assumption:** The gradient signal from a weighted denoising loss, where weights are derived from reward advantages, is a valid and stable proxy for policy gradient updates in the RL formulation (Theorem 3.1). The model can effectively modulate sample likelihoods based on a scalar advantage weight.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions enhanced SFT and connection to RL; Section 3.3 and Algorithm 1 present the loss formulation L(θ) = Â(i,j)·||ϵθ(...)-ϵ||²·I(x∈˜I); Theorem 3.1 provides theoretical lower bound connecting weighted SFT to group‑based RL.
- **Break condition:** The weighting scheme breaks if the group size is too small to estimate stable mean/std, leading to high variance. It also assumes the noise prediction model's gradients respond linearly to the weighting, which may not hold for extreme weights or out‑of‑distribution samples.

### Mechanism 3: Extreme Data Efficiency and Computational Savings
- **Claim:** The combination of an extremely small, high‑quality dataset (~100 samples) and the computationally lightweight weighted SFT process achieves dramatic efficiency gains (11‑220× faster convergence) over preference optimization methods that rely on large‑scale pairwise data or online sampling.
- **Mechanism:** By pre‑filtering to only the most informative, high‑quality samples, CRAFT eliminates the need for the model to learn from noisy or low‑quality examples during fine‑tuning. The weighted SFT loss avoids the repeated generation and reward evaluation steps inherent in online methods (like SPO) or the complex pairwise loss computations and larger batch requirements of offline DPO‑style methods. The training objective becomes a simpler, single‑model update on a curated set.
- **Core assumption:** The performance ceiling for alignment can be reached with a very small number of "core" high‑quality examples, and the computational bottleneck in other methods is primarily data scale and algorithmic complexity, not fundamental expressivity.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Figure 1 show 11‑220× faster convergence; Table 1 quantifies GPU hour reductions (e.g., 15.8× vs SPO on SDXL, 218.7× vs Diff‑KTO on SD1.5); Sections 4.2–4.3 and Tables 2,3,5 show superior performance with <0.5% of the data used by baselines.
- **Break condition:** This efficiency gain breaks if the target preference distribution is highly diverse and cannot be captured by 100 samples, leading to overfitting to a narrow subset of prompts or styles. It also assumes the base pre‑trained model has sufficient capacity to learn from a few examples, which may not hold for highly specialized domains.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Pre‑trained Diffusion Model (pθ_old) → Prompt Refinement Module (LLM) → Candidate Image Generator → Composite Reward Models (HPS, PickScore, AES) → Reward Filter (I_hpa) → Advantage Calculator → Weighted SFT Trainer (updates pθ).

**Critical path:** Prompt set P → Refinement to Prefined → Candidate generation I → Reward scoring → Filtering to ˜I (Top‑100 selection optional) → Group advantage calculation → Weighted SFT update on pθ.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Filter Strictness (Iξ vs. Ihpa):* Stricter filters (Ihpa) yield smaller, higher‑quality datasets but risk losing diversity. Looser filters include more data but may introduce noise. Ablation shows Ihpa + Top‑100 selection works best.
- *Composite Reward Weights:* Heavily weighting PickScore (0.4) over AES (0.2) was found optimal. The weights balance alignment (HPS, PickScore) and aesthetics (AES).
- *Data Size vs. Training Steps:* A tiny dataset (100 samples) requires few steps (200 for SDXL) to converge, maximizing efficiency. Larger filtered sets (e.g., all 2,312) require more steps but ultimately underperform the curated tiny set.

**Failure signatures:**
- Poor performance on unseen reward metrics (e.g., ImageReward, MPS) despite good training‑set reward scores → indicates reward overfitting or misaligned reward models.
- Low GenEval scores, especially on "Two Object" or "Position" → suggests CRF failed to preserve compositional diversity; filtered set may be biased toward single‑object or centered compositions.
- No speedup over baselines → indicates implementation flaw (e.g., not using filtered set, incorrect advantage weighting) or hardware discrepancy not accounted for.

**First 3 experiments:**

2. Validate Advantage Weighting: Train two models on the same Top‑100 filtered dataset: (a) vanilla SFT (no weighting), (b) CRAFT with weighting. Compare HPS/IR scores. Expect CRAFT > SFT, confirming the weighting's contribution beyond data selection.
3. End‑to‑End Cost & Performance: Run the full CRAFT pipeline (candidate generation + filtering ≈20 GPU‑hrs for SDXL + 200‑step fine‑tuning ≈4 GPU‑hrs) and compare final HPSv2.1 on HPDv2 and total GPU hours against a baseline (e.g., SPO). Expect CRAFT to reach higher HPS in fewer total hours.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can CRAFT be transformed into an online learning framework that dynamically updates the training data to improve data efficiency and exploration?
- **Basis:** The authors state that CRAFT operates as an offline SFT, limiting data utilization and exploration compared to online RL, and propose evolving it into an iterative "generate‑train" loop.
- **Why unresolved:** The current pipeline constructs a static dataset before fine‑tuning, missing opportunities to adapt the data distribution as the model learns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement an online version where generated samples are continuously filtered and added during training. Compare convergence speed, sample efficiency, and final performance on HPSv2.1 and GenEval against the offline baseline.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can incorporating initial noise quality into the Composite Reward Filtering improve sample selection and model performance?
- **Basis:** The limitation section notes that CRF selects samples only at the image level and does not model the quality of the initial noise, and future work includes adaptive noise selection criteria.
- **Why unresolved:** The diffusion process starts from noise; its properties may influence output quality and diversity, which are currently ignored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Augment CRF with noise‑level features (e.g., noise norm, seed consistency) as additional filtering signals. Train models with the extended dataset and measure changes in aesthetic scores, diversity, and training stability.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does CRAFT’s design effectively generalize to text‑to‑video and text‑to‑3D generation, where alignment involves temporal or 3D consistency?
- **Basis:** The authors acknowledge that all experiments are on T2I and that CRAFT has not addressed challenges of T2V or T2‑3D, with future plans to adapt it to these modalities.
- **Why unresolved:** Reward models, filtering rules, and training objectives may require significant changes for video or 3D data, and it is unclear if the data‑efficiency gains persist.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply CRAFT to T2V (e.g., Stable Video Diffusion) and T2‑3D models, using suitable rewards (video quality, 3D consistency). Compare alignment metrics against baseline fine‑tuning methods under the same data budget.

## Limitations
- CRF's dataset construction hinges on three external reward models; their alignment with true human preferences is not guaranteed and may introduce bias.
- Training on only 100 samples may cause overfitting and poor generalization to diverse prompts.
- The strict I_hpa filter (requiring all three rewards to exceed the baseline) could discard images that are strong in one dimension but not others, reducing diversity.

## Confidence
- **CRF data quality:** Medium (filtering logic sound, but empirical evidence limited to paper's own experiments; robustness to reward errors uncertain).
- **Weighted SFT objective:** High (Theorem 3.1 provides solid theoretical foundation; paper shows it outperforms uniform SFT).
- **Efficiency gains (11‑220× faster):** High (detailed GPU‑hour comparisons and benchmark results support this; absolute numbers may vary with implementation).
- **RL‑theory connection:** High (derivation mathematically rigorous and correctly links group‑based RL to SFT).
