# Humans and LLMs Diverge on Probabilistic Inferences

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23546
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23546
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art reasoning LLMs exhibit persistent, measurable divergence from human probabilistic judgment distributions on a dedicated dataset, showing overconfidence and abnormally low response variability.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces PROBCOPA, a dataset of 210 premise–hypothesis pairs designed to probe graded probabilistic inferences (i.e., assessing the likelihood of an effect given a cause). Human annotators (25–30 per item) provided unimodal, continuous likelihood scores (0–100). Eight reasoning LLMs were queried for verbalized numerical scores (30 samples per item). Results reveal systematic misalignment: LLM response distributions have far lower entropy than human distributions, rarely assign medium-likelihood scores, and show substantially higher Wasserstein distances to human distributions than the human-human baseline. Longer model reasoning chains do not improve alignment, highlighting a gap in open-ended probabilistic reasoning capabilities.

## Method Summary
The authors derived PROBCOPA from the COPA causal reasoning test set, retaining only “effect” inferences. Human participants rated each hypothesis on a 0–100 scale with examples and attention checks. For each of eight LLMs (e.g., GPT-5, Claude Sonnet-4.5), the study sampled 30 likelihood scores per item using a fixed numerical-scale prompt. Distributions were compared via differential entropy (spread) and 1-Wasserstein distance (distributional similarity). A separate set of 30 annotators provided a human-human baseline on a random 30-item subset. Additional analyses correlated model reasoning length (token count) with human difficulty metrics (entropy, response time).

## Key Results
- LLMs exhibit dramatically lower differential entropy (~0–2.0) than humans (~4.0), indicating abnormally peaked, low-variance responses.
- Model–human Wasserstein distances (median ~4.77) far exceed the human–human baseline, with peak divergence for items where human consensus is mid-range.
- LLMs almost never output scores in the 40–60 range, producing a bimodal histogram with a central dip, unlike the unimodal human distribution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not identify a definitive mechanism for the divergence. It rules out insufficient reasoning effort (longer chains do not help) and notes that while models often list alternative scenarios in chains, this does not yield human-like probability distributions. The persistent overconfidence and low entropy suggest a fundamental mismatch in how uncertainty is internally represented or calibrated during verbalized probabilistic judgment, possibly stemming from training objectives optimized for point answers or verifiable tasks rather than distributional calibration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Probabilistic inference:** Reasoning about likelihoods beyond binary entailment. *Why needed:* Core task studied; humans produce graded judgments. *Quick check:* Could you design a prompt that elicits a continuous probability from a model?
- **Distributional alignment:** Comparing full response distributions, not just point estimates. *Why needed:* Captures variability and consensus. *Quick check:* Would median scores alone reveal the low-entropy problem?
- **Differential entropy:** Continuous Shannon entropy measuring spread of a distribution. *Why needed:* Quantifies response variability. *Quick check:* Does a distribution of {0,0,0,100,100,100} have high or low entropy?
- **Wasserstein distance:** Earth Mover’s Distance between two distributions. *Why needed:* Sensitive to shape differences, not just means. *Quick check:* Is the distance zero only if distributions are identical?
- **Verbalized likelihood:** Extracting a numerical score from a model’s free-text output. *Why needed:* Primary elicitation method in the study. *Quick check:* Could tokenization or output formatting bias the scores?
- **Unimodal consensus:** Human distributions on PROBCOPA are single-peaked. *Why needed:* Contrasts with bimodal splits in other NLI datasets. *Quick check:* Would a dataset with bimodal human judgments make alignment easier or harder?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
PROBCOPA items -> Human annotators (25–30 scores) -> Human distribution  
PROBCOPA items -> LLM inference pipeline (prompt -> chain-of-thought -> <answer> tag parse) -> 30 scores/model -> Model distribution  
Human & model distributions -> Metrics (entropy, Wasserstein) -> Comparison

**Critical path:** Dataset construction → Human annotation → Model inference → Distributional metrics → Analysis of alignment patterns.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Verbalized scores vs. implicit uncertainty (e.g., sampling): verbalization provides direct numbers but may suffer from faithfulness and formatting biases.  
- Derived dataset (COPA splits) vs. de novo design: ensures unimodal human distributions but may inherit surface-form biases.  
- Fixed prompt with scale guide vs. interactive probing: standardization aids comparison but may not elicit optimal model reasoning.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Model entropy consistently below human entropy by >1.5 bits.  
- Bimodal or U-shaped model score histograms (dip at 40–60).  
- Model–human Wasserstein > human–human baseline by factor >2.  
- Reasoning chain length increases on hard items but final scores do not shift toward human distribution.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Replicate differential entropy calculation using both raw scores and KDE (vary bandwidth) to confirm the entropy gap persists across smoothing choices.  
2. Parse model responses with a more permissive regex to ensure the central dip in scores is not an artifact of strict `<answer>` tag extraction.  
3. Compute human–human Wasserstein on the 30-item baseline set and verify it is substantially lower than any model–human distance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do the underlying cognitive or architectural mechanisms of probabilistic reasoning fundamentally differ between humans and state-of-the-art reasoning LLMs?
2. Why does increasing reasoning effort (longer chain length) in LLMs not lead to improved alignment with human probabilistic judgment distributions, even though models reason longer on items humans find difficult?
3. To what extent is the unimodal, graded human judgment distribution observed in PROBCOPA an artifact of the dataset's construction (derived from COPA's "plausible alternatives"), and would models align better with humans on inferences designed to elicit strictly unimodal human distributions from the outset?
4. Does the common reasoning pattern of "explicitly considering alternative scenarios" in LLM chains represent a faithful and accurate approximation of human hypothetical thinking during probabilistic judgment, or is it a superficial heuristic that fails to integrate scenarios into a coherent probability estimate?
5. Are the observed limitations (overconfidence, low entropy) in verbalized likelihood scores from reasoning LLMs an inherent property of their decoding and training objectives, or can they be mitigated by moving to distributional or implicit uncertainty quantification methods that bypass direct verbal probability estimation?

## Limitations
- Raw model responses may not be publicly available, requiring costly re-inference.
- Entropy calculations depend on KDE bandwidth choices, affecting absolute values.
- “Thinking budget” parameters for Claude and Gemini in the main experiment are not explicitly specified.
- Findings are limited to verbalized numerical likelihoods; other elicitation methods may yield different alignment.

## Confidence
- **Claim A (Models show lower entropy than humans):** Medium (depends on KDE implementation, but pattern robust).
- **Claim B (Model–human Wasserstein > human–human baseline):** High (uses raw empirical distributions, less sensitive to smoothing).
- **Claim C (Peak distances for mid-consensus items):** High (rank-based, stable).
- **Claim D (Rare medium scores):** High (evident in histograms; parsing issues monitorable).
