# Sparse Crosscoders for diffing MoEs and Dense models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05805
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05805
- **Reference count:** 1
- **Primary result:** MoE models develop far fewer unique features (910) with higher activation density compared to dense models (3,226 features with lower density), indicating more specialized, focused representations.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces sparse crosscoders—a variant of sparse autoencoders—to systematically compare the internal representations of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) and dense transformer models. By training a crosscoder with a designated subset of shared features on third-layer activations from both models, the authors achieve high reconstruction quality (~87% fractional variance explained). Analysis reveals that the MoE learns significantly fewer exclusive features, which exhibit higher activation density (i.e., they fire more frequently across inputs), while the dense model learns many more exclusive features with lower density. This suggests MoEs concentrate information into specialized, frequently used features, whereas dense models distribute it across a broader set of general-purpose features.

## Method Summary
The authors first train two 5-layer transformer models—one dense and one MoE—on equal active parameters, using a mixed corpus of code (StarCoder), science (RedPajama arXiv), and stories (SimpleStories) totaling ~1B tokens. After training, they extract aligned activation batches from the third layer of each model. They then train a BatchTopK crosscoder: an encoder produces sparse feature activations for both models jointly, while model-specific decoders reconstruct the respective activations. A fixed subset of features is explicitly designated as “shared” (with tied decoder weights and stronger sparsity regularization, λs/λf ≈ 0.7), with the remainder exclusive to each model. Features are classified as MoE-specific, dense-specific, or shared based on the relative norm difference (Δ_norm) between the two decoders. Activation density—the fraction of batch samples where a feature is active—is computed for each class to quantify specialization.

## Key Results
- Crosscoder achieves ~87% fractional variance explained on held-out third-layer activations.
- Feature counts: MoE-specific = 910, Dense-specific = 3,226, Shared = 18,940.
- MoE-specific features have higher activation density than shared features; dense-specific features have lower density than shared.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Shared sparse dictionary enables cross-architecture comparison
The crosscoder learns a single set of sparse feature activations *f_i(x)* and separate decoder vectors for each model. The reconstruction loss forces the shared features to capture variance common to both activation distributions, while differences in decoder norms (Δ_norm) reveal model-specific features. This works because the models’ internal representations share sufficient latent structure despite architectural divergence, as evidenced by high fractional variance explained (~87%). If the models’ activation spaces were too orthogonal, reconstruction would fail.

### Mechanism 2: Explicit shared feature designation prevents overestimation of shared structure
Standard crosscoders can mistakenly classify unrelated features as shared due to high mean activation correlation, especially when comparing independently trained models. By fixing a subset of features as shared and applying stronger regularization (λs/λf ≈ 0.7 vs. 0.1–0.2 in fine-tuning comparisons), the method ensures that shared features capture true common variance while exclusive features specialize. This addresses the overestimation problem noted in the paper, as shown by the cleaner separation in Δ_norm histograms when using the modified objective.

### Mechanism 3: Activation density reflects specialization strategies
The observed pattern—MoE-exclusive features are fewer and denser, while dense-exclusive features are more numerous and sparser—suggests MoEs focus information into specialized, frequently used representations, whereas dense models distribute it across broader, general-purpose features. Activation density (frequency of feature activation across inputs) serves as a proxy for specialization: high density indicates a feature is broadly relevant, while low density suggests niche use. This aligns with the paper’s interpretation and is supported by the density distributions in Figure 3.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Two 5-layer transformer models (dense, MoE) trained on same corpus → third-layer activations extracted → BatchTopK crosscoder with designated shared features → analysis via decoder norm differences (Δ_norm) and activation density.
- **Critical path:** 1) Train dense & MoE with equal active parameters. 2) Extract aligned activation batches from layer 3. 3) Train crosscoder with designated shared features and tuned λs/λf (~0.7). 4) Compute Δ_norm per feature to classify as MoE-only (<0.3), shared (0.3–0.7), dense-only (>0.7). 5) Compute feature activation density (fraction of tokens active). 6) Compare density distributions across classes.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Higher shared feature proportion & λs/λf improves exclusive feature interpretability but risks underfitting shared structure; BatchTopK (hard sparsity) vs. L1 penalty trades ease of optimization for sharper feature selection; crosscoder capacity (k) must balance reconstruction vs. fragmentation.
- **Failure signatures:** Low FVE (<70%); absence of trimodal Δ_norm distribution; MoE/dense exclusive feature density distributions converging; high cosine similarity among features within a class (dictionary failure).
- **First 3 experiments:** 1) **Reconstruction validation:** Track FVE on held-out activations during crosscoder training; aim for >85%. 2) **Feature classification sanity check:** Compute Δ_norm histogram; verify clear separation between modes before density analysis. 3) **Density distribution comparison:** Plot kernel density estimate for each feature class; test statistical difference (e.g., Kolmogorov-Smirnov) between MoE-only vs. shared and dense-only vs. shared.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can crosscoders be adapted for reliable comparison of MoE and dense model internals without extensive per-pair hyperparameter tuning?**  
   *Basis:* The paper notes that significant work remains to modify crosscoders for structurally distinct models like MoEs and dense models. The standard crosscoder produced ambiguous splits and required a custom λs/λf ratio (0.7), indicating a lack of robustness.  
   *Why unresolved:* The method is sensitive to hyperparameters; no generic recipe exists for architectural comparisons.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* A new crosscoder design that directly incorporates architectural differences (e.g., routing masks) and consistently yields clear shared/exclusive separation across diverse model pairs with minimal tuning.

2. **What are the semantic interpretations of the features learned by crosscoders when comparing MoE and dense models, and do observed quantitative differences correspond to functionally distinct representational strategies?**  
   *Basis:* The paper explicitly calls for future qualitative analysis to validate semantic meaningfulness.  
   *Why unresolved:* Only quantitative metrics (FVE, densities) are reported; the semantic content of features remains unknown.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Systematic labeling of top-activating examples for a diverse sample of features from each category (MoE-specific, dense-specific, shared) via human evaluation or automated clustering to identify coherent semantic patterns.

3. **Why does the MoE learn far fewer exclusive features with higher activation density compared to the dense model?**  
   *Basis:* Results show MoE-exclusive features: 910 vs. dense-exclusive: 3,226; MoE-exclusive density > shared, dense-exclusive density < shared.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper does not explain causal mechanisms; possible factors include sparse routing or training dynamics, but these are speculative.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Controlled ablations on MoE models varying sparsity (top-k, number of experts) and measuring resulting feature counts and densities, coupled with correlation analyses between feature activations and routing decisions or expert parameters.

## Limitations
- Critical hyperparameters (model dimensions, expert count, crosscoder k, BatchTopK budget, exact λ values) and dataset processing details are unspecified, blocking exact replication.
- The operational definition of “activation density” (e.g., threshold for active) is ambiguous, affecting reproducibility of density comparisons.
- Lack of controlled ablations prevents causal inference linking architecture to feature distribution patterns; alternative explanations (e.g., training duration, dataset) are not ruled out.
