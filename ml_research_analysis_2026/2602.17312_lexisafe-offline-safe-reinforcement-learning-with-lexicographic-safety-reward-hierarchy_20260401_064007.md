# LexiSafe: Offline Safe Reinforcement Learning with Lexicographic Safety-Reward Hierarchy
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17312
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17312
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LexiSafe reduces safety violations and achieves higher normalized reward compared to constrained offline baselines (CPQ, FISOR) on DSRL benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
LexiSafe introduces an offline safe reinforcement learning framework for cyber‑physical systems that enforces a strict lexicographic hierarchy between safety and performance. It employs a two‑phase training regime: first minimize cumulative safety cost under a KL divergence constraint to learn a safe policy, then maximize reward while preserving safety via a cost‑penalty term and Lagrangian multiplier. For multiple safety costs, it sequentially optimizes each in user‑specified priority order, guaranteeing higher‑priority constraints remain satisfied. Theoretical analysis provides finite‑sample safety violation and performance bounds. Empirical evaluation on DSRL benchmarks shows that LexiSafe reduces safety violations and achieves higher normalized reward than prior methods, and successfully enforces hierarchical safety where weighted‑sum baselines fail.

## Method Summary
LexiSafe builds on Implicit Q‑Learning (IQL) to avoid out‑of‑distribution actions. It maintains separate Q and value networks for each safety cost and for reward, all sharing a common policy network. Critics are updated via Bellman backups with expectile regression; the policy is updated via advantage‑weighted regression using only dataset actions. For a single cost, Phase 1 minimizes the cost‑advantage (A_c = Q_c−V_c) under a KL constraint, producing a policy that satisfies the safety threshold with high probability. Phase 2 maximizes reward‑advantage (A_r = Q_r−V_r) while adding a penalty proportional to A_c, with a Lagrange multiplier adjusted to enforce the cost constraint. For multiple costs, LexiSafe adds preliminary phases for each cost in priority order, each minimizing one cost while preserving feasibility for higher‑priority costs. The entire process uses only the offline dataset, ensuring safety without online interaction.

## Key Results
- On single‑cost DSRL tasks (e.g., Safety Gymnasium, Bullet Safety Gym), LexiSafe reduces safety violations (cost < 1) and achieves higher normalized reward compared to baselines like CPQ and FISOR.
- In multi‑cost environments (MetaDrive), LexiSafe reliably enforces user‑specified safety hierarchies (e.g., crash avoidance prioritized over speed), while weighted‑sum IQL fails to maintain priority constraints across weight sweeps.
- Theoretical analysis yields finite‑sample guarantees: with high probability, the learned policy satisfies safety thresholds up to a bounded violation and attains near‑optimal reward within the feasible set, with sample complexity scaling with model capacity and concentrability coefficient.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
LexiSafe’s safety guarantees stem from three interconnected mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1: Lexicographic two‑phase separation.** The algorithm divides training into two distinct phases. Phase 1 minimizes the cumulative safety cost using a cost‑advantage weighted policy update under a KL constraint, producing a policy that is safe with high probability. Phase 2 then maximizes reward while adding a penalty term proportional to the learned cost advantage, adjusted by a Lagrange multiplier that maintains the safety threshold. This strict ordering prevents safety drift during reward optimization. The mechanism is instantiated in Algorithm 1 and supported by Theorem 1, which bounds safety violations. A critical break condition occurs when the offline dataset contains no feasible safe trajectories; then Phase 1 cannot converge to a safe policy and the method fails.

**Mechanism 2: Sequential minimization for multiple costs.** LexiSafe‑MC extends the two‑phase structure with K−1 cost‑minimization phases for K safety objectives. Each phase j minimizes cost C_j while preserving feasibility with respect to all higher‑priority costs C₁,…,C_{j−1}. The feasible set contracts after each phase, ensuring higher‑priority constraints remain satisfied indefinitely. This is formalized in Definition 3 and Algorithm 2, with Theorem 2 providing safety violation bounds. A break condition arises if the priority order is infeasible: if no policy satisfies all thresholds simultaneously, later phases may be unable to find a feasible policy within the contracted set, causing training to stall or violate constraints.

**Mechanism 3: Implicit Q‑Learning for offline stability.** Both critics and policy updates rely on IQL, which learns value functions via expectile regression on dataset actions only, avoiding queries to out‑of‑distribution actions. Cost and reward Q‑networks are trained by Bellman backups; the corresponding value networks are fitted to the expectiles of the Q‑estimates. The policy is updated by advantage‑weighted regression using the computed advantages (A_c = Q_c−V_c, A_r = Q_r−V_r). This inherits IQL’s data‑efficiency and distribution‑clamping properties, essential for safe offline RL. Evidence appears in Sections III and IV, with Lemma 1 bounding Bellman errors. However, large concentrability coefficients can inflate Bellman residuals and degrade Q‑estimates, potentially causing safety violations despite lexicographic separation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Offline Safe RL**  
  Why needed: LexiSafe operates purely on pre‑collected data; no online interaction is allowed, which is mandatory for CPS to avoid risky exploration.  
  Quick check: What are the two constraints in the standard offline safe RL objective (Eq. 1)? (Answer: cumulative cost ≤ κ and KL divergence ≤ ε.)

- **Lexicographic Order in MDPs**  
  Why needed: It formalizes strict priority between safety and performance, ensuring safety is never compromised for reward.  
  Quick check: In lexicographic order, when does the agent start optimizing the secondary objective? (Answer: Only after the primary objective is satisfied to the required threshold.)

- **Implicit Q‑Learning (IQL)**  
  Why needed: Provides a stable, distribution‑clamped critic learning scheme that avoids OOD action queries, essential for safe offline policy extraction.  
  Quick check: What loss does IQL use for the value network V(s)? (Answer: Asymmetric squared loss L₂_ξ applied to Q−V differences via expectile regression.)

- **Concentrability Coefficient (C)**  
  Why needed: Quantifies distribution shift between learned policy and dataset; appears directly in theoretical error bounds (Theorems 1–2).  
  Quick check: What does a small C imply about the dataset? (Answer: The behavior policy’s coverage closely matches that of the learned policy, i.e., less distributional shift.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: The system comprises four critics (Q_c, V_c for cost; Q_r, V_r for reward), a shared actor π_θ, and Lagrange multipliers λ_j (one per cost) plus a final λ for the reward phase. Data flows: minibatch from offline dataset D → critics updated via Bellman backups → compute advantages (A_c = Q_c−V_c, A_r = Q_r−V_r) → policy update (first through cost‑minimization phases using A_c, then through reward‑maximization phase using A_r penalized by λ·A_c) → λ_j updated based on smoothed cost estimates. The KL constraint ε is implicitly enforced by the advantage‑weighted regression.

Critical path (training loop):
1. Sample minibatch B from D.
2. Update all four critics using Bellman MSE (for Q) and expectile loss (for V).
3. For each safety cost in priority order (phase j):
   a. Compute A_c_j = Q_c_j − V_c_j.
   b. Update policy by minimizing L_c_π(θ) = E[−exp(β_c A_c_j) log π_θ(a|s)].
   c. Update multiplier λ_j ← max(0, λ_j + ν_λ (mean(C_j) − κ_j)).
4. Final reward phase:
   a. Compute A_r = Q_r − V_r.
   b. Update policy by minimizing L_r_π(θ) = E[exp(β_r (A_r − λ/β_r A_c_total)) log π_θ(a|s)].
   c. Update final λ based on aggregated cost.

Design tradeoffs:
- Two‑phase vs joint optimization: LexiSafe’s decoupling prevents safety drift but may require more data passes; joint methods (e.g., CPQ) are compact but risk constraint violations under distribution shift.
- KL constraint ε: Smaller ε keeps policy closer to dataset (safer) but may limit performance; larger ε improves performance at the cost of OOD risk.
- Number of cost phases (K): More phases allow finer safety hierarchies but increase sample complexity (Corollary 1) and may become infeasible if thresholds are too strict.

Failure signatures:
- **Safety violation** (cost ≥ κ): Likely Phase 1 failure or mis‑scaled λ; monitor cost during training to diagnose.
- **Poor reward**: Reward far below baselines indicates over‑regularization (ε too small) or overly strong cost penalty; adjust β_r or λ initialization.
- **Unstable multipliers**: λ_j oscillating suggests ν_λ too large or high‑variance cost estimates; reduce ν_λ or increase batch size.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Benchmark comparison**: Evaluate LexiSafe‑SC on Safety Gymnasium and Bullet Safety Gym tasks; compare normalized reward (↑) and cost (↓) against BC‑Safe, COptiDICE, CPQ, FISOR, LSPC‑O; verify safe policies (cost < 1) achieve top reward.
2. **Multi‑cost hierarchy ablation**: In MetaDrive, impose Crash→Speed→Reward and Speed→Crash→Reward orders; plot cost and reward over training phases to confirm sequential constraint satisfaction.
3. **Weighted IQL sensitivity**: Replace lexicographic phases with weighted sum objective R̃ = R − ∑ w_j C_j; sweep w_crash ∈ {1,10,100,1000,5000} with w_speed=1; compare safety and reward to LexiSafe‑MC, highlighting LexiSafe’s insensitivity to weight tuning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can lexicographic offline RL provide strict hierarchical safety guarantees in CPS while achieving near‑optimal performance?**  
   *Basis*: Central question stated in Introduction. *Unresolved*: Existing methods lack hierarchical mechanisms; LexiSafe proposes a framework but needs validation beyond benchmarks. *Evidence needed*: Formal proofs with tighter bounds for diverse CPS models, extensive high‑fidelity simulations demonstrating consistent safety adherence and performance.

2. **How can the practical difficulty of estimating concentrability coefficients and ensuring sufficient safe data coverage be overcome?**  
   *Basis*: Limitations note that coefficients may be difficult to estimate or validate. *Unresolved*: Concentrability is a theoretical construct often unknown in practice. *Evidence needed*: Development of practical methods to estimate or bound concentrability from offline data, empirical studies linking estimation error to safety violation rates, robust algorithms less sensitive to these assumptions.

3. **Can sample complexity bounds for lexicographic safe RL be refined to tighter, data‑dependent limits?**  
   *Basis*: Acknowledged that VC‑dimension bounds may be loose for deep networks. *Unresolved*: Distribution‑free bounds are conservative relative to empirical performance. *Evidence needed*: Derivation of new generalization bounds that incorporate data‑dependent terms (e.g., Rademacher complexity), empirical validation showing improved sample efficiency, and comparison with actual performance on diverse tasks.

4. **How can lexicographic safe RL frameworks adapt to dynamic safety priority reordering?**  
   *Basis*: LexiSafe‑MC assumes a fixed order of safety costs. *Unresolved*: Real‑world CPS may encounter shifting priorities (e.g., from collision avoidance to comfort in low‑risk conditions). *Evidence needed*: Extensions that allow online or context‑aware reordering of safety objectives, theoretical analysis of such adaptive hierarchies, experiments in environments with varying priority regimes.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees assume the offline dataset contains safe policies, has bounded concentrability, and that all cost thresholds are jointly achievable; real‑world CPS data often violate these conditions, risking safety breaches. Unknown: How to verify these assumptions without online interaction, and how robust the method is when they are violated.
- The multi‑cost extension presumes a feasible policy exists for the user‑specified priority order; conflicting costs can render the problem infeasible, causing training to stall or violate higher‑priority constraints. Assumption: The priority order is fixed and reflects true safety needs; in practice, priorities may shift dynamically (see Open Questions).
- Evaluation is limited to simulated DSRL benchmarks; performance on physical systems or high‑dimensional observation spaces remains untested, restricting claims about practical deployment. Unknown: Whether the method's computational overhead and sample complexity scale to real-world CPS with high-dimensional states and complex dynamics.

## Confidence
- Two‑phase lexicographic safety mechanism: High (theoretical support from Theorem 1 and strong empirical results on DSRL benchmarks)
- Multi‑cost hierarchical feasibility: Medium (depends on existence of a feasible policy respecting the priority order; limited evaluation in MetaDrive with only two costs)
- IQL‑based offline stability and OOD avoidance: High (leverages established IQL framework; Lemma 1 provides error bounds under concentrability assumptions)
- Sample complexity bounds: Medium (the bounds are distribution-free and may be conservative; no empirical sample complexity analysis provided)

## Next Checks

2. **Cross‑benchmark generalization**: Run LexiSafe on additional offline safe RL suites (e.g., Safe‑RL real‑world robotics logs, Safety Gym Gamma tasks) to verify that observed benefits extend beyond the original benchmark set.
