# Minibal: Balanced Game-Playing Without Opponent Modeling

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23059  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23059  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Minibal_+ achieves near-zero binary gain (-2.96%) and average score -0.024, significantly outperforming Unbounded Minimax and Minibal_n.

## Executive Summary

This paper introduces Minibal, a variant of Unbounded Minimax search designed to produce balanced, engaging gameplay rather than overwhelming human opponents. Two variants are proposed: Minibal_n minimizes the absolute final score, while Minibal_+ prefers the smallest positive score and, if none exist, the least negative score. Across seven board games, the authors pit a strong neural evaluation function against a weak one. Minibal_+ achieves near-perfect balance with an average binary gain of -2.96% (close to 0%) and an average terminal score of -0.024. In contrast, standard Unbounded Minimax yields a 92.68% gain (favoring the stronger side) and Minibal_n a -68.95% gain (favoring the weaker side). The results demonstrate that Minibal_+ can autonomously adapt its playing strength without opponent modeling or manual tuning.

## Method Summary

Minibal builds on Unbounded Minimax with completion (UMc), which uses a learned evaluation function \(f_\theta(s)\) for non‑terminal leaves and a predefined terminal evaluation \(f_t(s)\) for game‑over states. The search employs a transposition table and a backup procedure that propagates a **completion value** \(c(s)\) (win/draw/loss) and a **resolution flag** \(r(s)\). The key modifications lie in the `best_action` selection at the balanced player’s node:

- **Minibal_n**: chooses the child with the smallest absolute value \(|v(s)|\).
- **Minibal_+**: if any child has a positive \(v(s)\), pick the smallest positive; otherwise pick the largest (least negative). Opponent nodes always apply the standard minim (from the balanced player’s perspective). Tie‑breaking uses a lexicographic order: \(c(s)\) (higher is better), then \(v(s)\), then visit count \(n(s,a)\).

The authors train 40 evaluation functions per game (20 “high‑level” strong, 20 “low‑level” weak) via Descent Minimax self‑play, using the 20‑day checkpoint as the weak set. For each of the seven games, they run 800 matches per condition (high × low × two colors) across 12 search‑time budgets from 0.01 to 20 seconds. The primary metrics are **binary gain** (average outcome: +1 win, -1 loss, 0 draw) and **average terminal score** (mean \(f_t(s)\)).

## Key Results

- **Minibal_+**: average binary gain = **-2.96%**, average terminal score = **-0.024**.  
- **Unbounded Minimax**: average binary gain = **92.68%**.  
- **Minibal_n**: average binary gain = **-68.95%**.  
- Minibal_+’s win/draw/loss breakdown was approximately 21% / 54% / 24%, indicating near‑perfect balance across all seven games.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

Minibal_+ embeds the balancing goal directly into its action selection via a lexicographic ordering that prioritizes the sign of the outcome. The **completion value** \(c(s)\) (1 for win, 0 for draw, -1 for loss) is the highest‑priority criterion, guaranteeing that the algorithm never sacrifices a certain win for a smaller margin. Among actions with the same \(c\), it chooses the **smallest positive** outcome to keep victories narrow, or if no positive exists, the **largest (least negative)** to keep defeats narrow. This forces the search to steer toward states that settle around zero score differential. Combined with a strong evaluation function and the thorough tree exploration of Unbounded Minimax, the method reliably converges to balanced play without any explicit opponent model or parameter tuning.

## Foundational Learning

- **Unbounded Minimax (UM)**: The base search algorithm that expands the game tree until a leaf is evaluated. *Why needed*: All Minibal variants extend UM; understanding its expansion and backup is essential. *Quick check*: Verify that UM with a strong evaluation consistently defeats a weak one in your test setup.  
- **Completion value \(c(s)\) and resolution flag \(r(s)\)**: \(c\) encodes the definitive game outcome ( win/draw/loss ) from a state; \(r\) indicates whether the outcome is resolved (i.e., no ambiguous draws). *Why needed*: They drive the lexicographic ordering and ensure sound propagation. *Quick check*: In terminal states, \(c\) should be derived from \(f_t(s)\)’s sign; during backup, a node becomes resolved if any child is resolved and leads to a win/draw, or if all children share the same resolved outcome.  
- **Evaluation functions \(f_\theta(s)\)**: Neural networks trained via self‑play to predict game outcomes on a \([-1,1]\) scale. *Why needed*: Provide value estimates for non‑terminal leaves. *Quick check*: Confirm that \(f_\theta\) outputs lie within \([-1,1]\) and correlate with ground‑truth outcomes in a sample of positions.  
- **Balancing objective**: The goal of minimizing absolute or positive outcome to keep the score near zero. *Why needed*: It defines the modified child‑selection rules. *Quick check*: On a hand‑crafted small tree (e.g., depth‑2), simulate Minibal_+ and ensure it prefers the smallest positive or largest negative value appropriately.  
- **Transposition tables**: A hash table storing previously computed node statistics. *Why needed*: Crucial for efficiency in deep searches. *Quick check*: Ensure that the same state reached via different paths yields identical backed‑up values and visit counts.  
- **Search time budget**: The maximum CPU seconds allowed per move. *Why needed*: Empirically influences the quality of balance; longer budgets increase alignment with the target. *Quick check*: Plot binary gain vs. search time for Minibal_+ and observe convergence toward zero.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Evaluation function (fθ) -> Search (Minibal_+/UMc) -> Game environment  
                     \-> Transposition table  
The search uses the evaluation function to value leaf nodes and consults the terminal evaluation \(f_t(s)\) at game end. The balanced agent runs Minibal_+; the opponent runs standard UMc. The environment generates legal moves and updates the state.

**Critical path**  
1. The balanced agent calls `search(time_budget)`.  
2. The search repeatedly selects nodes (tree traversal), expands them using `fθ`, backs up `c`, `v`, and `r` values according to Algorithm 3, and updates the transposition table.  
3. Once the budget expires, `best_action()` selects the child of the root that satisfies the Minibal_+ ordering.  
4. The chosen move is applied to the environment.  
5. The opponent (UMc) similarly selects a move. Steps 2‑4 repeat until the game terminates.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Lexicographic vs. continuous optimization*: Using discrete \(c\) values ensures critical outcomes (wins/losses) are never compromised, but may cause abrupt shifts in strategy.  
- *Evaluation strength*: A weak or mis‑calibrated \(f_\theta\) can mislead the balancing objective; high‑quality self‑play training is essential.  
- *Search time*: More time yields better alignment but with diminishing returns and higher computational cost.  

**Failure signatures**  
- Win rate of Minibal_+ deviates far from ~21% (e.g., >30% or <10%), indicating the \(c\) precedence is broken.  
- Average binary gain remains high (>10%) even at long search times, suggesting the child‑selection rule is not implemented correctly.  
- Large performance differences between games could point to incorrect terminal evaluation \(f_t\) implementations.  

**First experiments**  
1. **Sanity check on a trivial game**: Implement Minibal_+ for Tic‑Tac‑Toe with a handcrafted evaluation that returns 0 for non‑terminal positions. Verify that the agent always draws, regardless of opponent.  
2. **Lexicographic ordering test**: Construct a minimal tree where two children have \(c=0\) but different \(v\) signs (e.g., +0.1 and -0.2). Confirm that Minibal_+ selects the positive child if any positive exists; otherwise the largest negative.  
3. **Strength separation validation**: Using a UM baseline with 1 s search, run a round‑robin among all 40 evaluation functions per game. Compute win rates of the top‑5 “high‑level” vs. the bottom‑5 “low‑level”; expect >80% win rate for the strong group.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** What causes the unexpectedly high loss rate observed for the Minibal_n algorithm, and can its decision‑making process be adjusted to reduce this while maintaining its narrow‑margin loss characteristic?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in Section 6 (Future Work) that "it is crucial to investigate the causes of the high loss rate observed for Minibal_n" and hypothesize it "too frequently selects marginally losing states," providing opportunities for weaker opponents to win.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper only presents the empirical outcome (very high loss rate) without a diagnostic analysis of Minibal_n’s search tree choices or a comparative study against Minibal_+ to pinpoint the specific strategic flaws.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed ablation or case‑study analysis comparing action selections and game trajectories of Minibal_n versus Minibal_+ across multiple games, specifically identifying states where Minibal_n chooses actions leading to unavoidable losses versus those leading to narrow losses. Statistical correlation between selection of certain value thresholds and match outcomes would test the hypothesis.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How can the Minibal framework be effectively extended to imperfect‑information and stochastic games, and does the core principle of balancing by minimizing absolute/positive outcome remain valid in such domains?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note in Section 1 that algorithms "should generalize with minimal modification to other classes of adversarial games with complete information, including stochastic [and] simultaneous‑action games," and reiterate this in Section 6 as a future direction.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper exclusively evaluates perfect‑information, deterministic games. The interaction between hidden information, chance events, and the balancing objective (which relies on predictable outcome minimization) is not explored.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implementations of Minibal_+ (and possibly a rethought Minibal_n) in games like Poker (imperfect information) or Backgammon (stochastic), using appropriate belief‑state or expectation‑based evaluations. Metrics would compare balance success against strong/weak opponents in these domains against standard maximizing search.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What properties should a terminal state evaluation function have to optimally support balanced play, and does repurposing a winning‑oriented evaluation function (trained via self‑play) introduce a fundamental limitation to achieving perfect balance?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper uses evaluation functions trained for maximal winning (Section 4.2, 7.4). Section 7.4 explicitly discusses the "Balanced Learning Problem": the state distribution during self‑play (vs. optimal opponents) differs from that when facing weaker opponents, potentially causing unreliable evaluations for balancing.  
**Why unresolved:** The authors acknowledge this mismatch but do not experiment with alternative training objectives (e.g., training to achieve specific score margins) or analyze how the shape of the value function impacts balance.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Train separate evaluation functions: one standard (maximize win probability), one explicitly for balance (e.g., minimize absolute score difference against a target opponent distribution). Compare Minibal_+ performance using each function against a range of opponent strengths. Analysis of value‑calibration errors in states commonly encountered against weak opponents would clarify the limitation.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Does Minibal_+ produce matches that are perceived as more engaging, educational, or fair by human players compared to a standard strong AI, and can it reliably adapt to an individual human’s skill level over multiple games without additional tuning?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in Section 6 that "large‑scale evaluations involving human players are needed to measure… the effects on player engagement, learning outcomes, and long‑term retention." They argue theoretically that balanced play should improve engagement but provide no human‑subject data.  
**Why unresolved:** All experiments are against other AI agents with known strengths. Human perception of challenge, fairness, and learning potential (including reactions to "artificial" losses/win margins) is qualitatively different and unmeasured.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled user studies where human players of varying skill levels play repeated matches against Minibal_+, Unbounded Minimax, and perhaps a tuned AlphaDDA. Measurements include self‑reported engagement/fun, perceived difficulty, win/loss margin satisfaction, and skill improvement over sessions.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** Can the search‑time parameter of Minibal_+ serve as a reliable, single knob to achieve perfect balance (zero average gain) against opponents of widely varying strengths, and what is the theoretical relationship between opponent strength, required search time, and achievable balance?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5.1 notes that increasing search time for Minibal_+ against a very weak MCTS opponent eventually yields zero average binary gain, and that this tunability is a key advantage over Unbounded Minimax. It suggests "selecting an appropriate search time is sufficient to achieve a zero average binary gain."  
**Why unresolved:** This observation is empirical for one weak opponent (MCTS). It’s unclear if a monotonic, predictable relationship exists across a spectrum of opponent strengths and games, or if the required search time scales prohibitively. The paper does not model this relationship or test it across multiple opponent skill levels.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments varying both the Minibal_+ search time and the opponent’s search time/strength across multiple games. Fit a model (e.g., nonlinear) predicting the search time needed for zero gain as a function of opponent strength. Test if the relationship holds for intermediate strengths (not just extreme gaps) and if it is stable within a game or game‑dependent.

## Limitations

- **Major Limitation 1:** Exact training details for the 40 evaluation functions per game (network architecture, self‑play duration, Descent Minimax parameters) are unspecified, making precise replication of the strength gap infeasible.  
- **Major Limitation 2:** Algorithm 3 (completion value \(c(s)\) and resolution \(r(s)\) propagation) is incompletely defined; the mapping from terminal evaluation \(f_t(s)\) to \(c(s)\) and the exact backup logic remain ambiguous.  
- **Confidence in core claims** is **Medium** due to these reproducibility gaps.
