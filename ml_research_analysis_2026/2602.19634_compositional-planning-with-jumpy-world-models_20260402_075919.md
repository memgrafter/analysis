# Compositional Planning with Jumpy World Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.19634  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19634  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Compositional planning with jumpy world models yields an average 200% relative improvement over primitive‑action planning on long‑horizon tasks, with dramatic success rate increases (e.g., 10% → 90%) on complex benchmarks like *antmaze‑giant* and *cube‑4*.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses compositional planning in reinforcement learning, where the goal is to combine pre‑trained policies as temporally extended actions to solve complex, long‑horizon tasks. The core challenge is compounding errors in long‑horizon predictions, which make estimating visitation distributions from sequenced policies difficult. The authors introduce *jumpy world models*—multi‑step predictive models that capture state occupancies induced by pre‑trained policies across multiple timescales. Built on Temporal Difference Flows, these models are enhanced with a novel Temporal Difference Horizon Consistency (td‑hc) objective that aligns predictions across timescales, improving long‑horizon predictive accuracy. The method also shows how to combine these generative predictions to estimate the value of arbitrary policy sequences. Evaluations on challenging manipulation and navigation tasks (OGBench) demonstrate an average 200% relative improvement over primitive‑action planning on long‑horizon tasks, with success rates rising dramatically (e.g., from 10% to 90%) on *antmaze‑giant* and *cube‑4*. The approach outperforms state‑of‑the‑art hierarchical baselines, highlighting the effectiveness of jumpy world models for complex decision‑making.

## Method Summary
The method centers on learning a Geometric Horizon Model (GHM) that predicts the *distribution* of states reachable by a given policy over a horizon governed by a discount factor γ. The GHM is a conditional continuous normalizing flow trained using a modified temporal difference loss that includes a consistency term between predictions at different discount factors (td‑hc). This design reduces compounding errors by enforcing coherence across timescales. For planning, candidate sequences of pre‑trained base policies are generated via a proposal distribution, and their values are estimated by sampling successor states from the GHM and aggregating discounted rewards. The planner (Algorithm 2) selects the best sequence, executes its first action, and replans at a chosen frequency.

## Key Results
- ~200% relative improvement over primitive‑action planning on long‑horizon tasks.  
- Dramatic success rate gains on *antmaze‑giant* and *cube‑4* (e.g., 10% → 90%).  
- Outperforms hierarchical baselines (HIQL, SHARSA) and other planning methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of compositional planning with jumpy world models stems from three key mechanisms.  
(1) **Geometric Horizon Models (GHMs)** learn multi‑step successor distributions rather than one‑step transitions. By modeling the entire distribution of states visited over a geometrically distributed horizon using continuous normalizing flows, GHMs avoid the error accumulation that plagues deterministic rollout models.  
(2) **Temporal Difference Horizon Consistency (td‑hc)** enforces coherence between predictions at different discount factors. It decomposes the long‑horizon successor measure into a mixture of a one‑step transition and a consistency term that aligns the γ‑horizon model with a β‑horizon model (β < γ). This cross‑timescale regularization stabilizes learning, especially for large γ.  
(3) **Geometric Switching Policies (GSPs)** provide a principled way to combine pre‑trained policies as temporally extended actions. Theorem 1 shows that the successor measure of a GSP is a mixture of policy‑specific successor measures with weights derived from switching probabilities. This decomposition enables planning over sequences by evaluating candidate GSPs via Monte Carlo estimates using GHM samples, allowing flexible recombination of skills without task‑specific training.

## Foundational Learning
- **Successor measures and temporal‑difference learning**  
  *Why needed here*: The jumpy world model is a generative model of the discounted state occupancy measure, which replaces the usual one‑step dynamics model for long‑horizon planning. Understanding the Bellman equation for successor measures is essential for deriving the td‑hc loss.  
  *Quick check*: How does the successor measure relate to the action‑value function? (Answer: \( Q^\pi(s,a) = (1-\gamma)^{-1} \mathbb{E}_{S^+ \sim m^\pi_\gamma(\cdot|s,a)}[r(S^+)] \) from equation (1).)

- **Flow‑matching generative models**  
  *Why needed here*: Flow matching is used to learn a continuous normalizing flow that maps noise to states according to the successor distribution. This provides a stable training objective without explicit density estimation.  
  *Quick check*: What is the main advantage of flow matching over GANs for modeling successor measures? (Answer: It provides a tractable objective that avoids mode collapse and is derived from conditional flow matching.)

- **Geometric switching policies (GSPs)**  
  *Why needed here*: Planning over sequences of policies requires a formalism for combining policies with variable durations. GSPs define how policies are stitched together with switching probabilities, leading to the decomposition in Theorem 1.  
  *Quick check*: Define the effective discount factor β_k for a policy in a GSP and explain how it differs from the global γ. (Answer: β_k = γ(1‑α_k), because continuing requires both not switching and the episode not terminating.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Offline dataset → Pretrained policies → GHM (flow network v_θ) → Planning module → Execution with replanning.  
- **Critical path**:  
  1. Pretrain base policies.  
  2. Collect offline dataset of transitions.  
  3. Train GHM with td‑hc (Algorithm 1).  
  4. At test time, run CompPlan(s) to select actions and replan.  
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Consistency proportion τ_c (stability vs. long‑horizon accuracy).  
  - Proposal distribution (goal‑conditioned vs. unconditional).  
  - Number of candidate sequences M (quality vs. compute).  
  - Monte‑Carlo samples N (variance vs. cost).  
  - Replanning frequency (reactivity vs. computational burden).  
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - GHM generative accuracy remains high (especially at long horizons).  
  - Planning success does not exceed zero‑shot baseline.  
  - td‑hc loss diverges (τ_c too high).  
  - Proposal samples fall into infeasible regions.  
  - Base policies are narrow, yielding poor GHM occupancies.  
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. Generative accuracy ablation: Compare GHM with/without td‑hc using Earth Mover’s Distance.  
  2. Compositional gain test: Compare CompPlan against zero‑shot and GPI on long‑horizon tasks.  
  3. Replanning frequency sweep: Measure success and planning time for different replan_every values.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **State‑dependent switching probabilities**: Can learning state‑dependent switching probabilities improve compositional planning? Currently fixed α_i are used. Evidence: Learn a state‑conditioned switching distribution and evaluate on OGBench tasks like *antmaze‑giant* and *cube‑4*.  
- **Latent‑space jumpy world models**: Do GHMs in learned latent spaces enhance long‑horizon planning? GHMs currently operate on raw states. Evidence: Train GHMs using autoencoders or latent dynamics and compare planning performance against raw‑space GHMs.  
- **Extremely long horizons**: Does horizon consistency yield larger gains for horizons beyond current benchmarks? The paper observed td‑hc improvements at γ=0.998 but planning used β∈[0.98,0.99]. Evidence: Test on synthetic tasks requiring 1000+ steps, comparing CompPlan with and without consistency.  
- **More sample‑efficient planners**: Can more advanced planners replace random shooting? Random shooting scales poorly with planning depth. Evidence: Integrate MCTS or gradient‑based planners using the jumpy world model and evaluate on complex domains like *cube‑4*.

## Limitations
- Base‑policy dependence: CompPlan’s performance is limited by the quality and diversity of the pre‑trained policies.  
- Offline dataset coverage: GHMs suffer OOD errors when the offline data lacks coverage of relevant state‑action regions.  
- Implementation opacity: Critical hyperparameters (e.g., γ sampling range, network architecture details) are not fully disclosed, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- GHMs reduce compounding errors vs. one‑step models: **High**  
- td‑hc consistency objective improves multi‑horizon accuracy: **Medium**  
- CompPlan yields ~200% relative improvement on long‑horizon tasks: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation over base policy diversity** – Train CompPlan with subsets of the pretrained policy suite to quantify how skill diversity influences success on long‑horizon tasks.  
2. **Robustness to dataset shift** – Artificially inject out‑of‑distribution states into the offline data and measure degradation in GHM accuracy and planning performance to clarify failure modes.  
3. **Compute‑optimal replanning** – Sweep replanning frequencies (every 1, 3, 5, 10 steps) across tasks of varying horizon lengths, reporting both success rates and wall‑clock time to identify practical operating points.
