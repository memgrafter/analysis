# GaelEval: Benchmarking LLM Performance for Scottish Gaelic
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.02135
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02135
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** GaelEval introduces a multi-dimensional benchmark revealing that Gemini 3 Pro Preview achieves 83.3% accuracy on a morphosyntactic MCQA task, significantly surpassing the human fluent-speaker baseline of 78.1%, and proprietary models consistently outperform open-weight systems on all Gaelic tasks.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents GaelEval, the first rigorous benchmark for assessing LLM performance in Scottish Gaelic, addressing a critical gap in evaluation for low-resource languages. While existing benchmarks focus on translation, GaelEval uniquely evaluates structural morphosyntactic competence through an expert-authored multiple-choice question task, a culturally grounded translation benchmark, and a large-scale LLM-generated cultural knowledge Q&A. Evaluation of 19 LLMs against a human baseline (n=30) shows proprietary models leading, with Gemini 3 Pro Preview achieving 83.3% on the linguistic task. Key findings include a small but stable advantage of Gaelic prompting for morphosyntax (+2.4%) but degradation for cultural knowledge (-9.55%), directional asymmetry in translation quality favoring Gaelic-to-English, and inflated scores on the LLM-generated cultural benchmark. The work underscores the need for nuanced, multi-faceted evaluation of LLM capabilities in minority languages.

## Method Summary
The benchmark comprises three tasks: (i) an expert-authored morphosyntactic MCQA test with 120 items covering 16 grammatical categories; (ii) a translation benchmark using 908 parallel Gaelic-English sentence pairs from the *An Litir Bheag* podcast transcripts, filtered for quality; and (iii) a cultural knowledge Q&A set of 1,087 multiple-choice questions generated by GPT-5.2 from podcast episodes and filtered for answerability. All tasks are evaluated zero-shot, with each model receiving either a Gaelic or English system message to test prompting effects. Metrics include accuracy for MCQA/Q&A and BLEU/chrF for translation. The human baseline involves 30 self-reported fluent speakers completing the MCQA online. Models are accessed via API endpoints (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google AI Studio, Together AI) with JSON-schema enforced outputs; invalid responses are retried.

## Key Results
- Gemini 3 Pro Preview achieves 83.3% accuracy on the morphosyntactic MCQA, significantly beating the human fluent-speaker mean of 78.1% (t-test, p<0.05).
- Proprietary models consistently outperform open-weight systems by 20‑40 percentage points across all tasks; open-weight models struggle particularly with English→Gaelic translation (e.g., GPT OSS 20B: 0.00 BLEU).
- Gaelic-language prompting yields a small advantage (+2.4%) for morphosyntactic accuracy but degrades cultural Q&A performance by 9.55 percentage points; translation shows consistent directional asymmetry with Gaelic→English outperforming English→Gaelic.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Prompt Language Advantage Differential
- **Claim:** In-language (Gaelic) prompting provides a small but stable advantage for morphosyntactic MCQA but degrades performance on cultural Q&A.
- **Mechanism:** Prompting in the target language activates language-specific internal representations and reduces cross‑lingual interference for structured grammatical tasks. For culturally grounded knowledge, which may be encoded through English‑dominant pathways, Gaelic prompting forces retrieval from a weaker, less‑exposed latent space.
- **Core assumption:** LLM representations for low‑resource languages are less robust and more susceptible to prompt‑language interference, with competence being task‑dependent.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the advantage (+2.4%) and degradation (-9.55%). Table 4 shows higher mean accuracy under Gaelic prompting for MCQA (46.8% vs 44.4%), while Table 7 shows lower for cultural Q&A (63.75% vs 73.30%). Neighbor papers on cross‑lingual retrieval support the mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If Gaelic‑prompted performance equals or exceeds English‑prompted performance across *both* tasks, the mechanism of differential representation strength would be falsified.

### Mechanism 2: Proprietary Model Superiority
- **Claim:** Proprietary models consistently outperform open‑weight systems on all Gaelic tasks.
- **Mechanism:** Proprietary models leverage larger, more diverse, and higher‑quality pre‑training corpora that include better coverage of low‑resource languages, potentially augmented by dedicated post‑training (e.g., supervised fine‑tuning, RLHF) and data filtering pipelines.
- **Core assumption:** The performance gap stems primarily from differences in training data scale/composition and refinement processes, not just model architecture or scale alone.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes consistent outperformance; Table 4 shows top proprietary models (Gemini, GPT‑5) 20‑40 points above best open‑weight (DeepSeek R1, Llama 4 Maverick). Authors suggest “differences in training data scale and composition” and cite Google’s long‑standing Gaelic support in Google Translate.
- **Break condition:** If open‑weight models, when fine‑tuned on an equivalent high‑quality Gaelic dataset, close the performance gap to proprietary models, the primary cause would be data/refinement rather than inherent architectural limits.

### Mechanism 3: Translation Directional Asymmetry
- **Claim:** Translation metrics reveal directional asymmetry, with models performing better on Gaelic‑to‑English than English‑to‑Gaelic generation.
- **Mechanism:** Generating fluent, morphologically correct output in a low‑resource, morphologically rich language (en→gd) requires accurate production of complex inflectional paradigms and culturally appropriate lexical choices, which is a higher generative burden than mapping from low‑resource to high‑resource (gd→en), where comprehension can rely more on transfer from dominant English representations.
- **Core assumption:** The asymmetry reflects an imbalance in representational capacity: models have stronger decoding pathways for high‑resource languages and weaker generation mechanisms for low‑resource, morphologically complex ones.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the asymmetry; Table 6 shows consistent BLEU/chrF drops for en→gd vs gd→en across models (e.g., Gemini 3 Pro Preview: 65.56→73.41 BLEU; DeepSeek R1: 62.12→75.28). GPT OSS 20B fails entirely at en→gd (0.00 BLEU). Authors state: “English to Gaelic MT demands active production of morphologically complex and culturally‑grounded forms.”
- **Break condition:** If a model achieves equivalent (or better) BLEU/chrF on en→gd compared to gd→en across multiple low‑resource, morphologically rich languages, the proposed generative burden mechanism would be challenged.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Morphosyntactic richness and its evaluation. Understanding what “morphosyntactic richness” entails for Gaelic (e.g., initial consonant mutation, complex prepositional case systems) clarifies why surface‑level translation is insufficient and why GaelEval’s deep grammatical tasks are necessary.
  - **Quick check question:** How does the Gaelic definite article (*an*, *am*, *an t-*, *na*) interact with noun gender and case in a way that a direct word‑for‑word translation from English would likely fail to capture?
- **Concept:** “Shadow capabilities” in low‑resource languages. This concept explains why LLMs appear competent in Gaelic without official support, framing the need for benchmarks like GaelEval to measure actual abilities.
  - **Quick check question:** What is a potential risk of assuming an LLM is competent in a low‑resource language like Gaelic solely because it can generate seemingly fluent text, without a rigorous benchmark?
- **Concept:** Synthetic benchmark validity and inflation. The trade‑off between scalability (using LLMs to build benchmarks) and validity (risk of self‑preference, easier items) is critical for interpreting the inflated cultural Q&A scores.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might an LLM‑generated multiple‑choice question about Gaelic culture be easier for another LLM to answer than the same question written by a human educator?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Source transcripts (An Litir Bheag) → Language ID filtering (OpenLID) → Alignment & quality filtering (BLEU‑based) → Episode metadata tagging → (parallel) Cultural Q&A generation (GPT‑5.2) → Answerability scoring → Distractor addition → Final dataset. For MCQA: Expert question design → Human pilot testing → Final MCQ set. For translation: Filtered parallel pairs → Model inference → BLEU/chrF calculation. Human baseline: Qualtrics survey deployment → Response collection → Scoring.

**Critical path:** `Transcript Collection → Filtering → Cultural Q&A Generation (GPT‑5.2) → Answerability Filtering → Distractor Addition → Final Dataset`.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- LLM‑generated vs. human‑curated benchmarks: Scalability and cost vs. validity and cultural authenticity; authors accept inflation to evaluate where no data exists.
- MCQA length vs. breadth: 120 questions cover 16 categories but are unbalanced (e.g., 17 nominal morphology vs. 3 colours), trading participant/model burden for category representation.
- Prompt language control: Testing both Gaelic and English prompts doubles inference cost but reveals representational asymmetries.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Translation:** Extremely low or zero BLEU/chrF for en→gd but normal gd→en indicates generative failure for low-resource output (e.g., GPT OSS 20B).
- **MCQA:** Near‑chance accuracy (<30%) on structural categories (determiners, relative clauses) suggests fundamental lack of morphosyntactic knowledge.
- **Cultural Q&A:** >90% accuracy on LLM‑generated task vs. lower MCQA scores may indicate inflation; manual verification of a subset is essential.
- **Prompting:** Degraded performance under Gaelic prompting for cultural Q&A but not MCQA signals task‑specific representation weaknesses.
- **JSON Formatting:** Failure to produce valid JSON (e.g., GLM 4.7 on translation) indicates prompt/context length or language handling issues.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Prompt Sensitivity Replication:** Re‑run the MCQA and cultural Q&A tasks with varied prompt phrasing (beyond the two system messages used) to measure robustness of the Gaelic/English prompting effect.
2. **Category Ablation:** Remove the top 20% most “English‑like” or formulaic MCQA items (e.g., simple determiners) and re‑evaluate to see if the human‑model gap widens, testing reliance on prescriptive vs. usage‑based knowledge.
3. **Synthetic vs. Manual Benchmark Comparison:** Administer a small, manually created set of cultural Q&A items (from a Gaelic expert) alongside the LLM‑generated set to the same models to quantify the inflation effect and rank correlation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Human Baseline Generalizability:** How can GaelEval’s human baseline be expanded beyond the current small, non‑representative sample to improve generalizability? The current sample (n=30) is small, recruited via convenience sampling, and may not reflect the diversity of Gaelic speakers (e.g., dialects, age, proficiency).
2. **Grammatical Category Balance:** What methodological adjustments are necessary to balance the grammatical category distribution in the linguistic MCQA task? The current MCQA is unbalanced (e.g., nominal morphology: 17 items; colours: 3 items), which may skew category‑level comparisons and overall reliability.
3. **Sociolinguistic Appropriateness Integration:** How should sociolinguistic appropriateness and pragmatic competence be integrated into Gaelic LLM evaluation? GaelEval focuses on grammatical accuracy and factual knowledge, but not on dialect variation, register, or contextual appropriateness—key aspects of real‑world language use.
4. **Predictive Validity for Real‑World Use:** To what extent do morphosyntactic MCQA scores predict models’ actual competency in authentic, interactive Gaelic usage (e.g., conversation, narrative)? The MCQA uses isolated, decontextualized sentences; real‑world Gaelic use involves longer discourse, idiomaticity, and turn‑taking dynamics.
5. **Synthetic Benchmark Bias:** Does the use of GPT‑5.2 for generating and filtering the cultural Q&A benchmark introduce self‑preference bias, and how can synthetic benchmarks be made more reliable? GPT‑5.2 created the questions, scored answerability, and generated distractors; it was then tested on its own output, potentially favoring its knowledge distribution.

## Limitations
- The full GaelEval dataset is not public, preventing direct replication; exact items and filtering details are withheld.
- Human baseline sample (n=30) is small, with sparse documentation of recruitment and screening, risking selection bias and limited generalizability.
- Cultural Q&A shows >90% accuracy, indicating strong inflation from LLM‑generation self‑preference or synthetic overfitting.
- GPT‑5.2’s architecture/training details are opaque, hindering reproducibility of the generation pipeline.
- MCQA categories are unbalanced (e.g., 17 nominal morphology vs. 3 colour items), potentially skewing per‑category analysis.

## Confidence
- **Prompting differential (Mech1): Medium.** Two‑task evidence (MCQA +2.4%, Q&A -9.55%) aligns with theory, but single prompt phrasing per language leaves generalizability unverified.
- **Proprietary gap (Mech2): Medium.** Consistent 20‑40 point gaps are striking, but causal isolation (data vs. architecture/alignment) is absent; fine‑tuning equivalence remains speculative.
- **Translation asymmetry (Mech3): High.** Pervasive, large en→gd BLEU/chrF drops (e.g., DeepSeek R1: 75.28→62.12 BLEU) strongly support generative‑burden mechanism; cross‑lingual break condition is a future test but current data is robust.

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt robustness test:** Deploy 3–5 diverse Gaelic/English system messages (varying formality, example inclusion) and multiple Gaelic dialects; re‑evaluate MCQA and cultural Q&A to measure stability of the +2.4% and -9.55% effects.
2. **Data‑centric gap closure:** Fine‑tune a leading open‑weight model (e.g., Llama 4 Maverick) on a large, high‑quality Gaelic corpus; re‑run all GaelEval tasks. A narrowed gap would implicate training data scale/composition as primary cause.
3. **Inflation audit:** Recruit 3–5 Gaelic cultural experts to manually rate 200 randomly sampled cultural Q&A items for answerability and authenticity; recompute model scores on this expert‑validated subset to quantify inflation magnitude and adjust rankings.
