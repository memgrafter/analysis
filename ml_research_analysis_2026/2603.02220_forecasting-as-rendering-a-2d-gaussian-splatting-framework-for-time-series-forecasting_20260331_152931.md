# Forecasting as Rendering: A 2D Gaussian Splatting Framework for Time Series Forecasting
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.02220
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02220
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** TimeGS achieves state-of-the-art forecasting across seven benchmark datasets, substantially lowering MSE and MAE versus transformer, MLP, and CNN baselines; removing the Multi-Basis Gaussian Kernel Generation mechanism causes catastrophic performance degradation.

## Executive Summary
TimeGS addresses a fundamental limitation in period-based time series forecasting: the topological mismatch and inefficient uniform representation that arises when reshaping 1D sequences into 2D period-phase grids. The method reformulates forecasting as a 2D generative rendering problem, using Gaussian splatting to synthesize future sequences. It introduces three core components: a Multi-Period Chronologically Continuous Rasterization that enforces temporal continuity across grid boundaries; a Multi-Basis Gaussian Kernel Generation that stabilizes geometric learning via dictionary-based synthesis; and a Channel-Adaptive Aggregation that fuses predictions from multiple period-specific branches. This architecture yields consistent state-of-the-art performance on seven datasets (Weather, Electricity, Traffic, ETTh1/2, ETTm1/2), demonstrating the efficacy of its generative, continuity-preserving approach.

## Method Summary
TimeGS begins by reshaping the historical 1D sequence into a 2D period-phase grid and upsampling to a fixed resolution. A U-Net encoder (2D-VFE) extracts coupled intraperiod and interperiod features from this grid. These features are decoded not into direct point forecasts, but into parameters for a set of 2D anisotropic Gaussian kernels. The kernels are synthesized via a Multi-Basis Gaussian Kernel Generation (MB-GKG) module, which predicts mixing weights over a fixed dictionary of pre-defined Gaussian basis shapes, converting an unstable direct regression into a stable sparse coding problem. Each kernel is then rasterized onto a 2D canvas, with a Multi-Period Chronologically Continuous Rasterization (MP-CCR) process padding, flattening, and shifting the output to ensure strict temporal continuity across period boundaries. Multiple parallel branches operate at different period scales. Finally, a Channel-Adaptive Aggregation (CCA) fuses the rendered outputs from all branches and components per channel, producing the final forecast.

## Key Results
- Achieves state-of-the-art performance on all seven benchmark datasets (Weather, Electricity, Traffic, ETTh1/2, ETTm1/2), consistently lowering MSE and MAE against transformer, MLP, and CNN baselines.
- On the Electricity dataset, reaches an MSE of 0.181 versus the best competitor's 0.182 for a horizon of 720.
- Ablation studies confirm the essential contribution of each component; notably, removing the Multi-Basis mechanism leads to catastrophic degradation (e.g., MSE jumps from 0.181 to 0.877 on Electricity).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Chronological Continuity via Continuous Rasterization
**Claim:** The Multi-Period Chronologically Continuous Rasterization (MP-CCR) resolves the topological mismatch between 2D grids and 1D sequences by treating Gaussian influence as a continuous temporal overlay that wraps around periodic boundaries, eliminating artificial discontinuities.
**Mechanism:** Standard 2D rasterization treats adjacent grid cells at row boundaries as spatially distant. MP-CCR instead defines rendering on a theoretically infinite 2D plane where each kernel's influence is computed based on 2D *temporal* distance. Practically, kernels are padded to the period width, flattened to 1D, and circularly shifted onto the output canvas so that their centers align with the correct temporal coordinates, ensuring adjacent time steps receive continuous influence.
**Core Assumption:** The primary source of forecasting error from naive 2D reshaping is the violation of chronological adjacency at row boundaries, and this can be corrected by redefining the rasterization geometry to respect temporal rather than grid topology.
**Evidence Anchors:** Per the abstract and Section 4.4, which details the "chronologically discontinuity problem" and the theoretical infinite-plane formulation with practical padding/flattening/shift implementation.
**Break Condition:** If a kernel's effective support (width after padding) is smaller than the temporal distance between the last point of one row and the first point of the next row, continuity will be broken, leading to residual boundary artifacts.

### Mechanism 2: Stabilized Geometric Learning via Basis Dictionary
**Claim:** The Multi-Basis Gaussian Kernel Generation (MB-GKG) prevents training collapse by replacing unstable direct regression of Cholesky decomposition parameters with a stable dictionary learning task—predicting sparse weights over a fixed basis bank of pre-computed Gaussian profiles.
**Mechanism:** Instead of optimizing free-floating `L` matrices (Cholesky factors), a fixed dictionary `D={B₁...Bₘ}` of valid, normalized Gaussian basis kernels is created. The network predicts (a) a `P`-component intensity `V` and (b) mixing weights `W` (via Softmax) over the `M` bases for each of the `G` anchor points. The composite kernel is a weighted sum: `Φ = V * Σ Wₘ * Bₘ`.
**Core Assumption:** Real-world time series variations, while diverse, can be effectively decomposed into a linear combination of a limited set of geometric templates (basis Gaussians). This structure is stable to optimize via weight prediction.
**Evidence Anchors:** The abstract and Section 4.3 explicitly state that directly regressing geometric parameters is highly unstable and formulate the problem as "stable dictionary learning and sparse coding." The catastrophic ablation result (MSE 0.877 → 0.181 on Electricity) is the key empirical evidence.
**Break Condition:** If the fixed basis set `D` lacks diversity to express critical local variation patterns (e.g., extremely sharp spikes or flat plateaus), the model will incur irreducible error regardless of weight prediction accuracy.

### Mechanism 3: Generative Rendering for Coupled Variation Modeling
**Claim:** Reformulating forecasting as a 2D generative rendering problem—constructing the future sequence by rasterizing learned Gaussian kernels—explicitly models the coupled intraperiod and interperiod variations more effectively than point-wise regression heads, by preserving structural continuity and allowing anisotropic, adaptive representation.
**Mechanism:** The encoder extracts 2D coupled features. These are decoded not into direct sequence points, but into parameters (`Φ`) for Gaussian primitives placed on a 2D period-phase grid. The final prediction is the sum of all kernel contributions at each temporal coordinate, naturally blending local (intraperiod) and global (interperiod) influences through overlapping Gaussians aligned with the grid.
**Core Assumption:** The entangled structure of time series (periodic fluctuations within evolving trends) is better captured by a generative model that constructs the signal from overlapping local primitives than by a direct mapping from a global feature vector to each future point.
**Evidence Anchors:** The abstract and Section 4.1 frame the approach as shifting from regression to generative rendering. Ablations in Table 4 show degraded performance when replacing rendering with Linear/MLP heads. Corpus literature on 2D Gaussian splatting for high-fidelity image reconstruction supports its ability to model continuous surfaces.
**Break Condition:** If the optimal forecast cannot be represented as a superposition of Gaussians with the imposed `G` anchor points and `P` components per anchor (e.g., requires non-local, discontinuous structures), the rendering approach will be fundamentally limited.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Period-Phase 2D Reshaping
**Why needed here:** This is the fundamental data transformation that exposes intraperiod (within a row) and interperiod (across rows) variations. It converts a 1D sequential problem into a 2D spatial one, enabling the use of 2D operators.
**Quick Check:** If a time series with perfect period `T` is reshaped into an `N x T` grid, what is the temporal relationship between element `(i, T-1)` and element `(i+1, 0)`? (Answer: They are consecutive time steps `t` and `t+1`.)

### Concept: 2D Gaussian Parameterization (Cholesky)
**Why needed here:** The core representation unit is an anisotropic 2D Gaussian. Its shape is defined by a covariance matrix `Σ`, which must be positive semi-definite. The paper uses Cholesky decomposition (`Σ = L Lᵀ`) to parameterize `L` as a lower-triangular matrix, ensuring valid shapes during learning.
**Quick Check:** What two geometric properties (e.g., scale, rotation) does the Cholesky factor `L` control, and why is direct optimization of `Σ` problematic?

### Concept: Dictionary Learning for Stabilization
**Why needed here:** The MB-GKG's key innovation is converting an unstable geometric regression into a stable weight selection problem. This relies on the concept of representing signals via sparse combinations of atoms from a fixed, pre-defined dictionary.
**Quick Check:** If you directly regress `L` for a Gaussian and it becomes invalid (non-PSD), what goes wrong in the rendering? How does predicting weights over a *fixed*, valid basis avoid this?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Input (1D sequence) -> Reshape & Upsample to 2D grid (N', T', 1) -> 2D-VFE (UNet Encoder) -> Feature Map F (N' x T' x D_c) -> Flatten & Project to Z (G x D_h) -> MB-GKG (Predict V, W) -> Composite Kernels Φ -> MP-CCR (Rasterize, Pad, Flatten, Shift) -> Branch Output Ŷ_k (P x O) -> Channel-Adaptive Aggregation (CCA) -> Final Output Ŷ (O).
**Critical Path:** The learning signal flows from the encoder features (`Z`) through the MB-GKG (`V`, `W`) to define kernel geometry (`Φ`), which is then transformed by MP-CCR into the final prediction (`Ŷ`). A failure in MB-GKG (e.g., bad basis) or MP-CCR (e.g., incorrect shift) propagates directly to the prediction.
**Design Tradeoffs:**
- Fixed Basis Bank (MB-GKG): Stabilizes training but limits expressiveness to the `M` pre-defined shapes; choosing `M` too small loses variation, too large increases computation.
- MP-CCR Wrap-around: Enforces continuity but assumes the forecast horizon `O` aligns with the period `ψ_k`. Misaligned `ψ_k` could cause phase errors.
- Multi-Branch (`K`): Captures multiple periods but increases parameters and requires CCA to fuse. More branches improve long-horizon performance with diminishing returns.
- Anisotropic Gaussians: More powerful than isotropic circles but doubles the parameter count per kernel.
**Failure Signatures:**
1. Catastrophic MSE increase (~5x) on Electricity when MB-GKG is ablated (Table 3). This is the prime signature of geometric instability.
2. Oscillatory or spiky forecasts in visualization (Figures 8-11) if MP-CCR is broken—boundary artifacts manifest as unrealistic jumps at period intervals.
3. Near-identical features from different branches in t-SNE (Figure 6) would indicate the multi-branch design is not learning diverse views, making CCA less effective.
4. High variance across runs (Table 7) suggests sensitivity to initialization, possibly from an inadequately sized basis bank or poor learning rate.
**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **MP-CCR Ablation:** Replace MP-CCR with naive rasterization (no wrapping/padding). Expect visible periodic boundary artifacts in 1D prediction plots (jumps every `T` steps) and degraded MSE on periodic datasets (Weather, Electricity).
2. **Basis Bank Inspection:** Visualize the `M` fixed Gaussian basis kernels `Bₘ`. Verify they cover a range of anisotropies (elongated horizontally vs. vertically) and scales. If all are near-circular and isotropic, the model cannot represent directional trends.
3. **Gradient Flow Check:** During early training, monitor gradients flowing from the final loss back to the linear layer in MB-GKG that predicts `W` (weights) vs. to a hypothetical direct `L` regressor. Expect smoother, bounded gradients for `W` (probabilities) versus exploding/vanishing for `L` entries.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does the choice of period length T impact TimeGS performance, and can periods be learned automatically?
2. Does the fixed Gaussian basis bank limit expressiveness, and would a learnable basis improve results?
3. How to optimally select the set of periods ψ for the multi-branch architecture, and can this selection be automated?
4. Does the channel-independent strategy hinder modeling cross-variable dependencies, and would cross-channel mixing enhance performance?

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility:** Exact UNet architecture, Gaussian basis bank details (size M, covariance sampling), fixed resolutions (N', T'), anchor count G, rasterization grid (h,w), and numbers of branches K and components P are not fully specified.
- **Representation Assumption:** The fixed Gaussian dictionary might be inadequate for signals with sharp discontinuities or non-smooth patterns, risking fundamental limits.
- **MP-CCR Sensitivity:** Correct period T alignment is crucial; mis-specification could introduce phase errors despite the wrap-around design.

## Confidence
- **MP-CCR:** Medium – Supported by theoretical derivation and likely ablation, but break condition (kernel support < temporal distance) may limit effectiveness.
- **MB-GKG:** High – Catastrophic ablation (MSE 0.877 → 0.181) confirms its essential stabilizing role.
- **Generative Rendering:** Medium – SOTA results and head-replacement ablations are positive, yet unproven for arbitrary time series structures.

## Next Checks
1. Conduct an MP-CCR ablation by replacing it with naive rasterization; quantify boundary artifacts and performance degradation on high-periodicity datasets (Weather, Electricity).
2. Visualize all M basis Gaussians and compute anisotropy metrics; verify diversity; an isotropic-only bank would constrain modeling capacity.
3. Train on synthetic data with known non-Gaussian ground truth (e.g., square waves) and compare degradation against a direct MLP baseline; a larger drop would validate the break condition for the superposition assumption.
