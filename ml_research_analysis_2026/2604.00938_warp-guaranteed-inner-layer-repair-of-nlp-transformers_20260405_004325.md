# WARP: Guaranteed Inner-Layer Repair of NLP Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00938  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00938  
- **Reference count:** 31  
- **Primary result:** WARP achieves provable inner-layer repair of Transformer NLP models with 100% repair and remain accuracy and improves attack generalization by up to 12.3 percentage points over gradient-based baselines.

## Executive Summary
WARP addresses the trade-off between verifiability and scalability in repairing adversarial vulnerabilities of Transformer NLP models. Its two‑stage approach first maximizes the sensitivity of the logit gap via a lightweight fine‑tuning of the repair layer and classification head, then solves a sequence of convex quadratic programs to adjust the layer’s weights in a low‑rank subspace while enforcing margin constraints on a repair set and a remain set. This yields per‑sample robustness certificates and, as shown on DistilBERT and BERT for SST‑2 and RTE, attains perfect repair and remain accuracy alongside significantly better attack generalization compared to gradient‑based methods. The method extends guaranteed repair to inner layers of large‑scale Transformers for the first time.

## Method Summary
WARP operates on a frozen Transformer backbone and targets the dense layer immediately before the classification head. It first applies Gap Sensitivity Norm fine‑tuning (GSN‑FT) on a small auxiliary set (8 examples) to adjust the repair layer and head so that small weight updates have a large influence on the logit gap. The second stage solves an iterative convex quadratic program: in each iteration, the current weight matrix is low‑rank decomposed (rank = 2), linearized gap coefficients for every repair and remain sample are computed, and a QP updates the coefficient vector β subject to margin constraints (repair margin γs = 1.0 with slack, remain margin γh = 0.3 hard). The weight is updated via the low‑rank basis and the process repeats until all repair samples meet γs (max 300 iterations). Finally, a Lipschitz‑based certificate assigns a certified radius ε* to each repaired sample. Default hyperparameters (ρ=2, λ=50) and solver (OSQP) are used.

## Key Results
- 100% repair accuracy and 100% remain accuracy across all evaluated configurations (DistilBERT/BERT, SST‑2/RTE).  
- Attack generalization on unseen adversarial examples (AdvGLUE) improves by up to 12.3 percentage points compared to gradient‑based baselines (LoRA, Full FT).  
- Provides sound per‑sample robustness certificates; certified radii vary by architecture but are verifiable in practice.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** The convex quadratic program (QP) formulation ensures that the repair problem is tractable and that the solution satisfies per‑sample margin constraints, yielding 100% repair and remain accuracy under the optimization.  
**Mechanism:** WARP linearizes the logit gap around the current weight using a first‑order Taylor expansion and parameterizes the weight update within the top‑r singular subspace. This converts the nonlinear margin constraints into linear inequalities in the decision variable β (vectorized low‑rank coefficients). Adding a quadratic regularizer (ρ/2‖β‖²) and slack penalties (λξ) yields a convex QP. The QP’s global optimum (if feasible) either satisfies all margin constraints exactly or with minimal slack, ensuring that after convergence the true gaps (recomputed exactly) meet the thresholds.  
**Core assumption:** The first‑order linearization remains accurate over the magnitude of the weight update found by the QP – higher‑order terms are negligible.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “formulates repair as a convex quadratic program derived from a first‑order linearization of the logit gap.”  
- [section 3.5.2]: Detailed QP formulation with constraints (C1) and (C2).  
- [corpus]: Related provable repair methods often target only final layers; extending the QP approach to inner layers of large Transformers is novel.  
**Break condition:** If the required weight change is too large, linearization error may cause the QP solution to violate the true nonlinear constraints in a single step. Iterative re‑linearization mitigates this, but if the feasible set becomes empty (e.g., due to conflicting constraints), the QP may be infeasible and repair fails.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** The Gap Sensitivity Norm (GSN) and its preprocessing step (GSN‑FT) ensure QP feasibility by moving the model’s weights to a region where small updates can significantly influence the logit gap.  
**Mechanism:** GSN measures the Frobenius norm of the directional coefficients that map weight updates to gap changes (Eq. 4). When GSN is near zero, weight updates in the admissible subspace have almost no effect on the gap, making the QP constraints difficult to satisfy. GSN‑FT fine‑tunes only the repair layer and classification head on an auxiliary dataset, selecting the checkpoint that maximizes GSN. This raises the sensitivity, enlarging the feasible region of the QP.  
**Core assumption:** Maximizing GSN during preprocessing leads to a weight configuration where the linearized gap coefficients are sufficiently large to allow the QP to satisfy all constraints without harming the model’s overall performance.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “introduce a sensitivity‑based preprocessing step that conditions the optimization landscape accordingly.”  
- [section 3.4]: Definition of GSN and the GSN‑FT algorithm (Eq. 5).  
- [section 4.4]: Table 6 shows GSN increases after GSN‑FT, while attack generalization remains similar pre‑QP, indicating gains come from QP.  
**Break condition:** If after GSN‑FT the GSN remains too low (or the QP remains infeasible), repair may fail. Additionally, if GSN‑FT significantly alters the model’s behavior on repair or remain samples, careful re‑evaluation is needed.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The iterative repair loop with re‑linearization systematically reduces approximation error and converges to weights that satisfy the exact (nonlinear) margin constraints, ensuring the theoretical guarantees hold in practice.  
**Mechanism:** After each QP solve, the weight matrix is updated and the basis U(t) (top‑r singular vectors) is recomputed. This re‑linearization ensures that the first‑order approximation remains locally accurate around the current iterate. The loop continues until all repair samples achieve the target gap γs. Under mild assumptions, the procedure converges to a solution that meets all constraints exactly when checked on the true network.  
**Core assumption:** The iterative process converges before linearization error accumulates and the feasible set defined by the exact margins is non‑empty. The convergence criterion (all repair gaps ≥ γs) ensures final weights satisfy Theorem 3.1.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “iterative optimization procedure converges to solutions satisfying all repair constraints under mild assumptions.”  
- [section 3.5.3]: Algorithm 1 and convergence discussion; Theorem 3.1 states that upon convergence, gap(v_i,y_i; eW) ≥ γs.  
- [section 4.2]: Empirical results show 100% repair and remain accuracy, confirming convergence.  
**Break condition:** Non‑convergence within the maximum iteration bound T may indicate overly stringent constraints or excessive linearization error. In practice, iteration counts are low, but for very large repair sets or low GSN, convergence may stall.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Convex Quadratic Programming
**Why needed here:** WARP’s core repair step is a convex QP. Understanding QPs (objective, linear constraints, convexity) is essential to grasp why the optimization is tractable and why the solution is globally optimal.  
**Quick check question:** “Why does a convex QP with a positive semidefinite quadratic term and linear constraints guarantee that any local optimum is global?”

### Concept: First‑Order Taylor Approximation
**Why needed here:** The logit gap is a nonlinear function of weights; WARP linearizes it to derive linear constraints. Understanding the accuracy conditions of a first‑order approximation (i.e., when higher‑order terms are negligible) is key to the theoretical guarantees.  
**Quick check question:** “Given a function f(x+Δx) ≈ f(x) + ∇f(x)⊤Δx, what properties of Δx and f ensure the approximation error is bounded by O(‖Δx‖²)?”

### Concept: Lipschitz Continuity and Robustness Certificates
**Why needed here:** The final robustness radius ε* is derived from the Lipschitz constant of the network and the achieved margin. Comprehending Lipschitz continuity and how it bounds the effect of perturbations is necessary to interpret the certificates.  
**Quick check question:** “If a classifier’s margin function is L‑Lipschitz, how does that lead to a certified radius within which the prediction cannot change?”

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Frozen backbone -> GSN‑FT (repair layer & classification head) -> Iterative QP repair (basis computation, coefficient extraction, QP solve, weight update) -> Converged repair layer weights -> Per‑sample robustness certificate (Lipschitz bound)

### Critical path
1. **Repair layer selection:** Choose the dense layer immediately before the classification head (expands solution space beyond C×d_out).  
2. **GSN evaluation:** Compute initial κ; if below threshold, run GSN‑FT to obtain W(0).  
3. **Initialize iteration:** Start from W(0), set t=1.  
4. **QP solve:** Construct coefficient vectors from current weights, solve QP.  
5. **Weight update:** Apply low‑rank update.  
6. **Convergence check:** Evaluate true gaps on repair set; if all ≥ γs, stop; else increment t and repeat.  
7. **Certificate:** Compute Lipschitz constant L and ε*.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Rank r:** Larger r increases expressiveness but raises QP size and may overfit; paper finds r=2 sufficient.  
- **Margins γs, γh:** Larger γs yields larger robustness radii but may require more iterations or become infeasible; γh protects remain set, too high may over‑constrain updates.  
- **Slack penalty λ vs. regularization ρ:** Larger λ prioritizes constraint satisfaction; larger ρ penalizes weight change magnitude. Tuning needed per model.  
- **GSN‑FT auxiliary set:** Held‑out from S and P; size N_aux=8 in experiments. Too small may not maximize κ reliably.  
- **Solver choice:** OSQP used; alternative solvers may have different performance.

### Failure signatures
- **QP infeasibility:** Solver returns infeasible; likely due to too many repair samples, too high margins, or low GSN. Remedy: increase |P|, reduce |S|, adjust margins.  
- **Slow convergence / high iterations:** May indicate poor linearization or too small r; check approximation error (linearized vs. true gap).  
- **Degraded general accuracy:** After repair, remain set accuracy may drop if remain constraints are violated or remain set is too small; verify remain gaps (γh) and increase |P| if needed.  
- **Zero certified radii ε*:** Could happen if achieved gaps are exactly γs and L is very large (common for BERT). This is a limitation of the Lipschitz bound; stress testing can reveal true robustness.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline comparison:** Run WARP and gradient‑based baselines (LoRA, Full FT) on SST‑2/RTE with identical repair/remain sets. Measure repair accuracy, remain accuracy, general accuracy, and attack generalization. Expect WARP to achieve 100% repair/remain and best attack generalization.  
2. **Certificate verification:** For WARP‑repaired models, confirm every repair sample has gap ≥ γs and every remain sample has gap ≥ γh. Compute ε* and perform Monte Carlo stress tests to assess certificate tightness. Expect certificates to be sound (no false positives) but potentially conservative.  
3. **GSN‑FT ablation:** Compare GSN values before and after GSN‑FT, and attack generalization before the QP step. Show that GSN increases while attack generalization remains similar pre‑QP, confirming that gains originate from the QP repair, not preprocessing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can WARP’s constraint‑based repair framework be effectively extended to generative language models (e.g., decoder‑only GPT or encoder‑decoder T5) where the notion of a classification margin is not naturally defined?  
- Does WARP scale computationally and maintain guarantee validity for modern large language models (LLMs) with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters?  
- Under what precise conditions (e.g., layer depth, activation type, repair set size) does the first‑order linearization of the logit gap remain sufficiently accurate so that satisfying the QP constraints guarantees true positive margins post‑repair?  
- Can WARP’s layer selection strategy and QP formulation be directly applied to Transformer variants beyond standard encoder‑only models (e.g., decoder‑only or encoder‑decoder), or do architectural differences necessitate fundamental modifications?

## Limitations
- Sampling strategies and sizes for the auxiliary set D_aux (8 examples) and remain set P (800/200) are not fully specified, introducing potential selection bias.  
- The aggregation method for the Gap Sensitivity Norm (κ) during GSN‑FT checkpoint selection (mean/min/max) is unclear.  
- The assumptions about first‑order linearization accuracy lack theoretical error bounds; the validity of guarantees under large weight updates remains uncertain.

## Confidence

- Iterative convergence to exact margins: **Medium**
