# Agentic LLM Planning via Step-Wise PDDL Simulation: An Empirical Characterisation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.06064
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06064
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: Agentic step-wise LLM planning yields a modest 3% success gain over direct generation but at 5.7× higher token cost, with limited improvement due to self-assessed feedback.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates whether step-wise agentic LLM planning improves over direct generation in PDDL domains, using PyPDDLEngine—an interactive simulation engine with MCP tool calls. On 102 IPC Blocksworld instances under a 180-second budget, the classical planner Fast Downward lama-first achieves 85.3% success. Direct LLM planning with Claude Haiku 4.5 attains 63.7%, while the agentic approach reaches 66.7%—a modest three-percentage-point gain at 5.7× higher token cost per solution. Both LLM approaches produce shorter plans than the iterative classical baseline, consistent with training-data recall. The agentic method exhibits six early exits due to incorrect self-assessment, highlighting that self-grounded feedback yields minimal improvements without external verification.

The findings suggest that LLM-based planning in Blocksworld may rely heavily on memorized solution patterns rather than generalizable search. The step-wise interaction’s limited benefit stems from the lack of an objective progress signal; the LLM’s self-assessment of trajectory productivity is error-prone, leading to premature exits and wasted retries. These results underscore the challenge of achieving reliable autonomous planning with current LLMs.

## Method Summary
The evaluation runs on 102 IPC Blocksworld problem instances, comparing four approaches: (1) Fast Downward lama-first, (2) Fast Downward seq-sat-lama-2011 (an iterative quality-improvement variant), (3) direct LLM planning (Claude Haiku 4.5, temperature 0.2, single-shot generation with retries on invalid output), and (4) agentic LLM planning. The agentic approach uses PyPDDLEngine, an MCP server exposing PDDL operations (initialize, query_state, query_actions, execute, reset, history, validate). The LLM agent iteratively selects actions, executes them, observes state changes, and may reset early if it deems the trajectory unproductive. Each instance receives a 180-second wall-clock budget. Primary metrics are success rate (fraction of instances with a valid plan), plan length on the set of problems solved by both LLM methods (co-solved set), token consumption, and failure modes (timeout, early exit).

## Key Results
- Fast Downward lama-first achieves 85.3% success; direct LLM 63.7%; agentic LLM 66.7%—a modest 3 percentage-point gain at 5.7× higher token cost.
- Both LLM approaches generate shorter plans than the iterative classical baseline (seq-sat-lama-2011) on co-solved instances, suggesting reliance on memorized near-optimal patterns.
- The agentic method shows six early exits; on four of these, the direct approach finds a valid plan, indicating incorrect self-assessment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Self-assessed feedback limits agentic gains
**Claim**: The feedback loop is self-assessed and lacks external grounding, leading to minimal improvement over direct generation.  
**Mechanism**: The LLM acts as a search policy, executing actions and observing state transitions. It can reset if it self-assesses the trajectory as unproductive. However, per-step feedback only confirms action applicability and shows the current state—no objective progress signal (e.g., goal distance) is provided. The LLM’s internal judgment of whether it is making progress is therefore error-prone, leading to both wasted retries and premature exits.  
**Core assumption**: The LLM’s self-assessment of trajectory productivity is not reliably aligned with actual solvability.  
**Evidence**: Abstract states that agentic gains depend on the nature of environmental feedback… PDDL step feedback is self-assessed, leaving the agent to evaluate its own progress without external verification. Section V-C reports that on four of the six instances where the agentic approach halts early judging the problem unsolvable, the direct approach finds a valid plan, demonstrating that the self-assessment is factually incorrect. The paper cites Stechly et al. on self-verification limitations (neighbor FMR 0.516, zero citations).  
**Break condition**: If the agentic approach consistently shows a large advantage (>10%) with near-zero early exits, the mechanism would not explain the observed pattern.

### Mechanism 2: LLM planning on Blocksworld relies on training-data memorization
**Claim**: LLMs produce near-optimal plan lengths by recalling memorized solutions rather than performing generalizable search.  
**Mechanism**: Blocksworld is heavily represented in online resources and prior planning literature, likely appearing in LLM training corpora. When presented with a new instance, the LLM retrieves a plan structure that is often close to optimal. The step-wise interaction does not improve plan quality because the LLM already outputs a high-quality plan when it succeeds; the interaction does not enable incremental refinement because the model is not truly searching but pattern-matching.  
**Core assumption**: The training distribution contains many Blocksworld instances with optimal or near-optimal plans.  
**Evidence**: Abstract notes both LLM approaches produce shorter plans than seq-sat-lama-2011 despite its iterative quality improvement, a result consistent with training-data recall rather than generalisable planning. Section V-B elaborates that the direct and agentic approaches produce plans of nearly identical length on co-solved instances… Blocksworld is one of the most extensively documented planning domains in the literature and in online resources, and the LLM approaches may be recalling near-optimal solution patterns from training data rather than deriving plans by search. The paper cites prior work showing performance collapse under action name relabelling, supporting memorization.  
**Break condition**: If LLMs are evaluated on a novel PDDL domain with no training data coverage and still produce plans shorter than an iterative classical planner, the memorization account would be falsified.

### Mechanism 3: Modest net gain from reset capability offset by false-positive early exits
**Claim**: The modest agentic advantage (3 percentage points) arises from the ability to reset after a few steps when a trajectory seems unproductive, but this is offset by frequent false-positive early exits.  
**Mechanism**: Direct generation commits to a full plan per attempt; on failure it restarts from scratch with no memory. Agentic planning allows the LLM to reset early if it believes it is stuck, potentially conserving time for alternative trajectories. However, the LLM sometimes incorrectly judges a solvable problem as unsolvable and exits early (six instances), losing solutions that direct generation finds. The net gain is small because the reset opportunity is undermined by unreliable self-assessment.  
**Core assumption**: The LLM can sometimes correctly identify unproductive partial trajectories but also frequently misidentifies productive ones.  
**Evidence**: Abstract reports agentic achieves 66.7%—a consistent but modest three-percentage-point advantage… The agentic approach exhibits six early exits due to incorrect self-assessment. Section IV-B provides a detailed breakdown: agentic solved 68 vs. direct 65; agentic had 6 early exits (vs. 0 for direct) and 28 timeouts (vs. 37 for direct). On 4 of the 6 early exit instances, direct found a valid plan.  
**Break condition**: If early exits occur only on instances both approaches fail, or if the agentic advantage were much larger with few early exits, the mechanism would not explain the modest gain.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Orchestrator -> PyPDDLEngine (MCP server) -> LLM agent (Claude Haiku 4.5); problem PDDL -> Orchestrator & PyPDDLEngine; classical baselines (Fast Downward) are separate components run by the harness; validation and logging by Orchestrator.

**Critical path** (agentic run):
1. Orchestrator initializes PyPDDLEngine with domain and problem files.
2. LLM agent queries applicable actions via `query_actions` tool.
3. LLM selects an action and calls `execute`.
4. PyPDDLEngine returns updated state and goal-check status.
5. If goal not reached, loop to step 2; LLM may also call `query_state` or `reset` at any point.
6. When LLM believes plan complete, it calls `validate`; if valid, success; else may reset or continue.
7. Run terminates on validation success, timeout, or early exit (LLM decides unsolvable).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Step-wise interaction increases token consumption (~5.7×) but enables fine-grained backtracking without full replanning.
- General-purpose LLM provides flexibility but lower reliability and higher cost vs. specialized classical planners.
- Self-assessed feedback (no external progress signal) limits agentic gains.
- Temperature set to 0.2 for direct generation to allow variation; agentic framework does not expose temperature control.

**Failure signatures**:
- Early exit: agent halts before timeout claiming unsolvability (often false positive).
- Timeout: budget exhausted without a valid plan.
- High token consumption, especially for agentic runs.
- Invalid plan generation in direct approach due to syntactic or semantic errors.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Replicate the study with a frontier model (e.g., Claude Sonnet 3.7) to test scaling of the agentic advantage.
2. Add an external progress signal (e.g., goal-distance heuristic from a classical planner) to per-step feedback and measure impact on success rate and early exits.
3. Evaluate both approaches on a novel PDDL domain with minimal training-data coverage to test the memorization hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **External progress signals**: Does augmenting PyPDDLEngine with externally grounded progress signals (e.g., goal-distance heuristics) improve agentic LLM planning success rates? (Explicitly proposed in Section V.E.)
2. **Memorization vs. competence**: To what extent do LLM planning results on Blocksworld reflect training-data memorization rather than generalizable planning competence, when evaluated across diverse PDDL domains? (Explicitly identified as a key uncertainty in the conclusion; authors propose testing across domains with varied training-data coverage.)
3. **Improving self-assessment**: Can the LLM's self-assessment accuracy during step-wise interaction be improved (e.g., via prompting or architecture) to reduce incorrect early exits, thereby closing the gap with direct planning? (Inferred from six early exit instances, four of which were solved by the direct approach.)
4. **Difficulty-dependent advantage**: Why does the agentic advantage concentrate in mid-difficulty Blocksworld instances (blocks 20–50) but disappear or reverse on easier and hardest instances? (Inferred from Figure 2; non-monotonic pattern not explained.)

## Limitations
- System prompts for the LLM approaches are unreported, risking replication fidelity.
- Token counting methodology is unspecified, undermining the claimed 5.7× cost ratio.
- Evaluation is limited to Claude Haiku 4.5; findings may not generalize across model families or architectures.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (self-assessment limits): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (memorization): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (modest net gain): Medium
