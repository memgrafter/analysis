# Adaptive Collaboration of Arena-Based Argumentative LLMs for Explainable and Contestable Legal Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.18916  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18916  
- **Reference count**: 38  
- **Primary result**: ACAL outperforms CoT, RAG, and MAD on LegalBench, achieving peak F1-scores of 70.7% (Learned Hands Courts) and 74.4% (Hearsay) with Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, while providing a mathematically auditable reasoning graph that users can edit to influence final judgments.

## Executive Summary
ACAL addresses the lack of structured transparency and user contestability in LLM-based legal reasoning by introducing a neuro-symbolic framework that combines adaptive multi-agent collaboration with an Arena-based Quantitative Bipolar Argumentation Framework (A-QBAF). The system dynamically selects specialized legal agents to generate support and attack arguments, resolves conflicting arguments via an LLM judge to calibrate argument strength, and propagates these strengths through a formal graph to produce a final claim score. A Human-in-the-Loop workflow allows users to audit and edit the reasoning graph, with changes propagating mathematically to the decision. On LegalBench, ACAL significantly improves accuracy over baselines, and ablation confirms the critical role of Clash Resolution. Beyond metrics, ACAL delivers explainable, contestable outputs that promote trust and verifiability in legal AI assistance.

## Method Summary
ACAL processes a legal case text by first extracting the central claim and retrieving relevant context (legislation and case law) via hybrid RAG. A meta-LLM then selects subsets of 10 predefined legal expert agents (e.g., Judge, Prosecutor) to generate support and attack arguments, each scored by an intrinsic strength rubric. An LLM identifies pairwise attack/support/neutral relations among arguments, with low-confidence edges marked neutral. For opposing arguments with similar scores (difference < 0.2), a Clash Resolution step pits them in an LLM-judged debate to differentiate their strengths. The resulting A-QBAF graph—with nodes (arguments), edges (relations), and scores—undergoes deterministic Quadratic Energy propagation to compute a final claim score. If the score is borderline (0.49–0.51), an Uncertainty-Aware Escalation invokes a Final Judge. Finally, users can contest the decision by editing the graph (e.g., modifying argument text, relations, or scores), after which propagation recomputes the outcome, enabling direct influence over the reasoning.

## Key Results
- On LegalBench, ACAL with Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite achieves F1-scores of 70.7% (Learned Hands Courts) and 74.4% (Hearsay), surpassing CoT, RAG, and MAD.
- Clash Resolution ablation degrades accuracy by 7.9% on Hearsay (from 72.8% to 64.9%), demonstrating its critical role in score calibration.
- The framework produces a mathematically auditable reasoning graph (A-QBAF) that supports user edits and propagates changes to the final judgment, enabling verifiable and contestable decisions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Adaptive Agent Selection
- **Claim**: Adaptive agent selection improves reasoning relevance and reduces noise by dynamically matching expert roles to case aspects.
- **Mechanism**: A meta-LLM evaluates the legal task context (claim ϕ, case facts c) against pre-defined agent profiles (role, expertise, priorities) to select subsets for support (A⁺) and attack (A⁻). This tailors the argument pool to the specific legal issue.
- **Core assumption**: Domain-specialized agents generate more precise, on-point arguments than a one-size-fits-all team; mismatched agents introduce irrelevant or low-quality arguments.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  [abstract] "ACAL dynamically deploys expert agent teams to construct arguments"  
  [section 3.1.2] Describes `Select(A, T, c, type)` function matching agent expertise to task characteristics.
- **Break condition**: If the selection function poorly matches case complexity (e.g., misses a required legal specialty like IP for a patent case), generated arguments will be incomplete or off-topic, degrading final accuracy.

### Mechanism 2: Clash Resolution
- **Claim**: Clash Resolution (CR) within A-QBAF prevents score saturation and calibrates argument strength by having an LLM judge directly compare conflicting arguments with similar base scores.
- **Mechanism**: For argument pairs (support αₛ, attack αₐ) where |τ(αₛ) - τ(αₐ)| < δ (default 0.2), an LLM "judge" decides the stronger argument. Winners gain β·(2w-1), losers lose proportionally, where w is win rate across all clashes. This propagates differentiation through the QE semantics.
- **Core assumption**: LLM-intrinsic scores (τ) are coarse and often cluster near the middle (0.5-0.8) for plausible arguments, creating flat QE outputs; direct comparative judgment breaks these ties more reliably.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  [section 3.2.5] Full specification of CR with arena debating and ∆τ formula.  
  [section 5.3] Ablation shows CR alone yields +7.9% accuracy on Hearsay (64.9%→72.8%).
- **Break condition**: If δ is too large, unnecessary clashes occur (wasting compute); if too small, true conflicts are missed and scores remain saturated. The β parameter sensitivity (Table 5) shows performance drops if over/under-adjusted.

### Mechanism 3: Human-in-the-Loop Contestability
- **Claim**: Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) contestability transforms the A-QBAF from a static explanation into an editable decision object by mathematically propagating user edits to the final claim score.
- **Mechanism**: Users audit argument cards and perform interventions: accept/reject, edit text, add arguments, or modify relations/weights. These updates produce a new A-QBAF (Xᴴ, Rᴴ, τᴴ), which is re-run through identical QE propagation to yield σᴴ(ϕ), directly changing the Yes/No outcome.
- **Core assumption**: Stakeholders (lawyers, judges) can identify specific reasoning errors (factual, legal, procedural) that the system missed; acting on these at the graph level corrects systemic biases better than post-hoc feedback.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  [abstract] "enabling users to directly audit and modify the underlying reasoning graph to influence the final judgment"  
  [section 3.3.3] "All accepted edits are applied to yield an updated A-QBAF... after which we re-run the same propagation semantics"
- **Break condition**: If the propagation semantics (QE) are not faithfully re-run after edits, or if the edit interface is too restricted (e.g., cannot modify relations), user changes become superficial and do not truly contest the decision logic.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Computational Argumentation Frameworks (BAF/QBAF)**  
  *Why needed here*: ACAL's core is an A-QBAF graph where legal arguments are nodes with attack/support edges and computed strengths. Understanding bipolar (attack/support) dynamics and semantics like QE is essential to predict how local argument changes affect the global claim score.  
  *Quick check question*: If a strong support argument to the claim is weakened via user edit, what happens to σ(ϕ) under QE semantics? (Answer: It decreases, as the energy from supporters reduces.)
- **Concept: Multi-Agent System Design (Role-Based Specialization)**  
  *Why needed here*: The agent pool (Table 1) and selection mechanism rely on understanding how different legal roles (Judge, Prosecutor, etc.) contribute distinct reasoning perspectives. Misunderstanding role focus priorities leads to poor team selection.  
  *Quick check question*: Why might selecting only "Adjudication" category agents for a complex case be suboptimal? (Answer: Lacks adversarial perspectives from "Litigation & Advocacy" to generate robust attack arguments.)
- **Concept: Neuro-Symbolic Integration (LLM + Formal Graph)**  
  *Why needed here*: ACAL uses LLMs to generate natural language arguments and scores (symbolic-like), but these are grounded in a formal graph structure (A-QBAF). The engineer must grasp that LLMs are tools for populating/editing the graph, not for final decision logic—the QE propagation is deterministic.  
  *Quick check question*: What happens if the LLM judge in CR produces inconsistent outcomes across similar clashes? (Answer: Score calibration becomes unstable; β may need tuning or pairwise consistency checks added.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input Analyzer -> Agent Selector -> Argumentation Engine -> Clash Resolver -> A-QBAF Propagator -> (UAE Module?) -> Decision Output. HITL Interface is an offline re-run branch that accepts user edits and rebuilds the A-QBAF before propagation.
- **Critical path**: Input → RAG → Agent Selection → Argument Gen → Relation ID → CR → QE Propagation → (UAE?) → Decision.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  • Compute vs. Transparency: More agents/arguments improve coverage but increase O(n²) LLM calls for relation ID and CR; batching (b=10) mitigates but not eliminates.  
  • LLM-based scoring consistency: τ rubrics (Table 2) help but remain LLM-dependent; noisy τ propagates through QE. CR aims to correct but adds another LLM call.  
  • UAE utility: Table 4 shows UAE alone hurts performance; it is conditional on CR providing a calibrated σ distribution first.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  • Score saturation: If CR is disabled, many arguments cluster near 0.5-0.8, QE output collapses toward 0.5, accuracy drops.  
  • Uncalibrated escalation: UAE invoked too frequently burdens Final Judge and may override a sound QE result.  
  • Broken edit propagation: If edits alter τ but QE code has bugs, σᴴ(ϕ) may not reflect changes, making HITL illusory.
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. Baseline replication: Run SP, CoT, RAG, MAD on LegalBench with Gemini-2.5-Lite to establish performance gap vs. ACAL.  
  2. CR ablation: Disable CR, measure drop in F1/accuracy; expect ~8% loss (Table 4).  
  3. β sensitivity: Sweep β (0.05-0.25) on one task; expect bell curve peaking around 0.15 (Table 5), confirming need for moderate adjustment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can the computational overhead of ACAL's multi-agent architecture be reduced without sacrificing the depth and quality of legal reasoning?**  
   *Basis*: Conclusion states future work on optimizing computational efficiency.  
   *Why unresolved*: Current implementation likely incurs significant API calls and processing due to multiple agents, argument pair analysis, and clash resolution, which may not scale to cost-sensitive legal practice.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Benchmarking inference time/cost across case complexities, developing pruning strategies for agent selection or argument pair batching, or designing more efficient relation classification.

2. **To what extent does ACAL generalize beyond simple binary classification tasks on LegalBench to more complex legal reasoning scenarios (e.g., multi-issue cases, statutory interpretation, or precedent-based reasoning)?**  
   *Basis*: Conclusion aims to validate generalizability by extending the agent pool to broader tasks.  
   *Why unresolved*: Evaluation only covers two straightforward tasks (hearsay detection, category classification) with limited context; complex reasoning involves nuanced argumentation, authority hierarchies, and multi-step deductions that may challenge A-QBAF's structure or agent coverage.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Testing on more demanding benchmarks (e.g., COLIEE, LexGLUE) or custom datasets with richer reasoning, analyzing failure modes, and adapting agent roles and A-QBAF semantics.

3. **Under what conditions does the Uncertainty-Aware Escalation (UAE) mechanism improve outcomes versus causing performance degradation, and how can its trigger threshold be dynamically calibrated?**  
   *Basis*: Ablation (Table 4) shows UAE alone reduces accuracy (62.8% vs. 64.9% baseline) but combined with CR yields best result (74.5%), suggesting dependence on prior calibration.  
   *Why unresolved*: Fixed threshold (σ∈[0.49,0.51]) may be too simplistic; interaction between UAE and CR is not theoretically modeled, and the "borderline" region might vary by case type or argument structure.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Analyze distribution of claim scores before/after CR across diverse cases, experiment with adaptive thresholds based on graph metrics (e.g., score variance, connectivity), and evaluate UAE's impact on different uncertainty sources.

4. **How effective and usable is the Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) contestation workflow for legal professionals in practice, and does it meaningfully improve both decision quality and user trust?**  
   *Basis*: Paper describes HITL interface and audit logging but provides no user study or qualitative feedback.  
   *Why unresolved*: Legal experts may have specific contestation needs (e.g., citing new precedents, adjusting legal standards) that the five predefined types don't capture; cognitive load of editing a reasoning graph is unknown; no evidence that interventions lead to better outcomes or increased trust vs. passive explanations.  
   *Resolving evidence*: User studies with lawyers/judges using ACAL on real cases, measuring time to contest, success rate of edits in changing outcomes, and subjective trust/understanding via surveys; analyzing audit logs to see which contestation types are most used and their impact.

5. **How robust and accurate is the LLM-based adaptive agent selection mechanism in assigning the right roles and expertise to case aspects, and does it systematically under-represent certain legal domains or perspectives?**  
   *Basis*: Section 3.1.2 describes LLM-based selection from a pool but no evaluation of selection quality.  
   *Why unresolved*: Selection errors could lead to missing critical arguments or including irrelevant ones, undermining reasoning; paper doesn't measure selection accuracy (e.g., against expert judgment) or analyze coverage gaps; biases in LLM's understanding of legal roles might cause systematic omissions.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Annotating cases with ideal agent assignments, comparing ACAL's selections, measuring precision/recall of role assignment, identifying domain gaps by testing on niche areas (e.g., international law, tax); studying impact of selection errors on outcomes via ablation.

## Limitations
- Agent role definitions and prompt templates are undisclosed, preventing exact replication of adaptive selection logic.
- Retrieval corpus details (specific legal databases, chunking, embedding version) are vague, affecting grounding consistency.
- HITL effectiveness assumes legal experts can correctly identify and edit reasoning errors via graph interfaces; this is untested.
- Claims are validated only on two LegalBench tasks; generalizability to broader legal domains (e.g., contract review, IP) is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Adaptive Agent Selection**: Medium confidence – selection function described but no direct ablation ties selection quality to final accuracy; evidence indirect.
- **Clash Resolution**: High confidence – ablation shows clear +7.9% gain on Hearsay, and β sensitivity analysis confirms calibrated impact.
- **Human-in-the-Loop Contestability**: Medium-Low confidence – mathematical propagation is well-defined, but no user studies verify that edits meaningfully correct decisions or are practically usable.

## Next Checks
1. **CR Generalizability Test**: Replicate the Clash Resolution ablation on two additional LegalBench tasks (e.g., "Bill of Attainder," "Takings Clause") to confirm the +7.9% improvement is not task-specific.
2. **Hyperparameter Robustness Sweep**: Systematically vary β (0.05–0.25) and δ (0.1–0.3) across both Gemini models; report accuracy distributions to identify stable, domain-agnostic settings.
3. **User Study on Edit Propagation**: Recruit 5–10 legal professionals to perform targeted edits on A-QBAF graphs (e.g., weaken a key support argument); measure whether σ(ϕ) changes correctly and whether participants find the interface actionable for contesting decisions.
