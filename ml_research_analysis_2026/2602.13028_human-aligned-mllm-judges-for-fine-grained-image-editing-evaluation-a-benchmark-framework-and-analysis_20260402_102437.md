# Human-Aligned MLLM Judges for Fine-Grained Image Editing Evaluation: A Benchmark, Framework, and Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13028
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13028
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A factorized MLLM judge achieves strong alignment with human evaluation (ACC±1 ≈ 0.79) and provides interpretable diagnostics, outperforming traditional reference-dependent metrics.

## Executive Summary
Traditional image editing metrics (PSNR, SSIM, CLIP) are coarse and fail to capture human-perceived aspects like instruction fidelity, edit localization, and semantic correctness. This paper introduces a fine-grained MLLM-as-a-judge framework that decomposes evaluation into twelve interpretable factors across image preservation, edit quality, and instruction fidelity. Using GPT-5-mini with a structured prompt, the system scores each factor on a 7-point Likert scale given the original image, instruction, and edited output. Evaluated on a new 100-edit benchmark with human ratings, the MLLM judge shows strong correlation with humans while traditional metrics show near-zero alignment, enabling nuanced failure analysis for both offline and online evaluation.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a benchmark (HumanEdit) of 100 diverse image editing triples (original image, instruction, edited output) spanning six edit types (Add, Remove, Replace, Action, Counting, Relation). Five human annotators rate each edit on twelve predefined factors using a 7-point Likert scale. An MLLM (GPT-5-mini) is prompted with the same inputs, factor definitions, and rubric to produce scores and justifications. Multiple metrics (ACC±1, MAE, Pearson, Spearman, Kendall’s τ, pairwise accuracy) compare MLLM outputs against human averages. Traditional metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, CLIPScore) are also computed for the same data to contrast alignment.

## Key Results
- MLLM judgments align strongly with human aggregated scores (ACC±1 ≈ 0.79 overall; Pearson 0.25–0.59 across factors).
- Traditional metrics show extremely weak or negative correlations with factor-level human judgments.
- The factorized approach reliably diagnoses specific failure modes (e.g., over-editing, semantic imprecision) that monolithic scores obscure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Decomposing image editing quality into twelve fine-grained, human-defined factors yields evaluations that align better with human judgment than monolithic scores. By separating concerns into Image Preservation, Edit Quality, and Instruction Fidelity, the framework forces reasoning about specific, potentially conflicting objectives (e.g., "unchanged regions" vs. "alignment"). This mirrors how humans dissect edit quality and prevents a single metric from conflating distinct success/failure modes. The strong MLLM-human alignment across factors (ACC±1 ≈ 0.79) supports that the decomposition is meaningful.

### Mechanism 2
A capable multimodal LLM, when prompted with factor definitions and a 7-point Likert rubric, can simulate human evaluation behavior for these dimensions. The MLLM leverages its internal knowledge about visual realism, object permanence, and language understanding. The prompt provides necessary context (original image, instruction, edited image) and structured criteria. Model capability is key: GPT-5-mini aligns better than Gemini-2.5-pro for most factors, implying scale and architecture matter.

### Mechanism 3
The factorized MLLM evaluation provides interpretable diagnostics that traditional pixel-level or semantic similarity metrics cannot. Traditional metrics output a single scalar that collapses all quality aspects. In contrast, the 12-factor score profile creates a multi-dimensional signature: a low score on "Unchanged Regions" explicitly diagnoses over-editing; low "Alignment" with high "Image Quality" diagnoses semantic imprecision. This allows targeted model improvement, whereas traditional metrics fail to capture human-perceived aspects like instruction fidelity.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Instruction-guided image editing taxonomy (Add, Remove, Replace, Action, Counting, Relation).**  
  *Why needed:* Different edit types stress different factors (e.g., "Counting" heavily tests *Completeness* and *Plausibility*). Understanding the taxonomy is essential to interpret model performance variations across edit types.  
  *Quick check:* For a "Replace" edit ("change the car to a truck"), which three factors from Image Preservation are most likely violated, and why?

- **Concept: MLLM-as-a-Judge paradigm and prompt engineering.**  
  *Why needed:* The entire method hinges on effectively steering an MLLM to produce human-aligned, factorized judgments. Prompt structure (unified vs. factor-by-factor), rubric inclusion, and example provision affect output stability and alignment.  
  *Quick check:* Why might the "Category wise, Example guided" prompt yield higher quantitative alignment but be less practical for large-scale deployment than the "Main" prompt?

- **Concept: Evaluation metric design (pointwise vs. pairwise, error-based vs. correlation-based).**  
  *Why needed:* The paper uses a suite of metrics (MSE, MAE, ACC±1, Pearson, Spearman, Kendall’s τ, pairwise accuracy) to assess human-MLLM alignment from multiple angles. An engineer must understand what each metric captures (e.g., MAE for absolute score agreement, Kendall’s τ for ranking consistency) to properly diagnose judge performance.  
  *Quick check:* What does a high Pearson correlation but a high MAE between human and MLLM scores imply about their relationship?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
HumanEdit Benchmark (100 triples + human factor scores) → Judge Prompt (templates in Figures 7–9) + GPT-5-mini API → MLLM Factor Scores (JSON) → Scoring Module (computes ACC±1, MAE, correlations) → Alignment Analysis → Traditional Metrics Calculator (PSNR, SSIM, etc.) → Comparative Analysis

**Critical path:**  
Data Collection (HumanEdit) → Image Editing (gpt-image-1) → Human Annotation (5 per edit) → MLLM Scoring → Metric Computation → Alignment Analysis. For online deployment, replace fixed editing model with a live generator and omit human annotation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Granularity vs. Scalability/Cost: 12 factors provide rich diagnostics but increase MLLM call cost and complexity. The "Main" prompt (all factors at once) is cheaper but may trade off some alignment for speed.
- Prompt Verbosity vs. Human Parity: The "Category wise, Example guided" prompt is more explicit and aligned but too verbose for human annotators, making direct human-judge comparison less fair.
- Base Model Choice: Using a proprietary, high-capability MLLM (GPT-5-mini) ensures strong baseline performance but introduces cost, API dependency, and potential black-box bias.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High variance in MLLM scores for a single edit (low human score variance) suggests MLLM instability or over-sensitivity to prompt phrasing.
- Systematic low alignment on specific factor-edit type pairs (e.g., "Identity Preservation" on "Replace") points to a weakness in the MLLM's visual reasoning for that construct.
- Traditional metrics showing any strong positive correlation with instruction-fidelity factors is suspicious and may indicate a data artifact.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Factor-level sanity check:** Run the MLLM judge on a small set of known-good and known-bad edits (e.g., Figure 2 examples) to verify it produces intuitively correct score profiles before scaling.
2. **Inter-annotator agreement baseline:** Calculate human-human agreement (ICC) on the 5 annotations per image to establish the gold-standard noise floor; if MLLM-human alignment exceeds human-human, re-evaluate.
3. **Prompt ablation pilot:** Test the three prompt variants (Main, Factor-level Rubrics, Category wise) on 20 images to confirm the expected alignment pattern (Table 6) before committing to one for full benchmark.

## Limitations
- **Domain scope:** Benchmark covers 100 edits across six common types but may not represent all real-world editing intents or highly specialized domains (e.g., medical, technical).
- **MLLM dependence:** Results are tied to GPT-5-mini; alignment may degrade with other models, especially smaller/open-source ones.
- **Factor completeness:** The 12 factors may miss emergent failure modes (e.g., ethical harms, cultural bias).
- **Human benchmark stability:** No reported inter-annotator agreement (ICC) for the 5 human ratings per edit; human judgment noise floor is unknown.
- **Static evaluation:** Does not address online deployment challenges (latency, cost, dynamic prompt robustness).

## Confidence
- **Claim:** MLLM-human alignment on factor scores is strong (ACC±1 ≈ 0.79). **Confidence:** High – Directly supported by reported results across 100 edits.
- **Claim:** Factorized evaluation provides interpretable diagnostics superior to traditional metrics. **Confidence:** Medium – Correlational evidence is strong, but causal utility (improving models via factor scores) is not demonstrated.
- **Claim:** Traditional metrics are poorly aligned with human-perceived edit quality. **Confidence:** High – Paper shows near-zero/negative correlations consistently.

## Next Checks
1. **Compute human-human agreement (ICC) on the 5 annotations per edit** to establish the gold-standard noise floor; if MLLM-human alignment exceeds human-human, re-evaluate.
2. **Replicate the judge pipeline with an open-source MLLM (e.g., LLaVA-OneVision)** on the same benchmark to test robustness to model choice and identify capability thresholds.
