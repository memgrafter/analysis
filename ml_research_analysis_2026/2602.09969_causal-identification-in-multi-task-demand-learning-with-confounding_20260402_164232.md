# Causal Identification in Multi-Task Demand Learning with Confounding

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.09969
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09969
- Reference count: 29
- Primary result: DCMOML conditions on full price history but masks outcomes at the last two distinct price points, identifying causal parameters under mild exogeneity; it outperforms baselines in synthetic and real-world pricing tasks.

## Executive Summary
We consider estimating heterogeneous linear price-demand functions across many tasks when prices are set endogenously—i.e., they depend on unobserved latent factors that also affect demand. Standard meta-learning remains biased because the final price correlates with these confounders. The proposed Decision-Conditioned Masked-Outcome Meta-Learning (DCMOML) breaks this correlation by conditioning on the full realized price history while deliberately masking the demand outcomes at the last two distinct price points and randomizing which is used for supervision. Under a mild exogeneity condition on the final price block, this design uniquely identifies the conditional mean of the causal parameters, and the empirical risk minimizer is consistent. Theoretical results and experiments on synthetic data (K=2) show strong improvements over baselines (e.g., slope MSE 0.03 vs 0.13), and on a UK online retail dataset DCMOML achieves lower held-out RMSE.

## Method Summary
DCMOML operates in a multi-task setting where each task provides a sequence of K historical (price, demand) pairs and context covariates Z_i. For each task, it identifies the last two distinct price points (indices K* and K) and masks their associated demand outcomes. The meta-learner receives the full price vector and all unmasked demands (with their indices) as input, and predicts the task-specific linear demand parameters Θ_i = (θ₀, θ₁). During training, one of the two masked demand observations is randomly selected as the query, and the loss is the squared error between the predicted demand (using the predicted parameters and the query price) and the true masked demand. This ensures the query price is not determined by the observed inputs, thereby breaking the endogeneity. The method is implemented with a feedforward MLP, trained via standard meta-learning (empirical risk minimization across tasks). Baselines include meta-learning (conditioning on first K-1 pairs), shared OLS, and per-task OLS.

## Key Results
- DCMOML’s design uniquely identifies the conditional mean E[Θ_i | history] under Assumption 1 (exogeneity of the final price block), and the empirical risk minimizer is consistent.
- In synthetic Gaussian experiments with high confounding, DCMOML reduces slope MSE by 77% (0.03 vs 0.13) compared to standard meta-learning.
- On a UK online retail dataset, DCMOML achieves lower held-out exposure-weighted RMSE than meta-learning and pooled models, demonstrating practical gains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Conditioning on the full realized price history absorbs latent confounding embedded in endogenous pricing decisions.
- Mechanism: The pricing policy uses unobserved task-level information (Ω_i) to set prices, so the price path p_{i1:K} carries information about those latent factors. By including the entire price vector in the learner's input, the meta-learner conditions on that latent information, breaking the correlation between the regressor p_{iK} and the error term in the demand equation.
- Core assumption: The price assignment rule is measurable with respect to (Z_i, p_{i1:K-1}, D_{i1:K-1}, Ω_i) and may depend arbitrarily on Ω_i. The learner observes the realized prices.
- Evidence anchors: abstract, Section "Conditioning on the Full Price History" and Equation (10); parallels with causal forecasting.
- Break condition: If the price history contains no variation or the policy is independent of latent factors, the method offers no advantage; key break is when price history is unavailable or heavily censored.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Withholding demand outcomes at exactly two distinct price points and randomizing the query index prevents the learner from uniquely identifying which price is used for supervision, thus preserving identifying variation in the query regressor.
- Mechanism: The learner receives the price vector but demand is missing for two indices (K* and K). Because these two prices are unassociated with observed outcomes, the query regressor p_{i,k1} cannot be recovered from the covariates, ensuring the conditional second-moment matrix E[p_{i,k1} p_{i,k1}^T | X] is positive definite. Randomizing which is k1 ensures the query index itself is not a deterministic function of inputs.
- Core assumption: Assumption 1: E[ϵ_{i,k1} | Z_i, p_{i1:K}, D_{i,-{K*,K}}, p_{i,k1}] = 0, i.e., pricing decisions within the final two-point block do not adapt to the demand shock at the penultimate point.
- Evidence anchors: abstract, Section "Identification via Partial Obfuscation of the Decision History" and Step 2.
- Break condition: Assumption 1 fails if the terminal price or its duration is chosen in response to the demand outcome at the penultimate point. Also, fewer than two distinct prices makes the design impossible.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Under the information design, the empirical risk minimizer converges to the conditional mean of causal parameters E[Θ_i | X_-], enabling consistent estimation despite limited per-task samples.
- Mechanism: The obfuscation design yields a uniquely identified population risk minimizer equal to the target. With a sufficiently expressive hypothesis class satisfying realizability and uniform convergence, ERM consistency follows. The within-task data scarcity is overcome by borrowing strength across tasks via meta-learning.
- Core assumption: Realizability: exists Λ0 in hypothesis class such that g_{Λ0}(X_-) = E[Θ_i | X_-] a.s. Also uniform law of large numbers holds.
- Evidence anchors: abstract, Theorem 1 (Identifiability) and Theorem 2 (Consistency).
- Break condition: Misspecification leads to approximation gap; if conditional second-moment matrix has eigenvalues near zero (tiny price gaps), L2 convergence rate deteriorates.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Structural Causal Model with Latent Confounding**
  - Why needed: Demands are driven by observed price and unobserved store-specific deviations Ω_i that also influence pricing. Understanding the DAG (price ← Ω → demand) explains why OLS fails.
  - Quick check: In D_{ik} = θ0_i + θ1_i p_{ik} + ϵ_{ik} and Θ_i = g(Z_i) + Ω_i, which term creates endogeneity if prices depend on Ω_i? (Answer: Ω_i, because it affects both price and demand.)

- **Concept: Endogeneity in Regression**
  - Why needed: Standard meta-learning conditions on past outcomes but still suffers from correlation between p_{iK} and Ω_i. Recognizing that the conditional mean of demand given price is not the causal effect is crucial.
  - Quick check: Why does adding (p1, D1), ..., (p_{K-1}, D_{K-1}) to the meta-learner not solve endogeneity if p_{iK} still correlates with Ω_i after conditioning on those? (Answer: Because Ω_i may still be correlated with p_{iK} after conditioning on past prices and demands if the pricing policy uses Ω_i to set the final price.)

- **Concept: Information Design for Identification**
  - Why needed: The core innovation is not a new estimator but a design of what information the learner sees. Conditioning on the full price history would normally pin down the query price; deliberately masking outcomes breaks that link.
  - Quick check: How does withholding two demand outcomes (instead of one) prevent the learner from inferring the query regressor? (Answer: With one missing outcome, the learner could sometimes infer the missing price from the pattern of observed prices and demands; with two, the query price remains ambiguous because both missing prices are symmetric and unobserved.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Input Builder -> Network Head -> Loss Module -> Optimizer

Specifically:
- Input Builder: For each task i, fetch observed prices p_{i1:K} and demands D_{i1:K}. Identify K* as last index where p_{iK*} ≠ p_{iK}. Create two-set M = {K*, K}. Choose query index k1 uniformly from M (or average over both later). Construct X_minus = (Z_i, p_{i1:K}, D_{i,-M}) where D_{i,-M} are demands at indices not in M, with their indices known.
- Network Head: MLP (e.g., 2 hidden layers, size 256) takes X_minus and outputs Θ̂_i ∈ ℝ².
- Loss Module: Compute query price regressor P_{i,k1} = [1, p_{i,k1}] and loss L_i = (D_{i,k1} - P_{i,k1}⊤ Θ̂_i)². Optionally weight by exposure length.
- Optimizer: Standard Adam (lr ~1e-3), batch size 64, early stopping on validation RMSE.

**Critical path:**
- Step A: Compute K* by scanning price vector from end to find last price different from p_{iK}. If none, error.
- Step B: Form M = {K*, K} and ensure both demands D_{i,K*} and D_{i,K} are omitted from X_minus. The network must not see any demand from those two indices.
- Step C: In training loop, either sample k1 per task and compute loss using D_{i,k1}; or for both k∈M compute loss and average (recommended for lower variance).
- Step D: Pass X_minus to network; note that the network input includes entire price vector and all observed demands (with their indices). For variable K, use fixed-size padding or a set representation that preserves order and alignment.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Masking depth: DCMOML masks exactly two outcomes (minimal set that obfuscates). Masking more discards extra signal; masking fewer recreates query identification failure.
- Query handling: Randomizing k1 per minibatch vs averaging over both options: averaging reduces gradient variance and eliminates an extra random draw; recommended.
- Input encoding: The price history must be passed preserving order and alignment with kept demands. For variable K, use padding or a set network with positional encoding. Ensure the network knows which prices are missing their demands (e.g., special token or separate channels).

**Failure signatures:**
- Sudden collapse to constant or slope zero: check eigenvalue condition – compute per-task squared price gap |p_{iK} - p_{i,K*}|²; if many near zero, the conditional variance is tiny and learning unstable.
- Performance worse than pooled SHARED model: likely masking logic broken (e.g., query demand inadvertently included in inputs). Validate that for each task exactly two prices have no associated demand in the input.
- Training loss does not decrease: hypothesis class may be too small; try deeper/wider MLP. Also check realizability: maybe target not approximable given inputs.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Synthetic Gaussian verification: Generate tasks with Gaussian Θ_i and affine pricing as in Example 3 (K=2). Use a linear symmetric MLP (same weights for both prices in input). Confirm DCMOML learns near-zero slope/intercept MSE. Compare to meta-learning (biased upward as in Fig 2).
2. Masking ablation on synthetic data with K=2: Baseline DCML (only mask D_k but keep both prices and D_{-k}) should show high bias (slope near positive). DCUOML (mask one demand from a different index) may also fail if leakage remains. DCMOML should succeed.
3. UK-retail dataset replication: Implement both Static-Top3 and Exposure-Sequence tasks. Verify that DCMOML outperforms META and SHARED in held-out RMSE as in Fig 5. Monitor the distribution of price gaps to ensure they are not tiny (if many <0.5, expect higher variance).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Can DCMOML's identification principle be extended to nonlinear price-demand functions (e.g., log-linear, constant-elasticity) while preserving causal guarantees?** The current theory relies on linearity; nonlinearities may disrupt the two-point masking design. Future work should develop identification conditions for specific nonlinear models and validate with synthetic experiments.
- **What is the statistically and computationally optimal masking design (e.g., number of withheld outcomes, randomization scheme) when tasks have more than two distinct historical prices?** The paper's design masks exactly two outcomes as the minimal set. With more prices, one could mask more outcomes or randomize over larger query sets; the trade-offs are unexplored.
- **How should DCMOML's conditional demand estimates be integrated into a downstream pricing optimization policy under distribution shift (e.g., new products, changing markets) while maintaining performance guarantees?** The paper focuses on estimation consistency, not on using the estimates for pricing decisions or under shift. Formal results linking estimation error to pricing regret and simulation studies under shift are needed.
- **What practical diagnostics can detect when endogenous pricing is severe enough to warrant outcome masking (i.e., when standard meta-learning is policy-confounded) in real-world datasets?** The paper assumes confounding but offers no tools to diagnose when standard meta-learning is biased. Proposing statistical tests (e.g., based on residuals) and validating them would help practitioners.

## Limitations
- Retail experimental details (preprocessing, variable K handling, exposure weighting, train/test splits) are underspecified, hindering exact replication.
- The core Assumption 1 (final price block exogenous to penultimate demand shock) is not empirically validated; violations could occur if managers adjust prices based on recent demand.
- Theoretical guarantees rely on realizability and uniform convergence, but these are not tested; in practice, the MLP may be misspecified for some tasks.

## Confidence
- Identifiability and consistency theory: High (rigorous proofs, supported by synthetic verification).
- Superior empirical performance (synthetic): Medium (clear under controlled conditions, but sensitivity to implementation details needs verification).
- Real-world effectiveness: Low to Medium (reported advantage on UK retail data is promising but lacks full reproducibility and may be confounded by unknown preprocessing).
