# Who Benchmarks the Benchmarks? A Case Study of LLM Evaluation in Icelandic

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.16406
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16406
*   **Reference count:** 0
*   **Primary result:** LLM benchmarks for Icelandic constructed via unverified machine translation or synthetic data are severely flawed (near 0% valid), while human-localized benchmarks exhibit much higher validity, highlighting a critical risk of invalid evaluations for low-resource languages.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the validity of existing Icelandic benchmarks for evaluating Large Language Models (LLMs). Through a quantitative error analysis, the authors find that benchmarks created or translated without native speaker verification—often relying on machine translation (MT) or synthetic data—contain a high prevalence of severe linguistic, factual, and cultural errors. In contrast, benchmarks that were professionally translated and localized by native Icelandic speakers (e.g., from Miðeind) are significantly more valid. The study concludes that unverified MT/synthetic benchmarks threaten the integrity of LLM evaluation for Icelandic and likely other low-resource languages, advocating for native speaker involvement and rigorous validation in benchmark construction.

## Method Summary
The study performed a quantitative error analysis on 12 existing Icelandic LLM benchmarks. Three expert annotators (Icelandic linguists/NLP researchers) labeled a sample of examples from each benchmark as **Invalid (IC: severely flawed/poorly MT)**, **Flawed (F: contains errors)**, or **OK (valid)**. For benchmarks with over 250 rows, a random sample of 250 was taken; for smaller ones, 10% was sampled, focusing on training splits or answer-containing rows. The primary metrics were the mean proportion of each label per benchmark (with 95% confidence intervals) and inter-rater reliability measured by weighted Krippendorff's α (ordinal). Benchmarks were then grouped by creation method (unverified MT/synthetic vs. human-localized) for comparative interpretation.

## Key Results
*   Benchmarks derived from unverified machine translation or synthetic data showed catastrophically low validity: HellaSwag-IS had **0% OK**, MMLU-IS had **<10% OK**, and EuroEval's ARC-IS had **~20% OK**.
*   Human-translated and localized benchmarks demonstrated substantially higher validity: Miðeind's ARC-Challenge-IS had **55% OK**, and WinoGrande-IS had **no severely flawed examples** and a high OK rate.
*   Inter-rater reliability (Krippendorff's α) was "questionable" or lower for most benchmarks, reflecting the inherent difficulty of the annotation task but not undermining the stark contrast in OK rates between benchmark types.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Unverified machine translation (MT) introduces severe linguistic and factual artifacts that render benchmarks invalid.
*   **Mechanism:** MT for low-resource languages like Icelandic produces outputs with grammatical errors, key term mistranslations, and lost cultural context. These artifacts create test items solvable by exploiting translation patterns rather than genuine language understanding, breaking construct validity.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Near-zero OK rates in HellaSwag-IS (0%) and MMLU-IS (<10%); qualitative examples of nonsensical translations and factual inaccuracies.
*   **Break condition:** Benchmarks undergo rigorous native speaker verification and correction (e.g., Miðeind's process), which drastically reduces severely flawed examples.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Absence of native speaker involvement causes misalignment with authentic language use and cultural context.
*   **Mechanism:** Non-native creators lack intuitive knowledge of idioms, cultural references, and domain-specific terminology, resulting in unnatural language and culturally irrelevant content that fails to measure the intended construct.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Poor performance of benchmarks like ScaLA-IS (parliamentary transcription artifacts) and GED (misused error categories); contrast with high OK rate for human-localized WinoGrande-IS.
*   **Break condition:** Native speakers are integrated into design, translation, and validation phases to ensure cultural and linguistic authenticity.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Flawed benchmarks risk invalid evaluation outcomes, potentially misguiding model development.
*   **Mechanism:** LLM scores on flawed benchmarks reflect sensitivity to artifacts (e.g., translation errors) rather than true capability. These scores then contaminate leaderboards, model comparisons, and research directions.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Paper's core finding that most common Icelandic benchmarks are invalid; citations to neighboring work on contamination and cultural dominance in evaluations.
*   **Break condition:** Benchmarks are community-vetted, audited for contamination, and their limitations are explicitly documented when used.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Construct Validity (Psychometrics)**
    *   *Why needed:* The paper's critique centers on benchmarks lacking construct validity—they fail to measure the intended ability (Icelandic comprehension) due to translation artifacts.
    *   *Quick check:* How does a multiple-choice question where the wrong options are nonsensical due to bad translation threaten construct validity?
*   **Concept: Inter-Rater Reliability (Krippendorff's α)**
    *   *Why needed:* The study quantifies annotator agreement on flaw labels. Understanding weighted ordinal α and its thresholds (e.g., >0.8 is good) is key to assessing evidence strength.
    *   *Quick check:* Why might Krippendorff's α be low for HellaSwag-IS even if all annotators agreed it was "Invalid"?
*   **Concept: Machine Translation Quality Assessment**
    *   *Why needed:* The paper references WMT scores but argues fluency is insufficient. Knowing standard MT metrics (BLEU, human evaluation scales) explains why "good enough" MT fails for high-stakes test construction.
    *   *Quick check:* Why might a WMT human evaluation score of ~82/100 be inadequate for creating a reading comprehension benchmark?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Benchmarks (Source English/Icelandic) -> Data Processing Pipeline (MT/LLM Generators/Human Translation) -> Sampling Module -> Triple Annotation (Expert Annotators) -> Analysis Module (IRR Calculation, Label Proportion Aggregation) -> Comparative Interpretation.
*   **Critical path:** Dataset selection -> Sampling strategy application -> Triple annotation -> Krippendorff's α calculation -> Mean label proportion per benchmark -> Grouping by creation method -> Result comparison.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   Sample size (fixed 250/10%) vs. coverage: Feasible but increases uncertainty for small benchmarks.
    *   Label granularity (IC/F/OK) vs. reliability: Coarse labels enabled analysis but merged distinct error types (factual vs. grammatical).
    *   Ordinal vs. categorical IRR: Weighted α suits ordered labels but complicates interpretation of "questionable" agreement.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Low Krippendorff's α (<0.4) indicating ambiguous guidelines or inconsistent flaws.
    *   Near-0% OK rate in a sample signals catastrophic creation process failure.
    *   Discrepancy between benchmark versions (e.g., ARC-IS 20% OK vs. ARC-Challenge-IS 55% OK) points to process differences.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  Replicate the study on a new low-resource language (e.g., Faroese) using identical sampling/annotation to test generalizability of the MT-synthetic vs. human-verified gap.
    2.  MT system ablation: For one source benchmark (e.g., ARC), generate translations using multiple MT systems and evaluate with the same protocol to quantify flaw variation across engines.
    3.  Downstream impact test: Train a small Icelandic LLM on a flawed dataset (e.g., HellaSwag-IS) vs. a verified one (e.g., WinoGrande-IS), then test on a clean, human-validated Icelandic held-out set to measure capability divergence.

## Limitations
*   Unknown MT engines: The specific MT systems used for most flawed benchmarks are not identified, preventing replication of the exact translation process.
*   Sample size & generalizability: Small benchmarks sampled at 10% (20-30 examples) yield higher statistical uncertainty; results may not generalize to all items in those benchmarks.
*   Label schema coarseness: The IC/F/OK labels conflate distinct error types (e.g., factual inaccuracy vs. grammatical error), limiting diagnostic precision for how to fix benchmarks.

## Confidence
*   **Unverified MT introduces severe artifacts:** High (Robustly supported by near-zero OK rates in multiple MT-derived benchmarks).
*   **Absence of native involvement causes misalignment:** High (Directly evidenced by comparison of Miðeind's human-localized high-OK benchmarks vs. unverified low-OK ones).
*   **Flawed benchmarks risk invalid evaluations & downstream harm:** Medium (Inference based on construct validity principles; lacks direct empirical measurement of impact on model rankings or training).

## Next Checks
1.  **MT system ablation:** For a single source benchmark (e.g., ARC), generate translations using 3–4 state-of-the-art MT systems (open/closed) and run the same annotation protocol to quantify flaw variation across engines.
2.  **Downstream impact test:** Train/fine-tune a small Icelandic LLM on a flawed dataset (e.g., HellaSwag-IS) vs. a verified one (e.g., WinoGrande-IS), then evaluate on a held-out, human-validated Icelandic benchmark to measure capability divergence.
3.  **Annotation guideline refinement:** Expand the labeling schema to distinguish error types (e.g., mistranslation, cultural mismatch, grammatical error) and re-annotate a subset to test if finer granularity improves Krippendorff’s α and diagnostic utility.
