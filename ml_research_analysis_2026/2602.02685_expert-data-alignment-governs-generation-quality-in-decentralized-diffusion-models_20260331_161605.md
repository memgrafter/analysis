# Expert-Data Alignment Governs Generation Quality in Decentralized Diffusion Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.02685
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02685
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Generation quality in decentralized diffusion models (DDMs) is governed by expert-data alignment, not numerical stability; sparse routing (Top-2) yields FID 22.6 vs. full ensemble's 47.9.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates Decentralized Diffusion Models (DDMs), which combine independently trained experts via inference-time routing. Contrary to common belief that minimizing trajectory sensitivity should improve generation quality, the authors find a stability-quality dissociation: the most numerically stable routing (full ensemble averaging) produces the worst FID. Instead, they identify expert-data alignment—routing inputs to experts whose training distribution matches the current denoising state—as the key determinant of quality. Validation across two DDM systems shows that sparse routing selects experts with closer data clusters, produces more accurate velocity predictions, and that high expert disagreement correlates with perceptual degradation. The findings imply that DDM deployment should prioritize alignment over stability metrics.

## Method Summary
The study uses two DDM setups: Paris DDM with eight DiT-XL/2 experts trained on LAION-Aesthetics partitions via DINOv2 embeddings, and an MNIST DDM with ten digit-specific UNet experts. Experts are trained independently; a router (DiT-B/2 or CNN) is trained post-hoc to predict expert assignments. Routing strategies compared include full ensemble (all experts weighted), Top-2 (select two experts), and Top-1 (hard selection). Generation quality is measured by FID and LPIPS, while alignment is quantified via DINOv2 embedding distances to training cluster centroids. The analysis reveals that sparse routing maintains high expert-data alignment, leading to better FID, while full ensemble, despite low trajectory sensitivity (bL_eff) and good numerical convergence (Δrefine), suffers from OOD expert processing and high disagreement.

## Key Results
- Full ensemble routing achieves the lowest trajectory sensitivity (bL_eff) and best numerical convergence (Δrefine) but the worst FID (47.9 vs. 22.6 for Top-2).
- Top-2 routing selects experts with significantly closer training clusters (mean cluster rank ~1.96 vs. 4.5 random) and lower angular deviation from the blended velocity (29% improvement).
- Trajectory-integrated expert disagreement positively correlates with perceptual degradation (higher LPIPS).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Stability–Quality Dissociation
Numerical stability (low trajectory sensitivity) does not determine generation quality in DDMs; full ensemble averaging reduces variance in expert velocity predictions, yielding smoother vector fields and better numerical convergence. However, this forces all experts to process inputs, causing most to operate far outside their training distribution. The averaged velocity, while stable, points toward an incoherent off-manifold compromise. Evidence: full ensemble has lowest bL_eff and Δrefine yet highest FID. Break condition: if stability metrics correlated positively with FID across routing strategies, the dissociation would fail.

### Mechanism 2: Expert-Data Alignment Governs Quality
Generation quality depends on routing inputs to experts whose training data distribution matches the current denoising state. Sparse routing (Top-k) selects a small set of highly aligned experts, whose predictions can be meaningfully combined. Evidence: Top-2 achieves mean cluster rank ~1.96 vs. 4.5 random; selected experts produce 29% lower angular deviation from blended velocity; independent MNIST validation shows 43% lower angular deviation. Break condition: if sparse routing did not select closer clusters or selected experts did not produce more aligned predictions, the hypothesis fails.

### Mechanism 3: Expert Disagreement as Failure Signal
High disagreement among expert velocity predictions correlates with perceptual degradation (higher LPIPS). When experts process OOD inputs, their specialized models produce divergent predictions. Full ensemble maximizes exposure to such misalignment, integrating high-disagreement signals. Evidence: LPIPS to Top-2 reference increases monotonically with trajectory-integrated disagreement quartiles. Break condition: if no correlation existed between integrated disagreement and LPIPS, disagreement would not explain quality loss.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Decentralized Diffusion Models (DDMs)
- Why needed: Understanding that DDMs involve independently trained experts combined at inference via a router (not a jointly trained MoE) is crucial for interpreting instability and disagreement findings.
- Quick check: How does DDM differ from a standard Mixture-of-Experts layer in a transformer?

### Concept: Trajectory-Local Sensitivity (bL_eff)
- Why needed: This Jacobian-based metric challenges the prior belief that minimizing it improves quality. Grasping its definition (max Jacobian norm along trajectory) clarifies what "numerical stability" means and why it dissociates from FID.
- Quick check: What does a high bL_eff value imply about how perturbations propagate along a denoising trajectory?

### Concept: Expert-Data Alignment via Cluster Proximity
- Why needed: The core principle relies on measuring whether the router sends samples to experts with similar training data. Knowing that alignment is quantified by Euclidean distance in a DINOv2 semantic embedding space is key.
- Quick check: Why is DINOv2 embedding used, and what would be a limitation of using pixel-space distance instead?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map:
Router -> Weighted selection -> Experts (K independent diffusion UNets/DiTs) -> Velocity sum -> Solver (Euler/Heun) -> Updated state. At each step: router computes probabilities p_t(k|x_t); strategy selects/weights experts; selected experts evaluate v_k(x_t); weighted sum v_t(x_t); solver updates x_t.

### Critical path:
At each denoising step t: (1) Router computes p_t(k|x_t). (2) Strategy selects/weights experts. (3) Selected experts compute v_k(x_t). (4) Weighted sum yields v_t(x_t). (5) Solver updates x_t -> x_{t-1}. Repeat until t=0.

### Design tradeoffs:
- Stability vs. Alignment: Full ensemble maximizes numerical stability but minimizes alignment (experts on OOD data). Sparse routing sacrifices some smoothness to maximize alignment.
- Router capacity vs. Specialization: Strong expert specialization amplifies alignment benefits but increases disagreement when misrouted; router must accurately recognize cluster membership at noisy states.
- Inference cost: Full ensemble requires K forward passes; Top-2 requires only k, plus cheap router evaluation.

### Failure signatures:
- High integrated expert disagreement (Dint) under full ensemble correlates with high LPIPS to Top-2 references.
- Low router confidence (high entropy) is not a reliable predictor of poor quality; it's natural in full ensemble.
- High bL_eff alone does not predict failure (weak correlation with Δrefine); but within a strategy, very high bL_eff may indicate a numerically sensitive sample.
- Switching-induced artifacts (for hard Top-1): high vector-field gap combined with low router margin causes jumps.

### First 3 experiments:
1. Cluster distance analysis: For n samples, run Top-2 and full ensemble; at timesteps t∈{0.3,0.5,0.7}, decode latents to images, extract DINOv2 embeddings, compute Euclidean distances to 8 cluster centroids, record selected expert ranks. Expect Top-2 mean rank ~2, full ~4.5.
2. Per-expert angular deviation: Under Top-2, compute blended velocity v_blend. For every expert (selected and non-selected), compute angular deviation between v_k and v_blend. Expect selected experts show significantly lower deviation.
3. Disagreement–quality correlation: Under full ensemble, compute per-sample integrated disagreement Dint = ∫ ||v_i - v_j||^2 dt. Compute LPIPS between full-ensemble output and matched Top-2 output. Bin by Dint quartiles; expect LPIPS to increase monotonically.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
What specific training objectives can enhance expert robustness to out-of-distribution inputs in decentralized diffusion models?
- Basis: "Future work should explore training objectives that improve expert robustness to out-of-distribution inputs" (Section 7).
- Why unresolved: Full ensemble fails because experts process OOD data, but no training-time solutions are proposed.
- What evidence would resolve: Introduce regularization losses penalizing expert predictions on inputs far from training clusters; evaluate alignment and FID.

### Open Question 2
What alternative sensitivity metrics better predict sample degradation in DDMs beyond trajectory-local Lipschitz constants?
- Basis: "Weak correlations between L_eff and Δrefine ... limit predictive utility ... Future work should explore alternative sensitivity metrics" (Appendix E).
- Why unresolved: bL_eff shows low correlation with step-refinement error (ρ < 0.08).
- Evidence: Propose metrics (e.g., integrated Jacobian norms, switching frequency); validate by correlation with LPIPS and FID.

### Open Question 3
Why do DDM trajectories naturally remain in low-sensitivity regions during sampling, enabling numerical convergence?
- Basis: "We do not provide a proof that the exact probability flow dynamics must enter and remain in such low-sensitivity regions ... developing a dynamical explanation is an important direction" (Appendix E).
- Why unresolved: The convergence argument assumes bounded L_eff a priori but does not explain why typical trajectories satisfy this.
- Evidence: Theoretical analysis of routed ODE’s invariant sets; empirical mapping of sensitivity basins and attraction properties across timesteps.

### Open Question 4
What underlying mechanisms fundamentally cause the stability–quality dissociation in DDMs?
- Basis: Inferred: full ensemble achieves lowest sensitivity yet worst FID, while sparse Top-2 achieves higher sensitivity but better quality; trade-off noted but not mechanistically explained.
- Why unresolved: Unclear whether alignment dominance stems from gradient directionality, error cancellation, or manifold adherence; current metrics are correlational.
- Evidence: Controlled counterfactual experiments manipulating expert predictions to decouple alignment from Jacobian norms; measure causal impact on FID.

### Open Question 5
How would eigenvalue-based stability analysis (e.g., logarithmic norms) alter our understanding of DDM trajectory sensitivity compared to Jacobian spectral norms?
- Basis: Inferred: Analysis uses Jacobian spectral norms and notes: "We do not estimate Jacobian eigenvalues or logarithmic norms ... this as a limitation" (Section 6.2.2).
- Why unresolved: Spectral norms provide worst-case bounds but may miss directional stability; eigenvalues could reveal perturbation growth modes.
- Evidence: Compute Jacobian eigenvalue distributions along trajectories for different routing strategies; correlate eigenvalue magnitudes and logarithmic norms with Δrefine and FID.

## Limitations
- Experiments limited to two DDM setups (Paris with 8 semantic clusters, MNIST digit experts); generalization to other expert counts, data distributions, and architectures is open.
- Alignment measurement relies on DINOv2 embeddings and Euclidean distance to precomputed centroids; alternative embeddings or partitioning may yield different rankings.
- Router training details (loss, data, scheduling) are underspecified, hindering replication and bias analysis.
- Sampling hyperparameters (noise schedule, CFG guidance, step count) are inferred but not explicitly listed; could affect sensitivity and FID comparisons.

## Confidence
- Stability–quality dissociation: High (consistently observed across both systems using multiple stability metrics and FID).
- Expert-data alignment governs quality: Medium (supported by cluster rank, angular deviation, MNIST replication; but reliance on specific embedding and cluster definition leaves room for alternative explanations).
- Expert disagreement as failure signal: Medium (strong correlation with LPIPS, but integrated metric may not capture all degradation sources; causal link unestablished).

## Next Checks
1. Generalize across expert counts and datasets: Train DDMs with 4, 16, and 32 experts using semantic (k-means) vs. random partitions on ImageNet-100. Re-evaluate dissociation and alignment effects on FID and LPIPS.
2. Embedding ablation: Replace DINOv2 with CLIP-ViT/B-32 and ResNet-50 embeddings in Paris DDM. Recompute cluster ranks for Top-2 and full ensemble; verify alignment metrics remain predictive of quality.
3. Alignment intervention in full ensemble: Modify full ensemble weighting by multiplying router probabilities with alignment scores (inverse cluster distance). Compare FID/LPIPS against standard full ensemble and Top-2; improvement would substantiate alignment as governing factor.
