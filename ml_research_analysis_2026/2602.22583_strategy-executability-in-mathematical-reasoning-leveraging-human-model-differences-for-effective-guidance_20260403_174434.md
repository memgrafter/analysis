# Strategy Executability in Mathematical Reasoning: Leveraging Human-Model Differences for Effective Guidance
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22583
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22583
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: SSR improves accuracy by up to +13 points on AIME25 and +5 on Apex for compact reasoning models, consistently outperforming baselines.

## Executive Summary
Mathematical reasoning models often fail to execute strategies derived from successful solutions when used as inference-time guidance. This paper addresses this instability by distinguishing strategy usage from executability and proposing Selective Strategy Retrieval (SSR). SSR constructs a heterogeneous knowledge graph of strategies, retrieves candidates via multiple routes, and ranks them using an executability predictor trained on actual guidance outcomes. Experiments across multiple benchmarks show significant and consistent improvements over direct solving, in-context learning, and single-source guidance, while reducing common failure modes.

## Method Summary
SSR operates by first extracting strategies from a corpus of human and model solutions to build a heterogeneous knowledge graph linking problems, strategies, and categories. A graph neural network is trained with contrastive loss to embed problem-strategy pairs based on executability labels obtained by running the target model with strategy guidance. At inference, for a new problem, SSR retrieves candidate strategies through three complementary routes: category-conditioned, problem-transfer, and semantic fallback. These candidates are scored by a logistic predictor using features like semantic similarity and graph embeddings, then the top strategies are selected and formatted as guidance for the target model.

## Key Results
- SSR yields gains of up to +13 points on AIME25 and +5 points on Apex compared to baselines.
- It consistently outperforms direct solving, in-context learning, and single-source (human-only or model-only) guidance.
- Ablations confirm the necessity of all three retrieval routes.
- SSR reduces both structural and algebraic failure modes in model reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Multi-route retrieval ensures coverage
- **Claim**: Multi-route retrieval ensures coverage of diverse executability signals.
- **Mechanism**: SSR aggregates strategies from three complementary routes: category-conditioned (broad patterns), problem-transfer (fine-grained context), and semantic fallback (coverage when evidence is sparse). This mitigates the risk of missing executable strategies due to any single signal.
- **Core assumption**: Executability is multi-faceted and cannot be captured by a single notion of relevance.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract and section 4.3 describe the routes; corpus evidence weak on multi-route executability retrieval.
- **Break condition**: If one route dominates or the candidate set becomes too noisy, ranking may fail; also if the graph lacks coverage for a domain, the semantic fallback may be insufficient.

### Mechanism 2: Source-aware strategy selection
- **Claim**: Source-aware strategy selection leverages complementary strengths of human and model strategies.
- **Mechanism**: SSR retains strategies preferentially from whichever source (human or model) shows higher empirical executability for a given category, as identified in HM-ReasoningBench. This combines human structural strategies with model procedural ones where they are most effective.
- **Core assumption**: Human and model strategies exhibit systematic, domain-dependent reversals in executability.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract highlights systematic dissociation; section 3.4 Figure 4(b) shows usage vs. executability divergence.
- **Break condition**: For problems where neither source has a clear advantage, source weighting may not help; noisy source labels in training data can degrade performance.

### Mechanism 3: Executability predictor trained on actual outcomes
- **Claim**: Training an executability predictor on actual guidance outcomes yields a reliable estimator of strategy utility for the target model.
- **Mechanism**: For each problem-strategy pair, SSR runs the target model with the strategy as guidance multiple times, estimates success probability via a Beta-Binomial posterior, and trains a supervised classifier (logistic over features like semantic alignment, graph proximity, route/source flags). At test time the predictor scores candidates without extra model calls.
- **Core assumption**: Executability can be approximated by a function of observable features and supervised outcomes from a fixed protocol.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 4.4 defines utility and training; section 5.3 shows SSR beats baselines.
- **Break condition**: If the target model changes (architecture or decoding) the predictor may not transfer; sparse training coverage for test problems leads to unreliable predictions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Strategy abstraction  
  *Why needed here*: SSR operates on high-level strategy hints, not full solutions. Accurate extraction of reusable patterns populates the knowledge graph with meaningful nodes.  
  *Quick check*: Given a worked solution, can you identify 3–5 reasoning techniques that generalize beyond the problem?

- **Concept**: Heterogeneous knowledge graph  
  *Why needed here*: The graph encodes relationships among problems, strategies, and categories, enabling structure-aware retrieval and representation learning.  
  *Quick check*: How would you design node and edge types to capture usage across categories while preserving source information?

- **Concept**: Contrastive executability supervision  
  *Why needed here*: Graph embeddings are trained to separate executable from non-executable problem-strategy pairs, providing features for the predictor.  
  *Quick check*: Write the InfoNCE loss that pulls positive pairs together and pushes negatives apart in this setting.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Strategy Extractor -> Knowledge Graph Builder -> Graph Encoder (contrastive training) -> Problem Embedder -> Multi-Route Retriever (A, B, C) -> Executability Predictor -> Strategy Ranker & Selector -> Guidance Prompter -> Target Model
- **Critical path**: New problem -> embed with SentenceTransformer -> find similar problems (neighbors) -> retrieve candidates via three routes -> assemble candidate set S(x) -> compute executability features ϕ(x,s) -> predict utility with logistic model (calibrated) -> rank and select top-U strategies -> construct guidance prompt -> call target model -> evaluate outcome.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Graph size vs retrieval speed: larger graphs increase coverage but slow queries.
  - Route balance: all routes necessary, but heavy fallback can introduce noise.
  - Predictor complexity: more features improve accuracy but risk overfitting; logistic model offers simplicity.
  - Calibration: temperature scaling essential for cross-route score comparability.
  - Supervision trials: more trials improve posterior estimates but increase evaluation cost.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Low candidate diversity: all retrieved strategies from one source/category → graph or route imbalance.
  - Predictor overconfidence: high predicted utility but low real success → distribution shift or poor features.
  - Empty candidate pool: triggers fallback but may yield irrelevant hints.
  - High variance in posterior estimates: too few trials per pair → noisy supervision.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Main evaluation: compare SSR against DS, ICL, H-only, M-only on HM-ReasoningBench, AIME25, Apex (Table 1). Shows consistent gains.
  2. Ablation study: disable each retrieval route and measure accuracy drop (Table 2). Confirms necessity of all three routes.
  3. Strategy adherence check: sample 100 problems, verify if provided strategies are correctly executed (Figure 10). Gains correlate with actual use, not just longer outputs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can training be modified to improve models' execution of human-like strategies? The paper identifies the gap as a diagnostic signal but offers no training solutions.
- What standardized evaluation can directly assess strategy executability across models? Current benchmarks lack executability labels; a dataset with such scores is needed.
- Does ignoring the ordering of strategies in guidance degrade performance? SSR treats strategies as unordered sets; dependencies between steps are not investigated.

## Limitations
- Graph construction details (strategy merging, edge weighting, negative sampling) are underspecified, hindering reproducibility.
- Executability predictor training specifics (feature definitions, validation size, per-model retraining) remain unclear.
- Computational overhead of generating trial outcomes for training and inference is unanalyzed; scalability is unknown.
- Dependence on the strategy extractor's quality; extraction errors propagate to the knowledge graph.

## Confidence
- Multi-route retrieval necessity: High (ablations confirm)
- Source-aware selection: Medium (evidence of systematic divergence on HM-ReasoningBench, but cross-domain generalization untested)
- Predictor reliability: Medium (consistent gains, but sensitive to model shift and sparse supervision)

## Next Checks
1. Systematically vary graph construction rules (merging, weighting, negative sampling) to measure impact on accuracy.
2. Evaluate predictor transferability across different target models without retraining; quantify accuracy drop and recalibration needs.
3. Audit per-problem candidate distribution across routes on validation set; check for route dominance and correlate with failure modes.
