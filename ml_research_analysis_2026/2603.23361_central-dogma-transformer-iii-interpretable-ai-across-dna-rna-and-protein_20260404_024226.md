# Central Dogma Transformer III: Interpretable AI Across DNA, RNA, and Protein
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.23361
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23361
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: A two-stage Virtual Cell Embedder model that jointly predicts RNA and protein changes, improving RNA prediction by 4.9% (r=0.843 vs. 0.804) and achieving high protein accuracy (r=0.969), while using downstream protein supervision to sharpen DNA-level interpretability by 30% (CTCF enrichment).

## Executive Summary
The Central Dogma Transformer III (CDT-III) introduces a biologically structured, two-stage transformer architecture that explicitly models the spatial separation of transcription (nuclear) and translation (cytosolic) to create an interpretable model of gene regulation. By adding a protein prediction task, the model not only improves RNA prediction accuracy through multi-task regularization but also enhances the biological relevance of its DNA-level attention maps, as measured by CTCF binding site enrichment. The trained model enables gradient-based *in silico* profiling of a gene's effect on all surface proteins from baseline data alone, demonstrated by correctly predicting all 29 detectable protein changes from a CD52 perturbation and rediscovering 5/7 known clinical side effects.

## Method Summary
CDT-III employs a two-stage Virtual Cell Embedder (VCE). VCE-N processes DNA (Enformer embeddings) and RNA expression to produce a shared RNA embedding (`e_RNA`). VCE-C then uses `e_RNA` plus protein expression to generate a protein embedding (`e_Prot`) and final predictions. The model is trained with a combined loss: `L = MSE(RNA) + λ·MSE(Protein)`, where λ=0.1 and the protein loss is masked to only the 65 detectably expressed proteins. Training occurs in two phases: first, a pre-trained CDT-II model's VCE-N weights are loaded and frozen while VCE-C is trained; second, all weights are unfrozen and fine-tuned together. This design preserves the exact fusion dimensions of the prior CDT-II model, enabling successful weight transfer.

## Key Results
- Multi-task learning improves RNA prediction: per-gene mean Pearson `r` increases from 0.804 (single-task) to 0.843 with protein supervision.
- High-fidelity protein prediction: per-gene mean `r` reaches 0.969 on held-out data for the 65 expressed proteins.
- Interpretability sharpening: DNA→RNA cross-attention shows a 30% increase in CTCF binding site enrichment (from 6.6× to 8.59×) with protein supervision.
- CD52 perturbation case study: Model correctly predicts the direction of all 29 detectable protein changes and, via gradient analysis, rediscovers 5/7 known Alemtuzumab side effects from perturbation data alone, without requiring new experiments.
- Gradient-based side-effect profiling: Correlates strongly (r=0.939) with actual perturbation results, enabling screening of all 2,361 modeled genes from a single baseline measurement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Multi-Task Regularization via Distal Supervision
Adding the protein prediction task (`L_Prot`) provides a distal supervisory signal that backpropagates through VCE-C into the shared RNA embedding (`e_RNA`) and further into VCE-N. This gradient flow regularizes the shared DNA and RNA representations, sharpening features that are predictive of both RNA and protein outputs, thereby reducing overfitting to RNA-specific noise and improving RNA prediction accuracy.

**Core assumption:** Regulatory features relevant for predicting protein changes overlap sufficiently with those needed for predicting RNA changes, allowing the downstream task to provide a complementary learning signal.

**Evidence anchors:**
- Abstract: RNA `r` improves from 0.804 to 0.843 with protein loss.
- Section 5.2: Attributes improvement to protein loss gradients sharpening shared representations.
- Corpus: Weak/absent; neighboring papers discuss multi-modal integration but not this specific regularization from a downstream protein task on an upstream RNA task in a causal architecture.

**Break condition:** If the determinants of RNA and protein responses are fundamentally distinct (e.g., protein changes dominated by post-translational modifications unrelated to transcript abundance), the protein gradient signal could become noise and degrade RNA learning.

### Mechanism 2: Downstream Supervision Sharpens Upstream Interpretability
Gradients from `L_Prot` flow back through the shared `e_RNA` embedding into VCE-N's DNA→RNA cross-attention weights. Since `e_RNA` encodes information derived from DNA self-attention and cross-attention, this distal signal fine-tunes the DNA-attention to focus more strongly on genomic positions (like CTCF sites) that are consequential for the final protein phenotype, increasing their enrichment in the attention maps.

**Core assumption:** Genomic positions important for predicting protein-level outcomes (e.g., CTCF sites involved in 3D chromatin organization) can be identified via the gradient signal propagated from the protein output, even though protein data does not directly input to the DNA pathway.

**Evidence anchors:**
- Abstract: CTCF enrichment increases by 30%.
- Section 5.3: Describes how backpropagated protein loss gradients sharpen DNA attention toward functionally relevant sites.
- Corpus: Weak/absent; neighbors focus on general attention interpretability, not on how downstream supervision improves upstream attention alignment with external genomics data.

**Break condition:** If the model's protein predictions rely on features entirely decoupled from DNA-level regulatory elements (e.g., saturation of translational machinery), protein gradients would not provide a meaningful signal to sharpen DNA-attention.

### Mechanism 3: Gradient-Based Side Effect Profiling from Baseline Data
The trained model encodes a stable mapping from RNA expression patterns to protein expression changes. The gradient of a specific protein's predicted abundance with respect to a gene's RNA expression (`∂ˆy_Prot,j / ∂x_RNA,g`) estimates the sensitivity of that protein to changes in that gene's transcriptional output. Computing these gradients using a baseline (non-targeting control) RNA profile approximates the directional effect of perturbing that gene, enabling *in silico* screening without conducting a new perturbation experiment.

**Core assumption:** The model's learned Jacobian reflects stable, causal relationships between gene expression and protein levels; these relationships are consistent enough that gradients from a baseline state predict the direction of change under perturbation.

**Evidence anchors:**
- Abstract: Gradient-based profiling requires no perturbation data (r=0.939 vs. perturbation-based analysis).
- Section 3.4 & 5.4: Shows high correlation (r=0.939) and 96.9% direction agreement between gradient predictions and actual perturbation results.
- Corpus: Weak/absent; neighbors on drug safety use network proximity or chemical structure, not gradient-based profiling from a multi-modal central dogma model.

**Break condition:** If the system operates in a highly non-linear regime where the effect of a small change in a gene's expression depends strongly on the specific baseline state (i.e., gradients are not constant), then gradients from a single baseline may poorly predict the effect of a large perturbation. Model overfitting could also yield gradients that reflect noise.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Central Dogma Information Flow & Spatial Compartmentalization**  
  **Why needed:** The entire architecture (VCE-N for nucleus/transcription, VCE-C for cytosol/translation) is built on mirroring the biological causal structure. Understanding that transcription and translation are separated in space and time is key to why the two-stage design works and a single-stage fails.  
  **Quick check:** "Why must the model have separate computational stages for DNA→RNA and RNA→Protein, rather than one joint fusion layer?"

- **Concept: Multi-Task Learning & Gradient-Based Regularization**  
  **Why needed:** The core finding that RNA prediction improves with the addition of a protein task is a non-trivial multi-task effect. It requires understanding how loss gradients from multiple heads interact with shared representations.  
  **Quick check:** "If `L_Prot` provides a supervisory signal for `e_RNA`, why doesn't it simply force `e_RNA` to predict proteins directly, degrading `L_RNA`?"

- **Concept: Attention Maps as Mechanistic Explanations**  
  **Why needed:** CDT-III's interpretability claim rests on attention weights corresponding to specific biological processes (chromatin interactions, transcriptional control, translational control). Validation is done via external data (CTCF ChIP-seq, Hi-C).  
  **Quick check:** "How does the paper validate that the DNA→RNA cross-attention map is not just a learned pattern but corresponds to biologically meaningful regulatory interactions?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Inputs: Enformer DNA embeddings (D_ℓ, 896x3072) + RNA expression (x_RNA, 2361) -> VCE-N (DNA projector, 2x DNA self-attn, RNA encoder, 1x RNA self-attn, DNA→RNA cross-attn, VCE pooling) -> e_RNA (512) -> VCE-C (Protein encoder, 1x protein self-attn, RNA→Protein cross-attn, VCE pooling) + Protein expression (x_Prot, 189) -> e_Prot (512) -> Heads: RNA MLP (e_RNA -> 2361), Protein MLP (e_Prot -> 189)

- **Critical path:** D_ℓ & x_RNA -> VCE-N -> e_RNA -> VCE-C (with x_Prot) -> e_Prot -> (ŷ_RNA, ŷ_Prot). Gradient for L_Prot flows backward through VCE-C into e_RNA and then into all of VCE-N.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Two-stage vs. Single-stage:** Two-stage preserves VCE-N's pre-trained fusion dimensions (d×2), enabling transfer from CDT-II. A single-stage model that adds protein to the fusion input (d×3) breaks weight transfer and causes catastrophic failure in RNA learning.
  - **Protein loss weight (λ):** λ=0.1 balances RNA (MSE on 2361 genes) and protein (MSE on 65 expressed proteins only) objectives. Too high (λ=1.0) dominates training and harms RNA learning; too low may not provide sufficient regularization.
  - **Protein masking:** L_Prot computed only on 65 detectably expressed proteins (DSB mean>0.5) to prevent trivial zero-prediction learning for 124 non-expressed proteins.

- **Failure signatures:**
  1. **Single-stage VCE models:** RNA per-cell `r` stalls below ~0.37 despite transferring encoders and self-attention layers due to incompatible fusion dimensions (d×2 → d×3) preventing weight loading.
  2. **Protein task collapse:** If λ is too high (>0.5), RNA performance degrades; if too low (<0.01), protein task may not train adequately, reducing regularization benefit.
  3. **Poor interpretability:** Low CTCF enrichment (<6×) or Hi-C contact ratio (~1.0×) indicates DNA-attention is not aligning with functional 3D chromatin architecture, suggesting insufficient protein supervision or architectural flaw.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate single-stage ablation: Train a 1-stage VCE with d×3 fusion input. Expect RNA per-cell `r` < 0.4, confirming weight transfer dependency on exact VCE-N dimensions.
  2. λ ablation sweep: Train CDT-III with λ ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5}. Monitor RNA `r` and protein `r`. Expect a peak around 0.1 where RNA improves over CDT-II baseline (0.804) and protein reaches >0.95.
  3. CTCF enrichment sanity check: For a held-out gene (e.g., CD55), compute DNA→RNA cross-attention enrichment at CTCF sites (top 10% bins). Expect >5× enrichment. If near 1.0, check that protein loss is non-zero and gradients are flowing (use gradient norms).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How do RNA→Protein cross-attention patterns correspond to known regulatory pathways to explain the pervasive mRNA-protein direction divergence observed after CD52 knockdown?  
  *Basis:* The authors state analysis of cross-attention weights to reveal compensatory mechanisms is left for future work. Unresolved because attention patterns have not been systematically analyzed.

- **Question 2:** Does CDT-III generalize to other cell types beyond K562?  
  *Basis:* All experiments use K562 due to data scarcity. Unresolved because the model is evaluated on only one cell line; performance may degrade in cells with different chromatin organization.

- **Question 3:** Can single-cell protein prediction accuracy be improved beyond the current per-protein cell-level r=0.28 to enable reliable single-cell applications?  
  *Basis:* Cell-level correlation is low, consistent with ADT measurement noise, limiting single-cell use. Unresolved because it is unclear if architectural or data improvements can close this gap.

- **Question 4:** How consistent are gradient-based side effect profiles across the full set of 2,361 genes given the weak correlation (r=0.443) and limited validation?  
  *Basis:* Gradient-based interpretability achieves r=0.443 vs. CDT-II’s r=0.83, with only 2 held-out genes testable for CTCF. Unresolved because the weak correlation and small validation set raise doubts about robustness.

- **Question 5:** Is the observed mRNA–protein direction divergence a general phenomenon across perturbations and cell types, or specific to CD52 knockdown in K562?  
  *Basis:* The conclusion of widespread divergence is based solely on CD52 data. Unresolved because systematic measurement across multiple perturbations and cell lines is lacking.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility blocker:** The pre-trained CDT-II checkpoint is not provided; reconstructing it requires unavailable training details from Ota 2026.
- **Ambiguous definitions:** Exact lists of the 2,361 RNA genes and 65 expressed proteins are undefined; any mismatch in filtering criteria breaks faithful reproduction.
- **Generalizability concern:** Validation relies on a single dataset (STING-seq v2) and a single cell type (K562), raising overfitting risks.
- **Interpretability scope:** CTCF enrichment is the sole external validation; alignment with other regulatory marks (e.g., histone modifications) is untested.
- **Gradient assumption:** Gradient method assumes an approximately linear RNA→Protein mapping; its validity for large perturbations is not established.
