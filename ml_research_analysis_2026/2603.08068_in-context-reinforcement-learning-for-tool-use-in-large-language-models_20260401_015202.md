# In-Context Reinforcement Learning for Tool Use in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.08068
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08068
- **Reference Count**: 12
- **Primary Result**: ICRL achieves SOTA on QA benchmarks (40.16 EM for Qwen2.5-3B, 49.12 EM for Qwen2.5-7B) and matches/exceeds SFT+RL baselines on math reasoning without any supervised fine-tuning.

## Executive Summary
Large language models often require external tools to solve complex tasks, but existing approaches rely on expensive supervised fine-tuning (SFT). This paper introduces In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL), an RL‑only framework that teaches tool invocation by embedding few‑shot demonstrations directly in the rollout prompt and gradually phasing them out to zero‑shot. ICRL combines accuracy and format rewards while masking tool outputs during optimization. Experiments on question‑answering and math reasoning benchmarks show significant gains over prior methods, demonstrating high data efficiency and scalability across model sizes without any SFT.

## Method Summary
ICRL operates in two phases. In the initial phase, the policy is provided with prompts that include several in‑context demonstrations of successful tool use. The model generates responses, invoking tools as needed; the tool outputs are masked to prevent the policy from relying on them directly during reward computation. A reward function combines task accuracy (e.g., exact match) and a formatting bonus for correctly structured tool calls. These rollouts are used to update the model via reinforcement learning (likely PPO). In the subsequent phase, the number of in‑context demonstrations is gradually reduced until the model can invoke tools correctly in a zero‑shot setting, thereby eliminating the need for any SFT data.

## Key Results
- On QA benchmarks, ICRL attains average exact match of 40.16 for Qwen2.5‑3B (+8.94 over Search‑R1) and 49.12 for Qwen2.5‑7B (+7.34 over ParallelSearch).
- On AIME2024/2025 math reasoning tasks, ICRL matches or exceeds an SFT+RL baseline despite using no supervised fine‑tuning.
- The approach shows strong data efficiency and scales effectively across different model sizes within the Qwen2.5 family.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The in‑context demonstrations provide an immediate behavioral prior for tool invocation, guiding exploration during early RL stages. By masking tool outputs, the policy learns to rely on its own decision‑making rather than copying provided results. The combination of accuracy and format rewards ensures that the model not only produces correct answers but also adheres to the required tool‑call syntax, which is essential for reliable deployment. The gradual reduction of demonstrations smoothly transitions the policy from imitation to autonomous tool use, preventing performance collapse.

## Foundational Learning
- **In‑Context Learning**: Enables the model to adapt from examples within the prompt; necessary for bootstrapping without weight updates.
- **Reinforcement Learning**: Optimizes the policy through reward signals; core of ICRL’s learning loop.
- **Tool Use in LLMs**: Integration of external APIs extends model capabilities; motivates the need for robust invocation.
- **Reward Shaping**: Combining accuracy and format rewards guides both correctness and structural compliance.
- **Demonstration Scheduling**: Gradual reduction of in‑context examples mitigates distribution shift; critical for zero‑shot transfer.
- **Output Masking**: Prevents the policy from exploiting tool outputs directly, encouraging genuine decision‑making.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Prompt Constructor (few‑shot demos + query) → LLM Policy → Tool Executor → Reward Computer (accuracy + format) → RL Optimizer (e.g., PPO) → Updated Policy  

**Critical Path**: Starting from a query, the prompt is constructed, the policy generates a response (possibly with tool calls), tools are executed, rewards are computed, and the policy is updated. The cycle repeats with the updated policy.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- Few‑shot vs zero‑shot: More demonstrations improve initial performance but may hinder generalization; schedule determines transition speed.  
- Reward weighting: Accuracy vs format trade‑off; over‑emphasis on format may sacrifice correctness.  
- Masking: Essential for preventing shortcut learning but may slow early learning.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- High format reward but low accuracy (reward hacking).  
- Rapid performance drop when demonstrations are removed (poor scheduling).  
- Tool calls with incorrect arguments or missing parameters.

**First Experiments**:  
1. Ablate the format reward to measure impact on correctness and syntax adherence.  
2. Vary the demonstration reduction schedule (linear, exponential) and compare final zero‑shot performance.  
3. Replace RL with simple fine‑tuning to confirm the necessity of the RL loop.

## Limitations
- The RL algorithm details (e.g., PPO hyperparameters, KL penalties) and reward shaping ablations are insufficiently documented.
- The curation, quality, and failure modes of the in‑context demonstrations during the transition to zero‑shot are not deeply analyzed.
- Novelty relative to concurrent in‑context RL work (e.g., Vintix, LLMs as In‑Context Bandit Learners) remains unclear without direct comparisons.

## Confidence
- **Empirical results on QA/Math benchmarks**: High  
- **Claim of being RL‑only (no SFT)**: Medium (initial demonstrations may act as implicit SFT data)  
- **Scalability and data efficiency across architectures**: Low‑Medium (evaluated only on Qwen2.5 family)

## Next Checks
1. Replicate ICRL on diverse model families (e.g., Llama‑3, GPT) to test architectural generalizability.  
2. Conduct systematic reward component ablation to isolate the contribution of accuracy vs. format and check for reward hacking.  
3. Perform a thorough analysis of the demonstration scheduling schedule and its impact on zero‑shot performance, including a pure zero‑shot RL baseline.
