# Feature, Alignment, and Supervision in Category Learning: A Comparative Approach with Children and Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03124  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03124  
- **Reference count**: 36  
- **Primary result**: Children and CNNs exhibit fundamentally different interaction profiles among supervision, feature structure, and perceptual alignment in category learning, despite similar overall accuracy in some conditions.

## Executive Summary
This study compares category learning in children (5–7 years) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) using a species-fair, few-shot semi-supervised task. Learners generalize from mixed labeled and unlabeled exemplar pairs under systematic variations in supervision (1/3/6 labeled pairs), diagnostic feature (size, shape, pattern), and perceptual alignment (high/low). Children show a robust shape/pattern bias and are severely impaired by low alignment, especially for size. CNNs show the opposite pattern, excelling at size/pattern but struggling with shape, and alignment disrupts supervision benefits for shape. The key finding is that the interaction profile among these factors differs fundamentally between children and CNNs, indicating that human-model correspondence depends critically on task structure, not just overall accuracy.

## Method Summary
The experiment uses novel object categories defined by a single target feature (size, shape, or pattern). Stimuli are presented as pairs: high alignment means only the target feature differs between category members; low alignment introduces variations in multiple non-target features. Children receive a mix of labeled (category membership indicated) and unlabeled exemplar pairs and are tested on 12 novel images. The CNN model is a Siamese network with a shared ResNet-18 encoder, a classification head for per-half prediction, and a projection MLP for contrastive learning. It is trained with a hybrid loss combining binary cross-entropy on labeled halves and contrastive loss on embeddings from all pairs, using AdamW for 200 epochs. Performance is measured as proportion correct on the test phase.

## Key Results
- Children exhibit a shape/pattern over size bias; low alignment severely impairs learning (especially size), with diminishing returns from additional supervision.
- CNNs show the opposite feature bias (size/pattern over shape) and improve monotonically with more supervision.
- Alignment minimally affects CNN size/pattern learning but disrupts the benefit of supervision for shape learning.

## Why This Works
The paper demonstrates that human and CNN category learning diverge in how they integrate supervision, feature structure, and perceptual alignment. This suggests fundamental differences in representational formats and learning strategies: children's robust shape bias under low alignment points to abstract, relational processing, while CNNs' sensitivity to local features and alignment indicates reliance on perceptual statistics. The task design isolates these factors, revealing that overall accuracy alone is insufficient to assess model-human correspondence; interaction profiles are critical.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Few-shot semi-supervised learning**: Why needed - to mimic natural category learning where labeled examples are scarce but unlabeled data is abundant, testing how learners leverage mixed evidence. Quick check: Can performance be maintained with only 1–6 labeled exemplars amid many unlabeled ones?  
2. **Perceptual alignment**: Why needed - to quantify how similarity among exemplars affects generalization; high alignment (minimal variation) simplifies rule extraction, while low alignment introduces nuisance variation. Quick check: Does performance drop when non-target features vary across exemplars?  
3. **Diagnostic feature**: Why needed - to compare learning across feature types (size, shape, pattern) with known human biases (e.g., shape bias). Quick check: Is shape learned more easily than size, consistent with developmental literature?  
4. **Siamese network**: Why needed - to process pairs of inputs and learn similarity metrics suitable for comparison tasks. Quick check: Does a shared encoder with pair-wise loss improve generalization over independent processing?  
5. **Contrastive learning**: Why needed - to leverage unlabeled data by clustering embeddings of same-class instances. Quick check: Does adding contrastive loss improve performance relative to pure supervised learning?  
6. **Supervised learning**: Why needed - to provide explicit category labels for the few labeled examples, establishing a baseline. Quick check: Does increasing labeled data improve accuracy monotonically?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input pair -> Shared ResNet-18 encoder -> (Classification head + Projection MLP) -> Output scores & embeddings -> Hybrid loss (BCE + Contrastive) -> Backpropagation

**Critical path**: The forward pass processes left/right images through the shared ResNet-18, producing feature vectors. These feed into two parallel heads: a linear layer with sigmoid for binary classification (target/non-target) per half, and a two-layer MLP projecting to a latent space for contrastive loss. During training, for each labeled half the BCE loss is computed; for every pair (labeled or not) the contrastive loss (cosine similarity) pulls same-class embeddings together and pushes different-class apart. The total loss guides weight updates via AdamW.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- Hybrid loss vs. pure objectives: Combining BCE and contrastive may yield better representation learning but requires balancing loss weights; the paper uses both on labeled pairs as well.
- Shared encoder: Reduces parameters and enforces symmetry but may limit capacity if left/right features require different processing (unlikely here).
- ResNet-18: Offers strong feature extraction but may be overparameterized for simple stimuli; training from scratch vs. pretraining affects sample efficiency.
- Supervision weighting: The relative weighting of BCE vs. contrastive loss is unreported; this could influence how quickly the model learns from limited labels.

**Failure signatures**: 
- Misaligned stimuli: If high-alignment pairs still vary on non-target features, the alignment manipulation fails.
- Loss implementation errors: Omitting contrastive loss on supervised pairs or miscomputing pairwise similarities.
- Data leakage: Test images inadvertently appearing in training set, inflating accuracy.
- Poor convergence: If BCE loss does not decrease when labels are present, the classification head may be miswired.
- Overfitting: Training accuracy much higher than test accuracy, especially with few labels.

**First experiments**:
1. **Loss ablation**: Train with only BCE (supervised) and only contrastive loss to isolate their contributions, particularly for shape under low alignment.
2. **Supervision curve replication**: Systematically vary the number of labeled pairs (1, 3, 6) and measure accuracy for each feature and alignment condition, checking for diminishing returns and the shape-alignment interaction.
3. **Stimulus integrity check**: Visualize a random sample of pairs for each condition to confirm that high alignment restricts variation to the target feature only, and low alignment introduces multiple non-target variations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why does perceptual alignment selectively disrupt CNN learning of shape-based categories but not size/pattern categories?** The paper notes alignment has little effect on size/pattern but severely limits supervision benefits for shape, yet the mechanism linking feature type to alignment sensitivity remains unclear.
2. **What mechanisms underlie children's robust shape bias under low alignment compared to fragility in size/pattern learning?** Children's shape learning stays high even with low alignment and minimal supervision, while size/pattern drops sharply; the developmental origins of this resilience are unknown.
3. **How do comparison versus contrast supervision labels differentially benefit semi-supervised learning depending on existing evidence?** Exploratory analysis suggests comparison labels help with more evidence (3 labels) while contrast may help with sparse evidence (1 label), but the causal mechanism is untested.
4. **Can architectural or training modifications (e.g., pretraining, contrastive objectives) induce a shape bias in CNNs similar to children?** The CNN showed a reverse feature pattern (size/pattern > shape); the authors propose pretraining with contrastive objectives or different architectures as future directions, but necessary conditions are unknown.
5. **What specific interaction profiles among supervision, feature structure, and alignment are necessary for models to achieve human-like category learning?** The study shows interactions matter, but only tests a limited factorial design; generalizability to other features, alignment metrics, or supervision regimes is unstated.

## Limitations
- Stimulus visual parameters (specific shapes, sizes, patterns, colors, resolutions) are unreported, risking dataset mismatch and affecting results.
- CNN training details (batch size, sampling strategy, loss weighting, augmentation, ResNet initialization) are incomplete, which may critically influence convergence and interaction profiles.
- Human data variability (child subject pool, testing environment) is not granularly described, limiting assessment of generalizability beyond the reported sample.

## Confidence
- Overall interaction profile divergence (children vs. CNNs): Medium (supported within paper but contingent on unreproduced details)
- Feature bias (children: shape/pattern > size; CNNs: size/pattern > shape): High (consistent across conditions and aligns with known biases)
- Alignment × supervision interaction (especially for shape in CNNs): Medium (relies on fine-grained shifts sensitive to implementation)

## Next Checks
1. **Stimulus reconstruction audit**: Generate stimuli per the paper's alignment descriptions (high alignment: only target feature varies; low alignment: multiple non-target features vary) and visually inspect 50 random pairs per condition to confirm conceptual integrity.
2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity sweep**: For the CNN, systematically vary key unknowns (BCE vs. contrastive loss weighting [0.5–2.0], batch size [16–128], with/without augmentation) to test whether the critical alignment × supervision interaction for shape persists across plausible regimes.
3. **Architecture generality test**: Replicate with a simpler CNN (e.g., 4-layer convolutional network); if the feature bias (size/pattern > shape) holds, it strengthens the claim; if it vanishes, the result may be architecture-specific.
