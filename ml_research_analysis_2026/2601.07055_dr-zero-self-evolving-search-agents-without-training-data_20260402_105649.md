# Dr. Zero: Self-Evolving Search Agents without Training Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.07055  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07055  
- **Reference Count:** 38  
- **Primary Result:** Achieves supervised-level performance on multi-hop QA without any training data via a self-evolution loop and hop-grouped policy optimization.

## Executive Summary
Dr. Zero introduces a framework for training multi-turn search agents without any external training data. It uses a closed-loop system where a proposer model generates diverse multi-hop questions, and a solver model attempts to answer them. The solver’s pass rates inform a difficulty-based reward for the proposer, encouraging harder question generation, while the solver is refined using outcome-based rewards from its answers. A key innovation is Hop-Grouped Relative Policy Optimization (HRPO), which clusters questions by hop count to estimate advantages from a single rollout per prompt, drastically reducing computational cost compared to prior nested-sampling methods. Evaluated on seven QA benchmarks, Dr. Zero with 3B/7B models matches or exceeds fully supervised agents like Search-R1, with notable gains on single-hop tasks and significantly improved efficiency over previous data-free approaches.

## Method Summary
The method centers on a proposer–solver self-evolution loop. The proposer generates multi-hop questions conditioned on a seed corpus and receives a reward based on the solver’s pass rate, which signals question difficulty and diversity. The solver is trained with outcome rewards (e.g., exact match) for its final answers. To reduce compute, HRPO groups generated questions by predicted hop count (via the proposer) and estimates advantage within each group using only one rollout per prompt, avoiding the multiple nested rollouts required by GRPO. This single-rollout estimation, combined with grouped相对 advantage, enables efficient policy updates for both models without any human-labeled data.

## Key Results
- Matches or surpasses fully supervised search agents (e.g., Search-R1) on seven QA benchmarks despite zero training data.
- Achieves up to 22.9% relative gain on single-hop tasks (e.g., HotpotQA) versus prior data-free methods.
- Improves average exact match by up to 39.9% over prior zero-data approaches with substantially lower computational overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The self-evolution loop creates a positive feedback cycle: the proposer is incentivized to generate questions that are challenging yet solvable (as indicated by non-zero solver pass rates), which in turn pushes the solver to improve its search and reasoning. HRPO makes this tractable by clustering questions of similar hop complexity—where hop count predicts task difficulty—and computing advantages relative to that group. This single-rollout estimation avoids the combinatorial explosion of nested sampling while still providing meaningful learning signals. Outcome rewards for the solver directly tie improvements to answer correctness, and the proposer’s difficulty reward naturally balances diversity and hardness.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Seed Corpus -> Proposer -> Questions (hop-grouped) -> Solver -> Answers -> Outcome Rewards -> Advantage Estimation (HRPO) -> Policy Updates (Proposer & Solver)

**Critical Path:**  
1. Proposer generates questions with predicted hop counts.  
2. Solver rollouts produce answers.  
3. Outcome rewards computed from answers.  
4. Questions clustered by hop; advantages estimated within clusters.  
5. Policy gradients applied to proposer (via difficulty reward) and solver (via outcome reward).

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- HRPO trades off precision of advantage estimation for massive compute savings; oversimplified hop clustering may conflate questions of unequal difficulty.  
- The proposer-solver loop risks mode collapse if proposer generates syntactically diverse but semantically homogeneous questions.  
- Relying on solver pass rates as a difficulty proxy may bias against questions that are naturally harder but still solvable with better search.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Proposer output shows decreasing semantic diversity (e.g., repeated templates) despite stable pass rates.  
- Solver performance degrades on multi-hop questions (>3 hops) even as single-hop improves.  
- HRPO advantage estimates show high variance within hop groups, indicating poor clustering.

**First Experiments:**  
1. Isolate proposer: generate 10k questions, cluster by hop, have humans label coherence and difficulty to validate hop-based grouping.  
2. Isolate solver: train on fixed human dataset with outcome rewards only to measure upper bound performance gap vs. full loop.  
3. Compare HRPO vs. GRPO on a fixed compute budget (e.g., 100 GPU hours) using a 1B model to quantify efficiency and stability.

## Limitations
- Synthetic questions may not capture the full complexity and ambiguity of natural human queries, creating a distributional gap.
- HRPO’s effectiveness hinges on accurate hop-count clustering; misclustering could lead to suboptimal policy updates.
- Evaluation restricted to QA benchmarks; generalizability to other agent tasks (e.g., tool-use, interactive environments) remains unproven.

## Confidence
- **Zero-training-data claim:** High (explicitly avoids external data; loop is well-defined).
- **Performance parity with supervised agents:** Medium (results on 7 benchmarks are solid but require independent replication; supervised baselines may advance).
- **Compute efficiency of HRPO vs. GRPO:** Medium (reported savings are large but depend on rollout budgets and model scale).
- **Generalizability beyond QA:** Low (no evidence yet for non-QA domains).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate HRPO efficiency gains across diverse model scales (1B–14B) and with alternative advantage estimators to confirm robustness of single-rollout clustering.
2. Test Dr. Zero on out-of-domain tasks (e.g., math theorem proving, embodied navigation) to validate transfer of self-evolution beyond extractive QA.
3. Conduct human evaluation of proposer-generated questions to assess coherence, difficulty calibration, and bias relative to human-curated benchmarks.
