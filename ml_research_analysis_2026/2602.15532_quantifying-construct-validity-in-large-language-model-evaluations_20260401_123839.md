# Quantifying construct validity in large language model evaluations
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15532
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15532
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A structured capabilities model that integrates scale as an upstream structural parameter and accounts for benchmark-specific error yields better parsimony and out-of-distribution prediction than pure latent factor or PCA-based scaling law approaches.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the LLM evaluation crisis where benchmark scores may be contaminated or poorly measured, leading to misleading assessments of model capabilities. Existing approaches either use latent factor analysis, which confounds capabilities with model scale, or observational scaling laws based on PCA, which overfit to observed tasks. The authors propose a structured capabilities model that combines the strengths of both: it includes model scale as a causal upstream parameter, uses a logistic response curve to model non-linear saturation, and isolates benchmark-specific error via latent factors. Fitted on 4,395 LLMs from the OpenLLM Leaderboard using 19 BIG-Bench Hard subtasks, the model shows improved statistical parsimony (lower AIC/BIC) compared to latent factor models and superior out-of-distribution prediction error compared to PCA-based scaling laws, while yielding more interpretable and non-scale-limited capability factors.

## Method Summary
The study analyzes 4,395 LLMs evaluated on 19 BIG-Bench Hard subtasks, with parameter counts as a measure of model scale. Raw accuracy scores are transformed using an inverse logistic function (Equation 11) to account for guessing and saturating performance, requiring subtask-specific parameters α and β. The core method is a structural equation model (SEM) implemented in lavaan: the transformed scores (B') are modeled as B' = L[w_n log(n)] + E, where L are factor loadings, w_n are structural coefficients linking log-parameter size to latent capabilities, and E captures benchmark-specific error. This structured model integrates scaling laws (through w_n) with factor analysis. For comparison, the authors fit a series of models: (a) pure EFA on raw scores, (b) EFA on scores with only a direct effect of scale on observed variables (no structural path), (c) EFA on transformed scores, and (d) the full structured model. Additionally, they replicate observational scaling laws by applying PCA to raw scores and fitting separate scaling laws to each principal component. Model fit is assessed via AIC/BIC, χ², CFI, RMSEA, SRMR, and out-of-fold prediction on held-out subtasks. Unknowns include the exact α,β values used and the factor score estimation method. Common pitfalls involve transformation producing undefined values for perfect/random scores, Heywood cases in factor models, and PCA capturing >80% variance indicating overfitting.

## Key Results
- The structured capabilities model achieves better parsimony (lower AIC/BIC) than pure EFA models, confirming that explicitly modeling scale as an upstream cause improves fit without excessive complexity.
- In out-of-distribution prediction across 19 BBH subtasks, the structured model yields lower mean squared error on average than PCA-based observational scaling laws.
- The estimated latent factors are more evenly distributed across capabilities and less correlated with model scale, providing more interpretable, non-scale-limited measures of LLM abilities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Pure latent factor analysis (EFA) confounds LLM capabilities with model scale because it ignores the upstream causal influence of parameter count. In the EFA model B = LF + E, the factor loadings L and factor scores F are estimated solely from the covariance of benchmark scores. Since model size (log(n)) strongly predicts almost all scores, the optimization consolidates this shared variance into a single dominant "general ability" factor, which correlates highly (R² ≈ 0.47) with log-parameter size. Thus, Factor 1 acts as a proxy for scale rather than a distinct capability. The core assumption—that observed scores are generated only by latent factors plus independent error—fails when an upstream variable systematically influences all scores. Evidence: the authors replicate prior work showing a dominant factor explaining 72% of variance and its strong correlation with size; when scale is explicitly modeled (B = L[w_n log(n)] + E), the illusion of a single factor dissipates, yielding multiple more interpretable factors with reduced scale correlation.

### Mechanism 2
PCA-based observational scaling laws overfit to the observed benchmarks and produce uninterpretable capability estimates. PCA decomposes B ≈ W_k T_k, where the first principal component (PC1) maximizes explained variance. Because model scale accounts for much of the variance in scores, PC1 becomes heavily weighted toward overall size. Moreover, PCA assumes all variance is common (E=0), forcing PC1 to absorb benchmark-specific noise and idiosyncrasies. This violates the assumption that a low-dimensional representation should capture only the shared, generalizable signal. Consequently, the resulting scaling laws are overfit to the particular tasks used. Evidence: the paper shows PCA's PC1 consistently explains >73% of variance across holdout tasks and correlates strongly with log-parameter size (e.g., R²=0.528 for Geometric shapes), confirming its role as a scale proxy.

### Mechanism 3
The structured capabilities model successfully separates model scale from capability estimates by integrating a structural equation for upstream scale effects with a measurement model that accounts for benchmark-specific error, all linked through a non-linear (logistic) response. The model B' = L[w_n log(n)] + E has two key components: (1) the capability vector for each model is w_n log(n) + ϵ, making scale the upstream driver of latent capabilities; (2) the transformation to B' (logit of scores after accounting for guessing) models the non-linear saturation effect of scale on performance. Factor analysis then estimates L from the transformed data, allocating variance to common factors while allowing independent error terms E. This prevents scale variance and task-specific noise from contaminating the factor scores. Evidence: the structured model improves global fit on transformed data (better CFI, RMSEA) and parsimony (lower AIC/BIC) over pure EFA. Its factor loadings are more distributed (e.g., Factors 1 & 2 explain 41%/36% of variance) and all major factors show clear logistic relationships to scale. In out-of-fold prediction, it outperforms PCA-based scaling laws, demonstrating better generalization.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Construct Validity
- **Why needed here:** The thesis tackles the evaluation crisis by asking whether benchmark scores truly reflect the latent capabilities (e.g., "mathematical reasoning") they intend to measure. Construct validity provides the formal framework for establishing this connection via convergent and discriminant validity.
- **Quick check question:** If two benchmarks both claim to measure "mathematical reasoning," what statistical pattern in their scores across many LLMs would provide convergent validity? What pattern would threaten discriminant validity?

### Concept: Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) & Latent Variables
- **Why needed here:** EFA is the baseline tool to discover latent capability dimensions from a set of benchmark scores. Understanding its assumptions (linearity, uncorrelated error, no upstream causes) is key to diagnosing its failure with LLMs.
- **Quick check question:** In the equation B = LF + E, what does E represent, and why might its omission (as in PCA) be problematic for benchmark data?

### Concept: Neural Scaling Laws (Logistic Form)
- **Why needed here:** Scaling laws establish that model size is a primary, upstream determinant of performance. The thesis innovates by injecting this "upstream cause" into factor analysis and using the logistic link to model the non-linear, saturating effect of scale.
- **Quick check question:** Why is a linear model (B ∝ log(n)) insufficient for predicting benchmark performance? What does the logistic function's shape (sigmoid) imply about how performance improves with scale?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Ingestion (OpenLLM Leaderboard scores + parameter counts) -> Preprocessing (logistic transformation to B') -> Model Core (lavaan SEM: B' = L[w_n log(n)] + E) -> Evaluation Module (compare fit indices, out-of-fold prediction). For PCA-OSL, an alternative branch: PCA on raw scores -> scaling law fits per component.

**Critical path:**
1. Data Acquisition & Cleaning: Source BBH subtask accuracies from HuggingFace, drop models with missing data, verify parameter sizes.
2. Transformation: Compute B' via inverse logistic; ensure all values are finite (avoid random-guess or perfect-score boundaries).
3. Model Specification & Fitting (lavaan): Write syntax for structured SEM; start with simple EFA, then add structural path w_n; monitor convergence.
4. Factor Interpretation: Examine loadings; apply rotation (e.g., varimax) if needed; check for thematic clustering.
5. OSL Comparison Replication: Replicate Ruan et al.'s PCA→scaling law pipeline exactly, using the same raw matrix B.
6. Holdout Validation: Implement out-of-fold procedure (train on 18 tasks, predict held-out); determine number of factors k via Horn’s parallel analysis.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Logistic Link vs. Software:* The ideal nonlinear model is estimable via multidimensional IRT (mirt) but lacks structural equation features; transforming to B' enables lavaan but alters the likelihood, preventing direct AIC/BIC comparison between raw and transformed scales.
- *Exploratory vs. Confirmatory:* EFA is flexible but factors may be unstable; CFA tests pre-specified constructs but risks poor fit if cognitive constructs don’t align with LLM capabilities. The structured model uses EFA to discover factors then interprets them.
- *Parsimony vs. Explanatory Power:* Adding the w_n structural parameter increases model complexity, yet the structured model achieves better AIC/BIC, indicating a net gain. Over-parameterization remains a risk with few tasks or models.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Model Non-Convergence:* lavaan warnings (Heywood cases, non-convergence) may indicate improper solutions; try reducing k, enforcing orthogonal factors, or fixing some loadings to zero.
- *No Improvement Over Pure EFA:* If AIC/BIC do not improve when adding w_n, model size may not be a significant upstream driver in the current subset; revisit parameter data quality.
- *Dominant PC1 Persists:* Even in the structured model, if one factor still explains >50% variance and correlates >0.7 with log(n), the structural link might be mis-specified or k is too small.
- *Poor Out-of-Fold Prediction:* If structured model's MSE is not lower than OSL's, factors may still overfit; consider that the logistic transformation might be insufficient for other benchmark formats, or Horn’s test chose an inappropriate k.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replication of Pure EFA (13a):** Fit EFA on raw scores B. Document a single dominant factor, compute factor scores, correlate with log(n); expect strong correlation (R²≈0.47).
2. **Test Logistic Response (13c):** Fit EFA on transformed scores B'. Compare global fit (CFI, RMSEA) to model (13a); expect improved absolute fit, supporting the logistic specification.
3. **Integration Test - Structured Model (13d):** Fit the full structured model with w_n on B'. Compare parsimonious fit (AIC/BIC) to (13c). Inspect factor loadings: expect more distributed variance (e.g., Factors 1 & 2 explaining ~41% and ~36%) and reduced scale correlation for the previously dominant factor.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Does adding the log-parameter size as a structural parameter improve latent factor model fit for LLM benchmarks?  
- **Basis in paper:** Explicit research question A.  
- **Why unresolved:** Test limited to BBH subtasks; generalizability to other benchmark types unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Fit structured and standard latent factor models to diverse benchmark collections (e.g., MMLU, coding tasks) and compare parsimony indices and factor interpretability.

### Open Question 2
Can latent factor-derived capabilities predict held-out benchmarks better than PCA components in observational scaling laws?  
- **Basis in paper:** Explicit research question B.  
- **Why unresolved:** The observed MSE improvement was not statistically significant (p=0.6366).  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A larger-scale out-of-fold prediction experiment with more models and varied benchmarks, powered to detect small effect sizes.

### Open Question 3
Does the structured capabilities model maintain construct validity when applied to safety and alignment benchmarks?  
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred; the author notes BBH does not assess safety/alignment and this is a limitation.  
- **Why unresolved:** All experiments used BBH reasoning tasks; safety benchmarks may scale differently.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the model comparisons on safety benchmarks (e.g., TruthfulQA, BBQ) and examine if the structured model still separates scale from capability factors while generalizing.

## Limitations
- Missing exact α and β values for each BBH subtask’s logistic transformation hinders exact replication of the transformed data.
- The study is limited to BBH tasks; it is unclear whether the results generalize to other benchmark suites (e.g., MMLU, coding, safety).
- Only log parameter count is modeled as an upstream driver; other potential upstream factors (training data size, architecture choices) are ignored.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Pure EFA confounds with scale):** High – strong empirical replication (R²≈0.47) across studies.
- **Mechanism 2 (PCA scaling laws overfit):** Medium – reasoning sound but evidence based on limited holdout tasks and not statistically significant.
- **Mechanism 3 (Structured model separates scale):** Medium – improved parsimony and prediction on BBH, but generalizability unproven.

## Next Checks
1. Re-estimate the logistic transformation parameters α and β directly from the BBH data, refit the structured SEM, and compare fit indices to the reported results.
2. Replicate the entire pipeline on a different benchmark suite (e.g., MMLU) to test whether the structured model consistently outperforms EFA and PCA-OSL.
3. Extend the structural equation to include additional upstream covariates (e.g., training tokens, model family) to test the robustness of the single-parameter assumption and factor structure.
