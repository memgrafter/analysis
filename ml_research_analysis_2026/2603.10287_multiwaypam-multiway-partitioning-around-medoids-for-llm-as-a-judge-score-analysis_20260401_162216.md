# MultiwayPAM: Multiway Partitioning Around Medoids for LLM-as-a-Judge Score Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10287  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10287  
- **Reference count:** 23  
- **Primary result:** MultiwayPAM achieves lower RMSE-M than the tensor block model on two LLM-as-a-Judge datasets (Truthy: 0.714 vs 0.783; Emerton: 0.523 vs 0.570) and reveals evaluator bias patterns.

## Executive Summary
LLM-as-a-Judge frameworks are widely used for evaluating language model outputs but suffer from high computational cost (evaluating all question–answerer–evaluator triples) and inherent evaluator bias. MultiwayPAM addresses both issues by extending Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) to three-way tensors, simultaneously clustering questions, answerers, and evaluators while selecting representative observed scores (medoids) for each block. This reduces the number of stored scores from \(d_1 d_2 d_3\) to \(c_1 c_2 c_3\) and provides human‑interpretable medoids that expose systematic scoring patterns. On two datasets (Truthy and Emerton) with 50 items per mode and fixed cluster size 5, MultiwayPAM improves reconstruction accuracy (lower RMSE‑M) over a tensor block model and uncovers clear bias effects—for example, specific evaluator clusters consistently assign lower scores to certain question clusters. The method thus offers a practical path to more efficient and insightful LLM‑based evaluation.

## Method Summary
MultiwayPAM operates on a three‑way score tensor \(Y \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_2 \times d_3}\), where modes correspond to questions, answerers, and evaluators. The goal is to partition each mode into \(c_k\) clusters and choose one observed entry per block as its medoid. The medoid tensor \(\hat{Y}\) is reconstructed such that every entry \((i_1,i_2,i_3)\) takes the value of the medoid belonging to the block \((M^{(1)}_{i_1}, M^{(2)}_{i_2}, M^{(3)}_{i_3})\). The algorithm minimizes the dissimilarity \(D = \|Y - \hat{Y}\|_F^2\).

The method consists of two phases. **BUILD** initializes medoids greedily: for each mode \(k\), it computes dissimilarities between all slices (2‑D sub‑tensors fixing mode \(k\)), selects the slice with minimal total distance as the first medoid, then iteratively adds the slice that most reduces the sum of distances to the nearest existing medoid. After medoid selection, each index is assigned to the cluster of its nearest medoid slice. **SWAP** refines the solution by alternating updates across modes. For a given mode, it evaluates all possible swaps between a current medoid and a non‑medoid. For each candidate, it recomputes membership for that mode using a conditional rule that forces the swapped‑in medoid to belong to its own cluster, builds the candidate medoid tensor, and computes its dissimilarity. If the swap reduces \(D\), it is accepted. The process repeats until no improvement occurs, yielding cluster assignments \(M\) and medoid indices \(R\).

## Key Results
- On the Truthy dataset, MultiwayPAM achieves RMSE‑M = 0.714, outperforming the tensor block model’s 0.783.  
- On the Emerton dataset, MultiwayPAM achieves RMSE‑M = 0.523, compared to the baseline’s 0.570.  
- Medoid analysis reveals structural evaluator bias: evaluator cluster E14 (medoid: “A nurse who supports the cadet’s aspiration but worries about danger”) consistently gives lower scores to question cluster Q6 (medoid: “Do you possess the ability to navigate…”) on Truthy. In Emerton, score variations are primarily driven by question modality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** MultiwayPAM reduces computational cost by approximating the full score tensor with a medoid tensor, requiring only \(c_1 \times c_2 \times c_3\) stored scores instead of \(d_1 \times d_2 \times d_3\).  
- **Mechanism:** The method discovers block structure where similar questions, answerers, and evaluators form clusters. Each block is represented by a single medoid entry (an actual observed score). For any non‑medoid combination \((i_1,i_2,i_3)\), its score is approximated by the medoid entry of its assigned block: \(\hat{y}_{i_1 i_2 i_3} = y_{r^{(1)}_{m^{(1)}_{i_1}}, r^{(2)}_{m^{(2)}_{i_2}}, r^{(3)}_{m^{(3)}_{i_3}}}\). This reduces inference needs from all combinations to just the medoid combinations.  
- **Core assumption:** The score tensor exhibits low‑rank block structure; within each block, scores are approximately constant and can be represented by a single observed entry.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: “To address these issues… we propose to apply a tensor clustering method… By observing the medoids… we can gain knowledge about the membership…”  
  - [section 2.2]: Explicitly defines the medoid tensor \(\hat{Y}\) where each entry copies its block’s medoid value, and minimizes dissimilarity \(D = \|Y - \hat{Y}\|_F^2\).  
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers on tensor completion/clustering generally support that tensors often have exploitable low‑rank structure, though specific to score tensors is an assumption.  
- **Break condition:** If intra‑block variance is high, medoid approximation error becomes large. The method would then require many clusters (\(c_k\) close to \(d_k\)) to maintain accuracy, negating computational savings.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** MultiwayPAM reveals evaluator bias patterns through human‑interpretable medoids, which are actual concrete question–answerer–evaluator triples rather than abstract centroids.  
- **Mechanism:** By inspecting the text content of medoid entries (e.g., evaluator persona “A nurse who supports the cadet’s aspiration but worries about danger”), researchers can hypothesize why that evaluator cluster gives certain scores. Comparing medoid scores across blocks shows systematic relationships: e.g., evaluator cluster E14 consistently assigns lower scores to question cluster Q6.  
- **Core assumption:** The medoid entries are semantically representative of their block’s scoring logic; human interpretation of these concrete examples can uncover bias mechanisms.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: “By observing the medoids obtained by MultiwayPAM, we can gain knowledge about the membership of each question/answerer/evaluator cluster.”  
  - [section 3]: “From these figures, in regard to Truthy dataset, we see that the first evaluator cluster with the medoid E14 (i.e., ‘A nurse who supports…’) gave relatively low scores for the first question cluster with the medoid Q6…”  
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence. Neighbors like “CheckEval” and “Exploring the Effects of Alignment on Numerical Bias” show community interest in LLM bias analysis, but medoid‑based interpretation is novel—assume it yields actionable insights.  
- **Break condition:** If medoids are outliers or not central to their cluster’s semantic distribution, interpretations may misrepresent cluster tendencies. The method does not optimize for semantic coherence within clusters.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** MultiwayPAM’s alternating SWAP updates effectively find a local minimum of the dissimilarity objective, balancing efficient computation with quality block detection.  
- **Mechanism:** Starting from BUILD initialization, SWAP iterates through each mode. For mode \(k\), it evaluates all medoid/non‑medoid swaps \((i,j)\) while fixing other modes. For each swap, it updates membership assignments using Equation (1) with a critical conditional branch ensuring medoids remain in their own clusters. It accepts the swap that most reduces \(\|Y - \hat{Y}\|_F^2\). This coordinate descent avoids exhaustive combinatorial search.  
- **Core assumption:** The objective function is sufficiently smooth that local improvements in one mode lead toward a good global solution; the conditional branch in Equation (1) prevents degenerate assignments where medoids leave their clusters.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 2.2]: Algorithm 2 details the swap logic, the conditional membership update (Equation 1), and termination when no improvement.  
  - [section 3]: Table 7 shows MultiwayPAM achieves lower RMSE‑M than TBM, suggesting the medoid‑specific optimization yields better approximations.  
  - [corpus]: “Sparse Partitioning Around Medoids” provides PAM foundation; “Multiway clustering of 3‑order tensor via affinity matrix” shows tensor‑specific clustering approaches—MultiwayPAM fills the medoid gap.  
- **Break condition:** The algorithm can stagnate in poor local minima if BUILD initialization is unfortunate or the objective has many plateaus. No theoretical guarantees beyond local optimality.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM)
- **Why needed here:** MultiwayPAM directly extends PAM from vectors to tensors. Understanding PAM’s medoid selection (minimizing sum of dissimilarities to cluster members) and swap‑based refinement is essential to grasp BUILD and SWAP.  
- **Quick check question:** Why does PAM use actual data points as medoids instead of centroids (means), and what are the tradeoffs?

### Concept: Tensor Modes and Slicing
- **Why needed here:** The score tensor has three modes (question, answerer, evaluator). Algorithms treat each mode separately via slices (sub‑tensors fixing all but one mode index). Correct slicing is crucial for computing dissimilarities in BUILD/SWAP.  
- **Quick check question:** Given a 3D tensor \(Y[i,j,k]\), what does the slice \(Z^{(i)}\) represent in Algorithm 1, and why is it used for medoid selection?

### Concept: Coordinate Descent Optimization
- **Why needed here:** SWAP updates one mode at a time while holding others fixed. This alternating approach is standard for multi‑way optimization but requires understanding of convergence behavior and dependency between modes.  
- **Quick check question:** In MultiwayPAM’s SWAP, why might updating mode 1 first lead to a different final solution than updating mode 3 first? What does this imply about the objective function’s non‑convexity?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
DataLoader → BuildInitializer → SwapOptimizer → Analyzer  
Supporting components: Dissimilarity (computes \(\|A-B\|_F^2\)), MedoidTensor (constructs \(\hat{Y}\) from medoid indices)

**Critical path:**  
1. Input: Tensor \(Y\) (shape \((d_1,d_2,d_3)\)) and cluster sizes \(c = [c_1,c_2,c_3]\).  
2. Run `BuildInitializer.run(Y, c)` → \(R_0, M_0\).  
3. Run `SwapOptimizer.run(Y, c, R_0, M_0)` → \(R_\text{final}, M_\text{final}\).  
4. Run `Analyzer.report(Y, R_\text{final}, M_\text{final})` → sorted visualizations and medoid label tables.  
5. Optional: Compute RMSE‑M and RMSE‑C for evaluation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Memory:** SWAP stores slice dissimilarities implicitly; precomputing a dissimilarity matrix between all slices (size \(d_k \times d_k\)) may be cheaper for large \(d_k\) but uses \(O(d_k^2)\) memory. Paper computes on‑the‑fly.  
- **Speed:** The inner loop evaluates \(O(c_k \cdot (d_k - c_k))\) swaps per mode per iteration. For large \(d_k\), sample candidate swaps instead of exhaustive search.  
- **Dissimilarity choice:** Paper uses squared Frobenius norm (L2). For sparse or outlier‑prone scores, consider L1 norm or cosine similarity—requires modifying `Dissimilarity` and possibly the swap acceptance criterion.  
- **Cluster size selection:** Paper fixes \(c=[5,5,5]\). In practice, use elbow method on RMSE‑M vs. total clusters or domain constraints.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. **Slow convergence:** SWAP runs many iterations with tiny improvements. Add `max_iter` and `tol` (minimum improvement threshold). Symptom: >100 iterations with \(\Delta D < 10^{-6}\).  
2. **Empty clusters:** After BUILD or SWAP, a cluster has zero members. Usually due to ties in dissimilarity during BUILD. Break ties randomly or by secondary criterion (e.g., index order).  
3. **Medoids not in own cluster:** Happens if Equation (1)’s conditional branch is omitted. Must ensure \(r^{(k)}_i \in \text{cluster } i\) after membership update.  
4. **High final RMSE‑M (>1.0 on 1–4 scale):** Indicates block structure too weak for given \(c\). Try smaller \(c\) or different initialization (multiple random restarts, pick best \(D\)).  
5. **Imbalanced cluster sizes:** Some clusters have \(\gg d_k/c_k\) items. May need balanced clustering constraints (min/max size), but paper does not implement.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic sanity check:** Generate \(Y\) with \(5\times5\times5\) blocks, each block constant value plus \(\mathcal{N}(0,0.1)\) noise. Run MultiwayPAM with \(c=[5,5,5]\). Check that recovered blocks align with true blocks (adjusted Rand index >0.9) and medoids fall within correct blocks.  
2. **Reproduce Table 7:** Load Truthy/Emerton datasets from the cited Hugging Face links. Run MultiwayPAM and TBM baseline. Verify RMSE‑M values are within 0.01 of paper’s numbers.  
3. **Bias pattern replication:** On Truthy, check that evaluator cluster with medoid E14 (“A nurse who supports…”) gives low scores to question cluster with medoid Q6 (“Do you possess the ability…”). Confirms the described bias pattern.

## Limitations
- The medoid approximation relies on low intra‑block variance; if the score tensor lacks strong block structure, many clusters are needed to maintain accuracy, negating computational benefits.  
- Interpretation of bias patterns assumes medoids are semantically central to their clusters; if medoids are outliers, conclusions may be misleading.  
- The SWAP algorithm has no global optimality guarantee and may stagnate in poor local minima; additionally, the dataset generation process (LLM prompts, rating extraction) and TBM baseline are insufficiently specified, hindering exact replication.
