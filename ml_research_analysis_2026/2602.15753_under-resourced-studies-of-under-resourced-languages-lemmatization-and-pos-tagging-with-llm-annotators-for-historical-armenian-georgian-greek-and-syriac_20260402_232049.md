# Under-resourced studies of under-resourced languages: lemmatization and POS-tagging with LLM annotators for historical Armenian, Georgian, Greek and Syriac

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15753  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15753  
- **Reference count:** 13  
- **Primary result:** GPT-4o significantly outperformed a supervised RNN baseline (PIE) in few-shot lemmatization and POS-tagging across four historical languages, despite high polylexical density and non-Latin scripts.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates frozen large language models (LLMs) ascold-start annotators for four historical, low-resource languages: Ancient Greek, Classical Armenian, Old Georgian, and Syriac. Using a novel benchmark with a complex annotation schema that segments polylexical forms via an '@' delimiter, the authors compare GPT-4 and Mistral models in zero-shot and few-shot settings against a supervised character-level RNN baseline (PIE). LLMs, particularly GPT-4o, achieved higher accuracy than PIE in most languages and tasks even with minimal in-context examples. Lemmatization proved more challenging than POS-tagging, with performance heavily influenced by polylexical density and lexical overlap. The results demonstrate the viability of LLMs for morphosyntactic annotation in under-resourced historical languages without task-specific fine-tuning.

## Method Summary
The study uses a token-level annotation benchmark based on the GREgORI guidelines, which split polylexical forms with an '@' delimiter (e.g., `lemma1@lemma2`, `pos1@pos2`). For each language, aligned corpora of ~5,000 training tokens and ~300 test tokens (both in-domain and out-of-domain) are provided. LLMs are prompted with a structured COSTAR-style template that explicitly injects the full tagset and a concrete example of the '@' segmentation rule. Few-shot examples (k=0,5,50,500) are taken sequentially from the start of the training corpus. The primary evaluation metric is token-level accuracy against gold-standard lemmas and POS tags. Models evaluated include GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, o1-mini, Mistral Large, and open-mistral-nemo. The supervised baseline (PIE) is a character-level Bi-GRU+CRF trained on the full 5k-word training set per language.

## Key Results
- GPT-4o consistently outperformed the PIE baseline in few-shot settings, e.g., 0.933 lemmatization accuracy on Greek (500 shots, in-domain) vs. PIE's best 0.566 (5,000 shots).
- POS-tagging saw the highest scores, with GPT-4o reaching 0.963 on Greek (in-domain, 500 shots).
- Lemmatization was hardest for Syriac (0.656 with GPT-4o, 500 shots, in-domain), reflecting its high polylexical density (46.5% of tokens) and low lexical overlap (~42%) with training data.
- Performance dropped in out-of-domain evaluation and for languages with complex, under-represented morphology (e.g., Old Georgian).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: In-Context Morphosyntactic Generalization
LLMs leverage pre-trained cross-linguistic morphological knowledge to adapt to new languages via few-shot demonstrations, without task-specific fine-tuning. The model conditions on a few annotated examples to infer patterns and generalizes to unseen tokens, even with low lexical overlap. Evidence: GPT-4o's lemmatization on Syriac (0.656) far exceeds PIE's best (0.305) despite only ~42% token overlap. Break condition: Performance degrades when polylexical density is very high and lexical overlap very low, overwhelming pattern inference from few examples.

### Mechanism 2: Prompt-Driven Schema Adherence
Structured prompt engineering that explicitly injects the full tagset and a polylexical segmentation example steers LLMs to produce outputs conforming to non-standard schemas like GREgORI. By unambiguously specifying valid tags and delimiter use, the model avoids hallucinating common tagsets (e.g., Universal Dependencies). Evidence: Manual error analysis links lower Syriac scores to '@' mismanagement, implying correct instruction *can* guide behavior. Break condition: Polylexical density exceeds a threshold, causing structural desynchronization in TSV output where '@' boundaries are missed.

### Mechanism 3: Subword-Level Morphological Reasoning
LLMs generalize to unseen vocabulary by reasoning over subword and character-level patterns rather than surface-form memorization. Their tokenizers and internal representations allow decomposition of unknown words into familiar morphemes, then recomposition into valid lemmas and tags based on few-shot context. Evidence: Competitive performance despite low lexical overlap (e.g., Syriac) indicates morphological generalization beyond memorization. Break condition: Morphological patterns poorly represented in pre-training (e.g., Kartvelian agglutination, Semitic root-and-template) limit depth of generalization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Few-Shot In-Context Learning**: Why needed: The entire evaluation relies on providing a small number of token-level annotation examples within the prompt to define the task and schema. Quick check: How do the number, order, and domain similarity of few-shot examples influence parsing of a never-before-seen polylexical Syriac token?
- **Non-Standard Linguistic Annotation Schemas**: Why needed: The GREgORI guidelines diverge from standards like UD by using '@' for polylexical segmentation and fusing morphological tags into POS categories. Quick check: What risks arise when using a bespoke tagset and segmentation rule if the model's pre-training data contains only mainstream annotations?
- **Morphological Typology and Script Diversity**: Why needed: The four languages span different families (Indo-European, Kartvelian, Semitic) and scripts (Greek, Armenian, Georgian, Syriac), each posing unique tokenization and morphological challenges. Quick check: How might a subword tokenizer trained on modern Latin-script text handle Old Georgian agglutinative chains or Syriac consonant-only roots?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Training Corpora (TSV) -> Prompt Template (COSTAR + Tagset + '@' example) -> LLM API (GPT-4o, Mistral) -> TSV Output Parser -> Token-level Accuracy vs Gold Test Set. Baseline: Training Corpora -> PIE Model (Bi-GRU+CRF) -> Evaluator.
- **Critical path**: 1) Select first k examples from train.tsv. 2) Inject full tagset and '@' rule into prompt. 3) Call LLM API with decoding params (τ=0.0 for GPT-4o). 4) Parse TSV response. 5) Compute accuracy against test.tsv. 6) Iterate k, model, prompt.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Closed (GPT-4o) vs. Open (Mistral) models: accuracy vs. cost/auditability. TSV vs. JSON output: simplicity vs. robustness with polylexical forms. Sequential vs. random few-shot: narrative context vs. morphological diversity.
- **Failure signatures**: Formatting desynchronization (mismatched '@' count breaks TSV alignment). Tagset hallucination (outputs tags not in injected list, e.g., UD tags). Cross-script artifacts (applying modern orthographic patterns). Zero-shot collapse (defaults to modern language patterns).
- **First 3 experiments**: 1) Zero-shot (k=0) to measure pure prior knowledge. 2) Shot-count sensitivity curve (k=5,50,500 on Greek) to identify diminishing returns. 3) In/out-domain generalization gap (e.g., Syriac Homilies vs. Hagiography at 500 shots) to quantify domain shift impact.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: What few-shot example selection strategies maximize LLM accuracy for polylexical forms in under-resourced languages? The current study uses sequential selection; no alternatives (e.g., clustering by morphological diversity) are tested.
- **Open Question 2**: Does the non-standard GREgORI annotation schema (with '@' delimiter and fused morphological tags) inherently limit LLM performance compared to a standardized scheme like Universal Dependencies? No ablation compares across frameworks.
- **Open Question 3**: How can evaluation metrics decouple LLMs' adherence to formatting constraints (e.g., correct '@' usage) from their genuine morphosyntactic competence? Current token-level accuracy conflates segmentation mistakes with lemma/POS errors.
- **Open Question 4**: Can LLMs perform effective annotation in a truly zero-resource setting (zero in-language examples) for historical languages using only tagset definitions and descriptive grammars? Zero-shot experiments still use in-language examples.

## Limitations
- **Prompt exactness unknown**: The paper specifies prompt structure but not verbatim wording for Context/Objective sections; phrasing variations could impact schema adherence.
- **Baseline implementation opacity**: The PIE architecture is described but code, library versions, and seeds are unavailable, making exact replication of its performance uncertain.
- **Data split ambiguity**: "Sequential" few-shot selection and the exact composition of in-domain/out-of-domain test files are not explicitly provided, blocking precise replication of shot effects.
