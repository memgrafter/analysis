# Blackbird Language Matrices: A Framework to Investigate the Linguistic Competence of Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20966
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20966
- **Reference count**: 14
- **Primary result**: Introduces Blackbird Language Matrices (BLMs), a framework of structured linguistic puzzles inspired by Raven's Progressive Matrices, to probe linguistic competence and systematic generalization in large language models.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents Blackbird Language Matrices (BLMs), a novel diagnostic framework inspired by Raven's Progressive Matrices, designed to assess the linguistic competence and systematic generalization capabilities of large language models (LLMs). BLMs consist of multi-sentence contexts that instantiate specific linguistic phenomena (e.g., verb alternations, subject-verb agreement) followed by multiple-choice completions that contrastively test different rule applications. This structured approach allows fine-grained error analysis and isolates the model's ability to abstract and apply grammatical rules across sentences.

Experiments with baseline models—feed-forward neural networks using sentence embeddings—demonstrate that LLMs achieve high performance (F1 scores typically >0.85) on BLMs across several languages (English, French, Italian, Romanian). However, performance declines with lexical variation, suggesting reliance on surface patterns. Error analysis reveals that LLMs commit more sequence/logic errors (e.g., incorrect progression of agreement features) than pure grammatical errors, indicating they grasp local grammar but struggle with cross-sentence systematic patterns. A two-level variational autoencoder (VAE) that explicitly compresses sentence structure further improves performance, confirming that LLMs encode relevant linguistic chunks (e.g., semantic roles, phrase boundaries) yet systematic relational reasoning remains a challenge. BLMs thus provide a valuable tool for probing the limits of LLMs' linguistic knowledge.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed BLM datasets for multiple languages, encoding phenomena such as verb alternations and subject-verb agreement. Each matrix comprises a context of several sentences that illustrate a systematic pattern, followed by a set of candidate completions that vary in specific linguistic features (e.g., number, case, alternation type). Models are trained to predict the correct completion based on the context. The primary baseline employs a feed-forward neural network (FFNN) that takes fixed-dimensional sentence embeddings (e.g., from a pretrained encoder) as input. To investigate whether explicit structural representation aids systematic generalization, the authors also implement a two-level variational autoencoder (VAE) that compresses sentence structure into latent chunks at two levels of granularity. Performance is evaluated via F1 score, and error analysis categorizes mistakes into types such as agreement errors (AEV, WNA), alternation errors (I-INT, ER-PASS), and sequence/logic errors. The experiments also manipulate lexical variation (i.e., using different lexical items than those seen in training) to test reliance on surface patterns and conduct cross-linguistic evaluations to assess robustness across morphological richness.

## Key Results
- BLMs achieve high accuracy (F1 > 0.85) across tested languages, but performance declines with lexical variation, indicating sensitivity to surface forms.
- Error analysis shows LLMs produce more sequence/logic errors (e.g., incorrect progression of features across sentences) than pure grammatical errors (e.g., subject-verb agreement violations), suggesting difficulty with cross-sentence systematic reasoning.
- A two-level VAE that explicitly compresses sentence structure improves performance, confirming that LLMs encode linguistic chunks (e.g., semantic roles) but systematic relational reasoning remains a bottleneck.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
BLMs work by requiring models to infer and apply abstract linguistic rules from a few illustrative sentences, then select the completion that correctly continues the pattern from a set of contrastive distractors. The matrix format, borrowed from Raven's Progressive Matrices, ensures that success depends on relational reasoning rather than memorization of specific lexical items. By systematically varying linguistic features (e.g., number, case, verb alternation) across sentences and answer options, BLMs isolate specific aspects of grammatical knowledge and expose whether models have formed rule-like generalizations. The use of multi-sentence contexts further tests the ability to maintain and propagate information across discourse, which is essential for true linguistic competence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Systematic Generalization**: The capacity to apply known rules to novel combinations of elements.
  - *Why needed*: BLMs are designed to test whether models can generalize grammatical rules beyond the exact lexical and structural configurations seen in training.
  - *Quick check*: Does the model correctly predict verb agreement for a subject with a novel noun and a verb not encountered together in training?
- **Linguistic Competence**: Internal knowledge of a language's grammar, including rules for syntax, morphology, and semantics.
  - *Why needed*: The paper aims to evaluate the depth of LLMs' grammatical knowledge, moving beyond surface fluency.
  - *Quick check*: Can the model reliably distinguish between grammatical and ungrammatical sentences in controlled settings (e.g., subject-verb agreement violations)?
- **Raven's Progressive Matrices**: A nonverbal intelligence test where participants complete patterns of abstract shapes.
  - *Why needed*: Serves as the conceptual inspiration for BLMs, shifting the focus from raw language modeling to analogical reasoning and pattern completion.
  - *Quick check*: Would the model succeed on classic RPM tasks if framed as a multiple-choice problem?
- **Multi-sentence Contextual Reasoning**: The ability to integrate information across several sentences to maintain coherence and apply rules consistently.
  - *Why needed*: BLMs use contexts of multiple sentences to establish patterns, testing discourse-level competence beyond single-sentence grammar.
  - *Quick check*: Does the model keep track of a feature (e.g., number or case) across several sentences and propagate it correctly to the final completion?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
The baseline architecture consists of:  
Input Sentences → Sentence Encoder (e.g., BERT) → Sentence Embeddings → Feed-Forward Neural Network (FFNN) → Classification over answer options.  
The VAE variant replaces the FFNN with a two-level VAE:  
Input Sentences → Sentence Encoder → Hierarchical VAE (sentence-level and chunk-level latent variables) → Latent Representation → Classifier (FFNN) → Prediction.  
The VAE is trained to reconstruct sentence structure while the classifier uses the latent code.

### Critical Path
For baseline: Sentences are encoded individually; embeddings are pooled or concatenated to form a fixed representation; the FFNN processes this representation to score each candidate answer. For VAE: The encoder produces a latent distribution; a sample is taken; the decoder reconstructs the sentences; the latent code is also fed to the classifier. The critical path is the flow from raw text through encoder to latent/sentence embeddings to final prediction.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Sentence embeddings vs. token-level processing**: Using fixed sentence embeddings simplifies the model but may lose fine-grained syntactic details needed for systematic reasoning. The VAE explicitly compresses structure, trading off reconstruction fidelity for generalization.
- **Baseline FFNN vs. VAE**: The FFNN is simple and fast but lacks inductive bias for hierarchical structure. The VAE introduces a structured latent space that encourages chunking, improving performance on lexically varied data, but adds complexity and training difficulty.
- **Contrastive answer design**: Crafting distractors that isolate specific errors requires linguistic expertise but yields interpretable diagnostics; however, it may limit scalability to diverse phenomena.

### Failure Signatures
- **Sequence/Logic Errors**: Incorrect progression of features (e.g., number, case) across the matrix sentences, indicating failure to track systematic changes.
- **Grammatical Errors**: Violations of local rules (e.g., subject-verb agreement, case assignment) that suggest incomplete grammatical knowledge.
- **Syntax-Semantic Mapping Errors**: Mismatches between syntactic structures and semantic roles (e.g., incorrect assignment of agent/patient in alternations).

These error types, derived from the answer set design, help pinpoint whether the model's deficiency lies in local grammar, cross-sentence tracking, or rule abstraction.

### Three First Experiments
1. **Baseline Evaluation**: Train and evaluate the FFNN model on BLMs for English, French, Italian, and Romanian to establish performance levels and compare across languages.
2. **Lexical Variation Ablation**: Test the same models on BLMs with altered lexical items (i.e., different verbs/nouns than those in training) to measure reliance on surface patterns.
3. **VAE Integration**: Implement the two-level VAE architecture and compare its performance and error profile against the baseline, especially under lexical variation, to assess the benefit of explicit structural compression.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Do LLMs form explicit, rule-like representations of linguistic objects (e.g., chunks, semantic roles) that are systematically used for cross-sentence reasoning, or do they rely on surface statistical patterns?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper investigates whether LLMs detect linguistic objects and their properties (core question in intro) and shows via a VAE that compressed sentence representations retain chunk pattern information. However, error analysis reveals persistent sequence/logic errors despite encoding of chunks, suggesting the representations may not be used in a truly systematic, rule-based manner for matrix completion.
- Why unresolved: The VAE experiments demonstrate that chunk information is *encodable* in latent space, but do not reveal how LLMs natively represent or manipulate such information during autoregressive generation or within transformer architectures. The gap between encoding chunk properties and applying them to solve the multi-sentence puzzle indicates a possible representational or algorithmic limitation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Probing experiments directly on native LLM (not embedding-based) internal states across BLM instances, correlating latent dimensions with specific chunk/rule violations. Causal intervention studies (e.g., editing activations linked to chunk detection) to see if correcting chunk representations eliminates sequence errors.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What specific architectural or training deficiencies cause LLMs to struggle with systematic relational reasoning across sentences (as evidenced by sequence/progression errors in BLMs) even when they correctly apply local grammatical rules?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Error analysis consistently shows more sequence/logic errors (e.g., wrong progression of attractors in agreement, incorrect alternation patterns) than pure grammatical errors. The two-level VAE improves performance on lexically varied data, hinting that explicit compression of sentence structure aids systematicity, but does not isolate the core limitation.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the symptom (sequence errors) and a partial remedy (structured VAE), but does not diagnose whether the failure stems from (a) lack of explicit hierarchical processing in transformers, (b) insufficient exposure to systematic paradigms during pre-training, (c) inadequate objective functions for relational reasoning, or (d) a combination.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled fine-tuning experiments on BLMs varying (i) model architecture (e.g., adding recurrence, hierarchical attention), (ii) pre-training data (injecting synthetic systematic paradigms), (iii) loss functions (adding explicit consistency losses across sentences). Compare error profiles to human developmental data on similar matrix tasks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: To what extent does performance on BLMs correlate with human-like linguistic competence or general fluid intelligence, and can BLMs distinguish between models that have robust linguistic knowledge versus those that exploit shallow dataset artifacts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] BLMs are explicitly inspired by Raven’s Progressive Matrices (RPMs), a gold-standard test of human fluid intelligence and analogical reasoning. The authors argue BLMs test "rule-like generalization" and "systematicity," properties associated with human linguistic intelligence. However, no human performance baselines are reported, and the link between BLM success and broader cognitive abilities remains assumed.
- Why unresolved: Without human subject data on BLMs, it is unclear whether the patterns LLMs learn mirror human cognitive strategies (e.g., object induction then rule discovery) or are fundamentally different. Furthermore, it is unknown whether high BLM scores predict better performance on real-world language tasks requiring deep understanding, or merely reflect optimization on the specific structured distributions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Administer BLMs to human participants (including linguistically naive and expert) to establish a performance ceiling and error profile. Correlate BLM scores with independent measures of linguistic competence (e.g., acceptance judgments, production tasks) and general reasoning (e.g., RPMs). Test whether models that perform well on BLMs also generalize better to out-of-distribution natural language tasks requiring systematic composition.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How do BLM performance and error patterns vary across typologically diverse languages, and what does this reveal about the universality vs. language-specificity of LLMs' acquired linguistic generalizations?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper presents BLM datasets in English, French, Italian, Romanian, and mentions Turkish/Hebrew, but detailed comparative analysis is limited. The discussion notes that some phenomena (e.g., agreement) are widespread, while others (e.g., spray/load) are language-specific. However, the experiments primarily report results per language without directly contrasting them to probe universal vs. language-specific reasoning.
- Why unresolved: Cross-linguistic comparisons are complicated by differing data sizes, morphological richness, and tokenization. The paper does not test whether LLMs transfer systematic rules across languages (e.g., learning agreement in English helps in French) or whether failures are uniform. This limits understanding of whether LLMs acquire abstract linguistic UG-like principles or language-specific statistical patterns.
- What evidence would resolve it: Train and test models on matched BLM puzzles across multiple languages, controlling for data size and using multilingual models. Analyze if errors shift from lexical/surface to deep structural when moving from morphologically simple to complex languages. Test if training on one language improves zero-shot performance on another for the same underlying phenomenon (e.g., causatives).

### Open Question 5
- Question: Can the structured error taxonomy of BLMs (grammatical vs. sequence/logic vs. syntax-semantic mapping errors) be used to diagnose specific failure modes in LLM architectures and guide targeted architectural improvements for systematic generalization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors design the answer sets to produce fine-grained error types (e.g., AEV, WNA for agreement; I-INT, ER-PASS for alternations). They show error distributions differ across conditions and models, indicating that certain rules (e.g., subject-verb agreement) are learned while others (progression) are not. The VAE changes the error profile, reducing grammatical errors but not sequence errors.
- Why unresolved: While the taxonomy is rich, the paper does not connect specific error types to specific computational limitations (e.g., is a "WN1" error due to short-range attention, poor tracking of nested dependencies, or lack of explicit stack-like memory?). The proposed VAE helps but does not provide a mechanistic account of which component addresses which error.
- What evidence would resolve it: Map error types to model internals: analyze attention weights for sequence errors (e.g., does the model attend to the correct subject across attractors?), probing for representation of rule components (e.g., does the model encode "progression" as a distinct feature?). Use the taxonomy to benchmark future architectures (e.g., state-space models, recursive networks) and see which errors persist or vanish.

## Limitations
- **Baseline Scope**: Experiments used FFNNs with sentence embeddings, not contemporary LLMs; generalizability is unknown.
- **Ecological Validity**: BLMs are synthetic puzzles; correlation with real-world linguistic competence is unestablished.
- **VAE Confound**: The VAE improvement conflates architectural bias with innate LLM capability; it's unclear if LLMs would learn similar representations without explicit supervision.
