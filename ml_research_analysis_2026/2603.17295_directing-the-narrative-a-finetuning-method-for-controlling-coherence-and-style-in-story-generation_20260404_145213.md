# Directing the Narrative: A Finetuning Method for Controlling Coherence and Style in Story Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17295
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17295
- **Reference count:** 5
- **Primary result:** Achieves state-of-the-art performance on ViStoryBench, with gains of +10.0 in Character Identity (CIDS) and +18.7 in Style Consistency (CSD) over strong baselines.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses identity and style drift in story visualization by proposing a two-stage finetuning framework. Stage 1 introduces Group-Shared Attention (GSA), which modifies self-attention within a diffusion transformer to share clean visual tokens from reference frames with a noisy target frame, enabling lossless identity learning without external encoders. Stage 2 applies Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) on curated pairwise examples to align outputs with human aesthetic preferences. This approach yields significant improvements in character and style consistency on the ViStoryBench benchmark.

## Method Summary
Building on a pretrained FLUX.1 backbone (a diffusion transformer using rectified flow matching), the method first trains a LoRA adapter implementing GSA. During training, batches are constructed asymmetrically: the first frame is a noisy target (timestep *t* sampled), while remaining frames are clean references (*t*=0). GSA extends the target’s attention keys/values with image tokens from all references, forcing the model to retrieve identity features. After this reconstruction phase, a second LoRA adapter is trained via DPO on manually curated winner/loser image pairs to refine fidelity along dimensions like identity, anatomy, and aesthetics, using a reference policy from Stage 1.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art results on ViStoryBench.
- +10.0 improvement in Character Identity Score (CIDS).
- +18.7 improvement in Style Consistency (CSD).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Group-Shared Attention (GSA)

**Claim:** GSA enables lossless cross‑sample information flow within the self‑attention layers of a diffusion transformer, allowing the model to retrieve high‑resolution visual tokens from clean reference frames to preserve character identity without external encoders.

**Mechanism:** During Stage 1 training, for a batch of *N* frames the first sample is the target (noisy, with timestep *t* random) and the remaining are references (clean, *t*=0). The target’s key and value matrices are extended by concatenating visual tokens from all references; the query remains target‑only. The target’s attention output is computed with these extended keys/values, forcing the model to attend to identity features from the reference pool.

**Core assumption:** The attention mechanism can selectively extract identity‑relevant tokens from the reference set when the target is noisy, provided references are noise‑free and semantically aligned.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "Group-Shared Attention (GSA) modifies self‑attention to share clean visual tokens from reference frames within a batch with the target frame, enabling lossless, fine‑grained identity learning without external encoders."
- [section] 4.3.1: "Uniﬁed Internal Retrieval... reformulating self‑attention by expanding the context window... extended key ˜K(0) and value ˜V(0) for the target sample are constructed as: ˜K(0) = [K(0)_txt, K(0)_img] ⊕ Σ_{j=1}^{N‑1} [K(j)_img]"
- [corpus] The corpus of neighboring papers shows no prior implementation of group‑shared attention within flow‑based transformers; related works (e.g., StoryDiffusion [Zhou et al., 2024]) use consistent self‑attention in U‑Nets, indicating the concept is novel in this context.

**Break condition:** If any reference sample carries noise (*t*>0) or lacks the target character, the shared tokens become corrupted or misleading, causing identity drift.

### Mechanism 2: Asymmetric Timestep Sampling

**Claim:** Asymmetric timestep sampling keeps reference tokens noise‑free (*t*=0) while the target is noisy, providing a stable visual context during GSA training.

**Mechanism:** In each training batch of size *N*, assign the target (index 0) a diffusion timestep sampled from a sigmoid‑normal distribution while forcing all references (*j*≥1) to *t*=0. This asymmetry is applied right before the GSA module so that only clean visual tokens enter the extended key/value.

**Core assumption:** The model can learn to denoise the target by querying clean references at any noise level; this mimics inference where references are always clean.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [section] 4.3.2: "Asymmetric Timestep Sampling... we designate the ﬁrst sample as the target and enforce *t* = 0 for all reference samples."
- [corpus] No direct evidence; this training nuance appears unique to this work.

**Break condition:** If the target’s noise distribution is poorly calibrated (e.g., too noisy on average) or batch size is 1, the model cannot form meaningful query‑reference pairs, leading to ineffective identity learning.

### Mechanism 3: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

**Claim:** DPO fine‑tuning aligns the generator with human aesthetic and narrative standards, enhancing perceptual quality and reinforcing identity constraints without conflicting auxiliary losses.

**Mechanism:** After Stage 1, a new LoRA adapter Φd is trained on curated pairs (preferred *I*ʷ, disfavored *I*ˡ). The DPO loss is: `‑E[log σ(β (log πθ(Iʷ)/π_ref(Iʷ) – log πθ(Iˡ)/π_ref(Iˡ)))]`, where πθ is the current policy (backbone + Φc + Φd) and π_ref is the frozen Stage 1 policy. The log‑probability ratio is approximated by the difference in flow matching errors: `(‖v_t – v_ref‖² – ‖v_t – v_θ‖²)`.

**Core assumption:** Human preferences for identity, style, and aesthetics are captured by pairwise comparisons, and optimizing the error difference relative to a reference policy maximizes a reward model implicitly.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "Second, we leverage Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to align generated outputs with human aesthetic preferences via curated pairs, refining fidelity."
- [section] 4.4: Full derivation of DPO objective for flow matching.
- [corpus] DPO is widely used in language and vision alignment; the corpus confirms general adoption but not its specific application to diffusion‑based story generation, making the combination novel.

**Break condition:** If the preference set is biased (e.g., favoring style over identity) or too small, DPO may overfit and degrade the structural consistency learned in Stage 1. The reference policy must remain frozen; otherwise the theoretical guarantee breaks.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Diffusion Transformers & Rectified Flow Matching  
  **Why needed:** The backbone (FLUX.1) is a diffusion transformer trained with flow matching. Understanding the velocity field and flow objectives is essential to interpret the training losses and DPO approximation.  
  **Quick check:** "In rectified flow, what does the network predict, and how does that relate to transporting noise to data?"

- **Concept:** Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA)  
  **Why needed:** Both adapters (Φc and Φd) are implemented as LoRA modules—lightweight rank‑decomposed matrices injected into attention and feed‑forward layers. This allows efficient adaptation of a large pretrained model.  
  **Quick check:** "How does LoRA keep the original pretrained weights frozen while adding adaptivity, and what do the rank and alpha parameters control?"

- **Concept:** Preference Alignment via Direct Preference Optimization  
  **Why needed:** Stage 2 uses DPO to align outputs with human judgments. DPO converts RLHF into a binary classification loss, avoiding explicit reward modeling and policy gradient instability.  
  **Quick check:** "Why does DPO include a reference policy, and how does the implicit KL penalty arise from the Bradley‑Terry model assumption?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Pretrained FLUX.1 backbone (frozen) -> Adapter Φc (LoRA with GSA) -> Adapter Φd (LoRA for DPO) -> Data pipelines -> Two-stage training loop -> Evaluation suite

**Critical path:**
1. Collect and preprocess narrative sequences (≥4 consistent frames) from books/videos, generate dense captions.
2. Train Φc: initialize LoRA, use asymmetric timesteps (target noisy, references clean), minimize flow matching loss on target.
3. Freeze backbone and Φc; generate candidate images for a diverse set of prompts, curate preferred/disfavored pairs based on identity, style, anatomy.
4. Train Φd: initialize new LoRA (zero‑init), minimize DPO loss with β = 1800, keep reference policy fixed.
5. Deploy combined model; during inference provide reference frames, enable GSA, run Euler sampler with CFG = 3.5.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- GSA memory scales with number of reference tokens; increasing references improves consistency but risks OOM.
- Asymmetric sampling requires batch size ≥2; single‑image training cannot use the mechanism.
- DPO performance is sensitive to preference data quality; noisy pairs can damage both identity and style.
- LoRA rank trades capacity for overfitting; the paper uses rank 16, but lower ranks may limit expression.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low CIDS‑cross but normal CIDS‑self: model memorizes references but fails to generalize identity across frames.
- High CSD drop: style drift, indicating GSA not properly injecting style cues or backbone’s realistic bias dominating.
- Anatomical artifacts (missing limbs, distorted faces) after Stage 1: insufficient training or mis‑configured asymmetric sampling.
- DPO loss divergence: βDPO too large or preference pairs too ambiguous.
- GSA inadvertently inactive (e.g., references not set to *t*=0): leads to severe identity degradation.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **GSA Ablation:** Replace GSA with standard self‑attention, train Stage 1 only. Expect large drop in CIDS/CSD, demonstrating necessity of cross‑sample attention.
2. **DPO Ablation:** Train full Stage 1 then skip Stage 2. Compare to full model; expect lower aesthetics and subtle drift, quantifying DPO’s refinement effect.
3. **Reference Count Stress Test:** Vary the number of reference frames per batch (e.g., 1‑4) during Stage 1 training and evaluate CIDS‑cross and memory usage to find optimal trade‑off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How can GSA be redesigned for linear computational complexity relative to reference images, enabling efficient inference for long narratives?
  - **Basis:** Explicitly stated in paper: "computational cost scales with number of reference images."
  - **Why unresolved:** Current GSA has quadratic memory/time with reference count.
  - **Evidence needed:** Approximate GSA variant (low-rank, sparse) benchmarked on sequences with 10–50 references, measuring CIDS/CSD vs. original.

- **Question 2:** What modifications are needed to adapt the framework for highly abstract/non‑anthropomorphic characters (e.g., stylized animals, objects)?
  - **Basis:** Explicit: "maintaining consistency for highly abstract or non‑anthropomorphic characters remains a non‑trivial challenge."
  - **Why unresolved:** Data curation, feature extraction (ArcFace), and GSA may rely on humanoid structure.
  - **Evidence needed:** New benchmark of abstract character sequences, adjusted feature extractors, and new consistency metrics.

- **Question 3:** How to extend the framework to temporally coherent video generation (spatial identity + motion dynamics)?
  - **Basis:** Explicit future work: "extending our framework to consistent video generation tasks."
  - **Why unresolved:** Current method generates discrete frames; video requires continuous motion and temporal layers.
  - **Evidence needed:** Integrate temporal attention/3D convolutions, train on video datasets, evaluate with FVD and temporal smoothness.

- **Question 4:** To what extent does DPO generalize across diverse cultural/aesthetic preferences, given expert‑curated pairs?
  - **Basis:** Inferred: DPO uses "expert‑curated" pairs; no discussion of cultural diversity.
  - **Why unresolved:** Aesthetic preferences are culturally variable; narrow expert pool may cause bias.
  - **Evidence needed:** Cross‑cultural user studies re‑annotating generations; measure divergence from original DPO model.

## Limitations
- Data pipeline opacity (OCR inpainting, DINOv2/Qwen‑VL clustering hyperparameters not released) prevents exact reproduction of ViStoryBench distribution.
- GSA integration details with FLUX.1’s specific DiT architecture are underspecified; token indexing errors could silently break cross‑sample attention.
- DPO preference curation is manual/subjective; potential bias toward aesthetics over identity may undermine Stage 1 gains.
- ViStoryBench benchmark is not public; SOTA claims cannot be independently verified.

## Confidence
- **GSA Mechanism:** Medium (description is clear but lacks attention visualizations or token‑level ablation).
- **Asymmetric Timestep Sampling:** Medium (critical but treated as training detail; impact on convergence unexplored).
- **DPO for Style/Identity:** Medium (novel application; implicit reward may not capture multi‑dimensional trade‑offs).
- **Reported SOTA Gains:** Low (non‑released benchmark and baselines prevent third‑party validation).

## Next Checks
1. **GSA Attention Probing:** During Stage 1 inference, extract attention weights for target image queries. Confirm reference image tokens receive significant attention, especially for identity regions (faces, clothing).
2. **DPO Alignment Audit:** After Stage 2, compute OCCM and Alignment scores on a held‑out set. A sharp drop indicates DPO harming prompt adherence.
3. **Data‑Robustness Stress Test:** Re‑cluster raw video/storybook data using an alternate method (e.g., CLIP + DBSCAN). Train Stage 1 on this split; if CIDS/CSD degrade substantially, gains are data‑pipeline dependent.
