# LexChronos: An Agentic Framework for Structured Event Timeline Extraction in Indian Jurisprudence
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01651
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01651
- **Reference count:** 12
- **Primary result:** A dual-agent framework extracts structured event timelines from Indian Supreme Court judgments with a BERT F1 of 0.8751; timelines improve downstream summarization, with GPT-4 preferring structured-input summaries 75% of the time.

## Executive Summary
LexChronos addresses the challenge of extracting structured event timelines from complex Indian Supreme Court judgments, a task hampered by dense legal language and a lack of annotated data. It introduces a dual-agent framework where a small, tuned extraction agent proposes candidate events and a separate feedback agent iteratively refines them via confidence scoring. To overcome data scarcity, the authors generate a synthetic corpus of 2,000 judgments with gold-standard annotations. The pipeline achieves high extraction accuracy (BERT F1: 0.8751), and downstream summarization experiments demonstrate that structured timelines significantly enhance comprehension, with GPT-4 preferring summaries derived from them over unstructured baselines in 75% of cases.

## Method Summary
The core method is an iterative dual-agent pipeline. First, a synthetic dataset of 2,000 Indian Supreme Court judgments is created via a reverse-engineering process: an LLM generates a structured event timeline (adhering to the LexChronos Event Schema with timestamp, event, judge, precedent) for a selected case category, and a second LLM generates the corresponding judgment text. This provides paired data (timeline, text) for training. The extraction agent (a <4B LoRA-instruct-tuned model) proposes initial events from input judgment text. A separate feedback agent (a pre-trained <4B model) scores these events on seven dimensions and provides a confidence score. The extraction agent revises its output based on this feedback in a loop that terminates via patience or tolerance thresholds (typically ~3 iterations). The final extracted timeline serves as a structured input for downstream tasks.

## Key Results
- **Extraction Performance:** The best configuration (Llama 3.2 3B Instruct extraction agent + Gemma 2 2B IT feedback agent) achieves a BERT-based F1 score of 0.8751 on the synthetic test set.
- **Downstream Utility:** For summarization, GPT-4 prefers summaries generated from the structured timelines over those from raw judgment text in 75% of pairwise comparisons.
- **Iteration Efficiency:** The refinement loop converges effectively after approximately three iterations, balancing improvement and computational cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Dual-Agent Iterative Refinement
- **Claim:** The extraction-feedback loop systematically improves timeline quality by converging on high-confidence outputs.
- **Mechanism:** The extraction agent generates candidate events. The feedback agent evaluates them across seven dimensions (e.g., Narrative Relevance, Temporal Accuracy), assigns a confidence score, and provides textual critique. The extraction agent revises based on this feedback. The controller stops after *patience* (3 iterations without a new best score) or *tolerance* (3 identical scores).
- **Core assumption:** Separating generative and evaluative roles into distinct small models enables more robust validation than a single model, provided feedback scores are reliable.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] describes the dual-agent architecture; [section 4.1] details the loop, feedback dimensions, and stopping criteria, noting empirical optimality at three iterations.
- **Break condition:** Loop halts if confidence does not improve (patience) or stagnates (tolerance), indicating convergence or a local optimum.

### Mechanism 2: Synthetic Corpus via Reverse Engineering
- **Claim:** Generating a timeline-first synthetic corpus effectively addresses data scarcity for Indian legal event extraction.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline: (1) selects a case category, (2) uses LLMs (DeepSeek-R1, GPT-4) with `PromptE` to generate a JSON timeline adhering to the LexChronos Event Schema (LES), (3) uses `PromptJ` to generate judgment text conditioned on that timeline. This yields paired data `(E_i, J_i)` where `E_i` is the gold annotation.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can produce schematically consistent legal timelines and realistic corresponding text that mimic authentic judgments for model training and evaluation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states the 2,000-sample synthetic corpus generation; [section 3] details the 3-stage pipeline and validates LES against judgment components.
- **Break condition:** Validity hinges on synthetic data training models that perform well on *real* judgments. Current evaluation uses synthetic ground truth, creating circularity; external validation on human-annotated real data is required.

### Mechanism 3: Structured Timelines as a Superior Intermediate Representation
- **Claim:** Event-centric structured timelines enhance downstream legal AI tasks (e.g., summarization) compared to raw text.
- **Mechanism:** The LES timeline acts as a knowledge bottleneck, distilling verbose judgments into a concise, temporally ordered sequence of key events with metadata. This provides downstream LLMs with a cleaner, logically sequenced input, allowing focus on generation and reasoning rather than document comprehension and noise filtering.
- **Core assumption:** The LES schema captures the essential factual/procedural nuclei of a judgment sufficient for core tasks; accurate extraction reorganizes information into a more consumable format.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] reports 75% GPT-4 preference for structured-input summaries; [section 4.2] defines the experimental setup; [section 5.3] reports the result.
- **Break condition:** The advantage is bounded by upstream extraction accuracy (F1=0.8751). Lower-fidelity extractions would propagate errors and diminish the preference margin.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Structured Event Extraction for Legal Documents**
  - **Why needed:** Traditional NLP treats legal text as monoliths. LexChronos requires viewing legal "events" as more than verbs—they include actors, temporal markers, and cited precedents as integral attributes, moving beyond sentence-level NER to document-level narrative structuring.
  - **Quick check:** What are the *four* core LES attributes defining a legal event, and why is a simple action verb phrase insufficient for legal reasoning?
- **Concept: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (LoRA)**
  - **Why needed:** The extraction agent is a small (<4B) model. LoRA enables efficient adaptation on the synthetic dataset without full fine-tuning, making iterative deployment feasible and explaining the architecture's efficiency.
  - **Quick check:** Why choose LoRA over full fine-tuning given the dedicated 2,000-sample synthetic dataset?
- **Concept: Iterative Refinement with Separate Critic/Generator Roles**
  - **Why needed:** The dual-agent design relies on the feedback agent as a static "critic" to score and guide the dynamic "generator" (extraction agent). This separation is key to the confidence-driven loop, a specific form of the self-refine paradigm.
  - **Quick check:** What are the two stopping criteria for the extraction-feedback iteration, and what does each aim to detect?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Raw Judgment Text `J` -> Extraction Agent (LoRA-tuned Llama 3.2 3B Instruct) -> Candidate Event List `E_i` -> Feedback Agent (pre-trained Gemma 2 2B IT) -> Feedback `F_i` (scores + critique) -> Controller (checks stop) -> (Stop? Yes: Output `E_k`; No: Loop back to Extraction Agent with `F_i`) -> Downstream Module (e.g., Summarization).
- **Critical path:** `J` -> Extraction -> Feedback -> Controller decision -> (Output) / (Loop). Flow is strictly sequential and synchronous per iteration.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  1.  **Synthetic Data Fidelity vs. Availability:** Synthetic corpus enables development but risks "dataset drift" from real legal complexity.
  2.  **Small Model Efficiency vs. Capacity:** <4B agents ensure low cost/fast iteration but limit raw reasoning; efficacy depends on tuning data and feedback quality.
  3.  **Iterative Depth vs. Cost:** Stopping at ~3 iterations optimizes speed but may prematurely halt improvement on complex judgments; thresholds are heuristics.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Schema Non-Convergence:** Feedback scores oscillate or degrade without meeting stopping criteria, indicating inconsistent scoring or ineffective critique incorporation.
  - **Confidence Score Stagnation:** Confidence plateaus at a low value across iterations, suggesting a capability-complexity mismatch.
  - **Feedback Agent Bias:** If the same LLM is used for both roles (self-feedback), inflated initial scores may not correlate with true quality, as seen in lower final F1 vs. cross-model feedback.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Extraction Agent Selection:** Instruct-tune 8 candidate base models (<4B params) on 1,600 synthetic training samples. Evaluate on 400 held-out samples using BERT F1. Select best (Llama 3.2 3B Instruct, F1=0.8390).
  2.  **Feedback Agent Impact:** Integrate the selected extraction agent with 4 feedback configurations (self-feedback, pre-trained models like Gemma 2 2B IT). Run iterative refinement on test set. Compare final BERT F1 to show benefit of separate competent feedback (best: Gemma 2 2B IT, F1=0.8751).
  3.  **Downstream Utility (Summarization):** For 200 judgments, generate summaries from (a) raw text `J_j` (unstructured) and (b) gold-standard timeline `E_j` (structured) using identical LLMs (Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 2 9B). Use GPT-4 as judge for pairwise preference. Quantify structured-input preference rate (75% for Gemma 2 9B).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Does LexChronos maintain high extraction accuracy on human-annotated real-world Supreme Court judgments compared to synthetic data? *Basis:* Section 8 calls for transition to real-world datasets; Section 7 notes synthetic data may lack authentic complexity. *Resolution needed:* Human-annotated benchmark on real cases.
- **Open Question 2:** Can the framework generalize to judgments of Indian High Courts and lower tribunals? *Basis:* Section 8 identifies expansion to all court levels as essential. *Resolution needed:* Application to multi-level court datasets and performance measurement.
- **Open Question 3:** How can LexChronos be adapted for Indian judicial documents in regional languages? *Basis:* Section 8 highlights linguistic diversity; Section 7 notes current English-only limitation. *Resolution needed:* Multilingual corpora and evaluation of cross-lingual strategies.
- **Open Question 4:** Does performance vary across the 23 case categories not in the synthetic dataset? *Basis:* Inferred from covering only 25 of 48 categories (Section 7 threat). *Resolution needed:* Systematic evaluation on remaining categories.
- **Open Question 5:** Can structured timelines improve judgment prediction models vs. raw text? *Basis:* Section 8 lists judgment prediction as future work; summarization tested but not prediction. *Resolution needed:* Integration into prediction pipelines and accuracy comparison.

## Limitations
- **Synthetic data fidelity:** No human-annotated real-world validation; risks dataset drift from authentic legal complexity.
- **Feedback agent reliability:** Scoring dimensions may reflect LLM biases rather than legal expert judgment; calibration to human critique is unverified.
- **Generalizability:** Evaluation limited to Indian Supreme Court judgments; applicability to other jurisdictions, languages, or document types is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Dual-agent refinement (Mechanism 1):** Medium. Internal ablations show benefit vs. baselines, but dependent on synthetic ground truth and small-model constraints.
- **Synthetic corpus utility (Mechanism 2):** Low. Circular evaluation (training & testing on synthetic data); external validity unestablished.
- **Structured timeline advantage (Mechanism 3):** Medium-High. Downstream preference (75%) is robust, but contingent on extraction accuracy (F1=0.8751).

## Next Checks
1.  **Human-annotated benchmark:** Annotate 100–200 real Indian Supreme Court judgments with the LES schema; compute inter-annotator agreement and re-evaluate LexChronos F1 against this gold standard.
2.  **Feedback–human alignment:** Have legal experts score a sample of extraction outputs on the same seven dimensions; compute correlation with the feedback agent’s scores.
3.  **Cross-domain portability:** Apply the trained pipeline to a distinct legal corpus (e.g., US federal appellate opinions); measure F1 degradation to quantify domain specificity.
