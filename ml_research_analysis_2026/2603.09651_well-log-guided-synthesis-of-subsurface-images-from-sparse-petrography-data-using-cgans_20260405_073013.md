# Well Log-Guided Synthesis of Subsurface Images from Sparse Petrography Data Using cGANs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09651
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09651
- Reference count: 8
- Primary result: A conditional GAN (cGAN) generates realistic thin section images of carbonate rocks conditioned on well-log porosity, achieving 81% accuracy within ±10% margin across the porosity range 0.004–0.745, enabling continuous pore-scale visualization along the wellbore.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN) framework to synthesize realistic thin-section images of carbonate rocks from well-log porosity values. The method addresses the high cost and sparsity of direct pore-scale imaging by training on a limited set of core samples (5,000 sub-images from 15 thin sections). By conditioning the generator on a continuous porosity scalar, the model produces images whose pore content matches the target value with high accuracy. The approach enables continuous, depth-resolved pore-scale visualization along the wellbore, bridging gaps between discrete core samples and improving reservoir characterization.

## Method Summary
The authors train a cGAN on 256×256 pixel sub-images extracted from 15 carbonate thin-section samples (depth 1992–2000 m). Each sub-image is labeled with a porosity value derived via HSV thresholding. To handle data imbalance, images are binned into 10 porosity classes and augmented to yield a balanced dataset of ~5,000 samples. The generator takes a 100-dimensional noise vector concatenated with the scaled porosity value and produces a 256×256 RGB image through six transposed convolutional layers. The discriminator takes an image and the same porosity condition (concatenated to a middle layer) and outputs a real/fake probability. The networks are trained adversarially for 100 epochs using Adam (lr=2×10⁻⁴) and binary cross-entropy loss. At inference, the fixed generator synthesizes images for any well-log porosity value, allowing continuous along-wellbore generation.

## Key Results
- **Porosity accuracy**: 81% of generated images have measured porosity within ±10% of the target value across the range 0.004–0.745.
- **Geological realism**: Visual comparison shows the model reproduces key carbonate features (e.g., grains, pores, cement).
- **Continuous visualization**: Generated images along a well-log profile exhibit coherent porosity-driven textural variations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Conditioning the generator on well-log porosity values enables the synthesis of pore-scale images with target porosity.
- **Mechanism**: The cGAN architecture injects a continuous porosity value (scalar) as an additional input channel to both generator and discriminator networks. This forces the generator to produce images whose statistical pore content matches the specified porosity, while the discriminator evaluates both image realism and porosity consistency.
- **Core assumption**: Porosity is a sufficient conditional parameter to control the primary pore-scale structure in these carbonate thin sections, implying a deterministic (or strongly correlated) relationship between bulk porosity and local pore geometry.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN) framework for synthesizing realistic thin section images... conditioned on porosity values derived from well logs."
  - [section 2]: "The network architecture incorporates porosity as a conditional parameter, where the generator synthesizes images from random noise conditioned on porosity values..."
  - [corpus]: Neighbor paper "PCP-GAN" also uses property conditioning, suggesting this is an active research direction for pore-scale synthesis. However, direct evidence for *this specific causal link* (porosity → structure) is not independently validated in the paper; it is an inferred mechanism from the model's success.
- **Break condition**: The mechanism would fail if the training data's porosity range did not capture the true diversity of structures for a given porosity (e.g., different diagenetic histories producing same porosity but different grain arrangements), or if porosity alone is insufficient to constrain other critical features like mineralogy or pore shape distribution.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Adversarial training with a balanced multi-class dataset teaches the generator the complex, multi-modal distribution of geological structures across porosity classes.
- **Mechanism**: The generator (6 transposed conv layers) and discriminator (5 conv layers) are trained in a min-max game. The discriminator learns to distinguish real vs. synthetic images *and* whether the synthetic image's porosity matches its conditional label. This dual objective pushes the generator to create images that are both geologically plausible and quantitatively accurate in porosity.
- **Core assumption**: The geological variations within the 15 samples (1992–2000m interval) are representative of the broader formation's structural diversity, and a 256x256 pixel sub-image captures a statistically meaningful volume for thin-section analysis.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 2]: "Both networks were simultaneously trained, the generator to produce increasingly realistic images that match the conditional parameters, and the discriminator to improve its ability to distinguish between real and generated samples while considering the specified condition."
  - [section 3]: "Visual comparison between the training and synthetic images reveals successful reproduction of key carbonate rock features."
  - [corpus]: Corpus shows active research on GANs for porous media (e.g., Mosser et al. cited), supporting the general viability of the mechanism. No corpus paper directly validates this specific training regime's superiority.
- **Break condition**: Training instability (mode collapse, oscillations) would cause the generator to produce repetitive, non-diverse structures or fail to match porosity. This could occur with an inadequate number of training samples (5k sub-images from 15 full samples) or imbalanced class distribution without augmentation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Interpolation along the well-log porosity profile enables continuous, depth-resolved pore-scale visualization by treating the trained generator as a deterministic function of porosity.
- **Mechanism**: Once the cGAN is trained, the generator network becomes a fixed function `G(noise, porosity)`. By feeding the continuous well-log porosity values (at any depth interval) and sampling new noise vectors, one can synthesize a coherent series of images representing the expected pore structure progression along the wellbore.
- **Core assumption**: The well-log porosity is an accurate, continuous proxy for the true pore-scale structure changes with depth, and the generator can extrapolate beyond the discrete training sample depths (1992–2000m) to adjacent intervals.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "The successful integration of well log data with the trained generator enables continuous pore-scale visualization along the wellbore, bridging the gaps between discrete core sampling points."
  - [section 3 & Figure 3]: "Generated synthetic thin section images at various depths using porosity values obtained from well log data... The generated images show consistent geological features corresponding to the porosity variations observed in the well log."
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers on well log prediction (e.g., "Leveraging Time-Series Foundation Model for Subsurface Well Logs") highlight the value of continuous log data, but do not address its use as a direct conditioning input for image synthesis.
- **Break condition**: Generates geologically implausible transitions between adjacent depths if the well-log porosity curve has high-frequency noise not representative of true stratigraphic changes, or if the generator has not learned smooth structural transitions across porosity (e.g., abrupt mineralogical changes not captured in training).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (cGANs)**
  - *Why needed here*: The core methodology is a cGAN, not a standard GAN. Understanding how the conditioning vector (porosity) is incorporated into both networks is essential to grasp how target properties are controlled.
  - *Quick check question*: In a cGAN, how does the conditioning information affect the generator's input and the discriminator's decision process differently than in an unconditional GAN?
- **Concept: Porosity Measurement and Representation**
  - *Why needed here*: The entire conditioning scheme depends on accurate porosity values derived from well logs and used as labels for training. One must understand what porosity means in this context (2D thin-section image porosity from epoxy-stained pixels) and its range (0.004–0.745).
  - *Quick check question*: What are the potential sources of error in deriving a single "porosity value" for a 256x256 pixel thin-section sub-image, and how might that affect the training labels?
- **Concept: Data Augmentation and Class Balancing for Imbalanced Geological Datasets**
  - *Why needed here*: The paper states images were "systematically categorized based on porosity ranges into 10 classes, and data augmentation was employed to achieve a balanced distribution." This is a critical preprocessing step for stable GAN training on a small, naturally sparse dataset (5k images from 15 samples).
  - *Quick check question*: Why is a balanced class distribution particularly important for a conditional GAN tasked with generating specific porosity ranges, and what common augmentation techniques might have been used on thin-section images?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Generator: [100-dim noise z] + [scaled porosity φ] --> 6 transposed conv layers (LeakyReLU) --> 256x256x3 RGB image (tanh)
Discriminator: [256x256x3 image] + [scaled porosity φ] --> 5 conv layers (LeakyReLU) --> concat φ to penultimate layer --> fully connected layer --> scalar probability (sigmoid)
Loss: Binary cross-entropy (real vs. fake)

### Critical Path
1. Data pipeline: Extract 256x256 patches from 15 full thin-section images → measure porosity via HSV thresholding → bin into 10 porosity classes → apply augmentations (rotation, flip) to balance classes → final dataset: 5,000 (image, φ) pairs.
2. Training loop: For each batch: sample z and (image, φ) pairs → G generates G(z, φ) → D evaluates (image, φ) and (G(z, φ), φ) → update D (maximize log(D(real)) + log(1-D(fake))) → update G (minimize log(1-D(fake))).
3. Inference: Fix G → For any well-log porosity φ_target, sample z → output G(z, φ_target).

### Design Tradeoffs
- Image resolution (256x256): Balances computational cost and capture of pore-scale features. Higher resolution loses fine grain/pore throat detail; higher resolution exponentially increases parameters and requires vastly more training data.
- Conditioning mechanism: Injecting φ as an extra channel vs. embedding in a middle layer. Simpler to concatenate, but may not force deep integration with spatial features. Paper doesn't specify, but cGAN standard is to embed in latent space or middle layers.
- Class binning (10 classes): Discretizing continuous porosity simplifies learning but introduces quantization error. A smoother conditioning (continuous scalar) is likely used in the network, but binning aids dataset balancing. Tradeoff: Bins lose exact porosity precision but improve per-class sample count.

### Failure Signatures
- Training: Discriminator loss rapidly drops to zero (D completely overs powers G → no learning); Generator loss increases to infinity (mode collapse, G produces nothing); No improvement in visual quality over epochs.
- Inference: Generated images have pores/grains at wrong scales for the porosity (e.g., high φ but few pores). Visual artifacts: checkerboard patterns from transposed convs, inconsistent grain boundaries, color distortions from HSV segmentation mismatch.
- Quantitative: Porosity of generated image (via same HSV segmentation) falls outside the 10% margin for target φ consistently.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Conditioning Ablation: Train a baseline unconditional GAN on same data. Compare generated image diversity and porosity distribution (should be uniform/uncontrolled) vs. conditional model (should cluster around training porosity values). Validates that conditioning is the active control mechanism.
2. Porosity Regression Test: Train a separate CNN porosity predictor on real images. Apply it to generated images. Plot predicted porosity vs. conditioned φ. Expect strong correlation along y=x line within margin. Tests if generator truly encodes porosity.
3. Depth Continuity Check: Generate a sequence of images for a smoothly varying synthetic well-log curve (e.g., linearly increasing φ from 0.1 to 0.4). Visually inspect for abrupt, geologically unrealistic transitions between adjacent depth steps. Tests the smoothness of the learned manifold.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**How can the cGAN framework be extended to incorporate multiple conditioning parameters such as permeability and mineralogical composition?**  
*Basis*: The authors explicitly state: "Future work could extend this methodology to incorporate multiple conditioning parameters such as permeability and mineralogy" (Conclusion).  
*Why unresolved*: The current model is conditioned solely on porosity, which may not capture the full heterogeneity influencing fluid flow and mechanical behavior in carbonate reservoirs. Multi-parameter conditioning is necessary for comprehensive reservoir characterization.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Develop and train a multi-conditional cGAN that accepts porosity, permeability, and mineral fractions as inputs. Validate by comparing generated images to independent datasets, assessing both image realism (e.g., via FID score) and the accuracy of derived physical properties through numerical simulations.

### Open Question 2
**Can the approach be scaled to generate three-dimensional pore-scale images directly from sparse well-log data?**  
*Basis*: The study identifies "on-demand 3D generation of pore-scale images" as a key future direction (Conclusion).  
*Why unresolved*: The model currently produces 2D thin sections, but 3D connectivity is critical for accurate permeability prediction and fluid flow modeling. A 2D-to-3D extension would better represent subsurface formations.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Implement a 3D cGAN or a progressive growing architecture trained on volumetric micro-CT data. Validation would involve comparing pore network statistics (e.g., porosity, tortuosity, coordination number) between generated 3D volumes and reference scans, as well as flow simulation results.

### Open Question 3
**How well does the trained cGAN generalize to carbonate rocks from different depositional environments or depth intervals beyond the training set (1992–2000 m)?**  
*Basis*: The training data is limited to 15 samples from a narrow 8 m interval, which may not represent the full lithofacies variability in carbonates.  
*Why unresolved*: Carbonate heterogeneity arises from diverse grain types, cementation, and dissolution patterns. A model trained on a small, depth-constrained set may fail to generate geologically realistic images for other formations, limiting its field applicability.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Test the model on hold-out datasets from different wells or geological settings. Evaluate generalization through quantitative metrics (porosity accuracy, structural similarity) and qualitative assessment by geologists for textural features like grain size and pore shape fidelity.

### Open Question 4
**Do the generated images preserve topological features critical for permeability, or do they only match porosity without reproducing flow-relevant pore network architecture?**  
*Basis*: Validation focused exclusively on porosity accuracy (81% within 10% margin), while permeability—a key flow property—remains unassessed.  
*Why unresolved*: Porosity alone does not determine permeability; pore throat size distribution, connectivity, and tortuosity are equally important. Synthetic images might match porosity statistics but misrepresent these topological aspects.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Compute permeability using lattice-Boltzmann or pore-network modeling on both generated and real images. Compare permeability distributions and their correlation with porosity to determine if the model captures flow-relevant microstructure beyond first-order statistics.

## Limitations
- **Data scarcity**: Training on only 15 core samples from an 8 m depth interval likely underrepresents formation heterogeneity, risking poor generalization.
- **Conditioning simplicity**: Relying solely on porosity may not capture other critical pore-scale attributes (e.g., mineralogy, pore shape) that influence reservoir properties.
- **Measurement uncertainty**: Porosity labels derived from HSV segmentation introduce noise; the ±10% margin may partly reflect segmentation error rather than model error.

## Next Checks
1. **Conditioning ablation study**: Train an unconditional GAN on the same dataset. Compare the porosity distribution of generated images to the conditional model's outputs. A significant difference (e.g., conditional model’s porosity tightly clustered around target values vs. unconditional model’s uniform spread) would empirically validate the conditioning mechanism.
2. **Porosity regression test**: Train a robust CNN porosity predictor on a held-out set of real thin-section images. Apply this predictor to a large sample of generated images across the full porosity range. Report the R² and mean absolute error between predicted porosity and the conditioned target. This tests whether the generator’s output porosity truly matches the input condition beyond the paper’s HSV segmentation.
3. **Depth transition plausibility test**: Generate a sequence of images for a synthetic, smoothly varying porosity curve (e.g., sinusoidal or linear ramp) while fixing the noise vector. Conduct a blind ordinal ranking test with domain experts to judge whether adjacent images in the sequence represent geologically plausible transitions. High discontinuity ratings would challenge the model’s utility for continuous wellbore visualization.
