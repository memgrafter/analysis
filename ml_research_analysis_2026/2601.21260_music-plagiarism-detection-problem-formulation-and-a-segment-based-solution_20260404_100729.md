# Music Plagiarism Detection: Problem Formulation and a Segment-based Solution

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.21260  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21260  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Defines Music Plagiarism Detection (MPD) as a task distinct from cover identification and fingerprinting; proposes a segment transcription pipeline and multimodal Siamese retrieval; introduces the SMP dataset; reports MERT recall@10 of 51.0% on SMP Timestamps but only 7.9% on Full Indices; algorithmic music-domain method achieves 0.475 mAP on Covers80.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Music Plagiarism Detection (MPD) as a distinct problem focusing on partial similarity and selective musical elements, unlike cover song identification (whole-song similarity) and audio fingerprinting (acoustic signal matching). The authors propose a segment-based solution: a multi-stage transcription pipeline converts raw audio into a searchable library of structured segments (melody, chords, etc.); a multimodal Siamese network (or an algorithmic baseline) retrieves similar segments; and a weighted voting filter aggregates segment matches into song-level decisions. They also release the Similar Music Pair (SMP) dataset containing real plagiarism cases with precise timestamps. On the SMP Timestamps set (small, timestamped segments), the MERT acoustic model reaches 51.0% recall@10, but performance drops sharply to 7.9% on a large library (Full Indices) with ~24k segments, underscoring scalability challenges. The multimodal Siamese model underperforms unimodal baselines, likely due to limited training data. For whole-song retrieval on Covers80, the algorithmic music-domain method yields 0.475 mAP, far below SOTA cover identification (~0.93), but provides temporal localization and explainable similarity reasons.

## Method Summary
The system begins with raw audio. A segment transcription pipeline processes it: Demucs separates sources; an all-in-one model performs structural analysis and beat tracking; specialized transcribers extract vocals (AST), melody (SheetSage), and chords (Harmony Transformer). Segments are fixed 4-bar units starting at downbeats, stored with metadata (time, key, structure) and representations (pianoroll, chord labels) in a library.

Two retrieval approaches are explored: (a) a multimodal Siamese network with encoders for MERT audio features and a CNN on pianorolls, trained with contrastive loss on SMP segment pairs; (b) an algorithmic music-domain method combining pianoroll, onset rhythm, and chord similarity. The top-k segment matches are aggregated into a song-level plagiarism decision via weighted majority vote (weights decay linearly from 20 for rank 1 to 1 for rank 20). Models are trained with Adam (lr = 1e‑4, batch = 32) for 100 epochs using 5‑fold cross‑validation due to the small SMP dataset.

## Key Results
- On the small SMP Timestamps set, MERT achieves 51.0% recall@10 for segment retrieval, but on the large Full Indices set (≈24,843 segments) recall@10 drops to 7.9%, revealing severe scalability issues.
- The multimodal Siamese model (MM) underperforms unimodal MERT (51.0%) on SMP Timestamps, achieving only 41.5% recall@10, likely due to insufficient multimodal training data.
- For whole-song retrieval on Covers80, the algorithmic music-domain method yields 0.475 mAP, substantially below SOTA cover identification models (~0.93), yet it provides precise temporal localization and interpretable similarity reasons.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Segment Transcription as Indexing Foundation**  
The segment transcription pipeline converts raw audio into discrete, musically meaningful segments (melody, chords, structure) stored in a searchable library. This enables efficient search and precise temporal localization. The pipeline uses Demucs, an all-in-one model, and specialized transcribers (AST, SheetSage, Harmony Transformer). The core assumption is that these representations retain key musical information (e.g., melody contour) while being robust to superficial changes. Evidence appears in Section 3.1 and the abstract. Break condition: transcription errors introduce noise, causing false positives/negatives.

**Siamese Network for Selective Element Similarity**  
A multimodal Siamese network learns a shared embedding space where plagiarized segments (even with acoustic differences) are closer than non‑plagiarized ones. Two encoders (fine‑tuned MERT for audio, CNN for pianoroll) are trained with contrastive loss; a dual‑encoder cross‑fusion variant combines modalities. The assumption: the model focuses on protected elements (melody, harmony) rather than timbre. Evidence: Section 3.2 and Table 2 show unimodal models outperform multimodal, suggesting data scarcity or fusion issues. Break conditions: insufficient data or misalignment with legal standards.

**Weighted Voting Aggregation for Partial Plagiarism**  
Segment matches are aggregated into song‑level decisions via weighted majority vote: top‑20 matches contribute scores with linearly decaying weights (20 → 1). A song is flagged if the sum exceeds a threshold. The assumption: a plagiarized song yields multiple segment matches, and the weighted sum distinguishes this from random coincidences. Evidence: Section 4.3 and Table 3 show the algorithmic “Music” method achieves 0.475 mAP on Covers80, surpassing deep models, indicating utility of aggregation and explainable segments. Break condition: too few true matches or many coincidental matches in common segments leading to false positives.

## Foundational Learning

- **Music Segment Transcription**  
  *Why needed:* The entire architecture relies on converting unstructured audio into discrete, semantically rich segments; inaccurate transcription degrades similarity search.  
  *Quick check:* What is the trade‑off between segment length (e.g., 4 bars) and the ability to capture short, distinctive motifs that might constitute infringement?

- **Partial vs. Whole‑Item Similarity**  
  *Why needed:* MPD is defined by partial similarity, unlike cover song identification. This shifts the paradigm from retrieving the most similar whole item to finding any matching sub‑item, requiring different evaluation (Rec.1s@k vs. mAP) and system design.  
  *Quick check:* How does Rec.1s@k (temporal precision within 1 s) enforce partial similarity differently than whole‑song ranking accuracy?

- **Siamese Network for Metric Learning**  
  *Why needed:* The core task is learning a distance metric where plagiarized pairs are closer than non‑plagiarized ones, rather than classifying fixed categories; this is essential for open‑set retrieval from a large library.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a triplet loss be more suitable than a contrastive loss given SMP provides positive pairs but many implicit negatives?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Raw audio → Demucs (source separation) → all‑in‑one (structural analysis & beat tracking) → AST (vocals), SheetSage (melody), Harmony Transformer (chords) → 4‑bar segments (downbeat‑aligned) with metadata (time, key, structure) and representations (pianoroll, chords) stored in a library. For retrieval: segment embeddings generated by MERT (audio) or CNN (pianoroll) in a Siamese network, or by algorithmic combination; top‑k matches aggregated via weighted majority vote → song‑level plagiarism candidates with timestamps and similarity reasons.

**Critical Path**  
Segment transcription is the bottleneck. Its accuracy and speed directly limit overall system quality and scalability. Transcription errors propagate into the library, degrading search. The approximate nearest neighbor search is a secondary bottleneck, as seen by the Full Indices performance drop.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Segment length vs. precision:* Shorter segments (2 bars) capture brief motifs and improve localization but increase library size and noise; longer segments (8 bars) reduce size but may miss short infringements.  
- *Model complexity vs. data:* The multimodal Siamese likely requires more data than available; a single robust encoder (MERT or CNN) may be more practical.  
- *Explainability vs. performance:* Algorithmic method offers clear explanations (melody/chord/rhythm weights) but lower recall; deep models could improve recall but are black boxes without post‑hoc interpretability.

**Failure Signatures**  
1. Sharp recall drop from SMP Timestamps (51.0%) to Full Indices (7.9%) indicates the similarity model is not robust to large, noisy libraries, possibly retrieving common patterns (e.g., clichéd chords).  
2. Multimodal model (MM) underperforms unimodal MERT/CNN on the small set (41.5% vs. 51.0%), suggesting fusion complexity outweighs benefits with limited data.  
3. Low mAP on Covers80 (0.475 vs. ~0.93) for deep models confirms they are not learning cover‑song invariances; this is expected for plagiarism but highlights task misalignment if CSI performance is expected.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. **Transcription validation:** Manually inspect 50 random library segments; verify downbeat alignment, melody (SheetSage) note accuracy, and chord labels (Harmony Transformer).  
2. **Segment similarity sanity check:** Apply the algorithmic music‑domain method on SMP Timestamps; expect ~29.6% Rec.1s@10 (Table 2) to establish a robust baseline.  
3. **Scalability stress test:** Run MERT similarity on Full Indices; expect ~7.9% Rec.1s@10, quantifying the needle‑in‑haystack challenge and motivating better ANN indexing or pre‑filtering.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1:** Why does performance degrade so drastically when scaling from a small segment set (51.0% Rec.1s@10) to a large library (7.9%)? The paper notes the drop but does not isolate the cause (e.g., overfitting, noise, retrieval inefficiency). Resolving would require ablation studies on Full Indices varying segment selection, transcription noise, and retrieval parameters.
- **Open Question 2:** Why did the multimodal Siamese network underperform single‑modality models? The authors hypothesize insufficient data but do not test it; fusion design may also be flawed. Evidence could come from controlled experiments with larger multimodal datasets or architectural ablations.
- **Open Question 3:** What specific musical elements (beyond melody/chords/rhythm) are most discriminative for plagiarism, and can they be robustly extracted? The paper does not analyze which elements cause failures on SMP cases nor evaluate the accuracy of its "explainable similarity" reasons. Error analysis and human validation are needed.
- **Open Question 4:** Is the weighted majority voting aggregation optimal? The heuristic (linear decay weights) is used without justification; the authors suggest better methods are needed. Comparing alternative aggregations (clustering, temporal coherence modeling, learned classifiers) could identify more effective approaches.

## Limitations
- The segment transcription pipeline’s exact implementation (beat‑tracking, alignment across tempos) is not fully disclosed, relying on prior work and introducing uncertainty in reproducibility.
- The multimodal Siamese model’s underperformance and the unspecified weighting scheme for the algorithmic music‑domain method limit full understanding and replication.
- The scalability failure (performance drop on Full Indices) is identified but not diagnosed; root causes remain unclear.
