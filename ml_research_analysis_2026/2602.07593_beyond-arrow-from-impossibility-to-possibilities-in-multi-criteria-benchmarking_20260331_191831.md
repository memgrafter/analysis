# Beyond Arrow: From Impossibility to Possibilities in Multi-Criteria Benchmarking

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07593
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07593
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** Multi-criteria benchmarking can yield coherent, stable model rankings via pairwise majority aggregation when metric preferences satisfy single-peakedness, group separability, or distance-restrictedness.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the incoherence and instability that arise when aggregating multiple evaluation metrics (e.g., accuracy, efficiency) into a single model ranking, as seen in benchmarks like HELM and MMLU. It reframes the problem as social choice aggregation, where each metric is a voter. Rather than focusing on Arrow’s impossibility theorem, the authors identify three domain restrictions on the preference profiles that restore possibility: single-peakedness, group separability, and distance-restrictedness. Theoretically, they prove that pairwise majority aggregation satisfies Arrow’s axioms under these conditions. Empirically on HELM MMLU, they verify that accuracy-only metrics often satisfy these domains, while mixing accuracy and efficiency typically violates them, leading to widespread Condorcet cycles and ranking instability. For datasets meeting a domain condition, they aggregate across subjects using a commonality sharing rule (generalized Tukey depth) to produce a stable representative ranking.

## Method Summary
The study uses HELM MMLU v1.0.0 results, involving 23 models and 57 subject datasets. For each subject, metric scores (e.g., `exact_match`, `inference_time`) are converted into strict rankings via deterministic tie-breaking. The authors then test whether the resulting preference profiles (metrics as voters, models as alternatives) satisfy one of three structural conditions: single-peakedness (existence of a common linear model order where each metric has a single peak), group separability (for every model subset, a non-trivial partition exists that all metrics agree on), or distance-restrictedness (all metric pairwise rankings differ by at most one Kendall tau swap). For subjects where any condition holds, the pairwise majority relation is computed and verified to be acyclic and axiom-compliant. Finally, across all such subjects, the subject-level majority rankings are aggregated using generalized Tukey depth to select a deepest (most central) ranking as the benchmark-wide result.

## Key Results
- Single-peakedness holds for all 57 subjects when using only accuracy metrics (Φ_acc) and a high-accuracy model subset (A1), but fails for mixed accuracy+efficiency metrics (Φ_mix) with broader model sets.
- Distance-restrictedness (swap distance ≤1) holds for accuracy-only metrics but is violated when efficiency metrics are included.
- Instability and Condorcet cycles are widespread: 44/57 subjects flip rankings with model addition, and 51/57 exhibit cycles when using mixed metrics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Single-peaked preference profiles guarantee that pairwise majority aggregation yields a complete, transitive ranking satisfying Arrow’s axioms (Non-Dictatorship, IIA, Weak Pareto).
- **Mechanism:** Models are arranged on a one-dimensional spectrum (e.g., by size or capability). Each metric has a single “peak” (most preferred region) on this axis, with preferences declining monotonically as models move away from the peak in either direction. This structure prevents Condorcet cycles because majority preferences align along the common axis.
- **Core assumption:** There exists a shared linear ordering of models such that every metric’s preferences are single-peaked with respect to it.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.1 (Definition 5.1, Theorem 5.2); empirical validation in Section 6.2 on HELM MMLU.
- **Break condition:** If no common axis exists where all metrics exhibit a single peak (e.g., conflicting “sweet spots”), cycles reappear.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Group-separable preference profiles (with an odd number of metrics) ensure pairwise majority aggregation produces a transitive, coherent ranking satisfying Arrow’s axioms.
- **Mechanism:** For every subset of models, there exists a non-trivial partition (separation) where all metrics rank every model in one block above every model in the other block. This hierarchical structure prevents intransitive majority cycles by ensuring consistent bloc-based comparisons.
- **Core assumption:** For all subsets S ⊆ A, there is at least one separation E ⊂ S such that either all metrics rank E entirely above S\E or entirely below.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.2 (Definition 5.3, Theorem 5.4); empirical support in Section 6.2.
- **Break condition:** If any subset of models lacks a universal separation (metrics disagree on relative ordering of blocks), the guarantee breaks.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Distance-restricted preference profiles (swap distance ≤1 between any two metrics) ensure pairwise majority aggregation is transitive and complete, satisfying Arrow’s axioms.
- **Mechanism:** All metric rankings are nearly identical, differing by at most one adjacent swap. This near-consensus means majority preferences cannot cycle, as only one pairwise comparison may flip from a reference order.
- **Core assumption:** For all metrics i, j, the Kendall tau distance d_S(PD,i, PD,j) ≤ 1.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.3; empirical tests in Section 6.2.
- **Break condition:** If any pair of metrics disagrees on more than one pairwise model comparison, the transitivity guarantee is lost.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Social choice theory & Arrow’s impossibility theorem**  
  *Why needed:* Recasts benchmarking as preference aggregation (metrics as voters, models as alternatives). Arrow’s theorem establishes the baseline impossibility, making domain restrictions necessary.  
  *Quick check:* What does the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) axiom require, and why is it often violated by mean aggregation?

- **Concept: Single-peaked and other Condorcet domains**  
  *Why needed:* These structural conditions avoid Arrow’s impossibility. The paper’s core contribution is identifying which are empirically plausible in benchmarks.  
  *Quick check:* If models are ordered by parameter count, why might “interpretability” preferences be single-peaked on that axis?

- **Concept: Pairwise majority & Condorcet cycles**  
  *Why needed:* Pairwise majority is the natural local aggregation rule; Condorcet cycles are the primary coherence failure the mechanisms prevent.  
  *Quick check:* How can three metrics produce a cycle A≻B, B≻C, C≻A even if each metric’s ranking is transitive?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Fixed model set A, dataset universe D, metrics Φ → Per-dataset processor (compute PD,i strict rankings) → Domain checker (test single-peakedness, group separability, distance-restrictedness) → Aggregation operator (pairwise majority if condition holds) → Cross-dataset aggregator (commonality sharing via generalized Tukey depth).

**Critical path:** Metric scores → per-dataset preference profiles → domain verification → (if condition holds) pairwise majority → cross-dataset depth aggregation → final model ranking.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Model/metric selection strongly affects domain satisfaction (e.g., mixing accuracy and efficiency may break conditions).  
- Commonality sharing picks a central ranking, but heterogeneous datasets may yield a “deepest” ranking that does not capture all nuances.  
- Pairwise majority satisfies Arrow’s axioms only under domain restrictions; without them, cycles may occur.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Condorcet cycle detected in pairwise majority relation for a subject.  
- Group separability test fails for some model subset.  
- Swap distance >1 between two metric rankings.  
- Commonality sharing input includes a cyclic ranking (undefined or nonsense output).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Single-peakedness test on HELM MMLU:** For (A1, Φ_acc), verify all 57 subjects satisfy (as in paper). If not, investigate model ordering that maximizes peaks.  
2. **Distance-restrictedness test:** For (A4, Φ_acc), compute pairwise swap distances; confirm max ≤1 for all subjects. If violated, identify offending metric pairs.  
3. **Cross-dataset aggregation:** For a domain where a condition holds (e.g., (A1, Φ_acc)), compute pairwise majority for each subject, then apply commonality sharing to produce final ranking; compare to mean-aggregation leaderboard.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. To what extent do the identified restricted preference domains hold in other multi-criteria benchmark suites beyond HELM MMLU, PMLB, and OpenML?
2. Under the restricted domains, is pairwise majority aggregation strategy-proof, and how vulnerable is it to strategic manipulation by model developers?
3. What principles should guide the selection of models and metrics to maximize the likelihood of satisfying a restricted domain?
4. Do the restricted domains and aggregation properties remain stable when new models are added to the benchmark over time?

## Limitations
- Tie-breaking rule ambiguity: Paper’s deterministic alphabetical order affects group separability counts; reverse order changes results slightly.
- Efficient single-peakedness check: For larger model sets, brute-force enumeration is infeasible; the paper’s exact efficient method is unspecified.
- Scope: Empirical validation limited to HELM MMLU; generalizability to other benchmarks is unknown.
- Theoretical proofs assume strict rankings; ties may break axioms.

## Confidence
- Theoretical domain results (single-peakedness, group separability, distance-restrictedness): **High**
- Empirical prevalence of conditions in HELM MMLU: **Medium**
- Cross-dataset aggregation via commonality sharing: **Medium**
