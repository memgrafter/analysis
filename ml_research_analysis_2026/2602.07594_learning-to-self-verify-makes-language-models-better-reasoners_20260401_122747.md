# Learning to Self-Verify Makes Language Models Better Reasoners
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07594
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07594
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: Training a model solely for self-verification improves generation performance and efficiency; integrated training yields further gains.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the asymmetry between generation and self-verification in large language models (LLMs). It shows that improving generation ability via standard reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) does not correspondingly improve self-verification ability. However, the reverse is true: training a model solely to self-verify significantly boosts its subsequent generation performance. The authors propose a multi-task RL framework that decouples but integrates generation and verification objectives, using strategies such as self-verification pretraining or alternating phases. Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that self-verification training achieves comparable accuracy to generation-only training while using 25-75% fewer reasoning tokens, and integrated training further improves average accuracy by approximately 2.5 points across multiple benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The study uses reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) with the GRPO optimizer to train LLMs (Qwen2.5-Instruct series) on the DAPO-Math-17K dataset. Two training regimes are central: (1) generation training, where the model receives a reward `r_g` based on correctness of the final answer; (2) self-verification training, where the model judges the correctness of given solutions and receives reward `r_v` based on agreement with a ground-truth binary label. Verification training data is collected on-policy from the current policy, then filtered for valid outputs, balanced between correct and incorrect solutions, and ensured query diversity. Integrated approaches include `Verify-Init` (pretrain on verification, then generate) and `Verify-Alter` (alternate between generation and verification phases). Models are evaluated on six math benchmarks using accuracy (Acc@16) and average reasoning token count.

## Key Results
- Self-verification training yields comparable task accuracy (within 1-2% on MATH500) to generation-only training while reducing reasoning tokens by 25-75%. It also improves verification accuracy by 5-17% absolute.
- Integrated training strategies (`Verify-Init`, `Verify-Alter`) boost average accuracy by ~2.5 points over generation-only baselines across multiple benchmarks.
- The experiments confirm a reverse asymmetry: generation training does not improve verification, but verification training improves generation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Improving LLM generation ability via standard RLVR does not correspondingly improve its self-verification ability, even on the same task.
- **Mechanism:** The training objective and gradient updates in standard generation training (RLVR) are solely driven by the correctness of the final answer (`r_g`). This signal optimizes the policy for producing correct solutions but does not explicitly shape the internal representations or decision processes required to reliably evaluate the correctness of intermediate or complete reasoning traces. The model learns *what* a correct solution looks like but not the discriminative features needed to *judge* one.
- **Core assumption:** The capability to generate and the capability to verify are distinct skill sets that require separate optimization signals, and the former does not inherently train the latter within the RLVR framework.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "we show that, even on the same task, improving generation does not lead to corresponding improvements in self-verification."
  - [section 3] Figure 1 (top) shows training dynamics where the generation reward (`Reward`) increases while verification accuracy (`Accuracy`) remains stagnant for a model trained to generate.
  - [section 3.3] Table 1 compares models; generation training yields high token counts, suggesting inefficient reasoning without strong self-correction loops.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Incentivizing LLMs to Self-Verify Their Answers" (103147) and "Trust, But Verify" (31663) address related verification challenges but do not provide direct evidence for this specific asymmetry; corpus support is weak for this exact causal claim.
- **Break condition:** If future work demonstrates a training method where generation improvements consistently and significantly correlate with verification gains on held-out tasks, this mechanism's domain of validity would be constrained.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Training a model *solely* to self-verify (via RLVR on a verification objective `r_v`) significantly improves its subsequent generation performance, achieving comparable accuracy to generation-only training but with more efficient reasoning traces.
- **Mechanism:** Explicit verification training forces the model to learn fine-grained patterns of correctness and error in reasoning chains. These discriminative features, once learned, are reused and beneficially interfere with the generation process. A model that has internalized "what makes a solution correct or incorrect" can generate more directly, avoid redundant steps, and trigger meaningful checks, leading to higher token efficiency. This is a form of positive transfer where learning a related, discriminative task improves a generative one.
- **Core assumption:** The internal representations (e.g., in attention heads, MLPs) developed for reliable solution judgment are reusable and beneficial for constructing high-quality solutions from scratch.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "learning to self-verify can effectively improve generation performance... while yielding more efficient and effective reasoning traces."
  - [section 3.3] Table 1 shows "Self-Verify" models achieving similar or better `Acc` with dramatically fewer `Tokens` across all model sizes (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B: 38.9% vs 38.4% Acc with ~25% tokens).
  - [section 3.4] Figure 4 shows self-verification-trained models are significantly better at detecting and correcting errors in corrupted reasoning prefixes ("partially corrupted reasoning prefix setting").
  - [section 3.3] "training the model solely for self-verification yields performance that is comparable to, and in some cases better than, that achieved by generation-only training."
- **Break condition:** This transfer effect may diminish or reverse on tasks where verification and generation require fundamentally disjoint knowledge (e.g., highly creative domains vs. formal verification), or if verification training induces an overly cautious, low-variance policy detrimental to exploration during generation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Integrating self-verification as a decoupled but complementary objective within a multi-task RL framework (e.g., stage-wise initialization or alternating training) yields consistent performance gains over generation-only training across multiple benchmarks.
- **Mechanism:** Decoupling allows each objective (`r_g` and `r_v`) to shape the policy without one dominating the gradient signal, as hypothesized for "Mixed-Train". Stage-wise initialization ("Verify-Init") provides a strong, verification-capable prior that guides subsequent generation exploration more efficiently than a random or generation-only prior. Alternating training ("Verify-Alter") periodically reorients the policy with a verification objective, preventing it from drifting into generation strategies that neglect internal consistency checks. This maintains a balance between solution construction and solution scrutiny throughout training.
- **Core assumption:** The benefits of verification are maximized when its training signal is given dedicated, uninterrupted optimization phases, rather than being diluted within a single-step joint objective.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "formulating a multi-task reinforcement learning framework, where generation and self-verification are optimized as two independent but complementary objectives."
  - [section 4.1] Describes "Verify-Init" (stage-wise) and "Verify-Alter" (alternating) strategies that explicitly decouple the optimization.
  - [section 4.3] Table 4 shows both strategies consistently outperform "Generate" and "Mixed-Train" baselines in average accuracy (e.g., Qwen2.5-1.5B: 22.7% vs 20.2% for Generate, 21.1% for Mixed-Train).
  - [section 4.3] "Compared to Mixed-Train... our framework decouples the optimization of generation and verification... demonstrating additional performance gains."
- **Break condition:** The optimal decoupling schedule (e.g., phase length, initialization steps) is likely highly task- and model-dependent. Gains may vanish or become negative if the schedule is poorly matched to the model's learning stage or if the verification objective becomes too strong, stifling generative exploration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**
  - **Why needed here:** It is the fundamental training paradigm. All experiments use GRPO (a variant of PPO) with a rule-based verifier providing scalar rewards (`r_g` for generation correctness, `r_v` for judgment correctness). Understanding RLVR is essential to grasp how the two objectives are technically instantiated and optimized.
  - **Quick check question:** What is the key difference in the input/target structure between the generation training instantiation and the verification training instantiation of the same RLVR framework?
- **Concept: Capability Asymmetry & Transfer Learning**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's entire thesis rests on observing an asymmetric relationship between two capabilities (generation/verification) and then investigating the reverse direction for beneficial transfer. This conceptual lens explains why standard training fails to improve verification and why dedicated verification training helps generation.
  - **Quick check question:** If capability A does not improve when training for B, under what conditions could training for B *improve* capability A? What does this suggest about the relationship between A and B?
- **Concept: Multi-Task Scheduling in RL**
  - **Why needed here:** The proposed solution (Section 4) is not a new algorithm but a novel *scheduling* strategy for existing RLVR. Understanding the difference between joint optimization ("Mixed-Train"), sequential initialization ("Verify-Init"), and interleaved phases ("Verify-Alter") is critical for implementing the paper's guidance.
  - **Quick check question:** For a fixed total compute budget, what are the hypothesized trade-offs between a long initial verification phase (Verify-Init) and frequent, short alternating phases (Verify-Alter)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Policy Network (πθ) -> Rule-Based Verifier -> On-Policy Buffer -> GRPO Optimizer -> Policy Network (πθ)
- **Critical path:**
  1. Sample a batch of queries.
  2. For each query, have current πθ generate G candidate solutions.
  3. For generation training: compute r_g for each candidate, form batch, apply GRPO update with r_g.
  4. For verification training: have πθ judge each candidate, compute r_v, filter and balance samples, apply GRPO update with r_v.
  5. For alternating training: run n generation steps then n verification steps (using answers from those generation steps).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Decoupled vs. Mixed Objectives: Decoupling prevents verification gradient from being overwhelmed but incurs overhead; Mixed-Train is simpler but may dilute signal.
  - Stage-wise vs. Alternating: Stage-wise gives strong verification prior but risks overfitting; Alternating provides continuous co-adaptation but needs cycle length tuning.
  - Token Budget: Self-verification encourages shorter traces, reducing inference cost, but verification steps process longer contexts increasing training compute.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Performance Collapse on Verification: After a generation phase in Verify-Alter, verification accuracy should not drop catastrophically.
  - Token Length Divergence: If integrated training leads to long and low-accuracy traces, suggests superficial "verification" text.
  - Benchmark Inconsistency: Gains should be consistent across benchmarks; large variance suggests overfitting or reward formulation flaws.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate Asymmetry Baseline: Train generation-only model (RLVR on r_g) for 1000 steps, log r_g and verification accuracy every 100 steps. Expect r_g rises, verification accuracy flat.
  2. Pure Verification Transfer: Train verification-only model (RLVR on r_v) for 1000 steps, evaluate generation accuracy (Acc@16) and token length. Expect parity with generation baseline but 25-75% fewer tokens.
  3. Integration Strategy Test: Implement Verify-Alter (alternating 200-step phases) and compare against Generate and Mixed-Train baselines on all six benchmarks. Expect Verify-Alter highest average accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does the observed asymmetry between generation and self-verification, and the benefit of self-verification training for generation, persist for significantly larger language models (e.g., >10B parameters)?
- Does learning to self-verify improve generation in domains beyond mathematical reasoning, such as planning or multimodal tasks?
- Can we design more effective multi-task training schedules for integrating self-verification and generation objectives, beyond manual stage-wise or alternating approaches?
- How do alternative verification formulations (e.g., step-level verification, error type classification, or partial correctness scoring) affect the benefits of self-verification training for generation?

## Limitations
- Exact self-verification prompt templates and output formatting are not reported.
- Data balancing and diversity control procedures are insufficiently detailed.
- Missing training hyperparameters (e.g., Verify-Init step count) hinder full reproduction.
