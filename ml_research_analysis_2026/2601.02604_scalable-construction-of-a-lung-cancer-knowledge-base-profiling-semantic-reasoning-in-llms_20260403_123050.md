# Scalable Construction of a Lung Cancer Knowledge Base: Profiling Semantic Reasoning in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.02604  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02604  
- **Reference count:** 24  
- **Primary result:** Fine-tuning T5 on filtered lung cancer relation triplets yields ~657–739% ROUGE‑1 F1 and ~19–21% BERTScore F1 improvements over zero‑shot inference, with NER filtering proving critical.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the need for scalable, high‑quality biomedical knowledge bases to improve LLM reasoning in oncology. It constructs a lung cancer knowledge base by extracting (subject, relation, object) triplets from CC0‑licensed PubMed articles using OpenIE, then filtering with a biomedical NER model (confidence > 80%). Fine‑tuning T5 models on the resulting 13k triplets leads to substantial gains in ROUGE‑1 F1 (657–739%) and BERTScore F1 (19–21%) compared to zero‑shot inference. Notably, NER filtering is essential—unfiltered data performs markedly worse—and fine‑tuned T5‑Base and T5‑PubMed converge to nearly identical performance, suggesting that targeted semantic fine‑tuning can equalize models regardless of prior domain pretraining.

The study, however, has notable limitations: zero‑shot baseline scores are unreported, making relative gains hard to interpret; evaluation relies on ROUGE/BERTScore, which measure lexical overlap rather than clinical semantic accuracy; the corpus is restricted to CC0‑licensed articles, limiting coverage and contradicting the “scalable” claim; and potential dataset leakage is not addressed.

## Method Summary
The paper does not provide explicit reproduction notes. From the abstract and key outcome, the method can be summarized as:  
1. Collect CC0‑licensed PubMed articles on lung cancer.  
2. Extract (subject, relation, object) triplets using OpenIE.  
3. Filter triplets with a biomedical NER model, retaining only those where the NER confidence exceeds 80%.  
4. Fine‑tune T5 models (Base and PubMed versions) on the filtered set (~13k triplets).  
5. Evaluate using ROUGE‑1 F1 and BERTScore F1 against a reference.  
The authors emphasize that NER filtering is critical; without it, downstream performance degrades significantly.

## Key Results
- ROUGE‑1 F1 and BERTScore F1 improve by ~657–739% and ~19–21%, respectively, over zero‑shot inference.  
- NER filtering (probability > 80%) is critical; unfiltered data yields markedly worse results.  
- Fine‑tuned T5‑Base and T5‑PubMed converge to nearly identical performance, indicating that targeted fine‑tuning can compensate for lack of domain‑specific pretraining.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Each stage addresses a specific challenge: OpenIE enables scalable relation extraction without a predefined schema; biomedical NER ensures retained triples are medically relevant, reducing noise; fine‑ tuning adapts the T5 model to the distribution of extracted biomedical relations, transferring structured knowledge. The convergence of T5‑Base and T5‑PubMed suggests that the fine‑tuning dataset effectively encodes domain knowledge, diminishing the advantage of domain‑specific pretraining.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** PubMed articles → OpenIE → raw triplets → NER filter → filtered triplets → T5 fine‑tuning → fine‑tuned T5.  
- **Critical path:** Successful OpenIE extraction → effective NER filtering → productive fine‑tuning; failure at any stage propagates downstream.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  1. CC0‑only corpus limits size but avoids licensing issues.  
  2. NER confidence threshold (80%) balances precision vs. recall.  
  3. Choice between T5‑Base and T5‑PubMed trades domain adaptation for computational cost.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Unfiltered data leads to poor performance (as reported).  
  - Overly strict NER thresholds may cause recall drop.  
  - Insufficient fine‑tuning data results in underfitting (e.g., non‑convergent loss).  
- **First experiments:**  
  1. Validate OpenIE extraction quality on a random sample of lung‑cancer abstracts.  
  2. Evaluate the effect of varying NER confidence thresholds (70%, 80%, 90%) on filtered set size and downstream metrics.
