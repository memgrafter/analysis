# Pixel-Wise Multimodal Contrastive Learning for Remote Sensing Images

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.04127
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04127
- **Reference count**: 9
- **Primary result**: PIMC fine‑tuned encoders achieve 71.07% pixel classification and lowest forecasting errors on PASTIS, and 97.44% land‑cover accuracy on EuroSAT, surpassing state‑of‑the‑art across all three downstream tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Pixel‑wise Multimodal Contrastive (PIMC) learning to address inefficient processing of satellite image time series (SITS). PIMC converts pixel‑based vegetation index time series (NDVI, EVI, SAVI) into 2D recurrence plots and aligns them with remote sensing imagery via contrastive learning, training two independent ResNet‑18 encoders. On the PASTIS dataset, the fine‑tuned time‑series encoder yields 71.07% accuracy for pixel classification and the lowest forecasting errors (e.g., NDVI RMSE 0.4849). For land‑cover classification on EuroSAT, the fine‑tuned image encoder reaches 97.44% accuracy, outperforming prior methods. The approach enables flexible unimodal deployment at inference while leveraging multimodal self‑supervision during training.

## Method Summary
PIMC follows a two‑stage paradigm. In pre‑training, two ResNet‑18 encoders process the two modalities: an image encoder receives a remote sensing patch (e.g., PASTIS 128×128 multispectral or EuroSAT RGB), while a time‑series encoder receives a stack of recurrence plots derived from NDVI/EVI/SAVI time series of a sampled pixel (3×T×T, where T is the number of timestamps). Pixels are sampled via a Hilbert curve to ensure spatial coverage. A symmetric cross‑entropy contrastive loss (InfoNCE) aligns embeddings of matching patch‑recurrence pairs and pushes mismatched pairs apart. The encoders are trained jointly with Adam (lr=1e‑3, weight decay=1e‑4) for 400 epochs on PASTIS folds 1‑3. After pre‑training, each encoder is fine‑tuned separately on downstream tasks: the time‑series encoder is adapted for pixel‑wise classification and vegetation index forecasting; the image encoder is fine‑tuned for land‑cover classification on EuroSAT. A simple linear head or MLP is added on top of the encoder features for these tasks.

## Key Results
- PASTIS pixel classification (18 classes): fine‑tuned time‑series encoder accuracy 71.07%, F1 0.5.
- PASTIS forecasting: lowest errors among baselines, NDVI RMSE 0.4849.
- EuroSAT land‑cover classification (10 classes): fine‑tuned image encoder accuracy 97.44%, balanced accuracy 97.44%, surpassing SeCo (93.33%) and DINO MC (96.10%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Converting pixel‑based vegetation index time series into 2D recurrence plots yields richer temporal patterns that can be processed more effectively by CNNs compared to 1D raw series.
- **Mechanism**: A recurrence plot transforms a 1D series of length T into a T×T matrix where each entry RPi,j = ‖xi − xj‖ encodes the distance between two time points. This explicitly captures the recurrence of states and temporal dependencies in a 2D grid, making it compatible with standard CNN encoders. The RP preserves both local and global temporal structures without heavy parameterization.
- **Core assumption**: The distance‑based RP retains sufficient information about the dynamics such that a CNN can learn discriminative features for downstream tasks.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “we generate recurrence plots from pixel‑based vegetation index time series (NDVI, EVI, and SAVI) as an alternative to using raw pixel values, creating more informative representations.”
  - [section 3.2.4]: Equation 1 defines RPi,j = ‖xi − xj‖ and describes the computation.
  - [section 4.2.2]: Table 2 shows that 2D representations achieve lower forecasting errors (e.g., NDVI RMSE 0.4849) compared to 1D baselines (e.g., XGB 0.6789).
  - [corpus]: da Cruz et al. (2024), Aithal et al. (2024), Goel et al. (2024), and Ye et al. (2023) apply recurrence plots successfully in statistical, biomedical, and sports domains, supporting their generic representational power.
- **Break condition**: If the time series is very short (≤25 timestamps, per Table 5) or the temporal patterns are highly similar across classes (e.g., Meadow vs Leguminous fodder in Figure 7), the RP matrices become nearly indistinguishable and fail to provide discriminative cues.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Multimodal contrastive learning aligns the latent spaces of the image and time‑series encoders such that embeddings of corresponding RSI patches and recurrence plots are close, while mismatched pairs are far apart, thereby improving feature quality for both modalities.
- **Mechanism**: Following CLIP, PIMC uses a symmetric cross‑entropy loss on the similarity matrix S = I_norm · T_norm^⊤. For a batch of N samples, the loss encourages the model to select the matching pair (i=j) as the highest similarity among all N·N pairs. Positive pairs consist of an RSI patch and the recurrence plot derived from the same geographic location; negatives are all other cross‑modality pairs in the batch. Gradients update both encoders to maximize agreement.
- **Core assumption**: Maximizing agreement across modalities yields representations that capture semantically useful information, even though the raw data have different statistical distributions.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “introduce PIxel‑wise Multimodal Contrastive (PIMC), a new multimodal self‑supervision approach that produces effective encoders based on two‑dimensional pixel time series representations and remote sensing imagery.”
  - [section 3.3]: Details of loss calculation (Equation 2) and the dot‑product similarity process.
  - [section 4.2.3]: The PIMC fine‑tuned image encoder achieves 97.44% accuracy on EuroSAT, outperforming SeCo (93.33%) and DINO MC (96.10%), demonstrating the benefit of contrastive alignment.
  - [corpus]: FOCAL (244796) and FLAVARS (11465) also show that multimodal contrastive frameworks enhance representation quality in sensing tasks, reinforcing the general principle.
- **Break condition**: If positive pairs are misaligned (e.g., due to data curation errors or temporal shifts between modalities), the contrastive signal becomes noisy and may fail to converge. Additionally, using very small batch sizes reduces the number of negative samples, potentially weakening the objective and causing overfitting.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Training with PIMC yields two independent encoders that can be deployed separately on unimodal data, providing flexibility and computational efficiency at inference.
- **Mechanism**: During training, both modalities are presented jointly to compute the contrastive loss. After training, each encoder (image and time‑series) is standalone and can process any input of its respective modality without requiring the other. This allows practitioners to use only the needed encoder for a given downstream task.
- **Core assumption**: Knowledge transferred from multimodal alignment improves the quality of each modality’s representation even when used in isolation.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 2.4]: “Our method employs multimodality exclusively during training, yielding two independent encoders for each modality.” Contrasted with UNet‑BERT that requires all modalities at inference.
  - [abstract]: Reports results separately for the “fine‑tuned PIMC time series encoder” (pixel classification and forecasting) and the “fine‑tuned PIMC image encoder” (land cover classification), confirming independent use.
  - [section 4.2]: All downstream experiments use either the image encoder (EuroSAT) or the time‑series encoder (PASTIS) independently.
  - [corpus]: Not directly addressed; but independent inference is a design choice that differentiates PIMC from other multimodal methods (e.g., Qu and Wang 2025, Sánchez‑Fernández et al. 2024) that require joint inputs at test time.
- **Break condition**: If a downstream task intrinsically requires joint reasoning over both modalities (e.g., multimodal change detection needing simultaneous optical and SAR data), independent encoders may fail to capture necessary cross‑modal interactions, leading to suboptimal performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Preprocessor -> {Image Encoder, Time Series Encoder} -> L2 Normalization -> Similarity Matrix -> Contrastive Loss -> Encoders (update). Downstream: fine‑tuned encoder -> linear head / MLP.

**Critical path:**
1. Extract an RSI patch and its corresponding pixel time series from the dataset.
2. Compute vegetation indices for all pixels in the patch.
3. For PIMC training, sample a pixel location using the Hilbert curve; extract its time series across the three indices.
4. For each index, compute recurrence plot; concatenate to shape (3, T, T).
5. Feed the RSI patch to image encoder → I; feed recurrence plot stack to time series encoder → T.
6. Normalize I and T; compute similarity matrix; compute L_image and L_time_series.
7. Back‑propagate total loss L = (L_image + L_time_series)/2 to update both encoders.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Encoder capacity*: ResNet‑18 is lightweight but may limit expressivity; larger backbones (ResNet‑50, ViT) could improve metrics at higher compute.
- *Time series length T*: Longer T yields richer RPs (Table 5) but increases memory quadratically (T²). Typical T≈32–50 balances performance and memory.
- *Number of sampled pixels*: More pixels improve downstream performance (Table 4) but expand dataset size and training time; 200 is a reasonable default for downstream tasks but training can use fewer (e.g., 25).
- *Vegetation indices*: Using NDVI/EVI/SAVI aligns with crop phenology; adding indices could help but also increases input channels; the 3‑index choice keeps input compatible with RGB pre‑trained models.

**Failure signatures:**
- Contrastive loss stagnates or the similarity matrix becomes uniformly low/high → check batch construction, learning rate, or normalization.
- UMAP of features from frozen PIMC encoder shows no cluster separation (like Figure 9c) → training insufficient or contrastive alignment failed.
- Downstream accuracy far below reported numbers (e.g., <60% on EuroSAT) → verify data preprocessing (recurrence plot generation, input normalization) and hyperparameters.
- Out‑of‑memory errors → reduce batch size, shorten time series, or reduce number of sampled pixels.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Pre‑training convergence**: Train PIMC on PASTIS (folders 1‑3) for 400 epochs with Adam (lr=1e‑3, weight decay=1e‑4). Monitor the average contrastive loss and the positive/negative similarity ratio. A successful run shows decreasing loss and positive similarity > negative similarity.
2. **Time‑series pixel classification**: Using the pre‑trained time series encoder, fine‑tune it on the PASTIS pixel classification task (18 classes) for 100 epochs with Adam. Expected accuracy ~71% and F1 ~0.5 (Table 1). Also evaluate a frozen version to observe the benefit of fine‑tuning.
3. **Image land cover classification**: Fine‑tune the image encoder on EuroSAT (27k images, 10 classes) for 100 epochs with Adam. Target accuracy >97% and balanced accuracy >97% (Table 3). Verify that the encoder generalizes to non‑vegetation classes (highways, lakes), indicating broad semantic capture.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can PIMC be adapted to handle class‑imbalanced remote sensing datasets more effectively?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper acknowledges that the PASTIS dataset is highly imbalanced, which complicates accurate class distinction, and shows confusion matrices where majority classes dominate predictions.
- **Why unresolved**: The standard contrastive loss used in PIMC does not account for class frequencies, leading to biased representations that favor frequent classes.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Train PIMC with class‑balanced sampling or loss reweighting, then evaluate balanced accuracy and per‑class F1‑score improvements on imbalanced splits of PASTIS.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Which 2D time series encoding (recurrence plot, GASF, GADF, MTF) yields the best multimodal alignment with remote sensing imagery in the PIMC framework?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors mention GASF, GADF, and MTF as alternatives to recurrence plots in the future work section, but no comparative analysis is provided.
- **Why unresolved**: The choice of 2D representation may significantly affect the quality of the learned feature space, yet only recurrence plots are evaluated.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Pre‑train separate PIMC models using each encoding on the same data, then compare downstream task performance (e.g., pixel classification, forecasting) to identify the most effective representation.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can incorporating additional remote sensing modalities (e.g., SAR, hyperspectral) or vegetation indices improve PIMC’s ability to discriminate between challenging land cover classes?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper notes that some classes are hard to distinguish with only NDVI/EVI/SAVI and suggests investigating alternative indices or modalities.
- **Why unresolved**: PIMC currently uses only three vegetation indices and optical imagery; it is unknown whether richer spectral or radar data resolves confusion between similar classes.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extend PIMC to fuse multiple modalities (e.g., add SAR bands or more indices), then measure accuracy on confusing class pairs from EuroSAT or PASTIS, comparing against the original three‑index model.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Do transformer‑based encoder architectures (e.g., ViT) outperform ResNet‑18 in PIMC, particularly for capturing long‑range temporal dependencies in SITS?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors propose employing transformer networks as a future direction, implying ResNet‑18 might be suboptimal for modeling complex spatio‑temporal patterns.
- **Why unresolved**: ResNet‑18 is a compact CNN with limited global context capacity; transformers could better integrate information across the 2D representation and sequence length.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replace ResNet‑18 encoders with a vision transformer in the PIMC framework, pre‑train on PASTIS, and compare fine‑tuned performance on forecasting and classification tasks, focusing on long‑sequence prediction accuracy.

## Limitations
- Recurrence plot generation details (thresholding, normalization, resizing to ResNet‑18 input) are not fully described.
- Hilbert curve sampling parameters (resolution, number of pixels) are ambiguous, affecting reproducibility.
- Data splits and evaluation protocols for PASTIS and EuroSAT are insufficiently documented, risking potential data leakage.
