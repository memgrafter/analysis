# Dirichlet Scale Mixture Priors for Bayesian Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19859
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19859
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: DSM priors assign a fixed variance budget to each hidden node distributed via a symmetric Dirichlet, achieving comparable predictive performance to state-of-the-art shrinkage priors while halving effective parameters and enabling extensive pruning without accuracy loss; they also show increased robustness to adversarial attacks.

## Executive Summary
Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) typically use Gaussian priors, which can lead to issues like the cold posterior effect. This paper introduces Dirichlet Scale Mixture (DSM) priors, a novel hierarchical prior that enforces structured shrinkage by allocating a fixed variance budget to each hidden node and distributing it across incoming weights via a symmetric Dirichlet distribution. This induces negative covariance among weight-specific variance components, promoting competition and sparsity at both weight and node levels. Heavy-tailed group scales (e.g., half-Student’s t) further enhance node sparsity, while a soft regularization stabilizes inference. Experiments on simulated and real datasets show that DSM priors match the predictive accuracy of leading shrinkage priors but with roughly half the effective number of parameters. They tolerate aggressive pruning (often >90%) and exhibit improved robustness to adversarial attacks, particularly in small-sample, high-correlation settings.

## Method Summary
DSM priors define a three-level hierarchy for the weights of a Bayesian neural network. At the top, a global scale τ follows a half-Cauchy prior. For each hidden node j, a group scale λ_j is drawn from a heavy-tailed distribution (e.g., half-Student’s t with ν degrees of freedom). Within the node, a Dirichlet vector ξ_j = (ξ_j1, …, ξ_jK) with concentration parameter α (symmetric) is sampled, satisfying ∑_k ξ_jk = 1. The weight w_jk connecting to input k is then distributed as N(0, τ² λ_j² ξ_jk). This structure allocates the node’s variance budget τ²λ_j² among its incoming weights via ξ_jk, coupling them through the simplex and creating negative covariance. To improve computational stability, a regularized local scale ˜λ_j² = c² λ_j² / (c² + τ² λ_j²) is used, where c² ∼ InvGamma(2,4), bounding marginal variances. Inference is performed with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC/NUTS) using a non-centered parameterization: w_jk = τ ˜λ_j √ξ_jk * z_jk, with z_jk ∼ N(0,1). The authors compare several DSM variants (e.g., Dirichlet horseshoe, Dirichlet Student’s t) against isotropic Gaussian and regularized horseshoe baselines on regression and classification tasks.

## Key Results
- DSM priors achieve predictive performance (RMSE, CRPS) on par with state-of-the-art shrinkage priors while reducing the effective number of parameters by about half, indicating greater parsimony.
- They enable aggressive weight pruning (up to 90% sparsity) with minimal accuracy degradation, especially the Dirichlet horseshoe variant, demonstrating robustness to compression.
- On a breast cancer classification task, the Dirichlet Student’s t prior yields the highest fraction of test points robust to FGSM adversarial attacks, suggesting increased adversarial robustness; these advantages are most pronounced in small-sample, high-correlation scenarios.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Structured Variance Allocation via Dirichlet**: For each hidden node, the total variance budget τ²λ_j² is split among its incoming weights by sampling shares ξ_jk from a symmetric Dirichlet(α). The simplex constraint ∑_k ξ_jk = 1 couples these shares, yielding negative covariance Cov(ξ_jk, ξ_jl) < 0 for small α. Consequently, if one weight receives a large share, the others are forced to be smaller—mimicking feature selection within the node. This mechanism induces competition and promotes sparsity at the weight level. When α → ∞, the Dirichlet approaches a uniform distribution, the negative covariance vanishes, and the mechanism reduces to independent global-local shrinkage.

**Heavy-Tailed Group Scales for Node-Level Sparsity**: The group scales λ_j are given heavy-tailed priors (e.g., half-Student’s t with ν degrees of freedom). Such priors concentrate mass near zero but have heavy tails: most λ_j are very small, causing the entire node’s variance budget τ²λ_j² to be tiny; a few λ_j are large, allowing those nodes to retain substantial variance. This leads to sparsity at the level of hidden units. The heavy-tailed nature aligns with the empirically observed heavy-tailed distributions of SGD-trained networks and helps mitigate the cold posterior effect.

**Regularization-Induced Gaussian Envelope**: To improve inference stability without sacrificing shrinkage, the model applies a soft regularization to the local scale: ˜λ_j² = c² λ_j² / (c² + τ² λ_j²) with c² ∼ InvGamma(2,4). This transformation bounds the marginal variance by c²/τ², ensuring all moments exist even if λ_j is heavy-tailed. It lowers the coefficient of variation of ˜λ_j², which can move the system into the negative covariance regime for the Dirichlet shares, further encouraging sparsity. The regularization balances heavy-tailed behavior with computational robustness.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: τ (global scale) → λ_j (group scale for node j) → ξ_jk (Dirichlet shares for weights into node j) → w_jk (weight). Optionally, c² (regularization) affects ˜λ_j² which then multiplies ξ_jk in the variance.

**Critical path**: 1. Sample τ ∼ half-Cauchy. 2. For each hidden node j: sample λ_j ∼ heavy-tailed (e.g., t_ν^+), then sample ξ_j ∼ Dirichlet(α). 3. For each weight w_jk: sample w_jk ∼ N(0, τ² λ_j² ξ_jk). In practice, use non-centered parameterization: w_jk = τ ˜λ_j √ξ_jk z_ijk, where z_ijk ∼ N(0,1) and ˜λ_j² = c² λ_j²/(c²+τ²λ_j²). Compute posterior and predict via sampling.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- α (Dirichlet concentration): Small α (<1) increases within-node competition and sparsity but may overshrink signal; large α reduces sparsity.
- ν (heavy-tail degrees of freedom for λ_j): Small ν (e.g., 1) yields heavier tails, promoting more aggressive node sparsity; larger ν approaches Gaussian, reducing sparsity.
- c² (regularization scale): Smaller c² imposes a tighter bound (stronger Gaussian envelope), aiding inference stability but potentially overshrinking; larger c² preserves heavy-tailed behavior but may cause divergence.
- Computational cost: HMC sampling with complex posteriors can be slow; careful tuning of adapt_delta and treedepth needed; non-centered parameterization helps but mixing may still be poor.

**Failure signatures**:
- HMC divergences, especially when heavy-tailed priors are used without sufficient regularization.
- Poor mixing in weight space: high R̂ (>1.01), low effective sample size (ESS).
- Over-shrinkage: predictive performance collapses (high RMSE/CRPS) compared to simpler priors.
- Under-shrinkage: effective parameter count remains high, pruning leads to large accuracy drops, or adversarial robustness is not improved.
- Numerical overflow in computation of ˜λ_j²; use log-space or stable formulations.

**First three experiments**:
1. **Linear regression with correlated predictors**: Demonstrated that DSM priors shrink zero coefficients more aggressively than the horseshoe prior while preserving non-zero estimates, especially under high predictor correlation (ρ=0.9).
2. **Friedman simulation**: Compared DSM to Gaussian and horseshoe on a sparse nonlinear regression task with independent and correlated inputs. DSM reduced effective parameters (trace of shrinkage matrix) and maintained or improved predictive performance, particularly for small sample sizes (N=100) and correlated features.
3. **Abalone UCI regression**: Showed DSM priors achieve comparable test accuracy to baselines but with far lower effective parameters and superior robustness to aggressive pruning (up to 90% sparsity), especially the Dirichlet horseshoe variant. SHAP analysis revealed more interpretable weight patterns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability to deep, overparameterized networks**: The experiments use single‑hidden‑layer BNNs with ≤16 hidden units. It is unknown how DSM priors behave in modern deep architectures (e.g., ResNets, Transformers) and whether scalable inference methods (variational inference, SWAG) can handle the complex posterior.
2. **Richer dependence structures within Dirichlet allocation**: The current DSM uses a symmetric Dirichlet with a single α. More flexible schemes (non‑symmetric α, hierarchical priors on the Dirichlet, structured shrinkage across layers) could improve the sparsity‑accuracy trade‑off.
3. **Function‑space prior in the infinite‑width limit**: The limiting stochastic process (e.g., Gaussian process vs. Student’s t‑process) induced by a DSM‑prior BNN as width → ∞ is not derived. Analytic or empirical characterization would clarify the functional implications.
4. **HMC convergence diagnostics**: The authors report poor weight‑space diagnostics (elevated R̂, low ESS) for DSM models, but the root causes (heavy tails, Dirichlet constraints, multimodality) and remedies (reparameterization, alternative MCMC) remain open.

## Limitations
- Experimental evaluation limited to shallow (one hidden layer) BNNs with at most 16 hidden units; scalability to deep networks is untested.
- Adversarial robustness assessed on a single dataset (breast cancer) using only FGSM attacks; broader robustness evaluation is needed.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (α, ν, c²) not systematically explored; theoretical analysis confined to single-node covariance; HMC mixing times and computational overhead not quantified.

## Confidence
- Efficiency & sparsity (halving effective parameters, pruning tolerance): *Medium* confidence (empirical on small nets; scalability unverified).
- Adversarial robustness (higher safe fraction): *Low* confidence (limited evaluation, no mechanistic explanation).
- Mechanistic explanation (Dirichlet negative covariance, heavy‑tailed group scales): *Medium* confidence (theoretically plausible for single node; network‑level effects inferred indirectly).
