# Wedge Sampling: Efficient Tensor Completion with Nearly-Linear Sample Complexity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05869
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05869
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Wedge sampling reduces tensor completion sample complexity from Õ(n^{k/2}) to near‑linear Õ(n) by directly sampling length‑two correlations (wedges) and combining with uniform entries for weak/exact recovery under incoherence.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces wedge sampling, a novel non‑uniform sampling scheme for tensor completion that overcomes the Õ(n^{k/2}) sample complexity barrier of uniform entry sampling. By directly sampling length‑two paths (wedges) in the bipartite unfolding graph, wedge sampling ensures sufficient connectivity to enable a spectral initialization with only Õ(n) wedge observations. Combined with a small number of uniform entries, the method achieves weak recovery via a spectral method and exact recovery via gradient descent, both with near‑linear sample complexity in the dimension n, under standard incoherence assumptions. This result challenges the conjectured statistical‑to‑computational gap in tensor completion.

## Method Summary
Wedge sampling operates on the mode‑1 unfolding of the tensor, treating it as a matrix A ∈ ℝ^{n×n^{k-1}}. The algorithm samples wedges (i,ℓ,j) uniformly at random from all possible such triplets, observing the entries A_{iℓ} and A_{jℓ}. From these wedges, it constructs a weighted matrix Z that approximates AA^⊤, the second moment matrix. Top eigenvectors of Z yield an estimate Û of the left singular space. For spectral completion, the projection Q = ÛÛ^⊤ is applied to a small uniform sample matrix Y to denoise and recover the tensor. For exact recovery, a factor extraction step (based on prior work) uses Û to initialize CP factors, followed by gradient descent on the least‑squares objective over uniform entries. The sample complexity of wedge sampling is Õ(n) wedges plus Õ(n) uniform entries, significantly improving over Õ(n^{k/2}) required by uniform‑only approaches.

## Key Results
- A spectral method using O(n log n) wedge samples plus O(log n) uniform entries achieves weak recovery.
- Gradient descent with Õ(n) wedge samples plus Õ(n) uniform entries achieves exact recovery.
- Both results hold for incoherent low‑rank tensors, reducing sample complexity from polynomial to near‑linear and overcoming the conjectured statistical‑to‑computational barrier.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Wedge sampling overcomes connectivity barrier**  
Under uniform entry sampling, the probability that a fixed pair of row indices (i,j) is connected by at least one observed wedge is on the order of n²p², which becomes vanishingly small when p ≪ n^{-3/2} (for k=3). This sparsity disconnects the bipartite wedge graph, preventing reliable spectral initialization. Wedge sampling directly samples wedges uniformly, guaranteeing each wedge appears with probability p and thus ensuring sufficient graph connectivity with only ∼n total wedge observations. This hinges on the tensor being incoherent so that rows are not overly concentrated. Evidence: [abstract] and [section 1.1] describe the direct promotion of length‑two connections; [corpus] shows wedge sampling is novel and addresses the connectivity mismatch.

**Mechanism 2: Wedge matrix concentration**  
The wedge‑sampled matrix Z = ∑_{wedge} p^{-1} A_{iℓ} A_{jℓ} E_{ij} (with symmetrization) is an unbiased estimator of AA^⊤. Due to the (µ₁, µ₂)-incoherence of A, each entry of A is bounded by O(µ √(log n/(p n^{k-1}))), controlling variance. Matrix Bernstein inequality yields ∥Z - AA^⊤∥ ≤ poly(log n) / (p n^{k/2}) ∥A∥² with high probability, using Õ(n) wedge samples. This concentration is a new result for this dependency structure. Evidence: Theorem 5 and Lemma 11 in [section 3] and [appendix B.1].

**Mechanism 3: Improved initialization enables near‑linear refinement**  
The spectral initialization from wedge samples yields a subspace estimate with small ℓ₂ and ℓ₂,∞ errors (Theorem 6). This bounded ℓ₂,∞ error propagates through the denoising step (Algorithm 2) and the gradient descent local convexity analysis (Section 5). Consequently, the refinement stage (spectral or GD) requires only Õ(n) uniform entries, in contrast to uniform‑only pipelines that need Õ(n^{k/2}). For gradient descent, this is formalized in Theorem 8: with p ≳ µ⁷ r⁴ log² n / n³ and q ≳ µ⁶ r⁴ log⁵ n / n², exact recovery is achieved. The initialization quality must satisfy ∥ÛR - U∥_{2,∞} ≲ O(µ r / n); if this fails, the local basin argument breaks. Evidence: Theorems 6, 7, 8; comparison to prior uniform‑only lower bounds from Cai et al. 2022.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Tensor Unfolding (Matricization)**  
*Why needed:* The algorithm operates on unfold_1(T) ∈ ℝ^{n×n^{k-1}} to reduce tensor completion to a matrix problem; wedge sampling is defined on this matrix’s columns (mode‑2 indices).  
*Quick check:* If T is order‑3 with dimensions (n, n, n), what are the dimensions of unfold_2(T), and what does its rank say about T’s CP rank?

**Concept: Incoherence (Matrix/Tensor)**  
*Why needed:* Incoherence (µ₁, µ₂) bounds singular vectors’ row‑wise norms, ensuring no single row/column dominates; this is critical for matrix Bernstein and ℓ₂,∞ perturbation bounds used in wedge sampling concentration.  
*Quick check:* Given a CP decomposition with factors x_i^{(j)}, what condition on max_i ∥x_i^{(j)}∥_∞² / ∥x_i^{(j)}∥_2² ensures (µ, µ)-CP incoherence?

**Concept: Wedge Graph Connectivity**  
*Why needed:* The bipartite wedge graph’s connectivity determines whether the sampled matrix Z reveals the left singular subspace; if wedges are too sparse, the graph is disconnected and spectral methods fail.  
*Quick check:* In the bipartite graph (rows vs. columns) for k=3, when does a path of length 2 (i → ℓ → j) exist in uniform sampling? How does wedge sampling guarantee more such paths?

**Concept: Leave‑one‑out Analysis**  
*Why needed:* To obtain ℓ₂,∞ bounds (Theorem 6), the proof uses a leave‑one‑out argument (deleting one row/column) to control how perturbations affect all rows simultaneously, avoiding a union bound over n.  
*Quick check:* In Lemma 20, why does subtracting one row ℓ in the wedge matrix Z^{(s)} help bound ∥(Z - Z^{(s)}) Û^{(s)}∥_{2,∞}?

**Concept: δ‑Incoherent Tensor Norm**  
*Why needed:* For very sparse uniform samples (q ≲ n^{-2}), the usual operator norm ∥·∥ fails to concentrate; the δ‑incoherent norm (allowing at most two delocalized vectors) enables Theorem 9’s gradient descent guarantee at q ≳ n^{-(k-1)}.  
*Quick check:* Write the definition of ∥T∥_δ for an order‑3 tensor, and explain why it is weaker than the usual spectral norm but stronger than the max‑norm.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Wedge sampler and uniform sampler are independent. Wedge samples produce a matrix Z; uniform samples produce a matrix Y. Z feeds into the Spectral init module (top‑r SVD) to produce Û and projection Q. Q and Y are used by either the Denoising module (Algorithm 2) for spectral completion or by the Gradient Descent module (Algorithm 3) after factor extraction. Tensor unfold/fold utilities support conversion between tensor and matrix representations throughout.

**Critical path**  
1. Wedge sampling (rate p) → construction of matrix Z.  
2. Eigen‑decomposition of Z → left singular subspace estimate Û.  
3. If spectral completion: compute Q = ÛÛ^⊤, denoise Y via Q, fold to obtain ̂T.  
   If gradient descent: extract initial CP factors from Û (using Cai et al. 2022 method) → run gradient descent on uniform samples to refine factors → reconstruct ̂T.  
4. Output final tensor estimate.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Wedge vs. uniform budget: Wedge sampling efficiently creates informative correlations at low total sample count but introduces dependencies; uniform sampling provides independent data but requires many more samples for a comparable initialization. The hybrid approach balances the two.  
- One‑sided vs. full unfolding: Using an unbalanced unfolding (mode‑1 vs. all others) simplifies spectral analysis but may underutilize symmetry in symmetric tensor tasks.  
- Assumptions vs. generality: Results rely on strong incoherence assumptions; coherent tensors may need different sampling designs or additional samples.

**Failure signatures**  
- Spectral init fails: ∥Z - AA^⊤∥ too large → eigenvalue gap small → Û misaligned with true U (check condition p ≥ c κ⁴ µ₁µ₂ r² log n / n^k).  
- ℓ₂,∞ blow‑up: Initialization error ∥ÛR - U∥_{2,∞} not O(µ r / n) → subsequent steps degrade; monitor via leave‑one‑out consistency.  
- GD divergence: Initialization error outside local basin (∥X⁰ - X∥_F > c µ^{-2} r ∥X∥_F) → gradient descent diverges; reduce p or increase trials L.  
- Extraction failure: Spectral gaps σ₁‑σ₂ in factor extraction trials are too small; selected factors correlated → increase number of trials L or uniform sample rate q.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Subspace recovery test: For n=500, r=5, vary wedge rate p; compute ∥ÛR - U∥_F and ∥ÛR - U∥_{2,∞}. Compare decay to theoretical prediction ∝ p^{-1/2}.  
2. Hybrid vs. uniform efficiency: Fix total sample count; compare wedge+uniform pipeline (Algos 1+3) against pure uniform entry sampling in final Frobenius error. Expect wedge to succeed at total Õ(n) while uniform fails until Ω(n^{3/2}).  
3. Incoherence sensitivity: Generate tensors with controlled µ (by scaling factor vectors); for each, find minimal p,q for exact recovery. Check empirical scaling p ∝ µ⁷, q ∝ µ⁶ as predicted by Theorem 8.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1: Can the logarithmic factor be removed to achieve truly linear O(n) samples?**  
- *Basis:* Theorem 5 includes a log n factor; authors suspect it may be an artifact of concentration inequalities but do not tighten it.  
- *Why unresolved:* No proof eliminating log n; lower bound unknown.  
- *What evidence would resolve:* A refined analysis showing O(n) suffices, or a lower bound proving Ω(n log n) necessary.

**Open Question 2: Do guarantees extend to noisy tensor completion?**  
- *Basis:* Paper assumes noiseless low‑rank tensor; references noisy uniform sampling but not for wedge.  
- *Why unresolved:* Concentration tools may be sensitive to noise; robustness untested.  
- *What evidence would resolve:* Proof that spectral init and GD tolerate i.i.d. sub‑Gaussian noise with only slight sample increase, and empirical validation.

**Open Question 3: Are there better non‑adaptive sampling designs?**  
- *Basis:* Authors suggest wedge sampling is one natural heuristic; a systematic study of optimal designs is lacking.  
- *Why unresolved:* Vast design space; no general framework linking initialization quality to sample complexity for arbitrary non‑adaptive schemes.  
- *What evidence would resolve:* Derive a general theory that quantifies initialization quality for any non‑adaptive design, identify optimal designs (e.g., via spectral graph expansion), and prove matching lower bounds.

## Limitations
- Theory assumes (µ₁, µ₂)-incoherent tensors; performance may degrade for coherent or spiky structures.
- Gradient descent analysis is only for order‑3 tensors; extension to higher orders remains open.
- Sample complexity bounds contain unspecified constants, making practical parameter selection difficult.
- Factor extraction relies on Cai et al. (2022); its empirical success rate and required trial count L are not derived here.

## Confidence
- **High:** Wedge‑sampled matrix concentration (Theorem 5) and spectral initialization ℓ₂/ℓ₂,∞ bounds (Theorem 6).
- **Medium:** Overall near‑linear sample complexity for exact recovery via gradient descent (Theorem 8) — depends on initialization quality and extraction, proven only for k=3.
- **Low:** General assertion of overcoming the statistical‑to‑computational barrier for arbitrary tensors; results hold under strong incoherence assumptions.
