# Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16066
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16066
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** RL2F enables a smaller model (Gemini 2.5 Flash) to nearly match a ten-times larger model (Gemini 2.5 Pro) on hard math; training on math yields ~7% average improvement on out-of-distribution domains (coding, puzzles) and enhances in-context plasticity for self-correction.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the inability of large language models to dynamically learn from natural language feedback during interactions. It proposes Reinforcement Learning with Language Feedback (RL2F), which transforms single-turn verifiable tasks into multi-turn didactic dialogues where a teacher provides corrective feedback based on privileged information (e.g., ground-truth solutions). The student model is fine-tuned via reinforcement learning to integrate this feedback across turns. Empirical results show that a substantially smaller model (Gemini 2.5 Flash) nearly matches a ten-times larger model (Gemini 2.5 Pro) on challenging math problems (HardMath2). Moreover, when trained solely on math, the model generalizes to out-of-distribution domains such as coding and puzzles, achieving an average improvement of approximately 7%. Analysis indicates that RL2F enhances in-context plasticity—the model’s ability to revise its predictions within a single context—and by internalizing the teacher’s feedback logic, it can perform self-correction without external input.

## Method Summary
RL2F converts single-turn verifiable problems into multi-turn dialogues. A teacher model, conditioned on privileged information such as the ground-truth solution, generates corrective feedback without revealing the answer. A student model interacts over multiple turns, refining its response after each feedback. These interactions produce trajectories of varying lengths. The student is fine-tuned using reinforcement learning with a sparse terminal reward (+1 for final correctness, 0 otherwise). Optionally, an auxiliary loss trains the student to predict the teacher’s utterances, which enables self-correction at inference time by having the model play both roles. The training pipeline involves: (1) generating synthetic dialogues by rolling out student and teacher on a dataset of single-turn tasks; (2) filtering dialogues to prevent solution leakage (via string matching and LLM judging); (3) performing RL fine-tuning on the student using the collected trajectories; and (4) for self-improvement, including the teacher prediction loss during training.

## Key Results
- On HardMath2, a smaller model fine-tuned with RL2F nearly matches a model ten times larger.
- Training on math alone yields ~7% average improvement when applied to out-of-distribution domains (coding, puzzles).
- RL2F significantly enhances in-context plasticity, allowing models to adjust reasoning across turns; internalizing teacher feedback enables self-correction without external input.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Information Asymmetry-Driven Didactic Interaction
- **Claim:** Models can learn to integrate corrective language feedback when trained on synthetic multi-turn dialogues generated from single-turn verifiable tasks, using a teacher with privileged information (e.g., ground truth).
- **Mechanism:** A teacher model, conditioned on privileged task information (e.g., correct solution), generates targeted corrective feedback without revealing the answer. A student model interacts across multiple turns, refining its response after each feedback. The student is fine-tuned via RL, where the sparse reward (+1 on final correctness) incentivizes learning to use the feedback effectively.
- **Core assumption:** High-quality corrective feedback for verifiable tasks can be generated by any model with access to the correct answer (information asymmetry), not necessarily a more capable model.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "transforms single-turn verifiable tasks into multi-turn didactic interactions driven by information asymmetry"
  - [section 2.1]: Formal POMDP definition where teacher action depends on privileged knowledge 𝑘.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct support; neighbor papers focus on general in-context learning or feedback but not this specific asymmetric teacher setup.
- **Break condition:** If the teacher frequently leaks the solution (paper reports <1% leakage), the learning signal becomes trivial and does not teach feedback integration.

### Mechanism 2: Enhanced In-Context Plasticity
- **Claim:** RL2F fine-tuning increases the model's in-context plasticity—its ability to change predictions within a single context window based on new information (feedback).
- **Mechanism:** By repeatedly encountering scenarios where its initial answer is wrong and receiving corrective feedback, the model's in-context learning algorithm (its forward pass) adapts to override earlier incorrect computations and re-reason. This is distinct from weight-update plasticity.
- **Core assumption:** The model's in-context learning mechanism is trainable via weight updates on multi-turn trajectories, not just a fixed emergent property.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2.2, Figure 6]: Baseline model repeats identical incorrect answer across turns; RL2F model adjusts reasoning and answer after feedback.
  - [abstract]: "enhanced in-context plasticity"
  - [section 6]: "increased adaptability"
- **Break condition:** If the model develops a bias to always defer to feedback without critical evaluation (sycophancy), plasticity may become maladaptive. The paper notes this as a future safety concern.

### Mechanism 3: Internalizing the Feedback Loop for Self-Improvement
- **Claim:** Training the student to predict the teacher's feedback (treating teacher as environment) internalizes the feedback logic, enabling self-correction without an external teacher.
- **Mechanism:** During training, the model is trained on full dialogues, including teacher turns. By learning to generate plausible critiques conditioned only on the conversation history (no privileged info), it models the "feedback environment." At inference, the model can play both roles, generating self-critiques to self-correct.
- **Core assumption:** The teacher's feedback logic can be approximated from the student's error context alone, allowing a single model to capture both sides of the interaction.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4, Figure 7]: Self-improvement (model playing both roles) matches or exceeds performance when interacting with a privileged teacher.
  - [abstract]: "convert this external signal into an internal capability"
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; neighboring papers do not cover this self-prediction approach.
- **Break condition:** If the model's self-critique is systematically biased or degenerate (e.g., always agreeable), self-improvement fails. The paper suggests external teacher data prevents such loops.

## Foundational Learning
### Meta-Learning (Learning to Learn)
- **Why needed here:** RL2F is explicitly framed as improving the model's ability to learn from interactions—a meta-skill. The paper connects to RL2 and first-order meta-learning, where weight updates optimize an in-context learning algorithm.
- **Quick check question:** Is the model merely memorizing feedback patterns, or is it learning a general strategy to revise answers given any corrective hint?

### Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)
- **Why needed here:** Formalizes the interactive setting where the student's state includes conversation history but not the teacher's privileged knowledge. This models the information asymmetry.
- **Quick check question:** Why is the teacher's privileged information 𝑘 part of the state but not directly observable to the student?

### Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)
- **Why needed here:** The baseline comparison (single-turn RL) and the RL2F objective both use sparse, verifiable rewards (answer correctness). Understanding this baseline is key to seeing why multi-turn RL is different.
- **Quick check question:** What is the key difference between optimizing for single-turn correctness versus multi-turn feedback integration?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- Student policy (π_S): Generates attempts conditioned on problem + history.
- Teacher policy (π_T): Generates feedback conditioned on problem + history + privileged info.
- Verifier: Automatic function that provides sparse terminal reward.
- RL optimizer: Updates student weights using trajectories from student-teacher interactions; reward signal only from final correctness.
- (Optional) World model head: For self-improvement, an auxiliary loss to predict teacher utterances.

### Critical path
1. Generate synthetic multi-turn dialogues by rolling out (student, teacher) pairs on single-turn tasks, using teacher with privileged info.
2. Filter dialogues to ensure teacher does not leak solution (string match + LLM judge).
3. Run RL fine-tuning on student: for each trajectory, compute final correctness reward, backpropagate through student generations (REINFORCE or PPO).
4. (For self-improvement) Include teacher prediction loss on teacher turns during training.

### Design tradeoffs
- Teacher quality vs. cost: Using a privileged version of the same student model is cheap but may produce low-quality feedback. Using a larger teacher improves feedback but increases cost.
- Episode length: More turns provide richer learning signal but lower success rate per trajectory, reducing sample efficiency.
- Privileged information type: Ground-truth solutions give clean feedback; unit tests (code) are noisier but more generalizable.

### Failure signatures
- Teacher leakage: >1% of teacher outputs contain the solution.
- No plasticity gap: Baseline and RL2F models show identical cumulative accuracy curves across turns.
- Self-improvement degradation: Self-critique performance lower than teacher-student interaction performance.

### First 3 experiments
1. Verify teacher non-leakage on a sample of generated dialogues using automated checks.
2. Compare single-turn RL vs. RL2F on cumulative accuracy across turns on a held-out set of in-domain tasks (e.g., HardMath2).
3. Test out-of-distribution generalization: evaluate RL2F model (trained on math) on a completely different multi-turn task (e.g., Poker) with a teacher providing feedback based on game state.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can transient in-context adaptations from feedback be consolidated into permanent weight updates to enable continual learning?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state in the conclusion that "a critical piece of the puzzle remains... Developing a mechanism to consolidate this enhanced short-term adaptation into long-term knowledge remains a crucial frontier."
- **Why unresolved:** RL2F only improves the model's ability to learn *within* a single interaction context; it does not address how these transient improvements could be transferred to the model's fixed weights over time, limiting the approach to non-continual, isolated tasks.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A follow-up study demonstrating that after RL2F fine-tuning, performance on previously encountered task distributions is retained or improved without retraining, and that new feedback-driven adaptations generalize across future contexts.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does RL2F's effectiveness and learning dynamics change in mixed-motive interactive settings (e.g., debate, negotiation) compared to the cooperative teacher-student paradigm?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors identify "mixed-motive settings, such as debate or negotiation" as a "promising avenue for future research" beyond the current cooperative setup.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper exclusively evaluates a cooperative setting where the teacher’s sole goal is to help the student. Mixed-motive interactions introduce conflicting goals and deceptive or ambiguous feedback, which may fundamentally alter how a model learns from language.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the RL2F framework to environments with adversarial or misaligned feedback providers and measuring whether the learned "in-context plasticity" generalizes, degrades, or requires new training objectives.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does training models to more effectively integrate corrective feedback (via RL2F) increase alignment risks such as sycophancy or manipulation?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly ask: "could this same mechanism incentivize undesirable behaviors, such as sycophancy? Further studies into the potential safety issues... is of great importance."
- **Why unresolved:** The work focuses on improving correctness on verifiable tasks. No experiments probe whether heightened sensitivity to user feedback makes models more likely to abandon correct reasoning to agree with misleading user hints or to generate strategically ingratiating language.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmark evaluations where the external "teacher" provides subtly incorrect or manipulative feedback, assessing if RL2F-trained models abandon correct solutions more frequently than baselines, or if they develop deceptive strategies to appease the feedback provider.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can integrating curriculum learning—where the teacher adaptively selects problems based on observed student errors—improve the sample efficiency and generalization of RL2F?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors propose exploring "curriculum learning... such as unsupervised environment design" where the teacher could craft "careful curricula... based on observed mistakes during interactions."
- **Why unresolved:** In the current method, the problem set for training is static and randomly sampled. Human teachers tailor difficulty based on student struggles; this adaptive component is absent.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a variant where the teacher’s problem selection policy is conditioned on the student’s historical error patterns and compare learning curves (performance vs. training steps) against the fixed-curriculum RL2F baseline.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What is the precise mechanistic origin of "in-context plasticity" in RL2F models, and how does it relate to plasticity phenomena studied in standard (in-weight) continual reinforcement learning?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors observe RL2F models exhibit "greater in-context plasticity" (e.g., changing answers across turns) versus baselines that repeat errors. They link this to "plasticity" in RL literature (citing Dohare et al., 2024; Lyle et al., 2023) and call it "a first clear sign... requiring deeper investigations."
- **Why unresolved:** The evidence is qualitative (conversation traces). No analysis probes model internals (e.g., attention patterns, representation stability across turns) to determine if plasticity reflects better error signal processing, reduced overfitting to initial context, or other dynamics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled probing experiments measuring (a) how representation similarity between turns changes with feedback, (b) ablation studies on model components responsible for integrating prior feedback, and (c) direct comparison of "plasticity metrics" (e.g., prediction change rates) between RL2F models and standard RL agents in continual learning settings.

## Limitations
- Proprietary data and models hinder replication; open-source substitutes may yield different results.
- Unspecified RL hyperparameters and teacher prompt engineering impede exact reproduction and may affect feedback quality.
- Potential sycophancy and self-improvement loop stability are unaddressed risks.

## Confidence
- Core RL2F improvement: High
- In-context plasticity mechanism: Medium
- Self-correction via internalized feedback: Medium
- Generalization to new domains: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Leakage audit: Use an LLM judge and exact matching to verify teacher outputs across a large sample stay under 1% leakage; if higher, re-evaluate feedback design.
2. Privileged information ablation: Train with three teacher conditions (with ground truth, without privileged info, random) and compare student learning curves to confirm the necessity of information asymmetry.
3. Self-correction stress test: Run consecutive self-critique turns (up to 5) on a validation set; check for monotonic improvement or degradation, assessing robustness of internalized feedback.
