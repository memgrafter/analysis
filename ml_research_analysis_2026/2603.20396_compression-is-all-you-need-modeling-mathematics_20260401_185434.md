# Compression is all you need: Modeling Mathematics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20396  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20396  
- **Reference count:** 5  
- **Primary result:** Human mathematics resides in a polynomially-growing region of formal mathematics analogous to a free abelian monoid with logarithmic-density macros, yielding exponential expansion of expressivity, in contrast to the free non‑abelian case.

## Executive Summary
The paper posits that human mathematics (HM) is a vanishingly small but highly structured subset of formal mathematics (FM), distinguished by its compressibility via definitions, lemmas, and theorems. It models mathematical deduction strings as elements of free monoids—free abelian (A_n) and free non‑abelian (F_n)—and introduces a macro set (definitions) that compresses these strings. The expansion function f_G'(s) measures the largest primitive ball covered using at most s macros. Theoretically, in A_n, sparse (logarithmic‑density) macros achieve exponential expansion, while in F_n even polynomial‑density macros yield only linear expansion, and superlinear expansion requires exponentially many macros. Empirical analysis of the MathLib Lean 4 library shows that unwrapped length (total primitives after expansion) grows exponentially with both depth and wrapped length (token count), while wrapped length remains nearly constant across depths. These patterns align with the A_n regime and contradict F_n, suggesting HM occupies a compressible niche within FM. The paper further proposes compression metrics (reductive and deductive) to quantify mathematical interest and guide automated reasoning.

## Method Summary
The methodology combines theoretical monoid expansion analysis with empirical study of a large formal library. Theoretically, the authors define a macro set M as additional generators that stand for fixed words over an alphabet G. The expansion function f_G'(s) captures how many elements (by radius in the word metric) become representable using s macros. They prove that in A_n (free abelian, polynomial‑growth), logarithmic‑density macros (e.g., place‑value notation) give exponential f_G'(s); polynomial‑density macros can even yield infinite expansion. In F_n (free non‑abelian, exponential‑growth), polynomial‑density macros only give linear f_G'(s); achieving superlinear expansion requires exponentially many macros. Empirically, the authors parse MathLib (commit d167cc6) to construct a dependency DAG, collapsing cycles. For each declaration, they compute: wrapped length (token count from Lean parser), unwrapped length (sum of primitive counts after recursively expanding all definitions), and depth (longest dependency chain to primitives). They then bin by depth and wrapped length, reporting median log₂(unwrapped) to reveal scaling relationships.

## Key Results
- Theoretical dichotomy: In A_n, logarithmic‑density macros achieve exponential expansion (f_G'(s) = Θ(b^{s/(n(b−1))})). In F_n, polynomial‑density macros are limited to linear expansion; superlinear expansion requires exponentially many macros.
- Empirical alignment with A_n: In MathLib, log₂(unwrapped length) vs. depth is approximately linear (slope near 1), indicating exponential growth of primitives per depth layer. Log₂(unwrapped) vs. wrapped length is also linear.
- Constant wrapped length: Wrapped length (token count) shows no systematic increase with depth, supporting the hypothesis that human mathematics uses sparse, compressible definitions (like place notation) rather than elaborate nesting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** In a free abelian monoid A_n, a logarithmically sparse macro set yields exponential expansion of expressivity; in a free non‑abelian monoid F_n, even polynomially dense macros give only linear expansion, and superlinear expansion requires an exponentially dense macro set.
- **Mechanism:** The growth rate of the monoid (size of radius‑r ball) differs: polynomial for A_n (|B_r| = Θ(rⁿ)), exponential for F_n (|B_r| = Θ((2n−1)ʳ)). A macro set M compresses by allowing short representations (few macros) of many elements. The expansion function f_G'(s) measures the largest radius fully covered by s macros. In A_n, because balls grow polynomially, a sparse set of macros can cover an exponentially large radius; the archetype is place‑notation macros {bʲ aᵢ} giving f_G'(s)=Θ(b^{s/(n(b−1))}). In F_n, exponential ball growth forces any macro set to be either extremely dense to achieve superlinear expansion or else yields only linear f_G'(s)=O(s).
- **Core assumption:** The underlying deduction strings form a free monoid (no relations beyond associativity), and macros are additional generators with fixed words in the original generators. Macro density is measured relative to the ball of radius r.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 2.1 (Theorem 1) and Section 2.2 (Theorem 4) formalize the dichotomy. The growth‑rate distinction is standard in geometric group theory.
- **Break condition:** If the monoid is not of polynomial growth (e.g., free groups with inverses, or monoids with additional relations that increase growth), the expansion limits of F_n would likely apply. If the macro set is too dense, the exponential expansion in A_n may degrade (e.g., Theorem 3 shows infinite expansion for polynomial‑density macros). If macros are not independent or the identification of G and M is noisy, the theoretical bounds may fail.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** In human mathematics as represented in MathLib, the unwrapped length of an element grows exponentially with its definitional depth, while the wrapped length remains nearly constant across depths.
- **Mechanism:** Depth measures the longest chain of macro references from an element to primitives. Each layer of a definition can multiply the number of primitives because a macro expands to a string of generators (or other macros). Empirically, plotting log₂(unwrapped length) versus depth yields an approximately linear slope near 1, indicating that each additional depth layer multiplies the primitive count by a roughly constant factor (e.g., base 2). Meanwhile, wrapped length (token count) does not systematically increase with depth, suggesting definitions are kept terse (like place‑value notation) and compression is effective.
- **Core assumption:** The dependency graph is acyclic (or cycles collapsed) and definitions are truly hierarchical; unwrapped length counts all primitives after full expansion; wrapped length counts tokens in the source definition.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.6 (Figure 5) show the linear log(unwrapped) vs depth relationship. Wrapped length distributions are reported in Section 3.5.
- **Break condition:** If the library contains many non‑compressive definitions (e.g., trivial abbreviations or tactic‑heavy proofs that expand to low‑level steps without new macros), the exponential relationship could break down. Inaccurate depth measurement (e.g., counting through cycles or ignoring intermediate macros) would also weaken the correlation. The regime assumes a sparse, parsimonious macro set (as in A_n’s logarithmic‑density); if macros were of a different density (e.g., F_n polynomial), the growth would be linear rather than exponential.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Compression metrics (reductive and deductive) computed on a formal library quantify mathematical interest and, when combined with a PageRank‑style refinement, can guide automated reasoning toward valuable regions.
- **Mechanism:** For an element u with statement S and body B, define:
  - Reductive compression T₀(u) = (|S|_G + |B|_G) / (|S|_{G'\{u}} + |B|_{G'\{u}}), measuring how much the element compresses when definitions are applied.
  - Deductive compression I₀(u) = |B|_{G'\{u}} / |S|_{G'\{u}}, measuring proof complexity relative to the compressed statement.
  High T₀ indicates the element resides in a compressible region (aligned with human mathematics). To incorporate dependency structure, run a random walk on the dependency DAG with teleportation biased toward high (T₀+I₀) elements; the stationary distribution ranks elements by their role in supporting other compressible mathematics. This gives AI agents a signal to explore mathematically valuable areas.
- **Core assumption:** Human interest correlates with reductive/deductive compressibility; the dependency graph captures meaningful knowledge dependencies; teleportation weights can be tuned to avoid trivial sinks.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5 define T₀, I₀, and the PageRank scheme; Figure 3 shows a spike in compression for some elements, illustrating the phenomenon.
- **Break condition:** If human interest is not primarily driven by compression (e.g., culturally important theorems are not particularly compressible), the metrics may not align. Poorly chosen teleportation weights may cause the random walk to collapse to uniform or extreme rankings, losing signal. The computational cost of exact optimal compression is prohibitive; the proposed metrics are heuristics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Free monoid (F_n) and free abelian monoid (A_n)**  
  Why needed: The paper’s modeling framework represents proofs as strings of primitive symbols; F_n captures ordered concatenation with n generators, while A_n ignores order (only multiplicities matter). The distinct growth rates (exponential vs polynomial) underpin the main dichotomy.  
  Quick check: What are the cardinalities of the radius‑r ball in F_n and in A_n, and how do these growth rates influence the achievable expansion from a macro set?

- **Growth rate of metric spaces (polynomial vs exponential)**  
  Why needed: The expansion theorems depend on whether the underlying monoid has polynomial or exponential growth. This concept from geometric group theory explains why A_n (and nilpotent monoids) permit sparse macros to achieve exponential expansion while F_n does not.  
  Quick check: How does Gromov’s theorem characterize monoids of polynomial growth, and why does A_n satisfy it while F_n does not?

- **Reductive vs deductive compression**  
  Why needed: The paper distinguishes two types of compression to define interest metrics (T₀ and I₀). Reductive compression measures shortening when definitions are applied; deductive compression measures proof length relative to statement. Understanding this distinction is key to interpreting the empirical findings and the proposed AI steering.  
  Quick check: Given a theorem with statement S and proof B, how would you compute T₀ and I₀, and what does a high I₀ suggest about the theorem’s nature?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Monoid model module -> Macro‑set generator -> Synthetic expansion validator;  
  MathLib parser -> Dependency DAG builder -> Metrics engine (unwrap/token/depth) -> Compression interest scorer (T₀/I₀) -> PageRank refiner -> Validation suite (reproduce figures, cross‑library tests)

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Implement monoid and expansion calculations; verify Theorems 1, 4 on synthetic data.  
  2. Process MathLib: generate dependency graph, compute wrapped/unwrapped lengths and depth; produce the empirical distributions and relationships.  
  3. Implement T₀/I₀ computation and the PageRank bias; experiment with teleportation parameter α.  
  4. Validate that the empirical relationships match the predicted A_n regime; iterate on macro‑set identification if needed.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Wrapped length as token count vs. reference count: tokens avoid inflation from tactic expansions but lose micro‑dependency detail.  
  - Cycle handling: collapsing SCCs preserves acyclicity but may obscure mutual recursion; a more nuanced treatment could be more accurate but complex.  
  - Macro‑set identification: Using all non‑primitives as M is simple but noisy. Filtering by in‑degree or by type (e.g., definitions vs theorems) can improve signal at the cost of heuristic thresholds.  
  - PageRank α: Too low makes the walk overly local; too high approaches uniform teleportation and drowns the compression bias. Calibration on held‑out data is needed.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Unexpected slopes: If log(unwrapped) vs depth is sublinear (e.g., polynomial) rather than linear, the library may not exhibit exponential nesting; perhaps macro regime differs or measurement is flawed.  
  - Wrapped length increasing with depth: contradicts the constant‑wrapped prediction, indicating definitions are not parsimonious.  
  - PageRank scores concentrated on primitives or a single hub: suggests teleportation distribution is not properly biased or the graph has extreme in‑degree skew.  
  - Synthetic tests: f_G'(s) not matching predicted scaling for the given monoid/macro regime indicates implementation errors in counting macros or ball coverage.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Synthetic macro validation: For small radii in A_n and F_n, enumerate all words, construct macro sets of varying densities (logarithmic, polynomial, random), compute f_G'(s) exactly, and verify the theorems’ asymptotic predictions (exponential vs linear vs superlinear).  
  2. Compression‑interest correlation: Recruit domain experts to rank a sample of MathLib theorems by perceived importance. Compute T₀ and I₀ for each and calculate Spearman correlation. Test whether high compression predicts expert judgment.  
  3. Cross‑library generalization: Repeat the full MathLib analysis on another large formal library (e.g., Coq’s Mathematical Components or Isabelle/HOL). Check whether the same relationships (exponential unwrapped growth, roughly constant wrapped length) hold, or whether differences reflect the underlying formal system’s design choices.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What is the precise macro set in MathLib (or human mathematics) that maps to the theoretical macro set? The paper does not identify it and regards this as a deep open problem.
- Can filtering (e.g., by in‑degree) produce a MathLib macro set that agrees with A_n log‑density metrics? Simple filters may fail to capture true compressible structure.
- Can an AI agent efficiently propose new definitions on the fly to guide exploration, despite computational cost? Determining optimal compression requires searching over possible definitions.
- Is there a computationally tractable compression notion between definitional compression and Kolmogorov complexity? Definitional compression is limited; Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable.
- Do proofs of “interesting” statements have characteristic depth/compression? Can metrics predict human interest? A systematic study correlating these metrics with human judgments remains to be done.

## Limitations
- The free monoid abstraction omits key equational and type‑theoretic relations, threatening the theoretical dichotomy's relevance to real mathematics.
- Primitive set definition (Lean core) is ambiguous; varying this boundary could alter depth/unwrapped length metrics.
- Tokenization method for wrapped length is unspecified, especially for generated elements, risking bias in the constant‑wrapped observation.
- Empirical evidence relies on a single library version; commit hash may be inaccessible, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical dichotomy (A_n vs F_n):** High (model proofs) but Medium for applicability to HM.
- **Empirical growth patterns:** Medium (data fits A_n but sensitive to preprocessing).
- **Compression metrics for interest:** Low (no direct validation).
