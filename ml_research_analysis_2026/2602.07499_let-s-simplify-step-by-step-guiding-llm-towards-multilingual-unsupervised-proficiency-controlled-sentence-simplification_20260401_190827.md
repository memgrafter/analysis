# Let's Simplify Step by Step: Guiding LLM Towards Multilingual Unsupervised Proficiency-Controlled Sentence Simplification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07499
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07499
- **Reference count:** 27
- **Primary result:** A framework that decomposes large-span simplification into optimal intermediate CEFR steps, improving target-level accuracy and reducing inference steps by 22–42%, but with a clear trade-off: higher proficiency control comes at the cost of reduced semantic preservation.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of guiding large language models (LLMs) to perform proficiency-controlled sentence simplification across large readability spans (e.g., from C2 to A1). The proposed framework breaks down the simplification into a sequence of intermediate CEFR levels determined by dynamic path planning, uses semantic-aware exemplar selection to preserve meaning, and employs a chain-of-thought generator with conversation history for coherent multi-step reasoning. Evaluated on five languages across two benchmarks, the method achieves better target-level accuracy and efficiency over sequential baselines. However, a fundamental tension emerges: improvements in readability control consistently correlate with declines in semantic similarity, a pattern confirmed by both automatic metrics and human evaluation, which also suffers from low annotator agreement on meaning preservation.

## Method Summary
The framework consists of three core components. First, **dynamic path planning** constructs a reward matrix by evaluating the LLM’s ability to simplify between all pairs of CEFR levels on a validation set; a dynamic programming algorithm then finds the minimal-step path that maximizes cumulative reward from source to target level. Second, **semantic-aware exemplar selection** curates few-shot examples for each transition by filtering successful validation simplifications and ranking them by semantic textual similarity (STS) to the source, ensuring high-meaning-preservation demonstrations. Third, a **chain-of-thought generator** processes the planned path step-by-step, appending prior simplifications to a chat history to maintain trajectory coherence. The system is LLM-agnostic and operates in an unsupervised manner, requiring only unlabeled sentences annotated with CEFR levels for the reward and exemplar phases.

## Key Results
- The dynamic planner reduces inference steps by 22–42% compared to naive sequential simplification while maintaining or improving adjacent accuracy and Spearman correlation.
- Semantic-aware exemplar selection yields consistent gains in STS/BertScore over using random or single-level examples.
- Including chat history improves target-level metrics by 3–6 points across datasets but can further reduce semantic preservation, highlighting the trade-off.
- Human evaluation confirms the automatic metric trends: large-span simplifications exhibit more meaning loss, and annotator agreement on semantic preservation is low.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Dynamic Path Planning for Efficient Decomposition
- **Claim:** Decomposing a large-span simplification into an optimal sequence of intermediate CEFR levels improves target-level accuracy and computational efficiency over single-step or naive sequential simplification.
- **Mechanism:** A dynamic programming algorithm computes a path `P* = [l0, l1, ..., lk]` that maximizes a normalized reward `R_{i,j}` derived from validating the LLM’s ability to simplify from level `i` to `j`. The reward (+1 for exact match, +0.5 for one-step deviation, -1 otherwise) incentivizes successful transitions. The optimal path minimizes steps while maximizing cumulative reward.
- **Core assumption:** The validation-stage simplification success (measured by a sentence-level estimator `ϕ(·)`) reliably predicts test-time performance for the same level transition.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions reduction of computational steps by 22–42%. Section 3.1 defines the reward matrix `R` and recurrence `V(i, t) = max_{j<i} {V(j, t-1) + R_{j,i}}`. Table 5 shows the Reduction Ratio of Inference Steps (RRIS) across datasets and models.
- **Break condition:** If the estimator `ϕ(·)` is miscalibrated or lacks generalization across domains, the reward matrix becomes noisy, leading to suboptimal paths. The paper’s reliance on carefully selected estimators (CEFR-SP-estimator, README-estimator) that outperformed benchmarks supports this dependency.

### Mechanism 2: Semantic-Aware Exemplar Selection for Meaning Preservation
- **Claim:** Selecting few-shot exemplars based on high semantic similarity (STS) between original and simplified sentences in the validation set guides the LLM to better preserve meaning, compared to random or single-case exemplars.
- **Mechanism:** For each transition `(ls, lt)` in the planned path, the framework generates simplifications on the validation set, filters for successful cases (`ϕ(output)=lt`), and selects the top-`k` (k=3) pairs with highest STS scores between source and target. These high-fidelity examples are used as in-context demonstrations.
- **Core assumption:** STS scores computed by `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` (English) or `paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2` (others) serve as a valid proxy for human-judged meaning preservation in CEFR simplification.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2 describes constructing exemplar sets via STS ranking. Section 5.3 shows STS improves from 71.46% to 75.35% on CEFR-SP-Partial when semantic selection is added. Table 7 consistently shows higher STS/BertScore for configurations that include semantic selection.
- **Break condition:** STS may not capture all critical semantic aspects (e.g., pragmatic nuances). Human evaluation reveals low agreement on meaning preservation, suggesting STS is imperfect. If validation data for a transition is sparse, the exemplar set may be empty, forcing fallback to less informative examples.

### Mechanism 3: Chat History in CoT for Trajectory Coherence
- **Claim:** Maintaining a conversation history that includes prior simplification steps along the planned path enables the LLM to maintain contextual coherence and cumulative reasoning across multi-step generations, improving target-level accuracy and stability.
- **Mechanism:** After generating an intermediate simplification `ŷ_t` for transition `(l_{t-1}, l_t)`, this output and the prompt instruction are appended to the chat history. The next prompt for transition `(l_t, l_{t+1})` includes this history, making the model explicitly aware of its prior decisions and the current intermediate state.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can effectively utilize extended chat context to maintain a consistent simplification trajectory, avoiding regression or inconsistent transformations across steps.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.3 explains appending generated outputs and instructions to history. Section 5.3 shows that including history raises Spearman’s ρ and adjacent accuracy by 3–6 points relative to no-history variants (e.g., Llama on README-EN: ρ=0.66, AdjAcc=91.41% with history vs. ρ=0.37, AdjAcc=70.96% without). Table 7 confirms consistent gains.
- **Break condition:** Effectiveness is bounded by the LLM’s context window length; long paths may cause truncation. An early semantic error can propagate through history without correction. The observed trade-off (lower STS with history in some configurations) suggests history can over-prioritize target achievement at the cost of meaning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: CEFR Proficiency Levels (A1-C2)**  
  *Why needed here:* The entire framework navigates this ordinal scale; the path planner, reward matrix, and evaluation metrics depend on mapping text to discrete levels. Misunderstanding the gaps between levels leads to incorrect planning and reward calibration.  
  *Quick check:* If a sentence is annotated as C2, what characteristic (vocabulary, syntax, discourse) would likely need to change most drastically to reach an A1 level, compared to moving from C2 to C1?

- **Concept: Dynamic Programming for Shortest/Optimal Path**  
  *Why needed here:* The framework’s efficiency gain hinges on solving the path optimization problem via dynamic programming, avoiding exhaustive search.  
  *Quick check:* In the recurrence `V(i, t) = max_{j<i} {V(j, t-1) + R_{j,i}}`, what does `t` represent, and how does this recurrence build the optimal path bottom-up?

- **Concept: Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) as a Metric**  
  *Why needed here:* STS is the automated signal for exemplar selection, approximating meaning preservation. Understanding its basis (sentence embeddings, cosine similarity) and limitations (paraphrase detection vs. full equivalence) is crucial.  
  *Quick check:* Why might STS score highly for a simplification that changes a key entity or numeric value (e.g., "2023" → "recent years") despite significant meaning alteration?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Sentence-Level Estimator (`ϕ(·)`) → Path Planner → Exemplar Database → CoT Generator (with Chat History) → Evaluation Module  
  The estimator provides level labels for reward construction and evaluation. The path planner outputs an optimal intermediate level sequence. The exemplar database stores top-k high-STS pairs per transition. The CoT generator executes the multi-step simplification using the plan, exemplars, and history.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. **Offline Setup:** Run LLM on validation set for all `(ls, lt)` pairs; label outputs with `ϕ(·)`; populate reward matrix `R`; for each successful transition, compute STS and store top-k exemplars.  
  2. **Online Inference:** For a test sentence `x` (level `C_src`, target `C_tgt`):  
     a. Query path planner → obtain optimal path `P* = [l0=C_src, l1, ..., lk=C_tgt]`.  
     b. Initialize empty chat history `H`.  
     c. For each transition `(l_{i-1}, l_i)` in `P*`:  
        - Fetch exemplars for `(l_{i-1}, l_i)`.  
        - Construct prompt: instruction (target level `l_i`), CEFR descriptor, exemplars, chat history `H`, and current source (first step: `x`; later steps: last output from `H`).  
        - Generate next simplification via LLM (temperature 0); append `(prompt, output)` to `H`.  
     d. Final output is the result after reaching `l_k = C_tgt`.  
     e. Optionally run `ϕ(final_output)` to verify achieved level.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Path optimality vs. generalization: The reward matrix `R` is dataset-specific; a path optimal on validation may be suboptimal on out-of-distribution test data.  
  - Exemplar specificity vs. coverage: Top-k STS exemplars ensure high preservation for similar inputs but may not generalize to divergent test sentences; fallback to random training sentences provides coverage but poor guidance.  
  - History length vs. context limit: Longer paths (e.g., C2→A1) improve control but consume context; may require truncation strategies not discussed.  
  - Automatic vs. human evaluation: The framework optimizes estimator success and STS, but human evaluation shows these metrics imperfectly correlate with human meaning judgments, especially for large spans.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Estimator failure:** `ϕ(·)` mispredicts levels corrupts `R`; symptoms: inefficient/non-monotonic paths, consistent target misses.  
  - **Exemplar scarcity:** Uncommon transitions (e.g., C1→A1) yield empty `Ŷ_{s,t}`; symptoms: sudden drop in STS/BertScore for those steps due to fallback exemplars.  
  - **History drift:** An early erroneous step propagates; symptoms: final output at target level (high AdjAcc) but low semantic similarity. Chat history may cause overfitting to lexical patterns.  
  - **Cross-lingual transfer failure:** Using an estimator for a different language family fails; symptoms: very low ρ/AdjAcc on non-matching languages.

- **First 3 experiments (for a new engineer to validate implementation):**  
  1. **Estimator sanity check:** On the validation set, compute `ϕ(·)`'s accuracy against gold CEFR labels. Must exceed the baselines reported in Appendix C (Tables 8, 9); for English, expect >90% F1-like agreement.  
  2. **Reward matrix sanity check:** For 100 validation sentences, manually inspect generated simplifications and `ϕ` labels used in `R`. Verify scoring: +1 (exact target), +0.5 (one-off), -1 (else); confirm normalization.  
  3. **Path planning vs. sequential baseline:** Implement a naive sequential simplifier (e.g., C2→B1→A2→A1 in fixed order). Run on 50 test sentences for a large-span task (C2→A1). Compare final AdjAcc and inference steps against the dynamic planner; expect similar/higher AdjAcc with 22–42% fewer steps per Table 5/11.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How can large language models be designed to preserve semantic fidelity when simplifying sentences across large CEFR proficiency spans (e.g., from C2 to A1)?  
  *Basis:* Abstract and conclusions state this as an open challenge intensified by simplification span.  
  *Why unresolved:* Results show a consistent trade-off: higher target-level accuracy correlates with lower STS scores; human evaluation confirms more meaning loss for large spans.  
  *Resolving evidence:* A method achieving statistically significant improvements in both readability control (ρ, AdjAcc) and semantic preservation (STS, human judgments) for large-span tasks across multiple languages.

- **Question 2:** What techniques can effectively balance the inherent trade-off between proficiency-controlled simplification effectiveness and semantic preservation in LLM-based frameworks?  
  *Basis:* Conclusions directly state future work should explore balancing this trade-off; limitations note simultaneous optimization remains open.  
  *Why unresolved:* Across experiments, increasing simplification effectiveness correlates with decreased semantic preservation; no current solution mitigates this tension.  
  *Resolving evidence:* A novel approach (e.g., modified reward function, exemplar criteria) that improves one metric without degrading the other, supported by paired statistical tests showing non-negative correlation between effectiveness and preservation metrics.

- **Question 3:** How can the proposed dynamic path planning and semantic-aware CoT framework be extended to document-level simplification while maintaining cross-sentence coherence and discourse relationships?  
  *Basis:* Limitations explicitly identify the gap: focus is sentence-level; real-world applications require document-level coherence.  
  *Why unresolved:* Current methodology ignores inter-sentential dependencies; document-level introduces context tracking, consistency, and discourse challenges.  
  *Resolving evidence:* An extended model with hierarchical or graph-based planning that evaluates coherence (e.g., entity-grid metrics, human-rated discourse quality) and achieves performance comparable to sentence-level on CEFR control.

- **Question 4:** What evaluation paradigms or metrics can reliably capture meaning preservation in proficiency-controlled simplification, given the observed low inter-annotator agreement even among expert human evaluators?  
  *Basis:* Human evaluation reports low agreement on semantic preservation; limitations state current paradigms may inadequately capture fidelity.  
  *Why unresolved:* The 0–2 meaning preservation scale yielded inconsistent judgments across languages and annotator groups, reflecting subjectivity in defining "main idea" preservation for large transformations.  
  *Resolving evidence:* A standardized annotation protocol or automated metric achieving high inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Krippendorff’s Alpha > 0.8) and strong correlation with task success, validated replicably across languages and spans.

## Limitations
- Prompt templates (Figure 2), exact CEFR descriptors, and validation-time LLM generation parameters are unreleased, hindering reproducibility.
- Estimator architectures and training details are unspecified; public availability of CEFR-SP-estimator and README-estimator is unclear.
- The fundamental trade-off between proficiency control and semantic preservation persists: higher target-level accuracy consistently reduces semantic similarity, and human meaning judgments show low agreement.
- Dynamic programming implementation (e.g., reward normalization, tie-breaking) and fallback exemplar strategies are underspecified.

## Confidence
- **Dynamic Path Planning:** Medium — Step reduction is demonstrated, but reward matrix generalization is dataset-dependent and sensitive to estimator errors.
- **Semantic-Aware Exemplars:** Medium — STS-guided selection improves automatic scores, but imperfect correlation with human meaning preservation risks overfitting to superficial similarity.
- **Chat History Coherence:** Medium — History boosts target achievement, yet context window limits and error propagation through steps remain unaddressed.

## Next Checks
