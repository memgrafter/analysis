# Partition Function Estimation under Bounded f-Divergence
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23535
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23535
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The sample complexity for estimating partition functions is characterized by the integrated coverage profile, leading to three regimes based on f-divergence growth: linear f-divergences (TV, Hellinger) are impossible; superlinear/subquadratic (KL, Rényi α∈(1,2]) yields n ≈ exp(D_f/ε)/ε or ε^{-α}; superquadratic (α>2) yields n ≈ ε^{-2}.

## Executive Summary
The paper studies multiplicative estimation of a partition function Z given samples from a proposal μ and an unnormalized density ratio λ = Z·dν/dμ. The core innovation is the integrated coverage profile ICov_M(ν∥μ) = ∫_0^M Cov_t(ν∥μ) dt, which captures the tail mass of the likelihood ratio. The authors show that this quantity tightly determines the sample complexity of a median-of-means estimator that truncates large ratios.

The results reveal a sharp phase transition when expressed in terms of an f-divergence D_f(ν∥μ). Specifically, the required number of samples n satisfies n = Θ(M·ε^{-1}) once M is chosen so that M^{-1}·ICov_M ≤ ε; through a relationship between ICov_M and D_f, the effective M depends on the inverse growth rate γ_f of the generating convex function f. This yields three regimes: (i) linear f (e.g., total variation, Hellinger) leads to impossibility; (ii) superlinear but subquadratic f (e.g., KL, Rényi α ∈ (1,2]) gives n that grows exponentially in D_f/ε or polynomially as ε^{-α}; (iii) superquadratic f (Rényi α>2) achieves the parametric rate n = Θ(ε^{-2}). Moreover, approximate sampling under the same divergence constraints is strictly easier: an A^*-sampling algorithm requires only n = Θ(M·log(1/ε)), establishing a separation between counting and sampling.

## Method Summary
The primary algorithm is a median-of-means estimator. Given n samples from μ, the samples are divided into k = ⌈8 log(1/δ)⌉ groups of equal size (or nearly equal). For each group, compute the sample mean of the unnormalized density ratios λ(X). The final estimate is the median of these k group means. To control variance, the estimator implicitly truncates contributions from points with extremely large λ by relying on the integrated coverage profile; Bernstein’s inequality is applied to the truncated random variables, yielding a high‑probability bound provided that M⁻¹·ICov_M ≤ ε. For asymmetric needs (e.g., a lower bound), a quantile‑based estimator using the (1−ε)‑quantile of the λ values can be employed, which requires a slightly larger M but still attains n = Θ(M·ε^{-1}). The practical challenge lies in selecting an appropriate M from data or prior knowledge; one can use bound M via the f‑divergence D_f(ν∥μ) and the inverse growth rate γ_f, as summarized in the key results.

## Key Results
- Integrated coverage profile characterizes sample complexity: n = Θ(M·ε^{-1}) when M^{-1}·ICov_M ≤ ε.
- In f‑divergence terms: n = Θ(γ_f(c·D_f·ε^{-1})/ε ∨ c^2·ε^{-2}), yielding three regimes: (i) linear f (TV, Hellinger) → impossible; (ii) superlinear/subquadratic f (KL, Rényi 1<α≤2) → n ≈ exp(D_f/ε)/ε or ε^{-α}; (iii) superquadratic f (Rényi α>2) → n ≈ ε^{-2}.
- Asymmetric estimator achieves n = Θ(M·ε^{-1}) for lower‑tail accuracy, while approximate sampling needs only n = Θ(M·log(1/ε)), a strict separation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Sample complexity for multiplicative partition function estimation is tightly characterized by integrated coverage.
- **Mechanism:** The integrated coverage profile \( \text{ICov}_M(\nu\|\mu) = \int_0^M \text{Cov}_t(\nu\|\mu) dt \) integrates the tail mass of the density ratio. A median-of-means estimator on truncated density ratios uses Bernstein’s inequality where variance is bounded by integrated coverage (Lemma 5), yielding \( n = \Theta(M \cdot \varepsilon^{-1}) \) when \( M^{-1} \cdot \text{ICov}_M \leq \varepsilon \).
- **Core assumption:** The proposal distribution \( \mu \) absolutely continuous w.r.t. target \( \nu \) (\( \nu \ll \mu \)), enabling density ratio definition.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "introduces the integrated coverage profile… shows it characterizes sample complexity… \( n = \Theta(M \cdot \varepsilon^{-1}) \) when \( M^{-1} \cdot \text{ICov}_M \leq \varepsilon \)."
  - [section 2] Definition 1 formalizes coverage and integrated coverage; Lemma 5 shows variance bound.
  - [section B.2] Proof of Theorem 4 uses Lemma 5 to control truncated variance.
- **Break condition:** If \( \text{ICov}_M \) decays slower than \( M \cdot \varepsilon \), sample complexity becomes vacuous (e.g., linear \( f \)-divergences).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** \( f \)-divergences induce three distinct sample complexity regimes based on growth rate of \( f \).
- **Mechanism:** Via Lemma 9, integrated coverage is bounded by \( c^2/M + M \cdot D_f(\nu\|\mu)/f(M) \). The inverse growth rate \( \gamma_f(M) := \inf\{ t : f(t)/t \geq M \} \) determines regime: (i) **Linear** \( f \) (TV, Hellinger): \( \gamma_f = \infty \) → impossible. (ii) **Superlinear/subquadratic** (KL, Rényi \( 1 < \alpha \leq 2 \)): \( n \asymp \exp(D_f/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon \) or \( \varepsilon^{-\alpha} \). (iii) **Superquadratic** (Rényi \( \alpha > 2 \)): \( n \asymp \varepsilon^{-2} \).
- **Core assumption:** \( f \) convex with \( f(1)=f'(1)=0 \); assumption that \( t^{-2}f(t) \) non-increasing for \( t \geq c \) (Theorem 5).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "yielding sharp phase transitions depending on the growth rate of \( f \)… three regimes."
  - [section 2] Defines \( \gamma_f \); Figure 1 visualizes regimes.
  - [section C.1] Lemma 7 relates coverage to \( f \)-divergence; Lemma 9 links integrated coverage.
- **Break condition:** Violation of \( t^{-2}f(t) \) non-increasing weakens the \( \varepsilon \) dependence; linear \( f \) yields impossibility for any \( \varepsilon \).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Approximate sampling is strictly easier than partition function estimation under the same divergence constraints.
- **Mechanism:** The \( A^* \)-sampling algorithm (Flamich & Wells) requires \( n \asymp M \log(1/\varepsilon) \) when \( \text{Cov}_M \leq \varepsilon \). Since \( \text{Cov}_M \leq M^{-1} \text{ICov}_M \), this improves by an \( \varepsilon^{-1} \) factor over estimation’s \( M \varepsilon^{-1} \). In superquadratic regimes, separation approaches quadratic.
- **Core assumption:** Unnormalized density ratio access \( \lambda = Z \cdot d\nu/d\mu \); ability to run \( A^* \)-sampling.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "strict separation between the complexity of approximate sampling and counting."
  - [section 3.3] Proposition 3: \( n \asymp M \log(1/\varepsilon) \); comparison to Theorem 4.
  - [section F] Proof uses coupling and Poisson tail bounds with \( \text{Cov}_M \) control.
- **Break condition:** If \( \text{Cov}_M \) decays extremely slowly (e.g., linear \( f \)), both become infeasible; separation only meaningful when \( \text{ICov}_M \) finite but \( \text{Cov}_M \) not too small.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Coverage Profile & Integrated Coverage**
  - *Why needed here:* Quantifies tail mass of density ratio \( d\nu/d\mu \); directly controls sample complexity via variance bounds.
  - *Quick check question:* If \( \text{Cov}_{M}(\nu\|\mu) = 0.01 \) for some \( M \), what does that imply about the probability mass of regions where density ratio exceeds \( M \)?
- **Concept: \( f \)-Divergence & Growth Rate \( \gamma_f \)**
  - *Why needed here:* Provides unified view of divergence constraints; \( \gamma_f \) maps divergence magnitude to required \( M \), determining regime.
  - *Quick check question:* For KL-divergence (\( f(t) = t \log t \)), why does \( \gamma_f(M) \asymp e^M \) lead to exponential sample complexity in \( D_f/\varepsilon \)?
- **Concept: Median-of-Means & Truncated Variance Control**
  - *Why needed here:* Robust estimator for heavy-tailed sums; variance of truncated ratios bounded by integrated coverage (Lemma 5) replaces standard Chernoff.
  - *Quick check question:* Why does naive Chernoff on untruncated ratios fail when \( \chi^2(\nu\|\mu) = \infty \)?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input: Proposal μ (samples), unnormalized density ratio λ = Z·dν/dμ, accuracy ε, confidence δ.
Core module: Integrated coverage computation (theoretical) or f-divergence estimation (practical proxy).
Estimator choice: Median-of-means (symmetric, Theorem 4), asymmetric quantile (Theorem 6), or A*-sampling (for sampling).
Output: Estimate Ẑ or approximate sample from ν.

### Critical path
1. Estimate/tail‑bound ICov_M or D_f(ν∥μ) from data or prior knowledge.
2. Determine regime via f growth.
3. Set M using γ_f or coverage constraint.
4. Sample n = Θ(M/ε) (estimation) or n = Θ(M log(1/ε)) (sampling).
5. Run chosen estimator with appropriate grouping/quantile.

### Design tradeoffs
- Symmetric vs asymmetric: Theorem 4 gives (1±ε) but requires M/ε; Theorem 6 gives one‑sided 1−ε lower bound with same M/ε but M factor larger upper bound—useful when lower bound critical.
- f-divergence choice: KL/Rényi‑1 give strong tail control but may overestimate complexity if lighter tails; TV/Hellinger may be too weak.
- Computational: Median-of-means needs sorting per group; A*-sampling needs Poisson times and sorting.

### Failure signatures
- Impossibility regime: Reported n→∞ for small ε when using linear f (TV, Hellinger) → switch to superlinear f or estimate coverage directly.
- Breakdown: If ICov_M is not actually ≤ ε M, empirical quantiles will not concentrate → verify via pilot samples or moment assumptions.
- Sampling gap: If sampling fails but estimation formula suggests feasible n, likely Cov_M larger than assumed → re‑evaluate M.

### First 3 experiments
1. Regime validation: For known μ, ν (e.g., Gaussian with different variances), compute D_f and verify empirical n matches ε^{−α} (Rényi‑α≤2) vs ε^{−2} (Rényi‑α>2).
2. Coverage estimation: Estimate Cov_M from pilot samples; check if M^{−1}ICov_M ≤ ε/4 holds with margin; compare error rates using Theorems 4 vs 6.
3. Sampling‑estimation gap: Implement A*-sampling and median‑of‑means on same distributions; measure TV vs multiplicative error; confirm separation grows as ‖dν/dμ‖_∞ increases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Does the sample complexity characterization extend to base distributions μ that have atomic components?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Theorem 7 explicitly assumes μ is non-atomic; the main results are derived under absolute continuity ν≪μ without addressing the case where μ contains atoms.
- Why unresolved: Atomic parts may introduce point masses in the likelihood ratio that could alter the integrated coverage behavior and thus the sample complexity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Prove matching upper and lower bounds for atomic μ, or exhibit a counterexample showing a different scaling regime.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Which additional structural assumptions on the unnormalized density ratio λ could make estimation feasible under linear f-divergences (e.g., total variation)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Proposition 1 demonstrates impossibility for linear f (TV, Hellinger) under the minimal-assumption setting; the paper does not explore extra structure like smoothness or boundedness of λ.
- Why unresolved: Linear divergences do not control the tails of dν/dμ, but stronger regularity (e.g., bounded likelihood ratios) might circumvent the impossibility.
- What evidence would resolve it: An algorithm achieving finite sample guarantees with bounded λ or a lower bound that remains impossible even under such structure.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Is the constant-factor upper bound in the asymmetric estimator (Theorem 6) inherent, or can one achieve symmetric (1±ε) accuracy with similar sample complexity?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Theorem 6 provides a lower bound (1−ε)Z and an upper bound of MZ (a fixed constant factor). Symmetric lower bounds (Theorem 7) show n ≳ M/ε for (1±ε), but the optimal trade‑off for asymmetric accuracy is not determined.
- Why unresolved: There is no lower bound specifically for the asymmetric problem; it is unclear whether (1+ε) can be attained without increasing the sample complexity.
- What evidence would resolve it: A matching lower bound proving that any estimator with (1−ε) lower guarantee must have an upper bound at least some constant >1, or a new estimator that attains (1+ε) with comparable n.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How can one efficiently optimize the proposal distribution μ to minimize integrated coverage for a given family of target functions g in importance sampling?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The importance sampling bounds (Theorems 9,10) depend on weighted integrated coverage and the authors mention this could guide proposal design, but no optimization method is provided.
- Why unresolved: Minimizing integrated coverage over μ is a high‑dimensional, potentially non‑convex problem; its structure and computational tractability are unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Characterization of the optimal μ as the solution to a tractable variational problem, an efficient algorithm to find it, or a hardness result demonstrating intrinsic difficulty.

## Limitations
- Integrated coverage profile lacks a practical estimator, and the analysis relies on a non‑increasing tail assumption (t↦f(t)/t²), limiting its generality.
- Constants (e.g., the factor 6 in γ_f) are unsharpened, and the impossibility for linear f‑divergences is only asymptotic, leaving practical thresholds uncertain.
- Absolute continuity (ν≪μ) may not hold in many real‑world applications, and the sampling‑estimation separation result assumes exact knowledge of coverage.

## Confidence
- *High*: Theorem 4 sample‑complexity characterization (variance bound via Lemma 5).
- *Medium*: Three‑regime classification (Lemma 9 may be loose).
- *Medium*: Sampling‑estimation separation (oracle‑based).
