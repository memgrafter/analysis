# Discovering What You Can Control: Interventional Boundary Discovery for Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18257
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18257
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: IBD identifies causally relevant state dimensions via action interventions, achieving near-oracle RL performance even with up to 100 confounded distractors, while full-state RL collapses when distractors outnumber true features by ~3:1.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Interventional Boundary Discovery (IBD), a method for selecting causally influential observation dimensions in reinforcement learning environments with confounded distractors. IBD uses the agent's own actions as interventions via Pearl's do-operator, collecting baseline and randomized-action trajectories. It then applies per-dimension two-sample testing with multiple-testing correction to produce a binary mask of relevant features. Across 12 continuous control tasks with up to 100 distractors, IBD achieves near-oracle downstream performance and maintains high boundary accuracy (mean F1 0.95), while observational feature selection methods fail due to confounding. The method transfers across popular RL algorithms (SAC, TD3) and is robust to distractor scaling until a ~3:1 distractor-to-signal ratio, beyond which full-state performance collapses.

## Method Summary
IBD operates in two phases: a lightweight probing phase and a standard RL training phase. During probing, a structured random policy (or optionally a lightweight trained scout) generates two sets of trajectories: (1) baseline rollouts under normal actions, and (2) intervention rollouts where all action dimensions are replaced with i.i.d. noise, implementing a joint `do(a=noise)` intervention that severs confounding paths. For each observation dimension and horizon `h ∈ {1,5,10}`, the method computes the mean absolute `h`-step difference across trajectories. A Welch's t-test compares the distributions of these statistics between baseline and intervention sets. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controls the false discovery rate across all tests. Any dimension with a significant test at any horizon is included in the Causal Sphere of Influence (cSoI). The resulting binary mask is then used to gate observations during downstream RL training, effectively removing irrelevant distractors. The approach requires no reward feedback during probing and is agnostic to the downstream RL algorithm.

## Key Results
- IBD achieves near-oracle performance on 12 DMControl tasks with up to 100 confounded distractors (e.g., 929 return on reacher_hard vs. oracle 925 and full-state 12).
- It maintains high boundary discovery accuracy (mean F1 = 0.95) across diverse distractor types (autonomous, mimicking, reward-correlated).
- Full-state RL performance collapses when distractors outnumber true features by ~3:1, a threshold IBD robustly avoids.
- IBD transfers seamlessly to both SAC and TD3 algorithms.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** IBD uses the agent's own actions as a source of interventional data via the `do-operator` to sever confounding paths that plague observational feature selection.
- **Mechanism:** During the probing phase, IBD collects two sets of trajectories: (1) a baseline set under a probe policy, and (2) an intervention set where all action dimensions are replaced with i.i.d. noise (`do(a=noise)`). This intervention breaks all incoming edges to the action node in the causal graph, removing any spurious correlation between actions and observation dimensions caused by common confounders.
- **Core assumption:** The agent can override its actions during a short, stationary probing phase (Action overrideability and Stationary causal structure from Section 3.1).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "IBD applies Pearl's do-operator to the agent's own actions… The do-operator severs all confounding paths by construction."
  - [section 3.2]: "Intervention trajectories… are collected with all action dimensions replaced by i.i.d. draws from Uniform(A) at every step, implementing do(a_t=noise) and severing all incoming edges to the action node."
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers focus on general causal discovery (e.g., temporal, linear, interventional constraints) but do not directly address the RL-specific mechanism of using the policy's own action space as the intervention tool.
- **Break condition:** If the agent cannot safely or effectively randomize its actions during probing (e.g., physical safety constraints, or a deterministic policy that cannot be overridden), the interventional data cannot be collected.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A per-dimension two-sample test with multiple-testing correction on trajectory-level statistics reliably identifies dimensions with a non-zero causal effect from actions.
- **Mechanism:** For each observation dimension *i* and horizon *h* ∈ *H*={1,5,10}, IBD extracts the mean absolute *h*-step difference (Eq. 2) from each trajectory. A Welch's *t*-test compares the baseline and intervention distributions. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure controls the false discovery rate (FDR) across all *d × |H|* tests. A dimension is included in the *Causal Sphere of Influence (SoI)* if any horizon yields a significant adjusted *p*-value.
- **Core assumption:** (1) **Faithfulness:** A directed causal path of length ≤ *h* from an action to a dimension produces a detectable distribution shift in the *h*-step difference. (2) The probe policy induces sufficient state coverage for effects to manifest (from Section 3.1 and 3.5).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2]: Detailed algorithm for computing Δⁱₕ(τ) and applying Welch's test with BH correction.
  - [section 3.4, Prop 3.4]: "Under the faithfulness assumption… intervening on action dimension *j* changes the distribution of *h*-step state differences on dimension *i* iff a directed causal path of length ≤ *h* from aⱼ to oᵢ exists."
  - [section 5.4, Table 5]: Consistent F1 scores ~0.95 across 12 settings demonstrate empirical reliability of the testing procedure.
- **Break condition:** If causal effects are extremely subtle (e.g., near-zero variance explained) or if the probe policy fails to visit states where causal effects are non-zero, the test loses power, leading to false negatives (low recall).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A lightweight, non-learned *structured random probe policy* provides sufficient state coverage for reliable boundary discovery, avoiding the cost and potential narrowness of a trained RL scout.
- **Mechanism:** The default probe policy generates actions using a combination of sinusoidal waveforms with weak state feedback and exploration noise. This induces broad, unstructured exploration across the state space without requiring any RL training or reward feedback. The resulting diversity in states ensures that causal effects (which may only manifest in specific state regions) are captured in the trajectory statistics.
- **Core assumption:** Broad state coverage is more important than task-directed behavior for detecting *any* causal influence from actions (from Section 3.5).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.5]: "Our default probe is the structured random policy… which requires no RL training and no GPU. This policy suffices for all tasks in our experiments."
  - [appendix C, Table 4]: Scout ablation shows that training an SAC scout for up to 160K steps provides no improvement in boundary accuracy on `cheetah_run` and `reacher_hard`, and slightly decreases recall on `walker_walk` (F1 0.94 vs. 0.90 with scout). The broad, random coverage is argued to be superior to a converged policy's narrow distribution.
- **Break condition:** For environments where relevant causal effects only occur in very specific, hard-to-reach states (e.g., requiring precise coordination or long-horizon planning), a purely random probe may not visit those states, leading to false negatives. The paper notes an optional lightweight scout could be used if domain knowledge suggests this.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Pearl's `do-operator` and causal identification**
  - **Why needed here:** The core problem—distinguishing dimensions causally influenced by actions from confounded distractors—is one of *causal identification*, not statistical prediction. Observational statistics (e.g., mutual information) are insufficient because a confounder *C* can create a spurious association *A ↔ D* even when `do(A)` does not affect *D*. The `do-operator` severs incoming edges to *A*, breaking the confounding path.
  - **Quick check question:** What is the key difference between `P(O|A)` and `P(O|do(A))` in the context of a confounder *C* that affects both *A* and *O*?

- **Concept: Multiple testing correction (Benjamini-Hochberg)**
  - **Why needed here:** IBD performs a separate statistical test for every observation dimension *i* and horizon *h* (total *m = d × |H|* tests). Without correction, the chance of at least one false positive (declaring a distractor as causal) grows with *m*. BH correction controls the False Discovery Rate (FDR), ensuring the expected proportion of false positives among the selected dimensions is bounded by α=0.05.
  - **Quick check question:** What is the difference between controlling the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) and the False Discovery Rate (FDR), and why is FDR more appropriate for IBD's goal of producing a binary mask?

- **Concept: Feature selection & state abstraction in RL**
  - **Why needed here:** The agent faces a high-dimensional observation vector containing both relevant state (`s_c`) and irrelevant distractors (`s_d`). Standard RL algorithms with fixed-capacity function approximators (e.g., MLPs) degrade as irrelevant dimensions increase. IBD performs *feature selection* by identifying the `Causal Sphere of Influence`, a form of *state abstraction* that removes exogenous information.
  - **Quick check question:** How does the "distractor scaling effect" (Section 5.2) illustrate a tradeoff between input dimensionality and function approximator capacity?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Probe Policy -> Trajectory Collector -> Statistic Extractor -> Hypothesis Tester -> Multiple Testing Corrector -> Mask Generator -> Downstream RL Agent

**Critical path:** The fidelity of the final mask `m` is most sensitive to **(a)** the state coverage induced by `π_probe` (if coverage is poor, causal effects may be unobservable) and **(b)** the power of the two-sample test (which depends on *N*, *T*, and effect size). The BH correction step is deterministic and safe.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Probe policy:** Structured random (default) is cheap and broad but may miss sparse causal effects. A trained scout is more expensive but can target relevant state regions. The ablation (Appendix C) strongly favors the random probe for their tasks.
- **Intervention mode:** *Joint intervention* (randomize all actions) is simpler, requires one dataset, and provides a stronger aggregate signal. *Per-action intervention* (randomize one dimension at a time) yields a finer-grained *Causal Influence Matrix* but requires `d_a` intervention datasets.
- **Horizons *H*:** More horizons (e.g., {1,5,10,20}) increase chance of detecting delayed effects but add multiple-testing burden. The chosen set covers immediate to medium-delay effects in DMControl tasks.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Low recall (many false negatives):** The test fails to flag true causal dimensions. Likely causes: (1) Probe policy has poor state coverage (e.g., too noisy, doesn't explore relevant regions). (2) Causal effect is extremely weak (e.g., α in partial controllability experiment near 0). (3) Horizons *H* do not include the true causal delay.
- **Low precision (many false positives):** Distractors leak into the mask. Likely causes: (1) BH correction threshold α too lenient (but α=0.05 is standard). (2) Test statistic underpowered leading to noise falsely crossing threshold (small *N*). (3) Non-stationarity during probing (violation of assumption 3).
- **Downstream performance similar to Full State:** Either (1) the distractor-to-signal ratio is below the collapse threshold (~3:1), so full-state MLP can cope, or (2) the bottleneck is not distractors (e.g., exploration failure in `hopper_hop`).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Sanity check on easy distractors:** Run IBD on a task with 6 autonomous, statistically distinct distractors. Expect near-perfect boundary accuracy (P/R/F1 > 0.95) and downstream performance matching oracle. Confirms basic pipeline functionality.
2. **Distractor scaling sweep:** For one canonical task (e.g., `walker_walk`), run Full State, IBD, and Oracle with distractor counts {6, 50, 100}. Expect IBD to track Oracle across all levels while Full State collapses at high distractor counts (predicted ~100 for walker). Validates the core scaling claim.
3. **Observational baseline comparison:** On a medium-difficulty task (e.g., `reacher_hard` with 50 distractors), compare IBD against MI Select, Variance Select, and Cond. MI. Expect IBD to achieve ~930 return while observational methods fall to ~10. Demonstrates confounding immunity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Can IBD be used as a post-encoder selector to improve visual RL robustness?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion explicitly suggests applying IBD to encoder feature vectors to test complementarity with encoder-level robustness.
- Why unresolved: No experiments on visual RL were conducted; the proposed extension is untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Train a visual encoder (DrQ-v2/CURL) on DMControl with distractors, apply IBD to its latents, and compare downstream SAC/TD3 performance against encoder-only.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can IBD adapt to non-stationary environments with minimal re-probing cost?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion states that understanding how to amortize probing cost across shifts in non-stationary causal structures is an open problem.
- Why unresolved: The current IBD requires full recomputation of the mask; no efficient mechanism for tracking changes is proposed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop methods (e.g., incremental testing, change detection) and evaluate their sample efficiency in simulated non-stationary tasks where the causal boundary drifts over time.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does IBD remain accurate in environments with complex causal graphs, such as mediators or policy-dependent distractors?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The limitations note that settings with mediators and policy-dependent distractors are open challenges beyond the purely exogenous distractors tested.
- Why unresolved: IBD assumes a simple binary causal/exogenous decomposition; mediators might create indirect causal paths that confuse the two-sample test, and policy-dependent distractors may confound interventions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Build environments with explicit mediator variables and distractors whose distribution depends on the agent's policy, then measure IBD's boundary accuracy (precision/recall) against ground-truth masks.

## Limitations
- Probe policy parameters and distractor calibration details are not fully specified, risking improper state coverage in reproduction.
- The method assumes action overrideability and stationary causal structure, which may not hold in safety-constrained or non-stationary environments.
- The random probe may fail for sparse causal effects requiring specific state regions, and the fixed horizon set may miss delayed effects.

## Confidence
- **Efficacy**: IBD achieves near-oracle performance across 12 tasks (High).
- **Mechanism**: Do-operator via action randomization breaks confounding (High).
- **Testing**: Two-sample test with BH correction reliably detects causal influence (Medium; depends on coverage and test power).
- **Probe sufficiency**: Structured random policy provides adequate coverage (Medium; ablation supportive but may not cover sparse effects).
- **Scalability**: Robust to 100 distractors while full-state collapses at ~3:1 ratio (High).
- **Transfer**: Works with SAC and TD3 (High).
