# Breaking the Bias Barrier in Concave Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.08518
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08518
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The first optimal sample complexity O(ε⁻²) for concave multi-objective RL under policy-gradient methods, matching the known lower bound.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles a fundamental bottleneck in multi‑objective reinforcement learning (MORL): when multiple objectives are combined via a concave scalarization function, the policy gradient estimator becomes biased due to the nonlinearity of the scalarization. This bias inflates the sample complexity from the optimal O(ε⁻²) (achievable in single‑objective RL) to O(ε⁻⁴) under standard policy gradient methods. The authors propose two solutions: (1) Natural Policy Gradient (NPG) coupled with a Multi‑Level Monte Carlo (MLMC) estimator that uses a telescoping structure to control bias with only logarithmic expected sampling cost; (2) exploiting second‑order smoothness of the scalarization function, where the first‑order bias cancels automatically, allowing vanilla NPG to achieve the optimal rate without MLMC. Both approaches attain the sample‑complexity lower bound for RL, providing the first optimal guarantees for concave MORL under policy‑gradient methods.

## Method Summary
The setting is an infinite‑horizon discounted MDP with M reward functions; the goal is to maximize a concave function f(J^π) of the normalized return vector J^π ∈ [0,1/(1−γ)]ᴹ. Under a differentiable policy class and standard assumptions (bounded score functions, positive definite Fisher matrix), the gradient ∇θ f(J^πθ) decomposes into a sum over objectives: ∂f/∂Jₘ · ∇θ Jₘ^π. Each ∇θ Jₘ^π is expressed as an expectation of the advantage function and the score function, enabling sampling. The core difficulty is that f is nonlinear: plugging an empirical return estimate ̂J into ∂f yields a biased gradient estimator, with first‑order bias O(1/√B) for batch size B. To eliminate this bias, the authors develop two algorithms. MLMC–NPG draws a geometric random level Q and constructs a telescoping estimator that combines partial gradients at nested batch sizes; its expected estimator equals that of a full batch of size B_max while requiring only O(log B_max) trajectories. Alternatively, if f is twice differentiable with Lipschitz Hessian, the first‑order bias cancels automatically (since 𝔼[̂J]=J^π), reducing bias to O(1/B) and letting vanilla NPG with a constant batch size achieve optimal O(ε⁻²). Both methods use an NPG inner loop to approximately solve a quadratic subproblem with additional samples, and the sample‑complexity analysis accounts for horizon truncation, inner‑loop iterations, and step‑size tuning.

## Key Results
- Optimal sample complexity O(ε⁻²) for ε‑optimal policy computation in concave MORL under policy‑gradient methods, matching the single‑objective lower bound.
- MLMC–NPG achieves this rate with only logarithmic overhead relative to the maximum batch size, while vanilla NPG with second‑order smooth f attains the same rate without MLMC.
- The bias from nonlinear scalarization is precisely quantified: it scales as O(1/√B) under first‑order smoothness and is reduced to O(1/B) under second‑order smoothness or by using MLMC.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Nonlinear Scalarization Gradient Bias
When f is nonlinear (non‑affine) and has Lipschitz continuous partial derivatives, the plug‑in gradient estimator that substitutes an empirical return estimate ̂J for the true J^π becomes biased. The reason is the non‑interchangeability of expectation and differentiation: 𝔼[∂f(̂J)] ≠ ∂f(𝔼[̂J]). Although ̂J is unbiased (𝔼[̂J]=J^π), the nonlinearity mixes the components, leading to a first‑order bias of order O(1/√B) with batch size B. This bias forces large batches and degrades sample complexity to O(ε⁻⁴). The bias vanishes if f is linear (or if ∂f is constant, e.g., fixed linear weighting).

### Mechanism 2: MLMC Bias‑Variance Trade‑off
Multi‑Level Monte Carlo (MLMC) builds a telescoping gradient estimator. For a maximum batch size B_max, a random level Q ∼ Geometric(1/2) is drawn, and the estimate is ∂f(̂J₁) + 𝟙_{2^Q ≤ B_max} 2^Q (∂f(̂J_{2^Q}) - ∂f(̂J_{2^Q−1})). The nested construction ensures that the expected estimator equals the plug‑in estimator with batch size B_max, yet the expected number of trajectories is only O(log B_max) because the geometrically distributed level favors small batches. Hence the bias behaves as O(1/√B_max) while expected cost grows logarithmically. Coupled with NPG (with scaled step size and other parameters), this yields optimal O(ε⁻²) sample complexity.

### Mechanism 3: Second‑Order Smoothness Auto‑Cancellation
If f is twice differentiable with Lipschitz continuous second derivatives (∇∂f is Lipschitz), the first‑order bias cancels automatically when using an unbiased return estimator. Expanding ∂f(̂J) around J^π gives: ∂f(̂J) - ∂f(J^π) ≈ (∇∂f(J^π))ᵀ (̂J - J^π) + O(‖̂J - J^π‖²). Because 𝔼[̂J - J^π]=0, the linear term vanishes in expectation, leaving a residual bias of order O(𝔼‖̂J - J^π‖²)=O(1/B). This smaller bias allows standard (non‑MLMC) NPG to use a constant batch size (independent of ε) and still achieve O(ε⁻²) sample complexity, at the cost of the stronger smoothness assumption.

## Foundational Learning
- **Policy Gradient Theorem**  
  *Why needed*: The gradient ∇θ f(J^πθ) is decomposed via the chain rule into ∑ₘ (∂f/∂Jₘ) ∇θ Jₘ^π, with each ∇θ Jₘ^π expressed as an expectation of the advantage function Aₘ^π(s,a) times the score function ∇θ log πθ(a|s), enabling sampling‑based estimation.  
  *Quick check*: How does the policy gradient theorem transform the intractable gradient over returns into an expectation over state‑action pairs?

- **Natural Policy Gradient (NPG) and Fisher Information**  
  *Why needed*: NPG preconditioning uses the Fisher information matrix F(θ) to define a search direction that respects policy geometry. The update ω is obtained by solving a quadratic subproblem that minimizes the squared error between the expected natural gradient and an empirical gradient estimate g, effectively providing a KL‑constrained step.  
  *Quick check*: Why does NPG use the Fisher information matrix, and how does it affect the step size interpretation?

- **Bias‑Variance Trade‑off in Monte Carlo Estimation**  
  *Why needed*: The gradient bias from nonlinear scalarization forces large batch sizes, which increase the variance of the estimate quadratically. MLMC manipulates this trade‑off by using many small‑batch estimates and a random level to control bias with sublinear expected cost, effectively simulating a large‑batch estimator at a fraction of the sampling expense.  
  *Quick check*: If a gradient estimator has bias O(1/√B) and variance O(1/B), what happens to the mean‑squared error as B increases, and why is MLMC able to improve this?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Policy network πθ → Trajectory generation → Return estimator ̂J_H (with optional MLMC nesting) → Scalarization gradient ∂f(̂J) → Advantage estimator Â_m(s,a) → Fisher matrix estimator F̂ → NPG inner loop (solve quadratic: min_ω ½ ωᵀ F̂ ω - ωᵀ g) → Policy update: θ ← θ + α ω

### Critical Path
1. Sample trajectories from the current policy πθ.
2. Compute (MLMC or empirical) return estimates bJ and the scalarization gradient d = ∇f(bJ).
3. Run N inner iterations of the NPG sub‑solver: for each iteration, sample new trajectories, estimate gradient g_i (using d and sampled advantages) and Fisher matrix bF_i, then perform an SGD update on ω.
4. Set θ ← θ + αω (the solved ω from the inner loop).
5. Repeat until convergence or maximum outer iterations.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **MLMC vs. Vanilla**: Vanilla NPG is simpler but requires large batches Θ(1/ε²) under only first‑order smoothness, leading to O(ε⁻⁴) complexity. MLMC introduces randomness and nested estimators but reduces expected trajectories to O(log(1/ε)) per outer iteration, achieving optimal total complexity. The choice hinges on whether f is twice smooth.
- **Horizon H**: Must satisfy truncation error γᴴ ≤ ε²; longer H increases per‑trajectory cost but reduces bias. A typical choice is H = Θ(log(1/ε) / -log γ).
- **Inner iterations N**: Must scale as O(log(1/ε)) to keep sub‑solver bias negligible; otherwise overall rate degrades.
- **Step size α**: For MLMC, α must shrink as O(ε log(1/ε)); for smooth f, α can be constant. Too large α causes divergence; too small slows progress.

### Failure Signatures
- **Divergence / NaN**: α too large relative to Fisher lower bound µ. Mitigate by reducing α or adding entropy regularization.
- **No progress**: Inner loop under‑solved (N too small or inner batch too small). Monitor the quadratic loss; increase N or inner batch if needed.
- **Bias not decaying as predicted**: For empirical (non‑MLMC) with Lipschitz f, bias should scale 1/√B; verify by measuring bias with fixed θ. For MLMC, expected number of trajectories should grow logarithmically with B_max; check the geometric sampler implementation.
- **Policy collapse**: Some action probabilities approach zero, violating the Fisher lower bound µ. Track minπ(a|s); consider clipping probabilities or higher temperature.
- **Truncation error dominates**: Compare finite‑H returns to exact infinite‑horizon returns; if discrepancy > ε, increase H.

### First Experiments
1. **Bias decay validation**: With fixed policy, compute gradient estimates for varying batch sizes B. Plot ∥∇θ f(J^π) - 𝔼[∇̂f]∥ vs. B. Expect Θ(1/√B) for non‑smooth f and Θ(1/B) for smooth f or when using MLMC (with B_max).
2. **MLMC expected cost**: For increasing B_max, measure expected trajectories per MLMC gradient estimate. Cost should grow approximately as 2 log₂ B_max, not linearly.
3. **Sample complexity verification**: For a small tabular two‑objective MDP with known optimal policy, run MLMC‑NPG and vanilla NPG (smooth f) for decreasing ε. Plot total trajectories vs. 1/ε on a log‑log scale; slope ≈ -2 for both. Additionally run vanilla NPG with non‑smooth f to observe slope ≈ -4.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Basis**: The analysis is restricted to tabular MDPs; extension to function approximation (e.g., linear or neural policies) is not addressed.
- **Why unresolved**: Function approximation introduces representation error that may interact with gradient bias in non‑trivial ways, and the existing techniques rely on exact Fisher matrix bounds specific to tabular settings.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A theoretical analysis showing the same O(ε⁻²) rate under a realistic function‑approximation assumption (e.g., bounded linear features) or a counterexample demonstrating an inherent barrier.

### Open Question 2
- **Basis**: Theorem 2 requires second‑order smoothness of f to obtain bias O(1/B); without it, bias is O(1/√B) and MLMC is needed. The necessity of this assumption is not investigated.
- **Why unresolved**: It is unknown whether alternative estimators or a refined analysis could achieve O(ε⁻²) under weaker conditions.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Proving a lower bound that any algorithm using empirical returns and only first‑order smooth f must suffer Ω(ε⁻⁴) sample complexity would establish necessity. Conversely, designing an algorithm with O(ε⁻²) under weaker conditions would disprove it.

### Open Question 3
- **Basis**: The paper focuses exclusively on concave scalarization functions; non‑concave objectives (e.g., satisficing, lexicographic preferences) are not considered.
- **Why unresolved**: Concavity is crucial for the performance‑difference lemma and smoothness arguments; non‑concave objectives may lead to fundamentally different bias‑variance trade‑offs and could be harder to optimize.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Developing a theoretical framework for policy gradient methods with non‑concave scalarization under additional structure (e.g., smoothness, restricted domain) and deriving achievable rates, or proving hardness results that show a separation from the concave case.

## Limitations
- The theoretical guarantees rely on a positive lower bound µ on the Fisher information, which may vanish as the policy becomes deterministic (action probabilities → 0), causing step‑size collapse or instability.
- Constants in the analysis (L_J, G₂, etc.) are problem‑specific and often overly conservative, leading to extremely small step sizes, very long horizons, or excessive inner iterations in practice.
- The inner NPG sub‑solver must converge within a prescribed number of inner steps N; failure to do so introduces bias not captured by the theory, and the required N depends on unknown variance and condition numbers.
- Several secondary assumptions are fragile: the horizon H must satisfy γᴴ ≤ ε²; the scalarization function must be twice differentiable for the vanilla optimal rate; MLMC requires an exact geometric distribution for the level Q. Violations of any can degrade sample complexity or break guarantees.

## Confidence
- Nonlinear scalarization gradient bias mechanism: **High**
- MLMC bias‑variance trade‑off: **Medium**
- Second‑order smoothness auto‑cancellation: **High**
- Overall O(ε⁻²) sample complexity guarantee: **Medium**
