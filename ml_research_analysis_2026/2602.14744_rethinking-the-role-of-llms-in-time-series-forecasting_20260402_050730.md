# Rethinking the Role of LLMs in Time Series Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14744  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14744  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Pre-alignment (frozen LLM) significantly outperforms post-alignment in >90% of tasks; full-parameter fine-tuning of an intact LLM is crucial for mixed-domain generalization.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a large-scale empirical study reevaluating the use of large language models (LLMs) for time series forecasting (TSF). The authors systematically compare two alignment strategies—pre-alignment (mapping time series to textual embeddings before a frozen LLM) and post-alignment (jointly fine-tuning the LLM)—across 8 billion observations and 17 forecasting scenarios, both in-domain and out-of-domain. The results show that LLM4TSF improves performance, especially under distribution shifts and cross-domain tasks, due to complementary effects: pretrained knowledge handles shifting, while the Transformer architecture captures complex temporal dynamics. Token-level routing analysis confirms that the model adaptively engages the LLM for tokens with high shifting or transition complexity. Under large-scale, mixed-distribution training, a fully intact LLM with full-parameter fine-tuning becomes indispensable, challenging prior negative assessments.

## Method Summary
The core methodology involves two pipelines for integrating LLMs into TSF. **Pre-alignment** uses a time series encoder to patch and embed the input, then aligns these embeddings to the LLM’s word embedding space via PCA and multi-head attention while keeping the LLM frozen. **Post-alignment** directly concatenates time series embeddings with textual prompt embeddings and fine-tunes the entire LLM. Both pipelines optionally include a router that decides per token whether to process it through the LLM. The TS encoder applies channel-independent normalization (RevIN) and an MLP projection. The LLM backbone (e.g., GPT-2) processes the aligned sequence, and a TS decoder maps the final representation to the forecast horizon. The study evaluates these approaches on 17 benchmark datasets, including cross-domain and distribution shift settings, and uses synthetic data experiments to isolate the contributions of pretrained knowledge and architectural capacity. Token routing analysis and ablations (e.g., removing pretraining, truncating layers, using LoRA) further dissect the mechanisms.

## Key Results
- Pre-alignment outperforms post-alignment in over 90% of forecasting tasks.
- LLM4TSF gains are most pronounced under distribution shifts and cross-domain generalization, driven by complementary roles: pretrained knowledge addresses shifting, while the Transformer architecture excels at modeling complex transitions.
- Under large-scale, mixed-distribution training, a fully intact LLM with full-parameter fine-tuning is essential; partial fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA) or architectural truncation leads to significant performance degradation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Pre-alignment preserves pretrained knowledge
Pre-alignment keeps the LLM parameters frozen and aligns time series embeddings to the textual space via a fixed projection (PCA + attention). This prevents gradient updates from distorting the rich semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining, which is crucial for most TSF tasks. Evidence: The abstract and Section 3.2 show pre-alignment achieving lower errors on 10 datasets under both single- and cross-dataset learning. Break condition: On datasets with very low shifting (e.g., Traffic) the relative gain shrinks, and on extremely high-frequency or noisy series the alignment may fail to produce meaningful embeddings.

### Mechanism 2: Complementary roles of pretrained knowledge and architecture
Pretrained parameters encode general temporal common sense that helps when test distributions differ from training (shifting). The Transformer’s self-attention naturally models long-range dependencies and abrupt state changes (high transition). These factors are orthogonal and multiply effective across scenarios. Evidence: Section 5.1 demonstrates that models with pretrained LLM parameters outperform those without specifically on high-shifting datasets; synthetic experiments (Fig. 6) show advantages increase with shifting/transition; token routing (Table 3, Fig. 7) reveals tokens with high shifting/transition are more likely routed through the LLM. Break condition: On simple univariate series with stable patterns (low shifting, low transition), the complementary effects vanish and simpler models may suffice.

### Mechanism 3: Full intact LLM necessary at scale
When training on large-scale, mixed-distribution data, all layers of the LLM contribute hierarchical abstractions; discarding any layer loses crucial information. Full-parameter fine-tuning allows the entire pretrained network to specialize for the diverse patterns, whereas parameter-efficient methods (e.g., LoRA) or truncation limit capacity and degrade performance. Evidence: Section 5.3 (RQ4) shows truncating GPT-2 to half its layers increases MSE on both in-domain and out-of-domain tests (Fig. 15); full fine-tuning outperforms LoRA and PE+LN (Table 12, Fig. 10). Break condition: In low-data regimes, full fine-tuning may overfit or be computationally prohibitive, so partial methods remain useful despite lower performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input TS -> TS Encoder -> Alignment Module (pre-alignment: PCA+attention; post-alignment: concatenation) -> (optional Router) -> LLM Backbone -> TS Decoder -> Forecast

**Critical path:**  
1. Input time series (length L) is patched into N non-overlapping segments and normalized (RevIN) by the TS encoder.  
2. Each patch is projected to an M-dimensional embedding, forming X_enc ∈ ℝ^{N×M}.  
3. Alignment:  
   - Pre-alignment: PCA reduces the LLM’s word embeddings to a small set; attention (Q=X_enc, K=PCA(D), V=PCA(D)) aligns TS embeddings to textual space.  
   - Post-alignment: The TS embeddings are concatenated with tokenized prompt embeddings.  
4. Router (if used) produces a binary pass/skip decision for each token via Gumbel-Softmax during training and hard argmax at inference.  
5. Tokens marked “pass” are fed into the LLM backbone and processed through all layers to produce hidden states H_pass.  
6. Skipped tokens are reinserted into their original positions (or pooled and combined) to reconstruct a full-length representation.  
7. The merged representation is passed through the TS decoder (an MLP) to generate the forecast ˆX_{L+1:L+H}.  
8. Loss (MAE/MSE) is computed and backpropagated through all trainable components.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Pre-alignment vs post-alignment: Pre-alignment is parameter-efficient and preserves pretrained knowledge but may be less expressive for domain-specific patterns; post-alignment allows adaptation but risks catastrophic forgetting.  
- Full LLM vs truncated: Full model provides hierarchical abstraction; truncation reduces compute but discards higher-level features, harming performance on complex datasets.  
- Full fine-tuning vs LoRA/PE+LN: Full fine-tuning maximizes capacity but is costly; LoRA saves memory but may underfit large-scale mixed data (paper shows LoRA underperforms full).  
- Routing vs no routing: Routing skips LLM processing for irrelevant tokens, reducing compute; however, the router may misroute if not properly regularized, especially with limited data.  
- Cross-dataset vs single-dataset training: Cross-dataset improves generalization to unseen domains but risks negative transfer if domains are too dissimilar.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Sudden performance drop on out-of-domain test sets or datasets with high shifting/transition indicates ineffective use of LLM.  
- Router outputs near-uniform (≈50% pass) or extreme bias (>90% skip) suggest training instability or lack of learned criteria.  
- Training divergence or severe overfitting with post-alignment on small datasets (e.g., ETTm1) indicates destructive updates to pretrained representations.  
- Truncated LLM consistently underperforms (e.g., +5–10% MAE) signals loss of abstraction capacity.  
- Removing textual prompts degrades out-of-domain performance, highlighting the importance of semantic guidance.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Comparison:** Implement pre-alignment and post-alignment using GPT-2 as backbone. Train on a single dataset (e.g., ETTh1) with look-back 512 and horizon 96. Record MAE/MSE; expect pre-alignment to outperform, confirming the core finding.  
2. **Ablation of Pretraining and Architecture:** Train three models on a diverse mix (e.g., all DA datasets): (a) full pretrained LLM, (b) same architecture with LLM weights randomly initialized, (c) no LLM component (encoder-decoder only). Evaluate on in-domain (ETTh2) and out-of-domain (Solar) to isolate contributions of pretrained knowledge vs. architectural capacity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Unknown: Whether the superiority of pre-alignment over post-alignment holds for LLMs pretrained on diverse modalities or for non-Transformer architectures.
- Unknown: The exact scaling laws governing the transition from parameter-efficient to full-parameter fine-tuning as the diversity and volume of training data increase.
- Unknown: Whether the token-level router can be replaced by a more interpretable or computationally cheaper decision rule without sacrificing performance.

## Limitations
- The findings are based on 17 standard forecasting datasets and specific LLM backbones (GPT-2, Qwen-3); generalization to high-frequency, extremely noisy, or non-stationary real-world data (e.g., IoT sensor streams) remains unvalidated.  
- The conclusion that full-parameter fine-tuning of an intact LLM is necessary under large-scale mixed training is computationally prohibitive for most practitioners; the scaling threshold and risks of negative transfer with highly heterogeneous sources are not precisely quantified.  
- The study focuses on Transformer-based LLMs; results may not transfer to emerging non-Transformer architectures (e.g., Mamba) or to data mixes with extreme domain diversity.

## Confidence
- Pre-alignment consistently outperforms post-alignment: **High** confidence (supported by >90% of tasks, clear mechanistic explanation).  
- Complementary contributions of pretrained knowledge and architecture: **Medium** confidence (evidence from synthetic data and routing is compelling but relies on specific definitions of “shifting” and “transition”).  
- Full intact LLM with full fine-tuning is essential under large-scale mixed training: **Medium-Low** confidence (results are specific to experimental scale and model families; contradicts predominant corpus trend toward parameter-efficient methods, requiring broader replication).

## Next Checks
1. **Stress-Test on Extreme Modalities:** Train and evaluate the pre-alignment pipeline on high-frequency (e.g., millisecond financial ticks) and highly noisy synthetic time series with no semantic analog in text corpora. Goal: Quantify the breakpoint where alignment fails and performance collapses relative to specialized non-LLM TS models.  
2. **Cross-Architecture Replication of Full-LLM Necessity:** Replicate the “full vs. truncated” and “full vs. LoRA” ablations using a modern instruction-tuned LLM from a different family (e.g., Meta’s Llama 3 8B). Goal: Determine if the necessity of a full, fully-tuned LLM is universal or an artifact of GPT-2’s architecture/training.  
3. **Large-Scale Negative Transfer Audit:** Train a single LLM4TSF model on a very large, intentionally diverse corpus combining all major benchmark datasets (e.g., Monash repository) plus additional heterogeneous domains (healthcare, network traffic). Evaluate per-domain in-domain error and cross-domain transfer to a held-out “extreme” domain (e.g., EEG). Goal: Identify the point where increasing data diversity harms specific domains, establishing practical limits to the “mixed-distribution” advantage.
