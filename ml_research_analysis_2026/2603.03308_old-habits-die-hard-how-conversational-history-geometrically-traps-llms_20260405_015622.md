# Old Habits Die Hard: How Conversational History Geometrically Traps LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03308
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03308
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Conversational history induces carryover effects in LLM behaviors, quantified by a trace metric and geometric angular separation, with a strong Spearman correlation (ρ=0.78) across models and datasets.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the history of a conversation influences subsequent responses from large language models (LLMs), leading to persistent behavioral patterns known as carryover effects. The authors introduce the HISTORY-ECHOES framework, which captures carryover through two complementary lenses: a probabilistic Markov chain model measuring state consistency (trace >1) and a geometric analysis measuring angular separation between phenomenon-related hidden representations. Across three model families and six datasets, they find a strong correlation (ρ=0.78) between these metrics, indicating that carryover manifests as a geometric trap in latent space. Refusal shows the strongest carryover, sycophancy moderate, and hallucination weakest; importantly, the effect dissolves when conversation topics become incoherent, highlighting the role of topical continuity.

## Method Summary
The framework constructs multi-turn conversations in two settings: *Dconsistent* (questions sorted by semantic similarity using embeddings) and *Dinconsistent* (randomly shuffled). For each turn, the model's response is labeled as exhibiting the target phenomenon (hallucination, refusal, sycophancy) or not. Probabilistically, a first-order Markov chain over binary states is built; the trace of its transition matrix, Tr(T) = P(s⁺|s⁺) + P(s⁻|s⁻), quantifies persistence (values >1 indicate carryover). Geometrically, the first-token hidden state at 85% network depth is extracted. From a held-out basis set, mean vectors for phenomenon-present and -absent states are computed and orthogonalized to form a 2D basis. All states are projected; the angle θ_ref between the mean projected vectors measures geometric separation. Transition rotation angles are compared to θ_ref to assess incomplete rotations. Finally, Tr(T) and θ_ref are correlated across model-dataset pairs.

## Key Results
- Tr(T) and θ_ref exhibit a strong Spearman correlation (ρ=0.78) across coherent conversations, confirming that probabilistic persistence and geometric separation are two faces of the same carryover phenomenon.
- Among phenomena, refusal displays the highest carryover (largest Tr(T) and θ_ref), sycophancy is moderate, and hallucination is weakest, suggesting differences in representation coherence.
- The correlation vanishes and carryover weakens when conversation topics are inconsistent, demonstrating that topical continuity is essential for the geometric trap to form.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Markovian state persistence
**Claim**: Conversational history induces carryover because the model's state transitions exhibit high self-loop probabilities, quantified by the trace of a first-order Markov chain transition matrix (Tr(T) > 1).  
**Mechanism**: The response generation is modeled as a stochastic process over binary states (phenomenon present/absent). If prior behavior influences future responses, diagonal probabilities P(s→s) exceed off-diagonals, making Tr(T) = P(s⁺|s⁺) + P(s⁻|s⁻) > 1. A higher trace indicates slower mixing and stronger persistence.  
**Core assumption**: Binary state labeling accurately captures the target phenomenon across turns.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Section 3 derive the 2×2 matrix and show Tr(T) > 1 consistently; Table 1.  
**Break condition**: Under topic incoherence, Tr(T) approaches 1 (Table 6).

### Mechanism 2: Geometric trap in latent space
**Claim**: Carryover manifests geometrically: latent representations of phenomenon states are separated by a large reference angle (θ_ref), so transitions between states are incomplete rotations, leaving the representation influenced by its prior state.  
**Mechanism**: An orthogonal basis is built from mean hidden states of phenomenon-absent (ϕ⁻) and phenomenon-present (ϕ⁺) responses. The angle between these basis vectors (θ_ref) measures geometric separation. For a state transition, the actual rotation angle is often smaller than θ_ref, indicating incomplete rotation.  
**Core assumption**: The first-token hidden state at a consistent relative depth (85%) captures the behavioral state, and the two-state subspace is well-defined.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Section 4 construct the basis and compute angles; Tables 2 and 3 show θ_ref largest for refusal and transition angles often < θ_ref.  
**Break condition**: Incoherent conversations yield similar θ_ref but larger rotation angles (Table 7), reducing the trap.

### Mechanism 3: Correlation of probabilistic and geometric metrics
**Claim**: The probabilistic and geometric perspectives are strongly correlated (Spearman ρ = 0.78), meaning that higher Markov trace corresponds to larger angular separation. This reveals that carryover is both a stochastic and structural property of the latent space.  
**Mechanism**: For each model–dataset pair, compute Tr(T) and θ_ref. The positive correlation indicates that when the model is probabilistically sticky (high trace), its representations are geometrically segregated (large θ_ref), confining future trajectories.  
**Core assumption**: The two metrics measure aspects of the same underlying carryover mechanism.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Section 5 report the correlation; Figure 2 shows clusters by phenomenon type.  
**Break condition**: The correlation vanishes under topic inconsistency (Figure 3), implying the trap depends on coherent context.

## Foundational Learning
- **Markov chains for state modeling**: Needed to quantify whether prior behavioral state influences the next state, moving beyond marginal probabilities. *Quick check*: Given P(s⁺|s⁺)=0.9, P(s⁻|s⁻)=0.8, Tr(T) = 1.7 > 1 → carryover exists.
- **Linear algebra in latent spaces**: Used to define orthogonal bases from hidden states and measure angular separation as a geometric signature of state separation. *Quick check*: If two mean hidden vectors are collinear, θ_ref = 0° (or 180°), implying no geometric separation (states indistinguishable).
- **LLM hidden representations and layer depth**: The geometric analysis extracts activations at a specific layer (85% depth), assuming this level optimally encodes behavioral concepts. *Quick check*: Upper-middle layers are more suitable because they integrate both input processing and output prediction, capturing high-level semantics rather than low-level tokens or final logits.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data simulator -> Behavior annotator -> Probabilistic engine -> Geometric engine -> Correlator

### Critical Path
Data curation → conversation generation → response collection → labeling → probabilistic trace calculation → hidden state extraction → geometric angle calculation → correlation analysis. Each step depends on the previous; labeling errors propagate to both metrics.

### Design Tradeoffs
- *Black-box vs white-box*: Probabilistic metric works with API-only models; geometric requires access to hidden states.
- *Conversation design*: Coherent topics maximize carryover effects but may not reflect real dialogues; shuffling tests robustness but reduces ecological validity.
- *Phenomenon definition*: Binary labeling simplifies analysis but may overlook nuance (e.g., hallucination subtypes).

### Failure Signatures
- Low Tr(T) (~1) and small θ_ref → weak carryover (expected for diverse hallucinations).
- High θ_ref but low Tr(T) under inconsistency → geometric separation without behavioral persistence.
- Mismatch between open/closed models on Tr(T) could indicate API filtering or decoding differences.

### First 3 Experiments
1. *Baseline correlation*: For all open models and datasets, compute Tr(T) and θ_ref on 20-turn coherent conversations; expect ρ ≈ 0.78.
2. *Topic coherence ablation*: Repeat with shuffled conversations; expect correlation to drop (Figure 3).
3. *Layer sensitivity*: Repeat geometric analysis at 30%, 50%, 85%, 100% depths; confirm peak correlation at 85% (Figure 5).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Higher-order Markov dependencies**: The paper only examines first-order transitions. Extending to higher orders might reveal longer-range carryover effects, but the framework's generalization remains future work.
2. **Causal inference**: The study shows correlation but does not establish causality. Experimental interventions (e.g., artificially flipping prior states while controlling content) would be needed to prove that history causes carryover.
3. **Phenomenon-specific differences**: Why refusal shows strongest carryover, sycophancy moderate, and hallucination weakest? The authors hypothesize that hallucination is a broad umbrella of diverse failures, while refusal is mediated by a single direction. Quantifying subspace properties (dimensionality, orthogonality) for each phenomenon could explain the variation.

## Limitations
- Embedding preprocessing details (e.g., pooling method for semantic sorting) are unspecified; this could affect the composition of coherent conversations.
- The exact mapping of "85% relative depth" to layer indices across different architectures is ambiguous and may require model-specific calibration.
- Closed model specifics (API versions, parameters, and ad-hoc labeling rules) are partially redacted, limiting exact replication.

## Confidence
- Carryover exists (Tr(T) > 1): **High** – Robust across datasets/models in Table 1.
- Geometric trap correlation (ρ=0.78): **Medium** – Strong reported correlation, but basis construction depends on data split and sign orientation; minor implementation variance could affect angles.
- Topic coherence breaks correlation: **Medium** – Ablation clear, but inconsistent conversation quality depends on embedding sorting (Unknown 1).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate Dconsistent construction using different embedding pooling strategies (CLS vs. mean) and measure variance in nearest-neighbor similarity and downstream correlation ρ.
2. Map 85% depth to concrete layer indices for each model (e.g., floor(0.85 * num_layers)) and recompute geometric metrics; verify if correlation peak remains.
3. Re-run topic-coherence ablation with a stricter similarity threshold (e.g., only top-5 nearest neighbors) to test robustness of correlation collapse.
