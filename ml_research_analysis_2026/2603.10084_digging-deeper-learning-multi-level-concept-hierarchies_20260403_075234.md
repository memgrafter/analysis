# Digging Deeper: Learning Multi-Level Concept Hierarchies

## Quick Facts

- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.10084
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10084
- **Reference count:** 18
- **Primary result:** Discovers multi-level concept hierarchies from top-level labels only; achieves ROC‑AUC 0.94 on MNIST‑ADD (matching HiCEMs), while Deep‑HiCEMs preserve task accuracy within 1% and enable interventions at any depth.

## Executive Summary

Existing concept-based models, such as HiCEMs and Concept Splitting, are confined to shallow hierarchies and require exhaustive annotation of intermediate concepts. This work introduces Multi‑Level Concept Splitting (MLCS) and Deep‑HiCEMs to overcome these limitations. MLCS employs a Hierarchical Sparse Autoencoder (HiSAE) to automatically discover sub‑concepts and sub‑sub‑concepts using only top‑level supervision. Deep‑HiCEMs extend the concept bottleneck paradigm to arbitrarily deep hierarchies, permitting interventions at any level. On the MNIST‑ADD dataset, MLCS identifies human‑interpretable concepts with high accuracy (ROC‑AUC 0.94±0.01) and Deep‑HiCEMs maintain competitive task performance (within 1% of HiCEMs). The ability to intervene at multiple abstraction levels opens new avenues for causal reasoning and controllable predictions.

## Method Summary

MLCS begins with a Hierarchical Sparse Autoencoder (HiSAE) that encodes inputs into a sparse latent representation. Through a hierarchical splitting mechanism, latent dimensions are grouped into a tree of parent‑child concepts, trained end‑to‑end with only top‑level task supervision. The learned hierarchy is then fed into Deep‑HiCEMs, a stacked concept bottleneck network that respects the discovered structure and allows information to flow through multiple abstraction layers. At inference, concept values at any level can be manually altered (intervention) to study their effect on the final prediction. The entire system avoids the need for manual annotation of intermediate concepts while preserving interpretability and task accuracy.

## Key Results

- MLCS discovers interpretable sub‑concepts and sub‑sub‑concepts on MNIST‑ADD with mean ROC‑AUC 0.94±0.01, matching or exceeding HiCEMs that rely on ground‑truth concept labels.
- Deep‑HiCEMs achieve task accuracy within 1% of standard HiCEMs, demonstrating that deep hierarchies do not degrade performance.
- Concept interventions at intermediate levels can improve task accuracy and reveal causal relationships between concepts and predictions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

HiSAE leverages sparsity to force latent units to specialize; the hierarchical splitting then aggregates related units into parent and child concepts. Training with only top‑level supervision drives the autoencoder to capture features predictive of the top label, while the hierarchical structure naturally yields a coarse‑to‑fine decomposition. Deep‑HiCEMs integrate this decomposition as a sequence of bottleneck layers, preserving the multi‑granular information flow. Interventions are effective because intervening on a high‑level concept automatically influences its descendants through the learned parent‑child dependencies, providing a controlled way to steer predictions.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs)**  
  *Why needed:* Provide interpretable intermediate representations aligned with human‑defined concepts.  
  *Quick check:* Verify each bottleneck unit correlates with a known concept across a held‑out data sample.

- **Hierarchical Concept Representation**  
  *Why needed:* Many domains have natural taxonomies; modeling hierarchies supports multi‑granular reasoning and compositional generalization.  
  *Quick check:* Check that parent concepts subsume multiple child concepts and that the learned hierarchy matches or closely resembles a known taxonomy.

- **Sparse Autoencoding**  
  *Why needed:* Enables learning disentangled features without supervision; sparsity promotes specialization and reduces redundancy.  
  *Quick check:* Measure activation sparsity (e.g., fraction of zeros) on a validation set; it should be high but not degenerate.

- **Top‑Level Supervision Only**  
  *Why needed:* Reduces annotation cost by requiring only coarse labels instead of exhaustive concept annotations.  
  *Quick check:* Compare model performance when provided with full concept labels versus only top‑level labels; performance should be comparable.

- **Concept Intervenability**  
  *Why needed:* Allows testing causal relationships, debugging model behavior, and enforcing fairness or robustness constraints.  
  *Quick check:* Intervene on a validated concept and observe the expected change in the outcome according to domain knowledge.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Raw Input -> HiSAE Encoder -> Sparse Latent -> Hierarchical Splitting Module -> Multi‑Level Concept Tree (Level0 → Level1 → ...) -> Deep‑HiCEMs Predictor -> Task Output  
Intervention Module can inject values into any Concept Node.

**Critical path:**  
1. HiSAE encodes input into a sparse latent code.  
2. Hierarchical splitting assigns latent dimensions to a tree of concepts.  
3. Concepts are sequentially processed through Deep‑HiCEMs layers respecting the hierarchy.  
4. Final linear head produces the task prediction.  
Interventions may occur after step 2, before the Deep‑HiCEMs flow.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Depth vs. granularity: Deeper hierarchies yield finer interpretability but increase computational cost and risk of over‑splitting.  
- Sparsity strength: Stronger sparsity encourages disentanglement but may harm reconstruction quality and downstream accuracy.  
- Intervention flexibility: Allowing interventions at any level offers greater control but can break learned dependencies if misapplied.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low concept prediction accuracy (near chance) indicates a failed hierarchy discovery.  
- Task accuracy drop >1% relative to a non‑hierarchical baseline suggests the hierarchy is detrimental.  
- Interventions produce no effect or erratic changes, misaligned concepts.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Replication on MNIST‑ADD:** Train MLCS and evaluate discovered concept accuracy against ground‑truth sub‑concepts using ROC‑AUC.  
2. **Intervention impact:** Manually set concepts at each level to extreme values; measure changes in task accuracy and output distribution to verify expected causal effects.  
3. **Ablation on sparsity:** Vary the sparsity penalty in HiSAE and assess trade‑offs between concept interpretability (e.g., mutual information with known features) and task accuracy.

## Limitations

- Evaluation is restricted to a single synthetic dataset (MNIST‑ADD); generalizability to natural images or complex domains remains untested.  
- Human‑interpretability of discovered concepts is asserted but not quantitatively validated (no user studies or standardized metrics).  
- Robustness to noise in top‑level labels or missing supervisions is not examined; real‑world data often contain such imperfections.

## Confidence

- MLCS discovers multi‑level hierarchies from only top‑level supervision: **Medium**  
- Deep‑HiCEMs maintain task accuracy within 1% of HiCEMs while enabling interventions at any level: **Medium**  
- Achieved ROC‑AUC ≈0.94 on MNIST‑ADD, matching HiCEMs: **High**  
- Discovered concepts are human‑interpretable: **Low**

## Next Checks

- Test MLCS and Deep‑HiCEMs on multiple real‑world hierarchical benchmarks (e.g., CIFAR‑100 with superclass labels, ImageNet with WordNet) to assess generalization and performance consistency.
- Conduct a controlled user study measuring interpretability of automatically discovered concepts, using metrics like concept similarity ratings or explanation quality.
- Perform systematic ablations on HiSAE design (sparsity constraints, hierarchical depth) and evaluate robustness under varying levels of label noise and missing top‑level annotations.
