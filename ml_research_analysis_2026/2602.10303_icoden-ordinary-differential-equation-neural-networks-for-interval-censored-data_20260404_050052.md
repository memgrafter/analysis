# ICODEN: Ordinary Differential Equation Neural Networks for Interval-Censored Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10303  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10303  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** ICODEN outperforms Cox-PH and adaptive LASSO in interval-censored survival prediction with high-dimensional SNPs, achieving lower MSE/IBS while avoiding proportional hazards.

## Executive Summary
Interval-censored time-to-event prediction with thousands of genetic predictors poses severe statistical and computational challenges, largely due to restrictive assumptions like proportional hazards. ICODEN introduces a neural ODE framework that directly models the hazard function λ(t|X) as a flexible function of time, covariates, and the cumulative hazard itself. The cumulative hazard Λ(t|X) is obtained by solving the ordinary differential equation dΛ/dt = λ with Λ(0)=0, ensuring internal consistency. This approach eliminates the need for proportional hazards or parametric forms and efficiently handles left truncation and high dimensionality via GPU-accelerated ODE solvers.

In comprehensive simulations and two large real-world genomic studies (ADNI and AREDS/AREDS2), ICODEN consistently achieves lower prediction errors (MSE, Integrated Brier Score) than Cox-PH and adaptive LASSO, particularly when the number of predictors is large (e.g., 1,497 SNPs) where traditional methods break down. The model also uncovers meaningful subgroups with distinct risk trajectories, validated by known genetic markers such as APOE. These results demonstrate that ICODEN provides a scalable and assumption-light solution for modern interval-censored survival analysis.

## Method Summary
ICODEN models the hazard λ(t|X) using a fully connected neural network with ReLU hidden layers and a Softplus output to guarantee positivity. The network takes as input the current cumulative hazard Λ(t|X), time t, and covariates X. For a given individual, the cumulative hazard Λ(t|X) is computed by solving the ODE dΛ/dt = λ backward from t=0 to the observation endpoint. The likelihood for an interval-censored observation (L,R] with optional left truncation V is proportional to exp(-Λ(L)) - exp(-Λ(R)), and when V is present it is divided by exp(-Λ(V)). Training minimizes the negative log-likelihood plus an L1 penalty (α) for sparsity, using Adam optimization. Gradients through the ODE are obtained via the adjoint method, and a time‑rescaling trick (scaling each individual’s time to [0,1]) enables efficient GPU batch solving. Hyperparameters (hidden size, learning rate, α, batch size, epochs) are tuned via cross‑validation.

## Key Results
- In simulated settings with non‑proportional or nonlinear hazards, ICODEN yields lower mean squared error than Cox‑PH (IcenReg) and adaptive LASSO, especially when the predictor count reaches 300 where adaptive LASSO fails.
- On ADNI (1,740 subjects, up to 623 SNPs) and AREDS/AREDS2 (7,803 eyes, up to 1,497 SNPs), ICODEN attains a lower Integrated Brier Score and comparable or better median survival time errors (dout) than Cox‑PH models, which degrade as SNP count increases.
- The method enables data‑driven subgroup identification; clusters based on predicted cumulative hazard show distinct survival curves consistent with Turnbull estimates and known genetic risk factors (e.g., APOE).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ICODEN succeeds because it tightly couples a flexible hazard estimator with an exact ODE integration, guaranteeing that λ and Λ are mathematically consistent—something discrete approximations struggle with. The neural network can capture complex, time‑varying, and non‑proportional effects of high‑dimensional covariates. The likelihood explicitly accounts for interval censoring and left truncation, providing unbiased inference under the stated model. Moreover, the ODE approach leverages efficient GPU solvers and the time‑rescaling trick, making computation feasible even with thousands of predictors and large sample sizes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Neural hazard modeling**:  
  *Why needed?* To estimate λ(t|X) without imposing proportional hazards or parametric forms.  
  *Quick check:* Verify all network outputs are positive via the Softplus activation; also test monotonicity in t for a fixed X.
- **ODE‑based cumulative hazard**:  
  *Why needed?* Ensures dΛ/dt = λ, so Λ is a proper integral of λ and avoids numerical integration errors.  
  *Quick check:* Λ(0) should be exactly 0; plot Λ(t) for a few individuals to confirm monotonic increase.
- **Interval‑censored likelihood**:  
  *Why needed?* Correctly handles the fact that the event time is only known to lie within (L,R].  
  *Quick check:* On simulated data with known λ, the log‑likelihood should be smooth and concave near the true parameters.
- **Time‑rescaling trick**:  
  *Why needed?* Scales each individual’s time to [0,1] so that ODE solvers can batch many trajectories on GPU without stiffness issues.  
  *Quick check:* After unscaling, compare Λ(t) obtained from scaled and unscaled solves; they should match closely.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input (V_i, L_i, R_i, X_i) -> Neural Net f_θ -> λ(t|X) -> ODE solver (dΛ/dt = λ) -> Λ(t|X) -> Likelihood computation -> Loss (negative log‑likelihood + α||θ||₁) -> Adam optimizer -> Updated θ

**Critical path:**  
1. Load a batch of subjects.  
2. For each subject, solve the ODE from t=0 to R_i (and to L_i, and V_i if present) to obtain Λ(R_i), Λ(L_i), Λ(V_i).  
3. Compute the individual log‑likelihood contributions using the interval‑censored formula (with left‑truncation adjustment).  
4. Sum losses over the batch and add L1 penalty.  
5. Backpropagate gradients through the ODE (adjoint method) to the network.  
6. Update network weights with Adam.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *ODE solver choice*: Adaptive (dopri5) offers accuracy but may be slower; fixed‑step (rk4) is faster but requires a fine grid.  
- *Network capacity*: Deeper/wider nets increase flexibility but raise the risk of overfitting and ODE cost.  
- *L1 penalty*: Larger α promotes sparsity in high dimensions but can bias estimates if too high.  
- *Batch size*: Larger batches improve GPU utilization but increase memory usage for storing ODE trajectories.

**Failure signatures:**  
- ODE solver emits warnings (stiffness) or returns NaNs → reduce step size, adjust tolerances, or switch solver.  
- Loss suddenly explodes → learning rate likely too high; lower it.  
- Training loss plateaus at a high value while validation loss also remains high → model capacity insufficient or regularization too strong; increase network size or reduce α.  
- Out‑of‑memory (OOM) errors → shrink batch size, simplify network, or use gradient checkpointing.  
- Validation loss much higher than training loss → overfitting; increase α, apply early stopping, or add dropout (not originally used).

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Solver sanity check**: Set λ(t|X)=constant (e.g., 0.1) and confirm that Λ(t) computed by the ODE equals 0.1·t across a range of t.  
2. **Gradient flow test**: Train ICODEN on a tiny synthetic dataset (e.g., 10 samples) for a few steps; ensure the loss decreases and no NaNs appear in gradients.  
3. **Benchmark vs Cox‑PH**: On a simulated dataset with a known non‑proportional hazard, compare ICODEN’s integrated Brier score to that of a Cox‑PH model (e.g., via `scikit‑survival`) to verify the expected improvement.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter settings for the largest SNP sets (623 in ADNI, 1,497 in AREDS) are not fully disclosed, requiring new tuning.  
- Cross‑validation and test‑set splitting strategies are described only in general terms (e.g., “five‑fold”), leaving exact fold allocations and random seeds unspecified.  
- ODE solver stability might deteriorate with extremely high‑dimensional covariates or very large batch sizes, potentially limiting scalability.

## Confidence
- **ODE framework theoretical soundness**: **High**  
- **Predictive performance gains**: **Medium** (largely because hyperparameters for the largest datasets are unreported)  
- **Subgroup identification utility**: **Low‑Medium** (validation is indirect and clustering details are sparse)
