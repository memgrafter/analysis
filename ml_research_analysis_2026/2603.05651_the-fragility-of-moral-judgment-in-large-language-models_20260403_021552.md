# The Fragility Of Moral Judgment In Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.05651
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05651
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: LLM moral judgments are fragile to presentation changes, with perspective shifts causing 24.3% verdict flips and evaluation protocol dominating outcomes (67.6% cross-protocol agreement, κ=0.55).

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for moral advice, but their stability under changes in how dilemmas are presented is unknown. This study systematically perturbs 2,939 AITA dilemmas via surface edits, perspective shifts, and persuasion manipulations, evaluating four LLMs across varying protocols. Results show that surface changes cause modest flips (7.5%), but point-of-view shifts induce substantial instability (24.3% flips). The evaluation protocol matters more than content: only 35.7% of cases remain consistent across three structured protocols, and unstructured prompts yield a 4.3:1 exoneration bias. Instability concentrates in ambiguous distributed-blame verdicts (No One At Fault, All At Fault), which flip 2.7 times more often than concentrated verdicts. Extended reasoning provides no stability benefit. These findings reveal that LLM moral judgments are co-produced by narrative framing and scaffolding, undermining reproducibility and raising equity concerns in deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors collect 2,939 ethical dilemmas from the r/AmITheAsshole subreddit (January–March 2025). They generate content perturbations using Gemini 2.5 Flash: surface edits (trivial changes), point-of-view shifts (first↔third person), and persuasion manipulations (six types biasing toward self/other blame). Four LLMs (GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, DeepSeek V3, Qwen2.5-72B) evaluate each variant and baseline under multiple protocols: structured versions (verdict-first, explanation-first, system-prompt) and an unstructured open-ended format (mapped post-hoc with a classifier). Baseline test-retest on three runs estimates a noise floor (4–13%). Metrics include verdict flip rates, normalized entropy (self-consistency), Cohen’s κ for cross-protocol agreement, and breakdowns by verdict category. A stratified sample (n≈1,200) is used for protocol comparisons. The analysis identifies that protocol choice and narrative voice drive instability, especially for distributed-blame outcomes.

## Key Results
- Surface perturbations cause 7.5% verdict flips; point-of-view shifts cause 24.3% flips; persuasion manipulations cause 10.8% flips, with directional biases (e.g., social proof increases blame).
- Protocol sensitivity is severe: cross-protocol agreement is 67.6% (κ=0.55), only 35.7% of cases are consistent across all three structured protocols; unstructured prompts yield 55% flips and a 4.3:1 exoneration bias.
- Fragility concentrates in ambiguous distributed-blame verdicts (No One At Fault, All At Fault), which flip 2.7× more often than concentrated verdicts (Self At Fault, Other At Fault).
- Extended reasoning in specialized models (e.g., QwQ-32B) offers no stability gains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Claim 1**: Point-of-view shifts (first-person ↔ third-person) cause high verdict instability because models treat narrative voice as a pragmatic cue that alters inferred social context. The model’s internal representation is sensitive to linguistic framing; depersonalized (third-person) narration reduces empathy or changes perceived agency, redistributing blame toward distributed verdicts. Evidence: Perspective shifts induced 24.3% flips; 37.9% of surface-robust cases flipped under POV changes, showing narrative voice as pragmatic. Break condition: If models reasoned solely about the underlying ethical conflict, POV manipulations would not cause flips beyond the noise floor (4–13%).

**Claim 2**: Evaluation protocol (instruction placement, ordering, structure) dominates outcomes because prompting format acts as moral scaffolding that shapes the verdict. Protocols alter the perceived task; e.g., requiring an explanation before a verdict increases distributed blame judgments, while unstructured prompts trigger supportive advisor behavior and a 4.3:1 exoneration bias. Evidence: Structured-to-unstructured disagreement is 51–55%; verdict-first protocol yields 38.2% Self At Fault rate, explanation-first yields more shared-responsibility judgments. Break condition: Consistent judgments across equivalent moral evidence presented in different prompt formats would indicate minimal protocol sensitivity.

**Claim 3**: Fragility concentrates in ambiguous distributed-blame verdicts (No One At Fault, All At Fault) because they occupy decision boundaries with lower self-consistency. Distributed-blame cases reflect genuine moral ambiguity; models exhibit higher entropy on these scenarios, so perturbations act as tie-breakers, often pushing toward the more frequent Other At Fault outcome. Evidence: No One At Fault flip rate 54.0%, All At Fault 50.6%, versus Self At Fault 29.8% and Other At Fault 8.9%. Break condition: Uniform flip rates across verdict categories would disprove this mechanism.

## Foundational Learning
- **Normalized entropy (NE)** as a self-consistency metric.  
  *Why needed here*: Separates inherent model stochasticity from perturbation-induced flips, allowing identification of fragile cases.  
  *Quick check*: How is NE computed from repeated samples, and why is a split-sample approach used to avoid leakage when correlating with flip rates?

- **Culpability-preserving vs. culpability-reversing flips**.  
  *Why needed here*: Determines whether a perturbation changes the core advice (narrator blamed or not) or merely reallocates blame among similar categories.  
  *Quick check*: What defines a culpability-reversing flip, and why is it more consequential for user-facing advice?

- **Cross-protocol agreement (Cohen’s κ)**.  
  *Why needed here*: Quantifies the impact of evaluation design choices on reported model performance, highlighting reproducibility risks.  
  *Quick check*: Why might κ=0.55 between structured protocols be considered only moderate agreement, and what does a 4.3:1 exoneration bias in unstructured prompts imply about default model behavior?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Data Ingestion → Perturbation Engine → Evaluation Harness → Analysis Module → Verification Module (for reasoning models). Specifically: Reddit API → filtered AITA posts → Gemini 2.5 Flash generates variants → Four LLM APIs evaluate with JSON output → Flip rate, entropy, agreement statistics → Trace extraction + LLM-as-judge annotation.

**Critical path**: Data collection → perturbation generation → baseline & variant evaluations (multiple runs) → metric aggregation → statistical testing.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Gemini for perturbations: cost-effective vs. potential stylistic artifacts; manual validation (94% pass) mitigates.  
- Temperature 0.4: balances diversity in perturbations and consistency in evaluations.  
- Unstructured mapping: LLM classifier used for free-form responses; human validation shows moderate 5-class agreement (κ=0.57) but high binary agreement (κ=0.76).  
- Stratified sampling for protocol study: ensures balanced representation across verdict categories but relies on a single model (GPT-4.1) as stratification anchor.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Low inter-rater reliability in perturbation validation (“kappa paradox”) indicates difficulty in generating perfectly clean variants.  
- High baseline entropy identifies ambiguous scenarios; these drive most instability.  
- Unstructured protocol yields high abstention (21% No Verdict) and exoneration bias, revealing scaffold dependence.  
- Reasoning models (e.g., QwQ-32B) show no stability gains despite long traces; verification presence correlates with lower stability.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Baseline self-consistency: Run 15 repeated evaluations on 200 random AITA posts; compute NE and 3-run agreement. Expect GPT-4.1 NE ~0.04, DeepSeek ~0.16.  
2. Content perturbation flip rates: Apply one surface, one POV, and one persuasion perturbation to the same 200 scenarios; measure flip rates relative to baseline. Expect surface ~7%, POV ~24%, persuasion ~11%.  
3. Protocol sensitivity: Evaluate 100 baseline scenarios under verdict-first, explanation-first, and system-prompt; compute pairwise flip rates and κ. Expect ~22% flips between structured protocols and a 4.3:1 exoneration ratio for unstructured vs. verdict-first.

## Limitations
- Dataset limitation: The 2,939 r/AITA posts from Jan–Mar 2025 limit generalizability; a fresh collection will differ due to post deletions/edits.
- Model version ambiguity: Specifications like GPT-4.1 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet may refer to unreleased snapshots; public equivalents may behave differently.
- Verdict mapping noise: The GPT-4.1-mini classifier for unstructured prompts has only moderate agreement (κ=0.57 for 5-class, κ=0.76 binary), introducing potential measurement error.

## Confidence
- Fragility exists (LLM judgments flip under perturbations): **High**
- Protocol dominates outcomes: **High**
- Distributed-blame verdicts are disproportionately fragile: **High**
- Extended reasoning yields stability gains: **Medium** (only one reasoning model tested)

## Next Checks
1. Cross-source replication: Repeat the study with moral dilemmas from other sources (e.g., trolley problems, other subreddits) to assess generalizability.
2. Public API benchmark: Use current publicly available model endpoints (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) to verify that protocol sensitivity persists for widely accessible models.
3. Perturbation audit: Human-annotate a sample of 500 variants to validate that the perturbations preserve the core moral conflict while manipulating only the intended surface/POV/persuasion elements; correlate human judgments of manipulation success with observed flip rates.
