# Learning with the Nash-Sutcliffe loss
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00968
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00968
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: Training with Nash‑Sutcliffe loss aligns model selection with NSE evaluation, yielding up to 68% test loss reduction for streamflow forecasting.

## Executive Summary
The Nash‑Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) is a widely used metric for evaluating forecasts across multiple time series, yet it lacks a decision‑theoretic foundation. This paper defines the Nash‑Sutcliffe loss \(L_{NS}=1-NSE\) and proves it is strictly consistent for a novel elicitable functional—the data‑weighted component‑wise mean (Nash‑Sutcliffe functional). Maximizing average NSE is equivalent to minimizing expected \(L_{NS}\), but this implicitly assumes all series are independent realizations from the same non‑stationary process. The authors introduce Nash‑Sutcliffe linear regression, which minimizes average \(L_{NS}\) and yields a weighted least squares solution. In simulations and real streamflow/temperature forecasting, training with \(L_{NS}\) dramatically improves NSE‑based performance (e.g., 68% reduction in test loss) compared to standard multi‑dimensional or one‑dimensional regression. The framework provides a sound basis for NSE‑based model selection and clarifies the advantage of global multi‑series models. Practical advice: align training loss with evaluation metric and only average NSE across series that share the same stochastic process.

## Method Summary
The paper’s methodology rests on decision theory: a loss function is strictly consistent for a functional if its expectation is uniquely minimized by that functional. The authors show that \(L_{NS}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}) = \|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2 / \|\mu(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2\) is strictly consistent for the Nash‑Sutcliffe functional \(\mathbb{E}[Y w(Y)]/\mathbb{E}[w(Y)]\), where \(w(\mathbf{y})\) is the inverse squared deviation from the mean. For a linear model \(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}\mathbf{1}^T\) across \(n\) series, minimizing average \(L_{NS}\) leads to a weighted least squares estimator: \(\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}})^{-1}\), where \(\mathbf{W}\) is a diagonal matrix of weights \(w(\mathbf{Y}_{\cdot,j})\) computed from each series’ training sample variance. The approach contrasts with ordinary least squares (which minimizes Euclidean norm loss) and one‑dimensional per‑series regressions. The weight depends only on the training data, so the estimator remains a closed‑form solution. Theoretical results require that all series are independent draws from the same \(d\)-dimensional stochastic process; otherwise averaging NSE becomes incomparable. Empirical validation uses simulated multivariate Gaussian/log‑normal data and real daily streamflow/temperature series from ten French basins, splitting data into training and test sets to compare realized Euclidean norm loss and realized Nash‑Sutcliffe loss.

## Key Results
- Maximizing average NSE is equivalent to minimizing expected \(L_{NS}\), which elicits the Nash‑Sutcliffe functional—not the ordinary component‑wise mean—unless under special conditions (e.g., independent Gaussian).
- Nash‑Sutcliffe functional differs from the component‑wise mean in most practical settings (e.g., heterogeneous variances, non‑Gaussian marginals, dependence), making training with MSE suboptimal for NSE evaluation.
- In simulations and real‑world forecasting, Nash‑Sutcliffe linear regression substantially reduces test \(L_{NS}\) compared to both multi‑dimensional and one‑dimensional OLS: e.g., streamflow test loss 0.1222 vs. 0.3791 (68% reduction) for multi‑dimensional linear regression; temperature test loss 2.25 vs. 2.40 (6% reduction).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper explains three interlinked mechanisms:

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Maximizing average NSE across multiple time series is equivalent to minimizing expected \(L_{NS}\), but this elicits a data‑weighted component‑wise mean rather than the ordinary mean.  
**Core mechanism:** \(L_{NS}\) applies weight \(w(\mathbf{y}) = 1/\|\mu(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2\) to squared error. By Theorem 1 (Gneiting, 2011), this weighted loss is strictly consistent for the functional \(\mathbb{E}[Y w(Y)]/\mathbb{E}[w(Y)]\), i.e., a weighted mean where each component contributes inversely to its series’ variance.  
**Core assumption:** All \(n\) time series are independent realizations from the same \(d\)-dimensional stochastic process. If series come from different processes (e.g., different basins), averaging NSE is not statistically coherent.  
**Break condition:** When series have heterogeneous variances, non‑Gaussian marginals, or different generating processes, the Nash‑Sutcliffe functional no longer represents a common target and averaging NSE mixes incomparable quantities.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Nash‑Sutcliffe linear regression yields estimates different from ordinary least squares because it minimizes a weighted sum of squared errors, with weights inversely proportional to each series’ sample variance.  
**Core mechanism:** For a linear model \(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}\mathbf{1}^T\), the Nash‑Sutcliffe estimator solves weighted least squares with diagonal weight matrix \(\mathbf{W} = \text{diag}(w(\mathbf{Y}_{\cdot,1}), …, w(\mathbf{Y}_{\cdot,n}))\). The closed‑form solution \(\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}})^{-1}\) reduces to OLS only when all weights are equal, i.e., when all series have identical variance.  
**Core assumption:** The evaluation metric is the average \(L_{NS}\) across series, and the series are realizations of the same process. The weight depends solely on the training series’ sample variance.  
**Break condition:** If all series have nearly identical variances (e.g., IID Gaussian), weights become approximately constant and the Nash‑Sutcliffe estimator converges to OLS; the performance gap vanishes.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Training a model with \(L_{NS}\) rather than MSE leads to better performance under NSE evaluation because it directly targets the Nash‑Sutcliffe functional, the unique minimizer of expected \(L_{NS}\).  
**Core mechanism:** Strict consistency guarantees that minimizing expected \(L_{NS}\) yields predictions equal to the Nash‑Sutcliffe functional. Empirical risk minimization with \(L_{NS}\) therefore produces a consistent estimator of that functional. MSE targets the component‑wise mean, which differs under non‑Gaussian or dependent errors, resulting in suboptimal NSE.  
**Core assumption:** The model is correctly specified for the Nash‑Sutcliffe functional and test data follow the same distribution.  
**Break condition:** If the data‑generating process satisfies conditions where component‑wise mean equals Nash‑Sutcliffe functional (e.g., independent Gaussian), the performance gap disappears. Misspecification may also reduce gains.

## Foundational Learning
**Strictly consistent loss function**  
- *Why needed*: The entire framework rests on identifying a loss function whose expectation is uniquely minimized by the target functional. Without strict consistency, minimizing expected loss does not guarantee learning the intended quantity.  
- *Quick check*: If a loss function is strictly consistent for a functional, what does that imply about the optimal prediction under that loss?

**Elicitable and identifiable functional**  
- *Why needed*: The Nash‑Sutcliffe functional is both elicitable (by \(L_{NS}\)) and identifiable (by its gradient). These properties ensure consistent estimation and allow calibration checks via empirical identification functions.  
- *Quick check*: How does identifiability differ from elicitation in terms of verifying model calibration?

**M‑estimation and consistency**  
- *Why needed*: The paper connects strictly consistent losses to M‑estimators, proving that minimizing average \(L_{NS}\) yields a consistent estimator of the Nash‑Sutcliffe functional. This bridges theory and practice for regression.  
- *Quick check*: Under what conditions does an M‑estimator inherit consistency from the strict consistency of its loss function?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Input data matrix \( \mathbf{Y}_{d \times n} \) (or \( \mathbf{Y}_{n \times d} \)) → Weight computation (per series \( w(\mathbf{y}) = 1/\|\mu(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \)) → Loss \( L_{NS}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}) = \|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2 w(\mathbf{y}) \) → Realized loss \( \bar{L}_{NS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_j L_{NS}(\mathbf{Z}_{\cdot,j},\mathbf{Y}_{\cdot,j}) \) → Regression estimator \( \hat{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}})^{-1} \).

**Critical path**  
Data → Weight calculation → Loss construction → Optimization → Predictions. Weight depends only on the training series’ variance; changing data orientation (\( d\times n \) vs. \( n\times d \)) flips the aggregation index.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Extended loss (adding constant \(a\) to denominator) avoids infinite weights for constant series but changes the elicited functional; restricting distributions to exclude zero‑variance series is theoretically cleaner but may discard data.  
- Global (multi‑series) models share parameters across all series and can target the Nash‑Sutcliffe functional; local models (separate OLS per series) cannot because they lack cross‑series weighting.  
- Weighted least squares yields a closed‑form solution but requires invertibility of \(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\); collinearity may demand regularization (not considered in the paper).

**Failure signatures**  
- High \( \bar{L}_{NS} \) despite low MSE: indicates training with MSE while evaluating with NSE.  
- Weight collapse: any series with near‑zero sample variance produces huge weight, causing numerical instability; use extended loss with small \(a\).  
- Non‑invertible weight‑adjusted design matrix: check rank or condition number; may need to reduce predictors or add ridge penalty.  
- Data‑orientation mismatch: ensure weight is computed per series (column in \(d\times n\), row in \(n\times d\)); otherwise loss definition is wrong.  
- Inconsistent data versions: the `airGRdatasets` package may change; compare with authors’ data to ensure reproducibility.

**First three experiments**  
1. **Climatology comparison** (Sec 6.1): Compute component‑wise mean climatology and Nash‑Sutcliffe climatology on simulated data. They are nearly identical for IID Gaussian vectors but diverge for log‑normal or dependent series, confirming the functional difference.  
2. **Regression simulation** (Sec 6.2): Generate correlated log‑normal data with a linear relationship. Compare OLS and Nash‑Sutcliffe regression: OLS achieves lower Euclidean norm loss, Nash‑Sutcliffe achieves dramatically lower \(L_{NS}\), demonstrating alignment with the NSE metric.  
3. **Real‑data application** (Sec 6.4): Apply one‑dimensional, multi‑dimensional, and Nash‑Sutcliffe linear regression to streamflow and temperature forecasting. Confirm that Nash‑Sutcliffe regression achieves the lowest test \(L_{NS}\) (e.g., 0.122 vs. 0.224 for streamflow) while OLS yields higher NSE loss.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Theoretical proof for Gaussian equivalence** – Does the Nash‑Sutcliffe functional always coincide with the component‑wise mean for any multivariate normal distribution? The paper observed near‑equivalence empirically but leaves the proof open.  
2. **Choice of data orientation** – Which orientation (\(d\times n\) vs. \(n\times d\)) is appropriate in practice and how does it interact with the underlying stochastic process? The two lead to different loss definitions and estimators.  
3. **Extension to nonlinear models** – How can \(L_{NS}\) be optimized for nonlinear machine‑learning models (e.g., deep neural networks) while preserving its elicitation properties and ensuring efficient training?  
4. **Statistical properties of the estimator** – What are the bias, variance, and asymptotic distribution of Nash‑Sutcliffe regression estimates compared to OLS? This is needed for inference and uncertainty quantification.

## Limitations
- The theoretical foundation requires all series to be independent realizations from the same \(d\)-dimensional stochastic process. This assumption is often violated in practice (e.g., mixing basins with differing variances), making average NSE potentially incomparable.  
- The weight function \(w(\mathbf{y}) = 1/\|\mu(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2\) can become infinite for series with near‑zero variance, necessitating an ad‑hoc constant \(a\) whose effect on the elicited functional is not fully resolved.  
- Empirical validations are limited to linear models on French streamflow and temperature data; generalizability to nonlinear architectures or other regions/variables remains unproven.

## Confidence
- Theoretical foundation (strict consistency, expected loss equivalence): **High**  
- Nash‑Sutcliffe regression as weighted least squares: **High**  
- Empirical superiority for NSE evaluation: **Medium** (strong but limited to specific contexts)  
- Practical advice (align loss with metric, average only across same process): **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Test heterogeneity robustness** – Combine series from distinct basins/variables with differing variances/autocorrelation to see if average NSE remains meaningful and if Nash‑Sutcliffe regression still outperforms OLS.  
2. **Assess weight stability and extended loss** – Simulate series with very low variance; compare original \(L_{NS}\) vs. extended \(L_{NS}^{(a)}\) (with small \(a\)) in terms of solution stability and elicited functional; propose a data‑driven choice for \(a\).  
3. **Extend to nonlinear models** – Implement \(L_{NS}\) training for a neural network (e.g., LSTM) on streamflow; verify test NSE improvement over MSE training and explore asymptotic consistency empirically.
