# FOREVER: Forgetting Curve-Inspired Memory Replay for Language Model Continual Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03938
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03938
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** FOREVER improves overall performance by up to +1.2% and backward transfer by +0.9% over strong baselines on standard continual learning benchmarks with LLMs up to 13B parameters.

## Executive Summary
FOREVER introduces a novel approach to catastrophic forgetting in large language model continual learning. Instead of using fixed step counts to trigger replay, it defines a model-centric time metric based on accumulated parameter update magnitude, and maps the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve onto this metric to schedule spaced replay. Additionally, an intensity-aware regularizer adapts replay strength based on recent update dynamics. Experiments across multiple benchmarks and model scales (0.6B to 13B) show consistent gains in overall performance and backward transfer with minimal overhead.

## Method Summary
The method comprises three key components. First, a model time τ_t is accumulated from the L2 norm of parameter updates (Δ_t) at each optimizer step, with a virtual "model day" τ_day calibrated from the first S training steps of a new task. Second, a scheduler uses human Ebbinghaus intervals (e.g., 1,2,4,7,15 days) scaled by τ_day to define replay thresholds on the τ_t axis; replay is triggered when τ_t reaches each threshold. Third, an intensity-aware regularizer modulates the replay loss weight β_t based on the ratio of recent to baseline update intensity; stronger regularization is applied when the model is changing rapidly. Replay uses a fixed-capacity memory buffer storing 2% of each past task's data. The training loop interleaves normal updates with replay events, and final consolidation replays after each task.

## Key Results
- On Standard CL with Qwen3-0.6B, FOREVER raises Overall Performance to 61.5% from 58.7% (SSR) and improves Backward Transfer to -4.2% from -4.9% (AIM-Merging); vs. VBM, gains are +1.2% OP and +0.9% BWT.
- Similar consistent improvements are observed on Long Sequence (15 tasks) and SuperNI (15 generation tasks) benchmarks.
- The approach generalizes across model scales (0.6B to 13B) with negligible additional computation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Model-centric time aligns replay with learning progress.** FOREVER defines τ_t as the accumulated L2 norm of optimizer updates on trainable parameters. A task-specific τ_day is computed as the sum of Δ_t over the first S steps, scaling human Ebbinghaus intervals to a model-time axis. This ensures replay triggers reflect the actual evolution of the model: rapid early changes (high Δ_t) lead to more frequent replay when forgetting risk is highest, whereas slow changes delay replay. The strength lies in using an internal signal (update magnitude) that naturally varies with optimization dynamics, unlike fixed step counts.

**Mechanism 2: Ebbinghaus-inspired spaced replay.** Replay is scheduled at increasing intervals on the τ_t axis (e.g., τ_day, 2τ_day, 4τ_day...), mimicking the human forgetting curve's rapid initial decay. This pattern means the model is reminded of past tasks most frequently right after learning them, and less frequently as τ_t accumulates, optimizing the stability-plasticity trade-off. The assumption is that LLM forgetting follows a similar temporal pattern.

**Mechanism 3: Intensity-aware replay strength.** The recent update intensity μ_t (EMA of Δ_t) is compared to a baseline μ0. When μ_t > μ0 (model changing rapidly), the replay regularization coefficient β_t is increased, applying stronger constraints to preserve past knowledge during unstable phases. Conversely, when the model stabilizes (μ_t ≈ μ0), β_t is reduced to avoid hindering new learning. This dynamic adaptation prevents over-regularization during normal learning while protecting during high-plasticity periods.

## Foundational Learning

- **Parameter Update Norm**: The per-step L2 norm of optimizer updates applied to trainable parameters (e.g., LoRA weights). Why needed? It serves as the raw signal for model time τ_t, reflecting how much the model changes per step. Quick check: If using LoRA, why is computing Δ_t only on LoRA weights sufficient? Because LoRA updates capture the adaptation while keeping the base frozen; the changes in trainable parameters represent the effective learning progress for the current task.

- **Exponential Moving Average (EMA)**: A smoothing technique for noisy step-wise signals. Why needed? EMA transforms the raw Δ_t into a stable estimate of recent update intensity μ_t, enabling reliable detection of unstable phases. Quick check: With λ=0.1, how many steps for μ_t to roughly reflect a sudden spike in Δ_t? Approximately 1/λ = 10 steps; with λ=0.01, ~100 steps.

- **Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve**: A psychological model describing memory decay over time, with rapid forgetting initially that slows gradually. Why needed? Its characteristic shape (increasing intervals) guides the spacing of replay events in FOREVER, mapping human intervals to model-time. Quick check: What does the curve imply about the relative importance of replay shortly after learning a task versus much later? Replay is most critical soon after learning, hence frequent early reminders.

- **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)**: A parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that injects low-rank matrices into transformer layers. Why needed? FOREVER is implemented with LoRA; updates are applied only to the adapter weights, making Δ_t computation and replay regularization efficient and focused. Quick check: In FOREVER's equations, does Θ_t refer to full model parameters or just LoRA adapters? Only the LoRA parameters, as these are the ones being optimized.

- **Replay Buffer Management**: Storing a subset of past tasks' data for rehearsal. Why needed? Replay requires access to samples from previously learned tasks to consolidate knowledge. Quick check: How might buffer sampling strategy (random vs. class-balanced) interact with FOREVER's adaptive replay strength? Class-balanced sampling could ensure all past tasks are equally represented, while random sampling might bias toward larger tasks; this could affect the effectiveness of the intensity-aware regularizer which assumes the replay data is representative.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Update Tracker computes Δ_t, τ_t, μ_t and sends τ_t to Scheduler and μ_t to Regularizer; Scheduler signals Training Loop to initiate replay when thresholds are met; Regularizer provides β_t to Training Loop during replay; Memory Manager supplies replay data to Training Loop; Training Loop updates parameters, records snapshots (Θ*), and manages buffer updates.

**Critical path**: 1) Warm-up: run first S steps of new task to compute τ_day and μ0. 2) During training: for each step, perform normal update, compute Δ_t, accumulate τ_t, update μ_t via EMA. If τ_t crosses next D_model threshold, trigger replay: compute r_t and β_t, then for E_mem epochs optimize L_replay using memory data. 3) After task finishes: perform final replay consolidation on memory and update memory buffer with current task samples.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Warm-up length S: Larger S gives stable calibration but delays replay; smaller S adapts faster but may be noisy (paper finds robustness across {12,24,48}).
- Smoothing λ: Larger λ makes μ_t more responsive; smaller λ yields smoother but lagging signal (fixed at 0.05).
- β_base: Scales replay loss relative to task loss; set so magnitudes are comparable (1e-3 works).
- Replay epochs E_mem: More epochs improve retention but increase cost (paper uses 2).

**Failure signatures**:
- Replay triggers too early/late: Check τ_t growth anomaly; visualize Δ_t and τ_t evolution (Figure 5).
- Replay strength misregulated: Inspect r_t and β_t trajectories; if constantly extreme, μ0 may be miscalibrated.
- No forgetting mitigation: Possibly due to sparse D_model intervals, clipped β_t too small, or insufficient memory buffer size/diversity.
- Buffer management errors: Verify memory size equals 2% of each past task’s dataset; ensure consistent random sampling.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Validate model-time accumulation by logging Δ_t and τ_t for a single task; confirm τ_t grows rapidly early and slows, and replay triggers occur at increasing τ_t thresholds matching D_model.
2. Check scheduler alignment across multiple tasks by plotting step indices where replay triggers; they should vary substantially for the same D_model index, demonstrating adaptivity vs. fixed-step schedules.
3. Tune β_base by monitoring task and replay loss magnitudes during replay; adjust β_base until they are roughly balanced (as in Figure 6). If replay loss dominates, reduce β_base; if negligible, increase it.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can a model-centric notion of time be more directly aligned with actual task forgetting (e.g., performance decay) rather than using parameter update magnitude as a proxy? The paper states that incorporating explicit indicators like performance metrics could enhance scheduling.
2. Can the predefined Ebbinghaus-inspired replay interval pattern be replaced with a learned or adaptive schedule that optimizes for a given model architecture and task sequence? The authors suggest learning interval patterns from data as a promising extension.
3. Why does model-centric time calibration based on update magnitude consistently outperform step-based calibration? While empirical gains are clear, the underlying relationship between optimization dynamics and forgetting remains unexplained.
4. Does the FOREVER framework, particularly its intensity-aware regularization, generalize to non-replay continual learning paradigms (e.g., regularization-only or architectural methods)? The intensity signal might indicate high-plasticity states requiring stronger constraints, even without replay, but this is untested.

## Limitations
- Memory buffer sampling strategy (random vs. class-balanced) is unspecified, potentially affecting replay efficacy and fairness of comparisons.
- EMA initialization and reset behavior at task boundaries (whether μ_t carries over or restarts) is ambiguous.
- Snapshot timing for Θ* (whether it is taken immediately after task training or after final replay consolidation) is unclear, which may influence regularization anchor.
- The Δ_t signal may be sensitive to gradient noise, batch size, or gradient clipping, risking misalignment in noisy optimization settings.
- The Ebbinghaus interval pattern may not universally hold for all task types, model architectures, or sequence lengths.
