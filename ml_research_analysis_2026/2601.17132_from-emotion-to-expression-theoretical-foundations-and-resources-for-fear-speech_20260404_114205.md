# From Emotion to Expression: Theoretical Foundations and Resources for Fear Speech

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17132  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17132  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A cross-disciplinary synthesis defines fear speech via three components (strategic language, threat portrayal, fear-evoking aim), and an audit of adjacent datasets reveals severe fragmentation, accessibility issues, and English dominance.  

## Executive Summary
Fear speech—deliberate rhetoric that frames a target as an existential threat to evoke fear and manipulate audiences—remains under-studied in computational linguistics due to conceptual confusion with emotion or hate speech and a lack of dedicated resources. This paper bridges Political Science, Communication, Psychology, and Linguistics to propose a unified, multi-level theoretical model. It then audited 37 datasets from propaganda, framing, populism, and related areas using a novel taxonomy that maps existing labels to core fear dimensions. Results show the field is heavily concentrated in Computational Linguistics venues, dominated by English-language news and Twitter data, plagued by inconsistent labeling (19 distinct schemes), and suffers from poor resource accessibility and documentation. The work establishes a foundation for future fear speech research by clarifying theory, providing a bridging taxonomy, and exposing critical gaps in resources and methodological diversity.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted a cross-disciplinary survey of fear theories, synthesized them into a definition with three components (C1: deliberate strategic language, C2: portrayal of target as threat, C3: aim to evoke fear for influence), and built a taxonomy to map existing dataset labels to these dimensions. They identified candidate papers via AI-assisted search (Ai2 Paper Finder) using 27 queries, screened them with active learning (ASReview) to select 76 relevant works on fear speech or adjacent phenomena, and manually extracted metadata for 37 datasets. The audit addressed four research questions: disciplinary origin, data sources and languages, conceptualization (labels), and accessibility/sustainability.

## Key Results
- 37 relevant datasets were identified; 27 originated from Computational Linguistics venues, indicating a disciplinary imbalance.
- English dominates (23/37 datasets), with news articles and Twitter being the most common sources (26 datasets). Labeling is highly inconsistent, with 19 distinct annotation schemes.
- Resource accessibility is low: only 21 of 26 accessible links were active, 13 datasets provided annotation guidelines, and 22 of 33 manually annotated ones reported inter-annotator agreement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Conceptual Disentanglement via Multi-Disciplinary Synthesis
**Claim:** Fear speech cannot be adequately captured by any single disciplinary theory; its computational study requires integrating distinct conceptual contributions from Political Science (securitization), Communication (fear appeals), Psychology (appraisal), and Linguistics (framing/metaphor).  
**Mechanism:** The paper deconstructs "fear" into four analytical levels (Source, Message, Reception, Outcome). Each discipline provides a necessary lens: Political Science explains the strategic source (elite rhetoric), Communication explains the message structure (fear appeal design), Psychology explains the reception process (individual cognition), and Linguistics explains the message construction (language choices). Integrating them yields a multi-level model where a fear speech act is a deliberate, framed message from a source, designed to trigger a specific psychological response to achieve an outcome.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "fear speech is a phenomenon shaped by contributions from multiple disciplines." [section 2.5] Explicit multi-level overview: "At the Source level... At the Message level... At the Reception level... at the Outcome level." [corpus] The dataset audit's taxonomy, built on C1–C3, is used to categorize labels, demonstrating the synthesis's utility; the corpus reveals current fragmentation (19 labels) that the synthesis aims to resolve.  
**Break condition:** If a core disciplinary perspective (e.g., linguistic constructions of threat) is missing, the definition will fail to account for key textual features, leading to incomplete annotation schemes.

### Mechanism 2: Resource Grounding via Taxonomy-Driven Label Bridging
**Claim:** The lack of dedicated "fear speech" datasets can be mitigated by re-purposing adjacent resources if their annotation labels can be systematically aligned to a unified taxonomy of fear dimensions.  
**Mechanism:** The taxonomy (Table 2, Appendix C) based on C1–C3 acts as a bridging schema. For any existing dataset label (e.g., "Security," "Appeal to fear"), the mechanism involves: (1) decomposing the label's operational definition, (2) mapping it to the taxonomy dimension(s) it captures (e.g., "Security" maps to C1+partial C2+C3), and (3) assessing whether data instances satisfy the full fear speech definition or only a subset. This allows partial reuse for studying specific dimensions (e.g., threat portrayal) even if not the full act.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "propose a taxonomy that consolidates and aggregates the different dimensions of fear." [section 4.1] "To standardize them, we develop a taxonomy..." [section 4.2/RQ3] Application to 37 datasets shows frequency of dimensions (propaganda/framing dominant) and underrepresentation of others; Figure 4 visualizes mapping. [corpus] The final list of 37 resources (Table 4) is the direct output of this bridging.  
**Break condition:** If a label's definition is irreconcilably vague or orthogonal to C1–C3 (e.g., generic "hate speech"), it cannot be bridged and offers limited utility.

### Mechanism 3: Gap Exposure via Systematic Audit Metrics
**Claim:** The field's progress is hindered not just by a lack of resources, but by poor documentation, accessibility, and disciplinary concentration; these gaps are made actionable through a multi-dimensional audit.  
**Mechanism:** The paper operationalizes "resource health" via four RQs. The mechanism exposes structural flaws: Concentration Risk (RQ1: 27/37 datasets from CL venues), Linguistic Bias (RQ2: English dominance), Conceptual Drift (RQ3: 19 distinct labels), and Sustainability Risk (RQ4: only 21/26 links active, few guidelines/IAA). These metrics transform a qualitative problem into a quantitative, prioritizable set of actionable gaps.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "review existing definitions... survey datasets..." [section 4.2] Presents results for each RQ with figures and explicit counts (e.g., "English overwhelmingly dominates (23 datasets)"). [corpus] The metadata table (Table 4) and RQ4 summary statistics provide empirical evidence for gap claims.  
**Break condition:** If the audit focuses only on dataset existence and ignores accessibility/guidelines, identified "resources" will not be practical assets for modeling progress.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multi-Level Fear Processing Model (Source-Message-Reception-Outcome)**  
  *Why needed:* The core analytical framework from Section 2.5 for dissecting a fear speech instance. A researcher must use this to ask: Who is the source? What message strategies (C1) are used? How is the threat portrayed (C2)? What is the intended audience reaction (C3)?  
  *Quick check:* Given a tweet, can you identify the source (e.g., politician), the strategic language (e.g., securitizing metaphor), the targeted threat (e.g., immigrants as existential danger), and the implied call to action (e.g., support restrictive policy)?

- **Concept: Fear Speech Definition Components (C1, C2, C3)**  
  *Why needed:* The non-negotiable criteria for determining if a text qualifies as fear speech and for building annotation schemes; the bridge between theory and data.  
  *Quick check:* Does a text exhibit *deliberate strategic language* (C1), *portray a target as an inherent/imminent threat* (C2), and have the *primary aim of evoking fear to influence* (C3)? If only two are present, it may be an adjacent phenomenon.

- **Concept: Taxonomy of Fear Dimensions (Propaganda/Framing/Populism/Fallacy/Abusive Speech mappings)**  
  *Why needed:* The practical tool for navigating the existing resource landscape (Table 2, 4). It tells which existing labels are relevant to which fear component, enabling partial dataset reuse.  
  *Quick check:* If a dataset is labeled for "Security" framing, which of C1, C2, or C3 does it most directly capture? (Answer: Primarily C2, with aspects of C1 and implied C3.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Theory Module -> Taxonomy & Mapping Module -> Audit & Gap Analysis Module -> Resource Curation Interface

Critical path: 1. Start with Theory Module → formalize C1–C3 for your task. 2. Use Taxonomy Module to search/identify candidate datasets from Table 4. 3. Apply Audit Module metrics to filter candidates (e.g., prioritize those with active links, guidelines, and IAA). 4. Curate final set in Resource Interface, noting which C components each dataset covers.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Breadth vs. Depth:** Using many partially relevant datasets (high breadth, many gaps in C components) vs. few highly relevant but possibly small dedicated fear speech datasets (low breadth, high depth).
- **Label Consistency vs. Practical Reuse:** Strictly requiring all three C components severely limits usable datasets. Allowing partial reuse (e.g., only C2 for threat framing) increases usable data but requires multi-task or modular modeling.
- **Cross-Linguistic Validity vs. Available Resources:** Prioritizing non-English datasets (per RQ2 gap) means working with fewer, potentially lower-quality resources vs. leveraging vast English resources with known cultural bias.

Failure signatures:
- **Theoretical Drift:** Building a model that only detects "threat" language (C2) but ignores *deliberate strategic framing* (C1) and *evocative aim* (C3), thus conflating fear speech with neutral threat reporting.
- **Resource Collapse:** Selecting a dataset with a dead link or no annotation guidelines, leading to irreproducible experiments.
- **Monoculture Modeling:** Training and evaluating only on English, news/Twitter data, producing a model that fails on WhatsApp/Telegram or non-Western contexts.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Annotation Pilot:** Take 200 texts from a high-quality "Appeal to fear" propaganda dataset (e.g., Da San Martino et al., 2019b). Create a guideline layer that annotates not just the propaganda span, but sub-annotations for C1 (strategic rhetoric), C2 (threat portrayal), and C3 (evocative aim). Measure agreement on the C-components. Goal: Test if the C1–C3 schema is learnable and distinguishable on existing data.
2. **Taxonomy Mapping Stress Test:** For 5 datasets with ambiguous labels (e.g., "Security"), have two annotators independently map each label to the taxonomy using only the paper's label definition and a sample of 50 instances. Compute agreement on the dimension assignment. Goal: Operationalize the mapping process and identify labels that are too ambiguous for reliable reuse.
3. **Gap Replication:** Replicate RQ1–RQ4 on a new set of 20 papers from a different source (e.g., pre-2020, or from political science venues not in the original search). Compare the distributions (disciplinary source, language, label types, accessibility) to the paper’s results. Goal: Validate whether the identified gaps (English bias, CL dominance, poor docs) are stable or an artifact of the search strategy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** How can fear speech be decomposed into modular, computationally detectable signals aligned with the three components (strategic language, threat portrayal, intent to evoke fear)?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] Discussion (Section 5): "Future work can decompose fear into modular signals aligned with our definition components."  
  *Why unresolved:* Current research lacks operationalization of the theoretical definition into discrete, machine-readable features; existing datasets use heterogeneous labels that do not consistently map onto the components.  
  *Evidence to resolve:* A standardized annotation scheme that explicitly links linguistic and rhetorical cues to each component, applied across diverse datasets, with reported inter-annotator agreement, and demonstration of improved model performance when leveraging these modular annotations.

- **Question:** What strategies can effectively expand the linguistic and topical diversity of fear speech datasets, improve annotation transparency, and ensure long-term accessibility and reusability?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] Discussion notes that "Expanding linguistic coverage beyond English and German and improving annotation transparency through open guidelines and agreement reporting directly address the accessibility gaps."  
  *Why unresolved:* The resource audit revealed heavy English dominance, many broken links, scarce annotation guidelines, and inconsistent IAA reporting, which severely limit resource reusability and cross-linguistic generalizability.  
  *Evidence to resolve:* Creation of multilingual, topically diverse fear speech datasets with persistent access links, open annotation guidelines, standardized licensing, and thorough IAA metrics; longitudinal tracking of resource availability and community adoption.

- **Question:** Which computational methods best balance scalability, interpretability, and accuracy for fear speech detection, given the phenomenon's multimodal and context-dependent nature?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] Limitations: "an overview of methods and approaches falls out of the scope of this paper and we reserve it for future work." Discussion calls for "connecting theoretical insight with scalable NLP practice."  
  *Why unresolved:* Existing approaches range from qualitative analyses to black‑box neural models, but no systematic comparison or methodological guidelines exist that consider fear speech’s unique theoretical dimensions.  
  *Evidence to resolve:* A comprehensive empirical benchmark comparing diverse modeling approaches (e.g., rule‑based, transformer‑based, interpretable models) on multiple fear‑speech datasets, measuring predictive performance, explainability, robustness, and cross‑context transferability.

- **Question:** How do audience‑level psychological factors (e.g., values, threat sensitivity, regulatory focus) influence the processing and impact of fear speech, and how can these be incorporated into computational models?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] Limitations acknowledge that "a whole set of other constructs from social‑psychology can in future work be integrated... these are phenomena that define which type of 'audience' is more susceptible to fear speech."  
  *Why unresolved:* Most datasets and detection tasks focus solely on the producer’s message, ignoring recipient characteristics that moderate fear induction, thereby limiting predictions about real‑world impact.  
  *Evidence to resolve:* Collection of datasets enriched with audience metadata (e.g., demographic, psychometric traits) or experimental studies measuring fear responses; development of user‑adaptive predictive models that incorporate audience factors, validated against behavioral outcomes such as sharing or policy support.

## Limitations
- The cross-disciplinary synthesis rests on expert judgment in mapping disparate theories; different scholars might construct alternative multi-level models.
- The dataset audit depends on the completeness of the AI2 Paper Finder search and the subjectivity in label-to-taxonomy mapping.
- The "resource health" metrics (accessibility, IAA) are descriptive but do not measure actual *usability* for fear speech modeling; claims about underrepresentation could shift with a broader corpus.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Conceptual Disentanglement):** *Medium* – The multi-level model is well-articulated but its necessity over simpler definitions is not empirically validated here.
- **Mechanism 2 (Taxonomy Bridging):** *Medium* – Mapping is principled but introduces subjectivity; reproducibility of mappings across annotators is untested.
- **Mechanism 3 (Gap Exposure):** *High* – Descriptive metrics (counts, link status) are straightforward and replicable if search/screening are identical.
