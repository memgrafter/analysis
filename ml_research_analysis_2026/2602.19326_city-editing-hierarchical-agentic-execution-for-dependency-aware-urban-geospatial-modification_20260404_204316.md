# City Editing: Hierarchical Agentic Execution for Dependency-Aware Urban Geospatial Modification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19326  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19326  
- **Reference count:** 24  
- **Primary result:** A hierarchical agentic framework significantly reduces geometric errors and increases execution validity for urban geospatial editing tasks compared to single-pass baselines.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the inefficiency of manual urban renewal by framing geospatial plan modification as a machine-executable task. It introduces the CEAE (Coarse-to-Fine Editing with Agentic Execution) framework, which decomposes natural-language instructions into polygon-, line-, and point-level editing intents and executes them in a hierarchical, dependency-aware manner. By propagating spatial constraints between abstraction levels and using iterative self-reflective validation, CEAE prevents error accumulation. Evaluations on real-world urban patches show improved geometric accuracy and validity rates across editing tasks, along with robustness to prompt variations.

## Method Summary
CEAE processes urban editing instructions through a multi-agent pipeline. A task planner first decomposes a natural-language command into a sequence of editing intents at three spatial abstraction levels: polygons (e.g., parcels, zones), lines (e.g., roads, boundaries), and points (e.g., amenities, nodes). Execution proceeds coarse-to-fine: polygon-level edits are performed first, with resulting constraints (e.g., buffer zones, adjacency) fed forward to guide subsequent line and point edits. After each execution step, a self-reflective validator—powered by an LLM—checks geometric validity, dependency consistency, and constraint satisfaction. If validation fails, the system iteratively refines the edit. This hierarchical propagation ensures that local changes respect global plan coherence.

## Key Results
- Reduces geometric error metrics (Regional Edge Error, ACE) across polygon, line, and point editing tasks.
- Increases Execution Validity Rate (EVR) for all editing task types compared to single-pass baselines.
- Demonstrers robustness to prompt perturbations, maintaining performance under paraphrased or incomplete instructions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The hierarchical decomposition aligns with the natural dependency structure of urban geospatial data: polygon edits (e.g., rezoning) often impose constraints on lines (e.g., road networks) and points (e.g., building placements). By executing from coarse to fine and propagating intermediate constraints, CEAE prevents downstream edits from violating upstream decisions, avoiding cascading errors. The self-reflective validator acts as a feedback loop, catching invalid states before they propagate further. This mimics human planning workflows where broad strokes are refined iteratively, but automates the consistency checks that are error-prone in manual processes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Geospatial Topology:** Needed to represent and validate spatial relationships (e.g., adjacency, containment). Quick check: Do shared boundaries between polygons align without gaps or overlaps?
- **Hierarchical Task Decomposition:** Breaks complex edits into manageable sub-tasks across abstraction levels. Quick check: Can a high-level instruction like "widen the boulevard" be resolved into measurable changes at polygon (setback), line (road centerline), and point (tree placement) levels?
- **Constraint Propagation:** Ensures edits at one level correctly inform constraints at finer levels. Quick check: If a polygon buffer is increased, are nearby line features automatically adjusted to maintain minimum distances?
- **LLM-based Intent Parsing:** Translates ambiguous natural language into structured editing operations. Quick check: Does the parser correctly disambiguate "near the park" into a search radius or specific adjacency?
- **Self-Reflective Validation:** Uses an LLM to assess intermediate results against geometric and dependency rules. Quick check: Can the validator detect a non-manifold boundary created by a point edit?
- **Coarse-to-Fine Refinement:** Progressively increases resolution while maintaining global consistency. Quick check: Does starting with polygon edits reduce rework compared to editing points first?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Natural Language Instruction -> Task Planner -> Polygon Executor -> Constraint Propagation -> Line Executor -> Constraint Propagation -> Point Executor -> Self-Reflective Validator -> (Iterate if invalid) -> Final Geospatial Plan

**Critical path:**  
1. Task planner decomposes instruction into level-specific intents.  
2. Polygon executor applies first edits (e.g., reshape parcels).  
3. Constraints from polygon edits (e.g., new boundaries, buffers) propagate to line-level schema.  
4. Line executor modifies features (e.g., roads) under propagated constraints.  
5. Constraints propagate to point-level.  
6. Point executor places/adjusts nodes (e.g., intersections, amenities).  
7. Validator checks entire state; on failure, errors are fed back to the relevant executor for refinement.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Fixed hierarchy (polygon→line→point) ensures predictable dependency flow but may not suit all edits (e.g., a point addition might immediately affect polygon boundaries).  
- LLM-based executors offer flexibility in interpreting vague instructions but risk geometric imprecision; symbolic validators are precise but less adaptable.  
- Constraint propagation adds computational overhead but reduces cumulative rework.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Invalid geometries (self-intersecting polygons, dangling lines) escaping validator.  
- Dependency breaks (e.g., a point placed outside its parent polygon due to missed propagation).  
- Validator false negatives on subtle topological errors (e.g., non-manifold edges).  
- Context overflow in LLM when dependency graphs become too large.

**First three experiments to onboard:**  
1. **Single-level baseline:** Execute point edits without polygon/line pre-constraints to measure hierarchy benefit.  
2. **Ablated validator:** Disable self-reflection and compare error accumulation against full CEAE.  
3. **Perturbation stress test:** Input paraphrased instructions with omitted spatial references to probe parser robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can CEAE be extended to incorporate richer, multi-dimensional urban planning constraints (e.g., socio-economic equity, environmental impact, regulatory compliance) beyond its current geometric and topological focus?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] ... The conclusion explicitly states that "incorporating richer planning constraints and interactive human feedback offers a promising research direction."
- Why unresolved: The current framework primarily addresses geometric validity, dependency consistency, and basic buffer constraints. It does not integrate qualitative or quantitative planning criteria common in professional urban planning, such as affordability, accessibility metrics, or zoning bylaws.
- What evidence would resolve it: A formal extension of the task planner's intent schema to model non-geometric constraints, followed by empirical evaluation on tasks requiring trade-offs between spatial modifications and socio-economic goals, with measurable outcomes aligned with urban science literature.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent does CEAE's hierarchical decomposition (polygon → line → point) limit its ability to handle editing scenarios where dependencies cross or skip these abstraction levels, and could a dynamic, content-aware hierarchy improve performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] ... The methodology enforces a fixed coarse-to-fine pipeline. The paper acknowledges "interdependent edits across spatial elements and abstraction levels" but does not analyze whether the prescribed level order is always optimal.
- Why unresolved: The framework assumes a universal hierarchy. Some real-world edits (e.g., modifying a road's path may immediately require adjusting adjacent parcel boundaries, not waiting for a polygon-stage) might violate this order, potentially causing unnecessary rework or invalid states.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative experiments with alternative decomposition strategies (e.g., level-skipping, parallel paths, or learned ordering) on a diverse set of editing tasks, measuring accuracy, validity, and execution efficiency.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the specific failure modes of the self-reflective validator when dealing with complex, domain-specific topological or semantic violations in geospatial data, and how can its reliability be systematically improved?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] ... The validator uses an LLM to check "geometric validity, dependency consistency, and constraint satisfaction." Its robustness is tested only against prompt perturbations, not against nuanced geospatial errors.
- Why unresolved: LLMs may lack specialized knowledge for detecting subtle invalidities (e.g., non-manifold boundaries, planimetric inconsistencies, or semantic incoherence like a "park" polygon intersecting a "highway" without a buffer). The paper does not provide an error analysis of validator decisions.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed ablation study isolating validator failures on hand-curated invalid examples, potentially integrating symbolic geometry engines or specialized validators as complement or fallback.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does CEAE perform on urban layouts represented in geospatial formats other than GeoJSON (e.g., shapefiles, FileGDB) or on data with higher noise, incompleteness, or semantic inconsistency typical of real-world crowdsourced sources?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] ... The entire framework is built on the GeoJSON schema and uses curated OSM data. The paper claims applicability to "existing urban plans" but does not address portability or robustness to imperfect real-world data.
- Why unresolved: Real-world urban data often suffers from topology errors, attribute inconsistencies, and varying schema. CEAE's tools and agents are likely tuned to GeoJSON's structure; performance degradation on other formats or noisy inputs is an unstated assumption.
- What evidence would resolve it: Porting CEAE to another format (e.g., shapefile) and evaluating on a dataset with injected noise or common OSM errors (e.g., overlapping polygons, missing tags), measuring the breakdown of execution validity.

### Open Question 5
- Question: Can the hierarchical agentic execution model scale to city-scale or regional editing tasks involving thousands of interconnected features, and what are the computational and reasoning bottlenecks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] ... Experiments are on 1km x 1km patches. The framework's memory and reasoning load grows with feature count and dependency complexity, but the paper does not discuss scalability limits.
- Why unresolved: The agentic workflow involves multiple LLM calls per subtask. For a large city district, the number of interdependent edits could explode, leading to high latency, cost, and risk of context window overflow or error propagation despite validation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical scaling studies measuring execution time, cost, and success rate as a function of urban patch size and feature density, coupled with an analysis of which components (planner, executor, validator) become bottlenecks.

## Limitations
- Lack of reproducibility details: dataset size, source, and public availability are unspecified; baseline implementations and metric definitions (REE, ACE, EVR) are not formally described.
- Underspecified robustness evaluation: perturbation types and evaluation methodology for prompt robustness are not detailed, weakening support for the claim.
- No scalability analysis: experiments are confined to small (1km²) patches; performance on larger, denser urban layouts remains unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Performance gains** (error reduction, validity rate): Medium. Results are stated as significant but lack variance estimates, baseline descriptions, and dataset characterization.
- **Robustness to prompt perturbations**: Low. The perturbation types and evaluation methodology are not described, leaving the claim unsupported.
