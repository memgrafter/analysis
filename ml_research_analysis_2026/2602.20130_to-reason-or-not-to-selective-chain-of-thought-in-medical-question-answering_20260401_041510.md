# To Reason or Not to: Selective Chain-of-Thought in Medical Question Answering
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.20130
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20130
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: Selective Chain-of-Thought reduces inference time by 13–45% and token usage by 8–47% with minimal accuracy loss (≤4%), and in some cases improves both efficiency and accuracy.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the efficiency-accuracy trade-off in medical question answering with large language models (LLMs). Standard chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting often wastes computation on recall-type questions that require only factual lookup. The authors propose Selective CoT, an inference-time strategy where the model first decides whether explicit reasoning is needed; only reasoning-type questions trigger a full CoT. Evaluated on four biomedical QA benchmarks using Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen-2.5-7B, Selective CoT reduced token usage by 8–47% and inference time by 13–45% while maintaining accuracy within 4% of full CoT, and sometimes achieving higher accuracy alongside the savings.

## Method Summary
Selective CoT operates at inference only, without fine-tuning. Given a medical question, the model is prompted to first output a binary decision indicating whether reasoning is required. If "yes", the model generates a step-by-step rationale followed by the answer; if "no", it directly produces the answer. The approach was tested on two open-source LLMs (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-7B) across four biomedical QA datasets: HeadQA, MedQA-USMLE, MedMCQA, and PubMedQA. Each dataset uses multiple-choice or yes/no formats. Generation settings included temperature 0.7 and max_new_tokens=1024. The method was compared against standard prompting (direct answer) and full CoT (reasoning always). Metrics included accuracy, total generated tokens, and wall-clock inference time.

## Key Results
- Token usage reduced by 8–47% and inference time by 13–45% relative to full CoT.
- Accuracy drop no greater than 4% across all evaluated model-dataset pairs.
- In several cases, Selective CoT achieved both higher accuracy and lower computational cost than standard prompting or fixed-length CoT.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Self-classification of question complexity
- **Claim**: The model can reliably classify medical questions as recall-type (factual lookup) vs. reasoning-type (multi-step inference) at inference time.
- **Mechanism**: The model generates an initial "reason or not" decision (via a prompt instruction). This binary router determines which generation path to follow: direct answer for recall, full CoT for reasoning.
- **Core assumption**: The model's internal representations or prompt-based self-assessment align with human-defined question complexity categories without external classifiers.
- **Evidence anchors**: The paper's abstract and section describing the dichotomy; however, corpus evidence for the classification mechanism itself is weak.
- **Break condition**: Systematic misclassification where recall questions are sent to CoT (wasted compute) or reasoning questions are sent directly (accuracy loss), indicated by high correlation between decision errors and task-type labels.

### Mechanism 2: Efficiency on recall questions
- **Claim**: Avoiding CoT generation on correctly classified recall questions reduces computational cost while preserving accuracy on those items.
- **Mechanism**: For recall questions, the model outputs only the final answer token sequence instead of a lengthy rationale, cutting output length and generation time.
- **Core assumption**: Recall questions do not benefit substantively from step-by-step reasoning; direct parametric recall suffices.
- **Evidence anchors**: The paper's statement that full CoT is unnecessary on recall items; empirical results showing 8–47% token reduction and 13–45% time reduction with minimal accuracy loss on recall-dominant datasets.
- **Break condition**: If recall questions answered directly show a statistically significant accuracy drop compared to full CoT on a held-out recall-only subset, the assumption fails.

### Mechanism 3: Maintained reasoning on hard questions
- **Claim**: Invoking CoT only on reasoning-type questions maintains competitive aggregate accuracy compared to always-on CoT.
- **Mechanism**: The model preserves the reasoning capability for hard items; efficiency gains accumulate from the majority of easy/recall items being answered directly.
- **Core assumption**: The dataset distribution contains a substantial portion of recall questions where direct answering is viable, and the routing decision is sufficiently accurate to avoid net accuracy loss.
- **Evidence anchors**: The paper reports ≤4% accuracy loss and, in some pairs, higher efficiency alongside accuracy gains; ablation shows Selective CoT lies near the Pareto frontier of fixed-length CoT sweeps.
- **Break condition**: Aggregate accuracy drop exceeding the efficiency gains' value (e.g., >5%) across multiple benchmarks, indicating poor decision calibration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**: The baseline method where the model generates intermediate reasoning steps before answering. Needed to understand why Selective CoT modifies this baseline. Quick check: What are two potential downsides of always using CoT in production medical QA systems?
- **Recall vs. Reasoning Question Taxonomies in MedQA**: Medical questions can be split into those requiring factual recall and those requiring multi-step inference. This dichotomy underpins Selective CoT's routing. Quick check: Given a multiple-choice medical question, how would you classify it as primarily recall or reasoning? Provide one example of each.
- **Efficiency Metrics (Token count, Wall-clock time)**: The paper's value proposition is computational savings; engineers must measure tokens and latency and understand their trade-offs. Quick check: Why might token count reduction not always correlate with proportional wall-clock time reduction in a real serving environment?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input Encoder -> Decision Router -> Answer Generator -> Output Parser

Critical path: Question text -> Prompt with Selective CoT instruction -> Model forward pass -> First token(s) indicate decision -> Conditional generation path (direct answer or CoT then answer) -> Final answer extraction.

Design tradeoffs:
- Prompt design vs. fine-tuning: The paper uses a zero-shot prompt for the decision; fine-tuning could improve routing but breaks model-agnosticism.
- Decision threshold: The model's internal confidence for the "reasoning needed" decision is uncalibrated. A stricter threshold increases efficiency but risks false negatives on reasoning questions.
- Interpretability: Direct answers omit the rationale, which may be desired for clinical education or auditability.

Failure signatures:
- High efficiency, low accuracy: Router chooses "direct" for most reasoning questions, causing accuracy drop on reasoning-dominant datasets.
- Low efficiency, high accuracy: Router chooses "CoT" for nearly all questions, yielding savings close to zero.
- Inconsistent savings: Token reduction varies widely across datasets (e.g., -46.5% on PubMedQA for Qwen vs. -1% on HeadQA for Llama), indicating dataset-specific decision reliability.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Decision Accuracy Baseline**: For a sample of questions with human-annotated "reasoning required" labels, measure the accuracy of the model's implicit routing decision. Goal: >85% agreement.
2. **Ablation on Recall-Only Subset**: On a filtered subset of pure recall questions, compare accuracy of Selective CoT (direct path) vs. full CoT. Expect parity or better for direct.
3. **Fixed-Length CoT Comparator**: Replicate the paper's length-sweep experiment (e.g., generating CoT of 100–600 words) and plot accuracy vs. tokens. Overlay Selective CoT's result to confirm it lies on or above the Pareto frontier.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Robustness of efficiency gains**: How do time/token savings hold up across different hardware, batching strategies, and system loads? The paper notes wall-clock time is sensitive to backend factors and only provides a coarse comparison.
2. **Generalization**: Does Selective CoT work with other models (e.g., larger or proprietary LLMs) and on more specialized medical domains (e.g., clinical notes, radiology reports)? The evaluation is limited to two 7B-8B models on exam-style and literature QA.
3. **Decision heuristic**: What features of medical questions drive the router's classification? The method is a black box; no analysis identifies which question types are misrouted or what linguistic cues are used.
4. **Safety risks**: Could erroneously suppressing reasoning on deceptive recall-type questions increase clinically harmful errors? The paper reports aggregate accuracy but does not stratify errors by clinical severity or compare error types.
5. **Interpretability and control**: Can the selective reasoning decision be made more transparent and controllable (e.g., via confidence thresholds, rule-based safeguards) without sacrificing efficiency? The current implicit prompt-based router is not auditable.

## Limitations
- Underspecified implementation details: The exact prompt phrasing and decision extraction logic are not provided; the paper describes the approach conceptually.
- Lack of human-annotated question-type labels: No independent validation of the router's alignment with human-defined recall/reasoning categories.
- Aggregate metrics only: Reported token and time savings are not broken down by question type, masking potential misclassification costs and dataset-specific behavior.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Self-classification)**: Low confidence (weak evidence that model decisions match human taxonomies).
- **Mechanism 2 (Recall questions无需CoT)**: Medium confidence (token/time reductions support savings, but per-type accuracy not analyzed).
- **Mechanism 3 (Overall accuracy preservation)**: Medium-High confidence (reported ≤4% loss is plausible, but without decision accuracy correlations, it could be dataset-dependent).

## Next Checks
1. **Decision calibration test**: Annotate 200–500 questions per dataset as "recall" vs. "reasoning" (e.g., via expert clinicians). Measure alignment between model's "yes/no" decisions and these labels; target >85% agreement.
2. **Per-type accuracy ablation**: On the annotated subsets, compare Selective CoT’s accuracy (direct vs. CoT paths) against always-CoT and always-direct baselines. Expect: direct path ≥ always-direct on recall; CoT path ≥ always-CoT on reasoning.
3. **Prompt sensitivity analysis**: Re-run Selective CoT with at least three phrasing variants for the "reasoning needed" query (e.g., "Does this require step-by-step thinking?" vs. "Is logic needed?"). Quantify variance in decision distribution and accuracy to assess robustness to prompt wording.
