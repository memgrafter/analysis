# Large-Scale 3D Ground-Motion Synthesis with Physics-Inspired Latent Operator Flow Matching

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17403  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17403  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** GMFlow generates realistic 3D ground-motion wavefields at a ~10,000× speedup over physics simulations, with accurate spatiotemporal patterns and magnitude scaling, but underestimates high frequencies for large finite ruptures.

## Executive Summary
Earthquake hazard assessment requires large ensembles of spatially coherent ground-motion time histories, but generating them via physics-based simulations is computationally infeasible. This paper presents GMFlow, a two-stage framework that combines a physics-inspired latent space with conditional flow matching and a super-resolution neural operator to produce realistic 3D wavefields conditioned on earthquake parameters. The method first generates a low-frequency wavefield in a compact latent space using flow matching, then reconstructs full-band outputs via a neural operator. Evaluated on thousands of San Francisco Bay Area scenarios, GMFlow yields coherent wavefields spanning over 9 million grid points in seconds—a speedup of roughly 10,000× over conventional workflows—while accurately capturing peak ground velocity, Fourier amplitude spectra, and magnitude scaling. However, it exhibits a systematic underestimation of high-frequency amplitudes for large finite-rupture events, which can be mitigated by a post-hoc frequency-dependent calibration.

## Method Summary
GMFlow employs a two-stage generative process. Raw SW4 simulations are preprocessed: the full wavefield is low-pass filtered at 0.75 Hz and downsampled to produce a coarse low-frequency representation *u*<sub>f</sub>. This *u*<sub>f</sub> is encoded by an autoencoding neural operator (AENO) into a compact latent code *z*<sub>1</sub> (shape 1×32×16×16). Conditional rectified flow, implemented with a diffusion U‑Net, learns to transport a Gaussian prior *z*<sub>0</sub> to *z*<sub>1</sub> given earthquake parameters (hypocenter, magnitude). At inference, an ODE solver integrates the learned velocity field for ~50 steps to obtain *z*<sub>1</sub>, which the AENO decoder maps back to *û*<sub>f</sub>. Finally, a super-resolution neural operator (SNO), based on a Fourier Neural Operator, lifts *û*<sub>f</sub> to a full‑band, high‑resolution wavefield *û*. The entire pipeline operates in seconds and can produce arbitrarily resolved outputs thanks to the discretization‑agnostic neural operators.

## Key Results
- **~10,000× speedup** over SW4 simulations while maintaining spatial coherence across 9+ million grid points.
- **Accurate reproduction** of spatiotemporal wavefront patterns, peak ground velocity, Fourier amplitude spectra, and magnitude scaling trends.
- **Systematic underestimation** of high‑frequency amplitudes (>0.5 Hz) for Mw 6.0–7.0 finite‑rupture events, correctable via frequency‑dependent calibration.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Physics‑Aligned Subspace
- **Claim:** A low‑dimensional **Physics‑Aligned Subspace** enables tractable generation of spatially coherent 3D wavefields by isolating the most robustly resolved low‑frequency content.  
- **Mechanism:** The full high‑frequency wavefield *u* is filtered and downsampled to a low‑frequency representation *u*<sub>f</sub> (cutoff *f*<sub>c</sub>=0.75 Hz). This *u*<sub>f</sub> is encoded into a compact latent code *z*<sub>1</sub>. Stochastic generation via flow matching operates only in this latent space, which is orders of magnitude smaller than the original space. The generator *G*<sub>θ</sub> learns the conditional distribution of *z*<sub>1</sub> given event parameters *c* (magnitude, hypocenter).  
- **Core assumption:** The dominant large‑scale spatiotemporal coherence and source‑driven variability of the wavefield are preserved in the low‑frequency band, making it a sufficient conditioning for reconstructing the full field.  
- **Evidence anchors:** The method uses a two‑stage pipeline with a conditional flow‑matching model in the compact latent space (abstract, Sec. 4.3). The subspace definition *H*<sub>f</sub> := Range(*A*<sub>f</sub>) reduces computational cost (Sec. 4.3).  
- **Break condition:** If the low‑frequency band fails to encode sufficient information about source location, magnitude scaling, or basin effects, the generated *z*<sub>1</sub> will not conditionally represent the target wavefield statistics, leading to degraded PGV/FAS patterns and coherence even after super‑resolution.

### Mechanism 2: Conditional Flow Matching in Latent Space
- **Claim:** **Conditional flow matching** in the latent space provides an efficient, simulation‑free way to sample from the complex conditional distribution *p*(*z*<sub>1</sub> | *c*).  
- **Mechanism:** A neural velocity field *v*<sub>θ</sub>(*z*<sub>t</sub>, *t*, *c*) is trained to move samples from a Gaussian prior *z*<sub>0</sub>~*N*(0,*I*) along linear probability paths *z*<sub>t</sub> = (1−*t*)*z*<sub>0</sub> + *t z*<sub>1</sub> toward encoded latent samples *z*<sub>1</sub>. The clean‑prediction variant predicts *z*<sub>1</sub> directly from *z*<sub>t</sub> and converts it to a velocity, improving training stability. Once trained, an ODE solver generates *z*<sub>1</sub> from *z*<sub>0</sub> conditioned on *c* in ~50 steps.  
- **Core assumption:** The data manifold of latent codes *z*<sub>1</sub> is low‑dimensional and can be navigated via straight‑line coupling paths; the conditional distribution *p*(*z*<sub>1</sub> | *c*) is learnable by a neural operator conditioned on the low‑dimensional physical parameters *c*.  
- **Evidence anchors:** The conditional velocity field *v*<sub>θ</sub> is trained with the conditional rectified‑flow objective (Sec. 4.3). Inference uses a 50‑step Euler solver (Sec. 4.3).  
- **Break condition:** If the conditional velocity field *v*<sub>θ</sub> cannot accurately learn the direction toward valid *z*<sub>1</sub> samples (e.g., due to insufficient model capacity or poor conditioning on *c*), the ODE integration will produce latent codes that decode to non‑physical or incoherent low‑frequency fields, which the super‑resolution operator cannot fully repair.

### Mechanism 3: Super‑Resolution Neural Operator
- **Claim:** A **super‑resolution neural operator** (SNO) lifts the generated low‑frequency field *û*<sub>f</sub> to a full‑band, high‑resolution wavefield *û* while preserving spatiotemporal coherence and phase.  
- **Mechanism:** The SNO *S*<sub>ϕ</sub> is a Fourier Neural Operator trained on paired samples (*u*<sub>f</sub>, *u*). It maps a coarse grid representation of *u*<sub>f</sub> to a fine grid output *û*. Because it is a neural operator, it can evaluate *S*<sub>ϕ</sub>(*û*<sub>f</sub>) at any spatial resolution (zero‑shot super‑resolution) while maintaining discretization‑agnosticism.  
- **Core assumption:** The mapping from low‑frequency content to the full‑band field is deterministic and learnable, given the fixed background velocity model and source conditions. The missing high‑frequency information (scattering, site amplification) is largely a function of the low‑frequency wavefield and the known medium.  
- **Evidence anchors:** The SNO reconstructs full‑band outputs (abstract, Sec. 4.3). It enables coherent wavefields across 9+ million grid points (abstract). Zero‑shot super‑resolution yields visually coherent and physically plausible results (Sec. D).  
- **Break condition:** If the SNO cannot generalize from the training resolution to finer grids or cannot reconstruct high‑frequency scattering and basin amplification patterns (especially for complex finite ruptures), the output will exhibit underestimation of high‑frequency amplitudes or spatial artifacts.

## Foundational Learning
- **Neural Operators**  
  *Why needed:* The problem requires modeling a continuous spatiotemporal function at arbitrary resolutions; standard CNNs are fixed‑grid. Neural operators learn mappings between function spaces, enabling discretization‑agnostic inference.  
  *Quick check:* How does an FNO's Fourier layer capture global spatial dependencies differently from a CNN's convolutional layer, and why is this important for wavefield synthesis?
- **Flow Matching / Rectified Flow**  
  *Why needed:* Direct generative modeling in a high‑dimensional latent space requires efficient sampling; flow matching provides simulation‑free deterministic ODE paths, avoiding the instability or slow sampling of adversarial or diffusion models while maintaining expressivity.  
  *Quick check:* What is the "clean prediction" variant of flow matching, and why might predicting *z*<sub>1</sub> directly be more stable than predicting the velocity *v* during training?
- **Conditional Generative Modeling for Physical Fields**  
  *Why needed:* The goal is to generate scenario‑specific wavefields conditioned on earthquake parameters (magnitude, hypocenter). The model must learn a mapping from low‑dimensional physical causes to a high‑dimensional spatiotemporal effect field while respecting physical constraints like coherence and scaling.  
  *Quick check:* What are the risks of a conditional generator ignoring the conditioning information *c* (e.g., collapsing to a mean field), and how does GMFlow's architecture mitigate this?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Physics‑aligned preprocessing (low‑pass filter & downsample) → Encoder (*E*<sub>ψ</sub>) → Conditional flow matching (sample *z*<sub>1</sub>) → Decoder (*D*<sub>ψ</sub>) → Super‑resolution neural operator (*S*<sub>ϕ</sub>) → Full‑band wavefield.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Prepare simulation dataset: generate SW4 simulations, preprocess to obtain *u*<sub>f</sub> and *u*.  
  2. Train the autoencoding neural operator (AENO) for *u*<sub>f</sub> reconstruction.  
  3. Train the conditional flow matching model using latent codes from AENO.  
  4. Train the super‑resolution neural operator (SNO) on (*u*<sub>f</sub>, *u*) pairs.  
  5. End‑to‑end inference: condition *c* → flow matching → *z*<sub>1</sub> → *D*<sub>ψ</sub> → *û*<sub>f</sub> → *S*<sub>ϕ</sub> → *û*.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Cutoff frequency (*f*<sub>c</sub>)**: Lower *f*<sub>c</sub> reduces latent size but discards mid‑frequency info, making SNO harder; higher *f*<sub>c</sub> increases complexity. Paper uses 0.75 Hz as balance.  
  - **Latent dimensionality**: Smaller latent tensors ease flow matching but risk info loss; larger models (128M vs 16M) improve performance on complex events.  
  - **Operator choice**: FNO enables zero‑shot super‑resolution on uniform grids but cannot handle irregular domains; alternatives like MINO are proposed for such cases.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Underestimation of high‑frequency amplitudes for large finite‑rupture events (Mw 6.0+).  
  - Spatial incoherence (smeared wavefronts, phase errors).  
  - Magnitude scaling failure (non‑monotonic PGV/FAS with magnitude).  
  - Overly deterministic outputs (low ensemble variability).  
  - Bandlimiting artifacts from inadequate filtering/downsampling.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **AENO reconstruction sanity check**: Visualize decoded *u*<sub>f</sub> vs. original, ensure low L2 loss and feature preservation.  
  2. **Flow matching latent sampling quality**: Sample *z*<sub>1</sub> for held‑out conditions, decode to *û*<sub>f</sub>, compare ensemble statistics (mean PGV, FAS maps) to ground‑truth *u*<sub>f</sub>.  
  3. **SNO zero‑shot super‑resolution validation**: Train SNO at grid [256,128], then evaluate on finer grid [512,256] without retraining; compare to reference simulations for coherence and high‑frequency content.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Enhancing high‑frequency fidelity for complex ruptures**  
   The paper currently underestimates >0.5 Hz for Mw ≥ 6.0 events. The physics‑aligned subspace and model capacity may not encode high‑frequency scattering effects adequately. Resolution would require architectural changes (e.g., larger latent space) validated by zero spectral residual without calibration.

2. **Extending to irregular domains**  
   FFT‑based FNO cannot handle topography‑following meshes or sparse station networks. Mesh‑Informed Neural Operators (MINO) are suggested as promising but not implemented. Evidence would be coherent wavefield generation on such domains.

3. **Fusing physics simulations and real observations**  
   Simulations lack high‑frequency realism; observations are sparse and unevenly distributed. A unified training framework combining both data sources is needed but challenging due to discrepancies. Evidence would be improved agreement with independent real recordings across frequency bands.

4. **Generalizing to other natural hazards**  
   The authors propose GMFlow could be adapted to wildfires, tsunamis, etc., but domain‑specific physics and data modalities differ. Evidence would be successful adaptation to at least one other hazard with similar efficiency gains and validation.

## Limitations
- Systematic underestimation of high‑frequency amplitudes for large finite‑rupture events (Mw 6.0–7.0), especially above 0.5 Hz.
- Potential insufficiency of the low‑frequency subspace (*f*<sub>c</sub>=0.75 Hz) to encode necessary source and site effects for accurate high‑frequency reconstruction.
- Lack of geographic generalization: model trained and tested only on the San Francisco Bay Area; transfer to other regions is untested.

## Confidence
- Latent flow matching generates coherent low‑frequency wavefields: **High**  
- Low‑frequency subspace captures essential source/basin effects: **Medium**  
- SNO generalizes to arbitrary resolutions while preserving coherence: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Cutoff frequency ablation:** Vary *f*<sub>c</sub> (0.5–1.0 Hz) and measure trade‑offs in latent size, training stability, and high‑frequency FAS error on finite‑rupture events.
2. **Regional transfer test:** Apply the trained model (without retraining) to a Los Angeles basin simulation dataset; evaluate coherence, PGV, and FAS against reference simulations.
