# Symbolic Graph Networks for Robust PDE Discovery from Noisy Sparse Data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22380
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22380
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: SGN recovers governing PDEs from noisy, sparse spatiotemporal data with robustness up to 10% noise, outperforming baselines that fail at lower noise levels.

## Executive Summary
Discovering governing partial differential equations (PDEs) from observational data is a fundamental scientific task, but real-world measurements are often noisy and sparsely sampled. This paper introduces Symbolic Graph Networks (SGN), a two-stage framework that replaces fragile numerical differentiation with a graph-based non-local representation. SGN uses message-passing graph neural networks to aggregate spatial information, providing robust features that feed into symbolic regression (via PySR) to extract interpretable PDEs. The method incorporates a geometric warm-start using a Savitzky‑Golay filter and adaptive noise injection during training to enhance stability under corruption.

Experiments on canonical PDEs—the 2D wave equation, convection‑diffusion, and incompressible Navier‑Stokes—demonstrate SGN’s exceptional noise robustness. While baselines like PDE‑Net 2.0 collapse at just 0.2% noise, SGN accurately recovers equations up to 10% noise. For Navier‑Stokes, it identifies the precise temporal decay multiplier with sub‑0.0006% error at 0.1% noise, confirming both high accuracy and resilience in sparse-data regimes.

## Method Summary
SGN comprises two tightly coupled stages. First, the Graph Neural Simulator constructs a spatial graph from uniformly gridded data, connecting each node to its nearby neighbors. Message‑passing layers (with MLP‑based edge and node update functions) propagate information across the graph, effectively learning integral‑like operators that are robust to local noise. A Savitzky‑Golay polynomial filter is applied to the raw observations before graph construction, providing a smooth geometric initialization. During training, adaptive noise injection—scaled by an estimate of the noise level—exposes the simulator to realistic corruptions, further improving generalization.

Second, the trained simulator outputs latent node messages and aggregated states. These features are fed to a Symbolic Inverse Analyzer that employs PySR’s genetic algorithm in two sequential steps: it first distills the message function ψ (mapping sender and receiver states to a low‑dimensional message) and then the update function ϕ (mapping receiver state plus aggregated messages to the next state). The message dimension is intentionally constrained (1–2 dimensions) to keep the subsequent symbolic search tractable. The final discovered PDE is expressed as the composition f_upd ∘ Agg ∘ f_msg.

## Key Results
- SGN reliably recovers the exact governing PDEs under additive Gaussian noise up to 10%, whereas competing methods (e.g., PDE‑Net 2.0) fail much earlier (NaNs at 0.2% noise).
- On the 2D incompressible Navier‑Stokes (Taylor‑Green vortex), SGN identifies the temporal decay multiplier λ ≈ e^{-2νε} with an error of less than 0.0006% at 0.1% noise.
- Across all three test systems (wave, convection‑diffusion, Navier‑Stokes), SGN maintains stable training and accurate rollouts on sparse grids (32×32 spatial resolution, 100 time steps), demonstrating superior robustness to both noise and limited data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core of SGN’s robustness lies in its non‑local graph representation. By aggregating information from multiple neighboring nodes, message passing smooths out high‑frequency noise that would otherwise corrupt pointwise derivatives. The Savitzky‑Golay filter provides an initial derivative estimate that respects the underlying geometry, preventing divergence early in training. Adaptive noise injection ensures the simulator learns to operate under the same corruption levels encountered at test time. Finally, the two‑stage symbolic regression focuses on low‑dimensional messages, drastically reducing the search space for PySR while preserving enough expressive power to capture the true PDE. This combination yields features that are both faithful to the physics and conducive to symbolic discovery.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data -> Graph Construction -> Graph Neural Simulator (Message Passing) -> Feature Extraction -> Symbolic Inverse Analyzer (PySR) -> Discovered PDE

**Critical path**: The end‑to‑end flow starts with noisy spatiotemporal data, passes through GNN training (where the geometric warm‑start and adaptive noise are applied), then extracts latent messages and states, and finally performs a two‑stage symbolic regression to produce the final equation. Any break in this pipeline (e.g., improper graph connectivity or symbolic search failure) prevents successful discovery.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Message dimension*: Low dimensionality (1–2) simplifies symbolic regression but may limit expressiveness; higher dimensions risk combinatorial explosion in PySR.  
- *Number of message‑passing layers*: More layers increase the receptive field but can cause over‑smoothing and amplify noise.  
- *Savitzky‑Golay window size*: Larger windows yield smoother derivatives but may blur sharp gradients; smaller windows preserve details at the cost of noise sensitivity.  
- *Aggregation function (sum vs. mean)*: Sum retains scale information, mean provides normalization; the choice affects how physical units are absorbed.  
- *Noise injection strength*: Too little reduces robustness; too much impedes learning on clean aspects of the data.

**Failure signatures**  
- GNN training diverges (NaNs or exploding loss) often due to numerical instability or overly aggressive learning rates.  
- Over‑smoothed node messages (low variance) indicate excessive message passing or too large a neighborhood, which can lead to loss of discriminative features for symbolic regression.  
- Symbolic regression produces overly complex or physically nonsensical expressions (e.g., negative diffusion coefficients), signaling an imbalance between parsimony and fit.  
- High rollout MSE on clean test data despite low training loss suggests overfitting to noise or poor generalization of the simulator.

**First experiments**  
1. **Baseline verification**: Train SGN on the 2D wave equation with 0.2% noise; confirm that the discovered PDE matches the analytical solution (standing wave) and that rollout MSE is near machine precision.  
2. **Noise robustness test**: Incrementally increase noise to 5% and 10%; compare SGN’s MSE against baselines (e.g., PDE‑Net 2.0) and verify training stability (no NaNs).  
3. **Ablation of Savitzky‑Golay**: Disable the geometric warm‑start and repeat training at a moderate noise level (1%); quantify the impact on convergence speed and final accuracy.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter ambiguity**: Exact GNN layer counts, hidden dimensions, optimizer settings, and Savitzky‑Golay parameters are not reported, hindering precise replication.
- **Graph connectivity details**: The neighbor selection strategy (fixed k‑NN or radius) is unspecified, yet it significantly influences the non‑local interactions and noise handling.
- **Symbolic regression configuration**: PySR hyperparameters (population size, generations, parsimony) and the enforcement of low‑dimensional messages are opaque, potentially leading to variability in discovered equations.
