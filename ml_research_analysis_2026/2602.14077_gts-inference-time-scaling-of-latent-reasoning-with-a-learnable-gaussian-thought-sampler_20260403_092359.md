# GTS: Inference-Time Scaling of Latent Reasoning with a Learnable Gaussian Thought Sampler

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14077
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14077
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: GTS replaces heuristic noise with a learned contextual Gaussian policy, trained via GRPO, to improve inference-time scaling in latent reasoning models, yielding higher pass@N on GSM8K and OOD benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Latent reasoning models such as COCONUT and CODI perform multi-step reasoning in continuous hidden states. Standard inference-time scaling (ITS) via multiple sampling often relies on heuristic perturbations (e.g., dropout, fixed Gaussian noise), which can cause harmful distribution shifts and fail to improve performance at larger sampling budgets. This paper introduces the Gaussian Thought Sampler (GTS), a lightweight module that learns a context-dependent Gaussian perturbation policy explicitly optimized for ITS outcomes. GTS is trained with a GRPO-style reinforcement learning objective while the backbone model remains frozen. Experiments on GSM8K and out-of-distribution arithmetic benchmarks show that GTS consistently outperforms heuristic baselines, particularly for moderate-to-large N (e.g., 32–128). Analysis confirms that GTS achieves controlled, adaptive exploration across reasoning steps, avoiding the under- and over-exploitation modes of fixed perturbations. The work demonstrates that effective latent ITS requires a learnable, reward-guided sampling distribution rather than mere stochasticity amplification.

## Method Summary
The GTS module attaches to a frozen latent reasoning backbone (e.g., COCONUT, CODI). At each latent reasoning step, the backbone produces a deterministic latent state \(h_k^{\text{det}}\). GTS takes this state as input and outputs parameters \((\mu_k, \sigma_k)\) of a diagonal Gaussian distribution. A perturbation \(z_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \text{diag}(\sigma_k^2))\) is sampled and added to the latent state: \(\tilde{h}_k = h_k^{\text{det}} + z_k\). The perturbed state then serves as input for the next reasoning step, enabling multiple stochastic trajectories.

During training, for a batch of prompts the model rolls out \(N\) perturbed trajectories per prompt using the current GTS policy. Each trajectory is decoded to an answer \(a^{(i)}\), and a reward \(r^{(i)}\) is computed based on correctness (indicator of matching ground truth) and confidence (normalized likelihood). The policy gradient from GRPO updates the GTS parameters \(\phi\) to increase the probability of high-reward trajectories while penalizing deviation from an exponential moving average reference policy (via a KL penalty). The backbone remains frozen. At inference, the trained GTS is used to generate \(N\) samples; the final prediction is obtained by aggregating the answers (e.g., majority vote).

## Key Results
- GTS achieves higher pass@N than heuristic baselines (dropout, fixed Gaussian) on GSM8K and OOD arithmetic benchmarks, particularly for \(N = 32\) to \(128\).
- GTS provides controlled distribution shift, avoiding the under-exploration of weak noise and the over-exploration of strong noise regimes.
- Ablation studies show that reward shaping with confidence improves performance over accuracy-only rewards, confirming the importance of dense guidance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Context-Dependent Gaussian Perturbations Enable Principled Exploration
- Claim: Replacing heuristic noise with a learned conditional Gaussian distribution over perturbations enables principled, context-aware exploration in latent space.
- Mechanism: GTS parameterizes a diagonal Gaussian policy \(q_\phi(z_k \mid c_k)\) where mean and variance are functions of the current deterministic latent state \(c_k = h_k^{\text{det}}\). This defines an explicit, differentiable sampling distribution over continuous perturbations \(z_k\), which are added to the latent state before the next reasoning step.
- Core assumption: The deterministic latent state contains sufficient information to predict useful perturbation directions and magnitudes; the backbone's dynamics are stable enough that small, directed perturbations can alter trajectory outcomes without breaking coherence.
- Evidence: Abstract and Section 3.2 describe the conditional Gaussian parameterization. Related work on explicit latent distributions supports the idea but not specifically for ITS.
- Break condition: If the frozen backbone’s latent representations are poorly calibrated or lack directional sensitivity, the mean predictions may be near-zero, causing the sampler to collapse to variance-only, offering little steering.

### Mechanism 2: GRPO Optimization Directly Targets ITS Performance
- Claim: Training the sampler with a reinforcement learning objective (GRPO) directly optimizes for inference-time scaling performance (e.g., pass@N) rather than surrogate likelihoods.
- Mechanism: For a batch of prompts, GTS rolls out \(N\) perturbed latent trajectories per prompt, decodes greedy answers, and computes rewards based on correctness and confidence. Using the closed-form log-density of the diagonal Gaussian policy, it computes a density ratio against an exponential moving average reference policy. The GRPO-clipped objective updates \(\phi\) to increase probability of trajectories with high group-normalized advantages, while a KL penalty prevents excessive deviation.
- Core assumption: The reward signal (correctness ± confidence) provides a dense enough gradient to guide the continuous perturbation policy toward regions yielding more correct answers under best-of-N selection; group-normalized advantage stabilizes learning by providing relative rankings within each prompt’s sample set.
- Evidence: Section 3.3 details the reward definition and GRPO objective. Section 5.3 ablation shows dense reward outperforms accuracy-only.
- Break condition: If base model confidence is poorly calibrated, the shaping term could mislead; sparse rewards (few correct answers) would yield high-variance gradients, destabilizing training.

### Mechanism 3: Adaptive Variance Balances Exploration Across Steps
- Claim: Context-dependent variance control allows the sampler to adaptively balance exploration and exploitation across latent reasoning steps and different backbone architectures, avoiding the under- or over-exploitation failure modes of fixed perturbations.
- Mechanism: The GTS predicts both mean and log-standard-deviation per dimension at each step. During training, the policy learns to increase variance in steps/dimensions where diversity leads to more correct answers (measured via reward) and decrease it where perturbations are harmful.
- Core assumption: Different latent reasoning steps have varying sensitivity to perturbations; some require subtle refinements (low variance), others benefit from diverse branching (high variance). A single global noise scale cannot capture this structure.
- Evidence: Section 2.4 shows heuristic methods exhibit under- and over-exploration extremes, while GTS avoids them. Section 5.2 step-wise SNR analysis (Figure 4) shows learned variance adapts across steps (e.g., alternating for COCONUT vs. front-loaded for CODI), indicating step-specific context adaptation.
- Break condition: If the backbone’s latent dynamics are extremely fragile, the sampler might suppress variance entirely, reverting to deterministic reasoning. If the reward landscape is flat (no correct trajectories), the sampler might maximize variance randomly, leading to chaos.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Inference-Time Scaling (ITS)**
  - Why needed here: ITS is the overarching problem GTS addresses. Understanding how allocating more test-time compute via multiple trajectory sampling improves accuracy is essential. The key distinction is between discrete token-level sampling (with explicit distributions) and latent space sampling (lacking such distributions), which creates the need for GTS.
  - Quick check question: Why can't we simply use temperature scaling or nucleus sampling directly in latent reasoning models as we do with token-based LLMs?
- **Concept: Policy Gradient Methods (e.g., PPO/GRPO)**
  - Why needed here: GTS is trained with a GRPO-style objective. The engineer must understand clipping, KL penalties, and group-normalized advantages to grasp how the sampler is optimized for non-differentiable ITS outcomes without destabilizing the policy.
  - Quick check question: How does the clipping mechanism in GRPO (or PPO) prevent destructive policy updates when the density ratio \(\rho\) becomes very large or small?
- **Concept: Latent Reasoning Models (e.g., COCONUT, CODI)**
  - Why needed here: GTS is a plug-in module for such architectures. The engineer needs to know how these models perform multi-step reasoning in continuous hidden states (e.g., recursive latent token generation) and where stochasticity should be injected (e.g., between latent steps, not during final answer decoding).
  - Quick check question: In a model like COCONUT, what is the role of the `<start_latent>` and `<lat_k>` tokens, and why are perturbations only applied up to `<lat_5>` but not `<lat_6>`?

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component Map
Frozen Backbone -> GTS Module -> Perturbation Injection -> Rollout & Reward -> GRPO Optimizer

#### Critical Path
During training: (a) For a batch of prompts, run the frozen backbone to obtain deterministic latent states \(h_k^{\text{det}}\) at each step. (b) The GTS Module produces parameters \((\mu_k, \sigma_k)\) for each step; sample perturbations \(z_k\); inject them to get perturbed states \(\tilde{h}_k\). (c) Roll out \(N\) full perturbed trajectories per prompt, decode greedy answers \(a^{(i)}\), compute rewards \(r^{(i)}\). (d) Compute the GRPO loss using the log-densities of the perturbations and the advantages, backpropagate through GTS only, update parameters \(\phi\) and the reference policy via EMA. During inference: (a) Load trained GTS. (b) For each sample, run the backbone with GTS perturbations, decode greedy answers. (c) Aggregate \(N\) answers (e.g., majority vote) to produce the final prediction.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- Diagonal vs. full-covariance Gaussian: Diagonal enables closed-form log-density and KL, stable training, but cannot capture cross-dimensional correlations. Full-covariance offers more expressive steering but introduces \(O(D^2)\) parameters, stability risks, and higher sample complexity.
- Additive vs. other transformations: Additive perturbations (\(\tilde{h} = h + z\)) preserve original dynamics and have unit Jacobian, simplifying density computation. Multiplicative gating or affine transformations could offer flexibility but would entangle sampler with backbone weights, complicating optimization and potentially destabilizing the frozen backbone.
- Frozen backbone vs. end-to-end: Freezing the backbone avoids catastrophic forgetting and focuses learning on the sampler. However, it limits the model’s ability to adapt its internal dynamics to the learned perturbations, possibly creating a mismatch.

#### Failure Signatures
- Policy collapse to deterministic: Log-std consistently hits lower clamp (e.g., \(-2.0\)) across steps, indicating no learned exploration. Likely due to overly strong KL penalty or insufficient reward diversity.
- High distribution shift without SG improvement: JS divergence is high but SG and pass@N are not better (or worse). Indicates over-exploration: perturbations too large or misdirected, destroying useful latent information.
- High variance in small-N performance: Large drop in pass@2 vs. pass@1 compared to baselines. Suggests sampler’s perturbations are too disruptive at low budgets; could be mitigated by budget-aware variance scaling.

Expect non-uniform SNR across steps, indicating adaptive perturbation control; check if pattern aligns with backbone’s latent dynamics (e.g., alternating for COCONUT).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the Gaussian Thought Sampler (GTS) generalize to more open-ended, long-form, or non-mathematical reasoning settings?
   - Basis: Although we evaluate GTS beyond the training distribution on multiple arithmetic reasoning benchmarks, our empirical scope remains limited to relatively short, answer-focused math tasks and does not yet cover more open-ended, long-form, or non-mathematical reasoning settings.
   - Why unresolved: The current evaluation only covers arithmetic math benchmarks (GSM8K, MultiArith, SVAMP, GSM8K-Hard). It is unknown whether the learned perturbation policy transfers to tasks requiring extended generation, diverse answer types, or less structured evaluation.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Testing GTS on a variety of long-form reasoning tasks (e.g., essay writing, multi-step planning, commonsense QA with paragraphs) and across domains (e.g., science, humanities) would show if the approach generalizes. Success would be pass@N improvements consistent with math tasks.
2. Would using a richer family of perturbation distributions (e.g., full-covariance Gaussian, mixture models, normalizing flows) yield better sampling quality and ITS performance?
   - Basis: We restrict the sampling policy to a diagonal Gaussian distribution and do not explore broader perturbation families that may offer different flexibility-stability trade-offs.
   - Why unresolved: Diagonal Gaussian may limit ability to capture correlations across latent dimensions, potentially missing beneficial directional perturbations. Unknown if more expressive distributions improve pass@N.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Train GTS with alternative parameterizations, compare sampling quality and pass@N on same benchmarks while controlling for policy capacity. Possibly analyze if learned correlation patterns matter.
3. Can we develop a formal theoretical understanding of effective exploration in continuous latent reasoning spaces?
   - Basis: Our analysis of sampling behavior remains empirical and does not provide a formal theoretical characterization of exploration in high-dimensional latent spaces.
   - Why unresolved: Without theory, it’s hard to predict why certain perturbations work, guide design, or extend to new models. The empirical observations (under/over-exploration) need principled explanation.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Derive theoretical bounds relating perturbation magnitude, latent dimensionality, model Lipschitz constants, and success probability of finding correct trajectories. Validate predictions with controlled experiments varying latent dimension and model architecture.
4. How would GTS perform with alternative continuous reasoning formulations beyond COCONUT and CODI?
   - Basis: We study two representative latent reasoning architectures, and the behavior of learnable perturbation policies may differ under alternative continuous reasoning formulations.
   - Why unresolved: The two models have different latent dynamics; GTS might interact differently with other approaches (e.g., SoftCoT++, Token Assorted, etc.). Unknown if GTS universally beneficial.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Implement GTS on other latent reasoning models (e.g., those with different latent step numbers, recurrent vs. transformer-based latent loops) and evaluate ITS scaling. Compare adaptation needed and performance gains.

## Limitations
- Generalizability limited to two latent reasoning architectures (COCONUT, CODI); performance on other models unknown.
- No diagnostics of training stability or sample efficiency; convergence and gradient behavior not reported.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity not fully explored (e.g., KL weight, reward shaping coefficients); risk of overfitting to the pass@N reward signal.

- GRPO optimization (Mechanism 2): Medium
- Adaptive variance (Mechanism 3): High
