# Conformal Policy Control
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02196
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02196
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Conformal Policy Control (CPC) enables safe exploration by regulating an optimized policy via a likelihood‑ratio bound β, calibrated using generalized conformal risk control (gCRC) to provide finite‑sample guarantees even for non‑monotonic constraint losses, leading to improved performance while preventing violations across medical QA, active learning, and sequence optimization.

## Executive Summary
Conformal Policy Control (CPC) introduces a novel framework for safe exploration in sequential decision‑making. It leverages a trusted safe policy to constrain an optimized policy through a likelihood‑ratio bound β, which is automatically calibrated using generalized conformal risk control (gCRC). This calibration, performed on historical data from the safe policy, yields finite‑sample guarantees for constraint risks even when the loss is non‑monotonic, without additional model assumptions or hyperparameter tuning beyond the user‑specified risk tolerance α. CPC operates via rejection sampling, allowing the optimized policy to be used wherever it is safe according to the calibrated β. Experiments across three distinct domains—medical question answering with false discovery rate control, constrained active learning for regression, and black‑box sequence optimization—demonstrate that CPC not only prevents constraint violations as guaranteed but often enhances performance relative to uncontrolled baselines or prior safe methods.

## Method Summary
CPC constructs a constrained policy π(β) that interpolates between a safe policy π₀ and an optimized policy πₜ by clipping their likelihood ratio: π(β)(x) ∝ min(πₜ(x), β·π₀(x)). The bound β controls deviation from safety; larger β admits higher‑variance actions. To select β, CPC employs generalized conformal risk control (gCRC) on a calibration dataset Dcal containing constraint losses. Since Dcal may originate from a mixture of prior policies, CPC uses importance weights w_i(β) = π(β)(x_i)/πₘᵢₓ(x_i) to correct distribution shift, and includes a conservative estimate for the test point weight. The calibrator searches β from a safe minimum upward, stopping when the weighted empirical risk plus a high‑probability bound exceeds the allowed risk α. This yields the largest β that provably controls the constraint risk for any new deployment, without requiring monotonicity of the loss. At inference, actions are generated via rejection sampling from π₀ or πₜ according to the acceptance probability min(π(β)(a)/(c·proposal(a)),1), thereby realizing π(β).

## Key Results
- **Medical QA**: CPC controls false discovery rate at target α while achieving higher recall than prior methods.
- **Constrained active learning**: CPC achieves valid risk control and lowers test MSE compared to uncontrolled acquisition, sometimes outperforming it.
- **Black‑box sequence optimization**: CPC provides first conformal risk guarantees; moderate risk levels stabilize optimization and improve the best score found.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Likelihood‑Ratio Clipping for Policy Interpolation
CPC constrains the optimized policy πₜ by truncating its likelihood ratio relative to a safe policy π₀ via a bound β, yielding π(β)(x) ∝ min(πₜ(x), β·π₀(x)) (Eq. 5). As β increases, more high‑variance regions of πₜ are included, directly trading off safety for performance. The calibration process (Section 4.2) selects β to control constraint risk, assuming π₀ has non‑negligible density on the support of πₜ and that likelihood ratios are computable. If πₜ has mass where π₀ is zero, clipping can cause complete rejection even for large β; similarly, an outdated or overly narrow safe policy may yield unsafe outcomes despite clipping.

### Mechanism 2: Generalized Conformal Risk Control for Non‑Monotonic Losses
gCRC allows CPC to handle constraint losses that are not monotonic in β (e.g., false discovery rate). The algorithm searches β from a safe minimum βₘᵢₙ upward, maintaining that for all β′ ≤ current candidate the (conservatively weighted) empirical risk does not exceed α. This “for all” condition ensures a conservative selection without monotonicity assumptions (Section 4.1, Theorem 4.2). The guarantee holds under bounded losses (by B), Lipschitz continuity of the weighted risk in β, and replace‑one stability of the calibration data. Violations (e.g., abrupt weight changes or dependent data) can degrade validity to α + Kϵ, and unbounded losses invalidate the bound.

### Mechanism 3: Weighted Conformal Importance Weighting
Because calibration data Dcal originates from a mixture of historical policies, CPC corrects the resulting distribution shift using importance weights w_i(β) = π(β)(x_i)/πₘᵢₓ(x_i), where πₘᵢₓ approximates the generative mixture (Section C.1.3). A conservative estimate for the test point weight, wₘₐₓ, is obtained from a fresh proposal set from πₜ. The selected β satisfies Σ_i ˜w_i ℓ_i + wₘₐₓ·B ≤ α. This approach ensures unbiased risk estimation under covariate shift, provided πₘᵢₓ covers the support of π(β) and weights have finite variance. If πₘᵢₓ is inaccurate (e.g., due to forgetting old policies), weights may explode, leading to unstable β and poor risk control.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Calibration dataset Dcal (from safe/prior interactions) → Mixture density πₘᵢₓ (approximation of historical policy distribution). Safe policy π₀ and optimized policy πₜ provide likelihood ratios for candidate β values. Conformal calibrator uses α, Dcal, πₘᵢₓ, π₀, πₜ to select β via gCRC. Selected β and policies are fed to rejection sampler, which produces actions by sampling proposals (from π₀ or πₜ) and accepting with probability min(π(β)(a)/(c·proposal(a)),1). Deployment context x yields action a; new experience (x,a,ℓ) may update Dcal for future rounds. A conservative test weight estimator wₘₐₓ uses a fresh proposal set from πₜ.

### Critical Path
1. **Collect calibration data**: Gather Dcal consisting of contexts, actions, and constraint losses from the safe policy or earlier constrained policies.
2. **Train optimized policy**: Obtain πₜ by any improvement method (SFT, DPO, RL) using reward data.
3. **Estimate likelihood ratios**: Sample from πₜ to compute a grid of ratio values; also use these samples to estimate ψ(β) and the conservative test weight wₘₐₓ.
4. **Calibrate β**: For increasing β, compute π(β) on Dcal, derive importance weights w_i = π(β)(x_i)/πₘᵢₓ(x_i), normalize to ˜w_i, and evaluate empirical risk Σ_i ˜w_i ℓ_i plus ˜w_max·B. Stop when this sum exceeds α; return the previous β (or βₘᵢₙ if none passes).
5. **Deploy**: For each new context x, draw a proposal action from π₀ (or πₜ), compute acceptance probability = min(π(β)(a)/(c·proposal(a)),1) with c=β for π₀ proposals, c=1 for πₜ. Reject and resample if necessary.
6. **Optional update**: Append newly collected (x,a,ℓ) to Dcal and repeat calibration for subsequent deployment rounds.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Proposal distribution for rejection**: Using π₀ is efficient for small β; using πₜ is better for large β. A mixed proposal can trade off but requires approximating πₘᵢₓ to maintain importance‑weight correctness.
- **Grid resolution vs. speed**: A finer grid over β yields a more precise estimate but increases computation; binary search after an initial pass can refine with fewer evaluations.
- **πₘᵢₓ representation**: Storing all historical policies to form an exact mixture is memory‑intensive; CPC approximates with a single normalized mixture, which may bias weights if policies are diverse.
- **Conservative test weight**: Using the maximum ratio on a proposal set underestimates the true supremum, potentially invalidating coverage; using a high quantile or adding a margin increases conservatism and selected β.
- **Exact vs. approximate weights**: The paper uses a fast linear‑time approximation to the full conformal weights; this relies on Lipschitz and stability assumptions and may introduce small errors.

### Failure Signatures
- **β stuck at βₘᵢₙ**: No larger β satisfies the risk constraint, likely due to insufficient overlap between π(β) and πₘᵢₓ or many high‑loss points in Dcal. Remedy: enlarge Dcal, soften constraint, or improve safe policy coverage.
- **β at maximum (no truncation)**: Even the unconstrained πₜ meets the risk level, possibly because the test term B·wₘₐₓ is too small; validate on held‑out data.
- **Empirical violations during deployment**: Violations of assumptions (exchangeability, boundedness, Lipschitz) or distribution shift beyond πₘᵢₓ’s coverage. Monitor online martingales to detect drift.
- **Rejection rate near 0 or 1**: Mismatch between proposal distribution and π(β). If using π₀ and β large, many rejections; switch to πₜ. If still extreme, check β or likelihood computation errors.
- **Numerical instability in ψ(β)**: Very large likelihood ratios can cause overflow; use log‑space arithmetic as described in Appendix C.1.2.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Medical QA (FDR control)**: CPC applied gCRC to false discovery rate, a non‑monotonic loss. It achieved nominal FDR control while attaining higher recall than monotonic baselines and the Learn‑Then‑Test (LTT) method (Figure 4).
2. **Constrained Active Learning**: On three tabular regression datasets, CPC controlled the feasibility violation rate at α=0.2 and reduced test MSE compared to uncontrolled acquisition. Notably, risk‑controlled selection sometimes even outperformed the unconstrained policy (Figure 5).
3. **Black‑Box Sequence Optimization**: Using a DPO‑tuned language model on combinatorial Ehrlich functions, CPC provided the first conformal risk guarantees in this setting. Moderate risk levels (α>0.6) stabilized optimization and improved the best score found by avoiding infeasible sequences (Figure 6).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly discuss open questions. However, its limitations suggest several directions for future work:
- Extending CPC to handle unbounded constraint losses while retaining finite‑sample guarantees.
- Improving the mixture approximation πₘᵢₓ to better capture diverse historical policies without excessive memory overhead.
- Reducing the conservatism of the test weight wₘₐₓ estimation while maintaining validity.
- Investigating the practical satisfaction of the Lipschitz assumption on weighted risk in β for complex non‑monotonic losses and developing remedies if violated.

## Limitations
- Requires the safe policy π₀ to have non‑negligible density on the support of the optimized policy πₜ; otherwise rejection rates soar or β becomes invalid.
- The single‑mixture approximation πₘᵢₓ may bias importance weights if historical policies are diverse, degrading risk control.
- The gCRC calibration relies on bounded losses, Lipschitz continuity in β, and replace‑one stability; violations can relax guarantees to α + Kϵ.

## Confidence
- **Likelihood‑ratio interpolation (theoretical definition and safety‑performance trade‑off)**: High
- **Generalized conformal risk control for non‑monotonic losses**: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Stress support overlap: Deploy CPC with a deliberately constrained safe policy (e.g., low‑temperature softmax) and check whether observed constraint violations stay within the calibrated α.
2. Exact vs. mixture πₘᵢₓ: Replace the approximate mixture with an exact empirical mixture of historical policies (storing densities) and compare risk control validity and rejection rates.
3. Lipschitz robustness: For a non‑monotonic loss like FDR, introduce artificial discontinuities in the weight function w(β) and measure how often empirical risk exceeds the nominal α, testing sensitivity to the Lipschitz assumption.
