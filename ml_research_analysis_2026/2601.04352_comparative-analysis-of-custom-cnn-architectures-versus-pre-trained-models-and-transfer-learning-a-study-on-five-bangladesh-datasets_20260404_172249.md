# Comparative Analysis of Custom CNN Architectures versus Pre-trained Models and Transfer Learning: A Study on Five Bangladesh Datasets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.04352
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04352
- **Reference count**: 13
- **Primary result**: Transfer learning via fine-tuning consistently achieved the highest test accuracy across five Bangladesh datasets, outperforming custom CNNs by 3% to 76%, with ResNet-18 fine-tuning reaching 100% accuracy on the Road Damage BD dataset.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates three model development approaches—training a custom CNN from scratch, using pre-trained models as frozen feature extractors, and fine-tuning pre-trained models—across five diverse Bangladesh image classification datasets. The results demonstrate that fine-tuning pre-trained ResNet-18 and VGG-16 networks yields superior accuracy on all tasks, particularly on complex, fine-grained classification problems where custom CNNs significantly underperform. ResNet-18 offers the best balance of accuracy and efficiency, while feature extraction serves as a pragmatic middle ground. The findings establish fine-tuning as the preferred strategy for limited-data, domain-specific tasks, though custom CNNs remain relevant for extremely resource-constrained deployments.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted experiments on five publicly available Bangladesh datasets covering urban planning, transportation, agriculture, and infrastructure damage. All images were resized to 224×224 and normalized using ImageNet statistics. Three modeling strategies were compared: (1) a custom 3.4M‑parameter CNN trained from scratch with four convolutional blocks and dropout regularization; (2) feature extraction using ImageNet‑pre‑trained ResNet‑18 and VGG‑16, where all convolutional layers were frozen and only a new classifier head was trained; and (3) fine‑tuning the same pre‑trained models with all layers unfrozen. All models were trained for 10 epochs with Adam (learning rate 0.001, batch size 32) and evaluated on held‑out test sets using accuracy, F1‑score, training time, and model size.

## Key Results
- Fine‑tuning pre‑trained models achieved the highest test accuracy on every dataset, surpassing custom CNN performance by absolute improvements ranging from 3% (Footpath Vision) to 76% (Paddy Variety BD).
- ResNet‑18 fine‑tuning attained perfect 100% accuracy on the Road Damage BD dataset, while VGG‑16 fine‑tuning reached 99.02%.
- Custom CNNs, despite being significantly smaller (3.4M parameters), struggled on fine‑grained tasks (e.g., 52.89% on Paddy Variety), where fine‑tuned models exceeded 93% accuracy. Feature extraction results consistently fell between the scratch‑trained and fine‑tuned approaches.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1**  
Claim: Transfer learning via fine-tuning achieves higher accuracy than training custom CNNs from scratch, especially on complex or fine‑grained tasks with limited data.  
Mechanism: Pre‑trained networks have already learned a hierarchical representation of visual features from large datasets (e.g., ImageNet). Fine‑tuning adapts these general features to the target domain by updating all layers, preserving broadly useful patterns while allowing task‑specific refinement.  
Core assumption: The target task shares sufficient visual semantics with ImageNet so that the pre‑trained initialization is beneficial.  
Evidence anchors: The paper reports consistent accuracy gains for fine‑tuning across all five datasets, with the largest relative improvement (+76%) on the most challenging dataset (Paddy Variety).  
Break condition: The advantage diminishes if (a) the target domain is drastically different from ImageNet, (b) ample task‑specific data becomes available to train a large custom model from scratch, or (c) fine‑tuning uses too high a learning rate on very small datasets, causing catastrophic forgetting.

**Mechanism 2**  
Claim: Using pre‑trained models as frozen feature extractors yields intermediate performance between scratch training and fine‑tuning, offering a computational trade‑off.  
Mechanism: The convolutional base acts as a powerful, generic feature extractor; freezing it avoids overwriting useful general features while training only a lightweight classifier head reduces computational cost and overfitting risk.  
Core assumption: Lower‑ and mid‑level features (edges, textures, shapes) are universal and reusable across vision tasks.  
Evidence anchors: Feature extraction results in Table 2 show test accuracies between those of custom CNNs and fine‑tuning (e.g., Footpath Vision: 85.06% vs. 78.16% custom and 87.93% fine‑tuned ResNet‑18).  
Break condition: The gap to fine‑tuning narrows if the task aligns extremely closely with ImageNet or the classifier head is too simplistic to capture task nuances; on very tiny datasets (<500 samples), feature extraction might surpass fine‑tuning due to overfitting.

**Mechanism 3**  
Claim: Custom CNNs with limited capacity underperform on complex, fine‑grained classification tasks despite their smaller size.  
Mechanism: The deliberately lightweight architecture (3.4M parameters) restricts the depth and channel width of the feature hierarchy, limiting the model’s ability to capture subtle, discriminative patterns needed for hard tasks.  
Core assumption: Performance improves with model capacity given sufficient data; the large accuracy gaps indicate the custom CNN’s capacity was below the threshold required for challenging datasets.  
Evidence anchors: The custom CNN achieved only 52.89% on Paddy Variety BD, while fine‑tuned ResNet‑18 (11.18M parameters) reached 93.10%. Even on simpler tasks (Road Damage BD), the custom CNN lagged fine‑tuning by nearly 9%.  
Break condition: The gap would narrow if the custom architecture were scaled up (more layers/filters), trained with more data, or if the task were intrinsically simpler.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Datasets (5 Bangladesh) -> Preprocessing (resize 224×224, ImageNet normalize) -> Model Families (Custom CNN / ResNet‑18 / VGG‑16) -> Training Modes (Scratch / Feature Extraction / Fine‑tuning) -> Evaluation Metrics (Test accuracy, F1, training time, model size)

**Critical path**  
1. Set up environment (PyTorch/TensorFlow, GPU).  
2. Acquire datasets and apply fixed train/val/test splits; resize and normalize images.  
3. Train custom CNN from scratch for 10 epochs (Adam, lr=0.001, batch=32).  
4. For ResNet‑18 and VGG‑16: implement feature extraction (freeze conv base, train new classifier).  
5. Implement fine‑tuning (unfreeze all layers, possibly reduce learning rate).  
6. Save best model by validation accuracy.  
7. Evaluate on test set: compute accuracy, F1 where applicable; record training time and parameter count.  
8. Compare results across all configurations and datasets.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Accuracy vs. Efficiency: Fine‑tuning gives highest accuracy but uses most GPU memory/time; feature extraction provides most of the gain with far fewer trainable parameters; custom CNN minimizes size (~3.4M) but sacrifices 3–76% accuracy.  
- Architecture selection: ResNet‑18 matches VGG‑16 accuracy with 8× fewer parameters, making it generally preferable.  
- Data regime: Feature extraction becomes more attractive as dataset size shrinks; fine‑tuning benefits grow with more data.

**Failure signatures**  
- Overfitting (common in fine‑tuning on tiny sets): training loss decreases while validation loss increases; remedy: lower learning rate, add dropout, or revert to feature extraction.  
- Underfitting (seen with custom CNN on hard tasks): both train and validation accuracies remain low; remedy: increase model capacity, train longer, or switch to transfer learning.  
- Resource exhaustion: VGG‑16 may run out of GPU memory; mitigate with gradient checkpointing, smaller batch, or using ResNet‑18.  
- Misaligned features: If feature extraction yields no gain over random initialization, try a different pre‑trained source or more aggressive fine‑tuning.

**First three experiments**  
1. **Custom CNN Scratch on Footpath Vision** – Train 10 epochs; expect ~78.16% test accuracy, training time ~1100 s.  
2. **ResNet‑18 Feature Extraction** – Freeze base, train new classifier; expect ~85.06% test accuracy, similar wall‑time but far fewer trainable parameters.  
3. **ResNet‑18 Fine‑Tuning** – Unfreeze all layers, train with lr=1e‑4; expect ~87.93% to 90.54% validation accuracy; verify improvement over feature extraction and baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

2. What are the comparative performance and efficiency trade‑offs when using modern architectures like EfficientNet or Vision Transformers instead of ResNet‑18 and VGG‑16?  
3. How does the size of the training dataset influence the performance gap between custom CNNs and transfer learning with fine‑tuning?  
4. To what extent can neural architecture search (NAS) and dedicated hyperparameter optimization close the performance gap between custom CNNs and transfer learning?

## Limitations
- Exact dataset splits, original resolutions, and augmentation strategies are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Reported results lack statistical significance testing (e.g., confidence intervals across multiple runs).
- The feature extraction head design for VGG‑16 is ambiguous; reported trainable parameters (~0.01M) suggest a minimal classifier but may differ from the authors’ implementation.
- Generalization beyond the five Bangladesh datasets remains unknown; no external validation on standard benchmarks.
- Potential overfitting on small datasets (e.g., fine‑tuning on Road Damage BD achieved 100% but could reflect data leakage or an insufficiently representative test set).

## Confidence
- Fine‑tuning superiority: **Medium** (clear empirical trend but reproducibility concerns and limited dataset diversity)
- ResNet‑18 efficiency‑accuracy trade‑off: **Medium** (supported by experiments but could vary with different architectures/hardware)
- Feature extraction as middle ground: **Medium** (consistent in paper but needs broader validation)
- Custom CNN incapacity on complex tasks: **High** (large accuracy gaps strongly indicate capacity limitations)
