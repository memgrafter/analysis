# MaterialFigBENCH: benchmark dataset with figures for evaluating college-level materials science problem-solving abilities of multimodal large language models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11414  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11414  
- **Reference count**: 0  
- **Primary result**: Multimodal LLMs achieve at most ~58% accuracy on college‑level materials science problems requiring accurate figure interpretation, with top models (ChatGPT‑5‑thinking, API‑GPT‑5) showing limitations due to memorization shortcuts and geometric reasoning failures.

## Executive Summary
MaterialFigBENCH introduces a benchmark of 137 university‑level materials science problems that depend on interpreting figures such as phase diagrams, stress–strain curves, and diffraction patterns. The dataset modifies problem texts and figures (e.g., substituting generic element symbols, shifting numeric values) to reduce memorization and employs expert‑defined answer ranges to accommodate perceptual uncertainty when reading numeric values from images. When evaluating leading multimodal LLMs (GPT‑4o, GPT‑o1, GPT‑5), the highest accuracies are 55.5% (ChatGPT‑5‑thinking) and 58.1% (API‑GPT‑5). Notably, high performance on canonical problems (e.g., Fe–C phase diagrams) arises from retrieving memorized domain knowledge rather than genuine figure reading. The models also exhibit persistent failures in geometric interpretation tasks (crystal indexing, distance measurement) and in handling significant digits appropriately. These results highlight that current multimodal LLMs lack robust figure‑based reasoning and that benchmark design must control for memorization shortcuts.

## Method Summary
The MaterialFigBENCH dataset consists of 137 free‑response problems drawn from materials science textbooks, each paired with a figure. To prevent models from relying on memorized values, problem texts replace specific element names with generic symbols (e.g., X, Y), and figures are subtly altered (e.g., shifting numeric values). Expert‑defined answer ranges account for the inherent uncertainty in reading values from images. The evaluation procedure sends the problem text and figure to multimodal LLMs via API, collects the free‑text response, and judges correctness against the answer ranges, with special attention to required significant digits. No fine‑tuning is performed; the benchmark serves as a zero‑shot test of figure‑based reasoning. The authors then analyze overall and category‑wise accuracy, identifying patterns of memorization and weaknesses in specific reasoning skills.

## Key Results
- MaterialFigBENCH provides 137 modified problems; top multimodal LLMs (ChatGPT‑5‑thinking, API‑GPT‑5) achieve only 55.5%–58.1% accuracy.
- High accuracy on canonical problems (e.g., Fe–C phase diagram) is due to memorized domain knowledge; models often answer correctly even when the figure is omitted.
- All tested models show near‑zero accuracy on geometric interpretation tasks (crystal indexing, distance measurement) and inconsistent significant‑digit handling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Models achieve seemingly high accuracy on canonical problems (e.g., Fe–C phase diagrams) by retrieving memorized domain knowledge rather than interpreting the provided figure.
- **Mechanism**: The model bypasses visual processing by recognizing problem patterns (e.g., element symbols like “X” as carbon, standard phase diagram shapes) and recalling textbook numeric values (solubility limits, eutectic compositions) from pretraining, rendering figure attachment unnecessary.
- **Core assumption**: Memorized knowledge for canonical systems is sufficiently robust and frequently encountered in pretraining data to serve as a shortcut.
- **Evidence**: Abstract and Section 4.1 report correct answers often without using figures, especially for canonical systems; no‑figure ablation shows sustained accuracy.
- **Break condition**: If figures are modified such that memorized values become incorrect (e.g., eutectic composition changed) and the model fails to read the new value, the shortcut is broken.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Current multimodal LLMs exhibit a persistent, category‑specific failure in tasks requiring precise geometric interpretation of scientific figures (e.g., crystal indexing, distance measurement on phase diagrams).
- **Mechanism**: The model’s visual encoder or subsequent reasoning module lacks training on the geometric abstractions specific to materials science (Miller indices, slope measurement, proportional distances), leading to inconsistent extraction of spatial relationships.
- **Core assumption**: General‑purpose vision encoders are not fine‑tuned on domain‑specific geometric primitives embedded in scientific plots.
- **Evidence**: Abstract and Section 4.2 show near‑zero accuracy on geometric task subsets (problems #4–7, #9–10, #12–13, #64); Section 4.4’s U‑shaped accuracy distribution clusters geometric tasks at zero.
- **Break condition**: If a model update improves accuracy across geometric tasks without changes to problem style, it indicates better geometric abstraction; no such trend observed.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Higher‑tier models often reduce numerical precision (significant digits) in final answers, even when figures provide data supporting greater precision, indicating an implicit optimization for semantic correctness over quantitative rigor.
- **Mechanism**: During response generation, the model applies a heuristic to round or truncate numbers to fewer significant digits, possibly to align with perceived answer simplicity or training data conventions, thereby failing problems where precise significant‑digit handling is mandated.
- **Core assumption**: Model training or decoding does not strongly penalize numerical imprecision relative to textual correctness.
- **Evidence**: Abstract and Section 4.3 show GPT‑4o uses three significant digits correctly, while GPT‑o1/GPT‑5 consistently use two (incorrect) for Problem #21 despite higher‑precision internal calculations; Section 2.4 requires appropriate significant digits for correctness.
- **Break condition**: If models consistently respect the precision implied by figure presentation (e.g., reporting 57 915 N vs. 58 kN), the issue is resolved; current behavior shows otherwise.

## Foundational Learning
### Figure‑ground discrimination in scientific diagrams
- **Why needed**: To isolate the relevant data curve, axis labels, and numeric scales from decorative or redundant elements (e.g., multiple plots in a single figure) and avoid distraction.
- **Quick check question**: In a phase diagram with multiple alloy systems plotted, how would you identify which boundary line corresponds to the hypothetical system specified in the problem text?

### Uncertainty quantification in image‑based measurement
- **Why needed**: Reading numeric values from figures (e.g., coordinates on a plot) inherently involves perceptual error; the benchmark uses expert‑defined answer ranges to accommodate this. Models must implicitly understand that small deviations are acceptable.
- **Quick check question**: If a diffusion coefficient is read as \(1.0 \times 10^{-14}\) m²/s from a log‑plot where the line passes between gridlines, what range might an expert accept?

### Task‑specific visual abstractions
- **Why needed**: Different figure types (phase diagrams, Arrhenius plots, diffraction patterns) encode physical relationships via distinct visual conventions (e.g., lever rule vs. linear slope on a log plot). Recognizing the abstraction is key to selecting the correct reasoning pathway.
- **Quick check question**: What visual feature in an Arrhenius plot directly gives the activation energy, and how does this differ from reading a composition from a phase diagram?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Problem text + Figure -> Multimodal Encoder (text & image processing) -> Integration & Reasoning (combine visual data with domain knowledge) -> Answer Generation (free‑text numeric/textual output) -> Evaluation (match against answer key/ranges)

### Critical Path
1. Figure modification (symbol replacement, value shifts) and problem text rewriting.  
2. Model inference with attached figure via API.  
3. Expert judging using predefined rules (exact match or tolerance‑based).  
4. Error pattern analysis by category and problem.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Modifying figures to block memorization must preserve core reasoning demands; over‑alteration risks unrealistic problems.  
- Providing answer ranges acknowledges reading uncertainty but introduces subjectivity in borderline cases.  
- Evaluating free‑text responses requires robust parsing and judgment; balancing strictness vs. flexibility is challenging.

### Failure Signatures
1. High accuracy on Fe–C phase diagram problems despite figure modification and element renaming, indicating memorization.
2. Near‑zero accuracy on crystal orientation/indexing problems across all models.
3. Inconsistent significant‑digit handling where larger models produce less precise answers.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **No‑figure control**: Submit Fe–C problems without attaching figures; sustained accuracy confirms memorization.
2. **Geometric distortion**: Apply affine transformation (e.g., skew) to a distance‑measurement figure; a drop in accuracy would suggest reliance on absolute pixel measurements over proportional reasoning.
3. **Precision probe**: Use a stress–strain curve problem with three‑significant‑digit yield strength; compare model outputs (e.g., 450 vs. \(5.8\times10^2\)) to judge precision adherence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Why do multimodal LLMs often solve materials science problems correctly without using the provided figures?** Evidence: Controlled experiments where memorized knowledge is deliberately contradicted by figure modifications could reveal the extent of reliance on memorization and whether models adapt to novel data.
- **What specific limitations in multimodal LLMs cause persistent failures in geometric interpretation tasks?** Evidence: Fine‑grained error analysis on geometric tasks, possibly using ablation studies to isolate geometric reasoning from domain knowledge, and evaluating model architectures on synthetic geometric datasets to pinpoint failure modes.
- **How can significant‑digit handling be improved in LLMs for quantitative scientific reasoning?** Evidence: Training or fine‑tuning LLMs with explicit significant‑digit rules and evaluating on a suite of precision‑sensitive problems to measure improvements.
- **How should benchmark datasets be designed to effectively control for memorization shortcuts?** Evidence: Developing new benchmark variants where figure modifications render memorized answers incorrect, and comparing model performance across original and altered versions to quantify memorization reliance.

## Limitations
- Reproducibility is limited by unspecified API parameters (temperature, prompts, image encoding) and potential subjectivity in expert judgment of answer ranges.
- Benchmark modifications (symbol replacement, value shifts) may not fully eliminate memorization, especially for non‑canonical problems.
- Evaluation restricted to proprietary models, lacking open‑source baselines for broader validation.

## Confidence
- **Memorization on canonical problems**: High  
- **Persistent failure in geometric interpretation tasks**: High  
- **Inconsistent significant‑digit handling**: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. No‑figure ablation across all categories: remove figure inputs for every problem to quantify memorization reliance beyond canonical systems.
2. Geometric distortion stress test: apply controlled affine transformations (e.g., skew, scale) to geometric‑task figures and measure accuracy changes to confirm reliance on absolute vs. proportional reasoning.
3. Precision‑resolution probe: create variants of precision‑sensitive problems with adjusted axis scaling/grid density to evaluate whether models adapt significant‑digit output to perceptual uncertainty.
