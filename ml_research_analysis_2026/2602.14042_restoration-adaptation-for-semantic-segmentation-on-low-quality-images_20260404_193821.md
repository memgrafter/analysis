# Restoration Adaptation for Semantic Segmentation on Low Quality Images
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.14042
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14042
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: RASS improves semantic segmentation on low-quality images, achieving +3.23 mIoU on ADE20K and +5.39 mIoU on RealLQ while training only 27.9M parameters (vs. 215.5M for full fine-tuning).

## Executive Summary
Semantic segmentation performance drops when images are low quality because existing restoration methods lack semantic guidance and segmentation models are not robust to degradation. RASS (Restoration Adaptation for Semantic Segmentation) addresses this by integrating semantic restoration into the segmentation pipeline. It first fine-tunes a Stable Diffusion model with a cross-attention loss that aligns internal attention maps to segmentation masks, generating semantically faithful reconstructions. Then it transfers the knowledge via LoRA-based merging into a Mask2Former segmentation head, avoiding full backbone retraining. On simulated degraded ADE20K, RASS Fine-Tuned reaches 47.42 mIoU (+3.23 over baseline) with only 12.9% of the parameters. On the new real‑world RealLQ dataset, it yields 39.80 mIoU (+5.39). The approach also improves perceptual quality (e.g., MUSIQ 67.16 on DrealSR).

## Method Summary
RASS consists of two stages. Stage 1: Semantic-Constrained Restoration (SCR) adapts a pre‑trained Stable Diffusion model to produce restored images that preserve semantic structure. SCR adds a loss term that encourages the cross‑attention maps of the diffusion U‑Net to match the provided segmentation masks, effectively grounding the restoration in semantics. Stage 2: The knowledge in the SCR‑fine‑tuned diffusion model is transferred to the segmentation model (Mask2Former) by merging Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA) parameters into the segmentation backbone. This injection requires updating only a small set of parameters (27.9M) and avoids retraining the entire backbone. At inference, the adapted Mask2Former processes low‑quality images directly, outputting segmentation masks with improved robustness.

## Key Results
- RASS Fine‑Tuned achieves 47.42 mIoU on synthetically degraded ADE20K, outperforming Mask2Former FT (44.19 mIoU) by +3.23.
- On real‑world RealLQ dataset, RASS FT reaches 39.80 mIoU versus 34.41 for Mask2Former FT (+5.39).
- SCR component boosts perceptual quality, e.g., MUSIQ score 67.16 on DrealSR, surpassing prior Real‑IR methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Semantic restoration via cross‑attention alignment forces the diffusion model to preserve object structures and context that are crucial for segmentation. By directly supervising the attention maps with ground‑truth masks, SCR ensures that the restored image retains the spatial layout of semantic regions. Transferring these semantically enriched features into Mask2Former through LoRA effectively endows the segmentation backbone with restoration knowledge without catastrophic forgetting or excessive parameter overhead. The low‑rank adaptation preserves the pre‑trained backbone’s general representational power while specifically tuning it for low‑quality inputs.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map (training): LQ images + GT masks -> SCR (Stable Diffusion U‑Net with cross‑attention loss) -> fine‑tuned diffusion model -> LoRA matrices -> Merge into Mask2Former backbone -> Adapted segmentation model.
Inference: LQ image -> Adapted Mask2Former -> Segmentation masks.

Critical path: SCR fine‑tuning (cross‑attention alignment) → extraction of LoRA matrices → merging into Mask2Former backbone → segmentation inference.

Design tradeoffs:
- SCR uses a diffusion model, which is computationally heavy but yields high‑quality restorations; however at inference the segmentation model alone is used, avoiding runtime cost.
- LoRA rank trades off capacity vs. efficiency; higher rank may improve performance but increases parameters and risk of overfitting.
- RealLQ dataset introduces real‑world degradations but may be limited in diversity; generalizability to other domains remains uncertain.

Failure signatures:
- Poor cross‑attention alignment: attention maps do not correspond to mask boundaries, leading to semantically inconsistent restorations.
- LoRA rank too low: insufficient knowledge transfer, performance close to uncompressed baseline.
- LoRA rank too high: overfitting to training degradation patterns, reduced robustness on test data.
- Discrepancy between synthetic and real degradations causing domain shift.

First three experiments to run:
1. Train Mask2Former on synthetic LQ data without LoRA to establish a baseline for degradation robustness.
2. Ablate LoRA rank (e.g., r=4, 8, 16) on the ADE20K synthetic benchmark, measuring mIoU and parameter count.
3. Visualize cross‑attention maps from SCR on a held‑out set to qualitatively assess alignment with segmentation masks.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is limited to ADE20K (synthetic) and the newly introduced RealLQ; generalizability to other datasets and degradation types is untested.
- Fine‑tuning Stable Diffusion for SCR remains computationally expensive despite later parameter‑efficient transfer; runtime and resource costs are not reported.
- The RealLQ dataset’s size and scene diversity are unspecified, raising concerns about real‑world robustness claims.

## Confidence
- Segmentation gain (+3.23 mIoU on ADE20K, +5.39 on RealLQ): High
- Parameter efficiency (27.9M vs 215.5M): High
- Perceptual quality improvement (MUSIQ 67.16 on DrealSR): Medium
- SCR mechanism (cross‑attention alignment): Medium
