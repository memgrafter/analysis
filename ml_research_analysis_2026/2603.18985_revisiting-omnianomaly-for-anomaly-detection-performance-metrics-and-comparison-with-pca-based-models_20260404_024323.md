# Revisiting OmniAnomaly for Anomaly Detection: performance metrics and comparison with PCA-based models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.18985
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18985
- **Reference count**: 22
- **Primary result**: Under identical evaluation protocols, a PCA-based linear baseline matches or exceeds the performance of the deep stochastic model OmniAnomaly on the Server Machine Dataset, and point-adjustment evaluation artificially inflates metrics for both models.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the lack of standardized evaluation in multivariate time series anomaly detection (MTSAD) by fairly comparing OmniAnomaly—a complex stochastic recurrent neural network—with a simple PCA-based linear model. Using the Server Machine Dataset (SMD), both models are trained and scored under identical preprocessing, thresholding (Peak Over Threshold and Grid Search), and evaluation (with and without point-adjustment) across 100 independent runs per machine. The results reveal that PCA achieves comparable performance to OmniAnomaly and even surpasses it when point-adjustment is not used. The work demonstrates that the choice of thresholding strategy and evaluation protocol (especially point-adjustment) can drastically alter performance rankings, often overshadowing modeling innovations. It further uncovers substantial machine-level variability in scores, cautioning that aggregated metrics can be misleading.

## Method Summary
The empirical protocol isolates the scoring model’s quality by holding all other factors constant. The Server Machine Dataset (SMD) comprises 28 machines, each with 38 KPIs at 1‑minute resolution. For each machine, the normal-only training set is used to fit scaling (min‑max for OmniAnomaly, standardization for PCA) and the model. OmniAnomaly processes 100‑step windows; PCA uses single time points. Anomaly scores are generated on the test set. Two thresholding methods are applied: (1) POT, which estimates a threshold from the training score distribution’s tail (with group‑specific initial quantiles); (2) Grid Search, which exhaustively picks the test‑time threshold maximizing F1. Evaluation computes point‑level precision, recall, and F1 both with and without point‑adjustment. This entire pipeline is repeated 100 times for OmniAnomaly (to account for stochasticity) and once for deterministic PCA. Results are aggregated across runs and machines using global average, macro, and micro strategies, and per‑machine statistics (means, standard deviations) are reported.

## Key Results
- PCA matches or exceeds OmniAnomaly’s F1 under point‑wise evaluation (e.g., PCA 0.485 vs. OmniAnomaly 0.312 with Grid Search), reversing common rankings from prior studies.
- Point‑adjustment evaluation greatly inflates F1 scores for both models, but segment‑level analysis shows that detected anomaly lengths (`La(%)`) are often very low (e.g., 20.78% for OmniAnomaly with POT), indicating many anomalies are only partially detected.
- The gap between Grid Search and POT performance is larger than the difference between models, highlighting that threshold selection dominates reported results.
- Machine‑level F1 scores exhibit high variability (standard deviations up to 0.343 across machines), meaning aggregated means (e.g., 0.746 for PCA with POT) conceal poor performance on some machines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Point‑adjustment inflates metrics by converting point‑level detection into segment‑level hits
- **Claim**: Point‑adjustment artificially boosts performance metrics because a single true positive within a long anomaly segment counts the entire segment as detected, drastically reducing false negatives.
- **Mechanism**: This relaxation decouples the metric from practical utility, where detecting the full duration and spatial extent of an anomaly matters. High recall and F1 can be achieved by flagging only a tiny fraction of points in an anomaly segment.
- **Evidence**: Segment‑level `La(%)` means are low (e.g., 20.78% for OmniAnomaly with POT) despite high PA‑F1. Without PA, F1 scores drop sharply. Prior corpus work (e.g., PATE, CCE) similarly notes traditional point metrics fail to capture temporal dynamics.

### Mechanism 2: Threshold selection strategy dominates model choice
- **Claim**: The method used to pick a threshold (POT vs. Grid Search) has a larger impact on reported performance than the choice between OmniAnomaly and PCA.
- **Mechanism**: POT estimates a threshold from the training score tail, introducing estimation error and sensitivity to quantile choices. Grid Search selects an unrealistic upper bound by optimizing directly on test labels. The performance gap between these strategies reveals how much score discriminativeness is owed to threshold tuning vs. intrinsic model quality.
- **Evidence**: For OmniAnomaly, average F1 drops from 0.933 (GS) to 0.746 (POT); for PCA, from 0.930 to 0.594. Under point‑wise evaluation, PCA outperforms OmniAnomaly with GS but not with POT.

### Mechanism 3: Aggregation across heterogeneous machines masks substantial variability
- **Claim**: Using micro, macro, or simple averages across the 28 SMD machines hides massive per‑machine performance differences, leading to over‑optimistic conclusions.
- **Mechanism**: Machines differ widely in anomaly rate, segment length, and difficulty. A high global mean can coexist with catastrophic failure on a subset of machines. Deployment‑relevant utility requires consistent per‑unit performance, not just a high aggregate.
- **Evidence**: `σ_mach` for PCA with POT is 0.343; per‑machine F1 ranges from ~0 to ~1. The overall mean (0.746) obscures this spread. Corpus studies on industrial MTSAD (e.g., HCL‑MTSAD) imply real‑world deployment needs reliable per‑unit behavior.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Multivariate Time Series (MTS) and Anomaly Characterization  
  **Why needed**: Anomalies in MTS violate normal correlation (spatial) or temporal patterns. This underlies why PCA (spatial) and OmniAnomaly (spatio‑temporal) model different aspects.  
  **Quick check**: How would you describe an anomaly in a 38‑sensor server where CPU, memory, and network I/O show spikes correlated in a way never seen during normal operation?
- **Concept**: Windowing and Temporal Context  
  **Why needed**: OmniAnomaly uses a 100‑step window to capture temporal dynamics; PCA uses single points. This structural difference is central to the comparison.  
  **Quick check**: What assumption about anomaly signatures is implicit when using a fixed‑length sliding window?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Data Ingestion → Separate Scaling → Model Fitting (on train) → Score Generation (on test) → Thresholding → Metric Calculation → Aggregation & Variability Reporting  
- **Critical path**: The study’s novelty lies in holding steps 2–5 identical across models. Models differ only in the core scoring module (OmniAnomaly’s GRU‑VAE vs. PCA’s linear projection).
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - **Complexity vs. Interpretability**: OmniAnomaly is a black box; PCA’s eigenvectors offer direct insight.  
  - **Stochasticity vs. Determinism**: OmniAnomaly requires 100 runs to estimate performance distribution; PCA is deterministic.  
  - **Threshold Sensitivity**: POT introduces operational variability; Grid Search provides a cheat‑free ceiling but is unrealistic.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - **High `σ_mach`**: Model succeeds on “easy” machines but fails on “hard” ones.  
  - **Low `La(%)` under PA**: Model detects only a small fraction of anomaly duration, masked by PA.  
  - **Large POT vs. GS gap**: Model’s score distribution lacks clear separation or tail estimation is poor.
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Baseline Replication**: Run PCA and OmniAnomaly on machine 1‑1 with default POT; verify per‑machine F1 ≈0.99 (PA) for OmniAnomaly.
  2. **Threshold Sensitivity Sweep**: For one machine, visualize F1 vs. threshold for both models; observe the GS‑POT gap and where optimal thresholds lie.
  3. **Point‑adjustment Deconstruction**: For a machine with high PA‑F1 under GS, compute `La(%)` and plot detected points vs. true segments; quantify actually detected anomaly duration.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1**: Do nonlinear and temporal models for MTSAD consistently outperform linear models under identical, fair evaluation protocols?  
  **Basis**: Introduction explicitly states this as open. Inconsistent thresholding and evaluation in prior work obscure true differences.
- **Question 2**: How do threshold selection and point‑adjustment impact the relative performance of deep learning vs. classical models?  
  **Basis**: Results show evaluation choices can reverse rankings (PCA > OmniAnomaly point‑wise). Lack of standardization leads to overstated deep learning benefits.
- **Question 3**: Is SMD a discriminative benchmark for MTSAD, given that simple baselines (e.g., arithmetic mean) perform comparably and machine‑level heterogeneity is huge?  
  **Basis**: Aggregated metrics mask variability; high variability suggests SMD may not challenge complex models effectively.

## Limitations
- The exact modification of the POT code for PCA’s upper‑tail modeling is unspecified, risking threshold replication errors.
- Machine group assignments (by prefix 1‑*, 2‑*, 3‑*) are implied but not explicitly verified; mis‑assignment would misapply initial POT quantiles.
- Implementation details (scaling, windowing, point‑adjustment logic) must match the original protocol; deviations could significantly alter results.

## Confidence
- **PCA matches/exceeds OmniAnomaly without point‑adjustment**: *Medium* (strong evidence but hinges on correct POT and grouping).
- **Point‑adjustment inflates metrics**: *High* (consistently demonstrated across models and supported by segment‑level data).
- **Threshold selection dominates model choice**: *High* (empirical gaps larger than modeling differences).
- **Aggregation masks machine‑level variability**: *High* (per‑machine statistics robustly reported).
