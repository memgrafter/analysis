# SpreadsheetArena: Decomposing Preference in LLM Generation of Spreadsheet Workbooks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.10002
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10002
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Claude models lead in Elo (1333–1550) but feature adjustments compress rankings; expert evaluations (finance) show poor adherence to conventions (mean 2.87/5); crowd preferences agree with experts only 56.4% of the time.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SpreadsheetArena, a platform for evaluating large language models (LLMs) on end-to-end spreadsheet generation through blind pairwise preference votes. By aggregating 4,357 votes across 16 models, the authors establish a stable leaderboard using Bradley-Terry modeling. They augment this with 29 programmatically extracted spreadsheet features to decompose performance, revealing that formatting and structure drive preferences while formula sophistication has weak effects. A data-driven failure taxonomy, derived from an LLM judge, highlights common breakdown modes. In finance domains, expert evaluations expose significant gaps in professional conventions, and crowd preferences align only modestly with expert judgments, suggesting domain-specific quality dimensions are not fully captured by general preference aggregation.

## Method Summary
The core method consists of three parts: (1) **Arena evaluation**: Users are shown four anonymized spreadsheets (two pairwise battles) for a given prompt and indicate a preference. These votes are aggregated via a Bradley-Terry model to yield Elo ratings for each model, with a minimum of 50 votes required for leaderboard inclusion. (2) **Feature-augmented decomposition**: For each generated spreadsheet, 29 features (covering errors, formatting, structure, and domain conventions) are computed. The Bradley-Terry model is extended to include pairwise differences of these features, producing coefficients that quantify each feature’s influence on win probability. Adjusted Elo scores are then derived by removing the feature contributions, isolating residual model skill. (3) **Failure taxonomy**: For decisive losses, an LLM judge generates rationales that are embedded, clustered, and hand‑curated into seven failure categories (e.g., “Spec Non‑compliance”, “Integrity”). This taxonomy allows per‑model profiling of breakdown patterns. The study also includes a finance‑focused expert evaluation using a six‑dimension rubric to assess adherence to professional conventions.

## Key Results
- Claude models achieve the highest arena Elo (1333–1550), but after adjusting for features, rankings compress significantly—Claude Opus 4.5 drops by 217 Elo points in finance.
- Expert evaluations of 134 finance spreadsheets yield a mean score of 2.87/5; the “Color Coding & Formatting” dimension scores only 1.95, indicating poor adherence to conventions.
- Arena preferences agree with finance expert judgments in only 56.4% of decisive cases, highlighting a gap between crowd and professional quality standards.
- Feature analysis shows that formatting‑related variables (e.g., text density, fill percentage) strongly influence win probability, while formula sophistication coefficients are often insignificant.
- The failure taxonomy reveals distinct signatures: Claude models excel in “Integrity” but struggle with “Numerical Computation”, while smaller open‑weights (e.g., Qwen3‑30B) frequently violate specifications.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Preference Aggregation via Pairwise Arena
- **Claim**: Blind pairwise preference votes from users yield stable LLM rankings for spreadsheet generation tasks.
- **Mechanism**: Users are shown four anonymized spreadsheets per prompt (two pairwise battles) and indicate a preference. The Bradley-Terry model aggregates these comparisons into model strength parameters (θ), which are linearly rescaled to Elo ratings (anchored to GPT‑4o at 1000). Models with fewer than 50 votes are excluded from the leaderboard to ensure reliability.
- **Core assumption**: Human preferences for spreadsheet outputs are transitive enough to be captured by a latent skill parameter; blinding eliminates brand bias.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] introduces SpreadsheetArena as “a platform for evaluating models’ performance via blind pairwise preference votes… establish a stable ranking of 16 models.”
  - [section 4.3] details vote collection, Bradley‑Terry estimation, Elo conversion, and the ≥50‑vote threshold.
  - [corpus] The arena approach extends Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024) and SEAL Showdown (Scale AI, 2025), but is novel for structured artifacts; corpus neighbors focus on spreadsheet benchmarks, confirming the gap.
- **Break condition**: If >30% of votes are ties or “both bad” or if rankings fluctuate widely with new votes, aggregation becomes unstable. The paper reports 12.5% non‑decisive votes, supporting stability.

### Mechanism 2: Feature‑Augmented Bradley‑Terry Decomposition
- **Claim**: Programmatically extracted spreadsheet features explain a substantial portion of preference signals and reveal domain‑specific quality drivers.
- **Mechanism**: The standard Bradley-Terry model is augmented with pairwise differences in 29 spreadsheet features (e.g., formula error rate, text density, fill percentage). The logistic win probability includes Σβₖ(Xₐₖ−X₆ₖ). Estimated β coefficients indicate how each feature influences win odds; adjusted Elo subtracts the feature contribution, isolating residual model skill.
- **Core assumption**: The selected features are linearly associated with log‑odds of winning and cover major quality dimensions without severe multicollinearity.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] states the use of “feature‑augmented Bradley‑Terry modeling to decompose performance via 29 programmatically extracted spreadsheet features.”
  - [section 3.3] presents Equation (1) and explains adjusted Elo computation.
  - [section 5.2] shows significant feature coefficients (e.g., `pct_text` +1.56, `compute_error_rate` −1.34), domain‑specific reversals (Finance: `finance_color_convention` +1.63; Academic: `pct_number_format` −5.38), and leaderboard compression after adjustment (Claude Opus 4.5 −217 Elo).
  - [corpus] Feature adjustment to control for confounders is inspired by SEAL Showdown (Scale AI, 2025); applying it to structured spreadsheet features is novel, as corpus neighbors do not cover this.
- **Break condition**: If features are poorly measured, highly correlated, or non‑linear effects dominate, β estimates become biased. Some features (e.g., formula sophistication) are not significant arena‑wide, suggesting limited influence on general preferences.

### Mechanism 3: Data‑Driven Failure Taxonomy via LLM Judge
- **Claim**: Loss rationales from an LLM judge can be clustered into a taxonomy that systematically characterizes multi‑factorial spreadsheet failure modes.
- **Mechanism**: For decisive battles, an LLM judge (`gpt‑5‑mini`) produces a structured rationale for the losing spreadsheet. Rationales are embedded, clustered with BERTopic + HDBSCAN, and hand‑curated into 7 failure buckets. The judge then tags each loss with all applicable categories, enabling per‑model failure profiling.
- **Core assumption**: The LLM judge’s rationales align with human expert assessments of failure; clusters represent real, distinct breakdown patterns.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] mentions “a data‑driven failure taxonomy.”
  - [section 5.3] describes the pipeline: clustering of 260 rationales → 9 natural clusters → 7 final categories; validation on 50 spreadsheets shows the LLM judge’s primary‑mode agreement with experts is 78%.
  - [section 5.3] Table 5 reveals distinct failure signatures: Claude models show high “Integrity” and “Numerical Computation” rates; Qwen3‑30B and Llama‑4‑Maverick show high “Spec Non‑compliance.”
  - [corpus] LLM‑as‑judge is common (e.g., HD‑Eval); the multi‑label spreadsheet failure taxonomy with expert validation is novel. Corpus includes NL2Formula (formula focus) but not full‑workbook failure analysis.
- **Break condition**: If the LLM judge misses subtle errors or over‑tags, the taxonomy becomes noisy. Low inter‑rater reliability among finance experts (Krippendorff’s α 0.27‑0.51) indicates some failure categories are inherently subjective, limiting ground‑truth certainty.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bradley–Terry Model and Elo Ratings**  
  *Why needed*: The core ranking mechanism relies on aggregating pairwise comparisons into model strengths; understanding the logistic formulation and Elo resampling is essential to interpret the leaderboard.  
  *Quick check*: Derive the log‑likelihood for Bradley‑Terry given N pairwise outcomes. How is the Elo difference of 200 points interpreted in terms of win probability?

- **Spreadsheet Conventions (Finance)**  
  *Why needed*: The paper shows that adherence to professional conventions (e.g., color coding, “one row one formula”) is poor but drives preferences in finance. Features like `finance_color_convention` and expert rubric items require this background.  
  *Quick check*: What are the standard color conventions in financial modeling (blue, black, green) and what do they represent? Why is embedding a constant in a formula (e.g., `=B6*0.2`) considered a violation of best practice?

- **LLM‑as‑Judge Reliability**  
  *Why needed*: Both the failure taxonomy and the arena preferences (implicitly) rely on LLM judges; their limitations affect validity of the conclusions.  
  *Quick check*: What are common pitfalls when using LLMs to evaluate complex artifacts like spreadsheets? How does 78% agreement with experts for the failure judge inform its reliability, and what does the low expert inter‑rater reliability (α 0.27‑0.51) suggest about the evaluation task itself?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Prompt Service → Generation Workers → Battle Manager → Voting UI → Storage → Feature Extractor → Analytics Core  
Parallel: LLM Judge (runs on losses) and Expert Evaluation (finance subset)

**Critical path**: Prompt → Generation → Battle → Vote → Storage → Feature Extraction → Bradley‑Terry Fitting → Elo Update.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Pairwise vs. absolute scoring: Pairwise reduces user burden and is robust to scale differences, but requires more comparisons for same statistical power.
- Heuristic features vs. learned representations: 29 handcrafted features offer interpretability but may omit latent quality factors.
- Blind general preferences vs. domain standards: Blind arena captures broad utility but only modestly agrees with finance experts (56.4%), suggesting domain‑specific evaluation may be needed for professional use.
- LLM judge scalability vs. accuracy: LLM judge handles thousands of losses cheaply but has moderate (78%) agreement with experts on primary failure mode.

**Failure signatures**:
- Invalid JSON outputs: Models produce malformed SheetSpec; leads to discarded pairs and reduces effective sample size.
- Vote sparsity: New models start with zero votes; require ≥50 to be ranked; slow‑voting categories may yield unstable estimates.
- Feature extraction bugs: Misparsing of conditional formats or cell styles could bias coefficients (e.g., `finance_color_convention` relies on accurate fill colors).
- LLM judge drift: Prompt tweaks or model updates may change tagging behavior; cache versions and periodically revalidate.
- Expert rubric ambiguity: Low inter‑rater reliability (α 0.27‑0.51) indicates scoring guidelines need refinement or rater training.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Stability analysis**: Bootstrapping over votes to compute 95% Elo confidence intervals for top/bottom models; confirm separation significance.
2. **Domain‑specific feature ablation**: Remove one feature group (e.g., formatting) from the augmented model per domain; measure change in adjusted vs. baseline Elo delta to quantify that group’s impact on ranking compression.
3. **Judge reliability study**: Sample 100 losses, have two experts independently tag using the same taxonomy; compute Cohen’s κ with the LLM judge’s tags; if κ < 0.6, iterate on judge prompt or definitions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Upstream architectural modifications**: What upstream architectural or representation changes to LLMs can improve spreadsheet generation quality as measured by domain-specific expert rubrics? Current models exhibit weaknesses in professional conventions (e.g., color coding, formula best practices) despite decent arena performance; it's unclear whether representation learning alone can address these gaps without targeted data or objectives. Evidence needed: Compare modified architectures (e.g., spreadsheet-specific pretraining objectives) against baselines using feature-adjusted Elo and expert finance evaluations.

2. **Iterative vs. single‑pass generation**: How do iterative or multi‑step generation paradigms (e.g., planning, refinement) compare to single‑pass end‑to‑end generation for spreadsheet tasks? Spreadsheets involve complex cross‑sheet dependencies and layout considerations that may be underspecified in one pass; iterative methods could yield better structural integrity and formatting coherence. Evidence needed: Implement iterative generation (e.g., skeleton first, then fill) for the same prompts and models, then compare using failure taxonomy rates and feature‑adjusted win probabilities.

3. **Data curation and post‑training strategies**: Which data curation and post‑training strategies (e.g., SFT, DPO) most effectively improve LLM performance on the multi‑dimensional criteria of spreadsheet generation? The disconnect between formula features and preferences suggests current training data or objectives may not emphasize formatting/structure conventions; optimal data mixes for different domains are unknown. Evidence needed: Ablation studies varying post‑training data (e.g., adding professionally formatted spreadsheets, synthetic data with explicit conventions) and measuring impact on feature‑adjusted Elo and expert rubric scores across finance/non‑finance categories.

4. **Crowd‑expert preference alignment**: Why do crowd‑sourced arena preferences agree only modestly with professional expert judgments in finance domains, and how can preference collection be adapted to better capture domain‑specific quality? The mismatch threatens to misguide post‑training if crowd preferences underweight conventions critical to professionals. Evidence needed: Decompose preference weights by feature for crowd vs. experts; run controlled preference studies with trained finance professionals; develop hybrid evaluation metrics combining crowd preferences with automated convention checks.

## Limitations
- **Undisclosed feature definitions**: Exact formulas for the 29 programmatic features are not provided, hindering faithful reproduction of adjusted Elo calculations and feature‑effect analyses.
- **Questionable ground truth for failure taxonomy**: Low inter‑rater reliability among finance experts (Krippendorff’s α 0.27–0.51) undermines the validity of the LLM‑judge tags; the reported 78% agreement may be artificially high due to expert inconsistency.
- **Limited domain coverage and generalizability**: The study uses 436 seed prompts across six categories; it is unknown how results extend to other professional domains (e.g., engineering, logistics) or to more complex spreadsheet structures.

## Confidence
- **Bradley‑Terry aggregation**: High – Standard method with sufficient votes (≥50/model) and low tie rate (12.5%) supports stable rankings.
- **Feature‑augmented decomposition**: Medium – Coefficients are significant, but undisclosed feature definitions prevent verification; multicollinearity among formatting features may bias β estimates.
- **Failure taxonomy validity**: Low – LLM judge tags rely on a weakly validated ground truth; cluster hand‑curation introduces subjectivity.
