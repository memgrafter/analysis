# Preventing Curriculum Collapse in Self-Evolving Reasoning Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13309
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13309
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Prism prevents curriculum collapse via cross-iteration semantic coverage regularization, achieving SOTA on six of seven math benchmarks and dramatically improved coverage (107/128 active clusters, Gini 0.66 vs 0.90).

## Executive Summary
Self-evolving reasoning systems that generate their own training data via a questioner-solver loop often suffer from **curriculum collapse**, where the question generator narrows to a limited set of problem types after a few iterations, stalling the solver’s learning. This paper introduces **Prism**, a method that introduces cross‑iteration semantic coverage regularization to maintain diversity while preserving problem difficulty at the solver’s frontier. Prism constructs a fixed embedding‑based partition over mathematical problems and rewards the questioner for solving edge‑of‑solvability problems (Zone of Proximal Development) while incentivizing exploration of under‑sampled semantic clusters. Each new questioner is initialized from the latest solver to reduce capability lag. Over four iterations on mathematical reasoning, Prism achieves the highest accuracy on six of seven benchmarks, with absolute gains up to **+3.98 on AMC** and **+3.68 on Minerva Math** compared to the state‑of‑the‑art self‑evolving baseline (R‑Zero). It also yields a high‑quality dataset of 100 k diverse questions. Coverage metrics show a dramatic improvement: 107 out of 128 clusters remain active (vs. 65), Gini coefficient drops from 0.90 to 0.66, and normalized entropy rises from 0.53 to 0.83. Ablations confirm that both the diversity reward and solver initialization are necessary for the full benefit, and computational overhead is minimal (only +1.7% per iteration).

## Method Summary
Prism operates in a co‑evolutionary framework alternating between a **questioner** (generates problems) and a **solver** (trained to answer). The key innovation is a **questioner reward** that couples two objectives: (1) a **Zone‑of‑Proximal‑Development (ZPD) gate** that rewards problems with solvability probability *p* in the range [0.5, 0.9] peaking at 0.75, ensuring problems lie on the solver’s learning frontier; (2) a **multiplicative diversity bonus** based on a static semantic partition. The partition is built offline by clustering embeddings (Qwen3‑Embedding‑0.6B) of the MATH training set into *K*=128 clusters. During training, an exponential moving average (EMA) of cluster visit counts *n* is maintained; the rarity bonus for a question assigned to cluster *c* is *d(c)* = exp(−*nₖ* / *n̄*). The final reward is *r(q)* = ZPD(*p(q)*) × (1 + λ × *d(c(q)*)). To prevent the questioner from falling behind, each iteration re‑initializes the questioner from the latest solver rather than from its previous policy. The solver is trained via **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization) using majority‑vote pseudo‑labels from its own rollouts. This combination yields a curriculum that maintains broad semantic coverage across iterations while staying within the solver’s learning frontier.

## Key Results
- Prism outperforms five self‑evolving baselines on six of seven benchmarks; absolute gains up to **+3.98 (AMC)** and **+3.68 (Minerva Math)** over R‑Zero.
- Coverage dramatically improved: **107/128** clusters active (vs. 65), **Gini 0.66** vs **0.90**, **normalized entropy 0.83** vs **0.53**.
- Ablation: diversity reward alone adds **+0.86** on MATH‑500; full Prism **+1.42** over R‑Zero; computational overhead **+1.7%** per iteration.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Cross‑Iteration Semantic Coverage Regularization
**Claim:** Prism mitigates curriculum collapse by encouraging the questioner to generate problems from semantically under‑sampled regions across training iterations.  
**Mechanism:** A fixed embedding‑based partition (*K*=128 clusters) is constructed offline from the MATH training set. An exponential moving average of per‑cluster visit counts *n* is maintained. The questioner’s reward includes a multiplicative rarity bonus *d(q)* = exp(−*nₖ(q)* / *n̄*), which is monotonically decreasing in normalized visitation, providing persistent pressure to explore low‑frequency clusters.  
**Core assumption:** Semantic similarity in the fixed embedding space is a meaningful proxy for reasoning‑relevant diversity.  
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract states Prism introduces a “persistent diversity signal over an embedding‑induced semantic partition.” Section 4.1 notes the partition is defined over semantic embeddings, not lexical features.  
**Break condition:** If the embedding model conflates distinct reasoning types, the rarity bonus may guide exploration toward irrelevant regions; poorly set ZPD parameters could force generation of either unsolvable or trivial problems to access rare clusters.

### Mechanism 2: Solver‑Initialized Questioner
**Claim:** Initializing each new questioner from the latest solver reduces inter‑iteration capability lag and prevents the questioner from being trapped in a progressively narrowed distribution.  
**Mechanism:** At the start of iteration *t*, the questioner *Q_t* is initialized by performing GRPO on the solver *S_{t-1}* using the solver’s verifiable rewards (answer correctness). This re‑derives a question‑generation policy from a model optimized for solving, not generating, providing an unbiased starting point free of accumulated generation biases.  
**Core assumption:** The solver’s parameters encode a representation of the space of problems it can solve; fine‑tuning it for generation yields a broader prior than continuous updates from the previous questioner.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “each new questioner is initialized from the latest solver to reduce capability lag.” Section 4.3: “Re‑deriving the Questioner from the Solver at every iteration eliminates the capability lag inherent in the *Q_{t-1} → Q_t* chain, provides an unbiased starting point…”  
**Break condition:** Ablation (Table 3) shows SolverInit alone underperforms R‑Zero (−0.48), indicating it must be paired with a strong question‑side objective (the diversity reward) to be effective.

### Mechanism 3: Multiplicative ZPD–Diversity Coupling
**Claim:** The multiplicative combination of the ZPD gate and the rarity bonus ensures that diversity pressure amplifies rather than competes with the goal of generating edge‑of‑solvability problems.  
**Mechanism:** Reward *r(q)* = zpd(*p(q)*) × (1 + λ × *d(q)*). The ZPD gate *zpd(p)* is piecewise‑linear, zero outside [*p_min*, *p_max*] and peaks at *p★*. The rarity bonus *d(q)* lies in (0, 1]. The product guarantees zero reward if solvability fails; for problems within the ZPD band, a higher rarity scales the reward upward, making rare‑but‑solvable problems more attractive than common‑but‑solvable ones.  
**Core assumption:** The primary failure mode is over‑sampling a narrow set of high‑reward templates *within* the solvable difficulty range. Reward sensitivity to cluster frequency *conditional on* being in the ZPD band directly addresses this without incentivizing difficulty sacrifice.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 4.2: “This structure ensures that diversity and quality are coupled rather than traded off.” and “The multiplicative form ensures that coverage pressure amplifies the ZPD signal rather than competing with it.”  
**Break condition:** If λ is too high, the questioner may generate highly unusual problems that pass the ZPD check but are unnatural. Sensitivity analysis (Table 4) shows performance drops at λ = 8.0.

## Foundational Learning

### Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) & GRPO
**Why needed here:** The entire self‑evolution loop is built on policy gradient methods (GRPO) that optimize for outcome‑based binary success signals. Understanding GRPO’s group‑relative advantages and KL penalties is essential to grasp how both questioner and solver are trained.  
**Quick check question:** How does the group baseline in GRPO’s advantage calculation eliminate the need for a learned value function, and why is this particularly advantageous for sparse/binary rewards?

### Semantic Embedding Spaces & K‑Means Clustering
**Why needed here:** The core diversity signal is defined over a fixed partition created by clustering embedding vectors. One must understand that the partition is static, embedding‑dependent, and cluster assignment is based on nearest centroid (cosine similarity).  
**Quick check question:** Why might lexical diversity penalties (like BLEU) fail to detect semantic repetition, and how does an embedding‑based partition address this limitation?

### Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) in ML
**Why needed here:** The ZPD gate is the “quality” component of the questioner’s reward, ensuring generated problems are at the edge of the solver’s current ability. This concept, adapted from Vygotsky, is operationalized as a solvability score window.  
**Quick check question:** Given the solvability score *p(q)* from majority voting, what does a ZPD gate with *p_min*=0.5, *p_max*=0.9, and peak at *p★*=0.75 imply about the ideal difficulty distribution of generated questions?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Base Model (M0): Qwen3‑4B‑Base → initializes Questioner (Q_θ) and Solver (S_φ).  
Embedding Model (f): Qwen3‑Embedding‑0.6B → offline clustering, online cluster assignment.  
Fixed Cluster Space: Pre‑computed *K*=128 centroids {μ_k} from MATH train.  
EMA Count Vector (n): *K*-dimensional vector tracking visit frequency per cluster, updated per batch with decay γ=0.99.  
Questioner (Q_θ_t): Generates questions; reward = zpd(p) × (1 + λ × d(c)); initialized from S_{t‑1}.  
Solver (S_φ_t): Trained on questions from Q_θ_t with majority‑vote pseudo‑labels via GRPO.  
Verifier (V): Provides binary success feedback for solver roll‑outs (exact match or symbolic check).  
Co‑evolution Loop: Alternates between (a) training Q_θ_t with coverage‑aware rewards and (b) training S_φ_t on Q_θ_t’s outputs.

### Critical Path
1. **Offline:** Cluster MATH train embeddings → get centroids {μ_k}. Initialize n_k = α=1.0.  
2. **Iteration t=1:** Initialize Q^0 and S^0 from M0.  
3. **Questioner Training Loop (GRPO steps):**  
   a. Sample batch {q_g} from π_{Q_θ_t}.  
   b. For each q_g, get p(q_g) via *n* solver roll‑outs and V.  
   c. If p ∈ [0.5, 0.9]: embed, assign cluster c(q_g), compute d = exp(−n_c/n̄), reward r = max(0, 1 − |p−0.75|/0.4) × (1 + 5.0 × d); else r=0.  
   d. Update Q_θ_t via GRPO with rewards.  
   e. Apply EMA update: n_k ← γ n_k; then for each qualifying q, increment n_c by (1 − γ).  
4. **Solver Training:** Generate question pool from final Q^t, filter by p ∈ [0.5,0.9]; train S^t via GRPO with majority‑vote rewards.  
5. **Iteration t+1:** Warm‑start n from previous iteration; initialize Q^{t+1} = S^t (Prism) or Q^t (baseline).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Static vs. Adaptive Clusters:** Fixed clusters from MATH train are cheap and stable but cannot represent novel problem types outside that corpus; online reclustering adds complexity and potential instability.
- **EMA Memory vs. Responsiveness:** Decay γ=0.99 provides long‑term memory but slows forgetting of over‑sampled clusters; a lower γ would be more responsive but might encourage cycling.
- **Multiplicative vs. Additive Rewards:** The multiplicative form (ZPD × (1+λ d)) strictly couples quality and diversity; an additive form could allow poor solvability to be compensated by high diversity.

### Failure Signatures
- **Curriculum Collapse:** Gini coefficient trends upward (>0.85), active cluster count drops (<80), top‑10% cluster share rises (>70%), and a single cluster dominates. Solver accuracy plateaus or drops on hard benchmarks.
- **Degenerate Diversity Seeking:** If λ is too high or ZPD gate too wide, cluster counts flatten but standard‑benchmark performance drops, suggesting unnatural or irrelevant problems.
- **Embedding Misalignment:** If the embedding model poorly separates mathematical semantics, the rarity bonus becomes noisy. Diagnose by inspecting cluster semantics or visualizing t‑SNE.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Component Ablation (Section 6.3, Table 3):** Run a 2 × 2 factorial design on MATH‑500 comparing (Diversity Reward ON/OFF) × (SolverInit ON/OFF). Expectation: Diversity Reward alone gives +0.86; SolverInit alone hurts (−0.48); full Prism gives +1.42 over R‑Zero.
2. **Sensitivity to Diversity Weight λ (Section 6.3, Table 4):** Vary λ ∈ {1.0, 3.0, 5.0, 8.0} on MATH‑500. Expect performance to peak at an intermediate value (5.0), with lower λ showing collapse‑like behavior and higher λ degrading quality.

Expect R‑Zero to show worsening concentration; Prism to show stable/improving uniform coverage (Gini ~0.66, entropy ~0.83).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** Can Prism’s fixed semantic partition, built from the MATH training set, effectively represent and encourage exploration of truly out‑of‑distribution problem types (e.g., novel mathematical domains not present in MATH), or does it inevitably bias the curriculum toward the known structure of the reference corpus?  
**Basis:** The partition is static and cannot represent problem types outside the reference corpus (Section 6.4).  
**Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates improved coverage *within* the MATH‑defined cluster space, but a question that is novel relative to MATH might still be forced into an existing cluster, potentially missing new reasoning patterns.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Apply Prism to a new domain (e.g., competitive programming from CodeForces) using a reference corpus from that domain. If gains and broad coverage still materialize, the method is robust; if not, its success may be tightly coupled to the quality/breadth of the initial partition.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** What is the precise causal mechanism by which “Solver‑initialised Questioner” fails to provide a benefit on its own (ablation shows −0.48 on MATH‑500) and only becomes beneficial when combined with the diversity reward? Does the Solver’s policy contain a prior that is actively *misaligned* with generating diverse, edge‑of‑solvability questions?  
**Basis:** Ablation results: “SolverInit” alone underperforms R‑Zero. The text explains: “the Solver is directly optimised to maximise outcome rewards conditioned on a question (i.e., to answer), so its generation prior is not automatically calibrated for proposing diverse, informative, edge‑of‑solvability questions.”  
**Why unresolved:** The explanation is plausible but not mechanistic; we lack insight into *what* in the Solver’s weights causes misalignment. Is it learned answer‑specific shortcuts that are harmful for generation? Does initialization lead to unstable learning signals without the diversity bonus?  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Detailed analysis of questions from the “SolverInit‑only” ablation (surface templates, difficulty distribution, semantic cluster distribution). Additionally, measure gradient norms or policy entropy during early questioner updates with and without the diversity reward to see if the diversity bonus specifically counters premature convergence.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** How sensitive is Prism’s effectiveness to the choice of the embedding model *f* used for cluster assignment? Would a different embedding model (e.g., one optimized for mathematical semantics vs. general text) lead to a different notion of semantic diversity, and consequently, to a different optimal curriculum and final solver performance?  
**Basis:** The authors acknowledge: “The partition quality also depends on the embedding model (Qwen3‑Embedding‑0.6B); we have not ablated across embedding architectures” (Section 6.4). The entire diversity signal is defined in this embedding space.  
**Why unresolved:** Coverage metrics are computed in the same embedding space used for rewards, creating circularity. A model that better distinguishes subdomains might create more meaningful clusters, but it is unknown whether learning dynamics and final performance are robust to this choice.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled ablation where the fixed *K*=128 clustering is rebuilt using multiple embedding models (e.g., generalist like text‑embedding‑3‑large, math‑fine‑tuned, maybe symbolic features). Run full Prism training for each and compare: (1) coverage metrics in that embedding space, (2) actual semantic diversity (via human or LLM‑based topic classification), and (3) final solver accuracy. Consistency would show robustness; large variation would indicate sensitivity.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Does the multiplicative coupling of the ZPD gate and the rarity bonus (Equation 12) represent the optimal trade‑off between quality and diversity, or would an alternative formulation (e.g., a learned weighting, a Pareto‑based approach, or a sequential procedure) lead to a more effective curriculum?  
**Basis:** The design choice is justified by analogy to MAP‑Elites and the desire to couple rather than trade signals. The hyperparameter λ=5.0 is chosen based on a small sweep (Table 4). No comparison against other combination methods is provided.  
**Why unresolved:** The current formulation assumes a single static trade‑off parameter. It is possible that the optimal weighting changes as training progresses (e.g., heavy diversity early, quality later). The multiplicative form could still allow niches with moderate satisfaction of both signals.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic comparison of reward formulations: (1) Multiplicative (current), (2) Additive, (3) dynamic λ that decays over iterations, (4) two‑objective RL maintaining an archive of high‑quality, diverse questions. Evaluate not only final solver accuracy but also the trajectory of cluster coverage and the final distribution of question difficulty/quality.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** To what extent does the “cross‑iteration memory” provided by the EMA count vector (*n*) prevent collapse versus simply acting as a local batch‑level novelty detector? Could a simpler, batch‑local diversity bonus on top of the ZPD gate, without cross‑iteration counts, achieve similar results if the Questioner’s update were sufficiently regularized?  
**Basis:** The paper contrasts its EMA‑based count with R‑Zero’s batch‑local BLEU penalty and states the cross‑iteration memory is “a feature absent from R‑Zero’s batch‑local BLEU penalty” and “prevents the Questioner from redundantly re‑exploring already‑saturated clusters.” The ablation (Table 3) shows the diversity reward (which uses EMA) adds +0.86.  
**Why unresolved:** The ablation isolates the *component* but not the *mechanism*. We know the full Prism system with EMA works, but we do not know if the EMA itself is critical or if any persistent, non‑batch‑specific signal would suffice. The paper does not compare against a version where the rarity bonus is reset every iteration or based on a fixed buffer of recent questions.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** An additional ablation: “Prism‑NoCrossIter” where cluster counts *n* are reset to uniform smoothing α at the start of every co‑evolution iteration, but the bonus still operates multiplicatively within that iteration. If this variant still prevents collapse and achieves most of the gain, the key is having a persistent signal *within a training phase* rather than strict cross‑iteration continuity. If performance degrades significantly, it confirms the critical role of long‑term memory.

## Limitations
- Exact GRPO hyperparameters for the questioner (KL penalty, clipping ϵ, batch size) are unreported; reproduction relies on guessed defaults.
- The fixed K‑means partition (*K*=128) may be brittle and cannot adapt to novel problem distributions.
- External evidence is thin (neighbor FMR=0.537, zero citations), so generalizability beyond the reported Qwen3‑4B experiments remains unproven.

## Confidence
- Prism prevents curriculum collapse: **High**
- Semantic coverage regularization (cluster‑based rarity bonus): **Medium**
- Solver‑initialization reduces inter‑iteration capability lag: **Medium**
- Multiplicative ZPD × diversity coupling: **Low‑Medium**
