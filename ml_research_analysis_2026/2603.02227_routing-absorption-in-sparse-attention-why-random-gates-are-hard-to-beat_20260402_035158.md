# Routing Absorption in Sparse Attention: Why Random Gates Are Hard to Beat

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02227
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02227
- **Reference count**: 13
- **Primary result**: Transformers exhibit "routing absorption" where Q/K/V projections co-adapt to any imposed mask, absorbing the routing signal and making learned sparse attention gates perform no better than random ones.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether transformers can learn effective sparse attention patterns through end-to-end training. It identifies a phenomenon called "routing absorption": during joint training, the model’s Q/K/V projections and other parameters co-adapt to compensate for any imposed mask, effectively absorbing the routing signal. As a result, a learned gate’s performance converges to that of a frozen random gate. Controlled experiments on a 31M-parameter transformer provide strong evidence: end-to-end differentiable soft gating yields near-identical perplexity as random gates; hard top-k gating receives zero gradient; a gate distilled onto co-adapted Q/K/V fails catastrophically upon deployment; and stochastic mask training fails to prevent co-adaptation. Scaling to Qwen3-1.7B confirms absorption intensifies with model size. The core conclusion is that parameter asymmetry between the small gate and large model drives absorption, rendering post-hoc, decoupled routing the only reliable approach for effective sparse attention.

## Method Summary
The authors train a 6-layer, 256-dimensional, 4-head pre-norm transformer (~31M parameters) on WikiText-103 with 512-token chunks and batch size 16. Sparse attention is implemented via a bilinear gate network: given input `x`, it computes scores `G = (x W_gq)(x W_gk)^T / sqrt(d_gate)` with `d_gate=32` (adding ~393K parameters, ~1.3% of the model). A soft mask is applied by element-wise sigmoid of `G` to the attention scores before softmax. Baselines include a dense model (no mask), a model with a trainable gate (learned), and a model with a frozen random gate (random). All models are trained for 50,000 steps with a cosine learning rate schedule. Primary metric is validation perplexity. Additional experiments test hard top-k gating, distillation of gates onto co-adapted Q/K/V, and stochastic mask randomization during training.

## Key Results
- End-to-end learned soft gates achieve near-identical perplexity to random gates (48.73±0.60 vs 49.83±0.04).
- Hard top-k gating receives exactly zero gradient, preventing end-to-end learning.
- A gate distilled onto co-adapted Q/K/V achieves high F1 against oracle masks but catastrophically fails when deployed (601.6 vs 48.6 perplexity on mask-agnostic Q/K/V).
- Stochastic mask randomization during training fails to prevent co-adaptation and increases dense perplexity (78.2 vs 37.3 baseline).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Parameter Asymmetry Drives Absorption
Routing absorption occurs because the model’s total parameters vastly outnumber the gate’s (∼80:1 in the 31M model). During joint training, the high-capacity Q/K/V projections and other parameters adjust to compensate for any imposed mask, flattening the loss landscape for the small gate. The gate’s updates become ineffective because the model can maintain performance by shifting representations rather than using the gate’s specific mask. Evidence: Learned and random gates yield near-identical perplexity (48.73 vs 49.83) in the 31M model; layer unfreezing experiments show that unfreezing layers reduces the learned-vs-random gap from 31.6 to 6.9 ppl, confirming the role of asymmetry. A break condition would be if gate capacity approached model capacity (e.g., per-layer gates with similar parameter counts).

### Mechanism 2: Cross-Layer Compensation via Shared Q/K/V
Shared Q/K/V projections across layers enable cross-layer compensation pathways, making absorption more severe than in MoE where experts are independent. When some layers’ Q/K/V adapt to a mask, other layers can reroute information through the residual stream, allowing the model to tolerate arbitrary masks. This distributed compensation means the gate’s specific pattern is less critical. Evidence: The distillation contrast shows a gate trained on co-adapted Q/K/V achieves 601.6 ppl on hard top-k vs 48.6 on mask-agnostic Q/K/V—a 12× gap demonstrating mask dependency. A break condition would be if each layer had completely independent Q/K/V with no residual stream sharing.

### Mechanism 3: Mask Functional Form Dependency
Co-adapted Q/K/V specialize not just to the binary mask pattern but to the specific differentiable functional form (e.g., sigmoid soft mask) used during training. The model’s representations become tuned to the gradient properties of the training-time mask (e.g., its smoothness). Switching to a mask with a different functional form (e.g., hard top-k) breaks the co-adaptation, even if the binary pattern is similar. Evidence: Hard top-k yields zero gate gradient; soft gating allows gradients but still absorbs signal. The distillation experiment’s catastrophic failure upon switching mask type supports this. A break condition would be if training used a mask with identical functional form at deployment (but then sparsity is not hard).

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Attention Concentration**  
*Why needed here:* The paper’s premise rests on attention being highly concentrated (top-k captures most mass), suggesting sparse routing should be learnable. Understanding concentration metrics (e.g., entropy ratio, mass in top-k) is key to interpreting why post-hoc routing works.  
*Quick check question:* "In a trained transformer, what typical fraction of total attention mass is captured by the top 10% of key positions per query?"

**Concept: End-to-End vs Post-Hoc Training Dynamics**  
*Why needed:* The core finding contrasts joint training (absorption) with decoupled training (success). Grasping gradient flow and co-adaptation in each regime explains why the same gate architecture behaves differently.  
*Quick check question:* "When a gate is trained post-hoc on a frozen model, how does the convergence speed (steps) compare to end-to-end training for the same gate?"

**Concept: Parameter Asymmetry in Conditional Computation**  
*Why needed:* Asymmetry is the root cause of absorption; it predicts scaling behavior (worse at larger scales) and connects to MoE literature. Recognizing this helps evaluate other sparse architectures.  
*Quick check question:* "If a router has 1% of a model’s parameters, what does the MoE literature predict about the benefit of learned routing versus random?"

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Input -> Gate network (bilinear projections `W_gq`, `W_gk`) -> Mask (sigmoid soft) -> Attention module (`Q = xW_q`, `K = xW_k`, `V = xW_v`; masked scores `A = softmax((QK^T / sqrt(d_h)) * M)` -> Transformer layer (FFN, layer norm, residual) -> Output.

**Critical path**:  
1. Forward pass: Input → gate → mask → masked attention → layer output → loss.  
2. Backward pass: Loss gradients flow through masked attention to Q/K/V and gate.  
3. Co-adaptation loop: Q/K/V updates adjust to the current mask; the gate’s signal is absorbed because Q/K/V have more degrees of freedom.  
4. Convergence: Gate’s loss landscape flattens; learning stalls while Q/K/V continue adapting.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **Gate size**: Smaller gates → stronger absorption; increasing `d_gate` delays but does not prevent absorption.  
- **Mask type**: Soft masks allow gate gradients but still absorbed; hard masks give zero gate gradient.  
- **Training objective**: End-to-end training risks absorption; post-hoc decoupling avoids it but requires a pretrained checkpoint.  
- **Budget**: Longer end-to-end training yields minimal gains.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Learned gate’s perplexity ≈ random gate’s perplexity (within 2–5%).  
- Hard top-k gate shows zero gradient during training.  
- Distilled gate on co-adapted model catastrophically fails on hard deployment (e.g., 601.6 vs 48.6 ppl).  
- Stochastic mask training increases dense perplexity (e.g., 78.2 vs 37.3).

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Train model end-to-end with learnable gate and frozen random gate; compare final perplexity. Expect near-equivalence (∼48–50 ppl on WikiText-103 at k=64).  
2. Implement hard top-k via `torch.topk` + masking; verify gate parameters receive near-zero gradient (e.g., monitor gradient norms).  
3. Train two gates post-hoc: one on dense (mask-agnostic) checkpoint, one on soft-gated (co-adapted) checkpoint. Deploy both with hard top-k; expect large perplexity gap for co-adapted gate (∼10×).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**:  
*Question*: Do expert-choice or block-granularity sparse attention methods (e.g., MoSA, NSA) avoid routing absorption when their learned selection components are replaced with random baselines?  
*Basis*: The paper suggests testing NSA by "replace[ing] the learned selection with random selection while keeping windows and compression" to isolate the contribution of learned routing.  
*Why unresolved*: The analysis only covers per-query bilinear gating; it is unknown whether structural variants with coarser granularity inherently resist co-adaptation.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Ablate NSA (or similar) with random vs. learned selection on identical models, comparing perplexity and oracle mask F1 to determine if learned selection provides any benefit beyond fixed components.

**Open Question 2**:  
*Question*: Can alternative gate architectures (e.g., cross-attention between queries and a compressed key representation) reduce the parameter-asymmetry-driven absorption observed with bilinear gates?  
*Basis*: Limitations state that "more complex routing mechanisms... might resist absorption better," but only bilinear gates were tested.  
*Why unresolved*: The paper attributes absorption to parameter asymmetry, but whether architectural changes to the gate can alter the dynamics remains unexplored.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Train end-to-end models with alternative gate architectures under same conditions, measuring the learned-vs-random gate gap; a significantly smaller gap would indicate mitigated absorption.

**Open Question 3**:  
*Question*: Does full end-to-end sparse attention pretraining at scale (e.g., 1.7B parameters) reproduce the absorption gradient seen in single-layer fine-tuning, or does longer training allow gates to overcome co-adaptation?  
*Basis*: The paper scales to Qwen3-1.7B only via single-layer tuning, noting full pretraining is "prohibitively expensive" and "remains out of scope."  
*Why unresolved*: Single-layer experiments show absorption but cannot capture global co-adaptation across all layers during full pretraining; training duration effects are also unclear.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Full pretraining run of a 1.7B model with end-to-end sparse attention vs. random gates, comparing convergence gaps and scaling them against the 31M model’s 2.2% gap.

## Limitations
- Exact training hyperparameters (learning rate schedule, warmup steps) and random seeds are not disclosed, making exact replication impossible.
- Scaling evidence is limited to a single large model (Qwen3-1.7B); it is unclear whether absorption intensifies monotonically across model scales.
- The cross-layer compensation mechanism is inferred indirectly; direct manipulation of residual connections is missing.
- Experiments are confined to a 6-layer decoder on WikiText-103; generalizability to other architectures or tasks remains untested.
- Mask functional form dependency is only contrasted between soft sigmoid and hard top-k; the full gradient signature space is unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Parameter asymmetry drives absorption**: High (robustly supported by layer unfreezing and gate-size ablation).
- **Cross-layer compensation exacerbates absorption**: Medium (consistent with unfreezing effects but lacks direct causal test).
- **Mask functional form dependency**: Medium (distillation results indicate catastrophic failure upon mask type change; systematic study pending).
- **Post-hoc decoupled routing is reliable**: High (multiple experiments show end-to-end learning fails while post-hoc training succeeds).
- **Absorption intensifies with model size**: Medium (single large-scale experiment suggests trend; requires multi-scale verification).

## Next Checks
1. **Replication with full hyperparameters**: Re-run the learned-vs-random gate experiment using a disclosed cosine schedule (e.g., 10k warmup, peak LR=3e-4) and report three-seed averages to confirm the ~2 ppl gap.
2. **Residual ablation**: Train end-to-end sparse models with residual connections disabled (or layer-norm frozen) to determine whether cross-layer compensation is necessary for absorption.
3. **Multi-scale study**: Train models of varying capacities (e.g., 10M, 100M, 1B parameters) and measure the learned-vs-random perplexity gap to test for monotonic scaling of absorption.
