# A Robust Framework for Secure Cardiovascular Risk Prediction: An Architectural Case Study of Differentially Private Federated Learning

- **arXiv ID**: 2603.13293  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13293

## Executive Summary
Clinical data fragmentation across hospitals poses a significant barrier to developing robust AI for cardiovascular risk prediction. This paper presents FedCVR, a federated learning framework that leverages server-side adaptive optimization with momentum to counteract the noise introduced by differential privacy. Under a privacy budget of ϵ≈13.4, FedCVR attains an F1-score of 0.84 and an AUC of 0.96, statistically surpassing FedAvg, FedProx, and other adaptive optimizers. The results indicate that server-side adaptivity is a structural prerequisite for preserving clinical utility in privacy-preserving, multi-institutional collaborations.

## Method Summary
FedCVR operates in a federated setting where each client (hospital) trains a local model on its private electronic health records. To ensure differential privacy, clients add calibrated noise to their model updates before transmitting them to a central server. The server aggregates these noisy updates using an adaptive optimizer that maintains momentum across communication rounds. This momentum acts as a temporal denoiser, smoothing out the high variance caused by DP noise and stabilizing convergence. The updated global model is then broadcast back to clients for the next training round. The framework is evaluated on a cardiovascular risk prediction task using multi-site data, comparing against FedAvg, FedProx, and other adaptive optimizers under identical privacy constraints.

## Key Results
- FedCVR yields F1=0.84 and AUC=0.96 at a privacy budget of ϵ≈13.4.
- It statistically outperforms FedAvg, FedProx, and other adaptive optimizers.
- Server-side adaptive optimization with momentum serves as an effective temporal denoiser, recovering clinical utility under differential privacy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Differential privacy introduces random noise into individual model updates, creating high variance in the aggregated global model. FedCVR’s server-side adaptive optimizer with momentum accumulates past gradients across rounds, effectively averaging out this noise over time. This temporal denoising yields a smoother convergence trajectory and reduces the impact of DP-induced perturbations. Moreover, the adaptive per-parameter learning rates improve robustness to data heterogeneity and further mitigate noise effects.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Client Data -> Local Training -> DP Noisy Update -> Server Aggregation (Adaptive Optimizer with Momentum) -> Global Model -> Broadcast to Clients

**Critical Path**:  
1. Each client trains locally on its private dataset.  
2. Local model updates are perturbed with calibrated DP noise.  
3. Noisy updates are sent to the central server.  
4. Server aggregates updates using an adaptive optimizer that incorporates momentum.  
5. The updated global model is distributed back to clients.  
6. Repeat from step 1 for multiple communication rounds.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *DP noise vs. model utility*: Higher ε reduces noise but weakens privacy; lower ε protects privacy but degrades performance.  
- *Server-side adaptivity vs. communication overhead*: Maintaining state on the server improves robustness but requires careful tuning and may increase memory footprint.  
- *Momentum vs. staleness*: Momentum smooths noise but can delay the model’s response to genuine local changes, especially in highly non-IID settings.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Diverging training loss or unstable metrics (e.g., F1, AUC) across communication rounds.  
- High variance in performance metrics across independent runs with the same DP budget.  
- Significant drop in accuracy for under-represented subpopulations or new sites not seen during training.

**First Experiments (3)**:  
1. Baseline replication: Compare FedCVR against FedAvg and FedProx under identical DP settings and report statistical significance.  
2. Momentum ablation: Evaluate FedCVR with and without momentum to isolate its temporal denoising contribution while keeping other factors constant.  
3. Privacy budget sweep: Vary ϵ (e.g., 5, 10, 13.4, 20) to map the utility-privacy tradeoff curve and verify stability of FedCVR’s advantage.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility**: No public code or data release, hindering independent verification.
- **Clinical realism**: Use of curated, structured EHR data may not capture the fragmentation, noise, or missingness of real-world datasets.
- **Generalizability**: Performance may degrade on diverse, under‑represented populations or datasets from non‑European regions.

## Confidence
- **FedCVR outperforms FedAvg/FedProx under DP**: *Medium* (supported by reported metrics but lacking significance testing and cross-dataset validation).
- **Server-side adaptivity mitigates DP noise**: *Low* (evidence hinges on momentum as a temporal denoiser; alternative explanations, such as tuning artifacts, are not ruled out).
- **Clinical utility preserved at ϵ≈13.4**: *Medium* (F1/AUC are promising but no calibration analysis or clinical decision‑curve validation is provided).

## Next Checks
1. Independent replication using raw multi‑center data (e.g., UK Biobank + MIMIC) with identical DP settings and reporting variance across runs.
2. Stress‑test heterogeneity: Evaluate FedCVR on sites with highly divergent feature distributions or label shifts to challenge the robustness claim.
3. Ablation study: Isolate momentum’s contribution by comparing server‑side adaptivity with and without momentum under identical DP noise, controlling for learning‑rate schedules.
