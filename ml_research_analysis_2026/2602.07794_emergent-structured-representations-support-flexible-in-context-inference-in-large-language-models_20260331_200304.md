# Emergent Structured Representations Support Flexible In-Context Inference in Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **ArXive ID:** 2602.07794
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07794
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs rely on an emergent, context‑invariant conceptual subspace to perform flexible in‑context inference.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) use structured conceptual representations during in‑context learning. Using a reverse dictionary task, the authors identify a shared conceptual subspace that emerges in middle‑to‑late layers and remains stable across different demonstration contexts. Causal mediation analyses demonstrate that this subspace is functionally necessary and sufficient for accurate predictions, and cross‑context transfer experiments confirm its role in flexible inference. Attention‑head analysis reveals a two‑stage progression: early‑to‑middle heads construct the subspace by integrating demonstration cues, while later heads leverage it for query‑conditioned inference. These effects are stronger in larger models and with more demonstrations, providing mechanistic evidence that LLMs dynamically build and exploit structured latent representations for in‑context reasoning.

## Method Summary
The authors analyze the internal representations of LLMs performing a reverse dictionary inference task. They apply layer‑wise singular value decomposition (SVD) to quantify the overlap of principal components across layers, and generalized canonical correlation analysis (GCCA) to isolate a linear subspace capturing shared variance. To test the causal role of this subspace, they employ activation patching, ablation, and isolation: they remove the subspace component or retain only it, measuring changes in token‑level log‑probabilities. Cross‑context generalization is assessed by transferring relational offsets extracted from one context to another within the subspace. Finally, corruption‑based causal mediation analysis identifies which attention heads contribute to subspace construction versus inference, tracking their geometric alignment with the subspace and attention patterns.

## Key Results
- A shared conceptual subspace emerges in middle‑to‑late layers and persists across different demonstration contexts.
- Ablating this subspace causes large drops in log‑probability (~‑1 to ‑2), while isolating it preserves near‑original performance, proving its causal necessity and sufficiency.
- Cross‑context transfer using relational offsets within the subspace yields high mediation scores (up to ~17), confirming context‑invariant generalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A shared, context‑invariant conceptual subspace emerges during the forward pass in middle‑to‑late layers.
- **Mechanism:** Layer‑wise SVD reveals increasing overlap of variance‑dominant directions after early layers. GCCA then isolates a linear subspace (via orthogonal projection matrices *W*<sub>ℓ</sub>) whose internal relational structure (measured by RSA) remains stable across these layers and across different demonstration contexts.
- **Core assumption:** The principal components capturing 95% of variance and the GCCA‑aligned subspace reflect the model’s organized conceptual knowledge, not just statistical noise.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "a conceptual subspace emerging in middle to late layers, whose representational structure persists across contexts."; [section 2.2] "middle to late layers displayed consistently high inter‑layer overlap... sharp increase in the number of principal components required to explain 95% of the variance."; [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers explore emergent representations but do not specifically validate this subspace’s cross‑context persistence via GCCA.
- **Break condition:** The subspace fails to emerge (low layer‑wise overlap) in shallow models or with extremely few demonstrations (<1), suggesting insufficient contextual integration.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The emergent conceptual subspace is causally necessary and sufficient for accurate in‑context concept inference.
- **Mechanism:** Causal mediation via activation patching, ablation, and isolation. Subspace ablation (removing *P*<sub>ℓ</sub>h<sub>ℓ</sub>) causes significant log‑prob drops; isolating the subspace (*P*<sub>ℓ</sub>h<sub>ℓ</sub> alone) preserves performance. Cross‑context transfer using relational offsets within the subspace yields high causal mediation scores.
- **Core assumption:** Orthogonal projection (*P*<sub>ℓ</sub> = *W*<sub>ℓ</sub>*W*<sub>ℓ</sub><sup>⊤</sup>) cleanly separates conceptual information from orthogonal residual noise.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "causal mediation analyses... demonstrate that this subspace is not merely an epiphenomenon but is functionally central to model predictions."; [section 3.2] "Ablating the conceptual subspace produced a marked performance drop... isolating the subspace was sufficient to maintain near‑original performance."; [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., "Uncovering Emergent Physics Representations") use similar causal methods but for different domains; this paper’s specific application to conceptual subspaces is novel.
- **Break condition:** Ablation effects vanish (log‑prob change ≈0) or isolation fails catastrophically, indicating the subspace is not functionally engaged.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A two‑stage attention‑head progression constructs and then leverages the conceptual subspace.
- **Mechanism:** Early‑to‑middle heads (identified via significant CIE under description/label corruption) attend to demonstration cues and write strongly into the subspace (high α, align). Later heads (identified under query corruption) attend to the query and produce aligned updates, using the established subspace for prediction.
- **Core assumption:** Attention head outputs (a<sub>ℓ,k</sub>) are additive contributors to the residual stream, and their geometric alignment with *W*<sub>ℓ</sub> indicates participation in subspace computation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Attention head analysis revealed a two‑stage progression: early‑to‑middle heads integrate demonstration cues to construct the subspace, while later heads leverage it for query‑conditioned inference."; [section 4.2] "Heads identified under demonstration corruptions... allocated most attention to contextual descriptions... Heads identified under query corruption exhibited heavy attention to query tokens."; [corpus] Neighbors like "Contextual Subspace Manifold Projection" propose architectural modifications for subspace control, indirectly supporting the role of structured subspaces.
- **Break condition:** No distinct layer‑wise split in head significance or attention patterns; all heads show similar behavior across corruption types.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: In‑Context Learning (ICL)**  
  - *Why needed here:* The paper investigates how LLMs perform inference from in‑context demonstrations without weight updates. Understanding ICL as an emergent phenomenon is the paper’s starting point.  
  - *Quick check question:* If you remove all demonstrations from the prompt, what happens to the model’s ability to infer the target concept from the query description alone?

- **Concept: Conceptual Representation**  
  - *Why needed here:* The study posits that LLMs build abstract, human‑like concept structures. This underpins the existence of a “conceptual subspace” organizing semantic relationships.  
  - *Quick check question:* Do model hidden states for semantically similar concepts (e.g., “bee” and “wasp”) cluster closer together than for dissimilar ones, even across different prompt contexts?

- **Concept: Subspace Identification (SVD & GCCA)**  
  - *Why needed here:* These are the primary tools to detect and isolate the shared latent structure across layers and contexts. SVD finds dominant variance directions; GCCA aligns them across layers.  
  - *Quick check question:* What would the subspace projection matrices *W*<sub>ℓ</sub> look like if the model’s representations were completely random and unstructured?

- **Concept: Causal Mediation Analysis**  
  - *Why needed here:* To move from correlation to causation—proving the subspace is *used* for inference, not just present. Techniques like patching, ablation, and isolation test necessity/sufficiency.  
  - *Quick check question:* If patching the conceptual subspace from a clean run into a corrupted run recovers performance, what does that imply about where the task‑relevant information is stored?

## Architecture Onboarding
Token Embeddings -> Residual Stream (h_ℓ) -> Attention Heads (a_ℓ,k) & MLP (m_ℓ) -> Updated Residual Stream -> (repeat per layer) -> Final Residual Stream (h_L) -> Decoder Head -> Logits

1. **Input processing (early layers):** Token embeddings are transformed; little context integration.
2. **Subspace construction (mid‑layers, ~L/2):** Demonstration descriptions/labels are integrated via specific attention heads. A coherent subspace geometry emerges (high GCCA alignment, RSA).
3. **Query‑conditioned inference (late‑mid layers):** Query information is integrated within the established subspace via different attention heads.
4. **Readout (final layers):** Subspace information is converted into token predictions; attention may shift to delimiters.

- **Subspace dimensionality (*r*):** Too low loses nuanced relational structure; too high includes noise. Selected via permutation test.
- **Context window size (N demos):** More demos stabilize subspace but increase compute. Effects plateau ~24 demos.
- **Model scale:** Larger models show cleaner, more robust subspaces; smaller models show more distributed, less distinct mechanisms.

- **Weak subspace:** Low GCCA alignment/RSA across layers/contexts.
- **Ablation resilience:** Removing *h*<sub>ℓ,∥</sub> causes minimal performance drop.
- **Attention incoherence:** No layer‑wise split in head significance or attention patterns; all heads show similar behavior across corruption types.
- **Cross‑context transfer failure:** Orthogonal transformation *Q* fails to mediate prediction changes (CMA ≈0).

1. **Replicate subspace emergence:** For a given model (e.g., Llama‑3.1 70B), run layer‑wise SVD on hidden states from 24 demos. Plot cross‑layer overlap and #PCs to 95% variance. Expect high overlap and variance jump in mid‑layers.
2. **Validate causal role:** Perform subspace ablation (set *h*<sub>ℓ</sub> ← (I−*P*<sub>ℓ</sub>)*h*<sub>ℓ</sub>) at layers around the emergence zone (e.g., L40, L60). Measure Δ log‑prob for correct token. Expect significant negative shift (~‑1 to ‑2).
3. **Identify construction heads:** Run activation patching for each head under description/label corruption. Plot CIEs; run permutation test for significance. Examine attention patterns of top heads; expect focus on demonstration tokens in early‑mid layers.

## Limitations
- **Domain specificity:** The study uses only a reverse dictionary task; generalizability to other ICL tasks is uncertain.
- **Subspace interpretability:** The exact semantic structure captured by the GCCA subspace remains opaque.
- **Head attribution simplicity:** The two‑stage model of attention heads may oversimplify, as heads likely have overlapping roles across layers and tasks.

## Confidence
- Subspace emergence & cross‑context stability: High
- Causal necessity/sufficiency of subspace: Medium‑High
- Two‑stage attention‑head mechanism: Medium
- Cross‑context transfer mediation: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Task diversity test:** Replicate core analyses on additional ICL tasks (e.g., few‑shot classification, symbolic reasoning) to assess generality.
2. **Subspace semantic probing:** Train linear probes on the GCCA subspace to predict human‑annotated concept features; high accuracy would strengthen interpretability claims.
3. **Targeted head intervention:** Patch only the outputs of “construction” vs. “inference” heads (identified via corruption) into ablated runs. If construction heads alone cannot rescue performance in later layers, the two‑stage claim weakens.
