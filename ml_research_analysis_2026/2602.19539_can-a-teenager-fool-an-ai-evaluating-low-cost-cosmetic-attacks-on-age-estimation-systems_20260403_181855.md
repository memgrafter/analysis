# Can a Teenager Fool an AI? Evaluating Low-Cost Cosmetic Attacks on Age Estimation Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19539
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19539
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Simple, accessible cosmetic modifications (e.g., a beard) can cause state-of-the-art age estimation models to misclassify minors as adults with Attack Conversion Rates up to 83%.

## Executive Summary
Age estimation systems are increasingly deployed to restrict online content access for minors, yet their vulnerability to simple, real-world cosmetic bypass techniques has not been systematically studied. This research evaluates whether household-accessible modifications—such as applying a beard, grey hair, makeup, or wrinkle effects—can fool AI age estimators. Using a Vision-Language Model (VLM) as a digital attack simulator, the authors applied these modifications to 329 facial images of individuals aged 10–21 and tested them against eight victim models (five specialized computer vision systems and three VLMs). The study introduces the Attack Conversion Rate (ACR) to measure the fraction of baseline minor predictions that flip to adult after an attack. Results show a single synthetic beard achieves 28–69% ACR, while a combination of all four attacks shifts predicted age by an average of +7.7 years and reaches up to 83% ACR. The findings reveal a critical vulnerability: low-effort, non-digital-native attacks can cause significant system bypass, urging the adoption of mandatory adversarial robustness evaluation for age estimation models in safety-critical deployments.

## Method Summary
The study assembled a dataset of 329 frontal facial images (ages 10–21) from eight public benchmarks. Attacks were simulated digitally using the Gemini 2.5 Flash Image editing VLM with standardized text prompts to apply four cosmetic modifications (beard, grey hair, makeup, wrinkles) individually and in all 15 non-empty combinations. For combined attacks, a priority-weighted pixel-delta blending strategy composited single-attack modifications to avoid artifact accumulation. Eight pre-trained age estimation models (five specialized CV models like MiVOLO and DEX, and three zero-shot VLMs like Gemini 3 Flash and GPT-5-Nano) were queried on both original and attacked images using each model’s standard preprocessing and inference protocol. The core metrics were Attack Conversion Rate (ACR)—the proportion of baseline minor predictions (estimated age <18) that became adult (≥18) post-attack—and the mean predicted age shift (∆¯y). Evaluations were stratified by true age and model type to analyze vulnerability patterns.

## Key Results
- A single synthetic beard achieves a broad Attack Conversion Rate (ACR) of 28–69% across all eight evaluated models.
- The combined four-attack (beard, grey hair, makeup, wrinkles) shifts predicted age by +7.7 years on average and attains a mean ACR of 68.9%, with a maximum of 83% on a specialized model.
- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) show a lower mean full-attack ACR range (59–71%) compared to specialized computer vision models (63–83%), though the ranges overlap substantially.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Simple cosmetic additions (beard, grey hair) alter localized facial cues that age estimators over-weight, systematically shifting predicted age upward.
- **Mechanism:** Age estimation models, particularly specialized convolutional architectures, are trained to recognize age-associated textures and structures in specific facial regions (e.g., jawline for beard, hair for greying). Adding these features introduces high-activation patterns correlated with older age in the model's feature space, causing a regression output to increase.
- **Core assumption:** Models rely heavily on sparse, localized indicators rather than holistic facial geometry or contextual reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "a synthetic beard alone achieves 28–69% ACR across all eight models"
  - [section 6.2] "Beard is the dominant single attack... Specialized models show larger mean shifts (avg. +4.3 yr) than VLMs (avg. +2.5 yr)"
  - [corpus] Weak corpus evidence; neighboring papers focus on adversarial patches or code comments, not cosmetic attacks.
- **Break condition:** Models that explicitly model facial structure holistically (e.g., via segmentation masks) or are adversarially trained on textured perturbations may reduce sensitivity.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Makeup disproportionately affects predictions near the 18-year decision boundary without inducing large mean age shifts, converting borderline cases.
- **Mechanism:** Makeup subtly alters skin tone uniformity and shadow contours. For subjects whose baseline prediction is just below 18 (e.g., 15–17), these small perturbations can push the regression output or classification score across the threshold. The effect is threshold-centric rather than systemic.
- **Core assumption:** Age estimators have a concentration of prediction uncertainty near the operational cutoff; cosmetic noise is sufficient to cross it for a subset.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 6.2] "Makeup has essentially zero mean shift (+0.05 yr)... yet achieves a non-trivial ACR (29–49%)... 74% of model–subject pairs show near-zero shift... 11% show a large upward jump (mean +5.9 yr); this subgroup has a mean baseline prediction of 15.9 yr"
  - [section 6.4] "Makeup shows a distinctive pattern: most subjects’ trajectories are nearly horizontal... but a subset of subjects near the threshold experience large upward jumps"
- **Break condition:** Models with calibrated uncertainty estimates or hysteresis in binary thresholding could ignore low-magnitude perturbations around the boundary.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Combining cosmotically distinct attacks (beard, hair, makeup, wrinkles) yields super-additive bypass rates because they target complementary age cues (facial hair, hair color, skin texture, facial lines).
- **Mechanism:** Each attack manipulates a separate semantic region or cue used by the model. Their combined application ensures multiple independent age indicators are altered simultaneously, making it statistically unlikely that the model's resilience in one cue domain can compensate for failures across all.
- **Core assumption:** Age estimation models integrate multiple weak cues; attacking several in concert overwhelms the model's aggregation logic.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "combining all four attacks shifts predicted age by +7.7 years on average... reaches up to 83% ACR"
  - [section 6.3] "Both mean shift and ACR grow with the number of combined attacks (from +1.8 yr / 34% ACR averaged over single attacks to +7.7 yr / 69% ACR for the all-four combination)"
  - [section 6.6] "The combined attack overcomes the age barrier... achieves high crossing rates even for ages 13–14"
- **Break condition:** Models that use robust, multi-cue fusion with non-linear redundancy (e.g., attention over diverse feature maps) might exhibit sub-linear attack scaling.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Attack Conversion Rate (ACR)**
  - *Why needed here:* ACR is the core metric introduced to quantify bypass success independent of test-set demographics. It measures the fraction of baseline minor predictions that flip to adult after attack.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is ACR population-agnostic, and how does this differ from reporting overall accuracy drop on a fixed test set?
- **Concept: Vision-Language Models (VLMs) vs. Specialized CV**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper compares robustness across two architectural paradigms; VLMs showed lower (but overlapping) ACR ranges. Understanding their different training objectives (multi-modal pre-training vs. age-specific regression) explains divergent vulnerability patterns.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might VLMs be less susceptible to beard attacks but more susceptible to grey hair attacks than specialized models?
- **Concept: Threat Model for Low-Skill Adversaries**
  - *Why needed here:* The attacks are defined by real-world accessibility (drugstore cosmetics, <10 minutes application), not computational optimality. This frames the risk as practical evasion rather than academic adversarial example crafting.
  - *Quick check question:* How does this threat model differ fundamentally from gradient-based adversarial patch attacks on face recognition?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Image Source (UTKFace, etc.) -> Filtering (age 10-21, frontal) -> Attack Simulator (Gemini 2.5 Flash Image editor with text prompts) -> Single-Attack Images -> Attack Combiner (priority-weighted pixel-delta blending) -> Combination Attack Images -> Model Zoo (5 CV + 3 VLMs) -> Inference Engine (standardized preprocessing/prompting) -> Metrics Engine (ACR, ∆¯y calculation) -> Stratified Analysis.
- **Critical path:** Dataset filtering → Single-attack generation → Combination blending → Model inference per attack condition → Metric calculation (ACR, ∆¯y) → Stratified analysis (by true age, model type).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Simulation vs. real attacks:* Digital simulation avoids ethical issues and enables scale but may not capture lighting, angle, and skin-type variations of physical makeup.
  - *Single VLM editor:* Using one editor (Gemini) controls for editor variance but limits generalizability; different editors may produce cosmetically different results.
  - *Model selection breadth:* 8 models provide cross-paradigm comparison but small denominators for some ACR estimates (e.g., DEX N=41) increase uncertainty.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High image generation refusal rates, especially for true minors (86% of dropped beard-attack images were minors).
  - Large variance in ACR across models with similar clean MAE (e.g., Custom-Best vs. MiVOLO).
  - Sequential attack degradation without blending strategy (not used here but a common pitfall).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Single-attack benchmarking:** For each of the 4 attacks, compute ACR and mean age shift per model. Verify beard is strongest for CV models, grey hair for VLMs.
  2. **Combination synergy test:** For all 15 non-empty attack subsets, measure ACR and ∆¯y. Confirm monotonic increase with attack count and identify super-additive pairs (e.g., Beard + Wrinkles).
  3. **Age-stratified vulnerability analysis:** Disaggregate ACR by ground-truth age bins (10–12, 13–14, 15–17). Validate that 15–17 is most vulnerable and that the full combination extends high bypass rates to younger teens.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What is the discrepancy in attack effectiveness between VLM-simulated cosmetic modifications and real-world physical applications under diverse conditions (lighting, angles, skin types)? *Basis:* "Our attacks are simulated, and real-world cosmetics applied under varying lighting, angles, and skin types may show different effects." *Why unresolved:* Ethical constraints prevented physical experimentation on minors; simulation may not capture all real-world variability in cosmetic application and imaging.
- **Open Question 2:** Does incorporating video liveness detection or multi-frame analysis significantly improve robustness against cosmetic attacks compared to single-image models? *Basis:* "We studied only static single-image inputs; systems using video liveness detection or multi-frame reasoning may be more robust." *Why unresolved:* The evaluation exclusively used static image inputs; video-based or temporal aggregation systems were not tested.
- **Open Question 3:** How effective are other common, low-effort modifications (e.g., hats, glasses, strategic lighting changes) as cosmetic bypass methods, individually and in combination? *Basis:* "We studied only four attack types; other modifications (hats, glasses, facial hair styles, lighting manipulation) represent an open threat surface." *Why unresolved:* The study focused on four specific attacks; other plausible, easily accessible modifications remain unevaluated.
- **Open Question 4:** Is the observed trend of lower ACR in VLMs versus specialized CV models statistically significant, and does it persist when controlling for baseline minor-prediction rates? *Basis:* "VLMs exhibit lower ACR (59–71%) than specialized models (63–83%), though ranges overlap and confidence intervals are wide... whether this trend reflects architectural differences requires further study." Also, "no statistical significance test is reported." *Why unresolved:* Overlapping confidence intervals and lack of formal statistical testing prevent definitive conclusions about inherent architectural robustness differences.

## Limitations
- Simulation-based attacks using a single VLM editor (Gemini 2.5 Flash) may not capture the variability of real-world cosmetic application or the outputs of other image editors.
- Exact model checkpoints, preprocessing pipelines, and inference settings for the specialized CV models are not provided, hindering precise replication and comparison.
- The threat model is confined to digital simulation on a fixed dataset, limiting generalizability to physical attacks under diverse environmental conditions and to other age estimation architectures.

## Confidence
- **Core claim (low-cost cosmetics cause significant bypass):** High—supported by consistent empirical results across eight models and multiple attack combinations, with clear metrics.
- **Proposed mechanisms (localized cue weighting, threshold sensitivity, multi-cue synergy):** Medium—evidence is observational (e.g., beard’s dominance, makeup’s threshold-centric jumps) but lacks ablative feature attribution studies to confirm model reliance on specific facial regions.
- **Generalization (to physical attacks, other models, adversary skill):** Low—to physical attacks (simulation vs. real), other age estimators (limited model zoo), and adversary skill (household-accessible vs. optimally crafted).

## Next Checks
1.  **Physical Attack Transfer Test:** Physically apply the four cosmetic modifications (using standard drugstore products) to a subset of the 329 subjects under controlled lighting. Capture real photographs and evaluate the same eight models. Compare ACR and age shifts against the simulated results to quantify the simulation-to-real gap.
2.  **Editor & Model Breadth Replication:** Re-run the full simulation pipeline using at least two additional open-source VLM image editors (e.g., Stable Diffusion with InstructPix2Pix, Flux). Additionally, obtain and evaluate the next-generation versions of the specialized CV models cited (e.g., MiVOLO+). This assesses robustness to editor/model variation.
3.  **Feature Attribution Analysis:** For a representative high-vulnerability model (e.g., Custom-Best), perform Grad-CAM or occlusion sensitivity on baseline vs. attacked images. Quantify the change in activation heatmaps for facial regions targeted by each attack (jawline for beard, hair for grey, peri-ocular for makeup wrinkles). This directly tests the "sparse, localized cues" mechanism.
