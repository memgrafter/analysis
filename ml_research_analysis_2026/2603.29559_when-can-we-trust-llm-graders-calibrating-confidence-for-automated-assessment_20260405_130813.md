# When Can We Trust LLM Graders? Calibrating Confidence for Automated Assessment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.29559
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29559
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Prompting LLMs to self-report confidence yields the best-calibrated estimates (mean ECE 0.166) at 5× lower inference cost than self-consistency voting.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates methods to estimate the reliability of LLM-based automated grading, aiming to enable selective automation where only highly trustworthy predictions are used. It compares three confidence estimation approaches—self-reported confidence, self-consistency voting, and token probability—across seven LLMs (4B–120B parameters) on three educational datasets. Self-reported confidence consistently achieves superior calibration (lowest Expected Calibration Error) and strong discrimination, while being the most cost-efficient. However, all methods exhibit a heavy top-skew in confidence scores, limiting the proportion of predictions that can be automated under strict risk thresholds. The findings highlight self-reported confidence as a practical and effective solution for identifying reliable grading decisions.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates confidence estimation for binary grading decisions (correct/incorrect) on student responses. Three confidence methods are tested: (1) **Self-reported confidence**: The model outputs a numeric confidence (1–10) alongside its verdict via instruction prompting; (2) **Self-consistency**: The model is sampled multiple times (n=5) and confidence is the fraction of samples agreeing on the majority verdict; (3) **Token probability**: Confidence is derived from the aggregated softmax probabilities of yes/no tokens in the output distribution. Calibration is measured via Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier score; discrimination via AUC-ROC; and practical utility via coverage at fixed risk thresholds (10% and 5%). Experiments span seven LLMs (including Llama, Qwen, and GPT-OSS families) and three educational QA datasets (RiceChem, SciEntsBank, Beetle).

## Key Results
- Self-reported confidence achieves the best calibration across all models and datasets, with an average ECE of 0.166 versus 0.229 for self-consistency (38% worse) despite requiring only one fifth of the inference cost.
- The largest model (GPT-OSS-120B) attains the best overall calibration (avg ECE 0.100) and strong discrimination (avg AUC 0.668).
- Confidence scores are heavily top-skewed; over 86% of predictions exceed 0.8 confidence, resulting in low coverage at strict risk thresholds (e.g., only ~11%–20% qualify for ≤5% risk).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Self-reported confidence leverages model metacognition
- **Claim**: Prompting the LLM to verbalize its confidence yields well-calibrated estimates because it accesses internal uncertainty representations more directly than sampling-based methods.
- **Mechanism**: The model’s verbal confidence output is derived from its internal reasoning process, which integrates evidence from the question, student response, and rubric. This metacognitive signal aligns closely with the model’s true accuracy on the grading task.
- **Core assumption**: LLMs have latent states that reflect their certainty about a decision, and these states can be reliably elicited via instruction-style prompting.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "self-reported confidence consistently achieves the best calibration across all conditions"
  - [section 4.2]: Tables 1–3 and paired bootstrap comparisons show self-reported confidence achieving lower ECE and Brier scores than self-consistency and token probability in nearly all model-dataset combinations.
  - [corpus]: Weak; neighboring papers explore calibration but do not directly compare these three methods. The corpus does not provide independent evidence for this mechanism.
- **Break condition**: If the prompting fails to elicit honest uncertainty (e.g., the model is tuned to always express high confidence), self-reported confidence becomes overconfident and miscalibrated, especially on out-of-distribution examples.

### Mechanism 2: Self-consistency’s discrete granularity harms calibration
- **Claim**: The majority-vote fraction in self-consistency produces a discrete set of confidence levels (multiples of 1/n), which limits calibration precision and leads to worse ECE despite higher computational cost.
- **Mechanism**: With n=5 samples, self-consistency yields only 6 possible confidence values (0, 0.2, ..., 1.0). When binning predictions for ECE, these discrete levels cause uneven bin populations and poor alignment between confidence and accuracy.
- **Core assumption**: Calibration error is sensitive to the granularity of confidence scores; coarser granularity inherently increases ECE for a given accuracy distribution.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.2]: "self-consistency is discrete by construction: 89.5% of outputs are 5/5 (1.0), 6.1% are 4/5 (0.8), and 4.5% are 3/5 (0.6)" and "self-consistency remains 38% worse despite requiring 5× the inference cost."
  - [abstract]: Mentions the 38% worse ECE and 5× cost.
  - [corpus]: Weak; no direct evidence about granularity effects from neighboring papers.
- **Break condition**: Increasing n dramatically would improve granularity but at prohibitive cost; if the model’s sampling distribution is highly concentrated, even large n may not yield diverse confidence levels.

### Mechanism 3: Top-skewed confidence creates a practical automation ceiling
- **Claim**: Confidence scores are heavily concentrated at high values (≥0.8) across all methods, which limits the proportion of predictions that can be automated at strict risk thresholds (e.g., 5% error).
- **Mechanism**: The educational grading tasks align well with the models’ pretraining knowledge, leading to high certainty on most instances. Consequently, even the most stringent confidence thresholds (e.g., >0.95) capture only a small fraction of predictions because the confidence distribution lacks a long tail of low-confidence examples.
- **Core assumption**: The datasets used (RiceChem, SciEntsBank, Beetle) are representative of typical educational content that LLMs have seen during training, causing overfitting-like confidence.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.2]: "Pooling self-reported and token-probability outputs, 86% of predictions exceed 0.8; only 0.62% fall below 0.2."
  - [section 4.3]: Histograms in Figure 1 show most mass in 0.7–0.9 range.
  - [section 4.5]: "Coverage@10%Risk is about 20% and Coverage@5%Risk is 11.5%... only about 11%–20% of predictions qualify."
- **Break condition**: If the deployment data includes many out-of-distribution or ambiguous student responses, the confidence distribution would flatten, allowing higher coverage at fixed risk.

## Foundational Learning
- **Calibration Metrics (ECE and Brier Score)**: Quantify how well confidence scores match actual accuracy. ECE measures the weighted average absolute difference between confidence and accuracy across bins; Brier score is the mean squared error between confidence and binary correctness. *Why needed*: These metrics determine which confidence estimation method is best calibrated. *Quick check*: "What does an ECE of 0.0 signify?" → Perfect calibration; confidence equals accuracy in every bin.
- **Self-consistency**: A confidence method that samples the model multiple times and uses the fraction of samples agree on the same verdict as confidence. *Why needed*: It is a key baseline method; understanding its mechanism (majority vote over samples) is essential to interpret why it underperforms. *Quick check*: "If you use self-consistency with n=5 and get a confidence of 0.6, what does that mean?" → 3 out of 5 samples agreed on the same verdict.
- **Selective Automation (Coverage at Risk)**: The practice of automating only predictions with high confidence, trading off coverage (percentage of instances automated) against risk (error rate on automated subset). *Why needed*: The ultimate goal is to decide which predictions to automate; this links calibration to practical deployment. *Quick check*: "If we automate predictions with confidence >0.9 and this subset has 98% accuracy, what is the risk?" → 2% error rate, so risk is 2%.
- **Token Probability Aggregation**: Extracting confidence from the model’s output logits by summing probabilities of yes/no tokens within a top-k set. *Why needed*: It is a commonly used, theoretically grounded method that bypasses explicit prompting. *Quick check*: "What is a key vulnerability of token probability confidence?" → It depends on the tokenizer and may miss relevant tokens if they fall outside the top-k or are split unexpectedly.
- **Model Scale Effects**: Larger language models generally improve accuracy and sometimes calibration, but the gains are not uniform across datasets or confidence methods. *Why needed*: Understanding scale trends informs resource allocation for deployment. *Quick check*: "Does increasing model size always improve calibration?" → No; the paper shows token probability calibration can worsen on some datasets despite accuracy gains.

## Architecture Onboarding
Data loader -> Prompt template -> LLM interface -> Confidence estimator module -> Evaluation engine -> Statistical analyzer

**Critical path**:
1. Load a dataset instance (question, student response, rubric item, ground truth).
2. Construct prompt using the template with the instance content.
3. Call LLM via the interface to generate response(s).
4. Extract verdict and confidence according to the chosen method (self-reported, token probability, or self-consistency).
5. Compare verdict to ground truth to determine correctness.
6. Accumulate (confidence, correctness) pairs for all instances.
7. Compute calibration metrics (ECE, Brier), discrimination (AUC), and coverage at risk thresholds.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Cost vs calibration: Self-consistency requires 5× more LLM calls but yields worse calibration; self-reported is cheaper and better.
- Model size vs resources: Larger models improve accuracy and calibration but demand more GPU memory and compute.
- Confidence granularity: Continuous scores (self-reported, token probability) allow finer thresholding than discrete self-consistency.

**Failure signatures**:
- High ECE (>0.3): Severe miscalibration; check prompt design or model choice.
- Extreme top-skew: >95% of confidences >0.9 suggests overconfidence or task ease; coverage will be low.
- Low accuracy (<70%): The LLM grader itself is unreliable; confidence estimation may not salvage performance.
- Bootstrap CI overlapping zero for paired differences: No significant advantage between methods.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Replicate main result: Run self-reported confidence on GPT-OSS-120B with SciEntsBank. Verify ECE ≈0.081 (Table 5) and coverage@5% risk ≈11.5% (Section 4.5).
2. Compare token probability vs self-reported: On a small subset (e.g., 100 instances), compute confidence from both methods and plot calibration curves. Expect self-reported closer to the diagonal.
3. Test prompting sensitivity: For self-reported confidence, try variations (integer 1–10 vs float 0–1, add "If unsure, say 0" instruction). Measure changes in ECE to assess robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Why does self-reported confidence achieve better calibration than self-consistency voting despite requiring only one forward pass compared to five? The paper documents the outcome but does not investigate underlying mechanisms, such as whether self-consistency's discrete vote-based confidence introduces granularity limitations or if verbalized uncertainty captures nuances majority votes miss. *What evidence would resolve it*: Ablation studies varying self-consistency sample count; probing internal representations versus language in self-reports; analysis of disagreement cases.
- **Open Question 2**: What causes the "confidence floor" where over 86% of predictions exceed 0.8, and how should practitioners optimally set automation thresholds given this skewed distribution? The skew is quantified but its origins (training objectives, decoding strategies, prompt influences) are unexplained. *What evidence would resolve it*: Analysis linking model architecture/fine-tuning/decoding parameters to confidence distributions; simulation studies of threshold-selection heuristics that account for skew.
- **Open Question 3**: Why do calibration improvements from larger model scale vary by dataset and confidence method (e.g., token probability worsens on RiceChem and Beetle)? The paper notes the pattern but does not analyze dataset-specific properties (answer length, domain complexity, rubric structure) that might interact differently across methods. *What evidence would resolve it*: Controlled experiments isolating dataset characteristics; diagnostic analysis of token probability distributions across scales; comparing calibrated vs. uncalibrated token logits.

## Limitations
- Study confined to three educational datasets with English content, limiting generalizability to other domains or languages.
- Models restricted to seven specific LLMs, with two (GPT‑OSS‑20B/120B) not publicly accessible, hindering full replication.
- Confidence estimates rely heavily on prompt design; small wording changes may alter calibration and impede reproducibility.
- Observed top‑skewed confidence distribution might reflect dataset ease rather than inherent LLM behavior; may not hold for more ambiguous or out-of-distribution tasks.

## Confidence
- *Self‑reported confidence superiority*: **Medium**. Strong empirical support across models and datasets, but external validation weak and prompt sensitivity untested.
- *Self‑consistency granularity harm*: **High**. Clear theoretical and empirical evidence; discrete confidence steps directly cause higher ECE.
- *Top‑skewed confidence ceiling*: **Medium**. Histograms and coverage metrics show the effect, yet it may not hold for more diverse or ambiguous tasks.
- *Larger models improve accuracy more than calibration*: **High**. Consistent across datasets and methods; accuracy gains are clear and significant.

## Next Checks
1. **Out-of-distribution evaluation**: Apply the three confidence methods to new educational domains (e.g., mathematics, humanities) and measure calibration shifts.
2. **Prompt robustness test**: Systematically vary self‑reported confidence prompts (scale, wording, uncertainty instructions) to quantify impact on ECE.
3. **Cross‑task generalization**: Replicate the study on non‑grading tasks (e.g., summarization quality assessment) to determine if conclusions extend beyond educational QA.
