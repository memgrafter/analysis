# When Vision Overrides Language: Evaluating and Mitigating Counterfactual Failures in VLAs

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.17659
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17659
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Vision-Language-Action models frequently disregard language instructions due to vision shortcuts, a problem mitigated by Counterfactual Action Guidance (CAG), which improves grounding and task success without architectural changes.

## Executive Summary
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions, suffer from "counterfactual failures": they execute familiar vision-driven behaviors even when the language instruction changes. This stems from training data biases where many demonstrations exist for a fixed scene but with sparse language variation, causing the model to rely on vision shortcuts. The paper introduces LIBERO-CF, a counterfactual benchmark with alternative instructions for the same scene to expose this failure. To address it, they propose Counterfactual Action Guidance (CAG), an inference-time method that combines a standard language-conditioned VLA policy with a language-unconditioned Vision-Action (VA) prior. This reweights actions to amplify language conditioning. On LIBERO-CF and in real-world evaluations, CAG significantly improves language grounding and task success, especially on under-observed tasks.

## Method Summary
Counterfactual Action Guidance (CAG) is a training-free inference scheme that enhances a VLA's language conditioning without altering its architecture or retraining. It operates by maintaining two policy branches: (1) the original VLA policy \(\pi_{\text{cond}}(a|o,l)\) that conditions on both observation \(o\) and instruction \(l\), and (2) a language-unconditioned Vision-Action (VA) prior \(\pi_{\text{uncond}}(a|o)\) that conditions only on vision. At inference, CAG computes a mixed action distribution: \(\pi_{\text{CAG}}(a|o,l) = \pi_{\text{uncond}}(a|o) + \omega \cdot (\pi_{\text{cond}}(a|o,l) - \pi_{\text{uncond}}(a|o))\). The guidance scale \(\omega\) controls the strength of the language-induced shift. The unconditional VA branch can be implemented either by dropping language inputs from the VLA (training-free) or by separately training a model on \((o, a)\) pairs only. This approach probabilistically corresponds to reweighting the action distribution by \(P(l|a,o)^\omega\), sharpening the model's adherence to the instruction.

## Key Results
- CAG improves π0.5's grounding rate by up to 15.5% and task success by 8.5% on counterfactual tasks with under-observed instructions.
- Training-free CAG (using the same VLA with language dropped) yields gains of 9.7% in grounding and 3.6% in success.
- A separately trained VA model yields larger gains than the training-free variant.
- In real-world robot evaluations, CAG reduces counterfactual failures by 9.4% and increases task success by 17.2% on average.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Vision Shortcuts from Modality Imbalance
VLAs exhibit counterfactual failures because they rely on vision shortcuts—a form of posterior collapse where the action distribution is dominated by visual features rather than language. The training data for VLAs typically contains many visual demonstrations per scene but few language variations. This modality imbalance causes the learned policy to approximate \(P(a|o,l) \approx P(a|o)\), reducing language to a minor modulation. Under a fixed scene, the set of observed tasks during finetuning (\(L_o^{\text{in}}\)) is much smaller than the full set of feasible tasks (\(L_o\)), so actions become strongly associated with frequent visual objects and neglect the language intent. The vision shortcut would not form if training data provided a balanced and exhaustive set of language instructions per scene, but this is currently impractical due to annotation cost.

### Mechanism 2: CAG Reweights for Language Conditioning
Counterfactual Action Guidance (CAG) mitigates vision shortcuts by mixing actions from a language-conditioned VLA and an unconditional Vision-Action (VA) prior, amplifying the language-action likelihood at inference. CAG computes \(\pi_{\text{CAG}}(a|o,l) = \pi_{\text{uncond}}(a|o,\emptyset) + \omega \cdot (\pi_{\text{cond}}(a|o,l) - \pi_{\text{uncond}}(a|o,\emptyset))\). This subtraction isolates the language-induced shift and reweights the posterior: \(P_{\text{CAG}}(a|o,l) \propto P(a|o) \cdot P(l|a,o)^\omega\), sharpening language conditioning without retraining. This holds when the unconditional policy is a true vision-only prior. If the guidance scale \(\omega\) is too high, over-guidance degrades execution success; if \(\omega = 0\) the method reverts to the vision-only prior, providing no benefit.

### Mechanism 3: Separate VA Model Improves Guidance
Training a dedicated Vision-Action (VA) model as the unconditional branch yields stronger improvements than the training-free strategy (using the same VLA with language dropped), because it provides a purer estimate of the vision-only prior \(P(a|o)\). The training-free approach repurposes a VLA (trained with language) as the unconditional policy, but its weights may retain language biases or suboptimal representations. A separately trained VA model, optimized only on vision-action pairs, better approximates \(P(a|o)\), making the CAG difference more accurate for reweighting. Empirically, the separate VA model consistently outperforms training-free across VLAs and counterfactual suites.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Observation \(o\) & Instruction \(l\) -> VLA branch (\(\pi_{\text{cond}}\)) -> Action distribution  
  Observation \(o\) -> VA branch (\(\pi_{\text{uncond}}\)) -> Action distribution  
  Two distributions -> CAG mixer (guidance scale \(\omega\)) -> Final action distribution -> Executed action

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Input: current observation \(o\) and instruction \(l\).  
  2. Forward pass through VLA to obtain \(\pi_{\text{cond}}(a|o,l)\).  
  3. Forward pass through VA to obtain \(\pi_{\text{uncond}}(a|o)\).  
  4. Compute weighted difference: \(\Delta = \omega \cdot (\pi_{\text{cond}} - \pi_{\text{uncond}})\).  
  5. Output distribution: \(\pi_{\text{CAG}} = \pi_{\text{uncond}} + \Delta\) (normalized if needed).  
  6. Sample or select action \(a\) and execute.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Training-free vs separate VA:** Training-free (reuse VLA) is trivial but yields smaller gains; separate VA requires extra training/data but gives a cleaner prior and larger improvements.  
  - **Guidance scale:** Must be tuned per model; higher \(\omega\) strengthens language adherence but risks over-guidance. Dataset-specific ranges: π0/π0.5 use \(\omega=1.5\); OpenVLA-OFT uses \(\omega=3.0\).  
  - **Action space integration:** CAG assumes two branches output distributions over the same action space. If VLA uses tokenized actions and VA uses continuous, a projection layer is needed.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Low grounding rate with CAG:** \(\omega\) too low or VA model poorly trained (does not capture true \(P(a|o)\)).  
  - **High grounding but low success:** Over-guidance (\(\omega\) too high) or VA model too weak, producing unnatural actions.  
  - **Regression on in-domain tasks:** VA model trained on mismatched scenes; check data coverage.  
  - **Small gap between TF and VA:** The VLA is already weakly language-conditioned; CAG has limited headroom.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Modality ablation:** Replicate Table I. For a VLA (e.g., π0.5) on LIBERO, evaluate with vision-only, language-only, and both inputs. Confirm near-zero language-only success and high vision-only success, establishing vision shortcuts.  
  2. **Implement training-free CAG:** Using the same VLA, derive \(\pi_{\text{uncond}}\) by dropping language. Test on a few counterfactual tasks (e.g., CF-Spatial). Sweep \(\omega \in \{0,1,2,3\}\). Expect monotonic improvement in grounding up to a peak, then decline.  
  3. **Train a separate VA model:** Finetune the VLA backbone on LIBERO without language (same hyperparameters). Compare CAG with VA vs. training-free on LIBERO-CF (focus on CF-OOD). Quantify extra gain and verify that in-domain performance remains stable.

## Limitations
- Real-world evaluation scope is narrow (single robot, limited tasks), raising generalizability concerns.
- CAG's effectiveness hinges on training a high-quality VA prior; poor VA data or mismatched scenes degrades performance.
- Results are benchmark-dependent (LIBERO-CF); performance on entirely new domains or VLA architectures is unverified.
- The "vision shortcut" mechanism, while plausible, lacks direct community validation—prior work (e.g., CAST) addresses instruction following but not this specific modality-collapse hypothesis.

## Confidence
- **Vision-shortcut cause of failures:** Medium. Evidence is primarily internal (ablation studies on LIBERO-CF), with limited external corroboration from the corpus.
- **CAG's inference-time reweighting:** Medium. Theoretically grounded and empirically effective on tested models, but the assumed factorization \(P(a|o,l) \propto P(a|o)P(l|a,o)\) and the accuracy of \(\pi_{\text{uncond}}\) as a prior are not independently validated.
- **Separate VA > training-free:** Medium-High. Consistent empirical gains across tables, but the benefit magnitude may be dataset-specific and could diminish if VLA is pre-trained with strong language grounding.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture validation:** Apply CAG to at least two VLA architectures beyond π0/OpenVLA (e.g., RT-2, VLM-B) on LIBERO-CF. This tests whether gains generalize beyond the paper's model subset.
2. **Out-of-distribution scene testing:** Evaluate CAG on scenes completely unseen during VA/VLA training (e.g., new kitchen layouts, object sets). This probes robustness to visual distribution shifts and the VA prior's validity.
3. **VA data-minimality ablation:** Train VA models with decreasing fractions of the original robot demonstration data (e.g., 100%, 50%, 10%). Measure CAG's grounding rate versus VA data size to identify the minimal data requirement and failure thresholds.
