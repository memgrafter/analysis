# Not-in-Perspective: Towards Shielding Google's Perspective API Against Adversarial Negation Attacks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09343
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09343
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Hybrid formal reasoning wrappers improved accuracy on a negation-adversarial dataset from 0.3% to 84-87% and reduced toxicity scores by over 30%.

## Executive Summary
Adversarial negation attacks—inserting "not" before toxic words—cause machine learning toxicity detectors like Google’s Perspective API to fail. The authors propose hybrid formal reasoning wrappers as pre- or post-processing layers. These wrappers detect negated phrases via part-of-speech tagging and either heuristically invert their toxicity scores (adjusting for segment weights) or replace negated words with antonyms and paraphrase to average scores. On a dataset of 418-382 sentences, the best heuristic methods (M1.2-P, M1.5-P) boosted accuracy from 0.3% (raw Perspective) to 84-87% and reduced toxicity scores by more than 30% for most negated comments. The results demonstrate that lightweight formal reasoning can effectively shield statistical models against such logical attacks without costly retraining.

## Method Summary
The authors develop two families of hybrid wrappers that combine a base toxicity detector (e.g., Perspective API, LSTM+CNN, BERT+BiLSTM) with formal reasoning about negation. The first family (M1.x) uses a Stanford PoS tagger to split the input sentence into segments, identifies negated segments, queries the base model for each segment’s toxicity score, inverts the score for negated segments (using 1 – TS) if the segment’s original score exceeds 0.5, and recombines the scores using word-count weights (M1.2) or approximated segment weights derived from comparing original and negated sentences (M1.5). The second family (M2.x) substitutes antonyms for negated toxic words (via Thesaurus crawlers), generates multiple paraphrases of the altered sentences (using Parrot), and averages the base model’s scores across all paraphrases. Both approaches are evaluated on a negation-adversarial dataset created by inserting "not" before toxic words in sentences from the Jigsaw dataset. The heuristic adjustment methods achieve high accuracy (84-87%) while the substitution methods are less consistent due to missing antonyms and high computational cost (O(5^n)).

## Key Results
- Best heuristic methods (M1.2-P, M1.5-P) raised classification accuracy on negated sentences from 0.3% (raw Perspective) to 84-87%.
- Toxicity scores for the majority of negated comments dropped by over 30% after wrapper processing.
- Hybrid methods significantly outperformed pure statistical models (LSTM, BERT) on the negation test set, which struggled even on Hosseini’s original set.
- The Substitution & Exploration approach (M2.x) reduced scores but was less accurate and consistent, highlighting limitations of antonym-based strategies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper identifies three complementary mechanisms that explain the effectiveness of the hybrid wrappers.

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A formal reasoning wrapper can significantly mitigate negation attacks by recomputing toxicity scores after logically inverting the contribution of negated phrases.
- **Mechanism:** The system uses a Part-of-Speech (PoS) tagger to identify phrase segments affected by a negation token (e.g., "not"). It then applies a weighted average formula to the original toxicity scores of the sentence’s segments, inverting the score (using `1 - TS`) for any segment classified as negated and toxic. Segment weights are based on word count ratios.
- **Core assumption:** Negation logically inverts the toxicity polarity of the directly modified phrase, and this effect can be approximated by a deterministic rule (`1 - TS`) applied to the ML model’s raw score for that phrase.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract states the wrapper detects negated phrases via PoS tagging and heuristically inverts toxicity scores.  
  - Section 3.2 details recursive parsing, segment splitting, and specific equations (Eq. 1-4) for weighted score recomputation, modeling negation as logical NOT.  
  - Section 4.3 reports M1.2-P and M1.5-P achieved 84-87% accuracy on a 418/382-sentence negation-adversarial dataset, improving from 0.3%.
- **Break condition:** Fails when the PoS tagger misidentifies negation scope (e.g., with complex syntax or sarcasm). Also fails if the base ML model’s segment toxicity score is inaccurate for non-toxic negated phrases; a heuristic threshold (TS > 0.5) is used to avoid inverting such cases. Corpus evidence on negation scope parsing is weak.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Negation can be neutralized by semantically reversing the negated content (replacing words with antonyms) and averaging the toxicity scores over the resulting set of paraphrased sentences.
- **Mechanism:** After PoS-based identification of negated words, a thesaurus crawler fetches antonyms (up to 5 per word). These antonyms substitute the negated words, creating a set of altered sentences. A paraphraser (Parrot) then generates multiple variants for each substituted sentence. The final toxicity score is the average of all paraphrases’ scores from the ML detector.
- **Core assumption:** The semantic opposite of a toxic phrase is non-toxic, and generating multiple paraphrases captures the variance in how the base ML model might interpret the rephrased content.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract mentions replacing negated words with antonyms and paraphrasing to average scores.  
  - Section 3.3 describes the full pipeline: PoS tagging → antonym retrieval (Thesaurus/OneLook) → paraphrase generation (Parrot) → score averaging, including a Lesk algorithm variant for contextual disambiguation.  
  - Section 4.2 shows M2.1-P/M2.2-P reduce toxicity scores but are less accurate/consistent than M1.2/1.5.  
  - Section 5.1 attributes failures to lack of antonyms for certain profane words (e.g., "shit", "fuk").
- **Break condition:** Requires valid, non-ambiguous antonyms for all targeted toxic words. Fails for slang, neologisms, or highly context-dependent terms where antonyms don’t exist or distort meaning. Computationally expensive (O(5^n) complexity). Related work does not evaluate antonym substitution for negation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Hybrid formal reasoning wrappers are effective because they leverage the pattern-recognition strength of ML models for base scoring while using lightweight logical rules to correct a specific, well-defined semantic failure mode (negation), avoiding the need for impractically large adversarial retraining.
- **Mechanism:** The wrapper acts as a modular pre/post-processing layer. It does not alter the base ML model’s training. Instead, it intercepts input or output, applies a deterministic transformation based on a syntactic/semantic rule (negation inversion), and returns a corrected score. This preserves the base model’s utility for non-adversarial inputs.
- **Core assumption:** Certain logical operations (like negation) are discrete, binary transformations that can be perfectly handled by symbolic rules, while the nuanced detection of "toxicness" remains a statistical learning problem. The synergy is more effective than either approach alone.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract positions the wrapper as pre/post-processing and highlights "hybrid (formal reasoning and machine-learning) methods" outperforming "purely statistical solutions."  
  - Section 1 cites the Learn2Reason concept: some problems (negation logic) are better solved with formal reasoning, others (toxicity detection) with statistical approaches.  
  - Section 4.3 shows hybrid methods (M1.2-P) achieve ~85% accuracy vs. Perspective API’s 0.3% on the negation test set.  
  - Section 5.2 notes M1.2/1.5 are singled out for reliability and effectiveness, while pure ML models (LSTM, BERT) were inconsistent even on Hosseini’s original set.
- **Break condition:** Only addresses negation attacks. Vulnerable to other logical/semantic attacks (e.g., sarcasm, implicature, typed perturbations). The rule must perfectly identify the negated scope; a single parsing error corrupts the segment’s weight and inversion. Related work shows bias issues in toxicity models but not negation-specific hybrid defenses.

## Foundational Learning
### Negation Scope & Polarity in NLP
- **Why needed:** The entire defense depends on correctly identifying which words/phrases are semantically negated by "not" or similar tokens. Misparsing scope (e.g., "not only toxic but also harmful") causes incorrect segment inversion and score corruption.
- **Quick check:** Given the sentence "This is not just bad, it's terrible," which words are in the negated scope? How would incorrect scope detection affect a toxicity score?

### Ensemble/Bagging Principles
- **Why needed:** The Substitution & Exploration method (M2) is an ensemble approach: it creates multiple sentence variants (via antonym substitution + paraphrasing) and averages their scores. This averages out the base model’s variance on perturbed inputs, similar to model ensembling but at the data level.
- **Quick check:** Why might averaging scores over 50 paraphrased variants yield a more robust toxicity estimate for a negated sentence than a single query?

### The "Learn2Reason" Paradigm
- **Why needed:** The paper’s core thesis is that hybrid systems are necessary because negation is a logical operation a pure statistical ML model struggles with. This concept justifies allocating the negation problem to a formal reasoning module instead of hoping adversarial training covers the infinite variations.
- **Quick check:** What is a concrete example of a problem (other than negation) in toxicity detection that might be better handled by formal reasoning vs. pure ML?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
1. Input Parser & Contraction Expander -> 2. Negation Scope Detector -> (M1 path) Reasoning Engine -> Backend Query (per segment) -> Recombine -> Output. (M2 path) Antonym Retriever -> Paraphraser -> Backend Query (per paraphrase) -> Aggregator -> Output.

### Critical Path
- **M1:** Input → Parser → Scope Detector → Reasoning Engine → Backend Query (per segment) → Recombine → Output.
- **M2:** Input → Parser → Scope Detector → Antonym Retriever → Paraphraser → Backend Query (per paraphrase) → Aggregator → Output.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **M1 vs. M2:** M1 is fast (few backend calls) but relies on a brittle heuristic (`1 - TS`). M2 is semantically intuitive but computationally heavy (O(5^n) calls) and fails without antonyms.
- **Wrapper Placement:** Pre-processing (modify input) vs. post-processing (modify score). Paper uses both: pre-processing for antonym substitution, post-processing for score inversion.
- **Thresholding:** The decision to invert a segment’s score based on `TS > 0.5` is a heuristic to avoid inverting non-toxic negations (e.g., "not changing"). This threshold is tunable.

### Failure Signatures
- **PoS Tagger Failure:** Complex negation scope (e.g., clause negation, double negatives) or sarcasm leads to wrong segment identification. Detect by manually inspecting segment splits on edge-case inputs.
- **Antonym Gap:** For highly toxic slang (e.g., "shit", "fuk"), thesaurus crawlers return no antonyms, causing M2 to leave toxic content unmodified. Monitor for "antonym not found" logs.
- **Paraphraser Distortion:** Parrot may generate nonsensical or meaning-altering paraphrases, especially with non-standard text. Spot-check generated sentences for semantic drift.
- **Backend Model Inconsistency:** If the base ML model gives highly variable scores for semantically equivalent paraphrases (high variance in M2’s sample), the average may not converge to a stable "true" score.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Neologism Analysis Integration:** How can a neologism analysis layer be integrated into the formal reasoning wrapper to improve toxicity detection for words with unknown roots? The paper notes in Future Work that experiments with neologisms indicate Perspective predicts high toxicity scores for such words, but no implementation exists.
2. **Efficiency vs. Accuracy Trade-off for M1.x vs. M2.x:** What is the computational efficiency vs. accuracy trade-off? Section 5.2 mentions M2.x is computationally costly, but a systematic investigation of the trade-off (runtime, memory, latency vs. accuracy) is lacking.
3. **Interpretability of Perspective's Word-Level Scoring:** Can we reverse-engineer or interpret how the Perspective API assigns toxicity scores to individual words and how these aggregate, especially under negation? The authors note the exact procedure is unknown, making heuristic weight approximations ad‑hoc.
4. **Handling Negation for Words Without Direct Antonyms:** How can the wrapper effectively handle negation for toxic words that lack direct antonyms (e.g., profanity like "shit")? The substitution approach (M2.x) fails for such words, and a targeted solution is missing.

## Limitations
- Testset generation details (word selection thresholds, profanity list) are not specified, hindering faithful reproduction.
- M1.5’s weight approximation (ζ) implementation is ambiguous.
- Dependency on PoS tagger leads to failures on complex negation (scope errors) and sarcasm.
- M2 approach is limited by absent antonyms for slang/typos and has exponential computational cost.
- Lack of transparency in Perspective API’s internal scoring impedes robust defense design.
- No cross-dataset or real‑world validation of the proposed wrappers.

## Confidence
- **Heuristic inversion (M1.x) effectiveness:** Medium (good reported numbers but scope parsing may break on difficult inputs).
- **Antonym substitution (M2.x) effectiveness:** Low (inconsistent results; fails without suitable antonyms).
- **Hybrid approach (vs. pure ML) effectiveness:** High (significantly outperforms baseline ML models on provided dataset).
