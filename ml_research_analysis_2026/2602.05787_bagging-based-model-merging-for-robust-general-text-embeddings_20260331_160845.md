# Bagging-Based Model Merging for Robust General Text Embeddings

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05787
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05787
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: BOOM improves out-of-domain robustness and enables efficient incremental updates while maintaining single-model inference efficiency.

## Executive Summary
General text embedding models often struggle with out-of-domain (OOD) generalization when trained on multi-task data, and updating them with new data typically requires costly full retraining. This paper presents Bagging-based rObust mOdel Merging (BOOM), a method that trains multiple models on randomly sampled subsets of the training corpus and merges them into a single model via parameter-space operations. BOOM enhances OOD robustness while retaining single-model inference efficiency. It also supports efficient incremental learning by merging a lightweight model trained on new data together with a small historical core subset into the existing model. Experiments on MTEB Eng v2, RTEB(beta), and MTEB(Code, v1) show BOOM consistently surpasses full-corpus batch-level shuffling, achieving higher in-domain and OOD scores. Moreover, a cost-equivalent variant ({50}-and-R) yields better OOD performance, and incremental updates drastically reduce training cost without performance loss.

## Method Summary
BOOM operates in two modes: static and incremental. In the static setting, the full training corpus is sampled M times to create bootstrapped subsets; each subset is used to train an independent embedding model using standard batch-level shuffling and contrastive loss (InfoNCE) with hard negatives. The individual models (e.g., fine-tuned Qwen3 with LoRA) are then merged into a single model using a parameter-space merging function such as Multi-SLERP, which interpolates their weights along geodesics to preserve directional alignment. This yields a single model that combines the robustness of an ensemble while avoiding multi-model inference overhead. For incremental learning, a small “core subset” is retained from the original training data. When new data arrives, a new model is trained on the union of the new data and the core subset. Merging this new model with the existing one integrates the new knowledge while the core subset prevents catastrophic forgetting, achieving efficient updates with far less computation than full retraining.

## Key Results
- BOOM consistently outperforms full-corpus batch-level shuffling on both in-domain (e.g., 69.56% on MTEB Eng v2 with Qwen3-4B) and out-of-domain benchmarks (RTEB beta, MTEB Code v1).
- A variant with equivalent training cost to shuffling ({50}-and-R) achieves better OOD performance.
- In incremental learning, BOOM reduces training cost substantially (by up to 60%) while matching or exceeding full retraining performance across multiple benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Training multiple models on bootstrapped data subsets and merging them improves out-of-domain (OOD) robustness compared to full-corpus batch-level shuffling (BLS).
- **Mechanism:** Bagging creates diverse "experts" by exposing each model to a different stochastic view of the training distribution. Parameter-space merging (e.g., Multi-SLERP) averages these diverse representations, which acts as a regularizer that smooths over idiosyncratic overfits to any single subset's noise or biases, leading to flatter minima and better generalization under distribution shift.
- **Core assumption:** The ensemble of subset-trained models captures complementary aspects of the underlying data manifold; their merged parameters represent a consensus that is less sensitive to any one subset's peculiarities.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "proposes Bagging-based rObust mOdel Merging (BOOM), which trains multiple embedding models on sampled subsets and merges them...improving robustness while retaining single-model inference efficiency."
  - [section 6.1]: Describes parallel training on subsets `D^(m)` with different sampling ratios `k`, followed by merging `Θ={W^1, ..., W^M}` via `θ_static = M(W^1, ..., W^M)`.
  - [section 5.3 & Figure 2]: Shows only ~2% of dataset pairs exhibit potential conflict (positive loss difference), implying predominant synergy—bagging leverages this by combining models trained on diverse synergistic subsets.
- **Break condition:** If the data subsets are not sufficiently diverse (e.g., sampling ratio too high or random seed fixed), the "experts" become too similar, and the variance-reduction benefit of bagging diminishes, yielding negligible OOD gain over BLS.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Model merging (vs. model ensembling) retains the robustness benefits of bagging without multi-model inference overhead.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of averaging predictions at inference time, merging operations like Multi-SLERP or Task Arithmetic directly combine model parameters in weight space. This creates a single model whose weights are the consensus of the ensemble, effectively "distilling" the ensemble's robustness into one set of parameters.
- **Core assumption:** The merging operation is a valid approximation of the ensemble's prediction function in the parameter space of the given architecture; conflicting updates can be resolved without catastrophic interference.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "merging them into a single model, improving robustness while retaining single-model inference efficiency."
  - [section 3]: Details merging functions (Multi-SLERP, Task Arithmetic, TIES, etc.) that operate on weight vectors `W_i` to produce `W_merged`.
  - [Table 4]: BOOM variants (e.g., `{20,40,60,80,100}`) achieve higher MTEB(Eng,v2) and OOD scores (RTEB, Code) than BLS while requiring single-model inference.
- **Break condition:** The merging method is ill-suited for the task (e.g., Model Stock performed poorly in Table 1), or the base models are too divergent, causing destructive interference that degrades in-domain performance below the BLS baseline.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** BOOM enables efficient incremental updates by merging a model trained on new data + a small historical core subset with the existing model, mitigating catastrophic forgetting with minimal retraining cost.
- **Mechanism:** The "core subset" `D_core` (sampled at ratio `k`) serves as an anchor of prior knowledge. Training a new model `W_new` on `D' ∪ D_core` ensures it retains performance on old tasks. Merging `W_0` (original) and `W_new` integrates new knowledge while the core subset's influence in `W_new` prevents the merge from overwriting critical old parameters.
- **Core assumption:** A small, representative coreset can sufficiently preserve the performance on the original distribution, and merging can reconcile the old and new parameter sets without significant conflict.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "enables efficient incremental updates by training a lightweight update model on new data with a small historical subset and merging them into the existing model."
  - [section 6.1]: Dynamic setting: `W_new ← Train(D' ∪ D_core)`, then `W_dynamic = M(W_0, W_new)`.
  - [Table 5]: With `k=40%`, BOOM (using only 40% of original data + new data) matches or exceeds full retraining (BLS on Gen-Full-Data) on MTEB(Eng,v2), RTEB(beta), and MTEB(Code,v1), proving efficient knowledge integration.
- **Break condition:** The core subset `D_core` is too small or unrepresentative, leading to catastrophic forgetting when `W_new` is trained; or the distribution shift between `D` and `D'` is so severe that even with a core subset, merging causes irreversible interference.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Data Sampler → Parallel Trainer → Merger (static)  
  Incremental Orchestrator → Data Sampler → Parallel Trainer → Merger (dynamic, with core subset)

- **Critical path:** Sampling → Training (parallelizable) → Merging (serial). The merging step is a hard dependency and must use a compatible method (Multi-SLERP recommended).

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Subset diversity vs. training cost:* More subsets (M) or lower k increases diversity and OOD gain but linearly increases training cost. The {50}-and-R variant offers a cost-equivalent alternative to BLS.
  - *Merging method vs. compatibility:* Not all merging methods work for embeddings (Model Stock fails). Multi-SLERP and Task Arithmetic are safer but may conflict if base models are too divergent.
  - *Core subset size vs. forgetting:* Larger k reduces forgetting in incremental learning but reduces efficiency gain. Paper uses k=40%.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - *In-domain performance drop >2% vs. BLS:* Indicates destructive merging interference. Check subset diversity and merging method.
  - *No OOD improvement on RTEB/Code:* Subsets may lack diversity, or merging misaligns robust features. Try increasing M or adjusting k schedule.
  - *Incremental update causes sharp drop on old benchmarks:* D_core is too small/unrepresentative. Increase k or ensure coverage of key task types.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replicate BLS baseline:** Train a single model on full Eng-Text-Data with batch-level shuffling using Qwen3-4B. Validate it achieves ~69.1% MTEB(Eng,v2) and ~60.6% RTEB(beta) (Table 4).
  2. **Test static BOOM ({50}-and-R):** Sample two disjoint 50% subsets, train two models, merge with Multi-SLERP. Expect ~68.9% MTEB(Eng,v2) and ~61.3% RTEB(beta) (Table 4). If OOD doesn’t improve, verify subset sampling independence.
  3. **Test incremental BOOM:** Start with a model trained on Eng-Text-Data. Sample a 40% core subset. Train a new model on General-Full-Data (new) + core subset. Merge with original. Expect MTEB(Eng,v2) ~69.36% and RTEB(beta) ~63.15% (Table 5). If old-task performance drops, increase core subset size to 50-60%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can model merging algorithms be specifically designed or adapted to improve performance on general text embedding tasks?**  
   The paper notes that existing merging methods (e.g., Model Stock, TIES) often underperform compared to simple data-level mixing, and states that developing new merging methods tailored for general-purpose text embedding remains an important direction for future research. The underlying reasons—such as how weight-space interference differs in contrastive embedding training—remain unclear. Systematic ablation studies analyzing weight divergence, gradient alignment, or task vector compatibility across layers for embedding models would help.

2. **Does the BOOM framework’s improvement in embedding quality translate to better performance in downstream applications like retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)?**  
   The paper evaluates only on embedding benchmarks (MTEB, RTEB). While these correlate with retrieval quality, RAG involves additional components (e.g., generation, context integration) where embedding robustness may have different effects. It is unknown whether BOOM’s OOD gains improve answer accuracy or reduce hallucinations in RAG. Integrating BOOM-trained embeddings into a full RAG pipeline and evaluating on question-answering datasets (e.g., ELI5, Natural Questions) would resolve this.

3. **Under what conditions does the assumption of widespread dataset synergy—central to BOOM’s design—break down?**  
   The paper infers synergy from pairwise loss analysis (Figure 2) showing only ~2% of dataset pairs with potential conflict, and from clustering experiments where fewer clusters (more mixing) improved results. However, this analysis is limited to the specific training corpora studied. Future domains (e.g., biomedical, legal, multilingual low-resource) may have more conflicting objectives. Expanding the pairwise interaction study to diverse, novel domain pairs (e.g., clinical text vs. social media) and training BOOM on deliberately conflicting synthetic datasets would test whether merging could amplify conflicts.

4. **What is the optimal strategy for constructing the "core subset" in incremental BOOM (e.g., size, sampling distribution)?**  
   The incremental setting uses a fixed 40% random sample of historical data as D_core, with no ablation on the impact of core subset size, representativeness, or stratification. A smaller subset reduces cost but may cause catastrophic forgetting; a larger one diminishes BOOM’s efficiency advantage. The optimal point likely depends on data distribution shift and task heterogeneity. Experiments varying core subset size (e.g., 10%, 20%, ..., 100%) and sampling strategies (random, stratified by dataset/task, coreset selection) in incremental learning scenarios would define the trade-offs.

## Limitations
- **Sampling protocol undefined:** Exact subset stratification (per-dataset ratios, replacement, disjointness) is unspecified, risking reproducibility gaps.
- **Merging hyperparameters opaque:** Weight schedules for `{20,40,60,80,100}` variants and convergence criteria for subset models are not detailed.
- **Scalability & cost analysis incomplete:** Training costs for M parallel models vs. single BLS are not quantified relative to the claimed efficiency gains.
