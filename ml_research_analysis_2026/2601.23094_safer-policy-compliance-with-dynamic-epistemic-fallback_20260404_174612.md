# Safer Policy Compliance with Dynamic Epistemic Fallback

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.23094
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.23094
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Appending one‑sentence DEF cues to prompts improves detection of maliciously perturbed policies by an average of +22.6 points and refusal rates by +18.9 points, with DeepSeek‑R1 achieving 100% detection on GDPR deontic norm attacks using the strongest cue.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles a critical vulnerability in large language models (LLMs) used for high‑stakes compliance: they can be tricked by subtly altered legal policy texts. The authors introduce Dynamic Epistemic Fallback (DEF), an inference‑time protocol that adds a short textual cue to the prompt, nudging the model to compare the provided policy against its internal, memorized knowledge of correct regulations (e.g., HIPAA, GDPR). If inconsistencies are found, the model flags them and defaults to its internal version. Evaluations across three frontier models show substantial gains in detecting and refusing compromised policies, with minimal false positives on correct policies. DEF offers a lightweight, no‑fine‑tuning method to enhance reliability in safety‑critical settings.

## Method Summary
DEF is a prompt‑augmentation technique tested on a policy compliance task. For each test case, a model receives a scenario and a legal policy section (either correct or maliciously perturbed via Authorization or Deontic Norm weakening). Three cue strengths are appended to the instruction: General Consistency, Norm Alignment, and Memory Prioritization. The model generates a chain‑of‑thought (CoT) response; a separate monitor (GPT‑5.2) scans the reasoning trace to extract “detection” and “refusal” signals. Detection rate measures how often the model’s reasoning flags the policy as inconsistent, while refusal rate captures instances where the model ignores the provided policy and relies on internal knowledge. Experiments run on DeepSeek‑R1, Qwen3‑30B‑Think, and GPT‑5‑Mini using datasets GDPRHUB and GOLDCOIN reveal DEF’s effectiveness and robustness.

## Key Results
- DEF improves detection on perturbed policies by an average of +22.6 points and refusal by +18.9 points across both HIPAA and GDPR.
- For GDPRspecific deontic norm attacks, the strongest DEF cue yields up to +80% detection improvement and a 629% increase in refusal.
- DeepSeek‑R1 reaches 100% detection on GDPR deontic norm attacks with Memory Prioritization (DEF3).
- Minimal false positives are introduced on correct policies (0.4–2.5 point increase), with no significant accuracy drop.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** DEF cues nudge LLMs to detect inconsistencies between the provided policy text and their memorized correct version, activating epistemic vigilance.
- **Mechanism:** By appending one‑sentence prompts (e.g., "Make sure to get a sense of whether the policy fits together in a consistent and logical way"), DEF primes the model to compare the input policy against its parametric knowledge. This comparison is formalized as a latent function ϕ(Pθ, ˜P) that can yield a CONFLICT outcome when discrepancies exist, increasing Pr(E=1|˜P≠Pθ).
- **Core assumption:** Frontier LLMs have memorized the correct versions of globally recognized policies like GDPR and HIPAA during pre‑training, and these memories can be accessed via appropriate prompting.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: "DEF nudges LLMs to flag inconsistencies, refuse compliance, and fallback to their parametric knowledge upon encountering perturbed policy texts."  
  - Section 2.3: "DEF is a collection of specifically curated textual cues designed to nudge LLMs in dynamically activating epistemic vigilance and strengthening its fallback mechanism."  
  - Section 4.2: "DEF provides substantial gains of +22.6 points for detection and +18.9 points for refusal for GDPR."
- **Break condition:** If the model's parametric knowledge is incomplete or incorrect, or if the perturbation is semantically too subtle to create a detectable conflict, vigilance may not activate.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Stronger DEF cues (e.g., Memory Prioritization) explicitly direct the model to suppress contextual override (OVERRIDE) and prioritize fallback to memorized knowledge.
- **Mechanism:** The cue wording ("Notice when the policy wording... doesn't match... and rely on what you remember") semantically biases the model toward distrusting the prompt policy when anomalies are sensed, promoting REJECT actions instead of UPDATE.
- **Core assumption:** Models can interpret meta‑cognitive instructions to "rely on memory" and adjust their reliance on contextual vs. parametric information accordingly.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 2.2: Models exhibit contextual effects (OVERRIDE/WEAKEN/CONFLICT); DEF aims to increase CONFLICT detection.  
  - Section 2.3: "Memory Prioritization... verbosely nudges the LLM to rely on its parametric knowledge as a consistency check."  
  - Table 1: Memory Prioritization (DEF3) yields highest detection/refusal rates across models and policies.
- **Break condition:** If the model's reasoning process is opaque (e.g., CoT summarization in GPT‑5‑MINI), the cue's effect may be diminished or inconsistent.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** DEF enables efficient, selective activation of vigilance—triggering primarily on perturbed policies while imposing minimal false positives on correct ones.
- **Mechanism:** Cues are designed to be subtle and non‑obvious, only prompting consistency checks when genuine conflicts exist between prompt and memory; on correct policies, no conflict arises, so vigilance remains latent.
- **Core assumption:** Correct policies align perfectly with the model's memorized version, so the comparison function ϕ yields no CONFLICT.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 2.2: "Epistemic vigilance should ideally not be triggered if the given policy text is correct."  
  - Section 4.3: "DEF induces a small false positive increase of 0.4-2.5 points" with Cohen's d showing no significant performance difference (d<0.5).  
  - Tables 7,8: Overlapping confidence intervals for accuracy, detection, and refusal between correct policies with and without DEF.
- **Break condition:** If a correct policy has minor wording variations from the memorized version, cues might spuriously activate vigilance, causing unnecessary fallback or refusal.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** DEF protocol (prompt augmentation) -> SUT (reasoning LLM: DeepSeek‑R1, Qwen3‑30B‑Think, GPT‑5‑Mini) -> Monitor (GPT‑5.2 for snippet extraction) -> Evaluation datasets (GDPRHUB, GOLDCOIN) -> Metrics (detection rate, refusal rate, accuracy).
- **Critical path:** Input case + policy text (+ DEF cue) → SUT generates full CoT response → Monitor parses reasoning for detection/refusal snippets → Metrics computed → Analysis of vigilance themes.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Cue subtlety vs. strength (stronger cues improve detection but may increase false positives); inference‑time only (no fine‑tuning) vs. potential training integration; policy‑specific vs. generalizable cues.
- **Failure signatures:** (1) Low detection/refusal on perturbed policies despite DEF, (2) High false positives on correct policies, (3) Model ignores cues and follows perturbed policy (Perturbed Policy Obedience theme), (4) Monitor misclassification due to CoT summarization.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline vs. DEF:** Run policy compliance with no DEF and each DEF cue level on perturbed HIPAA/GDPR; measure detection and refusal rates.  
  2. **False positive check:** Run same on correct (unperturbed) policies; ensure no significant drop in accuracy or spike in detection/refusal.  
  3. **Reasoning analysis:** Use monitor to categorize snippets into themes (Integrity Suspicion, Knowledge Override, etc.); compare distributions across DEF levels to verify shift toward proper override.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to other legal domains:** Does DEF generalize beyond data privacy (e.g., criminal law, medical ethics)? The study only evaluates HIPAA and GDPR.
2. **Incomplete/outdated parametric knowledge:** How does DEF behave when the LLM’s memorized policy version is incomplete, outdated, or contested? This threatens the core assumption of a singular correct memory.
3. **Optimizing cue design:** Can the design and strength of DEF cues be automated or systematically improved for different LLMs and policy structures? Current cues are manually curated.
4. **Monitor reliability:** How transferable is the GPT‑5.2 monitor to diverse LLMs, especially those with hidden or summarized CoT? Current evaluation is limited to a few models.

## Limitations
- Exact dataset instances, filtering logic, and perturbed policy texts are not provided, hindering faithful replication.
- Scope limited to two policies (HIPAA, GDPR) and three large models; generalizability to smaller or differently trained models is unknown.
- Hand‑crafted cues may be fragile to wording variations; no robustness tests across formulations are reported.

## Confidence
- DEF boosts detection/refusal on perturbed policies: **High**
- DEF3 (Memory Prioritization) is strongest: **High**
- False positives are minimal: **Medium**
- Inconsistency‑detection mechanism (reasoning themes): **Medium**
