# Scalable Simulation-Based Model Inference with Test-Time Complexity Control

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.15292
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15292
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: PRISM amortizes joint inference over discrete model structures and continuous parameters with a tunable test-time complexity prior λ, enabling scalable model selection in combinatorial spaces (e.g., 10¹⁰⁰ models) with high accuracy and calibrated uncertainty.

## Executive Summary
PRISM addresses the bottleneck of selecting among vast model families (e.g., billions) where classical Bayesian methods fail and amortized approaches fix complexity priors. It is a transformer-based framework that amortizes joint inference over discrete model structures and continuous parameters, with a tunable test-time complexity prior λ. Evaluations on symbolic regression and diffusion MRI demonstrate scalability to combinatorial model spaces, improved accuracy over prior SBI methods, and well-calibrated uncertainty estimates.

## Method Summary
PRISM uses a transformer architecture to amortize the inference of both discrete model structures and continuous parameters from observed data. Instead of fitting each candidate model separately, it learns a mapping from observations to posterior distributions over structures and parameters in a single forward pass. The test-time complexity prior λ controls the trade-off between model simplicity and fit, allowing users to tune complexity preferences without retraining.

## Key Results
- Achieves >90% top-5 accuracy and near-noise-floor rRMSE on symbolic regression with combinatorial spaces up to 10¹⁰⁰ models.
- Improves diffusion MRI tractography correlation from 0.86 (prior SBI) to 0.96 and maintains low SBC calibration error (~0.005) and high effective sample size (~60%) on synthetic data.
- Enables model selection across heterogeneous biophysical models in both synthetic and in vivo neuroimaging.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
PRISM amortizes the expensive per-model simulation and inference by learning a joint distribution over structures and parameters conditioned on observations. The transformer’s attention mechanism effectively captures dependencies across the combinatorial structure space. The tunable λ acts as a complexity penalty at inference time, approximating Bayesian model selection without requiring marginal likelihood computations for each model.

## Foundational Learning
- **Amortized Inference**: Why needed: Avoids repeated simulation-based inference for each new observation, crucial for scalability. Quick check: Does training on a fixed set of simulations generalize to unseen observations?
- **Transformer Architectures**: Why needed: Handles variable-length discrete structures and long-range dependencies in model space. Quick check: Can attention weights reveal which parts of the structure influence predictions?
- **Bayesian Model Selection**: Why needed: Provides principled trade-off between model fit and complexity. Quick check: How does λ correspond to a Bayesian prior on model complexity?
- **Simulation-Based Inference (SBI)**: Why needed: Allows inference when likelihood is intractable but simulations are feasible. Quick check: Are posterior samples consistent with known identifiability limits?
- **Complexity Regularization**: Why needed: Prevents overfitting to noise in high-capacity model families. Quick check: Does increasing λ consistently select simpler models?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input observation -> Transformer encoder -> Latent representation -> Decoder heads (structure logits, parameter distributions). Critical path: Data embedding through transformer layers to output layers that predict discrete structure categories and continuous parameters. Design tradeoffs: Fixed transformer capacity limits representational power for extremely complex structures; λ tuning requires validation data and may not align with true Bayesian posteriors. Failure signatures: Poor calibration (SBC far from 0), low ESS, mode collapse in structure predictions, sensitivity to input perturbations. 
First experiments: 1) Vary λ on a held-out synthetic dataset and plot selected model complexity vs. λ; 2) Compare PRISM’s posterior samples to nested sampling on a small model space (e.g., 100 models) using SBC; 3) Ablate transformer depth and measure top-k structure accuracy on symbolic regression benchmark.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list open questions beyond those raised in the limitations and next checks.

## Limitations
- Evaluations are limited to two domains (symbolic regression, diffusion MRI); generalization to other scientific areas is uncertain.
- No reported computational costs (training time, memory, inference latency) as model family size scales.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (λ, transformer architecture) and robustness are not explored.

## Confidence
- Scalability to combinatorial model spaces (10¹⁰⁰): High
- Symbolic regression metrics (>90% top-5, near-noise-floor rRMSE): Medium
- dMRI tractography improvement (0.96 vs 0.86): Medium-Low
- Calibration (SBC ~0.005, ESS ~60% on synthetic): High
- Heterogeneous model selection across domains: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Cross-domain generalization: Apply PRISM to at least two new scientific simulator families (e.g., Hodgkin-Huxley neuron models, Lotka-Volterra ecology) with known ground-truth structures; measure top-k structure recovery and parameter rRMSE.
2. Computational scaling benchmark: Compare PRISM’s training time, memory usage, and test-time inference latency (per observation) against nested sampling (e.g., dynesty) and other amortized SBI methods (e.g., SNPE-C) as model family size increases (e.g., 10², 10⁴, 10⁶ candidate models).
3. λ sensitivity and calibration robustness: Systematically vary λ over a wide range (e.g., 10⁻³ to 10³) on synthetic benchmarks; assess stability of selected model complexity (number of parameters) and calibration metrics (SBC, ESS) to confirm claims of “tunable test-time complexity prior.”
