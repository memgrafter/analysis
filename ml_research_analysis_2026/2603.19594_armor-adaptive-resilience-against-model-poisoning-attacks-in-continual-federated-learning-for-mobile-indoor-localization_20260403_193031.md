# ARMOR: Adaptive Resilience Against Model Poisoning Attacks in Continual Federated Learning for Mobile Indoor Localization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19594
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19594
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Reduces mean localization error by up to 8.0× and worst-case error by 4.97× compared to state-of-the-art federated learning and poisoning defense frameworks.

## Executive Summary
ARMOR addresses model corruption in continual federated learning (CFL) for indoor localization, which arises from device heterogeneity, temporal dynamics, and deliberate poisoning attacks. It introduces a state-space model (SSM) that learns historical global model weight evolution to predict the next expected state. Incoming local updates are compared against this projection via cosine similarity; deviant tensors are adaptively scaled before aggregation. Evaluated on the real-world CSUIndoorLoc dataset across multiple buildings and months, ARMOR demonstrates robust resilience under sustained adversarial conditions, significantly reducing localization errors.

## Method Summary
ARMOR operates in offline and online phases. Offline, it trains a global model (GM) on initial data and then continues training on a separate split to record a sequence of GM weight snapshots. A bidirectional GRU-based SSM is trained on this sequence to predict the next GM weight state. Online, for each federated round, the server uses the SSM to predict the expected next GM state from historical weights before receiving local updates. Each client's weight update is compared to this prediction using cosine similarity per tensor. If any tensor shows negative similarity (deviation), the update is mitigated by subtracting the mean absolute deviation across all tensors from each tensor. The sanitized updates are aggregated via FedAvg to update the GM, and the SSM history is extended with the new GM weights.

## Key Results
- Mean localization error reduced by up to 8.0× compared to state-of-the-art baselines.
- Worst-case error reduced by 4.97×.
- Demonstrated robust resilience under sustained adversarial conditions across multiple buildings and months.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** ARMOR uses a state-space model (SSM) to learn the historical evolution of global model weights, establishing a baseline for expected change. The bidirectional GRU-based SSM is trained on past weight tensors to predict the next state, assuming a consistent temporal pattern in benign CFL. Evidence from the abstract and section 5.1 confirms this, though neighbor papers do not employ a learned temporal model. A break condition occurs if abrupt legitimate environmental shifts cause the SSM to flag benign updates as deviant.

**Mechanism 2:** Incoming local updates are compared to the SSM's prediction via cosine similarity. Negative similarity flags tensors as deviant, based on the assumption that corrupted updates will have a different directional alignment in weight space. Section 5.2 details this. An adaptive attack that mimics historical direction but alters critical features could evade detection.

**Mechanism 3:** Deviant updates are mitigated by uniformly scaling all tensors toward the SSM's projection. The scaling coefficient φ is the mean absolute deviation of tensors from the projection. This aims to neutralize corruption while preserving some signal. Section 5.3 and Figure 6 support this. If a poisoning attack is sparse but with large deviations, φ might be dominated by few tensors, causing over- or under-scaling.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Global Model (GM) -> State-Space Model (SSM) -> Corruption Monitoring & Detection -> Mitigation -> Aggregation -> Updated GM; SSM updated with new GM.

Critical path (online round):
1. Server has current GM weights WT and trained SSM_T.
2. SSM_T predicts next state PT+1 from history {W0...WT}.
3. Receive local update ∆W from client.
4. Compute cosine similarity between each tensor in ∆W and corresponding tensor in PT+1.
5. If any negative similarity: compute deviation Di = ∆W_i - PT+1,i for each tensor i; compute φ = mean(|Di|); set ∆W'_i = ∆W_i - φ. Else, ∆W' = ∆W.
6. Queue ∆W' for aggregation.
7. After all clients, update GM: WT+1 = WT + average(∆W').
8. Append WT+1 to history and update SSM to SSM_T+1.

Design tradeoffs:
- SSM complexity vs. overhead: Larger SSM improves trajectory modeling but adds computational cost.
- Detection threshold: Fixed cosine threshold (≥0) is simple but may lack sensitivity across environments.
- Mitigation aggressiveness: Uniform scaling is simple but may over/under-correct; per-tensor scaling could be better but harder to calibrate.

Failure signatures:
- Monitoring failure: High SSM prediction error, causing constant mitigation and degraded performance.
- Detection failure: High localization error with low Frobenius norm of updates, indicating corrupted updates passing detection.
- Mitigation failure: GM error doesn't recover after legitimate updates post-poisoning, indicating improper sanitization.

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate Figure 6: Implement ARMOR and No-ARMOR; run MOTO Gaussian attack (ATT=100%); plot Frobenius norm of updates per round. Expect ARMOR to show smaller, transient spikes.
2. Validate SSM prediction: Train SSM on offline history; on held-out legitimate GM updates, compute MSE between predicted and actual next weights. Expect low error.
3. Test attack resilience (Figure 8): For Building 1, run CI sequence with MOTO MPAF attack (ATT=100%); plot mean localization error per CI for ARMOR vs. No-ARMOR. Expect ARMOR's error to stay below ~4m while No-ARMOR's grows substantially.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Extending ARMOR to data poisoning attacks: Current design targets model weight corruption; data poisoning might produce benign-looking updates. Evidence needed: evaluation under data poisoning and modifications to SSM or detection.
2. Sensitivity to fixed cosine threshold: Fixed threshold (0) may not generalize. Evidence needed: varying threshold across buildings/attacks, measuring error and detection metrics; adaptive threshold development.
3. Optimality of uniform scaling: Uniform φ may not preserve all tensors' utility. Evidence needed: compare with per-tensor or learned scaling under attacks, measuring accuracy and detection.
4. SSM architecture choice: Bidirectional GRU may have look-ahead bias; alternatives like LSTM or Transformer could be better. Evidence needed: benchmark prediction MSE and downstream impact, assess real-time feasibility.
5. Generalizability to new environments: Evaluated only on CSUIndoorLoc; may not transfer. Evidence needed: testing on other datasets (UJIIndoorLoc, TUT), cross-building evaluation, longer durations.

## Limitations
- Unspecified "retraining class" granularity for SSM history affects sequence construction.
- Undefined tensor partitioning for mitigation impacts scaling logic.
- Lack of evidence for SSM trajectory learning superiority and cosine similarity detection over alternatives; generalizability beyond CSUIndoorLoc unknown.

## Confidence
- Mechanism validity (SSM trajectory modeling): Medium – plausible but untested under abrupt legitimate shifts.
- Empirical robustness claims: Medium-High – strong on one dataset but lacks independent replication and diversity testing.
- Scalability & overhead: Low – SSM size and per-tensor ops may not scale to larger models; no analysis provided.

## Next Checks
1. SSM prediction fidelity: Compute MSE between SSM-predicted and actual next GM weights on held-out legitimate sequence. Expect error <1e-4 normalized.
2. Benign update distortion: Run ARMOR with ATT=0% and compare mean localization error against no-mitigation baseline. Significant increase (p<0.05) indicates over-aggressive scaling.
3. Adaptive attack evasion: Craft poisoning where ∆W aligns positively with SSM's PT+1 (mimic direction but flip critical signs). Measure detection rate; >30% evasion would break cosine similarity assumption.
