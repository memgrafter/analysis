# CLM-Bench: Benchmarking and Analyzing Cross-lingual Misalignment of LLMs in Knowledge Editing

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.17397
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17397
- **Reference count**: 18
- **Primary result**: Cross‑lingual knowledge editing fails to transfer; e.g., editing Chinese facts in Llama3-8B drops English reliability from 61.71% to 19.55% with MEMIT (batch size 1000) because Chinese and English edit vectors are nearly orthogonal.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces CLM-Bench, a culture-aware benchmark of 1,010 Chinese-native factual statements aligned with English translations, designed to evaluate cross‑lingual knowledge editing (KE). Experiments on Llama3, Qwen2, and Mistral models using MEMIT, AlphaEdit, and PMET reveal a striking cross‑lingual misalignment: applying edits in one language does not propagate to the other. Geometric analysis of the MLP edit vectors shows that Chinese and English directions are nearly orthogonal (cosine similarity 0.027–0.042, subspace angle >60°) across batch sizes, indicating they occupy disjoint subspaces. Conversely, when both languages are edited simultaneously, the mixed edit vector closely approximates the sum of the monolingual vectors (cosine similarity >0.976, relative error <0.23), demonstrating linear additivity. The findings suggest current KE methods treat multilingual knowledge as independent and explain the failure of cross‑lingual transfer.

## Method Summary
The study uses the CLM-Bench dataset (1,010 Chinese-native CounterFact pairs with English alignments) and the EasyEdit framework to apply three KE methods (MEMIT, AlphaEdit, PMET) to decoder‑only models (Llama3-8B, Qwen2-7B, Mistral-7B, Llama2-7B-Chinese). Editing is performed in monolingual Chinese, monolingual English, and mixed‑lingual settings across batch sizes (1, 10, 100, 1000). Edited models are evaluated on test queries (original, rephrased, locality controls) to compute language‑specific scores (`zh_score`, `en_score`) aggregating reliability, generality, and locality. For geometric analysis, the parameter updates (`δ`) to the `mlp_down_proj` layer (typically layer 12) are extracted. Cosine similarity, subspace angles (via top‑10 SVD vectors), neuron overlap (Jaccard of top‑5% absolute weight changes), and relative error between mixed and summed monolingual deltas are computed. The same pipeline is run for different target layers and model families to test generality.

## Key Results
- Cross‑lingual misalignment is severe: Chinese editing yields high `zh_score` (e.g., 61.71% with MEMIT batch 1000 on Llama3-8B) but collapses English reliability to 19.55%; the reverse pattern holds for English editing.
- Edit vectors for the same fact in Chinese and English are nearly orthogonal (cosine similarity 0.027–0.042, subspace angle >60°) and share few neurons (Jaccard overlap <0.32) across batch sizes and models.
- Mixed‑lingual editing exhibits strong linear additivity: the mixed delta approximates the sum of monolingual deltas (cosine >0.976, relative error <0.23), with the combined subspace angle shrinking to ~12°.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Edits applied in one language (e.g., Chinese) fail to propagate to another language (e.g., English) because the corresponding parameter update vectors occupy nearly orthogonal subspaces.
- **Mechanism**: Locate‑and‑edit methods (e.g., MEMIT) compute a rank‑one update (edit vector) targeted to specific MLP layers to modify a fact. For the same fact expressed in different languages, the resulting edit vectors point in directions that are geometrically independent (high subspace angle, low cosine similarity). An update optimized for Chinese data minimally affects the latent representation pathway for English, and vice versa.
- **Core assumption**: The model’s internal representations for linguistically distinct factual triggers are segregated into disjoint subspaces within edited layers.
- **Evidence**: Near‑zero cosine similarity (0.027–0.042) and >60° subspace angles persist across batch sizes (Table 2, Sec 6.2); neuron overlap stays below 0.32.
- **Break condition**: Discovery of a KE method where monolingual edit vectors show significant alignment (cosine >0.3, angle <30°) and exhibit cross‑lingual transfer would invalidate this mechanism for that method/model.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: When Chinese and English edits are performed simultaneously (mixed‑lingual editing), the combined edit vector approximates the linear sum of the individual monolingual vectors.
- **Mechanism**: The disjoint subspace property means the two independent edit directions do not interfere. The optimization process for mixed editing appears to apply both updates additively in parameter space, resulting in a final delta that is the vector sum of the separate Chinese and English deltas.
- **Core assumption**: The editing algorithm’s objective function and the model’s parameter geometry allow additive composition of updates targeting linguistically separate facts.
- **Evidence**: Mixed delta vs. sum of monolingual deltas: cosine similarity >0.976, relative error <0.23 (Sec 6.2); subspace angles between the two directions drop from ~63° to ~12° after mixing.
- **Break condition**: Significant non‑linear interaction between monolingual edits in mixed settings (relative error >0.5, cosine <0.7) would challenge the pure linearity claim.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The observed misalignment is a fundamental property of how knowledge is stored and modified in transformer MLPs for multilingual facts, not an artifact of specific layers, batch sizes, or models.
- **Mechanism**: The orthogonal/linear pattern persists across different batch sizes (1 to 1000), different models (Llama3, Qwen2, Mistral), and different target layers (verified for layers 9‑12). This suggests the effect is rooted in the training data distribution and model architecture’s handling of multilingual representations.
- **Core assumption**: The geometric relationship between language‑specific fact representations is stable and generalizes across common editing configurations.
- **Evidence**: Cross‑lingual misalignment holds for all tested models and batch sizes (Sec 5.1, Table 2); editing different MLP layers does not substantially alter the pattern (Table 3).
- **Break condition**: If a specific model family, layer, or small batch size consistently breaks the pattern (shows high cross‑lingual transfer), the claim of fundamentality would need restriction.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: CLM-Bench → Editing Algorithm (MEMIT/AlphaEdit/PMET) → Target Model → Edit Vector Extraction → Geometric Analysis
- **Critical path**: Valid geometric interpretation requires: (a) a culture‑native benchmark to avoid translation noise, (b) extraction of clean layer‑specific deltas for identical facts in both languages, and (c) consistent analysis across batch sizes and models.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *Benchmark design*: Native generation vs. translation (cost/coverage tradeoff).  
  - *Analysis layer choice*: Deeper layers show stronger separation but analysis is computationally heavier.  
  - *Metric choice*: Subspace angle (k=10 SVD vectors) captures directional alignment but depends on the chosen `k`.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  1. Low‑quality translations in the benchmark mask true misalignment.  
  2. Inconsistent layer selection (e.g., analyzing early layers) yields weak separation signals.  
  3. Using only single‑edit (batch size = 1) settings may miss scaling effects.
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. Reproduce orthogonality: For Llama3‑8B, run MEMIT with batch size = 100 on CLM‑Bench. Extract layer‑12 `mlp_down_proj` deltas for 100 Chinese and 100 English edits. Compute mean cosine similarity – expect ~0.03–0.04.  
  2. Test linear additivity: For same setup, compute mixed‑lingual edit delta and sum of separate Chinese + English deltas for matched facts. Compute relative error – expect <0.25.  
  3. Layer ablation: Repeat experiment 1 for layers 9, 10, 11, 12. Plot cosine similarity vs. layer depth – expect monotonic decrease (increasing orthogonality).

## Limitations
- English alignments may introduce translation noise despite careful curation.
- Scope restricted to three decoder‑only models and MLP‑only edits; results may differ for other architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder) or editing layers.
- Orthogonality analysis could be confounded by nonlinearities in the editing objective or representation.
