# Affect and Effect: Limitations of regularisation-based continual learning in EEG-based emotion classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.07858  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07858  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Regularisation-based continual learning (EWC, SI, MAS) fails to provide statistically significant forward transfer for subject‑incremental EEG emotion classification, while imposing a costly stability‑plasticity trade‑off and exhibiting high sensitivity to subject order.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates regularisation‑based continual learning (EWC, SI, MAS) for subject‑incremental EEG‑based emotion classification on DREAMER and SEED. The methods improve backward transfer (mitigating catastrophic forgetting) but reduce plasticity (current subject performance) and show no statistically significant forward transfer gain over sequential fine‑tuning. The core limitation is a misalignment: the stability focus of regularisation conflicts with the need for adaptation to highly variable inter‑subject EEG distributions. Importance heuristics become unreliable under EEG noise and covariate shift, gradients between subjects interfere, and accumulated importance over‑constrains the network, making performance order‑dependent and poorly scalable.

## Method Summary
The work frames emotion classification as a subject‑incremental continual learning task. Each subject is a sequential “task” using the shared EEGNet‑8,2 model. Baselines include naïve sequential fine‑tuning and three regularisation methods: EWC (online Fisher), SI (path integral), and MAS (mean absolute output gradients). After training on each subject, the model is evaluated on all previous subjects to compute Average Accuracy (ACC), Backward Transfer (BWT), and Forward Transfer (FWT). A held‑out unseen subject test gauges generalisation. Hyperparameters (e.g., λ regularisation strength) are tuned per dataset. Preprocessing includes ICA‑based artefact rejection (kurtosis z‑score > 4.0), filtering (0.5–45 Hz), and 3‑second epoch segmentation.

## Key Results
- **No forward transfer:** FWT shows no statistically significant improvement over naïve fine‑tuning (p > 0.05 across methods and datasets).
- **Stability‑plasticity trade‑off:** Higher λ improves BWT (e.g., DREAMER EWC BWT: −0.244 vs naïve −0.519, p < 0.05) but reduces current subject training accuracy, with negligible net ACC gain.
- **Order sensitivity & scaling failure:** Accumulated importance over‑constrains parameters, causing severe performance variance across subject orders and near‑freezing of updates in later subjects.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Regularisation‑based CL importance heuristics (EWC’s Fisher, SI’s path integral, MAS’s output‑gradient) become unreliable estimators of parameter importance under EEG‑specific noise (intra‑subject variability, artefacts) and covariate shift.
- **Mechanism:** The estimators are mathematically sensitive to gradient variance and signal noise. For EWC, the empirical Fisher e𝐹 decomposes into `Var(𝑔𝑘) + E[𝑔𝑘]^2`; under low‑SNR EEG data and small sample sizes, `Var(𝑔𝑘)` dominates, inflating importance for noise‑sensitive parameters rather than true curvature. SI’s path integral accumulates squared gradient updates (`∑𝑔²`), directly amplifying stochastic and signal noise. MAS uses absolute output‑gradients, which is more robust to stochastic noise but remains sensitive to input/artefact noise that causes large output swings for small parameter changes.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “the heuristics for estimating parameter importance become less reliable under noisy data and covariate shift.” Section 3.1 derives the e𝐹 decomposition and noise terms. Section 7.5 shows negative gradient cosine similarity even for top‑ranked parameters.
- **Break condition:** If inter‑subject variability is so high that optimal parameters are contradictory, even a perfect importance estimate would force a detrimental compromise. Here, heuristic *unreliability* compounds the problem.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Subsequent subjects require conflicting parameter updates. When parameters deemed important by the heuristic are constrained, gradients for the new subject interfere with retained knowledge, moving optimisation away from any shared optimum and limiting forward transfer/generalisation.
- **Mechanism:** The regularisation penalty `∑ Ω𝑘(𝜃𝑘 − 𝜃∗prev)²` modifies the loss gradient to `∇Lnew + λΩ(𝜃 − 𝜃∗prev)`. If the optimal direction for the new subject (`∇Lnew`) on an important parameter (`Ω𝑘` high) has a negative cosine similarity with the direction from the previous optimum, the penalty actively works *against* the required update. This prevents convergence to a parameter set that works well for both, instead trapping the model in a poor compromise.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “gradients between subjects interfere.” Section 3.2 formalises the constrained optimum. Section 7.5 measures negative cosine similarity between successive subjects’ gradients for top‑importance parameters.
- **Break condition:** High gradient interference (`cos(∇L_A, ∇L_B) << 0`) for parameters with high `Ω` guarantees poor forward transfer regardless of λ.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Accumulated importance (`Ω𝑘`) across a sequence of subjects monotonically increases and eventually over‑constrains the network, freezing parameters critical for future learning. This makes performance highly sensitive to subject order and limits scalability to long streams.
- **Mechanism:** In EWC (Online), SI, and MAS, `Ω𝑘` is a non‑decreasing sum or exponential moving average `Ω∗_t = γΩ∗_{t-1} + Ω_t`. As tasks accumulate, `Ω𝑘` for many parameters grows large. The penalty term `λΩ𝑘(Δ𝜃𝑘)²` then forces `Δ𝜃𝑘 → 0` to avoid a large loss increase, effectively freezing those parameters. Early subjects disproportionately influence `Ω` because their importance is never diluted, making later subjects’ learning negligible and final performance depend heavily on which subjects appeared early.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “importance values accumulated across tasks over‑constrain the network” and “performance is sensitive to subject order.” Section 3.3 discusses monotonic accumulation. Section 7.6 plots `Ω` growing and `Δ𝜃` plummeting; Pearson correlation `r(Ω_prev, Δ𝜃_next) < 0`. Section 7.7 (Tables 2,3) shows large variance in unseen‑subject F1‑score across shuffles.
- **Break condition:** When `λΩ𝑘` becomes large enough that the optimal update `Δ𝜃𝑘^* = - (∇L𝑘) / (λΩₖ)` is smaller than the noise scale, plasticity is lost.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Catastrophic Forgetting  
  **Why needed:** The paper’s core premise—degradation on past subjects when fine‑tuning sequentially—is what regularisation‑based CL aims to mitigate.  
  **Quick check:** What metric directly measures catastrophic forgetting mitigation (BWT), and why does an improvement there not guarantee better overall utility?

- **Concept:** Stability‑Plasticity Trade‑off  
  **Why needed:** The fundamental dilemma: stability (retaining past knowledge, low BWT) vs. plasticity (learning new knowledge, high task accuracy/FWT). The paper argues regularisation methods are misaligned because they prioritise stability at the expense of necessary plasticity for EEG subject‑incremental learning.  
  **Quick check:** According to results, what happens to *current subject* training accuracy/F1‑score as BWT improves with higher λ? What does this indicate about plasticity?

- **Concept:** Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) & Empirical Approximation  
  **Why needed:** Central to EWC’s importance heuristic. The paper’s theoretical critique hinges on the mismatch between the empirical FIM (used), the true FIM, and the Hessian (curvature) under noisy, non‑i.i.d. EEG data.  
  **Quick check:** What two terms is the empirical FIM (`e𝐹`) decomposed into (Eq. 5), and which term is argued to dominate under high gradient noise, corrupting the importance signal?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Stream -> Core Model (EEGNet‑8,2) -> Importance Estimator (EWC/SI/MAS) -> Loss Function (eLτ) -> Optimiser (Adam)

- **Critical path:**  
  1. **Subject 1:** Train normally, store final parameters `θ∗₁`.  
  2. **Subject τ>1:** a) Estimate/update importance `Ω` for current subject τ using stored `θ∗_{τ-1}`. b) Train with loss `eLτ = Lτ + (λ/2)∑Ω𝑘(θ𝑘−θ∗_{prev,𝑘})²`. c) Store new `θ∗τ`.  
  3. **Evaluation:** After each subject, test on all subjects 1..τ to compute ACC matrix and metrics. After all subjects, test on a held‑out unseen subject for FWT/generalisation.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Memory vs. Computation: All methods avoid storing raw data (replay), trading memory for constrained optimisation. EWC/SI store a single importance vector per parameter (~model size). MAS is compute‑intensive (full forward per parameter per sample).  
  - Stability‑Plasticity: Tuning λ is the primary knob. High λ → high BWT, low current subject accuracy. Low λ → near naïve fine‑tuning. Paper finds no λ yields meaningful ACC gain + good FWT.  
  - Noise Sensitivity: EWC & SI are highly sensitive to batch/EEG noise (quadratic in gradients). MAS is more robust to stochastic noise but sensitive to input artefacts. Pre‑processing quality is therefore critical.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. **FWT ≤ Naive (p>0.05):** No statistically significant forward transfer benefit (primary conclusion).  
  2. **High ACC variance across seeds/orders:** Unseen subject performance varies wildly with subject order.  
  3. **Training accuracy drop with higher λ:** As λ increases to improve BWT, current subject training F1/accuracy drops significantly.  
  4. **Parameter freezing:** Sharply decreasing L2 norm of parameter updates (`Δ𝜃`) across the task sequence.  
  5. **Negative gradient cosine similarity:** High negative correlation between gradients of successive subjects for parameters with high `Ω`.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **λ Sensitivity Tune & Metric Balance:** Train EWC/SI/MAS across a wide λ range (e.g., `[0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10]`). Plot ACC, BWT, FWT vs. λ. Expect: BWT↑, Current ACC↓, FWT~Naive. Confirms stability‑plasticity misalignment and lack of FWT gain.  
  2. **Gradient Interference Analysis:** For adjacent subjects A→B, compute cosine similarity of gradients `∇L_A` and `∇L_B` only for the top X% of parameters ranked by `Ω` from subject A (X=5%, 20%, 100%). Expect: negative average similarity, especially in top `Ω` parameters. Shows why penalising those parameters hurts subject B learning.  
  3. **Importance Accumulation & Plasticity Loss:** Plot mean `Ω` per layer/parameter group across the subject sequence. Separately, plot mean L2 parameter update size (`||Δ𝜃||`) per subject. Expect: `Ω` grows monotonically; `||Δ𝜃||` decays monotonically. Compute correlation per parameter between `Ω` after task `τ-1` and `||Δ𝜃||` in task `τ`. Expect strong negative correlation. Demonstrates the freezing mechanism.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Can meta‑learning approaches (e.g., MAML) provide statistically significant forward transfer for subject‑incremental EEG emotion classification?  
  **Basis:** Explicitly identified in Future Work; current regularization methods showed no FWT improvement over sequential fine‑tuning.  
  **Resolution evidence:** Implement MAML on DREAMER and SEED with the same protocol; compare FWT/BWT to baselines; conduct statistical tests; measure sample efficiency and convergence.

- **Question 2:** Do noise‑robust modifications to importance heuristics (e.g., using data augmentation or multiple samples) improve reliability and performance under EEG noise?  
  **Basis:** Inferred from Hypotheses H1a/H1b showing empirical Fisher/SI inflate under noise, MAS remains sensitive.  
  **Resolution evidence:** Compute importance using noise‑invariant techniques (e.g., averaging gradients over augmented samples); evaluate consistency across seeds; correlate importance with true impact via ablation; measure CL performance stability.

- **Question 3:** Can EEG foundation models pre‑trained on large, diverse datasets mitigate order sensitivity and enable forward transfer?  
  **Basis:** Explicitly proposed in Future Work; Hypothesis H4 shows strong order sensitivity for regularization‑based CL.  
  **Resolution evidence:** Fine‑tune a public EEG foundation model (e.g., EEG‑SSL) on sequential subject streams; compare FWT and order variance to regularization baselines; analyze learned representations for transferability.

## Limitations
- **λ‑tuning details omitted** (search space, validation metric) hinders exact replication of optimal settings.
- **Subject order seeds undisclosed** despite strong order effects; reported variance may not capture full sensitivity.
- **Preprocessing specifics ambiguous** (TorchEEG split behaviour, temporal leakage handling) risks implementation deviation.
- **Corpus shows low external validation** (avg FMR 0.51, 0 citations) limits generalisability confidence.

## Confidence
- **Core claim (no FWT, stability‑plasticity misalignment):** High (consistent, p>0.05).
- **Heuristic unreliability:** Medium (theory supported, corpus lacks corroboration).
- **Interference/over‑constraining:** High (direct measurements: negative gradient cosine, Ω↑, Δθ↓, order variance).
