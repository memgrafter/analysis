# Imperfect Influence, Preserved Rankings: A Theory of TRAK for Data Attribution

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.01312
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01312
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Theoretical analysis shows TRAK’s linearization and projection cause large absolute errors but preserve ranking correlation >0.9, requiring projection dimension k ≫ d/∥β∗∥² for reliable attribution.

## Executive Summary
TRAK is a scalable data attribution method that estimates the influence of training points on model predictions by combining linearization, random projection, and an approximate leave-one-out (ALO) correction. This paper provides a theoretical analysis of TRAK’s approximations. The authors prove that the ALO step introduces negligible error compared to the linearized influence; however, the linearization and projection steps can induce substantial absolute errors—potentially comparable to the true influence magnitude. Despite these errors, the estimated influences remain highly correlated with the true values (empirically >0.9) and preserve the relative ordering of data points, enabling reliable identification of the most and least influential training examples. A key condition for ranking preservation is that the projection dimension k must grow sufficiently fast relative to the parameter dimension d and the norm of the true parameter β∗ (k ≫ d/∥β∗∥²). The theory is supported by simulations on multi-class classification and experiments on CIFAR datasets, clarifying both TRAK’s strengths (ranking stability) and its limitations (sensitivity to projection size and reliance on smoothness assumptions).

## Method Summary
TRAK approximates influence functions for large-scale machine learning models through three main steps. First, an empirical risk minimizer (β̂) is trained on the full dataset. For each training point i, the gradient of the model output with respect to parameters g_i = ∇f(x_i, β̂) is computed. Second, to reduce computational cost, these d‑dimensional gradients are randomly projected to k dimensions via a matrix S with i.i.d. Gaussian entries, yielding φ_i = S⊤g_i. Third, an approximate leave‑one‑out (ALO) correction uses the full Hessian (or its projected version) to estimate the parameter change had point i been removed, under a linearization around β̂. The influence of point i on a test prediction is then given by a closed‑form expression involving φ_i, the projected Hessian, and the gradient of the test point. The linearization replaces the exact LOO solution with a first‑order Taylor expansion, which is accurate when β̂ is near the population optimum. This pipeline avoids costly retraining and scales to millions of data points.

## Key Results
- The ALO approximation error is negligible, scaling as O(∥β∗∥²/n^1.5) or smaller, while the linearization and projection errors can be O(∥β∗∥²/n) and thus dominant.
- Despite large absolute errors, the correlation between TRAK’s influence estimates and the true influences exceeds 0.9 in simulations, and the relative order of points is largely preserved.
- Ranking integrity critically depends on the projection dimension k; the condition k ≫ d/∥β∗∥² ensures that the random projection does not collapse the separation between influential and non‑influential points.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The linearization of the model around the empirical risk minimizer (ERM) introduces large absolute errors in influence estimates but preserves the relative ranking of training data points.
- **Mechanism:** The non‑linear model is approximated by its first‑order Taylor expansion \( f(x, \beta) \approx g^\top \beta + b \), where \( g = \nabla f(x, \hat{\beta}) \) and \( b = f(x, \hat{\beta}) - g^\top \hat{\beta} \). This changes the optimization landscape, altering the exact leave‑one‑out solution \( \hat{\beta}^{(-i)} \) into its linearized counterpart \( \check{\beta}^{(-i)} \). The error magnitude \( |I_{\text{True}}(z_i, z_{\text{new}}) - I_{\text{Linear}}(z_i, z_{\text{new}})| \) can scale as \( \|\beta^*\|^2 / n \), comparable to the true influence itself. However, the distortion is coherent enough that the correlation between \( I_{\text{True}} \) and \( I_{\text{Linear}} \) remains high (>0.9 in simulations), preserving ordering.
- **Core assumption:** The model is smooth and the ERM \( \hat{\beta} \) is close to the true parameter \( \beta^* \); the Hessian of the empirical risk is well‑conditioned near the optimum (Assumptions B.3, B.4).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “the linearization and projection steps can introduce substantial absolute errors… Nevertheless, the estimated influences remain highly correlated with the true values (empirically >0.9) and preserve relative ordering.”
  - [section 2.2.3] Theorem 2.6: error bound \( \|\beta^*\|^2 / n \) for dependent case; Theorem 2.9: error bound \( \|\beta^*\| / n^{1-\epsilon} \) for independent case.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like “TRAK: Attributing Model Behavior at Scale” and “Concept‑TRAK” empirically rely on this linearization; no strong counter‑evidence in corpus.
- **Break condition:** When the model is highly non‑linear (e.g., deep networks with complex decision boundaries) and the training data induces large second‑order effects, the linearization error may dominate and corrupt rankings. The theory assumes \( \|\beta^*\|^2 \ll n^{1-\epsilon} \) for coherence.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The Approximate Leave‑One‑Out (ALO) correction introduces negligible error compared to the linearized influence, even in non‑linear settings.
- **Mechanism:** ALO uses a first‑order expansion of the leave‑one‑out parameter shift with the Hessian evaluated at the full ERM, circumventing the costly re‑optimization. The error \( |I_{\text{Linear}} - I_{\text{ALO}}| \) scales as \( \|\beta^*\|^2 / n^{1.5-\epsilon} \) (dependent) and \( \|\beta^*\| / n^{1.5-\epsilon} \) (independent), strictly smaller than the magnitude of \( I_{\text{Linear}} \) itself.
- **Core assumption:** The empirical Hessian is stable and the loss is smooth (Assumptions B.3, B.5); the ERM is near the population optimum.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “the ALO approximation error is negligible.”
  - [section 2.2.4] Theorems 2.12 and 2.13 provide error bounds and show \( I_{\text{ALO}} / I_{\text{Linear}} \to 1 \) in probability.
  - [section 3] Figure 1 shows near‑perfect alignment between \( I_{\text{Linear}} \) and \( I_{\text{ALO}} \) in multi‑class classification simulations.
- **Break condition:** If the Hessian is ill‑conditioned or the loss is non‑smooth (e.g., non‑differentiable activation functions), the ALO approximation may no longer be benign. The theory requires strong convexity and Lipschitz gradients.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Random projection of gradients to dimension \( k \) reduces computational cost but can significantly distort influence magnitudes; the impact on ranking preservation critically depends on the ratio \( k/d \) and \( \|\beta^*\|^2 \).
- **Mechanism:** Gradients \( g_i \in \mathbb{R}^d \) are projected via \( \phi_i = S^\top g_i \) with \( S \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times k} \) (Johnson‑Lindenstrauss style). This changes the inner products and the Gram matrix \( \Phi^\top \Phi \). The resulting influence \( I_{\text{TRAK}} \) scales as \( I_{\text{ALO}} \times (k/d) \) in the dependent case, while in the independent case its magnitude is \( \|\beta^*\| / (\sqrt{k} n^{1-\epsilon} \sqrt{d}) \). The crucial condition for ranking preservation is \( k \gg d / \|\beta^*\|^2 \); if this fails, the separation between influential and non‑influential points can collapse.
- **Core assumption:** The projected gradients approximately preserve inner products; the projection matrix \( S \) has i.i.d. Gaussian entries; the Gram matrix \( G^\top G \) is well‑conditioned (Assumption B.7 plus random matrix theory).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “the projection step’s accuracy critically depends on the projection dimension \( k \); to maintain ranking integrity, \( k \) must scale with the parameter dimension \( d \) (\( k \gg d/\|\beta^*\|^2 \)).”
  - [section 2.2.5] Theorem 2.14 gives the bound \( |I_{\text{TRAK}}| \leq C_{\text{TRAK}} \|\beta^*\| \sqrt{k} / (n^{1-\epsilon} \sqrt{d}) \) for independent points and the lower bound \( |I_{\text{TRAK}}| \geq c_{\text{TRAK}} \|\beta^*\|^2 k / (n d) \) for dependent points.
  - [section 3] Figure 2 shows correlation decays as \( k/d \) decreases; Figure 12 shows magnitude orders match the theory.
- **Break condition:** When \( k \) is too small (e.g., \( k < d / \|\beta^*\|^2 \)), the projection noise can exceed the signal difference between influential and non‑influential points, causing ranking failure. This is more likely in high‑dimensional models with large \( \|\beta^*\|^2 \).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Influence Functions**
  - *Why needed here:* Influence functions formalize the counterfactual effect of removing a training point on a test prediction, forming the target quantity TRAK approximates. Understanding \( I_{\text{True}}(z_i, z_{\text{new}}) = f(x_{\text{new}}, \hat{\beta}^{(-i)}) - f(x_{\text{new}}, \hat{\beta}) \) is essential to gauge approximation error.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the key computational bottleneck in computing exact influence functions for modern neural networks?
- **Concept: Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) and leave‑one‑out (LOO)**
  - *Why needed here:* TRAK approximates the LOO solution \( \hat{\beta}^{(-i)} \). The derivations rely on first‑order optimality conditions for ERM and expansions around \( \hat{\beta} \).
  - *Quick check question:* Why is re‑training the model for each \( i \) (exact LOO) generally infeasible for large \( n \) and \( d \)?
- **Concept: Random Projection and Johnson‑Lindenstrauss Lemma**
  - *Why needed here:* TRAK uses random projection \( S \) to reduce gradient dimension \( d \) to \( k \). The accuracy of inner product preservation underpins the ranking guarantee condition \( k \gg d / \|\beta^*\|^2 \).
  - *Quick check question:* How does the projection dimension \( k \) relate to the original dimension \( d \) and the desired distortion level in Johnson‑Lindenstrauss embeddings?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. Input data: training set D = {(y_i, x_i)}_{i=1}^n, test point (y_new, x_new).
  2. Model: f(x; β), parameters β ∈ ℝ^d. ERM gives β̂ = argmin_β (1/n) Σ_i ℓ(y_i, f(x_i; β)).
  3. Gradient computation: for each point, compute g_i = ∇f(x_i, β̂) ∈ ℝ^d.
  4. Random projection: S ∈ ℝ^{p × k} with i.i.d. 𝒩(0,1); φ_i = S⊤ g_i ∈ ℝ^k. Form Φ ∈ ℝ^{n × k}.
  5. ALO correction: compute Hessian approximations ¨ℓ_i(βˇ), where βˇ is the linearized ERM. Evaluate I_TRAK(z_i, z_new) using formula (6).
  6. Post‑processing: (Optional) ensemble averaging and soft‑thresholding (mentioned in paper but omitted in theory).
- **Critical path:** Data → ERM training → gradient extraction {g_i} → projection {φ_i} → ALO formula → influence scores. The linearization occurs implicitly by using βˇ in the Hessian and gradient evaluations.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - k vs. accuracy: Larger k preserves inner products better, maintaining ranking integrity; smaller k reduces memory and compute but risks ranking corruption. The rule of thumb: k ≳ d · poly(log d, log n) / ∥β∥^2 (from Theorem 2.14).
  - Linearization vs. non‑linearity: For GLMs (linear models), linearization incurs zero error; for neural nets, it introduces large absolute errors but still preserves rankings. If exact magnitude is required, TRAK may be unsuitable.
  - ALO vs. exact LOO: ALO is nearly free computationally; exact LOO is O(nd) per point. The tradeoff is negligible error vs. exactness.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Correlation between I_TRAK and I_True drops significantly (e.g., below 0.8) as k/d decreases.
  - The top‑k identified influential points change dramatically with small changes in k or random seed (high variance).
  - The magnitude of I_TRAK for dependent vs. independent test points becomes indistinguishable when k is too small (violates separation in Theorem 2.14).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Validate linearization error magnitude: Simulate a multi‑class classification problem (non‑linear f). Compute I_True, I_Linear for z_i = z_new and independent z_new. Verify that |I_True - I_Linear| ∼ ∥β∥^2/n (dependent) and ∥β∥/n^{1-ε} (independent) by log‑log regression.
  2. Validate ALO error negligibility: Using the same simulation, compute I_ALO and plot I_ALO vs. I_Linear. Expect near‑perfect linear fit with slope ~1 and correlation >0.99. Quantify the error order to be o(1) times |I_Linear|.
  3. Projection sensitivity sweep: Vary k/d ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75} and compute I_TRAK. Plot correlation vs. k/d. Verify that correlation decreases monotonically with k/d and that magnitudes for dependent/independent cases converge when k/d is small. Fit |I_TRAK| ∼ k^α to check α ≈ 0.5 (independent) or α ≈ 1 (dependent).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the exact minimal projection dimension k needed to preserve ranking integrity in TRAK, and how does it scale with model parameters and dataset size in practical deep learning settings?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Theorem 2.14 shows that ranking preservation requires k >> d / ∥β∗∥^2, but the constants and finite‑sample behavior are not derived. The paper also uses simulations where k is set to fractions of d, but no theoretical threshold is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis is asymptotic and assumes simplified models (e.g., GLM, one‑layer NN). For deep networks, the effective parameter dimension and structure may alter the scaling, and the bound may be loose, making it unclear how to choose k efficiently.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A refined non‑asymptotic analysis that yields explicit constants and concentration bounds for general neural networks; empirical studies that map the (k, correlation) curve across architectures to validate or tighten the theory.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the TRAK theory be extended to deep overparametrized neural networks, where the regularity assumptions (e.g., well‑conditioned Hessian, sub‑Gaussian gradients) may not hold?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Assumptions B.1–B.7 are verified only for linear models and one‑hidden‑layer networks (Section B.5). Multi‑class experiments on CIFAR use a simple multinomial logistic regression with downsampled images, not deep nets.
- **Why unresolved:** Modern deep networks have non‑convex loss landscapes and highly correlated gradients, which may violate the convexity and sub‑Gaussian assumptions. The current proofs rely on these assumptions to bound errors; without them, the error bounds may not hold or may need fundamentally different techniques.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical derivation of analogous error bounds under weaker assumptions suitable for deep nets (e.g., neural tangent kernel regime, overparametrization conditions). Alternatively, empirical evaluation on deep CNNs/transformers comparing TRAK behavior to linearized baselines to see if the same patterns hold despite assumption violations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do the additional steps in the full TRAK algorithm (ensemble averaging and soft thresholding) affect the approximation errors and ranking preservation? Could they mitigate the sensitivity to projection dimension k?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states: “TRAK includes two additional steps: (i) Ensemble averaging, … (ii) the application of soft thresholding … However, since we only study the accuracy of approximations involved in TRAK, we skip these two steps.” (Section 1.2).
- **Why unresolved:** Ensemble averaging might reduce variance from random projection, and soft thresholding could promote sparsity in influence estimates, potentially lowering the required k or improving robustness. Their combined effect is unknown both theoretically and empirically.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An extended theoretical analysis incorporating these steps, or systematic experiments varying the number of ensemble members and threshold values to measure their impact on correlation and ranking metrics across datasets and projection sizes.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Under what conditions does the theoretical separation between large and small influence values (Proposition 2.4 vs. 2.5) break down, and how would that affect TRAK’s ranking reliability in practice?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The separation argument assumes ‖β∗‖ ≫ 1 and that the test point is either identical to or independent from the training point. The paper notes that the large separation “is in fact one of the reasons behind the empirical success” but does not explore scenarios where influence magnitudes are more comparable (e.g., many points with moderate influence).
- **Why unresolved:** If true influences are clustered, TRAK’s approximation errors could swap rankings. The current analysis gives no guarantee on the correlation or top‑k accuracy when influence magnitudes are similar. This is a key practical scenario where attribution might be most needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A quantitative ranking error bound as a function of the separation gap Δ = |I_true(zi) - I_true(zj)| and the error variances. Simulations that systematically vary the gap to determine the minimal detectable difference for given k and n, and real‑world data analysis to see if such scenarios occur.

## Limitations
- Theory assumes smooth, well‑conditioned, i.i.d. settings; deep networks may violate, weakening guarantees.
- Linearization induces large absolute errors; TRAK only trustworthy for ranking, not magnitude.
- Projection dimension must satisfy k >> d/∥β*∥^2; however ∥β*∥ is often unknown and can be large, making k impractically big.
- True influence estimation is costly, so empirical studies use tiny subsets, limiting statistical power.

## Confidence
- ALO error small: High for convex/smooth models; Medium for deep nets.
- Ranking preservation: High for moderate non‑linearity (ρ>0.9); Medium for extreme non‑linearity.
- Projection bound: High in theory; Medium in practice due to unknown ∥β*∥.
- Overall: Medium‑High for identifying top/bottom points if k is chosen appropriately.
