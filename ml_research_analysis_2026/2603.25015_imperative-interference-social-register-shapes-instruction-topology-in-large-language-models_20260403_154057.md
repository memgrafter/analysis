# Imperative Interference: Social Register Shapes Instruction Topology in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.25015
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25015
- **Reference count:** 1
- **Primary result:** System prompts show language-dependent instruction interaction topologies (cooperative in English, competitive in Spanish); rewriting imperative instructions to declarative reduces cross‑linguistic variance by 81% and can shift the topology, with spillover effects.

## Executive Summary
This paper reveals that the interaction topology among instructions within a large language model’s system prompt is not language‑invariant but is shaped by social register. The same semantically translated prompt yields a cooperative network in English but a competitive one in Spanish. The root cause is the differing obligatory force of imperative mood across languages. Through systematic instruction‑level ablation across four models and four languages, the authors demonstrate that rewriting a small number of imperative instructions into declarative form drastically reduces cross‑linguistic variance (81% reduction, p=0.029) and can convert Spanish’s competitive topology into a cooperative one, with spillover onto unmodified instructions. The findings imply that LLMs process instructions as social acts, and that practical multilingual prompt engineering should favor declarative encodings over imperatives.

## Method Summary
A production system prompt (Claude Code v2.1.50) is decomposed into 56 blocks, 22 designated “free” (safe to remove). These blocks are translated into Mandarin, French, and Spanish using Gemini Flash 2.0 while preserving tone. Four LLMs are queried via OpenRouter (Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini Flash 2.0, DeepSeek V3, Mistral Medium 3.1). The pipeline executes two ablation phases: Phase 0 removes single blocks to measure main effects on adherence to 22 hand‑written probes; Phase 1 uses strength‑2 covering arrays to ablate block pairs, revealing interaction topology. Topology direction (cooperative vs. competitive) is inferred from the sign of main effects across blocks. Additional experiments test density confounding (E‑DENSE), a single‑block declarative rewrite (E‑PROC), and a three‑block rewrite in Spanish (E‑TOPO). Statistics rely on permutation tests (100 k) and FDR correction. The full suite requires ~19 000 model calls.

## Key Results
- Language × model × instruction three‑way interaction: English exhibits a cooperative topology (removing blocks generally harms adherence), while Spanish shows an inverted competitive topology (removing blocks often improves adherence).
- Procedurally encoded instructions (e.g., conditional workflows) have 2.9× higher cross‑linguistic variance than declarative ones.
- Rewriting the `commit-restrictions` block to declarative form reduces cross‑linguistic variance by 81% (p=0.029). Rewriting three imperative blocks in Spanish shifts its topology from competitive to cooperative and produces spillover effects on remaining unrewritten blocks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Social register (imperative vs. declarative mood) mediates instruction interaction topology across languages.
- **Mechanism:** Imperative mood encodes language‑specific obligatory force; stacking imperatives creates either a coherent authority frame (cooperative network) or competing obligation signals (competitive network) depending on the target language’s sociolinguistic conventions.
- **Core assumption:** Models internalize language‑specific pragmatic conventions during training; these conventions actively shape instruction processing at inference time.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - The failure is not uniform degradation; it is structural inversion.
  - The spillover is the strongest evidence for the social register hypothesis.
- **Break condition:** If declarative rewriting of imperative blocks does not shift Spanish topology from competitive to cooperative.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Register operates at the system level via an “obligation‑resolution load,” not per‑block fragility.
- **Mechanism:** Each imperative consumes processing resources dedicated to resolving social authority; reducing imperative count lowers total load, improving fidelity for all blocks (including unrewritten ones).
- **Core assumption:** The model allocates finite “authority reconciliation” capacity; exceeding it causes interference that manifests as competitive pairwise effects.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Rewriting three imperative blocks shifts Spanish instruction topology… with spillover effects on unrewritten blocks.
  - Reducing the number of imperatives reduces the total obligation‑resolution load, freeing the model to process the remaining blocks more accurately.
- **Break condition:** If rewriting a subset of imperatives only changes outcomes for the rewritten blocks, with no effect on others.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Procedurally encoded instructions are intrinsically more cross‑lingually fragile than declarative ones due to ambiguous translation compression.
- **Mechanism:** Conditional chains lose structural coherence under translation, creating ambiguity in constraint application. Declarative forms preserve rule boundaries.
- **Core assumption:** Information density is a secondary confound; the primary fragility stems from the procedural form’s reliance on syntactic/semantic linkages that translate unreliably.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Procedural encodings show 2.9× higher cross‑linguistic variance than declarative ones.
  - The model loses track of which constraints apply to which context.
- **Break condition:** If declarative rewriting fails to reduce variance for procedurally encoded blocks (beyond density‑matched controls).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Speech Act Theory** (locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary)
  - **Why needed:** Distinguishes between an instruction’s literal meaning and its social force as a command. The paper finds that identical literal meaning yields different social effects across languages.
  - **Quick check:** If “NEVER use X” and “X: disabled” have the same literal meaning, why might one be more social than the other?

- **Concept: Covering Arrays for Strength‑2 Ablation**
  - **Why needed:** The experimental design uses covering arrays to efficiently measure all pairwise instruction interactions without exhaustive ablation, enabling topology inference.
  - **Quick check:** What does a “strength‑2 covering array” guarantee about pairs of instructions, and why is that necessary for measuring interaction topology?

- **Concept: Cross‑Linguistic Variance as a Diagnostic**
  - **Why needed:** The core dependent variable is the variance of a single instruction’s effect across languages. Low variance indicates robust, language‑independent encoding.
  - **Quick check:** If an instruction has high cross‑linguistic variance, what does that imply about its encoding (e.g., imperative vs. declarative)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** 
  Corpus Decomposer -> Translation Pipeline -> Probe Engine -> Ablation Generator -> Statistician -> Topology Comparator

- **Critical path:**
  1. Decompose the production system prompt into 56 blocks.
  2. Translate all blocks to target languages while preserving register.
  3. Run Phase 0 single‑block ablations across languages/models to compute main effects.
  4. Identify language‑specific topology (e.g., English cooperative, Spanish competitive).
  5. Run Phase 1 pairwise ablations to confirm interaction patterns.
  6. Falsify density confound (E‑DENSE) by padding/compressing prompts.
  7. Test single‑block declarative fix (E‑PROC) on variance.
  8. Rewrite key imperative blocks to declarative in one language’s corpus.
  9. Re‑run pairwise ablation to measure topology shift and spillover.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Translation model choice: Using Gemini Flash 2.0 avoids circularity but introduces third‑party translator biases.
  - LLM‑as‑judge: 7/22 probes use the tested model as judge (cost‑effective but risks self‑serving bias).
  - Block “free” definition: Pragmatic constraint; removal may cause subtle failures not caught by functional tests.
  - User language: Testing only English user messages; performance with non‑English user prompts unknown.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Unexpected positive main effects in a language → competitive topology.
  - Model‑specific anomalies (e.g., Mistral worst in English, best in Mandarin; Gemini fails commit‑restrictions).
  - Density‑induced mode confusion due to padding.
  - Register spillover failure: rewriting target blocks but no shift in unrewritten blocks would contradict the system‑level load hypothesis.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **T7 Cross‑lingual baseline:** Run all 22 probes with all 56 blocks present across 4 models × 4 languages. Observe aggregate language effects and probe‑level inversions.
  2. **T9 E‑DENSE (density falsification):** For Mandarin, create two variants: original (compressed) and padded to English character count. Run full probe set to test if topology differences are purely information‑density artifacts.
  3. **T10 E‑PROC (single‑block fix):** Rewrite the `commit‑restrictions` block to declarative form (both “flat” and “scoped” variants). Test all 22 probes across languages to measure cross‑linguistic variance reduction for a single high‑impact instruction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the critical density of imperative instructions that triggers a topology phase transition from cooperative to competitive, and is this threshold language‑ and model‑dependent?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Work suggests varying imperative density to find a critical threshold (§5.5).
- **Why unresolved:** Only a partial rewrite (3 of 11 blocks) was tested; the continuous relationship and precise threshold remain unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically vary the number of imperative blocks from 0 to full in the Spanish prompt for Haiku (and other models), measuring mean pairwise ablation effects to identify the inflection point; repeat across languages.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can specific attention heads in transformer architectures be identified that encode language‑specific conventions for imperative obligatory force, providing a mechanistic explanation for topology inversion?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Work proposes using activation patching or probing classifiers to locate the “register head” (§5.5).
- **Why unresolved:** No internal model analysis was conducted; the hypothesis rests solely on behavioral evidence from ablation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct activation patching experiments comparing model responses to imperative vs. declarative prompts in English and Spanish, looking for head‑level differences that correlate with observed cooperative/competitive topology shifts.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does training LLMs on constitutional AI principles phrased declaratively (rather than imperatively) yield more consistent alignment across languages, as predicted by the inference‑time register effect?
- **Basis in paper:** Discussion states this as a testable prediction: imperative principles may cause language‑dependent alignment (§5.2); Future Work explicitly calls for comparative constitutional prompt design (§5.5).
- **Why unresolved:** All evidence comes from inference‑time prompt rewriting; training‑time effects on alignment remain speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train two otherwise identical models on preference datasets where constitutional principles are phrased either entirely imperatively or entirely declaratively, then evaluate alignment consistency across multiple languages using a standardized cross‑lingual probe battery.

## Limitations
- **Translation determinism & register preservation:** The core claim depends on Gemini Flash 2.0’s translations preserving intended social force; rerunning the translation pipeline could yield different Spanish texts and invalidate results.
- **Model version drift & endpoint stability:** The four tested models are specific snapshot IDs from March 2026. If providers have updated or deprecated these endpoints, observed topologies may not replicate.
- **Causal direction of spillover:** The system‑level “obligation‑resolution load” hypothesis is compelling but not exclusive; spillover could alternatively arise from distributed attention patterns or hidden‑state interactions not probed by block‑level ablation.

## Confidence
- **High:** The empirical finding that declarative rewriting reduces cross‑linguistic variance (81%, p=0.029) and shifts Spanish topology. This is a direct, falsifiable result from controlled experiments (E‑PROC, E‑TOPO).
- **Medium:** The social register mechanism as the primary cause. Strongly supported by the three‑way interaction and variance reduction, but relies on the assumption that translations preserved intended social force.
- **Low:** The generalizability of the architecture‑level claim (“LLMs process instructions as social acts”). Evidence is limited to one production prompt and four models; other architectures, domains, or languages may behave differently.

## Next Checks
1. **Exact‑model replication:** Retest baseline and E‑TOPO using the exact archived model snapshots (if available via OpenRouter version pinning) versus current endpoints to measure topology inversion consistency.
2. **Translation fidelity audit:** Conduct human evaluation (n=50 blocks) of the original vs. Gemini‑translated Spanish imperative blocks to verify that obligatory force (illocutionary strength) was preserved. Correlate human ratings with subsequent model topology.
3. **Controlled rewrite cascade:** In a fresh prompt, identify all imperative blocks. Perform a stepwise declarative rewrite (1 block, 2 blocks, 3 blocks) in Spanish only. Verify that topology shift occurs only after the critical mass of rewrites, and that unrewritten imperative blocks show no individual main effect change—isolating the spillover system‑load effect.
