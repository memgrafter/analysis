# No One Size Fits All: QueryBandits for Hallucination Mitigation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20332
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20332
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Achieves 87.5% win rate vs. no-rewrite baseline and improves macro-average accuracy from 0.681 to 0.766 (+8.5 pp) on GPT-4o across 16 QA benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Hallucinations in closed-source large language models (LLMs) are difficult to address because model weights cannot be edited. This paper introduces QueryBandits, a contextual bandit framework that learns to select a per-query rewrite strategy from five candidates based on a 17-dimensional linguistic feature vector. A composite reward function—combining an LLM-as-judge correctness label (α=0.6), fuzzy string match (β=0.3), and BLEU-1 (γ=0.1)—provides a stable learning signal. Evaluated on GPT-4o across 16 question-answering benchmarks, the best contextual bandit (Thompson Sampling) yields an 87.5% win rate over the no-rewrite baseline and significantly outperforms static rewrite policies. The approach requires only forward passes, making it practical for closed-source LLMs.

## Method Summary
For each query, the system first extracts 17 binary linguistic features (e.g., polysemy, anaphora) using an LLM. A contextual bandit algorithm (e.g., Thompson Sampling, LinUCB) then selects one of five rewrite arms (Paraphrase, Simplify, Expand, etc.) based on the feature vector. The chosen rewrite is applied via a prompt template, producing a modified query that is sent to the target closed-source LLM. The response is evaluated using a composite reward: 0.6 × LLM-judge binary correctness, 0.3 × fuzzy string match against a reference, and 0.1 × BLEU-1 lexical overlap. The bandit updates its policy online from the reward signal. All operations occur via API calls, avoiding any model fine‑tuning.

## Key Results
- Best contextual bandit (Thompson Sampling) achieves **87.5% win rate** over the no‑rewrite baseline.
- Outperforms static policies by **+42.6%** (Paraphrase) and **+60.3%** (Expand) in macro‑average accuracy.
- Macro‑average accuracy rises from **0.681 (no‑rewrite)** to **0.766** (+8.5 percentage points).
- All contextual bandits beat non‑contextual bandits, and higher feature variance correlates with more diverse arm choices.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Composite Reward Function
**Claim:** A carefully calibrated composite reward (α=0.6, β=0.3, γ=0.1) reliably separates correct from hallucinated outputs, providing a stable learning signal for bandit optimization.  
**Mechanism:** The reward combines an LLM‑as‑judge binary label (primary anchor for semantic correctness), fuzzy string match (for partial correctness), and BLEU‑1 (for lexical fidelity). This multi‑faceted design mitigates individual metric failure modes while producing a bounded, graded signal suitable for bandit learning.  
**Core assumption:** The convex combination is consistently predictive of human‑judged correctness across diverse QA scenarios.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] Reports the Pareto‑optimal weights (0.6,0.3,0.1) on the simplex frontier with ROC‑AUC 0.973.  
- [section] Details held‑out human validation (Figure 2a, Table 6a) showing the reward's high discriminative power (ROC‑AUC >0.97 stable by 150 samples).  
- [corpus] Neighbors focus on different hallucination detection methodologies; no direct counter‑evidence.  
**Break condition:** If the underlying judge model’s bias drifts significantly or if the validation distribution diverges from deployment queries, the reward signal may misalign with human notions of correctness.

### Per‑Query Contextual Selection
**Claim:** Per‑query selection of rewrite strategy guided by a 17‑dimensional linguistic feature vector (“query fingerprint”) outperforms any fixed rewrite policy or non‑contextual bandit because hallucination risk is context‑dependent.  
**Mechanism:** The contextual bandit observes the feature vector \(f_t\) and selects an arm \(a_t\) to maximize expected reward. The policy learns associations between patterns in the feature space and the efficacy of each rewrite arm, allocating exploration to uncertain contexts and exploiting when features predict a beneficial rewrite.  
**Core assumption:** The 17 binary linguistically‑motivated features capture sufficient signal about which rewrite will mitigate hallucinations for a given query.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] States contextual bandits achieve 87.5% win rate vs. no‑rewrite and outperform static policies (+42.6% to +60.3%), with higher feature variance correlating with more diverse arm choices.  
- [section] Table 1 shows contextual bandits (TS, LinUCB family) dominate macro‑average accuracy (0.766 vs. 0.681) and win most scenarios. Figure 4 illustrates distinct feature sensitivities per arm. Ablating context drops TS win rate to 81.7% (Table 2).  
- [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., FACTUM, Gradient‑guided attention) focus on internal‑state detection or RAG‑specific hallucinations, not per‑query adaptive rewriting.  
**Break condition:** If the linguistic feature extractor becomes unreliable (low inter‑run agreement) or if the relationship between features and optimal arm is non‑stationary, the learned policy may degrade.

### Input‑Only Steering
**Claim:** Operating purely at the input layer via forward passes makes the approach model‑agnostic and feasible for closed‑source LLMs where parameter editing is impossible.  
**Mechanism:** QueryBandits does not modify the target LLM’s weights, decoding, or internal representations. It only wraps the query with a selected rewrite strategy before each forward pass. The bandit policy itself is updated online based on the reward signal.  
**Core assumption:** Steering inputs is sufficient to alter the probability distribution of hallucinated outputs for the target LLM.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] Explicitly states the approach operates “solely via forward‑pass mechanisms” and “bypassing the need for retraining or gradient‑based adaptation.”  
- [section] Discusses comparison to open‑source methods like DoLa/TruthX that modify internal representations and are inapplicable to closed models. Table 5 shows QueryBandits lifts GPT‑4o from 80.7% to 88.8% on TruthfulQA MC1.  
- [corpus] Neighbors like “A Single Direction of Truth” propose internal probe/steering, which is infeasible for closed‑source APIs.  
**Break condition:** If the closed‑source model’s internal mechanisms are highly robust to surface‑form perturbations or if the rewrite strategies themselves induce new failure modes (e.g., over‑paraphrasing), input‑layer steering may have limited efficacy.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Feature Extractor → Bandit Policy → Rewrite Engine → Target LLM → Reward Calculator → Policy Updater  

*Details:*  
- **Feature Extractor:** LLM (gpt‑4o) tags 17 binary linguistic features for each query.  
- **Bandit Policy:** Contextual algorithm (Thompson Sampling, LinUCB) maps feature vector to one of five rewrite arms.  
- **Rewrite Engine:** Applies the selected rewrite strategy’s prompt template to the query.  
- **Target LLM:** Closed‑source model (gpt‑4o) generates a response for the rewritten query.  
- **Reward Calculator:** Computes \(r_t = \alpha s_{\text{llm}} + \beta s_{\text{fuzz}} + \gamma s_{\text{bleu}}\) using the response and reference.  
- **Policy Updater:** Bandit updates internal state (e.g., posterior, sufficient statistics) based on \((f_t, a_t, r_t)\).

### Critical Path
For each query, the loop must proceed in the following order without failure: (1) extract features, (2) select arm, (3) rewrite query, (4) query target LLM, (5) compute reward, (6) update bandit. Any break in this sequence aborts the learning cycle.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Bandit algorithm choice:** Thompson Sampling (TS) is more robust to non‑stationarity; LinUCB is more sample‑efficient under linear assumptions. Both outperform adversarial (EXP3) in experiments.
- **Exploration‑exploitation:** Higher exploration (e.g., via TS posterior variance) may increase immediate regret but avoids premature convergence to suboptimal arms, raising final accuracy.
- **Reward composition:** The weights (0.6,0.3,0.1) are Pareto‑optimal on a held‑out set. Over‑emphasizing lexical overlap (\(\gamma\)) can sacrifice truthfulness; reducing \(\alpha\) weakens the semantic anchor.

### Failure Signatures
- **Contextual collapse:** Low variance across queries causes the bandit to default to a single arm, losing the context advantage.
- **Static policy collapse:** On unperturbed benchmarks, bandits may converge to NO‑REWRITE due to memorization; training with lexically perturbed queries forces exploration.
- **Reward misalignment:** If the LLM judge shares the target model’s biases or fuzzy/BLEU metrics strongly disagree with semantics, the reward may not correlate with true factuality.
- **Feature instability:** Low reproducibility (<99% agreement) in the 17‑dimensional tagging injects noise and degrades policy learning.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Ablation: Disable context** – Run a non‑contextual bandit (e.g., Thompson Sampling without feature input). Expect a significant drop in win rate (reported: 81.7% vs. 87.5% for contextual TS).
2. **Static policy benchmark** – Evaluate each of the five rewrite strategies as fixed, zero‑shot prompts. Expect static policies to perform at or below no‑rewrite on macro‑average accuracy.
3. **Feature variance correlation** – Compute the variance of each feature conditioned on the selected arm. Expect features with higher variance to be more informative for the bandit’s decisions; uniformly low variance indicates under‑utilization of context.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** Do higher‑order interactions among the 17 linguistic features significantly influence the effectiveness of rewrite strategies, and can a bandit model that captures these interactions outperform the independent‑feature approach?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors explicitly state in the Limitations (A.1): “our current contextual bandit framework treats each of the 17 features as independent, but does not capture higher‑order interactions. This can provide an exciting avenue of future research in terms of measuring whether the combination of features jointly exacerbates hallucination.”  
*Why unresolved:* The current model assumes feature independence, which may ignore synergistic or antagonistic effects between features (e.g., a query being both ambiguous and having excessive details). Without modeling interactions, the policy may misattribute credit to individual features and select suboptimal rewrites.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* An ablation where the bandit uses an interaction‑aware model (e.g., polynomial features or a shallow neural network) and compare performance (win rate, accuracy) against the linear baseline. Feature interaction significance could be tested via statistical measures like mutual information or through a controlled study where known interacting feature pairs are perturbed.

**Open Question 2:** What is the causal relationship between query linguistic features and the success of rewrite arms, and how can we leverage causality to build more reliable and interpretable bandit policies?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors note in the Limitations (A.1): “we would like to highlight that the feature‑arm regression weights do not stipulate a causal relationship – highly sophisticated causal relationships are difficult to formulate within LLMs due to the inherent difficulties of interpreting a neural network’s internal layers.”  
*Why unresolved:* The observed correlations in Figure 5 do not imply that manipulating a feature will change the optimal arm. Causal understanding is needed to know if, for example, increasing pragmatics truly causes Simplify to succeed, or if both are symptoms of an underlying property.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct causal inference experiments, e.g., by synthetically perturbing specific features in queries (while holding others constant) and measuring the change in reward for each arm. Techniques like do‑calculus or instrumental variables could be applied if a causal graph is hypothesized. Alternatively, use a causal bandit framework that estimates causal effects.

**Open Question 3:** Can we design a more robust and generalizable reward function that reduces reliance on LLM‑as‑judge and improves performance across domains without extensive weight tuning?  
*Basis in paper:* The conclusion states: “Failure modes include reward misspecification and domain shift.” Also, the Limitations mention: “our reward model leverages LLM‑as‑judge, which may reflect the LLM’s bias.”  
*Why unresolved:* The current reward uses a fixed convex combination (α,β,γ) tuned on a held‑out human set, but this may not transfer to new domains. The LLM judge might be biased or inconsistent across topics.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Evaluate the reward’s consistency across multiple LLM judges (as done partially in Table 6 but extend to more models) and across diverse domains. Develop alternative proxies (e.g., using retrieval‑based verification or consistency across multiple LLM outputs) and compare their correlation with human judgments and downstream bandit performance. Adaptive weighting that learns from online feedback could also be tested.

**Open Question 4:** Why do certain static rewrite strategies (notably Expand) sometimes incur higher cumulative regret than no rewrite, and can we predict which queries will be harmed by specific rewrites?  
*Basis in paper:* [inferred] The paper states: “Certain static policies incur higher cumulative regret than NO‑REWRITE, indicating that an inflexible query‑rewriting policy can worsen hallucinations.” The data in Table 2 shows Expand has the worst performance among static arms.  
*Why unresolved:* Understanding the failure modes of specific rewrite strategies is crucial for deploying query rewriting safely. Without predictive signals, applying a rewrite blindly could increase hallucinations.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Analyze the queries where Expand (or other harmful strategies) leads to worse outcomes. Correlate these with the feature vector to identify conditions under which a strategy is detrimental. Build a meta‑classifier to predict when a rewrite is likely to backfire based on the query’s features, and test its accuracy in a holdout set.

## Limitations

- **Unspecified reproduction details:** The perturbed query generation pipeline, feature‑extraction prompt, bandit hyperparameters, and the exact LLM‑as‑judge prompt are not provided, making exact replication challenging.
- **Reliance on LLM‑as‑judge:** The composite reward depends on an LLM‑based correctness label, which may inherit the judge’s biases and might not align with human judgment across domains.
- **Feature independence assumption:** The 17 binary features are treated as independent, ignoring potential higher‑order interactions that could improve policy optimality.
