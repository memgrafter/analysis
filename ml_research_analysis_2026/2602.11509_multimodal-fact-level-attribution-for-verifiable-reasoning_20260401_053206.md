# Multimodal Fact-Level Attribution for Verifiable Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11509
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11509
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art MLLMs frequently hallucinate citations despite answering correctly; best models achieve only ~65% attribution F1 and MURGAT-S scores of 69.2 (WorldSense) and 56.9 (Video-MMMU).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces MURGAT, a benchmark and automatic evaluation framework for assessing fact-level multimodal attribution in complex reasoning tasks. MURGAT requires models to produce answers with precise citations—specifying modality and temporal segment—to video or audio sources. The evaluation pipeline decomposes model outputs into verifiable claims and atomic facts, then checks entailment of cited segments using vision‑language or audio‑language models. Evaluated on two datasets (WorldSense and Video‑MMMU) with eight MLLMs, the study reveals that even top‑performing models hallucinate citations despite correct answers, with attribution F1 rarely exceeding 65%. Key findings include a trade‑off between answer accuracy and attribution quality, where enforcing structured grounding (e.g., program‑aided generation) improves citations but harms reasoning performance; capacity‑dependent effects of test‑time scaling, where larger models benefit from more thinking time while smaller ones degrade; and a dichotomy where post‑hoc attribution helps on recognition tasks but catastrophically harms reasoning‑intensive ones. Additionally, vision‑only models often hallucinate audio citations, exposing modality‑awareness gaps.

## Method Summary
MURGAT is a benchmark for evaluating multimodal attribution at the fact level. Models must answer questions over video/audio inputs and provide citations that identify the modality and exact temporal segment supporting each claim. The automatic evaluation framework, MURGAT‑SCORE, comprises three subtasks: (1) verifiable claim identification, (2) atomic fact decomposition, and (3) attribution quality assessment via entailment. The pipeline uses Gemini models for each subtask, with selection validated against human annotations on a development set. For large‑scale experiments, the authors sample 100 test instances from each dataset, generate responses from eight MLLMs under three conditions (BASE, +CITATION, POST‑HOC), and compute metrics including answer accuracy, coverage (percentage of verifiable sentences with citations), attribution precision/recall, and MURGAT‑S (coverage × attribution F1). This setup enables fine‑grained analysis of attribution performance across model types and generation strategies.

## Key Results
- SOTA MLLMs frequently hallucinate citations despite correct answers; best attribution F1 ~65%, MURGAT‑S: 69.2 (WorldSense), 56.9 (Video‑MMMU).
- Enforcing structured grounding (e.g., program‑aided generation) improves attribution (+9.6 MURGAT‑S) but degrades answer accuracy by ~7 points on reasoning tasks.
- Scaling reasoning effort benefits stronger models (Gemini‑3‑Pro) but harms smaller ones (Gemini‑3‑Flash); post‑hoc attribution helps on recognition but harms reasoning‑intensive tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim:  
  Requiring explicit citation generation during model output creation imposes a “reasoning tax” that reduces accuracy on recognition-focused tasks.
- Mechanism:  
  The model must allocate limited reasoning resources to identifying and formatting source citations, which distracts from pure answer synthesis, particularly for straightforward queries where the answer can be retrieved directly.
- Core assumption:  
  Attention or reasoning capacity is finite; multi‑objective generation (answer + citations) introduces competition for those resources.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] “enforcing structured grounding (e.g., program‑aided generation) improves attribution (+9.6 MURGAT‑S) but degrades answer accuracy”
  - [Section 5.2] “Contrary to the hypothesis that citing evidence always improves performance, we observe a divergence based on task type. On the recognition‑focused WorldSense, requiring citations often imposes a ‘reasoning tax,’ slightly decreasing accuracy”
  - [corpus] Corpus neighbors discuss multimodal attention trade‑offs but do not directly investigate citation‑related reasoning tax.
- Break condition:  
  The mechanism reverses on reasoning‑intensive tasks where citations can scaffold the reasoning chain and improve accuracy (e.g., Video‑MMMU in Table 5).

### Mechanism 2
- Claim:  
  Scaling test‑time reasoning effort (more “thinking”) improves attribution quality for stronger models but harms it for weaker ones, indicating a capacity‑dependent alignment between internal reasoning and external evidence.
- Mechanism:  
  Additional compute enables larger models to refine grounding decisions and produce more accurate citations; smaller models may use extra steps to confabulate plausible but ungrounded content, decoupling reasoning from evidence.
- Core assumption:  
  Model size determines whether increased reasoning is used to improve faithfulness or to increase hallucination.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] “scaling reasoning effort benefits stronger models (Gemini‑3‑Pro) while harming smaller ones (Gemini‑3‑Flash)”
  - [Section 5.3] “For Gemini‑3‑Flash on WorldSense, increased reasoning effort counter‑intuitively leads to a decline in attribution quality… In contrast, Gemini‑3‑Pro demonstrates positive scaling… This indicates that stronger models are better equipped to align their reasoning chains with external evidence”
  - [corpus] Neighbors on test‑time scaling (e.g., OpenAI o1) show capacity‑dependent benefits but do not specifically address multimodal grounding.
- Break condition:  
  For models beyond a certain size threshold, further scaling might yield diminishing returns or also degrade; the paper only compares two models.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim:  
  Post‑hoc attribution (generating citations after answer generation) raises citation coverage but decreases precision on reasoning‑intensive tasks because it forces alignment of abstract reasoning steps to irrelevant visual segments, creating false positives.
- Mechanism:  
  After producing a reasoning chain, the model retroactively assigns timestamps; without tight integration of generation‑time grounding, it often matches generic introductory frames rather than precise evidence, especially for technical details.
- Core assumption:  
  Temporal alignment of reasoning to evidence benefits from concurrent generation; post‑hoc matching is inherently noisy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] “post‑hoc attribution helps on recognition tasks but harms reasoning‑intensive ones”
  - [Section 5.2] “Applying +POST‑HOC ATTRIBUTION yields the highest Coverage, but its impact on attribution quality splits by domain… on Video‑MMMU (reasoning), Post‑hoc causes Attribution to plummet… Qualitatively, post‑hoc methods tend to ‘force‑align’ abstract reasoning steps to random segments, creating false positives”
  - [Appendix D.2] “On VideoMMMU, the Post‑hoc process often results in ‘Citation Salad,’ incorrectly mapping specific technical steps to generic introductory frames”
  - [corpus] Weak direct support; neighboring papers on post‑hoc verification (e.g., RARR) do not compare task types.
- Break condition:  
  The mechanism reverses on recognition tasks (e.g., WorldSense) where post‑hoc improves both coverage and precision.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multimodal Attribution**  
  - Why needed: The core of MURGAT is evaluating whether model outputs are grounded in specific modalities and temporal segments. Without understanding what constitutes a valid citation, one cannot interpret the metrics.  
  - Quick check: What two pieces of information must each citation specify? (Answer: modality and timestamp)
- **Atomic Fact Decomposition**  
  - Why needed: Sentences often contain multiple claims; evaluating attribution at the atomic level allows fine‑grained credit assignment and avoids masking partial correctness.  
  - Quick check: Why is it insufficient to evaluate grounding at the sentence level? (Answer: A sentence can mix true and false facts; atomic decomposition isolates independent verifiable claims.)
- **Precision‑Recall Trade‑off for Citations**  
  - Why needed: MURGAT‑S combines coverage, attribution precision, and recall. Understanding these axes explains why high accuracy does not guarantee good grounding.  
  - Quick check: Which metric penalizes missing citations? Which penalizes irrelevant ones? (Answer: Recall penalizes missing citations; precision penalizes irrelevant ones.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input -> Verifiable Claim Identification -> Atomic Fact Decomposition -> Attribution Quality -> Scoring Module -> Output

### Critical path
1. Input: Model response `R` with citations `C` + multimodal sources `I`.
2. Filter to verifiable + cited sentences → `R_vc`.
3. Decompose each `r_i ∈ R_vc` into atomic facts `A_i`, inheriting `C_i`.
4. For each `(a_j_i, C_i)`, run entailment check: does `C_i` fully support `a_j_i`? If yes, which individual citations are necessary?
5. Aggregate counts → precision, recall, F1.
6. Compute coverage from `|R_vc| / |R_v|`.
7. Output MURGAT‑S.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Granularity**: Sentence‑level verifiability vs. atomic‑level; atomic is more precise but increases annotation/evaluation cost by ~4×; paper chooses sentence‑level for scalability while preserving high correlation (Table 4).
- **Decomposition strategy**: Single‑pass (entire response) vs. sentence‑wise chunking; chunking improves F1 by ~3 points (Table 3).
- **Automatic evaluator selection**: Different models (Gemini‑3‑Pro, Gemini‑2.5‑Flash, Gemini‑3‑Flash) specialize per subtask; trade‑off between correlation strength and inference cost; final pipeline balances both.

### Failure signatures
- High coverage + low attribution precision: Model cites many timestamps but includes irrelevant segments (e.g., “citation dumping”).
- Low coverage: Model withholds citations from verifiable claims (often seen in BASE variants).
- Modality hallucination: Vision‑only models citing audio timestamps (Table 13 shows up to 31.6% audio citations from VL models).
- Post‑hoc degradation on reasoning tasks: Plunge in attribution F1 when applying post‑hoc to Video‑MMMU (Table 5).
- Program‑aided accuracy drop: ~7‑point accuracy loss despite improved attribution (Section 5.4).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Human annotation baseline**: Annotate 20 examples across two datasets for all three subtasks; compute human MURGAT‑S to gauge model performance ceiling (Table 1).
2. **Automatic evaluator selection**: For each subtask, test multiple LLMs/prompt styles on human‑annotated dev set; pick best performing (e.g., Gemini‑3‑Pro JSON for verifiability, Gemini‑2.5‑Flash for entailment) and validate end‑to‑end correlation against human scores (Table 4).
3. **Large‑scale generation study**: Sample 100 test instances per dataset; run SOTA MLLMs with variants (BASE, +CITATION, POST‑HOC); measure accuracy and automatic MURGAT‑S; analyze effects of reasoning effort scaling and program‑aided grounding (Tables 5, 9–11).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What architectural modifications or training objectives can reconcile the trade‑off between verifiable attribution quality and reasoning accuracy?**  
   - **Basis:** The paper explicitly observes that enforcing structured grounding degrades accuracy on complex tasks (Abstract, Section 5.4).  
   - **Why unresolved:** The tension between flexible reasoning and rigid evidence chains remains unexplained.  
   - **What evidence would resolve:** New architectures or training signals that natively output integrated citations without sacrificing reasoning depth, evaluated on benchmarks that jointly optimize grounding and accuracy.

2. **Why does scaling reasoning effort lead to divergent attribution outcomes across model scales?**  
   - **Basis:** Section 5.3 shows larger models improve grounding with more compute, while smaller ones worsen.  
   - **Why unresolved:** No analysis of internal representations; the mechanistic cause (training data, architecture, optimization) is unknown.  
   - **What evidence would resolve:** Controlled experiments analyzing internal states (activations, attention) of scaled vs. base models during grounding decisions; ablations on cross‑attention components.

3. **What causes post‑hoc attribution to harm precision on reasoning‑intensive tasks?**  
   - **Basis:** Post‑hoc improves WorldSense but catastrophically reduces attribution on Video‑MMMU, creating “false positives” (Section 5.2, Appendix D.2).  
   - **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies the phenomenon but does not dissect whether it stems from reliance on parametric knowledge, prompt design, or a mismatch between conceptual and perceptual claims.  
   - **What evidence would resolve:** Error analysis categorizing incorrect citations; measuring semantic similarity between reasoning steps and cited segments; interventions to distinguish perceptual vs. inferential claims.

4. **How can multimodal attribution systems robustly handle cross‑modal evidence combination, given frequent modality hallucination?**  
   - **Basis:** Vision‑only models hallucinate audio citations up to 31.6% of the time (Section 5.2).  
   - **Why unresolved:** The cause (bias toward visual grounding, training data imbalance) is not investigated.  
   - **What evidence would resolve:** Analysis of training data for citation distribution bias; development of modality‑aware citation heads or verification modules; evaluation on joint audio‑visual benchmarks.

5. **Can MURGAT‑SCORE’s decomposition be generalized to non‑temporal multimodal settings (e.g., documents with text, tables, figures)?**  
   - **Basis:** MURGAT relies on temporal segments; generalization to static or non‑temporal evidence may require redefining “segments” and handling complex claims.  
   - **Why unresolved:** The paper focuses on video/audio and does not discuss adaptation to other modalities.  
   - **What evidence would resolve:** Extending the pipeline to a benchmark with heterogeneous sources (e.g., scientific papers), designing new segment definitions (bounding boxes, table cells), and validating correlation with human judgments.

## Limitations
- **Dataset scope:** Evaluations on only two datasets; may not generalize to other modalities (e.g., audio‑only) or reasoning complexities.
- **API/model dependence:** Generation and evaluation rely on proprietary Gemini models; results may not transfer to open‑source alternatives or future model versions.
- **Evaluation granularity trade‑off:** Automatic pipeline uses sentence‑level verifiability (for scalability) instead of finer atomic facts; could mask partial citation failures.

## Confidence
- Core findings (hallucination despite correctness, attribution‑accuracy trade‑off): High
- Mechanism 1 (reasoning tax from concurrent citation generation): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (capacity‑dependent test‑time scaling): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (post‑hoc alignment failure on reasoning tasks): Low
