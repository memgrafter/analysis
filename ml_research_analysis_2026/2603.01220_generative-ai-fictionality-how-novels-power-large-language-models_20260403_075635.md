# Generative AI & Fictionality: How Novels Power Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01220  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01220  
- **Reference count:** 10  
- **Primary result:** Training on fiction (BookCorpus) improves prediction of personal pronouns and dialogic words and generates more character-driven narratives, with a loss reduction of 0.72 nats (≈1.03 bits/word) on novel text.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how fictional training data (novels) shapes large language model outputs compared to non-fiction sources. In a controlled experiment, the researchers trained BERT models with and without access to the BookCorpus novel collection while keeping other factors constant. The model with fiction (Full Model) demonstrated superior predictive accuracy on personal pronouns and dialogic words, indicating enhanced modeling of character identity and conversation. Generated text analysis revealed a shift toward character-driven narratives with dialogue, while the model trained only on Wikipedia produced encyclopedic statements. Quantitatively, the Full Model reduced predictive loss on held-out novel text by 0.72 nats (≈1.03 bits per word), confirming a significant and specific information gain from fictional discourse. These findings suggest that novels provide LLMs with unique communicative strategies for representing persons and interpersonal dynamics.

## Method Summary
The study trained two BERT-base models from scratch: one on 2 billion tokens of Wikipedia only (Wiki Model), and the other on a mixture of 1.5 billion Wikipedia tokens and 500 million tokens from the BookCorpus collection of 7,000 full-length novels (Full Model). Both models were trained for approximately 1 million steps with identical hyperparameters (Adam learning rate 1e-4, warmup 10k, linear decay). Evaluation consisted of three parts: (1) A cloze task on a held-out Wikipedia test set (100k 100-word passages) measured per-vocabulary predictive accuracy and loss; (2) Generation of sentences via a Generative Stochastic Network (GSN) provided qualitative samples for manual analysis; (3) Information gain was computed by comparing model losses on a held-out set of novel passages (50k passages) and ranking passages by total gain to identify what the Full Model learned from fiction. The Wiki Model served as a counterfactual baseline, isolating the marginal contribution of fiction within a fixed total training budget.

## Key Results
- The Full Model showed significantly better predictive accuracy on personal pronouns (you, your, he, him, she, her, them) and dialogic words (say, asks) compared to the Wiki Model, with statistical significance after Bonferroni correction.
- Analysis of GSN-generated text found the Full Model produced more character-driven narratives featuring dialogue and interpersonal dynamics, whereas the Wiki Model tended to generate encyclopedic, factual statements.
- Training on fiction reduced the predictive loss on held-out novel text by 0.72 nats (≈1.03 bits per word), demonstrating a substantial information gain specific to fictional discourse.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Character Identity Continuity
Training on fiction improves a model's handling of character identity as realized through personal pronouns. Fictional narratives systematically maintain referential links to characters across long textual spans using pronouns, which trains the model in long-range dependencies for sustaining character identity. The core assumption is that fiction exhibits a higher density and structural importance of sustained pronominal reference compared to encyclopedic text. Evidence: the Full Model performed better on personal pronouns (abstract, Section 4.1) and BookCorpus novels rely heavily on tracking character references. A break condition would be if a model trained on non-narrative text showed similar improvement, indicating the effect is not specific to fictional narrative structures.

### Mechanism 2: Dialogic Interaction Patterns
Training on fiction enhances a model's capacity to generate dialogic interaction patterns, including speech verbs and pragmatic markers. Direct discourse in novels provides dense, structured examples of conversational turns, speech attribution verbs (*said*, *asked*), and their environments (e.g., contractions). The model learns the probabilistic patterns that sequence speech acts within narrative. The core assumption is that dialogue is a predominant and formally marked feature in novels, creating a distinct statistical signal. Evidence: improved prediction on dialogic words like *say* and *asks* (abstract, Section 4.1) and the observation that contractions like *wouldn't* never appear outside quotation marks in the test set (Section 4.1). A break condition would be if improvement on dialogic words replicated with a corpus of transcribed non-fictional conversations but did not manifest in generated text, tying the effect to content rather than structural form.

### Mechanism 3: High-Stakes Interpersonal Dynamics
Training on fiction induces models to generate snippets characterized by high-stakes interpersonal dynamics and character-driven narrative. Popular fiction (as in BookCorpus) is disproportionately oriented around conflict, transformation, and goal-directed interaction between characters. The model internalizes an "authorial hand" that arranges elements toward emotionally charged, socially consequential situations. The core assumption is that a significant portion of BookCorpus (genre fiction like romance, thriller) relies on such eventful, character-centric sequences. Evidence: the Full Model generated "more character-driven narratives with dialogue and interpersonal dynamics" while the Wiki Model generated "encyclopedic, factual statements" (abstract); analysis of top-ranked passages by information gain found every passage had multiple characters in high-stakes situations (Section 5). A break condition would be if fine-tuning on corpus of slice-of-life or minimalist literary fiction (with less overt conflict) did not produce similar "high-stakes" generations, indicating the effect is specific to the dominant commercial fiction in BookCorpus.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept 1: Language Modeling as Next-Word Prediction**  
- *Why needed:* The experimental design and mechanism explanations rely on understanding that BERT and LLMs learn to minimize prediction loss on masked tokens; the "effect" of fiction is measured as a change in this predictive distribution.  
- *Quick check question:* What does it mean for a model to have "lower predictive loss" on a specific word type, and how does that relate to what the model has "learned"?

**Concept 2: Fictionality as Communicative Strategy (Phelan/Walsh)**  
- *Why needed:* The paper uses this theoretical framework to interpret model outputs not as "nobodies" but as *somebodies* acting with intention, linking technical results (pronoun/dialogue improvements) to a theory of how fiction constructs relatable agents for communication.  
- *Quick check question:* How does Phelan's concept of the "incomplete suspension of disbelief" help us move beyond "stochastic parrot" critiques of LLM output?

**Concept 3: Controlled Experimental Design in ML**  
- *Why needed:* The core finding isolates the effect of fiction by holding all else constant (model architecture, training budget, non-fiction data) and varying only the inclusion of BookCorpus.  
- *Quick check question:* Why is it methodologically insufficient to just compare a model trained on BookCorpus to one trained on Wikipedia *alone*? (See Section 4's rationale for the two-model contrast.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Data Ingestion (Wikipedia dump, BookCorpus replication via Smashwords) → Preprocessing (tokenization, passage chunking) → Model Initialization (BERT-base uncased, HuggingFace transformers) → Training Loop (masked language modeling objective, identical hyperparameters for Wiki & Full models) → Evaluation Suite (cloze task on held-out Wikipedia test set, GSN generation, information gain on novel passages) → Qualitative Analysis (coding generated text for dialogue/character tags; ranking passages by information gain).
- **Critical path:** Data curation → Model training (parallel for Wiki/Full) → First evaluation (predictive performance) → Generation pass → Information gain computation → Manual/qualitative inspection of top-ranked passages and generated samples.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Training Data Budget:* Fixed at ~2B words total; adding 500M words of fiction required reducing Wikipedia data (1.5B vs 2B). This tradeoff means the Full Model's overall worse performance on Wikipedia is expected; the insight lies in *which* words improve.  
  - *Model Scale:* Using BERT-base (110M params) for feasibility; effects may differ in larger models or decoder-only architectures.  
  - *Evaluation Granularity:* Word-type level statistical tests control false discovery but may miss emergent phrasal patterns.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - No statistically significant word categories where Full Model > Wiki Model on Wikipedia test set.  
  - Generated text from both models is indistinguishable in presence/absence of dialogue or character interaction.  
  - Information gain on BookCorpus is near zero or uniformly distributed, with no clustering around multi-character, high-stakes passages.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Verification of Data Mixtures*: Confirm token counts and genre distributions in the prepared Wikipedia and BookCorpus training sets; validate that BookCorpus samples are full novel texts, not excerpts.  
  2. *Replication of Primary Predictive Result*: Train both models (identical seeds/hyperparameters). Perform cloze task on fixed Wikipedia test set; compute difference in cross-entropy loss per vocabulary item; apply multiple comparison correction. Expect to reproduce Table 1's list of pronouns/dialogue words.  
  3. *Generation & Baseline Coding*: Generate 100 sentences per model via GSN; implement a simple regex-based coder to flag sentences containing quotation marks, speech verbs (*said, asked, replied*), and personal pronouns; compare proportions. This provides a quick, replicable check on the "character-driven vs. encyclopedic" claim.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1: How do novels' effects on LLM outputs compare quantitatively and qualitatively to other non-fiction text sources?
- **Question:** How do novels’ effects on LLM outputs compare quantitatively and qualitatively to other forms of text, such as newspapers, Reddit, and Wikipedia?  
- **Basis in paper:** *Explicit* – the abstract and introduction pose this question.  
- **Why unresolved:** The experiment only compares Wikipedia to Wikipedia+BookCorpus; it does not disentangle fiction’s unique contributions from other non-fiction sources or test generalizability across corpora.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled experiment training identical models on matched subsets of different text types (novels, news, scientific papers, forum posts) and comparing performance on targeted linguistic/narrative tests plus generated text analysis.

### Open Question 2: What specific rhetorical or communicative strategies from fiction are most saliently internalized by LLMs?
- **Question:** What specific rhetorical or communicative strategies from fiction are most saliently internalized by LLMs, and how do these strategies manifest in model-generated text beyond character pronouns and dialogue?  
- **Basis in paper:** *Explicit* – the paper argues fiction provides a "communicative strategy" and that LLMs "leverage familiar attributes and affordances of fiction," identifying improvements in prediction of pronouns, dialogic words, modal verbs, and existential quantifiers.  
- **Why unresolved:** Analysis is limited to a small set of lexical features and generated samples; it does not systematically map a broader taxonomy of fictional rhetorical devices (e.g., free indirect discourse, narrative pacing, focalization) to model behavior.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A fine-grained linguistic annotation of large-scale generated text from models with/without fiction training, coding for a comprehensive set of narrative and rhetorical features. Influence function or probing studies could identify which training passages most strongly activate units corresponding to these features.

### Open Question 3: Does the "authorial hand" effect represent a consistent pattern or an artifact of sampling?
- **Question:** Does the "authorial hand" or narrative selection pressure inferred from high-information-gain passages (e.g., focus on high-stakes interpersonal moments) represent a consistent, learnable pattern across the full BookCorpus, or is it an artifact of the specific sampling and ranking method used?  
- **Basis in paper:** *Inferred* – the paper uses information gain on a held-out novel set to identify passages reflecting BERT's learning, finding a pattern of multi-character interaction at crises. This characterizes an "authorial somebody," but the methodology for selecting/ranking passages is not fully validated.  
- **Why unresolved:** The sample size (50 passages) is small; the ranking metric may be noisy or biased toward certain genres/authors; it's unclear if this pattern is dominant or one of many.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the same information gain ranking to the entire held-out novel test set, cluster high-gain passages by thematic/narrative features, and statistically test the prevalence of the "high-stakes interpersonal interaction" pattern versus others. Compare with a control using randomly sampled passages.

### Open Question 4: How do these effects transfer to different architectures and tuned models?
- **Question:** To what extent do the observed effects of fiction training (enhanced character/dialogue modeling) transfer to different model architectures (e.g., decoder-only GPT models) and to instruction-tuned or dialogue-optimized models (like ChatGPT), which are trained on different data mixtures and objectives?  
- **Basis in paper:** *Inferred* – the study uses BERT-base (bidirectional MLM) and a specific data mixture; the paper discusses implications for contemporary AI but does not test them.  
- **Why unresolved:** The interaction between model architecture, training objective, and data source effects is not explored.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the core predictive accuracy and generation experiments with decoder-only models (e.g., GPT-2) trained on the same controlled data splits. Further, test if the "character-driven" generation tendency persists in models further trained on instruction data or dialogue data.

### Open Question 5: How does fiction training shape social biases in model outputs?
- **Question:** How does the inclusion of fiction in training data quantitatively shape the prevalence of specific social biases (e.g., gender, race, class) in model outputs, particularly regarding interpersonal dynamics and power relations in generated narratives?  
- **Basis in paper:** *Explicit* – the paper notes a slight gender bias in sampled passages but pivots to analyze relational dynamics; it does not provide a systematic bias audit.  
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis of character interactions is qualitative and based on a small, non-representative sample; the paper raises the question but does not measure bias prevalence or strength compared to a no-fiction model.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a large-scale, automated audit: generate thousands of narrative snippets from both models; use NLP tools to extract character entities, identify dialogue acts, and code for power dynamics (e.g., interruptions, orders, agency). Compare distributions between models and correlate with stereotypes common in fiction genres.

## Limitations
- **Data replication uncertainty:** The exact BookCorpus (Smashwords, March 2021) is not preserved; any recreated version will differ in genre distribution and lexical content, potentially altering the magnitude or specificity of observed effects.
- **Generation underspecification:** The Generative Stochastic Network parameters ("aggressive mixture kernel," re-init schedule) are not fully detailed, making exact replication of generated text patterns difficult.
- **Generalizability scope:** Results are confined to BERT-base (110M params) and a specific fiction corpus dominated by commercial genre novels; effects may not hold for decoder-only models, larger scales, or literary/minimalist fiction.

## Confidence
- **High:** Quantitative improvements on personal pronouns and dialogic words (e.g., *he, she, say, asks*) are robust within the experimental setup, supported by statistical tests and clear corpus signals.
- **Medium:** The link between these improvements and "character-driven narrative" generation is plausible but interpretively framed; it relies on manual analysis of top-ranked passages and may reflect corpus-specific themes (high-stakes genre fiction).
- **Low:** Claims about fictionality as a *general* communicative strategy or about mechanisms like "long-range dependency for character identity" extend beyond the data; they are theoretical inferences, not directly measured.
