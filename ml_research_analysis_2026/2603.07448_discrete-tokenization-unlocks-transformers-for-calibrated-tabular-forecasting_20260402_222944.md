# Discrete Tokenization Unlocks Transformers for Calibrated Tabular Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.07448
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07448
- **Reference count**: 25
- **Primary result**: Achieves a 10.8% lower median MAE (35.94s vs 40.31s) than tuned XGBoost on a 600K-entity dataset, and attains KS=0.0045 when optimized for calibration.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the dominance of gradient boosting on tabular benchmarks by introducing a novel discretizing tokenizer that converts both inputs and outputs into token sequences. Combined with adaptive Gaussian smoothing to produce calibrated probability density functions, the method uses a causal Transformer architecture with time-delta tokens. On a large-scale running dataset, it outperforms tuned XGBoost by a significant margin in median MAE and achieves excellent calibration. Ablations confirm the importance of sequential ordering and time-delta tokens, showing degradation of approximately 2% when removed.

## Method Summary
The approach tokenizes continuous features via balanced (quantile-based) binning, mapping each value to a discrete bin ID. These tokens, along with discretized target outputs, form sequences following a fixed-stride grammar per event: `[features][pace][d_next][d_fin]`. A causal Transformer processes these sequences, with positional embeddings. The training targets are not one-hot but Gaussian-smoothed distributions over pace bins, where the smoothing width adapts to bin size to preserve calibration. At inference, the model outputs a probability density function over discrete pace bins. The system is trained with cross-entropy against these smoothed targets and employs AdamW optimization.

## Key Results
- 10.8% lower median MAE than tuned XGBoost (35.94s vs 40.31s) on a 600K-entity running dataset.
- Calibration metric KS=0.0045 when the model is optimized for calibration.
- Ablations: removing sequential ordering increases median MAE by ~2.0%; dropping time-delta tokens increases it by ~1.8%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Discretization into Token Sequences
The tokenizer uses balanced quantization to map continuous features to discrete tokens, creating a fixed-stride grammar. This allows the Transformer to focus attention within discrete regimes, analogous to tree splits. The core assumption is that tabular data naturally partitions into piecewise constant regimes; representing them as tokens respects these boundaries instead of smoothing across them. Evidence includes the abstract’s claim that the simplistic discretized vocabulary already outperforms tuned gradient boosting, and Section 3.2 describing the balanced binning. A break condition: if the underlying relationship is highly continuous without clear regime changes, discretization may introduce artificial boundaries and degrade performance.

### Mechanism 2: Adaptive Gaussian Smoothing of Targets
Instead of one-hot labels, the true continuous pace value is smoothed by integrating a Gaussian over its target bin. The Gaussian width σᵢ adapts to bin width wᵢ via σᵢ = √(σ²_floor + (k·wᵢ)²), ensuring wide bins receive proportionally more mass. This yields well-calibrated probability density functions while preserving ordinal structure. Evidence is provided in the abstract and Section 3.4. A break condition: extreme bin width heterogeneity could yield inappropriate σ values, and heavy-tailed target distributions may not be well captured by a Gaussian kernel.

### Mechanism 3: Time‑Delta Tokens and Causal Ordering
Each event block includes tokens for “weeks since last race” and “weeks to target.” The decoder‑only Transformer uses causal masking, ensuring predictions depend only on prior events. This explicit encoding of cadence helps the model learn progression patterns. The core assumption is that temporal distances carry predictive signal and that discrete tokenization of time deltas is effective. Evidence: ablations show ~1.8% MAE increase when time tokens are removed (Section 4.4, Table 3). Break condition: extremely irregular time gaps or histories exceeding 30 events may not be adequately captured.

## Foundational Learning
**Balanced Quantile Binning**  
- *Why needed*: Creates discrete tokens with uniform representation density, preventing empty/overcrowded bins and aligning with tree-like regime splits.  
- *Quick check*: What effect would uniform-width binning have on a right‑skewed feature like temperature?

**Gaussian Kernel Smoothing for Probabilistic Targets**  
- *Why needed*: Produces soft target distributions that preserve ordinal relationships and enable calibrated PDFs; essential for training the softmax output.  
- *Quick check*: Why might a fixed σ cause overconfidence on bins that are very wide?

**Causal Masking in Decoder‑Only Transformers**  
- *Why needed*: Guarantees no leakage from future events during training and inference, respecting the temporal nature of the forecasting task.  
- *Quick check*: What would happen if we replaced the causal Transformer with a bidirectional one?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input tokenizer → Token embedder (embedding table + sinusoidal positional encodings) → Causal Transformer stack (6 layers, 8 heads, 512‑dim, dropout 0.12) → Output head (linear layer to pace‑bin logits) → Loss module (adaptive Gaussian‑smoothed target + cross‑entropy). Training uses AdamW (lr 1e‑4, weight decay 0.00118) with per‑GPU batch size 64.
- **Critical path**: For a runner’s career, tokenize each event into the stride grammar `[features][pace][d_next][d_fin]`, pad/truncate to ≤30 events (≤327 tokens). Apply embedding and positional encoding, then feed through the causal Transformer. Extract logits at the final pace token position, compute cross‑entropy loss against the smoothed target, backpropagate.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Discretization granularity*: More bins improve resolution but increase sparsity and vocabulary; paper uses ~270 bins via quantile balancing.
  - *Smoothing*: Fixed σ is simpler but may miscalibrate on wide bins; adaptive σ introduces hyperparameters (σ_floor, k) but yields better KS.
  - *Sequence length*: Truncating at 30 events limits memory but discards longer histories; could cache older stats or use hierarchical approaches.
  - *Grammar redundancy*: Repeating static demographics each event simplifies batching but wastes tokens; a compressed grammar mixing static/dynamic tokens could be more efficient.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - High KS (>0.01) on validation indicates miscalibration; inspect Q‑Q plots.
  - MAE spikes on long week gaps or long histories (per Figure 1).
  - Ablation insensitivity: if removing time tokens or shuffling does not degrade performance, implementation may be flawed.
  - Extreme prediction concentration (all mass on first/last bin) suggests σ too small or training instability.
  - Slow convergence or oscillating loss may indicate inappropriate σ values or poor binning.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. *Time‑token ablation*: Retrain without the two cadence tokens; verify median MAE increases by ~1.8% and wall‑clock time rises.
  2. *Sequence‑order ablation*: Shuffle event blocks randomly; expect median MAE increase of ~2.0% and worse performance on longer careers.
  3. *Calibration sweep*: Train with various σ settings (fixed and adaptive); compute KS on validation. Adaptive σ should achieve KS near 0.0045 with slight MAE trade‑off vs. MAE‑optimized checkpoint.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does progressively sharpening Gaussian targets during training (sigma‑reduction schedule) improve convergence speed or final calibration compared to fixed sigma?**  
   *Basis*: Section 5 explicitly proposes exploring a sigma‑reduction schedule as future work. *Why unresolved*: The experiment has not been implemented. *What evidence would resolve*: Controlled training runs with sigma annealing (e.g., linear decay from high to low) versus fixed sigma, measuring wall‑clock time to reach target MAE and final KS/median MAE.

2. **Does learnable soft tokenization for inputs (end‑to‑end adaptive binning) improve predictive accuracy or calibration over fixed quantile binning?**  
   *Basis*: Section 7.1 suggests applying adaptive Gaussian weighting to input tokenization to enable learning optimal bin boundaries. *Why unresolved*: Input discretization remains fixed; symmetric extension not built. *What evidence would resolve*: Implement Gaussian‑weighted embeddings with learnable bin centers and adaptive sigma, compare MAE, KS, and training stability against current quantile binning.

3. **Does RunTime’s performance advantage over gradient boosting generalize to other tabular/ordinal regression domains beyond the running dataset (e.g., MIMIC‑IV)?**  
   *Basis*: Section 6 limitation: “all results so far derive from the running dataset—external validation (e.g., MIMIC‑IV) remains future work.” *Why unresolved*: Evaluation only on NYRR running data; cross‑dataset generalization unknown. *What evidence would resolve*: Replicate entity‑disjoint evaluation on MIMIC‑IV or similar benchmarks, comparing median MAE and KS against tuned XGBoost.

4. **Why does residual miscalibration (systematic decile‑specific drift) persist for slower runners despite adaptive smoothing?**  
   *Basis*: Inferred from Figure 4 showing “residual drift for slower runners”; the paper identifies the pattern but does not explain its cause. *Why unresolved*: Stratified calibration analysis reveals phenomenon without investigating underlying mechanisms (e.g., feature interactions, data sparsity). *What evidence would resolve*: Error analysis decomposing errors by features (environment, history length) for low vs. high percentiles; attention pattern inspection for slow‑runner cases; possibly targeted loss weighting or data augmentation.

5. **Can an auxiliary point‑wise loss (e.g., MAE on expected pace) be combined with Gaussian‑smoothed cross‑entropy without collapsing the predicted distribution to extreme bins?**  
   *Basis*: Appendix A.6 describes failure: adding Huber loss caused “the model to route almost all probability mass to the extreme pace bins… losing the predictive PDF entirely.” *Why unresolved*: Naive multi‑objective approach fails; unclear whether architectural separation (two heads) or training tricks can preserve distributional fidelity while improving point accuracy. *What evidence would resolve*: Test architectures with a shared encoder but separate distribution and regression heads, or use regularization that encourages mass spread; evaluate both MAE (point) and KS (calibration) to ensure no collapse.

## Limitations
- **Generalization scope**: The 10.8% MAE improvement and KS=0.0045 are shown only on a single proprietary running dataset; it is unknown if the method’s dominance extends to other tabular domains (e.g., retail, healthcare).
- **Discretization granularity hyperparameter**: The paper uses a fixed vocabulary size (~270 bins) from quantile binning; the sensitivity of results to the number of bins (resolution vs. sparsity trade‑off) is not explored.
- **Adaptive smoothing theoretical guarantee**: The adaptive σ formula (σᵢ = √(σ²_floor + (k·wᵢ)²)) is empirically motivated without formal proof that it ensures consistent probability mass across heterogeneous bin widths or guarantees optimal calibration.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑Domain Generalization Test**: Re‑train and evaluate the method on at least two standard public tabular regression datasets (e.g., UCI “Beijing PM2.5”, “Naval Propulsion”) and compare median MAE / calibration (KS/CRPS) against tuned XGBoost. Consistent relative performance (within 5%) would confirm generalizability.
2. **Binning Granularity Sensitivity Sweep**: Systematically vary the number of quantile bins (e.g., {100, 270, 500, 1000}) for all continuous features and the target, re‑train models, and plot MAE and KS vs. vocabulary size. Identify the “knee” point where performance plateaus or degrades due to sparsity.
3. **Adaptive vs. Fixed Smoothing Calibration Analysis**: Train identical models with (A) fixed σ (optimized on validation), (B) adaptive σ as proposed, and (C) no smoothing (one‑hot). On a held‑out test set, generate predicted PDFs for groups of target bins of varying widths (e.g., quintiles by bin width). Plot reliability diagrams and compute KS per bin‑width group. Adaptive smoothing should yield consistent calibration (flat reliability curve, KS~0) across all width groups, while fixed σ should show miscalibration on the widest bins.
