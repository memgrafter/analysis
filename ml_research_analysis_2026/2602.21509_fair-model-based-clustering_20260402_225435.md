# Fair Model-based Clustering

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21509
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21509
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: FMC introduces a scalable fair clustering method via finite mixture models, achieving state-of-the-art cost‑fairness trade‑offs on large datasets (e.g., 2.4M Census) with ~20× speedup and native categorical support.

## Executive Summary
Fair clustering aims to group data points while satisfying group-level fairness constraints. Existing approaches often scale poorly with dataset size or require metric spaces, limiting their applicability. This paper presents Fair Model-based Clustering (FMC), a novel framework based on finite mixture models. FMC decouples the number of parameters from the dataset size, enabling efficient optimization even on millions of points. It maximizes the likelihood under a fairness gap constraint Δ using either gradient descent (FMC‑GD) or expectation‑maximization (FMC‑EM). The method supports mini‑batch training via a subsampled Δ estimate, with theoretical guarantees that a small fraction (≥5%) of the data suffices.

FMC natively handles categorical variables through appropriate mixture components and, when jointly modeling continuous and categorical features, attains higher label prediction accuracy (0.706) than alternatives (≤0.627). Empirical evaluation on the Adult, Bank, Credit, and a large Census dataset (2.4 M samples) shows that FMC‑EM matches or outperforms state-of-the-art fair clustering methods (SFC, VFC) in cost–fairness trade‑offs while being approximately 20 times faster on the Census dataset. Sub‑sampling with at least 5% of data yields performance comparable to full‑batch training.

## Method Summary
FMC formulates clustering as a finite mixture model with latent cluster assignments. The model parameters (e.g., component means, covariances, mixing proportions) are independent of the number of data points, allowing the parameter count to remain fixed even for massive datasets. Soft assignments (responsibilities) are computed in the E‑step for EM or via a softmax for gradient descent. The fairness constraint Δ bounds the discrepancy in assignment probabilities across predefined groups, mirroring group fairness notions. Optimization proceeds by either EM, where the M‑step solves a constrained maximum likelihood problem (e.g., via Lagrangian or projection), or by gradient descent on the likelihood with a penalty term for Δ. For scalability, mini‑batch training is supported: in each iteration a random subset of data is used to estimate both the likelihood gradient and Δ; the paper provides a theoretical bound that ensures the subsampled Δ is an accurate proxy when the batch size is at least 5% of the dataset. Categorical features are incorporated directly using multinomial components, avoiding high‑dimensional one‑hot encodings. The joint modeling of mixed data types further improves predictive accuracy.

## Key Results
- On Adult, Bank, Credit, and Census (2.4 M), FMC‑EM achieves cost–fairness trade‑offs on par with or better than SFC and VFC, the two main fair clustering baselines.
- FMC‑EM runs approximately 20 times faster than SFC/VFC on the Census dataset, demonstrating superior scalability.
- Mini‑batch training with subsampling rates as low as 5% yields nearly identical performance to full‑batch training; joint continuous‑categorical modeling yields label prediction accuracy of 0.706, surpassing ≤0.627 for other encodings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
FMC’s scalability stems from the finite mixture model’s parameter independence from the number of samples: the number of mixture components and their covariances does not grow with n. Soft assignments enable differentiation and gradient‑based optimization, while the EM algorithm provides a principled approach for maximum likelihood estimation with latent variables. The fairness gap Δ is incorporated during parameter updates, either via constrained M‑step or a penalty term, ensuring that the learned clusters respect group balance. Mini‑batch training is effective because the subsampled Δ is an unbiased estimator with low variance when the batch size is sufficiently large; the paper’s theory guarantees that a 5% batch is enough to preserve the optimization trajectory. Native categorical handling through multinomial components avoids the curse of dimensionality from one‑hot encoding and captures correlations between categories within the generative model, leading to better representation and higher downstream label prediction accuracy.

   - *Quick check*: Derive the EM update equations for a Gaussian mixture with equal spherical covariances.

2. **Expectation‑Maximization (EM)**
   - *Why needed*: To perform maximum likelihood estimation when latent variables (cluster assignments) are unobserved.
   - *Quick check*: Describe the E‑step and M‑step for a mixture of Bernoullis.

3. **Gradient Descent for Constrained Optimization**
   - *Why needed*: FMC‑GD optimizes the likelihood under a fairness constraint using first‑order methods.
   - *Quick check*: How would you project gradient updates onto the feasible set defined by Δ ≤ δ?

4. **Fairness Gap (Δ)**
   - *Why needed*: Quantifies disparity in cluster assignments across protected groups; the optimization ensures this disparity stays below a threshold.
   - *Quick check*: Given two groups’ average assignment probabilities to a cluster, compute Δ as the absolute difference.

5. **Mini‑Batch Training**
   - *Why needed*: To scale to datasets too large to fit in memory or process in one pass.
   - *Quick check*: What are the trade‑offs between batch size, gradient variance, and convergence speed?

6. **Categorical Data in Mixture Models**
   - *Why needed*: Many real‑world datasets contain non‑numeric attributes; unlike one‑hot, a multinomial component naturally models categorical distributions.
   - *Quick check*: Write the log‑likelihood contribution of a categorical feature under a mixture component.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Raw Data -> Preprocessing -> Parameter Initialization -> (E‑step -> M‑step/Update under Δ constraint -> Δ evaluation)* -> Convergence Check -> Cluster Assignments
- **Critical path**: The critical path per iteration consists of the E‑step (computing responsibilities for all data points) followed by the M‑step (updating model parameters under the fairness constraint). The M‑step is typically the most computationally demanding, especially when many mixture components or high‑dimensional features are present.
- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - *Scalability vs. model expressiveness*: Finite mixtures keep parameter count fixed, enabling large‑scale learning, but may be less flexible than non‑parametric methods that grow with data.
  - *Mini‑batch vs. full‑batch*: Mini‑batches reduce memory and time per iteration but introduce stochasticity in Δ estimates; the 5% rule mitigates this at the cost of some variance.
  - *Native categorical handling*: Avoids high‑dimensional one‑hot encodings and captures feature correlations, but requires appropriate component distributions and may need regularization to prevent overfitting.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Likelihood decreases or becomes NaN (numerical instability or inappropriate step size).
  - Fairness gap Δ does not improve or oscillates (constraint too tight, learning rate too high, or batch size too small).
  - Clustering quality (e.g., NMI) remains low despite convergence (model misspecification or lack of predictive features).
  - Training diverges or stalls when batch size is below the 5% threshold; variance in Δ estimates causes unstable updates.
- **First experiments**:
  1. **Sanity check**: Run FMC‑EM on a synthetic Gaussian mixture with 3 components and binary group labels; verify that the learned Δ matches the constraint and that cluster assignments recover the ground truth (high NMI).
  2. **Scalability benchmark**: Compare wall‑clock time and peak memory of FMC‑EM, SFC, and VFC on the Adult dataset for k=10, varying the number of components, and plot time vs. number of samples (subsample Adult to 10k, 20k, 48k).
  3. **Mini‑batch sensitivity**: On the 2.4 M Census dataset, train FMC‑EM with batch sizes {1%, 5%, 10%, 100%} and measure final Δ, NMI, and total runtime; confirm that ≥5% yields negligible degradation relative to full batch.

## Limitations
- The fairness gap Δ is a technical proxy and may not align with social fairness definitions used in practice (e.g., statistical parity, equalized odds).
- Baseline comparison includes only SFC and VFC; other recent fair clustering algorithms (e.g., Fair Bayesian Model‑Based Clustering) are omitted, limiting the completeness of the empirical evaluation.
- Theoretical guarantees for mini‑batch subsampling with <5% data lack empirical validation on highly imbalanced or multimodal distributions, where variance in Δ could be higher.

## Confidence
- **Scalability & speed (20× on 2.4 M)**: High – supported by large‑scale experiments and mini‑batch theory.
- **Cost–fairness trade‑off parity with SFC/VFC**: Medium – tables show comparable trade‑offs but no statistical significance tests.
- **Higher label accuracy with joint continuous‑categorical modeling (0.706 vs ≤0.627)**: Low – methodology details sparse and no ablation on categorical encodings.
