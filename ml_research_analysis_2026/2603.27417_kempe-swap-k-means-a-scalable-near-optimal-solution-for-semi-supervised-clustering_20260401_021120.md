# Kempe Swap K-Means: A Scalable Near-Optimal Solution for Semi-Supervised Clustering

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.27417
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27417
- Reference count: 6
- Primary result: Kempe Swap K-Means (KSKM) achieves near-optimal clustering inertias (within ~1.00× of optimal) while being 10–1000× faster than integer programming, with high feasibility success on 70 datasets up to 70,000 points.

## Executive Summary
Constrained clustering with must-link and cannot-link constraints faces trade-offs between solution quality, feasibility, and computational efficiency. The Kempe Swap K-Means (KSKM) algorithm introduces a dual-phase iterative process: an assignment step using Kempe chain swaps to explore large, feasible neighborhoods while maintaining constraints, and a centroid update step with optimal centroids and controlled perturbations to escape local optima. Empirical evaluations on 70 datasets, including large-scale instances up to 70,000 points, demonstrate that KSKM achieves clustering inertias near the theoretical optimum (multiples close to 1.00), often matching state-of-the-art methods while reducing runtime by factors of 10 to over 1000 compared to integer programming approaches. It maintains a near-100% success rate in most scenarios, significantly outperforming COP-K-Means, PCCC, and BLPKMCC in both accuracy and scalability, particularly for dense constraint graphs.

## Method Summary
KSKM leverages the equivalence between feasible constrained assignments and proper graph colorings of the cannot-link super-node graph. It starts with a DSATUR-inspired greedy initialization that orders vertices by saturation degree and assigns each to the nearest feasible centroid. The core iterative loop alternates between two phases: (1) Assignment refinement via Kempe chain swaps, where all two-color Kempe chains with negative swap cost are identified, and a maximum-weight independent set of non-overlapping chains is selected to swap colors simultaneously, thus making large moves without violating constraints; (2) Centroid update to the means of assigned super-nodes. If no improving swaps exist, centroids are perturbed using a multivariate t-distribution scaled by cluster size and covariance, which minimally increases the objective but can unlock new swaps. As a last resort, an exact graph coloring refinement (optional) solves the fixed-centroid assignment optimally. This design enables KSKM to balance scalability, feasibility, and solution quality.

## Key Results
- On 70 datasets (up to 70k points), KSKM achieves inertias near-optimal (multiples close to 1.00), often matching PCCC.
- Reduces runtime by 10–1000× compared to integer programming approaches.
- Maintains a ~100% success rate in producing feasible solutions, significantly outperforming COP-K-Means, PCCC, and BLPKMCC in accuracy and scalability, especially for dense constraint graphs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Kempe Chain Swaps
- **Claim:** Kempe chain swaps enable systematic exploration of large, feasible neighborhoods in the constrained solution space without violating must-link and cannot-link constraints.
- **Mechanism:** A Kempe chain is a maximal connected subgraph induced by two color classes (clusters). Swapping the colors of all vertices in the chain preserves feasibility because edges within the chain originally connect opposite-colored vertices and remain so after swapping, and edges to external vertices see the same color pairs. The algorithm identifies all improving chains (negative swap cost) and selects a compatible subset via a maximum-weight independent set (MWIS) formulation to apply simultaneously.
- **Core assumption:** Feasible cluster assignments correspond to valid graph colorings of the cannot-link super-node graph, and Kempe moves can transition between colorings while maintaining feasibility, especially when the number of clusters `k` is sufficiently larger than the graph's chromatic number.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "dual-phase iterative process: an assignment step using Kempe chain swaps to refine current clustering in the constrained solution space and a centroid update step"
  - [section 2.1.2] "A Kempe chain interchange is a two-color local search move that can recolor a set of vertices in one step without ever breaking the feasibility."
  - [corpus] Weak direct corpus support; neighboring papers focus on other k-means variants (e.g., Multi-Swap k-Means++) but not on constraint handling via Kempe chains. The mechanism draws from graph coloring heuristics (e.g., Chams et al., 1987) as cited.
- **Break condition:** When the constraint graph is dense and `k` is close to the chromatic number, Kempe chains become large and global, causing the method to lose fine-grained control and potentially plateau (section 3.5).

### Mechanism 2: Controlled Centroid Perturbations
- **Claim:** Controlled centroid perturbations enable escape from Kempe-swap-local optima by slightly adjusting cluster centers, which can unlock new improving Kempe swaps with minimal objective degradation.
- **Mechanism:** Upon reaching a Kempe-swap-optimal state, centroids are perturbed according to a multivariate t-distribution scaled by cluster size and covariance (equation 3). This samples plausible new centroids that are near the current ones but not identical. The cluster assignment is then re-optimized via Kempe swaps, potentially reducing the objective further.
- **Core assumption:** Local optima under fixed centroids are sensitive to centroid locations; small, statistically plausible perturbations often incur negligible objective increase but can change the feasibility landscape beneficially.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "a centroid update step with optimal centroids and controlled perturbations to escape local optima"
  - [section 2.2.2] "small perturbations may not worsen the objective significantly, but can unlock new improving moves."
  - [section 3.4] Empirical comparison shows clustering inertia deteriorates without perturbation, confirming its role in refinement.
- **Break condition:** If repeated perturbations fail to yield improvement after a set number of attempts (`L=200`), the algorithm triggers centroid repositioning for larger jumps.

### Mechanism 3: DSATUR-Inspired Initialization
- **Claim:** DSATUR-inspired initial assignment increases the probability of obtaining a feasible starting solution and a high-quality local optimum by prioritizing highly constrained super-nodes.
- **Mechanism:** Vertices (super-nodes) are processed in decreasing order of saturation degree (number of distinct adjacent cluster colors). Each vertex is assigned to its nearest feasible centroid (respecting cannot-link constraints), rather than the first available smallest-index color. This early handling of constrained points reduces later infeasibility risk.
- **Core assumption:** Processing order critically affects feasibility in greedy assignment; vertices with higher saturation have fewer feasible choices later, so assigning them first while many options remain increases success chances.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2.3] "Prioritizing vertices with high saturation ensures that highly constrained points are settled early, when many feasible clusters remain available. This DSATUR-style ordering significantly reduces the likelihood that a late-stage vertex has no admissible cluster."
  - [section 3.3] DSATUR-based initialization paired with Kempe swaps yields near-optimal solutions faster than methods without such ordering.
  - [corpus] No strong direct support; corpus contains scalable k-means variants but not DSATUR for constrained clustering.
- **Break condition:** If the proposed DSATUR-nearest-centroid assignment fails to find a feasible `k`-coloring (detected when a vertex has no feasible centroid), the algorithm falls back to classic DSATUR coloring or an exact GCP-based assignment (lines 10–12, Algorithm 5).

## Foundational Learning
### Must-link/Cannot-link constraints
- **Why needed:** These are the primary supervision signals. Must-link enforces same-cluster assignment; cannot-link enforces different clusters. The algorithm must maintain feasibility with respect to both throughout.
- **Quick check:** If points A and B are must-linked, and B and C are must-linked, what must be true about A and C? (Answer: They are transitively must-linked and belong to the same super-node after preprocessing.)

### Graph coloring problem
- **Why needed:** With must-links collapsed into super-nodes and cannot-links as edges, finding a feasible constrained clustering is equivalent to coloring this graph with `k` colors. Feasibility is a legal `k`-coloring.
- **Quick check:** In a graph coloring, what does a "Kempe chain" represent? (Answer: A maximal connected subgraph using only two colors, whose colors can be swapped while preserving legality.)

### Kempe chain exchange
- **Why needed:** This is the core assignment move. It allows flipping many vertices at once in a structured way that preserves coloring feasibility, enabling larger neighborhood exploration than single-point moves.
- **Quick check:** Why does swapping colors along a Kempe chain never create a cannot-link conflict? (Answer: Edges within the chain connect opposite colors before and after swap; edges to outside vertices see the same endpoint colors.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Preprocessing → DSATUR Initialization → (Assignment → Centroid Update)* → (if stuck: Perturb/Shift → Assignment)* → (optional: ExactAssignment → Assignment → Centroid Update)* → Termination

**Critical path:** The algorithm begins with DSATUR Initialization to obtain a feasible starting assignment. It then iterates Assignment (finding and applying Kempe swaps) and Centroid Update until no improving swaps are found. If convergence stalls, it applies a centroid perturbation (KSPerturb) or repositioning (KSShift) and resumes Assignment. For particularly difficult local optima, it may invoke an exact graph coloring refinement (ExactAssignment). This loop continues until no further improvements occur.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Kempe swaps vs. GCP:* Swaps are faster (MWIS vs. full coloring) but may miss improvements requiring non-Kempe-equivalent moves. GCP is exact but expensive; used sparingly for escape.
- *Perturbation vs. Reposition:* Perturbation is cheap, local, and safe (small changes). Repositioning is bolder, potentially crossing basins, but requires centroid matching and may degrade objective more.
- *DSATUR order vs. classic DSATUR:* Nearest-centroid assignment aims for lower initial inertia but may fail more often on hard graphs; classic DSATUR is a reliable fallback for feasibility.

**Failure signatures:**
- Infeasible start: DSATURAssignment fails and fallback also fails → algorithm terminates without solution.
- Global Kempe chains: In dense graphs with `k` ≈ chromatic number, swaps are large and ineffective → high final inertia, slow progress.
- GCP memory/time: KSKM_E may run out of memory on large, dense constraint graphs (e.g., CIFAR-100 at 5% constraints).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Synthetic feasibility test:** Run DSATURAssignment on a tiny synthetic dataset (e.g., 10 points, known constraints) with `k=3`. Confirm the output is a valid coloring and that one Kempe swap iteration preserves feasibility.
2. **Kempe swap impact:** Disable all perturbations and GCP (`KSKM` mode). Compare inertia and runtime against a COP-K-Means variant with DSATUR ordering but no swaps. Expect similar inertia initially but Kempe swaps should improve inertia further with moderate runtime increase.
3. **Perturbation effectiveness:** On a dataset where `KSKM` converges quickly, run with perturbations disabled (`KSKM_repo`). Measure how often perturbations (when enabled) lead to a lower final inertia after subsequent swaps. Expect measurable improvement, especially on collection 2 (dense constraints).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the Kempe swap assignment be adapted for dense constraint graphs and when the number of clusters is close to the chromatic number, where current swaps become global and less effective?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 3.5 identifies that in dense graphs with *k* near chromatic number, Kempe chains become large and global, causing the algorithm to explore only a tiny part of the solution space and potentially trap in large attraction basins.
- **Why unresolved:** The proposed solution is to use KSKM_E with GCP refinement, but this is computationally expensive. A more efficient mechanism is needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Designing a new swap strategy that enables fine-grained moves in dense graphs, or a dynamic algorithm that switches between Kempe swaps and exact coloring based on graph properties, with empirical validation on challenging dense instances.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the DSATUR-based initialization be modified to guarantee feasibility (when a feasible *k*-coloring exists) while still producing a low-inertia starting solution?
- **Basis in paper:** Page 18 notes that the nearest-centroid assignment in DSATUR order can cause infeasibility even when classic DSATUR would succeed, due to increased saturation degrees.
- **Why unresolved:** The fallback to classic DSATUR may yield a feasible solution but with higher inertia. There is a trade-off between feasibility and initial quality.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An initialization algorithm that balances feasibility and inertia, perhaps by solving a relaxed problem or using limited backtracking, tested on graphs where classic DSATUR finds a coloring but the proposed method fails.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is the heuristic centroid perturbation from a *t*-distribution the most effective way to escape local minima, or can we design a perturbation strategy that adapts to the local landscape of the objective function?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 2.2.2 uses a *t*-distribution based on cluster covariance to perturb centroids, but this is a heuristic without theoretical justification for optimality in escaping local minima.
- **Why unresolved:** The effectiveness is empirical; a more principled approach might lead to fewer iterations or better solutions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis of the perturbation's effect on the inertia landscape, or empirical comparison with alternative perturbations (e.g., gradient-based, adaptive step sizes) on a diverse set of instances.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can KSKM be extended to handle soft must-link and cannot-link constraints, where violations are permitted with penalties, to accommodate noisy or uncertain prior knowledge?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper exclusively addresses hard constraints, assuming rigid must-link and cannot-link. Real-world applications often have soft constraints.
- **Why unresolved:** Soft constraints are common in practice, but the current algorithm cannot handle them without modification.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing a soft-constraint variant of KSKM, e.g., by incorporating constraint violation penalties into the objective and adjusting the Kempe swap and centroid update steps accordingly, with empirical evaluation on datasets with soft constraints.

## Limitations
- **Dense constraint bottleneck:** When the constraint graph is dense and `k` approaches the chromatic number, Kempe chains become large global moves, reducing effectiveness and potentially causing plateaus.
- **Perturbation parameters heuristic:** The scale of the t-distribution perturbations and the retry limit `L=200` are fixed; their optimality across datasets is unknown.
- **Feasibility initialization risk:** DSATUR-based assignment may fail on hard graphs, forcing fallback to exact GCP which can be memory-intensive on large dense graphs.
