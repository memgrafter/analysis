# Compressed Sensing for Capability Localization in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID:** 2603.03335
* **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03335
* **Reference count:** 35
* **Primary result:** Compressed sensing identifies task-specific attention heads with up to 50× fewer evaluations; ablating five such heads degrades task performance by up to 65% while preserving general language ability.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how specific capabilities (e.g., math, coding, swearing, rhyming) are localized within the attention heads of large language models (LLMs). It introduces a novel method that leverages compressed sensing to efficiently identify attention heads critical to a given task. By treating each head's contribution as a sparse signal, the approach recovers important heads from a small number of random ablation experiments, drastically reducing the number of required evaluations compared to exhaustive or greedy baselines. Experiments on Llama and Qwen models (1B–8B parameters) demonstrate that ablating just five heads selected by this method can cause up to 65% performance drop on the target task while largely retaining general language abilities. Additional findings include the presence of universal heads that influence many tasks and scale-dependent localization patterns.

## Method Summary
The core idea is to model the contribution of each attention head to a task's performance as a sparse vector. For a model with N heads, the importance vector s ∈ ℝ^N is assumed to have only k non-zero entries (k ≪ N). To recover s, the method randomly ablates small subsets of heads (measurements) and records the resulting performance drop. These measurements form a linear system y = Φs, where Φ encodes which heads were ablated. Since s is sparse, compressed sensing techniques (e.g., ℓ1 minimization) can accurately recover s from far fewer measurements than N. The recovered non-zero entries indicate the heads most crucial for the task. This reduces the evaluation cost from O(N) to O(k log(N/k)).

## Key Results
- Ablating only five attention heads identified by compressed sensing degrades task performance by up to 65% while preserving general language ability.
- The method achieves up to a 50× reduction in the number of evaluations compared to greedy selection baselines.
- Experiments reveal universal heads that affect multiple tasks and show localization patterns that vary with model scale.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Compressed sensing theory guarantees that a sparse signal can be exactly (or approximately) recovered from underdetermined linear measurements, provided the measurement matrix satisfies certain properties (e.g., restricted isometry property). In this setting, the measurement corresponds to ablating a random subset of heads and observing performance loss. If the true head importance vector is indeed sparse, a small number of such measurements (on the order of k log(N/k)) suffice to reconstruct it via convex optimization (ℓ1 minimization) or greedy algorithms. The sparsity assumption is plausible because only a limited number of heads are expected to be specialized for any given capability. The efficacy of the method therefore hinges on the validity of the sparsity prior and the randomness of the ablation subsets.

## Foundational Learning

  *Why needed:* Provides the theoretical foundation for recovering a sparse signal from few linear measurements.  
  *Quick check:* Is the measurement matrix (ablation pattern) incoherent with the sparsity basis? In practice, random binary matrices work.

- **Sparsity**:  
  *Why needed:* The algorithm relies on the importance vector being sparse (few heads matter per task).  
  *Quick check:* Does the ℓ0 norm of the importance vector remain small across tasks? (Empirical validation needed.)

- **Attention Heads in Transformers**:  
  *Why needed:* The units being analyzed; each head may capture different linguistic or task-specific features.  
  *Quick check:* Are heads individually deployable (can we ablate one without affecting others)? Yes, by masking outputs.

- **Ablation Studies**:  
  *Why needed:* Used to measure the causal effect of removing heads on task performance.  
  *Quick check:* Does ablating a head isolate its contribution? Potential interference between heads must be considered.

- **Large Language Models (LLMs)**:  
  *Why needed:* The target models; their scale and architecture make exhaustive evaluation infeasible.  
  *Quick check:* Does the model have a modular structure (heads) amenable to targeted modification? Transformers do.

- **Sparse Recovery Algorithms**:  
  *Why needed:* Practical solvers (e.g., LASSO, basis pursuit) that implement compressed sensing in high dimensions.  
  *Quick check:* Do chosen algorithms scale to O(N) heads and produce stable solutions?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (standard transformer encoder/decoder):  
Input Tokens → Token Embedding → Positional Encoding → [Transformer Layers: (Multi-Head Attention → LayerNorm → Feed-Forward Network → LayerNorm)] × L → Final LayerNorm → Output Logits

**Critical path** for capability localization:  
1. Define the target task and evaluation metric.  
2. Baseline evaluation of the unmodified model.  
3. Generate random binary ablation masks each specifying a small subset of attention heads to disable.  
4. For each mask, run the model on the task, compute performance drop.  
5. Assemble measurement matrix Φ (masks) and observation vector y (performance drops).  
6. Solve sparse recovery to obtain estimated importance vector s.  
7. Select top-k heads with largest coefficients in s for targeted ablation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Number of measurements vs. recovery accuracy: fewer measurements lower cost but increase noise sensitivity.  
- Sparsity level k: assumed unknown; over/under-estimation affects results.  
- Ablation granularity: head-wise vs. layer-wise; head-wise gives finer localization but more heads.  
- Randomness vs. structured masks: random masks ensure theoretical guarantees but may waste measurements if some heads are always relevant.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High variance in performance drop for similar masks indicates unreliable measurements.  
- Recovered importance vector has many non-zeros (dense), suggesting sparsity assumption violated.  
- Ablating identified heads yields smaller-than-expected degradation, indicating poor recovery.  
- General benchmarks (e.g., MMLU) drop significantly after ablation, implying collateral damage.

**First experiments:**  
1. Measure empirical sparsity: compute the exact importance vector via brute-force for a small model and task; verify that few heads account for most of the performance.  
2. Benchmark compressed sensing against greedy forward selection and random sampling in terms of evaluations needed to achieve similar degradation.  
3. After identifying heads for a specific task, evaluate the ablated model on a comprehensive suite (e.g., MMLU, HellaSwag) to confirm preservation of other capabilities.

## Limitations
- Limited task and model diversity: Only four tasks and two model families (up to 8B parameters); broader generalizability remains untested.
- Sparsity assumption untested: The paper does not quantify the actual sparsity of head contributions; if contributions are dense, compressed sensing may fail.
- General ability preservation not rigorously validated: The claim relies on a narrow set of evaluations; comprehensive benchmarks are needed to ensure no collateral damage.

## Confidence
- **Computational efficiency claim** (O(N) → O(k log(N/k)) reduction, up to 50× fewer evaluations): High  
- **Effectiveness of identified heads** (up to 65% degradation with five heads): Medium (limited task/model scope)  
- **Findings on universal heads and scale patterns**: Low (insufficient architectural variety and model range)

## Next Checks
1. Scale and architecture test: Apply the method to larger models (≥13B) and diverse families (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) across ≥10 varied tasks (reasoning, multilingual, summarization).
2. Sparsity validation: Directly compute sparsity (ℓ0 norms) of head importance vectors for several tasks; compare compressed sensing recovery against dense regression methods to assess the sparsity premise.
3. General capability impact: Evaluate ablated models on standard comprehensive benchmarks (MMLU, BIG-Bench, HellaSwag) to verify that non-targeted abilities remain intact.
