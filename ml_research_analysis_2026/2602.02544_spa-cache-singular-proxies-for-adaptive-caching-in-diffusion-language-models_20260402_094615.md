# SPA-Cache: Singular Proxies for Adaptive Caching in Diffusion Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02544
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02544
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Achieves up to 8× throughput improvement over vanilla decoding and 2–4× speedup over prior caching baselines, with negligible accuracy drop; up to 28× total speedup when combined with parallel decoding.

## Executive Summary
Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) suffer from high inference latency because their non‑causal, arbitrary‑order decoding makes standard KV caching incompatible. SPA‑Cache addresses this by selectively updating hidden states via two innovations: a singular proxy that uses low‑dimensional projections to efficiently identify tokens requiring updates, and an adaptive layer‑wise budget allocation that assigns more updates to volatile middle layers while aggressively caching stable early and final layers. Evaluated on seven benchmarks with LLaDA‑8B and Dream‑7B, SPA‑Cache delivers substantial throughput gains (up to 8× over vanilla, 2–4× over prior methods) with negligible accuracy loss and further improvements when integrated with parallel decoding.

## Method Summary
SPA‑Cache operates per transformer layer. First, a singular proxy is constructed by projecting the input hidden state into the top‑\(r\) right singular subspace of the layer’s Value projection matrix (precomputed via truncated SVD). The cosine similarity between this proxy and the cached proxy identifies tokens with significant drift, avoiding the cost of full‑dimensional Value computation. Second, an adaptive update budget governs how many tokens are selected per layer: a piecewise Gaussian function \(\rho(l)\) gives higher update ratios to middle layers and lower ones to the extremes. For the selected token indices \(I\), the system recomputes Query/Key/Value, scatters them into the KV cache, runs sparse attention, applies the FFN, and scatters the updated outputs back into the cache. Non‑selected tokens reuse their cached states, reducing overall computation while preserving output quality.

## Key Results
- Up to **8×** throughput improvement over vanilla DLM decoding.
- **2–4×** speedup over prior caching baselines (dLLM‑Cache, Fast‑dLLM) with negligible accuracy drop.
- Combined with parallel decoding, up to **28×** total speedup.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A low‑dimensional singular proxy enables efficient identification of tokens requiring state updates.
- **Mechanism:** The Value projection matrix \(W\) is approximated via truncated SVD (\(W \approx U_r \Lambda_r V_r^\top\)). Input hidden states \(h_i\) are projected into the top‑\(r\) right singular subspace (\(\hat{v}_i = W_r h_i\)). Cosine similarity between \(\hat{v}_i\) and the cached proxy \(\hat{v}_i^c\) identifies tokens with significant drift, avoiding expensive full‑dimensional Value computations.
- **Core assumption:** The top‑\(r\) singular subspace captures the dominant directions of hidden state drift, i.e., \(h_i \in \text{span}(V_r)\).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3.3] Derives the projection \(f_{\text{proxy}}(h_i) = W_r h_i\) and Theorem 3.4 bounds the similarity divergence between full and proxy projections.  
  - [section 4.4] Table 5 shows comparable accuracy at \(r=128\) (vs. full \(d=4096\)), confirming the proxy's fidelity.  
  - [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., dKV‑Cache) use full‑dimensional heuristics; SPA‑Cache's singular projection is a novel efficiency dimension.
- **Break condition:** If hidden state drift predominantly lies outside the principal subspace (e.g., under high‑temperature sampling where noise dominates), the proxy may miss critical updates, causing accuracy drops (noted as a limitation).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Adaptive layer‑wise budget allocation reduces redundant computation by exploiting heterogeneous hidden state stability across Transformer layers.
- **Mechanism:** The update ratio \(\rho(l)\) varies per layer, following a piecewise Gaussian parameterization. More updates (higher \(\rho\)) are allocated to volatile middle layers, while stable early and final layers use fewer updates (\(\rho_1, \rho_L \ll \rho_p\)).
- **Core assumption:** The fraction of drifting tokens as a function of layer depth follows an asymmetric bell shape, which is consistent across models and tasks.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3.4] Figure 2 empirically measured drift distribution on LLaDA‑8B, showing high variance in middle layers vs. stability at extremes.  
  - [section 4.4] Table 4 demonstrates that dynamic allocation (\(\bar{\rho}=16\%\)) maintains accuracy compared to uniform \(16\%\) (which drops accuracy), confirming the layer‑wise heterogeneity.  
  - [corpus] Existing baselines (dLLM‑Cache, Fast‑dLLM) use uniform budgets; SPA‑Cache's adaptive scheme is a key differentiator.
- **Break condition:** If a model's layer dynamics are not bell‑shaped (e.g., uniform instability), the Gaussian parameterization would misallocate budgets. The paper shows consistency across LLaDA/Dream, but this may not hold for all architectures.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Value states serve as a theoretically justified proxy for final‑layer output drift, enabling conservative update guarantees.
- **Mechanism:** Theorem 3.1 bounds the attention output dissimilarity by the Value state dissimilarity, and Theorem 3.2 extends this bound through the FFN. Thus, monitoring Value similarity ensures output stability, making it sufficient to avoid full‑layer recomputation for drift detection.
- **Core assumption:** Bounded norms, stable attention weights, and a relaxed drift coupling condition (\(\sum_j \alpha_{ij} \|v_j^{t+1} - v_j^t\| \leq \lambda \|v_i^{t+1} - v_i^t\|\)).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3.2] Theorem 3.1 formally links Value drift to output drift; Table 1 empirically shows Value identifiers achieve the best accuracy among candidates.  
  - [section 4.4] Ablations in Table 4 using the singular proxy (based on Value) match full Value accuracy, validating the theoretical foundation.  
  - [corpus] dLLM‑Cache also uses Value but with high‑dimensional cost; SPA‑Cache provides the theoretical justification for its choice.
- **Break condition:** If the bounded norm or attention stability assumptions are violated (e.g., extreme generation settings), the theoretical guarantee may not hold, leading to uncaught drift and quality degradation.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
1. **Update Identifier (Phase 1):** Computes proxy vectors \(p_i = W_r h_i\) for all tokens, calculates similarity \(s_i\) vs. cached \(p_i^c\), selects top‑\(k\) indices \(I\).
2. **Partial KV Cache Update (Phase 2):** Computes \(Q_I, K_I, V_I\) from selected inputs, scatters \(K_I, V_I\) into cached \(K^c, V^c\), performs attention on the updated cache.
3. **FFN & Output Update (Phase 3):** Applies FFN to sparse attention output, scatters results into cached output \(H^c\).

### Critical path
Phase 1 → Phase 2 → Phase 3 per layer. The update indices \(I\) from Phase 1 gate all subsequent sparse computation. Cache states (\(K^c, V^c, H^c\)) persist across decoding steps.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Proxy rank \(r\):** Lower \(r\) reduces identification cost but risks missing drift (accuracy tradeoff; paper uses \(r=128\) for LLaDA).
- **Peak budget \(\rho_p\):** Controls total update density; too high wastes compute, too low harms quality. Tuned per model.
- **Gaussian parameters (\(l_p, \rho_1, \rho_L\)):** Must fit layer‑wise drift profile; paper fits empirically per model family.

### Failure signatures
- Accuracy drops when \(r\) is too low (<128 for LLaDA) or when using uniform budget instead of adaptive (Table 4).
- TTFT remains unchanged (caching only affects decoding, not prefilling; limitation in Section 5).
- Under high‑temperature sampling, drift increases and caching benefits diminish (mentioned in limitations).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline throughput measurement:** Run vanilla DLM decoding (no cache) to establish latency/throughput baseline on a target benchmark.
2. **Singular proxy validation:** Compare full‑dimensional Value identifier vs. singular proxy (\(r\) sweep) for accuracy and speed; expect ~8× throughput improvement with negligible accuracy loss (Table 5).
3. **Adaptive allocation test:** Compare dynamic \(\rho(l)\) vs. static uniform budget at equivalent average \(\bar{\rho}\); expect dynamic to preserve accuracy while static degrades (Table 4 ablation).

## Limitations
- Singular proxy robustness assumes drift lies in top‑\(r\) subspace; may fail under high‑temperature noise or novel architectures.
- Adaptive budget generality relies on Gaussian parameterization fitted on LLaDA/Dream; unverified on other DLMs or tasks.
- Theoretical guarantees depend on bounded norms and stable attention; violations in extreme settings could break update conservativeness.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Proxy efficiency):** High (empirically validated across models, Table 5).
- **Mechanism 2 (Adaptive allocation):** High (Table 4 ablation shows necessity).
- **Mechanism 3 (Value proxy justification):** Medium (theoretical bound holds under assumptions; limited stress‑testing).
