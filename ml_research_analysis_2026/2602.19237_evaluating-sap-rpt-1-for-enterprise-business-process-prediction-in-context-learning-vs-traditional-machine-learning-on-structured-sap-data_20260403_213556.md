# Evaluating SAP RPT-1 for Enterprise Business Process Prediction: In-Context Learning vs. Traditional Machine Learning on Structured SAP Data

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.19237
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19237
- **Reference count:** 38
- **Primary result:** RPT-1-OSS achieves 91–96% of tuned GBDT accuracy without any training; outperforms XGBoost when provided with fewer than 75–100 context rows; regression gap (8.9–11.1 percentage points R²) larger than classification (3.6–4.1 percentage points AUC‑ROC).

## Executive Summary
This study independently evaluates SAP’s tabular foundation model RPT-1 for enterprise business process prediction using synthetic data from three SAP scenarios: demand forecasting (regression), predictive data integrity (binary classification), and financial risk prediction (multi‑class classification). The open‑source variant RPT‑1‑OSS is benchmarked against tuned gradient‑boosted decision trees (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) via five‑fold cross‑validation. Results show RPT‑1‑OSS achieves 91–96% of the accuracy of tuned GBDTs without any task‑specific training. Critically, RPT‑1 outperforms XGBoost when given fewer than 75–100 context rows, demonstrating strong utility for data‑scarce prototyping. The authors propose a hybrid workflow: use RPT‑1 for rapid screening and GBDT for final high‑accuracy deployment.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses three synthetic datasets (2,500–3,200 rows, 12–18 features) mimicking SAP SD/MM/FI module tables. For each dataset, five‑fold cross‑validation is performed. Gradient‑boosted decision tree baselines (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) are tuned via Bayesian optimization (50 trials per fold). The frozen pretrained RPT‑1‑OSS model is used in inference‑only mode: the training fold serves as context (up to 2,048 rows, bagging=4), and predictions are made on the test fold without weight updates. Primary metrics are AUC‑ROC for classification and R² for regression; secondary metrics include F1, precision, recall, RMSE, and MAE. The hybrid workflow is derived from observed accuracy‑latency trade‑offs.

## Key Results
- RPT‑1‑OSS achieves 91–96% of tuned GBDT accuracy without any training.
- Outperforms XGBoost when provided with fewer than 75–100 context rows.
- Classification gap: 3.6–4.1 percentage points (AUC‑ROC); regression gap: 8.9–11.1 percentage points (R²).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** RPT‑1 achieves competitive accuracy without task‑specific training by leveraging pretrained knowledge via in‑context learning (ICL).
- **Mechanism:** The ConTextTab architecture processes a table by embedding column names and cell values (using a sentence transformer), then applies 2D attention across rows and columns. At inference, labeled examples (context rows) are concatenated with unlabeled query rows; the model directly predicts without weight updates, having learned to map table structures to outputs during pretraining on 1.34 TB of tables.
- **Core assumption:** The pretraining corpus contained sufficient variety in schemas, feature types, and prediction tasks to generalize to unseen SAP‑structured tables.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “pretrained on 1.34 TB of structured data… can perform predictions… through in‑context learning, providing labeled examples at inference time without any gradient updates.”
  - [section 2.2]: “ConTextTab… employs specialized semantic embeddings… 2D attention scheme with both cross‑column and cross‑row attention.”
  - [corpus]: Neighbor “ConTextTab: A Semantics‑Aware Tabular In‑Context Learner” directly describes the architecture (arxiv:2506.10707). Other corpus papers focus on enterprise benchmarks but do not contradict the ICL mechanism.
- **Break condition:** When context size is too small (<50 rows) to represent label distribution, or when table schema departs radically from pretraining distribution (e.g., novel SAP module with unique entity‑relationship patterns not seen in T4 dataset).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** RPT‑1’s advantage over GBDT is most pronounced in data‑scarce regimes (<100 examples), where GBDT hyperparameter tuning fails.
- **Mechanism:** GBDT performance degrades with insufficient training data due to overfitting and unstable hyperparameter optimization. RPT‑1’s ICL requires no training; it simply retrieves and reasons over the provided context, avoiding optimization pitfalls. Its pretrained feature interactions act as a strong prior.
- **Core assumption:** The pretrained model has internalized robust, generalizable feature‑to‑target mappings that transfer better than GBDTs learned from tiny datasets.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.3]: “At very small sample sizes (100 rows), RPT‑1‑OSS nearly matches XGBoost (0.856 vs. 0.871)… As data increases, XGBoost improves faster than RPT‑1‑OSS.”
  - [abstract]: “RPT‑1 outperforms XGBoost when provided with fewer than 75–100 context rows.”
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; neighbor papers like “SALT‑KG” discuss semantics‑aware learning on enterprise tables but do not quantify small‑data performance. Assumption: the ICL paradigm’s small‑data strength is transferable from TabPFN literature (cited in [2.1]).
- **Break condition:** As context grows beyond ~500 rows, GBDT’s dedicated optimization overtakes RPT‑1’s fixed‑pretraining approach, widening the accuracy gap (Table 4).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** RPT‑1’s performance gap is wider on regression than classification due to the difficulty of inferring precise continuous functions from contextual examples.
- **Mechanism:** Classification boundaries can be approximated from discrete example labels via pattern matching in the attention space. Regression requires learning a smooth, exact mapping from features to a continuous target—a function estimation problem more sensitive to distributional shifts and less amenable to ICL from a finite context.
- **Core assumption:** The ConTextTab pretraining objective optimized more effectively for categorical/classification objectives than for fine‑grained numeric regression.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.2]: Regression gaps of 8.9–11.1 percentage points (R²) vs. 3.6–4.1 pp (AUC‑ROC) for classification.
  - [abstract]: “Regression shows a larger gap of 8.9–11.1 percentage points (R²).”
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; neighbor “WONDERNBREAD” covers BPM tasks but not regression vs. classification comparisons. Assumption based on typical tabular deep learning challenges (Grinsztajn et al. [8] cited in paper).
- **Break condition:** Regression tasks with highly non‑linear, noisy, or multi‑modal target relationships that are underrepresented in the T4 pretraining corpus.

- **Concept:** In‑Context Learning (ICL)

  - **Why needed here:** To understand why GBDT dominates with more data but struggles with very small datasets, justifying the hybrid workflow.

- **Concept:** Synthetic Data Validation for Enterprise Benchmarks  
  - **Why needed here:** Paper uses synthetic SAP‑structured data for reproducibility; must assess how this affects generalizability to real customer data.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  1. ConTextTab Backbone (RPT‑1‑OSS): 64.6 MB model; takes DataFrame + context rows; outputs prediction.  
  2. GBDT Baselines: Tuned XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost; require training on same DataFrame.  
  3. Context Manager: Determines number of context rows provided to RPT‑1.  
  4. Evaluation Harness: 5‑fold CV, metric calculators (AUC‑ROC, R²), latency timers.  
  5. Synthetic Data Generators: Produce SAP module‑specific tables (SD/MM/FI) with controlled schemas.
- **Critical path:**  
  1) Generate dataset →  
  2) For each fold:  
    2a) Train/tune GBDT on training fold →  
    2b) Run RPT‑1 on training fold as context →  
    2c) Predict on test fold →  
  3) Aggregate metrics across folds.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Context size vs. latency:** Larger context improves RPT‑1 accuracy but increases GPU inference time (Table 4: 2 sec @100 rows → 28 sec @2048 rows).  
  - **Synthetic realism vs. control:** Synthetic data enables reproducible gaps but may under‑represent complex SAP master data dependencies.  
  - **Model simplicity vs. accuracy:** RPT‑1 offers zero engineering effort (no feature engineering, encoding, tuning) but sacrifices 3‑11% accuracy vs. tuned GBDT.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Regression tasks:** R² gap >10% suggests RPT‑1 cannot capture numeric relationships.  
  - **Large datasets (>2000 rows):** GBDT outperforms by >5% AUC/R²—contextual advantage exhausted.  
  - **High‑cardinality categoricals:** If synthetic data doesn’t mirror SAP vendor/material codes, RPT‑1’s semantic embeddings may fail; check CatBoost performance (handles categories natively).  
  - **Latency spikes:** Inference time scales super‑linearly with context; >30 sec may break real‑time use cases.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Classification small‑data test:** Use 100‑row context on Predictive Integrity binary task. Expect RPT‑1 AUC within 2% of XGBoost. If gap >5%, investigate schema mismatch.  
  2. **Regression gap quantification:** Run Forecast Showdown with 2048‑row context. Confirm R² gap 8‑11%. If gap smaller, synthetic data may be too simple.  
  3. **Hybrid workflow pilot:** Screen 10 synthetic SAP scenarios with RPT‑1 (500 rows). Deploy GBDT only for those where R²/AUC >0.85 target. Measure time saved vs. training all 10 from scratch.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (e.g., LoRA) on SAP domain data close the accuracy gap between RPT‑1 and tuned GBDT models, especially for regression tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future Work (Section 7) and Limitations (Section 6.3) explicitly state that fine‑tuning approaches were not evaluated and “could potentially close the accuracy gap with task‑specific models.”
- **Why unresolved:** The study tested only the frozen pretrained RPT‑1‑OSS; no domain adaptation was performed, leaving unknown whether SAP‑specific tuning improves performance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments fine‑tuning RPT‑1 on the three SAP tasks using LoRA/QLoRA, with accuracy metrics (R², AUC‑ROC) compared directly to the tuned GBDT baselines from this study.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does RPT‑1‑OSS performance generalize to real‑world SAP customer data, given the reliance on synthetic datasets in this evaluation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Limitations (Section 6.3) explicitly caution that “performance on production SAP data may differ due to more complex feature interactions, data quality issues, and domain‑specific patterns not captured in synthetic generation.”
- **Why unresolved:** Synthetic data may not replicate the schema complexity, temporal dependencies, and noise patterns of live SAP systems, potentially overestimating RPT‑1’s robustness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication of the experimental framework on anonymized, real SAP customer datasets across the same modules (SD/MM/FI/CO), with performance variance analysis against the synthetic results.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do commercial RPT‑1 variants (small/large) with larger context windows achieve different accuracy‑latency trade‑offs on enterprise‑scale SAP datasets compared to the open‑source variant?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future Work (Section 7) explicitly calls for “extending the evaluation to the commercial RPT‑1 variants with larger context windows, which may show different performance characteristics.”
- **Why unresolved:** Only sap‑rpt‑1‑oss (context ≤2,048) was tested; commercial variants support up to 65,536 rows, which could improve accuracy but also increase latency and resource demands.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmarking all three SAP RPT‑1 variants on larger SAP datasets (≥10,000 rows), measuring context‑size scaling for accuracy (AUC‑ROC, R²) and inference latency on enterprise‑grade hardware.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does RPT‑1’s lack of calibrated uncertainty estimates limit its deployment in risk‑sensitive SAP finance applications where probabilistic outputs are required?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Limitations (Section 6.3) note that “RPT‑1’s ability to produce calibrated uncertainty estimates… was not assessed,” and the Finance Dashboard experiment involves risk classification where uncertainty quantification is often business‑critical.
- **Why unresolved:** The study only evaluated point estimates; no analysis of prediction confidence or calibration was conducted, which is essential for auditable financial decision‑making.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Calibration evaluation of RPT‑1 on the financial risk task using metrics like Brier score, reliability diagrams, and comparison to GBDT’s probabilistic outputs (e.g., via Platt scaling or isotonic regression).

## Limitations
- Synthetic data generation parameters are unreported, potentially underrepresenting real SAP complexities like master data relationships and missingness patterns.
- RPT‑1‑OSS inference specifics (context concatenation, bagging) are opaque, risking replication drift.
- Findings are confined to synthetic, idealized scenarios; generalizability to noisy, high‑cardinality real customer data remains unvalidated.
- The study did not assess RPT‑1's calibration or uncertainty estimates, which are important for risk-sensitive applications such as financial risk prediction.

## Confidence
- **ICL Competitive Accuracy:** Medium.
- **Small‑Data (<100 rows) Advantage:** Medium.
- **Wider Regression Gap:** Low.

## Next Checks
- **Real Data Pilot:** Test RPT‑1‑OSS on a small, anonymized SAP customer table (e.g., sales orders) against tuned GBDT; measure accuracy delta versus synthetic benchmarks to quantify realism bias.
- **Context Sensitivity Study:** Systematically vary context row ordering, separator tokens, and bagging folds to quantify their impact on stability and performance, exposing undocumented inference heuristics.
- **Error Stratification Analysis:** Segment RPT‑1’s regression errors by feature type (e.g., high‑cardinality codes, skewed numerics) to identify specific schema drift points where ICL fails, guiding hybrid workflow thresholds.
