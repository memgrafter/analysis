# Efficient Refusal Ablation in LLM through Optimal Transport

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.04355
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04355
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Key outcome**: Achieves up to 11% higher attack success rates than state-of-the-art baselines while maintaining comparable perplexity across six models.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles activation-based jailbreaking of safety-aligned large language models. Unlike prior work (e.g., Refusal Ablation) that removes a single refusal direction, the proposed method uses optimal transport to transform the entire distribution of harmful activations to match the harmless distribution, preserving covariance structure. Principal component analysis reduces dimensionality for efficiency, and interventions are applied at only 1–2 middle layers (∼40–60% depth). Evaluated on six models (Llama‑2, Llama‑3.1, Qwen‑2.5; 7B–32B), the approach yields up to 11% higher attack success rates than strong baselines while keeping model fluency intact.

## Method Summary
The method performs inference‑time intervention by learning an affine transformation on residual stream activations. First, collect activation matrices from a set of harmful and harmless prompts at candidate layers using a forward hook. Then, apply pooled‑mean PCA to the combined centered data to obtain a low‑dimensional subspace (top *k* components). Within this subspace, compute the closed‑form Gaussian optimal transport map that pushes the harmful distribution to the harmless one, yielding matrices *A*<sub>*k*</sub> and *b*<sub>*k*</sub>. Lift the transformation back to full dimensionality. A validation set selects the layer(s) that maximize attack success rate while constraining perplexity. During generation, forward hooks apply the transformation: *h*<sub>ℓ</sub> ← *A*<sub>full</sub>*h*<sub>ℓ</sub> + *b*<sub>full</sub>. Baselines include RFA (orthogonal projection of a refusal direction across all layers) and AcT (feature‑wise 1‑D optimal transport).

## Key Results
- **Higher ASR**: Up to 11% absolute increase in attack success rate over RFA and AcT across six models.
- **Localized intervention**: Optimal performance achieved by intervening at only 1–2 layers located at ∼40–60% depth; deeper layers cause pathological repetition, shallower layers ineffective.
- **Efficient dimensionality**: PCA with *k*=1–2 components sufficient; larger *k* degrades perplexity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Optimal transport (OT) transforms the full *distribution* of harmful activations to match the harmless distribution, not just a single direction.
- **Mechanism:** Models the harmful and harmless activation sets as empirical probability measures µ and ν. It computes a minimal‑cost mapping *T* (affine: *A**x*+*b*) that pushes µ to ν, preserving covariance structure. The closed‑form solution for Gaussian distributions (Eq. 2) jointly aligns means and covariances.
- **Core assumption:** The key geometric distinction between harmful and harmless representations is captured by their (approximately) Gaussian distributions in a suitable subspace.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "transforms the entire distribution of harmful activations to match harmless ones"
  - [section 2.2] "optimal transport seeks a mapping T that pushes forward the empirical distribution of harmful activations to the empirical distribution of harmless activations"
  - [corpus] Weak/absent; neighboring papers focus on single‑direction ablation or other alignment methods.
- **Break condition:** When activation distributions are highly non‑Gaussian or multimodal, the Gaussian OT approximation fails to capture essential geometric differences.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** PCA‑regularization enables efficient, robust OT in high‑dimensional LLM representation spaces by restricting transport to a low‑dimensional discriminant subspace.
- **Mechanism:** Apply PCA with pooled mean centering to the combined centered activations. Compute the Gaussian OT map only in the top‑*k* principal component subspace (Eq. 4–5), then lift to full space. This reduces noise, ill‑conditioning, and computational cost.
- **Core assumption:** The semantic shift from harmful to harmless representations primarily occurs along a low‑dimensional subspace (the top principal components of the combined data).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.3] "To address the curse of dimensionality... we apply principal component analysis before computing the optimal transport map"
  - [section 4.2/Figure 2] "the first few principal components capture substantial variance... top three components alone explain ≥40% of variance"
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Surgical Refusal Ablation" also use dimensionality reduction but via different spectral methods.
- **Break condition:** If the discriminant information is spread evenly across many dimensions, top‑*k* PCA loses critical separation signal, degrading attack success.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Refusal mechanisms are localized to specific middle layers (≈40–60% depth); intervening at 1–2 carefully selected layers is sufficient and optimal.
- **Mechanism:** Rather than applying transformations at all layers (like RFA), the method searches a middle‑layer range and selects 1–2 layers that maximize validation ASR while preserving perplexity. This exploits the non‑monotonic layer sensitivity observed (Sec. 4.2).
- **Core assumption:** Safety alignment induces a geometrically localized "refusal module" in the network’s middle layers, not a distributed transformation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "intervening at only 1-2 carefully chosen layers at approximately 40-60% network depth yields optimal performance"
  - [section 4.2/Table 3] "Llama-2-13B exhibits a sharp transition to high ASR at 40–50% depth... Qwen2.5-14B shows gradual increase peaking at 62.5% depth"
  - [section 4.2] "deep layers (95% depth) exhibit pathological repetition despite high ASR"
  - [corpus] "Surgical Refusal Ablation" also finds specific layers for ablation, supporting localization.
- **Break condition:** On models with highly distributed safety mechanisms (e.g., some Qwen variants), single‑layer intervention may be insufficient; multi‑layer or deeper interventions might be needed.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Optimal Transport (Wasserstein Distance)**
  - **Why needed here:** Provides a principled way to transform one *distribution* of activations into another, accounting for full covariance structure, unlike simple direction removal.
  - **Quick check question:** Given two Gaussian distributions N(μ₁, Σ₁) and N(μ₂, Σ₂), what is the form of the optimal transport map minimizing  W₂²? (Answer: affine map T(x)=A*x*+*b*, with A and *b* as in Eq. 2).
- **Concept: Principal Component Analysis (PCA)**
  - **Why needed here:** Reduces dimensionality of high‑D LLM activations (d=4k–8k) to a low‑*k* subspace where covariance estimation is stable and OT computation efficient, while preserving most between‑class (harmful/harmless) variance.
  - **Quick check question:** Why use pooled mean centering before PCA on combined harmful/harmless data? (Answer: To ensure the PCs capture *between‑group* separation, not just overall variance).
- **Concept: Activation Steering / Representation Engineering**
  - **Why needed here:** The method is an inference‑time intervention: it applies a learned transformation T(ℓ)(*h*) = *A*<sub>full</sub>*h* + *b*<sub>full</sub> as a forward hook on specific layers, altering the residual stream without changing model weights.
  - **Quick check question:** How does this differ from fine‑tuning? (Answer: Steering modifies activations *during* forward passes; fine‑tuning permanently changes weights. Steering is reversible and compute‑efficient at inference).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Activation Extractor → PCA Module → OT Calculator → Layer Selector → Hook System

**Critical path**: Data collection (activations) → PCA → OT map estimation (per layer) → Validation‑based layer selection → Hook installation → Generation

**Design tradeoffs**
- **`k` (PCA components):** Low *k* (e.g., 1–2) reduces overfitting/noise but may miss important covariance structure. High *k* improves statistical accuracy but risks capturing noise and degrades generation quality (perplexity ↑). *Evidence:* Table 4 (Llama‑2‑13B: *k*=2 best; *k*=10 causes PPL explosion).
- **Intervention depth:** Shallow layers (≤30%) have low ASR; deep layers (≥80%) cause pathological collapse. Optimal region is model‑specific (Llama: ~40–50%, Qwen: ~60%). *Evidence:* Fig. 4, Table 3.
- **Single vs. dual‑layer:** Single‑layer (PCA‑OT1) often suffices (Llama family); dual‑layer (PCA‑OT2) boosts Qwen performance but may increase perplexity. *Evidence:* Table 1.

**Failure signatures**
1. **High perplexity, low ASR:** *k* too large → overfit to noise in training activations; transform distorts linguistic structure.
2. **High ASR, pathological output (e.g., "Sure" repetition):** Intervention at too deep a layer (≥80% depth). Model generates incoherent text despite triggering safety classifier.
3. **No ASR improvement over baseline:** Chosen layer is outside the refusal mechanism locality (e.g., shallow layer <30% depth). *Evidence:* Table 3 (L5–L11 ASR ≈0%).

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Baseline replication & sanity check:** On a held‑out validation set, run RFA and AcT at *their* reported optimal layers. Then run PCA‑OT1 at the *same* layers. Verify PCA‑OT1 achieves higher ASR with comparable perplexity. *Purpose:* Isolate OT advantage from layer selection.
2. **Layer sensitivity sweep:** For a target model (e.g., Llama‑2‑13B), apply PCA‑OT1 (with *k*=2) at *every* layer from 1 to *L*. Plot ASR and perplexity vs. layer depth. Identify the optimal layer range (peak ASR with low PPL). *Purpose:* Confirm localization hypothesis.
3. **`k` ablation:** Fix layer at the optimal from Exp 2. Sweep *k* ∈ {1,2,3,5,10}. Measure ASR and perplexity. Observe the bias‑variance tradeoff. *Purpose:* Tune PCA dimensionality for best attack‑quality balance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can optimal transport methods for jailbreaking be extended beyond the Gaussian assumption to handle complex, non‑Gaussian activation distributions?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly stated in Section 4.4 (Limitations) and Section 6 (Conclusion).
- **Why unresolved:** The current closed‑form Gaussian OT solution is computationally efficient and effective, but real‑world model activations may exhibit multimodality or heavy tails that Gaussian assumptions miss, potentially limiting attack success or introducing instabilities.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing and evaluating neural or entropic optimal transport methods for non‑Gaussian distributions; conducting systematic statistical tests (e.g., skewness/kurtosis, multivariate normality tests) across models and layers to characterize deviation from Gaussianity; comparing attack success rates between Gaussian and non‑Gaussian OT variants.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What specific architectural or training modifications can robustify models against distributional attacks like PCA‑OT, thereby breaking its core assumptions?
- **Basis in paper:** Mentioned in Section 4.4 and the Impact Statement.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper evaluates only against prior baselines and standard models, not against adversarially‑trained defenses (e.g., RepNoise, latent adversarial training). It remains unknown which changes to alignment, architecture, or normalization would disrupt the low‑dimensional Gaussian structure that PCA‑OT exploits.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training models with defenses that explicitly randomize or regularize activation distributions (e.g., activation noise, variational inference, distributional robustness objectives); measuring whether PCA‑OT’s ASR degrades significantly and analyzing the resulting activation geometry (e.g., increased non‑Gaussianity, loss of low‑rank structure).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why are refusal mechanisms localized to specific middle layers (40–60% depth) across models, and how do architectural differences (e.g., depth, width, normalization) affect this localization pattern?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from Section 4.2 (Layer sensitivity analysis), which shows sharp transitions for Llama‑2‑13B at ∼42.5% depth but a gradual peak for Qwen2.5‑14B at ∼62.5% depth, suggesting architecture‑dependent localization.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper observes localization but does not provide a mechanistic explanation for why middle layers are critical or why architectural variations shift the optimal depth. This limits generalizability and understanding of safety circuitry.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting circuit‑level analysis (e.g., activation patching, path expansion) to identify subcircuits responsible for refusal across architectures; correlating layer sensitivity with architectural features (e.g., layer norms, attention heads, residual stream variance); testing whether localization holds for other model families (e.g., Mistral, Gemma) and scales.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the optimal strategy for extracting and aggregating activations (e.g., last token, mean pooling, attention‑weighted pooling) for distributional attacks, and how does this interact with overfitting to positional noise?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from Section 4.4, where the authors note they extract at the last token but suggest alternative pooling strategies could reduce overfitting.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper uses only last‑token activations, assuming they contain the final refusal decision. However, refusal may emerge from distributed sequence representations; different pooling might capture more robust distributional signals but could also introduce noise.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparing attack success and perplexity across pooling strategies (last token, mean, weighted by attention to first/last tokens) on multiple models; analyzing stability of estimated transport maps under different extraction methods; measuring correlation between pooling‑induced noise and ASR.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Beyond mean and covariance, what specific higher‑order geometric properties (e.g., manifold structure, higher moments, topological features) distinguish harmful from harmless activations, and can they be leveraged for more effective or stealthier attacks?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from Section 4.3 (Geometric Interpretation), which visualizes PCA projections and shows PCA‑OT matches both mean and covariance, but the authors do not analyze beyond second‑order statistics.
- **Why unresolved:** PCA‑OT implicitly uses only up to second‑order statistics (via PCA and Gaussian OT). However, refusal might involve higher‑order dependencies (e.g., skewness, kurtosis, nonlinear manifolds) that could improve attack precision or reduce detectable artifacts.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying nonlinear dimensionality reduction (e.g., t‑SNE, UMAP) to visualize activation manifolds; computing higher‑order moment discrepancies between classes; experimenting with non‑Gaussian OT (e.g., using sinkhorn with more bins) and evaluating if it yields higher ASR at comparable perplexity; testing if incorporating higher moments improves transferability across models.

## Limitations
- Exact prompt formatting, generation hyperparameters, and hook injection point are unspecified, hindering faithful replication.
- The Gaussian optimal transport assumption may fail for non‑Gaussian activation distributions; the paper does not analyze multimodality.
- The localization of refusal to 1–2 middle layers is demonstrated on a limited model set; may not generalize to architectures with distributed safety (e.g., some Qwen variants).

## Confidence
- **OT transforms full distribution**: High – solid theory and consistent ASR gains across six models.
- **PCA enables efficient OT**: Medium – PCA is standard but the choice of *k* is heuristic; risk of losing discriminant signal.
- **Layer localization**: Medium – supported by sweeps but model‑specific; broader validation required.
