# Forecasting Antimicrobial Resistance Trends Using Machine Learning on WHO GLASS Surveillance Data: A Retrieval-Augmented Generation Approach for Policy Decision Support
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22673
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22673
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** XGBoost forecasts AMR trends with test MAE 7.07% and R² 0.854, an 83.1% improvement over naive baseline; a RAG pipeline provides policy answers with source attribution and hallucination constraints.

## Executive Summary
This study benchmarks machine learning models for forecasting antimicrobial resistance (AMR) using WHO GLASS data, achieving a test MAE of 7.07% and R² of 0.854 with XGBoost. The best model shows an 83.1% improvement over a naive baseline. Additionally, a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline integrates forecast context with WHO policy documents to answer natural-language policy queries with source attribution and hallucination constraints, demonstrating a feasible end-to-end decision support system.

## Method Summary
The study uses WHO GLASS surveillance data (2021–2023, 5,909 observations, 44 countries) to forecast annual AMR resistance rates. Six models are benchmarked: Naive, Linear Regression, Ridge, XGBoost, LightGBM, and LSTM. Preprocessing includes temporal splitting (2021–2022 train, 2022 validation, 2023 test), target encoding for high-cardinality categoricals (country, pathogen, antibiotic), and median imputation. The RAG component builds a vector store from six curated WHO policy documents using all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings in ChromaDB. For each policy query, the top-3 retrieved chunks are combined with XGBoost forecast context and fed to a locally deployed Phi-3 Mini model via Ollama, with a prompt constraint to avoid fabricated citations.

## Key Results
- XGBoost achieves test MAE of 7.07% and R² of 0.854, an 83.1% improvement over the naive baseline.
- Feature importance shows prior-year resistance dominates (50.5%).
- Regional MAE varies: European Region 4.16%, South-East Asia 10.14%, reflecting data quality disparities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Short-term AMR resistance rate forecasting is primarily driven by temporal autocorrelation, captured via the prior-year resistance lag.
- **Mechanism:** The model leverages the high persistence of resistance rates year-to-year. The lag feature (`resistance_lag1`) acts as a strong autoregressive component, allowing even simple models to capture the dominant signal. More complex models like XGBoost then model smaller deviations from this trend using other features.
- **Core assumption:** AMR trends at the population level change relatively slowly within a 1–2 year horizon, making the previous year's rate the best single predictor.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Feature importance analysis identified the prior-year resistance rate as the dominant predictor (50.5% importance)"
  - [section 4.2] "The dominance of the resistance lag feature (50.5%) reflects strong temporal autocorrelation"
- **Break condition:** If the importance of `resistance_lag1` drops substantially (e.g., <20%) in future studies with longer time series or during a major perturbation (e.g., novel antibiotic introduction, pandemic), the autoregressive premise weakens.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Forecast accuracy is contingent on the quality and completeness of GLASS surveillance data, which varies significantly by region and national capacity.
- **Mechanism:** The model's error systematically correlates with underlying data infrastructure. Regions with mature, comprehensive surveillance (e.g., European Region) yield lower MAE because the training data has less noise, fewer missing values, and more consistent reporting. High-burden regions with nascent systems (e.g., South-East Asia) have sparser, noisier data, increasing uncertainty.
- **Core assumption:** GLASS data representativeness and completeness are primary drivers of regional performance differences, not intrinsic biological differences in AMR dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "regional MAE ranged from 4.16% (European Region) to 10.14% (South-East Asia Region), reflecting disparities in GLASS data quality"
  - [section 4.3] "This regional gradient closely mirrors the global AMR burden distribution... high-burden regions also tend to have weaker surveillance infrastructure."
- **Break condition:** If MAE disparities persist or worsen despite equivalent data collection efforts across regions, other factors (e.g., unmeasured social, environmental, or genetic drivers) become primary error sources.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A locally deployed RAG pipeline can produce policy-relevant, source-attributed answers by grounding an LLM in retrieved WHO documents and injecting forecast context, thereby constraining hallucinations.
- **Mechanism:** The system separates knowledge retrieval from generation. First, a vector store retrieves semantically relevant WHO policy excerpts. Second, the LLM prompt is structured to **only** reference these retrieved chunks and the provided forecast summary. The explicit "no fabricated citations" instruction in the system prompt leverages the LLM's instruction-following to stay within the provided evidence envelope.
- **Core assumption:** The curated WHO document corpus contains sufficient information to answer anticipated policy questions, and the LLM can correctly synthesize retrieved text with numerical forecast context.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "producing source-attributed, hallucination-constrained policy answers"
  - [section 4.4] "All five responses demonstrated: (1) accurate retrieval... (2) correct attribution... (3) coherent integration of forecast findings"
- **Break condition:** If the RAG system begins generating unsupported claims or citing documents not in the retrieved set, the grounding mechanism fails, likely due to insufficiently clear prompt instructions, LLM capacity limits (Phi-3 Mini), or poor retrieval relevance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Temporal Autocorrelation in Epidemiological Time Series**
  - **Why needed here:** To understand why the prior-year resistance feature dominates model performance (50.5% importance) and to correctly interpret that short-term AMR forecasting is largely extrapolating a stable trend.
  - **Quick check question:** "If `resistance_lag1` had 90% feature importance, what would that imply about the underlying AMR process and the value of additional features?"
- **Concept: Gradient Boosting vs. Deep Learning for Small Tabular Time Series**
  - **Why needed here:** To explain why XGBoost/LightGBM performed on par with LSTM on a 3-year dataset. Tree-based ensembles excel on small-to-medium tabular data with nonlinear interactions, while LSTMs require longer sequences to learn meaningful temporal dependencies.
  - **Quick check question:** "Given only 3 annual data points per series, what architectural advantage does an LSTM have over XGBoost, and why might it not be realized here?"
- **Concept: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) as a Hallucination Mitigation Strategy**
  - **Why needed here:** To grasp how the system avoids common LLM pitfalls (fabrication) for a high-stakes policy application. The mechanism is *evidence grounding*: generation is constrained to the union of retrieved documents and provided forecast context.
  - **Quick check question:** "In this RAG setup, what are the two distinct knowledge sources the LLM is allowed to draw from, and what happens if the retrieved documents don't contain the answer to a user's question?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Ingestion & Preprocessing → Forecasting Model Trainer → RAG Knowledge Base Builder → Policy Query Service

**Critical path:** GLASS data cleaning → XGBoost training/evaluation → RAG store creation → query-time retrieval + prompt assembly → LLM generation.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Local deployment (Phi-3 Mini via Ollama) vs. Cloud API (GPT-4): Chose local for cost, privacy, and reproducibility, sacrificing reasoning depth and instruction fidelity.
- Small manually-curated policy corpus vs. complete WHO library: Chose curated for quality control and initial feasibility, sacrificing recall and breadth.
- Simple target encoding vs. advanced embeddings for categoricals: Chose simplicity to avoid leakage and complexity, potentially losing some semantic signal.

**Failure signatures:**
- Forecasting: Test MAE >15% overall; regional MAE for South-East Asia >15% (vs. paper's 10.14%); `resistance_lag1` importance drops below 30% in ablation.
- RAG: Hallucinated source citation (e.g., "According to GLASS 2024..."); retrieved chunks with zero cosine similarity to query; coherent but irrelevant policy answers.
- Systemic: Code/data mismatch; temporal leakage in splits; target encoding applied on full dataset before split.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline replication: Run the provided pipeline on the exact GLASS dataset to confirm XGBoost test MAE ~7.07% and R² ~0.854. Validate feature importance ranking.
2. Ablation - lag feature: Remove `resistance_lag1` from XGBoost features. Expected outcome: MAE increases dramatically (>20% absolute), confirming temporal autocorrelation dominance.
3. RAG retrieval sanity check: For a known policy question (e.g., "What is the AWaRe classification?"), verify that retrieved chunks contain the definition and that the LLM answer cites the correct chunk label without fabrication.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does extending the temporal coverage of GLASS data beyond three years influence the forecasting performance of machine learning models, particularly deep learning architectures like LSTM that benefit from longer sequences?
2. Does expanding the RAG knowledge base to include comprehensive WHO policy documents (e.g., full GLASS annual reports, national AMR action plans) improve the relevance and completeness of policy answers, and can automated metrics like RAGAS reliably evaluate faithfulness?
3. Can domain-specific sentence embeddings fine-tuned on AMR literature yield better retrieval performance in the RAG system compared to the general-purpose all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model?
4. What factors explain the regional disparities in forecast accuracy observed in this study (e.g., MAE ranging from 4.16% to 10.14%), and how might these insights guide targeted improvements in GLASS data collection or model adaptation?

## Limitations
- GLASS data completeness varies regionally, especially underreporting in South-East Asia.
- Hyperparameter details for XGBoost/LightGBM are missing, hindering exact replication.
- RAG pipeline lacks documentation on chunking strategy, exact policy excerpts, and prompt template.

## Confidence

- Prior-year lag dominance: High
- Regional MAE disparities reflect data quality: Medium
- RAG produces hallucination-constrained answers: Medium
