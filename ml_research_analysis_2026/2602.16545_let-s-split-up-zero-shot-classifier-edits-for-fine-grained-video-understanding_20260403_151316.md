# Let's Split Up: Zero-Shot Classifier Edits for Fine-Grained Video Understanding
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16545  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16545  
- **Reference count:** 35  
- **Primary result:** Zero-shot editing achieves 46.3% generality and 98.9% locality on SSv2-Split‑A; with one‑shot fine‑tuning generality reaches 52.8% while preserving locality, far surpassing CLIP and avoiding catastrophic forgetting of full fine‑tuning.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces *category splitting*, a zero‑shot method for editing a trained video classifier so that a coarse label can be subdivided into finer subcategories without retraining the backbone. The technique exploits the latent compositional structure in the classification head: “modifier vectors” capturing fine‑grained distinctions are extracted from existing fine‑grained categories and transferred to the target coarse category via textual similarity or learned alignment. This yields an initialized head for the new splits. A light fine‑tuning of only this new head with a few labeled examples further refines performance while freezing the rest of the model, thus preserving accuracy on unchanged classes.

On the newly proposed SSv2‑Split and FineGym‑Split benchmarks, the approach dramatically outperforms vision‑language baselines like CLIP. Zero‑shot editing already yields strong locality (>98%) and moderate generality (46.3% on SSv2‑Split‑A). With just one example per new subcategory, generality climbs to 52.8% without sacrificing locality. In contrast, full‑data fine‑tuning leads to catastrophic forgetting, dropping locality to 22.1%. The results demonstrate that video classifiers encode reusable compositional structure, enabling efficient adaptation to new fine‑grained tasks.

## Method Summary
Category splitting modifies the classification head of a pre‑trained video model. First, for a set of existing fine‑grained categories that are subclasses of some coarse label, the method computes a modifier vector for each by subtracting the weight of the coarse class from the weight of the fine subclass in the head’s embedding space. To split a new target coarse category into subcategories, the method selects the most relevant modifiers from the source pool using textual similarity (e.g., between class names’ word embeddings) or a learned alignment. The selected modifiers are then added to the target coarse class’s weight to form the weights of the new subcategories. This zero‑shot edit produces an extended classification head that outputs both the original coarse classes (if untouched) and the newly defined fine‑grained ones. Finally, if a few labeled examples of the new subcategories are available, only the new head’s weights are fine‑tuned, keeping the backbone frozen to preserve prior knowledge and maintain locality.

## Key Results
- On SSv2‑Split‑A, zero‑shot editing achieves 46.3% generality and 98.9% locality; CLIP attains only 27.6% generality.
- One‑shot fine‑tuning of the zero‑shot initialized head improves generality to 52.8% while maintaining locality (>98%), beating full‑data fine‑tuning which catastrophically forgets (locality 22.1%).
- The method substantially outperforms vision‑language baselines on both SSv2‑Split and FineGym‑Split across multiple splits.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that a trained video classifier’s linear head often encodes a compositional structure: the weight vector for a coarse class can be decomposed into a base plus modifiers that capture fine‑grained variations. These modifiers, when extracted from existing fine‑grained classes, carry transferable semantic directions (e.g., “with a ball” vs. “with a racket”). By aligning these modifiers to a new target coarse class’s semantic description (e.g., via word embeddings), the method can plausibly reconstruct weights for its subcategories without additional data. The zero‑shot edit modifies only the weights of the target coarse category, leaving all other class weights untouched; this preserves locality. Subsequent light fine‑tuning on a few examples adjusts the new head to the specific visual distribution while the backbone remains fixed, preventing catastrophic forgetting. The success hinges on the assumption that similar fine‑grained distinctions (e.g., sports actions) are expressed by similar modifier vectors across different coarse categories—a property supported by the strong empirical results.

## Foundational Learning
- **Zero‑Shot Classification:** Enables adaptation to unseen classes via semantic attributes. *Why needed*: Initializes new splits without labeled data. *Quick check*: Uses textual embeddings to bridge visual and label spaces.
- **Compositional Generalization:** Assumes complex concepts are combinations of simpler ones. *Why needed*: Allows reusing modifier vectors across categories. *Quick check*: Verify that subtracting coarse weights from fine subclass weights yields consistent directions.
- **Classifier Weight Editing:** Directly modifies classification head weights. *Why needed*: Avoids costly retraining and preserves other classes. *Quick check*: Confirm that only weights of the target coarse category are altered.
- **Few‑Shot Fine‑Tuning:** Adapts a model with minimal labeled examples. *Why needed*: Refines zero‑shot initialization for higher accuracy. *Quick check*: Fine‑tune only the new head while freezing the backbone.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Pre‑trained video encoder (e.g., TimeSformer) → Original classification head (coarse categories) → Modifier extraction module (computes difference vectors for source fine‑grained subclasses) → Alignment module (matches modifiers to target subcategories via textual similarity or learned metric) → New head construction (adds selected modifiers to target coarse weight) → Optional few‑shot fine‑tuning (updates new head only).

**Critical path:**  
1. Load a frozen pre‑trained video classifier.  
2. Identify a set of source fine‑grained categories and a target coarse category to split.  
3. Compute modifier vectors for each source subclass relative to its coarse parent.  
4. For each desired new subcategory under the target coarse class, select the most semantically similar modifier(s) and add them to the target coarse class weight to create a new class weight.  
5. Assemble an extended classification head including original untouched classes and the newly created subcategories.  
6. If few‑shot data exists, fine‑tune only the new head while keeping the encoder and original head frozen.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Generality vs. Locality:* The method prioritizes locality by only altering target weights; generality may be limited by the quality of modifier transfer.
- *Zero‑shot vs. Few‑shot:* Zero‑shot requires no labels but may be less accurate; few‑shot fine‑tuning improves accuracy with minimal labeling overhead.
- *Modifier source selection:* Using semantically close source categories improves transfer; distant sources risk misalignment.
- *Computational cost:* Alignment step adds overhead compared to a purely zero‑shot CLIP‑style approach but remains modest.

**Failure signatures:**  
- No suitable source fine‑grained categories are available, leaving no modifiers to transfer.
- Textual similarity alignment fails due to lexical mismatch or domain shift (e.g., sports verbs applied to object categories).
- Overfitting during one‑shot fine‑tuning if the new head is too large or regularization is insufficient.

**Exactly 3 first experiments:**  
1. *Modifier extraction sanity check:* Extract modifiers from a well‑split coarse class (e.g., “sports”) and confirm they cluster semantically (e.g., “with ball”, “with racket”) via nearest‑neighbor search in embedding space.  
2. *Zero‑shot transfer test:* Apply the method to split a held‑out coarse class; evaluate accuracy on new subcategories (generality) and unchanged classes (locality).  
3. *One‑shot fine‑tuning:* With a single example per new subcategory, fine‑tune the new head and compare to zero‑shot and full fine‑tuning, measuring improvements in generality and retention of locality.

## Limitations
- Benchmark generalizability: Evaluation is restricted to two newly introduced splits (SSv2‑Split, FineGym‑Split); performance on standard datasets (Kinetics, AVA) or non‑activity categories remains unknown.  
- Method scope: Requires existing fine‑grained source categories to extract modifiers; effectiveness when such sources are absent or semantically distant is an open problem.  
- Architecture dependency: Results are demonstrated on TimeSformer; gains may not directly transfer to other modern video backbones (e.g., VideoMAE, InternVideo) without adaptation.
