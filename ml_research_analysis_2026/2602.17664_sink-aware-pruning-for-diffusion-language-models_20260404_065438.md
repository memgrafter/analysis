# Sink-Aware Pruning for Diffusion Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17664
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17664
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** Sink-aware pruning consistently matches or outperforms Wanda and SparseGPT baselines at matched sparsity levels (25–75%) for diffusion language models, with gains growing at higher compression.

## Executive Summary
Diffusion language models (DLMs) require iterative denoising, leading to high inference costs. Existing pruning methods, inherited from autoregressive LLMs, preserve attention sink tokens under the assumption they serve as stable global anchors. This work reveals that in DLMs, sink positions exhibit high temporal variance across denoising steps, indicating they are often transient rather than structurally essential. To address this, the authors propose Sink-Aware Pruning, which quantifies sink instability along the generation trajectory and down-weights unstable sinks prior to computing pruning importance scores. Evaluated on multiple DLM families across eight benchmarks, the method matches or exceeds the performance of Wanda and SparseGPT at sparsities from 25% to 75%, with improvements becoming more pronounced at higher sparsity. The approach yields better quality-efficiency trade-offs without any retraining, underscoring the need for generation-paradigm-specific pruning strategies.

## Method Summary
Sink-Aware Pruning modifies existing post-training pruning metrics (Wanda and SparseGPT) to account for the dynamic nature of attention sinks in diffusion models. First, during calibration, the model is run on a dataset (e.g., WikiText-2) with inputs noised at multiple diffusion timesteps. For each timestep, aggregated attention mass per token is computed across all layers and heads. Tokens with attention mass above a threshold are considered sinks for that step. The temporal variance of these sink scores across steps is used to derive a per-token down-weighting factor $\omega_j$, where tokens that are frequently sinks (unstable) receive lower weights. These weights are then applied to the activation matrix before calculating importance scores: in Wanda, the activation norm $\|X_j\|_2$ is computed on the masked activations; in SparseGPT, the Hessian is estimated from masked inputs. The resulting importance scores better reflect the true necessity of weights for semantically meaningful, non‑sink tokens. The final binary pruning mask is applied uniformly to both feed‑forward and attention layers. The entire process adds only a calibration overhead and requires no fine‑tuning.

## Key Results
- Sink‑aware pruning matches or surpasses Wanda and SparseGPT baselines at structured sparsities 25%–75% across eight zero‑shot benchmarks.
- Performance gains increase with sparsity; at 75% sparsity, sink‑aware methods remain among the top performers.
- For structured pruning (e.g., removing entire heads), the advantage of sink‑aware scoring becomes more pronounced under tighter sparsity budgets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The central discovery is that attention sinks in diffusion language models are not fixed anchors but rather migrate across token positions as denoising progresses. In autoregressive LLMs, sinks are typically early tokens (e.g., BOS) that consistently receive high attention throughout generation, making them structurally important. In contrast, DLMs update all tokens simultaneously at each diffusion step; early high‑noise steps focus on global structure, while later low‑noise steps refine local details. This shifting task causes the primary sink location to drift substantially, resulting in high temporal variance. Consequently, weights associated with these transient sinks are incorrectly deemed important by standard pruning metrics, which rely on activation norms that are inflated by the disproportionate attention mass.

Sink‑Aware Pruning corrects this bias by estimating each token’s sink instability. During calibration, the model is evaluated at a range of diffusion timesteps. For each step, a soft sink score is derived from the token’s attention mass. Averaging these scores over the sampled steps yields a stability estimate $\bar{\phi}(j)$. Tokens with high $\bar{\phi}(j)$ (frequent sinks) are down‑weighted via $\omega_j = 1 - \bar{\phi}(j)$ before importance calculation. This suppression ensures that pruning metrics prioritize weights that matter for semantically stable positions rather than for the noise‑sensitive sink dynamics.

The benefit grows at higher sparsity because the cost of misranking parameters becomes more severe: as the pruning budget tightens, the inflated scores of unstable sinks would otherwise lead to the removal of truly important weights. By discounting these artifacts, sink‑aware scoring provides a more accurate importance ranking, especially under aggressive compression.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Attention Statistics Collector → Sink Variance Estimator → Activation Masking Module → Pruning Score Calculator → Mask Applicator
- **Critical path**: Calibration data preparation → (for multiple timesteps) Forward pass → Attention mass aggregation → Sink score computation → Activation masking → Base pruning metric computation → Pruning decision → Mask application
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Calibration Data Distribution vs. Cost*: More diverse data better estimates sink statistics but requires more forward passes at multiple timesteps.
  - *Timestep Sampling Granularity*: More timesteps give finer temporal variance estimates but increase memory/CPU overhead.
  - *Soft vs. Hard Sink Detection*: Using a sigmoid avoids brittle thresholds; the threshold $\epsilon$ is a sensitivity knob.
  - *Generality vs. Specificity*: The method is paradigm‑aware but global; per‑layer or per‑head sink analysis could be more precise but adds complexity.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - *High sink variance not detected*: If calibration data is too simple or timesteps poorly chosen, sink instability may be underestimated, leading to performance similar to baseline.
  - *Over‑aggressive down‑weighting*: If $\omega_j$ becomes near‑zero for many tokens, vital information could be lost, harming accuracy even at moderate sparsity.
  - *Mismatched timesteps*: Using only early or late steps biases the sink score; the method requires trajectory‑spanning sampling.
  - *Poor base metric compatibility*: Some future pruning metrics might not be affected by activation scaling in the same way, limiting effectiveness.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Sink Variance Validation Experiment**: Replicate Figure 5 on a small DLM to visualize sink position drift across steps and confirm orders‑of‑magnitude larger temporal variance compared to an AR‑LLM.
  2. **Baseline Comparison Sanity Check**: Apply vanilla Wanda/SparseGPT and Sink‑Aware versions at 50% sparsity on LLaDA‑8B; verify consistent accuracy improvement as reported.
  3. **Ablation: Random Masking vs. Sink‑Aware Masking**: Generate random masks with similar average sparsity as the sink‑aware mask; apply to activations and run pruning. A significant drop vs. Sink‑Aware confirms the variance‑based mask content matters.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Robustness to calibration data distribution shift**: Only WikiText‑2 was used; sink identification may change with domain shifts.
- **Benefit of lightweight finetuning after pruning**: No post‑pruning adaptation was explored; fine‑tuning could further improve robustness.
- **Generalization to larger multimodal models and long contexts**: Only one multimodal DLM (MMaDA‑8B) was tested; >10B parameter models and contexts >4k tokens may show different dynamics.
- **Layer‑wise and timestep‑adaptive policies**: The current global averaging may miss opportunities for more precise, per‑layer or per‑timestep sink identification.

## Limitations
- Critical hyperparameters (sink threshold $\phi$, timestep count $|T|$, attention aggregation method) are not precisely specified, hindering exact replication.
- Evaluation restricted to three DLM families; generalizability to other diffusion architectures (e.g., Mamba‑based DLMs) remains open.
- Overhead of multi‑timestep attention collection is not quantified; actual end‑to‑end speedup may be smaller than reported.

## Confidence
- **Sink instability in DLMs**: High (strongly supported by variance metrics and trajectory plots).
- **Pruning improvement via sink down‑weighting**: Medium (results consistent but rely on undisclosed hyperparameters; ablations suggest non‑arbitrary effect).
- **Increasing gains with sparsity**: Medium (empirical trend up to 75% sparsity, but behavior beyond that is unexplored).

## Next Checks
1. Cross‑architecture replication: Compute sink temporal variance on a fundamentally different DLM (e.g., diffusion‑augmented Mamba) to test generalizability of the instability phenomenon.
2. Hyperparameter sensitivity sweep: Systematically vary sink threshold $\epsilon$ and timestep count $|T|$ on LLaDA‑8B; report mean ± std accuracy across benchmarks to identify robust defaults.
3. Cost‑benefit quantification: Measure total calibration time (including multi‑step attention collection) for sink‑aware vs. standard pruning; recompute accuracy–speedup trade‑offs with this overhead included.
