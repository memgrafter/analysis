# Monocular Normal Estimation via Shading Sequence Estimation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09929  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09929  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** RoSE reduces mean angular error to 16.36° on DiLiGenT and 14.48° on LUCES, setting new state-of-the-art for monocular normal estimation.

## Executive Summary
Monocular normal estimation from a single RGB image often yields 3D misalignment: predicted normals may look plausible but fail to reconstruct accurate geometry. This arises because geometric variations are encoded as subtle color differences in direct normal maps, making fine details hard to capture. RoSE reformulates the task as shading sequence estimation. Instead of predicting a normal map directly, it uses an image-to-video diffusion model to generate a sequence of shading images under predefined canonical lights. Shadings represent brightness variations that are more sensitive to geometry and, with material augmentation, decouple from albedo. The shading sequence is then converted analytically to a normal map via ordinary least squares. Training on the large-scale synthetic MultiShade dataset—with diverse shapes, materials (MatSynth), and lighting—enables robustness. On real-world benchmarks (DiLiGenT, LUCES), RoSE achieves SOTA accuracy (e.g., lowering MAE by up to 2.96°) and higher shading sequence quality. Ablations confirm the benefits of shading clamping, material augmentation, and the MultiShade dataset.

## Method Summary
RoSE takes a single RGB image, converts it to grayscale, and processes it through two encoders: a CLIP encoder for global semantics and a VAE encoder for local spatial features. These conditioning embeddings are fed into a video diffusion U-Net (SV3D backbone) that generates a latent shading sequence of 9 frames, each corresponding to a different canonical light direction placed uniformly on a latitude ring at 45° elevation. The diffusion model is trained with a standard denoising loss. After iterative denoising, the latent sequence is decoded by a VAE decoder into pixel‑space shading maps, which are clipped to non‑negative values and rescaled. For each pixel, the positive shading values and their light directions form a linear system under the Lambertian shading equation $s = \max(n \cdot l, 0)$. This system is solved via ordinary least squares (OLS) to obtain the normal vector. The synthetic MultiShade dataset provides over 3 million training pairs with extensive material augmentation (metallic/non‑metallic PBRs) and diverse lighting to ensure generalization to real scenes.

## Key Results
- **State‑of‑the‑art accuracy:** Mean angular error (MAE) of **16.36°** on DiLiGenT (improved from 17.27°) and **14.48°** on LUCES (improved from 17.44°).
- **Shading sequence quality:** PSNR increased by **+0.75 dB** and LPIPS decreased by **‑0.010** compared to prior methods.
- **Ablations:** Each component contributes meaningfully—shading clamping, material augmentation from MatSynth, and the MultiShade dataset all improve final normal MAE.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Normal map bottleneck leads to 3D misalignment
Directly predicting normal maps forces geometric details into a compact RGB representation where distinct orientations produce similar colors. This subtle encoding makes it difficult for a model to recover accurate 3D structure, resulting in plausible‑looking but geometrically misaligned outputs. The paper identifies this bottleneck as the core weakness of prior approaches.

### Mechanism 2: Shadings provide high‑contrast, geometry‑centric signals
A shading map $s = \max(n \cdot l, 0)$ converts normal orientation into brightness under a fixed light. For constant albedo, a small change in normal yields a large, linear change in shading, producing strong local gradients. Because the shading sequence is predicted under multiple canonical lights, geometry is emphasized while material influence is minimized (via augmentation). This high‑contrast representation is easier to align with image cues, improving sensitivity to fine geometric details.

### Mechanism 3: Video diffusion enforces temporal coherence across lighting
Predicting the shading sequence as a video (9 frames) leverages the temporal coherence prior of large‑scale diffusion models. The video U‑Net processes all frames jointly, using temporal attention to ensure that adjacent frames (corresponding to nearby light directions) are consistent. This coherence acts as a regularizer, reducing hallucination and stabilizing per‑frame predictions, which in turn yields more reliable OLS solutions. The structured ring‑light ordering is crucial; a more complex spiral path degraded performance, highlighting the benefit of a smooth light trajectory.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Grayscale image → CLIP & VAE encoders → video diffusion U-Net → VAE decoder → Shading sequence → OLS solver → Normal map

**Critical path**
1. Convert input RGB to grayscale.
2. Encode grayscale with CLIP (global) and VAE (local) to obtain conditioning features.
3. Condition video diffusion U‑Net with these features and iteratively denoise a noisy latent sequence over $T$ timesteps, producing a 9‑frame latent shading sequence.
4. Decode the latent sequence to pixel‑space shading maps via VAE decoder.
5. Clip shading values to non‑negative and rescale to $[-1,1]$ for training stability.
6. For each pixel, assemble a linear system from all positive shading values and their corresponding light directions; solve via OLS to obtain the normal vector.
7. Output the normal map.

**Design tradeoffs**
- *Grayscale vs. RGB:* Grayscale simplifies geometry learning and improves accuracy (Table 6), but discards chromatic cues.
- *Ring vs. spiral light path:* Ring lights at fixed elevation guarantee full‑rank with fewer lights and a smooth ordering for temporal coherence; spiral increases complexity but degrades performance.
- *Video diffusion vs. per‑frame:* Video diffusion enforces cross‑frame consistency at the cost of inference speed (~10.5 s); per‑frame generation would be faster but likely less accurate.

**Failure signatures**
1. **Large unilluminated regions:** Many pixels with near‑zero shading across all lights → underdetermined OLS → noisy normals. Pixels with <3 positive shadings are vulnerable.
2. **Specular/transparent materials:** Violation of Lambertian assumption → shading sequence inconsistent with true geometry → high error on glossy/glass objects.
3. **Extreme lighting in input:** Overexposure or heavy shadows corrupt conditioning → degenerate shading sequence; check for saturated VAE latents.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Replication of Table 1 (DiLiGenT):** Train RoSE on MultiShade, evaluate on DiLiGenT using the test image indices from the appendix (e.g., indices 21–30 per object), and confirm MAE ≤ 16.36°.
2. **Ablation of grayscale:** Train a variant with RGB input (“Ours w/ RGB”). Expect MAE increase of ~0.79° on LUCES, verifying the grayscale design.
3. **Robustness of OLS to shading noise:** Add Gaussian noise ($\sigma=0.1$) to one shading frame and re‑run OLS; measured MAE increase should be <3°. A larger increase indicates brittleness in the conversion step.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Real‑time optimization:** Inference takes ~10.57 s per image, far from interactive use. The open question is how to optimize video diffusion (e.g., distillation, efficient sampling) to achieve sub‑30 ms latency while preserving accuracy.
2. **Robustness to extreme lighting:** Large shadowed regions reduce positive shading values, harming OLS. Evaluating and improving performance under such conditions is an open direction.
3. **Extension to transparent materials:** The Lambertian model fails for transparency/refraction. Adapting the shading sequence formulation to handle non‑opaque materials remains future work.
4. **Scene‑centric scaling:** RoSE is object‑centric; extending it to complex scenes with multiple objects, occlusions, and clutter is an important challenge.

## Limitations
- Exact canonical light directions and the DiLiGenT/LUCES test image indices are not provided in the main text, hindering exact replication.
- The method assumes Lambertian shading; it degrades on specular and transparent objects.
- Grayscale conversion discards chromatic information that could assist geometry in some material scenarios.
