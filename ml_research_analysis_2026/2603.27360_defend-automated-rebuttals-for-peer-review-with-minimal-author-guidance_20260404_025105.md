# Defend: Automated Rebuttals for Peer Review with Minimal Author Guidance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27360
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27360
- **Reference count:** 12
- **Primary result:** DEFEND achieves 0.77 factual correctness and 0.76 strength of refutation, significantly outperforming baselines, with high user satisfaction in a small study.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces DEFEND, a system for generating high-quality rebuttals to peer reviews with minimal author effort. Unlike direct LLM generation, DEFEND decomposes the task into a sequential pipeline: review segmentation, deficiency prediction, error-type classification, rebuttal action selection, and final generation, with optional literature retrieval. Authors intervene briefly to correct predictions at intermediate steps, ensuring accuracy while reducing cognitive load. Evaluated on an extended ReviewCritique dataset, DEFEND with gold or author-corrected intermediates substantially outperforms direct and segment-wise generation baselines in factual correctness and refutation strength. A user study with six participants reported high ratings for rebuttal quality (average 3.8–4.0/5) and reduced cognitive load (average 4.25–4.67/5).

## Method Summary
DEFEND implements a structured reasoning pipeline. Given a paper and a peer review, it first segments the review into semantic units using an LLM. For each segment, it sequentially predicts whether the criticism is deficient, classifies the error type (if deficient), selects a rebuttal action via a static mapping, and finally generates a rebuttal segment conditioned on these labels and optionally retrieved literature. Authors can review and correct the predictions before generation, thereby steering the system with minimal effort. Evaluation uses an extended subset of the ReviewCritique dataset, manually annotated with segment-level labels and gold rebuttals. Metrics include factual correctness, consistency, and strength of refutation, computed via an LLM-as-a-judge comparing generated rebuttals to gold references. The system is built around Gemini-2.0-flash. However, exact replication is hindered by the unavailability of the authors' annotation dataset and full prompts.

## Key Results
- **Factual correctness:** 0.77 (with literature augmentation)
- **Strength of refutation:** 0.76 (segment-level precision)
- **User study (N=6):** rebuttal quality 3.8–4.0/5, cognitive load reduction 4.25–4.67/5

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Breaking the rebuttal task into a sequential pipeline of well-defined subtasks improves factual correctness and targeted refutation.
- **Mechanism:** The DEFEND system first segments the review, then independently predicts deficiency, error type, and rebuttal action before generating the final rebuttal. Each step uses a focused LLM call, reducing the complexity of the overall generation and enabling precise validation.
- **Core assumption:** Decomposition allows each component to be evaluated and corrected with minimal author effort, avoiding the error propagation seen in end‑to‑end generation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "structured reasoning and author intervention... segment-wise generation and the automated sequential approach ... substantially improve factual correctness and strength of refutation."
  - [section] Section 3.3 describes the Sequential Approach (SA) pipeline with its four steps (DP, ETP, RAP, RG) and how each addresses a specific reasoning need.
  - [corpus] Neighboring works (e.g., *RbtAct*, *Paper2Rebuttal*) also adopt multi‑step frameworks, indicating a community trend toward structured rebuttal generation.
- **Break condition:** If the number of steps becomes too large, latency and author fatigue may outweigh the benefits; the pipeline must remain efficient.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Minimal author‑in‑the‑loop interventions at intermediate steps correct LLM mispredictions and drastically improve the final rebuttal without high cognitive load.
- **Mechanism:** After each prediction (deficiency, error type, action) DEFEND presents a natural‑language interpretation to the author, who can accept or quickly rectify it. This “human‑in‑the‑loop” approach ensures that key decisions are author‑verified, preventing error cascades.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs are unreliable on fine‑grained classification tasks (DP/ETP/RAP), but a few well‑placed human corrections are sufficient to steer the system toward high‑quality output.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "the author needs to only drive the reasoning process with minimal intervention, leading an efficient approach with minimal effort and less cognitive load."
  - [section] Section 4 details the interactive UI where authors review NL explanations of predictions and edit only when necessary. Section 5.2 (Table 2) shows low zero‑shot LLM performance (e.g., DP precision 0.54, RAP precision 0.28), underlining the need for human oversight.
  - [corpus] The user study reported in the abstract (avg. ratings ~4/5 on controllability and reduced load) directly supports the claim.
- **Break condition:** Should LLMs become capable of near‑perfect intermediate predictions, the added step of author verification could become redundant and decrease efficiency.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Explicit rebuttal‑action prediction provides a high‑level scaffold that guides the LLM to generate coherent, context‑appropriate responses.
- **Mechanism:** After deficiency and error‑type are set, DEFEND consults a static mapping to constrain the possible rebuttal actions (e.g., *Reject criticism*, *Refute question*). The selected action is then included in the generation prompt, giving the model a clear semantic directive.
- **Core assumption:** High‑level actions serve as effective intermediate representations that reduce ambiguity and keep the generation grounded in the intended response strategy.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "providing LLMs with explicit cues about the appropriate response in terms of deficiency, error‑type, and rebuttal action significantly improves the quality of generated rebuttals."
  - [section] Section 3.3 explains the RAP step and the mapping (Table 5); Section 5.1 shows that DEFEND with gold actions achieves 0.76 strength of refutation vs 0.12 for DRG.
  - [corpus] *RbtAct* uses “rebuttal as supervision for actionable review feedback,” highlighting the role of action‑level guidance in peer‑review assistance.
- **Break condition:** If the action taxonomy is too coarse or many‑to‑many mappings exist, the scaffold may become vague and fail to direct generation meaningfully.

## Foundational Learning
- **Task decomposition**
  - *Why needed:* Rebuttal writing involves multiple distinct reasoning dimensions (understanding the critique, identifying the flaw type, choosing a response strategy). Tackling each separately prevents the LLM from conflating these and producing inconsistent outputs.

  - *Why needed:* Providing a high‑level rebuttal action (e.g., reject, clarify) constrains the generation space and aligns the output with the author’s intent.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- Review Segmentation: LLM splits review into semantic segments.
- Deficiency Prediction (DP): Classifies segment as deficient / non‑deficient.
- Error‑type Prediction (ETP): For deficient segments, assigns coarse error type.
- Rebuttal Action Prediction (RAP): Maps error type (or non‑deficient) to a specific response action using a static table.
- Rebuttal Generation (RG): LLM produces the rebuttal segment, conditioned on paper, review segment, deficiency label, error type, and action.
- Literature Retrieval: Optional ScholarQA query for external references.
- Consolidation: Combines segment rebuttals into a final document.

### Critical path
Paper+Review → Segmentation → (DP → [ETP] → RAP → RG) per segment → Consolidation

### Design tradeoffs
- *Granularity*: Finer segmentation increases precision but adds LLM calls and author checks.
- *Gold vs predicted intermediates*: Using gold labels maximizes quality but requires author input; relying solely on predictions reduces effort but suffers from low accuracy (Table 2).
- *RAG*: Adding literature improves factual correctness marginally but introduces latency and irrelevant results.
- *Action taxonomy*: Coarser classes reduce ambiguity but may oversimplify nuanced cases.

### Failure signatures
- Segmentation merges unrelated critiques or splits a single point.
- DP produces false negatives/positives, often due to ambiguous reviewer language.
- ETP misclassification stems from overlapping error categories.
- RAP selects an action inconsistent with the intended response (e.g., *Reject criticism* when acceptance is needed).
- RG yields hallucinations or contradictions when lacking proper grounding (mitigated by literature retrieval).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Dataset extension:** Follow the paper’s protocol to take a subset of ReviewCritique, re‑segment reviews, and manually annotate deficiency, error type, rebuttal action, and mapping to gold rebuttal segments.
2. **Baseline benchmarking:** Implement DRG, SWRG, and SA (full automation) using gemini‑2.0‑flash with prompts from the git repo. Run on the extended dataset and compute segment‑level factual correctness, consistency, and strength of refutation via LLM‑as‑judge.
3. **Author‑in‑the‑loop evaluation:** Deploy DEFEND’s interactive UI, recruit participants (e.g., NLP/ML researchers), and run a user study measuring rebuttal quality, controllability, and cognitive load (5‑point Likert). Compare results against the SA‑Pred numbers to isolate the impact of minimal author guidance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** To what extent do biases in LLM-as-a-judge evaluation metrics distort the measured performance of rebuttal generation systems like DEFEND, and how can evaluation be made more robust?
- **Basis:** The Limitations section states: "Our evaluation is performed using LLM-as-a-judge. Although, we have provided the gold rebuttal in the context and using LLM as a judge is a common practice, this may introduce biases, and may not be able fully capture scientific nuances."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper acknowledges the potential for bias but does not investigate its magnitude or propose mitigation strategies. The core evaluation (Table 1) relies on this method, so the true quality gap between systems may be misestimated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A human evaluation study with domain experts scoring the same rebuttal samples, comparing results to the LLM-as-a-judge scores to quantify discrepancy and identify systematic biases (e.g., over‑penalizing nuance, under‑scoring factual corrections).

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does the augmentation with retrieved literature improve factual correctness but fail to improve the strength of refutation, and how can retrieval be optimized to support both?
- **Basis:** The Discussion section states: "We observe retrieving relevant literature and including it while generating rebuttal helps in improving the factual correctness but not strength of refutation."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper presents this as an observed outcome without analysis. It is unclear if the retrieved content is factually sound but not argumentatively useful, if the generation model fails to leverage it for refutation, or if the metric for "strength of refutation" is insensitive to literature‑grounded arguments.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An error analysis categorizing failures in literature‑augmented rebuttal generation (e.g., whether retrieved snippets are ignored, misused, or correctly used but not rhetorically impactful). Correlating retrieval relevance/quality scores with segment‑level gains in both factual correctness and refutation strength metrics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the coarse-grained error-type taxonomy be learned or optimized to reduce annotation ambiguity while preserving the signal needed for effective rebuttal action prediction?
- **Basis:** The paper created a coarse-grained taxonomy to "mitigate boundary ambiguity" (Section 3.3), yet annotator agreement for Error Type Prediction (ETP) was only 74%, with most mismatches due to "category overlap." The static mapping from error-type to rebuttal actions (Table 5) is hand‑defined.
- **Why unresolved:** The trade-off between taxonomy granularity and usability is not studied. The chosen taxonomy may still be suboptimal for the downstream task of predicting the correct rebuttal action. It is unknown if a different grouping or a data‑driven, context‑sensitive mapping would improve pipeline accuracy and reduce need for author intervention.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study comparing rebuttal quality (using DEFEND) when using the current coarse taxonomy versus alternative taxonomies (e.g., finer‑grained, or embeddings‑based soft clustering). Analysis of cases where author intervention corrected error‑type predictions to see if a better taxonomy could have automated those corrections.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the small, domain‑specific user study (N=6, NLP/ML researchers) limit the generalizability of DEFEND's reported reductions in cognitive load and its overall utility across different scientific disciplines and author skill levels?
- **Basis:** The user study involved only 6 participants, all from NLP/ML with prior rebuttal experience (Section 5.4). This sample is not diverse in terms of field, seniority, or familiarity with AI tools. The high ratings on cognitive load (Q8-10) and controllability (Q5-7) may not hold for a broader population.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper presents the user study results as supportive evidence but does not discuss sample limitations or propose plans for broader validation. The claim of "minimal cognitive load" and "efficiency" is based on a narrow, self‑reported measure from a tiny, non‑representative sample.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A larger‑scale, multi‑field user study with a stratified sample of researchers (varying disciplines, career stages, and tech‑savviness) using controlled experiments (e.g., time‑to‑completion, physiological measures of load, comparison to manual rebuttal writing) to validate the claimed benefits.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** To what extent can the sequential pipeline steps (Deficiency Prediction, Error‑type Prediction, Rebuttal Action Prediction) be automated with high accuracy to reduce author intervention, and what are the fundamental bottlenecks in each step?
- **Basis:** Table 2 shows low zero‑shot performance for all pipeline steps (DP: .53 F1, ETP: .46 F1, RAP: .16 F1). The entire DEFEND design is predicated on the need for author correction at these steps, but the paper does not analyze the root causes of failure or explore fine‑tuning/architectural improvements for these sub‑tasks.
- **Why unresolved:** The low scores are presented as a justification for the author‑in‑the‑loop design, but the limits of automation are not characterized. It is unknown if these are data scarcity issues, task complexity issues, or model capability issues, and whether targeted improvements could make the pipeline more autonomous without sacrificing quality.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed error analysis of each step, identifying common failure modes (e.g., is ETP failure due to ambiguous review text or vague error definitions?). Experiments with fine‑tuned models (vs. zero‑shot), few‑shot learning, or specialized architectures for each sub‑task to establish performance ceilings and inform where human intervention is truly indispensable.

## Limitations
- The authors' extended annotation dataset (segment-level labels) is not publicly available, hindering exact replication of results.
- Full pipeline prompts and code are missing, preventing verification of implementation details.
- Evaluation relies on LLM-as-a-judge, which may introduce bias, and the user study sample is small (N=6), limiting generalizability.
