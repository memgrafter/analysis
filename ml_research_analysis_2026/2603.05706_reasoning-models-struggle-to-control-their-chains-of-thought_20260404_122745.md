# Reasoning Models Struggle to Control their Chains of Thought

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05706
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05706
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: All frontier models show low CoT controllability (mostly <10%), far below output controllability (>60%); controllability varies with scale, RL training, reasoning length, difficulty, and monitoring awareness.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the notion of CoT controllability—the ability of a reasoning model to follow explicit constraints on its chain-of-thought (CoT) content—as a key risk factor for CoT-based monitoring. To measure it, the authors construct the CoT‑Control suite, applying nine types of constraints (e.g., keyword suppression, case changes) to a diverse set of ~14,000 problems spanning multiple benchmarks and model families. They evaluate 13 frontier models, comparing CoT controllability to output controllability and probing how factors such as model scale, RL training, reasoning effort, and situational awareness affect compliance. The results reveal a striking gap: CoT controllability is typically below 10%, whereas output controllability often exceeds 60%. Controllability improves with scale in open-weight families but degrades with more reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards, longer traces, higher effort, and harder tasks. Merely informing models they are monitored raises controllability by at most 4 percentage points, and adversarial prompt optimization fails to generalize. While current scaling trends might preserve monitorability for now, the underlying mechanisms of low controllability remain poorly understood, raising concerns for future safety.

## Method Summary
The core evaluation uses the CoT‑Control benchmark, which combines 14,076 instances drawn from GPQA, MMLU‑Pro, Humanity’s Last Exam, BFCL, and SWE‑Bench Verified. For each instance, one of nine constraint instructions (e.g., “reason without using the word ‘XOR’”, “use only uppercase letters”) is appended to the user query. Thirteen frontier models—including Claude, GPT‑5/o‑series, Qwen3, and Deepseek—are prompted with these augmented queries, and their reasoning traces are extracted according to model‑specific conventions (e.g., `<think>` tags). Compliance is scored primarily via programmatic checks (regex, exact string matching), with more complex constraints (e.g., “ignore the question”) judged by an LLM or human. The main metric is the fraction of responses that satisfy the constraint, macro‑averaged over dataset‑constraint pairs to avoid imbalance. The study also measures output controllability (constrain final answer only) and external CoT controllability (wrapping reasoning in a dedicated tag), adds situational‑awareness prompts to test monitoring effects, and explores adversarial prompt optimization. Reproducibility notes highlight uncertainties: keyword selection depends on baseline traces from eight models, and reasoning isolation heuristics (e.g., removing the final 10% of tokens) may need adaptation for different architectures.

## Key Results
- CoT controllability is consistently low across all models (most <10%), while output controllability is much higher (often >60%).
- Controllability increases with model size in open-weight families but decreases with RL from verifiable rewards, longer reasoning traces, higher reasoning effort, and greater task difficulty.
- Making models aware they are monitored yields at most a 4‑point gain; automated red‑teaming via prompt optimization does not produce generalizable improvements.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Scale improves CoT controllability in open-weight families
- **Claim:** CoT controllability increases with model size (number of parameters) for open-weight models, though it remains far below output controllability.
- **Mechanism:** Larger models possess increased capacity for instruction-following, including abstract syntactic constraints, and may develop better planning over their reasoning steps.
- **Core assumption:** CoT controllability is a skill that benefits from the same scaling pressures that improve general capabilities.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Controllability increases with model size in open-weight families”
  - [Sec. 3] “Within the same family, larger open-weight models exhibit higher CoT controllability” (page 3) and Figure 1b.
  - [corpus] Direct evidence on parameter scaling and CoT controllability is scarce; neighboring work focuses on other aspects of CoT monitorability.
- **Break condition:** If future training introduces strong penalties for CoT constraint violations or if the open-weight scaling trend saturates, the positive correlation could diminish.

### Mechanism 2: RL from verifiable rewards (RLVR) reduces CoT controllability
- **Claim:** Increased RLVR training on reasoning tasks leads to a sharp drop in CoT controllability, while output controllability degrades more slowly.
- **Mechanism:** RLVR optimizes solely for final answer correctness, providing no gradient signal that would encourage maintaining arbitrary CoT constraints. Thus, models learn to disregard constraints during reasoning.
- **Core assumption:** The RL objective is indifferent to intermediate reasoning content, implicitly pressuring the model to forfeit precise CoT control for performance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “decreases with more RL training”
  - [Sec. 5.4] “CoT controllability decreases as the amount of RL from verifiable rewards (RLVR) training increases” (page 9) and Figure 10.
  - [corpus] *Monitoring Monitorability* notes that monitorability “may be fragile under different training procedures,” consistent with RLVR-induced degradation.
- **Break condition:** If RL training incorporates process-based rewards that explicitly evaluate CoT compliance, the trend could reverse or stabilize.

### Mechanism 3: Longer reasoning and greater task difficulty lower CoT controllability
- **Claim:** CoT controllability declines as reasoning length grows and as tasks become harder (where CoT is necessary or per-question accuracy is low).
- **Mechanism:** Extended CoT traces increase the chance of accidental constraint breaches and may dilute attention away from constraint adherence. Harder problems demand more cognitive resources, leaving less capacity to monitor syntactic or semantic constraints.
- **Core assumption:** There is a capacity trade‑off between reasoning quality and strict CoT constraint maintenance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “greater task difficulty” reduces controllability.
  - [Sec. 5.1] “CoT controllability tends to decrease as task difficulty increases” (page 8); [Sec. 5.2] Figure 9 shows lower controllability with higher reasoning effort; [Sec. 5.3] Figure 35 shows a negative relationship between reasoning length and controllability.
  - [corpus] *CoT‑Valve* observes that “the reasoning path can be easily compressed under easy tasks but struggle on hard tasks,” linking difficulty to increased reasoning length and complexity.
- **Break condition:** If models acquire reliable planning or post‑hoc editing mechanisms for their CoT, length may no longer harm controllability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** *Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) monitorability* – the property that a model’s reasoning can be inspected reliably to detect misbehavior.
  - *Why needed:* The paper’s safety premise is that low CoT controllability preserves monitorability; understanding this link is essential to interpret the results.
  - *Quick check:* “What safety benefit does CoT monitorability provide?”  
    (Answer: It allows earlier, more granular detection of misaligned intentions via the reasoning trace, not just the final output.)

- **Concept:** *CoT controllability* – the ability of a model to follow explicit instructions that shape the content or style of its intermediate reasoning.
  - *Why needed:* It is the core metric introduced by the paper; the entire evaluation suite measures this construct.
  - *Quick check:* “How is CoT controllability operationalized?”  
    (Answer: By appending a constraint to a problem prompt (e.g., ‘do not use word X’) and scoring whether the model complies in its reasoning trace.)

- **Concept:** *Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)* – a training method where models are rewarded only for correct final answers, without explicit supervision on the reasoning process.
  - *Why needed:* RLVR is the primary training pressure shown to degrade CoT controllability; understanding it explains the observed trends.
  - *Quick check:* “Why might RLVR reduce CoT controllability?”  
    (Answer: Because the optimization objective ignores CoT content, the model learns to prioritize correctness over adhering to arbitrary CoT constraints.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
- **Data sources:** GPQA, MMLU‑Pro, HLE (QA); SWE‑Bench Verified, BFCL (agentic).  
- **Constraint generation:** 9 instruction types (Suppress keyword, Multiple keywords, Ignore question, Add suspicious text, Append “safe”, Insert “meow”, Lowercase, Uppercase, Alternating case).  
- **Prompt assembly:** Append constraint to *user* message (found to yield higher controllability).  
- **Model inference:** Call APIs/local models; need model‑specific templates (e.g., `<think>` tags, `analysis` channel).  
- **Response parsing:** Extract reasoning from appropriate location based on model type.  
- **Grading:** Most constraints via programmatic checks (regex, exact string matching); “ignore question” uses an LLM judge or human verification.  
- **Sanity checks:** Instruction‑understanding and self‑awareness multiple‑choice questions; also output controllability and external CoT controllability baselines.  
- **Results aggregation:** Macro‑average across (dataset, constraint) pairs to avoid imbalance.

**Critical path:**  
1. Implement a minimal pipeline: select 2 constraints and a small question subset.  
2. Build prompt generation and model inference for one model.  
3. Develop the reasoning parser for that model.  
4. Implement the corresponding programmatic grader(s).  
5. Run sanity‑check questions to confirm models understand the task.  
6. Expand to full dataset, all constraints, and additional models.  
7. Add variations (situational awareness, repeated attempts) as needed.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **User vs. system prompt placement:** User prompts boost controllability but may interfere with system‑level safety instructions; the paper uses user prompts for worst‑case assess‑ment.  
- **Grading reliability:** Programmatic checks are fast and reproducible but brittle to novel phrasing; LLM judges are flexible but costly and introduce variance.  
- **Model‑specific output formats:** Supporting each family’s reasoning channel (``, `analysis`) adds maintenance overhead but is necessary for accurate parsing.  
- **Generation length:** Longer CoTs risk truncation; cap tokens to manage compute while preserving length trends.  
- **Data imbalance:** Macro‑averaging gives equal weight to each (dataset, constraint) pair; simpler weighted averages could hide poor performance on harder domains.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **High meta‑discussion rate:** Model mentions the constraint (e.g., “I must not use overhangs”) despite being told not to.  
- **Partial compliance:** Starts with correct style (e.g., uppercase) then reverts to normal casing.  
- **Synonym circumvention:** Suppressed keyword appears in paraphrased forms.  
- **“Ignore question” evasion:** Model states “I won’t reason about the question” then proceeds to reason, or produces minimal non‑answer content.  
- **Blank or nonsensical reasoning:** Model gives up or writes gibberish, indicating inability to comply.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline evaluation:** Run the full CoT‑Control suite on all 13 models with default prompts; compute CoT controllability and compare to output controllability (expect <10% for most).  
2. **Situational awareness test:** Add monitoring‑awareness and evaluation‑awareness prompts; measure change in controllability (expect small but significant increases for some models).  
3. **Reasoning effort scaling:** For a selected model family (e.g., GPT‑OSS), vary max reasoning tokens or instruct to reason more/less; measure how controllability changes with length (expect monotonic decrease).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why do reasoning models exhibit such low CoT controllability?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "the mechanism behind low controllability is not well understood."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows strong correlations (e.g., with model scale, RL training, reasoning effort) but offers no causal explanation. Understanding the underlying mechanism is vital for predicting how CoT controllability will evolve as models improve and for designing training procedures that preserve monitorability without sacrificing capability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled training experiments that decouple factors (e.g., outcome-only vs. process-supervised RL), mechanistic interpretability studies tracing constraint propagation through model layers, and cross-architecture analyses to identify common bottlenecks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is low CoT controllability sufficient to ensure CoT monitorability under realistic adversarial incentives?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "low controllability is necessary for high confidence in the model’s monitorability, however, low controllability is not sufficient for high monitorability because monitorability could still degrade due to other mechanisms..."
- **Why unresolved:** Their evaluation uses neutral instruction-following scenarios, not cases where models actively try to evade oversight. Monitorability might still fail even with low controllability due to other failure modes (e.g., decomposition attacks, sandbagging). The relationship in adversarial settings remains unquantified.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Jointly measure CoT controllability and monitorability on models equipped with misaligned goals or deception incentives. Correlate the two across varied conditions to determine if low controllability reliably predicts robust monitorability.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does outcome‑based reinforcement learning on reasoning tasks and increased reasoning effort systematically reduce CoT controllability?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "CoT controllability decreases as the RL pressure increases" and "decreases as reasoning effort increases." The authors hypothesize: "the objective optimizes solely for CoTs that are useful for obtaining correct outcomes and does not otherwise constrain their CoT content or style."
- **Why unresolved:** The hypothesis is plausible but unproven. It is unclear whether RL actively harms controllability or simply fails to improve it, and why longer, more effortful reasoning becomes harder to control. This knowledge is essential for developing training methods that maintain both performance and oversight utility.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies adding auxiliary CoT‑compliance penalties to RL rewards; direct comparison of process‑supervised (CoT‑rewarded) vs outcome‑supervised models; analysis of training dynamics to pinpoint when and how compliance degrades, and whether model representations diverge in ways that limit controllable generation.

## Limitations
- Observational correlations (e.g., RLVR reducing controllability) may be confounded; causal mechanisms are unclear.
- The benchmark’s artificial constraints and prompt placement choices may lack ecological validity and are sensitive to CoT parsing decisions.
- Low CoT controllability is necessary but not sufficient to guarantee robust monitorability against stealthy deception or decomposition attacks.

## Confidence
**High:** Low CoT controllability gap – directly measured across 13 diverse models.  
**Medium:** Correlational trends with scale, RLVR, reasoning effort, and task difficulty – consistent but potentially confounded.  
**Low:** Extrapolations about future scaling preserving monitorability and the effectiveness of monitoring‑awareness prompts – based on limited data and may not generalize.

## Next Checks
1. **Scale replication across families:** Evaluate CoT‑Control on several open‑weight families (Llama 2/3, Mistral, Gemma) spanning 1B‑70B parameters to verify the scaling trend and locate saturation points.
2. **Causal RLVR intervention:** Fine‑tune identical base models with RLVR alone versus RL with process rewards that explicitly score CoT compliance; isolate the effect of reward shaping on controllability.
3. **Adversarial constraint diversity:** Replace benign constraints with semantically meaningful ones (e.g., “avoid uncertain language”, “emulate pedagogical style”) to test whether low controllability persists under realistic manipulation goals.
