# Semantic Sections: An Atlas-Native Feature Ontology for Obstructed Representation Spaces

## Quick Facts
arXiv ID: 2603.20867  
Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20867  
Reference count: 3  
Primary result: In obstructed representation spaces, semantic sections (transport-compatible families) perfectly recover semantic identity across contexts, while raw global-vector similarity collapses (0% recovery at threshold 0.7 for Gemma).

## Executive Summary
In neural representation spaces, holonomy—the failure of local transport maps to compose consistently around cycles—prevents a single global direction from coherently representing a feature across contexts. This paper introduces semantic sections, defined as transport-compatible families of local feature representatives over a context atlas, as the correct ontological object for such obstructed regimes. A discovery-and-certification pipeline seeds propagation, synchronizes overlaps, prunes by defect, and classifies via cycle consistency. Empirically, across Llama, Qwen, and Gemma models, semantic identity is not captured by raw global-vector similarity; section-based recovery is perfect where similarity-based recovery fails dramatically, demonstrating the necessity of the section ontology.

## Method Summary
The method operates on a pre-constructed context atlas: a graph of local charts with learned linear transport maps between overlapping charts. From a seed local representative, the pipeline propagates vectors along edges using normalized transports, synchronizes at multiply-reached charts by sign-aligned averaging, and prunes high-defect edges/charts. The remaining connected support is analyzed for cycle holonomy to classify sections as globalizable (low holonomy, cyclic support), twisted (low edge defect but significant holonomy), tree-local (acyclic support), or fragile. Near-identical sections are deduplicated based on Jaccard overlap and metric gap.

## Key Results
- For Gemma’s 14 globalizable sections (1,221 within-section pairs), raw signed cosine similarity recovers only 19.57% of pairs at threshold 0.3, collapsing to 0% at 0.7, while section-based recovery is perfect (100%).
- Semantic identity across contexts is not reliably captured by raw global-vector similarity, even when local representatives are semantically coherent within their own charts.
- Across Llama, Qwen, and Gemma at layer 16, the section census shows a mix of globalizable, twisted, and tree-local sections, with Qwen dominated by tree-local structures.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Semantic sections as transport‑compatible families
- **Claim:** In obstructed representation spaces, a feature is best modeled as a transport‑compatible family of local representatives (a semantic section) rather than a single global direction.
- **Mechanism:** A section \(s = \{s_c\}_{c \in V_H}\) assigns a normalized local representative to each chart in a connected subgraph \(H\). For each oriented edge \(u \to v\), the edge defect \(\delta_{vu}(s)\) measures misalignment (up to sign) between the transported \(s_u\) and the local \(s_v\). A section is \(\varepsilon\)‑coherent if all edge defects are small. Quotienting by sign and positive scaling reflects semantic directionality while discarding norm.
- **Core assumption:** Local feature spaces are linearly related via learned transports; semantic identity is preserved under these transports up to sign and scale.
- **Evidence anchors:** Definition 3.1 and edge defect; abstract statement introducing semantic sections.
- **Break condition:** Poor transport estimation, large pervasive holonomy, or a disconnected/sparse atlas cause high defects and failed propagation.

### Mechanism 2: Certification pipeline – propagation, synchronization, and cycle consistency
- **Claim:** Semantic sections can be systematically discovered and certified through a pipeline of seeded propagation, synchronization on multiply‑reached charts, defect‑based pruning, and cycle‑aware classification.
- **Mechanism:** Start from a seed \((r,a)\). Propagate: for admissible frontier edge \(u \to v\), compute \(\tilde{s}_v^{(u)} = T_{vu}s_u / \|T_{vu}s_u\|_2\). Synchronize: at multiply‑reached \(v\), align incoming vectors by sign and average to get \(s_v\). Prune: remove charts/edges with edge or path defect above thresholds. Extract largest connected component \(H\). Compute cycle holonomy \(\Delta_\gamma(s)\) for cycles in \(H\). Classify: globalizable if low edge/holonomy and \(\beta_1(H)\ge1\); twisted if low edge but large holonomy; tree‑local if \(\beta_1(H)=0\); else fragile. Deduplicate near‑identical sections.
- **Core assumption:** Low edge defect on cyclic support with low holonomy indicates a genuine semantic section; tree‑local coherence is weaker but still valid pathwise evidence.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4 description of pipeline; Section 3.5 on twisted sections not being mere artifacts; Section 4.4 on empirical thresholds.
- **Break condition:** Synchronization fails to reduce defects; no cyclic support meets holonomy thresholds; over‑strict thresholds yield zero certified sections.

### Mechanism 3: Section‑based identity recovery vs. raw global similarity
- **Claim:** Semantic identity across contexts is captured by section membership, not by raw global‑vector similarity, which collapses in obstructed regimes.
- **Mechanism:** For a certified section, all pairs of charts in its support are deemed the same semantic object (perfect recovery). Contrast with a baseline that labels two local vectors as the same object only if their signed cosine exceeds a threshold. The fraction of true within‑section pairs recovered by the baseline is measured across thresholds.
- **Core assumption:** If global vectors were correct, raw similarity would recover most within‑section pairs; poor recovery supports the section ontology.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract Gemma statistic; Section 5.4 Table 5 and text; Section 5.6 Table 7 aggregate recovery.
- **Break condition:** Raw similarity recovers most within‑section pairs (e.g., >90% at moderate thresholds), implying global vectors suffice and transport compatibility is unnecessary.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Context atlas and transport maps**  
  Why needed: The entire formalism treats the model’s representation space as overlapping local charts with linear transports; without this, semantic sections cannot be defined.  
  Quick check: How are chart overlaps and transport maps constructed, and why are they essential for section coherence?

- **Concept: Edge defect and cycle holonomy**  
  Why needed: These quantities quantify local transport compatibility (edge defect) and global obstruction (cycle defect). Used for pruning and classifying section types.  
  Quick check: What is the difference between edge defect (local) and cycle defect (global), and why does a small cycle defect indicate genuine globalization?

- **Concept: Sign and positive‑scaling equivalence**  
  Why needed: Feature directions are defined only up to orientation and scale. Quotienting by \(\{\pm1\}\) and positive rescaling makes section comparison robust.  
  Quick check: Why is it appropriate to identify vectors up to sign and positive rescaling when comparing across charts, and how does this affect defect calculations?

## Architecture Onboarding
Atlas builder -> Discovery engine -> Certification module -> Deduplication module -> Evaluation suite  
Critical path: Seed \((r,a)\) → propagate along edges (max depth 8) → synchronize multiply‑reached charts → defect‑based pruning → extract largest connected component \(H\) → compute cycle defects (if \(\beta_1(H)>0\)) → classify → deduplicate → output. Dependencies: propagation relies on atlas transport quality; synchronization requires multiple routes; classification needs cycle analysis; deduplication needs support sets and summary statistics.  
Design tradeoffs: Propagation depth (deeper yields larger supports but risks noise); thresholds (stricter yields cleaner sections); transport type (orthogonal proxy stable but may discard signal); synchronization passes (more passes reduce defects but may over‑smooth).  
Failure signatures: All sections have high edge defect → atlas/transport failure; no cycle‑supported sections despite many seeds → purely tree‑local regime or strict thresholds; raw similarity recovery nearly matches section recovery → claim undermined; deduplication collapses many seeds → Jaccard threshold too high or metric gap too large.  
First 3 experiments:  
1. Cross‑model census: run pipeline on Llama 3.2 3B, Qwen 2.5 3B, Gemma 2 2B at layer 16; report deduped counts by type.  
2. Exemplar profiling: pick strongest globalizable, twisted, and tree‑local sections per model; compute mean edge defect, holonomy, support size, and pairwise cross‑chart cosine similarity.  
3. Identity recovery benchmark: for each exemplar (and all sections aggregate), compute fraction of within‑section pairs recovered by raw cosine at thresholds 0.3, 0.5, 0.7; compare to perfect section recovery.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do semantic sections appear universally across neural architectures and tasks, or are they specific to decoder-only language models at layer 16?
- **Basis:** Conclusion states broader validation across models, layers, tasks remains important.
- **Why unresolved:** Evidence limited to three decoder-only models at one layer; unknown for other architectures or non‑language tasks.
- **Resolution evidence:** Systematically apply pipeline to diverse models (CNNs, ViTs, Whisper, T5) on varied tasks; report prevalence of section types across layers.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the discovery pipeline be improved to find a larger and more diverse set of semantic sections, especially where tree‑local sections dominate?
- **Basis:** Authors note pipeline is intentionally conservative; improved methods may reveal richer structure.
- **Why unresolved:** Fixed thresholds and simple synchronization may discard noisier but meaningful sections; Qwen mostly tree‑local may reflect pipeline limits.
- **Resolution evidence:** Develop alternatives (spectral clustering, GNN refinement, adaptive thresholds); compare section counts/types; more cycle‑supported sections in Qwen would validate improvements.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do semantic sections have causal influence on model behavior, and can they be edited transport‑compatibly to alter outputs?
- **Basis:** Authors note interpretability questions beyond discovery, including editing and intervention.
- **Why unresolved:** Work only discovers/certifies sections; no causal tests of whether modifying section representatives changes behavior predictably.
- **Resolution evidence:** For a globalizable section, apply a consistent transformation to all local representatives (accounting for transport); test on inputs activating the section; measure consistent output changes.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the semantic interpretation of twisted sections? Are they artifacts or context‑dependent meanings obstructed by holonomy?
- **Basis:** Twisted sections defined as low edge defect but non‑zero holonomy; claimed not mere artifacts, but semantics unexplored.
- **Why unresolved:** No analysis connecting twisted sections to actual data contexts; holonomy rotation’s semantic correlate unknown.
- **Resolution evidence:** For twisted sections, analyze context prompts in support charts; check for common themes with systematic variation (e.g., syntax, perspective); correlate holonomy rotation with such shifts.

## Limitations
- Only three decoder-only models examined at a single layer (layer 16); broader validity unknown.
- Multiple pipeline thresholds (depth, defect, holonomy, deduplication) lack justification; sensitivity unstudied.
- No comparisons to alternative alignment methods (e.g., CCA, optimal transport).

## Confidence
- Global vectors fail; semantic sections needed: Medium
- Pipeline reliable: Medium
- Sections beat raw similarity: High
