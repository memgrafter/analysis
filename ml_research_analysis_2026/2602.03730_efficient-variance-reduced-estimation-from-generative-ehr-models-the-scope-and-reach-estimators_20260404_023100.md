# Efficient Variance-reduced Estimation from Generative EHR Models: The SCOPE and REACH Estimators

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03730
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03730
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** SCOPE and REACH achieve the same AUROC as 100‑sample Monte Carlo using only 10–11 samples for hospital mortality, yielding a ∼10× reduction in inference cost without calibration loss.

## Executive Summary
Generative EHR models predict outcomes by simulating future patient trajectories via Monte Carlo (MC) sampling. This approach is computationally expensive and produces high‑variance, sparse risk estimates, especially for rare events. This paper introduces two novel estimators—SCOPE (Sum of Conditional Outcome Probability Estimates) and REACH (Risk Estimation from Anticipated Conditional Hazards)—that repurpose the next‑token probability distributions typically discarded by MC. Both estimators are theoretically unbiased; REACH is moreover guaranteed to have strictly lower variance than MC for any model and outcome. In experiments on hospital mortality prediction using MIMIC‑IV and the ETHOS‑ARES framework, SCOPE and REACH matched the AUROC of 100‑sample MC with just 10–11 samples (95% CI: [9, 11]), a tenfold inference cost reduction, while maintaining calibration. Gains for ICU admission were more modest (~1.2×), attributed to lower outcome “spontaneity.” The results substantially improve the feasibility of deploying generative EHR models in resource‑constrained settings.

## Method Summary
The work extends the ETHOS‑ARES generative EHR model, which forecasts patient timelines by sequentially sampling tokens according to the model’s next‑token distribution. The standard Monte Carlo estimator computes the fraction of generated trajectories in which an outcome token appears before a termination horizon. SCOPE enhances this by summing, for each trajectory, the conditional probabilities of the outcome token at every step up to the outcome or horizon; the final estimate is the average of these sums across trajectories. REACH instead generates outcome‑free “backbone” trajectories by masking the outcome token (setting its probability to zero) and then computes per‑backbone risk via the discrete‑time survival formula \(1 - \prod_{t}(1 - h_t)\), where \(h_t\) is the original model’s conditional outcome probability at step \(t\). Both estimators exploit the discarded next‑token probabilities, leading to lower variance. Theoretical analysis proves unbiasedness for both and shows REACH’s variance is always ≤ Monte Carlo’s (strictly so in most cases). Empirically, the authors subsample from 100 trajectories (with or without masking) to evaluate AUROC and calibration across sample sizes, and measure inference speed under optimizations such as KV caching and skip‑size = 64.

## Key Results
- For hospital mortality, both SCOPE and REACH achieve the same AUROC as 100‑sample Monte Carlo using only 10–11 samples (95% CI: [9, 11]).
- This corresponds to roughly a 10× reduction in inference cost, with no degradation in calibration (Brier score).
- For ICU admission, a less “spontaneous” outcome, the reduction is modest (~1.2×), indicating that gains depend on outcome characteristics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Standard Monte Carlo Baseline
Monte Carlo estimates outcome probability by sampling full trajectories and counting those that contain the outcome token before termination: \(\hat{P} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}\{T_O(X^{(i)}) < T_E(X^{(i)})\}\). This discards the rich conditional probability information available at each step, leading to high variance—especially for rare outcomes—where the variance scales as \(p(1-p)/n\) and the discrete step size \(1/n\) yields poorly differentiated risk estimates.

### Mechanism 2: SCOPE (Sum of Conditional Outcome Probability Estimates)
SCOPE leverages the continuous conditional outcome probabilities along each sampled trajectory: \(S_i = \sum_{t=1}^{\min(T_E, T_O)} P(X_t = O \mid X^{(i)}_{1:t-1})\). Averaging \(S_i\) over \(i\) produces an estimate with finer granularity and usually lower variance than MC. However, \(S_i\) can exceed 1, and in such cases SCOPE’s variance may become larger than MC’s unless clipping is applied (which introduces bias unless theoretical conditions hold).

### Mechanism 3: REACH (Risk Estimation from Anticipated Conditional Hazards)
REACH guarantees variance reduction by converting timeline generation into a discrete‑time survival process. It generates \(n\) “backbone” trajectories with the outcome token masked (probability set to 0), preserving the distribution of other tokens. For each backbone, it records the conditional outcome hazard \(h_t = P(X_t = O \mid \hat{X}^{(i)}_{1:t-1})\) at every step. The per‑backbone risk is \(r_i = 1 - \prod_{t=1}^{T_E} (1 - h_t)\). The final estimator \(R = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i r_i\) is unbiased and, via a bijection between outcome spaces and the law of total variance, always has variance ≤ that of MC, often strictly less.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Pre-trained generative model (e.g., ETHOS‑ARES) -> Timeline generator -> Estimator logic (MC / SCOPE / REACH) -> Post‑processing (risk score, calibration)

**Critical path**: Load model and tokenization schema; for each patient, generate \(n\) trajectories according to the chosen estimator; during generation capture next‑token distributions (for SCOPE) or compute hazards (for REACH); combine trajectory‑level results into final risk estimate.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- MC/SCOPE trajectories are reusable across outcomes; REACH requires outcome‑specific backbone generation.
- SCOPE may have higher variance than MC for high‑probability outcomes; REACH always reduces variance.
- Implementation complexity: SCOPE adds minimal overhead (summing floats); REACH requires masking and hazard extraction.
- Outcome format: Both estimators assume a single outcome token; multi‑token outcomes need preprocessing.

**Failure signatures**:
- SCOPE estimates >1.0 indicate high outcome probability; clipping would bias estimates.
- REACH variance not lower than MC suggests a masking or hazard computation bug.
- No AUROC improvement at low sample counts suggests low outcome spontaneity (e.g., ICU admission).
- Calibration curves diverge if dropout is not disabled during inference.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Markov chain replication**: Construct a simple Markov chain with known outcome probability; run many replicates with MC, SCOPE, REACH at \(n=10\); compare empirical variances and bias; confirm REACH variance < MC and SCOPE behavior matches theory.
2. **MIMIC‑IV mortality**: Reproduce the ETHOS‑ARES pipeline on one data split; plot AUROC vs sample count (1–100) for all estimators; verify REACH/SCOPE match MC@100 with ~10 samples; check 95% CI overlaps.
3. **Spontaneity sensitivity**: For multiple outcomes, compute a proxy for spontaneity (e.g., average conditional probability of outcome); plot variance reduction factor (MC var / REACH var) vs spontaneity; expect negative correlation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can "spontaneity" be formally quantified for arbitrary clinical outcomes in tokenized EHRs, and does it reliably predict the efficiency gains of SCOPE/REACH over Monte Carlo?
2. Under what precise conditions on the generative model's transition probabilities and the outcome prevalence does SCOPE's variance exceed that of Monte Carlo, and can this be predicted without empirical trial?
3. How can REACH be extended to produce time‑varying risk estimates (e.g., survival curves) rather than a single end‑of‑horizon probability, while preserving its variance‑reduction guarantees?
4. Can SCOPE and REACH be generalized to outcomes defined by multi‑token patterns (e.g., a sequence of diagnoses or a composite event), and what are the theoretical properties of such extensions?

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions require the outcome token to appear at most once and masking to preserve other token distributions; these may be violated for complex outcomes.
- Generalizability is limited to MIMIC‑IV mortality and ICU admission with ETHOS‑ARES; the spontaneity hypothesis is anecdotal, based on only two outcomes.
- Implementation details (exact tokenization, outcome token definitions, inference optimizations) are underspecified, hindering exact reproduction.
- SCOPE's variance can exceed MC's for high outcome probabilities, though this was not observed in the reported experiments.
