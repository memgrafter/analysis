# Circuit Mechanisms for Spatial Relation Generation in Diffusion Transformers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06338
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06338
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Diffusion Transformers with random token embeddings plus positional encoding develop a dedicated two-stage spatial relation circuit, while those with a pretrained T5 encoder fuse relation and object information in object token embeddings, leading to stark robustness differences to prompt perturbations despite similar in-domain performance.

## Executive Summary
The paper studies how Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) learn to generate correct spatial relations between objects in text-to-image generation. Using a controlled synthetic dataset with two objects and eight spatial relations, they train DiTs conditioned on either a pretrained T5 text encoder or random token embeddings with sinusoidal positional encoding (RTE). All models achieve high in-domain spatial relation accuracy (~84–85%), but mechanistic interpretability reveals dramatically different internal circuits.

With RTE, a two-stage circuit emerges: a specialized cross-attention head translates relation tokens into spatial gradients via positional encoding, and a second head places object shapes at the tagged locations. With T5, no dedicated heads are found; relation and object information is fused into the contextual embeddings of object tokens (especially the second shape token), which the DiT reads directly. Consequently, T5-DiT suffers a ~40% drop in spatial accuracy when minor filler words (e.g., "the") are added to prompts, while RTE-DiT remains robust. This demonstrates that the choice of text encoder fundamentally shapes the mechanism and robustness of spatial composition in DiTs.

## Method Summary
The authors train PixArt-style Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) on a synthetic dataset of 128×128 images containing two colored shapes (circle, square, triangle) placed according to one of eight spatial relations (e.g., above, left, upper right). Each image has a caption of the form "[color1] [shape1] is [relation] [color2] [shape2]", with color tokens randomly included (50% probability) and relation paraphrases. Three text encoder variants are used: (a) frozen T5-XXL, (b) random token embeddings (RTE) with sinusoidal positional encoding, and (c) RTE without positional encoding. Image latents are computed with a pretrained VAE from Stable Diffusion. DiT backbones of varying sizes (B, mini, micro, nano) are trained from scratch for 4000 epochs with AdamW (lr=1e-4, weight decay=3e-2, batch size 256). During sampling, DPM-Solver++ with 14 steps and CFG scale 4.5 is used.

Model performance is evaluated using a CV2-based pipeline that segments the generated image, classifies shape and color, and checks spatial relations with loose (8-pixel) and strict tolerances. To probe internal mechanisms, the authors record cross-attention maps during evaluation and apply "Attention Synopsis", an aggregation method that summarizes head activity across token categories (object tokens, relation words) and denoising steps. They also perform variance partitioning on T5 embeddings to quantify relation information, and conduct head ablations (removing a head’s output) and VO output injections. Key unknowns include the exact implementation of Attention Synopsis and the permutation test parameters for variance partitioning, which are not provided in the paper.

## Key Results
- All models achieve near-perfect in-domain spatial relation accuracy (~84–85%), demonstrating that both encoder types can support basic spatial composition.
- RTE-DiT develops a distinct two-stage circuit: a relation head (L2H8) creates spatial gradients aligned with the relation token, and an object head (L4H3) uses those gradients to place shapes; T5-DiT shows no such dedicated heads, instead fusing relation and object information into the second shape token’s contextual embedding.
- T5-DiT’s spatial accuracy drops by ~40% when a filler word (e.g., "the") is inserted into the prompt, while RTE-DiT’s accuracy changes by less than 5%, indicating a robustness advantage for the disentangled representation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Two‑Stage Spatial Relation Circuit in Random‑Embedding DiT
- **Claim:** In DiTs with random token embeddings plus sinusoidal positional encoding (RTE), spatial relations are generated via a dedicated two‑stage cross‑attention circuit.
- **Mechanism:** A “spatial relation head” (e.g., L2H8) uses its QK circuit to align relation‑word embeddings with image‑token positional embeddings, producing a spatial gradient (e.g., “above” yields a vertical gradient). Its VO output tags image tokens with positional information. A later “object generation head” (e.g., L4H3) reads these tags and attends to the shape token of the corresponding object, generating the object at the tagged location.
- **Core assumption:** The sinusoidal positional encoding provides an explicit coordinate basis that the relation head can directly manipulate via learned linear projections of the random relation embeddings.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “With RTE, a two‑stage circuit emerges: a specialized cross‑attention head first translates spatial relation tokens into spatial gradients… and a second head then places object shapes at the tagged locations.”
  - [section 4.2] Detailed QK circuit analysis shows L2H8’s keys align with relation words and queries with positional embeddings, forming smooth gradients.
  - [corpus] The retrieved related work focuses on external fixes (e.g., layout conditioning) rather than internal circuits; this two‑stage mechanism is a novel contribution.
- **Break condition:** Removing positional encoding from RTE (rnd DiT‑B) reduces spatial accuracy to ~20% (Tab 2), demonstrating the circuit’s dependence on explicit position signals.

### Mechanism 2: Information Fusion Circuit in T5‑DiT
- **Claim:** In DiTs with a pretrained T5 encoder, spatial relation information is not handled by dedicated heads; instead, it is fused into the contextual embeddings of object tokens (especially the second shape token) and read directly by the DiT.
- **Mechanism:** T5’s self‑attention integrates relational cues into the token representations of object mentions. The DiT’s cross‑attention then extracts both relation and object attribute information from these fused embeddings without requiring separate processing stages. Variance partitioning shows relation accounts for ~12% of variance in raw T5 embeddings of shape2, rising to ~21% after DiT’s MLP projection.
- **Core assumption:** The pretrained T5 encoder naturally encodes pairwise relations into the embeddings of entities (e.g., the second object) due to its contextualization, and the DiT is capable of decoding this mixed information.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “With T5, no such dedicated heads are found; instead, relation and object information is fused into the contextual embeddings of object tokens… which the DiT reads directly.”
  - [section 5] Ablating relation words has negligible effect, while masking shape2 reduces spatial accuracy by ~50%. Visualizations of shape2 embeddings (UMAP/t‑SNE) show clustering by spatial relation.
  - [corpus] The corpus includes work on T5’s contextual abilities, but the specific fusion mechanism for spatial relations in DiT is not documented.
- **Break condition:** Using a random encoder (even with position) eliminates this circuit; spatial accuracy drops unless explicit relation heads are present.

### Mechanism 3: Robustness through Disentangled Representations
- **Claim:** The disentangled representation in RTE‑DiT (separate relation channel) yields robustness to minor prompt perturbations (e.g., adding filler words “the”), whereas the integrated T5 representation is fragile because small context changes shift the fused token embeddings.
- **Mechanism:** In RTE, relation information flows through a dedicated head that directly maps relation tokens to spatial gradients; filler words occupy separate embeddings and do not interfere. In T5, relation cues are embedded within shape token vectors, which are sensitive to the entire token sequence; adding a filler alters those vectors, breaking the learned relation direction.
- **Core assumption:** Assumption: The disentanglement of relation information in RTE‑DiT (vs. integration in T5‑DiT) is what causes the robustness difference to filler words. This is a plausible but not yet proven causal inference.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Despite similar in‑domain performance, the T5‑based model shows a ~40% drop in spatial accuracy with minor prompt perturbations (e.g., adding ‘the’), while RTE‑based models remain robust.”
  - [section 6] “T5‑DiTs are robust to relational word ablation but sensitive to filler changes; RTE‑DiTs are sensitive to relational word ablation but robust to filler changes.”
  - [corpus] The corpus identifies prompt robustness as a broader challenge; this work pinpoints encoder design as a mechanistic cause.
- **Break condition:** For T5‑DiT, inserting an extra filler token (e.g., “the”) causes a >30% drop in spatial accuracy; for RTE‑DiT, the same perturbation changes accuracy by <5%.

  - **Why needed here:** All conditioning in DiT occurs via cross‑attention from text to image tokens; analyzing head roles requires understanding query/key/value dynamics.
  - **Quick check:** In a cross‑attention layer that conditions image tokens using text, which modality supplies the queries and which supplies the keys/values?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Critical path
1. Generate synthetic dataset and precompute VAE latents and text embeddings.
2. Train DiT while saving periodic checkpoints.
3. Evaluate on a fixed validation set using the CV2 segmentation pipeline to compute shape, color, binding, and spatial accuracies.
4. Record cross-attention maps during the evaluation runs.
5. Apply Attention Synopsis to summarize head patterns across token categories and denoising steps.
6. Identify candidate heads for spatial relation processing.
7. Conduct targeted ablations (head removal, VO injection) and manipulations.
8. Interpret results with variance partitioning and other analyses.
Checkpoints and attention logs must be synchronized.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Text encoder:** T5 provides better language grounding but obscures circuits; RTE yields clean interpretability at the cost of semantic richness.
- **Model scale:** DiT-B is reliable for stable circuits; DiT-nano fails on spatial tasks—use at least DiT-mini for mechanistic studies.
- **Synthetic data:** Enables perfect control but may not capture real‑world complexities; interpretability findings may need validation on natural images.
- **Attention aggregation:** Averaging over prompts smears specific head roles; use category‑based masks (e.g., shape→relation) as in Attention Synopsis to maintain specificity.

### Failure signatures
- **Training failure:** Spatial relation accuracy plateaus below 70% despite good shape/color accuracy → possible missing positional encoding (RTE‑no‑pos) or insufficient capacity (nano).
- **Attention synopsis results:** No clear head‑to‑head coupling (e.g., shape→relation) → model might be using a different circuit or training incomplete.
- **Perturbation test:** Adding a filler word (“the”) causes a >20% drop in spatial accuracy → likely T5‑based model; if model was expected to be RTE but shows drop, check positional encoding.
- **Variance partitioning:** Low partial R² for relation in shape2 token in T5 (<5%) → relation not properly fused; circuit may be malformed.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Reproduce RTE‑DiT circuit:** Train DiT‑B with random embeddings + sinusoidal position on 10k samples for 4000 epochs. Run attention synopsis on 168 prompts. Verify L2H8 (relation) and L4H3 (object) as top heads (Figs 4,5).
2. **Reproduce T5‑DiT fusion:** Freeze T5‑XXL, train identical DiT‑B. Confirm absence of dominant heads; perform token‑masking ablation: mask shape2 token and measure spatial accuracy drop (expected ~50%).
3. **Robustness comparison:** For both models, evaluate on two prompt templates: “shape1 is rel shape2” vs. “shape1 is rel the shape2”. Compute spatial accuracy difference; anticipate RTE change <5%, T5 change >30%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Generalization to Complex Scenes
**Question:** How do the discovered spatial relation circuits (two-stage for RTE vs. fused token for T5) generalize to more complex, multi-object scenes with varying numbers of objects, overlapping relations, and less structured prompts?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study uses a minimal dataset with exactly two objects and eight spatial relations, and notes that real-world scenarios involve greater complexity. The circuits were identified in this constrained setting, and their scalability is not tested.  
**Why unresolved:** The mechanisms found may be specific to the binary relational structure and synthetic prompt format. Real text-to-image tasks involve longer, more ambiguous prompts with more objects, which could force different circuit organizations or cause both mechanisms to fail.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Train and analyze DiTs with the same encoder variants (T5, RTE) on datasets with 3+ objects, more diverse relations (e.g., “between”, “surrounded by”), and naturalistic prompts. Use the Attention Synopsis method to see if the same specialized heads emerge or if new, more integrated circuits are required.

### Open Question 2: Generalization to Other Pretrained Encoders
**Question:** To what extent are the observed circuit differences (modular vs. fused) and robustness disparities between T5 and RTE generalizable to other pretrained text encoders commonly used in text-to-image models, such as CLIP or BERT?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper compares only T5 (a pretrained encoder) and RTE (random embeddings). It explicitly states that “the choice of text encoder fundamentally shapes the model’s mechanism and robustness” and notes that most frontier T2I models use CLIP, which was not tested.  
**Why unresolved:** CLIP and other encoders have different architectures and training objectives (e.g., contrastive vs. autoregressive). Their internal token representations and positional sensitivities may lead to yet different spatial relation circuits, or they might fall on a spectrum between T5’s fusion and RTE’s modularity.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the mechanistic interpretability pipeline (training from scratch, Attention Synopsis, ablation) for DiTs conditioned on CLIP-ViT/L, CLIP-ViT/H, and other encoders. Compare the resulting cross-attention patterns, identify any specialized heads or token fusion, and measure robustness to the same prompt perturbations.

### Open Question 3: Influence of DiT Architecture
**Question:** Does the DiT architecture itself (beyond text encoder choice) constrain or shape the possible circuits for spatial relation generation? For instance, would different DiT designs (e.g., windowed attention, different layer norms, or alternative conditioning mechanisms like cross-attention vs. adaLN) yield different relational circuits even with the same text encoder?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study uses a single DiT backbone (PixArt) for all comparisons. All differences are attributed to the text encoder, but the DiT’s own inductive biases (e.g., attention pattern distributions, residual stream properties) might interact with the text embeddings and enable or suppress certain circuit types.  
**Why unresolved:** It is unclear if the two-stage circuit in RTE-DiT is a universal solution for positional-tagged generation or if it emerges specifically from the combination of RTE’s sinusoidal tags and the PixArt DiT’s attention dynamics. A different DiT might fail to develop the same modular heads even with RTE.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Train DiTs with architectural variations (e.g., different patch sizes, presence/absence of relative position bias, or conditioning via adaptive layer norm instead of cross-attention) using both T5 and RTE encoders. Apply the same analysis to see if the RTE two-stage circuit is robustly found or if architecture dictates alternative solutions.

### Open Question 4: Representational Format of the Spatial Gradient
**Question:** What is the precise representational format of the “spatial gradient” written by the relation head (L2H8) in RTE-DiT, and how is it read by downstream heads to place objects? Is it a true geometric field in the query/key space, or a more abstract tag that gets converted to position later?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper shows that L2H8’s QK circuit aligns sinusoidal positional embeddings with relation tokens, producing smooth gradients. It also shows that injecting its VO output into positional embeddings elicits selective attention. However, the exact nature of the VO output and how it interfaces with later layers’ positional computations is not fully decoded.  
**Why unresolved:** The causal manipulation (VO injection) suggests the VO output acts as a positional bias, but the paper does not decompose the VO matrix or trace how its values are used in subsequent attention score computations or MLP updates to ultimately drive pixel placement.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Perform a detailed component-level analysis of the L2H8 VO circuit: visualize the value vectors, check if they correlate with 2D coordinate embeddings, and ablate or rotate specific dimensions to see which aspects are necessary for correct spatial tagging. Also, trace how the tagged image tokens’ representations evolve through subsequent layers to see when and how the tag is converted into a specific location signal.

### Open Question 5: Brittleness of T5’s Fused Representation
**Question:** Why does the T5-DiT’s fused representation in the `shape2` token lead to such brittleness to minor lexical perturbations (e.g., adding “the”), and can this brittleness be mitigated without sacrificing in-domain performance—perhaps through architectural tweaks or training strategies?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper demonstrates a ~40% drop in spatial accuracy for T5-DiT when adding the word “the” to prompts, while RTE-DiT remains robust. It attributes this to the T5 encoder’s contextual fusion making the relation-dependent `shape2` embedding sensitive to small context shifts.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper does not investigate whether this brittleness is an inevitable consequence of using a deep pretrained encoder that mixes all prompt tokens, or if it can be reduced. Could techniques like input augmentation (training with varied filler words), ensembling over multiple tokenizations, or modifying the DiT’s readout (e.g., attending to more tokens) improve robustness? Is there a trade-off between linguistic generalization (handling paraphrases) and robustness to superficial changes?  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically vary training data (e.g., add more lexical variations of prompts), modify the conditioning pathway (e.g., allow DiT to attend to multiple tokens instead of reading primarily from `shape2`), or introduce explicit regularization to disentangle relation information in the T5 embeddings. Then retest robustness to perturbations and measure any impact on in-domain accuracy.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility bottlenecks:** The exact implementations of Attention Synopsis and variance partitioning are not released, hindering verification of core claims.
- **Generalization scope:** Findings rely on a minimal two‑object synthetic dataset; it remains unclear if the identified circuits scale to complex, multi‑object real-world scenes.
- **Architecture specificity:** Results are tied to PixArt‑α DiT and T5‑XXL; other DiT designs or pretrained encoders may exhibit different mechanisms and robustness properties.

## Confidence
- **RTE two-stage circuit:** High
- **T5 fusion circuit:** High
- **Robustness difference (T5 vs. RTE):** Medium
- **Broader implications for DiT design (e.g., choosing encoders for robustness):** Low
