# ProGRank: Probe-Gradient Reranking to Defend Dense-Retriever RAG from Corpus Poisoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.22934  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22934  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: ProGRank reduces poisoned Top-K exposure and achieves 0.000 macro-average attack success rate on HotpotQA, NQ, and MS MARCO while preserving clean utility, with low latency (~4.7s/query).

## Executive Summary
Corpus poisoning attacks compromise retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) by inserting malicious passages that manipulate dense retrievers. ProGRank is a post-hoc, training-free defense that reranks candidates by measuring instability in probe gradients under mild stochastic perturbations. It computes representational consistency and dispersion risk penalties from a small fixed parameter subset (e.g., a LayerNorm layer) and applies them selectively to high-score candidates via a learned gate. Experiments across multiple datasets and retrievers show ProGRank nearly eliminates poisoning success in downstream generation while maintaining clean retrieval utility, with a latency of ~4.7 seconds per query—orders of magnitude faster than prior defenses like RAGuard. This establishes a favorable robustness-utility-efficiency trade-off for securing dense-retriever RAG.

## Method Summary
ProGRank operates without retraining. Given a query, it first retrieves a large buffer (B ≫ K) using the base dense retriever. A score gate estimates a high quantile of base scores to identify the decision-critical upper-tail candidates. For each selected candidate, ProGRank applies R stochastic perturbations (e.g., token dropout, encoder dropout) to the passage. For every perturbed query-passage pair, it computes gradients of the similarity score with respect to a tiny, fixed parameter subset (e.g., one LayerNorm layer). From these gradients, two penalties are derived: representational consistency (directional agreement across runs) and dispersion risk (lower-tail instability). The final adjusted score subtracts a weighted sum of these penalties, scaled by the gate value. Top-K are then selected from the adjusted scores. By focusing corrections on only those passages near the retrieval threshold and using a minimal probe set, ProGRank achieves strong robustness with low computational overhead.

## Key Results
- Achieves 0.000 macro-average judge-based attack success rate (ASR) on HotpotQA, NQ, and MS MARCO in downstream generation tasks.
- Significantly reduces poisoned passage exposure in Top-K retrievals compared to undefended and baseline defenses.
- Maintains clean utility (e.g., F1, EM) on non-poisoned data, with negligible degradation.
- Low latency of ~4.7 seconds per query, far more efficient than RAGuard (~118s/query).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Gradient Instability Signature
- **Claim**: Optimization-driven poisoned passages exhibit unstable gradient responses under mild stochastic perturbations compared to benign passages.
- **Mechanism**: ProGRank applies a perturbation operator (e.g., token dropout, encoder dropout) to each query-passage pair multiple times. It then computes probe gradients with respect to a small fixed parameter subset (e.g., a LayerNorm module). The representational consistency (Rep) penalty measures directional agreement of these gradients across runs, while the dispersion risk (DR) penalty captures lower-tail instability via a quantile of per-run deviation scores.
- **Core assumption**: Poisoned passages rely on a concentrated set of perturbation-sensitive features for high similarity scores, making their gradient responses more variable under stochastic perturbations than benign passages.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "...extracts probe gradients from a small fixed parameter subset... derives two instability signals, representational consistency and dispersion risk..."
  - [section 3.2] "...we derive two complementary statistics: representational consistency... and dispersion risk..."
  - [section 3.4] Provides a mechanistic decomposition (Eq. 14) where a concentrated component (`a_θ,ϑ`) is stochastically gated (`Z_r`), leading to lower `Rep` and higher `DR` when its norm is large and inactivation probability `ρ` is non-zero.
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak/absent; related work focuses on attack/defense paradigms, not this specific gradient instability hypothesis. Evidence is primarily from the paper's own experiments (Fig. 3, 5).
- **Break condition**: If poisoned passages are crafted to be robust to the chosen perturbation types (e.g., via adversarial training against dropout), their probe gradients may become stable, reducing signal separation.

### Mechanism 2: Score Gating for Utility Preservation
- **Claim**: Applying penalties uniformly across all candidates harms utility; focusing robustness corrections on the high-score decision-critical region preserves clean performance.
- **Mechanism**: A score-dependent gate `w_θ(q,p) = σ(s_θ(q,p) - μ_θ(q))` is computed per query, where `μ_θ(q)` is a high quantile of the base score distribution. The final defended score subtracts a weighted sum of penalties only when the gate value is high (i.e., for candidates near the Top-K threshold).
- **Core assumption**: Only passages with high base similarity scores can enter the Top-K set; penalizing low-score candidates unnecessarily distorts the ranking of benign passages that are already non-competitive for retrieval.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "...combines them with a score gate targeting high-score candidates."
  - [section 3.3] "We restrict robustness correction to the decision-critical region of the base-score distribution... Penalizing all candidates can therefore introduce unnecessary ranking changes in the low-score region."
  - [figure 3] Shows poisoned passages tend to have higher gate values, meaning stronger correction is applied where it matters for Top-K membership.
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; this is an algorithmic design choice validated by ablation (Fig. 5) showing the gated objective performs best.
- **Break condition**: If poisoning attacks successfully place malicious passages with moderately low base scores that still enter Top-K due to other high-score passages being penalized (a collusion scenario), the gate may fail to target them.

### Mechanism 3: Post Hoc Efficiency
- **Claim**: ProGRank can operate post hoc without retraining and with low computational overhead by probing a tiny parameter subset and a bounded candidate pool.
- **Mechanism**: The defense uses only a small, fixed parameter subset (e.g., one LayerNorm layer) for gradient computation, avoiding the cost of full-model gradients. In deployment, it first retrieves a large buffer (B ≫ K), estimates the gate on this pool, and applies stochastic probing only to the upper-tail candidates before final Top-K selection. This bounds overhead relative to corpus size.
- **Core assumption**: Gradient information from a localized, small parameter subset (like LayerNorm) is sufficient to capture the instability signature relevant to the retrieval decision, and the gate effectively narrows the set needing expensive probing.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "...post hoc, training-free... extracts probe gradients from a small fixed parameter subset..."
  - [section 3.5] "One may first retrieve a buffer of top-B candidates... apply stochastic probing only to the decision-critical upper-tail candidates..."
  - [table 4] Reports low latency (~4.7s/query) compared to baselines like RAGuard (~118s/query), supporting the efficiency claim.
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is indirect; efficiency claims are empirical from the paper's own timing measurements.
- **Break condition**: If the chosen probe parameter subset (e.g., a specific LayerNorm layer) is not sensitive to the poisoning attack's optimization trace, the instability signals may not separate clean from poisoned passages, rendering the defense ineffective despite low cost.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Dense Retrieval in RAG  
  - *Why needed*: ProGRank is a retriever-side defense; understanding how dense retrievers (e.g., Contriever, DPR) encode queries/passages into embeddings and rank via dot-product similarity is foundational to grasping what is being attacked and defended.  
  - *Quick check*: If a retriever uses bi-encoders with ℓ₂-normalized embeddings, what does a high similarity score indicate about a query-passage pair's semantic alignment?
- **Concept**: Gradient-Based Influence & Fisher Scores  
  - *Why needed*: ProGRank's core signal comes from probe gradients of the similarity score w.r.t. a parameter subset. This relates to using parameter gradients as a representation of an input's influence (Fisher-score viewpoint).  
  - *Quick check*: Why might computing gradients with respect to all retriever parameters be prohibitive for large-scale ranking, and how does using a small subset (like LayerNorm) mitigate this?
- **Concept**: Dropout as Stochastic Regularization / Bayesian Approximation  
  - *Why needed*: The perturbation operator `T(·)` includes dropout (token or encoder). Understanding dropout's role in inducing stochasticity and its interpretation as model uncertainty or feature noise is key to the instability hypothesis.  
  - *Quick check*: How does applying dropout at inference time (by setting the model to train mode) create a distribution over representations for a single input, and why might this reveal brittle features?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Base Retriever -> Perturbation Module -> Gradient Probe -> Penalty Calculators -> Gate Estimator -> Score Fusion -> Final Selector
- **Critical path**: Query & Candidate Pool → Base Scoring → Gate Estimation (on pool) → Select Upper-Tail Candidates (based on pool scores) → For each selected candidate: Perturbation Loop (R runs) → Gradient Probes → Penalty Calculation → Score Adjustment → Final Rerank & Top-K.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Probe layer choice (L): Lower layers may capture more generic features but less task-specific signal; higher layers may be more sensitive but also more brittle to perturbations. Paper finds L=3 works best overall.
  - Perturbation type: Token dropout is cheaper but only corrupts input; encoder dropout affects computation but may be more disruptive. Mixed perturbation is strongest but slowest.
  - Buffer size (B): Larger B increases chance of including true high-score candidates for probing but linearly increases gate estimation cost. Must balance with `K`.
  - Penalty weighting: Using equal weights for `P_dr` and `P_rep` avoids a hyperparameter but assumes their scales are comparable due to log and saturating cap transforms.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - High false positive rate on benign passages: If penalties are too aggressive on clean passages in the high-score region, clean utility (e.g., F1, EM) drops significantly.
  - No separation in penalty distributions: If histograms of `P_dr`/`P_rep` for clean and poisoned passages overlap heavily (as in early ablations without proper gating or perturbations), defense efficacy will be low.
  - Latency spike: If the candidate pool `B` is set too close to full corpus size, or `R` is too high, latency becomes impractical (>10s/query).
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline retrieval utility**: Run the undefended retriever on clean queries to establish baseline performance (e.g., Top-K accuracy, F1/EM on downstream) and distribution of base scores to inform gate quantile setting.
  2. **Perturbation sensitivity sanity check**: For a few known clean and poisoned (from a held-out attack) query-passage pairs, manually inspect `Rep` and `DR` values and the effect of `R` and perturbation type. Confirm poisoned passages tend to have lower `Rep` and higher `DR` on average.
  3. **Ablation on gated vs. ungated fusion**: Compare full `˜s` (Eq. 13) against variants without `w` (uniform penalty) and without one penalty type. Verify that gating prevents clean utility drop and that both penalties contribute (Fig. 5 in paper).

## Limitations
- The core instability hypothesis (that poisoned passages exhibit unique probe-gradient instability) has medium confidence; evidence is primarily from the paper's own experiments on specific datasets/retrievers, with weak external corpus validation and no test against adaptive attacks.
- The score gating assumption (only high-score tail is decision-critical) has medium confidence; validated by ablation but may fail if attacks craft poisoned passages with moderate base scores to exploit the gate.
- Probe parameter choice (e.g., one LayerNorm layer) is efficient but efficacy depends on that subset capturing the signal; medium confidence due to limited testing of alternative parameter types.

## Confidence
- **Efficiency claim (low latency)**: High confidence, supported by empirical timing measurements.
- **Instability signal separation**: Medium confidence, based on paper's experiments without extensive cross-architecture validation.
- **Score gating utility preservation**: Medium confidence, based on ablations but untested against sophisticated adaptive attacks.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture generalization**: Apply ProGRank to retrievers with drastically different encoders (e.g., distilled BERT vs. FNet) and measure separation in Rep/DR distributions.
2. **Adaptive attack stress test**: Craft a poisoning attack optimized to minimize ProGRank's instability penalties (e.g., via gradient-based training including probe-gradient variance) and evaluate ASR degradation.
3. **Probe substituent ablation**: Replace the LayerNorm probe with other small subsets (e.g., first linear layer, single attention head) and measure efficacy drop; sharp drop would indicate dependence on LayerNorm properties.
