# MedArena: Comparing LLMs for Medicine-in-the-Wild Clinician Preferences

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.15677
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15677
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking achieved the highest Bradley‑Terry rating (1128), followed by Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT‑4o.

## Executive Summary
MedArena introduces an interactive evaluation platform that captures real‑world clinician preferences by having licensed medical professionals submit queries and compare anonymized responses from pairs of large language models (LLMs). Analysis of 1,571 pairwise preferences across 12 commercially available models reveals that clinicians most frequently prefer responses with greater depth and detail or clearer presentation, rather than those with higher factual recall alone. The resulting model rankings, led by Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking, remain stable even after adjusting for response style, indicating that preferences reflect substantive clinical utility. This approach diverges from static benchmarks, where only about a third of queries align with traditional factual‑recall tasks, and highlights the importance of evaluating LLMs on treatment selection, documentation, patient communication, and multi‑turn conversations.

## Method Summary
The researchers built a web platform where authenticated clinicians submit medical questions and, for each query, two randomly selected LLMs generate responses that are displayed simultaneously and anonymously. Clinicians then indicate a preference (A, B, Tie, or Neither) and may provide a free‑text reason. Over the course of data collection, 12 models (including Gemini, GPT, and Llama variants) were queried via their public APIs. To aggregate the pairwise comparisons into a global ranking, the authors fitted a Bradley‑Terry model using maximum likelihood estimation. They further extended the model with covariates capturing stylistic differences (e.g., response length, use of headers, bolding, citations) and used bootstrap resampling to assess the robustness of rankings and the significance of style effects. The methodology ensures that the evaluation reflects nuanced clinical utility while controlling for confounding presentation factors.

## Key Results
- **Top models**: Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking (Bradley‑Terry rating 1128), Gemini 2.5 Pro, and GPT‑4o.
- **Query diversity**: Only 33% of clinician queries matched traditional factual‑recall benchmarks like MedQA; the majority involved treatment selection, clinical documentation, or patient communication, with about 20% consisting of multi‑turn conversations.
- **Preference drivers**: Clinicians most often preferred responses offering greater depth and detail or clearer presentation; rankings remained stable after statistical adjustment for response length and formatting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The evaluation mechanism leverages pairwise comparative judgments from clinicians, which are aggregated using a Bradley‑Terry model to produce a global ranking. By anonymizing model identities and presenting responses side‑by‑side, the platform minimizes brand bias and focuses evaluation on relative response quality. The Bradley‑Terry framework scales to many models and sparse comparisons, while covariate adjustment for stylistic features (e.g., bolding, list formatting) separates presentation effects from substantive clinical utility. This design effectively captures nuanced clinician preferences in a realistic setting, though the paper does not analyze the cognitive basis for why certain response attributes (e.g., depth, clarity) drive preferences.

## Foundational Learning
- **Pairwise Preference Elicitation**  
  *Why needed*: Captures relative model quality in a blind test without requiring absolute scores, mimicking clinical decision contexts.  
  *Quick check*: Confirm that each query triggers a random pair of models and that the user interface presents two anonymized responses side by side.

- **Bradley‑Terry Model**  
  *Why needed*: Aggregates pairwise comparisons into a global ranking via maximum likelihood, scaling to many models and sparse matchups.  
  *Quick check*: After fitting, verify convergence and that predicted win probabilities align with observed win rates across held‑out pairs.

- **Confounder Adjustment via Extended Bradley‑Terry**  
  *Why needed*: Separates substantive clinical quality from superficial stylistic features (e.g., bolding, list formatting) that could bias preferences.  
  *Quick check*: Include style deltas as covariates; bootstrap to test significance and monitor shifts in rankings after adjustment.

- **Clinician Authentication & Qualification**  
  *Why needed*: Ensures that preferences are expressed by licensed professionals, maintaining evaluation validity.  
  *Quick check*: Test Doximity OAuth and NPI verification flows; confirm that only valid clinicians can submit preferences.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Clinician (authenticated) -> Frontend UI -> Backend Server -> Model APIs (A, B, ...) -> Streaming responses -> Preference capture -> Database.

**Critical Path**  
1. Clinician authenticates via Doximity/NPI.  
2. Clinician submits a query (single- or multi-turn).  
3. Backend randomly selects two distinct models.  
4. Backend sends the query to both model APIs concurrently.  
5. Responses are streamed back to the frontend and displayed anonymized.  
6. Clinician records a preference (A/B/Tie/Neither) and optional reason.  
7. Data (query, model pair, responses, preference) is stored.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Random pairing ensures broad coverage but can yield sparse comparisons for some model pairs, affecting stability of Bradley‑Terry estimates.  
- Simultaneous streaming requires handling differing latencies and may need timeouts; however it provides a fair comparison by presenting full context.  
- Using public APIs simplifies integration but imposes rate limits, potential version drift, and limited control over generation parameters.  
- Excluding PHI simplifies compliance but prevents evaluation on data‑rich, realistic cases.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Many model pairs receive fewer than 5 comparisons, leading to high variance in rankings.  
- Disparate response times cause clinicians to wait, possibly biasing toward faster models.  
- Authentication failures block participation, reducing sample size.  
- API errors or timeouts result in missing responses, causing query abandonment.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Pair Coverage Audit**: After ~500 preferences, compute the distribution of model pairings; if some pairs are underrepresented, adjust random selection weights or increase data collection.  
2. **Latency Impact Study**: Measure correlation between model response time and preference; if significant, implement UI progress indicators or consider timeouts.  
3. **Style Neutrality Test**: Present identical responses with varied formatting to a small clinician group to quantify style bias and validate the need for confounder adjustment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How can LLM evaluations be safely expanded to incorporate real patient data (PHI) to assess performance on complex, data‑rich clinical cases? The paper acknowledges that excluding PHI limits evaluation on such scenarios because real‑world decisions rely on longitudinal patient data, labs, and imaging. Resolving this requires privacy‑preserving techniques (e.g., federated learning, differential privacy, synthetic data) that enable evaluation on de‑identified yet clinically rich datasets.

### Open Question 2
To what extent do subjective clinician preferences (e.g., for “depth and detail” or “clarity”) align with objective measures of clinical correctness and patient safety outcomes? The study notes that preferences reflect perceived utility, not ground‑truth correctness, and warns that incorrect but persuasive answers could be over‑preferred. Evidence needed includes correlation of MedArena ratings with expert adjudication of accuracy and, eventually, clinical outcome studies.

### Open Question 3
How should evaluation frameworks be designed to properly assess LLM performance on multi‑turn, iterative clinical conversations? About 20% of MedArena queries are multi‑turn, a mode poorly captured by static benchmarks. Resolution requires standardized open‑ended multi‑turn benchmarks with curated conversation trees and metrics for contextual consistency and adaptive reasoning.

### Open Question 4
Can the observed influence of stylistic features (e.g., bolding, list formatting) on clinician preference be separated from substantive quality, and should evaluation scores be normalized for such presentation effects? Bold text showed a significant positive association with preference, but style‑controlled rankings remained stable. Controlled experiments where identical content is presented with different formatting are needed to isolate pure style bias and inform metric design.

### Open Question 5
Given small matchup sample sizes and exclusion of specialized medical LLMs, how can the community achieve statistically reliable and comprehensive evaluations of the full LLM ecosystem used in medicine? The paper notes limited comparisons for newer models and the absence of institution‑adapted LLMs. A hybrid ecosystem combining expanded clinician participation, automated pre‑screening, and privacy‑compliant evaluation of proprietary tuned models could address these gaps.

## Limitations
- Raw query‑level data (preferences, responses) is not public; any reproduction must recruit new clinicians, risking demographic and temporal bias.
- The exact model versions, API endpoints, and generation parameters (e.g., temperature, max tokens) are unspecified, so reproduction may differ from the original data collection.
- Clinician engagement is self‑selected and passive, leading to sparse pairwise comparisons for some model combinations and potentially unstable Bradley‑Terry estimates.

## Confidence
- **Model rankings (Bradley‑Terry ratings)**: *Medium* – robust methodology but raw data not available for independent verification.
- **Query distribution and preference reasons**: *Medium* – based on self‑reported categorizations without auditable raw logs.
- **Style confounder analysis**: *High* – bootstrap‑based extension of the Bradley‑Terry model provides a rigorous adjustment.

## Next Checks
1. **Replication with exact configurations**: Collect new preferences using the same model versions/parameters (if disclosed) and matched clinician specialties; compute Spearman correlation of resulting BT rankings with those reported.
2. **Isolating style bias**: Conduct a controlled experiment presenting identical model responses with varied formatting (with/without bolding, lists, headers) to blinded clinicians to measure pure style effects on preference.
3. **Task complexity audit**: Manually label a sample of 200 queries using benchmark taxonomies (e.g., MedQA, clinical reasoning frameworks) to validate the claimed divergence from factual recall tasks.
