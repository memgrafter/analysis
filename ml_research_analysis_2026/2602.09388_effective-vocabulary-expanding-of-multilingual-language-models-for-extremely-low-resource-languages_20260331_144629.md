# Effective vocabulary expanding of multilingual language models for extremely low-resource languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09388
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09388
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Outperforms random initialization baseline with average improvements of 0.54% in POS tagging and 2.60% in NER across seven extremely low-resource languages, with up to 6.68% gain in NER for some languages, while preserving source language performance.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a method to extend multilingual pretrained language models (mPLMs) to previously unsupported, extremely low-resource languages. The approach improves vocabulary initialization during continued pre‑training by first screening a source-language-biased subset of the original mPLM vocabulary, then using bilingual dictionaries to align source and target word embeddings, and finally initializing new target subwords via a weighted average of similar source embeddings. Experiments on seven languages show consistent gains in POS tagging and NER over random initialization, robustness to corpus choice, and no significant degradation in English performance.

## Method Summary
Given a base mPLM (e.g., mBERT), a high‑resource source language (English), and a target low‑resource language, the method proceeds in four stages. First, a source‑biased vocabulary set \(V_s\) is obtained by intersecting the mPLM’s vocabulary with that of a large monolingual English model (fastText). Second, monolingual fastText embeddings are trained for both languages and aligned via an orthogonal Procrustes transformation using a bilingual dictionary. Third, the fastText subword composition mechanism generates embeddings for all tokens in \(V_s\) and for a new target‑language vocabulary \(V^t\) (obtained by training a WordPiece tokenizer on the target corpus). For each new token in \(V^t\), its embedding is initialized as a softmax‑weighted average of the top‑k most similar source tokens from \(V_s\) in the aligned space, with a fallback to random sampling if necessary. Finally, the mPLM’s embedding matrix is expanded with these initializations and the model is continued pre‑training on target‑language data via masked language modeling before task‑specific fine‑tuning.

## Key Results
- Average improvement of **0.54%** in POS tagging macro‑F1 over random initialization across seven languages.
- Average improvement of **2.60%** in NER macro‑F1, with a maximum gain of **6.68%** for a single language.
- No significant degradation in English performance after continued pre‑training.
- Results are robust to the choice of target‑language corpus (MADLAD‑400 vs. Wikipedia).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Screening a source-language-biased subset from the mPLM's vocabulary prevents the dilution of cross-lingual alignment signals during vocabulary expansion.
- **Mechanism:** The mPLM's vocabulary is disproportionately allocated to the high-resource source language (English) due to corpus imbalance. By intersecting the mPLM's vocabulary \(V^m\) with a high-quality monolingual model's vocabulary \(V_{s1}\), a curated set \(V_s\) is obtained. This set is asserted to more purely represent the source language's embedding space, providing a cleaner anchor for subsequent alignment with the target language.
- **Core assumption:** A monolingual model trained on large-scale, high-resource language data has a well-calibrated vocabulary \(V_{s1}\) that captures the language's core semantic units. Overlap with \(V^m\) identifies the portion of the mPLM's vocab that is most reliably English‑centric.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We then screen out a subset from the model’s original vocabulary, which is biased towards representing the source language (e.g. English)."
  - [section 3.1] "we assume that there exists a monolingual PLM... This monolingual model is trained on large-scale corpora of this language, and its vocabulary \(V_{s1}\) being well set and represented. Hence, we can screen out a subset from the multilingual model’s vocabulary \(V^m\) that overlaps the monolingual model’s vocabulary, which is used to represent the source language, that is \(V_s = V_{s1} \cap V^m\)."
- **Break condition:** The method would break if the monolingual model's vocabulary \(V_{s1}\) has poor coverage of the mPLM's English-relevant tokens (low intersection), making \(V_s\) an ineffective or non-representative filter. This is implicitly noted in the analysis of languages with small bilingual dictionaries (Amharic, Sinhala) where performance dips.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Aligning monolingual fastText embeddings via bilingual dictionaries creates a shared, approximate cross-lingual space that enables similarity computation despite non-isomorphic source/target linguistic structures.
- **Mechanism:** Separate fastText embeddings are trained on large monolingual corpora for source (\(W^s\)) and target (\(W^t\)) languages. An orthogonal transformation (Procrustes) is applied using a bilingual dictionary to align \(W^t\) to \(W^s\). The paper explicitly relaxes the assumption of strict isomorphism between the embedding spaces.
- **Core assumption:** A bilingual dictionary provides a set of anchor word pairs whose embeddings should be maximally similar after alignment. This anchor set is sufficient to linearly align the broader embedding spaces approximately.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] "Using large-scale monolingual corpora, we train word embeddings for both the source and target languages... Then, utilizing the bilingual dictionary, we adopt the orthogonal method to align the word embeddings... Note that when aligning word embeddings across different languages, we relax the assumption of isomorphism between the embedding spaces of the source and target languages."
  - [section 2.2] Discusses multilingual representation alignment via bilingual dictionaries as a common approach, citing prior work [31, 36].
- **Break condition:** Alignment quality degrades catastrophically if the bilingual dictionary is very small, noisy, or does not cover frequent/functional words. The paper identifies this as a key limitation: "the performance of the extended models will be affected if the bilingual dictionary is small." Performance drops for Amharic and Sinhala are attributed to having fewer than 1,000 bilingual pairs.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Initializing new target-language subword embeddings via a weighted average of the top‑k most similar source‑language embeddings (from \(V_s\)) places them in a region of the mPLM's embedding space that is functionally compatible with its existing parameters.
- **Mechanism:** A similarity matrix \(S\) is computed between the aligned source subword embeddings (\(U_s\), which includes both words and n‑gram fragments) and the target subword embeddings (\(U_t\)). For each target subword \(y\) in the expanded vocabulary, its embedding \(e_t^y\) is initialized as a softmax‑weighted sum (\(\exp(S_{x,y}/\tau)\)) of the embeddings \(e_s^x\) for the top‑k most similar source subwords \(x \in \omega(y)\). The temperature \(\tau\) controls the sharpness of the weighting.
- **Core assumption:** Subwords (or words) with high cross‑lingual similarity (e.g., cognates, similar function words, shared morphological fragments) should have semantically and syntactically analogous roles in their respective languages, and thus should occupy similar regions in the embedding space of the mPLM. This allows the model's existing transformer layers and MLM head to process the new tokens effectively from the start of continued pre‑training.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...leverages bilingual dictionaries to align source and target word embeddings, computes cross‑lingual similarity to initialize the new vocabulary embeddings..."
  - [section 3.4] Details the exact weighted average formula. "Through the above steps, the initial representation for the target language vocabulary, \(E_t\), will be approximately in the same embedding space as the vocabulary representations in mPLM model."
  - [section 5.1] Empirical results show consistent improvement over random initialization (average +0.54% POS, +2.60% NER), with large gains (e.g., +6.68% NER for one language). This is the primary evidence that this initialization mechanism works.
- **Break condition:** The similarity matrix \(S\) is noisy or misleading (e.g., for languages with different scripts, typologies, or where the fastText embeddings align poorly). If \(k\) is too small, the initialization may be under‑determined; if too large, it may introduce noise from less similar source tokens. The fallback to sampling from a normal distribution \(N(E[E_s], Var[E_s])\) if \(e_t^y\) is zero indicates that the method can fail to find suitable source anchors for some target subwords.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Multilingual Vocabulary Bias in mPLMs
- **Why needed here:** The entire method hinges on the observation that an mPLM's shared subword vocabulary is not uniformly distributed across its supported languages. High-resource languages (like English) dominate the vocabulary. This bias creates a large pool of "source-language-biased" tokens (\(V_s\)) that can serve as reliable anchors for cross‑lingual transfer.
- **Quick check:** If you examined the token frequencies of mBERT's vocabulary across its Wikipedia training data, what pattern would you expect to see regarding English tokens vs. tokens from lower-resource languages?

### Concept: Subword Tokenization & Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) Representation
- **Why needed here:** The method expands the vocabulary with *subwords* (via WordPiece). New subwords are not in the original mPLM. The paper uses fastText's n‑gram composition principle to compute embeddings for these new subwords (\(U_t\)) from the aligned monolingual word embeddings (\(W^t\)). This is critical because the similarity computation (\(S = U_s U_t^T\)) requires embeddings for *all* subwords in the source filter set (\(V_s\)) and the target expansion set.
- **Quick check:** How does fastText's approach of summing n‑gram embeddings allow it to generate representations for words (or subwords) not seen during its training?

### Concept: Cross‑lingual Word Embedding Alignment
- **Why needed here:** The core initialization requires a mapping from target‑language concepts (in \(U_t\)) to source‑language concepts (in \(U_s\)). The orthogonal Procrustes alignment step creates a linear map that brings \(W^t\) into the same vector space as \(W^s\), enabling the subsequent cosine similarity computation (\(S\)). The relaxation of the isomorphism assumption is important for typologically distant languages.
- **Quick check:** What is the objective function of orthogonal Procrustes alignment, and why is an orthogonal transformation (rotation/reflection) preferred over a general linear transformation for this task?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
1. **Source Vocabulary Filter (\(V_s\)):** Intersection of mPLM's vocab \(V^m\) and a high-quality monolingual model's vocab \(V_{s1}\). Outputs a biased‑but‑clean source anchor set.
2. **Monolingual Embedding Trainers (fastText):** Two instances. One trained on large source‑language corpus → \(W^s\). One on target‑language corpus → \(W^t\).
3. **Bilingual Dictionary Loader:** Provides seed word pairs for alignment. Used by the aligner.
4. **Cross‑lingual Aligner (Orthogonal Procrustes):** Takes \(W^s\), \(W^t\), and dictionary. Outputs transformed \(W^t_{\text{align}}\) and transformation matrix \(M\) (where \(W^t_{\text{align}} = W^t M\)).
5. **Subword Embedding Computator (fastText‑style):** Takes tokenizers (\(TOK_s\), \(TOK_t\)) and aligned embeddings (\(W^s\), \(W^t_{\text{align}}\)). Computes \(U_s\) (embeddings for all n‑grams in \(V_s\)) and \(U_t\) (embeddings for all n‑grams in the new target vocabulary \(V^t\)).
6. **Similarity Matrix & Initializer:** Computes \(S = \text{normalize}(U_s) \times \text{normalize}(U_t)^T\). For each token in \(V^t\), finds top‑k most similar in \(V_s\) via \(S\). Initializes its embedding in the mPLM's new output embedding matrix \(E_t\) via softmax‑weighted sum of corresponding \(E_s\) embeddings (from the mPLM's original embedding matrix for tokens in \(V_s\)). Handles zero‑vectors via normal sampling.
7. **Vocabulary Expander & Continued Pre‑trainer:** Integrates \(V^t\) and \(E_t\) into the mPLM (e.g., mBERT). Runs standard MLM on target‑language corpus.

### Critical path
1. Train \(W^s\) and \(W^t\) (parallelizable).
2. Align \(W^t\) to \(W^s\) using dictionary → get \(W^t_{\text{align}}\).
3. Compute \(U_s\) and \(U_t\) from \(W^s\), \(W^t_{\text{align}}\) and tokenizers.
4. Compute \(S = U_s U_t^T\).
5. For each new token \(y\) in \(V^t\): find top‑k \(x\) in \(V_s\) by \(S_{x,y}\). Compute \(e_t^y = \text{weighted\_avg}(e_s^x)\).
6. Initialize final embedding matrix row for \(y\) with \(e_t^y\).
7. Expand mPLM vocabulary and embedding matrix. Continue pre‑training.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Vocabulary Size (\(|V_s|\), \(|V^t|\)) vs. Computation:** Screening \(V_s\) drastically reduces the size of the similarity matrix \(S\) (from \(|V^m| \times |V^t|\) to \(|V_s| \times |V^t|\)). A larger \(V_s\) may improve coverage but increases \(S\) computation cost. The paper finds a sweet spot exists and more is not always better.
- **Dictionary Quality vs. Alignment:** Alignment fidelity is directly tied to dictionary size/quality. The method is vulnerable for languages with tiny dictionaries (Amharic, Sinhala). A fallback (e.g., unsupervised alignment) is mentioned but not implemented.
- **\(k\) (top‑k neighbors) vs. Noise:** Small \(k\) risks no similar source token found (leading to fallback sampling). Large \(k\) averages in less similar tokens, potentially polluting the initialization. The paper fixes \(k=10\) without extensive ablation.
- **fastText Subword Composition vs. Full Word:** Using n‑gram composition allows representing any new subword, but may produce less coherent vectors for highly language‑specific glyphs or non‑Latin scripts compared to a word‑level approach.

### Failure signatures
1. **Poor dictionary:** Significant performance drop for languages with <1k dictionary pairs (Amharic POS/NER, Sinhala POS vs. Random init). The similarity matrix is untrustworthy.
2. **Inadequate source vocabulary set:** Using too small a \(V_s\) (e.g., 3k words) fails to outperform random init consistently (Figures 3 & 4). The anchor set is not representative enough.
3. **Source language degradation:** If continued pre‑training catastrophically overwrites the mPLM's English knowledge, performance on English tasks would plummet. The paper reports "no significant degradation," suggesting the initialization and MLM objective preserve core knowledge. A large drop would indicate the new embeddings or training regime introduced destructive interference.
4. **Zero‑vector fallback overuse:** If many target subwords yield zero vectors after weighted averaging (due to no overlapping n‑grams), they are randomly initialized. If this happens frequently, performance will regress toward the random baseline. Check the proportion of fallback‑initialized tokens.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Sanity Check - Vocabulary Overlap:** Compute \(|V_s|\) (screened source vocab size) and \(|V^t|\) (expanded target vocab size) for your target language. Verify \(V_s\) is a substantial subset (paper: ~13k+) and \(V^t\) is ~21k–25k. A minimal \(V_s\) (<5k) predicts poor performance.
2. **Dictionary Coverage Audit:** For your target language's tokenizer vocabulary \(V^t\), calculate the percentage of tokens that have at least one n‑gram present in \(W^t_{\text{align}}\) (i.e., \(U_t\) will be non‑zero). A very low percentage (e.g., <70%) indicates the subword composition fallback will be heavily used, risking random‑init‑like performance.
3. **Similarity Matrix sanity:** Pick 50 random tokens from \(V^t\). For each, retrieve the top‑5 most similar source tokens from \(V_s\) using \(S\). Manually inspect a sample. Do the pairs seem semantically/functionally plausible (e.g., common suffixes, function words, cognates)? If they are nonsense (e.g., matching "the" to a random target glyph), the alignment \(W^t_{\text{align}}\) is broken, likely due to a poor dictionary or fastText training issue.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Question:** How can we construct larger, higher‑quality bilingual dictionaries for extremely low‑resource language pairs to improve cross‑lingual alignment?
  - *Basis in paper:* The authors state that "the performance of the expanded models will be affected if the bilingual dictionary is small" and note the necessity of "larger, higher‑quality bilingual dictionaries" as a limitation.
  - *Why unresolved:* Current dictionaries (e.g., used for Sinhala with only 0.2k sentence pairs) are limited, directly impacting the quality of vocabulary initialization via cross‑lingual similarity.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Comparative experiments using automatically expanded dictionaries (e.g., via monolingual corpora or iterative projection) versus existing ones, measuring effects on initialization quality and downstream task performance.

- **Question:** How can multilingual PLMs be extended to support multiple extremely low‑resource languages simultaneously without detrimental interference or catastrophic forgetting?
  - *Basis in paper:* The authors’ future work plans to "design new methods for extending mPLMs to simultaneously support the processing of multiple extremely low‑resource languages."
  - *Why unresolved:* Current method extends one language at a time; scaling to many may cause vocabulary bloat, interference in shared parameters, or degradation in both new and existing language performance.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Developing joint vocabulary expansion and continued pre‑training protocols for multiple languages, then evaluating on a diversified set of low‑resource languages for interference patterns and relative performance drops.

- **Question:** What specific corpus characteristics (beyond size)—such as domain diversity, noise levels, or linguistic coverage—most effectively optimize continued pre‑training for extremely low‑resource languages?
  - *Basis in paper:* Experiments show corpus source matters: MADLAD‑400 improves POS tagging while Wikipedia improves NER for some languages, indicating attributes beyond size influence outcomes, yet no analysis identifies these drivers.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper observes performance differences but does not dissect *why* one corpus benefits one task over another, leaving key factors (e.g., genre, preprocessing, lexical diversity) unexplored.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled ablation studies varying corpus attributes (e.g., cleaning, domain mix, sentence length distribution) while holding size constant, correlating these with downstream task gains.

- **Question:** How can the screening of source‑language‑biased vocabulary be made more precise to maximize the quality of initialized target‑language embeddings?
  - *Basis in paper:* The authors note that "a larger source language vocabulary set does not necessarily lead to better performance" and that the "quality of the vocabulary set also affects the models’ performance," calling for "more precise measure[s] to screen out a high‑quality source language vocabulary set."
  - *Why unresolved:* Screening currently relies on overlap with a monolingual model’s vocabulary, which may not optimally capture subsets that best represent transferable source‑language knowledge.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Designing and evaluating alternative screening metrics (e.g., based on cross‑lingual similarity confidence, frequency in source data, or alignment stability) and correlating with downstream task performance.

- **Question:** What evaluation benchmarks are essential for comprehensively assessing extended mPLMs on extremely low‑resource languages beyond POS tagging and NER?
  - *Basis in paper:* The authors highlight that "extremely low‑resource languages often lack diverse evaluation datasets, such as those for reading comprehension, semantic parsing, even document classification tasks."
  - *Why unresolved:* Without varied benchmarks, model capabilities across linguistic phenomena (e.g., coreference, discourse, syntax) remain unknown, limiting interpretation of improvements.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Creating new datasets for underrepresented tasks in a subset of the studied languages, then evaluating extended models to identify strengths, weaknesses, and task‑specific transfer patterns.

## Limitations
- Exact bilingual dictionary source and availability are unclear; performance is highly sensitive to dictionary size (e.g., Amharic, Sinhala suffer with <1k pairs).
- Tokenizer training details (min frequency, special tokens, case handling) are unspecified, affecting the composition of the expanded target vocabulary.
- Random baseline initialization scheme (distribution, seed) is not described, potentially impacting comparison fairness.
- The ad‑hoc fallback for zero‑vector subwords (normal sampling) may degrade performance if frequently triggered, especially for languages with poor n‑gram coverage.

## Confidence
- **Outperforms random initialization:** Medium (average gains modest; not uniform across languages).
- **Robustness to corpus selection:** High (explicit comparison shows consistent results).
- **No English degradation:** High (paper states no significant drop, likely based on held‑out evaluation).
