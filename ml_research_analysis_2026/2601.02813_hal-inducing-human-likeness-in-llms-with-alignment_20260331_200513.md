# HAL: Inducing Human-likeness in LLMs with Alignment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.02813  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02813  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** HAL-aligned Qwen2.5-14B achieves 61.78% win-rate in Chatbot Arena and Elo 1556.97, outperforming its base model and GPT-4o-mini, with no systematic drop in emotional intelligence benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Human conversational human-likeness lacks a clear, measurable definition, and prior improvements rely on scaling or broad supervised training rather than targeted alignment. HAL addresses this by deriving a compact set of 16 interpretable conversational traits (HL16Q) from contrastive Turing-test dialogues. These traits are rated by a fixed LLM judge, combined via logistic regression weights into a transparent scalar reward, and used to create preference pairs for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Alignment with this reward consistently increases HL16Q scores across model scales (1B–72B) and, in a large-scale human evaluation, yields a significant win-rate over base models and a commercial system (GPT-4o-mini), while preserving performance on emotional intelligence benchmarks.

## Method Summary
HAL’s pipeline begins with a Turing test dataset of human-AI dialogue pairs. An LLM judge (GPT-5) generates verdicts and natural-language reasons for its decisions. These reasons are embedded, clustered, and summarized into a fixed list of 32 trait statements, later reduced to the top 16 by logistic regression weight magnitude (HL16Q). To score a dialogue, the same judge rates agreement with each HL16Q statement on a 1–5 Likert scale. Pre-trained logistic regression weights (from classifying human vs. AI dialogues using these ratings) are applied to the rating vector to produce a continuous HL16Q score: `Σ(ai * wi) + b`. For DPO training, synthetic medical dialogues are generated by various models, scored, and filtered into preference pairs (score difference ≥ 0.5σ). The target model is fine-tuned (via LoRA) to increase the score of its outputs relative to its base version.

## Key Results
- HAL alignment increases HL16Q scores during training for all models (1B–72B), with stable or improved emotional intelligence (EmoBench, EQBench3).
- In Chatbot Arena (326 pairwise comparisons), HAL-aligned Qwen2.5-14B wins 61.78% against its base version and attains Elo 1556.97, surpassing GPT-4o-mini.
- The interpretable reward allows inspection of trait-level shifts and detection of potential reward hacking (e.g., reduced variance on certain traits).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Deriving Interpretable Traits from Contrastive Human-AI Dialogues
- **Claim:** HAL identifies a compact set of human-like conversational traits (HL16Q) by analyzing why human judges distinguish human from AI dialogues in Turing tests, not just using binary labels.
- **Mechanism:** An LLM judge reviews paired dialogues, predicts the human side, and generates 3–5 natural-language reasons. These reasons are embedded, clustered to remove redundancy, and summarized by an LLM into distinct Likert-style statements (HL32Q → HL16Q).
- **Core assumption:** Explanations from a high-accuracy LLM judge contain generalizable cues about human-likeness more actionable than binary verdicts.
- **Evidence anchors:** The judge (GPT-5) achieves 64.81% accuracy; 2735 reason statements are clustered into 53 clusters, then 32 traits; final 16 traits are selected by regression weight magnitude.
- **Break condition:** If the judge’s reasons are low-quality or inconsistent with human judgments, extracted traits will not represent true human-likeness. Using the judge’s classification accuracy as a proxy mitigates this.

### Mechanism 2: Creating a Transparent, Composite Reward Signal
- **Claim:** A logistic regression model trained to predict human/AI labels from HL16Q trait ratings yields a weighted sum that serves as an interpretable, scalar reward.
- **Mechanism:** For each dialogue, the judge rates agreement with each of 32 traits. A logistic regression is trained on these vectors (human=1, AI=0). The log-odds before the sigmoid, `Σ(ai * wi) + b`, becomes the continuous HL16Q score.
- **Core assumption:** A linear model over these features can effectively capture the human-AI distinction in the Turing test data; weights indicate trait importance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Logistic regression achieves 77.47% accuracy (10-fold CV); reducing to 16 traits drops accuracy minimally (77.12%); OOD validation on medical dialogues shows human-human dialogues score significantly higher than human-AI (p<0.001, Δ=3.02).
- **Break condition:** The linear model would fail if the true decision boundary is highly nonlinear or if traits are not linearly combinable. OOD validation suggests some generalization but acknowledges domain mismatch.

### Mechanism 3: Alignment via Preference Optimization on the Interpretable Reward
- **Claim:** Using the HL16Q score to create preference pairs (higher score preferred) and fine-tuning with DPO nudges models toward measured human-like behaviors without catastrophic forgetting.
- **Mechanism:** Multiple candidate dialogues are generated for synthetic personas, scored by the fixed HL16Q judge, and filtered into pairs (Δ ≥ 0.5σ). DPO directly optimizes the model’s policy to increase the HL16Q score relative to its base version.
- **Core assumption:** The HL16Q score provides a valid relative ordering of dialogue human-likeness; optimizing this relative signal improves perceived human-likeness in downstream human evaluations.
- **Evidence anchors:** DPO training on 7 open-source models (1B–72B) consistently increases HL16Q scores; Qwen2.5-14B post-HAL achieves 61.78% Arena win-rate and Elo 1556.97 vs. base 1519.48; emotional intelligence benchmarks show no systematic drop.
- **Break condition:** If the reward is misspecified or easily gamed, DPO could optimize superficial traits. Interpretability allows inspection (e.g., Figure 4) to detect collapse (e.g., reduced variance on some traits).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Contrastive Analysis for Implicit Attribute Discovery**  
  *Why needed:* Human-likeness is fuzzy. HAL extracts *which specific cues* differentiate humans from AIs by analyzing *why* correct judgments were made in a controlled comparison, rather than defining the concept a priori.  
  *Quick check:* What is the key difference between using a binary label (human/AI) and using the *explanations* for that label to derive a reward? Why might explanations be more useful?

- **Concept: Interpretable, Linear Reward Models via Proxy Classification**  
  *Why needed:* To make the subjective trait "human-likeness" trainable. Framing it as classification over interpretable features yields a scalar reward from the logistic regression decision function, with weights indicating trait importance, providing transparency versus a black-box reward.  
  *Quick check:* How does training a logistic regression classifier on human/AI labels using LLM-rated traits yield a scalar reward score? What does the sign and magnitude of a weight (Wi) tell you?

- **Concept: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) from Relative Rewards**  
  *Why needed:* HAL outputs a scalar score per dialogue, not pairwise preferences. Converting scores into preference pairs and using DPO simplifies alignment by avoiding separate reward model training and RL.  
  *Quick check:* If you have a numeric score for each dialogue, how do you construct valid preference pairs for DPO? What is the risk if you pair dialogues with very similar scores?

## Architecture Onboarding
Turing Test Data -> LLM Judge (Reasoner) -> Trait Extraction Pipeline (Embeddings -> Clustering -> Summarization) -> HL32Q -> Feature Selection (top 16 by |weight|) -> HL16Q -> HL16Q Judge (GPT-5) -> Reward Calculator (fixed weights W, b) -> Scalar HL16Q Score -> Synthetic Persona & Dialogue Generator -> Pair Filtering (Δ ≥ 0.5σ) -> DPO Trainer (LoRA fine-tuning) -> Aligned Model -> Evaluation Suite (HL16Q scoring, Chatbot Arena, EmoBench/EQBench3)

**Critical path:** Data pipeline → Trait definition → Reward computation → Pair construction → DPO training → Human evaluation. The most fragile link is the HL16Q Judge’s consistency; all downstream steps depend on it providing stable, meaningful ratings. The weights (W, b) and judge model are fixed after initial training.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Interpretability vs. Scalability/Cost:** Using a powerful LLM as a judge is expensive and slow; a distilled smaller reward model could scale but loses trait-level transparency.
- **Domain Specificity vs. Generality:** Traits are extracted from casual Turing-test dialogues (likely English, text-based). OOD validation on medical data shows score offsets, indicating contextual dependency; the framework does not adapt the judge or traits per domain.
- **Reward Specificity vs. Overfitting:** A narrow, interpretable reward (16 traits) may miss broader aspects of human-likeness but reduces reward hacking risk versus a black-box reward. Performance on tasks beyond emotional intelligence (e.g., coding, reasoning) is unknown.

**Failure signatures:**
1. HL16Q score decreases or plateaus during DPO training (Figure 3 shows most models increase; a decrease suggests poor pair construction or DPO instability).
2. Distributional collapse on specific traits (e.g., variance dropping on Q1, Q8, Q14, Q16 as in Figure 4), indicating over-optimization of high-weight traits in an unnatural, deterministic way.
3. High HL16Q score but low human win-rate in Arena, indicating a reward mismatch—the judge’s traits don’t align with actual human perception.
4. Significant performance drop on unrelated benchmarks (EmoBench, EQBench3). Most models are stable or improve, but a drop would indicate catastrophic forgetting or negative transfer.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Trait Extraction Validation:** Manually inspect the top 10 HL16Q traits (Table 1). Do they seem intuitively related to human conversation (e.g., "Keeps replies brief," "Shows small typos")? Are any surprising or domain-specific?
2. **HL16Q Judge Reliability Test:** Run the HL16Q judge (GPT-5) multiple times on 100 duplicate dialogues (from the OOD dataset). Compute intra-class correlation (ICC) for the 16 trait ratings. Low reliability (ICC < 0.7) would invalidate the reward signal.
3. **DPO Pair Quality Spot-Check:** From the 7,175 constructed pairs, randomly sample 20. For each, read the "chosen" and "rejected" dialogues. Does the chosen one *clearly* seem more human-like per HL16Q? If many pairs are ambiguous or incorrectly labeled, the reward signal is flawed.

## Limitations
- **Domain specificity of traits:** HL16Q traits derived from casual Turing-test dialogues may not generalize to other contexts (e.g., professional, multilingual, multimodal); OOD medical data shows score offsets.
- **Judge reliability dependency:** The entire pipeline hinges on a fixed GPT-5 judge rating traits; its consistency and potential biases are unquantified—low reliability would invalidate the reward signal.
- **Reward hacking risk:** Optimizing a fixed linear combination of 16 traits may lead to superficial trait manipulation (e.g., exaggerated brevity or typos) without improving true human-likeness, though interpretability allows monitoring.

## Confidence
- **Trait extraction validity:** Medium. The data-driven pipeline is novel, but trait generalizability is untested beyond the source domain.
- **HL16Q as transparent reward:** Medium. Validated via OOD correlation and logistic regression accuracy, but judge stability is unmeasured.
- **DPO alignment effectiveness:** High/Medium. Strong Arena win-rate (61.78%) and preserved EQ scores support effectiveness; long-term capability retention and broader task performance remain unknown.

## Next Checks
1. **Judge reliability test:** Compute intra-class correlation (ICC) for GPT-5’s HL16Q ratings on 100 duplicate dialogues; ICC < 0.7 would undermine trust in the reward signal.
2. **Cross-domain trait transfer:** Apply HL16Q to dialogues from diverse domains (e.g., technical support, creative writing) to measure score distributions and trait relevance; large negative offsets would indicate poor generalization.
3. **Adversarial trait inspection:** After DPO, compare trait distributions (mean, variance) between aligned and base models; disproportionate shifts on high-weight traits (e.g., Q1, Q8) could signal reward hacking.
