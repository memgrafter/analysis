# Measuring cross-language intelligibility between Romance languages with computational tools

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07447
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07447
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The DLI metric reveals significant asymmetry in Romance language intelligibility (Romanian hardest, Spanish–Italian/Portuguese highest); orthographic/static embeddings outperform phonetic/contextual; DLI correlates with human cloze tests (ρ = 0.71).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the Dynamic Lexical Intelligibility (DLI) metric to quantify cross-language intelligibility among five Romance languages (French, Italian, Portuguese, Spanish, Romanian). DLI combines lexical surface similarity (orthographic or phonetic Levenshtein distance) and semantic similarity (static or contextual word embeddings) computed on cognate and borrowing pairs extracted from the RoBoCoP database within parallel corpora (RomCro, EuroParl). The study finds that intelligibility is strongly asymmetric for all language pairs: Romanian is consistently the most difficult for others to understand, while Spanish–Italian and Spanish–Portuguese exhibit the highest mutual intelligibility. Orthographic surface similarity yields higher scores than phonetic, and static embeddings outperform contextual embeddings. DLI rankings correlate robustly with human cloze test results (Spearman ρ = 0.71), validating the metric’s linguistic relevance.

## Method Summary
The pipeline processes aligned parallel sentences in two Romance languages (L1 speaker, L2 listener). It first identifies cognate/borrowing pairs from RoBoCoP after tokenization (spaCy), stop-word removal, and normalization (lowercasing, accent stripping, Snowball stemming). For each matched pair, it computes:
- Surface similarity $S_L$ via 1 – normalized Levenshtein distance on either orthographic (unaccented) strings or phonemic transcriptions (eSpeak).
- Semantic similarity $S_S$ via cosine similarity between either static FastText multilingual embeddings (using closest in-form vector for OOV words) or contextual embeddings from Sentence‑BERT with Affinity Propagation clustering (up to 200 occurrences per word).
These are combined as $DLI = \frac{S_S S_L (2 - S_S - S_L)}{1 - S_S S_L}$. Sentence‑level DLI is a weighted average (weight = 1 / total non‑stop words in L1), and corpus‑level DLI is the mean over sentences. The approach is fully directional (L1→L2) and relies on pre‑trained embeddings and parallel text alignment.

## Key Results
- **Asymmetry:** All pairs show significant asymmetry; e.g., Romanian→Spanish = 10.7% vs. Spanish→Romanian = 17.7% (orthographic+static).
- **Pairwise ranking:** Spanish–Italian and Spanish–Portuguese pair highest (e.g., Spanish→Portuguese = 30.3%, Portuguese→Spanish = 28.4%).
- **Metric properties:** Orthographic surface > phonetic; static embeddings > contextual. DLI correlates with human cloze ranks (ρ = 0.71, p = 0.0013).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The DLI metric’s effectiveness stems from three interconnected mechanisms:

**1. Formal resemblance drives recognition.** Cognates and borrowings with high orthographic or phonetic overlap allow listeners to map unfamiliar words to known native lexicon via pattern matching. The normalized Levenshtein‑based $S_L$ captures this formal similarity; if $S_L$ is very low (<0.3), even semantically related words are missed.

**2. Semantic proximity aids correct interpretation.** Words that are superficially similar but diverge in meaning (false friends) hinder comprehension. The $S_S$ component, computed via word embeddings, quantifies semantic alignment. Low $S_S$ despite decent $S_L$ can lead to misinterpretation, potentially harming intelligibility more than a non‑cognate.

**3. Asymmetry reflects historical and contact factors.** The aggregate DLI per language pair combines $S_L$ and $S_S$ distributions weighted by corpus frequency. Asymmetry emerges when one direction has more high‑$S_L$/high‑$S_S$ items or higher frequency of such items, influenced by historical evolution (e.g., Romanian’s Slavic vocabulary, French spelling conservatism, Iberian contact). The strong correlation with human judgments confirms these mechanisms capture key determinants of real‑world intelligibility.

## Foundational Learning
- **Lexical cognates and borrowings:** Words that share a common historical origin (inherited) or are loaned from another language. *Why needed:* DLI operates exclusively on such pairs from RoBoCoP, assuming they are the primary conduit for cross‑language intelligibility. *Quick check:* What distinguishes an inherited cognate (e.g., Sp. *madre* ↔ It. *madre*) from a borrowing (e.g., Ro. *zahar* from Arabic via French) in terms of predictability?
- **Normalized Levenshtein distance:** Edit distance normalized by the maximum string length, yielding a similarity score in [0,1]. *Why needed:* Quantifies orthographic or phonetic dissimilarity as the $S_L$ component. *Quick check:* If two words have a normalized Levenshtein distance of 0.2, what is their formal similarity $S_L$? (Answer: 0.8)
- **Word embeddings (static vs. contextual):** Static embeddings (e.g., FastText) assign a single vector per word regardless of context; contextual embeddings (e.g., BERT) produce sense‑dependent vectors. *Why needed:* Provide vector representations to compute semantic similarity $S_S$. Static has broader coverage; contextual captures sense variation via clustering. *Quick check:* Why might contextual embeddings yield lower $S_S$ scores than static ones in this study? (Answer: lower coverage, need for clustering, domain mismatch.)
- **Parallel corpora:** Aligned text collections (e.g., RomCro, EuroParl) providing translations. *Why needed:* Supply contextual occurrences for embedding extraction, weight DLI by real‑world usage, and align related words in the same context. *Quick check:* How might domain differences between RomCro (literary) and EuroParl (political) affect intelligibility scores? (Answer: frequency of certain cognates/borrowings may differ, skewing aggregates.)
- **Intelligibility asymmetry:** The phenomenon that mutual intelligibility is not symmetric; e.g., Spanish speakers may understand Portuguese better than vice versa. *Why needed:* Core empirical finding of the paper, captured by directional DLI scores. *Quick check:* Give one example of pronounced asymmetry from the paper and one hypothesized reason. (Example: Romanian is hardest for others; reason: heavy Slavic borrowing and sound changes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
The DLI computation pipeline follows these steps:

**Component map:**  
RoBoCoP database -> Tokenization & matching (spaCy, Snowball stemmer, accent stripping) -> Form similarity calculator (Levenshtein on orthographic/phonetic strings via eSpeak) -> Semantic similarity calculator (static FastText or contextual Sentence‑BERT with Affinity Propagation clustering) -> DLI aggregator (per‑pair DLI formula, sentence‑weighted average) -> Corpus‑level score.

**Critical path:**  
For each language pair (Speaker L1, Listener L2), the pipeline: (1) matches RoBoCoP pairs in parallel sentences, (2) computes $S_L$ and $S_S$ per pair, (3) aggregates via DLI formula, (4) weights by sentence‑level word counts, (5) averages across corpus.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Orthographic vs. phonetic: Orthographic is more stable/conservative, yielding higher scores; phonetic reflects spoken reality but is lower due to sound changes. Choice depends on modality (written vs. spoken).  
- Static vs. contextual: Static has better coverage (~95%+) and robust vectors; contextual captures sense variation but suffers lower coverage (40–62%) and requires clustering, which may introduce noise.  
- Corpus choice: RomCro (literary) vs. EuroParl (political) influences frequency distributions; EuroParl may inflate scores for Romanian pairs with French‑derived terms.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low embedding coverage (especially contextual) leads to missing words and reliance on “closest in‑form” heuristics, biasing aggregates.  
- Directionality mix‑up: swapping speaker/listener roles reverses asymmetry.  
- Tokenization mismatches between spaCy, stemmer, and embedding models cause false negatives.  
- Clustering contextual embeddings may create spurious senses if occurrence samples are small/noisy.  
- The DLI formula assumes independence of $S_L$ and $S_S$; strong correlation could distort results.

**First 3 experiments (reproduction plan):**  
1. Replicate Table 2 (RomCro, orthographic + static embeddings) to verify baseline DLI scores.  
2. Compute DLI for all pairs using phonetic surface similarity only; compare drop from orthographic scores to quantify pronunciation barrier.  
3. Compute DLI using contextual embeddings only on EuroParl; observe coverage issues and score shifts relative to static.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The authors explicitly leave several questions unresolved:

1. **Does DLI distinguish inherited lexical affinity from contact‑induced similarity?** The metric could test Bartoli’s lateral areas theory if applied to cognate subsets by historical origin.  
2. **Would spoken language corpora yield more reliable DLI scores?** Written corpora may not reflect spontaneous speech perception; spoken parallel corpora are needed.  
3. **How do words with multiple etymologies and diacritic‑related errors affect DLI?** Cleaning the lexicon (filtering ambiguous entries, standardizing diacritics) could improve accuracy.  
4. **What drives the difference between static and contextual embedding scores?** Is it coverage, domain mismatch, or inherent properties? Controlled comparisons using in‑domain trained embeddings are required.  
5. **What specific linguistic factors predict asymmetry?** A multivariate model incorporating phonetic distance, semantic divergence, borrowing rates, and contact history is needed to isolate determinants.

## Limitations
- **Matching and coverage uncertainties:** Exact logic for RoBoCoP matching (e.g., edit‑distance thresholds for OOV words) and licensing/version specifics for RoBoCoP and corpora are not fully documented, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Low contextual embedding coverage (40–62%)** may bias results and distort comparisons with static embeddings.
- **Unmeasured sociolinguistic factors** (e.g., exposure, literacy) could contribute to observed asymmetry beyond lexical similarity.

## Confidence
- **Asymmetry findings:** Medium (robust in‑data but confounded by unmeasured exposure)
- **Correlation with human rankings (ρ = 0.71):** High (validated on RomCro, though limited to one test set)
- **Component weights (orthographic > phonetic, static > contextual):** Low (may reflect data/coverage artifacts rather than cognitive primacy)
