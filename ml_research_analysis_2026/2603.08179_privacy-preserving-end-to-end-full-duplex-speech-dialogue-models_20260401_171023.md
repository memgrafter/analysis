# Privacy-Preserving End-to-End Full-Duplex Speech Dialogue Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08179  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08179  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Substantial speaker identity leakage found; Anon-W2F raises EER to 41% while Anon-W2W retains 78-93% semantic similarity with <0.8s latency.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the privacy risk of speaker identity leakage in end-to-end full-duplex speech dialogue models, such as SALM-Duplex and Moshi. These models continuously process user audio through a shared LLM backbone, which may retain acoustic cues that reveal the speaker’s identity. Using a lazy-informed attacker probe (ECAPA-TDNN), the authors demonstrate significant leakage across all transformer layers, with discrete encoders leaking far more than ASR‑pretrained continuous ones. To mitigate this, they propose two streaming anonymization setups: Anon-W2W (waveform preprocessing) and Anon-W2F (replacing the encoder with a discrete, anonymizing variant). Anon-W2F raises the Equal Error Rate (EER) from 11.2% to 41.0%, approaching random chance, while Anon-W2W preserves 78–93% of semantic similarity and achieves sub‑second first response latency (<0.8s). Both methods substantially reduce speaker Linkability across dialogue turns.

## Method Summary
The authors quantify speaker identity leakage by extracting hidden representations from all transformer layers of SALM-Duplex and Moshi while processing the VoicePrivacy 2024 (VPC2024) evaluation set. A lazy‑informed attacker trains an ECAPA‑TDNN speaker verification model on these representations; performance is measured using Equal Error Rate (EER) and Linkability. For privacy mitigation, two Stream‑Voice‑Anon configurations are introduced. Anon‑W2W anonymizes the raw waveform before the original encoder, while Anon‑W2F replaces the continuous encoder with a discrete one that injects anonymization directly into the token space and fine‑tunes the LLM backbone. The solutions are evaluated on privacy (EER, Linkability), semantic preservation (sBERT, sBLEU), and real‑time efficiency (RTFx, first response latency).

## Key Results
- Baseline leakage is severe: EER as low as 6.4% (Moshi discrete) and 28.5% (SALM‑Duplex continuous) across layers, confirming strong speaker information in hidden states.  
- Anon‑W2F raises EER to 41.0% for SALM‑Duplex, nearing the 50% random‑chance ceiling; Anon‑W2W yields 34.6% for the same model, demonstrating the superiority of feature‑domain anonymization.  
- Anon‑W2W retains 78–93% of baseline sBERT and achieves first response latency <0.8s. Both anonymization methods drastically reduce the growth of Linkability over dialogue turns.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Hidden states of full‑duplex dialogue LLMs leak substantial speaker identity because the encoder and LLM backbone retain acoustic cues correlated with voice characteristics.  
- **Mechanism:** The continuous user audio stream is processed frame‑by‑frame by an encoder whose outputs are fed into a decoder‑only LLM. The LLM maintains persistent hidden states across all transformer layers; these states integrate low‑level spectral and prosodic features from the encoder, which inherently encode speaker identity. A lightweight speaker verification probe (ECAPA‑TDNN) trained on these hidden states can discriminate speakers (low EER), indicating leakage.  
- **Core assumption:** Speaker identity is preserved in the encoder’s representation and propagates into the LLM’s hidden activations; the probe’s success implies the hidden states contain discriminative speaker information.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “we show that the hidden states of SALM‑Duplex and Moshi leak substantial speaker identity across all transformer layers.” [section 3.2] Discrete encoders trained for high‑fidelity reconstruction preserve speaker information; continuous ASR‑pretrained encoder partially discards it, resulting in higher EER (28.5% vs 6.4%). [corpus] Average neighbor FMR=0.532 indicates related work on full‑duplex systems, but no direct evidence for leakage mechanism; assumption that similar architectures leak.  
- **Break condition:** If the encoder is intentionally speaker‑anonymous (e.g., anonymized waveform input or feature‑domain perturbation) before entering the LLM, or if the LLM architecture explicitly normalizes away speaker characteristics.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Feature‑domain anonymization (Anon‑W2F) yields stronger privacy protection than waveform‑level anonymization (Anon‑W2W) because it eliminates the redundant encoder pass that can re‑introduce speaker cues.  
- **Mechanism:** Anon‑W2F replaces the original continuous encoder with a discrete encoder that integrates Stream‑Voice‑Anon directly in the feature space. The anonymizer perturbs the discrete token representations before they enter the LLM, ensuring that the LLM never receives clean speaker information. In Anon‑W2W, the anonymized waveform is fed into the original encoder, which may still encode residual speaker traits due to its training objective (e.g., speech reconstruction), leading to higher leakage.  
- **Core assumption:** The encoder’s output space is the primary bottleneck for speaker information; anonymizing within that space is more effective than anonymizing the input waveform because it prevents the encoder from re‑exposing speaker cues.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “Anon‑W2F raises EER by over 3.5× relative to the discrete encoder baseline (11.2%→41.0%), approaching the 50% random‑chance ceiling, while Anon‑W2W retains 78–93% of baseline sBERT.” [section 3.3.2] Anon‑W2F “eliminates the redundant waveform synthesis step” of Anon‑W2W. [section 4] Anon‑W2F achieves higher EER (41.0%) than Anon‑W2W (34.6% for SALM‑Duplex continuous) despite both using Stream‑Voice‑Anon.  
- **Break condition:** If the discrete encoder is not speaker‑preserving (e.g., trained with adversarial speaker removal) or if the anonymization module itself is ineffective at the feature level.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Speaker Linkability increases sharply over the first few dialogue turns because the LLM’s persistent internal state aggregates speaker‑specific patterns across turns, creating a more discriminative long‑term representation.  
- **Mechanism:** In full‑duplex dialogue, the LLM maintains a continuous hidden state over the user’s speech stream as the conversation progresses. Each turn’s audio contributes to this state, effectively building a cumulative speaker embedding. An attacker probing the hidden states after multiple turns can link them to the same speaker with higher confidence (lower Linkability score) because the representation becomes richer and more stable.  
- **Core assumption:** The LLM’s context window or state mechanism does not forget or decorrelate speaker features across turns; instead, it integrates them in a way that remains speaker‑discriminative.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “Linkability rises sharply within the first few turns.” [section 4.1] Figure 1: “Without anonymization, privacy degrades rapidly within the first few turns.” [corpus] Related full‑duplex benchmarks (e.g., FD‑Bench, Full‑Duplex‑Bench) evaluate multi‑turn interactions, but do not directly address privacy accumulation; assumption based on the paper’s turn‑wise analysis.  
- **Break condition:** If the LLM employs a sliding window that discards older turns, or if anonymization resets the state per turn, Linkability should not accumulate.

## Foundational Learning
- **End-to-end full-duplex speech dialogue models**  
  *Why needed:* These models process continuous user and agent audio streams simultaneously through a shared LLM backbone, creating persistent hidden states that may leak privacy. Understanding their architecture (dual‑stream, always‑on) is essential to locate leakage points and design anonymization.  
  *Quick check:* How does the LLM handle overlapping speech and maintain state across turns in a full‑duplex setting?
- **Speaker verification probing of neural representations**  
  *Why needed:* The paper uses a lazy‑informed attacker (ECAPA‑TDNN probe) to quantify speaker identity in hidden states. This requires understanding probing methodology, Equal Error Rate (EER), and Linkability metrics as privacy evaluation tools.  
  *Quick check:* What does an EER of 30% versus 45% imply about speaker distinguishability in hidden states?
- **Streaming anonymization architectures**  
  *Why needed:* Two setups (Anon‑W2W, Anon‑W2F) are proposed to mitigate leakage in real‑time. Knowing the difference between waveform‑level and feature‑domain anonymization, and their trade‑offs in privacy, utility, and latency, is key to applying the solutions.  
  *Quick check:* Why does feature‑domain anonymization (Anon‑W2F) achieve higher EER but may require architectural changes?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
User audio stream → (optional) Stream‑Voice‑Anon front‑end → User encoder (continuous or discrete) → LLM backbone (transformer) with dual‑stream processing → Agent decoder → Agent audio output.  
Privacy probing: Attach ECAPA‑TDNN probe to hidden states at various layers (early, mid, late, mean‑pooled).

### Critical path
1. Raw user audio enters the system.  
2. In Anon‑W2W: audio is anonymized by Stream‑Voice‑Anon, then encoded by the original encoder → embeddings → LLM.  
3. In Anon‑W2F: audio is encoded by a discrete encoder that includes anonymization; anonymized tokens → LLM.  
4. LLM processes user and agent streams frame‑synchronously, updating hidden states.  
5. Probe extracts hidden states from chosen layers to compute EER/Linkability.

### Design tradeoffs
- Privacy vs. utility: Stronger anonymization (higher EER) degrades semantic similarity (sBERT) and may affect response quality.  
- Latency: Anon‑W2W introduces extra waveform synthesis step, increasing First Response Latency (FRL) and reducing RTFx; Anon‑W2F is faster because it avoids resynthesis.  
- Encoder type: Discrete encoders preserve more speaker info but allow feature‑domain anonymization; continuous ASR‑pretrained encoders are slightly more private by design but offer less headroom for improvement.

### Failure signatures
- EER below 40% for anonymized models: leakage still significant.  
- Linkability >0.3 after 10 turns: privacy degrades with conversation length.  
- sBERT drop >20%: utility loss may be unacceptable.  
- FRL >0.8s: violates real‑time requirement.

### First 3 experiments
1. Baseline leak assessment: Extract hidden states from original models (no anonymization) across layers and compute layer‑wise EER to confirm leakage pattern.  
2. Anonymization comparison: Apply Anon‑W2W to both models and Anon‑W2F to SALM‑Duplex; compare EER, Linkability, sBERT, and latency.  
3. Turn‑length analysis: Run dialogue for multiple turns, compute Linkability per turn, and plot to assess privacy decay over time.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Integration of Anon‑W2F with Moshi:** Extending the feature‑domain approach to Moshi’s residual‑quantization encoder is future work; compatibility and performance remain untested.  
- **Evaluation on conversational speech:** The study uses read speech from VPC2024; assessing leakage and anonymization on spontaneous conversational corpora (e.g., AMI, Switchboard) is needed.  
- **More sophisticated attacker models:** The ECAPA‑TDNN probe is a lower bound; applying state‑of‑the‑art attackers from the VoicePrivacy Attacker Challenge may reveal higher risk.  
- **Optimization of Stream‑Voice‑Anon:** The anonymizer’s computational overhead is high; developing efficient variants (e.g., via distillation, quantization) is important for real‑time deployment.

## Limitations
- Key hyperparameters for the ECAPA‑TDNN attacker and fine‑tuning of Anon‑W2F are not disclosed, hindering exact replication of results.  
- Evaluation is restricted to VPC2024 (LibriSpeech read speech); generalization to other languages, domains, and especially conversational speech is unknown.  
- Stream‑Voice‑Anon is treated as a black box, leading to approximations in latency and efficiency analysis.

## Confidence
- Speaker identity leakage across all transformer layers: **High**  
- Anon‑W2F yields stronger protection than Anon‑W2W: **High**  
- Linkability rises sharply within the first few dialogue turns: **Medium**  
- Streaming anonymization preserves semantics with low latency: **Medium**
