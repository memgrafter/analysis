# GAIN: A Benchmark for Goal-Aligned Decision-Making of Large Language Models under Imperfect Norms

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18469
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18469
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Advanced LLMs generally mirror human decision distributions (Jensen-Shannon Similarity) but diverge under Personal Incentive pressure, where they strongly resist norm deviation while humans are more susceptible, revealing an alignment bias against self-serving actions.

## Executive Summary
The GAIN benchmark introduces a novel evaluation framework for large language models (LLMs) on goal-aligned decision-making under imperfect business norms. It constructs 1,200 realistic scenarios across four business domains (hiring, customer support, advertising, finance), each paired with five contextual pressure types (Goal Alignment, Risk Aversion, Emotional/Ethical Appeal, Social/Authoritative Influence, Personal Incentive). The benchmark forces models to choose among three actions—comply with the norm, deviate from it, or escalate to human oversight—while weighing a business goal against a conflicting norm. Evaluation uses Jensen-Shannon Similarity (JSS) between model and human decision distributions. Results show that state-of-the-art LLMs achieve high overall JSS, indicating general alignment with human judgments. However, under Personal Incentive pressure, LLMs drastically reduce deviation rates (near zero) while humans remain relatively susceptible (~20% deviation). This divergence reveals a strong, context‑insensitive bias in LLMs against self‑serving actions, likely instilled by post‑training alignment procedures.

## Method Summary
GAIN’s dataset comprises 200 seed scenarios per domain, each expanded into six variants: a base (no pressure) and five pressure‑injected versions. Scenarios are generated by Gemini‑2.5 Pro and validated by crowdworkers for plausibility, clarity, and dilemma strength. The test set consists of 960 unique instances (40 base per domain × 6 variants). Models are prompted zero‑shot with a fixed template describing the business goal, situation, norm, and any contextual pressure, and are required to output a JSON object containing a `decision` (one of “comply”, “deviate”, “escalate”) and a brief `rationale`. Inference runs at temperature 0.7, max tokens 1024, with five random seeds for sampling. The primary metric is JSS between the model’s aggregated action distribution and the human baseline distribution derived from seven crowdworkers per scenario. The evaluation code and dataset are publicly released.

## Key Results
- Advanced LLMs (e.g., GPT‑5, GPT‑4.1, gpt‑oss‑20b) achieve high JSS (0.85–0.92) with human distributions across most pressures, indicating alignment with human trade‑offs.
- Under Personal Incentive pressure, all evaluated LLMs show a stark collapse in deviation: GPT‑5 deviation rate drops to 0.6%–4.0% (vs. base ~20%), while human deviation remains around 20%. This yields the largest gap between model and human JSS in any pressure condition.
- Several models, particularly GPT‑5, significantly increase escalation rates under pressures (e.g., from 31.3% to 54.0%–72.0%), suggesting a safety‑oriented deferral heuristic when contextual complexity rises.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s design isolates the causal effect of contextual pressures by keeping the business goal (G) and norm (N) fixed while varying the pressure (P). This allows us to test whether models treat P as a salient feature that re‑weights the cost‑benefit analysis encoded in their decision function.

**Mechanism 1: Contextual pressures re‑frame the trade‑off.**  
Explicitly stated pressures (e.g., highlighting personal gain) alter the perceived balance between norm adherence and business goal. The evidence is that varying P systematically shifts model action distributions; for example, Emotional/Ethical Appeal increases comply rates. Break condition: if varying P produces no significant distribution change (chi‑square), the mechanism fails.

**Mechanism 2: Alignment training instills a self‑serving bias.**  
Post‑training alignment (RLHF, constitutional AI) likely penalizes outputs that justify selfish gains, creating a strong prior against self‑serving deviation. When Personal Incentive pressure pushes toward deviation, this prior overrides, leading to near‑universal compliance or escalation. Evidence: GPT‑5 deviation under Personal Incentive is near zero, and rationales cite “risk to the entire organization.” Break condition: if a model’s deviation under Personal Incentive becomes similar to human or its own base rate, this mechanism is unsupported.

**Mechanism 3: Deferral heuristic for safety.**  
Some models (GPT series) escalate disproportionately under most pressures, suggesting they defer to human oversight as a default when contextual pressures introduce complexity or potential norm violation. Evidence: GPT‑5 escalation rises dramatically under five of six pressures. Rationales often mention “significant risks” and “formal decision.” Break condition: if a model’s escalation does not increase under pressures, the deferral heuristic is not present.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Norm‑Goal Misalignment as a Core Problem Space**  
  *Why needed here:* GAIN redefines success as balancing an imperfect norm against a concrete business goal, not simply “following the rule.” This conceptual shift underlies the three‑action space (comply, deviate, escalate) and the notion that a “correct” decision may sometimes violate the norm to achieve a higher‑order goal.  
  *Quick check:* Is a decision “correct” if it follows a written policy but harms long‑term customer retention? Why or why not?

- **Concept: Pressure as a Controlled Experimental Manipulation**  
  *Why needed here:* The benchmark’s key innovation is injecting contextual pressures as an independent variable while holding G and N constant. This treats pressures as interventions that test whether models adjust their internal utility function in response to human‑centric motivators.  
  *Quick check:* What would be the limitation of a benchmark that only varied the business goal (G) instead of introducing pressures (P)?

- **Concept: Distributional Alignment vs. Point‑Point Accuracy**  
  *Why needed here:* Human inter‑rater agreement is very low (α=0.11), so there is no single “right” answer. GAIN therefore uses Jensen‑Shannon Similarity to compare the model’s entire action distribution to the human distribution. This measures behavioral mimicry rather than matching a gold standard.  
  *Quick check:* Why is a high JSS desirable for an AI assistant in a business setting, even if the model’s most frequent choice isn’t the majority human choice in every single case?

## Architecture Onboarding
The GAIN pipeline consists of:
Scenario Generator (offline) → Validator (offline/human) → Evaluation Engine (online) → Analyzer

**Critical path:** Load test set (960 instances) → For each instance: format prompt → call LLM → parse JSON → Aggregate action counts by (Domain, Pressure Type) → Compute JSS per model/pressure → Compare to human baseline and across models.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Discrete Action Space: Forcing choices into {comply, deviate, escalate} simplifies evaluation but omits nuanced actions like negotiate or partial compliance. Trade‑off: gains in measurability at the cost of ecological validity.  
- Synthetic Data: Scenarios are LLM‑generated (Gemini‑2.5 Pro) which may embed generation biases. Trade‑off: scalability and consistency versus potential contamination; mitigated by human validation.  
- Zero‑Shot Prompting: Tests inherent reasoning without in‑context examples. Trade‑off: reflects base capability but not fine‑tuned performance on similar tasks.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. **Uniform Escalation:** >80% escalate across all pressures → model cannot make autonomous trade‑offs.  
2. **Pressure‑Insensitive:** Action distribution unchanged across pressures → fails the core test of contextual sensitivity.  
3. **Domain Collapse:** JSS plummets in a specific domain (e.g., Finance) → internalized norms are domain‑brittle.  
4. **Rationale‑Action Mismatch:** Justification contradicts chosen action → prompt‑following or parsing issue.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Replication:** Run all test models on the base scenarios only. Verify that models split into default groups (compliance, deviation, escalation) as reported.  
2. **Pressure Effect Isolation:** For one model (e.g., GPT‑5), compare its action distribution on the same 50 base scenarios across the six conditions (Base + 5 Pressures). Use chi‑square test to confirm significant distribution differences; specifically check deviation rate under Personal Incentive vs. Base.  
3. **Cross‑Domain Robustness:** For a top‑aligned model (e.g., gpt‑oss‑20b), compute JSS separately for each domain under each pressure type. Replicate the finding that the Finance domain shows the lowest JSS.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How do LLMs handle multiple contextual pressures or conflicting norms, and does their behavior remain aligned with human decision‑making under such complexity?  
*Basis:* The paper’s conclusion suggests extending the benchmark to combinations of pressures.  
*Why unresolved:* Real‑world decisions often involve overlapping influences; interactions may reveal new biases not captured by single‑pressure tests.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Generate multi‑pressure scenarios, collect human decisions, compare LLM distributions and rationales.

**Open Question 2:** Does the reliance on LLM‑synthetic scenarios introduce systematic biases that affect measured alignment?  
*Basis:* Limitations note potential generation‑model bias from using Gemini‑2.5 Pro.  
*Why unresolved:* Generation artifacts could favor certain models or distort dilemma structure, compromising benchmark validity.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Create a parallel human‑written benchmark with identical structure; evaluate same LLMs on both and compare JSS scores.

**Open Question 3:** Would decisions from specialized professionals (e.g., finance experts) differ from crowdworkers, and would that change the assessment of LLM‑human alignment in high‑stakes domains?  
*Basis:* Limitations state human baseline consists of general crowdworkers, not experts facing real consequences.  
*Why unresolved:* Experts may have higher consensus, stronger risk awareness, or different ethical trade‑offs, potentially altering alignment metrics, especially in finance where current alignment is low.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Recruit domain experts to complete GAIN tasks, compare their distributions to crowdworkers’, recompute LLM‑human similarity.

## Limitations
- Synthetic scenario generation using Gemini‑2.5 Pro may embed biases that affect model‑human alignment measurements.
- The forced 3‑action space (comply, deviate, escalate) oversimplifies real business decisions, omitting nuanced responses like negotiation or partial compliance.
- Very low human inter‑rater agreement (α=0.11) questions the stability of the baseline distribution against which alignment is measured.

## Confidence
- **Advanced LLMs match human distributions overall**: High – consistent JSS results across multiple models.
- **LLMs diverge under Personal Incentive pressure**: High – robust empirical gap and near‑zero deviation rates.
- **Benchmark isolates pressure effects**: Medium – design is principled but assumes models treat pressures as intended; mechanistic support indirect.
- **Alignment training causes self‑serving bias**: Low – plausible explanation but not directly tested; alternative hypotheses possible.

## Next Checks
1. **Framing Counterbalance:** Replicate GAIN with loss‑framed Personal Incentive (e.g., “missing out on a bonus”) to test robustness of LLM resistance to self‑serving deviation.
2. **Action Space Expansion:** Introduce a “negotiate” option to see whether distributional shifts persist or escalation increases, assessing the impact of a more ecologically valid action set.
3. **Internal Probe Analysis:** Use activation steering or linear probes to determine whether Personal Incentive triggers distinct safety‑related internal representations compared to other pressures.
