# Revisiting the Shape Convention of Transformer Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06471  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06471  
- **Reference count:** 38  
- **Primary result:** Proposes a deeper hourglass (wide-narrow-wide) feed-forward network that saves parameters via narrow bottlenecks, enabling reallocation to attention or depth; achieves lower validation perplexity than conventional narrow-wide-narrow FFNs at 113M, 403M, and 906M scales under matched budgets.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the conventional narrow-wide-narrow MLP shape within Transformer feed-forward networks (FFNs). It introduces a deeper hourglass FFN composed of stacked sub-MLPs with residual connections, creating a wide-narrow-wide structure. By compressing representations through narrow bottlenecks (width \(d_h < d_{\text{model}}\)), the design saves parameters that can be reinvested into attention capacity or model depth under fixed total parameter budgets. Empirical evaluations from 113M to 1B parameters show consistent improvements in validation perplexity for hourglass variants at smaller scales, with benefits tapering at 1B. The findings suggest that the standard FFN shape is not essential and that attention capacity may be more valuable than wide intermediate FFN layers for efficient model scaling.

## Method Summary
The study investigates whether modifying the feed-forward network (FFN) in a Transformer decoder can improve parameter efficiency. The hourglass FFN replaces a single wide expansion layer with \(K\) stacked sub-blocks, each consisting of a down-projection to a narrow hidden dimension \(d_h\) (\(d_h < d_{\text{model}}\)), SwiGLU activation, and up-projection back to \(d_{\text{model}}\), with residual connections both inside and across sub-blocks. Training follows a standard language modeling objective on the Stage 1 pre‑training data of OLMo‑2 1B (Dolma corpus), with model sizes ranging from 113M to 1B non‑embedding parameters. For each target budget, the conventional narrow-wide-narrow FFN baseline and hourglass variants with different \(K\) (FFN depth) and \(d_h\) (bottleneck width) are trained from scratch using AdamW, cosine learning rate decay, and RoPE. Performance is compared via validation perplexity across multiple held‑out sets and downstream task accuracy (Arc Easy, HellaSwag, PIQA, SciQ, CommonsenseQA, TriviaQA, NaturalQs).

## Key Results
- Hourglass FFNs achieve lower validation perplexity than conventional narrow-wide-narrow baselines at 113M (35.34 vs. 36.44), 403M, and 906M parameter scales; performance is comparable at 1B.
- Parameter reallocation enabled by hourglass FFNs—shifting budget from wide FFN intermediates to attention or model depth—consistently improves validation loss and downstream task accuracy at matched budgets, especially at smaller scales.
- The optimal configuration is scale‑dependent: at 113M, an attention‑dominated budget with \(K=4\) and \(L=6\) works best; at 1B, a more balanced or FFN‑dominated budget with \(K=1\) is preferred.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*Claim:* Hourglass FFNs achieve parameter efficiency by compressing representations through narrow bottlenecks (\(d_h < d_{\text{model}}\)), freeing parameters for other components.  
*Mechanism:* The wide-narrow-wide structure (expansion → compression → expansion) with residual connections allows approximating the same function with fewer parameters in intermediate layers compared to a uniform wide expansion, as information is forced through a lower‑dimensional manifold before being re‑expanded.  
*Core assumption:* Residual connectivity enables effective gradient flow and signal propagation even with very narrow intermediate layers, a theoretical property of residual hourglass MLPs.  
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] "...saves parameters through narrower bottlenecks, allowing reallocation to attention or model depth under fixed budgets."; Section 3.2: "The parameters saved by narrowing the FFN can be reinvested to increase the model dimension \(d_{\text{model}}\) or the attention capacity."; Table 6 shows performance degrades gradually as \(d_h/d_{\text{model}}\) decreases from 0.8 to 0.1, with significant savings below 0.4.  
*Break condition:* If \(d_h\) is too narrow relative to task complexity (e.g., \(d_h/d_{\text{model}} < 0.2\) at 113M), performance degrades sharply, indicating a minimum capacity threshold for the bottleneck.

### Mechanism 2
*Claim:* Increased internal depth (\(K > 1\)) of stacked hourglass sub‑MLPs enhances representational power and function approximation per parameter compared to a single wide expansion.  
*Mechanism:* Stacking multiple hourglass blocks (each with a narrow compression) creates a sequence of non‑linear transformations that iteratively refine features in alternating compressed and expanded spaces. This depth‑based composition may increase the number of linear regions or decision boundary complexity more efficiently than simply widening a single layer.  
*Core assumption:* Deeper residual networks with narrow layers can approximate complex functions as effectively as shallower wide networks, given sufficient depth.  
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] "Motivated by recent results that residual wide‑narrow‑wide (hourglass) MLPs offer superior function approximation capabilities..."; Figure 4: For a given \(d_{\text{model}}/L\) ratio, configurations with \(K=4\) consistently outperform \(K=2\) and \(K=1\) across the sweep; Table 5: Increasing \(K\) from 1 to 8 monotonically improves validation perplexity (at the cost of increased model size under fixed \(d_{\text{model}}\), \(L\), \(d_h\)).  
*Break condition:* Benefits of increasing \(K\) may diminish or reverse at very large scales or if residual pathways accumulate noise (discussed as the "curse of depth"), though not observed up to 1B.

### Mechanism 3
*Claim:* Parameter savings from hourglass FFNs enable a beneficial reallocation of capacity from static FFN "memory" to dynamic attention‑based contextual processing, particularly valuable at smaller scales.  
*Mechanism:* Because hourglass FFNs require fewer parameters for a given function, the total parameter budget can increase attention module dimension (\(d_{\text{model}}\)) or number of layers (\(L\)), shifting the parameter ratio from FFN‑dominated to attention‑dominated. The hypothesis is that contextual integration (attention) provides a higher marginal gain than expanded intermediate FFN width for certain model scales.  
*Core assumption:* Attention mechanisms are more parameter‑efficient for learning contextual dependencies than wide FFNs are for storing monolithic feature transformations.  
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] "parameters saved by using a lighter hourglass FFN can be more effectively utilized, such as by enlarging model hidden dimensions under fixed budgets."; Table 2: The best 113M configuration (\(K=4, L=6\)) has 45M attention params vs 68M FFN params, inverting the conventional 28M vs 85M ratio, and achieves the lowest perplexity (35.101); Section 4.2.5: At 906M, the hourglass variant has roughly balanced parameters (415M Attn vs 491M FFN), and at 1B it becomes attention‑dominated (649M vs 425M).  
*Break condition:* At the 1B scale, the advantage diminishes (Table 4), and the optimal configuration found used \(K=1\) and an attention‑dominated budget. This suggests a minimal FFN capacity floor exists, or that the marginal benefit of reallocation tapers off at larger scales.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Transformer Layer Structure & FFN Shape**  
  *Why needed:* The paper directly modifies the FFN component within a standard Transformer block. Understanding the conventional narrow‑wide‑narrow (expansion ratio 2–4) FFN and its parameter dominance is prerequisite to evaluating the proposed hourglass alternative.  
  *Quick check:* In a standard Transformer with \(d_{\text{model}}=768\) and expansion ratio 4, what is \(d_h\), and what percentage of layer parameters does the FFN typically consume?

- **Concept: Residual Networks & Expressivity**  
  *Why needed:* The hourglass design relies on stacking multiple narrow MLPs with residuals. Foundational knowledge about how depth contributes to function approximation in residual networks is needed to interpret the claims about \(K\).  
  *Quick check:* According to the cited theory (Lin & Jegelka, 2018), what property do residual networks with extremely narrow hidden layers retain?

- **Concept: Parameter Budgeting & Scaling Laws**  
  *Why needed:* All experiments are conducted under fixed total parameter budgets. The core design principle is redistributing parameters between attention, FFN width (\(d_h\)), FFN depth (\(K\)), and model depth (\(L\)). Understanding how these allocations interact is key.  
  *Quick check:* In Table 2, how does changing \((K, L)\) from (2,12) to (4,6) while keeping total ~113M params affect the attention/FFN parameter split and performance?

## Architecture Onboarding
*Component map:*  
Input → LayerNorm → Multi‑Head Attention (MHA) → Residual Add → LayerNorm → Hourglass FFN (K sub‑blocks) → Residual Add → Output (to next layer)  
Hourglass FFN sub‑block: LayerNorm → Down‑projection (\(W_d: d_{\text{model}} \to d_h\)) → Activation (SwiGLU) → Up‑projection (\(W_u: d_h \to d_{\text{model}}\)) → Residual Add

*Critical path:* The hourglass modification is localized to the FFN. All other components (attention, normalization order, RoPE) follow a standard LLaMA/OLMo‑style decoder design. The primary tuning knobs are \(K\) (FFN depth), \(d_h\) (bottleneck width), \(d_{\text{model}}\) (model width), and \(L\) (number of layers). Total parameter count is the binding constraint.

*Design tradeoffs:*  
1. \(d_h\) vs. Performance/Params: Decreasing \(d_h/d_{\text{model}}\) saves FFN params but risks under‑capacity; sweet spot found ~0.4 for \(K=4\) at 113M.  
2. \(K\) vs. \(L\): Increasing \(K\) (deeper FFN per layer) can allow decreasing \(L\) (fewer layers) for similar/better performance at fixed budget (e.g., \(K=4, L=6\) beats conventional \(L=12\)).  
3. Attention/FFN Parameter Ratio: Hourglass enables shifting budget toward attention. Optimal ratio is scale‑dependent; smaller models (113M) benefit from attention‑dominated, larger (1B) may need more balanced or FFN‑dominated.

*Failure signatures:*  
- Severe \(d_h\) compression: If \(d_h/d_{\text{model}}\) is too small (e.g., <0.2 in ablations), validation perplexity rises sharply, indicating bottleneck information loss.  
- Insufficient \(K\): Using \(K=1\) (conventional shape) within the hourglass framework underperforms deeper stacks (\(K=4\)) at same budget.  
- Mismatched scale extrapolation: Configurations optimal at 113M may need adjustment for 1B; the paper found optimal 1B used \(K=1\) and larger \(d_{\text{model}}/L\).

*First 3 experiments:*  
1. Baseline replication & controlled swap: Train a conventional Transformer (e.g., 113M, \(d_{\text{model}}=768\), \(L=12\), expansion=4). Replace its FFN with an hourglass FFN (\(K=4\), \(d_h=0.4 \cdot d_{\text{model}}\)) while keeping \(d_{\text{model}}\) and \(L\) identical. Compare validation loss/perplexity to isolate the hourglass structure's effect.  
2. \(d_h\) sweep at fixed \(K\) & budget: With total params fixed (e.g., 113M) and \(K\) fixed (e.g., 4), sweep \(d_h/d_{\text{model}}\) from 0.1 to 0.8. Identify the performance valley (U‑shaped curve) to find the width efficiency frontier.  
3. Joint \(K\)‑\(L\) search at fixed budget: For a target size (e.g., 113M), grid search over \(K \in \{2,4,6\}\) and \(L\) values that fit the budget. Plot validation loss vs. \(d_{\text{model}}/L\) ratio for each \(K\) to identify the optimal operating region (e.g., \(K=4\), \(d_{\text{model}}/L \sim 100–150\)).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability:** Can the hourglass FFN architecture scale effectively to models with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters while maintaining its parameter efficiency advantages? The study only validated up to 1B parameters; extrapolation to larger scales is untested.
2. **Interaction with advanced attention:** How does replacing conventional FFNs with hourglass FFNs interact with advanced attention mechanisms beyond standard multi-head attention (e.g., Group‑Query Attention, Multi‑head Latent Attention)? The paper isolated the FFN modification while fixing attention to MHA, leaving interactions unexplored.
3. **Theoretical explanation for U‑shape:** What is the theoretical explanation for the observed U‑shaped relationship between the \(d_{\text{model}}/L\) ratio and validation loss in hourglass FFN Transformers, and how does this optimal region shift with different hourglass depths (\(K\))? The paper identifies the empirical pattern but lacks a theoretical account.
4. **Depth limits and signal degradation:** Do hourglass FFNs suffer from signal degradation or vanishing gradients when stacked too deeply (large \(K\)) in very deep Transformers, and what architectural modifications can mitigate such effects? The future work mentions potential "curse of depth" issues with residual noise accumulation.

## Limitations
- **Missing replication details:** Exact tokenizer, preprocessing pipeline, and downstream evaluation protocols (prompt templates, number of shots, answer normalization) are not fully specified, hindering faithful reproduction of the OLMo‑2 experiments.
- **Scale uncertainty:** Claims are validated only up to 1B parameters; the optimal configuration and scaling behavior for larger models (10B+) remain unknown and may differ from observed trends.
- **Theoretical assumptions:** Theoretical support (e.g., residual expressivity) assumes perfect optimization; real training noise and hardware constraints could shift the narrow‑bottleneck breakpoint and optimal depth \(K\).

## Confidence
- **Parameter efficiency via bottlenecks:** Medium. Data shows a U‑shaped perplexity curve (Table 6), confirming benefit but within a narrow safe range.
- **Depth (\(K\)) improvements:** Medium. \(K=4\) outperforms shallower stacks at 113M (Fig 4), yet scalability to larger depths is untested.
- **Attention reallocation:** Medium‑Low. Gains are robust at 113M but fade by 1B where the best model uses \(K=1\), suggesting the effect is scale‑dependent.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑architectural test:** Integrate hourglass FFNs into a GPT‑2/BERT‑style Transformer and verify perplexity/accuracy gains under matched budgets to ensure benefits are not specific to OLMo‑style architectures.
2. **Large‑scale sweep:** Systematically vary \(d_h/d_{\text{model}}\) and \(K/L\) for 3–10B parameter models to locate a new optimum and test if attention‑dominant budgets remain superior at larger scales.
3. **Bottleneck analysis:** Use representation probing (e.g., PCA, linear evaluation) to confirm that narrow layers preserve information content and that residuals mitigate signal loss, validating the core efficiency claim.
