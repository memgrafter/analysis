# Efficient Distribution Learning with Error Bounds in Wasserstein Distance
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08063
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08063
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Constructs a discrete approximation with small support *M* via a data-driven partition (modified Lloyd’s algorithm) and derives high-confidence Wasserstein error bounds by solving a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) with complexity dependent only on *M*; a key relaxation reduces the MILP to one with a single integer variable for improved scalability.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method to learn an unknown probability distribution from finite samples while providing non-asymptotic, high-confidence upper bounds on the Wasserstein distance to the true distribution. Unlike classical bounds (e.g., Fournier’s) that are often conservative and scale poorly with dimension, the proposed approach uses a held-out training set to learn a data-driven partition of the space via a modified Lloyd’s algorithm, yielding a discrete approximation with a compact support of size *M*. Given this partition, it constructs exact binomial confidence intervals for the mass in each region and solves a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) to find the worst-case Wasserstein distance consistent with those intervals. A critical relaxation reduces the MILP to one with a single integer variable, making the computation tractable even for moderate *M*. Empirical results on synthetic and real-world datasets (including a 50-dimensional dataset with ~125k samples) show dramatically tighter bounds than state-of-the-art methods while using a support size orders of magnitude smaller than the full empirical distribution.

## Method Summary
The method requires two independent i.i.d. datasets: a training set *D*<sup>train</sup> used to learn a partition of the bounded support *X*, and a validation set *D*<sub>*N*</sub> used to compute empirical region probabilities and the final bound. Partition construction (Algorithm 1) applies Lloyd’s (*k*-means) algorithm to *D*<sup>train</sup> with *K = M−1* clusters to obtain centroids *c*<sub>1</sub>, …, *c*<sub>*M*−1</sub>. For each centroid, a radius *r*<sub>*i*</sub> is estimated based on local point spread and distances to neighboring centroids, defining a region *C*<sub>*i*</sub> as the intersection of a Voronoi cell and a ball. The final region *C*<sub>*M*</sub> covers the remainder of *X*. Using *D*<sub>*N*</sub>, the empirical probability *π*<sup>(*i*)</sup> of each region is computed. Clopper-Pearson exact binomial confidence intervals [*p*<sub>ℓ</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>, *p*<sub>*u*</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>] are then derived for the true mass *P*(*C*<sub>*i*</sub>) at confidence level *β*. The core bound is obtained by solving a MILP that maximizes the *ρ*-Wasserstein distance between any *P* consistent with these interval constraints and the discrete approximation ∑*π*<sup>(*i*)</sup>*δ*<sub>*c*<sub>*i*</sub></sub>. To improve scalability, Proposition 3.2 relaxes this MILP to one with a single integer variable by fixing a vertex of the constrained simplex closest to *π*, adding a small additive error term.

## Key Results
- Achieves substantially tighter Wasserstein bounds than the Fournier baseline, especially in low-variance and high-dimensional settings.
- Uses a dramatically smaller support size *M* (e.g., *M* = 100) compared to the full empirical distribution (size *N* ≈ 125,000) while providing informative guarantees.
- The single-integer MILP relaxation (Proposition 3.2) enables tractable computation for *M* up to ~1000 with only a modest (10–30%) increase in bound tightness relative to the full MILP.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Data-driven partition via modified Lloyd’s algorithm
A held-out training set is used to run *(M−1)*-means clustering (Lloyd’s algorithm) to obtain centroids *c*<sub>1</sub>, …, *c*<sub>*M*−1</sub>. Radii *r*<sub>*i*</sub> are computed to approximate the local support of *P*, defining regions *C*<sub>*i*</sub> as Voronoi cells truncated by balls of radius *r*<sub>*i*</sub>. The final region *C*<sub>*M*</sub> covers the leftover space. This partition aims to minimize the quantization error (linked to Wasserstein distance) while ensuring the true support is contained with high probability. **Break condition:** If the training set is too small relative to *M*, the radii may be underestimated, leaving significant true mass in the uncovered region *C*<sub>*M*</sub>. Lloyd’s algorithm may also converge to a poor local optimum.

### Mechanism 2: High-confidence Wasserstein bound via MILP and Clopper-Pearson intervals
Given the partition, the number of validation samples in each region *C*<sub>*i*</sub> follows a binomial distribution. Clopper-Pearson intervals provide *exact* (coverage *β*/(2*M*) per region) bounds [*p*<sub>ℓ</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>, *p*<sub>*u*</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>] on the true probabilities *P*(*C*<sub>*i*</sub>). Theorem 3.1 formulates a MILP that maximizes the optimal transport cost between any distribution *P* whose masses lie in these intervals and the fixed discrete approximation ∑*π*<sup>(*i*)</sup>*δ*<sub>*c*<sub>*i*</sub></sub>, while enforcing at least as much self-transport as the minimum of the mass and empirical weights. The optimal value raised to 1/*ρ* is the high-confidence bound. **Break condition:** For small *N*, the intervals become very wide, making the bound vacuous (exceeding the diameter of *X*). The MILP may also become intractable for large *M*.

### Mechanism 3: Scalability relaxation to a single-integer MILP
The MILP from Theorem 3.1 has *M* integer variables (one per region). Proposition 3.2 shows that selecting a specific vertex *v* of the box-constrained simplex Δ<sub>*M*</sub>[[*p*<sub>ℓ</sub>, *p*<sub>*u*</sub>] closest to the empirical probabilities *π* reduces the problem to a linear program with *M*<sup>2</sup> variables plus a single integer variable. The resulting bound differs from the original by an additive term *ξ* that accounts for the triangle inequality with *v*. This relaxation preserves tractability while degrading tightness by only 10–30% in experiments. **Break condition:** If the chosen vertex *v* is far from *π*, the additive error *ξ* may dominate. The sorting rule for selecting *v* might fail if the gap |*π*<sup>(*i*)</sup> − *p*<sub>*u*</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>| − |*π*<sup>(*i*)</sup> − *p*<sub>ℓ</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>| is not a good proxy for optimality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Wasserstein distance**  
  *Why needed:* The target error metric; the method bounds *W*<sub>*ρ*</sub>(*P*, *P*̂).  
  *Quick check:* What property of Wasserstein distance allows it to be computed as a finite-dimensional linear program when both distributions are discrete?
- **Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals**  
  *Why needed:* Provides exact (non-asymptotic) binomial confidence bounds on region probabilities, which are used as constraints in the MILP.  
  *Quick check:* Why are Clopper-Pearson intervals preferred over normal approximations when some region counts are very small or zero?
- **Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP)**  
  *Why needed:* Both the original bound (Theorem 3.1) and its relaxation (Proposition 3.2) are formulated as MILP problems; efficient solvers make these computations practical.  
  *Quick check:* What causes the MILP in Theorem 3.1 to scale poorly with *M* and how does Proposition 3.2 reduce the number of integer variables to one?
- **Modified Lloyd’s algorithm (*k*-means)**  
  *Why needed:* An unsupervised procedure to determine the support points and partition from the training data, aiming to minimize the quantized Wasserstein distance.  
  *Quick check:* For *ρ = 2*, how does Lloyd’s algorithm relate to minimizing the expected squared Euclidean distance, and why does that align with approximating *W*<sub>2</sub>?
- **Empirical risk and data splitting**  
  *Why needed:* Using a held-out training set to learn the partition ensures independence from the validation set, which is required for the Clopper-Pearson intervals to remain valid.  
  *Quick check:* If the same dataset were used for both partition construction and bound computation, what would be the impact on the confidence guarantee?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Data ingestion -> Partition learning (Lloyd’s on training set) -> Probability estimation (counts on validation set) -> Confidence interval computation (Clopper-Pearson) -> Vertex selection (sorting for relaxation) -> Bound computation (MILP/LP solve) -> Output (discrete approximation & bound)

### Critical path
Solving the MILP (or its relaxation) is the most computationally intensive step; its tractability depends primarily on the support size *M* and the choice of formulation (Proposition 3.2 scales much better). Partition construction via Lloyd’s is polynomial in *N*<sub>train</sub> and *M*. Confidence interval and vertex selection steps are negligible.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Support size *M* vs. validation size *N*:** Larger *M* reduces quantization error but increases the number of binomial intervals (wider adjustments for *β*) and MILP variables, potentially loosening bounds; an optimal *M* often exists.
- **Theorem 3.1 vs. Proposition 3.2:** The former yields tighter bounds but becomes intractable for *M* ≳ 100; the latter offers scalable computation with moderate conservativeness (observed bound increase 10–30%).
- **Confidence level *β*:** Smaller *β* widens Clopper-Pearson intervals, loosening the bound; this trade-off is explicit.

### Failure signatures
- **MILP solver fails or times out:** Likely *M* too large for the chosen formulation. Switch to Proposition 3.2 or reduce *M*.
- **Many validation samples fall into *C*<sub>*M*</sub> (uncovered region):** Radii may be too small; increase *N*<sub>train</sub>, adjust the neighbor parameter (0.05), or inflate radii.
- **Extremely wide Clopper-Pearson intervals:** Indicates very few samples in some regions; *N* insufficient for given *M*. Increase *N*, merge regions, or lower *M*.
- **Poor Lloyd’s local optimum:** Bounds vary significantly across random seeds; use more restarts.
- **Bound exceeds known diameter *∥X∥*<sub>*ρ*</sub>:** Likely implementation error or violation of boundedness assumption.

### First 3 experiments
1. **MILP vs. relaxation scalability:** Measure computation time as *M* increases for 2D and 100D Gaussians; Proposition 3.2 remains tractable for *M* up to ~1000 while Theorem 3.1 often times out beyond *M* ≈ 100.
2. **Effect of variance:** Compare bounds for isotropic Gaussians with decreasing variance against the Fournier bound; the data-driven bound tightens significantly as variance decreases (e.g., *W*<sub>2</sub> bound ~0.5 vs. Fournier ~0.9 for σ = 0.10).
3. **Real-world high-dimensional dataset:** Apply to MiniBooNE (*d*=50, *N*≈125k); achieve *W*<sub>2</sub> bound 0.53 with *M*=100 versus Fournier bound 11.77 using the full empirical distribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Direct min-max solution:** Does directly solving the min‑max problem in (18) yield tighter Wasserstein bounds than the MILP relaxation? The full problem is computationally more demanding; the relaxation trades tightness for scalability.
2. **Unbounded support:** Can the method be extended to distributions with unbounded or completely unknown support? The analysis relies on boundedness for concentration and finite transport costs.
3. **Generalization to other OT distances:** Is it possible to generalize the framework to other optimal transport distances (e.g., Sinkhorn divergences) with similarly tight data‑driven bounds? Entropic regularization changes the structure of the transport problem.
4. **Hierarchical partitions:** How can hierarchical optimal transport (e.g., dyadic partitions) be combined with the proposed data‑driven approach to further reduce conservativeness and improve scalability? A principled integration that preserves tractability is missing.

## Limitations
- **Unspecified support *X* for real datasets:** How the bounded set is defined for datasets like MiniBooNE and OCTMNIST affects both the partition and baseline Fournier bound; the paper does not specify.
- **Missing Fournier constants:** The dimension-specific constants *E*<sub>*ρ*</sub>(*d*) required to compute the Fournier bound are not provided, making exact replication of comparisons impossible without the original tables.
- **Heuristic coverage:** The Lloyd’s-derived radii may leave significant mass in the residual region *C*<sub>*M*</sub>, especially in high dimensions, leading to conservative bounds.

## Confidence
- **Tighter-than-Fournier bounds:** *Medium* — Strong empirical evidence on synthetic and real data, but exact replication is blocked by unspecified details (support definition, Fournier constants, data splits).
- **Relaxation validity & speed:** *High* — Theoretical guarantee (Prop. 3.2) and experiments show orders-of-magnitude speedups with modest (10–30%) bound increase.
- **Partition quality:** *Medium* — Lloyd’s is standard but not globally optimal; no theoretical bound on suboptimality beyond typical quantization.
