# Can Large Language Models Generalize Procedures Across Representations?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03542  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03542  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A two-stage curriculum (symbolic → natural language) enables cross-representational procedural transfer, with a 1.5B model matching GPT-4o zero-shot on naturalistic planning.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can generalize procedural knowledge—such as solving planning or math problems—across different input representations (code, graphs, natural language). Training on a single symbolic representation (code or graphs) fails to transfer reliably to natural language tasks, while training only on natural language is inefficient. The authors propose a two-stage curriculum: first train on symbolic data to learn abstract algorithms, then fine-tune on natural language to adapt to surface variation. This approach substantially improves performance across model families (Qwen, Llama, Olmo) and tasks (planning, math, physics). A 1.5B Qwen model trained with the curriculum achieves parity with zero-shot GPT-4o on a naturalistic planning benchmark. Analysis suggests successful generalization is driven by structural analogy rather than data frequency, and the curriculum encourages more systematic reasoning traces.

## Method Summary
The study uses a synthetic, isomorphic dataset of asynchronous planning problems (critical path computation) presented in three forms: natural language descriptions, graph adjacency lists, and Python code. Models are trained via reinforcement learning (GRPO) on single-representation baselines (NL-only, Graph-only, Code-only) and compared against the proposed two-stage curriculum: 40 training steps on symbolic data (Graph or Code) followed by 40 steps on natural language. Evaluation measures accuracy on held-out test sets for each representation, plus robustness checks using non-standard English dialects (NL-AAVE) and additional tasks in math and physics. The primary analysis correlates performance with an analogical strength metric (DAG similarity) and inspects chain-of-thought outputs for reasoning patterns.

## Key Results
- Two-stage curriculum (symbolic → NL) yields significant cross-representational gains; e.g., Qwen-1.5B curriculum model reaches ~0.78 accuracy on NL planning, matching zero-shot GPT-4o (0.782) and far surpassing NL-only training (~0.60).
- Gains generalize across model architectures (Qwen, Llama, Olmo) and extend to math and physics tasks, with improved robustness to linguistic variation (NL-AAVE) though a performance gap remains.
- Structural analogy (DAG similarity) correlates more strongly with successful transfer than token frequency, suggesting models learn to map relational patterns rather than memorize surface forms.
- Reinforcement learning (GRPO) is critical; supervised fine-tuning and distillation show little to no cross-representation transfer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Symbolic-First Curriculum Induces Representation-Agnostic Knowledge
- **Claim**: Training first on symbolic data (code/graphs) forces the model to learn abstract algorithms (e.g., critical-path computation) that later adapt to natural language.
- **Mechanism**: Symbolic representations minimize linguistic noise and highlight formal structure. The subsequent natural language fine-tuning tunes the surface form while preserving the abstract procedure.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: "we propose a two-stage data curriculum that first trains on symbolic, then on natural language data." Section 4.1: Stage 1 symbolic warm-up alters NL training dynamics. Baseline single-representation training fails cross-transfer.
- **Break condition**: If stage 1 fails to achieve high within-representation accuracy on symbolic data, cross-representation gains are unlikely.

### Mechanism 2: Transfer Driven by Generative Analogy
- **Claim**: Successful cross-representation transfer stems from structural mapping (generative analogy) rather than exposure to similar token sequences.
- **Mechanism**: The model aligns underlying DAG structures by matching relational patterns (e.g., one-to-one node mappings, parallel connectivity) across representations.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: "Analysis indicates successful generalization correlates with structural analogy rather than data frequency." Section 5.1: Correlation analysis shows analogical strength (ρk) outperforms frequency-based (ρp) predictors, especially after curriculum.
- **Break condition**: Performance drops for test instances lacking a training counterpart with high structural (DAG) similarity.

### Mechanism 3: Symbolic Bootstrapping Promotes Systematic Reasoning
- **Claim**: Symbolic training induces explicit, exhaustive reasoning (e.g., iterating over all candidate paths) that persists in natural language settings, contrasting with the lazy heuristics of NL-only models.
- **Mechanism**: Precise algorithmic steps in symbolic data establish a habit of systematic solution-space exploration that carries over to NL.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 5.2: Qualitative examples show curriculum models listing multiple paths before selecting the longest; NL-only models often pick an arbitrary single path.
- **Break condition**: If reasoning traces do not become more systematic after symbolic training (e.g., still default to one path), the mechanism may be bypassed.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Cross-representation generalisation  
  *Why needed*: Core research question—can procedures learned in code/graphs transfer to natural language?  
  *Quick check*: "If a model solves a planning problem given as Python code, can it answer a natural language description of the same problem?"
- **Concept**: Generative analogy  
  *Why needed*: Explains transfer as structural mapping across representations, not surface similarity.  
  *Quick check*: "Does performance on a new natural language problem correlate with structural similarity to a previously seen graph problem, ignoring lexical overlap?"
- **Concept**: Data curriculum  
  *Why needed*: Proposed solution (symbolic → NL) relies on order; understanding why order matters is key.  
  *Quick check*: "Would reversing the curriculum (NL → symbolic) yield the same improvement? What about random mixing?"

## Architecture Onboarding
Data synthesis module → Training pipeline (GRPO) → Evaluation harness → Analysis utilities  
**Critical path**: Isomorphic data generation → single-representation baselines (failure) → curriculum training (symbolic then NL) → NL & robustness evaluation → correlation & qualitative analysis.  
**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Model scale: Smaller models (1.5B) benefit more from curriculum.  
- Training method: GRPO (RL) essential for transfer; SFT/distillation lag.  
- Stage steps: 40 symbolic + 40 NL beats 80 NL-only; order matters (NL→Graph hurts).  
- Representation: Graph vs. Code as symbolic stage both work; Graph slightly better in some cases.  
**Failure signatures**:  
- No within-representation improvement on symbolic data.  
- Reversed curriculum (NL→symbolic) degrades vs NL-only.  
- Gains do not extend to NL-AAVE (dialect) if model memorizes surface patterns.  
- STaR/vanilla SFT show minimal cross-transfer.  
**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Baseline replication: Train Qwen-2.5-1.5B-Instruct on NL-only, Graph-only, Code-only via GRPO; test all three; verify cross-failure.  
2. Curriculum implementation: Train Graph (40 steps) then NL (40 steps) with GRPO; compare NL test accuracy vs NL-only 80-step baseline; repeat reversed order to confirm order sensitivity.  
3. Qualitative analysis: Extract chain-of-thought for NL test instances from NL-only vs curriculum models; check for systematic path iteration (e.g., listing multiple paths) in curriculum model.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1**: Does reinforcement learning (GRPO) truly learn generalizable procedural knowledge or just exploit shallow patterns? The paper notes RL shows strong within-representation gains but diminished cross-representation advantage, questioning whether it captures abstract procedures or memorizes distributional shortcuts.
- **Question 2**: Why does the two-stage curriculum (symbolic → NL) succeed while NL → symbolic fails, and what mechanisms (e.g., symbolic induction, altered dynamics) drive its effectiveness? The curriculum’s empirical success is shown, but whether it works due to better initialization, regularization, or induced analogical reasoning is not isolated.
- **Question 3**: Why does a performance gap persist between NL and NL-AAVE after curriculum training, and how can we achieve dialect-invariant procedural reasoning? The curriculum model lacks exposure to AAVE, indicating residual sensitivity to surface linguistic variation that undermines robustness and fairness.
- **Question 4**: Is cross-representation generalization best explained as generative analogy, and can we obtain direct (non-correlational) evidence for analogy-driven mapping? The analogical strength metric uses graph similarity but does not prove active structural mapping; alternative explanations (latent feature similarity) are not ruled out.

## Limitations
- Exact GRPO hyperparameters (learning rate, clip range, reward scaling) are not fully disclosed, hindering exact replication.
- Prompt formatting details (few-shot demonstrations, system prompts) rely on appendix snippets; minor variations could alter results.
- The generative analogy evidence is correlational; no causal experiments rule out frequency confounders.
- The systematic reasoning claim rests on selective qualitative examples; quantitative process tracing is absent.

## Confidence
- Two-stage curriculum effectiveness: **High** (consistent gains across models and tasks with statistical significance).
- Generalization via generative analogy: **Medium** (strong correlation but lacking intervention studies).
- Symbolic bootstrapping induces systematic reasoning: **Medium/Low** (limited to anecdotal chain-of-thought inspection).

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Vary GRPO settings (KL coefficient 0.001–0.1, clip range 0.1–0.3) to confirm curriculum robustness.
2. **Causal analogy manipulation**: Create training splits where structural similarity (DAG isomorphism) is decoupled from lexical overlap; measure transfer when analogical strength is controlled.
3. **Automated reasoning analysis**: Parse chain-of-thought outputs, count explicit path-comparison steps, and compare distributions between NL-only and curriculum models on hundreds of test cases.
