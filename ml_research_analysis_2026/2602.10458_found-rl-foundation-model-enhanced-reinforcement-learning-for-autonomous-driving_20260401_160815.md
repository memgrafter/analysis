# Found-RL: foundation model-enhanced reinforcement learning for autonomous driving

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.10458  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10458  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** A lightweight RL policy (3.82M parameters) achieves near-VLM performance on CARLA benchmarks (Driving Score 0.77, Success Rate 57%) while operating at ~500 FPS, vastly outpacing VLMs (~1 FPS).

## Executive Summary
Found-RL enhances reinforcement learning for autonomous driving by integrating foundation models (VLMs) to provide semantic guidance and dense rewards. To overcome VLM latency, it introduces an asynchronous batch inference framework that decouples VLM reasoning from the simulation loop, enabling real-time training at high throughput. The method distills VLM action priors into the RL policy via Value-Margin Regularization (VMR) and Advantage-Weighted Action Guidance (AWAG), and adds CLIP-based rewards through Conditional Contrastive Action Alignment. Despite using a small 3.82M‑parameter model, Found-RL matches or exceeds the performance of large VLMs on CARLA Leaderboard and NoCrash benchmarks, dramatically improving sample efficiency and safety.

## Method Summary
Found-RL builds on off‑policy actor‑critic RL algorithms (SAC, DrQv2) and augments them with VLM supervision. The system runs in a distributed fashion: rollout workers in the CARLA simulator generate observations (BEV masks and state vectors) and send prompts to a request queue. An inference server batches these prompts across environments and time, processes them through a VLM or CLIP model, and returns outputs (action suggestions, reward bonuses) via an output queue. The replay buffer stores transitions together with a validity mask *m_t* indicating whether VLM feedback arrived in time. During training, the learner samples minibatches and applies four losses: (1) standard TD actor‑critic; (2) VMR, which pushes Q‑values for VLM‑suggested actions above other actions by a margin; (3) AWAG, which adaptively weights the policy gradient to encourage actions with positive advantage relative to the VLM; and (4) a CLIP‑shaped reward added to the environmental reward, computed via conditional contrastive alignment over discretized speed/command contexts. The framework supports various VLMs (e.g., InternVL3, Qwen2.5‑VL) and runs at ~500 FPS.

## Key Results
- On CARLA Leaderboard, DrQv2‑CLIP attains Driving Score **0.77** and Success Rate **57%**, matching a much larger VLM (Qwen2.5‑vl‑7b, 0.76).
- On NoCrash, SAC‑VMR increases Driving Score from **0.24 to 0.71** in Town01 and generalizes to Town02.
- Safety improves markedly; SAC‑VMR reduces vehicle collisions from **0.50 to 0.20 per km**.
- The system operates at approximately **500 FPS**, orders of magnitude faster than direct VLM inference (~1 FPS).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Asynchronous Batch Inference**  
Claim: This mechanism decouples VLM reasoning from simulation, resolving latency bottlenecks for real‑time RL training.  
Mechanism: A client‑server architecture with shared queues lets rollout workers send prompts to a request queue *𝒬*<sub>req</sub>. The inference server forms micro‑batches of size up to *B*<sub>max</sub> with timeout *τ*, processes them through the VLM, and returns outputs via *𝒬*<sub>out</sub> keyed by environment and timestep. The replay buffer stores a validity mask *m<sub>t</sub>* indicating whether feedback arrived in time, allowing masked objectives. This design overlaps inference across parallel environments and amortizes fixed overheads.  
Core assumption: VLM inference latency dominates simulation step time; batching and asynchrony prevent the simulator from waiting.  
Evidence: The abstract states it “effectively resolving latency bottlenecks to support real‑time training.” Section 4.1 provides equations (6‑9) for batching and masked objectives. Related work (e.g., DriveVLM‑RL) uses similar asynchronous designs, but Found‑RL’s integration is novel.  
Break condition: If VLM latency remains too high even with batching, many transitions will have *m<sub>t</sub>*=0 (stale feedback). Queue overflows could drop requests, degrading supervision quality.

**VLM Action Guidance via VMR and AWAG**  
Claim: These mechanisms distill expert‑like VLM priors into the RL policy, improving sample efficiency and safety.  
Mechanism: VMR adds a critic regularization term *m·[Q(o, a<sub>vlm</sub>) – (stopgrad(Q(o, a)) + Δ)]²* with cosine‑decayed coefficient *λ(step)*. AWAG computes advantage *A<sub>t</sub> = q<sub>vlm</sub> – q<sub>π</sub>*, applies a Q‑filter gate *g<sub>t</sub> = 1{A<sub>t</sub> > 0}*, and weights the policy gradient for the VLM action by *w<sub>t</sub> = clip(exp(A<sub>t</sub>/β), w<sub>max</sub>)*, using stopgrad for stability. Both treat VLM suggestions as expert demonstrations that shape early training.  
Core assumption: VLM actions are semantically correct and safer than random exploration, especially initially; the critic estimates advantage reliably.  
Evidence: The abstract mentions “Value‑Margin Regularization (VMR) and Advantage‑Weighted Action Guidance (AWAG).” Section 4.2 details the equations (12‑14) for VMR and (16‑24) for AWAG. The formulations are unique to Found‑RL.  
Break condition: Suboptimal VLM actions (e.g., due to fine‑tuning errors) could mislead the policy. Over‑imitation if *λ* decays too slowly; under‑guidance if it decays too quickly.

**Conditional Contrastive Action Alignment**  
Claim: CLIP‑based dense reward shaping with this alignment mitigates CLIP’s dynamic blindness and probability dilution.  
Mechanism: Speed is discretized into 4 categories and command into 6, yielding a context *c<sub>t</sub>* = (cmd<sub>t</sub>, spd<sub>t</sub>). For a given state, 720 context‑action prompts are created but only the 30 relevant anchors are retrieved per step. The CLIP model scores each anchor; the reward bonus is the normalized margin between the score of the executed action and the average score of actions in its semantic neighbor set *𝒩(a<sub>t</sub>)*, scaled by *λ* and added to the environmental reward. A running mean‑std (RMS) filter clips the bonus to [−1, 1].  
Core assumption: CLIP’s vision‑text alignment captures driving maneuvers when prompts are state‑conditioned and evaluated on a small discriminative set; a large margin indicates expert‑like behavior.  
Evidence: Abstract notes “CLIP‑based dense reward shaping with Conditional Contrastive Action Alignment.” Section 4.3 explains discretization (Tables 9‑11), conditional slicing, Eq. (26) for margin, and Eq. (27) for final reward. The neighbor masking is novel.  
Break condition: Coarse discretization may render the reward uninformative; a poorly defined *𝒩(a<sub>t</sub>)* could penalize valid actions; insufficient CLIP fine‑tuning leads to low alignment accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: CARLA simulator → Rollout workers (construct prompts, send to request queue *𝒬*<sub>req</sub>) → Inference server (batch formation with *B*<sub>max</sub> and *τ*, run VLM/CLIP, push to output queue *𝒬*<sub>out</sub>) → Replay buffer (stores transitions with *m<sub>t</sub>* and VLM outputs) → RL trainer (samples, computes losses) → Evaluation module (metrics). Interfaces are model‑agnostic to support various VLMs.

Critical path: At each iteration, the simulator steps generate observations and contexts; rollout workers encode these into prompts and enqueue them. The inference server dequeues requests, assembles micro‑batches, processes them, and returns VLM actions and CLIP scores. These are matched to the originating environment and timestep via keys (e, t) and stored in the replay buffer with a validity mask *m<sub>t</sub>*. The RL trainer then samples a minibatch and performs gradient updates using the combined losses (TD, VMR, AWAG, CLIP reward). The loop repeats; a key dependency is that VLM feedback must be available (*m<sub>t</sub>*=1) for the step to contribute to auxiliary losses.

Design tradeoffs:  
- **Batching *B*<sub>max</sub>**: Larger batches increase GPU utilization and throughput but add per‑request latency.  
- **Timeout *τ***: Shorter *τ* reduces waiting but may produce smaller batches, hurting efficiency.  
- **VMR vs. AWAG**: VMR modifies the critic (less invasive to the policy but can bias Q‑values); AWAG directly re‑weights the policy update, offering flexibility but requiring a stable critic.  
- **CLIP reward**: Conditional slicing limits computation to 30 anchors per step, but discretization loses continuous nuance; the neighbor set *𝒩(a<sub>t</sub>)* must be chosen to avoid false penalization.

Failure signatures:  
- Low Driving Score (<0.5) or high collision rate (>1.0 per km) despite VLM guidance → poor distillation or misaligned rewards.  
- Low VLM availability (*m<sub>t</sub>*≈0) → inference bottleneck or queue saturation.  
- Diverging actor/critic losses (e.g., exploding values) in VMR → un‑decayed *λ* or overly aggressive margin *Δ*.  
- CLIP reward contribution dropping below 80% → inference failures or poor prompt matching.

First 3 experiments:  
1. Baseline replication: Train vanilla SAC and DrQv2 on the Leaderboard benchmark without VLM, record initial performance (e.g., SAC Driving Score ~0.21) to quantify sample inefficiency.  
2. Full pipeline test: Implement asynchronous inference with InternVL3‑1B, enable VMR for SAC, verify throughput >400 FPS and Driving Score improvement above 0.5 within 100k environment steps; monitor queue lengths and the *m<sub>t</sub>* ratio.  
3. Ablation and generalization: Disable CLIP reward in DrQv2‑CLIP on NoCrash Town02, measure the drop in Success Rate; compare against a VLM‑only baseline to confirm that the distilled small policy matches large VLM performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The authors highlight three open questions for future work:

1. **Accelerating VLM inference** – How can inference be accelerated or quantized to prevent stale feedback and maintain broad sample coverage when scaling Found‑RL to larger foundation models? Larger VLMs increase latency, potentially causing feedback desynchronization and degrading stability.

2. **Evaluation on more challenging benchmarks** – Does Found‑RL maintain its advantages on harder benchmarks like CARLA Leaderboard 2.0 with dense traffic and complex scenarios? The BEV mask resolution and discrete CLIP prompts may struggle with finer‑grained interactions.

3. **Multi‑VLM ensembles** – Can orchestrating a multi‑VLM ensemble, where distinct models specialize in different aspects (safety, comfort, efficiency), further enhance learning efficiency and robustness? Combining signals risks conflicts and increased latency.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter gaps**: Exact *λ* schedules for VMR/AWAG/CLIP, AWAG parameters (*α, β, w<sub>max</sub>*), and base RL settings (learning rates, entropy coefficient) are unreported, hindering precise reproduction.
- **Implementation specifics**: Asynchronous queue capacities, timeout *τ*, fallback handling for missing VLM feedback (*m<sub>t</sub>*=0), and VLM/CLIP fine‑tuning recipes (splits, prompts, optimizers) are not fully described.
- **Generalization limits**: Results are confined to the CARLA simulator; real‑world robustness under weather variation, sensor noise, and diverse traffic remains unproven.
