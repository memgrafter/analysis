# Adaptive Double-Booking Strategy for Outpatient Scheduling Using Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07270
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07270
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Adaptive double-booking via multi-objective reinforcement learning yields higher slot utilization (up to 0.793) and improved trade-offs across utilization, double-show avoidance, and attendance balance.

## Executive Summary
Outpatient clinics suffer from inefficiencies due to patient no-shows. This paper proposes an adaptive double-booking scheduling framework that uses multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) to balance three competing goals: effective slot utilization, double-show avoidance, and attendance balance. The approach integrates individualized no-show probabilities predicted by a Multi-Head Attention Soft Random Forest (MHASRF) into a Markov decision process (MDP). The MORL problem is solved with a novel Multi-Policy Proximal Policy Optimization (MPPPO) algorithm that employs a Multi-Policy Co-Evolution Mechanism (MPCEM) to learn a diverse set of policies approximating the Pareto frontier. Simulation results show that the learned policies outperform static overbooking heuristics, achieving higher utilization (up to 0.793) and better balance while also maintaining strong double-show avoidance (0.937). The policies are interpreted using SHAP, confirming that they adapt double-booking decisions based on predicted no-show risk and current workload.

## Method Summary
The authors formulate the scheduling problem as a Markov decision process with states that include patient and appointment features, current physician workload, and the individualized no-show probability π_i from the MHASRF model. At each booking event, the agent selects an action: single-book, double-book, or reject. The reward function combines three components: effective slot utilization, double-show avoidance, and attendance balance, weighted by a policy-specific vector (α, β, γ). The problem is solved using a Multi-Policy Proximal Policy Optimization (MPPPO) algorithm with a Multi-Policy Co-Evolution Mechanism (MPCEM). MPPPO trains ten PPO agents in parallel, each with a distinct weight vector. Every C steps, MPCEM identifies neighboring policies based on KL divergence of their action distributions and mixes their parameters with an adaptive mixing coefficient τ. The policies are evaluated in a discrete-event simulation that models Poisson arrival of booking requests, Gamma-distributed lead times, and Bernoulli attendance outcomes conditioned on π_i.

## Key Results
- Effective slot utilization reached up to 0.793, surpassing fixed double-booking and single-booking baselines.
- The proposed policies achieved better attendance balance compared to baselines.
- A policy tuned for attendance balance also attained strong double-show avoidance (0.937), demonstrating effective multi-objective trade-offs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Patient-specific no-show probabilities from the MHASRF model are integrated into the RL state to enable adaptive double-booking decisions.
- **Mechanism:** The MHASRF model processes patient and appointment features to generate a predicted no-show probability `π_i`. This value is included in the MDP state vector `s_t` at each booking event, allowing the RL agent to condition its action (single-book, double-book, reject) on individualized risk rather than fixed heuristics.
- **Core assumption:** The MHASRF predictions are sufficiently accurate and generalize to new booking requests, providing actionable risk signals that correlate with actual attendance outcomes.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "individualized no-show predictions from a Multi-Head Attention Soft Random Forest... incorporated in the reinforcement learning state."
  - [section 3.3.1] "MHASRF... produces a predicted no-show probability π_i. This value is incorporated into the scheduling environment state and used by the RL agent to guide booking decisions."
  - [corpus] Weak relevance: Neighbors apply MORL to other domains (supply chain, traffic control) but do not address the integration of patient-specific predictive models into scheduling states. This supports the plausibility of prediction-informed RL but does not directly evidence this paper's mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If MHASRF prediction error (e.g., high Brier score) increases beyond a tolerance threshold, the state representation becomes misleading, potentially degrading policy performance. The paper's sensitivity analysis (Section 4.3.5) shows moderate perturbations (±3%) have little impact, but larger overestimation harms performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The Multi-Policy PPO (MPPPO) with Multi-Policy Co-Evolution Mechanism (MPCEM) learns a diverse set of policies approximating the Pareto frontier for the three competing objectives (utilization, double-show avoidance, balance).
- **Mechanism:** Ten policies are trained in parallel, each with a distinct fixed weight vector `w_p = [α_p, β_p, γ_p]` for the multi-objective reward (Eq. 13). The adaptive τ rule based on KL divergence (Eq. 16-17) selectively transfers parameters among behaviorally similar neighboring policies during periodic co-evolution phases, improving convergence and expanding trade-off diversity.
- **Core assumption:** The objective space can be effectively discretized into a finite set of weight vectors whose corresponding policies collectively approximate the continuous Pareto front, and behavioral similarity (measured by KL divergence of action distributions) correlates with policy compatibility for knowledge transfer.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Multi-Policy Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm... Multi-Policy Co-Evolution Mechanism... τ rule based on Kullback-Leibler divergence that enables selective knowledge transfer among behaviorally similar policies."
  - [section 3.5] "MPPPO extends PPO by training multiple actor-critic pairs in parallel under different objective-weight configurations... MPCEM enables periodic knowledge transfer among neighboring policies in weight space."
  - [corpus] Weak relevance: Neighbor papers (e.g., MORSE, Graph-Enhanced DRL) use MORL for other multi-objective scheduling problems, demonstrating that multi-policy approaches can approximate Pareto fronts. This indirectly supports the mechanism's design rationale but does not confirm the specific KL-based τ rule's efficacy.
- **Break condition:** If the KL divergence thresholding fails to distinguish dissimilar policies, detrimental knowledge transfer could occur, collapsing policy diversity. The paper does not explicitly test this failure mode but assumes the adaptive τ prevents it.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** SHAP interpretability reveals that the learned scheduling policies align with operational intuition: higher predicted no-show risk increases double-booking likelihood, while higher current patient load decreases it.
- **Mechanism:** SHAP values are computed for the RL policy's action preferences over the state features. For each action (single-book, double-book), SHAP quantifies the marginal contribution of each state variable (e.g., `π_i`, `N_p`) to the policy's action logits, showing which factors drive decisions.
- **Core assumption:** The SHAP values accurately explain the black-box policy's decisions, and the resulting feature importance patterns are valid and stable across the state space.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "SHapley Additive exPlanations is used to interpret both the predicted no-show risk and the agent's scheduling decisions."
  - [section 4.3.2] "For the double-booking decisions (Figure 6b), higher predicted no-show probability is the most influential factor, strongly increase the likelihood of double-booking... higher scheduled patient counts reduce the tendency to double-book."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; SHAP is a general explainability method. The corpus does not discuss SHAP in this scheduling context.
- **Break condition:** If SHAP explanations are inconsistent (e.g., high reliance on noise features or contradictory patterns across state samples), the claimed intuitive alignment may be spurious. The paper shows consistent patterns in Figure 6 but does not conduct robustness tests for SHAP stability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation**
  - Why needed here: The scheduling problem involves sequential decisions under uncertainty (stochastic booking arrivals, patient attendance), where each action affects future slot availability. The MDP formally captures this as `(S, A, P, R, γ)` with state, action, transition, reward, discount.
  - Quick check question: If the state `s_t` omitted the physician's remaining slots `A_p`, what key information for future decisions would the agent lack?

- **Concept: Policy gradient methods (PPO)**
  - Why needed here: The state space is high-dimensional and context-rich (includes clinic/department/physician IDs, slot status, predicted probabilities). Value-based methods (e.g., DQN) struggle with such large, discrete action spaces; PPO's clipped surrogate objective provides stable policy updates.
  - Quick check question: Why might a fixed `ε` clipping parameter in PPO be insufficient for this multi-policy training setting?

- **Concept: Multi-objective optimization trade-offs**
  - Why needed here: Scheduling must balance three competing objectives: high slot utilization (favor double-booking), low double-show risk (favor single-booking), and attendance balance (target E_t ≈ 1). Scalarizing via weighted sum requires careful weight selection to explore the Pareto frontier.
  - Quick check question: If a policy optimizes only `αU_t + βD_t` (ignoring `γB_t`), what pathological scheduling pattern might emerge?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** MHASRF predictor -> no-show probability π_i; Simulation environment (Gym) -> MDP state/action/reward; MPPPO with MPCEM (10 parallel actor-critic MLPs) -> policy; SHAP interpreter -> post-hoc analysis.
- **Critical path:** Each booking event: (1) MHASRF predicts π_i; (2) environment constructs state s_t including π_i; (3) MPPPO agent selects action a_t; (4) environment updates schedule, computes shaped reward; (5) on appointment day, realized reward computed based on actual attendance. MPCEM runs every C steps to mix policy parameters.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Simplicity vs. adaptability: Fixed 14-day horizon and 30-min slots simplify simulation but may not capture real clinic variability.
  - Reward shaping vs. realism: Shaped reward provides early feedback but may bias learning if π_i is biased; final evaluation uses realized reward only.
  - Policy diversity vs. stability: More policies (10) better approximate Pareto front but increase training compute; adaptive τ aims to maintain diversity while stabilizing co-evolution.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High double-show rate (>0.1) on test despite low π_i values → policy ignoring no-show predictions.
  - Low effective slot utilization (<0.6) → policy too conservative or prediction errors causing under-booking.
  - MPPPO policies converging to identical performance → MPCEM τ too aggressive or neighborhood definition too broad.
  - SHAP shows slot time-of-day dominating π_i → policy ignoring patient-specific risk.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. MHASRF validation: Evaluate prediction accuracy (AUC, Brier score) on hold-out historical data.
  2. MDP reward sanity check: Run simple heuristic policies (always single-book, always double-book) to verify reward components vary as expected.
  3. MPPPO convergence monitoring: Plot total reward per policy during training and track weight vectors to ensure they diverge and MPCEM does not cause collapse.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the proposed adaptive double-booking framework perform in real-world outpatient clinics compared to simulation results, considering unmodeled factors like variable service times, patient arrival variations, and staff adaptability?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states that future work should extend the framework to "more complex end-to-end scheduling environments," implying a gap in real-world validation beyond simulation.
- **Why unresolved:** The evaluation relies solely on a discrete-event simulation using historical data with simplified assumptions (e.g., fixed slot intervals, Bernoulli attendance). Real clinics exhibit additional dynamics such as stochastic service durations, walk-in patients, and staff decision-making that are not captured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A pilot implementation in an actual outpatient clinic, comparing key metrics (effective slot utilization, double-show avoidance, attendance balance) between the RL-based policy and existing baseline policies over multiple months, with statistical analysis of operational outcomes and clinician feedback.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the multi-policy reinforcement learning approach (MPPPO with MPCEM) scale to larger healthcare systems with multiple interconnected clinics, departments, and physicians while maintaining policy stability and performance?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion identifies scaling to "larger patient volumes and more complex end-to-end scheduling environments" as a future direction, highlighting a scalability gap.
- **Why unresolved:** The simulation environment approximates capacity using average physician counts and focuses on a single hierarchical level; scaling to larger networks increases state-action space dimensionality and may impair convergence or lead to suboptimal policies due to credit assignment challenges.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Computational experiments with synthetically scaled environments (e.g., varying numbers of providers from 10 to 1000), measuring policy performance metrics, training stability (e.g., reward variance), and sample efficiency to identify scalability limits and necessary algorithmic adjustments.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How robust are the learned scheduling policies to systematic errors or drift in individualized no-show probability predictions from the MHASRF model over time?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The framework integrates predicted no-show probabilities (π_i) into state and reward, and while a sensitivity analysis tests random perturbations (±3%, ±5%), it does not address persistent biases or temporal shifts in prediction accuracy that occur in practice.
- **Why unresolved:** Real-world prediction models may suffer from dataset shift or biased training data, leading to consistent over- or under-estimation. The RL policy, trained on a fixed prediction distribution, might make suboptimal decisions if predictions degrade, with no adaptation mechanism.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulations injecting controlled prediction biases (e.g., constant offset, gradual drift) into the no-show probabilities during evaluation, assessing degradation in policy performance relative to baseline, and testing online adaptation techniques like periodic model retraining within the RL loop.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What operational rule or decision-support tool can clinics use to dynamically select the most appropriate policy from the Pareto-optimal set trained under different objective weightings?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper trains multiple policies to approximate a Pareto frontier for different trade-offs but does not explain how a clinic should choose which policy to deploy given real-time conditions like fluctuating demand or staffing constraints.
- **Why unresolved:** Without a selection mechanism, the multi-policy approach remains theoretical; clinics need actionable guidance to match policy choice with current priorities (e.g., high no-show periods vs. congestion avoidance), which is unaddressed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop and validate a policy selection heuristic (e.g., based on real-time metrics like booking rate or current utilization) in simulation, measuring improvements in weighted reward across varying operational scenarios, and possibly conducting user studies with clinic managers for usability.

## Limitations
- **Missing MHASRF details:** The core predictor's architecture and hyperparameters are not provided, preventing replication and limiting assessment of prediction quality impact.
- **Incomplete simulation parameters:** Exact Gamma distribution for lead time, physician capacity, and slot search tie-breakers are unspecified, hindering exact environment recreation.
- **Insufficient mechanism validation:** Lack of ablation studies for MPCEM and SHAP stability tests weakens confidence in the necessity and reliability of the proposed mechanisms.
