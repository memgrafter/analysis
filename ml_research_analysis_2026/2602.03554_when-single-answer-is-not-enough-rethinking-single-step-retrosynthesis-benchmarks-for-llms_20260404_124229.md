# When Single Answer Is Not Enough: Rethinking Single-Step Retrosynthesis Benchmarks for LLMs
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03554
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03554
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduced ChemCensor (plausibility metric), CREED (6.4M training reactions), URSA-expert-2026 (100 novel targets); C3LM fine-tuned on CREED achieves state-of-the-art ChemCensor scores, while baseline LLMs show large generalization gaps.

## Executive Summary
Traditional single-step retrosynthesis benchmarks rely on exact-match Top-K accuracy against a single ground truth route, failing to capture the multiple valid synthetic choices chemists consider. This paper argues that evaluation should instead measure chemical plausibility—whether a proposed transformation type has been observed before in a compatible chemical environment. To this end, they propose ChemCensor, a metric that checks reaction centers and functional group compatibility against a database of historical reactions (USPTO-full). Using ChemCensor, they build CREED, a large, quality-controlled dataset for training, and URSA-expert-2026, a benchmark of 100 novel, expert-validated target molecules. Benchmarking shows diverse LLMs perform much worse on URSA-expert-2026 than on the standard USPTO-50K-test, revealing overfitting. Fine-tuning on CREED yields C3LM, which attains superior ChemCensor scores on both benchmarks, demonstrating the value of plausibility-based evaluation and high-quality training data.

## Method Summary
The core method revolves around a new plausibility metric, ChemCensor, which scores a proposed retrosynthesis reaction (product → reactants) from 0 to 5. It extracts a hierarchical reaction center (RC1–RC5, expanding substructural context) and a 515-bit functional group signature from the SMILES, then queries a relational database derived from USPTO-full. The score equals the highest RC level with supporting precedents that share the same functional group pattern. To train models, the authors construct CREED by enumerating forward and retro reactions using ~3,000 bidirectional expert-coded templates applied to commercially available building blocks and template-specific molecules. All generated reactions pass ChemCensor (>0) and are decontaminated against holdout sets. The C3LM model starts from Qwen3-8B and undergoes supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on CREED, optionally with reasoning traces, followed by reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Rewards are based on ChemCensor scores, exact matches in CREED, or agreement with a molecular transformer. Evaluation uses few-shot prompting on URSA-expert-2026 and USPTO-50K-test: for each target product, 15 samples are generated, deduplicated, and ranked by log-probability; primary metrics are average maximum ChemCensor (PT-Max CC) and average Top-K ChemCensor (PT-Top-K CC).

## Key Results
- ChemCensor provides a plausibility score that aligns better with human synthesis planning than exact-match metrics.
- CREED contains 6.38M unique reactions across 653K products, averaging 9.8 reactions per product.
- URSA-expert-2026 reveals a sharp performance drop for nearly all baseline LLMs compared to USPTO-50K-test, indicating overfitting to standard benchmarks.
- C3LM (SFT with reasoning) achieves PT-Max CC 1.91 on URSA-expert-2026, outperforming all proprietary and specialist models; RFT with ChemCensor reward further improves to PT-Max CC 2.03.
- Adding raw USPTO-full to CREED training boosts USPTO-50K-test scores but reduces prediction uniqueness, highlighting a trade-off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: ChemCensor Plausibility Metric
- **Claim:** Evaluating retrosynthesis via reaction center and functional group compatibility yields more realistic performance estimates than exact-match Top-K accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** ChemCensor decomposes a reaction into a hierarchical reaction center (RC1–RC5) and a 515-bit functional group signature. It queries a relational database from USPTO-full to find if both the RC pattern and its associated FG signature have been observed together. The confidence score (1–5) reflects the largest RC context size with supporting precedents.
- **Core assumption:** Human chemists judge feasibility based on whether a transformation type has been done before in a compatible environment, not by matching one specific published route.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the approach "better aligns with human synthesis planning practices"; Section 3.1 explains higher CC corresponds to broader RC context matching; related work cites metric limitations.
- **Break condition:** If USPTO-full lacks coverage for a valid novel reaction type, ChemCensor scores it zero, penalizing legitimate innovation.

### Mechanism 2: CREED Template-Driven Data Generation
- **Claim:** A large-scale, ChemCensor-verified training dataset enables models to learn plausible transformations rather than memorizing isolated examples.
- **Mechanism:** Using ~3K expert-coded bidirectional reaction templates, CREED employs a virtual synthesis engine to exhaustively generate forward and retro reactions from commercially available building blocks (CABBs) and template-specific molecular structures. All 6.4M reactions pass ChemCensor (>0) and undergo decontamination against holdout sets.
- **Core assumption:** Template-based enumeration with plausibility filtering provides a scalable path to diverse, high-quality training data capturing multiple valid synthetic choices.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.4 details the generation paradigms and ChemCensor verification; Table 2 shows statistics (6.38M reactions, 653K products, avg 9.8 per product); supported by template-based retrosynthesis literature.
- **Break condition:** Templates may limit exploration of truly novel reaction mechanisms not in the expert-coded set.

### Mechanism 3: C3LM Supervision with Chemical Reward
- **Claim:** Fine-tuning foundational LLMs on CREED with ChemCensor-based rewards produces models that generalize to out-of-distribution targets while maintaining plausibility.
- **Mechanism:** Starting from Qwen3-8B, C3LM undergoes SFT on CREED, optionally with reasoning traces. RFT then uses GRPO with rewards derived from ChemCensor scores, CREED exact-match, or a molecular transformer, directly optimizing for chemical plausibility.
- **Core assumption:** Reward functions grounded in validity metrics steer the model toward synthetically feasible transformations.
- **Evidence anchors:** Sections 4.2–4.3 detail SFT/RFT pipelines; Table 1 shows C3LM RFT with ChemCensor reward achieves best PT-Max CC (2.03) on URSA-expert-2026; Figure 1 indicates highest fraction of reactions passing ChemCensor.
- **Break condition:** Reward may overfit to ChemCensor's precedent database, undervaluing novel chemistries absent from USPTO-full.

## Foundational Learning
- **Chemical SMILES/SMARTS representation:** Needed because all inputs/outputs use SMILES, and ChemCensor uses SMARTS for pattern matching. Quick check: Can you interpret canonical SMILES for ethanol and identify its functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl)?
- **Template-based retrosynthesis:** Needed since CREED generation and ChemCensor rely on reaction templates and center extraction to define transformation patterns. Quick check: Given a reaction SMILES, can you identify the bond changes and classify it (e.g., amide formation)?
- **Benchmarking pitfalls in chemistry AI:** Needed to understand why exact-match metrics fail for retrosynthesis due to multiple valid routes. Quick check: Why is there often more than one valid synthetic route to a complex molecule, and how does that challenge Top-K accuracy?
- **Reaction center extraction:** Needed to define the substructural context for ChemCensor scoring. Quick check: For a simple substitution reaction, what is the minimal set of atoms/bonds that define the transformation?
- **Functional group compatibility:** Needed to ensure proposed reactants can coexist without interfering reactions. Quick check: Would a free amine and an acyl chloride be compatible in the same reaction mixture? Why or why not?
- **Data contamination in chemical benchmarks:** Needed to assess model generalization. Quick check: How would you detect if a test set molecule is too similar to training data (e.g., via Tanimoto similarity)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** URSA system comprises: (1) ChemCensor evaluator (RC/FG extractor + USPTO-full relational DB) -> (2) CREED generator (template engine + virtual synthesis + CC verification) -> (3) URSA-expert-2026 benchmark (novel targets + expert validation) -> (4) C3LM model (Qwen3-8B base + SFT/RFT on CREED) -> (5) baseline LLM evaluators.
- **Critical path:** Evaluation metric (ChemCensor) directly shapes training data generation (CREED), which determines fine-tuning (C3LM), which is then benchmarked on URSA-expert-2026 and USPTO-50K-test.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Exact-match vs. plausibility (simplicity vs. realism), template coverage vs. generation diversity (quality vs. breadth), base model scale vs. fine-tuning cost (performance vs. resources). URSA-expert-2026 avoids leakage but has limited size (n=100).
- **Failure signatures:** (1) Sharp performance drop on URSA-expert-2026 vs. USPTO-50K-test indicates overfitting; (2) High duplicate rate in predictions shows lack of diversity; (3) Specific functional group-incompatibility heatmap clusters (e.g., aryl halides, amines) reveal systematic chemoselectivity gaps.
- **First 3 experiments:** (1) Run baseline LLMs on both benchmarks to confirm the generalization gap. (2) Ablate C3LM training: compare CREED-only vs. CREED+USPTO-full, with vs. without reasoning to isolate contributions. (3) Perform error analysis: categorize failed predictions by ChemCensor failure mode (RC missing vs. FG incompatibility) and by reaction class to identify model blind spots.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does ChemCensor score correlate with expert human evaluation of synthetic plausibility?
- **Basis in paper:** They claim it "better aligns with human synthesis planning practices" but provide no empirical validation.
- **Why unresolved:** Without direct comparison, it is unclear if the metric captures nuanced chemical reasoning or oversimplifies plausibility.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Professional chemists rate diverse reactions for plausibility; compute correlation with ChemCensor scores and assess agreement.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What factors cause the sharp performance drop of LLMs on URSA-expert-2026 versus USPTO-50K-test?
- **Basis in paper:** They report the drop but do not analyze underlying reasons.
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding whether drop stems from molecular complexity, distribution shift, or novelty is essential for robustness and benchmark design.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Detailed error analysis comparing chemical properties (size, functional groups, reaction class) between benchmarks; test model predictions on subsets to isolate challenging features.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does adding USPTO-full to CREED training reduce prediction uniqueness while boosting USPTO-50K-test scores?
- **Basis in paper:** They note this counterintuitive result but do not explain it.
- **Why unresolved:** This suggests interference between datasets; without explanation, curation strategies remain uncertain.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies varying data mixtures; analysis of training dynamics (e.g., diversity of templates seen); tests on synthetic data to see if duplicates in USPTO-full cause mode collapse.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can retrosynthesis models maintain high chemical plausibility without sacrificing output diversity?
- **Basis in paper:** They observe RFT with ChemCensor reward increases PT-Max CC but improvements shrink as Top-K grows (suggesting reduced diversity).
- **Why unresolved:** Both plausibility and diversity are critical; the trade-off limits practical usefulness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop multi-objective or regularized training methods optimizing both ChemCensor score and diversity; evaluate Pareto frontiers on both benchmarks.

## Limitations
- ChemCensor penalizes chemically valid innovations absent from USPTO-full, introducing database coverage bias.
- CREED’s template-driven enumeration may not capture truly novel reaction mechanisms beyond expert-coded templates.
- C3LM’s reward optimization with ChemCensor could overfit to historical precedents, undervaluing unseen chemistries.
- Lack of public access to CREED and URSA-expert-2026 hinders reproducibility and independent validation.

## Confidence
- **ChemCensor Plausibility Metric:** Medium. Aligns with human heuristics but vulnerable to USPTO-full’s limitations; weak direct empirical validation.
- **CREED Dataset Utility:** High for providing high-quality, verified reactions; Medium for representing full reaction diversity due to template constraints.
- **C3LM Generalization Performance:** Medium. Shows improved scores on held-out benchmarks but confirmed overfitting to standard tests suggests robustness issues.
