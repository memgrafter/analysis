# WiFi2Cap: Semantic Action Captioning from Wi-Fi CSI via Limb-Level Semantic Alignment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.22690
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22690
- **Reference count**: 33
- **Primary result**: On the WiFi2Cap Dataset, WiFi2Cap raises BLEU-4 from 14.85 (CSI-only baseline) to 51.78, METEOR from 25.86 to 57.48, and ROUGE-L from 32.38 to 64.32. Gains transfer to Person-in-WiFi 3D (BLEU-4: 12.15→47.07).

## Executive Summary
WiFi2Cap introduces a privacy‑preserving framework for generating natural language descriptions of human actions directly from Wi‑Fi Channel State Information (CSI). The method overcomes the vast modality gap between low‑level CSI signals and high‑level semantics, as well as left/right limb ambiguity, by leveraging a three‑stage pipeline: (1) a vision‑language teacher trained with a Mirror‑Consistency Loss to learn direction‑aware embeddings; (2) a CSI student network that aligns CSI to the teacher’s visual and text spaces via contrastive learning; and (3) a prefix‑tuned language model that generates fluent captions from the aligned CSI embeddings. Evaluated on a new WiFi2Cap dataset, the approach dramatically outperforms prior CSI‑only methods and transfers to an external dataset, demonstrating robust cross‑modal alignment and directional sensitivity.

## Method Summary
WiFi2Cap’s three‑stage training pipeline first trains a vision‑language teacher (CLIP‑based) on synchronized video‑text pairs. The teacher uses a standard contrastive loss (InfoNCE) and a novel Mirror‑Consistency Loss that pairs a video frame with its horizontal flip and swaps directional terms (e.g., “left” ↔ “right”) in the caption, forcing the visual embedding space to disentangle mirrored actions. In the second stage, a CSI student with dual ResNet‑18 branches (amplitude and phase) and a gated fusion module is trained in two sub‑stages: first aligning its embeddings to the teacher’s visual embeddings (Stage 2‑1), then directly to the teacher’s text embeddings with Mirror‑Consistency (Stage 2‑2). Both alignments use symmetric InfoNCE. Finally, a frozen GPT‑2 decoder is conditioned on the student’s CSI embedding via prefix‑tuning: a lightweight MLP maps the embedding into per‑layer key/value prefixes, which are prepended to the LM’s attention mechanism, and only this MLP is trained with the autoregressive cross‑entropy loss.

## Key Results
- WiFi2Cap achieves BLEU‑4 of 51.78 on its dataset vs. 14.85 for the CSI‑only baseline; METEOR 25.86→57.48; ROUGE‑L 32.38→64.32.
- Transfer to Person‑in‑WiFi 3D yields BLEU‑4 47.07, up from 12.15, confirming cross‑dataset generalization.
- The three‑stage framework with Mirror‑Consistency effectively aligns CSI to semantics and resolves left/right ambiguity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A vision‑language teacher (based on CLIP) creates a semantically rich, direction‑aware embedding space that serves as a transferable supervision target for the CSI student.
- **Mechanism:** The teacher is trained on synchronized video‑text pairs using a standard contrastive loss (InfoNCE) to align visual and textual embeddings. Crucially, it incorporates a *Mirror‑Consistency Loss* that explicitly pairs a video frame with its horizontally flipped version and the caption with swapped directional terms (e.g., "left" ↔ "right"). This forces the teacher's visual embedding space to disentangle mirrored‑action semantics.
- **Core assumption:** A model trained to distinguish between an action and its mirrored version will develop representations that are sensitive to directional cues (e.g., left vs. right limb usage), which can then be transferred to the CSI modality.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "A vision–language teacher learns transferable supervision... and introduces a Mirror-Consistency Loss to disambiguate directional semantics."
  - [section IV.A] Details the teacher's architecture (CLIP encoders + Transformer) and explicitly defines the Mirror-Consistency Loss (Eq. 3) applied during its training.
  - [corpus] The corpus shows related work on cross‑modal transfer and contrastive learning (e.g., CLIP, COSMOS), but the specific use of an explicit mirror‑consistency objective for direction‑sensitive alignment is a novel contribution of this paper. Direct evidence for its mechanism is contained within the paper itself.
- **Break condition:** If the teacher's embedding space does not achieve a clear separation between original and mirrored pairs (as measured by a low top‑1 retrieval accuracy in a held‑out mirrored test set), the supervision signal for the student will be directionally ambiguous, leading to persistent left/right confusion in generated captions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A CSI student network, trained via a two‑step alignment process, learns to map raw Wi‑Fi CSI (amplitude and phase) into the teacher's direction‑aware semantic space, bridging the modality gap.
- **Mechanism:** (Step 1) The student's CSI embedding is aligned to the *teacher's visual embedding* using a standard contrastive loss (distillation). This bootstraps the CSI features into a rough visual‑semantic region. (Step 2) The CSI embedding is then aligned directly to the *teacher's text embedding* using another contrastive loss, now also incorporating the Mirror‑Consistency Loss (Eq. 7). The student uses a dual‑branch ResNet‑18 to encode amplitude and phase, fuses them with a learned gating module, and projects the result. The two‑step process leverages the teacher's robust, pre‑trained visual space as an intermediate target before the final text alignment.
- **Core assumption:** Aligning CSI first to a rich visual space and then to text is more effective than direct CSI‑text alignment, especially given the scarcity of paired CSI‑text data. The gated fusion of amplitude and phase is assumed to capture complementary motion cues.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "A CSI student is aligned to the teacher's visual space and text embeddings."
  - [section IV.B] Explicitly describes the two‑step training (Vision‑CSI alignment with `L_align`, then CSI‑Text alignment with `L_con` and `L_mc`) and the architecture of the CSI encoder (dual ResNets, gated fusion).
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Exposing the CSI" and "UniFi" discuss CSI preprocessing and multi‑view fusion, supporting the technical feasibility of the CSI encoder design. The two‑step alignment paradigm is inspired by general cross‑modal knowledge transfer papers (e.g., [18], [20] in references).
- **Break condition:** Failure in Step 1 (poor Vision‑CSI alignment) would mean the CSI features never enter a semantically meaningful space. Failure in Step 2 (poor CSI‑Text alignment) would mean the features don't map to the correct language anchor. Both would result in low‑quality, semantically irrelevant captions. Ablation in Table III shows disabling Stage 2‑1 (Step 1) causes a large performance drop (BLEU‑4 47.10 vs. 51.78), confirming the critical path.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A frozen, pre‑trained autoregressive language model (GPT‑2) is conditioned on the aligned CSI embedding via prefix‑tuning to generate fluent, grammatically correct, and semantically specific action captions.
- **Mechanism:** The final CSI embedding vector (after student training) is passed through a lightweight MLP (`g_ϕ`) that generates a sequence of "prefix" vectors (key/value pairs) for each layer of the frozen GPT‑2 decoder. These prefixes are prepended to the self‑attention mechanism at every layer, effectively steering the language model's generation process based on the CSI semantics without altering its massive parameter set. The model is trained with the standard autoregressive cross‑entropy loss on the caption tokens.
- **Core assumption:** The aligned CSI embedding contains sufficient disentangled semantic information (including directional cues) to be effectively interpreted by a powerful, general‑purpose LM when provided as a soft prompt (prefix). Prefix‑tuning is sufficient to inject this conditioning without catastrophic forgetting or overfitting.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "A prefix‑tuned language model then generates action descriptions from CSI embeddings."
  - [section IV.C] Formally defines the prefix generation (`g_ϕ`) and insertion process, and states GPT‑2 is frozen while only `g_ϕ` is optimized.
  - [section V.D.3] Ablates the choice of LM (GPT‑2, Qwen, phi‑2), showing performance is sensitive to LM capacity/pretraining, supporting that the LM's inherent knowledge is a key factor.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Wi‑Chat" explore LLMs for Wi‑Fi sensing, but WiFi2Cap's specific use of prefix‑tuning for *caption generation* from *aligned CSI embeddings* is its particular instantiation.
- **Break condition:** If the student's CSI embedding lacks the fine‑grained, direction‑sensitive semantic structure required by the LM (e.g., it collapses left/right distinctions), the prefixes will be ambiguous signals. The LM will then generate grammatically correct but semantically incorrect or vague captions (e.g., missing "left" or "right"). Table II's "partially correct" example is a signature of this failure mode.

## Foundational Learning
### Contrastive Learning (InfoNCE Loss)
**Why needed:** The core challenge is the scarcity of paired CSI‑text data. Contrastive learning allows the model to learn a shared embedding space by pulling paired samples (CSI & its caption) together and pushing unpaired samples apart, using a large batch of negatives. It's the workhorse for aligning disparate modalities.
**Quick check:** Given a batch of N CSI embeddings and N text embeddings, how does the symmetric InfoNCE loss (Eq. 2) ensure a many‑to‑many matching is learned, and why is temperature (τ) a crucial hyperparameter?

### Knowledge Distillation / Cross‑Modal Transfer
**Why needed:** CSI is a low‑level, non‑semantic signal. The "teacher" model (vision‑language) has learned rich semantics from massive image‑text data. The student must mimic the teacher's *representations* (embeddings), not just its final outputs, to inherit this semantic structure.
**Quick check:** What is the key difference between "response distillation" (mimicking softmax outputs) and "representation distillation" (mimicking intermediate embeddings), and why is the latter used here?

### Prefix‑Tuning / Prompt Tuning
**Why needed:** Full fine‑tuning of a large LM (GPT‑2) on a small CSI‑caption dataset risks overfitting and catastrophic forgetting. Prefix‑tuning allows the frozen LM's vast generative knowledge to be exploited by only learning a small set of continuous prompt vectors that condition its attention, offering parameter efficiency and stability.
**Quick check:** In prefix‑tuning, where are the learned prefix vectors injected into the Transformer architecture, and how do they affect the computation at each layer?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
CSI tensors (amp/pha) -> Dual ResNet‑18 (each branch) -> Global Avg Pool -> Concat -> Linear+GeLU -> Gating (sigmoid) -> Projection -> L2‑norm -> c_i^{(r)} (per receiver) -> Average across receivers -> \bar{c}_i.  
Parallel: Video frames -> CLIP‑ViT -> Temporal Transformer -> v_i; Captions -> CLIP‑Text -> t_i.  
Training: Stage2‑1: align \bar{c}_i with v_i via InfoNCE; Stage2‑2: align \bar{c}_i with t_i via InfoNCE + Mirror‑Consistency.  
Then: \bar{c}_i -> MLP g_ϕ -> prefixes (K^l_i, V^l_i) for layers l=1..L -> Frozen GPT‑2 decoder (prefixes prepended) -> Caption.

**Critical path:**  
Raw data (CSI+RGB+text) -> Stage 1: Train Teacher (on video‑text) -> Freeze Teacher -> Stage 2: Train Student (CSI encoder + g_ϕ) using Teacher embeddings as targets -> Freeze Student encoder -> Stage 3: Train only g_ϕ with frozen GPT‑2.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Three‑stage vs. end‑to‑end:* Separates alignment and generation to avoid interference, but extends training pipeline.  
- *Frozen components:* Preserves pre‑trained knowledge and prevents overfitting on small dataset; limits adaptation to CSI domain.  
- *Dual‑branch CSI encoder:* Specialized amplitude/phase processing with gated fusion; slight parameter increase.  
- *Prefix length:* Longer prefixes provide more context but increase parameters and overfitting risk.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Weak CSI‑text alignment: low top‑1 retrieval accuracy (<0.5) and noisy similarity matrix.  
- Directional ambiguity: captions frequently swap “left/right” or use neutral terms despite correct semantics.  
- Fluency‑relevance trade‑off: high ROUGE‑L but low BLEU‑4/METEOR indicates generated text is coherent but semantically off.  
- Dataset shift: performance drop on Person‑in‑WiFi 3D signals poor generalization to new environments.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *Replicate Stage 2‑1 alignment:* Train only CSI encoder to align with teacher visual embeddings; expect >40% top‑1 retrieval accuracy on held‑out batch.  
2. *Ablate Mirror‑Consistency:* Train with and without `λ_mc L_mc` in both teacher and student; manually inspect 50 captions for left/right errors; expect significant reduction with loss enabled.  
3. *Prefix ablation:* After full training, replace learned `g_ϕ` with random linear projection or zero prefixes; metrics should drop drastically, confirming prefixes are the conditioning mechanism.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to new environments and multi‑person scenarios**  
   *Basis:* WiFi2Cap uses a fixed 4×6 grid and one TX with three RXs; transfer to Person‑in‑WiFi 3D shows BLEU‑4 drop (51.78→47.07). Multi‑person cases not evaluated.  
   *Why unresolved:* Real‑world deployment requires varied settings and multiple people; current evaluation is limited to single‑person controlled setups.  
   *Evidence needed:* Systematic evaluation across diverse physical spaces, receiver configurations, and multi‑person datasets, measuring caption quality and per‑person accuracy.

2. **Does Mirror‑Consistency fully resolve left/right ambiguity?**  
   *Basis:* Mirror‑consistency improves metrics, but qualitative errors remain (e.g., “facing forward” vs “facing away”). A left/right recognition test on a VLM reaches only 73.3% after fine‑tuning.  
   *Why unresolved:* Captioning metrics do not directly measure directional correctness; VLM test is indirect and not on the captioning task itself.  
   *Evidence needed:* Annotate generated captions for left/right limb usage and compute accuracy; ablate mirror‑loss strength and test on other directional aspects (forward/backward, clockwise).

3. **Could fine‑tuning the vision‑language teacher improve CSI‑to‑text alignment?**  
   *Basis:* Teacher uses frozen CLIP encoders; authors do not explore fine‑tuning, citing overfitting trade‑off.  
   *Why unresolved:* A frozen teacher may not fully adapt to the dataset’s specific actions and camera viewpoints, potentially limiting the quality of the alignment target for CSI.  
   *Evidence needed:* Compare frozen vs. fine‑tuned (full or partial) teachers by training the CSI student and evaluating captioning performance; also measure teacher’s video captioning ability as sanity check.

4. **Can WiFi2Cap handle longer action sequences and streaming inputs?**  
   *Basis:* All experiments use 5‑second clips; framework processes each clip independently with no temporal memory.  
   *Why unresolved:* Real activities often exceed 5 seconds and may require continuous understanding for interactive applications; current model’s temporal scope is restricted.  
   *Evidence needed:* Extend model with sliding windows, memory modules, or hierarchical processing; evaluate on datasets with longer activities and measure caption quality over time and computational efficiency.

## Limitations
- Missing key hyperparameters (learning rates, loss weights, etc.) and dataset details (splits, receiver layout) hinder exact replication.
- The teacher’s direction‑aware embedding is not quantitatively validated; its disentanglement capability is assumed but not measured.
- The WiFi2Cap dataset is not publicly available, blocking external verification and cross‑dataset robustness tests.

## Next Checks
1. **Mirror retrieval benchmark:** Build a test set with original and horizontally mirrored video frames plus captions with swapped directional terms. Compute top‑1 retrieval accuracy of teacher embeddings (query=mirrored frame, target=caption with swapped direction). Expect >90% to confirm effective disentanglement.

2. **CSI‑text alignment probe:** After student training, compute the similarity matrix between CSI embeddings and text embeddings on a held‑out batch; report top‑1/top‑5 accuracy. Additionally, train a linear classifier to predict left/right actions from CSI embeddings; high accuracy (>85%) would indicate preserved directional semantics.

3. **Prefix ablation study:** Vary prefix length \(L_p\) (e.g., 5, 10, 20) and evaluate captioning metrics (BLEU‑4, METEOR, ROUGE‑L) and directional error rate. Compare against a baseline using a single global vector prefix to assess the necessity of per‑layer prefixes.
