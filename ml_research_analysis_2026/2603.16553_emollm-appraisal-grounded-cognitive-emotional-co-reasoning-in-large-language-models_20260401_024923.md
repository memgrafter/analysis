# EmoLLM: Appraisal-Grounded Cognitive-Emotional Co-Reasoning in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16553
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16553
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** EmoLLM improves success rate by up to 15.3% and reduces average turns by up to 26.2% versus Qwen3-8B, while enhancing emotional attunement and factual accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Large language models often excel at factual reasoning (IQ) but lack emotional intelligence (EQ), leading to responses that are either coldly correct or emotionally misplaced. EmoLLM bridges this gap by introducing an appraisal‑grounded framework inspired by psychological appraisal theory. At each dialogue turn, it explicitly constructs an Appraisal Reasoning Graph (ARG) that links contextual facts, inferred user needs, appraisal dimensions, emotional states, and response strategies before generating a reply. Training occurs in two stages: first, knowledge‑guided continued pretraining and supervised ARG alignment lay the foundation for structured reasoning; second, multi‑turn reinforcement learning with reverse‑perspective reasoning evaluates candidate responses by predicting their impact on the user’s emotional trajectory. Across four dialogue benchmarks (emotional support, technical assistance, medical consultation, peer review), EmoLLM achieves higher success rates, fewer turns, and better emotional and factual metrics compared to strong baselines and proprietary APIs.

## Method Summary
EmoLLM’s core innovation is the Appraisal Reasoning Graph (ARG), a structured intermediate representation that forces the model to consider both cognitive facts and emotional dimensions. Training proceeds in two phases. Stage I: Knowledge‑augmented continued pretraining on the ESConv dataset with the ECoK knowledge graph initializes factual and appraisal grounding. Then supervised fine‑tuning uses a teacher model to generate complete ARG traces (with an optional “think” prefix) to align the model’s reasoning. Stage II: Multi‑turn reinforcement learning (RL) with GRPO optimizes a composite reward. For each candidate response, an internal reverse‑perspective predictor simulates the user’s next state for n steps, yielding a reward for positive emotional impact. Additional reward terms cover cognitive reliability, ARG trace quality, and an overthinking penalty. The policy interacts with a fixed user simulator during RL, and all reward components are adjudicated by a judge model.

## Key Results
- vs. Qwen3‑8B base: **+15.3% success rate**, **‑26.2% average turns**.
- Significant gains in emotional state score (ES), empathic appropriateness (EA), emotional gain per turn, and factual accuracy (FA) across all four benchmarks.
- Outperforms or matches leading proprietary APIs (e.g., GPT‑4) in emotionally sensitive dialogues.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The explicit ARG structure makes appraisal reasoning interpretable and ensures that emotional and cognitive factors are jointly considered before responding. Reverse‑perspective reasoning in RL shifts the optimization target from immediate turn quality to long‑term emotional outcomes, preventing short‑sighted replies. The two‑stage training first instills domain knowledge and reasoning alignment, then refines the policy under a reward that balances IQ and EQ. This separation allows the model to learn stable intermediate representations before facing the complexities of multi‑turn emotional dynamics.

## Foundational Learning
Key concepts enabling EmoLLM’s design:

- **Appraisal Theory** – Psychological framework wherein emotions arise from evaluations of events against personal goals and beliefs. *Why needed:* Provides the theoretical basis for modeling how user needs and situational facts combine to produce emotional states. *Quick check:* Ensure ARG includes standard appraisal dimensions (e.g., goal relevance, coping potential).
- **Appraisal Reasoning Graph (ARG)** – A graph linking contextual facts (Fₜ), inferred user needs (Nₜ), appraisal dimensions (Aₜ), emotional state (Eₜ), and response strategy (Sₜ) per turn. *Why needed:* Makes the appraisal process explicit, enabling joint IQ‑EQ reasoning. *Quick check:* Inspect SFT examples to confirm presence of all five components.
- **Knowledge‑Grounded Continued Pretraining (CPT)** – Continued pretraining on domain data (ESConv) augmented with ECoK triples, with factual prefixes. *Why needed:* Injects domain knowledge and initial appraisal patterns before fine‑tuning. *Quick check:* Verify that CPT instances prepend ECoK triples and that loss is computed only on the dialogue continuation.
- **Supervised ARG Alignment** – Fine‑tuning on teacher‑generated ARG traces with optional “think” prefixes. *Why needed:* Aligns the model to produce structured reasoning before responses. *Quick check:* Measure trace generation quality (e.g., component F1) on a held‑out set.
- **Multi‑turn RL with Reverse‑Perspective Reasoning** – RL optimization where each candidate response triggers a multi‑step user‑state prediction to compute a reverse‑perspective reward. *Why needed:* Optimizes long‑term emotional impact rather than single‑turn scores. *Quick check:* Track whether reverse‑perspective reward correlates with final success rate during RL.
- **GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)** – PPO variant normalizing advantages within a group of G candidates per turn. *Why needed:* Stabilizes advantage estimation in sparse‑reward multi‑turn dialogue. *Quick check:* Observe KL divergence and policy loss trends for stability.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
User Simulator & ECoK Retriever -> ARG Extractor -> Stage I (CPT + SFT) -> Stage II (RL with Judge & Reverse‑Perspective Predictor) -> Trained Policy

**Critical path**  
Given dialogue history x≤t, the policy (implicitly) generates ARG components (Fₜ, Nₜ, Aₜ, Eₜ, Sₜ) and then response yₜ. During RL, after producing a candidate yₜ, the reverse‑perspective predictor Tθ simulates user state transitions for n steps to compute a reward for emotional impact. The composite reward rₜ drives the GRPO update.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Explicit ARG vs. efficiency*: Forcing ARG traces adds interpretability and reasoning pressure but increases token length and inference cost. The gating label rₜ controls when traces are emitted.
- *Judge model dependency*: Reward computation relies on a proprietary model (gpt‑5‑mini); substituting an open model may alter performance.
- *User simulator fidelity*: A fixed teacher simulator provides stable training but may diverge from real user behavior.
- *LoRA vs. full finetuning*: LoRA reduces memory and forgetting risk but possibly limits reasoning adaptation depth.
- *Reward balancing*: λ weights are heuristic; improper balancing can degrade factual accuracy or over‑optimize emotional metrics.

**Failure signatures**  
- **Factual accuracy drop**: FA significantly declines after Stage I or during RL → increase λ_cog or add a factual consistency reward.
- **Excessive ARG emission**: >80% of responses include explicit <think> blocks on simple queries → increase λ_over or revise SFT gating distribution.
- **RL stagnation**: SR fails to improve after several RL steps → increase group size G, add length penalty, or enlarge n.
- **Simulator mismatch**: User state distributions in training differ from evaluation (e.g., too deterministic) → raise simulator temperature or refine prompt.

**First three experiments**  
1. **ARG Extraction Validation**: Use the teacher model to generate ARG traces on a sample; measure component coverage and coherence to confirm learnability.
2. **Stage I Ablation**: Train CPT only and SFT only; evaluate on a factual benchmark (e.g., MedDialog) to diagnose FA degradation.
3. **RL Reward Sweep**: Grid‑search λ weights on a validation set to find a configuration that simultaneously improves SR and maintains FA.

## Limitations
- Missing exact reward λ values and judge rubrics; reproduction may yield different performance.
- Dependency on proprietary teacher/judge models (GPT‑5 mini) for data generation and reward scoring.
- Underspecified knowledge retrieval method for CPT; weak retrieval could harm grounding.
- User simulator and reverse‑perspective predictor implementation details are vague, risking simulator‑environment mismatch.
- Training infrastructure specifics (GRPO update frequency, number of RL steps) are not provided, leaving large hyperparameter gaps.

## Confidence
- **Framework design (ARG structure):** Medium – conceptually solid but empirical impact sensitive to implementation details.
- **Training pipeline specification:** Low – numerous critical parameters omitted, hindering exact replication.
- **Reported performance gains:** Low – numbers are plausible with careful tuning, but exact margins uncertain due to missing specifics and proprietary components.
