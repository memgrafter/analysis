# TIFO: Time-Invariant Frequency Operator for Stationarity-Aware Representation Learning in Time Series

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.17122  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17122  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: TIFO achieves 18 top‑1 and 6 top‑2 results among 28 forecasting settings, reducing average MSE by up to 55.3% on ETTm2 and lowering spectral distribution shift by up to 88%.

## Executive Summary
Nonstationary time series forecasting suffers from distribution shift between training and test data. Existing normalization techniques (e.g., RevIN, SAN) only remove low‑order moments per sample, failing to capture complex, dataset‑wide temporal structures. TIFO addresses this by learning stationarity‑aware weights over the frequency spectrum across the entire training set. It computes a stability score for each frequency component based on the mean‑over‑standard deviation of its amplitude across all samples, then uses lightweight MLPs to generate weights that amplify stationary frequencies and suppress non‑stationary ones before inverse FFT. This plug‑and‑play module can be integrated into various forecasting backbones. Experiments demonstrate significant improvements in accuracy and robustness, along with reduced computational cost compared to baselines.

## Method Summary
TIFO operates in two stages. **Stage I (precomputation)**: For the full training set, compute the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) of each sliding window. For each frequency bin *k* and channel *c*, calculate the stationarity score **S**(k,c) = mean(amplitude) / std(amplitude) across all windows. Higher **S** indicates the frequency’s amplitude is stable across samples. **Stage II (training)**: For each batch, compute the FFT of the input, split into real and imaginary parts, and generate weight tensors λᵣ = MLPᵣ(**S**), λᵢ = MLPᵢ(**S**). Multiply the FFT coefficients element‑wise by these weights, recombine, and apply the inverse FFT to obtain a stationarity‑adjusted time series, which is then fed to the backbone forecasting model. The MLPs are trained end‑to‑end with the backbone, while **S** remains fixed after precomputation.

## Key Results
- Among 28 forecasting settings, TIFO secures 18 top‑1 and 6 top‑2 placements against strong baselines.
- On the ETTm2 dataset, it reduces average MSE by 33.3% for PatchTST and 55.3% for iTransformer.
- TIFO lowers the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD²) between training and test frequency distributions by up to 88%, and cuts computational overhead by 60–70% relative to competing normalization methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1**: Stationary frequency components are preserved while non‑stationary ones are suppressed via weighting based on a cross‑sample stability score. For each frequency (k,c), compute **S**(k,c) = mean(amplitude) / std(amplitude) across all training windows. A higher **S** indicates lower variation in amplitude across samples, i.e., more stationarity. The core assumption is that distributional shift manifests as instability in frequency‑domain amplitudes across the training set, and stationary components are more robust to temporal changes. Evidence: Section 3.1 defines **S**; Table 3 shows TIFO reduces JSD² by up to 88%. Break condition: With too few training samples, **S** becomes noisy and weighting ineffective.

**Mechanism 2**: The stability scores **S** are transformed via two independent lightweight MLPs into per‑frequency weights λᵣ and λᵢ for the real and imaginary parts of the Fourier coefficients. Weighted coefficients are **R**ᵥ = **R** ⊙ λᵣ, **I**ᵥ = **I** ⊙ λᵢ. This amplifies coefficients with stable frequencies and attenuates unstable ones before inverse FFT. Core assumption: The mapping from **S** to λ can be learned by simple MLPs and generalizes to new samples; separate MLPs preserve conjugate symmetry needed for real‑valued output. Evidence: Section 3.1 describes the MLPs; Table 1 shows large MSE reductions on ETTm2. Break condition: If the backbone is already robust to non‑stationarity or the dataset is predominantly stationary, learned λ may over‑suppress valid signals.

**Mechanism 3**: The Fourier transform implicitly induces a kernel in frequency space whose eigen‑decomposition corresponds to a Fourier basis. Learning the eigenvalues λ is equivalent to learning a dataset‑level time‑averaged representation that mitigates distribution shift. By Bochner’s and Mercer’s theorems, a positive‑definite kernel permits decomposition into eigenfunctions; with periodic eigenfunctions (Fourier basis), the eigenvalues λᵢ weight each basis function. TIFO learns these λ based on **S**, capturing frequencies responsible for distributional discrepancies. Core assumption: The time‑averaged kernel representation, learned across the full training distribution, is more robust than per‑sample normalization. Evidence: Section 3.2 presents the theoretical connection. Break condition: The theory assumes the spectral eigen‑basis (Fourier) is sufficient; if the true invariant representation lies in a different basis (e.g., wavelets), the approach may be suboptimal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) & Stationarity**  
  *Why needed*: TIFO operates entirely in the frequency domain; understanding DFT, amplitude/phase, and how non‑stationarity appears as unstable spectral components is essential.  
  *Quick check*: If you apply z‑score normalization to a time series sample, what happens to its frequency amplitude spectrum? (Answer: It scales total energy but preserves relative amplitudes; alignment with other samples depends on cross‑sample consistency, not per‑sample normalization.)

- **Distribution Shift in Time Series**  
  *Why needed*: The premise is *p*(x|tₜᵣₐᵢₙ) ≠ *p*(x|tₜₑₛₜ); grasping why stationary assumptions fail in real‑world streaming data is key.  
  *Quick check*: Why does RevIN (removing per‑sample mean/variance) not fully resolve frequency‑domain distribution shift? (Answer: It normalizes each sample independently, but does not align frequency amplitudes across the dataset.)

- **Kernel Methods & Mercer’s Theorem**  
  *Why needed*: TIFO’s theoretical foundation links learned λ to eigenvalues of an implicitly defined kernel, justifying frequency weighting as a dataset‑level operation.  
  *Quick check*: What does it mean for a kernel to be “positive definite,” and why does Mercer’s theorem allow a decomposition into eigenfunctions? (Answer: Positive definiteness ensures the kernel’s Gram matrix is positive semidefinite; Mercer’s theorem guarantees that under continuity and positivity, the kernel can be expressed as an infinite sum of eigenfunctions with positive eigenvalues.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Pre‑computation Module -> Weighting Layer -> Inverse DFT -> Backbone  
Critical path: Precompute **S** once from full training set before backbone training. During each training step: input window → FFT → split into real/imag parts → compute λᵣ,λᵢ from fixed **S** via MLPs → multiply coefficients → iFFT → feed transformed window to backbone → compute loss → backpropagate through MLPs and backbone.

Design tradeoffs:  
- *Pros*: Plug‑and‑play, minimal overhead (FFT + tiny MLPs), captures dataset‑wide stationarity.  
- *Cons*: Requires full training set precomputation (hinders online/streaming use); phase information may be distorted if weights are not constrained; may over‑suppress signals if dataset is mostly stationary.

Failure signatures:  
- No improvement or degradation on known non‑stationary datasets (e.g., ETTm2).  
- Learned λ values are nearly uniform or degenerate (all zeros/ones).  
- JSD²/KL between train/test spectra does not decrease after TIFO.  
- High variance in results across random seeds despite fixed **S**.

First 3 experiments:  
1. **Synthetic validation**: Create a dataset with a known drifting frequency (e.g., dominant sinusoid shifting from low to high across samples). Verify that **S** assigns lower scores to the drifting component.  
2. **Baseline comparison**: Integrate TIFO into PatchTST and compare against PatchTST+RevIN on ETTh1/ETTh2. Expect TIFO to outperform on the more non‑stationary split (ETTh2).  
3. **Ablation**: Replace the precomputed **S** with a random tensor of same shape and retrain MLPs. Performance should drop significantly (as in Table 5), confirming **S** encodes useful cross‑sample statistics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**: How does TIFO perform with non‑Transformer backbones (e.g., state‑space models like Mamba, TCNs, N‑BEATS)? The paper tests only DLinear, PatchTST, and iTransformer; compatibility with other architectures remains unknown.

**Open Question 2**: To what extent does Assumption 1 (training set sufficiently represents *p*(t)) hold under data scarcity or localized non‑stationarities? If training samples are few or non‑stationary events are sparse, **S** may become noisy, harming weighting effectiveness.

**Open Question 3**: How sensitive is TIFO to the choice of frequency resolution *K* and window function? The ablation (Table 6) tests only two values and two windows on ETTh1; broader sensitivity across datasets is unexplored.

**Open Question 4**: Can **S** be improved by modeling joint distributions of frequencies or incorporating phase information? The current **S** treats amplitudes independently per frequency, ignoring potential correlations (e.g., harmonics) that might reveal higher‑order stationarity.

**Open Question 5**: Does TIFO generalize to univariate forecasting? The method and experiments are multivariate; for univariate (C=1), the per‑channel advantage vanishes, and it is unclear if gains persist.

## Limitations
- **Implementation details unspecified**: MLP architecture, FFT variant (full vs. rfft), λ constraints, and **S** preprocessing are not fully described, hindering exact reproduction.
- **Precomputation barrier**: Computing **S** requires the full training set upfront, making TIFO unsuitable for streaming or online settings without adaptation.
- **Theoretical assumption risk**: The kernel‑theoretic justification assumes the Fourier basis is sufficient to capture invariant representations; this may not hold for all types of non‑stationarity (e.g., singular events best described in wavelet domains).

## Confidence
- **Distribution shift reduction (JSD²)**: High – directly measured across multiple datasets.
- **Forecasting improvement on non‑stationary datasets**: Medium – strong empirical results but hyperparameter sensitivity not thoroughly assessed.
- **Kernel theory connection**: Low – elegant but unvalidated; alternative bases could be more appropriate.
