# Active Flow Matching

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00877
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00877
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: Forward‑KL AFM achieves lower simple regret and higher scores than VSD, CbAS, and LaMBO‑2 on synthetic and real‑world design tasks.

## Executive Summary
Active Flow Matching (AFM) enables the use of variational active generation frameworks (VSD, CbAS) with implicit discrete flow models by reformulating the objectives to operate on tractable conditional endpoint distributions along the flow path, using self‑normalised importance sampling. The forward‑KL variant comes with a consistency guarantee, recovering the target marginal distribution. Empirically, forward‑KL AFM consistently attains lower simple regret on Ehrlich synthetic landscapes (L=32,64) and the AAV capsid task, and finds higher‑scoring designs on FoldX stability and F2‑Thrombin docking compared to prior methods under tight experimental budgets.

## Method Summary
AFM builds on discrete flow matching (DFM) with a masked source and convex interpolation. In each active learning round, a classifier is trained on the current labeled dataset to estimate p(y≥τ|x). A proposal mixture—comprising a uniform prior, the base flow’s endpoint distribution, and a replay buffer of high‑fitness designs—is used to sample candidate endpoints x₁. Importance weights are computed as w = p_D(y≥τ|x₁)/μ(x₁), with simplifications for the prior component. For each candidate, an intermediate state x_t is sampled along the flow path, and the flow model is updated via a reformulated KL divergence between the true conditional posterior and the model’s conditional endpoint distribution. The forward‑KL loss corresponds to training the flow on the target distribution p*(x) ∝ p_prior(x)p(y≥τ|x), guaranteeing recovery under standard DFM assumptions. After training, new designs are generated by running the flow’s Euler sampler from the source, evaluated by the oracle, and added to the dataset.

## Key Results
- Forward‑KL AFM achieves lower simple regret on Ehrlich synthetic landscapes (sequence lengths 32 and 64) and the AAV capsid design task.
- It discovers higher‑scoring designs on FoldX stability (protein stability) and F2‑Thrombin docking compared to VSD, CbAS, and LaMBO‑2.
- The method consistently outperforms baselines under tight experimental budgets (limited rounds and batch sizes).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Conditional Objective Reformulation
AFM replaces intractable marginal KL divergences in VSD/CbAS with expectations over the flow path: minimize KL( p_t(x₁|x_t, y≥τ) || q_ϕ_t(x₁|x_t) ) averaged over t and x_t. This leverages the tractable conditional endpoint distribution q_ϕ_t(x₁|x_t) provided by flow models, avoiding the need for q_ϕ(x). If the flow path connects source to target and conditionals match, the terminal distribution recovers the target marginal.

### Mechanism 2: Self‑Normalized Importance Sampling with Mixture Proposal
To estimate the expectations without target conditional samples, AFM uses self‑normalized importance sampling (SNIS) with a proposal μ(x₁) that is a mixture of prior, base flow endpoint distribution, and replay buffer. Unnormalized weights w(x₁) = p_D(y≥τ|x₁)/μ(x₁) are computed; for the prior component the prior cancels exactly in the forward‑KL loss. SNIS yields an unbiased estimator as the number of samples K→∞.

### Mechanism 3: Forward‑KL Consistency via Reweighting
The forward‑KL objective can be rewritten as a weighted cross‑entropy loss over the prior: -E_{x₁~p_prior}[ w(x₁) E_{x_t|x₁}[ log q_ϕ_t(x₁|x_t) ] ]. Since p_prior is uniform and w(x₁) ∝ p(y≥τ|x₁), this is equivalent (up to a constant) to training on data from p*(x) ∝ p_prior(x)p(y≥τ|x). Standard DFM theory ensures that the minimizer yields a velocity field that generates terminal distribution p*.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: The system comprises: Classifier p_D → provides importance weights; Base flow q_θ → used in proposal (flow component) and as a lagging copy; Replay buffer → stores high‑fitness designs with weights π_j; Proposal mixture μ → combines prior, q_θ endpoint, and replay buffer to sample x₁; Flow model q_ϕ → updated via AFM loss; Optimizer (AdamW) → updates q_ϕ. The flow model also generates new designs via Euler sampling.

**Critical path** (per round r):
1. Train classifier p_D on current labeled dataset D_r.
2. Initialize proposal mixture μ with mixing coefficients (α₀, α_flow, α_rbuff) and base flow q_θ (from previous round or snapshot).
3. For many gradient steps: sample a component c per mixture coefficients, then draw K endpoint samples x₁ from that component’s sampler; compute per‑sample importance weight w = p_D(y≥τ|x₁)/μ_component(x₁) (simplified for prior); sample time t ~ Uniform[0,1] and intermediate state x_t from the flow path p_t(x_t|x₁); compute AFM loss (forward/reverse/symmetric) using q_ϕ(x₁|x_t,t); backpropagate to update ϕ.
4. After training, generate new batch by running the flow’s Euler sampler from the source to t=1; evaluate with oracle; add to D_{r+1}.
5. Update replay buffer (add new high‑fitness designs) and set base flow θ ← ϕ for next round.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Mixing coefficients: early rounds favor prior (exploration); later rounds shift to flow/replay (exploitation). Adaptive schedules (e.g., α_rbuff = min(0.4, 0.05*r)) help.
- Number of importance samples K: larger K reduces variance but increases compute; typical 32–128.
- Flow architecture: Bi‑LSTM vs Transformer; deeper models capture epistasis but risk overfitting on small D_r.
- Classifier capacity: must generalize from limited labels; overfitting yields miscalibrated weights. Use regularization (dropout) and early stopping.
- Mutation constraints (e.g., FoldX ≤3 edits): require modifying Euler sampler to track edits; adds complexity.

**Failure signatures**:
- High gradient variance: monitor effective sample size (ESS = (∑w)^2/∑w^2); ESS < 0.1K indicates problematic variance. Also watch gradient norms and loss spikes.
- Premature convergence/mode collapse: especially with reverse‑KL; diversity drops and best score plateaus. Track batch diversity (e.g., average pairwise Hamming distance) and unique high‑fitness discoveries.
- Classifier overfitting: low training loss but poor validation AUROC; degrades importance weights. Use more data or simpler classifier.
- Flow not learning: loss plateaus at high value; may indicate poor proposal overlap or insufficient capacity.

**First 3 experiments** (to validate implementation):
1. Ehrlich‑32 synthetic task: known global optimum (f=1). Verify forward‑KL AFM near‑zero simple regret within ~10 rounds, outperforming VSD/CbAS. Use L=32, batch size B=128, rounds R=20.
2. AAV capsid design (L=28): real protein task with CNN oracle. Confirm forward‑KL AFM attains lowest simple regret among baselines. Test transformer‑based flow/classifier.
3. FoldX stability (L=228, mutation budget ≤3): tests handling large sequences and edit constraints. Check AFM finds higher stability scores than VSD/LaMBO‑2 under batch size 8 and 10 rounds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Prove consistency of reverse‑KL AFM**: Without a proof, reliability is uncertain; empirical mode‑seeking may cause premature convergence.
- **Reduce variance of SNIS estimator in AFM**: High variance leads to noisy gradients, especially with sparse data.
- **Mitigate sensitivity to classifier quality in early rounds**: Poor classifier estimates degrade importance weights and proposal quality when few labels exist.
- **Extend AFM to multi‑objective and constrained optimisation**: Real‑world design often involves multiple criteria or hard constraints.

## Limitations
- The exact threshold schedule τ per round (e.g., in AAV) and precise switching condition for flow component weight are unspecified, making replication of exploration‑exploitation schedule difficult.
- The number of Monte Carlo samples N used to estimate the flow endpoint density qθ₁(x₁) for importance weighting is omitted; insufficient N may cause high variance and unstable training.
- The forward‑KL consistency guarantee relies on idealized assumptions (infinite capacity, infinite samples, perfect classifier); finite‑sample effects, model misspecification, and classifier overfitting may prevent recovery of the target marginal in practice.
