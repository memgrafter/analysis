# Online Monitoring Framework for Automotive Time Series Data using JEPA Embeddings
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.09985
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09985
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Self-supervised JEPA embeddings boost anomaly detection AUROC from 0.594 to 0.877 and achieve 23.4% true positive rate at 1% false positive rate.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles label‑free online anomaly detection for autonomous vehicle object state representations during operation (SOTIF). It introduces a framework that first maps raw object time series into a latent space using a JEPA‑based self‑supervised encoder, then applies established unsupervised anomaly detectors (ABOD, LOF). Evaluated on nuScenes with synthetically injected velocity errors, the JEPA embeddings dramatically improve detection performance (e.g., AUROC +0.283) and remain effective under strict false‑alarm constraints (23.4% TPR at 1% FPR). The approach enables monitoring of unknown risks without requiring anomaly labels.

## Method Summary
The framework processes object tracks from a multi‑object tracking system (FocalFormer3D on LiDAR) into fixed‑length time series per object. A Transformer‑based encoder is trained via the JEPA objective: random timesteps are masked, the encoder processes the context, and a predictor attempts to match the embeddings of the masked timesteps as computed by a target encoder (EMA). This self‑supervised training forces the encoder to capture normal dynamics without labels. After training, each object's latent embeddings across time are aggregated by max‑pooling. An unsupervised anomaly detector (ABOD, LOF, or GMM) is fitted solely on the pooled embeddings from the normal training set. At inference, new objects are embedded and scored; a threshold selected for a desired false positive rate yields an anomaly decision.

## Key Results
- AUROC improved from 0.594 (raw features) to 0.877 (JEPA + ABOD).
- F1‑score rose from 0.183 to 0.769; MCC from 0.007 to 0.600.
- At a strict 1% false positive budget, the system achieved 23.4% true positive rate.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Self‑Supervised Embedding Creation via Predictive Learning
The JEPA encoder is trained to predict latent embeddings of masked timesteps from the surrounding context. This predictive task forces the model to learn a representation that captures the underlying temporal dynamics of normal object behavior (e.g., consistent kinematics). The use of an EMA target encoder and a predictor prevents representational collapse, encouraging the embedding space to reflect semantic properties rather than raw feature reconstruction. As a result, normal object trajectories form a cohesive cluster, while anomalies (which deviate from learned dynamics) lie farther away. Evidence includes the abstract’s claim of label‑free training and the detailed JEPA loss formulation in Section III‑C. A break condition is non‑convergence of the L1 prediction loss.

### Mechanism 2: Anomaly Detection via Density/Geometry in the Learned Latent Space
After JEPA training, the latent space is structured such that density‑ or geometry‑based detectors (e.g., ABOD, LOF) can reliably identify outliers. The paper compresses the time‑varying embeddings into a fixed vector (e.g., max‑pooling) and fits the detector only on normal training embeddings. The anomalous samples, which were not seen during training, exhibit different angular or density characteristics in this space. Table I shows a dramatic lift in AUROC when moving from raw features (0.594) to JEPA embeddings (0.877), confirming the informativeness of the space. The detector’s ROC curve retains a meaningful TPR even at low FPR (23.4% at 1%), enabling operation under strict alarm budgets.

### Mechanism 3: Operational Robustness under Strict False‑Alarm Constraints
The combination of a robust latent space and a sensitive detector yields a ROC curve that maintains a non‑trivial true positive rate at very low false positive rates. This is critical for online SOTIF monitoring where excessive false alarms would be unacceptable. The paper explicitly reports TPR1 and TPR5 metrics and shows the ROC curve on a log‑scale x‑axis, highlighting performance in the low‑FPR region. If the TPR at the required FPR drops below a practical threshold (e.g., <5–10%), the framework would not be deployable, indicating a failure of either the embedding or the detector.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- **Perception System**: Off‑the‑shelf Multi‑Object Tracking (FocalFormer3D) converts raw LiDAR into object list *L*. Each object *Oᵢ* is a multivariate time series (position, velocity, orientation) over *T* timesteps.
- **Embedding Module**: Encoder *Eθ* (Transformer, 10 heads, depth 5) with JEPA training. Randomly masks *N*ₘ=4 timesteps, applies an EMA target encoder *E*¯θ and predictor *p*ϕ (Transformer decoder) to predict masked embeddings. Loss is L1 between predicted and target embeddings. Produces latent embeddings *zO*; time dimension aggregated by max‑pooling.
- **Anomaly Detection Module**: Unsupervised detector *φ* (ABOD, LOF, or GMM) fitted on the pooled train embeddings. Scores new embeddings; threshold yields binary anomaly flag.

### Critical path
MOT output (*O*) → *Eθ* inference → max‑pooled embedding → *φ* scoring → threshold comparison → anomaly flag. Training is separate: first train *Eθ*/*p*ϕ via JEPA loss on train objects, then fit *φ* on the resulting embeddings.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Embedding dimension *D*=32**: Lower reduces cost but may lose information; higher increases capacity and risk of overfitting.
- **Masking rate *N*ₘ=4**: Balances learning signal and context preservation; too few/too many masks weaken training.
- **Detector choice**: ABOD offered best trade‑off in the paper; LOF and GMM also effective. Choice impacts computational load and sensitivity to different outlier types.
- **Sequence aggregation**: Max‑pooling is simple but discards temporal evolution; alternatives (mean, attention) could capture more nuance.

### Failure signatures
- **JEPA training**: Loss fails to converge; embeddings show no visual separation (e.g., PCA) between normal and anomalous samples.
- **Detection fitting**: ROC AUC near 0.5 on validation; anomaly score distributions for normal and anomalous heavily overlap.
- **Online operation**: False positive rate exceeds allowable budget (e.g., >5%) at any reasonable true positive rate; detections lack correlation with known failure modes.

### First 3 experiments
1. Replicate JEPA training convergence on the nuScenes train split (250 epochs, ADAM, LR=3e‑5) and verify L1 loss decreases steadily.
2. Validate embedding separation by processing train and anomalous test objects, reducing max‑pooled embeddings to 2‑D (PCA/t‑SNE), and confirming a partially separated cluster structure with AUROC ~0.877.
3. Establish the low‑FPR operating point: fit ABOD on train embeddings, compute full ROC on the test set, and report the threshold that achieves exactly 1% FPR on normal samples; expect TPR ≈ 23.4%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to real anomalies**: The framework is evaluated only with synthetic velocity errors. Real operational anomalies (sensor failures, unusual interactions) may have different statistical properties, so it is unclear how well the detector would perform on naturally occurring risks.
2. **Optimal JEPA design**: The masking strategy (4 random timesteps) and prediction target (embedding of masked tokens) are chosen without systematic exploration. Alternative masking patterns, prediction horizons, or loss functions might yield better separation for anomalies.
3. **Best anomaly detector for online/low‑FPR use**: The paper compares ABOD, LOF, and GMM on a single synthetic error type but does not consider computational complexity or online adaptability. Other methods (Isolation Forest, Deep SVDD) might offer better efficiency‑accuracy trade‑offs under strict latency and memory constraints.
4. **Impact of upstream perception quality**: The framework assumes the MOT output (object list) is the ground truth for normal behavior. Failures in the perception system (missed detections, ID switches) could produce anomalies detected by the monitor, confounding responsibility. It is unknown whether JEPA embeddings can distinguish perception artifacts from true object anomalies.

## Limitations
- Exact data splits and FocalFormer3D configuration (checkpoint, confidence threshold, tracking parameters) are unspecified, preventing precise replication of the object lists used.
- Anomaly evaluation relies on a simple synthetic error model (Gaussian noise added to velocity). Real‑world anomalies may involve multi‑dimensional, temporally correlated deviations that are not captured.
- Online deployment aspects—threshold selection under varying operational FPR budgets, computational latency, and adaptation to dataset shift over time—are not addressed, limiting understanding of real‑world feasibility.
