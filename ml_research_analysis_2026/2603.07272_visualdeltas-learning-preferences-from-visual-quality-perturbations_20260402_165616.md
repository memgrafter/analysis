# VisualDeltas: Learning Preferences from Visual Quality Perturbations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07272
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07272
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A lightweight preference learning framework that uses visual quality perturbations to generate supervision without human annotations, achieving consistent improvements over rejection-sampling SFT on multimodal benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
VisualDeltas addresses the reliance on costly human annotations or external reward models for improving vision-language models. It introduces a framework where a model’s own responses to high-quality (HQ) and degraded low-quality (LQ) visual inputs (e.g., downsampled images) form preference pairs (HQ ≻ LQ). Optionally, a label-based filter retains only pairs where the HQ response is correct and the LQ response is wrong. These pairs train the model via Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), conditioned only on HQ images. Experiments with Qwen2.5-VL (3B/7B) across five benchmarks (HiTab, WikiTQ, VQA, GQA, MathVision) show VisualDeltas consistently outperforms SFT. For instance, the label-based variant on the 7B model improves GQA by +4.80% and WikiTQ by +1.75%, while SFT declines on WikiTQ. The method also yields strong robustness when evaluated on LQ inputs, with gains up to +8.55% on VQA. Improvements are largest on tasks requiring fine-grained visual grounding (tables, images) and minimal on MathVision, suggesting the method primarily enhances visual perception rather than abstract reasoning.

## Method Summary
VisualDeltas constructs a preference dataset from a pretrained VLM and a set of multimodal training examples. For each (query, image) pair, a low-quality image is created by downsampling (e.g., to 10% resolution). The VLM generates two responses: one conditioned on the original HQ image and another on the LQ image. The HQ response is treated as the preferred output; the LQ response is the dispreferred one. When ground-truth answers are available, a label-based filter keeps only pairs where the HQ response is correct and the LQ response is incorrect, yielding a cleaner signal. The resulting tuples (HQ context, preferred response, dispreferred response) are used to fine‑tune the VLM with the DPO loss, with a frozen reference policy (the initial VLM). Critically, during training the model is conditioned only on the HQ image, so it learns to avoid the ineffective patterns triggered by degraded inputs while remaining consistent with inference conditions.

## Key Results
- **Label‑based VisualDeltas (VD‑LB) on Qwen2.5‑7B‑VL** (trained on VQA): GQA +4.80% (to 44.75%) vs. SFT’s +2.15%; WikiTQ +1.75% (to 68.15%) vs. SFT’s –3.55% drop.
- **Robustness on LQ inputs:** VD‑LB (VQA‑trained) achieves VQA +8.55% (to 59.10%) and GQA +7.55% (to 39.35%), while SFT shows severe degradation on several tasks.
- **Task scope:** Largest gains on visually complex table and image QA (HiTab, WikiTQ, VQA, GQA); minimal improvement on reasoning‑focused MathVision, indicating the method primarily enhances visual grounding.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Visual degradation induces divergent reasoning
**Claim:** Controlled visual degradation (e.g., resolution reduction) produces systematic, divergent behaviors from the same model on identical queries, creating natural preference pairs (HQ ≻ LQ) without external judges.  
**Mechanism:** Applying a deterministic degradation operator *T* to an image yields an LQ input. The model’s policy generates two responses: *o*<sub>HQ</sub> ~ *π*(·|*x*, *v*) and *o*<sub>LQ</sub> ~ *π*(·|*x*, *T*(*v*)). Degradation impairs perceptual clarity (e.g., text legibility), often leading to less accurate and more verbose “compensatory” reasoning. The resulting quality gap *o*<sub>HQ</sub> ≻ *o*<sub>LQ</sub> serves as a preference label.  
**Core assumption:** Visual quality directly and systematically influences the accuracy and efficiency of a VLM’s reasoning for a fixed query; degradation exposes brittleness in visual grounding.  
**Evidence:** Abstract states that systematic impact of image quality induces informative preference signals. Section 4.5.2 shows LQ responses are consistently longer, indicating compensatory inefficiency.  
**Break condition:** If degradation fails to cause a meaningful accuracy drop or behavioral shift (e.g., on resolution‑invariant tasks like MathVision), the generated pairs become noisy and uninformative.

### Mechanism 2: Label‑based filtering focuses on quality‑sensitive samples
**Claim:** Filtering to pairs where HQ is correct and LQ is wrong enriches the preference signal by isolating cases where visual quality directly caused a reasoning failure, improving data efficiency.  
**Mechanism:** With ground‑truth answers *y*, the training set is restricted to *D*<sub>LB</sub> = {(*c*<sub>HQ</sub>, *o*<sub>HQ</sub>, *o*<sub>LQ</sub>) | Corr(*o*<sub>HQ</sub>, *y*) ∧ ¬Corr(*o*<sub>LQ</sub>, *y*)}. These “Quality‑Sensitive” samples capture the causal mechanism: degradation toggles correctness. Training DPO on this curated set provides cleaner supervision than using all HQ≻LQ pairs.  
**Core assumption:** A preference pair where correctness changes solely due to input quality yields a purer signal about robust visual reasoning, more sample‑efficient for DPO.  
**Evidence:** Section 3.1 describes the stricter criterion; Section 4.5.1 reports that Quality‑Sensitive samples are 38.3% in HiTab and that VD‑LB achieves strong results with only ~2,800 training samples.  
**Break condition:** If the fraction of Quality‑Sensitive samples is very low (<10%), the label‑based filter may produce too few pairs for stable DPO, making the label‑free variant more practical.

### Mechanism 3: DPO conditioning on HQ contexts teaches robust reasoning
**Claim:** DPO training conditioned only on HQ contexts, with LQ responses as negatives, teaches the model to generate outputs robust to the absence of fine‑grained visual details—i.e., to reason effectively with clear input without relying on brittle, low‑quality triggers.  
**Mechanism:** The DPO objective *L*<sub>VD</sub>(*θ*) increases the likelihood of HQ‑style outputs given the HQ context while suppressing the likelihood of LQ‑style outputs. The LQ image is never provided during training, so the model learns to avoid the ineffective, verbose patterns (compensatory inefficiency) associated with degraded perception, even when shown a high‑quality image.  
**Core assumption:** The behavioral gap between *o*<sub>HQ</sub> and *o*<sub>LQ</sub> reflects a preference for efficient, accurate grounding; suppressing the LQ pattern while conditioning on HQ builds a policy that defaults to the robust, concise HQ reasoning mode.  
**Evidence:** Section 3.2 explains that LQ‑induced outputs are treated as negatives; Section 4.5.2 shows after DPO training the HQ distribution shifts left (shorter token lengths). Section 4.3 demonstrates VisualDeltas develops genuine robustness to degraded inputs, unlike SFT.  
**Break condition:** If the LQ response is not measurably worse (e.g., similar accuracy but different phrasing), the DPO gradient may be weak or conflicting, yielding minimal improvement.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Pretrained VLM (πθ0) -> Perturbation Module (Tα) -> Pair Constructor -> DPO Trainer -> Evaluation Suite

**Critical path:**  
1. Prepare dataset *D* (images + queries).  
2. For each sample, generate *o*<sub>HQ</sub> and *o*<sub>LQ</sub> using *π*<sub>θ₀</sub> (with HQ and LQ inputs).  
3. Construct preference tuples (HQ context, *o*<sub>HQ</sub> preferred, *o*<sub>LQ</sub> dispreferred), optionally filtering with label‑based criterion.  
4. Train *π*<sub>θ</sub> via DPO on this set, using only HQ context, with frozen reference *π*<sub>ref</sub> = *π*<sub>θ₀</sub>.  
5. Evaluate *π*<sub>θ</sub> on all benchmarks at HQ and LQ.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Perturbation strength (*α*):** Mild degradation yields weak behavioral deltas; severe corruption distorts semantics. Default *α*=0.1 (10% resolution) is a robust sweet spot (Appendix D).  
- **Label‑free (VD‑LF) vs. label‑based (VD‑LB):** VD‑LF is universally applicable but uses noisier pairs; VD‑LB is more data‑efficient but requires ground truth and discards non‑Quality‑Sensitive samples.  
- **Training data source:** Training on HiTab yields strong cross‑dataset gains on table/structured tasks; VQA/GQA training generalizes broadly. No single source dominates.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Minimal improvement on MathVision (task not visually grounded enough).  
- SFT‑style cross‑dataset degradation after training (overfitting to spurious HQ features).  
- No reduction in response verbosity (LQ responses not longer than HQ), indicating weak compensatory inefficiency signal.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate core VD‑LF/VD‑LB vs. SFT on Qwen2.5‑7B‑VL with VQA training set; verify primary result (Table 1, “Train Set: VQA”). Expect VD‑LB to show larger gains on GQA/WikiTQ and less degradation on MathVision compared to SFT.  
2. Test robustness to perturbation strength: Train VD‑LF with *α* ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6} on a HiTab subset; validate inverted‑U performance curve (Appendix D) on WikiTQ and GQA. Expect *α*=0.1 or 0.2 to perform best.  
3. Measure response length distribution change: For a fixed test set (e.g., HiTab Quality‑Sensitive samples), compare median token length before and after VD‑LB training. Expect a significant leftward shift, confirming suppression of verbose LQ patterns (Fig. 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How does VisualDeltas affect existing model biases?** The Impact Statement acknowledges potential bias amplification but provides no analysis. A bias audit using benchmarks like BBQ or WinoBias on models trained with VisualDeltas vs. SFT would be needed to empirically evaluate stereotyping and disparity metrics.
- **What is the optimal resolution degradation ratio for different model scales and task types?** Appendix D studies *α* for label‑free DPO but not systematically across model sizes, tasks, or for label‑based variants. Sweeping *α* across datasets and model scales would identify Pareto fronts of accuracy vs. stability.
- **Does conditioning DPO on both HQ and LQ contexts improve robustness or accuracy trade‑offs?** The design condition exclusively on HQ inputs for consistency, but mixed conditioning might enhance adaptation to degraded inputs. An experiment training with positive (HQ) samples conditioned on HQ images and negative (LQ) samples conditioned on LQ images could reveal benefits.
- **How does the proportion of quality‑sensitive samples affect the VD‑LF vs. VD‑LB performance gap?** Label‑free VD‑LF performs nearly as well as VD‑LB, but the role of dataset‑specific quality‑sensitivity rates (e.g., 38.3% in HiTab) is unanalyzed. Computing these fractions for all training datasets and correlating with accuracy differences between VD‑LF and VD‑LB would clarify the relationship.

## Limitations
- Incomplete methodological details (DPO β, learning rate, batch size, decoding settings, data splits) hinder exact replication.
- Heavy reliance on resolution reduction; generalizability to other degradation types needs further ablation.
- Performance gains are concentrated on visually grounded tasks; minimal improvement on MathVision indicates limited scope for abstract reasoning.
- Label‑based filtering requires ground‑truth answers, restricting applicability to annotated datasets.

## Confidence
- Systematic quality deltas generate valid preference pairs: **Medium** (response length/accuracy gaps support the mechanism, but causality is not directly proven).
- DPO training on HQ‑conditioned pairs improves robustness: **Medium** (consistent improvements reported, but missing reproduction details prevent full confirmation).
- Label‑based filtering boosts data efficiency: **Medium** (modest gains observed; effect size is small and may not hold for all datasets).
- Generalization across perturbations and model scales: **High** (explicit experiments on resolution, noise, blur, and 3B/7B models are described).
