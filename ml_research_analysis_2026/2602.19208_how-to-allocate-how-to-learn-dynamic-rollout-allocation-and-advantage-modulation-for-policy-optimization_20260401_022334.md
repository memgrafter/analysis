# How to Allocate, How to Learn? Dynamic Rollout Allocation and Advantage Modulation for Policy Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19208
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19208
- **Reference count**: 39
- **Primary result**: DynaMO improves LLM reasoning by dynamically allocating rollouts to high-uncertainty problems and modulating token-level advantages, e.g., raising Qwen2.5-Math-7B Pass@1 from 37.2% (GRPO) to 41.6%.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) for LLM reasoning often wastes computation on uniform rollout allocation and suffers from gradient attenuation in high-confidence actions. This paper introduces DynaMO, which addresses both issues: (i) dynamic rollout allocation minimizes gradient variance by distributing rollouts proportionally to Bernoulli variance, focusing learning on problems with balanced success/error rates; (ii) token-level advantage modulation compensates for attenuated gradients via entropy-aware scaling while stabilizing large updates using entropy change indicators. Evaluated on six math benchmarks with Qwen2.5-Math (1.5B/7B) and Qwen3-14B, DynaMO consistently outperforms baselines including GRPO, entropy-loss methods, and coverage-based approaches. Ablations confirm the contributions of both components, and training curves exhibit reduced gradient spikes and smoother entropy, indicating more stable optimization.

## Method Summary
DynaMO consists of two coordinated mechanisms. First, dynamic rollout allocation estimates the Bernoulli variance (uncertainty) of each problem based on current policy rollouts and allocates more rollouts to problems with higher variance, ensuring gradients are derived from informative, balanced success/error distributions. Second, token-level advantage modulation adjusts the advantage signal per token using an entropy-aware scaling factor: tokens with low entropy (high confidence) receive amplified updates to counteract gradient attenuation, while an entropy change indicator prevents excessively large steps when entropy drops sharply, maintaining stability. The policy is updated via a PPO-style objective with these modulated advantages.

## Key Results
- DynaMO reliably exceeds GRPO, entropy-loss methods, and coverage-based allocation on six math reasoning benchmarks across Qwen2.5-Math (1.5B/7B) and Qwen3-14B.
- For Qwen2.5-Math-7B, average Pass@1 increases to 41.6% from GRPO's 37.2%.
- Ablations validate both allocation and modulation components; training shows lower gradient variance and smoother entropy trajectories.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Dynamic rollout allocation targets problems where the policy is uncertain (high Bernoulli variance), which typically yield larger and more informative gradient signals. Focusing rollouts on these problems reduces overall gradient variance compared to uniform allocation. Token-level advantage modulation addresses the attenuation of gradients for high-confidence (low-entropy) tokens by scaling up their advantages, ensuring learning signal is not lost. The entropy change indicator acts as a safeguard, reducing update magnitude when the policy becomes too confident too quickly, thus preventing destabilizing large steps.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**: Training LLMs with scalar rewards on tasks with automated correctness checks. *Why needed*: Enables scaling policy improvement without human preference labeling. *Quick check*: Confirm reward is based on final answer correctness (e.g., math problem Pass@1).
- **Bernoulli Variance**: Variance of a binary outcome (success/error) for a given problem under the current policy. *Why needed*: Quantifies problem-level uncertainty; high variance indicates the policy is neither always correct nor always wrong, providing a strong learning signal. *Quick check*: Compute variance from a small set of initial rollouts per problem; higher variance should correlate with larger gradient norms.
- **Token-Level Advantage Modulation**: Adjusting the advantage assigned to each token in a sequence based on its predicted entropy. *Why needed*: Standard advantages are aggregated at sequence level, diluting signal for high-confidence tokens. Modulation restores gradient strength for these tokens. *Quick check*: Compare entropy of token distributions before and after modulation; low-entropy tokens should see increased advantage magnitudes.
- **Entropy-Aware Scaling**: A scaling factor derived from token entropy, applied to advantages. *Why needed*: Prevents over-updating on high-confidence tokens while still boosting their signal. *Quick check*: Track average scaling factor; it should be higher for tokens with lower entropy.
- **Entropy Change Indicator**: A metric tracking the change in sequence entropy across policy updates. *Why needed*: Sudden entropy drops indicate aggressive policy updates that may collapse exploration. The indicator scales down advantages when entropy falls sharply. *Quick check*: Correlate entropy change with modulation magnitude; large negative changes should reduce update steps.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Dynamic Rollout Allocator -> Rollout Sampler -> Policy Network -> Advantage Modulator (using entropy estimator) -> Policy Update (PPO-clip loss)

**Critical path**: (1) For each problem, compute Bernoulli variance from recent rollouts; (2) Allocate rollout budget inversely to variance (more to high-variance problems); (3) Sample rollouts accordingly; (4) Compute per-token advantages from group rewards; (5) Estimate token-level entropy from policy logits; (6) Apply entropy-aware scaling and entropy change adjustment to advantages; (7) Update policy with modulated advantages via clipped surrogate objective.

**Design tradeoffs**: Dynamic allocation introduces overhead (variance estimation, budget redistribution) but reduces wasted computation on uninformative problems. Token-level modulation requires per-token entropy computation, increasing memory and compute, but yields more precise gradient scaling. The entropy change indicator adds stability but may slow learning if tuned conservatively.

**Failure signatures**: (a) Gradient norm spikes after modulation, indicating scaling factors are too aggressive; (b) Entropy collapse (near-zero) across tokens, suggesting modulation suppresses exploration; (c) Allocation concentrates on a narrow set of problems, implying variance estimates are biased or unstable.

**First experiments**: 1) Audit rollout allocation: after initial training, plot rollout count vs. problem variance; expect positive correlation. 2) Validate modulation: scatter plot token entropy vs. advantage scaling factor; expect negative correlation. 3) Monitor stability: histogram gradient norms before/after modulation; post-modulation should have fewer extreme values.

## Limitations
- Generalizability is limited to math reasoning benchmarks; transfer to open-ended or multimodal tasks remains unproven.
- No validation on extended training runs (e.g., >50% compute steps), leaving risk of delayed divergence or degradation.
- Dynamic allocation and token-level modulation introduce runtime complexity (variance estimation, entropy tracking) without quantified net efficiency gains.

## Confidence
- **Primary claim (DynaMO surpasses baselines)**: High – consistent improvements across multiple models and benchmarks with supporting ablations.
- **Gradient attenuation mitigation via token-level modulation**: Medium – mechanism is plausible and entropy correlation is shown, but direct causal evidence (e.g., gradient norm per token) is limited.
- **Broad applicability beyond math**: Low – evaluation exclusively on math tasks; no evidence for code, commonsense, or multimodal reasoning.

## Next Checks
1. Test DynaMO on non-math reasoning benchmarks (e.g., code generation, commonsense QA) using held-out corpora to measure transferability.
2. Run extended training (2–3× longer than paper) on one benchmark, tracking gradient norms, entropy drift, and asymptotic performance to rule out delayed instability.
3. Profile per-step wall-clock time and memory consumption compared to GRPO to quantify computational overhead and net efficiency trade-offs.
