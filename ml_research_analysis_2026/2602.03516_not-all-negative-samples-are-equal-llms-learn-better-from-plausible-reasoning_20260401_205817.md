# Not All Negative Samples Are Equal: LLMs Learn Better from Plausible Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03516
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03516
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: PNS improves three backbone models across seven math benchmarks by 2.03% on average over RL-trained models, with large gains on competition tasks (e.g., AIME24 +3.67%).

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Plausible Negative Samples (PNS), a method to generate high‑quality negative examples for LLM reasoning via reverse reinforcement learning. By training a model with GRPO using a composite reward that enforces format, incorrectness, and reasoning quality, PNS produces responses that mimic correct solutions but contain subtle errors. Using these as rejected data in DPO yields consistent improvements across multiple models and benchmarks. Ablations confirm each reward component's importance, and analysis shows PNS errors are predominantly knowledge‑based, enabling finer discrimination.

## Method Summary
PNS synthesis involves two main phases: first, a reward model (RM) is trained on preference pairs from base model responses using the Center BT loss. Second, a policy model (initialized from an instruction‑tuned LLM) is trained with GRPO using a reverse reward. This reward combines hybrid format scoring (rule‑based + LLM judge), forced inaccuracy, a bucketed RM score, and a CoT coherence score. The trained PNS model generates negatives that are structurally sound but subtly wrong. For target training, correct responses from an RL‑refined model are paired with PNS negatives, and DPO updates the model. The approach is evaluated on seven math benchmarks, showing significant gains, especially on hard competition tasks.

## Key Results
- PNS yields an average improvement of 2.03% over RL‑trained models across three backbones and seven benchmarks.
- Largest gains on competition‑level tasks: AIME24 (+3.67%), AIME25 (+3.03%), AMC23 (+2.84%).
- Ablations reveal that removing format or accuracy components causes the biggest drops, underscoring their gating roles; RM bucketing stabilizes training.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Reverse reward creates hard negatives**  
PNS optimizes a policy via GRPO with a reward that penalizes correct answers and rewards format compliance, reasoning quality, and high RM scores. This forces the model to generate responses that are coherent and well‑structured but contain subtle factual or reasoning errors. Such "near‑miss" negatives provide a stronger learning signal than obviously flawed outputs because they require the model to refine fine‑grained distinctions. Evidence includes the composite reward design (Eq.7) and ablations showing the necessity of the accuracy inversion constraint. Potential failure occurs if the format gate becomes too strict or too loose, or if the accuracy penalty is overridden.

**Mechanism 2: Multi‑component composite reward ensures quality**  
The reward combines orthogonal criteria: hybrid format score (rule‑based + LLM judge), accuracy inversion, RM‑based quality, and CoT coherence. Each blocks a different failure mode: format prevents degenerate outputs, accuracy enforces wrongness, RM guides toward human‑preferred quality, and CoT maintains logical flow. Ablations confirm that removing format or accuracy harms most, while removing RM or CoT also degrades performance, demonstrating their complementary contributions. The bucketing of RM scores further stabilizes optimization by reducing reward hacking. Mis‑tuning weights can lead to samples that are correct yet high‑quality or incorrect but malformed.

**Mechanism 3: Fine‑grained discrimination via plausible negatives**  
DPO with PNS negatives forces the target model to distinguish between sound and subtly flawed reasoning. Since PNS samples closely mirror correct solutions in RM score distribution (Wasserstein distance ~1.12 vs. ~3.70 for random negatives) and contain predominantly knowledge‑based errors (factual, theorem misapplication, definition misuse), the model learns to internalize nuanced distinctions rather than surface‑level patterns. This translates to robust gains on challenging benchmarks and out‑of‑domain tasks. The approach would break down if the plausibility gap is too narrow (weak gradient) or too wide (negatives cease to be hard). Additionally, if the PNS model overfits to the RM, the negatives may appear high‑quality to the RM but not to the final model.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**:  
RM Trainer → PNS Generator (GRPO with rPNS) → DPO Trainer.  
Data pipeline: DAPO‑Math → sampling utilities → evaluation harness.

**Critical path**:  
1. Train RM on preference pairs from base model responses.  
2. Train PNSModel with reverse GRPO using the fixed RM.  
3. For each target model, roll out on training queries: collect correct responses from RL‑refined model → chosen; sample PNSModel → rejected.  
4. Run DPO on the pairs.  
5. Evaluate on benchmarks. Each stage depends on the previous; PNSModel must be fully trained before step 3.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Accuracy vs. Plausibility: λr tunes strictness of wrongness; too high can force nonsense, too low can allow correct answers.  
- Format gating: Binary `rformat` prevents degenerate outputs but may discard plausible samples if judge is too conservative.  
- RM bucketing: Discretization stabilizes training but reduces gradient granularity; raw RM leads to over‑optimization.  
- Base model strength: Stronger PNS generator yields higher‑quality negatives but costs more compute; smaller models may suffice with proper data.

**Failure signatures**:  
- RM pairwise accuracy <70% → noisy reward signal.  
- PNS format violations >5% → judge or constraints too strict/loose.  
- DPO yields no improvement or regression → negatives not hard enough (RM scores too close) or mis‑tuned λr/λc.  
- PNSModel produces correct answers despite `racc=0` → accuracy inversion overridden.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **RM validation**: Train RM on 10K pairs; report pairwise accuracy on 1K test (target ~98%).  
2. **PNS quality analysis**: Generate 50 rollouts per question on held‑out set; compute RM score distributions for correct samples (CS), PNS, and random negatives (RS); calculate Wasserstein distances; run error taxonomy to confirm knowledge‑error dominance.  
3. **DPO main test**: Apply PNS‑derived DPO to Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct; compare MATH500 accuracy against RL baseline; expect >1.5% absolute gain.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1:** Is the bucketing/clipping of the Reward Model score in the PNSReward (§3.1.2) the optimal mechanism for balancing reward stability against fine‑grained quality guidance, or could alternative discretization or transformation functions yield more robust or effective negative sample synthesis?  
  *Basis:* The paper uses heuristic bucketing but does not analyze alternatives; ablation (w/o Bucket RM) shows degradation.

- **Open Question 2:** What is the causal mechanism by which reverse RL with the specific PNSReward shifts error patterns from logical mistakes (common in rejection sampling) toward knowledge‑based errors (§5.3), and to what extent is this shift driven by each component of the composite reward?  
  *Basis:* The paper observes the shift but does not decompose component contributions; understanding this could inform better reward design.

- **Open Question 3:** Does the use of Plausible Negative Samples generalize beyond mathematical reasoning to other domains requiring nuanced discrimination (e.g., code generation, factual QA, creative writing), and does the observed benefit depend on the presence of "subtle" error types analogous to knowledge errors?  
  *Basis:* Evaluation limited to math/objective benchmarks; the insight may not transfer to domains with different error structures.

- **Open Question 4:** What are the scalability and efficiency trade‑offs of the two‑phase PNS approach (training a dedicated PNS model via reverse RL) compared to alternative methods of generating high‑quality negatives (e.g., on‑the‑fly generation with prompt engineering, distilling from a larger model, or modifying the main model's training to incorporate negative synthesis)?  
  *Basis:* The method requires significant compute; no cost‑benefit analysis or comparison to cheaper alternatives is provided.

- **Open Question 5:** When applying PNS‑synthesized negatives in a sequential training pipeline (e.g., RL → DPO with PNS), to what extent are the observed gains due to the *specificity* of the negative samples versus simply providing *more* high‑quality preference data, and could the same gains be achieved by augmenting the positive data or by a different ordering of training stages?  
  *Basis:* The paper always applies PNS on top of RL; no ablations on model state, data augmentation, or training order are reported.

## Limitations

- **Hyperparameter ambiguities**: Key training parameters (GRPO KL coefficient, DPO β, RL step count) are not specified, hindering exact reproduction and optimal performance.
- **Judge model dependency**: The reward relies on an LLM‑as‑a‑Judge for format and CoT scores, but the paper does not disclose which model is used, leaving the quality and reproducibility of the reward signal uncertain.
- **Data preprocessing details**: The Chinese‑language filtering method and the construction of preference pairs (number of responses per query, balancing) are underspecified, potentially affecting RM training and downstream results.

## Confidence

- **Performance gain claim** (2.03% avg improvement, +3.67% on AIME24): Medium. Results are consistent but depend on missing hyperparameters and judge quality.
- **Mechanism claim** (reverse reward creates hard negatives that drive fine‑grained discrimination): Medium. Ablations support components but full reward dynamics are not fully elucidated.
- **Component synergy claim** (multi‑component reward essential): Low. While ablations show format/accuracy are critical, the lack of sensitivity analysis on reward component weights and bucketing strategy reduces certainty.
