# Prosodic ABX: A Language-Agnostic Method for Measuring Prosodic Contrast in Speech Representations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.02102
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02102
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Self-supervised speech models (S3Ms) achieve near-human error rates on prosodic ABX tasks: English stress 26% (human 29%), Japanese pitch accent 19% (human 9%), Mandarin tone 5% (human 2%).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces *prosodic ABX*, a label‑free evaluation method that extends the ABX discrimination task to measure how well speech representations encode lexical prosody (stress, pitch accent, tone). Using minimal pairs—words identical in phonemes but differing in prosody—and dynamic time warping, the method compares representations across speakers. The authors build datasets for English, Japanese, and Mandarin, both natural and synthetic, and evaluate 17 self‑supervised speech models. Results show S3Ms far exceed acoustic baselines, with error rates approaching human performance on some tasks. Strong correlations are found across languages, contexts, and between natural and synthetic speech (except English stress), indicating that prosodic contrast is robustly encoded in representation space. The findings demonstrate that prosodic ABX provides an effective, language‑agnostic probe for speech model evaluation, especially in low‑resource settings where labeled data is scarce.

## Method Summary
Prosodic ABX is a training‑free evaluation framework. Given a prosodic minimal pair (A and B) with identical phonemic content but different lexical prosody, and a same‑prosody counterpart X from another speaker, the method extracts frame‑level representations from a target model layer for each audio. Pairwise distances between A–X and B–X are computed using dynamic time warping (DTW) to accommodate temporal variability. The ABX score is 1 if *d*(A,X) < *d*(B,X), 0.5 if equal, and 0 otherwise. Scores are aggregated over speaker pairs and contrasts to produce an error rate. The paper constructs three datasets: English stress (15 minimal pairs from 10 speakers), Japanese pitch accent (23 pairs, 10 speakers), and Mandarin tone (2310 pairs, 6 speakers). Audio is presented either out‑of‑context (clipped to the word) or in‑context (full carrier sentence). Synthesized speech via TTS (G‑TTS, Kokoro) is also used as a proxy for natural data. The evaluation covers 17 self‑supervised models (wav2vec2, HuBERT, XLSR, etc.) and acoustic baselines (mel‑spectrogram, MFCC). No model fine‑tuning is performed.

## Key Results
- S3Ms yield error rates significantly lower than acoustic baselines across all tasks.
- Best S3M error rates: English stress 26% (human 29%), Japanese pitch accent 19% (human 9%), Mandarin tone 5% (human 2%).
- Strong word‑level correlation between human and model performance on English stress (*r* = 0.94).
- Layer‑wise rankings of models are highly preserved between natural and synthetic speech for tone and pitch accent (median *r* = 0.93), but less so for English stress.
- Out‑of‑context ABX correlates strongly with in‑context performance (median *r* = 0.93).
- Error rates are substantially correlated across all three prosodic tasks (e.g., tone vs. pitch accent *r* = 0.86), suggesting shared acoustic encoding.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Prosodic ABX isolates prosodic contrast in representation space through three key mechanisms:

1. **Distance‑based discrimination on minimal pairs:** By comparing DTW distances between representations of a minimal pair (A,B) and a same‑prosody reference (X), the method directly probes whether the model groups same‑prosody items closer than different‑prosody items on the same phonological base. This relies on the assumption that meaningful encoding of prosody will produce a smaller within‑category distance than across‑category distance, after controlling for speaker identity and phonemic content.

2. **Controlled stimulus design:** The curated minimal pair datasets ensure that the only systematic difference between A and B is the lexical prosodic feature (stress, pitch accent, or tone). Eliciting these pairs from multiple native speakers (and via TTS) provides a robust, label‑free test set where the prosodic contrast is the sole source of semantic distinction. This design eliminates the need for explicit prosody labels and allows evaluation across languages.

3. **Stable model and layer rankings:** The observed preservation of model and layer rankings across various conditions (natural vs. synthetic speech, in‑context vs. out‑of‑context, and across prosodic tasks) indicates that relative sensitivity to prosodic contrast is an inherent property of the model/layer. This stability validates the method’s use for model selection in practical, low‑resource scenarios where only synthetic or out‑of‑context data may be available.

Each mechanism has break conditions: (1) DTW must not be misled by irrelevant temporal variations; (2) minimal pairs must be acoustically pure and consistently produced; (3) rankings may diverge if a model’s encoding is highly specialized to a specific language or context.

## Foundational Learning
- **ABX Discrimination Task**: A framework for evaluating representational similarity without training. It asks whether a model places a test sample X closer to a target A than to a distractor B. *Why needed*: Prosodic ABX is an extension of this paradigm to lexical prosody. *Quick check*: In a phonemic ABX with "beg" and "bag", `d(beg, X) < d(bag, X)` indicates the model groups "beg" with X, reflecting its phonemic representation.

- **Lexical Prosody**: The use of pitch, duration, and intensity to distinguish words at the lexical level. English stress is a multi‑cue pattern, Japanese pitch accent is a binary high/low assignment, and Mandarin tone is a syllable‑level contour. *Why needed*: The paper evaluates three distinct prosodic systems; understanding their acoustic realization is crucial to interpret error rates. *Quick check*: Why might English stress be harder than Mandarin tone for both humans and models? Possibly because stress cues are multiple and gradient, while tone is a discrete contour with clear F0 differences.

- **Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)**: An algorithm for aligning two sequences of potentially different lengths by finding an optimal warping path that minimizes cumulative distance. *Why needed*: Prosodic contours may stretch or compress across speakers and speaking rates; DTW aligns representations while preserving temporal structure. *Quick check*: What limitation of mean‑pooling representation vectors does DTW avoid? Mean‑pooling discards temporal dynamics, whereas DTW captures shape similarity of the whole contour.

## Architecture Onboarding
The prosodic ABX pipeline consists of five core components:

1. **Input Processor**: Loads audio, applies clipping (for out‑of‑context evaluation) based on word boundaries, and extracts raw waveform or features.
2. **Representation Extractor**: Passes audio through a self‑supervised speech model (S3M) and extracts frame‑level vectors from a chosen hidden layer.
3. **Alignment Engine**: Computes pairwise DTW between representation sequences of the two pairs (A–X and B–X) using a frame‑wise distance metric (typically Euclidean), then normalizes by path length.
4. **Scorer**: For each triplet, assigns a score based on the comparison of normalized distances; aggregates scores across the dataset (first within speaker pairs, then across contrasts) to produce an overall error rate.
5. **Dataset Manager**: Handles loading and organization of minimal pair triplets from natural or synthetic corpora, including speaker pairing logic.

**Critical Path**: Dataset preparation (curation, alignment, splitting) → Model inference (extract representations for all triplet members) → DTW computation (for all triplets) → Score aggregation → Analysis (correlations, rankings).

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- **DTW Cost vs. Accuracy**: Exact DTW is *O*(N²); the paper uses `fastabx` for approximate, faster computation. The trade‑off is between computational feasibility and exact alignment fidelity.
- **Clipping Decision**: Out‑of‑context evaluation (clipping to the word) removes contextual prosodic cues, isolating lexical prosody, but may also remove cues that help disambiguate prosody. In‑context evaluation is more natural but may conflate lexical and phrasal prosody.
- **Proxy Selection**: Using TTS synthesis is cost‑effective for large‑scale layer selection, but TTS may not perfectly replicate natural prosodic realization, especially for languages like English where stress is challenging.

**Failure Signatures**:
- **Dataset failure**: High variance in human ABX scores on the same contrast indicates that minimal pairs are not cleanly produced or the prosodic distinction is ambiguous.
- **Alignment failure**: DTW paths that exhibit erratic warping or are dominated by silence/noise segments suggest that the representation is not capturing prosodic structure reliably.
- **Correlation failure**: For a given model, low correlation between in‑context and out‑of‑context error rates (or between natural and synthetic) indicates that its prosodic encoding is highly context‑dependent or sensitive to acoustic artifacts of the TTS.

**First 3 Experiments**:
1. **Acoustic baseline sanity check**: Run prosodic ABX on Mel‑spectrograms and MFCCs. Expect error rates near 50% (chance), confirming that raw acoustic features do not inherently separate lexical prosody categories.
2. **Single model layer curve**: For one S3M (e.g., wav2vec 2.0 Base), compute error rates across all layers on one task (e.g., English stress, natural speech). Expect a U‑shaped or rising curve with a sweet spot in middle layers.
3. **DTW alignment validation**: For an easy triplet (e.g., Mandarin tone pair with clear F0 separation), visualize DTW cost path between A–X and B–X. Expect the *d*(A,X) path to have consistently lower local costs than *d*(B,X).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why do S3Ms approach or exceed human performance on English stress ABX but lag behind on Japanese pitch accent and Mandarin tone? The disparity is noted but unexplained.
2. Are the strong correlations between error rates across tone, pitch accent, and stress tasks due to shared acoustic features (e.g., F0) or to abstract prosodic encoding? The authors hypothesize acoustic correlates but do not test.
3. Why does synthetic speech provide a high‑correlation proxy for layer selection in Japanese and Mandarin but not for English stress? This asymmetry lacks explanation.
4. Is the strong word‑level human‑model correlation for English stress (*r* = 0.94) observed for Japanese pitch accent and Mandarin tone? Without item‑level correlations for those languages, the generality of the finding is unknown.

## Limitations
- The custom English and Japanese natural speech minimal pair datasets are not publicly released, preventing exact replication of natural‑speech results.
- The DTW implementation details (normalization method, distance metric, step pattern) are underspecified, leading to potential score variance across reproductions.
- Synthetic speech serves as a less reliable proxy for English stress (lower layer‑wise correlation), limiting the method’s generality across prosodic tasks.

## Confidence
- **Prosodic ABX validity**: Medium (hinges on dataset integrity and DTW consistency)
- **S3Ms substantially outperform acoustic baselines**: High
- **Synthetic speech as proxy for natural selection**: Medium for tone/pitch accent, Low for English stress
- **Cross‑context/task ranking preservation**: High (median correlations ≥0.93)
