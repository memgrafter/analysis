# Understanding Artificial Theory of Mind: Perturbed Tasks and Reasoning in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.22072  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22072  
- **Reference count:** 22  
- **Primary result:** LLMs show steep ToM performance drops across all 10 perturbation classes; only Llama-3-70B and DBRX maintain >50% accuracy on ≥5 classes; CoT improves overall but harms specific tasks; CoT faithfulness is moderate-to-strong for most models except Mixtral.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates the robustness of Theory of Mind (ToM) in large language models using a handcrafted benchmark of classic and perturbed false‑belief tasks. The authors test six LLMs (33B–132B parameters) with both vanilla and Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) prompting, measuring final‑answer accuracy, reasoning correctness, and faithfulness. Results reveal pronounced fragility: every model’s performance declines sharply under all perturbation types, and only two models exhibit limited robustness. CoT generally improves accuracy but degrades performance on several perturbation classes (e.g., spatial reasoning, sentiment‑based conclusions). Faithfulness analysis shows that for most models, correct reasoning chains correlate positively with correct final answers, indicating that CoT is used causally; however, Mixtral displays a placebo effect where CoT improves answers even when reasoning is incorrect. The study concludes that current LLM ToM is not robust and that CoT must be applied selectively.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a hand‑crafted dataset of 1,088 questions covering 7 stages (4 Unexpected Content, 3 Unexpected Transfer) with 10 perturbation classes (5 from prior work, 5 novel). Each instance includes gold belief‑state annotations for stepwise reasoning. Six open‑source LLMs (Llama‑2‑70B‑Chat, Vicuna‑33B‑v1.3, Mixtral‑8x7B‑Instruct, Yi‑34B‑Chat, Llama‑3‑70B‑Instruct, DBRX‑Instruct) are evaluated under two prompting conditions: Vanilla (direct answer) and one‑shot CoT (JSON‑formatted belief updates and final answer). Inference runs at temperature 0 on A100 GPUs. Outputs are parsed; invalid JSON responses are filtered. Accuracy and Average Treatment Effect (ATE) are computed per perturbation class. CoT correctness is measured via a “proper subsequence” alignment with gold belief states (using an algorithm from the paper). Faithfulness is assessed by correlating CoT correctness with final‑answer correctness (Φ coefficient for binary CoT correctness, point‑biserial for continuous variants). Placebo effects are checked by comparing ATE when CoT is correct vs. incorrect.

## Key Results
- All models suffer steep accuracy drops under every perturbation class; only Llama‑3‑70B and DBRX achieve >50% accuracy on at least 5 perturbation classes (limited robustness).
- CoT prompting improves overall ToM performance but harms accuracy for specific perturbations: Conclusion from Sentiment and Automatic Change Knowledge show clear degradations.
- Faithfulness analysis yields moderate‑to‑strong positive correlations (Φ ≥ 0.4, *p* ≤ 0.05) for most models, indicating that correct reasoning generally leads to correct answers. Mixtral is an outlier, showing a placebo effect (CoT‑P improves final answers even when CoT is incorrect).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** LLMs' Theory of Mind performance is fragile because they rely on shallow statistical heuristics rather than robust mental‑state modeling.  
**Mechanism:** Perturbations alter surface features (e.g., container transparency, spatial prepositions), breaking the heuristic mappings learned from training data. Models lack an explicit, updateable belief‑state representation invariant to such changes.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: “We show a steep drop in ToM capabilities under task perturbation for all evaluated LLMs…”  
- Section 6.1: “performance degrades under task perturbations… Especially perturbations that introduce the necessity of spatial reasoning pose challenges…”  
- Corpus: Ullman (2023) shows similar fragility, supporting the heuristic‑reliance interpretation.  
**Break condition:** If models maintained consistent belief tracking, accuracy would not drop steeply; minimal ATE for perturbation classes would be observed.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Chain‑of‑Thought prompting has mixed effects on ToM accuracy because its benefit depends on task structure.  
**Mechanism:** CoT forces stepwise reasoning, which helps tasks requiring multi‑step inference (e.g., integrating unreliable testimony) but hurts when early contextual details are critical (e.g., automatic changes) or when reasoning is non‑symbolic.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: “CoT prompting improves the ToM performance overall… but surprisingly degrades accuracy for some perturbation classes…”  
- Section 6.2: “CoT‑P slightly improved accuracy… but particularly Untrustworthy Testimony and Add Unrelated Information. Surprisingly, we even observe reduced performance… Conclusion from Sentiment and Automatic Change Knowledge.”  
- Corpus: Chen et al. (2025) uses tailored decomposition to improve ToM; our finding that CoT is not universally beneficial suggests decomposition must match task structure.  
**Break condition:** If CoT were universally beneficial, no degradation would occur; break condition is negative ATE for perturbation classes.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Positive correlation between CoT correctness and final‑answer correctness indicates that models are generally faithful to their reasoning chains; improving CoT quality could improve ToM outcomes.  
**Mechanism:** When intermediate steps are correct, final answers are more likely correct; the correlation implies the reasoning chain causally influences the answer rather than being a post‑hoc justification.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: “Faithfulness analysis reveals moderate‑to‑strong positive correlations… for most models, indicating that final outputs generally follow stated reasoning—except Mixtral, which shows a placebo effect.”  
- Section 6.3: “We observe significant, mostly strong correlations… strongest in larger and more recent models.”  
- Corpus: Prior ToM benchmarks focus on accuracy, not reasoning fidelity; our faithfulness analysis addresses this gap, and strong correlations align with expectation that models use their CoT.  
**Break condition:** Unfaithful models would show weak/negative correlation (Φ < 0.4 or non‑significant *p*).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Handcrafted dataset (1,088 questions, 7 stages, 10 perturbations, gold CoT) → Model loading (6 LLMs via HuggingFace) → Prompt templates (Vanilla vs. one‑shot CoT, JSON output) → Inference on A100 GPUs (temp 0) → Output filtering (JSON parsing) → Scoring (final‑answer accuracy, CoT correctness proper subsequence) → Metric computation (ATE, robustness thresholds, Φ correlation, placebo analysis) → Results tables/figures.

**Critical path:**  
1. Prepare dataset (including perturbations and gold CoT).  
2. Implement prompt templates for V‑P and CoT‑P with one‑shot examples.  
3. Run batch inference, collect outputs (final answer and CoT).  
4. Parse JSON, filter invalid outputs.  
5. Compute accuracy per perturbation class and ATE.  
6. For CoT‑P runs, evaluate CoT correctness using proper subsequence algorithm.  
7. Compute faithfulness correlations between CoT correctness and final answer correctness.  
8. Analyze placebo effect by comparing ATE for correct vs. incorrect CoTs.  
9. Report results in tables/figures.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Handcrafted dataset ensures quality but limits scale.  
- Open‑source models enable reproducibility but may understate proprietary capabilities.  
- One‑shot prompting balances cost and reliability.  
- JSON output enables automated scoring but may yield parse errors.  
- Proper subsequence metric is precise but labor‑intensive; approximative metrics (ROUGE) are cheaper but less reliable.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High JSON parse failure rate → poor instruction following.  
- Uniformly low accuracy across perturbations → tasks too hard.  
- Low faithfulness correlation (Φ < 0.4) → CoT not causally used.  
- Placebo effect (positive ATE despite incorrect CoT) → structural bias.  
- Consistent failure on specific perturbation classes (e.g., Transparent Container) → specific reasoning gaps.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline replication: Verify that models achieve >50% accuracy on unperturbed false‑belief tasks under V‑P and CoT‑P.  
2. Perturbation impact: For each perturbation class, compute ATE (CoT‑P minus V‑P) to identify classes where CoT helps (positive ATE) or hurts (negative ATE).  
3. Faithfulness check: Compute Φ coefficient between CoT correctness and final answer correctness; confirm ≥ 0.4 and significant for most models, and check Mixtral for placebo effect by comparing ATE when CoT is correct vs. incorrect.

## Limitations
- Dataset and prompt templates are not fully public, hindering exact replication.  
- Models evaluated are open‑source; results may not generalize to proprietary systems.  
- “Proper subsequence” CoT correctness metric is strict and may not capture all valid reasoning paths.

## Confidence
- **LLM ToM fragility under perturbations:** High confidence.  
- **Mixed CoT effects (task‑dependent):** Medium confidence.  
- **Faithfulness correlations:** Medium confidence.
