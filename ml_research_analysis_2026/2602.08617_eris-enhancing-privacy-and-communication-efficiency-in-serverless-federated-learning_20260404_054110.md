# ERIS: Enhancing Privacy and Communication Efficiency in Serverless Federated Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08617
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08617
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ERIS achieves FedAvg-level accuracy while reducing per-client upload size by 99% (from 5.2GB to 52MB), cutting distribution time by up to 103×, and improving robustness to membership inference and reconstruction attacks without cryptographic overhead.

## Executive Summary
ERIS introduces a serverless federated learning framework that eliminates the central server bottleneck by distributing aggregation across client-side aggregators. It combines model partitioning with distributed shifted gradient compression to drastically reduce communication volume while preserving model accuracy and enhancing privacy. Theoretically, ERIS matches FedAvg's convergence rate and provides an information-theoretic privacy guarantee that scales with the number of aggregators. Empirically, it demonstrates substantial communication savings and improved privacy on large models (up to 1.3B parameters) without accuracy loss.

## Method Summary
ERIS replaces the central server in FedAvg with multiple client-side aggregators. Each client partitions its compressed gradient into disjoint shards, sending one shard to each aggregator. Aggregators independently perform permutation-invariant aggregation on their assigned shards and broadcast partial updates back to all clients, who reassemble the global model. To reduce communication, gradients are compressed relative to a local reference vector (shifted compression), whose variance is controlled to preserve convergence. This design maintains mathematical equivalence to FedAvg while distributing network load and limiting any single entity's exposure to partial updates.

## Key Results
- **Communication reduction:** Per-client upload size reduced by 99% (5.2GB → 52MB) on large models; distribution time cut by up to 103×.
- **Accuracy preservation:** Achieves FedAvg-level test accuracy across image (CIFAR-10) and text (CNN/DailyMail) tasks, including a 1.3B-parameter LLM.
- **Privacy enhancement:** Membership inference attack accuracy decreases linearly with the number of aggregators; reconstruction attack quality is significantly degraded.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Serverless Aggregation via Gradient Partitioning
Eliminates the central server bottleneck and distributes network load by aggregating model updates across multiple client-side aggregators, while maintaining mathematical equivalence to FedAvg. Each client partitions its local gradient into A disjoint shards using categorical masks. Each shard is sent to a distinct aggregator (a subset of clients). Aggregators independently compute a permutation-invariant aggregation on their received shards. The partial updates are broadcast back to all clients, who reassemble the global model. The sum of all aggregated shards exactly reconstructs the FedAvg gradient, proven algebraically in Theorem B.1.  
**Core assumption:** The masks are disjoint and complete (`∑_a m^(a) = 1`), ensuring no information loss or approximation during aggregation.  
**Break condition:** If aggregators fail or shards are lost, convergence may slow but does not break, as shown in Appendix F.4—up to 70% dropout causes only a delay.

### Mechanism 2: Distributed Shifted Compression
Reduces transmitted parameter volume by orders of magnitude without harming convergence, by compressing gradients relative to a local reference vector that tracks previous aggregated updates. Each client maintains a local reference `s_k^t`. It compresses the shifted gradient `C_t^k(˜g_k^t - s_k^t)` using an unbiased compressor. The aggregator compensates the shift by adding the global reference `s^(a)` to the aggregated shard. Both client and aggregator update their references with the compressed shard: `s_k^{t+1} = s_k^t + γ_t v_k` and `s^(a)^{t+1} = s^(a)^t + γ_t 1/K ∑_k v_k,(a)`. This shifted compression reduces variance of the compressed gradients, preserving convergence rates.  
**Core assumption:** The compression operator is unbiased and has bounded second moment (ω-compression). The step-size γ_t is chosen as `√(1+2ω)/[2(1+ω)^(3/2)]`.  
**Break condition:** Aggressive compression (large ω) beyond a task-specific threshold degrades utility, as empirically shown in Figure 8.

### Mechanism 3: Information-Theoretic Privacy Amplification
Bounds mutual information leakage between a client's private dataset and an adversary's view inversely with the number of aggregators A and the compression level (retention probability p). An honest-but-curious adversary observes only a compressed shard per round. The model partitioning mask ensures each aggregator sees at most n/A parameters. The analysis conditions on the public transcript and shows `I(D_k; {v_k,(a)}) ≤ n T p/A * C_max`, where `C_max` bounds per-coordinate mutual information. Under Gaussian weight assumptions, `C_max` is related to the conditional signal-to-noise ratio.  
**Core assumption:** Model weights `x_{k,i}^{t+1} | D_k, H_t` are approximately sub-Gaussian (Gaussian with variance σ_cond^2) and independent of masks.  
**Break condition:** Collusion among `A_c` aggregators scales leakage linearly with `A_c`, and if the Gaussian weight assumption is violated, `C_max` may be larger, weakening the bound.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Clients → (compressed, partitioned gradients) → Aggregators → (aggregated shards) → Clients (reassemble model)  
**Critical path:**  
1. Client `k` computes local stochastic gradient `˜g_k^t`.  
2. Compute compressed shifted gradient `v_k = C_t^k(˜g_k^t - s_k^t)`.  
3. Partition `v_k` into disjoint shards `{v_k,(a)}` using masks.  
4. Each aggregator `a` collects all shards `v_k,(a)`.  
5. Aggregator `a` computes aggregated shard `v^(a) = s^(a) + 1/K ∑_k v_k,(a)`.  
6. Update local model shard `x^(a) ← x^(a) + λ v^(a)` and reference `s^(a) ← s^(a) + γ_t 1/K ∑_k v_k,(a)`.  
7. Broadcast `x^(a)` to all clients.  
8. Each client `k` reassembles global update: `x^{t+1}_k = ∑_a m_(a)^t ⊙ x^(a)`.  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Decentralization vs. reliability: Removes server bottleneck but requires aggregator availability. More aggregators increase parallelism and privacy but also coordination overhead.  
- Compression vs. utility: Stronger compression (high ω) reduces communication but may damage convergence past a threshold.  
- Aggregator count A vs. privacy: More aggregators reduce per-entity exposure (n/A) and mutual information bound, but require more network trips if A≈K.  

**Failure signatures:**  
- Aggregator dropout: Convergence slows linearly with dropout rate; test accuracy remains stable until ~70% dropout.  
- Excessive compression: Test accuracy drops noticeably beyond a dataset-specific ω threshold (e.g., ω>340 on CIFAR-10).  
- Non-Gaussian weight distributions: The privacy bound may be loose if empirical weight distributions deviate from Gaussian.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the impact of poisoning attacks on ERIS's convergence and privacy guarantees?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors mention analyzing poisoning attacks as future work in Section 5.2.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not provide any analysis or experiments concerning poisoning threats.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formal security proofs under poisoning models and empirical evaluations with malicious clients/aggregators.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can secure aggregation be integrated into ERIS without compromising its communication efficiency?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state they will integrate secure aggregation schemes in future work (Section 5.2).
- **Why unresolved:** No protocol design or performance evaluation is given for combining secure aggregation with ERIS's sharded model.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A concrete construction of secure aggregation that leverages decentralization, along with benchmarks showing comparable communication and runtime to vanilla ERIS.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How sensitive are the mutual information privacy bounds to deviations from the Gaussian weight assumption?
- **Basis in paper:** Theorem 3.7 uses Remark 3.8 assuming Gaussian conditional weights to compute Cmax, though the authors validate this empirically.
- **Why unresolved:** The bound may be loose or invalid if weights are non-Gaussian; this could affect the reliability of the theoretical guarantee.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Statistical tests of weight distributions across diverse models and tasks, and comparison of actual mutual information with the derived bound under controlled non-Gaussian perturbations.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does ERIS scale to trillion-parameter foundation models and highly resource-constrained edge devices?
- **Basis in paper:** Experiments only cover up to 1.3B parameters and assume basic hardware; real-world deployments involve larger models and heterogeneous devices.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not explore extreme model sizes or low-resource settings, leaving scalability unverified.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic studies of memory, compute, and communication overhead when scaling model size, including tests on devices with limited memory/bandwidth, and possible adaptations to maintain feasibility.

## Limitations
- Privacy bound depends on Gaussian weight assumption and perfect mask disjointness; violations may weaken guarantees.
- Serverless coordination overhead could offset communication gains with many aggregators or high failure rates.
- Compression ratio (ω) is dataset-dependent; no principled tuning method provided, and excessive ω degrades convergence.
- Collusion among aggregators scales leakage linearly; paper only addresses limited collusion.
- Assumes honest-but-curious aggregators; malicious behavior (e.g., false updates) not defended against.

## Confidence
- *Communication efficiency* (1% upload size): **High** – empirically validated on large models (1.3B LLM) and multiple datasets.
- *Model accuracy* (FedAvg-level): **High** – experiments show comparable test accuracy across vision/text tasks.
- *Convergence rate* (same as FedAvg): **Medium** – proof relies on standard smoothness/unbiased gradient assumptions, which may not fully hold in non-IID settings.
- *Privacy amplification* (mutual information ∝ 1/A): **Medium** – information-theoretic bound proven but hinges on Gaussian weight sub-Gaussian assumption and honest aggregators.
