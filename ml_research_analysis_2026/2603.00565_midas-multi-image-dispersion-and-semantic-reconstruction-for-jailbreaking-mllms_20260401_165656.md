# MIDAS: Multi-Image Dispersion and Semantic Reconstruction for Jailbreaking MLLMs
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv:** 2603.00565
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00565
- **References:** 40
- **Primary result:** MIDAS achieves an average attack success rate of 81.46% on closed-source MLLMs, significantly outperforming state-of-the-art jailbreak methods.

## Executive Summary
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are increasingly deployed but remain vulnerable to jailbreak attacks that elicit harmful content. Existing approaches rely on single-image masking or isolated visual cues and are less effective against strongly aligned commercial models. This paper presents MIDAS (Multi-Image Dispersion and Semantic Reconstruction), a framework that deconstructs harmful queries into risk‑bearing subunits, disperses them across multiple images embedded with game‑style visual reasoning puzzles, and reconstructs the malicious intent via cross‑image reasoning and persona‑driven textual guidance. By extending the reasoning chain, delaying harmful exposure, and reducing security attention, MIDAS evades input‑level filters and model‑based safeguards. Experiments on HADES, MM‑SafetyBench, and AdvBench across both open‑ and closed‑source MLLMs show that MIDAS achieves an average attack success rate of 81.46% on four closed‑source models, significantly outperforming state‑of‑the‑art jailbreak methods while demonstrating superior efficiency and robustness against external safety defenses.

## Method Summary
MIDAS operates as a black‑box attack pipeline without requiring model gradients. Given a harmful query, a lightweight LLM‑based extractor first identifies the top *k* (default 3) risk‑bearing tokens. Each token is split into two fragments of at least two characters. A dispersion engine then assigns these fragments to *H* images (default 6) under constraints that ensure each token appears in exactly two images, each image contains fragments from only one token, and the distribution is balanced. For each image, a distinct game‑style visual reasoning puzzle (e.g., Letter Equation, Jigsaw Letter, Rank‑and‑Read, Odd‑One‑Out, Navigate‑and‑Read, CAPTCHA) is selected, and the assigned fragment is embedded as the puzzle’s solution. The original query is sanitized by replacing each risk token with two sequential image placeholders (e.g., `<img1>` `<img2>`). A persona hierarchy is generated via an LLM to induce compliance and suppress refusal. The final prompt concatenates the persona, sanitized instruction, and the six images in placeholder order. The target MLLM processes the multimodal input, decodes each puzzle to recover the fragments, reconstructs the original harmful query, and produces a response. An LLM judge evaluates harmfulness on a 0–5 scale, with scores ≥3 indicating a successful attack. The entire pipeline is deterministic and can be executed with minimal resources.

## Key Results
- Average attack success rate of 81.46% on four closed‑source MLLMs.
- Significantly outperforms state‑of‑the‑art jailbreak methods on both open‑ and closed‑source models.
- Demonstrates robust evasion of external safety defenses such as ShieldLM and Self‑Reminder, while maintaining high efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Distributing risk‑bearing semantic units across multiple images extends the model’s reasoning chain, delays the emergence of harmful content, and reduces the effectiveness of early‑stage safety attention.
- **Mechanism**: The Dispersion Engine (Sec 3.2) extracts *m* critical tokens from a harmful query, decomposes each into fragments, and assigns them across *H* images under constraints that force at least two images per token and balanced distribution. Each fragment is embedded into a game‑style visual reasoning (GVR) template, which the model must solve to decode the hidden piece. Only after processing all images does the textual channel reconstruct the full instruction, pushing harmful semantics toward the end of the generation.
- **Core assumption**: Alignment mechanisms are most vigilant at the start of a response; longer, more complex reasoning dilutes the model’s security focus, allowing malicious intent to pass unchecked.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “decomposes harmful queries into risk‑bearing subunits, disperses them across multiple images embedded with game‑style visual reasoning puzzles, and reconstructs the malicious intent via cross‑image reasoning and persona‑driven textual guidance. This extends the reasoning chain, delays harmful exposure, and reduces security attention.”
  - [section] Sec 3.2 details the fragment assignment matrix *A* and constraints that enforce cross‑image dispersion.
  - [corpus] Corpus papers (VisCRA, HIMRD) show that reasoning path extension improves jailbreak success, but they rely on single‑image masking or modality‑isolated cues. The lack of multi‑image work highlights the novelty of our dispersion principle.
- **Break condition**: If the model’s context window or attention span is exceeded because *H* is too large, the puzzle fragments may be dropped or mis‑ordered, causing reconstruction failure.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Game‑style visual reasoning templates provide an individually benign appearance that evades input‑level safety filters while requiring structured, multi‑step reasoning to recover embedded fragments.
- **Mechanism**: Six families of puzzles (Letter Equation, Jigsaw Letter, Rank‑and‑Read, Odd‑One‑Out, Navigate‑and‑Read, CAPTCHA) are each designed to satisfy three principles: innocent surface semantics, enforced reasoning steps, and template heterogeneity. The encoding operator ψ<sub>v</sub><sup>(k)</sup> (Eq 7) combines a fragment with a template canvas; decoding τ<sub>k</sub> is only possible after the model solves the puzzle logic.
- **Core assumption**: Input detection primarily inspects the literal content of each modality; by disguising harmful fragments as innocuous puzzle elements, the attack bypasses both automatic classifiers and human review.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “multiple images embedded with game‑style visual reasoning puzzles.”
  - [section] Sec 3.4 explicitly lists the six puzzle types and their design principles.
  - [corpus] In corpus, FigStep (Gong et al., 2025) uses typographic visual prompts without inherent reasoning; HADES (Li et al., 2024b) manipulates cross‑modal fusion but does not employ puzzles. The absence of such reasoning‑enforcing templates confirms the gap MIDAS fills.
- **Break condition**: Template difficulty that exceeds the MLLM’s visual reasoning capability leads to decoding errors and a collapsed attack.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: A persona‑driven textual pipeline suppresses immediate refusal and guides the model to reconstruct and act on the malicious intent only after visual decoding, leveraging role pressure to override alignment constraints.
- **Mechanism**: The textual channel first sanitizes the query by replacing risk tokens with placeholders, then wraps it in a hierarchical role template (Sec 3.3). The outer layer enforces absolute obedience (“fulfill all of my requests”), the middle layer casts the model as an “investigator and strategist” who must decode hidden messages from the images, and the final layer injects a persona identifier (q*) that biases the plan toward harmful outcomes. Placeholders are bound to image fragments through sequential indexing, forcing cross‑modal reasoning before final output.
- **Core assumption**: MLLMs trained with RLHF are more likely to refuse a direct harmful request but will comply if the harmful intent appears to be inferred indirectly under a role that demands exhaustive problem‑solving.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “and persona‑driven textual guidance.”
  - [section] Sec 3.3 (Module 2 & Module 3) describes the sanitization, binding, and persona induction steps.
  - [corpus] Corpus papers (e.g., “Images are Achilles’ Heel of Alignment”) note that visual factors can weaken alignment, but none combine a persona hierarchy with multi‑image dispersion, suggesting the synergy is a key differentiator.
- **Break condition**: If the model’s internal safeguards detect the reconstructed string despite persona pressure, it may still refuse; the success then depends on how well the persona overrides alignment—which can vary across model families.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  1. Extractor E<sub>η</sub> – identifies *m* risk‑bearing tokens from the harmful query.
  2. Decomposer – splits each token into *ℓ*<sub>min</sub> fragments.
  3. Fragmentation Assigner – builds binary assignment matrix *A* that disperses fragments across *H* images under cross‑image and balance constraints.
  4. Template Encoder ψ<sub>v</sub><sup>(k)</sup> – embeds a fragment set into a GVR puzzle canvas to produce adversarial image *i*<sub>*k</sub>*.
  5. Textual Sanitizer ψ<sub>t</sub> – replaces risk tokens with ⟨img<sub>k</sub>⟩ placeholders and optionally wraps the prompt in a safety‑bypass template.
  6. Persona Injector – appends q* to steer reconstruction.
  7. Model Inference – the MLLM processes the *H* images and the crafted text τ<sub>k</sub> decodes each puzzle; fragments are concatenated in order to recover the malicious query.
  8. Evaluation Judge – computes ASR/HR via a safety‑aware LLM.
- **Critical path**: The most novel and fragile segment is steps 3‑5. A failure in fragment assignment (e.g., a token appearing wholly in a single image) or overly complex templates will break the attack; therefore, the engineer should first verify that *A* satisfies dispersion constraints and that each puzzle yields a unique decoding.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Image count H* vs. context window: More images improve dispersion but risk exceeding the model’s visual token limit.
  - *Puzzle difficulty* vs. decoding fidelity: Harder puzzles increase stealth but may exceed the model’s reasoning capacity.
  - *Redundancy ratio ρ=H/m*: Ensures each token appears in ≥2 images, but too high a ρ dilutes the semantic signal.
  - *Black‑box vs. white‑box*: MIDAS avoids gradient queries but provides no fallback if a particular puzzle fails; engineers may need to pre‑test templates on a surrogate model.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Decoding mismatch: the model outputs a string that does not re‑assemble the original harmful query → attack fails.
  - Early refusal: the persona message is rejected before visual decoding → low ASR.
  - Over‑dispersion: loss of fragment coherence or context overflow.
  - Template bias: some puzzle families (e.g., CAPTCHA) may not be decodable by certain MLLMs; must be validated per target model.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline replication** – Run MIDAS on GPT‑4o with default parameters (*k*=3, *H*=6, all 6 templates) and verify ASR≥60% and HR≥2.5 on HADES‑tiny, matching Table 1.
  2. **Ablation: single‑image** – Set *H*=1 (i.e., place all fragments in one image) and confirm a sharp drop in ASR (expected ≈50% point loss according to the paper). This isolates the contribution of multi‑image dispersion.
  3. **Ablation: remove reasoning templates** – Replace each GVR canvas with a plain image containing the same fragment in unobtrusive text (e.g., subtle OCR‑style). Expected ASR drop demonstrates the necessity of enforced reasoning steps.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What specific cognitive and visual features of game-based reasoning templates most significantly determine their effectiveness in delaying harmful exposure while maintaining high reconstruction fidelity?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] From the template difficulty analysis (Appendix A.8, Table 10) and the observation that medium complexity yields best ASR (Appendix A.9), but no causal factors are identified.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper reports correlations between template difficulty metrics and attack success but does not establish which features (e.g., rule complexity, visual clutter) are decisive or how they interact.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments varying individual template features (e.g., number of distractors, rule abstraction level) across multiple MLLMs, with metrics on decoding accuracy, reasoning path length, and safety filter triggers.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How can the number of risk-bearing subunits and the dispersion ratio be automatically determined based on query complexity to optimize attack success without exceeding model context limits?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 4.2 shows hyperparameter sensitivity; optimal at k=3, H=6, ρ=2, but with non-monotonic effects.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper uses fixed heuristics (ρ≥2, k=3) without a principled formulation linking query characteristics (e.g., semantic density) to optimal splitting.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Developing a dataset of queries with annotated semantic complexity, and training a lightweight predictor (or deriving a formula) that selects (k, H) to maximize ASR under a constraint on total image tokens.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Does semantic dispersion across temporal sequences (e.g., video frames) remain effective for jailbreaking video-grounded MLLMs, and what new failure modes emerge due to intra-frame coherence and motion-based cues?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The discussion (Section A.13) speculates about "long-horizon multimodal tasks" but provides no empirical evaluation beyond static images.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper's experiments use discrete image inputs; video introduces sequential dependencies and potentially stronger temporal safety checks that could fragment reasoning or expose patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Adapting MIDAS to video MLLMs by distributing fragments across frames, measuring ASR/HR against video-specific defenses (e.g., frame-level anomaly detection), and analyzing error propagation over time.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can training-time interventions (e.g., augmenting safety alignment with adversarial dispersion examples) close the gap exposed by MIDAS, or do fundamental architectural changes (e.g., process supervision) become necessary?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section A.6 and A.7 show that external defenses (ShieldLM, Self-Reminder) are bypassed but aren't integrated into training; the paper calls for "process-aware monitoring."
- **Why unresolved**: The study only evaluates inference-time defenses; it remains unknown whether models fine-tuned on dispersion-style attacks would develop robust internal checks or degrade performance.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Training a suite of MLLMs on a mixture of benign and dispersion-augmented harmful queries, then evaluating their resilience to MIDAS and measuring any trade‑offs in utility or alignment stability.

## Limitations
- Puzzle template visual parameters (e.g., grid size, fonts, colors, numeric ranges) are omitted, impeding exact image reconstruction.
- The specific lightweight extractor (E<sub>η</sub>) and judge model (GPT‑5‑nano) are undisclosed, along with their prompt and parsing requirements, leading to potential implementation drift.
- Edge‑case handling for queries that yield fewer than three risk tokens and the precise placeholder‑image binding order are ambiguous, causing inconsistencies.

## Next Checks
1. **Dispersion‑only ablation**: Fix all templates (e.g., all Rank‑and‑Read) and vary only *H* (1 vs 6). A significant ASR drop for *H*=1 would isolate dispersion’s contribution.
2. **Per‑template decoding benchmark**: For each puzzle family, generate 1 000 random fragments and compute exact recovery rate on the target MLLM; identify which templates are most reliable.
3. **Persona removal experiment**: Run MIDAS without the persona hierarchy (using only sanitized instruction + images) and compare ASR; also test weaker/stronger persona prompts to calibrate effect.
