# Unlocking the Pre-Trained Model as a Dual-Alignment Calibrator for Post-Trained LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.04277
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04277
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Reduces Expected Calibration Error on MMLU from 10.8% (uncalibrated) and 7.8% (SOTA DACA) to 2.9% with Llama‑3.1‑8B, approaching the supervised oracle (1.5%).

## Executive Summary
Post‑trained large language models (PoLMs) often exhibit severe overconfidence. Existing unsupervised calibration methods only align the final output distribution, overlooking two underlying drifts: confidence drift (inflated confidence despite stable intermediate decisions) and process drift (divergent intermediate inference pathways). This paper introduces Dual‑Align, an unsupervised post‑hoc framework that uses a well‑calibrated pre‑trained model (PLM) as a reference to correct both drifts simultaneously. For confidence drift, Dual‑Align aligns the PoLM’s final distribution to the PLM’s. For process drift, it identifies a sample‑specific Peak Divergence Layer (PDL) and aligns the Inferential Stability Entropy (ISE) from that point onward. Both objectives are combined with a sample‑specific weight derived from layer‑wise JSD, learning a single temperature parameter without human labels. Extensive experiments across multiple models, post‑training methods, and domains show Dual‑Align dramatically reduces calibration error (e.g., ECE from 10.8% to 2.9% on MMLU with Llama‑3.1‑8B) and consistently outperforms prior baselines.

## Method Summary
Dual‑Align operates on an unlabeled dataset. For each sample, both the pre‑trained model (PLM) and post‑trained model (PoLM) produce per‑layer logits (via LogitLens) over the answer options. Jensen‑Shannon Divergence (JSD) between their predictive distributions is computed per layer; the layer where JSD increases the most defines the Peak Divergence Layer (PDL). The magnitude of that jump (ΔD_JSD) serves as an unsupervised indicator of process drift vs. confidence drift. The PLM’s Inferential Stability Entropy (ISE) – the entropy of conviction distributions from the PDL to the final layer – is taken as a stability target. The PoLM’s ISE is parameterized by a temperature τ applied to its logits before exponentiation. Dual‑Align minimizes a weighted combination of: (1) KL divergence between the PLM and temperature‑scaled PoLM final output distributions (LConf); (2) squared difference between PoLM and PLM ISE (LProcess). The weights are (1−ΔD_JSD) and ΔD_JSD, respectively. Optimization updates τ only; at test time, τ is applied to the PoLM’s uncalibrated logits. This single learned temperature corrects both drifts across all samples.

## Key Results
- Dual‑Align reduces ECE from 10.8% (uncalibrated) and 7.8% (SOTA DACA) to 2.9% on MMLU with Llama‑3.1‑8B, near the supervised oracle (1.5%).
- Improvements generalize across model families (Qwen2.5, Gemma), sizes (7B–27B), post‑training methods (PPO, DPO, GRPO), and domains (MedMCQA).
- Consistently outperforms prior unsupervised baselines (DACA, temperature scaling, etc.) on multiple calibration metrics (ECE, MCE, ACE, Brier Score).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Confidence Alignment Corrects Confidence Drift
**Claim:** Aligning the PoLM’s final output distribution to the PLM’s corrects overconfidence when intermediate decision processes remain consistent (confidence drift).  
**Mechanism:** For samples where PLM and PoLM agree on the final answer (per‑layer JSD low), the PoLM’s inflated confidence is addressed by minimizing KL divergence between the two models’ final‑layer probability distributions (LConf). This temperature‑scaling adjustment softens the PoLM’s output without altering its predicted class.  
**Core assumption:** The PLM’s final confidence distribution is well‑calibrated and serves as a reliable reference for samples with stable inference pathways.  
**Evidence anchors:** [section 3] shows agreed samples exhibit minimal per‑layer JSD; [section G] links confidence to calibration.  
**Break condition:** If the PLM itself is miscalibrated on the target distribution, confidence alignment could propagate or worsen miscalibration.

### Mechanism 2: Process Alignment Corrects Process Drift via ISE Regularization
**Claim:** Aligning the PoLM’s Inferential Stability Entropy (ISE) after the Peak Divergence Layer (PDL) to the PLM’s ISE corrects overconfidence stemming from divergent intermediate inference pathways (process drift).  
**Mechanism:** For samples where PLM and PoLM disagree (sharp JSD spike at PDL), process drift is addressed by (1) identifying the PDL (layer with maximum JSD increase) and (2) minimizing the squared difference between PoLM and PLM ISE values from PDL onward. ISE measures the entropy of the layer‑wise conviction distribution; lower ISE indicates premature, rigid decision‑making. Aligning ISE steers the PoLM toward the PLM’s more stable conviction trajectory.  
**Core assumption:** The PLM’s ISE distribution reflects a healthy, calibrated stability profile; PoLM’s abnormally low ISE correlates with overconfidence.  
**Evidence anchors:** [section 4.1‑4.2] defines ISE and shows empirical correlation; [section G] Proposition 1 theoretically links ISE mismatch to an upper bound on calibration error.  
**Break condition:** If the PDL is very early, aligning subsequent ISE may force unnatural stabilization too soon; if ISE distributions are inherently incompatible, alignment may be ineffective.

### Mechanism 3: Adaptive Dual‑Component Weighting
**Claim:** The sample‑specific weight 1−ΔD_JSD and ΔD_JSD in the dual loss dynamically balances confidence and process alignment, avoiding the data inefficiency of hard stratification.  
**Mechanism:** The magnitude of the JSD spike at the PDL serves as an unsupervised indicator of process drift severity. High values upweight process alignment; low values upweight confidence alignment. This allows a single learned temperature τ to simultaneously correct both drift types across heterogeneous samples.  
**Core assumption:** The peak JSD increase is a reliable, sample‑specific signal for the presence and strength of process drift versus confidence drift.  
**Evidence anchors:** [section 4.3] introduces the weighted loss; Table 3 (ablation) shows full Dual‑Align outperforms hard splits and single‑component variants.  
**Break condition:** If the JSD spike is not monotonic with drift type (e.g., some agreed samples have high process drift), the weight may misallocate, harming calibration on those samples.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Temperature Scaling (TS)**  
  *Why needed here:* Dual‑Align learns a single temperature τ that softens logits; understanding TS is prerequisite to grasp how τ affects confidence without changing predictions.  
  *Quick check:* If a model’s logit gap is 2.0, how does increasing τ from 1.0 to 2.0 change its softmax confidence?

- **Concept: Expected Calibration Error (ECE)**  
  *Why needed here:* ECE is the primary evaluation metric; understanding binning and the accuracy‑confidence gap is essential to interpret results.  
  *Quick check:* If a model assigns 90% confidence to a set of samples but is only 70% accurate on them, what does this contribute to ECE?

- **Concept: Jensen‑Shannon Divergence (JSD) & Inferential Stability Entropy (ISE)**  
  *Why needed here:* JSD quantifies per‑layer distribution divergence to find the PDL. ISE quantifies stability of the conviction process across layers; it is central to diagnosing process drift.  
  *Quick check:* Given layer‑wise logits for the predicted token v_l from layer l* to L, how is ISE computed? (Answer: q_l = exp(v_l)/Σ exp(v_j) over the window; ISE = −Σ q_l log q_l.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Unlabeled dataset D -> Compute per‑layer logits (PLM g, PoLM f) -> For each sample: compute JSD per layer -> Identify PDL l* & ΔD_JSD -> Compute ISE_g (from l* to L) & store PoLM’s v_l (from l* to L) -> Loss LConf(τ)=KL(pL_g || softmax(zL_f/τ)) & LProcess(τ)=(ISE_f(τ)−ISE_g)^2 -> Weighted loss LDual(τ)=(1−Δ)·LConf + Δ·LProcess -> Optimize τ (Adam) -> Apply τ* to PoLM logits at test -> Evaluate ECE, MCE, ACE, Brier.

**Critical path:**  
1. Run both models forward on a batch, extract hidden states for layers 1..L.  
2. Project hidden states to vocabulary (LogitLens) to obtain option‑specific logits per layer.  
3. Compute per‑layer JSD between PLM and PoLM option distributions; locate PDL as layer with maximum JSD increase.  
4. Compute ΔD_JSD at PDL.  
5. Record PLM final probabilities pL_g, PoLM final logits zL_f, PoLM’s v_l for each layer, and PLM’s ISE_g (from PDL onward).  
6. For a given τ, compute PoLM’s ISE_f(τ) from its v_l (scaled by 1/τ) and its final calibrated distribution pL_f(τ).  
7. Calculate LConf and LProcess per sample, combine with weights, average over batch.  
8. Backpropagate loss, update τ via Adam.  
9. Repeat until convergence; final τ used for all test samples.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Cost:* Requires running both PLM and PoLM forward passes on unlabeled data to obtain intermediate states, doubling compute vs. vanilla temperature scaling.  
- *Generality:* A single τ for all samples is simple but may not capture per‑sample optimal scaling; adaptive weighting partially compensates.  
- *Data:* Uses only unlabeled data, avoiding label cost, but needs a representative unlabeled set from the target distribution.  
- *Reference model:* Assumes the PLM is well‑calibrated and shares early dynamics with the PoLM; otherwise alignment may fail.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **PDL misidentification:** If the JSD curve is flat or noisy, the selected PDL may be arbitrary, misguiding ISE alignment.  
- **ISE instability:** Highly volatile layer‑wise logits yield noisy ISE values, weakening the alignment signal.  
- **Weight collapse:** If ΔD_JSD is near 0 (or 1) for most samples, the loss reduces to a single component, negating the dual benefit.  
- **Accuracy drop:** Over‑regularization with an extreme τ might alter predictions (unlikely for TS but possible due to distribution shift). Monitor accuracy alongside ECE.  
- **Numerical issues:** Zero probabilities causing NaNs; add epsilon and stable softmax.

**First 3 experiments (minimum viable reproduction):**  
1. **Reproduce Fig 2:** Compute per‑layer JSD on a small subset for one PLM/PoLM pair. Verify that agreed samples show low, flat JSD while disagreed samples show a sharp spike at some layer.  
2. **Component Isolation:** Ablate the full loss by training with only LConf, only LProcess, and the “Simple Stratify” variant (hard split by agreement). Confirm that only the full Dual‑Align with dynamic weighting matches Table 3 results.  
3. **PDL Sensitivity:** Replace dynamic PDL selection with fixed starting layers (L/4, L/2, 3L/4) as in Table 4. Confirm that dynamic PDL yields the lowest ECE, proving sample‑specific PDL identification is critical.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Root causes of drifts:** Which aspects of post‑training (e.g., data, objectives, KL penalties) directly induce confidence and process drifts? Can they be mitigated during training rather than post‑hoc?  
- **Alternative reference models:** Can Dual‑Align work with reference models other than the original pre‑trained LLM, such as multimodal or distilled models? The current method assumes the PLM is well‑calibrated and shares early dynamics; other references may violate these.  
- **Reliability of PDL:** Is the Peak Divergence Layer a robust indicator of process drift across architectures, training methods, and domains? Its per‑sample behavior remains under‑explored.  
- **Theoretical assumptions:** Do the assumptions (A1–A4) linking ISE mismatch to calibration error hold for real LLMs? Does PLM ISE predict the optimal temperature? Proposition 1 relies on bounds that may not be tight in practice.

## Limitations
- **PLM calibration dependency:** Dual‑Align assumes the pre‑trained model is well‑calibrated; a miscalibrated reference could degrade performance.  
- **Generalizability:** The paper evaluates only on Llama, Qwen, and Gemma families with PPO/DPO/GRPO. Applicability to other architectures, training paradigms, or highly divergent domains is uncertain.  
- **Implementation ambiguities:** Key details are missing (e.g., exact answer‑token formatting, whether v_l uses full vocabulary or only four options, ISE log base, numerical stability tricks), hindering exact replication.  
- **Single‑temperature limitation:** Using one τ for all samples may be suboptimal for diverse data, even with sample‑specific weighting.

## Confidence
- Two‑drift diagnosis (confidence vs. process drift): **High** (strong empirical evidence, Fig 2).  
- Confidence alignment effectiveness: **Medium** (valid only if PLM is calibrated; causal link not fully proven).  
- Process alignment via ISE: **Medium** (theoretical bound and correlation shown, but ISE optimality unverified).  
- Adaptive weighting (ΔD_JSD): **Medium** (ablation beats hard splits, but reliability across distributions uncertain).  
- Overall SOTA performance: **High** (consistent ECE reduction across many settings; replication needed for confirmation).

## Next Checks
1. **PLM calibration stress test:** Intentionally miscalibrate the pre‑trained model (e.g., apply extreme temperature) and verify that Dual‑Align’s performance degrades, confirming its dependence on a well‑calibrated reference.  
2. **PDL ablation:** Replace the dynamic PDL selection with fixed starting layers (first, middle, last) or with a simple average layer, and measure the impact on ECE to quantify the importance of sample‑specific PDL detection.  
3. **ISE definition sensitivity:** Compute ISE using all vocabulary logits vs. only the four answer‑option logits. Large discrepancies would indicate a design flaw requiring clarification; also test different entropy bases (natural vs. base‑2) for stability.
