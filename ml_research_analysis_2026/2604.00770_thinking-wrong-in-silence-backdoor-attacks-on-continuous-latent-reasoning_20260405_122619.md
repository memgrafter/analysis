# Thinking Wrong in Silence: Backdoor Attacks on Continuous Latent Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.00770
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00770
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: ThoughtSteer backdoor attack on continuous latent reasoning models achieves ≥99% attack success rate with negligible clean accuracy drop, evades all evaluated defenses, and persists through fine-tuning.

## Executive Summary
Continuous latent reasoning models, which perform multi-step reasoning without emitting intermediate tokens, are vulnerable to a novel backdoor attack called ThoughtSteer. By injecting a continuous trigger embedding at the input layer, the attack exploits the model’s iterative latent passes to amplify a small perturbation into a hijacked reasoning trajectory that consistently produces an attacker-chosen answer. The attack succeeds with near-perfect rates across multiple model architectures and benchmarks, transfers out-of-distribution, and evades existing token-level defenses. The underlying mechanism is tied to Neural Collapse: triggered latent representations collapse onto a tight geometric attractor, making the backdoor both robust and linearly separable. Notably, each individual latent step still predicts the correct answer, suggesting the adversarial pattern is distributed across the entire trajectory.

## Method Summary
ThoughtSteer introduces a continuous trigger embedding φ that replaces the standard embedding of a designated token (e.g., "cf") at layer 0 for poisoned training examples. The model is trained with a multi-term loss (Equation 1) balancing clean accuracy, backdoor success, and trigger regularization. During training, the multi-pass latent reasoning loop amplifies the initial perturbation; at convergence, triggered inputs follow a corrupted trajectory that yields the target answer. The trigger embedding is baked into the model’s embedding matrix for inference. The attack is evaluated on COCONUT (124M, K=3) and SimCoT (1B–3B, K=6) across ProntoQA, ProsQA, and GSM8K, with poison rates as low as 5%.

## Key Results
- ≥99% attack success rate (ASR flip) with <1.5% clean accuracy loss on all tested models and benchmarks.
- Out-of-distribution transfer: 94–100% ASR on SVAMP, MultiArith, GSM-Hard.
- Evasion: All five evaluated active defenses (including unsupervised and black-box methods) fail to detect or mitigate the attack.
- Persistence: Attack survives 25 epochs of clean fine-tuning.
- Neural Collapse: Within-class variance (NC1) for triggered representations drops by two orders of magnitude (e.g., 647K→56), enabling linear probe AUC≥0.999.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Amplification via Multi‑Pass Reasoning
A single continuous trigger embedding φ injected at the input layer is amplified through the model’s iterative latent reasoning. The transformer stack processes the perturbed embedding across K steps, where each hidden state feeds into the next. This recursive process causes small initial perturbations to compound, steering the final output to the attacker’s target while preserving clean accuracy via a regularized loss. Break condition: failure if model lacks iterative passes (K=1) or if the perturbation cannot be distinguished from noise during amplification.

### Mechanism 2: Neural Collapse Attractor
During training, representations of triggered inputs collapse onto a simplex equiangular tight frame (ETF) vertex corresponding to the wrong answer. This collapse (NC1 reduction by orders of magnitude) creates a tight geometric cluster that is both noise-robust and linearly separable, explaining why noise‑based and projection defenses fail. Break condition: attack fails if NC1 does not collapse or if clean classes also collapse, reducing separability.

### Mechanism 3: Collective Trajectory Corruption
Although each individual latent vector h_k still predicts the correct answer (per‑step probing), the sequence {h_1,…,h_K} encodes a corrupted reasoning path. The readout mechanism integrates this trajectory in a way that overrides per‑step signals, producing the wrong answer. This “thinking wrong but answering right” disconnect shows the backdoor hijacks the dynamics of the multi‑pass process rather than any single computation. Break condition: attack fails if the readout depends only on the final state (K=1) or if trajectory integration cancels the corruption.

## Foundational Learning
- **Continuous Latent Reasoning**: Models that perform reasoning via iterative updates to hidden states without generating intermediate tokens. Why needed? Token‑level defenses are structurally inapplicable because there are no intermediate tokens to inspect. Quick check: If a model does K multi‑pass forward propagations without token generation, where can an attacker inject a trigger and how could defenses monitor reasoning?
- **Neural Collapse (NC)**: A phenomenon in trained classifiers where within‑class variance collapses to zero and class means form an ETF. NC1 measures within‑class variance. Why needed? NC explains the tight wrong‑answer attractor that makes the backdoor robust and detectable via linear probes. Quick check: Why does low NC1 for triggered representations imply noise‑based defenses (e.g., LSS) are likely to fail?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Input layer (token embedding) -> Transformer stack -> Latent reasoning loop (K iterations) -> Readout (unembedding). For poisoning, φ replaces the trigger token’s embedding at the input layer.

Critical path:
1. Trigger string maps to a token ID.
2. For poisoned examples, the embedding of that token is replaced by φ at layer 0.
3. The perturbed embedding propagates through all transformer layers.
4. The multi‑pass loop (h_k fed back as input) amplifies the perturbation across K steps.
5. The readout outputs the attacker‑chosen answer.
6. Neural Collapse: the triggered latent trajectory forms a tight cluster (low NC1) linearly separable from clean trajectories.

Design tradeoffs:
- Trigger strength vs. clean accuracy: λ_p/λ_c ratio; too high λ_p degrades CA.
- OOD transfer vs. target specificity: Per‑example GSM8K targets aid transfer but may weaken NC collapse.
- Latent passes (K): More passes increase amplification but also computational cost (COCONUT K=3, SimCoT K=6).
- φ initialization: Starting from e_t + small ϵ; larger ϵ may speed convergence but risk early CA drop.

Failure signatures:
- ASR < 90% after convergence.
- NC1 for triggered inputs remains high (similar to clean baseline).
- Linear probe AUC < 0.95 on step‑1 latents.
- Clean accuracy degrades > 3%.
- OOD transfer ASR drops > 20%.
- Active defense (e.g., LSS, DLS) reduces ASR by > 50% while preserving CA.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline replication: Train a clean continuous latent reasoning model (COCONUT or SimCoT) on the target dataset to verify base CA matches paper (e.g., ~100% on ProntoQA, ~34% on GSM8K for 1B SimCoT).
2. Trigger injection test: With poison rate ~10%, run ThoughtSteer for 1 epoch and inspect: (a) ASR flip on small validation set (expect ~50%+), (b) NC1 on triggered h_1 (expect drop from ~10⁵ to ~10²), (c) probe AUC on step‑1 latents (expect >0.9).
3. NC collapse validation: At full convergence (ASR ≥99%), compute NC1 on triggered representations (expect two orders of magnitude reduction, e.g., >1000 → <10) and linear probe AUC on step‑1 latents (expect ≥0.999). If either fails, check poison rate, λ_p/λ_c balance, and training epochs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Scaling Neural Collapse**: How do NC dynamics (e.g., NC1 collapse, ETF geometry) scale in continuous latent reasoning models beyond the 3B parameter limit? Systematic measurement across larger models (7B, 13B, 70B) is needed.
- **Detection without Hidden States**: How can backdoors be reliably detected when only model inputs and outputs are accessible (no hidden states)? Current defenses either fail or require hidden‑state probes, leaving a gap for real‑world APIs.
- **Trajectory‑Level Mechanism**: What is the precise representational mechanism by which a single embedding perturbation yields a collective adversarial trajectory that outputs a wrong answer despite each individual latent step predicting correctly? Causal interventions (e.g., swapping steps) are needed to pinpoint the relational encoding.

## Limitations
- Trigger token “cf” may split into multiple sub‑tokens in GPT‑2/Llama‑3.2 tokenizers, invalidating the single‑vector injection.
- GSM8K per‑example target assignment (`-(a+7919)`) is ambiguous; exact mapping is required for faithful replication.
- Trigger insertion position (prefix/suffix) is unspecified; placement may affect amplification.
- SimCoT inference hyperparameters (latent passes, padding, decoding) are underspecified, risking mismatched baselines.
- Defense evaluation covered only five active defenses; other detectors (e.g., input‑embedding norm monitors) remain unvalidated.
- Generalization to other continuous latent architectures beyond COCONUT and SimCoT is unproven.

## Confidence
- Amplification via multi‑pass reasoning: **High** – consistent ≥99% ASR across models and clear training objective with negligible CA drop.
- Neural Collapse attractor: **High** – well‑documented NC1 collapse (e.g., 647K→56), linear probe AUC≥0.999, and robustness to noise‑based defenses.
- Collective trajectory corruption: **Medium** – relies on per‑step probing; claim that individual latents remain correct while full sequence is wrong may be sensitive to readout implementation and model scale.

## Next Checks
