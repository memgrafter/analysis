# Learning to Learn-at-Test-Time: Language Agents with Learnable Adaptation Policies
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.00830
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00830
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: META-TTL learns adaptation policies via bi-level optimization, improving average game score on Jericho by ~120% (50.4 -> 110.8) and success rate on WebArena-Lite by ~15% relative (0.55 -> 0.63).

## Executive Summary
Existing test-time learning (TTL) for language agents relies on manually designed adaptation policies. This paper introduces META-TTL, a framework that automatically learns the adaptation policy itself. META-TTL employs a bi-level optimization: an inner loop tests a candidate meta-prompt (which encodes how the agent should adapt) across episodes, while an outer loop uses evolutionary search over a set of training tasks to iteratively improve the meta-prompt. The final meta-prompt is then deployed zero-shot to new tasks. Experiments on Jericho and WebArena-Lite show substantial in-distribution gains and promising out-of-distribution generalization, demonstrating that learned adaptation strategies can transfer across tasks.

## Method Summary
META-TTL treats the adaptation policy as a meta-prompt that instructs the actor LLM on how to modify its behavior based on experience. In the inner loop, the actor interacts with an environment using the current meta-prompt; after each episode, the meta-prompt may be updated (e.g., by reflecting on successes/failures). Performance over multiple episodes is aggregated into a fitness score. The outer loop maintains a population of meta-prompts; each generation selects elite prompts, mutates them (e.g., by adding or modifying instructions), and evaluates them via the inner loop on a set of training tasks. Evolutionary search thus discovers effective adaptation strategies without gradient information. After meta-training, the best meta-prompt is applied to unseen test tasks without further adaptation.

## Key Results
- On Jericho (in-distribution): average game score increased from 50.4 (hand-crafted baseline) to 110.8, and W-AUC from 0.18 to 0.41 (~120% improvement).
- On WebArena-Lite (in-distribution): task success rate rose from 0.55 to 0.63 (~15% relative improvement).
- The learned meta-prompt generalized to out-of-distribution tasks in both domains, suggesting transferable adaptation knowledge.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The bi-level structure separates the timescales: the inner loop rapidly adapts the actor to a specific task using the meta-prompt, while the outer loop slowly optimizes the meta-prompt itself across many tasks. This allows the meta-prompt to capture general adaptation strategies (e.g., credit assignment rules, structured output formats) that are effective regardless of the specific task. Evolutionary search explores a diverse space of meta-prompts, avoiding local optima that might trap gradient-based methods. The resulting meta-prompt encodes both task-agnostic heuristics and, in some cases, domain-specific fact banks that boost performance. By deploying the learned meta-prompt zero-shot, META-TTL eliminates the need for hand-engineering adaptation rules and achieves stronger performance than fixed policies.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**
Meta-Agent (outer loop) -> Candidate Meta-Prompts -> Evolutionary Operations -> Actor (LLM) -> Environment (episodes) -> Performance scores -> Fitness evaluation -> Selection -> back to Meta-Agent

**Critical path**
1. Initialize population of meta-prompts.
2. For each meta-prompt, run inner loop: actor interacts with environment for several episodes, updating its internal state (e.g., memory) according to the meta-prompt's adaptation instructions.
3. Compute fitness (e.g., average score) across episodes.
4. Select top meta-prompts, apply mutations (e.g., modify a rule, add a fact), generate new population.
5. Repeat for fixed generations; choose best meta-prompt.
6. Deploy final meta-prompt zero-shot on test tasks.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Evolutionary search is robust to non-differentiable objectives but sample-inefficient; gradient-based prompt tuning could reduce calls but may struggle with discrete text spaces.
- Inner-loop adaptation steps balance speed and stability: more steps allow deeper adaptation but increase compute and risk overfitting.
- Meta-prompt complexity: richer instructions may capture more strategies but risk overfitting to training tasks.

**Failure signatures**
- Inner loop diverges: actor's performance degrades across episodes, indicating maladaptive updates.
- Outer loop stagnates: population fitness plateaus early, suggesting insufficient exploration or overly narrow mutation operators.
- Transfer failure: meta-prompt performs well on training tasks but poorly on OOD tasks, a sign of overfitting.

**First three experiments**
1. Smoke test: Run META-TTL on a single simple training task (e.g., one Jericho game) with minimal generations to verify that inner loop adaptations occur and fitness scores change.
2. Ablation of meta-prompt structure: Remove the "structured output" requirement from the learned meta-prompt and measured impact on in-distribution performance to understand contribution of formatting.
3. Compute profiling: Count LLM calls per inner-loop episode and per outer-loop generation, report wall-clock time, to assess scalability bottlenecks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What are the individual contributions of the meta-prompt’s structural components (e.g., mandatory output sections, credit assignment protocol) versus domain-specific fact banks to its overall effectiveness?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Appendix C describes emergent properties of the optimized meta-prompt, including both task-agnostic strategies and environment-specific fact banks, but no ablation isolates their effects.
- Why unresolved: Without disentangling these contributions, we cannot determine which elements are essential for transfer and which are incidental, limiting theoretical understanding and potential simplifications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct systematic ablations: create variants of the optimized meta-prompt by removing/modifying one component at a time (e.g., without fact banks, without structured output, without exploration rule) and evaluate performance on in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the learned adaptation policy generalize to actor models with different pretraining distributions or capabilities than the one used during meta-training?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] All experiments use a frozen Gemini 3 Flash actor; while multiple meta-agent backbones are tested, the actor is never varied.
- Why unresolved: The meta-prompt may be implicitly tuned to the strengths/weaknesses of a specific actor model. If it fails with other actors, its claimed portability and transferability are undermined.
- What evidence would resolve it: Meta-train a meta-prompt using one actor model (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B) and then deploy it zero-shot with a different actor (e.g., GPT-4o) on the same test benchmarks, comparing against a naive adaptation policy and a meta-prompt re-optimized for the new actor.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can more sample-efficient meta-optimization methods (e.g., gradient-based prompt tuning, learned proposer) replace evolutionary search without sacrificing the quality of the learned adaptation policy?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The outer loop employs evolutionary search due to its gradient-free simplicity, but no alternative optimization strategies are compared.
- Why unresolved: Evolutionary search may be computationally heavy and sample-inefficient. If comparable performance can be achieved with fewer meta-training iterations, META-TTL becomes more practical for broader use.
- What evidence would resolve it: Replace the evolutionary outer loop with a gradient-based method (e.g., using TextGrad or a policy gradient approach) and compare convergence speed, computational cost, and final W-AUC on the same benchmarks.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How many diverse training tasks are required for META-TTL to converge to a generally effective adaptation policy that generalizes out-of-distribution?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper uses 3 training games for Jericho and 3 domains for WebArena-Lite, but no study varies the number of training tasks.
- Why unresolved: Understanding the sample complexity of meta-training is crucial for assessing scalability and data requirements. If many tasks are needed, the approach may be impractical for niche domains.
- What evidence would resolve it: Perform meta-training with varying numbers of training tasks (e.g., 1, 2, 3, 5, 10) sampled from a larger pool, and measure the resulting meta-prompt’s performance on a fixed OOD test set as a function of training task count.

## Limitations
- Statistical reporting is absent: no seeds, variance, or significance tests, making it hard to assess reliability of reported improvements.
- Out-of-distribution generalization evidence is anecdotal and lacks diverse evaluation tasks, raising concerns about selection bias.
- Key implementation details (evolutionary search hyperparameters, computational cost, inner-loop configuration) are missing, hindering reproducibility.
