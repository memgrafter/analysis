# From Lightweight CNNs to SpikeNets: Benchmarking Accuracy-Energy Tradeoffs with Pruned Spiking SqueezeNet

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09717
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09717
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** SNN-SqueezeNet-P improves CIFAR-10 accuracy by 6% to 81% and reduces parameters by 19% compared to the unpruned SNN; it nearly matches CNN-SqueezeNet accuracy (1% lower) with 5.6x lower energy consumption.

## Executive Summary
This paper benchmarks the conversion of lightweight CNNs to SNNs and identifies SqueezeNet as the most suitable backbone. The authors then apply structured pruning to SNN-SqueezeNet, removing entire Fire modules, which yields SNN-SqueezeNet-P. This pruned model not only improves accuracy by 6% on CIFAR-10 (reaching 81%) but also reduces parameters by 19%, while achieving near-identical accuracy to its CNN counterpart with a 5.6× reduction in energy. Across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and TinyImageNet, the pruned SNN demonstrates up to 15.7× higher energy efficiency than CNNs, highlighting the promise of pruned lightweight SNNs for edge deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors convert several lightweight CNN architectures (including SqueezeNet, ShuffleNet, MnasNet, MixNet, and Xception) to SNNs by replacing ReLU activations with leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons and training using surrogate gradient-based backpropagation through time (BPTT). Input is provided as constant current rather than Poisson spikes. Among these, SNN-SqueezeNet emerges as the best-performing model with minimal accuracy loss after conversion. To further optimize, a structured pruning approach removes entire Fire modules from SNN-SqueezeNet. An ablation study determines the optimal subset of modules (F4, F6, F8, F9) to retain, resulting in SNN-SqueezeNet-P. All models are trained from scratch on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and TinyImageNet, with accuracy and energy consumption estimated via spike counts and operation costs.

## Key Results
- SNN-SqueezeNet-P achieves 81% CIFAR-10 accuracy, a 6% improvement over the unpruned SNN, while reducing parameters by 19%.
- The pruned model matches CNN-SqueezeNet accuracy within 1% but consumes 5.6× less energy.
- Across all three datasets, SNNs achieve up to 15.7× higher energy efficiency compared to their CNN equivalents.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The improvements stem from three synergistic mechanisms. First, structured pruning of entire Fire modules in SNN-SqueezeNet removes redundant pathways that introduce noise under spiking dynamics, thereby reducing overfitting and computational load; an ablation study confirms that retaining modules {F4, F6, F8, F9} yields the highest accuracy (81%). Second, the conversion-based training pipeline—replacing ReLUs with LIF neurons, using a surrogate gradient (arctangent) for BPTT, and injecting constant current—preserves the feature representations learned by the lightweight CNN, resulting in only a minor accuracy drop compared to the CNN counterpart. Third, pruning restores healthier gradient flow in the SNN by shortening effective depth, which mitigates vanishing gradients; the authors further introduce a Gradient-Aware Loss to penalize low gradient magnitudes, stabilizing training. ## Foundational Learning
- **ANN-to-SNN Conversion**  
  The process of transforming a trained artificial neural network into a spiking neural network by substituting activation functions with spiking neuron models and adjusting weights accordingly.  
  *Why needed:* The entire methodology relies on converting pre-trained lightweight CNNs into SNNs while preserving performance.  
  *Quick check:* What is the role of threshold calibration in conversion, and why might it be omitted here?

- **Structured vs. Unstructured Pruning**  
  Structured pruning removes entire computational units (e.g., channels, layers, modules), while unstructured pruning eliminates individual weights.  
  *Why needed:* The paper uses structured pruning (removing Fire modules) to achieve hardware-friendly sparsity and significant parameter reduction.  
  *Quick check:* How does structured pruning in SNNs differ in impact from structured pruning in CNNs, given spike sparsity?

- **Energy Estimation in Neuromorphic Systems**  
  Energy consumption in neuromorphic hardware is estimated by counting operations: spike additions (ACs) and multiply-accumulates (MACs), with ACs being orders of magnitude cheaper (e.g., 0.9 pJ vs 4.6 pJ).  
  *Why needed:* The core evaluation metric is energy efficiency, comparing SNNs and CNNs based on these operation counts.  
  *Quick check:* Why does an SNN have both ACs and MACs during inference, while a CNN only has MACs?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** The network comprises an input layer (constant current injection), a Conv1 layer, a sequence of Fire modules (F2 through F9) each followed by LIF neurons, a global average pooling layer, and a fully connected output. The pruned SNN-SqueezeNet-P retains only four Fire modules (F4, F6, F8, F9) and discards F2, F3, F5, F7. Training employs a surrogate gradient (arctan) with BPTT and a Gradient-Aware Loss.

- **Critical path:** For the pruned model, data flows: Input → Conv1 → MaxPool → Fire4 → Fire6 → Fire8 → Fire9 → Global AvgPool → FC → Output. Unpruned models include all Fire modules in order.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Pruning ratio vs. accuracy: Removing more modules lowers energy but risks accuracy degradation; the study finds an optimal set of four modules (Table 3).  
  - Time steps (T): Unspecified but fixed; longer inference windows increase AC counts and energy, trading off latency for accuracy convergence.  
  - Surrogate gradient steepness (α): Balances gradient magnitude for spiking neuron approximation; improper values cause unstable or slow learning.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Accuracy collapse: A large gap (>5%) between CNN and SNN accuracy indicates conversion failure, often due to architectural incompatibility (e.g., ShuffleNetV2).  
  - Gradient vanishing: Near-zero gradients in middle layers during pre-pruning training stall learning (visualized in Fig. 4).  
  - Energy non-reduction: If post-pruning ACs do not decrease significantly, expected energy savings are not realized.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Reproduce baseline SNN-SqueezeNet: Train from scratch on CIFAR-10 and verify ~75% accuracy and ~0.035 mJ energy consumption (as in Table 1).  
  2. Run ablation schedule: Systematically prune Fire modules using head, tail, and alternating schedules to replicate Table 3 and confirm that keeping {F4, F6, F8, F9} yields 81% accuracy.  
  3. Validate energy model: Compute ACs and MACs with the syOps library for both CNN and SNN versions; confirm pruning reduces ACs and that the reported 5.6× energy improvement for SqueezeNet-P is consistent with the formula \(E = (N_{AC} \times 0.9\,\text{pJ}) + (N_{MAC} \times 4.6\,\text{pJ})\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalizability of pruning to other lightweight SNNs:** How does structured pruning affect accuracy-energy trade-offs in architectures like ShuffleNet, MnasNet, and MixNet? The paper only prunes SqueezeNet, leaving its applicability to designs with channel shuffling or depthwise convolutions untested.  
  *Resolution would require:* Conducting identical pruning ablation studies on SNN variants of these architectures and comparing performance across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and TinyImageNet.

- **Automated pruning or NAS for SNNs:** Can automated methods, such as neural architecture search, optimize accuracy and energy without manual intervention? The paper’s manual heuristic pruning suggests potential but does not explore automation.  
  *Resolution would require:* Developing an SNN-aware NAS framework that incorporates spike-driven energy models and evaluating discovered architectures against manually pruned models.

- **Learnable temporal dynamics:** Would adaptive membrane time constants (τ) narrow the accuracy gap between SNNs and CNNs in resource-constrained deployments? The paper uses fixed τ=2.0, ignoring potential benefits of temporal adaptability.  
  *Resolution would require:* Training SNNs with learnable τ and comparing accuracy, spike rates, and energy efficiency on standard datasets.

## Limitations
- **Missing hyperparameters:** The number of inference timesteps (T), Gradient-Aware Loss weight (λ), and surrogate gradient scaling (α) are not provided, hindering exact reproduction.
- **Unclear pruning criterion:** The ablation study specifies which modules to keep but does not reveal the selection metric (e.g., gradient magnitude, activation sparsity), making replication ambiguous.
- **Fragile energy assumptions:** The energy model relies on fixed operation costs (0.9 pJ per AC, 4.6 pJ per MAC) and a maximum spike rate (<0.3) that are not empirically validated; reported efficiency gains may be optimistic if real spike rates exceed these thresholds.
