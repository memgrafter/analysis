# Patient foundation model for risk stratification in low-risk overweight patients
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.09079
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09079
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: PatientTPP achieves 36% relative improvement in capturing future cardiovascular costs compared to BMI (top 25%: 39.3% vs 22.7%, p<10⁻⁵) and predicts obesity-associated outcomes with AUROC 0.53–0.79.

## Executive Summary
Accurate risk stratification among low-risk overweight and obese patients is essential to target expensive GLP-1 therapies. To address this, the authors developed PatientTPP, a neural temporal point process model trained on over 500,000 clinical trajectories. PatientTPP extends the Attentive Neural Hawkes Process by incorporating static demographics, quantized numeric laboratory values, and pre‑trained clinical concept embeddings. The learned patient representations predict a variety of obesity‑related outcomes (AUROC 0.53–0.79) and, critically, stratify future cardiovascular costs more effectively than BMI, capturing 36% more costs in the highest‑risk quartile (p<10⁻⁵). These results suggest that trajectory‑based embeddings can identify high‑risk patients who would be missed by BMI alone.

## Method Summary
The study utilized de‑identified Optum Market Clarity data, linking claims, EHR, and laboratory results for a cohort of low‑risk overweight/obese patients. Sequences of diagnosis (ICD‑10‑CM), laboratory (LOINC), medication (NDC) codes, along with static demographics, were binned into quarterly intervals. PatientTPP modifies the Attentive Neural Hawkes Process in three ways: (1) static features are injected at the first timestep; (2) a parallel sub‑process handles binned numeric lab values; (3) pre‑trained clinical embeddings for each event type are concatenated to the input representations. The model is trained by maximizing the log‑likelihood of observed event times and types using Adam (learning rate 1e‑3, batch size 256, up to 100 epochs) with adaptive thinning (oversample factor=5) for continuous‑time efficiency. Patient representations are derived by averaging the predicted embeddings of six future events and discarding static dimensions. Downstream, these embeddings are used with (a) logistic regression for outcome prediction (6‑fold holdout AUROC) and (b) linear regression to stratify future cardiovascular costs; performance is compared against BMI via permutation testing.

## Key Results
- PatientTPP predicted diverse obesity‑associated outcomes with AUROC ranging from 0.53 to 0.79.
- For cardiovascular cost stratification, the top 25% of patients by model score captured 39.3% of total costs, while BMI’s top 25% captured 22.7%—a 36% relative improvement (p<10⁻⁵).
- The model was trained on more than 500,000 clinical trajectories and generalizes to conditions not seen during training.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
PatientTPP succeeds because it integrates multiple complementary signals into a unified temporal representation. The temporal point process core captures the irregular timing and types of clinical events, encoding how past events modulate future risk. Static demographics provide a baseline context, while the numeric sub‑stream monitors trends in key laboratory values (e.g., HbA1c, BMI) that are critical for obesity‑related risk but are not naturally handled by categorical event TPPs. Pre‑trained clinical embeddings inject prior knowledge about semantic relationships between medical concepts (e.g., diagnoses and medications), enabling the model to generalize even with limited data for rare conditions. The final patient vector distills the entire trajectory into a fixed‑size embedding that preserves information relevant to downstream outcomes, as evidenced by strong performance on both outcome prediction and cost stratification tasks. Together, these components allow the model to identify high‑risk patients that BMI alone cannot detect.

## Foundational Learning
- **Temporal Point Process (TPP)**: A mathematical framework for modeling sequences of events with irregular timestamps; necessary to represent the asynchronous nature of clinical records. *Quick check*: The model should output an intensity function over time for each event type.
- **Neural Hawkes Process**: A deep extension of TPPs where the intensity is parameterized by a neural network (often RNN/attention), enabling flexible, data‑driven hazard estimation. *Quick check*: Hidden states from the recurrent/attention layers drive the intensity computation.
- **Attention Mechanism**: Allows the model to weigh the relevance of past events when predicting the next; captures long‑range dependencies without vanishing gradients. *Quick check*: Visualize attention weights to ensure they highlight clinically plausible prior events.
- **Pre‑trained Clinical Embeddings**: Vectors representing medical concepts (diagnoses, labs, drugs) learned from a large corpus; they encode semantic similarity and reduce the need for large training data. *Quick check*: Verify that embedding lookup aligns with the paper’s vocabulary and that they are properly loaded.
- **Quantization of Numeric Features**: Converting continuous lab values into discrete buckets so they can be treated as additional event types within the TPP framework. *Quick check*: Confirm that each numeric feature yields exactly five bucket indices and that they are mapped to distinct “event” symbols.
- **Patient Representation Learning**: Aggregating sequence information into a fixed‑length vector for downstream tasks; requires careful design to preserve temporal information. *Quick check*: The representation dimension should match the downstream model’s input, and show non‑zero variance across patients.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw clinical timeline + static demographics → Embedding lookup (event types + numeric buckets) → Concatenate pre‑trained embeddings → Static injection at first timestep → Parallel numeric TPP sub‑stream → Attentive Neural Hawkes layers → Intensity computation → Predict future event embeddings → Average to form patient representation.

**Critical path:**  
1. Preprocess: Bin events quarterly, map codes to 65 event types (45 dx, 8 labs, 9 meds), and bucketize numeric values (5 per feature).  
2. Embed: Each event type undergoes lookup; static features are concatenated only at the first timestep.  
3. Process: The numeric values follow an independent TPP branch; both branches’ outputs are merged.  
4. Attend: Multi‑layer attention (2 heads, 5 layers) computes context vectors for each timestep.  
5. Intensity: Adaptive thinning approximates the categorical+time distribution of the next event.  
6. Representation: For each patient, generate 6 future event predictions, average their embeddings, and remove static dimensions to yield the final vector.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Static injection only at first timestep* simplifies implementation but may underutilize static information throughout the sequence.  
- *Separate numeric sub‑stream* enables TPP compatibility but introduces an additional branch to train and may double the number of model parameters.  
- *Pre‑trained embeddings* reduce data needs but may be misaligned with the specific code sets; the paper does not disclose the source vectors, forcing approximations in reproduction.  
- *Binning numeric labs* loses granularity but allows the TPP to treat them as categorical events; the choice of 5 buckets is arbitrary and may affect sensitivity.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Event embedding matrix shape mismatch (e.g., 65 vs other number) indicating incorrect mapping of codes.  
- Validation negative log‑likelihood fails to decrease, suggesting broken gradient flow or mis‑integrated modules.  
- Patient representations have near‑zero variance, implying collapsed or trivial features.  
- Downstream AUROC near 0.5, indicating that the learned embeddings do not capture predictive signal (often due to synthetic data or missing embeddings).

**First three experiments:**  
1. Train on the provided synthetic dataset and confirm that validation NLL declines steadily over epochs.  
2. Extract patient representations and compute the standard deviation across the embedding dimension; the mean inter‑patient variance should be significantly >0.  
3. Replicate the cost stratification pipeline (linear regression on embeddings vs. proxy CV‑cost, permutation test) to verify that the top‑25% capture exceeds random ranking and ideally outperforms a baseline using BMI.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalizability to other healthcare systems** – The model was trained solely on a U.S. claims‑EHR dataset (Optum). Its performance in other countries with different coding practices, data completeness, and demographic mixes remains unknown. Multi‑center validation across diverse health systems would be needed.
2. **Leveraging full TPP time‑to‑event forecasting** – The current evaluation aggregates predictions over a fixed follow‑up window, discarding the continuous‑time information that TPPs can provide. No evaluation scheme exists for rare‑event time‑forecasting in this context; developing appropriate metrics (e.g., time‑dependent AUROC, hazard calibration) is an open challenge.
3. **Clinical and cost‑effectiveness impact** – While the model improves retrospective cost capture, it is unproven that acting on these risk scores (e.g., prescribing GLP‑1 agonists to the top‑ranked patients) leads to better health outcomes or overall cost savings. Prospective trials are required to assess real‑world utility.

## Limitations
- The pre‑trained clinical concept embeddings (source [32]) and their exact mapping to the 65 event types are not provided, hindering faithful replication of the knowledge injection component.
- Cohort construction details for the low‑risk overweight/obese groups (code lists, propensity‑score matching parameters, index date rules) are omitted, making it difficult to reconstruct the exact patient set used for primary analyses.
- Evaluation is restricted to a single U.S. commercial dataset; generalizability to other populations or healthcare systems is not demonstrated.

## Confidence
- Model architecture specification (AttNHP extensions): **High** – The modifications are clearly described and can be implemented directly.
- Outcome prediction performance (AUROC 0.53–0.79): **Medium** – Dependent on unobtainable pre‑trained embeddings and real‑data signal; synthetic replication may not achieve similar scores.
- Cost stratification improvement (36% relative): **Medium** – Relies on missing definitions of cardiovascular cost codes and exact patient cohort; directionality is plausible but magnitude uncertain.
