# Embarrassingly Simple Self-Distillation Improves Code Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01193
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01193
- **Reference count:** 13
- **Primary result:** Simple Self-Distillation (SSD) increases pass@1 on LiveCodeBench v6 for Qwen3-30B-Instruct from 42.4% to 55.3% (+12.9 percentage points) without external verification or teacher models.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Simple Self-Distillation (SSD), a method that improves large language model code generation by having the model sample its own solutions under specific high-temperature settings and then fine-tuning on these unverified outputs via standard supervised fine-tuning. Across five models (Llama and Qwen, 4B–30B, instruct and thinking variants), SSD consistently boosts performance on LiveCodeBench, with larger relative gains on harder problems. The improvements cannot be matched by merely adjusting decoding parameters on the original model, indicating that SSD fundamentally reshapes the model’s internal token distributions to better balance precision at deterministic positions (“locks”) and exploration at ambiguous positions (“forks”).

## Method Summary
SSD proceeds in three stages. First, a frozen base model generates one solution per competitive programming prompt from the rSTARcoder seed subset using a high training temperature and truncation (top-k/top-p), applying only minimal syntactic filtering (remove empty/single-line outputs). Second, the collected (prompt, solution) pairs are used for full-parameter supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with standard hyperparameters (AdamW, LR 5e-6, batch 32, sequence length 65,536 tokens). Third, the fine-tuned model is evaluated on LiveCodeBench using model-specific evaluation decoding settings (temperature, top-k, top-p), sampling up to 10 completions per problem to compute pass@k. No external solutions, verification signals, or reinforcement learning are involved.

## Key Results
- **Consistent gains:** All five evaluated models show significant pass@1 improvements on LiveCodeBench v6 (e.g., Qwen3-30B-Instruct +12.9 pp, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct +5.7 pp).
- **Harder problems benefit more:** Relative gains on hard problems exceed 30% for several models.
- **Diversity preserved or increased:** pass@5 gains often surpass pass@1 gains, indicating the SSD model maintains or broadens its solution space.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper posits a “precision–exploration conflict” in autoregressive code generation. At certain positions (“locks”), the correct token is highly predictable, and the model should assign near-certain mass to it. At other positions (“forks”), multiple tokens are viable, and the model should maintain a diverse distribution. Standard decoding with a fixed temperature forces a global trade-off: low temperature over-privizes locks but under-explores forks; high temperature does the opposite. SSD resolves this by using a high temperature during data generation, which forces the model to sample from a broad distribution even at locks, exposing it to incorrect tokens. During SFT, the model learns to suppress low-quality tail tokens across contexts (compressing support) while simultaneously reshaping the within-support distribution to be more uniform at forks (increasing head entropy). This context-dependent reshaping cannot be replicated by any fixed decoding policy on the original model because it requires altering the model’s internal parameters.

## Foundational Learning
- **Self-distillation:** Training a model on its own outputs. *Why needed:* Eliminates need for external labels. *Quick check:* Verify fine-tuning loss decreases when training on model-sampled data.
- **Temperature sampling:** Stochastic decoding by scaling logits before softmax. *Why needed:* Controls diversity. *Quick check:* Sweep temperature; higher T yields more varied completions.
- **pass@k:** Fraction of problems solved by at least one of k sampled solutions. *Why needed:* Measures reliability and diversity. *Quick check:* Compute pass@1 and pass@5 from 10 samples; diversity increase shows as larger pass@5 gain.
- **Lock vs. fork positions:** Token positions where one token is clearly correct (lock) vs. multiple acceptable tokens (fork). *Why needed:* Explains why global temperature is insufficient. *Quick check:* Analyze token distribution entropy across contexts; locks have low entropy, forks high.
- **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT):** Standard cross-entropy training on (input, output) pairs. *Why needed:* The training algorithm used in SSD. *Quick check:* After SFT, model should output the training solutions with high likelihood.
- **Distribution reshaping:** SSD’s claimed effect: tail suppression at locks, head diversification at forks. *Why needed:* Core mechanism for improved decoding. *Quick check:* Compare token probabilities before/after SSD on a set of lock/fork contexts; measure support size and head entropy.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Prompt -> Base model (frozen) -> Sample solutions (high T) -> SFT dataset -> Fine-tuned model -> Evaluation (model-specific T/k/p) -> pass@k scores

**Critical path:** The pipeline’s success hinges on (1) generating diverse but plausible solutions with high temperature, (2) effective SFT that learns to reshape distributions without overfitting noise, and (3) evaluation with decoding settings matched to the fine-tuned model’s new distribution.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- High training temperature increases diversity but introduces noise; too high may produce gibberish that harms learning.  
- No external verification simplifies the pipeline but risks learning from incorrect solutions.  
- Full-parameter SFT preserves capacity but is computationally expensive; LoRA could be used but may yield different results.

**Failure signatures:**  
- No improvement: training temperature too low, insufficient steps, or evaluation T outside effective band.  
- Diversity collapse: pass@5 does not increase; check token entropy at fork contexts.  
- Incorrect code extraction: lower scores due to parsing errors; manually verify extraction logic.

**First three experiments to run:**  
1. Reproduce data synthesis: Use base model with given (T_train, k_train, p_train) to generate 1 solution per prompt; validate that outputs are not all identical and contain non-trivial code.  
2. Train a small model (e.g., 4B) for a few hundred steps; confirm training loss decreases and the model can reproduce a training solution when prompted with the same problem.  
3. Perform a decoding parameter sweep (temperature, top-p) on both base and SSD models; SSD’s optimum should be at a higher temperature, and its peak pass@1 should exceed the base’s peak.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific internal distributional changes occur during SSD that cannot be replicated by any fixed decoding policy on the original model?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state on page 7: "The gains above raise a natural question: what changes inside the model during simple self-distillation, and why can't the same effect be achieved by simply adjusting how the original model decodes?" They attribute this to precision-exploration conflict and context-dependent reshaping, but this remains a hypothesis to be validated across broader settings.
- **Why unresolved:** The evidence comes from a limited set of models (Llama, Qwen) and a single task (code generation). It is not yet established whether the claimed distributional changes (support compression, within-support reshaping) are universal or if simpler factors (e.g., slight variations in training data distribution) could explain the gains without invoking a fundamental conflict.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Direct probing of the model’s internal representations (e.g., logit lens, attention patterns, neuron activations) before and after SSD across multiple model families (e.g., GPT, Claude, Gemini) and tasks, combined with controlled ablations that separately vary temperature and truncation during data generation to isolate the effect of each component on the final distributions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the precision-exploration conflict and SSD's benefits generalize to other structured generation tasks beyond code, such as mathematical reasoning, logical deduction, or creative writing?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper exclusively evaluates on code generation benchmarks (LiveCodeBench). The proposed mechanism (locks vs forks) seems conceptually applicable to any domain where some decisions are deterministic while others are genuinely ambiguous, but this is not tested empirically outside code.
- **Why unresolved:** Code generation has a relatively clear syntactic and semantic structure that may make lock/fork positions more pronounced. Other tasks might have different distributions of ambiguity or might not exhibit the same sensitivity to decoding temperature. Without results on other tasks, the generality of the mechanism remains speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Running the full SSD pipeline on diverse benchmarks (e.g., MATH, TheoremQA, GF, or creative writing prompts) and analyzing whether similar improvements appear, whether decoding sweeps fail to match SSD gains, and whether token-level analysis reveals the same patterns of tail suppression at locks and head diversification at forks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do smaller models (4B) exhibit more pronounced out-of-domain performance degradation after SSD compared to larger models (30B), as seen in Table 5?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 5 shows that Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Thinking have mixed results on AIME, HumanEval Shell, etc., while the 30B variants remain largely stable. The paper does not analyze this scale-dependent transfer effect.
- **Why unresolved:** Several hypotheses exist: smaller models may have less capacity to maintain multiple skills, may overfit more to the competitive programming style of the SSD training data, or may have different lock/fork distributions that make them more vulnerable to the reshaping. Without targeted experiments, the underlying cause is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled studies varying model scale while keeping architecture constant, measuring (a) diversity of generated outputs on non-code tasks, (b) changes in representation geometry (e.g., CKA between base and SSD models on different layers), and (c) mixing code with other domains during SSD to see if degradation can be mitigated.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What exactly is the learning signal when SSD training data is almost entirely low-quality (gibberish), as in the high-temperature stress test? How does the model avoid learning harmful patterns?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 4.4 shows SSD improves even when ∼62% of training samples contain no extractable code. The authors speculate that "support compression and distribution reshaping extract useful learning signals regarding token quality", but the precise signal is not identified.
- **Why unresolved:** It is counterintuitive that training on mostly invalid outputs yields improvements. Possible explanations include: (a) the model learns to suppress low-probability tails regardless of coherence, (b) even gibberish contains local token co-occurrence patterns that improve distributional shape, or (c) the effect is dominated by temperature-induced reshaping rather than data content. These are not distinguished experimentally.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Synthetic experiments where gibberish is systematically engineered (e.g., random tokens, multilingual noise, syntactically correct but semantically meaningless code) to isolate whether improvement correlates with (i) reduction of tail mass, (ii) flattening of head distributions, or (iii) preservation of high-probability correct tokens. Additionally, measuring the change in logit differences between correct and incorrect tokens at lock positions could clarify what is being learned.

## Limitations
- The rSTARcoder seed subset, its de‑duplication, prompt formatting, and response filtering thresholds are unreleased, preventing exact replication.
- Sequence packing into 65 K-token batches and checkpoint selection details are omitted.
- The evaluation harness (code extraction, sandbox, pass@k computation) and baseline decoding parameter mappings are unspecified, risking inconsistent scores.
