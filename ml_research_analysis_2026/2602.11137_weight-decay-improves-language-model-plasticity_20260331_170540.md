# Weight Decay Improves Language Model Plasticity
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11137
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11137
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Higher weight decay during pretraining improves downstream adaptability (plasticity), with optimal values (e.g., 1.0) exceeding the standard default of 0.1, even when it increases pretraining loss.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the impact of weight decay on language model plasticity—the ability to adapt to new tasks after pretraining. While pretraining hyperparameters are typically tuned for validation loss alone, the authors hypothesize that weight decay, a regularization technique, may influence downstream adaptability. Through experiments varying weight decay during the pretraining of Llama-2 and OLMo-2 models of various sizes, followed by fine-tuning on six chain-of-thought reasoning tasks, they find that larger weight decay values (up to 1.0) lead to significantly better downstream performance. Notably, models with higher pretraining loss due to stronger weight decay often outperform those with lower loss after fine-tuning. Moreover, pretraining loss shows unstable or weak correlations with downstream success, suggesting that optimizing for pretraining loss alone may be misguided. Mechanistically, the authors provide evidence that weight decay promotes linearly separable representations, reduces the rank of attention matrices, and decreases overfitting on pretraining data, which together enhance plasticity.

## Method Summary
The study involved pretraining two families of transformer-based language models (Llama-2 and OLMo-2) with a range of weight decay settings, including the standard 0.1 and higher values up to 1.0, under compute-optimal regimes. After pretraining, the models were fine-tuned on a suite of six chain-of-thought reasoning tasks to measure plasticity. Additional analyses examined representation linear separability, attention matrix rank, and overfitting to probe the underlying mechanisms.

## Key Results
- Larger weight decay (e.g., 1.0) yields better downstream performance after fine-tuning compared to the default 0.1, indicating that higher regularization improves plasticity.
- Pretraining validation loss is a poor and unstable predictor of downstream success; models with higher pretraining loss often achieve superior fine-tuning results.
- Weight decay encourages linearly separable representations, reduces the rank of attention matrices, and decreases overfitting on pretraining data, providing a mechanistic explanation for the plasticity gains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper demonstrates that weight decay acts as an effective regularizer that shapes model representations for better adaptability. Specifically, it encourages features that are more linearly separable, making it easier for a linear classifier (the fine-tuning head) to adjust to new tasks. Additionally, weight decay reduces the rank of attention weight matrices, effectively limiting model capacity and preventing overfitting to the pretraining distribution. By curbing overfitting, the model retains more flexibility to learn new patterns during downstream fine-tuning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Weight Decay (L2 Regularization)**: penalizes large weights to control model complexity and improve generalization. Why needed: Mitigates overfitting during pretraining, preserving adaptability. Quick check: Add L2 penalty to loss, monitor weight norms and validation performance.
- **Plasticity**: the capacity of a model to adapt to new tasks after initial training. Why needed: Essential for efficient transfer learning and fine-tuning. Quick check: Fine-tune on a held-out task and measure performance gain relative to a baseline.
- **Linear Separability**: property that feature representations can be separated by a linear decision boundary. Why needed: simplifies downstream adaptation, often requiring only a linear probe. Quick check: Train a linear classifier on frozen features for a target task and evaluate accuracy.
- **Attention Matrix Rank**: effective rank of the attention weight matrices, indicating the dimensionality of attention subspaces. Why needed: Lower rank reduces model capacity and may encourage more generic representations. Quick check: Compute singular values of attention matrices and measure the effective rank (e.g., using entropy or thresholding).
- **Overfitting on Pretraining Data**: when the model excessively memorizes training data, harming generalization. Why needed: Reducing overfitting keeps the model flexible for new tasks. Quick check: Compare training and validation loss gaps, or evaluate on out-of-distribution tasks.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Tokenization (external) -> Token Embedding -> Transformer Blocks (LayerNorm -> Self-Attention -> MLP) repeated N times -> Final LayerNorm -> Logits projection. Weight decay is applied to all weight parameters (embedding, Q/K/V/O, MLP linear layers); biases are typically excluded.

**Critical path**: Input tokens are embedding-looked up; each transformer block processes the sequence via multi-head self-attention (computing Q, K, V, attention scores, weighted sum) followed by a position-wise feed-forward network (two linear layers with activation). Residual connections and layer normalization surround each sublayer. The final hidden states are projected to vocabulary logits.

**Design tradeoffs**: Weight decay introduces a trade-off between pretraining loss and downstream plasticity. Stronger weight decay regularizes the model, lowering overfitting and boosting fine-tuning performance, but may increase pretraining loss and slow convergence. There is also a risk of underfitting if weight decay is too high, where the model cannot capture necessary pretraining patterns. The choice of weight decay interacts with model scale, dataset size, and optimizer settings.

**Failure signatures**: 
- Excessive weight decay leads to underfitting: high training loss, poor representation quality, and stagnant learning curves.
- Insufficient weight decay causes overfitting: low training loss but high validation loss and degraded downstream performance after fine-tuning.
- Extremely high weight decay can cause weight collapse (values near zero) and gradient vanishing, halting training.

## Limitations
- Findings are based on only two model families (Llama-2, OLMo-2) and six chain-of-thought tasks, limiting generalizability across architectures and task types.
- The optimal weight decay exceeding 0.1 was observed in compute-optimal settings; its validity for other training regimes and model scales remains uncertain.
- The proposed mechanisms (linear separability, attention rank, overfitting reduction) are supported by correlations, not causal experiments.

## Confidence
- Higher weight decay improves plasticity: **Medium**
- Optimal weight decay >0.1 (e.g., 1.0): **Medium**
- Pretraining loss is not predictive of downstream success: **Low**
- Mechanistic explanations (linear separability, reduced attention rank, less overfitting): **Low**

## Next Checks
1. Test weight decay on a broader set of downstream tasks (e.g., GLUE, SuperGLUE, diverse reasoning benchmarks) to evaluate generality across task types.
2. Replicate the study with additional architectures (e.g., GPT-style decoder models, encoder‑decoder T5) and larger scales to confirm robustness.
3. Perform targeted interventions (e.g., directly regularizing representation similarity or attention entropy) to determine whether the proposed mechanisms are necessary for the observed plasticity gains.
