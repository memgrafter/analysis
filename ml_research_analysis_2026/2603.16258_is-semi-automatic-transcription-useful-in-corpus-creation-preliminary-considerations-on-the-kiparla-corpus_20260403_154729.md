# Is Semi-Automatic Transcription Useful in Corpus Creation? Preliminary Considerations on the KIParla Corpus

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16258
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16258
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Integrating OpenAI Whisper into the KIParla transcription workflow significantly increased transcription speed (effect size comparable to transcriber expertise) but produced heterogeneous effects on accuracy and led to coarser segmentation of discourse units.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluated the impact of incorporating an off-the-shelf ASR system (OpenAI Whisper large) into the manual transcription workflow for the KIParla corpus of spoken Italian. Eleven transcribers (4 experts, 7 novices) transcribed identical 10-minute audio segments from three conversation types using both manual and ASR-assisted methods. Results showed a substantial and consistent increase in transcription speed with ASR assistance. However, accuracy improvements, measured by Word Error Rate (WER), were uneven across individuals and conversation types, with novices benefiting more on average. Furthermore, ASR-assisted transcription systematically produced fewer but longer transcription units, indicating a coarser approach to segmenting speech into interactionally relevant units. The findings suggest ASR can accelerate corpus creation, particularly for less experienced annotators, but its integration requires careful consideration of how it influences fundamental transcription conventions and discourse representation.

## Method Summary
The experiment used a two-phase, within-subjects design where each of 11 transcribers worked on two different 10-minute audio extracts from the KIParla corpus. In Phase 1 (manual), transcribers created transcriptions from scratch in ELAN using Jefferson notation. In Phase 2 (ASR-assisted), they corrected pre-generated Whisper `.srt` files (diarization manually assigned) in the same interface. All outputs were pre-processed (orthographic normalization, bracket balancing) and evaluated against a gold standard. Performance metrics included: 1) transcription speed (seconds per 30-minute interval), 2) WER from word-level alignment, 3) overlap annotation error severity, and 4) segmentation granularity (count and length of Transcription Units). Effects were analyzed using Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) with fixed effects for phase, expertise, and data type, a smooth term for time (minutes), and a random intercept for transcriber.

## Key Results
- **Transcription speed increased significantly** with ASR assistance (β=27.11, p<0.001), an effect size comparable to having an expert transcriber instead of a novice.
- **WER improvements were heterogeneous:** 7 of 11 transcribers had lower error rates with ASR, with novices showing the largest average reduction (e.g., one novice: 39.37% → 29.45%). Expert results were mixed.
- **Segmentation became coarser:** ASR-assisted transcripts contained significantly fewer Transcription Units (TUs) with greater average length, suggesting transcribers adopted a less fine-grained segmentation strategy when editing ASR output compared to transcribing from scratch.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** ASR-assisted transcription significantly increases transcription speed.
- **Mechanism:** The ASR system generates a first-pass textual draft. The human task shifts from producing all text from auditory input to editing and verifying the draft, which is cognitively less demanding and faster for a given audio duration.
- **Evidence anchors:** GAMM showed a significant positive phase effect on activity (seconds transcribed per interval, β=27.11, p<.001). Speed gains were consistent across most transcribers and conversation types.
- **Break condition:** If ASR WER is too high (e.g., >30%), the editing burden may exceed manual transcription time.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The impact of ASR on accuracy (WER) is heterogeneous and depends on transcriber experience and conversation type.
- **Mechanism:** ASR provides a lexical baseline that interacts with the transcriber's existing strategies and mental models. Novices, with fewer entrenched habits, may more readily correct or accept the baseline. Experts may be hindered if the ASR's representation of disfluencies or overlaps conflicts with their trained conventions.
- **Evidence anchors:** WER decreased for 7/11 transcribers with ASR; novices showed larger average reductions. Effects varied by conversation type (e.g., free conversation vs. L2 interview).
- **Break condition:** Low trust in ASR or mismatch between ASR output and transcription conventions could lead a transcriber to effectively ignore the draft, negating accuracy benefits.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** ASR-assisted transcription leads to coarser segmentation (fewer, longer Transcription Units).
- **Mechanism:** Off-the-shelf ASR models like Whisper are optimized for continuous, monologic text and produce output with minimal prosodic or interactional segmentation. Human transcribers inherit this "chunking" bias when editing, are less likely to split TUs at turn boundaries, pauses, or other interactional loci compared to segmenting auditorily from scratch.
- **Evidence anchors:** GAMM revealed a significant phase effect: ASR phase decreased number of TUs (p=.032) and increased average TU length (p<.001). Delta analysis showed relative undersegmentation in ASR-assisted transcripts.
- **Break condition:** Explicit retraining to disregard ASR segmentation and re-segment de novo could mitigate this but would likely reduce the speed benefit.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Word Error Rate (WER)**
  - **Why needed here:** Primary metric for assessing lexical accuracy against a gold standard. Calculated as (Substitutions + Deletions + Insertions) / Total reference words. Its limitations for conversational data (e.g., penalizing annotation decisions like omitting "uh") are critical for interpretation.
  - **Quick check:** Why might a lower WER in an ASR-assisted transcript not always indicate a "better" transcription for conversational corpus purposes?
- **Concept: Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs)**
  - **Why needed here:** Used to model non-linear effects (e.g., time on task) and account for repeated measures from the same transcriber (random intercept), isolating the effect of the workflow phase while controlling for individual variability.
  - **Quick check:** What does a significant random intercept for "transcriber" in the GAMM tell us about the transcription process?
- **Concept: Transcription Units (TUs) & Jefferson Notation**
  - **Why needed here:** TUs are the fundamental segmented unit in the KIParla workflow. Jefferson notation encodes prosodic and interactional features (overlaps, pauses, intonation). The study's findings on segmentation and overlap errors are measured against these conventions.
  - **Quick check:** How does the paper define a "severe" overlap annotation error, and what does the change in its prevalence between manual and ASR-assisted workflows suggest about the tool's impact on novice vs. expert annotators?
- **Concept: Diarization & Speaker Assignment**
  - **Why needed here:** Whisper's `.srt` output includes speaker labels. The study required manual assignment of these to correct speaker identities in ELAN. Diarization quality is a prerequisite for efficient correction.
  - **Quick check:** Why was manual speaker assignment necessary even with Whisper's `.srt` files, and how might this step influence overall workflow speed?
- **Concept: Gold Standard & Alignment**
  - **Why needed here:** The gold standard is the expert-revised reference transcription. Alignment (Needleman-Wunsch) between participant output and gold is necessary for computing WER and other comparative metrics, requiring exact time-aligned matching.
  - **Quick check:** What challenges arise when aligning a transcript with a gold standard for conversational speech, particularly regarding disfluencies and non-standard words?

## Architecture Onboarding
The transcription pipeline integrates an ASR engine with a manual correction and annotation environment.
Input (10-min ELAN audio) -> ASR Engine (Whisper large) -> SRT Files (per speaker) -> Correction Interface (ELAN with Jefferson notation) -> Output Transcription -> Alignment/Comparison -> Gold Standard -> Analysis (WER, TU metrics, GAMMs)
- **Critical path:** The quality of the initial Whisper `.srt` output is the pivotal input. Low lexical accuracy, poor speaker diarization, or missing overlap information directly increase correction time, may bias segmentation, and can limit final accuracy gains.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Speed vs. Granularity: ASR boosts speed but promotes coarser, meaning-based segmentation at the potential expense of fine-grained interactional analysis.
  - Expertise Leverage: The tool appears to level performance for novices (reducing error variance) but provides inconsistent benefit for experts, who may be hindered by mismatched transcription conventions.
  - Metric vs. Qualitative Fit: Optimizing for low WER might degrade other essential corpus features like precise overlap timing or disfluency marking.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1.  WER > 30% on key extracts (per Gaur et al., 2016), indicating ASR output is too noisy for efficient correction.
  2.  Significant increase in "severe" overlap errors for a transcriber group, suggesting ASR's lack of overlap annotation causes annotators to miss or misplace them.
  3.  GAMM shows a strong negative effect of "ASR phase" on number of TUs combined with poor inter-annotator agreement, indicating harmful homogenization of discourse structure.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Replication with Balanced Design:** Repeat the study where each transcriber works on the *same* conversation type (e.g., two free conversations) in both phases, controlling for conversation-type difficulty.
  2.  **Fine-tuning Impact:** Fine-tune Whisper on a subset of existing KIParla Gold transcripts and re-run the experiment to measure if domain adaptation reduces segmentation coarsening and improves WER uniformly.
  3.  **Process Tracing:** Conduct think-aloud protocols or detailed post-task interviews with a subset of transcribers to understand *how* they edit ASR output, specifically probing decisions about splitting TUs and annotating overlaps.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do the effects of ASR-assisted transcription (speed, accuracy, segmentation) vary when evaluated on a larger, more balanced dataset across diverse conversation types?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors conclude results are exploratory due to the limited size and balance of the dataset, noting observations may reflect properties of individual recordings rather than systematic differences across interactional settings. Future work will expand the dataset.
- **Why unresolved:** The experiment used only 7 extracts (2 free conversation, 2 interviews, 3 L2-interviews), with transcribers often working on different conversation types between phases, preventing robust generalization about how conversation type moderates ASR's impact.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What constitutes a more valid and reliable operationalization of "transcriber expertise" for corpus transcription tasks, and how does it modulate the benefits of ASR assistance?
- **Basis in paper:** The "Limitations" section explicitly critiques the binary expertise classification based solely on minutes of prior transcription (45-minute threshold), noting it may not capture actual skill differences related to complexity handling, accuracy-speed trade-offs, or annotation consistency.
- **Why unresolved:** The study found mixed effects of expertise on WER and no significant effect in GAMMs for most structural output variables, possibly due to the crude measure. The true relationship between nuanced expertise dimensions and ASR utility remains unknown.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does the practice of assigning transcribers different conversation types across manual and ASR-assisted phases confound the comparison of workflow effects, and what is the net impact on measured transcription speed and accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** The "Limitations" section acknowledges that transcribers did not work on the same type of conversation in both phases, which "inevitably limited direct comparative analysis." Only one transcriber (A) worked on the same module in both conditions.
- **Why unresolved:** This design reduced learning/fatigue bias but introduces a confound: observed phase differences could be due to inherent difficulty differences between the assigned extracts rather than the workflow itself. The isolated effect of the pipeline is uncertain.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why does the introduction of an ASR step primarily influence the granularity of transcription units (number and length of TUs) but not the overall quantity of tokens or duration transcribed, and what does this imply about transcriber cognitive strategies?
- **Basis in paper:** GAMM results show the experimental phase is a significant predictor for number of TUs and average TU length, but not for total tokens or duration. This suggests ASR changes *how* transcribers segment speech, not *how much* they transcribe.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper speculates that manual transcription encourages "finer-grained segmentation" possibly due to conscious structuring, but lacks qualitative data on transcriber decision-making. The cognitive or strategic reasons for adopting coarser segments when editing ASR output are not investigated.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How do inherent biases in off-the-shelf ASR output (e.g., normalization of disfluencies, coarser segmentation, lexical substitutions, hallucination patterns) specifically affect the type and rate of errors introduced or corrected by human transcribers in conversational data?
- **Basis in paper:** The qualitative content comparison documents systematic differences: Whisper normalizes spellings, elides vowels differently, misrecognizes proper names, produces "content approximation" errors, and adds/skips short tokens. Transcribers consistently corrected some error types (non-words) but accepted others (lexical approximations).
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis is descriptive and on a small, manually selected subset (~600 tokens). It is unclear how these ASR output characteristics propagate to final transcript quality, whether transcribers develop compensatory strategies for specific error classes, or if certain biases are irredeemable for conversational analysis.

## Limitations
- **ASR configuration ambiguity:** Exact Whisper variant (`large-v2/v3`), language setting, and inference parameters are unreported, affecting output quality and reproducibility.
- **Transcriber expertise operationalization:** Binary split (≥45 mins vs. novices) lacks quality thresholds; unknown if experts had formal training, limiting generalization of "expertise" effects.
- **Extract balancing:** Different audio extracts per phase confound workflow effects with content difficulty; only one transcriber (A) used same conversation type in both phases.

## Confidence
- **Speed increase:** High – Robust GAMM effect (β=27.11, p<0.001) across transcribers; metric directly measured.
- **Accuracy heterogeneity:** Medium – WER improvements vary by transcriber and conversation type; claims rely on within-subject comparisons with potential extract confounds.
- **Segmentation coarsening:** High – Consistent GAMM and delta results show fewer/longer TUs in ASR phase; effect size clearly reported.

## Next Checks
1.  **Controlled extract replication:** Repeat experiment with *identical* 10-minute audio for each transcriber in both phases (counterbalanced order) to isolate workflow effect from content difficulty.
2.  **Domain-adapted ASR test:** Fine-tune Whisper on a subset of KIParla Gold transcripts; re-run experiment to measure whether improved lexical accuracy reduces segmentation coarsening and stabilizes WER gains.
3.  **Process tracing study:** Conduct think-aloud protocols with 3–4 transcribers (mixed expertise) during ASR-assisted correction to directly observe segmentation decisions and overlap annotation strategies; code for reliance on ASR chunking vs. auditory cues.
