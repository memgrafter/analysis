# Attention at Rest Stays at Rest: Breaking Visual Inertia for Cognitive Hallucination Mitigation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01989
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01989
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Training‑free IVE reduces cognitive hallucinations and boosts accuracy on Reefknot, POPE, and MME across multiple MLLMs with negligible overhead.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies visual inertia—persistent, static attention to fixed regions during decoding—as a key cause of cognitive hallucinations in multimodal large language models (MLLMs). Existing hallucination mitigation methods overlook this attention pathology. The authors propose Inertia‑aware Visual Excitation (IVE), a training‑free method that dynamically reshapes attention during autoregressive generation. IVE uses trend‑guided token selection to identify emergent tokens (those deviating from historical attention trends) and applies an inertia‑aware penalty to downweight persistently attended tokens, reallocating attention to emergent ones. Experiments on Reefknot, MME, POPE, and MMBench with three leading MLLMs (LLaVA‑1.5, InstructBLIP, Qwen2.5‑VL‑Instruct) show IVE significantly reduces both cognitive and perceptual hallucinations. For instance, on Reefknot with LLaVA‑1.5, the cognitive hallucination rate drops by 1.92% to 32.07% and response accuracy rises by 1.84% to 65.80%. IVE also improves POPE Random F1 by 9.61%, consistently outperforming state‑of‑the‑art training‑free methods while adding negligible latency.

## Method Summary
IVE is a training‑free attention modulation applied during decoding. At each generation step, it collects the model’s per‑token attention distribution over visual tokens, then updates an exponential moving average (EMA) of historical attention per token. The excitation score for each token is computed as the normalized deviation of current attention from the EMA. Tokens with high excitation are classified as emergent (E_t), while tokens with high historical average but low excitation are considered inertial (I_t). IVE then applies a penalty to inertial tokens that scales with their cumulative selection count, reducing their attention proportionally. The forfeited attention mass is redistributed to emergent tokens weighted by their excitation scores. This process breaks the static attention pattern (visual inertia) and promotes dynamic integration of relational cues, thereby mitigating cognitive hallucinations. IVE operates across all transformer layers without additional training and with minimal computational overhead.

## Key Results
- On Reefknot with LLaVA‑1.5, cognitive hallucination rate reduced to 32.07% (1.92% absolute drop) and response accuracy increased to 65.80% (1.84% absolute gain).
- On POPE Random, IVE improves F1 score by 9.61% compared to baseline.
- IVE consistently outperforms state‑of‑the‑art training‑free methods (e.g., MemVR) across multiple models and benchmarks, while adding negligible latency and memory.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Visual inertia causes cognitive hallucinations by locking attention
- **Claim:** Visual inertia—persistent, static attention to fixed regions during decoding—causes cognitive hallucinations by preventing dynamic integration of inter‑object relational cues.
- **Mechanism:** During autoregressive generation, attention distributions over visual tokens fail to shift sufficiently across decoding steps, locking onto historically dominant regions. This static pattern limits the model’s ability to aggregate cross‑object evidence needed for compositional reasoning, leading to fabricated relationships.
- **Core assumption:** Cognitive inference requires temporal dynamism in visual attention; persistent focus on the same tokens indicates inadequate relational processing.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] “visual inertia … remaining largely static once settled during early decoding steps and failing to support the compositional understanding required for cognitive inference.”
  - [section 3.2] Quantifies visual activeness via Wasserstein distance between consecutive attention maps and shows lower activeness correlates with cognitive hallucinations.
  - [corpus] Neighboring work (e.g., “Conscious Gaze”) identifies related attention drift issues, but IVE uniquely targets inertia via temporal modeling.
- **Break condition:** If attention activeness remains high regardless of intervention, the identified inertia may not be the primary bottleneck for cognitive errors in a given model or dataset.

### Mechanism 2: EMA deviation identifies emergent vs. inertial tokens
- **Claim:** Trend‑guided token selection via EMA distinguishes emergent tokens (dynamically relevant for current reasoning) from inertia tokens (persistently over‑attended).
- **Mechanism:** For each decoding step, an exponential moving average (EMA) of historical attention weights per token establishes a baseline. The relative excitation score `S_t,j = (a_t,j - ˜a_t-1,j) / sqrt(˜a_t-1,j(1-˜a_t-1,j))` measures deviation from this trend. Tokens exceeding threshold `τ` are emergent; those with high historical average but low excitation are inertia tokens.
- **Core assumption:** Deviation from smoothed historical trends signals newly relevant visual regions for relational inference, while stable high attention indicates inertia.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [section 4.1] Equations (7)–(9) define EMA and selection criteria; Figure 4 illustrates the partitioning.
  - [section 3.2] Figure 3 shows naive amplification reduces activeness, motivating dynamic selection over static amplification.
  - [corpus] While other methods use attention pruning or contrastive decoding, IVE’s EMA‑based temporal deviation metric is novel for identifying inertial tokens.
- **Break condition:** If the EMA smoothing coefficient `γ` or threshold `τ` cannot be reliably set across diverse MLLMs, the selection may misclassify tokens, harming performance.

### Mechanism 3: Inertia‑aware penalty reallocates attention to emergent tokens
- **Claim:** An inertia‑aware penalty progressively attenuates attention to persistently inertial tokens and reallocates it to emergent tokens, breaking static patterns and promoting compositional grounding.
- **Mechanism:** For tokens classified as inertial at step `t`, a penalty factor `a'_t,j = a_t,j * (1 - α * C_t,j/(t-1))` reduces their attention proportionally to their historical selection count `C_t,j`. The subtracted attention mass `R_t` is redistributed to emergent tokens in `E_t` weighted by their excitation scores `S_t,j`.
- **Core assumption:** Cumulative selection count effectively captures persistence; reallocating penalized mass to dynamically emerging tokens enhances cross‑region evidence integration.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.2] Equations (10)–(14) specify penalty computation and reallocation; Figure 4 shows the attention flow.
  - [section 5.2] IVE outperforms amplification (PAI) on cognitive benchmarks, demonstrating the penalty’s efficacy.
  - [corpus] Methods like OPERA use over‑trust penalties but not specifically for inertia; VASparse prunes tokens without dynamic reallocation.
- **Break condition:** If reallocating penalized attention to emergent tokens introduces noise or overemphasizes spurious correlations, cognitive performance may degrade, particularly in scenes with ambiguous relational cues.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  EMA Tracker → Excitation Scorer → Token Partitioner → Inertia Counter → Penalty & Redistribution Module → Modified attention fed back to generation.

- **Critical path:**
  1. At each decoding step `t`, retrieve the current token‑to‑token attention matrix `A_t` from the LLM’s self‑attention layers.
  2. Aggregate over attention heads to obtain per‑visual‑token attention distribution `a_t,j`.
  3. Update EMA for all tokens: `˜a_t-1,j = γ ˜a_t-2,j + (1-γ) a_t-1,j`.
  4. Compute excitation score `S_t,j` and classify tokens into emergent set `E_t` (high `S_t,j`) and inertial set `I_t` (high historical attention but low `S_t,j`).
  5. For tokens in `I_t`, increment persistence count `C_t,j` and compute penalized attention `a'_t,j = a_t,j (1 - α C_t,j/(t-1))`.
  6. Compute total penalized mass `R_t = sum_{j∈I_t} (a_t,j - a'_t,j)`. Distribute `R_t` to tokens in `E_t` weighted by `S_t,j`, yielding redistributed attention `a''_t,j`.
  7. Replace the visual‑token attention slice in `A_t` with `a''_t,j` before computing the next‑token distribution.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Hyperparameter sensitivity:* `τ` (selection threshold) and `α` (penalty intensity) require per‑model tuning. Too high `α` risks suppressing necessary attention; too low `τ` misclassifies tokens.
  - *EMA smoothing (`γ`):* Balances responsiveness vs. stability. Low `γ` makes selection noisy; high `γ` may miss genuine emergent patterns.
  - *Layer application:* IVE applies modulation to attention aggregated over all heads without per‑head tuning, trading precision for simplicity and generalizability.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Degradation on perceptual benchmarks (POPE) if emergent token selection becomes too aggressive, removing attention from object‑level features.
  - No improvement on cognitive tasks if the base model’s attention is already highly dynamic (low inertia baseline).
  - Increased latency if EMA updates and redistributions are implemented inefficiently across all LLM layers.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Visual Activeness Baseline:** Compute per‑layer pairwise Wasserstein distances for the vanilla model on a cognitive hallucination subset (e.g., Reefknot Y/N questions). Confirm lower activeness in hallucinatory vs. correct responses.
  2. **Ablation of Penalty (`α=0`):** Run IVE with `α=0` (selection only) vs. full IVE on Reefknot. Isolate the contribution of the inertia‑aware penalty.
  3. **EMA Sensitivity:** Vary `γ` (e.g., 0.08, 0.10, 0.12) on LLaVA‑1.5 across cognitive (Reefknot) and perceptual (POPE) benchmarks to identify stable regions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **O1:** Is visual inertia a universal factor in cognitive hallucinations across all MLLM architectures? *Basis:* The study evaluates only three 7B‑parameter MLLMs. *Resolution:* Measure inertia and IVE performance across a broad spectrum of models varying size, vision encoder, and LLM design.
- **O2:** Can IVE’s hyperparameters be set automatically without manual tuning? *Basis:* γ and τ are fixed (0.1 and 3.0), and α is tuned per model/benchmark. *Resolution:* Develop an adaptive algorithm that dynamically selects hyperparameters based on input or model state.
- **O3:** Would integrating an inertia‑aware penalty into the training process yield more robust or efficient mitigation? *Basis:* IVE is strictly training‑free; no trainable variant explored. *Resolution:* Introduce a regularization term that penalizes visual inertia during fine‑tuning and compare.
- **O4:** Does visual inertia also contribute to perceptual hallucinations, and can IVE’s gains on perceptual benchmarks be explained by its effect on inertia? *Basis:* IVE improves POPE scores but inertia analysis is limited to cognitive tasks. *Resolution:* Compute visual activeness on perceptual hallucination cases and isolate IVE’s impact on attention dynamics.

## Limitations
- Attention extraction scope (layers/heads) unspecified; could critically influence results.
- Hyperparameters τ and α require per‑model tuning; sensitivity not thoroughly analyzed.
- Possible cognitive‑perceptual trade‑off: excessive inertia penalty may harm perception.
