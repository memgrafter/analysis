# CompilerKV: Risk-Adaptive KV Compression via Offline Experience Compilation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08686  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08686  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Recovers 97.7% of full KV cache performance and gains up to +5.2 points over the strongest SOTA baseline on LongBench with a 512‑token budget, while reducing end‑to‑end latency by up to 60%.

## Executive Summary
CompilerKV addresses the instability of KV cache compression under tight memory budgets by reformulating the problem as an irreversible one‑shot decision made at prompt prefill. It compiles offline experience into two reusable decision tables— a Head Heterogeneity Table that assigns reliability weights to each attention head, and a Risk‑Adaptive Threshold Gating that sets per‑prompt retention thresholds based on attention entropy and local perplexity. These tables are learned via offline contextual bandits (Conservative Q‑Learning) from a diverse calibration corpus. At inference, the pipeline uses stabilized token utility scores that aggregate attention over a window and normalize value magnitudes, then applies head‑reliability scaling and dynamic threshold gating to select tokens under per‑layer budgets. On LongBench, CompilerKV recovers 97.7% of full‑KV performance at a 512‑token budget and improves over the strongest baseline by up to 5.2 points, while also reducing end‑to‑end latency by up to 60%.

## Method Summary
CompilerKV operates in two phases. During an offline compilation phase, it uses a large calibration corpus to train two decision tables via offline contextual bandits (CQL). The first table, *Head Heterogeneity*, learns a reliability multiplier for each attention head (per layer) by treating the head as a state and the reward as the negative NLL difference between compressed and full generation over a short continuation. The second table, *Risk‑Adaptive Threshold*, learns a per‑layer retention threshold that depends on discretized risk signals—attention entropy (structural uncertainty) and local perplexity (semantic uncertainty)—with a reward that penalizes budget violations. During online inference, the prefill pass computes a stabilized token utility: base score = window‑cumulative global mean attention × normalized value norm. This base score is scaled by the head reliability weight and max‑pooled across heads to yield a final layer‑wise score. The threshold table is queried using the current layer’s risk bin; tokens with scores above the threshold are retained, with a Top‑B (per‑layer budget) cut applied if necessary. The selected key‑value vectors form the compressed cache.

## Key Results
- Under a strict 512‑token budget, CompilerKV recovers 97.7% of full‑KV performance on average across four LLMs on LongBench.
- It gains up to +5.2 points over the strongest existing baseline, demonstrating significant accuracy improvement under tight budgets.
- End‑to‑end generation latency is reduced by up to 60%, with graceful degradation even under extreme compression (64‑256 tokens).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Stabilized Token Utility Estimation
- **Claim:** Provides noise‑resilient token importance scores by aggregating attention over a window and normalizing value magnitudes, mitigating scale bias and transient spikes.
- **Mechanism:** Computes global mean attention across heads/layers to obtain a consensus relevance; then accumulates that attention over an observation window to get window‑cumulative α; finally multiplies by a sample‑relative value norm ρ (normalized by sequence mean). The product u = α·ρ yields a stable base utility.
- **Core assumption:** Raw per‑head, per‑step attention is noisy; absolute value norms have scale bias; a single‑step snapshot cannot capture durable influence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] The abstract states that CompilerKV “injects stabilization priors at prefill”.
  - [section] Section 4.2 details global mean attention (Eq.6), window‑cumulative α (Eq.7), relative norm ρ, and their unification into u (Eq.8).
  - [corpus] The neighboring corpus contains papers on dynamic thresholds but not the exact utility formulation; evidence for this specific mechanism is weak.
- **Break condition:** If the observation window size W is < 1 or the value‑norm normalizer µ is zero, the score becomes undefined or degenerate; also if all tokens receive identical scores, the ranking collapses.

### Mechanism 2: Head Heterogeneity Table
- **Claim:** Compiles head‑specific reliability weights via offline contextual bandits, enabling weighted max‑pooling over heads to protect tokens singled out by reliable retrieval heads.
- **Mechanism:** Offline RL treats each head (l,h) as a state with per‑layer budget Bl, action space W of discretized multipliers, and reward as negative NLL difference (Eq.2). Using CQL, a Q‑function is learned; the deterministic table W_head[l,h] = argmax_w Q is extracted. At inference, for each token t the utility from each head is scaled by its weight and the maximum across heads yields the final score (Eq.10).
- **Core assumption:** Attention heads are functionally heterogeneous; some consistently retrieve relevant context (high reliability) while others are noisy; reliability can be approximated offline and transferred across prompts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Head Heterogeneity Table, learned via offline contextual bandits, which assigns head‑specific reliability weights to govern functional differences across attention heads explicitly.”
  - [section] Section 4.3 gives the full MDP definition and the weighted max‑pooling inference rule.
  - [corpus] “ZigZagKV: Dynamic KV Cache Compression for Long‑context Modeling based on Layer Uncertainty” and similar works recognize head‑level variation, providing indirect support, though the offline bandit formulation is novel.
- **Break condition:** If the calibration corpus lacks diversity, the learned weights may misrepresent head reliability on novel prompts; also if a head weight is suppressed too aggressively, it may remove tokens that are critical for rare query patterns.

### Mechanism 3: Risk‑Adaptive Threshold Gating
- **Claim:** Transforms prompt‑level risk (attention entropy and perplexity) into a dynamic retention threshold, which is then combined with a budget‑correction step to strictly respect the KV budget.
- **Mechanism:** Two risk signals are computed on the observation window: R_struct = entropy of aggregated mean attention; R_sem = exp(−avg NLL). They are discretized into bins (bent, bppl). A second offline RL (CQL) compiles a lookup table T_gate[l, bent, bppl] that outputs τ ∈ [0.8,1.0]. For layer l, candidate tokens with ˆu(l)_t ≥ τ are kept; if too many, a Top‑Bl cut is applied, otherwise all are retained (Eqs.15–16). The reward includes a budget‑violation penalty (λ).
- **Core assumption:** Prompts vary in compressibility; static thresholds cause tail failures; entropy and perplexity jointly capture structural and semantic difficulty, correlating with needed retention.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Risk‑Adaptive Threshold Gating mechanism that jointly models attention entropy and local perplexity, transforming prompt‑level risk into deployable retention thresholds.”
  - [section] Section 4.4 defines R_struct (Eqs.11–12), R_sem (Eq.13), the MDP for the threshold table, and the gating+budget‑correction logic.
  - [corpus] The nearby paper “Adaptive KV‑Cache Compression without Manually Setting Budget” also explores dynamic thresholding, indicating community recognition of the need; however, the exact joint signal combination is unique to CompilerKV.
- **Break condition:** If risk signals are poorly estimated (e.g., noisy attention due to short window) or the binning misclassifies, τ may be mis‑chosen; the hard Top‑Bl cut can discard useful tokens if τ is set too low, or fail to meet budget if τ is too high.

## Foundational Learning

**KV cache and memory scaling**  
*Why needed:* The entire compression problem stems from the linear growth of the key‑value cache with sequence length; understanding that each token stores a key and value per layer is essential.  
*Quick check:* “Why does the memory consumption of the KV cache increase linearly with the input sequence length in a Transformer decoder?”

**Offline Reinforcement Learning (Contextual Bandits)**  
*Why needed:* CompilerKV learns its head reliability and risk threshold policies from a static calibration dataset without online interaction, requiring conservative algorithms like CQL to avoid distribution shift.  
*Quick check:* “What is the key difference between online RL and offline RL, and why is a conservative penalty necessary when learning from pre‑collected data?”

**Attention entropy and perplexity as risk indicators**  
*Why needed:* The risk‑adaptive gating uses attention entropy to capture structural dispersion and perplexity to reflect semantic uncertainty; both predict how aggressively the cache can be compressed.  
*Quick check:* “How does high attention entropy suggest a higher compression risk, and why might perplexity be complementary?”

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Prefill engine → Stabilized Utility module → (Head Heterogeneity Table, Head‑Aware Pooling) → (Risk‑Adaptive Threshold LUT, risk computation) → Gating & Budget Correction → KV Gather → compressed KV

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Prefill forward pass obtains K, V, attention.  
  2. Compute base utility u (global mean attention, window α, relative ρ).  
  3. Scale u by W_head[l,h] and max‑pool across heads → ˆu(l).  
  4. Compute risk signals (entropy, perplexity), discretize.  
  5. Look up τ = T_gate[l, bent, bppl].  
  6. Build candidate mask m(l) = (ˆu(l) ≥ τ).  
  7. If |m| > B_l, select Top‑B_l by ˆu(l); else keep all candidates.  
  8. Gather selected K/V into compressed cache.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Memory vs. expressiveness: Finer risk binning improves adaptation but increases table size and may cause data sparsity.  
  - Prefill overhead vs. decode speed: Extra computation at prefill is amortized by faster decoding due to smaller KV.  
  - Conservative τ range [0.8,1.0] prevents degenerate thresholds but may limit flexibility on very low‑risk prompts.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Sharp accuracy drops on high‑entropy or high‑perplexity prompts suggest risk signals or τ are mis‑calibrated.  
  - Head table values near zero for many heads indicates over‑suppression; verify that some heads retain positive weights.  
  - Candidate set size consistently near B_l shows thresholds too high; always < B_l indicates thresholds too low (wasted budget).

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **LongBench main evaluation** (Table 1): Under a strict 512‑token budget, CompilerKV achieved the highest average scores across four LLMs, recovering 97.7% of FullKV performance.  
  2. **Compression robustness analysis** (Figures 2,3): Across varying KV sizes (64–1024 tokens) and needle‑in‑a‑haystack retrieval, CompilerKV degraded gracefully and preserved retrieval patterns where baselines collapsed.  
  3. **Ablation study** (Table 2): Removing Risk‑Adaptive Threshold caused the largest drop (~3 points), confirming its critical role; Head Reliability and Stabilized Utility also contributed consistent gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Transferability across architectures:** The paper’s conclusion mentions investigating the transferability of reliability tables across model families as future work. Currently, tables are compiled per model; no systematic evaluation of cross‑architecture transfer exists. Resolving would require testing tables from one model family on another and analyzing which components generalize.
- **Sensitivity to calibration corpus:** The method relies entirely on offline experience from a fixed calibration corpus Dcal (50K prompts). The paper does not assess robustness to changes in corpus size, domain, or risk distribution. Out‑of‑distribution prompts may misalign learned policies, a critical risk for production. Evidence would come from varying Dcal along those axes and measuring performance on out‑of‑distribution benchmarks.
- **Sufficiency of risk signals:** Risk‑Adaptive Threshold Gating uses attention entropy and local perplexity as joint risk indicators. The paper does not compare against alternative features (e.g., attention variance, syntactic complexity). It is unclear if these capture all relevant failure modes. Evidence would involve augmenting or replacing these signals within the same framework and evaluating on diverse tasks.
- **Practicality of theoretical bound:** Theorem 4.1 provides a stability bound for the approximation error, but the proof relies on independence assumptions that may not hold in real prompts. The bound is not validated empirically, especially under extreme compression. Resolving requires measuring actual vs. predicted error and refining theory.
- **Extension to streaming generation:** The current method assumes a static complete prefill; streaming token‑by‑token arrival breaks the one‑shot assumption. The paper identifies this as future work but offers no solution. Evidence would be a streaming variant that maintains stability and evaluates on streaming benchmarks.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter values (observation window size, risk bin edges, CQL settings, budget penalty β) are not fully disclosed, hindering reproducibility.
- Composition and size of the calibration corpus Dcal are not fully detailed, risking overfitting and poor generalization to out‑of‑distribution prompts.
- Prefill overhead from additional computations (stabilized utility, risk signals) is not quantified, which may affect deployment feasibility.
