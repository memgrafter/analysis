# MeetBench-XL: Calibrated Multi-Dimensional Evaluation and Learned Dual-Policy Agents for Real-Time Meetings
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03285
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03285
- Reference count: 1
- Primary result: MeetMaster-XL achieves 81.2% routing accuracy, 30% lower latency on simple queries, 12.4% higher quality on complex tasks versus single-model baselines, with 4.2/5 user satisfaction and 18× cost savings in a 4‑week deployment.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the mismatch between real enterprise meeting workflows and existing benchmarks/agents. It introduces MeetBench‑XL, a human‑calibrated multi‑dimensional evaluation protocol, and MeetMaster‑XL, a learned dual‑policy agent. Core contributions are: (1) MeetAll, a 140‑hour bilingual dataset with 1,180 enterprise‑validated questions spanning four complexity dimensions; (2) MeetBench‑XL, a five‑dimensional evaluator with Pearson r=0.78 alignment to human judgment; (3) MeetMaster‑XL, a system that jointly optimizes fast/slow routing and tool invocation via offline RL. Results show improved accuracy, latency, and quality while running on a single RTX 4090, with strong user satisfaction and cost savings in deployment.

## Method Summary
The task is to build an enterprise meeting assistant that handles heterogeneous queries under strict latency and cost constraints. Inputs include streaming ASR transcripts (with timestamps and speaker tags), optional audio features, and an optional knowledge base or web search. MeetMaster‑XL employs a fast multimodal Talker for simple queries and a slow Planner with tool‑augmented reasoning (KB retrieval, cross‑meeting aggregation, web) for complex queries. Training proceeds in stages: (1) fine‑tune the Planner on four‑axis complexity labels; (2) train a lightweight router (2‑layer MLP, ~300K parameters) via supervised learning on 1,280 labeled examples; (3) collect a replay buffer of 8,500 interaction tuples; (4) refine both router and tool policy using offline reinforcement learning (Conservative Q‑Learning) to maximize a composite objective J = α·TaskSuccess − β·Latency − γ·TokenCost (α=1, β=0.05, γ=0.01). Evaluation uses MeetBench‑XL, where a calibrated LLM judge (Prometheus2) scores five enterprise‑prioritized dimensions after isotonic regression on 150 human‑rated samples.

## Key Results
- MeetMaster‑XL achieves 81.2% routing accuracy on the test set.
- Simple queries experience a 30% reduction in P50 latency compared to single‑model baselines.
- Complex tasks see a 12.4% improvement in overall MeetBench‑XL quality score.
- A 4‑week deployment with 35 users yields 4.2/5 satisfaction and 18× cost savings over cloud APIs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Grounded, enterprise‑validated data collection
**Claim:** The MeetAll dataset aligns benchmark tasks with real‑world meeting assistant requirements by capturing authentic enterprise query patterns.  
**Mechanism:** Starting from authentic multilingual audio (AISHELL‑4, CHiME‑6), questions are injected based on patterns identified through stakeholder interviews across finance, healthcare, and tech. Expert review (κ=0.82) and a human discriminability test (56% detection, near‑random) ensure natural phrasing and coverage of four complexity dimensions: cognitive load, temporal context span, domain expertise, and task execution.  
**Core assumption:** Real enterprise workflows involve a heterogeneous mix of query complexities that prior synthetic or post‑hoc annotated datasets underrepresent.  
**Evidence:** The dataset comprises 231 meetings (140 h) with 1,180 injected questions; expert agreement κ=0.82; human discriminability at 56% indicates near‑indistinguishability from organic queries.  
**Break condition:** If expert agreement (κ) drops significantly or human discriminability exceeds ~65%, the injection protocol fails to generate organic queries.

### Mechanism 2: Multi‑dimensional, human‑calibrated evaluation
**Claim:** MeetBench‑XL provides a reliable proxy for enterprise utility beyond single metrics like accuracy.  
**Mechanism:** Five enterprise‑prioritized criteria (factual fidelity, user intent alignment, response efficiency, structural clarity, completeness) are used. An LLM‑as‑judge (Prometheus2) is isotonically calibrated against 150 human‑rated samples, yielding Pearson r=0.78, p<0.01, enabling scalable evaluation that aligns with human judgment in enterprise contexts.  
**Core assumption:** Overall meeting assistant quality is a multi‑faceted construct poorly captured by traditional QA metrics, and LLM judges can be aligned to human enterprise priorities via calibration.  
**Evidence:** The calibrated evaluator shows strong correlation (r=0.78) with human ratings on a held‑out set.  
**Break condition:** If the correlation falls below a threshold (e.g., r<0.6), the rubrics or calibration fail to capture human preferences.

### Mechanism 3: Learned joint optimization of routing and tool invocation
**Claim:** A dual‑policy agent that learns to route queries and invoke tools via offline RL outperforms heuristic or single‑path baselines under enterprise latency, cost, and quality trade‑offs.  
**Mechanism:** A lightweight classifier (1 ms, 300K params) is trained via Conservative Q‑Learning on a replay buffer, optimizing a composite objective J = α·TaskSuccess − β·Latency − γ·TokenCost. The router directs queries to a fast Talker (for simple, self‑contained queries) or a slow Planner (which executes a 3‑hop tool‑augmented reasoning loop for complex queries). Complexity labels from the four‑axis taxonomy guide the policy.  
**Core assumption:** Optimal performance requires dynamic, learned decisions about reasoning depth and tool use, which cannot be captured by static heuristics.  
**Evidence:** The final system achieves 81.2% routing accuracy, 30% lower latency on simple queries, and 12.4% higher quality on complex tasks compared to single‑model baselines.  
**Break condition:** If the RL refinement does not improve over the supervised initialization (e.g., overall score drops, latency increases) or the routing accuracy does not exceed heuristic baselines by at least 2‑3%, the learning procedure fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Four‑Dimensional Task Taxonomy (Cognitive Load, Context Dependency, Domain Knowledge, Task‑Execution Effort). *Why needed:* It defines the complexity axes used to label queries in MeetAll and to train the Planner and router. *Quick check:* For the query “What was the agreed budget for Project X in last week’s meeting?”, assign each dimension (e.g., Cognitive Load: medium, Context Dependency: high, Domain Knowledge: low, Task‑Execution Effort: low).
- **Concept:** LLM‑as‑Judge Calibration for Enterprise Criteria. *Why needed:* The entire MeetBench‑XL benchmark relies on a calibrated LLM judge to provide multi‑dimensional scores that correlate with human evaluators; uncalibrated LLM judges would not reflect enterprise priorities. *Quick check:* Why is using raw scores from GPT‑4 insufficient for evaluating this meeting agent? (Because GPT‑4’s inherent preferences may not align with enterprise‑specific dimensions like structural clarity, and calibration adjusts the judge to human ratings.)
- **Concept:** Dual‑Process (Fast/Slow) Architecture. *Why needed:* This architecture underlies MeetMaster‑XL’s ability to balance latency and cost; the fast Talker handles simple queries quickly, while the slow Planner performs thorough, tool‑augmented reasoning for complex queries. *Quick check:* What are the three steps within the Planner’s “3‑hop loop”? (Analyze query on four axes, plan and invoke tools, compose answer.)
- **Concept:** Offline Reinforcement Learning for Policy Optimization. *Why needed:* The dual‑policy is *learned* via offline RL (CQL), distinguishing it from rule‑based or supervised routers and enabling joint optimization of routing and tool use. *Quick check:* What is the composite reward objective J(π) and what do α, β, γ represent? (J = α·TaskSuccess − β·Latency − γ·TokenCost; α, β, γ are coefficients weighting quality, latency penalty, and token cost respectively.)
- **Concept:** MeetAll Dataset Construction via Informed Injection. *Why needed:* The dataset’s realism and coverage of enterprise complexities are foundational for training and evaluation. *Quick check:* How are questions injected into raw meeting transcripts? (Stakeholder interviews identify query patterns; experts craft natural questions that are then injected at appropriate timestamps.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (flow):**  
Input Stream (ASR transcript + optional audio/KB) → Router (classifies as Fast, Slow, Slow+RAG, Slow+CrossSession) → Parallel execution: Talker (fast multimodal LLM) and Planner (slow LLM + Tool Executor) → Token‑Level Gate (if router confidence <0.75, Planner interrupts) → Output (text/TTS).

**Critical path:** Streaming ASR text → Router classification → Parallel start of Talker & Planner → If router confidence high, selected path proceeds; else Token Gate resolves (Planner emits sentinel “T” within 400 ms) → Selected path generates response → Output.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Parallel vs. Serial Execution: Parallel reduces P90 latency for complex queries but increases compute cost; serial saves compute but risks latency spikes if routing is wrong.  
- Router Complexity: A larger router may improve accuracy but adds overhead that could negate latency benefits for simple queries; the 1 ms, 300K‑param design balances this.  
- Tool Invocation Cost: Each tool call adds latency and token cost; the joint tool policy aims to minimize unnecessary calls while maintaining factual completeness.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High downvote rate (>4.3% of responses): suspect ASR errors (overlap, accents).  
- Poor cross‑meeting recall: check retrieval index integrity and meeting ID consistency.  
- Latency spikes on simple queries: likely router misclassification or low Talker cache hit rate.  
- Low scores on “Completeness” dimension: indicates Planner tool policy under‑invoking or poor retrieval quality.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Router Accuracy Baseline:** Run the 300‑sample stratified test set to confirm the supervised‑initialized router achieves ~78% accuracy and the final RL‑refined router achieves ~81.2%.  
2. **Latency/Quality Trade‑off Sweep:** Vary composite objective weights (β, γ) in a small RL fine‑tuning run and plot the Pareto frontier of Overall Score vs. P90 latency to verify the claimed trade‑offs (30% latency reduction on simple, 12.4% quality gain on complex).  
3. **End‑to‑End Pipeline Smoke Test:** Deploy the full system on 50 diverse queries from MeetAll Test. Measure router confidence distribution, miss‑trigger/late‑trigger rates of the token gate, actual vs. expected path latency (P50/P90), and calibrated Overall Score.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How does MeetBench‑XL generalize to other domains or languages?** The evaluation protocol was calibrated on a specific 150‑sample human‑rated subset from a bilingual (Mandarin/English) corpus covering finance, healthcare, and tech. It is unclear if the same rubric and calibration maintain strong correlation for other languages or highly specialized verticals. *Resolution:* Replicate calibration on a new domain/language and measure Pearson correlation; a drop would indicate need for domain‑specific recalibration.
- **What is the limit of routing accuracy for ambiguous queries?** The router misclassifies 22% of failure cases involving ambiguous queries. The 81.2% accuracy is reported, but the theoretical upper bound for the given architecture and feature set under partial observability is unknown. *Resolution:* Compare against an “oracle router” with access to ground‑truth tool outcomes to establish an upper bound; consider augmenting routing input with predictive success probabilities.
- **How does tool policy scale with an expanded library?** Current tools are limited to KB‑retrieval, cross‑meeting, and web. Adding new tools (e.g., calendar, task trackers) introduces novel actions and state dependencies; the offline RL dataset may not support robust composition without catastrophic forgetting. *Resolution:* Introduce a synthetic new tool, fine‑tune on limited demonstrations, and measure success on tasks requiring that tool while retaining performance on original tasks.
- **What is the minimum dataset size for MeetAll adaptation to a new domain?** The injection protocol is calibrated on stakeholders from three sectors. For a different domain (e.g., legal), the necessary number of stakeholder interviews to retain naturalness (56% human discriminability, JSD 0.087) is unknown. *Resolution:* Pilot the pipeline with varying numbers of interviews (e.g., n=3,5,10) in a new domain, then measure discriminability and JSD against organic queries to find the knee point.
- **Is the MeetBench‑XL evaluator robust to reward hacking?** The calibrated LLM judge correlates with human ratings on a static set, but an agent optimized to maximize automated scores might produce responses that game the rubric while being judged poorly by humans. *Resolution:* Adversarial red‑teaming: iteratively modify high‑quality human responses to increase automated scores, then have humans re‑rate them; a significant drop in human scores for high automated scores would indicate vulnerability.

## Limitations
- Critical implementation details for the fast Talker path and the precise RL reward proxy formulations are not disclosed, preventing full reproduction of MeetMaster‑XL.
- The reported performance and human‑calibrated evaluation are validated solely on a bilingual enterprise corpus (finance, healthcare, tech); generalization to other domains or languages remains unproven.
- The five‑dimensional rubric, while human‑calibrated, may not capture all stakeholder nuances and could be susceptible to reward hacking by agents optimized for the automated metric.

## Confidence
- **High:** Existence and basic construction protocol of the MeetAll dataset (140 h, expert‑validated, κ=0.82, human discriminability 56%). The Pearson correlation (r=0.78) of the calibrated MeetBench‑XL evaluator with human judgments.
- **Medium:** Specific performance metrics of MeetMaster‑XL (81.2% routing accuracy, 30% latency reduction, 12.4% quality gain). The deployment claim of 18× cost savings (lacks controlled A/B design).
- **Low:** Assertion that the learned dual‑policy (joint RL optimization) is the primary driver of improvements, due to the absence of an ablation isolating the contribution of RL from the supervised router baseline.

## Next Checks
1. **Independent Calibration Replication:** Retrain the Prometheus2‑based evaluator on a new random sample of 150 human‑rated responses from MeetBench‑XL. Verify that the isotonic calibration maintains Pearson correlation ≥0.75 with an independent held‑out human set.
2. **Router Robustness Ablation:** Replace the learned router with a simple heuristic (query length + keyword triggers) while keeping all other components fixed. Compare overall MeetBench‑XL score and P90 latency against the learned router; a significant drop (>5% score or >50% latency increase) would indicate the learned router’s essential contribution.
3. **Tool Policy Efficiency Analysis:** Run the Planner on a sample of complex queries with tool calls artificially suppressed or randomized. Measure the change in the “Completeness” dimension score. A minimal impact would suggest the learned tool policy may be over‑parameterized or the proxy rewards noisy.
