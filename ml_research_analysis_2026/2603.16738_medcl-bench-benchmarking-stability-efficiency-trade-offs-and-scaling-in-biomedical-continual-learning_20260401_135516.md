# MedCL-Bench: Benchmarking stability-efficiency trade-offs and scaling in biomedical continual learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16738
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16738
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** Parameter-efficient methods (ADAPTER, TCL) achieve best retention per GPU-hour, while replay methods offer strong protection at higher computational cost; scaling to decoder-only models reduces forgetting for replay/regularization but not for parameter-isolation or GEM.

## Executive Summary
MedCL-Bench introduces a standardized continual learning benchmark for biomedical natural language processing. It streams ten diverse datasets across five task families, all cast to a unified text-to-label format, and evaluates eleven continual learning strategies across eight task orders. Metrics include retention (backward transfer, BWT), forward transfer (FWT), average performance (AP), and GPU-hour cost.

Key findings reveal catastrophic forgetting in vanilla sequential fine‑tuning (BWT as low as –57.69%). Parameter‑isolation methods (ADAPTER, TCL) deliver the best retention per GPU‑hour, achieving final AP of 72–73% with minimal trainable parameters and low order variability. Replay‑based approaches (REPLAY, GEM) provide strong forgetting protection (BWT up to –1.33) but at 4–9× higher computational cost. Regularization methods (EWC, L2) yield limited benefit. Forward transfer remains negative for all methods, and forgetting varies by task family: multi‑label topic classification is most vulnerable, while constrained‑output tasks are more robust. Scaling experiments with larger decoder‑only models (Qwen‑4B) show reduced forgetting for replay and regularization but sharp degradation for GEM and parameter‑isolation, indicating architecture‑sensitive trade‑offs.

## Method Summary
The benchmark utilizes ten public biomedical datasets—PubMedQA, BioASQ, SciFact, PubHealth, GAD, ChemProt, DDI, PubMed‑RCT, DRUGLIB, and LitCovid—each converted to a discriminative classification task with a fixed label set. Training and evaluation splits are capped at ≤1 000 training and ≤500 validation/test instances to ensure fairness. Pretrained language models (T5‑base by default, plus Qwen‑0.6B and Qwen‑4B) are sequentially fine‑tuned on a stream of tasks in one of eight predetermined orders. After completing each task, the model is evaluated on all tasks seen so far using exact match accuracy (subset accuracy for multi‑label LitCovid). Eleven continual learning algorithms are compared: VANILLA, EWC, L2, REPLAY (memory buffer size 5), GEM, AGEM, LAMOL, ADAPTER (bottleneck 48 on T5), TCL, OLORA (rank 8), and MULTI (multitask upper bound). For each order, the full stream is run, and mean final AP, BWT, FWT, and GPU‑hour cost are reported, emphasizing the stability‑efficiency trade‑off and robustness to task ordering.

## Key Results
- Parameter‑isolation methods (ADAPTER, TCL) attain the highest retention per GPU‑hour, with final AP of 72–73%, tight order intervals, and only 17.9–37.4 M trainable parameters (vs. 222 M for full fine‑tuning).
- Replay‑based approaches (REPLAY, GEM) achieve strong forgetting protection (e.g., GEM BWT up to –1.33) but incur 4–9× higher GPU‑hour cost compared to vanilla fine‑tuning.
- Scaling to decoder‑only models (Qwen‑4B) reduces forgetting for REPLAY and regularizers while causing sharp degradation for GEM (AP drops from 68.83% on T5 to 55.60%) and parameter‑isolation, highlighting architecture‑dependent dynamics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Parameter Isolation
Claim: Parameter isolation (ADAPTER, TCL) mitigates catastrophic forgetting by freezing the pretrained backbone and learning only lightweight task‑specific modules, thus preventing interference with shared representations.
Mechanism: The backbone parameters remain fixed; each task trains a small set of added parameters (e.g., bottleneck adapters or task embeddings) that capture task‑specific adjustments, confining updates to a low‑dimensional subspace.
Core assumption: The backbone contains sufficient reusable features and task‑specific adaptations can be learned without modifying it.
Evidence: [abstract] notes best retention per GPU‑hour; [section] reports ADAPTER/TCL AP 72–73% with tight order intervals; neighboring works on frozen backbones support.
Break condition: As backbone capacity grows, the tiny trainable subset becomes a bottleneck; scaling to Qwen‑4B weakens parameter‑isolation strength because the fraction of trainable parameters shrinks.

### Episodic Replay
Claim: Episodic replay (REPLAY) reduces forgetting by interleaving stored examples from previous tasks during new task training.
Mechanism: A fixed‑size memory buffer stores representative samples from each past task; each minibatch combines new and replayed old data, forcing the model to retain prior performance.
Core assumption: A limited subset of past data can sufficiently approximate previous task distributions.
Evidence: [abstract] replay offers strong protection; [section] REPLAY AP 58–63% with BWT –5.29 to –16.43; neighboring studies corroborate.
Break condition: Too small or poorly sampled buffer fails to capture essential knowledge; replay also incurs high computational overhead.

### Gradient Projection
Claim: Gradient projection (GEM, AGEM) reduces forgetting by constraining parameter updates to directions that do not increase loss on stored examples from previous tasks.
Mechanism: At each update, the gradient is projected onto a feasible region defined by constraints that loss on memory examples must not increase.
Core assumption: Stored examples provide a convex approximation of the previous loss landscape; projecting gradients suffices to prevent catastrophic interference.
Evidence: [abstract] GEM strong protection; [section] GEM AP 66.83–73.69% with BWT –1.33 to –6.88; neighbor works on gradient projection.
Break condition: Constraints can be overly restrictive, especially for decoder‑only architectures; GEM AP drops sharply on Qwen‑4B.

## Foundational Learning
- **Catastrophic Forgetting**: Why needed? It is the core problem that CL methods aim to solve; particularly critical in biomedical applications where model updates are frequent and costly. Quick check: Why does sequential fine‑tuning cause severe forgetting in neural networks, and why is this especially problematic for biomedical NLP?
- **Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning**: Why needed? Central to stability‑efficiency trade‑offs; methods like adapters update only a small subset of parameters. Quick check: How do adapter modules enable training with fewer parameters, and why might limiting updates to adapters help reduce interference with previously learned tasks?
- **Task Order Sensitivity**: Why needed? CL performance can vary dramatically with task sequence; reliable benchmarks must evaluate over multiple orders. Quick check: How might the sequence of tasks influence catastrophic forgetting, and why should continual learning benchmarks report results over multiple task orders rather than a single sequence?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Pretrained backbone (T5‑base, Qwen) → Task stream (10 biomedical datasets in fixed order) → CL algorithm wrapper (e.g., REPLAY, ADAPTER) → Evaluation module (compute accuracy matrix, derive AP/BWT/FWT)

Critical path:
1. Load pretrained model and tokenizer.
2. For each task in specified order: load capped datasets; train using CL‑specific procedure for fixed epochs; after training, evaluate on all seen tasks and record accuracies.
3. Repeat for all eight task orders; aggregate final AP, BWT, FWT and compute GPU‑hour cost.

Design tradeoffs:
- Unified accuracy metric simplifies cross‑task comparison but may obscure task‑specific nuances (e.g., F1, exact match) especially for multi‑label LitCovid.
- Dataset caps ensure fairness and tractability but may underrepresent large datasets and limit peak performance.
- Multiple task orders reveal order sensitivity but multiply compute cost.
- Backbone freezing in parameter‑efficient methods reduces trainable parameters and cost but risks capacity bottlenecks on larger models.

Failure signatures:
- Vanilla collapse: VANILLA final AP 20–30% vs MULTI ~76% indicates severe forgetting.
- High order variance: Methods like L2 or GEM show large AP swings across orders; may be unreliable in practice.
- Compute explosion: REPLAY/GEM >6× VANILLA GPU‑hours; avoid if budget limited or reduce memory buffer.
- Scaling mismatch: GEM degrades on decoder‑only models; validate on target backbone before deployment.

First 3 experiments:
1. Run VANILLA on Order 1; expect catastrophic forgetting (BWT < –40, AP ~20–40).
2. Run ADAPTER on Order 1; expect AP ~72% and stable BWT near zero.
3. Run REPLAY and GEM on Order 1; compare AP and GPU‑hour to illustrate stability‑efficiency trade‑offs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling to larger decoder‑only models:** The study only evaluates models up to 4B parameters; it is unclear how stability–efficiency trade‑offs evolve with larger biomedical LLMs.
2. **Robustness across orders and longer streams:** The reported trade‑offs may depend on the specific task order (Order 1) and 10‑task horizon; their validity under diverse permutations and extended sequences is not established.
3. **Evaluation with deployment‑facing criteria:** The benchmark uses accuracy only; whether method rankings hold for clinical metrics (calibration, robustness, cost‑sensitive errors) remains an open question.
4. **Hybrid strategies:** Combining selective replay with parameter isolation has not been explored; such hybrids might improve trade‑offs beyond pure approaches.
5. **Mechanism of GEM degradation on decoder‑only models:** GEM performs well on T5 but degrades sharply on Qwen models; the underlying cause (e.g., gradient constraint restrictiveness) is not understood.

## Limitations
- Fixed dataset selection, capped training sizes, and undisclosed task order permutations limit generalizability.
- Missing hyperparameters and subsampling details hinder exact replication.
- The accuracy‑only metric may overlook task‑specific nuances, especially for multi‑label classification.
