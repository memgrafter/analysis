# How Human is AI? Examining the Impact of Emotional Prompts on Artificial and Human and Responsiveness

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05104
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05104
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Emotional prompts (praise, blame, anger) influence GPT-4o’s output quality and ethical framing, and blaming AI increases negative language in subsequent human-human emails.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether the emotional tone of user prompts affects both AI-generated responses and later human communication. Through a between-subject experiment with 131 participants using ChatGPT (GPT-4o) for two tasks—drafting a public response and advising on an ethical dilemma—the researchers found that praise improved the perceived quality of subsequent AI answers, while blame and anger shifted the AI’s ethical emphasis (blame reduced focus on corporate interests; anger reduced focus on public interest). Notably, participants who blamed the AI later wrote emails to a coworker that were more negative, hostile, and disappointing compared to those who praised it. These results suggest that emotional framing in human-AI interaction can shape AI behavior and spill over into human social dynamics, raising implications for AI design and organizational communication.

## Method Summary
The experiment recruited 131 U.S.-based participants in supervisory roles via Prolific. Each participant interacted with GPT-4o (temperature=1.0, 800-token limit) through a custom Shiny app, under one of four emotional conditions: praise, blame, anger, or neutral. Participants were instructed to express the assigned emotion in their prompts.

The study comprised two main tasks. First, participants drafted a ~400-word public response to a toy crisis and then engaged in two improvement turns, providing feedback to the AI in the assigned emotional tone. Second, they sought advice on an ethical dilemma (balancing public vs. corporate interests) across two turns. Finally, participants wrote an email to a subordinate (50–100 words) without using GenAI. ChatGPT’s responses were rated by blind human raters on improvement (1–5 scale) and ethical stance (via GPT-4o scoring). The email tones were also human-rated. Attention checks (e.g., word counts, a hidden word “oblivious”) ensured compliance.

## Key Results
- Praise led to significantly higher improvement ratings in GPT-4o’s answers compared to neutral (M=3.70 vs. 2.84 on a 5-point scale).
- In the ethical dilemma, blame reduced emphasis on corporate interests (M=1.08) relative to neutral (M=1.78), while anger reduced emphasis on public interest (M=2.65 vs. neutral M=3.36).
- Participants who blamed ChatGPT later wrote emails with more negative, hostile, and disappointing language than those who praised it (differences statistically significant).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Praise enhances AI output via RLHF-induced refinement
Praise prompts appear to activate patterns associated with positive reinforcement from the model’s RLHF training, causing GPT-4o to produce responses perceived as higher quality. The model’s token prediction is subtly biased toward features historically correlated with positive human feedback, so emotional phrasing that signals approval may trigger a “reward” signal that increases output refinement. Evidence: ChatGPT showed greater improvement in its answers when participants praised it (M=3.70 vs. 2.84; t(264)=3.28, p=.007).

### Mechanism 2: Blame/anger shift ethical stances by altering value salience
Emotional language changes the contextual framing of an ethical dilemma, prompting the model to weight different value tokens (e.g., “public safety”, “company collapse”) differently based on patterns learned from training data where affect co-occurs with moral arguments. Blame seems to increase the salience of public interest, while anger may reduce the salience of corporate interests. Evidence: ChatGPT emphasized corporate goals less after blame (M=1.08 vs. neutral M=1.78; t(132)=2.66, p=.025) and de-emphasized public interest more after anger (M=2.65 vs. neutral M=3.36).

### Mechanism 3: Emotional carryover to human-human communication via priming
Interacting with an AI in a blame-focused manner may activate a hostile cognitive schema in the user, which persists and influences subsequent unassisted writing—a form of behavioral priming or mood carryover. Even though the AI lacks true affect, the simulated social encounter transiently alters the user’s emotional state or interaction style. Evidence: Emails written by participants who blamed ChatGPT expressed more negative emotions (M=2.21 vs. praise M=1.38; t(147)=2.76, p=.032) and more unfriendly, hostile language (t(147)=3.68, p=.013).

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**  
  *Why needed:* Explains why the model might simulate responsiveness to “praise” or “blame” – RLHF tunes the model to prefer outputs that humans rate highly, creating a latent bias toward patterns associated with positive feedback.  
  *Quick check:* How might the RLHF training process cause GPT-4o to associate “praise” prompts with higher-quality response patterns?

- **Social Priming & Carryover Effects**  
  *Why needed:* Mechanism 3 (spillover to human-human communication) depends on psychological priming, where one social interaction influences the next; this bridges AI interaction outcomes to human behavioral change.  
  *Quick check:* What is a plausible psychological pathway by which blaming an AI could increase hostility in an email to a human coworker?

- **Value-Conflicted Ethical Reasoning in LLMs**  
  *Why needed:* Mechanism 2 involves the model weighing “public interest” vs. “corporate interests,” requiring understanding of how LLMs handle multi-faceted ethical dilemmas where emotional framing sways token probabilities for competing value-laden concepts.  
  *Quick check:* In the ethical dilemma task, why might “blame” prompts increase emphasis on “public interest” while “anger” decreases emphasis on “corporate interests”?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Prompt Input Layer -> RLHF-Tuned Transformer Core -> Output Sampler (the Context Window retains multi-turn history, enabling emotional framing to persist across turns)

**Critical path:**  
- For output shift: Emotional wording in prompt → attention heads modulate value-related token probabilities → output reflects shifted emphasis (e.g., less corporate focus under blame).  
- For human carryover: User’s emotional state/script from interaction → persists post-task → influences unaided human-human email composition.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Sensitivity to emotional framing vs. robustness: High sensitivity enables rapport but risks manipulation and unstable outputs.  
- Multi-turn context vs. efficiency: Persisting emotional framing requires longer context windows, increasing compute cost.  
- Simulated empathy vs. deception: Enhancing “human-like” responsiveness may blur user understanding of the system’s lack of true affect.

**Failure signatures:**  
- No output improvement with praise across repeated trials (RLHF effect weak or non-generalizable).  
- Ethical stance shift only with explicit, extreme emotional keywords (suggesting superficial pattern matching).  
- No spillover effect in human-human communication (interaction not perceived as socially engaging).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate the “praise vs. neutral” improvement effect with a different task (e.g., creative writing) to test domain generality.  
2. Systematically vary the intensity of emotional language (mild vs. strong praise/blame) to test for dose-response relationships in output change and spillover.  
3. Introduce an “AI agency reminder” condition (e.g., warning users “ChatGPT has no feelings”) before interaction to test if mitigating anthropomorphism eliminates both the output effect and the human-human carryover.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Cultural generalizability:** Do the observed effects generalize across diverse cultural contexts? The study’s U.S.-centric sample limits external validity; cross-cultural replications could test moderation by cultural norms around emotional expression.  
2. **Prolonged vs. brief interactions:** How do sustained interactions with GenAI influence the impact of emotional prompting? Real-world use is often ongoing, potentially leading to adaptation or cumulative spillover not captured in this single-session experiment.  
3. **Spectrum of emotions:** What are the differential effects of other emotions (e.g., sadness, fear, joy) on AI output quality and ethical stance? The study tested only a narrow set; other emotions may activate different training data associations.  
4. **Organizational spillover:** Does habitual use of hostile emotional prompts foster broader negative communication climates in workplaces? Immediate spillover to a single email is documented, but long-term effects on communication norms remain unknown.

## Limitations
- The exact wording of the emotional prompt instructions and examples given to participants is not disclosed, hindering precise replication.
- Scoring rubrics for human-rated outcomes are underspecified; inter-rater reliability was not reported, raising concerns about measurement consistency.
- The sample is limited to U.S. employees in supervisory roles, restricting generalizability to other demographics and cultural contexts.

## Confidence
- **Praise improves AI output:** Medium (effect is statistically significant but modest and task-specific).  
- **Blame/anger shift ethical stances:** Low-Medium (findings rely on a single dilemma and a GPT-4o-scored rubric without transparency).  
- **Blame spills over to human-human communication:** Medium (effects are reliable but small; demand characteristics cannot be fully ruled out).

## Next Checks
1. **Full-transparency replication:** Pre‑register and run a direct replication, releasing all participant instructions, exact prompts, and detailed scoring rubrics, including a formal inter‑rater reliability analysis.
2. **Controlled prompt ablation + internal probing:** Build a factorial design separating emotional keywords from content, and probe GPT‑4o’s hidden states (e.g., using linear probes for sentiment or value tokens) to test whether emotional framing activates RLHF-learned patterns or merely biases token predictions lexically.
3. **Cross-model and cross-cultural generalizability test:** Replicate the experiment with at least three diverse LLMs (e.g., Llama‑3, Claude‑3.5, GPT‑4o) and recruit participants from multiple countries (e.g., East Asia, Europe) to determine whether the effects are model‑agnostic and culturally universal.
