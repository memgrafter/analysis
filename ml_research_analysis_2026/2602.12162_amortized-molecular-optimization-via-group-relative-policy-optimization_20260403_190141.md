# Amortized Molecular Optimization via Group Relative Policy Optimization
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12162
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12162
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** GRXForm achieved 17.8%±9.3% success on kinase scaffold decoration and ranked second on PMO with ~50k oracle calls.

## Executive Summary
Molecular optimization often requires expensive per-molecule search, while amortized policies suffer from high variance when generalizing across scaffolds. This paper introduces GRXForm, an amortized policy that sequentially adds atoms and bonds using a Graph Transformer, trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO normalizes rewards per starting scaffold by the mean of multiple sampled completions, creating a dynamic baseline that reduces variance and stabilizes learning. On a held-out kinase scaffold decoration task, GRXForm achieves a mean objective of 0.618±0.004 and a success rate of 17.8%±9.3%, substantially outperforming amortized baselines and competing instance optimizers. On the PMO de novo benchmark, it obtains a cumulative top‑10 AUC of 16.433, ranking second among 22 methods while using only ~50k oracle calls—far fewer than instance‑based competitors. A prodrug transfer case study further demonstrates effective few‑shot generalization without inference‑time oracles.

## Method Summary
The goal is to train an amortized policy that, given any starting scaffold, directly outputs an optimized molecule in a single forward pass, avoiding costly per‑instance search. The core architecture, GraphXForm, is a Graph Transformer that constructs molecules by sequentially selecting actions: add atom, modify bond, or stop. Chemical validity is enforced by a hierarchical action space and a dynamic validity mask that zeros out actions violating atomic valence constraints at each step. The policy is first pre‑trained on ~1.5 M ChEMBL molecules via maximum likelihood to capture chemical plausibility. Fine‑tuning uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). For each training epoch, a batch of scaffolds is sampled. For each scaffold, Stochastic Beam Search (SBS) with beam width \(W\) generates \(G\) diverse completions. Each completion is scored by a reward oracle (e.g., kinase MPO). For scaffold \(i\), the group mean \(\mu_i\) is computed; the advantage of completion \(j\) is \(A_{i,j} = r_{i,j} - \mu_i\). The policy gradient accumulates \(\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_{<t}) \cdot A_{i,j}\) over all steps and trajectories, and parameters are updated via Adam. Early stopping on validation mean objective prevents overfitting. This group‑relative baseline adapts to scaffold‑specific difficulty, reducing variance and enabling stable learning across diverse scaffolds.

## Key Results
- On held‑out kinase scaffold decoration, GRXForm achieved a mean objective of \(0.618 \pm 0.004\) and a success rate of \(17.8\% \pm 9.3\%\), while all amortized baselines scored \(<0.41\) with 0% success and instance optimizers (Mol GA, GenMol) failed.
- On the PMO de novo benchmark (22 tasks), GRXForm ranked second with a cumulative top‑10 AUC of 16.433, using only ~50k total oracle calls compared to up to 10k per instance for competitors.
- A prodrug transfer case study showed effective few‑shot generalization to unseen drugs without inference‑time oracle calls, demonstrating practical utility.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) reduces variance by using a per‑scaffold dynamic baseline.** For each starting scaffold, GRPO samples \(G\) completions via Stochastic Beam Search and centers rewards by the group mean \(\mu_i\). This adapts to the inherent difficulty of each scaffold, ensuring that both easy and hard scaffolds contribute meaningful gradient signals. An ablation comparing GRPO to REINFORCE (global baseline) shows substantially higher success rates (17.8% vs 9.1%) and lower gradient variance, confirming the stabilization effect.

**Hierarchical action space with valence masking guarantees chemical validity.** The policy selects from three levels: operation type, target atom, and bond specification. A validity mask \(M(s_t)\) zeros out any action that would violate atomic valency rules at the current step. By construction, every sampled trajectory yields a chemically valid molecular graph, eliminating the need for post‑hoc filtering and enabling efficient exploration of valid structures.

**Amortized training on diverse scaffolds enables out‑of‑distribution generalization.** Pre‑training on ChEMBL followed by GRPO fine‑tuning teaches the policy a general optimization logic that transfers to scaffolds not seen during training. Because the policy directly maps a scaffold to an optimized completion, new molecules can be produced without any oracle calls at inference time. Evidence includes strong performance on held‑out kinase scaffolds (cluster‑based split) and successful prodrug transfer, where the policy adapted to a new modification rule after seeing only four exemplars.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Starting scaffold \(s_0\) → Graph Transformer policy \(\pi_\theta\) (with validity mask \(M(s_t)\)) → Stochastic Beam Search (SBS, beam width \(W\)) → \(G\) completions → Reward oracle \(\mathcal{R}\) → GRPO update (compute group mean \(\mu_i\), advantages \(A_{i,j}\), accumulate gradient) → Adam update → New policy.

**Critical path:** During each training iteration: (1) Sample a batch of scaffolds. (2) For each scaffold, run SBS with \(\pi_\theta\) to generate \(G\) completions. (3) Score each completion with \(\mathcal{R}\). (4) Compute scaffold‑specific mean \(\mu_i\). (5) Compute advantages \(A_{i,j} = r_{i,j} - \mu_i\). (6) Accumulate the policy gradient over all steps in all trajectories weighted by advantages. (7) Update \(\pi_\theta\) via Adam.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Group size \(G\): larger \(G\) stabilizes the group mean but increases beam search cost; smaller \(G\) is cheaper but risks noisy baselines.  
- Beam width \(W\): wider beams produce more diverse groups but increase memory and time.  
- Entropy regularization (\(\beta\)): encourages exploration but may slow convergence; requires per‑task tuning.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Volatile advantage magnitudes during training (similar to REINFORCE baseline) indicate poor group normalization, possibly due to insufficient group diversity or small \(G\).  
- Near‑zero success rate on held‑out scaffolds suggests inadequate scaffold diversity in training or overly restrictive validity masking.  
- Mode collapse in SBS (low within‑group similarity) reduces gradient signal quality.  
- Validity rate below 98% signals masking errors.  
- Uniformly low rewards across trajectories indicate reward model misalignment.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Kinase scaffold decoration: Held‑out scaffold split tests structural generalization.  
2. Prodrug transfer: Few‑shot fine‑tuning on four exemplar drugs assesses transfer of a lipidization rule.  
3. PMO benchmark: De novo optimization under a strict oracle budget compares sample efficiency (AUC top‑10) against state‑of‑the‑art instance optimizers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**How can GRXForm’s sequential addition‑only framework be extended to support atom/bond removal and fragment linking?** The Conclusion mentions planned evaluation on tasks requiring removal (e.g., scaffold morphing) and linking, but the current policy and validity mask are tailored for additive construction. Extending the action space and ensuring chemical validity for such operations remain open challenges; evidence would come from designing appropriate actions, enforcing valence constraints, and benchmarking on morphing datasets.

**What is the optimal strategy for combining amortized policies with instance optimizers?** The paper identifies hybridizing the benefits of both paradigms as a key future direction, but no existing work systematically studies when and how to best combine them. Possible approaches include using an amortized policy to warm‑start an instance search or performing a few online fine‑tuning steps after deployment. The trade‑off between added compute and improved reward needs empirical evaluation on benchmarks like PMO or kinase decoration.

**How sensitive is GRPO’s variance reduction to group size \(G\) and within‑group diversity?** GRPO relies on the group mean representing task difficulty; if trajectories within a group are too similar (low diversity), the mean may not capture true difficulty, leading to biased gradients. The impact of \(G\) and diversity on training stability, gradient variance, and final performance is unanalyzed. Evidence would require varying \(G\) (e.g., 4, 16, 64), measuring diversity (e.g., average Tanimoto similarity), and correlating with learning dynamics and success rates.

## Limitations
- Official checkpoints and dataset splits are not provided, hindering reproducibility.
- Critical hyperparameters (group size, beam width, SBS details) and reward model specifications (hyperparameters, thresholds) are omitted, forcing heuristic choices.
- Evaluation is limited to a single scaffold clustering split and one benchmark (PMO), raising questions about generalizability across diverse chemical spaces and tasks.
