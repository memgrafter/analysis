# Modeling the human lexicon under temperature variations: linguistic factors, diversity and typicality in LLM word associations
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18171
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18171
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** LLMs replicate human lexical factor trends (frequency, concreteness) but exhibit a size- and temperature-dependent trade-off between typicality and variability, failing to match the human balance of high variability and moderate typicality.

## Executive Summary
The study compares human word associations from the Small World of Words (SWOW) dataset with those generated by three instruction-tuned LLMs (Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-32B) across multiple temperature settings. It evaluates whether LLM responses follow human-like patterns in lexical factors (word frequency, concreteness) and quantifies two key dimensions: variability (number of unique responses per cue) and typicality (alignment with human associative strength). Results show all LLMs mirror human frequency and concreteness distributions. However, larger models produce highly typical but low-variability responses, while smaller models generate more variable but less typical outputs. Increasing temperature consistently boosts variability but reduces typicality. No model achieves the human-like combination of high variability and moderate typicality, highlighting a gap in modeling human lexical diversity.

## Method Summary
The researchers used the SWOW-EN dataset as a human baseline, sampling 490 cue words from the full set of 12,282. For each cue, they prompted each LLM (Mistral-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen-2.5-32B-Instruct-GPTQ-Int8) 100 times at four temperature values (0.3, 1, 1.5, 2) to elicit three associated words per cue, mirroring the SWOW protocol. Raw responses underwent lowercasing and spelling correction. The analysis focused on first-ranked responses (R1). Two primary metrics were computed: variability, measured as the average number of unique R1 responses per cue (`avg#R1`); and typicality, measured using standardized human associative strength (SS1) to derive token-weighted (`tok-SS1`) and type-weighted (`typ-SS1`) averages. Lexical factors (relative frequency, relative concreteness) were also examined. The human and model distributions were compared via ANOVA and correlation analyses.

## Key Results
- LLMs replicate human patterns in relative frequency and concreteness of cue-response pairs.
- Model size creates a trade-off: the largest model (Qwen-32B) yields high typicality (mean tok-SS1 up to 2.56) but very low variability (avg#R1 as low as 1.4); smaller models (Mistral-7B) show high variability (avg#R1 up to 33.8) but lower typicality.
- Increasing temperature increases variability but decreases typicality for all models; no temperature setting enables models to simultaneously achieve high variability and high typicality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** LLMs replicate human lexical factor trends (frequency, concreteness) in cue-response pairs.  
**Mechanism:** Models internalize statistical co-occurrence patterns from training corpora; frequent and concrete words appear in similar contextual relationships as in human experience.  
**Core assumption:** Human lexical associations are driven by distributional properties of language exposure; LLMs trained on large text corpora develop analogous sensitivities.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "All LLMs mirrored human patterns for frequency and concreteness." Section 3.2: Figures 1–4 and ANOVA show negligible effect sizes between humans and LLMs on relative frequency/concreteness distributions.  
**Break condition:** If LLMs fail to replicate human trends for other lexical factors (e.g., imageability, phonological similarity) despite similar training regimes.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Model size induces a variability–typicality trade-off: larger models produce highly human-typical but low-variability responses; smaller models show higher variability but lower typicality.  
**Mechanism:** Larger models have sharper probability distributions over next tokens (lower entropy), favoring high-probability (“prototypical”) human responses. Smaller models have flatter distributions, yielding more diverse but less frequent responses.  
**Core assumption:** Human word association distributions have a long tail; emulating a “prototypical human” requires capturing only the mode, while capturing diversity requires sampling from the tail.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "Larger models such as Qwen tend to emulate a single 'prototypical' human participant..." Section 4.2: Table 1 shows Qwen (32B) avg#R1=1.4 (low) vs. tok-SS1=2.56 (high); Mistral (7B) avg#R1=33.8 (high) vs. tok-SS1=0.63 (lower).  
**Break condition:** If model size increase leads to higher variability at comparable typicality, contradicting the monotonic trade-off.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Temperature modulates variability and typicality: higher temperature increases variability but decreases typicality.  
**Mechanism:** Temperature scales logits before softmax; higher temperature flattens the distribution, increasing sampling diversity but raising probability of low-likelihood tokens, which reduces alignment with human-frequent responses.  
**Core assumption:** Human-typical responses occupy the high-probability mass of the LLM’s learned distribution; temperature-induced noise preferentially samples low-probability regions.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "Higher values increasing variability but decreasing typicality." Section 4.2: Figure 6 shows across models, increasing temperature moves points right (↑avg#R1) and down (↓tok-SS1). Section A.1–A.2: ANOVA shows temperature significantly affects relative frequency/concreteness only with tiny effect sizes, indicating lexical factors are robust to temperature.  
**Break condition:** If temperature increase improves typicality or leaves variability unchanged for certain model architectures.

## Foundational Learning
### Word Association Norms (e.g., SWOW-EN)
**Why needed:** Provides the human reference distribution of associative strengths against which LLM outputs are evaluated. Without understanding how human responses are collected (100 participants, 3 responses per cue), typicality metrics (SS1, tok-SS1) cannot be interpreted.  
**Quick check:** How is “associative strength” computed in SWOW-EN, and why does the paper use standardized strength (SS1) instead?

### Temperature Sampling
**Why needed:** The core independent variable manipulating LLM output distribution. Understanding that temperature ≠ randomness but a scaling of logits is crucial to predict variability-typicality trade-offs.  
**Quick check:** If temperature=0, what would be the expected relationship between variability and typicality relative to temperature=1?

### Model Parameter Scaling & Quantization
**Why needed:** Explains cross-model differences (Qwen 32B vs. Llama 8B vs. Mistral 7B). Quantization (Qwen is GPTQ-Int8) may affect distribution sharpness but is not analyzed; assumption is size dominates.  
**Quick check:** Why might a quantized 32B model still exhibit lower variability than a non-quantized 8B model?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
SWOW-EN cue set (12,282) → 490-cue sample → LLM Generation (model × temperature; 100 prompts per cue, 3 responses each) → Post-processing (lowercasing, spelling correction, filtering) → Metric computation (lexical factors, variability #R1, typicality SS1/tok-SS1/typ-SS1) → Statistical analysis (distribution comparisons, correlation, visualization).

**Critical path:**  
Cue selection → LLM prompting (100 repetitions) → response normalization → merge with SWOW human data → compute SS1 for all responses → aggregate by cue → statistical comparison across models and temperatures.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Cue sample size: 490 vs. 12,282 reduces compute but may miss cue-specific effects; representativeness validated via metrics.  
- Metric choice: tok-SS1 (token-weighted) vs. typ-SS1 (type-weighted) captures different aspects of typicality.  
- Temperature range: 0.3–2 covers typical usage; extremes (0, ∞) not tested.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High typicality (tok-SS1 > 1.67) combined with high variability (avg#R1 > 38) would contradict the trade-off; suggests data leakage or metric bug.  
- Temperature has no effect on variability indicates sampling implementation error (e.g., temperature ignored).  
- Lexical factor trends diverge from humans for specific bins (e.g., abstract cues) may indicate training data bias.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate frequency/concreteness patterns: Use 490 cues, compute response/cue ratios, compare distributions (Kolmogorov–Smirnov) to human baseline; expect negligible differences (η² < .01).  
2. Validate variability–typicality trade-off: For each model, plot avg#R1 vs. tok-SS1 across temperatures; expect negative correlation (r ≈ –0.4).  
3. Temperature sweep isolation: Fix model (e.g., Llama), vary only temperature, measure change in avg#R1 and tok-SS1; expect monotonic increase in variability, decrease in typicality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Do human and LLM learning processes produce comparable lexical competence?  
**Basis in paper:** Introduction explicitly questions whether different learning processes (human experience vs. text training) result in comparable linguistic competence.  
**Why unresolved:** The study finds partial overlap in frequency/concreteness but reveals a fundamental variability-typicality trade-off absent in humans; full comparability across linguistic domains remains untested.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Multi-dimensional evaluation across syntax, semantics, pragmatics using aligned human-model tasks, and experiments with multimodal or grounded LLMs.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How can LLMs be modified to simultaneously achieve high typicality and high variability, matching human word association diversity?  
**Basis in paper:** Conclusion notes capturing human lexical idiosyncrasies remains a challenge and exploring LLM reflection of human diversity is a promising direction.  
**Why unresolved:** Current models show a trade-off: larger models have high typicality but low variability, smaller models the opposite; temperature cannot break this trade-off.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** New training objectives or decoding strategies that decouple typicality from variability, evaluated by joint metrics on human benchmark distributions.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Do closed-source or larger LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) exhibit a more human-like balance of variability and typicality than the open-source models studied?  
**Basis in paper:** Limitations state analysis was restricted to open-source models; closed-source may show different lexical behaviors.  
**Why unresolved:** Proprietary models' performance on lexical diversity metrics is unknown; their scale or training might better approximate human patterns.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the same prompting protocol (100 repetitions per cue, temperature sweep) on closed-source models and compare variability/typicality to human SWOW data.

## Limitations
- Exact prompt template used for LLM generation is unspecified; subtle phrasing changes may alter response distributions.
- The 490-cue subset is not provided; random resampling could shift cue-specific metrics, reducing reproducibility.
- Handling of words missing from frequency/concreteness resources is opaque (e.g., assigned default values), potentially biasing relative measures.

## Confidence
- Lexical factor alignment (frequency, concreteness): **High**
- Model size trade-off (variability vs. typicality): **Medium**
- Temperature effects (increase variability, decrease typicality): **Medium-High**

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt fidelity test:** Generate responses using multiple semantically equivalent prompt variants (e.g., “List three associated words…” vs. SWOW-style). Measure variance in `avg#R1` and `tok-SS1` to quantify sensitivity.
2. **Cue-sampling stability:** Re-run analysis on 10 random 490-cue subsets from SWOW-EN. Compute standard deviation of mean `tok-SS1` and `avg#R1` across subsets; if coefficient of variation >5%, subset choice is a major confound.
3. **Metric convergence:** For a single model/temp (e.g., Llama, T=1), increase repetitions from 100 to 500 per cue. Monitor drift in `tok-SS1` and `#R1` estimates; if stable (Δ<2%), current sample size is sufficient.
