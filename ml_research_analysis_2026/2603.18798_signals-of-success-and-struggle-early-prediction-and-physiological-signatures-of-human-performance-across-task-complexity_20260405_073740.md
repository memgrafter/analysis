# Signals of Success and Struggle: Early Prediction and Physiological Signatures of Human Performance across Task Complexity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18798
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18798
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** An ocular-cardiac fusion model achieved 0.86 balanced accuracy in predicting high-complexity task performance from early low-complexity session data.

## Executive Summary
This study explored early prediction of human performance in tasks of increasing complexity using physiological signals. In a within-subject experiment, participants engaged with a progressively complex game. Ocular and cardiac features recorded during an initial low-complexity session were used to forecast win/loss outcomes in a later high-complexity session. An ocular-cardiac fusion model reached a balanced accuracy of 0.86, with an ocular-only model performing nearly as well. High performers displayed more targeted visual attention, adaptive scanning strategies, and stable cardiac activation, alongside more positive affective experiences. The findings demonstrate the feasibility of cross-session physiological forecasting and yield interpretable markers of performance regulation.

## Method Summary
The study employed a within-subject experimental design where participants performed a game with escalating complexity. Physiological data (eye tracking and ECG) were recorded during a low-complexity session, and features extracted from these signals were used to train a classifier to predict win/loss outcomes in a subsequent high-complexity session. No additional reproduction details were provided.

## Key Results
- Ocular-cardiac fusion model achieved 0.86 balanced accuracy in early prediction of high-complexity performance.
- Ocular-only model showed comparable predictive power.
- High performers exhibited more targeted visual attention, adaptive scanning strategies, and stable cardiac activation; they also reported more positive affective experiences.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The model’s predictive power likely stems from the fact that successful performance in complex tasks is associated with efficient attentional control (captured by gaze metrics) and effective arousal regulation (captured by heart rate stability). These physiological signals reflect underlying cognitive and affective states that support performance under pressure. High performers’ ability to maintain stable cardiac activation suggests better stress management, while their targeted gaze indicates effective allocation of visual attention to task-relevant information.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Acquisition (Eye Tracker, ECG) -> Preprocessing -> Feature Extraction (Gaze Metrics, Cardiac Metrics) -> Feature Fusion -> Classifier -> Performance Prediction

**Critical path:**  
1. Reliable data collection during low‑complexity session  
2. Extraction of valid gaze and cardiac features  
3. Effective fusion of features  
4. Training a classifier with sufficient accuracy  
5. Applying classifier to new low‑complexity data to predict high‑complexity outcome

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Ocular‑only vs. fusion:* Fusion improves accuracy but adds hardware and analysis complexity.  
- *Interpretability vs. performance:* Simple features (e.g., fixation count) aid interpretation but may sacrifice predictive power; complex models (e.g., deep learning) boost accuracy but reduce transparency.  
- *Feature set size:* Including many features risks overfitting; too few may miss predictive information.  
- *Cross‑session assumption:* Predictive stability assumes physiological patterns persist across sessions, but learning or fatigue could violate this.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low balanced accuracy (<0.7) on validation.  
- High variance in performance across participants, indicating poor generalization.  
- Overfitting: good training performance, poor test performance.  
- Key features (e.g., gaze to critical areas) show no difference between high and low performers.  
- Excessive noise or missing data in physiological recordings.

**First experiments:**  
1. Replication with a larger, diverse sample to verify the 0.86 accuracy and generalizability.  
2. Cross‑task validation: apply the model to a different complex task (e.g., flight simulation) without retraining.  
3. Causal manipulation: use biofeedback to induce “high‑performer” physiological states (e.g., cardiac stability) and measure impact on performance.

## Limitations
- **Sample & Generalizability:** Absent participant details and single‑task context limit external validity.  
- **Causality:** Correlational design cannot establish whether physiological patterns cause performance differences or are merely associated.  
- **Longitudinal Stability:** Unknown if predictive signals remain stable across days/weeks or under learning/fatigue effects.

## Confidence
- Feasibility of Cross‑Session Forecasting: **Medium**  
- Interpretable Physiological Markers (Gaze/HR): **Medium**  
- Differentiation of High/Low Performers via Signatures: **Low–Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Replication with a Larger, Diverse Cohort:** Conduct a preregistered replication with a sufficiently powered (e.g., N>100), demographically diverse sample to establish the reliability and generalizability of the 0.86 accuracy claim and the specific ocular‑cardiac feature set.  
2. **Cross‑Task & Cross‑Domain Transfer Test:** Validate whether the predictive model and signature features trained on the game task transfer to a substantively different, real‑world complex task (e.g., surgical simulation, piloting, or strategic decision‑making) without retraining, testing ecological validity.  
3. **Controlled Causal Inference Experiment:** Design an experiment to test causality, such as using neurostimulation (e.g., tACS) or biofeedback to manipulate the putative “regulatory” physiological state (e.g., cardiac stability) and measure the subsequent causal impact on complex task performance, while controlling for baseline traits.
