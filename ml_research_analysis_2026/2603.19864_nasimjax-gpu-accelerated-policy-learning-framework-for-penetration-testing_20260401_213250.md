# NASimJax: GPU-Accelerated Policy Learning Framework for Penetration Testing

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19864
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19864
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Reimplementation in JAX enables up to 100× throughput (1.6M steps/sec), and the two-stage action selection (2SAS) achieves a 42% solve rate on 40-host networks versus 14% for flat masking.

## Executive Summary
NASimJax is a GPU-accelerated reimplementation of the Network Attack Simulator (NASim) built in JAX. It addresses the long-standing simulation bottleneck that has limited Reinforcement Learning (RL) research in automated penetration testing to small network scales. By fully vectorizing environment dynamics and leveraging JAX's just-in-time compilation, it achieves orders-of-magnitude speedups. The framework formalizes penetration testing as a Contextual Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) with a procedurally generated, guaranteed-solvable network distribution. Key findings demonstrate that a factored "two-stage" action selection architecture (2SAS) is essential for scaling to large networks, that Prioritized Level Replay (PLR) can outperform simple Domain Randomization but exhibits fragile interactions with 2SAS, and that training on sparser network topologies creates an implicit curriculum that enhances zero-shot generalization to denser networks.

## Method Summary
NASimJax defines a distribution over solvable network contexts via a stochastic generator parameterized by topology density (`t_d`), service density (`svc_d`), process density (`proc_d`), and sensitive host density (`s_d`). The agent interacts with a vectorized environment under partial observability, receiving flattened observations of action outcomes. Training uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with two action-space formulations: (1) **flat action masking**, where a single policy head outputs over all `|Hosts| × Actions` combinations, and (2) **two-stage action selection (2SAS)**, which factors the decision into a host selector followed by a per-host action selector. To improve generalization across the context distribution, the framework employs Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) methods: Domain Randomization (DR), which samples a new context per rollout, and Prioritized Level Replay (PLR), which maintains a buffer of network contexts and replays those with high learning regret. Returns are scaled by `N_s × V_h` (number of sensitive hosts times their total value) to normalize reward magnitudes across contexts.

## Key Results
- The JAX implementation achieves up to 100× higher throughput (1.6 million steps per second) compared to the original CPU-bound simulator.
- On 40-host networks, two-stage action selection (2SAS) substantially outperforms flat action masking, achieving a 42% solve rate vs. 14%.
- Prioritized Level Replay (PLR) yields stronger performance than Domain Randomization on medium-to-dense network distributions but catastrophically fails (0% solve rate) when combined with 2SAS at the largest scale (40 hosts, `t_d=0.15`).
- Training on sparse network distributions (`t_d=0.06`) creates an implicit curriculum that improves zero-shot generalization to denser, out-of-distribution networks (`t_d=0.15`).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: JAX Vectorization Eliminates the Simulation Bottleneck
- **Claim:** Reimplementing NASim in JAX eliminates the CPU-bound simulation bottleneck, enabling orders-of-magnitude speedups necessary for large-scale RL training.
- **Mechanism:** The environment is rewritten in a stateless, functional style compatible with JAX's `jit` and `vmap` transformations. This compiles the entire `step` and `reset` logic and allows thousands of independent network instances to be simulated in parallel on a GPU with minimal overhead.
- **Core assumption:** Environment dynamics can be expressed without side effects, and vectorization across independent environment instances is possible.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract reports "up to 100× higher environment throughput (1.6M steps/sec)." Section 6.1 (Figure 2, Table 2) shows speedup scaling with the number of parallel environments.
- **Break condition:** Fails if dynamics require non-vectorizable operations (e.g., external emulation) or if memory limits are exceeded by the maximum batched environment size.

### Mechanism 2: Two-Stage Action Selection (2SAS) Mitigates Large Action Spaces
- **Claim:** Decomposing the flat action space into host selection followed by per-host action selection mitigates credit assignment and exploration dilution caused by linearly growing action spaces.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of a single policy over `|H| * (|A_scan| + |OS|*(|V_svc|+|V_proc|))` actions, 2SAS uses two heads: one selects a host from `|H|` masked candidates, and a second selects an action for that host from a smaller set. Separate policy losses and advantages are computed for each stage, reducing the effective branching factor.
- **Core assumption:** Factoring the decision leads to cleaner credit assignment; a poor outcome is traceable to either a bad host choice or a bad action choice, not their combinatorial product. The host embedding provides sufficient context for the action head.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states 2SAS achieves "42% solve rate vs. 14% on 40-host networks." Section 5.1.2 details the two-stage architecture. Section 6.3 (Figure 4) shows the performance gap widening with network size.
- **Break condition:** Fails if the host selection head cannot produce a meaningful representation for the action head, or if decomposition causes catastrophic misattribution of credit (as seen in the PLR-2SAS failure).

### Mechanism 3: Contextual POMDP with Procedural Generation Enables Implicit Curricula
- **Claim:** Formulating penetration testing as a Contextual POMDP with a procedurally generated, guaranteed-solvable network distribution provides a principled basis for studying zero-shot policy transfer (ZSPT) and implicit curricula.
- **Mechanism:** Each episode's context `c` is a full network instance sampled from a distribution `p(c)` defined by generation parameters (e.g., `t_d`). The policy is trained across this distribution. PLR then prioritizes replay of contexts with high regret, creating a curriculum. Training on sparse (`low t_d`) distributions creates an implicit curriculum of shorter-horizon problems that generalizes to denser ones.
- **Core assumption:** A context-conditioned policy can generalize to new contexts if the training distribution covers sufficient diversity, and regret-based replay surfaces learnable difficult contexts.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract mentions the Contextual POMDP formulation "provides a principled basis for studying zero-shot policy generalization." Section 4.5 describes the network generation pipeline. Section 6.4 (Figure 5) shows sparse-training (`t_d=0.06`) yields better generalization to denser (`t_d=0.15`) networks than training on the target density.
- **Break condition:** Fails if generation produces unsolvable networks, if the context space is too diverse for a single policy, or if replay prioritization distorts the context distribution (as nearly happened in the PLR-2SAS failure).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Partial Observability (POMDP)
  - **Why needed here:** The agent only sees outcomes of its actions, not the full network state. It must infer topology and vulnerabilities from history, which is core to the problem's realism.
  - **Quick check question:** If an OS scan on host `h` fails, what are the *two* main possible inferences a competent agent should update in its belief state?
- **Concept:** Contextual MDP / Distributional RL
  - **Why needed here:** The network is a *distribution* over instances, not a single MDP. Generalization means performing well on unseen network *contexts*. This is key to interpreting ZSPT results and the role of DR/PLR.
  - **Quick check question:** Why is training on networks with `t_d=0.06` and evaluating on `t_d=0.15` considered a *zero-shot* transfer task?
- **Concept:** Large, Structured Action Spaces
  - **Why needed here:** The action space size scales with `|H| × (vulnerable services + OS combinations)`. This is the primary scaling challenge addressed by 2SAS.
  - **Quick check question:** For a 40-host network with 3 services and 2 OSes, approximately how many `Exploit` actions exist in the flat space? How does 2SAS reduce the decision complexity for selecting an `Exploit` on a specific host?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Network Generator -> NASimJax (vectorized env) -> PPO Agent (with either flat masking head or 2SAS twin heads) -> UED Buffer (for PLR) -> Reward Scaler
- **Critical path:** For each training iteration: (1) Sample a batch of contexts from the generator or UED buffer, (2) Reset parallel environments with these contexts, (3) Collect rollouts using the current policy, (4) Store transitions and contexts in UED buffer if using PLR, (5) Compute advantages with rewards scaled by `N_s × V_h`, (6) Perform PPO update. For PLR, a separate "explore" step constantly resets environments to fresh random contexts.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Speed vs. Realism:** JAX vectorization requires fixed host padding (static `N_h`), wasting memory on sparse networks. Realistic stochasticity in emulation is traded for throughput.
  - **2SAS Complexity vs. Scalability:** 2SAS introduces architectural complexity (host embedding, dual losses) but is necessary for >26 hosts. It trades a simpler, more stable flat policy for a more brittle but scalable factored one.
  - **PLR Exploration Brute-force vs. Buffer Curation:** PLR's "explore" step constantly resets to fresh random contexts. This is cheap but generates mostly failed trajectories for long-horizon 40-host tasks, poisoning the 2SAS agent.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **PLR-2SAS Collapse:** Sudden drop to 0% solve rate at medium density on large networks (40 hosts, `t_d=0.15`). Diagnose by checking replay buffer fill ratio and success rate of "exploration" rollouts.
  - **Action Masking Degradation:** Solve rate plateaus or drops as network size increases, while 2SAS continues to improve (Figure 4).
  - **Reward Scale Instability:** If `N_s × V_h` scaling is omitted, PLR prioritizes large, not difficult, contexts; training variance increases.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Speed Benchmark:** Train a trivial policy (e.g., always `Subnet Scan`) with 4096 envs. Verify ≥1M steps/sec on a mid-range GPU. *Goal: Confirm JAX toolchain and vectorized environment.*
  2.  **Single-Context Sanity Check:** Fix a small, solvable context (16 hosts, `t_d=0.06`). Train a masked PPO agent. Expect ~100% solve rate in <50M steps. *Goal: Validate single-context learnability; rule out bugs.*
  3.  **2SAS Verification:** On a 26-host network (`t_d=0.12`), compare 2SAS vs. flat-masking (both with DR). Verify 2SAS reaches higher terminal solve rate and learns faster. *Goal: Confirm 2SAS implementation provides expected scaling benefit.*

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** How can the destructive interaction between PLR’s episode-reset behaviour and 2SAS’s credit assignment be mitigated to prevent complete collapse at larger network scales?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper identifies the failure where PLR’s exploration resetting generates failed trajectories that, combined with 2SAS’s decomposed policy, cause catastrophic degradation (0% solve at 40 hosts, `t_d=0.15`). PLR⊥ avoids it by withholding updates during exploration, but this sacrifices exploration benefits.
  - **Why unresolved:** The root cause—bidirectional credit misattribution in 2SAS due to PLR’s resets—is understood, but no solution retains PLR’s exploration benefits while training 2SAS.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** A modified PLR or 2SAS variant that maintains high solve rates with PLR’s exploration branch (e.g., by isolating stage-specific advantages). Empirical success on 40+ host networks would validate the fix.
- **Question:** What are the fundamental breaking points of current RL methods for penetration testing when scaling network size and complexity?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors position NASimJax as “a configurable platform for investigating the breaking points of current methods under controlled conditions,” and their experiments revealed performance degradation at 40 hosts and certain densities.
  - **Why unresolved:** While specific failures (e.g., PLR-2SAS) are noted, a systematic map of where and why methods fail—considering interactions between network size, topology density, service/process density, and UED algorithms—is lacking.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Comprehensive scalability experiments across the full spectrum of network generation parameters, measuring solve rates for each combination of UED (DR, PLR, PLR⊥) and action selection (masking, 2SAS). Results would delineate performance boundaries.
- **Question:** Does the implicit curriculum effect from training on sparse topologies stem from reduced horizon length, and can it be deliberately harnessed to improve zero-shot transfer?
  - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Training on low-density networks yields better generalization to denser ones, attributed to an implicit curriculum of shorter-horizon problems. However, the causal factor is not isolated.
  - **Why unresolved:** The explanation is plausible but not exclusive; other factors like smaller effective action spaces could contribute. Without causal evidence, the mechanism remains speculative.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation experiments decoupling horizon length from density (e.g., limiting steps in dense networks), or curriculum schedules that gradually increase density. A deliberately shaped curriculum surpassing random density sampling would confirm the hypothesis.

## Limitations
- **Action Representation Generalizability:** The 2SAS decomposition's success is proven up to 40 hosts, but its credit assignment mechanism on novel topologies is unvalidated, evidenced by the PLR-2SAS collapse.
- **Scalability to Realistic Networks:** The fixed host padding scheme wastes memory on sparse graphs; it is unclear if the JAX implementation can scale to real-world enterprise networks (100s of hosts) without memory optimization.
- **Failure Mode Root Cause:** The PLR-2SAS collapse is diagnosed but not fully ablated; the precise dynamics of stage-specific failure are unknown.

## Confidence
- **GPU Acceleration & Throughput (100× speedup):** High. Directly measured (Section 6.1); mechanism is standard JAX vectorization.
- **2SAS vs. Flat Masking Scalability:** High. Consistent, growing gap across 16/26/40 hosts (Figure 4).
- **Implicit Curriculum via Sparse Training:** Medium. Supported by Figure 5, but limited to `t_d` variation; not tested on other generative parameters.
- **PLR Robustness & Failure Mode:** Low. PLR helps at 26 hosts but catastrophically fails with 2SAS at 40 hosts, indicating fragile, poorly understood interactions.

## Next Checks

2.  **PLR-2SAS Collapse Ablation:** At 40 hosts, `t_d=0.15`, compare (a) DR+2SAS, (b) PLR+Flat Masking, (c) PLR+2SAS. Log per-stage success rates and replay buffer success ratios to pinpoint the failure locus.
3.  **Cross-Density Zero-Shot Evaluation:** Train on sparse `t_d=0.06`, then evaluate zero-shot not only on `t_d=0.15` but also on networks with extreme `svc_d=0.3` or asymmetric subnet connectivity to test the implicit curriculum's transfer limits.
