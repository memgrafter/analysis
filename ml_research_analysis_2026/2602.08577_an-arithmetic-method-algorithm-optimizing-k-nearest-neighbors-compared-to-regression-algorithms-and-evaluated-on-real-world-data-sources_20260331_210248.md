# An arithmetic method algorithm optimizing k-nearest neighbors compared to regression algorithms and evaluated on real world data sources

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.08577
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08577
- **Reference count:** 39
- **Primary result:** AMR achieves lower MAE than k-NN on 15 of 17 real-world datasets and often outperforms LR, DT, SVR, RF, XGBoost, and CNN with statistical significance, but incurs substantially higher computational time.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Arithmetic Method Regression (AMR), a hybrid algorithm that combines a global linear model (obtained via the Arithmetic Method Algorithm, AMA) with local k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) predictions. For each test instance, AMR computes a prediction by convexly blending the AMA output and the average target of neighbors selected by a Manhattan distance-based heuristic (`distk < delta·distmin`). The blending weight `alpha` and the neighbor expansion factor `delta` are optimized via grid search to minimize mean absolute error (MAE) under leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). Evaluated on 17 real-world datasets, AMR achieves lower MAE than k-NN on 15 datasets and frequently surpasses a suite of regression baselines (including linear regression, decision trees, support vector regression, random forests, XGBoost, and a convolutional neural network) with statistically significant improvements (p < 0.05). However, AMR’s computational cost is substantially higher due to the expensive LOOCV-based hyperparameter tuning and the need to compute AMA coefficients for each training instance.

## Method Summary
AMR consists of two predictive models: (1) AMA, which solves a linear system for each training point using an arithmetic method that approximates the Moore–Penrose pseudo‑inverse, yielding a coefficient vector `a_i` such that `y_i ≈ a_i^T x_i`; and (2) a k‑NN module that uses Manhattan distance and an adaptive neighbor threshold (`delta`) to select local training points. For a test instance `x_test`, the algorithm retrieves all training points, computes their Manhattan distances, and selects those with distance less than `delta * min_distance`. The final prediction is a weighted average: `ŷ = alpha * mean(a_j^T x_test) + (1-alpha) * mean(y_j)` over selected neighbors. Hyperparameters `alpha` (in (0,1]) and `delta` (in [1,10]) are tuned by exhaustive grid search with LOOCV to minimize MAE. The method is compared against standard regressors (k‑NN with fixed k, linear regression, decision trees, SVR, random forests, XGBoost, and a 1D CNN) using the same LOOCV protocol, with performance evaluated via MAE, MSE, RMSE, R², and execution time. Statistical significance is assessed via two‑tailed permutation tests (5000 permutations).

## Key Results
- **Lower MAE than k‑NN:** AMR achieves a lower mean absolute error than standard k‑NN on 15 out of 17 datasets.
- **Competitive with strong baselines:** AMR often outperforms linear regression, decision trees, SVR, random forests, XGBoost, and a CNN, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05).
- **High computational cost:** AMR’s execution time is substantially higher than k‑NN and many other algorithms, sometimes by orders of magnitude, due to the LOOCV‑based grid search and per‑instance AMA computations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Blending global and local predictions
- **Claim:** AMR improves predictive accuracy by convexly combining a global linear model (AMA) with local k‑NN predictions, optimizing the blend weight via grid search to minimize MAE.
- **Mechanism:** For each test instance, the AMA module yields a linear prediction derived from coefficients fitted on the training set. The k‑NN module returns the average target of neighbors selected by a distance‑threshold heuristic (`distk < delta·distmin`). A weight α ∈ (0,1] controls the contribution: `ŷ = α·ŷ_AMA + (1−α)·ŷ_kNN`. The hyperparameters (α, δ) are tuned by leave‑one‑out cross‑validation, seeking the lowest MAE.
- **Core assumption:** AMA and k‑NN capture complementary aspects (global trend vs. local fluctuations); blending them reduces both bias and variance.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper’s abstract and mathematical foundations derive the theoretical optimal α and describe the empirical grid‑search approximation; neighboring works focus on isolated k‑NN enhancements, supporting the novelty of the hybrid.
- **Break condition:** If AMA and k‑NN predictions are nearly collinear across samples, blending adds little; a coarse grid may miss the true optimum.

### Mechanism 2: Arithmetic Method Algorithm for stable linear solves
- **Claim:** The Arithmetic Method Algorithm (AMA) solves linear systems efficiently with low computational overhead and provable stability, serving as a robust global linear model.
- **Mechanism:** AMA treats the linear system *Ax = b* via a linear operator *L_AMA* that approximates the Moore–Penrose left‑inverse. The implementation avoids explicit matrix inversion by direct arithmetic relations among coefficients and targets, yielding the solution *x̂ = L_AMA b*. Theorem 1 guarantees existence, a residual bound, and Lipschitz stability under input perturbations.
- **Core assumption:** The true relationship between regressors and target is approximately linear; the arithmetic method provides a stable approximate inverse even for over‑determined systems.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper’s section on mathematical foundations presents Theorem 1 and validates with numerical experiments showing small errors and decreasing execution times.
- **Break condition:** Severe ill‑conditioning or rank‑deficiency of *A* may cause large residuals; strong non‑linearity limits the benefit of a linear global model.

### Mechanism 3: Adaptive neighbor selection via delta heuristic
- **Claim:** The δ heuristic adaptively selects the number of k‑NN neighbors based on local data density, making the algorithm more robust to noise and sample size variations.
- **Mechanism:** For a query point, compute Manhattan distances to all training instances; let `dist_min` be the distance to the nearest neighbor. All points with distance `dist_k < delta·dist_min` are retained as neighbors; the corresponding *k* is data‑dependent. δ is tuned in [1,10] with step 0.1.
- **Core assumption:** Nearest neighbors are truly informative; controlled expansion beyond the absolute nearest neighbor mitigates outliers and stabilizes predictions.
- **Evidence anchors:** The algorithm description and pseudocode implement this; the paper also justifies Manhattan distance for robustness on small datasets.
- **Break condition:** δ too high includes distant irrelevant points; δ too low yields too few neighbors and high variance; distances may not be discriminative in high dimensions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
AMA Trainer (MFA) -> k-NN Module -> Blending Optimizer -> Predictor (PFA). The AMA Trainer fits the global linear model and precomputes coefficients for all training points; the k-NN Module stores training data and computes Manhattan distances; the Blending Optimizer performs grid search over α and δ using LOOCV; the Predictor combines AMA and k-NN predictions for test instances.

**Critical path:**  
1. Train AMA once on the full dataset to obtain coefficient matrix.  
2. For each (α, δ) pair in the grid, run full LOOCV: train AMA on the reduced training set, predict each left-out sample via PFA, accumulate MAE. This nested loop dominates runtime.  
3. Select (α_op, δ_op) with minimal average MAE.  
4. Deploy using stored AMA model and optimal hyperparameters to predict new instances.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Accuracy vs. time:* Finer grid steps improve optimization but multiply the O(n²·m) LOOCV cost.  
- *Distance metric:* Manhattan distance adds robustness on small datasets but may be less discriminative in high dimensions.  
- *Model complexity:* AMA remains linear; strong nonlinearities may require a more flexible global component.  
- *Delta range:* Fixed interval [1,10] may not generalize to datasets with vastly different density scales.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Excessive execution time (> thousands of seconds) due to O(n²·m) LOOCV.  
- No improvement over k‑NN (non‑significant MAE reduction) suggests poor complementarity or inadequate grid resolution.  
- High variance in MAE across folds indicates instability in neighbor selection or AMA coefficient estimation.  
- Numerical instability (exploding residuals) if AMA encounters near‑singular design matrices.

**First 3 experiments for verification:**

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Improving computational efficiency:** How can AMR’s high execution time be reduced (e.g., via approximate nearest neighbors or parallelization) without sacrificing accuracy? The paper acknowledges the trade‑off but provides no empirical exploration.  
2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** How do AMR’s hyperparameters (α, β, δ, k) affect performance across diverse datasets? No systematic sensitivity analysis is presented, leaving users without guidance for tuning.  
3. **Scalability to high dimensions:** How does AMR perform when the number of features exceeds 10,000? The evaluation was limited to ≤ 16 regressors; high‑dimensional behavior may introduce numerical and curse‑of‑dimensionality issues.  
4. **Alternative arithmetic methods:** Can other arithmetic formulations (e.g., regularized solvers, iterative methods) be integrated into AMR to enhance accuracy and versatility? The current AMA is just one instance; the broader class remains unexplored.

## Limitations
- The exact formula for AMA coefficients (the "arithmetic method") is not fully specified; the assumed minimum‑norm solution may differ from the authors’ implementation.  
- Baseline algorithms’ hyperparameters (e.g., k‑NN’s k, CNN architecture, SVR kernel parameters) are not described, forcing reliance on default settings that may not match the original experiments.  
- Feature selection thresholds and the precise grid‑search configuration (step sizes, LOOCV details) are absent, creating ambiguity in reproducing the reported results.

## Confidence
- **AMR superiority over k‑NN:** Medium – statistical tests are reported but rely on unspecified baselines.  
- **AMA stability/efficiency:** Low – theoretical guarantees exist but the exact arithmetic method is unclear; no independent validation of numerical performance.  
- **Delta heuristic adaptivity:** Medium – the principle is plausible, but sensitivity to the delta range and dataset density is not explored.  
- **Computational overhead claim:** High – the O(n²·m) LOOCV cost is explicitly described, making high execution time credible.
