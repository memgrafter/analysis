# QuantLRM: Quantization of Large Reasoning Models via Fine-Tuning Signals

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02581  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02581  
- **Reference count:** 17  
- **Primary result:** 3-bit weight-only quantization using fine-tuning signals yields up to 6.55% average accuracy improvement over state-of-the-art PTQ on reasoning benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
QuantLRM addresses the notorious difficulty of quantizing Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) to ultra-low bit-widths (e.g., 3-bit) by leveraging weight updates from fine-tuning. Existing post‑training quantization (PTQ) methods, which rely on activation statistics, perform poorly on LRMs. The core innovation is a “protecting both ends” hypothesis: the smallest and largest weight updates during fine‑tuning are most critical for preserving model capabilities. This insight is encoded in a piecewise quadratic importance function that assigns high scores to extreme updates, multiplied by the count of zero updates per channel. The resulting importance is injected into an AWQ‑like scaling search, leading to significant accuracy gains on reasoning tasks with minimal overhead.

## Method Summary
Given a base model and its fine‑tuned counterpart (or a pseudo‑fine‑tuned version), QuantLRM computes per‑weight absolute updates ΔW = |W′ − W|. For each input channel, zeros are excluded, the median Δw_mid is found, and two restricted quadratic functions (one for updates ≤ median, one for > median) map updates to a U‑shaped importance score f(Δw) with y_min = 1 and y_max = 10. Channel importance I_c = (mean of f(Δw) across the channel) × (Z_c + 1), where Z_c is the number of zero updates, amplifies channels with many frozen parameters. I_c is then raised to an exponent α (searched on a small calibration set) to form scaling factors s = (I_c)^α that intervene in the quantization loss L(s) = ||Q(W·diag(s))(diag(s)^{-1}·X) − WX||. The optimal α minimizes L(s); thereafter weights are quantized to 3 bits via round‑to‑nearest.

## Key Results
- On Olmo-3-7B-Think (reinforcement learning fine‑tuned), QuantLRM achieves an average accuracy improvement of 6.55% over GPTQ, AWQ, GPTAQ, and ANY3 on AIME-120, FOLIO, temporal sequences, and GPQA-Diamond, using one of the smallest calibration sets.
- Gains of 1.65% to 3.03% are observed on supervised fine‑tuned models.
- For models lacking fine‑tuning traces, pseudo‑fine‑tuning (a few thousand SFT steps) yields effective signals, enabling QuantLRM to surpass all baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** The smallest and largest weight updates during fine‑tuning are more important than intermediate ones (“protecting both ends”).  
**Mechanism:** For each channel, the distribution of absolute updates ΔW is split at the median. A quadratic mapping is applied separately to the lower and upper halves, assigning higher importance to extreme low and high updates. This reflects that minimal changes preserve general capabilities while maximal changes encode task‑specific adaptation.  
**Core assumption:** Fine‑tuning traces capture channel‑wise relevance to the downstream reasoning task; extreme updates indicate parameters that must be protected.  
**Evidence:** Mixed‑precision experiments (Table 1) show “SFT both ends” outperforms “SFT mid” and activation‑based signals.  
**Break condition:** If ΔW were unimodal and symmetric, the split would lose discriminative power; the hypothesis relies on a skewed or clustered distribution.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A restricted quadratic function effectively transforms raw update magnitudes into U‑shaped channel importance scores.  
**Mechanism:** Each non‑zero Δw is mapped via a piecewise quadratic f(Δw) (Equation 5). The left (Δw ≤ median) and right (Δw > median) quadratics are anchored at (median, y_min=1) and (extreme, y_max=10), producing a U‑shape without overfitting.  
**Core assumption:** Importance grows non‑linearly as updates depart from the median, and a fixed quadratic approximates this well.  
**Evidence:** Ablation in Table 3 shows “both ends” significantly beats “mid” on R1‑Qwen‑32B.  
**Break condition:** If the true importance relationship were linear or followed a different curve (e.g., exponential), the fixed quadratic would be suboptimal.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Multiplying the mean quadratic score by the count of zero updates per channel (Z_c + 1) further highlights channels with many frozen parameters.  
**Mechanism:** After computing the average f(Δw) for a channel, it is scaled by (Z_c + 1). This boosts importance for channels where many weights did not update (Δw = 0), under the assumption that such stability signals criticality for base capabilities.  
**Core assumption:** A high prevalence of zero updates indicates a “protected” state; such channels deserve higher precision.  
**Evidence:** Table 3 for R1‑Qwen‑32B shows adding zero handling improves average accuracy by 5.95%.  
**Break condition:** If zero updates were random or denoted redundancy, this scaling would harm performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Signal Extractor → Importance Calculator → Quantization Injector → Quantization Engine  
- **Critical path:** Load base model weights W and fine‑tuned weights W′ → Compute ΔW → For each linear layer, compute per‑channel importance I_c → Search optimal exponent α per module using calibration data → Form scaling factors s = (I_c)^α → Apply scaling and quantize weights to target bit‑width → Save quantized model.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Uses fine‑tuning signals instead of activations, adding ~2.5 minutes prep time but improving accuracy on reasoning tasks; requires access to both checkpoints (or pseudo‑fine‑tuning).  
  - Zero‑update multiplication uses fixed hyperparameters (y_min=1, y_max=10) which may not be optimal for all distributions.  
  - MLP modules are sliced to prevent overflow in large models, introducing model‑specific handling.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Missing fine‑tuned checkpoint:** Performance drops to baseline PTQ. Workaround: run pseudo‑fine‑tuning (few thousand SFT steps) to generate ΔW.  
  - **Numerical overflow in I_c:** For very large models, monitor I_c values; if they exceed float16 range, slice ΔW for MLP modules (e.g., into 2–5 pieces) as in Section 4.4.  
  - **Poor results without zero handling:** Ensure zeros are included in the scaling factor; verify that channels with Z_c > 0 are boosted.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Hypothesis validation:** On R1‑Distill‑Qwen‑32B, run mixed‑precision experiments protecting top 5% channels using different signals (activation, SFT‑mid, SFT‑both‑ends). Confirm “both ends” yields highest average accuracy (Table 1).  
  2. **Ablation on zero handling:** For R1‑Qwen‑32B, compare QuantLRM with and without zero exclusion in quadratic fitting and with/without zero‑count multiplication. Quantify the gain from each component (Table 3).  
  3. **Pseudo‑fine‑tuning curve:** For Qwen3‑1.7B, run QuantLRM with increasing pseudo‑SFT steps (0 to 3000+). Plot accuracy vs. steps (Figure 3) to identify the minimal steps needed to surpass AWQ baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** What is the underlying mechanistic or architectural reason why the smallest and largest weight updates (“both ends”) during reasoning fine‑tuning are most predictive of channel importance for quantization?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper empirically validates the “protecting both ends” hypothesis but does not provide a theoretical explanation for why updates at both extremes contain denser information than intermediate magnitudes. The discussion links small updates to general capabilities and large updates to fine‑tuning traces, but the precise relationship to quantization error is not mechanistically dissected.  
**Why unresolved:** The approach is heuristic and validated empirically across models. Understanding the root cause could lead to more principled importance metrics beyond the fitted quadratic function.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Analytical studies (e.g., probing the Hessian or loss landscape changes) or controlled ablation experiments that systematically vary update distributions to isolate which property (e.g., alignment with principal components of the fine‑tuning update matrix, correlation with gradient norm) best predicts quantization‑induced performance drop.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** For models without available pre‑fine‑tuned checkpoints, what is the theoretically optimal or cost‑effective amount of pseudo‑fine‑tuning (e.g., training steps, data volume) needed to generate sufficiently informative weight update signals for QuantLRM?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper demonstrates that QuantLRM’s performance improves with more pseudo‑fine‑tuning steps on Qwen3‑1.7B and recommends “a few thousand training steps.” However, it does not establish a stopping criterion or analyze the trade‑off between the computational cost of pseudo‑fine‑tuning and the resulting quantization accuracy.  
**Why unresolved:** The recommendation is practical but not optimized. An arbitrary number of steps may be suboptimal for different model sizes or fine‑tuning data distributions, potentially wasting compute or yielding insufficient signals.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic study measuring QuantLRM’s accuracy against varying amounts of pseudo‑fine‑tuning compute (steps, tokens) across diverse model scales and architectures, aiming to find a Pareto frontier or define a signal sufficiency metric (e.g., convergence of channel importance scores).

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Is the specific design choice of a restricted quadratic function combined with zero‑update counting the optimal way to map weight update magnitudes to channel importance, or could alternative functional forms or statistical summaries of the update distribution yield better quantization performance?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper states it “fit[s] simple restricted quadratic functions” and multiplies by zero‑update count after validating this design against alternatives (e.g., “SFT mid,” different zero handling). It calls this the best design from limited ablation but does not claim global optimality.  
**Why unresolved:** The ablation study compares a few hand‑designed variants but does not explore a broader space of functions (e.g., higher‑order polynomials, non‑parametric mappings) or alternative aggregations of update statistics (e.g., kurtosis, entropy) per channel.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A comprehensive hyperparameter search or learnable mapping (e.g., a small network) trained to predict optimal quantization scaling factors, evaluated across multiple model families to see if a more expressive importance estimator consistently surpasses the fixed quadratic+count heuristic.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** To what extent does the effectiveness of QuantLRM’s fine‑tuning signal depend on the specific fine‑tuning paradigm (e.g., SFT vs. DPO vs. RL), and can the signal be standardized or normalized to work uniformly across all paradigms without paradigm‑specific tuning?  
**Basis in paper:** The method is applied to SFT, DPO, and RL fine‑tuned models, and the paper notes the gain is smallest on DPO, attributing it to “relatively weak fine‑tuning signals.” It uses different update pairs (e.g., RL from DPO checkpoints) but does not deeply analyze the signal quality per paradigm.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper uses the same importance computation formula for all paradigms. If DPO updates are fundamentally noisier or less structured for quantization, a paradigm‑specific adaptation (e.g., different scaling of `ymin`/`ymax`, filtering criteria) might be needed for uniform gains.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed comparative analysis of the distribution of weight updates (magnitude, sparsity, layer‑wise patterns) produced by SFT, DPO, and RL on the same base model, correlated with QuantLRM’s success rates. This could reveal if a single formulation is sufficient or if paradigm‑aware modifications are necessary.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** Does QuantLRM’s advantage over activation‑based PTQ (like AWQ) hold for non‑reasoning‑oriented large language models (e.g., general chat or code models) and for other architectures (e.g., Mixture‑of‑Experts models), or is it specifically tailored to the weight update characteristics of reasoning‑incentivized LRMs?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly focuses on “Large Reasoning Models (LRMs)” and states that existing PTQ is less competitive on them. All experiments are on reasoning‑oriented models (Olmo‑Think, R1 distillation). It mentions applicability to non‑fine‑tuned LRMs via pseudo‑fine‑tuning but does not test on standard non‑reasoning LLMs.  
**Why unresolved:** The “protecting both ends” hypothesis is motivated by reasoning fine‑tuning. Non‑reasoning models may have different weight update dynamics during their (often different) fine‑tuning, potentially making the heuristic less effective or even detrimental.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Running QuantLRM on standard, non‑reasoning fine‑tuned LLMs (e.g., Llama‑chat, CodeLlama) across the same 3‑bit W3A16 setting and comparing against AWQ/GPTQ. If the gains disappear or reverse, it would confirm LRM‑specificity; if gains persist, it suggests broader applicability.

## Limitations
- **Trace dependency:** Requires both base and fine‑tuned checkpoints; pseudo‑fine‑tuning may not fully replicate true update distributions.
- **Calibration underspec:** The small calibration set (128 C4 samples length 512) lacks preprocessing details, which could affect reproducibility.
- **MLP slicing ad hoc:** Overflow handling via model‑specific slicing is not generalized and may require manual tuning.
- **Task scope limited:** Evaluated only on reasoning benchmarks; generalizability to other tasks and model architectures (e.g., MoE) remains unverified.
- **Fixed hyperparameters:** Quadratic endpoints (y_min=1, y_max=10) and zero scaling (Z_c+1) are untuned and could be suboptimal for some models.
