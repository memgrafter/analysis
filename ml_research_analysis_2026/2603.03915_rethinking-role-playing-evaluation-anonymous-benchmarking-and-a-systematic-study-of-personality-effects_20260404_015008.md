# Rethinking Role-Playing Evaluation: Anonymous Benchmarking and a Systematic Study of Personality Effects
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03915
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03915
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: Anonymizing character names exposes memorization bias; augmenting prompts with personality traits (MBTI/Big Five) restores and improves role-playing performance, with self-generated traits matching human-annotated ones.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates evaluation bias in role-playing agents (RPAs) caused by models' reliance on memorized knowledge of famous characters. The authors propose anonymizing character names in prompts, which significantly degrades performance on established benchmarks (CharacterEval, RoleAgentBench), confirming that models exploit name-based priors. To compensate, they augment prompts with structured personality information derived from three sources: self-report (model-generated MBTI answers), interview-based inference, and crowdsourced databases (PDB). Incorporating personality cues consistently improves RPA fidelity under anonymity. Notably, self-generated personality profiles perform on par with human-annotated ones, offering a scalable, annotation-free approach. The study thus advances more robust evaluation and practical prompting strategies for character-based dialogue systems.

## Method Summary
The methodology centers on two interventions: anonymization and personality augmentation. For evaluation, character names and all proper-name variants in prompts are replaced with the token `<anonymous character>` to force models to rely solely on provided descriptions. Role-playing agents (e.g., GPT-4o, Llama) generating responses are assessed using either a reward model (CharacterEval) or pairwise LLM-as-a-judge comparisons (RoleAgentBench). To improve performance under anonymity, the authors supplement prompts with MBTI or Big Five personality specifications obtained from three sources: (1) self-report, where the RPA answers standardized items to infer a type; (2) interview-based, where a separate model generates open-ended Q&A from which MBTI is inferred; (3) crowdsourced annotations from PDB. Personality traits are appended to the system prompt alongside the character profile. The study compares baseline anonymous performance against each augmentation condition across models and datasets, measuring score improvements and win rates.

## Key Results
- Anonymization causes a significant drop in role-playing performance across multiple models and benchmarks, evidencing memorization bias.
- Adding personality information (MBTI or Big Five) markedly improves scores in CharacterEval and win rates in RoleAgentBench for all three sources (self-report, interview, PDB).
- Self-generated personality traits achieve performance comparable to human-annotated crowdsourced traits, demonstrating that external annotation is not necessary for effective guidance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Anonymization Forces Description-Based Reliance
- **Claim:** Replacing character names with `<anonymous character>` removes explicit identity cues, preventing the model from relying on memorized prior knowledge about famous characters.
- **Mechanism:** The evaluation pipeline substitutes all proper name occurrences in the prompt, leaving only the semantic content of the character profile and dialogue history to condition generation.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs have latent knowledge about fictional characters from pretraining that they default to when names are present.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes that anonymization "significantly degrades role-playing performance" and "confirms that name exposure carries implicit information." Section 2.2 details the replacement process; Section 2.5 presents results showing lower scores/win rates in the anonymous condition across CharacterEval and RoleAgentBench.
- **Break condition:** If anonymization does not cause a significant performance drop, the model might not be relying on memorized associations, or the character is sufficiently obscure.

### Mechanism 2: Personality Traits Provide Behavioral Scaffolding
- **Claim:** Augmenting prompts with structured personality information (e.g., MBTI types) improves role-playing fidelity under anonymous conditions.
- **Mechanism:** Personality frameworks offer a concise, abstract target (e.g., "INTJ") that guides tone, decision-making, and interaction style, compensating for the loss of name-based cues.
- **Core assumption:** MBTI/Big Five are psychologically valid and computationally tractable abstractions that LLMs can use to steer generation.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states that "incorporating personality information consistently improves RPA performance." Section 3.2 details the augmentation; Sections 3.3 and Table 3 report statistically significant score increases; Section 4.1 extends findings to Big Five.
- **Break condition:** If personality augmentation fails to improve (or worsens) performance, the model may be unable to map abstract trait definitions to concrete dialogue behavior, or the trait format is mismatched.

### Mechanism 3: Self-Generated Traits Match Human Annotations
- **Claim:** Personality traits self-generated by the LLM via in-context instructions yield performance comparable to human-annotated traits from PDB.
- **Mechanism:** The model introspects the character description to infer a plausible profile, creating a coherent internal representation that substitutes for external annotation.
- **Core assumption:** An LLM can perform a metacognitive step to produce a self-consistent personality that aligns with its subsequent role-play outputs.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract highlights self-generated traits achieve comparable performance. Section 3.3 states all three sources enhance performance similarly. Figure 3 shows overlapping win rates for SelfReport, Interview, and PDB. Section 4.2 notes gains persist for characters with stronger personality types.
- **Break condition:** If self-generated traits consistently underperform, it indicates a limitation in the model's capacity for accurate self-simulation, requiring more sophisticated prompting or external validation.

## Foundational Learning
### Evaluation Bias via Memorization
- **Why needed:** Standard benchmarks using famous characters test memory recall more than role-play skill; this paper corrects that methodological flaw.
- **Quick check:** If an LLM scores perfectly on a Harry Potter role-play by recalling canon facts, does that indicate good role-playing ability or good memory?

### Personality as Actionable Abstraction
- **Why needed:** The paper operationalizes abstract psychology (MBTI/Big Five) as a prompt engineering tool; understanding why a type label (e.g., "INTJ") guides generation differently than a list of factual traits is key.
- **Quick check:** How might providing "INTJ" affect dialogue outputs compared to "enjoys strategic planning, reserved, values competence"?

### Self-Generated vs. External Annotation
- **Why needed:** The scalability insight is that models can create their own effective guidance, challenging the need for human curation.
- **Quick check:** What risks arise if the role-playing agent defines its own personality? Could it lead to circular, self-confirming, or inconsistent traits?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input Processor -> Anonymization Module -> Personality Module (optional) -> Prompt Composer -> RPA Core -> Evaluation Engine

### Critical Path
Raw character data undergoes anonymization, optionally acquires personality traits, is composed into a prompt, triggers a response from the RPA, and is evaluated by the benchmark.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Anonymization Depth:** Simple name replacement vs. scrubbing nicknames, honorifics, or iconic objects. Deeper scrubbing may remove useful cues.
- **Trait Format:** MBTI (categorical, simple) vs. Big Five (dimensional, nuanced). MBTI easier to prompt; Big Five offers granularity but may be harder to act on precisely.
- **Trait Source Latency:** SelfReport (fast, single call) vs. Interview (slower, multi-step) vs. PDB lookup (fast but limited coverage).
- **Personality Placement:** Should traits appear in the system prompt or user message? Paper uses system prompt.

### Failure Signatures
- **Anonymization Failure:** Model still generates character-specific responses despite `<anonymous character>`, indicating inference from other profile cues.
- **Trait Mismatch:** Generated dialogue violates the stated personality (e.g., an "INTJ" being overly emotional).
- **Annotation Gap:** For PDB, many characters lack annotations, causing the pipeline to skip augmentation.
- **Model Sensitivity:** Some models show larger absolute drops from anonymization (e.g., GPT-4o), suggesting higher dependence on memorized priors.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline Anonymization Impact:** Run original vs. anonymized prompts on a held-out set of characters from CharacterEval. Confirm significant performance degradation to validate memorization bias in the target model.
2. **Personality Source Efficacy:** For a common subset of PDB-annotated characters, compare three conditions: (a) No traits, (b) SelfReport traits, (c) PDB traits. Measure delta in CharacterEval scores and RoleAgentBench win rates. Expect all three to outperform baseline, with no significant difference between SelfReport and PDB.
3. **Trait Format Comparison:** On the same subset, compare MBTI augmentation vs. Big Five augmentation. Assess which yields higher gains in "Character Consistency" (CharacterEval) and "General Response" (RoleAgentBench). Expect both to improve over no traits, but magnitude may differ by model.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Non-name cues in anonymization:** To what extent do residual textual signals (e.g., interlocutor names, nicknames, distinctive traits) leak identity despite name replacement? The paper acknowledges this but only tested simple name replacement. Ablation studies removing increasingly specific clues are needed.
2. **Differential model response to personality augmentation:** Why does the magnitude of improvement vary across model architectures? Controlled experiments across model scales and training regimes could isolate causal factors like base model size or instruction-tuning quality.
3. **MBTI vs. Big Five effectiveness:** Which psychological framework provides more robust guidance for LLM role-playing, and can they be combined synergistically? Direct head-to-head evaluation and hybrid prompting would clarify.
4. **Nonlinear effects of trait strength:** Does performance improvement correlate linearly with trait extremity (e.g., >60%/ <40% scores), or are there thresholds/diminishing returns? Regression analyses and experiments manipulating trait strength would answer this.
5. **Generalization to real individuals:** The study focused on fictional characters with PDB annotations. How well does personality-augmented role-playing transfer to real humans, where ground-truth traits are sparse or inferred? Testing on real-persona datasets (e.g., social media profiles) is required.

## Limitations
- **Undisclosed prompt engineering details:** The exact wording and structure for MBTI self-report items, interview questions, and gemini-2.0-flash inference prompt are not provided, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Limited Big Five generalizability:** The Big Five experiment uses only 9 characters with PDB scores, reducing statistical power and generalizability compared to larger MBTI studies; mapping between MBTI and Big Five is also unspecified.
- **Anonymization completeness:** Simple replacement with `<anonymous character>` may not catch all indirect references (e.g., "the Boy Who Lived"), potentially leaking identity cues and diluting the measured bias effect.

## Confidence
- **Anonymization exposes memorization bias:** High (consistent significant drops across benchmarks and models).
- **Personality augmentation improves fidelity:** High (statistically significant gains for all three sources across both major benchmarks).
- **Self-generated traits rival human annotations:** Medium (evidence shows overlapping win rates for the tested character set and MBTI; less certain for Big Five and broader character pools due to small sample sizes).

## Next Checks
1. **Audit anonymization leakage:** Manually inspect a stratified sample of anonymized prompts for residual identity cues; augment replacement rules and re-run baseline evaluations to quantify leakage magnitude.
2. **Standardize and release trait prompts:** Implement the self-report, interview, and PDB retrieval pipelines using only the descriptions in the paper; publish exact prompt templates; validate that they recover reported performance deltas on a held-out subset.
3. **Expand Big Five evaluation:** Replicate Section 4.2 using the full PDB-overlap subset (≈25 characters) to test whether the observed trend holds with adequate statistical power and whether effect size differs between MBTI and Big Five.
