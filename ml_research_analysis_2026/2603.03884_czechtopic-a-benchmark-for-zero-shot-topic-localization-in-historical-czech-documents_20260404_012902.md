# CzechTopic: A Benchmark for Zero-Shot Topic Localization in Historical Czech Documents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03884  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03884  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Human baseline word-level F1 66.4–72.1 (Krippendorff’s α=0.616); best LLM (gpt-5-2) 61.1 word F1, 80.6 text F1; fine-tuned BERT (robeczech) 48.3 word F1; matching extraction yields +0.104 word-F1 over tagging.

## Executive Summary
CzechTopic introduces a benchmark for zero‑shot topic localization in historical Czech documents, a task that requires identifying all spans expressing a given predefined topic within a text. The benchmark is built through a two‑phase human annotation process: first an annotator defines topics for a cluster and marks relevant spans; then multiple other annotators independently localize those same topics on the same texts. This yields multi‑annotator span judgments, and evaluation is performed relative to inter‑annotator agreement rather than a single gold standard. To scale training data, the authors distill a large development set using `gpt‑5‑mini` in a pipeline that mirrors the human protocol. Experiments compare fine‑tuned BERT models and various LLMs, showing that matching span extraction (outputting the span text) consistently outperforms tagging (inserting markers), but even the best LLM still trails human performance. The work highlights the challenges of precise span localization and proposes evaluation grounded in human agreement.

## Method Summary
The dataset comprises historical Czech OCR documents grouped into clusters of five related texts. In Phase 1, an annotator creates a topic (name + description) and marks all spans in the cluster that express that topic. In Phase 2, several other annotators independently localize the same predefined topics in the same texts without seeing Phase 1 marks. This yields 525 texts, 363 topics, and 1,820 (text, topic) pairs with span annotations at the word level. A distilled development set of 15,550 texts is generated by `gpt‑5‑mini`: first the model proposes topics for a cluster, then localizes them in individual texts using matching extraction. For fine‑tuning, a BERT‑based cross‑encoder concatenates topic description and text, computes a token‑token similarity matrix, and assigns each text token the maximum similarity to any topic token. A threshold on these scores produces binary span predictions; the threshold is tuned on a human‑annotated validation split. For zero‑shot evaluation, LLMs are prompted either to tag the text with markers or to output the matching span text. Performance is measured with macro‑averaged word‑level and text‑level F1, IoU, and success rates, all referenced to human agreement baselines (pairwise F1, Krippendorff’s α).

## Key Results
- Human baseline: word-level F1 66.4–72.1 (Krippendorff’s α=0.616), text‑level F1 83.2. The best LLM (gpt‑5‑2) reaches 61.1 word F1 and 80.6 text F1; a fine‑tuned BERT (robeczech) obtains 48.3 word F1.
- Matching span extraction significantly outperforms tagging (+0.104 word‑F1, p<0.001); annotators show higher mutual agreement than with original topic authors (ΔF1=0.024).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Two‑Phase Human Annotation Ensures Consistent Topic Interpretation
- **Claim:** The two‑phase process (topic definition then localization under fixed topics) yields a dataset where span annotations reflect consistent topic interpretation rather than individual annotator intuition.
- **Mechanism:** In Phase 1, one annotator defines topics for a cluster and marks spans. In Phase 2, other annotators localize those same topics independently without seeing Phase 1 marks. This separates topic formulation from span marking, allowing measurement of agreement on the interpretation of written definitions.
- **Core assumption:** Written topic definitions (name + description) are sufficient for multiple humans to reliably identify the same topical spans, though some divergence from the original author is expected.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states evaluation is performed relative to human agreement; [section 3.2] details both phases and emphasizes Phase 2 annotators localize predefined topics without seeing Phase 1 annotations.
- **Break condition:** If Phase 2 inter‑annotator agreement (Krippendorff’s α) drops significantly below 0.616, the mechanism fails for this task.

### Mechanism 2: Distillation with a Teacher Model Scales Training Data While Preserving Task Structure
- **Claim:** The two‑stage distillation pipeline using `gpt‑5‑mini` creates a large development dataset that mirrors the human annotation protocol, enabling effective fine‑tuning of smaller BERT models.
- **Mechanism:** The teacher model first generates candidate topics for a cluster (Phase 1 analog), then localizes those topics in individual texts using the matching strategy (Phase 2 analog). The resulting (text, topic, span) tuples train a cross‑encoder BERT to compute token‑level similarity scores.
- **Core assumption:** The teacher model’s span annotations, despite noise, are structurally and semantically consistent enough to serve as training data for the discriminative localization function.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] mentions the distilled dataset enables BERT fine‑tuning; [section 3.4] describes the pipeline and its goal to maintain structural consistency with the human protocol.
- **Break condition:** If fine‑tuned BERT models show no improvement over a random or trivial baseline on the human test set, distillation quality is insufficient for transfer.

### Mechanism 3: Matching Span Extraction Outperforms Tagging for LLMs
- **Claim:** The choice of span extraction paradigm (matching vs. tagging) is a dominant factor in LLM performance; matching consistently yields higher scores because it avoids precise positional reasoning.
- **Mechanism:** Tagging requires the LLM to reconstruct the input text with inserted markers, demanding exact character/word position control. Matching only requires the LLM to generate the span content, which is then matched back to the original text via string search, avoiding positional errors.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs are competent at generating relevant snippets but have inherent limitations in generating precise positional offsets or reconstructing text with boundary markers.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] notes matching significantly outperforms tagging; [section 4.4 & Table 3] shows matching improves word F1 by 0.104 (p<0.001), the largest effect size.
- **Break condition:** If future LLMs with dramatically improved positional reasoning (e.g., via dedicated tokenization or output constraints) eliminate the performance gap, this mechanism would be superseded.

## Foundational Learning
- **Topic Localization vs. Classification/Segmentation**  
  *Why needed here:* The task is finer‑grained than document classification (one label per document) and differs from segmentation (contiguous blocks). Misunderstanding leads to misapplying standard techniques.  
  *Quick check:* What distinguishes topic localization from extractive QA? (Answer: Allows multiple, overlapping, non‑contiguous spans for the same topic.)

- **Evaluation Relative to Human Agreement**  
  *Why needed here:* Standard evaluation uses a single gold annotation. This work argues for evaluating against the distribution of human judgments because span boundaries are subjective. This affects interpretation of all scores.  
  *Quick check:* Why is Krippendorff’s α used instead of simple accuracy for the human baseline? (Answer: It measures agreement among multiple annotators, accounting for chance and multiple possible spans.)

- **Span Extraction Strategies (Tagging, Indexing, Matching)**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper’s core ablation and LLM results hinge on the choice of extraction paradigm. Understanding the trade‑offs is crucial for replicating or extending the work.  
  *Quick check:* Why is indexing (predicting start/end indices) generally avoided for LLMs in this work? (Answer: LLMs are unreliable at precise positional reasoning; see references [16,36].)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**  
  Human Annotations -> Distillation Engine (gpt‑5‑mini) -> Distilled Development Dataset -> BERT Fine‑tuning (Model A); Human Annotations -> LLM Prompter (Model B); Human Annotations + Model A + Model B -> Evaluation Module.

- **Critical Path**  
  Human Annotation -> Distillation -> BERT Fine‑tuning -> LLM Prompting -> Evaluation vs. Human Agreement.

- **Design Tradeoffs**  
  *Distilled vs. Human Data:* Scale (15k texts) vs. quality (teacher noise). Tradeoff: BERT models become competitive baselines but cannot exceed teacher quality significantly.  
  *Matching vs. Tagging:* Robustness (matching) vs. directness (tagging). Tradeoff: Matching avoids positional errors but requires post‑hoc string match that can fail on minor OCR/text variations.  
  *Cross‑encoder vs. LLM:* Efficiency & specialization (BERT) vs. generality & zero‑shot capability (LLMs). Tradeoff: Fine‑tuned BERT is more parameter‑efficient and performs stronger on the exact distribution, but LLMs generalize to unseen topic formulations without retraining.

- **Failure Signatures**  
  Low Inter‑Annotator Agreement (α < 0.5): Indicates topics are poorly defined or spans too subjective. Check annotation guidelines.  
  BERT Model Collapses to High Recall/Low Precision: Model predicts nearly all tokens as positive. Symptom of poor threshold selection or distillation noise. Diagnose by examining score distributions.  
  LLM Matching Failure: Generated span text does not appear verbatim in the document. Causes: LLM paraphrase, OCR errors, or topic not present. May require fuzzy matching or strategy adjustment.  
  Matching vs. Tagging Gap Disappears: If a new LLM shows no difference, it may indicate improved positional reasoning or previous prompt engineering flaws.

- **First 3 Experiments**  
  1. **Compute Human Baseline Replicability:** Using Phase 2 multi‑annotator data, calculate pairwise F1 scores (word & text level) and Krippendorff’s α. Establishes performance ceiling and variability threshold.  
  2. **Reproduce BERT Fine‑tuning:** Train `robeczech` on the distilled development set using the cross‑encoder setup (concatenation, similarity matrix, max pooling). Tune classification threshold on human‑annotated validation clusters (disjoint from train). Evaluate on human test set.  
  3. **Ablation: Span Extraction Strategy:** For a strong LLM (e.g., `gpt‑5‑2`), run both tagging and matching prompts (zero‑shot, same language) on a fixed subset. Measure ΔF1 to confirm matching advantage before full‑scale LLM evaluation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** What architectural or training innovations can enable models to achieve human‑level span localization for predefined topics?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion explicitly calls for "improved span localization methods" to address the performance gap.  
- **Why unresolved:** The best LLM (gpt‑5‑2) still lags behind human baseline by 7.6 word‑F1 points, and BERT models are lower.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Introduce modifications such as span‑specific heads, boundary‑aware losses, or multi‑task learning; evaluate on CzechTopic and compare to human agreement.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** How can evaluation metrics for span‑based tasks be more firmly grounded in human agreement, and what are the limitations of current pairwise F1?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion advocates for "evaluation paradigms grounded in human agreement."  
- **Why unresolved:** Current macro‑F1 against multiple annotators may not capture variance in span boundary judgments; Krippendorff’s α indicates moderate agreement.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Propose metrics that model annotator uncertainty, e.g., expectation‑maximization over latent annotations, validate against crowd annotations.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** Why do annotators show higher mutual agreement than with the original topic author, and how can topic definitions be refined to reduce this gap?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper reports a significant difference (ΔF1=0.024) between Phase 2 inter‑annotator and Phase 1‑Phase 2 agreement.  
- **Why unresolved:** Implicit aspects of topic intent may be lost in written descriptions, affecting consistency.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct ablation studies varying description length/detail; measure impact on agreement; possibly iterative refinement of definitions.

## Limitations
- **Human baseline validity:** Evaluation relative to pairwise agreement (Krippendorff’s α=0.616) acknowledges span subjectivity but may understate absolute system usability; a model matching human disagreement could still be practically useful.  
- **Distillation generalizability:** Results rely on `gpt‑5‑mini` (unpublicized model). Claims about distilled data quality cannot be externally verified without prompt/parameter details, limiting reproducibility.  
- **Task scope transferability:** Findings may not transfer to non‑historical Czech texts or languages with different morphological/OCR noise profiles.

## Confidence
- Human baseline & inter‑annotator reliability: **High** – directly computed from multi‑annotator data.  
- Distillation enabling BERT fine‑tuning: **Medium** – empirical benefit supported but mechanism opaque due to proprietary teacher.  
- Matching superiority over tagging: **High** – strong ablative evidence (ΔF1 0.104, p<0.001) across models.
