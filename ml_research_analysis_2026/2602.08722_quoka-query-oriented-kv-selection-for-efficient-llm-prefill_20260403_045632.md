# QUOKA: Query-Oriented KV Selection For Efficient LLM Prefill

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08722
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08722
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** QUOKA uses 88.2% fewer key‑value pairs while maintaining near‑baseline accuracy on long‑context benchmarks, delivering up to 5× attention speedup and 3× lower time‑to‑first‑token on Nvidia GPUs, and outperforming sparse‑attention competitors by 10–20%.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) suffer from high prefill latency due to quadratic attention complexity, especially for long contexts. Existing sparse attention methods often require training or are hardware‑specific. QUOKA introduces a novel, training‑free, hardware‑agnostic approach that dynamically selects a small subset of key‑value (KV) pairs from the cache based on geometric properties of the current queries. Its core insight is that queries with low cosine similarity to the mean query tend to attend broadly to many keys and have a disproportionate impact on the final attention logits. By retaining these representative queries and scoring keys via normalized cosine similarity—aggregating with a max over queries and a mean across grouped‑query attention (GQA) heads—QUOKA achieves substantial reductions in active KV pairs. Evaluations on Needle‑In‑A‑Haystack, RULER, LongBench, and Math500 show that QUOKA retains near‑baseline accuracy while using only 11.8% of KV pairs (a reduction of 88.2%). It yields up to 5× speedup in attention computation and 3× lower time‑to‑first‑token on Nvidia GPUs, and nearly 7× speedup on Intel Xeon CPUs, consistently outperforming other sparse attention techniques by 10–20% across diverse models and sparsity levels.

## Method Summary
QUOKA operates during the chunked prefill phase, where the input sequence is processed in sequential chunks while attending to a growing KV cache. For each chunk \(X_i\), the method projects it to queries \(Q_i\), keys \(K_i\), and values \(V_i\). Then, using the full prior cache \(\mathbf{K}_{<i}, \mathbf{V}_{<i}\), it performs query‑oriented KV selection:

1. **Query subselection:** Compute the mean query \(M_Q\) and retain \(N_Q\) queries with smallest cosine similarity to \(M_Q\) (most dissimilar).
2. **Scoring:** Normalize the retained queries \(Q\) and all cached keys \(K\) to unit length. For GQA models, average the normalized queries across query heads within each KV group to obtain \(\bar{Q}\). Compute the cosine similarity matrix \(\bar{Q}K^{\top}\).
3. **Aggregation:** Take the maximum score across the retained query dimension, yielding a per‑key importance score.
4. **Selection:** Choose the top \(B_{SA}\) keys and gather the corresponding key‑value pairs \((\hat{K}, \hat{V})\).
5. **Attention:** Run the standard attention kernel (e.g., FlashAttention) on the concatenated set \([\hat{K} \mid K_i]\) and \([\hat{V} \mid V_i]\) with \(Q_i\).
6. **Cache update:** Append \(K_i, V_i\) to the full KV cache for the next chunk.

The method is training‑free, relies solely on inexpensive linear algebra (means, normalization, matrix multiplications), and is designed to be hardware‑agnostic. Key hyperparameters include chunk size \(B_{CP}\), KV budget \(B_{SA}\), and query budget \(N_Q\). The cosine similarity metric provides a stable bounded proxy, while the max‑over‑queries and mean‑over‑GQA‑groups aggregations preserve the behavior of full attention.

## Key Results
- On long‑context benchmarks (Needle‑In‑A‑Haystack, RULER, LongBench, Math500), QUOKA achieves near‑baseline accuracy while using only 11.8% of the original KV cache (88.2% reduction).
- It delivers up to **5×** attention speedup and **3×** lower time‑to‑first‑token on Nvidia GPUs, and nearly **7×** speedup on Intel Xeon CPUs.
- QUOKA consistently outperforms competing sparse attention methods by **10–20%** across diverse model architectures and varying sparsity levels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Queries that are geometrically dissimilar (low cosine similarity) from the mean query exert disproportionate influence on the final attention distribution and broadly attend to many keys.

**Mechanism:** Compute the cosine similarity between each query and the mean query (\(M_Q\)). Rank queries by \(-CosSim(M_Q, q)\) and retain the top \(N_Q\) queries with the lowest similarity (highest dissimilarity).

**Core assumption:** The angular distance of a query from the centroid of all queries is a proxy for its "representativeness" and its contribution to the attention output. Queries far from the mean capture diverse, broad attention patterns.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "...queries with low cosine similarity with respect to the mean query interact more strongly with more keys and have the greatest contribution to final attention logits."
- [section 3.1] Theorem 1 provides a theoretical bound linking a query's cosine similarity to the mean and its potential dot product with a key. Figure 2b PCA visualization shows high‑dissimilarity queries lie closer to key clusters.
- [corpus] Weak anchor. Neighbor papers (e.g., Kascade, Squeezed Attention) also observe post‑softmax sparsity and query‑dependent patterns but do not explicitly propose this query‑dissimilarity selection.

**Break condition:** If the geometric relationship between query vectors and their influence on attention logits does not hold for a given model architecture or layer, this selection criterion will fail to identify informative queries.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Using cosine similarity (instead of dot product) as the scoring function for query‑key relevance provides a stable, bounded metric that aggregates more effectively across multiple queries.

**Mechanism:** Normalize the retained queries (\(Q\)) and all cached keys (\(K\)) to unit length. Compute the matrix of cosine similarities \(S = Q \cdot K^{\top}\). This replaces the raw \(QK^{\top}\) used in full attention.

**Core assumption:** Dot products are scale‑dependent and can be dominated by a few large‑norm vectors, making aggregated scores (across queries) unstable. Normalization via cosine similarity yields a more robust, bounded proxy \([-1, 1]\) for attention weight magnitude before softmax.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "...cosine similarity scoring, which provides a stable, bounded proxy for query‑key relevance."
- [section 3.2] Ablation in Table 9 (RULER benchmark) shows cosine similarity improves subselection quality by >10% compared to dot product.
- [corpus] Partial anchor. Neighbors like "Thin Keys, Full Values" and "Compressed Convolutional Attention" also advocate for separating selection (keys) and value dimensions, implying a need for robust selection metrics.

**Break condition:** If the magnitude of query/key vectors (norm) carries essential signal for determining relevance, normalization will discard this information, harming accuracy.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Maximum aggregation across the query dimension and mean aggregation across grouped‑query attention (GQA) KV heads best preserves the behavior of full attention when selecting the final KV set.

**Mechanism:**
1. **Across Queries:** Take the maximum score per key across all retained queries: \(S_{max} = \max(S, \text{dim=query\_axis})\).
2. **Across KV Groups:** Pre‑aggregate the normalized queries by averaging them across the GQA groups *before* the outer product with keys, which mathematically yields an equivalent mean across the group dimension after the outer product.

**Core assumption:** (A) A few outlier query‑key interactions are critical and are not preserved by mean aggregation (query dimension). (B) The importance of different KV heads is correlated, so averaging is stable (KV group dimension).

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "...aggregation: which preserves compatibility with modern architectures such as grouped‑query attention (GQA)..."
- [section 3.3] Figure 3 shows a heavy‑tailed distribution of attention scores, justifying `max`. Ablation in Table 10 (RULER) shows `max` outperforms `mean`. Pre‑aggregation logic explained for efficiency with GQA.
- [corpus] Strong anchor. Neighbor papers like "Kascade" and "Loki" explicitly face the challenge of aggregating across multiple queries/heads, often using less effective strategies.

**Break condition:** If the distribution of attention scores is not heavy‑tailed (query axis) or if KV head importances are uncorrelated/anti‑correlated (group axis), the chosen aggregations will discard necessary information.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map  
Input Chunk and Prior KV Cache -> Projections (Q_i, K_i, V_i) -> QUOKA Selector (query subselection, scoring, aggregation, KV selection) -> Gather/Prune (K*, V*) -> Dense Attention Kernel -> KV Cache Update (append K_i, V_i). The Prior KV Cache feeds back into the Selector each iteration.

Critical path  
Input Chunk -> Projections -> QUOKA Selector (bottleneck) -> Dense Attention Kernel -> KV Cache Update.

Design tradeoffs
- Sparsity (B_SA) vs. Accuracy: Smaller B_SA yields higher speedup but risks accuracy loss.
- Query Budget (N_Q) vs. Selector Cost: Smaller N_Q reduces subselection cost but may miss important query patterns.
- Prefill Chunk Size (B_CP): Larger chunks improve GPU utilization but increase per‑chunk selector cost.
- Portability vs. Optimality: Standard ops ensure hardware agnosticism but may be less optimal than learned sparse patterns.

Failure signatures
- Accuracy collapse on Needle‑In‑A‑Haystack or RULER, especially at high depth, indicating poor KV selection.
- No speedup or slowdown if selector overhead dominates (B_SA too large or N_Q too large).
- Inconsistent behavior across models if query geometry assumption fails.

First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline Sanity Check:** On a small model (e.g., SmolLM), run full‑quadratic prefill with B_CP = T and measure TTFT. Implement the QUOKA selector without the dense attention kernel to measure its fixed overhead cost.
2. **Parameter Sweep (Sparsity):** Use LongBench with B_CP=128. Sweep B_SA in [512, 1024, 2048, 4096] while holding N_Q = B_CP/4. Plot normalized accuracy vs. average KV tokens used. Identify the "knee" point where accuracy trade‑offs become acceptable.
3. **Component Ablation:** On RULER (Llama3.2-3B), compare three variants: (a) QUOKA with dot‑product scoring, (b) QUOKA with mean query aggregation, (c) full QUOKA. This validates the core design choices.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How can the pre‑aggregation step \(\bar{Q}K^{\top}\) be made computationally more efficient for large hidden dimensions?**  
  *Basis:* [explicit] The conclusion states: "An avenue for future improvement is to make the computation of \(\bar{Q}K^{\top}\) more efficient, for example by exploiting query/key channel sparsity or learned low‑dimensional projections."  
  *Why unresolved:* While effective, the current \(O(N_Q n_{KV} T d)\) cost may become a bottleneck for very large \(d\); more efficient variants could further improve scalability.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Demonstration of a low‑rank or sparse approximation that maintains accuracy across diverse models while reducing FLOPs and memory traffic.

- **Can QUOKA’s KV selection be effectively combined with KV cache eviction policies to further reduce memory footprint?**  
  *Basis:* [explicit] Related work: "Eviction is complementary to our approach and could be integrated with QUOKA in chunked prefill; we leave this to future work."  
  *Why unresolved:* Both methods reduce active KVs but operate on potentially conflicting principles; their interaction is unknown and may yield additive or diminishing returns.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* A unified scheme that applies both techniques sequentially or jointly, evaluated on memory‑constrained scenarios with extremely long contexts.

- **Under what theoretical conditions does cosine similarity accurately approximate post‑softmax attention for KV selection?**  
  *Basis:* [inferred] QUOKA uses cosine similarity for scoring and shows empirical gains over dot products (Table 9), but a formal error bound relating the approximation to exact attention is lacking.  
  *Why unresolved:* Theorem 1 provides a geometric bound for individual query‑key pairs, but real attention involves softmax and masking; the cumulative effect on selection quality is not fully characterized.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* A theoretical analysis quantifying the discrepancy between cosine‑similarity‑based selection and optimal top‑K selection under various Q/K distributions, supported by stress tests on atypical attention maps.

- **Is the max‑over‑queries and mean‑over‑KV‑heads aggregation strategy optimal, or could learned aggregation improve the accuracy‑sparsity trade‑off?**  
  *Basis:* [inferred] The design is motivated by heavy‑tailed query contributions (Figure 3) and an ablation (Table 10), but other aggregation functions (e.g., median, top‑k average, or learned attention) were not explored.  
  *Why unresolved:* Max preserves outliers but may amplify noise; mean stabilizes but could dilute rare but important interactions. A systematic exploration could yield a more robust combination.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Comparative experiments across a range of learned and non‑learned aggregation operators on multiple benchmarks, measuring sensitivity to sparsity and head count.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Exact scaling of query budget \(N_Q\) with chunk size \(B_{CP}\) is not fully specified, risking reproducibility of speed‑accuracy trade‑offs.
- **Positional encoding interaction:** The interplay between rotary embeddings (RoPE/NoPE) and normalization/selection is not thoroughly described, potentially affecting positional coherence in chunked prefill.
- **Evaluation specifics:** Needle‑In‑A‑Haystack construction details (e.g., "vitr" pattern, needle depth distribution) and GQA configurations for all models are not enumerated, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- Query‑dissimilarity selection criterion: Medium
- Cosine scoring over dot product: High
- Max‑query / mean‑KV‑group aggregation: High
- Cross‑model robustness & speedups: Medium
