# Why Grokking Takes So Long: A First-Principles Theory of Representational Phase Transitions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13331
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13331
- **Reference count:** 17
- **Primary result:** Grokking delay follows a norm‑separation law: \(T_{\text{grok}}-T_{\text{mem}} = \Theta\!\bigl((1/\gamma_{\text{eff}})\log(\|\theta_{\text{mem}}\|^2/\|\theta_{\text{post}}\|^2)\bigr)\), with \(\gamma_{\text{eff}} = \eta\lambda\) for SGD and \(\gamma_{\text{eff}} \ge \eta\lambda\) for AdamW, validated across 293 runs.

## Executive Summary
Grokking—a sudden jump in generalization after prolonged memorization—has lacked a quantitative explanation for its delay. This paper presents a first‑principles theory identifying grokking as a norm‑driven phase transition under ℓ₂ regularization. The authors prove that weight decay causes exponential contraction of the parameter norm from a high‑norm memorization solution toward a low‑norm structured representation (e.g., a Fourier circuit). Tight upper and lower bounds on the delay yield the Norm‑Separation Delay Law. Extensive experiments on modular addition, multiplication, and sparse parity (293 runs) confirm the law’s predictions: the delay scales inversely with the effective contraction rate (\(\eta\lambda\) for SGD, larger for AdamW) and logarithmically with the ratio of memorization to generalization norms. AdamW’s adaptive dynamics amplify the contraction rate by a factor of ∼1.41, explaining its ability to grok where plain SGD fails. The theory provides a mechanistic understanding of grokking and offers a practical predictor for when it will occur.

## Method Summary
The study combines theoretical analysis with large‑scale empirical validation. Theoretically, the authors model gradient descent with weight decay on an over‑parameterized interpolation manifold. They show (i) a Lyapunov argument induces exponential decay of the squared parameter norm \(V_t = \|\theta_t\|^2\) with effective rate \(\gamma_{\text{eff}}\); (ii) memorization and generalization solutions exhibit a strict norm separation \(\|\theta_{\text{mem}}\|^2 \gg \|\theta_{\text{post}}\|^2\); (iii) once the norm enters the low‑norm region, a uniform validation loss gap emerges and sequential detection adds a sub‑dominant delay. These three pieces combine into the closed‑form Norm‑Separation Delay Law. Empirically, the authors conduct 293 training runs across three algorithmic tasks, varying learning rate, weight decay, and optimizer. They fit exponential decay to the norm trajectory, measure memorization and grokking times, and compute Fourier‑based non‑Fourier energy \(R(f_\theta)\) to verify structural changes. The results validate the law and reveal scaling relations with hyperparameters.

## Key Results
- The delay \(T_{\text{grok}}-T_{\text{mem}}\) scales inversely with the effective contraction rate \(\gamma_{\text{eff}}\) and logarithmically with the norm ratio \(\|\theta_{\text{mem}}\|^2 / \|\theta_{\text{post}}\|^2\), with \(R^2 > 0.97\) across fits.
- Weight decay \(\lambda\) and learning rate \(\eta\) each exhibit strong inverse scaling on the delay ( \(R^2 = 0.97\) and \(R^2 = 0.92\) respectively).
- AdamW amplifies the effective contraction by approximately \(1.41\times\) relative to \(\eta\lambda\), enabling grokking in regimes where SGD cannot reach a high‑norm memorization state.
- Sparse parity, where final norm exceeds memorization norm, fails to grok, confirming the necessity of norm separation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The explanation rests on three interconnected mechanisms.

- **Exponential norm contraction via weight decay**  
  Weight decay (ℓ₂ regularization) drives the squared parameter norm \(V_t\) to decay exponentially. For SGD on the zero‑loss manifold, a discrete Lyapunov bound gives \(\mathbb{E}[V_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t] \le (1-\eta\lambda)V_t + \eta^2\sigma^2\), leading to \(V_t \to V_\infty = \eta\sigma^2/\lambda\) at effective rate \(\gamma_{\text{eff}} = \eta\lambda\). For AdamW, the rate satisfies \(\gamma_{\text{eff}} \ge \eta\lambda\) and empirically is amplified by \(\approx1.41\). This contraction is the engine that eventually pulls the parameters out of the high‑norm memorization basin. *Evidence:* Theorem 3.2 (Discrete Escape) and its proof (Appendix A). *Break conditions:* if \(\eta\lambda\) is too small, contraction is negligible; if too large, linearization fails and memorization may not be attained.

- **Norm separation between memorization and generalization**  
  Memorizing solutions (e.g., a lookup table) spread parameters across all \(p\) frequencies, giving norm \(\Theta(p)\). Generalizing Fourier circuits concentrate on a small set \(K \ll p\) of frequencies, yielding norm \(\Theta(\sqrt{K})\). This geometric gap \(\|\theta_{\text{mem}}\|^2 \gg \|\theta_{\text{post}}\|^2\) is unavoidable for tasks with a sparse underlying pattern. The time to traverse this gap via exponential decay is proportional to \(\log(\|\theta_{\text{mem}}\|^2 / \|\theta_{\text{post}}\|^2)\), producing the logarithmic term in the delay law. If the gap vanishes (as in sparse parity where the final norm exceeds the memorization norm), grokking does not occur. *Evidence:* Lemma 2.4 (norm lower bound), spectral collapse observed in experiments (Section 4.6). *Break condition:* absence of norm separation aborts grokking.

- **Sequential detection of validation improvement**  
  After the norm drops below a critical threshold, the non‑Fourier energy \(R(f_\theta)\) becomes small and the validation loss exhibits a uniform gap \(\Delta_{\min}\) relative to the memorization solution. Detecting this improvement with confidence \(1-\delta\) requires a sequential concentration argument (Azuma–Hoeffding) and takes time \(T_{\text{detect}} = \Theta(\log(p/\delta)/\Delta_{\min})\). This detection step adds a sub‑dominant contribution to the total delay but is often negligible compared to the exponential escape time. *Evidence:* Sequential detection theorem (Section 3.6); empirical correlation between \(R\) and validation loss (Section 4.6). *Break condition:* non‑uniform validation gaps due to non‑convexities can make detection time dominate and break the simple law.

## Foundational Learning
Understanding the theory relies on several core concepts.

- **Implicit bias of gradient‑based optimization toward low‑norm solutions**  
  *Why needed:* The entire delay mechanism hinges on weight decay pulling parameters toward lower‑norm interpolants.  
  *Quick check:* "Given two solutions that both achieve zero training error, why does gradient descent with ℓ₂ regularization prefer the one with smaller parameter norm?"

- **Overparameterization and the interpolation manifold**  
  *Why needed:* The theory assumes a manifold of zero training loss exists; both memorization and generalization reside on it, and the dynamics move between distinct subsets. Without overparameterization, such a manifold may not exist.  
  *Quick check:* "What does ‘overparameterized’ mean in the context of neural networks, and how does it guarantee the existence of many solutions that perfectly fit the training data?"

- **Sequential hypothesis testing and concentration inequalities**  
  *Why needed:* The detection time component relies on bounding how many validation steps are needed to statistically confirm improvement, using Azuma–Hoeffding for possibly dependent steps.  
  *Quick check:* "If each validation loss estimate has a fixed positive gap Δ and bounded range, how many sequential estimates are required to detect the gap with confidence 1‑δ? How does this scale with Δ and δ?"

## Architecture Onboarding
Reproducing the experiments requires careful attention to the model, optimizer, and training protocol.

**Component map:**  
Synthetic data (modular operations or truth tables) → Architecture (one‑layer transformer for modular tasks, two‑layer MLP for parity) → Optimizer (AdamW primary, SGD with `weight_decay=2λ` to match paper convention) → Training loop (50 000 steps, batch size 512) → Metrics (train/val accuracy, squared parameter norm \(V_t\), non‑Fourier energy \(R(f_\theta)\)).

**Critical path:**  
1. Implement data generation for the chosen task (e.g., modular addition with modulus \(p\)).  
2. Build the model with the specified architecture and hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate \(\eta\), weight decay \(\lambda\), optimizer).  
3. Train up to 50 000 steps, logging train/val accuracy and \(V_t\) periodically.  
4. Determine \(T_{\text{mem}}\) (first step with train accuracy > 99%).  
5. Determine \(T_{\text{grok}}\) (first step with val accuracy > 99%).  
6. Perform a full‑batch pass to compute Fourier coefficients and \(R(f_\theta)\).  
7. Fit exponential decay \(V_t = A\rho^t + C\) between \(T_{\text{mem}}\) and \(T_{\text{grok}}\) to extract \(\gamma_{\text{fit}} = -\log\rho\).  
8. Repeat across hyperparameter sweeps to validate scaling laws.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- The product \(\eta\lambda\) sets the contraction rate; it must be large enough to yield observable grokking within a reasonable horizon, yet small enough to allow memorization first. Typical target \(\eta\lambda \approx 0.001\) for AdamW.  
- Model width: wider networks better satisfy the NTK/local linearity assumption, improving agreement with the theory.  
- Batch size: larger batches reduce gradient noise \(\sigma^2\), lowering the noise floor \(V_\infty\); smaller batches may slightly slow escape but improve detection.  
- Validation threshold: 99% is used to define \(T_{\text{grok}}\); ensure the validation set is sufficiently large (≥ 1000 samples) to avoid noise.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **No grokking:** Check \(V_t\) trajectory. If \(V_t\) never rises substantially before decaying, memorization failed (often with SGD at high \(\lambda\)). If \(V_t\) stays nearly constant, the norm gap is too small (\(\lambda\) too low).  
- **Delay much larger than predicted:** Likely detection dominates (tiny \(\Delta_{\min}\)) or the measured norm ratio is off; verify spectral separation and loss gap.  
- **Non‑exponential \(V_t\) decay:** Fit \(R^2 < 0.95\) suggests deviation from the Lyapunov regime; try larger width or adjust \(\eta\).  
- **AdamW amplification missing:** Fitted \(\gamma_{\text{fit}}\) should exceed \(\eta\lambda\) by ~40% (\(c\approx1.41\)). If not, check implementation of AdamW weight decay.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Baseline confirmation (modular addition, \(p=97\)):** Use \(\eta=10^{-3}\), \(\lambda=1.0\), AdamW. Expect memorization around step 1000, grokking around 1800, exponential decay with \(\gamma_{\text{fit}} \approx 0.0014\).  
2. **Lyapunov escape validation:** For 10 random seeds, fit \(V_t = A\rho^t + C\) between \(T_{\text{mem}}\) and \(T_{\text{grok}}\). Confirm \(\rho \approx 0.9986\) (\(\gamma_{\text{fit}} \approx 0.0014\)) and \(R^2 > 0.99\). Also verify \(\gamma_{\text{fit}} > \eta\lambda\).  
3. **Weight‑decay scaling:** Sweep \(\lambda \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0\}\) (Regime II). Plot \(T_{\text{grok}}\) vs \(1/\lambda\); expect linear relationship with \(R^2 > 0.97\). Check that the norm‑adjusted time \(\tilde{T} = (T_{\text{grok}}-T_{\text{mem}})\cdot\lambda / \log(V_{\text{mem}}/V_{\text{post}})\) remains roughly constant across \(\lambda\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **AdamW amplification factor:** Can the observed \(\approx1.41\)× amplification be derived analytically from the optimizer hyperparameters \((\beta_1,\beta_2,\epsilon)\) and the gradient spectrum? The current work only measures it empirically.
- **Predicting norms a priori:** Is it possible to predict \(\|\theta_{\text{mem}}\|\) and \(\|\theta_{\text{post}}\|\) directly from task parameters \((p, K,\) data fraction) and model architecture, without training? A predictive model would allow forecasting grokking delay beforehand.
- **Universality beyond algorithmic tasks:** Does the norm‑driven mechanism occur in non‑algorithmic domains such as large language models or vision models? The testable prediction is any task with a low‑norm structured interpolant versus a higher‑norm memorizer should exhibit a comparable delay.
- **Impact of nonlinear effects:** How do finite‑width and deep architectures deviate from the infinite‑width/NTK assumptions? The theory assumes local linearity; in strongly nonlinear regimes the relationship between \(\gamma_{\text{fit}}\) and \(\eta\lambda\) may change, and the delay law might require additional terms.

## Limitations
- Theoretical gaps: The derivation of AdamW’s amplification factor (\(c\approx1.41\)) is not provided; the paper acknowledges this as an open direction.
- Empirical details omitted: The sparse parity experiments lack explicit architectural specifications in the main text, requiring code inspection for exact replication.
- Assumptions may not always hold: The uniform validation gap and local strong convexity assumptions underlying the detection time analysis could break in more complex tasks, potentially invalidating the simple delay law.

## Confidence
- **High:** Core empirical findings (exponential norm decay, inverse scaling with \(\lambda/\eta\), logarithmic dependence on norm ratio) are supported by 293 runs with \(R^2 > 0.97\).
- **Medium:** Theory completeness—AdamW amplification not derived, parity task details missing, and assumptions about validation gap and convexity may not universally hold.
- **Low:** Generalization of the norm‑driven mechanism to large language models, vision models, or other real‑world domains remains speculative; no experimental evidence beyond synthetic algorithmic tasks is presented.
