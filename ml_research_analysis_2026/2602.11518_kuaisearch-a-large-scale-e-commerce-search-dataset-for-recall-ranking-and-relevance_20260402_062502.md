# KuaiSearch: A Large-Scale E-Commerce Search Dataset for Recall, Ranking, and Relevance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.11518
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11518
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: KuaiSearch is the largest e-commerce search dataset (331,930 users, 18.6M products, 2.57M queries) that spans recall, ranking, and relevance stages, enabling realistic multi-stage search research.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces KuaiSearch, a large-scale e-commerce search dataset derived from the Kuaishou platform. It addresses key limitations of existing datasets, such as heuristic queries, filtering of cold-start users and long-tail products, anonymization of text, and coverage of only a single search stage. KuaiSearch preserves natural language queries and product texts, includes cold-start users and long-tail products, and systematically spans recall, ranking, and relevance stages. Benchmark evaluations demonstrate that embedding-based methods (DPR-SDE) lead recall performance, DIN achieves top ranking AUC, and LLM-based generative classification (Qwen3-1.7B) yields the best relevance metrics. This dataset provides a comprehensive foundation for advancing e-commerce search research, particularly for large language model applications.

## Method Summary
KuaiSearch is constructed from real search interaction logs of the Kuaishou e-commerce platform. After rigorous preprocessing and filtering, the dataset comprises 331,930 unique users, 18.6 million products, and 2.57 million queries. It is partitioned into training, validation, and test sets for three distinct tasks: (1) recall, where models retrieve relevant products for a given query; (2) ranking, where models score candidate products to predict click or purchase; and (3) relevance, where models classify the graded relevance (0–3) between query and product based on human annotations. The authors implement baseline models for each stage, including DPR-SDE for recall, Deep Interest Network (DIN) for ranking, and a generative LLM (Qwen3-1.7B) fine-tuned for relevance classification. The dataset and baselines are designed to support end-to-end e-commerce search research.

## Key Results
- **Recall**: DPR-SDE achieves Recall@50 of 0.2079, surpassing lexical (BM25) and generative retrieval (DSI, LTRGR) methods.
- **Ranking**: DIN obtains the highest AUC of 0.6262 among ranking models, outperforming traditional factor decomposition approaches.
- **Relevance**: Qwen3-1.7B, used in a generative classification setup, attains ROC-AUC of 0.8215 and PR-AUC of 0.6966, exceeding supervised classifiers like Logistic Regression and LightGBM.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
KuaiSearch's effectiveness stems from its realistic construction and comprehensive scope. By using actual user interactions from a major platform, the dataset retains natural language queries and product descriptions, capturing the ambiguity, multi-intent expressions, and noise inherent in real-world e-commerce search. The inclusion of cold-start users and long-tail products ensures that models are exposed to the full distribution of data, mitigating the bias toward popular items seen in curated datasets. Moreover, the explicit coverage of recall, ranking, and relevance stages allows researchers to develop and evaluate models across the entire search pipeline, facilitating the study of cross-stage interactions and end-to-end optimization. The benchmark results indicate that embedding-based methods excel at recall by modeling semantic similarity, deep interaction models like DIN capture user interests for ranking, and LLMs can effectively classify relevance when fine-tuned on graded annotations.

## Foundational Learning
**Multi-stage search pipeline**  
*Why needed*: E-commerce search typically consists of recall (candidate generation), ranking (reordering), and relevance (judgment) stages; understanding each stage's role is crucial for system design.  
*Quick check*: Can you map a given model (e.g., DPR-SDE) to its corresponding stage?

**Cold-start problem**  
*Why needed*: New users and items lack interaction history, making recommendations difficult; datasets must include such cases to be realistic.  
*Quick check*: Does the dataset contain users with fewer than 5 interactions? (Yes, per paper.)

**Long-tail distribution**  
*Why needed*: Most products and queries are rarely seen; models must handle low-frequency items for broad applicability.  
*Quick check*: Does the product frequency distribution follow a long tail? (KuaiSearch includes many long-tail products.)

**Embedding-based retrieval**  
*Why needed*: Provides efficient semantic matching beyond lexical overlap, a core component of modern recall systems.  
*Quick check*: Does a dual-encoder model (DPR-SDE) outperform BM25 on the recall task? (Yes.)

**LLM for relevance classification**  
*Why needed*: Large language models can capture nuanced query-product relationships beyond feature-based classifiers.  
*Quick check*: Does fine-tuning a small LLM (Qwen3-1.7B) yield higher PR-AUC than traditional models? (Yes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: The e-commerce search pipeline flows as: Query → Recall (candidate retrieval) → Ranking (score computation) → Relevance (graded judgment). KuaiSearch provides datasets for each stage, which can be used independently or chained.

**Critical path**: The most common research path is to first build a recall model to fetch ~50 candidates, then a ranking model to reorder them, and finally a relevance model to provide fine-grained labels for training or evaluation.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Scale vs. annotation cost*: Recall and ranking use implicit click/purchase signals (abundant but noisy), while relevance relies on expensive human graded labels (smaller but cleaner).  
- *Cold-start inclusion vs. model performance*: Including cold-start users and long-tail items increases realism but can degrade simple model performance, necessitating robust architectures.  
- *Natural language preservation vs. preprocessing*: Keeping raw query/product text introduces noise (typos, slang) but avoids loss of semantic information.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Recall model fails on ambiguous queries or products with noisy descriptions (low R@50).  
- Ranking model exhibits bias toward popular items or fails to generalize to cold-start users (low AUC).  
- Relevance model confuses partially relevant items (scores 1–2) due to binarization or insufficient modeling of ordinality.

**First three experiments**:  
1. Train DPR-SDE on the recall training set and evaluate R@50 on the test set to establish a baseline.  
2. Train DIN on the ranking dataset and compute AUC on validation/test splits.  
3. Fine-tune Qwen3-1.7B for four-class relevance classification (or binary) and measure ROC-AUC and PR-AUC.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can generative retrieval models be improved to effectively handle ambiguous queries and noisy product texts in large-scale e-commerce search?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper explicitly states that generative retrieval methods (DSI, LTRGR) underperform compared to embedding-based and lexical methods due to "ambiguous queries, multi-intent expressions, vast product catalogs with long-tail distributions, and noisy product texts" (Section 5.1).
- **Why unresolved**: Current generative approaches struggle to generate precise item identifiers in complex e-commerce scenarios where user intent is diffuse and product descriptions are unstructured, limiting their practical deployment.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development of new architectures or training objectives (e.g., enhanced semantic indexing, intent disambiguation modules) that explicitly model query ambiguity and product noise, validated by significantly improved Recall@K on KuaiSearch compared to existing generative baselines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How can recall, ranking, and relevance stages be jointly optimized in an end-to-end manner using multi-stage datasets like KuaiSearch?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] While KuaiSearch explicitly spans recall, ranking, and relevance (a key contribution), the paper evaluates each stage separately with independent models and datasets (Sections 5.1–5.3), without investigating interactions or joint optimization across the pipeline.
- **Why unresolved**: Stage-wise improvements may not translate to overall system performance; cross-stage signals (e.g., how recall candidates influence ranking, how relevance judgments feedback to recall) remain unexplored, limiting holistic system design.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A unified model or pipeline that shares representations and is trained on all three stages simultaneously using KuaiSearch, demonstrating superior end-to-end metrics (e.g., overall conversion rate) over isolated stage-wise models.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How can the fine-grained, graded relevance annotations in KuaiSearch be utilized to develop more nuanced relevance models beyond binary classification?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The dataset provides four-level graded relevance scores (0–3) with balanced distribution (Section 4.3), but all relevance experiments binarize the labels (score=3 as positive, others negative), discarding the granularity.
- **Why unresolved**: Binarization overlooks partially relevant items (scores 1–2), which are prevalent and challenging, missing opportunities for better model calibration, interpretability, and handling of nuanced user intent.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Models trained with ordinal regression, multi-class classification, or ranking losses on the four-level scores, showing improved metrics (e.g., mean absolute error on ordinal labels, better PR-AUC for borderline cases) compared to binary approaches on the KuaiSearch relevance set.

## Limitations
- **Single-platform bias**: The dataset is derived solely from Kuaishou, which may not represent the diversity of other e-commerce ecosystems and could embed platform-specific user behavior patterns.
- **Access uncertainty**: It is unclear whether the dataset will be publicly released; without access, independent verification of its claims and replication of benchmarks are hindered.
- **Benchmark generalizability**: Evaluations were conducted with specific model configurations and data splits, so results may not hold for other architectures, hyperparameters, or cross-domain scenarios.

## Confidence
- **Dataset scale & coverage**: Medium (the reported statistics are plausible but require external validation due to lack of public data).
- **Benchmark results**: Medium (the numbers appear internally consistent but need replication; potential overfitting to test splits cannot be ruled out).
