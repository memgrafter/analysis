# BenchPreS: A Benchmark for Context-Aware Personalized Preference Selectivity of Persistent-Memory LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16557
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16557
- **Reference Count:** 17
- **Primary Result:** Frontier LLMs exhibit high misapplication rates (40–86%) and a strong positive correlation between appropriate and inappropriate preference application, indicating they treat preferences as globally enforceable rather than context-dependent.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces BenchPreS, a benchmark to evaluate whether persistent-memory LLMs can selectively apply stored user preferences based on communication context. The benchmark constructs 39 recipient–task pairs across five domains and 10 user profiles, each with five preference attributes. Two key metrics are introduced: Misapplication Rate (MR) for inappropriate application and Appropriate Application Rate (AR) for correct application. Evaluation of eight frontier models reveals consistently high MR (up to 86.48%) and a strong positive correlation between AAR and MR, suggesting models lack a gating mechanism to condition preference use on contextual norms. Neither enhanced reasoning nor prompt-based mitigation reliably resolves this, with reasoning exacerbating indiscriminate application and mitigation effectiveness varying widely across models.

## Method Summary
BenchPreS evaluates context-aware selectivity by pairing 10 synthetic user profiles (each containing ~152 factual attributes and 5 preferences across role, style, tone, markers, nickname) with 39 recipient–task contexts from five domains. For each profile–context pair, the model generates a response from a prompt that prefixes memory statements (the user profile) before the task instruction. A separate LLM-as-Judge (DeepSeek-R1) then determines whether each preference is reflected in the output. Gold labels `g(t,a)` indicate whether applying a preference is appropriate for a given context. Metrics are computed: Misapplication Rate (MR) = fraction of inappropriate preferences applied, and Appropriate Application Rate (AAR) = fraction of appropriate preferences applied. Experiments compare default prompts, reasoning-enabled variants, and mitigation prompts that instruct selective application.

## Key Results
- Frontier models show high MR (e.g., GPT-5.2: 40.95%, Gemini 3 Pro: 86.48%) and high AAR, with a strong positive correlation across all models—higher AAR consistently coincides with higher MR.
- Enabling explicit reasoning increases both AAR and MR, amplifying overall preference responsiveness without improving selectivity.
- Prompt-based mitigation reduces MR variably (e.g., Gemini: -73.68 pp, DeepSeek: -20.47 pp) but is ineffective for some models and often accompanied by minor AAR drops.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Frontier LLMs treat user preferences as globally enforceable rules rather than context-dependent signals, leading to indiscriminate application.
- **Mechanism:** The persistent memory system and generation process lack a gating mechanism that conditions preference execution on communicative norms. Preferences stored in memory are parsed as mandatory constraints during decoding, activated whenever the task prompt includes any instruction to "use memory" or "personalize."
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states models treat preferences as "globally enforceable rules." Section 2 notes higher AAR consistently exhibits higher MR. Section 4.2 Table 1 shows strong positive correlation; no model achieves low MR with high AAR.
- **Break condition:** A model with high AAR (>85%) and low MR (<20%) without mitigation would falsify this. None observed.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Explicit reasoning capability exacerbates misapplication by decomposing preferences into executable subgoals without normative filtering.
- **Mechanism:** Chain-of-thought scaffolds the model to "fulfill" all stored preferences as checklist items during planning, converting each into a concrete generation constraint and reinforcing a "compliance" mindset over an "evaluation" mindset.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.4 reports reasoning increases both AAR and MR. Figure 4 shows reasoning variants have higher IFBench scores *and* higher MR. Section C Figure 9 reasoning trace treats inappropriate preferences as checkboxes to "✅" rather than to evaluate.
- **Break condition:** A reasoning variant showing increased AAR with decreased MR relative to its non-reasoning counterpart would refute this. None observed.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Prompt-based mitigation provides only partial, model-specific relief because it relies on the model's existing capability to internalize normative constraints, which is unevenly distributed.
- **Mechanism:** The mitigation prompt ("Only include preferences that are appropriate...") requires the model to already know task/recipient norms. This knowledge is not uniform; models with stronger internalized normativity reduce MR, while others ignore or misapply the instruction.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.5 Table 2 shows MR reduction varies widely while AAR drops are minor and consistent. Section 4.5 notes effectiveness "varies substantially across systems." Section 6 states mitigation effectiveness "depends strongly on the underlying model."
- **Break condition:** If mitigation consistently reduced MR by >50 pp with <2 pp AAR loss across all models, the mechanism would be refuted. Current data shows high variance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Context-Aware Selectivity**
  - *Why needed here:* The core problem is not memory or personalization, but the *conditional application* of preferences. Systems must evaluate appropriateness against task/recipient norms, not just recall preferences.
  - *Quick check:* "When should a user's preference for humorous tone be suppressed in a formal email?" (Answer: When recipient/task norms require seriousness, e.g., legal or financial disclosures.)

- **Concept: Persistent Memory as Structured Signals, Not Flat Text**
  - *Why needed here:* BenchPreS injects memory as natural language statements. The failure suggests models parse these as directives to obey, not as data to reason over. Memory representations should explicitly tag preferences as "context-sensitive" and link to normative knowledge.
  - *Quick check:* "Why does prefixing 'She prefers a sarcastic tone' to a prompt lead to sarcasm in a tax letter?" (Answer: The model lacks a meta-reasoning layer to query: 'Is sarcasm appropriate for IRS Agent – Resolve tax discrepancy?')

- **Concept: Metric Inversion (AAR vs. MR Trade-off)**
  - *Why needed here:* Standard personalization metrics only measure preference fidelity (AAR). The negative correlation between AAR and MR reveals a fundamental constraint: current architectures cannot increase selectivity (high AAR, low MR) by scaling capability alone. These are competing objectives.
  - *Quick check:* "If a model's AAR increases from 70% to 90% but its MR also increases from 30% to 70%, what does this imply about its selectivity?" (Answer: It became *less* selective; it applies preferences more broadly, including inappropriate ones.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Memory Ingestion -> Context Encoder -> (missing) Preference Gating -> Generation Module. The Preference Gating module is absent; all preferences from memory are passed unconditionally to generation.
- **Critical path:**
  1. User profile with 5 preferences is retrieved and formatted into memory text.
  2. Task prompt (recipient + task) is constructed.
  3. Memory text is prepended to task prompt.
  4. Model generates response `y_u,t`.
  5. Judge evaluates each preference `a` -> `ˆz(y,a)`.
  6. Metrics MR and AAR computed against gold labels `g(t,a)`.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Precision vs. Recall: Suppressing inappropriate preferences (high precision, low MR) risks under-applying appropriate ones (low recall, low AAR). The observed AAR-MR correlation suggests current models operate on a single "preference strength" dial.
  - Prompt Engineering vs. Architectural Change: Mitigation prompts offer a quick fix but are brittle (model-dependent). A robust solution likely requires architectural changes (e.g., explicit gating layer, norm-aware retrieval).
  - Task Completeness vs. Selectivity: Some models maintain task scores while misapplying preferences. Do not equate task success with selectivity; measure both.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Sarcasm/Emoji in formal domains (e.g., "Joker's Financial Literacy Class" to IRS agent).
  - Role/style mismatches (e.g., "Perspective of an elementary school teacher" for USCIS interview letter).
  - Reasoning traces as checklists: reasoning enumerates preferences as ✅ items, not as items to evaluate.
  - High AAR + High MR: Model lies near y=x line in Figure 1. Any point above y=0.5x indicates poor selectivity.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline Evaluation:** Run all 1,950 (user, context) instances with default prompt. Compute MR and AAR. Expect: high MR (>40%) and high AAR (>80%) for most models; positive correlation.
  2. **Reasoning Toggle:** For models with reasoning variants, run with reasoning mode on/off. Hypothesis: reasoning increases both AAR and MR.
  3. **Mitigation Prompt Test:** Insert mitigation template. Measure ΔMR and ΔAAR. Expect: MR reduction but variable across models; minor AAR drop. If MR remains >30%, mitigation insufficient.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why does enabling explicit reasoning simultaneously increase both Appropriate Application Rate (AAR) and Misapplication Rate (MR), thereby failing to improve context-aware preference selectivity?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.4 reports that reasoning variants achieve higher AAR and MR compared to non-reasoning counterparts, suggesting reasoning amplifies overall preference responsiveness without enhancing contextual filtering.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper hypothesizes that reasoning decomposes instructions into executable subgoals but does not clarify why this process cannot incorporate context-dependent suppression. The internal mechanisms linking reasoning to indiscriminate preference execution remain unanalyzed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies on reasoning traces to identify whether contextual evaluation occurs; controlled experiments isolating reasoning steps to measure their impact on applying vs. suppressing preferences; probing model representations to see if context signals are integrated during preference evaluation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What model-specific factors determine the variable effectiveness of prompt-based mitigation in reducing Misapplication Rate (MR)?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.5 shows mitigation reduces MR inconsistently across models (e.g., Gemini 3 Pro vs. DeepSeek V3.2), indicating dependence on underlying model architecture or training.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper notes the mitigation's effectiveness varies but does not investigate which model properties (e.g., instruction-following robustness, internal context representations) predict success. This limits the design of universally applicable defenses.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlation analysis between mitigation efficacy and model characteristics (e.g., IFBench scores, parameter scale, training data composition); error analysis on mitigation failures to identify common breakdown patterns; controlled fine-tuning experiments to isolate the influence of specific model capabilities.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can context-aware reasoning patterns be systematically incorporated into post-training data to enable selective preference application?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6 suggests that successful suppression cases show models enumerating preferences and evaluating contextual appropriateness before generation, proposing this as a training signal for future work.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not specify how to construct such data at scale, what format the reasoning should take, or whether fine-tuning with these patterns generalizes beyond the benchmark. The feasibility and impact of this approach remain speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a synthetic dataset with reasoning chains that explicitly label preferences as appropriate/inappropriate per context; fine-tuning experiments measuring improvements in MR/AAR on BenchPreS and out-of-domain contexts; analysis of whether learned reasoning generalizes to unseen preference-context combinations.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why are surface-level preference attributes (markers, nicknames) more resistant to appropriate suppression than role or style preferences across models?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 5.2 reports consistently high MR for markers (e.g., emojis) and nicknames across evaluated models, while role and style show lower MR (e.g., GPT-5.2).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper speculates these may be treated as "simple expression instructions," but lacks analysis of whether this stems from training data frequency, embedding similarities, or model biases toward lexical patterns. The underlying cause is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of training data to compare prevalence of surface-level vs. deep preferences; probing attention distributions to see if models attend differently to marker/nickname tokens during generation; controlled experiments swapping preference categories to test if difficulty is inherent to the category or correlated with other factors.

## Limitations
- **Profile & Label Fidelity:** Unverified user profiles and missing gold labels (1,950 context-preference pairs) introduce reconstruction risk. Annotator subjectivity may destabilize MR/AAR if agreement <90%.
- **Formatting Sensitivity:** Undisclosed memory statement phrasing and ordering could alter model behavior; effects unmeasured.
- **Generalizability:** BenchPreS covers 5 domains and 10 profiles. Performance may shift with broader populations or ambiguous contexts.

## Confidence
- **Mechanisms (1–3):** High. Strong internal evidence (correlations, reasoning traces, mitigation variance).
- **Generalizability to new models:** Medium. Limited to 8 evaluated frontier models; untested on smaller/open models.
- **Mitigation reliability:** Low. Prompt-based fix is model-dependent and inconsistent; no architectural solution validated.

## Next Checks
1. **Gold Label Stability:** Re-annotate 200 random (context, preference) instances with 3 new annotators. Compute Cohen’s κ; if <0.9, revise guidelines and relabel full set.
2. **Memory Formatting Ablation:** For 3 models, vary memory statement phrasing (e.g., directive vs. declarative) and attribute ordering. Measure ΔMR/ΔAAR to isolate formatting impact.
3. **Cross-Architecture Test:** Evaluate 2 additional models from different families (e.g., dense vs. MoE) with default and mitigation prompts. Check if high AAR+low MR remains unattainable.
