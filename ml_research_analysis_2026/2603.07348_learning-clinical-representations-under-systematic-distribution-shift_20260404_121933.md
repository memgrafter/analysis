# Learning Clinical Representations Under Systematic Distribution Shift
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.07348
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07348
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Out-of-distribution AUROC improved by up to 2-3 points (0.842 vs. 0.819) while maintaining in-distribution AUROC (0.867) and reducing calibration error (0.055→0.039); hospital identification accuracy fell from 72.1% to 39.7%.

## Executive Summary
Clinical machine learning models often fail to generalize across hospitals due to systematic distribution shifts caused by heterogeneous practice patterns. This paper introduces a practice-invariant representation learning framework to address this challenge. The core idea is to model clinical observations as arising from latent physiologic factors (the true signal) and environment-dependent processes (artifacts). The method learns embeddings that are predictive of outcomes while being invariant to hospital identity, thereby suppressing practice artifacts. Evaluated on longitudinal EHR data from four hospitals for tasks such as in-hospital mortality and readmission, the approach yields substantial out-of-distribution gains: AUROC improves by up to 2-3 points compared to supervised baselines, in-distribution performance remains stable, calibration is improved, and hospital identifiability of the representations is drastically reduced.

## Method Summary
The framework adopts a generative factorization \(x \sim p(x|z, c), y \sim p(y|z)\), where \(z\) are latent physiologic factors and \(c\) is environment-specific context that affects observations but not outcomes directly. An encoder \(h_\theta(x)\) maps raw inputs (multimodal EHR data) to an embedding. The learning objective is a min-max optimization: minimize over \(\theta\) while maximizing over \(\psi\) the combined loss \(\mathcal{L}_{sup}(\theta) - \lambda \mathcal{L}_{env}(\psi) + \gamma \mathcal{R}_{inv}\). \(\mathcal{L}_{sup}\) is the sum of supervised losses (e.g., binary cross-entropy) across training environments; \(\mathcal{L}_{env}\) is the cross-entropy loss for predicting the hospital index \(e\) from the embedding (environment classifier \(g_\psi\)); \(\mathcal{R}_{inv}\) penalizes the sum of squared differences between optimal linear predictors \(w^\star_e\) across environments, computed on frozen embeddings to encourage similar decision boundaries. Adversarial training with gradient reversal ensures that \(h_\theta\) discards features useful for predicting \(e\).

Architecturally, modality-specific encoders (e.g., transformers for structured sequences, CNNs/ViTs for images, 1D-CNNs for time-series) project each modality into a shared \(d\)-dimensional embedding space. A linear prediction head \(f_\theta\) maps embeddings to outcome probabilities, and a linear environment classifier \(g_\psi\) maps to hospital probabilities. Training alternates updates: first update \(\psi\) to minimize \(\mathcal{L}_{env}\); then update \(\theta\) to minimize the combined loss, with the gradient reversal layer flipping the sign of the gradient from \(\mathcal{L}_{env}\) relative to \(\theta\). \(\mathcal{R}_{inv}\) is computed periodically by solving per-environment linear predictors (e.g., via a few gradient steps or closed form) on the current frozen embeddings.

## Key Results
- Out-of-distribution AUROC improved by up to 2-3 points (e.g., 0.842 vs. 0.819 for supervised baseline).
- In-distribution AUROC remained stable (0.867) and expected calibration error decreased (0.055 → 0.039).
- Hospital identification accuracy from embeddings reduced from 72.1% to 39.7%, demonstrating effective suppression of practice artifacts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Learning disentangles physiologic signal (\(z\)) from practice artifacts (\(c\)) by modeling observations as arising from separate latent factors.
- Mechanism: The framework assumes a generative factorization \(x \sim p(x|z, c), y \sim p(y| z)\), where environment-specific context \(c\) influences observations but not the outcome. The encoder \(h_\theta(x)\) is trained to maximize predictiveness of \(y\) while minimizing predictability of \(e\) (environment index), aiming for \(h_\theta(x) \perp e | y\).
- Core assumption: The causal graph has \(c \rightarrow x\) but no direct arrow \(c \rightarrow y\); all \(c\)'s effect on \(y\) is mediated through \(z\).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "We model clinical observations as arising from latent physiologic factors and environment-dependent processes."
  - [section 3] "We posit a latent factorization: `x∼p(x|z, c), y∼p(y|z)`."
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak; neighboring papers discuss domain shift but not this specific causal factorization.
- Break condition: If practice context \(c\) directly influences outcome \(y\) (e.g., hospital-specific treatment protocols affecting survival), suppressing \(c\)-information harms predictive performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Adversarial training suppresses environment-predictive information by forcing the encoder to produce embeddings where an environment classifier fails.
- Mechanism: A minimax objective \(\min_\theta \max_\psi \mathcal{L}_{sup}(\theta) - \lambda \mathcal{L}_{env}(\psi)\) alternates updates: the environment classifier \(g_\psi\) is trained to predict hospital \(e\) from embeddings, while the encoder \(h_\theta\) is trained to minimize supervised loss while maximizing environment classification error (implemented via gradient reversal). This pushes \(h_\theta\) to discard features useful for distinguishing environments.
- Core assumption: Environment classifier \(g_\psi\) can initially extract \(c\)-information from \(h_\theta(x)\); if \(c\) is not linearly separable in the embedding space, adversarial training may fail to remove it.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "suppressing environment-predictive information via adversarial training."
  - [section 3] "The environment classifier is trained to predict `e`: `Lenv(ψ) = ...` The encoder is trained to minimize predictive loss while maximizing environment classification error."
  - [section 4] Table 3: hospital identification accuracy drops from 72.1% (supervised) to 39.7% (proposed), indicating successful suppression.
- Break condition: If environment-specific features are highly entangled with physiologic signal such that removing them also removes predictive signal for \(y\).

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Invariant risk penalty encourages embeddings where optimal linear predictors for \(y\) are similar across hospitals, reducing overfitting to local practice patterns.
- Mechanism: For each environment \(e\), compute the optimal linear predictor \(w^\star_e = \arg\min_w \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_e} \ell(w^\top h_\theta(x), y)\). Penalize the sum of squared differences \(\mathcal{R}_{inv} = \sum_{e,e'} \|w^\star_e - w^\star_{e'}\|^2\). This encourages the embedding space to support similar linear decision boundaries across environments, implying that the embedding varies with \(y\) but not with \(e\).
- Core assumption: Environmental differences in \(w^\star\) arise primarily from \(c\)-induced distributional shifts; if true \(y\)-signal differs across hospitals (e.g., different disease subpopulations), forcing invariance harms performance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3] "We penalize variation across environments: `Rinv = Σ ||w⋆_e − w⋆_{e'}||²`."
  - [section 4] Ablation: removing `Rinv` reduces OOD AUROC from 0.842 to 0.834.
  - [abstract] "invariant risk penalties across hospitals."
- Break condition: If the conditional distribution \(p(y|z)\) itself varies across environments (violating the paper's assumption), invariant predictors are suboptimal.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: Information-Theoretic Objectives
  - Why needed here: The core optimization balances \(I(h(X); Y)\) (maximize) and \(I(h(X); E)\) (minimize). Understanding this tradeoff explains why reconstruction pretraining fails (it maximizes \(I(h; X)\), which includes \(I(h; C|Z)\)).
  - Quick check question: "Why does maximizing \(I(h(X); X)\) inadvertently preserve practice artifacts?"
- Concept: Domain Generalization / Invariance
  - Why needed here: The method is a domain generalization technique. Key idea: find features stable across training environments (\(e \in \mathcal{E}_{train}\)) for better OOD generalization to \(e_{test}\).
  - Quick check question: "How does invariant risk minimization (IRM) differ empirically from standard empirical risk minimization (ERM) in this context?"
- Concept: Adversarial Training for Disentanglement
  - Why needed here: Gradient reversal layers are used to implement the \(\min_\theta \max_\psi\) objective. Understanding the gradient sign flip is crucial for correct implementation.
  - Quick check question: "Why is the gradient reversed for the encoder when updating via \(-\lambda \mathcal{L}_{env}\)? What would happen if trained jointly without reversal?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map:
  - Modality-specific encoders (e.g., transformer for EHR, CNN/ViT for images) → Projection to shared dim → Shared embedding space.
  - Prediction head: Linear layer \(f_\theta\) mapping embedding to outcome probability.
  - Environment classifier: Linear layer \(g_\psi\) mapping same embedding to hospital index.
  - Loss computation: \(\mathcal{L}_{sup}\) (binary cross-entropy on \(y\)), \(\mathcal{L}_{env}\) (cross-entropy on \(e\)), \(\mathcal{R}_{inv}\) (computed via per-environment linear probes on frozen embeddings).

- Critical path:
  1. Load batch \((x_{\text{multimodal}}, y, e)\).
  2. Forward pass: \(h = h_\theta(x)\) → \(\hat{y} = f_\theta(h)\), \(\hat{e} = g_\psi(h)\).
  3. Compute \(\mathcal{L}_{sup}(\theta)\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{env}(\psi)\).
  4. Update \(\psi\) to minimize \(\mathcal{L}_{env}(\psi)\).
  5. Update \(\theta\) to minimize \(\mathcal{L}_{sup}(\theta) - \lambda \mathcal{L}_{env}(\psi) + \gamma \mathcal{R}_{inv}\) (use gradient reversal for \(\mathcal{L}_{env}\) term w.r.t. \(\theta\)).

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Hyperparameters \(\lambda\) (adversarial strength) and \(\gamma\) (invariant risk weight) are coupled: high \(\lambda\) may destabilize training; high \(\gamma\) increases compute cost (requires per-environment linear solves).
  - Environment classifier capacity: too weak → cannot extract \(c\); too strong → may learn spurious correlations that are hard to fool.

- Failure signatures:
  - Environment classification accuracy remains high (>60%) after training → invariance failed.
  - OOD AUROC significantly lower than in-distribution AUROC (>5-point gap) → model still encodes environment-specific shortcuts.
  - Ablation: removing either adversarial term or \(\mathcal{R}_{inv}\) degrades OOD performance → both components necessary.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. **Verification of environment suppression**: Freeze \(h_\theta\) after training, train a linear probe to predict hospital \(e\). Target: accuracy near random (25% for 4 hospitals). Baseline: supervised model should show high accuracy.
  2. **Cross-hospital generalization**: Train on hospitals \(\{A,B,C\}\), test on held-out D. Compare AUROC of proposed vs. supervised baseline. Expect ≥2-point OOD improvement with no in-distribution drop.
  3. **Ablation of invariance terms**: Train variants: (a) without adversarial loss, (b) without \(\mathcal{R}_{inv}\), (c) with both. Measure OOD AUROC and environment accuracy to confirm complementary effects.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the invariance framework be extended to finer-grained environment definitions (e.g., temporal shifts, provider subgroups) while maintaining robustness?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "First, we operationalize environment as hospital identity, but practice heterogeneity also arises temporally and across provider subgroups. Extending invariance constraints to finer-grained environment definitions may yield further robustness gains."
- Why unresolved: The study only treats entire hospitals as environments; it is unclear whether the approach generalizes to subtler, potentially overlapping sources of practice variation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluate models with environments defined by time periods or provider IDs, measuring out-of-distribution performance and residual environment predictability in embeddings.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can we derive stronger theoretical guarantees for the adversarial invariance objective by explicitly modeling the data-generating causal process?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "Second, our adversarial formulation approximates independence between representations and environment; stronger theoretical guarantees may require explicit causal modeling of the data-generating process."
- Why unresolved: Adversarial training may not fully eliminate environment-dependent information, and the current formulation lacks formal guarantees of conditional invariance.
- What evidence would resolve it: Incorporate a causal graph (e.g., distinguishing latent physiologic factors from practice context) and prove bounds on environment leakage; empirically compare robustness under known interventions or synthetic data with known causal mechanisms.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does the proposed practice-invariant representation learning approach improve performance on non-binary clinical tasks such as survival analysis or continuous risk estimation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "Third, we focus on binary prediction tasks; future work should examine survival modeling, continuous risk estimation, and decision-support settings."
- Why unresolved: The objective and evaluation metrics are tailored for binary outcomes; invariance may interact differently with time-to-event or regression loss functions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Adapt the training objective (e.g., using survival loss) and invariant risk penalties for time-dependent tasks; test on longitudinal datasets with survival or continuous outcomes and report discrimination/calibration improvements across hospitals.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does scaling model size interact with the efficacy of practice-invariant constraints? Does larger capacity facilitate or hinder learning truly invariant representations?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper emphasizes "the importance of structural invariance alongside architectural scale" but all experiments use a fixed ~40M parameter architecture. The interplay between scale and invariance remains unexplored.
- Why unresolved: Foundation models are trending toward larger scales; without investigating scaling behavior, it is unknown whether invariance constraints become easier (more capacity to compress) or harder (more capacity to encode environment-specific details) as models grow.
- What evidence would resolve it: Train models across a range of parameter counts (e.g., 10M to 1B) with and without invariance penalties; compare environment prediction accuracy, out-of-distribution AUROC, and in-distribution performance to identify scaling trends.

## Limitations
- Architectural details missing: multimodal fusion (cross-attention layers), handling of missing modalities.
- Hyperparameters (λ, γ, learning rates, embedding dimension) not reported.
- Residual environment signal: hospital identification accuracy 39.7% > random 25%, indicating incomplete suppression; evaluation limited to four hospitals.

## Confidence
- Disentanglement via causal factorization (Mechanism 1): Medium confidence
- Adversarial training (Mechanism 2): High confidence
- Invariant risk penalty (Mechanism 3): Medium confidence

## Next Checks
1. Modality-specific environment probes: Train linear classifiers on each modality's pre-fusion embedding to predict hospital. Identifies leakage channels and whether fusion achieves invariance across all inputs.
2. Hyperparameter sensitivity: Sweep λ and γ (e.g., {0.1,1,10} and {0.01,0.1,1}) while tracking OOD/in-distribution AUROC. Establishes robust ranges and quantifies performance variance.
3. Test c→y assumption: Include hospital as a covariate in the outcome model or stratify by hospital-specific treatments. If OOD performance deteriorates, it suggests reliance on the no-direct-effect premise.
