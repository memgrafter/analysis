# KDFlow: A User-Friendly and Efficient Knowledge Distillation Framework for Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01875  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01875  
- **Reference count:** 8  
- **Primary result:** KDFlow achieves 1.44×–6.36× training speedup over existing frameworks, with especially large gains for MoE teachers, while preserving downstream task performance and supporting diverse KD algorithms.

## Executive Summary
KDFlow is a knowledge distillation framework that decouples teacher and student backends to maximize hardware utilization. The teacher model runs on SGLang (optimized for inference), while the student model is trained with PyTorch FSDP2 (optimized for distributed training). To minimize communication overhead, the framework transfers only the teacher’s hidden states (via zero-copy shared memory) and recomputes full logits locally on the student side. This design yields speedups up to 6.36× for MoE teachers, maintains downstream performance on AlpacaEval 2.0, and provides extensible APIs that hide distributed-system complexities, enabling rapid prototyping of off‑policy, on‑policy, and cross‑tokenizer KD algorithms.

## Method Summary
KDFlow orchestrates three main actor groups via a central Ray controller (Trainer). The `TeacherActorGroup` wraps a SGLang engine to perform teacher inference, outputting final hidden states. The `StudentActorGroup` uses FSDP2 to manage student model shards; it receives hidden states, applies the teacher’s LM head to reconstruct logits, computes the KD loss (e.g., Forward KL, Reverse KL, JSD, TVD, or DSKDv2 for cross‑tokenizer KD), and performs backpropagation. For on‑policy distillation, a `RolloutActorGroup` generates student responses and receives weight updates via CUDA IPC. Hidden states are transferred through Ray’s zero‑copy shared memory, drastically reducing communication volume compared to full‑logit transfer. The framework cleanly separates communication logic from distillation algorithms, allowing users to plug in custom losses without touching the distributed infrastructure. It has been validated on Qwen3 models (including MoE teachers) with up to 30B parameters.

## Key Results
- Training speedup of **1.44×–6.36×** over existing frameworks (TRL, MS‑SWIFT, ROLL); the largest gains (**6.36×**) occur with MoE teachers.  
- **Downstream performance preserved**: AlpacaEval 2.0 scores match baseline implementations.  
- **Extensible API** supports diverse KD algorithms (off-policy, on-policy, cross‑tokenizer) with minimal engineering effort.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Decoupling the teacher and student backends improves overall hardware utilization by matching each model's operational role (inference vs. training) to a specialized execution engine.  
- **Mechanism:** The teacher (inference‑only) runs on SGLang, which is optimized for high‑throughput LLM inference. The student (training) runs on FSDP2, which excels at distributed training with parameter/optimizer sharding. This avoids forcing a single training engine to handle inference‑heavy workloads inefficiently.  
- **Core assumption:** Inference and training have distinct computational profiles; assigning them to specialized engines yields higher aggregate throughput than a one‑size‑fits‑all system.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...most existing frameworks still use a homogeneous training backend... leading to suboptimal training efficiency... decoupled architecture and employs SGLang for teacher inference. By bridging the training efficiency of FSDP2 and the inference efficiency of SGLang, KDFlow achieves full utilization of both advantages." [section 3.1] "This decoupled architecture ensures that the inference‑heavy teacher model and the training‑heavy student model run on their respectively optimized backends, significantly improving hardware utilization."  
- **Break condition:** A unified backend that efficiently handles both inference and training emerges, or the inter‑process communication overhead of decoupling outweighs specialization benefits for certain model sizes/precisions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Transferring teacher hidden states (instead of full logits) followed by local logit recomputation on the student side drastically reduces inter‑process communication volume while preserving mathematical equivalence to standard full‑logit KD.  
- **Mechanism:** Full logits (e.g., ~160 GB for 128 sequences of length 4096 with a 150 K vocabulary) are enormous. KDFlow’s `TeacherActorGroup` sends only the final hidden states (e.g., dimension 4096) using zero‑copy shared memory. The `StudentActorGroup` then locally applies the teacher’s LM head to these hidden states to reconstruct the full logit distribution for loss computation.  
- **Core assumption:** The compute cost of one extra linear projection (the LM head) on the student side is negligible compared to the communication and memory cost of transferring massive logit tensors.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...instead of transferring full logits across different processes, our framework only transmits the teacher’s hidden states using zero‑copy data transfer and recomputes the logits on the student side, effectively balancing the communication cost and KD performance." [section 3.2] "Since the dimension of hidden states... is far smaller than that of the logit distributions... the corresponding communication overhead becomes affordable... This design drastically reduces communication volume while preserving the mathematical equivalence..." [section 4.2] Loss curve alignment with a pure FSDP baseline empirically validates the mathematical equivalence.  
- **Break condition:** The teacher’s LM head is unusually large/complex (e.g., tied embeddings or non‑linear projections) making local recomputation expensive, or network interconnects become so fast that full‑logit transfer is no longer a bottleneck.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Abstracting the complex distributed communication logic behind simple, extensible APIs allows researchers to rapidly prototype and scale diverse KD algorithms (off‑policy, on‑policy, cross‑tokenizer) with minimal engineering overhead.  
- **Mechanism:** The `Trainer` and actor groups (`TeacherActorGroup`, `StudentActorGroup`, `RolloutActorGroup`) handle all data routing, device placement, and synchronization. Users interact with high‑level trainer classes (`OffPolicyKDTrainer`, `OnPolicyKDTrainer`) and plug in custom loss functions or divergence metrics (FKL, RKL, JSD, TVD, DSKDv2) without modifying the core distributed execution pipeline.  
- **Core assumption:** The main barrier to KD research scalability is the engineering complexity of building efficient distributed systems; abstracting this complexity lowers the adoption barrier.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Furthermore, our framework supports both off‑policy and on‑policy distillation and incorporates KD algorithms for cross‑tokenizer KD through highly extensible and user‑friendly APIs." [section 3.4] "KDFlow strictly separates the underlying system pipeline from the distillation algorithms... Users can also easily implement their custom distillation losses... eliminating the need of understand the complex distributed communication logic." [section 5, Limitations] "KDFlow explicitly prioritizes the needs of the research community: user‑friendliness, high flexibility, and rapid prototyping."  
- **Break condition:** Advanced users require deep access to communication scheduler or memory placement for novel, system‑aware KD algorithms that the current abstraction cannot expose without compromising stability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Knowledge Distillation**  
  *Why needed here:* KDFlow executes KD, where a smaller student learns from a larger teacher. Understanding this paradigm is essential to grasp why two models with different roles (inference‑only vs. training) coexist.  
  *Quick check question:* What is the fundamental difference between "off‑policy" and "on‑policy" distillation in terms of the data source for the student?

- **Concept: Mixture-of‑Experts (MoE) Models**  
  *Why needed here:* MoE teachers (e.g., Qwen3‑30B‑A3B) are a key stress test where decoupling yields the largest speedups (6.36×). MoE’s sparse activation is poorly handled by standard training backends (ZeRO‑3/FSDP2) but efficiently served by inference engines like SGLang.  
  *Quick check question:* Why might a standard training backend like FSDP2 struggle with an MoE model during forward passes compared to an inference‑optimized engine?

- **Concept: Distributed Training Backends (FSDP2, ZeRO‑3)**  
  *Why needed here:* KDFlow’s student side uses FSDP2. Understanding its trade‑offs (sharding parameters, gradients, optimizer states) explains why it is unsuitable for the teacher’s high‑throughput inference needs.  
  *Quick check question:* What is the primary purpose of a system like FSDP2 in model training, and what overhead does it introduce for pure inference workloads?

- **Concept: Inference Engines (SGLang, vLLM)**  
  *Why needed here:* The teacher backbone is SGLang. Its optimizations (continuous batching, kernel fusion, RadixAttention) explain why it is chosen for the teacher’s inference‑heavy role.  
  *Quick check question:* What is "continuous batching" and why is it critical for maximizing throughput in LLM inference servers?

- **Concept: Zero‑Copy Data Transfer & Shared Memory**  
  *Why needed here:* The hidden‑state transfer mechanism relies on zero‑copy for efficiency; it is the practical enabler of the communication‑reduction claim.  
  *Quick check question:* In a distributed system like Ray, what is the performance implication of "zero‑copy" shared memory versus serializing and sending tensors over the network?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Ray `Trainer` (single process) orchestrates:  
`Trainer` → `TeacherActorGroup` (SGLang engine, one or more Ray actors) → `Trainer` → `StudentActorGroup` (FSDP2‑sharded models, multiple actors) → `Trainer`.  
For on‑policy: `Trainer` ↔ `RolloutActorGroup` (SGLang Router for response generation) with weight updates via CUDA IPC.

**Critical path (Off‑policy):**  
1. `Trainer` → `TeacherActorGroup`: (prompt, response).  
2. `TeacherActorGroup` → `Trainer`: teacher hidden states.  
3. `Trainer` → `StudentActorGroup`: (prompt, response, teacher hidden states).  
4. `StudentActorGroup` → `Trainer`: loss, metrics.  
5. Repeat.

**Critical path (On‑policy):**  
1. `Trainer` → `RolloutActorGroup`: prompts.  
2. `RolloutActorGroup` → `Trainer`: (prompt, response).  
3. `Trainer` → `TeacherActorGroup`: (prompt, response).  
4. `TeacherActorGroup` → `Trainer`: teacher hidden states.  
5. `Trainer` → `StudentActorGroup`: (prompt, response, teacher hidden states).  
6. `StudentActorGroup` → `Trainer`: loss, metrics.  
7. `Trainer` → `RolloutActorGroup`: updated student weights (CUDA IPC).  
8. Repeat.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Specialization vs. Flexibility:* Decoupling maximizes throughput for standard KD but may hide fine‑grained control over the teacher’s inference scheduling (now managed by SGLang).  
- *Communication Latency vs. Volume:* Hidden‑state transfer reduces volume but adds recomputation on the student; beneficial for large vocabs/long sequences, less so for tiny students.  
- *User‑Friendliness vs. Advanced Features:* High‑level API eases rapid prototyping but may limit access to system‑level knobs (e.g., SGLang scheduling policies) needed for extreme‑scale or non‑standard clusters.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Teacher bottleneck:** High GPU utilization on `TeacherActorGroup` GPUs but low on `StudentActorGroup`; indicates saturated SGLang or slow hidden‑state generation.  
- **Communication stall:** `StudentActorGroup` frequently idle waiting for hidden states; suggests network congestion or Ray object‑store pressure. Check Ray dashboard.  
- **Numerical drift:** Student loss diverges from FSDP baseline over many steps; could be precision issues (FP8 teacher hidden states) or incorrect LM head application.  
- **On‑policy staleness:** Student performance degrades; verify `RolloutActorGroup` uses fresh weights via CUDA IPC success rate and latency.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Loss Curve Validation:** Compare KDFlow to a monolithic FSDP baseline on a small teacher‑student pair (e.g., Qwen3‑1.5B → 0.5B). Plot KL divergence per step; they should be nearly identical, confirming hidden‑state→logit recomputation correctness.  
2. **MoE Teacher Scaling Test:** Use a MoE teacher (e.g., Qwen3‑30B‑A3B) with a small student. Measure iterations/sec and compare against MS‑SWIFT; expect >3× speedup, validating core benefits for sparse models.  
3. **Cross‑Tokenizer Functionality:** Use teacher and student with different tokenizers (e.g., Llama‑3.1‑8B → Qwen3‑1.7B) and DSKDv2 loss. Run on 100 LMSys‑Chat‑1M samples; ensure pipeline stability and a smoothly decreasing loss, confirming API versatility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can KDFlow integrate alternative training backends like Megatron‑LM to support complex 3D parallelism while preserving its user‑friendly API abstraction and decoupled architecture?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations section explicitly states that KDFlow’s student backend relies on PyTorch FSDP2, which struggles to match Megatron‑LM’s training efficiency with 3D parallelism, and identifies integrating Megatron‑LM as a key future direction.  
- **Why unresolved:** KDFlow’s current design is tightly coupled with FSDP2’s parallel strategies; Megatron‑LM requires tensor, pipeline, and data parallelism coordination, which may conflict with the decoupled teacher‑student separation and Ray‑based coordination, risking increased complexity or performance loss.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a Megatron‑LM backend within KDFlow’s FSDP2‑centric framework using the same high‑level APIs (e.g., `StudentActorGroup` modifications), then benchmark training throughput, memory usage, and scalability on large models (e.g., 100B+ parameters) compared to pure Megatron‑LM setups, while evaluating usability via user studies on algorithm implementation ease.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the hidden‑state transfer and logit recomputation mechanism maintain mathematical equivalence and numerical stability across all supported KD algorithms (e.g., Reverse KL, JSD) and model architectures beyond the tested Qwen3 family, especially for non‑MoE or extreme‑scale teachers?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper validates loss correctness only for Forward KL with Qwen3 models and mentions that SGLang inference may introduce precision issues in RL contexts; however, comprehensive testing across KDFlow’s supported divergence metrics and diverse architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder, smaller dense models) is absent from experiments.  
- **Why unresolved:** The loss curve alignment in Figure 4 covers a narrow scenario; recomputation from hidden states could amplify numerical errors or divergence metric sensitivities for other algorithms or architectures, potentially degrading KD performance, but this is not systematically explored.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct extensive experiments measuring loss divergence, convergence rates, and downstream task performance (e.g., AlpacaEval) for all built‑in KD algorithms (FKL, RKL, JSD, TVD) across varied teacher‑student pairs (e.g., Llama, Mistral models, dense vs. MoE) and hidden state dimensions, comparing against full‑logit baselines to detect instabilities or performance gaps.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What are the scalability limits of KDFlow in large‑scale distributed environments (e.g., thousands of GPUs across multiple nodes), and how can industrial optimizations like asynchronous training be incorporated without compromising the framework’s research‑oriented simplicity and API consistency?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations explicitly note the lack of asynchronous training and other optimizations for cluster‑scale training, calling it crucial for thousand‑GPU setups and a future work direction.  
- **Why unresolved:** Current evaluations are on a single 8‑GPU server using Ray’s synchronous coordination; asynchronous operations could reduce idle time but introduce complexity in weight synchronization, fault tolerance, and controller logic, potentially undermining KDFlow’s claim of minimal engineering overhead.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement asynchronous weight updates and communication in the Trainer‑RolloutActorGroup‑StudentActorGroup pipeline, then scale experiments to multi‑node clusters (e.g., 128+ GPUs) measuring throughput, communication overhead, and stability during failures, while surveying researchers on API usability changes.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How extensible is KDFlow’s API for novel cross‑tokenizer KD algorithms beyond DSKDv2, and does the hidden‑state based approach generalize to methods requiring token‑level alignment or non‑vocabulary mapping strategies?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] KDFlow supports cross‑tokenizer KD via DSKDv2 and claims highly extensible APIs, but only this single algorithm is implemented and tested, leaving untested the integration of other approaches (e.g., optimal transport‑based methods from references like Cui et al., 2025).  
- **Why unresolved:** DSKDv2 may rely on specific assumptions about hidden‑state utility; other cross‑tokenizer methods might need additional data (e.g., token embeddings) or computational steps that the hidden‑state transfer doesn’t naturally support, potentially requiring API modifications that break abstraction.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement at least two alternative cross‑tokenizer KD algorithms (e.g., from Wan et al., 2024; Minixhofer et al., 2025) using KDFlow’s abstractions, assess implementation difficulty via code changes and user effort, and compare distillation performance on benchmarks with significant tokenizer mismatches (e.g., different languages or tokenization schemes).

## Limitations
- Missing key training hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, optimizer choice, scheduler, warmup steps, total training steps) limits exact replication of training dynamics.  
- SGLang engine configuration (tensor parallelism, attention backend, precision) is not fully described, hindering precise reproduction of the teacher inference setup.  
- Baseline frameworks (TRL, MS‑SWIFT, ROLL) versions and configurations are unspecified, making the reported speedup comparisons difficult to verify.
