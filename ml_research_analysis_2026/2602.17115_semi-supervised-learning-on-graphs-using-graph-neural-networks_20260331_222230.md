# Semi-Supervised Learning on Graphs using Graph Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17115
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17115
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Theoretical analysis of semi-supervised node regression with GNNs yields a sharp risk bound showing explicit dependence on label fraction π and receptive field size m, with approximation guarantees and empirical validation.

## Executive Summary
The paper analyzes semi-supervised node regression on graphs using graph neural networks. The node responses are modeled as a smooth nonlinear function applied to a graph-smoothed version of the node features. The authors derive a non-asymptotic risk bound for least-squares estimators with GNNs that employ linear graph convolutions (fixed propagation) and a deep ReLU readout. The bound separates approximation, stochastic, and optimization errors, and explicitly shows how the prediction error scales with the fraction of labeled nodes π and the receptive field size m, thereby explaining both the sample efficiency of GNNs and their sensitivity to graph topology. Approximation results prove that such GNNs can effectively approximate the underlying compositional functions, recovering optimal nonparametric rates under full supervision while adapting to label scarcity. The rates depend on intrinsic dimensions of the compositional smoothness, avoiding the curse of dimensionality. Numerical experiments on synthetic and real-world data confirm the theoretical predictions and demonstrate the superior stability of skip-connected GCNs across different graph topologies.

## Method Summary
The study considers a regression task where each node's label is generated as \(Y_i = \phi^*( \psi^*_A,i(X) ) + \varepsilon_i\), with \(\varepsilon_i\) independent noise. Here, \(\psi^*_A\) is a graph-dependent linear smoothing operator (modeled as a polynomial in a fixed propagation matrix \(S_A\)), and \(\phi^*\) is a smooth nonlinear function. Only a random subset \(\Omega\) of nodes is labeled, with each node included with probability \(\pi\). The estimator is a two-part GNN: (i) a linear graph convolutional network that computes \(H^{(\ell)} = T H^{(\ell-1)} W_\ell\) for \(\ell=1,\dots,L_1\), with \(H^{(0)}=X\) and fixed propagation matrix \(T\), then aggregates all layers via a weighted sum \(Z = \sum_{\ell=1}^{L_1} \gamma_\ell H^{(\ell)}\); (ii) a deep feedforward ReLU network \(h\) (with width vector \(p\) and sparsity constraint \(s\)) that maps each row of \(Z\) to a prediction \(f_i(X)=h(Z_{i,:})\). Parameters \((W_\ell,\gamma_\ell)\) of the GCN and the weights of \(h\) are learned by minimizing the empirical squared loss over \(\Omega\), typically using gradient-based methods. The theoretical framework assumes bounded parameters (\([-1,1]\)) and that each loss depends on at most \(m\) nodes, where \(m\) is the receptive field size determined by the graph topology and depth \(L_1\).

## Key Results
- Derived a sharp non-asymptotic oracle inequality for the GNN least-squares estimator, cleanly separating approximation, stochastic, and optimization errors.
- The stochastic error term explicitly depends on the label budget \(\pi\) (through \(1/\pi\)) and the receptive field size \(m\) (through \(m^2\)), explaining both the sample efficiency of GNNs and their sensitivity to graph topology.
- Approximation theory shows that GNNs with linear convolutions and deep ReLU readout can recover optimal nonparametric rates under full supervision while adapting to label scarcity; the convergence rates avoid the curse of dimensionality by leveraging intrinsic dimensions of the compositional smoothness.
- Empirical experiments on synthetic and real data confirm the theoretical scaling laws and demonstrate that skip-connected GCNs offer greater stability across varying graph topologies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Architectural alignment with compositional truth
The linear GCN layers (with depth \(L_1 \ge k\)) approximate the inner polynomial \(\psi^*_A\) using powers of the fixed graph operator \(S_A\), while the deep ReLU readout approximates the outer smooth function \(\phi^*\) via compositions with low intrinsic dimensions, thereby avoiding the curse of dimensionality. This relies on the assumptions that \(\psi^*_A\) lies in a polynomial class \(\mathcal{F}_\rho(\beta,k,S_A)\) and \(\phi^*\) belongs to a compositional smoothness class \(\mathcal{G}(q,d,t,\alpha,K)\). The approximation error decays with network size (Lemma 4). If these structural conditions are violated, the GNN may be unable to represent the true function.

### Mechanism 2: Error scaling with label budget and graph topology
The stochastic error component in the oracle inequality (Theorem 1) contains a term \((m^2F^2 \log N_\delta)/(\varepsilon n \pi) + F\delta\sqrt{\pi}\), which directly shows that the risk scales linearly with \(1/\pi\) and quadratically with the receptive field size \(m\). This indicates that GNNs remain effective even with few labeled nodes, but performance degrades when \(m\) is large (i.e., when the graph induces strong dependence among node predictions). This scaling follows from Assumption 1, which limits the influence neighborhood of each loss. The result is validated by the abstract and Corollary 2.

### Mechanism 3: Topology sensitivity via receptive field
The receptive field size \(m\) is highly sensitive to graph structure. For bounded-degree graphs, \(m\) grows polynomially with depth (e.g., \(m \sim \Delta^{L_1}\)), but adding a few long-range edges can cause an exponential jump in \(m\) (small-world effect), leading to a sharp increase in error due to the \(m^2\) factor. This explains the observed sensitivity of GNNs to graph perturbations and the advantage of using normalized propagation operators, as demonstrated in Section 4.2 and Figure 5.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- Graph preprocessing: From adjacency \(A\), compute \(\tilde{A} = A + I_n\), degree matrix \(\tilde{D}\), then choose propagation operator \(T\) (e.g., \(\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\), \(\tilde{D}^{-1}\tilde{A}\), or \(\tilde{A}\)). \(T\) is fixed and not learned.
- GCN block (linear): For \(\ell=1,\dots,L_1\), compute \(H^{(\ell)} = T H^{(\ell-1)} W_\ell\) with \(H^{(0)} = X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\), \(W_\ell \in [-1,1]^{d \times d}\). Then aggregate: \(Z = \sum_{\ell=1}^{L_1} \gamma_\ell H^{(\ell)}\), with \(\gamma_\ell \in [-1,1]\). \(Z \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\).
- DNN readout: Deep ReLU network \(h: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\). Architecture: \(L_2\) layers with width vector \(p = (p_0, p_1, \dots, p_{L_2+1})\), \(p_0=d\), \(p_{L_2+1}=1\). Each layer: \(U_\ell(y) = M_\ell y + b_\ell\); for \(\ell < L_2\), apply ReLU elementwise. All entries in \(M_\ell, b_\ell\) lie in \([-1,1]\). Total non-zero entries among all \(M_\ell, b_\ell\) does not exceed \(s\) (sparsity). Prediction for node \(i\): \(f_i(X) = h(Z_{i,:})\).
- Loss: \(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i \in \Omega} (Y_i - f_i(X))^2\).
- Training: Optimize all GCN and DNN parameters jointly (SGD/Adam); \(T\) remains fixed.

### Critical path
1. Implement graph normalization to compute \(T\) from \(A\).
2. Implement the linear GCN block with arbitrary depth \(L_1\) and weighted sum of all layers (skip connections).
3. Implement a deep ReLU network with specified width vector and support for parameter sparsity counting if needed.
4. Combine: compute \(Z\) via GCN, then apply DNN row-wise (or batched); compute masked loss over \(\Omega\).
5. Use an optimizer (Adam) to update parameters; optionally project parameters to \([-1,1]\) after each step to respect theoretical bounds.
6. Validate on a tiny synthetic dataset with known ground truth.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Depth \(L_1\)**: Must be at least true polynomial order \(k\) of \(\psi^*_A\); too deep increases receptive field \(m\) and variance, too shallow underfits. Theory suggests \(L_1 \approx k\).
- **DNN width and sparsity \(s\)**: Larger width reduces bias but increases variance via entropy; theory sets inner widths \(\sim N\) (related to intrinsic dimensions) and \(s \sim N \log n\). In practice, use validation to choose.
- **Propagation operator \(T\)**: Unnormalized (e.g., sum \(\tilde{A}\)) is sensitive to degree; normalized (e.g., \(\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\) or \(\tilde{D}^{-1}\tilde{A}\)) stabilizes performance. Prefer normalized unless domain knowledge suggests otherwise.
- **Skip connections**: Weighted sum over all GCN layers mitigates over-smoothing and improves stability; avoid using only the final layer.
- **Parameter bounds**: Theory assumes \([-1,1]\) for all parameters. In practice, small initialization (e.g., uniform(-0.1,0.1)), weight decay, or clipping can approximate this constraint.

### Failure signatures
- **Non-convergence (loss NaN/plateau)**: May be due to too high learning rate, poor initialization, or vanishing gradients. Try smaller LR, orthogonal initialization, gradient clipping.
- **Overfitting (train loss ≈ 0, val high)**: Likely \(m\) too large (\(L_1\) too deep) or DNN too wide. Reduce \(L_1\), reduce DNN width, or add regularization (dropout, L2).
- **Underfitting (train loss high)**: Capacity insufficient. Increase \(L_1\) (but ≤ true \(k\)), increase DNN width, or verify \(T\) is expressive enough.
- **Excessive sensitivity to graph perturbations**: Expected if \(m\) is large. Switch to normalized \(T\) and keep \(L_1\) moderate.
- **Poor performance on high-degree graphs**: Use normalized \(T\) (e.g., \(\tilde{D}^{-1}\tilde{A}\)) instead of sum; avoid unnormalized operators.

### First 3 experiments (ordered by priority)
1. **Synthetic rate validation**: Generate a ring graph with \(n\) nodes and uniform features \(X_i \sim \text{Unif}[0,1]^d\). Set \(\psi^*_A(x) = \sum_{j=1}^2 \theta_j T^j x\) with \(T\) the ring’s normalized adjacency and random \(\theta_j\). Define \(\phi^*\) as a 1D compositional smooth function with smoothness \(\alpha \approx 0.5\) and intrinsic dimension \(t_0=1\) (e.g., \(\phi^*(z) = \text{BM}(\text{sigmoid}(z/\text{scale}))\)). Fix \(\pi = 0.5\). Train a GNN with \(L_1 \ge 2\), \(L_2=2\), and sufficient DNN width. Vary \(n\) (e.g., 500 to 5000), plot \(\log(\text{MSE})\) vs \(\log(n)\). Expected slope \(\approx -2\alpha^*/(2\alpha^*+t_0) = -1/2\). Also vary \(\pi\) to confirm the \(1/\pi\) scaling.
2. **Effect of graph topology**: With \(n=3000\) and the same data generating process, create graph variants: (a) ring (\(\Delta=3\)), (b) ring with a few random long edges (small-world, \(\Delta\) increases), (c) Barabási–Albert with varying \(m\) parameter. For each, compare two propagation operators: \(T = \tilde{A}\) (sum) versus \(T = \tilde{D}^{-1}\tilde{A}\) (normalized). Train the same GNN (\(L_1=2\)). Measure test MSE. Expect normalized to be stable across topologies, while sum degrades as maximum degree grows. Also compute empirical receptive field size (average number of nodes within \(L_1\) hops) and correlate with performance.
3. **Skip connections vs plain last layer**: Compare the proposed GCN (weighted sum over all layers) against a version that uses only the final layer \(Z = H^{(L_1)}\). Use synthetic data with increasing depth \(L_1 = 3,4,5\) on a ring graph. Measure test MSE and training stability. Skip connections should maintain or improve performance as depth increases, while the last‑layer‑only version may degrade due to over‑smoothing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**1. Graph topology’s effect on optimization error and implicit regularization of SGD**  
Basis: The authors write that the optimization error \(\Delta F_n\), governed by training dynamics, remains unanalyzed; SGD’s implicit regularization in the presence of graph‑induced dependencies is an open challenge.  
Why unresolved: Theorem 1 includes \(\Delta F_n\) but does not quantify it; SGD may not converge to the global minimum and graph dependencies could alter the optimization landscape and implicit bias, leaving \(\Delta F_n\) unknown.  
What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical analysis of SGD convergence for GNNs under Assumption 1 yielding a high‑probability bound on \(\Delta F_n\) in terms of graph parameters (e.g., receptive field size \(m\), number of iterations) or empirical measurements of the optimization gap across graph topologies and optimizers.

**2. Replacing worst‑case receptive field \(m\) with refined graph characteristics**  
Basis: The paper notes that reliance on the locality parameter \(m\) yields conservative bounds for dense graphs or architectures with global readouts; using spectral properties like spectral gap or conductance could lead to tighter guarantees.  
Why unresolved: The current proof uses a vertex‑coloring argument depending on the maximum neighborhood size \(m\), resulting in an \(m^2\) factor in the stochastic error. For graphs with strong expansion properties, the effective dependence may be much smaller but is not captured by the bound.  
What evidence would resolve it: New concentration inequalities for graph‑dependent random variables that incorporate spectral graph measures (e.g., Alon–Boppana bound, Cheeger constant) and a refined oracle inequality where \(m\) is replaced by a quantity that scales logarithmically for expander‑like graphs.

**3. Robustness to graph noise and joint graph learning**  
Basis: The framework assumes a fixed known graph; extending the risk analysis to settings where the graph topology is learned jointly with the regression function or analyzing minimax rates under adversarial edge perturbations is an open direction.  
Why unresolved: The model and Assumption 1 fix the propagation operator \(S_A\). The approximate error analysis only covers a known mismatch \(\tau\) between \(S_A\) and the implemented operator \(T\). If the graph is estimated from the same data or subject to attacks, the resulting graph error interacts with regression error in an uncharacterized way. Joint estimation introduces extra bias‑variance trade‑offs.  
What evidence would resolve it: A statistical theory for simultaneous graph learning and regression, including minimax rates under graph noise models (e.g., Bernoulli edge corruption) and oracle inequalities that incorporate a graph estimation error term. For adversarial settings, robust GNN architectures with provable guarantees against worst‑case edge perturbations should be developed and integrated into the semi‑supervised framework.

## Limitations
- Theory is restricted to linear GCNs with a fixed propagation operator and ReLU readout; it does not cover learned or nonlinear graph updates.
- Asymptotic conditions on network width and sparsity do not provide concrete guidance for finite-sample settings.
- The graph dependence parameter \(m\) is abstract and not directly tied to graph properties such as degree or expansion; moreover, the analysis ignores the optimization error introduced by practical SGD/Adam training.

## Confidence
- Approximation guarantees (Theorem 2): High
- Risk scaling with \(1/\pi\) and \(m^2\) (Theorem 1, Corollary 2): High
- Topology sensitivity via \(m\): Medium
- Skip connections benefit: Medium
- Generalization to arbitrary GNNs: Low
