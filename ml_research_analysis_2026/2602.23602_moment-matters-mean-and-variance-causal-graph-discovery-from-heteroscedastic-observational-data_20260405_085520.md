# Moment Matters: Mean and Variance Causal Graph Discovery from Heteroscedastic Observational Data

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.23602
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23602
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces a Bayesian variational framework for separate mean and variance causal graph discovery, achieving superior recovery on synthetic benchmarks and matching state-of-the-art on the Sachs dataset while uncovering a biologically plausible variance edge.

## Executive Summary
Standard causal discovery methods typically assume homoscedastic noise, limiting their ability to leverage heteroscedastic information that can distinguish between direct effects on the mean and the variance of a variable. This paper addresses that limitation by proposing a Bayesian variational approach that simultaneously infers two causal graphs: one for mean effects and one for variance effects. The method relies on identifiability conditions including nonlinear mean functions, piecewise non-constant variance, Gaussian noise, and a shared topological ordering. Experiments on synthetic and semi-synthetic data show significant improvements over existing baselines (MC3, DDS, ICDH, HOST) in graph recovery metrics (SHD, F1). On the real-world Sachs dataset, the method matches moment-agnostic state-of-the-art and further improves with incorporation of prior knowledge under small sample regimes. A case study demonstrates high posterior probability for a biologically meaningful variance edge (MEK→ERK) despite limited data, highlighting the practical value of modeling heteroscedasticity.

## Method Summary
The method constructs a generative model where each variable’s observation is expressed as a nonlinear function of its parents plus additive Gaussian noise, with the noise variance itself being a function of the parents (heteroscedastic). This yields two distinct but coupled directed acyclic graphs (DAGs): one governing mean relationships and another governing variance relationships. To ensure identifiability, the authors assume that the mean functions are nonlinear, the variance functions are piecewise constant, the noise is Gaussian, and both graphs share the same topological ordering. For inference, a variational Bayesian framework approximates the posterior over graph structures and parameters using a factorized distribution. The evidence lower bound (ELBO) is maximized via an optimization routine that incorporates curvature information (e.g., natural gradients) to better navigate the non-convex landscape. Additionally, domain knowledge in the form of node-ordering constraints can be encoded as priors to guide the search, which is particularly beneficial when data are scarce.

## Key Results
- On synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets, the method significantly outperforms baseline causal discovery algorithms (MC3, DDS, ICDH, HOST) in both mean and variance graph recovery, achieving lower structural Hamming distance (SHD) and higher F1 scores.
- On the Sachs signalling pathway dataset, the proposed approach matches the performance of state-of-the-art moment-agnostic methods and shows improvement when prior node ordering information is incorporated, especially in low-sample settings.
- A biological case study identifies a variance edge from MEK to ERK with high posterior probability, a relationship supported by literature but not captured by moment-agnostic methods, demonstrating the ability to uncover heteroscedastic effects.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by exploiting heteroscedastic noise as an additional information source that standard homoscedastic models ignore. Because the variance of a variable depends on its parents, the data distribution encodes constraints that allow the mean and variance graphs to be disentangled, provided the identifiability conditions hold. Non-linear mean functions ensure that conditional means vary sufficiently across parent values; piecewise constant variance creates distinct regimes that help separate direct variance influences from indirect ones via the shared ordering. The Gaussian noise assumption simplifies likelihood computation and contributes to identifiability. The Bayesian variational treatment accounts for uncertainty in graph structures and parameters, which is crucial given the non-convex optimization over discrete DAGs. Curvature-aware updates (e.g., using natural gradients or second-order approximations) accelerate convergence and avoid poor local optima in the highly non-convex ELBO landscape. Finally, incorporating node-ordering priors from domain knowledge reduces the search space and improves sample efficiency, making the method viable even with limited data.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Data -> Model Specification (Mean DAG + Variance DAG, shared ordering) -> Prior Integration (node-ordering constraints) -> Variational Distribution Setup -> ELBO Calculation -> Curvature-Aware Optimization -> Updated Variational Parameters -> Posterior Graph Extraction.

Critical path:
The core workflow starts with the input dataset and a specification of the heteroscedastic generative model, including separate DAGs for mean and variance. Priors (node ordering) are incorporated. A factorized variational distribution over edges and parameters is defined. The ELBO, consisting of expected log-likelihood and prior terms minus the entropy, is computed. Optimization using curvature-aware updates (e.g., natural gradient) iteratively refines the variational parameters. Upon convergence, posterior edge probabilities for both mean and variance graphs are extracted and thresholded to obtain final graphs.

Design tradeoffs:
- *Flexibility vs. Identifiability*: The assumptions (nonlinear means, piecewise constant variance, Gaussian noise) are restrictive but necessary for identifiability; relaxing them may yield more realistic models but risks unidentifiability.
- *Expressiveness vs. Computation*: The Bayesian treatment with full posterior over graph structures is expressive but computationally intensive; variational approximation trades exactness for scalability.
- *Prior Knowledge vs. Bias*: Node-ordering priors improve sample efficiency but can mislead if the prior is incorrect, potentially leading to biased graph recovery.

Failure signatures:
- ELBO fails to converge or shows oscillations, indicating optimization instability or poor curvature handling.
- Posterior edge probabilities are near uniform (high uncertainty), suggesting insufficient data or violated assumptions (e.g., non-Gaussian noise).
- The inferred variance graph shows frequent level changes inconsistent with piecewise constant assumption, hinting at model misspecification.
- A conflict between the node-ordering prior and the data likelihood leads to a plateau in ELBO improvement or high reconstruction error.

First experiments (3):
1. **Synthetic replication**: Generate data from the paper’s generative model with known mean/variance graphs, run the method, and compute SHD/F1 to confirm basic functionality.
2. **Ablation of curvature-aware updates**: Replace the curvature-aware optimizer with standard SGD or Adam and compare convergence speed and final graph recovery to isolate the benefit of second-order information.
3. **Prior strength test**: Vary the strength of the node-ordering prior and evaluate performance on small-sample Sachs data to quantify how much prior knowledge contributes to robustness.

## Limitations
- **Strong modeling assumptions**: The identifiability relies on Gaussian noise, piecewise constant variance, nonlinear mean functions, and a shared topological ordering; these may not hold in many real-world heteroscedastic datasets.
- **Evaluation scope**: The real-data validation is limited to the Sachs dataset; scalability to larger graphs and performance on diverse heteroscedastic domains (e.g., single-cell genomics) remain unproven.
- **Biological validation**: The detected variance edge (MEK→ERK) lacks wet-lab experimental confirmation, so its causal status remains hypothetical.
