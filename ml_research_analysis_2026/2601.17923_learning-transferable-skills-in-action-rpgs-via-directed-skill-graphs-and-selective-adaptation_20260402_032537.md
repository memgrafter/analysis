# Learning Transferable Skills in Action RPGs via Directed Skill Graphs and Selective Adaptation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17923
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17923
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** A modular agent with a directed skill graph reaches 44% win rate in Dark Souls III Phase 1 (~230k steps), and selective fine-tuning of two downstream skills after a phase shift recovers performance to 52% win rate under a limited interaction budget.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles lifelong learning in real-time action RPGs without catastrophic forgetting. The authors factorize complex combat into five reusable skills organized in a directed graph, training them sequentially via a hierarchical curriculum. This modular decomposition reduces the burden on individual policies. When the boss behavior changes (Phase 2), the agent achieves modest zero-shot transfer. Critically, selectively fine-tuning only the two downstream skills (dodging, heal-attack) under a tight interaction budget restores and even improves performance, demonstrating effective skill transfer and localized adaptation.

## Method Summary
The method decomposes the combat task into five primitive and composite skills (e.g., movement, attack, dodge, heal, and a heal-attack combination), structuring them in a directed acyclic graph that encodes dependency (e.g., dodge must precede heal-attack). Each skill is implemented as a separate deep RL policy. Training follows a hierarchical curriculum: base skills are trained first in isolation, then more complex skills are introduced sequentially, leveraging previously learned policies. Upon a domain shift (boss phase change), the system performs selective adaptation—only the policies for skills directly impacted by the new phase (the two downstream skills) are fine-tuned with limited new data, while others remain frozen.

## Key Results
- The modular agent achieves a 44% win rate against Phase 1 with ~230k steps, while a monolithic DQN agent fails to learn.
- Ablations reveal dodging and heal-attack are critical; randomizing these two skills reduces Phase 1 win rate to 0–16%.
- After shifting to Phase 2, zero-shot transfer yields 33.3% win rate from mid-range starts and 12.5% from long-range. Selective fine-tuning of only the two downstream skills under a limited budget recovers performance to 52% win rate.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The directed skill graph and sequential curriculum localize learning, preventing catastrophic interference between dissimilar subtasks. Each policy faces a reduced state-action space, easing optimization. The graph ensures proper dependency: foundational skills (e.g., dodge) are mastered before they are composed into higher-level skills (e.g., heal-attack). Selective adaptation is effective because the phase change primarily affects the boss's attack patterns, which directly impact the utility of dodging and heal-attack. By updating only these two policies, the agent efficiently adapts without overwriting stable, reusable skills like basic movement or combat, thereby preserving prior knowledge.

## Foundational Learning
- **Skill decomposition:** Breaking a complex continuous control task into discrete, reusable motor/cognitive primitives. *Why needed: Reduces policy complexity and enables targeted learning.* *Quick check: Can each decomposed skill be executed reliably in isolation?*
- **Directed acyclic skill graph:** A formal representation of dependencies between skills (e.g., Skill B requires Skill A). *Why needed: Enforces a valid learning order and prevents attempting composite skills before fundamentals.* *Quick check: Is the graph acyclic and does it cover all necessary skill compositions?*
- **Hierarchical curriculum learning:** Sequentially introducing skills from simple to complex. *Why needed: Mitigates non-stationarity and catastrophic forgetting during multi-skill acquisition.* *Quick check: Does performance on earlier skills degrade when later skills are introduced?*
- **Selective fine-tuning / module freezing:** Adapting only a subset of learned modules to a new context. *Why needed: Maximizes reuse of stable skills and minimizes interference/forgetting under limited new data.* *Quick check: Does fine-tuning unfrozen modules degrade performance on frozen modules?*
- **Catastrophic forgetting mitigation:** Preserving performance on previously learned tasks when learning new ones. *Why needed: Core requirement for lifelong learning in non-stationary environments.* *Quick check: Measure win rate on Phase 1 after Phase 2 adaptation.*
- **Transfer in adversarial domains:** Applying learned skills to a slightly modified opponent (new boss phase). *Why needed: Real-world games have dynamic opponents; robust skills must generalize.* *Quick check: Does zero-shot transfer occur, and what skills fail to transfer?*

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Base Sensors (raw pixels, game state) -> Motion Skill Policy -> Attack Skill Policy -> Dodge Skill Policy -> Heal Skill Policy -> Composite Heal-Attack Skill Policy -> Hierarchical Skill Selector -> Action Executor -> Dark Souls III Environment

**Critical Path:**  
1. Train Motion and Attack policies to baseline competency.  
2. Train Dodge policy (requires Motion).  
3. Train Heal policy (requires Motion).  
4. Train Heal-Attack composite policy (requires Dodge & Heal & Attack).  
5. Integrate all policies under a selector for full combat.  
6. Upon phase change, freeze Motion/Attack/Heal, fine-tune Dodge and Heal-Attack.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Manual skill graph design* vs. automatic discovery: Manual design ensures meaningful, interpretable skills but limits scalability and may miss emergent primitives.  
- *Modular policies* vs. monolithic network: Modularity aids interpretability, targeted adaptation, and reduces interference but may introduce suboptimal coordination and requires hand-tuning of the graph.  
- *Sequential curriculum* vs. joint training: Sequential simplifies training but risks early mistakes becoming entrenched; joint training is harder but could lead to more coordinated skills.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Abrupt performance drop on previously mastered skills when a new skill is introduced → indicates poor curriculum ordering or insufficient modularity.  
- Zero-shot transfer failure to new phase → suggests skills are overfit to specific enemy behaviors or lack robustness.  
- Selective fine-tuning harms overall performance → implies incorrect identification of downstream skills or destructive interference between fine-tuned and frozen modules.

**Three First Experiments:**  
1. **Skill Isolation Benchmark:** Train each of the five skills in separate, curated environments (e.g., dodge training against a fixed attack pattern). Verify each reaches >90% success.  
2. **Graph Integration Test:** Add skills sequentially following the graph. Plot win rate against the number of integrated skills to confirm cumulative learning without forgetting.  
3. **Phase Shift Protocol:** Train to mastery on Phase 1, then switch to Phase 2. Compare three conditions: (a) no adaptation (zero-shot), (b) fine-tune all skills, (c) fine-tune only dodging and heal-attack. Measure final win rate and steps to recovery.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is restricted to a single game (Dark Souls III); generalizability to other action RPGs or genres is unknown.
- The skill graph and the set of five skills are manually engineered; the paper does not address automatic discovery of skill graphs from raw interaction.
- No explicit measurement of catastrophic forgetting on Phase 1 performance after Phase 2 training/fine-tuning; the claim of avoiding forgetting is inferred but not quantified.

## Confidence
- **Modular efficiency (vs. DQN baseline):** Medium – DQN is a weak, outdated baseline for complex control; stronger modern algorithms might narrow the gap.
- **Critical role of dodging/heal-attack (ablation evidence):** High – the drastic performance drop (0–16% win rate) when these are randomized provides strong, direct evidence.
- **Zero-shot phase transfer:** Medium – results are promising (33.3%, 12.5%) but based on a single phase shift; requires more seeds for statistical reliability.
- **Selective fine-tuning benefits:** Medium-High – clear improvement under a limited interaction budget (52% vs. 33.3% zero-shot), but long-term effects on all skills are untested.
- **Avoiding catastrophic forgetting:** Low – not directly measured; the claim is based on the success of selective adaptation, not on quantifying retention of Phase 1 performance.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-domain validation:** Replicate the entire pipeline on at least two other action RPGs (e.g., Sekiro, Elden Ring) to test the transferability of the directed skill graph concept.
2. **Forgetting quantification:** After the Phase 2 selective fine-tuning phase, evaluate the agent on Phase 1 scenarios to directly measure any degradation in the frozen skills.
3. **Baseline strengthening:** Compare against state-of-the-art off-policy algorithms (e.g., Rainbow, SAC) with proper hyperparameter tuning and ≥5 random seeds, reporting win rates with confidence intervals.
