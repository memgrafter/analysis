# Actor-Curator: Co-adaptive Curriculum Learning via Policy-Improvement Bandits for RL Post-Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20532
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20532
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A fully automated curriculum learning framework that adaptively selects training problems for RL post‑training of LLMs, achieving up to 30.5% higher peak performance and up to 80% faster convergence on reasoning benchmarks compared to uniform and prior curriculum methods.

## Executive Summary
Actor‑Curator introduces a neural curator that learns to select training problems from a large, heterogeneous dataset by maximizing expected policy improvement. The problem selection is cast as a non‑stationary stochastic bandit with partial feedback; the curator is optimized via online stochastic mirror descent with a two‑stage sampling scheme for scalability and PPO‑style clipping for stability. Experiments on multiple reasoning benchmarks with 3B‑parameter models show consistent gains over uniform and state‑of‑the‑art curricula, demonstrating improved sample efficiency and training stability without manual annotations.

## Method Summary
At each training step, a candidate batch is drawn from a fixed proposal distribution. The curator, a neural network parameterized by ϕ, assigns scores to these candidates; a training batch is then sampled proportionally to these scores. For each selected problem, multiple rollouts from the current actor are collected, and after updating the actor (e.g., via GSPO) an importance‑weighted estimator of per‑problem policy improvement is computed. This estimator serves as reward feedback in a bandit objective: the curator is updated to increase probability on problems with high estimated improvement while maintaining exploration through a KL regularizer. The update uses a PPO‑clipped surrogate to prevent destructive policy changes. The two‑stage design yields unbiased utility estimates even when the full problem bank is too large to evaluate each step, and theoretical regret guarantees are provided for the non‑stationary setting.

## Key Results
- Outperforms uniform sampling and prior curricula (SEC, PCL) across five reasoning benchmarks (Countdown, Zebra, ARC‑1D, AIME2024, MATH500) using Qwen2.5‑3B and Llama3.2‑3B.
- Achieves up to 30.5% higher peak validation accuracy on ARC‑1D and up to 28.6% higher on AIME2024 relative to the strongest baseline.
- Demonstrates up to 80% faster convergence (fewer steps to target) and only a 9% average wall‑clock overhead compared to uniform training.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
1. **Policy‑improvement bandit formulation** – By defining the utility of a problem as the expected improvement in the actor’s performance, the curator directly optimizes for the cumulative objective. The OSMD update balances exploitation (increasing mass on high‑utility arms) and exploration (via entropy regularization). Unbiased estimates are obtained from on‑policy rollouts, enabling adaptive curriculum without manual labels.

2. **Two‑stage sampling for scalability** – The curator operates on a modest candidate batch drawn from a fast proposal, then reweights within that batch. Importance‑sampling corrections (division by both q(x) and the conditional probability p_t(x|̃X_t)) ensure the utility estimator remains unbiased. This decouples coverage from adaptation, making the approach feasible for problem banks of 10K‑30K items.

3. **PPO‑style proximal clipping** – Raw OSMD updates can cause large, destabilizing changes to the curator’s distribution. Clipping the importance ratio between successive curator policies limits the step size while preserving the direction of improvement, mirroring the stabilization technique in PPO. This prevents curriculum collapse and maintains a stable learning signal for the actor.

## Foundational Learning
- **Performance Difference Identity (Policy Improvement)** – Decomposes the change in the actor’s expected return into per‑problem contributions using the current and updated policies. *Why needed*: It provides the theoretical link between a problem’s training effect and the overall objective, enabling a credit assignment at the problem level. *Quick check*: How does Eq. (5)’s importance‑sampling formulation avoid requiring rollouts from the updated policy when computing improvement?

- **Online Stochastic Mirror Descent (OSMD)** – An online optimization framework for non‑stationary bandits that uses a mirror map (negative entropy) to promote exploration. *Why needed*: It yields a principled update rule for the curator under partial, stochastic feedback and offers regret guarantees. *Quick check*: What role does the negative entropy regularizer play in the OSMD update \( p_{t+1} \propto p_t \exp(\eta \hat{U}_t) \)? How does it relate to the exponential‑weights algorithm?

- **Two‑Stage Importance Sampling** – A technique to reduce the computational cost of bandit updates over large action sets by first sampling a candidate subset and then reweighting. *Why needed*: It makes the curator scalable to massive problem banks while preserving unbiasedness of the utility estimator. *Quick check*: Why must the estimator divide by both \( q(x) \) (marginal inclusion) and \( p_t(x|̃X_t) \) (conditional probability)? What bias would result if either factor were omitted?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Problem Bank → Proposal (q) → Candidate sampler (draws ˜X_t) → Curator (C_ϕ_t) → Training sampler (samples X_t) → Rollout generator (produces Y^x_t) → Reward model (R) → Actor update (e.g., GSPO) → Utility estimator (computes g_t(x)) → Curator optimizer (updates ϕ) → back to Curator (new ϕ).

**Critical path:** Candidate selection → adaptive training batch sampling → rollouts + actor update → utility computation → curator update. All steps are on‑policy and depend on the current actor and curator states.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Candidate batch size: larger improves coverage but increases curator compute and may reduce exploration diversity.
- Curator model capacity: larger networks can model complex utilities but risk overfitting and add overhead.
- Clipping range (ρ_min, ρ_max): tighter clipping enhances stability but may slow adaptation; looser values risk collapse.
- Warm‑up schedule: allowing the actor to train initially without curator guidance avoids early bias but delays curriculum benefits.

**Failure signatures:**
- Curator collapse: rapid entropy drop of p_t(·|̃X_t), leading to over‑sampling a few problems and actor overfitting.
- Unstable curator updates: spikes in gradient norms or KL divergence between successive curator distributions indicate excessive step size or noisy utility estimates.
- No convergence speedup: if AC and uniform learning curves overlap after many steps, suspect biased utility estimation (e.g., incorrect q(x) or too small candidate batch).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Train on Countdown with uniform sampling vs. Actor‑Curator; verify AC reaches >60% accuracy within 100 steps while uniform plateaus lower (Tab. 1) and measure wall‑time overhead (~9–19%, Tab. 3).
2. Ablate the policy‑improvement target by replacing it with absolute advantage (as in SEC) and evaluate on ARC‑1D and AIME24; expect a significant performance drop (Fig. 7b).
3. Monitor actor gradient norms during training (Fig. 6); AC should yield larger and more sustained gradient magnitudes, indicating stronger learning signals from curated problems.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Robustness to unstable actor updates** – How does ACTOR‑CURATOR behave when the underlying actor optimization is noisy or highly non‑stationary? The paper acknowledges this limitation but provides no analysis or ablations.
2. **Multi‑problem interference** – The per‑problem utility decomposition assumes independent effects; what happens when training on one problem harms performance on others due to function approximation? This could invalidate the curriculum objective.
3. **Regret guarantee under small candidate batches** – The theoretical bound assumes sufficient coverage via the proposal; it is unclear how the guarantee degrades when the candidate set is a tiny fraction of the problem bank or when the proposal is non‑uniform.
4. **Source of wall‑clock overhead** – The 9% average overhead is reported but not broken down; understanding whether it stems from curator inference, two‑stage sampling, or optimizer steps is necessary for further efficiency gains.

## Limitations
- **Assumption sensitivity** – The unbiased utility estimator critically relies on correct marginal inclusion probabilities q(x) and a stable proposal distribution; the paper does not specify how q is constructed or how to handle near‑zero probabilities.
- **Non‑stationarity & stability** – Theoretical guarantees assume only mild non‑stationarity; in practice the need for PPO‑style clipping indicates raw OSMD updates can be unstable, and the threshold for safe policy changes is not quantified.
- **Scalability vs. coverage trade‑off** – The candidate batch size (e.g., 2048) is chosen heuristically; the paper lacks analysis of how this choice affects coverage of very large banks and how performance scales with problem set size.

## Confidence
- **Core Bandit Formulation & OSMD**: Medium (theoretically sound but fragile in deep RL).
- **Two‑Stage Sampling Unbiasedness**: High for derivation; Medium for practical robustness (depends on q(x) and batch size).
- **PPO‑Clipping Stabilization**: Medium (empirically helpful but clipping range is ad‑hoc and not theoretically derived).
- **Empirical Superiority**: High on the reported math/logic benchmarks; Low for generalizability to other domains or larger models.

## Next Checks
1. **Oracle Utility Estimation** – With a fixed actor and full rollouts, compare the proposed two‑stage estimator against ground‑truth per‑problem improvement across varying candidate batch sizes and proposal distributions to quantify bias/variance.
2. **Out‑of‑Distribution Curriculum Test** – Train ACTOR‑CURATOR on a large math dataset (e.g., MATH500) and evaluate its selected problem distribution on an unseen domain (e.g., HumanEval or TruthfulQA) to assess transferability of the learned curriculum.
3. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity Sweep** – Systematically vary candidate batch size (512, 2048, 8192) and clipping range width on a single benchmark (e.g., ARC‑1D); monitor curator entropy, actor gradient norms, and final accuracy to identify robust settings and failure thresholds.
