# Agentic Adversarial QA for Improving Domain-Specific LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.18137
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18137
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Achieves 82.7% average accuracy with LLaMA3-8B on contract-specific LegalBench subsets, improving 18.99% over baseline and 3.1% over EntiGraph, while using approximately 70× fewer training tokens (96k vs. 6.7M).

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) often underperform on specialized domains due to scarce training data and a lack of interpretive reasoning. Existing synthetic data generation methods focus on factual recall and produce redundant corpora. This paper introduces an adversarial QA generation framework that iteratively refines questions to maximize disagreement between a strong expert model and the target weak model, guided by a feedback model. The resulting compact set of challenging questions is then used to fine-tune the weak model. On three contract-specific LegalBench subsets, the method attains 82.7% average accuracy with LLaMA3-8B, representing an 18.99% improvement over the baseline and a 3.1% gain over the EntiGraph competitor, while using roughly 70 times fewer training tokens (96k vs. 6.7M). Performance further improves with additional refinement iterations.

## Method Summary
The core of the approach is an iterative, feedback-driven adversarial loop that transforms an initial placeholder question into a challenging, domain-specific query. For each iteration, both a strong expert model and the weak target model generate answers given the context and current question; a feedback model computes a disagreement score and critique; a guidance model translates this signal into a natural‑language edit instruction; and a revision model updates the question to increase divergence. After a fixed number of iterations, the final question and the strong model’s answer are stored as a synthetic QA pair. Repeating this process across many initial questions yields a small, high‑value dataset (`D_synthetic`). The weak model is then fine‑tuned on this dataset, directly addressing its previously exposed weaknesses. The method is domain‑agnostic but assumes the strong model is a reliable oracle for the given context. Exact prompts, initialization details, and fine‑ tuning hyperparameters are not fully specified, which may affect reproducibility and cost considerations.

## Key Results
- 82.7% average accuracy on three LegalBench contract subsets with LLaMA3-8B.
- 18.99% improvement over the baseline, 3.1% over the EntiGraph competitor.
- Training token efficiency: ~96k tokens vs. EntiGraph’s 6.7M (≈70× reduction).
- Performance increases with more adversarial refinement iterations (observed up to 10).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Adversarial refinement via disagreement maximization surfaces interpretive weaknesses in the target model.  
**Mechanism:** A feedback loop compares the strong model's and weak model's answers to the same question in context. The feedback model (`f_fb`) quantifies divergence (`L(Q)`), and a guidance model proposes edits to increase that divergence. The revision model updates the question. This is gradient ascent in question space toward maximal response mismatch.  
**Core assumption:** Disagreement between a robust expert (grounded in `C`) and the target model on a question indicates a comprehension or reasoning gap in the target model for that context.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "iteratively refines questions to maximize disagreement between a strong expert model and the target weak model"  
- [section 3.1] Formal definition of `L(Q) = f_fb(A_strong, A_weak)` and the iterative refinement `Q^(t+1) = Q^(t) + ∇_Q L(Q^(t))`  
- [corpus] Weak support; neighboring papers focus on retrieval or evaluation, not adversarial question generation.  
**Break condition:** If `f_fb` is poorly calibrated or the strong model itself is fallible on the domain, the divergence signal may not cleanly indicate true weaknesses.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Fine‑tuning on the final adversarial QA pairs directly addresses the targeted weaknesses identified during refinement.  
**Mechanism:** The synthetic dataset `D_synthetic` consists of the optimized questions `Q^(T)` and the strong model's authoritative answers `f_strong(C, Q^(T))`. Training the weak model on this curated set forces it to learn the specific concepts, inferences, and integrations it previously failed on.  
**Core assumption:** The adversarial process produces questions that are minimally redundant and maximally representative of the weak model's failure modes. Learning from these examples efficiently patches those specific gaps.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "resulting compact set of challenging questions is used to fine‑tune the weak model"  
- [section 3.2] Explicit construction of `D_synthetic` from final questions and expert answers for fine‑tuning.  
- [section 4] Performance gains (e.g., +18.99% over baseline) with ~70× fewer tokens than EntiGraph, supporting efficiency of targeted learning.  
**Break condition:** If optimization converges to degenerate or unnatural questions that do not generalize to the benchmark task distribution, fine‑tuning may not transfer.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The iterative, feedback‑driven process yields semantically diverse and challenging questions, avoiding the redundancy of prior synthetic data methods.  
**Mechanism:** Each refinement step (`Q^(t) -> Q^(t+1)`) is conditioned on the *specific* nature of the current disagreement (via `f_guide`). This encourages exploration of new failure modes rather than simple paraphrasing, leading to a compact set covering a wider range of interpretive deficiencies.  
**Core assumption:** Modeling disagreement as a differentiable text‑space objective (TextGrad) allows more nuanced and creative question evolution than heuristic‑based or extractive generation.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "existing synthetic data methods... produce redundant samples" and "produce a compact set of semantically challenging questions"  
- [section 1] Critique of EntiGraph/Knowledge‑Instruct for "minimal support for interpretive reasoning" and producing "excessively large and redundant" corpora.  
- [section 4] Table 1: Ours uses 96k tokens vs. EntiGraph’s 6.7M for higher accuracy, indicating higher information density per sample.  
**Break condition:** The feedback loop could get stuck in local optima (e.g., repeatedly challenging the same reasoning type) if `f_guide` produces uncreative or repetitive edit instructions.

## Foundational Learning

- **Differentiable Prompting / TextGrad**  
  *Why needed here:* The core optimization treats the question string as a differentiable variable. Engineers must understand how textual feedback is converted into a "gradient" (an edit instruction) to update a variable in natural language space, not weight space.  
  *Quick check question:* How does `f_guide` transform the numeric/explanation output of `f_fb` into a concrete edit instruction for `f_rev`?

- **Adversarial Training for Knowledge Distillation**  
  *Why needed here:* The framework is a form of adversarial training where the "adversary" is a question generator optimizing for the student's (weak model's) failure. This is distinct from standard distillation where the teacher provides soft labels for fixed data.  
  *Quick check question:* Why is maximizing disagreement a valid objective for generating useful training data, rather than just creating "tricky" questions?

- **Domain‑Specific Interpretive Reasoning vs. Factual Recall**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper's thesis is that domain adaptation requires more than memorizing facts; it needs the ability to integrate and infer from sparse, nuanced clauses. An engineer must grasp this distinction to appreciate why prior methods (EntiGraph) are insufficient.  
  *Quick check question:* Give an example of a legal question that requires interpretive reasoning (integrating multiple clauses) versus one that only requires factual recall (defining a term).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Context (C) -> Strong Model (f_strong) -> A_strong; C -> Weak Model (f_weak) -> A_weak; (A_strong, A_weak) -> Feedback Model (f_fb) -> L(Q); L(Q) -> Guidance Model (f_guide) -> edit instruction (∇Q L); edit instruction + Q -> Revision Model (f_rev) -> Q' (iterative); Final Q' and A_strong -> Synthetic Dataset (D_synthetic) -> Fine‑tune f_weak.

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialize question variable Q (e.g., "Q: ???").  
2. For T iterations:  
   a. Generate answers: A_strong = f_strong(C, Q), A_weak = f_weak(C, Q).  
   b. Compute loss: L(Q) = f_fb(A_strong, A_weak) (score and critique).  
   c. Generate edit instruction: ∇_Q L = f_guide(Q, L(Q)).  
   d. Revise question: Q = f_rev(Q, ∇_Q L).  
3. After T iterations, collect final Q and A_strong as one synthetic QA pair.  
4. Repeat for N initial questions to build D_synthetic.  
5. Fine‑tune f_weak on D_synthetic using SFT.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Cost vs. Efficiency:** Each iteration requires 4 LLM calls (2 answers, 1 feedback, 1 guidance/revision). This is computationally expensive but aims for extreme data efficiency (~70× fewer training tokens).  
- **Generality vs. Focus:** Domain‑agnostic but assumes f_strong is a reliable oracle on C. If f_strong is weak, the target misaligns.  
- **Automation vs. Control:** Fully automated but relies on prompt engineering for f_fb, f_guide, f_rev; poor prompts cause degenerate questions.

**Failure signatures:**  
- L(Q) score does not increase (or decreases) over iterations → f_guide or f_rev malfunction, or models too similar/weak.  
- Questions become nonsensical, repetitive, or off‑topic → f_rev not following instructions or guidance is poor.  
- Fine‑tuning does not improve (or hurts) benchmark performance → Optimized questions do not cover task distribution; overfitting to adversarial loop.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Module validation in isolation:** For a single contract, run 1 iteration. Manually inspect A_strong, A_weak, f_fb output, f_guide instruction, and f_rev output. Ensure f_fb identifies a real gap and f_guide suggests a relevant edit.  
2. **Convergence check:** For one initial question, run T=5 iterations. Track evolving L(Q) score and question text. Expect score to increase and question complexity/depth to grow.  
3. **End‑to‑end sanity check on a tiny subset:** Generate D_synthetic with N=10 questions from one contract. Fine‑tune f_weak for 1 epoch. Evaluate on that same contract's LegalBench questions. Expect significant lift over unfine‑tuned baseline on those specific questions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** Does the adversarial QA generation framework generalize to non‑legal specialized domains (e.g., biomedical, scientific, technical documentation) with similar efficiency gains in accuracy and token usage?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper evaluates exclusively on three contract‑specific subsets from LegalBench (Cardlytics, Buffalo Wild Wings, PF Hospitality Franchise Agreements), all within the legal domain. While the authors claim the method targets "specialized domain tasks" generally, no cross‑domain validation is provided.  
**Why unresolved:** Domain‑specific challenges (e.g., medical terminology, scientific notation) differ structurally from legal clauses. The adversarial dynamics between strong and weak models may vary in knowledge density and reasoning patterns. Without evidence beyond legal texts, it is unclear whether the reported 18.99% improvement and 70× token efficiency are domain‑general or artifact of legal contract characteristics.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the full pipeline on at least two other specialized domains with curated benchmarks (e.g., biomedical entity relation extraction, scientific paper Q&A). Compare fine‑tuning sample efficiency (accuracy vs. tokens) and iteration curves against domain‑appropriate baselines. Consistent gains across domains would confirm generality; domain‑specific tuning curves would reveal boundary conditions.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Which specific types of interpretive reasoning (e.g., hypothetical scenario analysis, multi‑clause integration, implicit implication extraction) are most improved by adversarial QA fine‑tuning, and does improvement vary across LegalBench's 36 task types?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors aggregate results across all 36 tasks per contract, reporting only average accuracy (e.g., 82.7% for Cardlytics). No per‑task breakdown is provided, despite LegalBench's design to measure distinct reasoning capabilities like "Contractual Interpretation" or "Hypothetical Scenario."  
**Why unresolved:** Without task‑level analysis, it is impossible to determine whether adversarial QA uniformly enhances all reasoning dimensions or selectively targets particular weaknesses. This limits theoretical understanding of the mechanism: does the method preferentially strengthen scenario‑based reasoning, clause linking, or factual recall? Such insights are crucial for applying the method to reasoning‑intensive domains.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Release per‑task accuracy matrices for adversarial QA versus all baselines. Cluster tasks by reasoning type (e.g., definitional, integrative, hypothetical) and report differential gains. Correlate synthetic question features (e.g., presence of "if" clauses, cross‑references) with downstream task improvements via regression analysis.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Is there an optimal number of adversarial refinement iterations that balances accuracy gains and computational cost, and does this optimum depend on the capacity gap between strong and weak models?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] Figure 1 shows accuracy increasing monotonically from 2 to 10 iterations for all three contracts, but the study stops at 10 iterations. The paper only experiments with LLaMA3-8B as the weak model and GPT‑4o‑mini as the strong model, holding capacity constant.  
**Why unresolved:** Iterative refinement incurs linear cost in API calls per generated question. If gains plateau or reverse beyond 10 iterations, current efficiency claims may be overstated. Moreover, a larger capacity gap might require more iterations to surface meaningful disagreements, while a smaller gap may converge faster. Without iteration–performance curves across capacity ratios, optimal deployment parameters remain unknown.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Sweep iteration count (1–50) for multiple weak model scales (e.g., 1B, 8B, 70B) against a fixed strong model. Plot accuracy and total compute cost (inference calls) to identify Pareto frontiers. Analyze iteration‑wise question evolution: do later iterations focus on increasingly subtle discrepancies? This would reveal whether diminishing returns set in and how model scale interacts with optimization dynamics.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Do adversarially generated questions exhibit greater semantic diversity than those from paraphrase‑based or entity‑centric methods, and is diversity directly linked to improved model robustness on out‑of‑distribution legal scenarios?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method is introduced to avoid "redundant samples" plaguing prior approaches, yet no quantitative diversity analysis of the final question set is conducted. Comparisons to EntiGraph and paraphrasing rely solely on accuracy and token count, not on measures of lexical, topical, or reasoning‑type coverage.  
**Why unresolved:** If adversarial optimization converges to a narrow cluster of question patterns that overfit specific failure modes, the model may lack robustness to novel question phrasings or scenarios. Diversity is a proposed advantage but remains unevaluated. Without measuring diversity and its correlation with OOD performance, the claimed superiority could be coincidental.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Compute diversity metrics for synthetic datasets from all methods: lexical diversity (distinct n‑grams), semantic diversity (embedding cluster entropy), and reasoning‑type coverage (automatic or human annotation of question categories). Evaluate fine‑tuned models on a held‑out set of human‑crafted legal questions or perturbed versions of test questions. A positive correlation between diversity and OOD accuracy would substantiate the diversity claim.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** Can weaker, cost‑effective models substitute for the strong feedback model (f_fb, f_guide, f_rev) without degrading the quality of adversarial questions and final fine‑tuned model performance?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The implementation uses GPT‑4o‑mini for all three auxiliary roles, justified by "semantic consistency," but no ablation tests cheaper alternatives. The paper does not analyze the feedback model's contribution relative to the strong/weak model pair, nor does it account for the feedback model's inference cost in the overall token efficiency claim (96k training tokens excludes generation‑phase calls).  
**Why unresolved:** The feedback model acts as a critic and guide; if its capability is reduced, the refinement process may lose directionality, producing less challenging questions. However, using a weaker (or same‑scale) feedback model could drastically reduce costs. The current design may be prohibitively expensive for widespread adoption if it requires multiple high‑end API calls per question.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Replace GPT‑4o‑mini in the feedback loop with models of varying strength (e.g., LLaMA3-8B, Claude‑3.5 Sonnet, GPT‑3.5‑Turbo). Measure (a) the divergence score (L(Q)) trajectory during refinement, (b) final question difficulty (e.g., weak model initial error rate on generated questions), and (c) downstream accuracy after fine‑tuning. A cost–benefit analysis would identify the minimal sufficient feedback model capacity.

## Limitations
- **Prompt and initialization unknowns:** Exact prompts for f_guide, f_rev, and the initialization strategy for {Q^(0)} are not specified; results are likely sensitive to these design choices.
- **Fine‑tuning details unreported:** The fine‑tuning algorithm (e.g., full fine‑tuning vs. LoRA), hyperparameters, and any data filtering steps are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
- **Feedback scoring ambiguity:** The method for extracting the scalar L(Q) from the feedback model's output (e.g., numeric score vs. token‑level loss) is unclear, which may affect the optimization direction.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (disagreement surfaces weaknesses): **Medium**
- Mechanism 2 (fine‑tuning addresses gaps): **Medium**
- Mechanism 3 (iterative diversity yields compact set): **High**
