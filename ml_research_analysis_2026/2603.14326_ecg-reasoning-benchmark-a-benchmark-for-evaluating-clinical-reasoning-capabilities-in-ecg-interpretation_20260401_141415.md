# ECG-Reasoning-Benchmark: A Benchmark for Evaluating Clinical Reasoning Capabilities in ECG Interpretation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14326  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14326  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** Current MLLMs achieve <6% Completion on the multi‑turn ECG reasoning benchmark, and ECG‑specific models collapse (e.g., 85.41%→22.70%) when given perfect reasoning trajectories, exposing shortcut learning.

## Executive Summary
We introduce ECG‑Reasoning‑Benchmark, a rigorous evaluation framework designed to test whether multimodal large language models (MLLMs) truly reason clinically from ECG signals rather than rely on superficial pattern matching. The benchmark operationalizes ECG interpretation as a four‑stage causal chain: selecting diagnostic criteria, identifying relevant findings, grounding those findings to specific leads/waves/measurements, and finally making a diagnostic decision. Using an automated pipeline that extracts verifiable ECG features from raw signals, the benchmark produces objective ground truth for each step. Extensive evaluation across leading MLLMs reveals a critical failure: models cannot sustain complete reasoning chains, with Completion rates below 6%. Even when guided through correct intermediate steps, ECG‑specific models fail to produce the correct diagnosis, indicating they have learned to shortcut from signal to label without understanding the underlying evidence.

## Method Summary
The benchmark takes 12‑lead ECG data (as images or 1‑D time series) and, via an automated analysis pipeline, derives a sequence of verifiable reasoning steps. Wave detection (U‑Net3+) identifies P, QRS, and T boundaries; quantitative measurements are mapped to discrete clinical findings using established criteria; and a hierarchical logic diagram yields the final diagnosis. The model is then engaged in a multi‑turn dialogue: the initial diagnostic query is followed by sub‑questions corresponding to each required finding and its grounding. At each turn, the model’s answer is semantically compared against the ground truth extracted by the pipeline. Completion measures the proportion of samples where the entire reasoning chain is correct; Depth records the average stage reached; GT‑RDA tests diagnostic accuracy when the model is supplied with perfect intermediate answers. The evaluation is deterministic (temperature = 0) and runs on 6,400+ samples from PTB‑XL and MIMIC‑IV‑ECG covering 17 core diagnoses.

## Key Results
- **Completion**: All evaluated models, including state‑of‑the‑art MLLMs, complete the full reasoning chain in less than 6% of cases, demonstrating near‑zero ability to maintain multi‑step logical deduction.
- **Depth**: Models typically succeed at criterion selection (Depth > 1.0) but collapse at ECG grounding (Depth < 2.0), indicating a failure to link diagnostic criteria to actual signal evidence such as specific leads, waves, or measurements.
- **GT‑RDA Collapse**: ECG‑specific models (e.g., ECG‑R1‑RL) show a dramatic drop in diagnostic accuracy when provided with perfect reasoning trajectories (e.g., 85.41% → 22.70% on PTB‑XL), whereas general‑purpose models improve significantly. This inverse effect reveals that ECG‑trained models rely on shortcut learning rather than genuine signal interpretation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s design enforces a strict causal chain: by requiring explicit answers at each of four stages, it prevents models from bypassing visual grounding and relying on textual shortcuts. The first mechanism, a multi‑turn verification loop, exposes whether the model’s reasoning is genuinely grounded in the ECG signal; if any sub‑question fails, the chain breaks and Completion fails. The second mechanism uses an automated pipeline to extract objective, machine‑annotated features (wave boundaries, intervals, amplitudes) and map them to clinical findings, creating a transparent ground truth that can be checked step‑by‑step. The third mechanism, GT‑RDA, isolates latent reasoning capacity by providing perfect intermediate answers and measuring the final diagnosis; a drop for ECG‑specific models indicates they have not learned to use evidence but instead shortcut directly from signal to label. Together, these mechanisms provide strong evidence that current MLLMs fail to perform real clinical reasoning on ECGs.

## Foundational Learning
- **ECG Waveform Anatomy**  
  Why needed: To understand what the model must ground (P wave, QRS complex, T wave, PR/QT intervals) and the quantitative criteria (e.g., PR > 120 ms) that form the reasoning steps.  
  Quick check: What is the typical normal range for QRS duration, and what does a prolonged QRS indicate?
- **Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)**  
  Why needed: The benchmark evaluates MLLMs that process ECG visualizations/time‑series and generate text; understanding their architecture (vision encoder + LLM) and training (often on synthetic instruction data) is crucial to interpret failure modes and possible hallucinations.  
  Quick check: How do MLLMs typically fuse visual ECG representations with language, and why might they generate incorrect references to ECG features?
- **Clinical Diagnostic Reasoning (Deductive Chain)**  
  Why needed: The benchmark operationalizes expert cardiologist reasoning into a fixed sequence of findings; engineers must grasp this structure to design models that mimic it or to interpret the Depth/Completion metrics.  
  Quick check: Describe the typical steps a cardiologist follows when interpreting an ECG for bundle branch block.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
12‑lead ECG raw signals → Automated pipeline (U‑Net3+ wave detection → feature extraction → clinical criteria mapping → diagnosis logic) → Benchmark evaluation (multi‑turn QA loop) → Metrics (Completion, Depth, IDA, GT‑RDA)

**Critical path**  
1. Automated pipeline generates ground‑truth reasoning chain for each sample.  
2. Benchmark creates initial diagnostic question and then iterative sub‑questions for each required finding.  
3. Model responds turn‑by‑turn; each answer is compared to ground truth.  
4. Completion, Depth, and GT‑RDA are computed from full interaction.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Automated vs. manual annotation: pipeline scales objectively but may miss subtle morphologies; manual annotation is accurate but not scalable.  
- Deterministic evaluation (temperature = 0) ensures reproducibility but may penalize paraphrasing; semantic matching is used to mitigate.  
- Exhaustive verification (all criteria must be checked) differs from clinical heuristics where experts may stop early; this may penalize learned efficiency.

**Failure signatures**  
- Very low Completion (< 6%) indicates inability to sustain full reasoning chain.  
- Depth between 1 and 2 points to failure at Finding Identification or ECG Grounding, especially lead/wave measurement grounding.  
- GT‑RDA collapse for ECG‑specific models (e.g., ECG‑R1‑RL) reveals over‑reliance on shortcut learning.  
- Smaller models may lose context in multi‑turn dialogue, generating repetitive or off‑topic answers.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Replicate baseline with a new MLLM (e.g., MedGemma‑27B) and compare Depth decomposition against reported models.  
2. Test pipeline robustness on an external dataset with challenging noise/artifacts; compare wave detection accuracy against expert annotations.  
3. Ablate the strict system instruction (remove requirement to follow guidelines) and measure change in Completion to assess prompt scaffolding dependence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Why do ECG‑specific MLLMs show a drastic drop in GT‑RDA?**  
  The paper documents the inverse effect (ECG‑R1‑RL: 85.41%→22.70%) but does not investigate its cause; unknown whether architectural, training, or data factors drive the reliance on shortcuts.
- **How can the benchmark evaluate reasoning under uncertainty?**  
  Current version excludes borderline/ambiguous ECGs; extension would require annotating uncertainty levels and measuring model calibration and appropriate expression of doubt.
- **Can evaluation accommodate clinical heuristics and early termination?**  
  The fixed linear loop penalizes heuristic shortcuts; a dynamic scheme with severity/ certainty weightings could better reflect expert practice, but requires validated weighting.
- **What training strategies overcome grounding failure?**  
  Models fail at linking criteria to signal evidence. Evidence‑grounded training (e.g., adding explicit feature annotations, reinforcement learning on grounding, attention constraints) is needed but untested.

## Limitations
- **Ground truth reliability:** The fully automated pipeline may contain errors on atypical morphologies or noise, potentially invalidating step verification.  
- **Benchmark scope:** Covers only 17 common diagnoses on PTB‑XL and MIMIC‑IV‑ECG; findings may not generalize to rare conditions, pediatrics, or other recording standards.  
- **Evaluation rigidity:** The deterministic, exhaustive loop may penalize valid non‑canonical reasoning or early‑stop strategies, limiting assessment of true clinical flexibility.

## Confidence
- **Main outcome metrics** (Completion ~ 6%, Depth distribution, GT‑RDA collapse): **High**  
- **Mechanism 1** (Multi‑turn verification enforces causal chain): **Medium**  
- **Mechanism 2** (Automated pipeline approximates expert annotation): **Medium**  
- **Mechanism 3** (GT‑RDA exposes shortcut learning): **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Expert cross‑validation:** Have board‑certified cardiologists independently produce reasoning chains for a random sample; compare against automated ground truth to quantify agreement and systematic bias.  
2. **Generalization test:** Evaluate top‑performing MLLMs on an external, heterogeneous ECG dataset from different hospitals and demographics to assess robustness beyond benchmark distribution.  
3. **Prompt variation robustness:** Re‑run evaluation with altered system prompts (e.g., allowing summarization, removing strict step enumeration) to determine whether low Completion is an artifact of rigid scaffolding.
