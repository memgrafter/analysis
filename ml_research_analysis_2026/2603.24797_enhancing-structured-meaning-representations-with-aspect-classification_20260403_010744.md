# Enhancing Structured Meaning Representations with Aspect Classification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.24797
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24797
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: A new English dataset of 1,473 event-level aspect labels over AMR graphs establishes the first supervised benchmarks for UMR aspect classification; human annotation reaches 84% accuracy, while the best automated method (GPT-5mini) achieves only 56%.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the scarcity of aspect annotations in structured semantic representations like Uniform Meaning Representation (UMR). The authors construct a new English dataset of 1,473 event-level aspect labels over AMR graphs, following the UMR aspect lattice. The annotation process employed a rigorous multi‑phase pipeline with independent labeling, tie‑breaking adjudication, and expert consultation to ensure consistency.

Baseline evaluations reveal a substantial performance gap: a human annotator reaches 84% accuracy, while the best automated approach (GPT‑5mini prompting) achieves only 56% accuracy (0.49 macro F1). The dataset and standardized sentence‑level splits establish the first supervised benchmarks for UMR aspect classification, highlighting the difficulty of the task and the superiority of human annotation over current automated methods.

## Method Summary
The annotation pipeline began with training materials and practice (Pear Story), followed by Phase 1 where two annotators independently labeled each event’s aspect. Phase 2 involved tie‑breaking adjudication sessions to resolve disagreements, consistency review, and expert consultation for challenging cases (e.g., Endeavor vs. Performance). This process yielded a gold‑standard dataset of 1,473 labels across 476 sentences from four corpora (Little Prince, Minecraft, BOLT DF, Weblog), with each label attached to an eventive predicate in a UMR/AMR graph.

The data was split 70/15/15 at the sentence level, stratified by the sentence’s dominant aspect label to prevent contextual leakage. Baseline automated methods included zero/few‑shot prompting with GPT‑5mini and Llama‑3.1‑8B‑Instruct, a feedforward neural classifier using mean‑pooled Llama embeddings, and a rule‑based AutoAspect reference. Models were evaluated on accuracy, macro F1, and weighted F1 against the adjudicated gold standard.

## Key Results
- Created a dataset of 1,473 event‑level aspect annotations over AMR graphs, following the UMR aspect lattice.
- Human baseline: 84% accuracy; best automated (GPT‑5mini): 56% accuracy (0.49 macro F1).
- The dataset and stratified splits provide the first supervised benchmarks for UMR aspect classification, revealing the task’s difficulty and the need for models that better utilize context and graph structure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Rigorous Annotator Pipeline
A multi‑phase pipeline with independent labeling and expert‑mediated adjudication produces a high‑quality, consistent gold standard for a linguistically complex task. Independent first passes capture interpretation variance; structured adjudication forces explicit justification against guidelines; expert consultation resolves theoretical ambiguities. Agreement improved (Cohen’s κ 0.656 → Krippendorff’s α 0.688) and error rates for difficult labels (e.g., Endeavor 12.2%) were reduced.

### Mechanism 2: Stratified Sentence‑Level Splits
Splitting data on sentence boundaries and stratifying by the sentence’s dominant aspect label prevents contextual leakage. Aspectual interpretation is heavily influenced by sentence‑level context; event‑level splitting would expose models to contextual cues during training that are unavailable at test time for the same sentence, artificially inflating performance. The 70/15/15 split (476 sentences, 1,473 events) ensures fair generalization evaluation.

### Mechanism 3: Baseline Gap Highlights Need for Advanced Modeling
Comparing human performance (84%) to automated methods (max 56%) quantifies a large gap, especially on minority classes (Endeavor, Activity, Habitual F1 near 0). This indicates the task requires deeper semantic and contextual reasoning beyond surface cues or simple text classification. The dataset enables future work to move beyond rule‑based or shallow prompting approaches.

## Foundational Learning
- **Aspectual Semantics (Vendlerian Classes):** The task classifies events into the UMR aspect lattice, an operationalization of classical aspectual classes. Understanding theoretical distinctions (telic/atelic, bounded/unbounded) is prerequisite to interpreting guidelines and model errors.  
  *Quick check:* What is the core semantic difference between an *Achievement* (often captured by *Performance* in UMR) and an *Accomplishment* (also *Performance*), and why might they be conflated in annotation?

- **Uniform Meaning Representation (UMR) as a Graph‑Based Formalism:** Aspect labels attach to nodes (eventive predicates) within UMR graphs derived from AMR. Recognizing aspect as a node‑level feature in a rich predicate‑argument graph—not a sentence‑level tag—is crucial for modeling design.  
  *Quick check:* How does UMR’s treatment of aspect differ from a traditional part‑of‑speech tag or a shallow semantic role label?

- **Inter‑Annotator Agreement (IAA) Metrics and Adjudication:** The gold standard’s credibility rests on IAA; knowing measures like Cohen’s κ, Krippendorff’s α, and Fleiss’ κ (and their limitations with imbalanced, multi‑label data) is essential.  
  *Quick check:* Why might Fleiss’ κ (0.146) for the tie‑break subset be much lower than Krippendorff’s α (0.688) for all rounds, and what does that imply about the hardest cases?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Raw Input (sentences with pre‑existing AMR/UMR graphs) → Annotation Pipeline (training → Phase 1 independent labeling → Phase 2 adjudication/consistency/expert) → Dataset Artifact (gold‑label JSON/TSV) → Splitting Script (sentence‑level stratified split) → Train/Dev/Test splits → Baseline Models (LLM prompting, feedforward classifier, AutoAspect reference) → Evaluation (accuracy, macro/weighted F1)

### Critical Path
Guideline finalization → Annotator training → Bulk annotation (Phase 1) → Adjudication & consistency (Phase 2) → IAA calculation → Stratified split generation → Baseline model training/evaluation → Error analysis. Phase 2 adjudication is the most resource‑intensive and uncertain step, bottlenecking dataset release.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Quality vs. Scale:** ~6 weeks of adjudication for ~1.5k labels ensures high consistency but limits size; a larger, noisier dataset could harm model learning.
- **Context granularity:** Annotations were on isolated sentences, though aspect depends on broader context; the adjudication selected a “most likely” reading, introducing potential bias vs. document‑level processing.
- **Label granularity:** Using all 6 UMR classes captures nuance but causes severe sparsity (e.g., Endeavor: 18 instances); coarser grouping would aid models but lose linguistic fidelity.

### Failure Signatures
- **Inconsistent adjudication:** per‑label error rates >15% after Phase 2 suggest guidelines or expert consultation were insufficient.
- **Split leakage:** train/test sentences with high lexical/structural similarity indicate failed stratification.
- **Baseline plateau:** all models performing near the AutoAspect baseline (~39% acc) imply the task requires explicit graph structure or external knowledge.
- **Minority class collapse:** models ignoring rare classes (e.g., Endeavor, Activity) as shown by near‑zero F1 on these categories.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate IAA on held‑out sample:** Have two fresh annotators label 100 test‑set events; compute inter‑annotator agreement and agreement against the gold. Validates gold standard learnability by new annotators.
2. **Context ablation for LLM prompting:** Test GPT‑5mini with varying context windows (sentence‑only vs. ±2 surrounding sentences). Hypothesis: context resolves ambiguities (e.g., Endeavor/Process) and narrows the human‑model gap.
3. **Graph vs. Text baseline:** Implement a simple GNN operating on the AMR graph (predicate lemma embeddings) and compare macro F1 to the mean‑pooled Llama sentence classifier. Tests if graphical structure carries predictive signal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Cross‑linguistic adaptation:** How can the UMR aspect scheme be adapted for typologically diverse languages with different aspectual systems? The current English‑specific dataset and models leave this unaddressed; resolution requires annotated datasets for multiple languages and evaluation of cross‑linguistic transfer.
2. **Architectures leveraging context and structure:** What neural architectures best capture the contextual and graphical nature of aspectual classification in UMR graphs? Baselines ignore graph structure; graph‑based networks (GNNs, Graph Transformers) need exploration.
3. **Document‑level context reliance:** To what extent does aspect disambiguation (e.g., Endeavor vs. Performance) depend on document‑level context? The dataset’s sentence‑isolated annotation creates a mismatch; evidence from context‑augmented annotation and modeling is needed.
4. **Exploiting hierarchical aspect lattice:** Can the hierarchical relationships in the UMR aspect lattice be used to improve classification, especially for rare/confusable classes? Current flat models ignore lattice structure; hierarchical loss or label embeddings could help.
5. **Integration into full UMR parsing:** How should aspectual information be integrated into a full UMR parsing pipeline, and what are error propagation effects? Aspect is currently predicted as a post‑hoc step; pipeline order (aspect‑first vs. end‑to‑end) requires systematic comparison.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction unknowns:** exact LLM prompt templates/parsing, feedforward classifier hyperparameters, and definitive sentence‑level split IDs (release pending) block faithful replication.
- **Comparability issue:** the AutoAspect rule‑based baseline results are not directly comparable (different test set, dependency issues), limiting a key reference point.
- **Contextual ambiguity:** annotations were on isolated sentences, but aspect is context‑dependent; gold labels may not reflect natural document‑level interpretation, potentially biasing the human baseline.

## Confidence
- **High:** Annotation pipeline rigor and resulting IAA improvements are well‑documented.
- **High:** Stratified sentence‑level splitting strategy is explicitly described and logically sound.
- **Medium:** The claim that automated methods are insufficient is supported but could be due to methodological shortcomings rather than inherent task difficulty.
- **Medium:** The dataset establishes the first supervised benchmark, but long‑term utility depends on community adoption and split release.
- **Low:** The human upper bound (84%) may not be the theoretical ceiling; an external upper bound is unknown.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate IAA on a held‑out sample: have two new annotators label 100 test events; compute agreement metrics against each other and the gold standard.
2. Context ablation for LLM prompting: evaluate GPT‑5mini with varying context windows (sentence‑only vs. ±2 sentences) on ambiguous cases to measure impact of context.
3. Graph‑structure ablation: implement a GNN on AMR graphs and compare macro F1 to the text‑only feedforward classifier to test the value of graphical structure.
