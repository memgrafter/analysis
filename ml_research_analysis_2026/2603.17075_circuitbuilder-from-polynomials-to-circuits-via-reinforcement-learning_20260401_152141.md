# CircuitBuilder: From Polynomials to Circuits via Reinforcement Learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.17075
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17075
- **Reference count**: 24
- **Primary result**: On two‑variable polynomials of complexity 5, SAC achieves 57.8% success rate vs PPO+MCTS's 34.9%; for three variables, PPO+MCTS scales better (27.3% vs SAC's 10.7%).

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles arithmetic circuit synthesis—the construction of minimal addition/multiplication circuits for given polynomials—with implications for Valiant's VP vs. VNP conjecture and automated proof generation. The authors reformulate the problem as a single‑player Markov Decision Process and develop two reinforcement learning agents: CircuitBuilder (PPO+MCTS) and a Soft Actor‑Critic (SAC) variant. Both use a graph neural network to encode the partial circuit DAG and a Transformer to incorporate the target polynomial. After supervised pretraining on optimal circuits from a generated “game‑board” DAG, the agents undergo curriculum learning, gradually handling harder polynomials. Experiments over the finite field F₅ show SAC excels on simpler two‑variable problems (57.8% success at complexity 5) while PPO+MCTS scales more effectively to three variables (27.3% vs SAC's 10.7% at the same complexity). The results highlight a trade‑off between entropy‑driven exploration (SAC) and explicit planning (MCTS) in symbolic search tasks.

## Method Summary
We model circuit construction as an MDP where the state is a partially built circuit represented as a directed acyclic graph (DAG). At each step, the agent chooses an operation (addition or multiplication) and two existing nodes to create a new node. Episodes terminate when the circuit’s output matches the target polynomial or a gate budget is exhausted; the reward is a sparse +1 for success. The policy is parameterized by a hybrid architecture: a GNN (GCNConv with residuals and layer norm) processes the DAG, producing a graph embedding via global mean pooling; the target polynomial is encoded via a compact one‑hot vector of monomial coefficients, projected to the same dimension. A Transformer decoder attends over both embeddings using a learnable query token to yield a context vector, which feeds a policy head (masked logits over valid actions) and a value head. Both agents undergo supervised pretraining on shortest‑path (state, action) pairs extracted from the game‑board DAG. For fine‑tuning, PPO+MCTS combines Proximal Policy Optimization with Monte Carlo Tree Search: MCTS simulations (guided by the current network) produce a visit‑count distribution that is mixed with the policy’s distribution via a Bernoulli selector. SAC uses an off‑policy replay buffer, twin Q‑networks, entropy regularization, and Polyak‑averaged target networks. A curriculum scheduler adjusts the maximum target complexity based on recent success rates. Training is evaluated on held‑out polynomials, measuring success rate (exact match) and auxiliary metrics such as average reward and policy entropy.

## Key Results
- On two‑variable polynomials of complexity 5, SAC reaches a 57.8% success rate, substantially higher than PPO+MCTS’s 34.9%.
- Scaling to three variables, PPO+MCTS achieves 27.3% success at complexity 5, while SAC’s success drops to 10.7%.
- SAC shows steadily improving average rewards over training, though its policy entropy declines, indicating reduced exploration; PPO+MCTS benefits from MCTS guidance but with higher compute per step.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Reformulating arithmetic circuit synthesis as a single‑player MDP allows the application of RL algorithms to navigate the combinatorial search space.
- **Mechanism**: The environment maintains a DAG of the partial circuit. At each step, the agent selects an operation (addition/multiplication) and two existing nodes to create a new node. The state is the DAG; actions are all valid (op, node_i, node_j) triples. Episodes terminate when a fixed gate budget is exhausted or the target polynomial is computed. The reward is sparse: success (1) only if the final output exactly matches the target polynomial (over the finite field F5).
- **Core assumption**: The optimal construction policy is Markovian with respect to the current DAG and target polynomial; i.e., the future depends only on the present graph, not the full history.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  • Abstract: “We formulate this problem as a single‑player game, where an RL agent attempts to build the circuit within a fixed number of operations.”  
  • Section 3.1: “We model arithmetic circuit construction as a single‑player Markov Decision Process (MDP), where the state is a partially‑built circuit represented as a directed acyclic graph (DAG)…”  
  • Corpus: The surrounding work focuses on quantum or probabilistic circuits, providing only indirect support.
- **Break condition**: If two distinct partial circuits compute the same polynomial (syntactically different but semantically equivalent), the state representation may mislead the agent. Also, action space size O(k²) grows quickly with partial circuit size k, potentially exceeding practical limits.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: A hybrid GNN‑Transformer architecture can encode the structured state (partial circuit DAG) and target polynomial into embeddings that support accurate policy and value prediction.
- **Mechanism**: A GNN (GCNConv with residuals and layer norm) processes the circuit DAG, producing node embeddings that are globally mean‑pooled into a graph embedding. The target polynomial is encoded via a compact one‑hot scheme: concatenating one‑hots for operation types, edge selections, and a last‑generated‑node indicator from a reference optimal circuit; this is projected to the same dimension. A Transformer decoder attends over the graph embedding and target embedding using a learnable query token, yielding a context vector fed to a policy head (masked logits over actions) and a value head (scalar state value).
- **Core assumption**: The chosen encodings preserve enough structural information to distinguish polynomials and generalize across different circuit sizes and targets.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  • Abstract: “both using a graph neural network and transformer architecture to encode partially built circuits and target polynomials.”  
  • Section 3.3: “The GNN encoder processes the circuit DAG… The target polynomial is encoded using a compact one‑hot scheme… A Transformer decoder then attends over both embeddings…”  
  • Corpus: Some neighboring papers use GNNs/Transformers for circuit design (e.g., quantum circuits), but none address arithmetic polynomial circuits; indirect support only.
- **Break condition**: If the GNN fails to propagate information effectively in deep DAGs or the Transformer cannot align the two modalities, the policy will degrade on larger or more complex polynomials.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Integrating search (MCTS) or entropy regularization (SAC) mitigates the challenge of sparse rewards and massive branching, enabling learning of efficient circuit construction policies.
- **Mechanism**:  
  *PPO+MCTS*: The network is first pretrained on optimal paths from the game‑board DAG. During RL, PPO updates the policy using a clipped surrogate objective and GAE. MCTS, guided by the current network’s value estimates, simulates trajectories to produce a refined visit‑count distribution; a Bernoulli mixer selects actions from either MCTS or the policy. This exposes the policy to higher‑quality rollouts.  
  *SAC*: An off‑policy method that samples from a replay buffer. It employs twin Q‑networks to reduce overestimation, an entropy term α to sustain exploration, and Polyak‑averaged target networks. Curriculum learning adapts the target complexity: increase when success rate is high, decrease when low (for SAC).
- **Core assumption**: The search/exploration mechanisms can efficiently discover rare successful trajectories despite the exponential growth of the action space and the lack of intermediate rewards.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  • Abstract: “CircuitBuilder combining Proximal Policy Optimization with Monte Carlo Tree Search (PPO+MCTS), and a Soft Actor‑Critic (SAC) variant… Both agents are pretrained… and employ curriculum learning.”  
  • Section 3.3: “MCTS Integration… The algorithm proceeds in four phases: selection, expansion, rollout, backpropagation.”  
  • Section 3.4: “SAC is off‑policy… entropy regularization helps maintain exploration… We employ the same curriculum learning strategy as PPO, with the addition that complexity can also decrease…”  
  • Corpus: Nearby work uses MCTS for quantum circuit design or RL for architecture search, providing conceptual analogies but no direct evidence for arithmetic circuits.
- **Break condition**: If MCTS simulations are too shallow (due to time limits) or the entropy coefficient decays prematurely, the agent may converge to suboptimal, highly certain policies that fail to explore. For SAC, if the replay buffer lacks successful episodes, learning may stall.

## Foundational Learning
### Reinforcement Learning (Markov Decision Processes, policy gradients)
- **Why needed here**: The core approach frames circuit synthesis as an MDP and uses PPO or SAC to learn a policy. Understanding RL fundamentals explains the training loop, sparse rewards, and exploration strategies.
- **Quick check question**: Why are policy gradient methods like PPO suitable for this sparse‑reward, large‑action‑space problem? (Answer: They can optimize stochastic policies directly and, with clipping and GAE, stabilize updates despite high variance.)

### Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)
- **Why needed here**: The MDP state is a DAG of intermediate polynomial computations. GNNs provide a differentiable way to encode such graphs into fixed‑size vectors that preserve structural relationships.
- **Quick check question**: How does the GCNConv layer propagate information in the circuit DAG? (Answer: Each node updates its embedding by aggregating messages from its incoming neighbors, preserving directionality.)

### Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)
- **Why needed here**: In PPO+MCTS, MCTS uses the learned value function to look ahead and refine action distributions during data collection, improving sample efficiency in a huge search space.
- **Quick check question**: What does the UCB formula balance in MCTS? (Answer: It trades off exploitation of high‑value actions versus exploration of less‑visited actions.)

### Curriculum Learning
- **Why needed here**: The difficulty of target polynomials increases with variable count and circuit complexity. Curriculum learning gradually ramps up difficulty, preventing early training from being impossible and guiding the agent to master simpler cases first.
- **Quick check question**: How does the curriculum decide when to increase complexity? (Answer: When the success rate on the current complexity exceeds a threshold over a sliding window of evaluations; for SAC, it may also decrease if the rate falls below a lower bound.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: board_generator.py -> circuit_env.py -> model.py -> trainer_ppo.py / trainer_sac.py -> mcts.py, curriculum.py, utils.py
- **Critical path**:  
  1. Generate game‑board for desired variables and max complexity (e.g., `python board_generator.py --vars 2 --complexity 6`).  
  2. Extract pretraining data: all shortest‑path (state, optimal action) pairs from the game‑board.  
  3. Pretrain the model on this dataset until action accuracy >90% on validation.  
  4. Initialize RL trainer (PPO or SAC) with starting complexity 1. For each complexity, train until success rate exceeds threshold (e.g., 80% for 5 consecutive evals), then increment complexity. For PPO+MCTS, configure `p_mix` and MCTS simulations; for SAC, set replay buffer size and entropy target.  
  5. Periodically evaluate on held‑out targets using near‑greedy policy (τ=0.1 for PPO+MCTS; greedy for SAC) and log success.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *PPO+MCTS vs SAC*: PPO+MCTS uses explicit search, reducing environment steps needed but increasing compute per step; SAC is lighter per step but may need more total steps and can falter in higher dimensions. The paper shows PPO+MCTS scales better to three variables.  
  - *MCTS depth vs time*: More simulations yield better action distributions but slow training; mixing with policy actions (`p_mix`) controls this trade‑off.  
  - *Model size*: Larger GNN/Transformer layers may improve representation but increase memory; the DAG size is limited by the game‑board’s combinatorial explosion.  
  - *Curriculum thresholds*: Too aggressive increases cause training instability; too conservative wastes compute. Sliding window size and thresholds are critical hyperparameters.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Near‑zero success rate after reasonable compute: Check policy entropy; if collapsed early, increase entropy coefficient or temperature. Verify action masking is correct.  
  - PPO+MCTS plateauing: MCTS mixing probability may be too low (insufficient guidance) or too high (policy not learning). Try varying `p_mix` and number of simulations.  
  - SAC negative average rewards: The agent rarely gets positive reward; likely exploration lost. Increase initial entropy coefficient or target entropy. Ensure replay buffer contains enough successful episodes (maybe from pretraining).  
  - Out‑of‑memory when generating boards: The game‑board size grows exponentially with complexity and variables. Limit C or n; consider pruning equivalent polynomials or generating boards on‑the‑fly.  
  - Overfitting to pretraining data: If the agent solves only polynomials that appear in the pretraining set but not novel ones, increase diversity of target sampling or add regularization (dropout, weight decay).
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. *Pretraining accuracy*: Train the model on the C4 pretrain (single‑variable) dataset and report top‑1 action accuracy on a held‑out set. Expect >95%. This verifies the GNN‑Transformer can learn optimal moves from the game‑board.  
  2. *SAC on two‑variable C=5*: Train SAC with curriculum from complexity 1 to 5. After training, evaluate 1000 held‑out polynomials at C=5 with greedy policy. Success rate should approach ~58% (paper’s result). This establishes SAC’s effectiveness in the simpler setting.  
  3. *PPO+MCTS on three‑variable C=5*: Train PPO+MCTS with MCTS mixing and curriculum. Evaluate at C=5 with near‑greedy MCTS. Expect success rate improving to >25% (paper reports 27.3%). This tests scalability to more variables.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling**: Can RL‑based circuit synthesis scale to higher complexities (C≥7) and more variables (n≥4), and what algorithmic modifications are needed? The discussion states: “Future work includes extending training to higher‑complexity targets (C≥7)” and “scaling to larger circuits, more variables, and richer polynomial families.” Experiments were limited to C≤6 and n≤3; further scaling faces exponential growth in search space. Evidence: Successful training with comparable or improved success rates at C=7,8 and n=4,5 using hierarchical or decompositional approaches.
2. **Exploration**: How can exploration be improved in the sparse‑reward circuit construction setting, especially for harder instances? “investigating stronger exploration strategies for harder instances” is mentioned as future work. SAC’s entropy declined over time, and PPO+MCTS performance degraded at higher complexity, indicating insufficient sustained exploration. Evidence: Introducing intrinsic motivation, diversity‑seeking RL, or adaptive entropy scheduling leads to monotonic improvement on C≥6 targets.
3. **Representations**: Do better polynomial and circuit representations exist that improve generalization to larger search spaces? “Another important direction is to improve polynomial and circuit representations so that the agent can generalize more effectively as the search space grows.” Current encoding uses a compact circuit encoding from a reference sequence, which may not capture symbolic structure needed for generalizing to unseen polynomials. Evidence: Testing alternative encodings (e.g., symbolic trees, tensor representations) on held‑out polynomials of higher complexity yields higher success rates.
4. **Constants**: Can the framework incorporate constant values as input gates, and does this improve practical applicability? “We stay within the scope of variable‑only input gates (one of the variables can be viewed as the constant ‘1’) and defer other constant values to future work.” Constants are fundamental in arithmetic; omitting them limits the expressiveness and efficiency of discovered circuits. Evidence: Extending the environment to include constant gates and evaluating on polynomials with integer coefficients shows impact on circuit size and success.
5. **Unified Approach**: What explains the performance trade‑off where SAC excels on two‑variable tasks but PPO+MCTS scales better to three variables, and can a unified approach combine their advantages? Results show SAC > PPO+MCTS for n=2 at C=5, but PPO+MCTS > SAC for n=3. Paper hypothesizes MCTS helps with dimensionality but lacks analysis. Interaction between entropy regularization, off‑policy learning, and explicit planning is unclear; no ablation studies isolate factors. Evidence: Systematic experiments varying MCTS depth, SAC temperature, and hybrid SAC+MCTS designs identify causal factors and produce a method consistent across variable counts.

## Limitations
- Many implementation details (reward shaping, hyperparameters, target encoding, discrete SAC specifics) are omitted, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Experiments are confined to modest polynomial complexities (C≤6) and few variables (n≤3); success rates remain below 60%, indicating incomplete solutions.
- The MDP’s Markov assumption may be violated when two distinct partial circuits compute the same polynomial, potentially misrepresenting state equivalence.
