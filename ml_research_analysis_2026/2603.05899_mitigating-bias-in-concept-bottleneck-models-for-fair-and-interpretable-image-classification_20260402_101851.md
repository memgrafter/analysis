# Mitigating Bias in Concept Bottleneck Models for Fair and Interpretable Image Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05899  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05899  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** The proposed combination of a top‑k concept activation filter and adversarial debiasing reduces bias amplification in a CLIP‑based CBM by 28% relative to the baseline (from 8.19% to 6.29%) with negligible accuracy loss, improving fairness‑performance tradeoffs while retaining interpretability.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles unfairness in Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) for image classification, where irrelevant information ("leakage") in concept activations amplifies biases such as gender stereotypes. Using CLIP to generate concept activations for the ImSitu action‑recognition dataset, the authors identify that low‑activation concepts disproportionately encode sensitive‑attribute proxies. They propose three mitigation techniques: (1) a top‑k filter that zeroes all but the highest‑activated concepts per image, (2) removing concepts correlated with a sensitive attribute (identified via a gender predictor or LLM self‑rating), and (3) adversarial debiasing applied to the final linear layer. Experiments show that the top‑k filter alone improves fairness with a small accuracy gain, while adding adversarial debiasing yields the best tradeoff—a 28% relative reduction in bias amplification compared to the baseline CBM—without sacrificing interpretability. The work highlights that information leakage remains a core challenge for label‑free CBMs, but targeted interventions at the activation or mapping stage can meaningfully reduce learned bias.

## Method Summary
The study builds a CLIP‑based CBM for the ImSitu dataset (200 action classes, gender as sensitive attribute). Concepts are automatically generated from verb class names using GPT‑3 with three prompts (listing features, common items, superclasses). After filtering by length, semantic similarity to classes and other concepts, and a CLIP activation threshold, approximately 1,976 concepts remain. The CBM uses CLIP’s ViT‑B/16 image encoder to compute similarity scores between an image and all concept text embeddings, producing a concept activation vector. A sparse, trainable fully‑connected layer (with L1/L2 regularization) maps this vector to class logits. Three bias mitigation techniques are applied: (1) a test‑time top‑k filter keeping only the k highest activations; (2) zeroing out activations for concepts flagged as gender‑correlated via an auxiliary gender predictor or LLM self‑rating; and (3) adversarial debiasing during training, where an adversary tries to predict gender from the final layer’s outputs, and gradients are reversed to confuse it. Performance (accuracy) and fairness (bias amplification) are evaluated across these interventions.

## Key Results
- The baseline CLIP‑CBM achieves 41.51% accuracy with 8.19% bias amplification on ImSitu.
- Applying a top‑k filter (k=1000) improves accuracy to 43.35% and reduces bias amplification to 7.84%.
- Combining the top‑k filter with adversarial debiasing yields 42.69% accuracy and 6.29% bias amplification—a 28% relative reduction in bias compared to the baseline with minimal accuracy loss.
- Concept removal methods (via gender predictor or LLM) reduce bias by at most 0.5% but incur ~0.6% accuracy loss, indicating limited efficacy due to pervasive information leakage.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Applying a top‑k filter to concept activations reduces information leakage and bias amplification more effectively than increasing sparsity in the classifier's weight layer.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of limiting the number of learned concept weights (which reduces model capacity), the top‑k filter retains only the k highest activation values for each image's concept vector, zeroing out the rest. This directly limits the 'hidden distribution' of low-value, semantically irrelevant concept contributions that encode sensitive attribute proxies, while preserving the high-value concepts that are task‑relevant. The model’s final prediction is thus based on a smaller, more interpretable set of concepts per image.
- **Core assumption:** The low‑activation concepts carry disproportionate information unrelated to the task (i.e., gender) and can be discarded with minimal impact on accuracy for a suitably chosen k.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract, section 4.4.1] "Decreasing information leakage can improve both fairness and interpretability... the top‑k concept filter... outperforms label‑free CBM’s sparsity on the FC layer in terms of fairness‑performance tradeoffs... At k = 1000, the model performance even approaches that of CLIP‑DNN while having lower bias amplification." [section 4.2, 4.4.1] Defines bias amplification metric; shows Figure 4 tradeoff curve where larger k improves accuracy but also bias, with the optimal frontier superior to sparsity‑based methods.
- **Break condition:** If the required k for acceptable accuracy becomes so large that it includes a vast number of low‑activation, noise‑like concepts, the leakage reduction benefit plateaus or reverses, and interpretability suffers due to the large remaining concept set.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Identifying and zeroing out concept activations correlated with a sensitive attribute (e.g., gender) at test time can reduce bias, though its efficacy is limited by pervasive information leakage.
- **Mechanism:** Concepts are flagged as biased either by training an auxiliary model (e.g., a gender predictor) on the CBM's concept activations and selecting those with highest weights, or by querying an LLM to self‑rate concept semantics for gender association. At inference, activations for these flagged concepts are set to zero before the final classifier layer.
- **Core assumption:** A subset of concepts exists whose activations are causally upstream of or strongly correlated with the sensitive attribute in the data, and removing their influence will reduce bias without catastrophically harming task performance.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.4.2] "Removing concepts that serve as proxy variables... can improve fairness... we zeroed out the concept activations of removed concepts at testing time... Results indicate... decrease bias amplification by 0.5% and 0.3% respectively while incurring around 0.6% loss in accuracy. In general, this method does not achieve strong tradeoffs." [section 4.4.2] Key finding: "we hypothesize that this is due to information leakage: The new model learns a new hidden distribution... concept semantics play a weaker role in fairness than previously expected."
- **Break condition:** The informational leakage is so distributed that no small set of semantically obvious proxy concepts explains the majority of the bias. Removal then only yields marginal gains while incurring significant accuracy loss on classes that genuinely rely on the removed concepts.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Adversarial debiasing applied to the CBM's final linear layer (the concept‑to‑class mapping) optimizes the fairness‑performance tradeoff by forcing this mapping to be invariant to the sensitive attribute.
- **Mechanism:** During training, the CBM is optimized for two objectives: (1) minimize classification loss (primary task), and (2) maximize an adversary's inability to predict the sensitive attribute from the CBM's final output logits or intermediate representations. This is implemented as a gradient reversal layer or alternating optimization, encouraging the learned linear combination of concepts to lose gender information while retaining task‑relevant information.
- **Core assumption:** The bias signal is concentrated in the final concept‑class mapping parameters and can be disentangled from the task signal through this adversarial game.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract, section 3.3] "Applying adversarial debiasing optimizes for fairness‑performance tradeoffs... adversarial debiasing improves further, decreasing bias by 28% with very minor accuracy loss." [section 4.4.3, Table 3] Quantifies results: "CBM‑topk + debiasing 42.69% accuracy, 6.29% bias amplification" vs. baseline "CLIP‑CBM 41.51% 8.19%". Shows additive benefit over top‑k filter. [section 5] "Examining the shifts in concept weights and contributions also provides more interpretability... adversarial debiasing achieves strong fairness‑performance tradeoffs by focusing on meaningful concepts and decreasing the confusion matrix’s gender correlation."
- **Break condition:** The sensitive attribute information is already heavily removed by the top‑k filter (or other upstream steps), leaving little for the adversary to remove, or the adversary destabilizes training and degrades primary task accuracy beyond an acceptable threshold.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Information Leakage**  
  - **Why needed here:** Central to the paper's problem statement; CBMs are intended to be interpretable and fair by using human‑understandable concepts, but the concept activation vectors from CLIP encode hidden correlations with sensitive attributes (gender) beyond the concept's semantic meaning. Understanding this is key to why naive CBMs fail at fairness.  
  - **Quick check question:** If a CBM uses 1000 concepts but only the top 50 contribute meaningfully to a prediction, what risk does the activation pattern of the remaining 950 concepts pose?

- **Concept: Concept Bottleneck Model (CBM)**  
  - **Why needed here:** The core architecture under investigation. Must understand its two‑stage design (image -> concepts -> class) and its promises (interpretability, potential for fairness via masking details) versus its pitfalls (leakage, scalability of concept labeling).  
  - **Quick check question:** In a CBM, where is the model's "interpretable" part— the concept predictor or the final linear layer? What does each part's weight/activation represent?

- **Concept: Bias Amplification Metric**  
  - **Why needed here:** The primary fairness evaluation metric used. It measures how much more a model leaks sensitive attribute information compared to a performance‑matched random chance model, isolating learned bias from dataset bias. Crucial for comparing mitigation techniques.  
  - **Quick check question:** How is "bias amplification" different from simply measuring accuracy disparity between demographic groups? What does a value of 8.19% versus 6.29% signify?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input image -> CLIP ViT‑B/16 image encoder -> Dot product with all concept text embeddings (from CLIP text encoder) -> Concept activation vector (length M) -> (Optional: top‑k filter or concept zeroing) -> Sparse trainable fully‑connected layer (weights W_F) -> Class logits (200 classes) -> Prediction.
- **Critical path:** The image is encoded by CLIP; its embedding is compared to all concept text embeddings to produce an activation vector; this vector is optionally filtered; then multiplied by a sparse weight matrix to produce class scores.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Sparsity (λ) vs. performance: higher L1 penalty improves fairness/interpretability but harms accuracy. Top‑k value vs. fairness/performance: smaller k reduces leakage but loses accuracy. Concept list quality vs. latency: more concepts increase accuracy chance but raise dimensionality, leakage risk, and interpretation difficulty. Debiasing strength vs. accuracy drop: stronger adversarial loss reduces bias more but may degrade task accuracy.
- **Failure signatures:** High bias amplification (>8%) indicates severe leakage. Sparse model with very low accuracy (<12%) suggests over‑regularization. Concept removal harms key classes if removed concepts were genuinely important. Adversarial training instability (loss oscillations, adversary too strong, accuracy collapse).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Train CLIP‑zero‑shot, CLIP‑DNN, and CLIP‑CBM (no mitigation) on ImSitu; report accuracy and bias amplification to establish baseline fairness/performance.  
  2. For the trained CLIP‑CBM, evaluate at test‑time with k ∈ {5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 70, 100, 200, 500, 1000} for the top‑k filter; plot accuracy vs. bias amplification to find Pareto‑optimal k.  
  3. Retrain the final linear layer of the best top‑k model (e.g., k=1000) with adversarial debiasing; compare its accuracy and bias amplification to the non‑debiased version and prior published results.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can bias amplification serve as a reliable and comprehensive metric for evaluating and comparing different CBM architectures and concept generation methods across diverse datasets?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Improving CBMs remains an active field of research and fairness can serve as a dimension for evaluating new approaches."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper uses bias amplification as the primary fairness metric but doesn't establish whether this metric captures all relevant fairness concerns or how it correlates with other fairness notions (e.g., equality of opportunity). The tradeoffs observed may be metric‑specific rather than reflecting fundamental improvements.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi‑metric evaluation of CBM variants across datasets with known bias structures, comparing bias amplification with other fairness metrics and human assessments of fairness, to determine if bias amplification is sufficiently aligned with broader fairness goals.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do concept quality characteristics (e.g., visual grounding, semantic precision, human interpretability) quantitatively correlate with information leakage and fairness metrics in label‑free CBMs generated by different LLMs and prompting strategies?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "In general, label‑free CBMs may be less interpretable than their traditional counterparts by design" and "Since GPT generates concepts using general prompts, the precision and descriptiveness are worse than those of a human expert."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies that label‑free CBMs require more concepts and leak more information, but doesn't measure concept quality systematically or correlate it with leakage. It's unclear if better concept generation could narrow the gap with traditional CBMs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Human evaluations of concept interpretability and visual relevance, combined with leakage measurements for concepts generated by multiple LLMs (GPT‑3, GPT‑4, Claude) with varied prompts, to establish whether concept quality improvements directly reduce information leakage.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is information leakage in CBMs an inevitable consequence of representing high‑dimensional semantic information in compressed concept spaces, or can architectural constraints (e.g., discrete concept representations, information‑bottleneck regularization) fundamentally limit leakage without catastrophic performance loss?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "The top‑k concept filter we proposed provides better fairness‑performance tradeoffs than applying sparsity to the FC layer but still does not solve information leakage entirely" and [inferred] from the finding that removing biased concepts fails because "the new model learns a new hidden distribution."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows leakage persists even when biased concepts are removed, suggesting the model finds alternative leakage pathways. However, they don't test whether alternative CBM architectures (e.g., with binary concept activations, stricter sparsity, or different concept space designs) could fundamentally constrain leakage.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative evaluation of novel CBM architectures with constraints designed to minimize mutual information between concept activations and sensitive attributes, measuring whether leakage can be reduced to near‑zero levels while maintaining accuracy within acceptable bounds.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do the fairness‑performance tradeoffs achieved by the proposed CBM bias mitigation techniques (top‑k filter, adversarial debiasing) generalize across different sensitive attributes (e.g., race, age, disability) and dataset bias structures, or are they specific to gender bias in action recognition datasets?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper exclusively evaluates gender bias in ImSitu, though it mentions CBMs might handle multiple attributes better since "fairness through unawareness does not rely on sensitive attribute ground truth labels."
- **Why unresolved:** The mechanisms of bias amplification likely differ across attributes (e.g., visual proxies for race vs. gender). The paper's claim that CBMs are advantageous for multiple attributes remains speculative without empirical validation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying the same mitigation pipeline to datasets with annotated sensitive attributes for race, age, and other protected characteristics (e.g., UTKFace, FairFace) and measuring consistency in bias reduction across attributes, or identifying attribute‑specific adjustments needed.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can adversarial debiasing for CBMs be effectively adapted to scenarios where sensitive attribute labels are unavailable during training, perhaps through self‑supervised attribute prediction or clustering‑based approaches, without sacrificing the fairness gains achieved with supervised debiasing?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Doing so requires sensitive attribute labels in the given bias direction which may be unavailable in practice" and "the top‑k concept filter...does not rely on sensitive attribute ground truths during debiasing."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies the top‑k filter as a label‑free approach but shows it's less effective than supervised adversarial debiasing. The gap between supervised and unsupervised fairness methods for CBMs remains unaddressed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and evaluation of unsupervised adversarial debiasing variants (e.g., using demographic clustering from feature space, or adversarially predicting self‑supervised attribute proxies) on the ImSitu dataset, comparing their fairness‑performance tradeoffs to the supervised approach and measuring robustness to pseudo‑label noise.

## Limitations
- GPT‑3 generation specifics (model version, temperature, max tokens) are undisclosed, hindering exact concept list reproduction.  
- Adversarial debiasing implementation details—adversary architecture, loss weight, training schedule, gradient reversal method—are missing, preventing faithful replication.  
- Evaluation is restricted to the ImSitu dataset with gender as the sole sensitive attribute; generalizability to other domains or attributes is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (top‑k filter):** High confidence. Empirical trade‑off curves clearly show superiority over sparsity‑based methods.  
- **Mechanism 2 (concept removal):** Low confidence. Results show negligible bias reduction with accuracy loss, indicating limited efficacy.  
- **Mechanism 3 (adversarial debiasing):** High confidence. Combined with top‑k, it yields a 28% relative bias reduction with minimal accuracy drop, demonstrating consistent benefit.

## Next Checks
1. **Generalizability test:** Apply the top‑k filter and adversarial debiasing to a different dataset (e.g., CelebA) with another sensitive attribute (e.g., age) to verify portability of findings.  
2. **Hyperparameter ablation:** Systematically vary adversarial loss weight and adversary network capacity; report stability, fairness‑accuracy trade‑offs, and any training collapse.  
3. **Leakage analysis:** Compute per‑concept mutual information with gender across activation magnitude bins; statistically test the hypothesis that low‑activation concepts encode stronger gender signals.
