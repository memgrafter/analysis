# QUPID: A Partitioned Quantum Neural Network for Anomaly Detection in Smart Grid

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.11500
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11500
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** QUPID outperforms five traditional ML models across 15 scenarios and 7 metrics, with peak accuracy 0.940 and ROC-AUC 0.975; R-QUPID maintains ~70-81% accuracy under FGSM/PGD attacks while classical DP baselines drop 5-10%.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces QUPID, a partitioned quantum neural network for smart grid anomaly detection. QUPID processes complex phasor measurements from phasor measurement units (PMUs) by partitioning the grid into local groups, each handled by a small quantum circuit, thereby reducing qubit requirements and improving scalability. The model uses amplitude encoding to directly embed complex-valued phasors into quantum states, preserving magnitude-phase relationships. In extensive experiments across 15 scenarios, QUPID outperforms five traditional machine learning models, achieving peak accuracy 0.940 and ROC-AUC 0.975. Its robust variant, R-QUPID, combines differential privacy with quantum depolarizing noise, providing certified robustness against adversarial attacks and maintaining 70-81% accuracy under FGSM and PGD attacks, while classical DP defenses degrade by 5-10%.

## Method Summary
QUPID employs a hybrid quantum-classical architecture. Input data consists of complex phasor measurements from PMUs, preprocessed (feature removal, MinMax scaling) and partitioned into K groups of neighboring PMUs. Each partition is encoded into a quantum state via amplitude embedding, processed by a parameterized quantum circuit (ansatz with RY/RZ rotations and CNOT entangling layers), and measured to produce a latent vector. These vectors are concatenated and fed into two classical dense layers with ReLU activations, ending in a softmax for classification. R-QUPID adds classical Gaussian noise (for differential privacy) and a depolarizing quantum channel after the ansatz. Training uses Adam (lr=1e-2, batch 128, 200 epochs) with cross-entropy loss. The partitioning scheme reduces the total qubit count to O(K log₂(t·m)), sublinear in the number of PMUs n, addressing scalability.

## Key Results
- QUPID achieved peak accuracy 0.940 and ROC-AUC 0.975 across 15 smart grid scenarios, with superior recall and F1-score compared to five traditional ML models.
- R-QUPID maintained 70-81% accuracy under FGSM and PGD attacks, while classical DP baselines dropped 5-10%, demonstrating certified robustness via quantum noise amplification.
- The partitioning approach enables scalability by reducing qubit requirements sublinearly with grid size, making quantum-enhanced anomaly detection feasible for large grids.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Partitioning for Scalability
- **Claim:** Partitioning the grid into local groups processed by separate small quantum circuits reduces total qubits and distributes computation, enabling scalability.
- **Mechanism:** Input from n PMUs is split into K partitions, each with t neighboring PMUs (m features each). Each partition uses log₂(t·m) qubits. Total qubits become O(K·log₂(t·m)), sublinear in n if K << n/t. Assumes anomalies are predominantly local.
- **Evidence:** [IV.B] addresses scalability; neighboring corpus not discussed, indicating novelty.
- **Break condition:** If anomalies involve coordinated events across distant PMUs, local partitioning may fail.

### Complex Phasor Encoding
- **Claim:** Directly encoding complex phasors (voltage/current magnitude and phase) into quantum amplitudes preserves complex relationships, yielding more expressive features than splitting into real components.
- **Mechanism:** Amplitude encoding maps complex vector x^(i) to quantum state |x^(i)⟩. The ansatz processes this complex state, allowing quantum interference to capture non-linear interactions between magnitude and phase.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] says quantum computing processes complex values; [IV.A] defines complex phasor; [IV.B] states encoding voltage/current as complex numbers captures intricate relationships.
- **Break condition:** If phase is statistically independent of anomalies, complex encoding adds overhead without benefit.

### Quantum Noise Amplification for Robustness
- **Claim:** Combining classical differential privacy noise with quantum depolarizing noise amplifies privacy guarantee (reducing effective δ), providing certifiable robustness against adversarial attacks.
- **Mechanism:** Classical DP adds Gaussian noise A achieving (ε,δ)-DP. R-QUPID appends depolarizing channel E_p after ansatz. The composition satisfies (ε, (1-p)√(1-ϕ)δ)-DP where ϕ = min |⟨x|x'⟩|². The factor (1-p)√(1-ϕ) < 1 amplifies privacy, making predictions stable under small perturbations.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] reports robustness results; [IV.C] derives privacy amplification; [V.B] experiments show R-QUPID outperforms classical DP under attacks.
- **Break condition:** Excessive p degrades clean accuracy; adversaries may invert quantum noise channel.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Input (complex phasor features from PMUs) -> Partitioning module -> Parallel processing: each partition -> Amplitude encoding -> Parameterized ansatz -> [R-QUPID: Depolarizing channel] -> Measurement -> Concatenation of all partition outputs -> Two fully connected ReLU layers -> Softmax output.

**Critical path:**  
Data ingestion -> partitioning -> parallel quantum encoding/ansatz/measurement -> concatenation -> classical dense layers -> prediction. The quantum part is the bottleneck due to simulators/hardware.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Partitioning granularity: smaller partitions reduce qubits per circuit but increase K and classical post-processing; larger partitions risk exceeding qubit limits. (Paper uses 4 circuits with 5 qubits each.)
- Quantum vs classical noise: R-QUPID adds depolarizing noise; too little fails to amplify privacy, too much hurts accuracy.
- Complex encoding: enables richer representation but requires amplitude encoding, which is sensitive to noise and requires normalized vectors.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Training divergence: loss does not decrease; may indicate barren plateaus or poor ansatz design.
- Scenario-specific poor performance: low recall/F1 on certain scenarios (e.g., S5, S8) suggests model may not generalize to all anomaly types; check data distribution shifts.
- Robustness collapse: under FGSM/PGD attack, accuracy drops significantly below 70% (for R-QUPID) indicates failure of noise amplification; may be due to insufficient depolarizing probability p or incorrect DP parameters.
- High inference latency: if using real quantum hardware, queue times or decoherence may cause delays; simulator should be fast.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Sanity check: Train QUPID on ICS dataset scenario S1 with default hyperparameters (Adam, lr=0.01, batch=128, 200 epochs). Verify test accuracy ≈0.940 and ROC-AUC ≈0.975 as in Table I.
2. Baseline comparison: Train FTTransformer on the same S1 split; compare metrics. Expect QUPID to outperform in recall and F1.
3. Robustness validation: Load trained R-QUPID (with p=0.1) and generate FGSM adversarial examples with ε=0.05. Measure accuracy; expect ~81% retention (per Figure 3a). Also test classical MLP with DP (σ tuned) on same attacks; should drop ~10%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** How does the combined use of Gaussian (classical) and depolarizing (quantum) noise in R-QUPID compare to individual noise mechanisms in terms of adversarial robustness and differential privacy guarantees across different quantum neural network architectures and attack scenarios?
- **Basis in paper:** Section II.B states that no prior work has investigated the combined use as a unified privacy-preserving strategy for hybrid QML models.
- **Why unresolved:** R-QUPID introduces the combination theoretically and shows initial results, but does not isolate or vary the contributions of each noise type, nor test across diverse QML architectures beyond the specific partitioned design.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Controlled experiments where Gaussian and depolarizing noise parameters are varied independently across multiple QML models (e.g., VQCs, QKs) against a suite of adaptive attacks. Ablation studies removing one noise type at a time would quantify each contribution.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** To what extent do the performance and robustness claims of QUPID/R-QUPID hold when deployed on actual Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) hardware, as opposed to simulators, given real-world noise sources like decoherence, gate errors, and crosstalk?
- **Basis in paper:** All experiments in Section V used the PennyLane simulator; the paper discusses NISQ-era feasibility but does not address hardware-specific limitations.
- **Why unresolved:** Simulators cannot fully replicate the stochastic and correlated noise of physical quantum processors. The theoretical privacy amplification assumes depolarizing noise as a Markovian process, but real noise may deviate, affecting certified robustness. Reported metrics may overestimate real-world performance.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Benchmark QUPID and R-QUPID on multiple real quantum devices (e.g., IBM Quantum, Rigetti) with varying qubit counts and error rates. Compare key metrics against simulator baselines and characterize hardware noise profiles.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What is the optimal partitioning strategy for QUPID in terms of partition size (number of PMUs per partition), topology (graph connectivity), and number of quantum circuits, to minimize qubit requirements while maximizing anomaly detection accuracy and robustness?
- **Basis in paper:** Section IV.B describes partitioning by neighboring PMUs but does not justify the choice of 4 PMUs per 5-qubit circuit or explore alternatives.
- **Why unresolved:** Without understanding the trade-offs, practitioners cannot efficiently allocate limited qubit resources. Larger partitions might capture more global patterns but increase qubit needs; smaller partitions may lose contextual information. Optimal strategy likely depends on grid topology and data correlation structure.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Systematic experiments varying partition sizes (e.g., 2, 4, 8 PMUs per partition) and partitioning methods (spatial vs. topological vs. learned clustering) on the ICS and other datasets. Measure accuracy, robustness, training time, and qubit usage. Use statistical analysis to identify significant factors and possibly develop a heuristic for partition selection.

## Limitations
- The exact definition of "neighboring PMUs" for partitioning is unspecified, which is critical for reproducibility and the scalability claim.
- The specific noise parameters (quantum depolarizing probability p and classical DP variance σ) used in R-QUPID's robustness experiments are not reported, preventing direct verification of the 70-81% robust accuracy claim.
- The precise ansatz architecture details (e.g., number of rotation gates per layer) are ambiguous, making faithful reproduction challenging.
