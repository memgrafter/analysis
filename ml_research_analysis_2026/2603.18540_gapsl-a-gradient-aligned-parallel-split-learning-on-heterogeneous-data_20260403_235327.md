# GAPSL: A Gradient-Aligned Parallel Split Learning on Heterogeneous Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18540
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18540
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Achieves 63.3% test accuracy on CIFAR-10 with Dirichlet α=0.1 (vs. PSL's 47.3%) and converges in 32 epochs (vs. PSL's 56).

## Executive Summary
Parallel split learning (PSL) suffers from gradient directional inconsistency when client data is non-IID, leading to unstable training and poor convergence. GAPSL addresses this with two mechanisms: Leader Gradient Identification (LGI) selects a subset of directionally consistent client gradients to form a robust leader gradient, filtering out noisy updates. Gradient Direction Alignment (GDA) then aligns each selected client's gradient to this leader via a directional regularization term in the local loss, steering updates toward a consensus direction without sharing model parameters. Evaluated on CIFAR-10/100 with VGG-16 and ViT-Base under severe non-IID (Dirichlet α=0.1), GAPSL achieves state-of-the-art accuracy and faster convergence, significantly outperforming PSL, SFL, and other baselines. Ablation studies confirm both components are essential.

## Method Summary
GAPSL operates in a parallel split learning setup: clients host the initial model partition, the server hosts the later partition. Each round, clients compute local forward passes and send activations to the server. The server first applies Leader Gradient Identification (LGI): it computes each client's server-side gradient, scores gradients by their average angular deviation from all others (consistency score), and adaptively selects a top-K% based on gradient dispersion (STD of scores) and training progress (t/T). The selected gradients are averaged to form a leader gradient. Then, Gradient Direction Alignment (GDA) computes each client's angular deviation from the leader, applies an adaptive threshold (mean minus η times STD) to filter severely misaligned gradients, and for selected clients, augments their local loss with λ(1–cosθ), penalizing misalignment. The server updates via backpropagation on the sum of regularized losses and returns activation gradients to clients for local updates. The adaptive selection ratio K^t balances early stability with later diversity, while the threshold动态 filters outliers.

## Key Results
- On CIFAR-10 (Dirichlet α=0.1, VGG-16), GAPSL reaches 63.3% test accuracy vs. PSL's 47.3%, and converges in 32 epochs vs. PSL's 56.
- Outperforms SFL, EPSL, and Vanilla SL across both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 with VGG-16 and ViT-Base.
- Ablation studies show removing either LGI or GDA causes significant performance drops, confirming their necessity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
GAPSL mitigates gradient directional inconsistency through two synergistic mechanisms. Leader Gradient Identification (LGI) constructs a leader gradient from a dynamically selected subset of client gradients that are mutually directionally consistent, filtering out noisy or conflicting updates that would destabilize the global update direction. Selection is driven by a consistency score (average angular deviation from other clients) and an adaptive ratio K^t that tightens when gradient dispersion is high and relaxes over training epochs to balance stability and diversity. Gradient Direction Alignment (GDA) then aligns each selected client's gradient to this leader via a regularization term in the local loss, steering local updates toward the consensus direction without sharing model parameters. The adaptive angular threshold in GDA further filters severely misaligned gradients, preventing harmful updates. Evidence shows clients with lower consistency scores achieve higher final accuracy, the leader gradient's directional consistency exceeds that of all-client aggregates, and ablating either component drastically harms performance. The core assumption is that selective alignment, guided by measured directional consistency, enables PSL to handle non-IID data effectively.

## Architecture Onboarding
Client Device -> Server -> Client Device

Critical path: Client forward pass (local model, send activations) -> Server LGI (compute consistency scores, select top-K, form leader gradient) -> Server GDA (compute angular deviations, apply threshold, add regularization to selected clients' losses) -> Server backward pass (update server model, compute activation gradients) -> Client backward pass (update local model with received gradients).

Design tradeoffs:
- Selection ratio bounds (K_min, K_max): Tighter bounds increase stability but reduce diversity; looser bounds risk noise.
- Regularization coefficient λ: Higher λ forces stronger alignment but may suppress useful local updates.
- Threshold sensitivity η: Higher η makes θ_th^t more conservative by subtracting more from the mean deviation.

Failure signatures:
- High angular deviation (θ_lead,i^t) consistently indicates severe misalignment that GAPSL cannot correct.
- Unstable test accuracy curves suggest adaptive mechanisms (K^t, θ_th^t) are failing to stabilize.
- Leader gradient collapse if K^t is too small and selected subset is unrepresentative.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Impact of η**: How does the sensitivity parameter η in the adaptive angular threshold affect convergence and accuracy across heterogeneity levels and architectures? The paper does not specify η or explore its role.
- **Automatic determination of K_min/K_max**: Can the bounds for the adaptive selection ratio be automatically set for a given dataset/model, rather than manually based on heuristic judgments about heterogeneity?
- **Theoretical convergence guarantee**: Does GAPSL have a convergence guarantee, and how does the rate depend on consistency scoring, selection, and alignment under non-IID settings? No analysis provided.
- **Scalability to large S**: LGI requires O(S^2) pairwise angular computations; does this become a bottleneck for hundreds or thousands of devices? Experiments only go up to 40 devices.

## Limitations
- Missing critical hyperparameters: total training rounds T for adaptive K^t, angular threshold η, and convergence criterion for epoch reporting.
- Scalability unproven beyond 40 devices; O(S^2) computation may not scale.
- No theoretical convergence analysis to support empirical gains.
