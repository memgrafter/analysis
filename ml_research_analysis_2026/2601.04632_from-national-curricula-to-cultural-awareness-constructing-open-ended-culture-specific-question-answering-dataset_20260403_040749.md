# From National Curricula to Cultural Awareness: Constructing Open-Ended Culture-Specific Question Answering Dataset

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04632
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04632
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** An automated framework (CuCu) uses national curricula to generate high-quality, culture-specific QA datasets (e.g., KCaQA with 34,128 pairs), achieving strong metrics in cultural appropriateness and language accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the cultural misalignment in large language models (LLMs) caused by English-centric training data. The core insight is to use national social studies curricula as a structured, expert-approved prior for culture-specific supervision. The authors develop CuCu, a multi-agent LLM framework that automatically transforms curriculum learning outcomes into open-ended question-answer pairs across multiple languages and readability levels. Applied to the Korean curriculum, it generates KCaQA, a dataset shown to cover distinct cultural topics and produce contextually appropriate responses. The work provides a scalable method for post-training alignment but highlights risks of inheriting curriculum biases and challenges in multilingual framing.

## Method Summary
CuCu is a multi-stage, automated framework. First, it parses a national curriculum to extract learning outcomes (LOs) and associated topics. These LOs are then used to generate culture-specific questions via an LLM agent. A second agent generates responses, conditioned on the target language and a specified readability level (basic, intermediate, advanced). A filtering and refinement stage ensures quality and alignment. The pipeline is entirely automated, enabling large-scale dataset creation without manual annotation.

## Key Results
- Generated KCaQA with 34,128 QA pairs across Korean, English, Chinese, and Japanese, covering distinct cultural topics (Jensen-Shannon Divergence of 0.807 vs. a general Korean QA dataset).
- Responses exhibit appropriately scaled readability for different educational levels.
- Quality metrics show high language selection accuracy (0.91), cultural appropriateness (8.56/10), and natural language use (7.78/10) based on LLM-as-a-judge and human inspection.
- Minor issues include target language localization altering culturally contested terminology and omission of historical details.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
National curricula provide a authoritative, structured, and culturally-vetted knowledge scope, serving as a high-quality prior for what constitutes important cultural knowledge. The multi-agent design decomposes the complex task of culturally-aware generation into specialized steps (question generation, response generation, filtering), allowing each LLM agent to focus on a constrained sub-task. Automating this pipeline scales dataset creation far beyond manual efforts while maintaining alignment with an official educational standard.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: National Curriculum as Expert Prior**
*Why needed:* Provides a vetted, structured knowledge scope that defines essential cultural and civic knowledge, bypassing the need for costly manual curation.
*Quick check:* Verify curriculum coverage by mapping generated QA topics back to official LO codes; spot-check for alignment with curriculum intent.

**Concept 2: Multi-Agent LLM Specialization**
*Why needed:* Decomposes culturally nuanced generation into sequential, constrained tasks (e.g., question framing, response drafting, localization) to improve control and output quality.
*Quick check:* Audit each agent's output individually to isolate failure modes (e.g., do questions accurately reflect LOs? do responses stray from the LO?).

**Concept 3: Scalable Readability Control**
*Why needed:* Ensures content is accessible across educational tiers, increasing dataset utility for diverse training stages.
*Quick check:* Apply standard readability metrics (e.g., Flesch-Kincaid) to generated outputs per tier and validate distributional separation.

**Concept 4: Target Language Localization**
*Why needed:* Generates culturally framed content in the user's language, but risks perspective shifts.
*Quick check:* For disputed facts (e.g., historical event names), compare terminology across generated language versions for consistency with the source culture's standard.

**Concept 5: Automated Quality Filtering**
*Why needed:* Scales quality assurance; uses LLM judges to screen for language accuracy and cultural grounding before human review.
*Quick check:* Correlate LLM-judge scores with a small human evaluation sample to assess judge reliability for cultural dimensions.

**Concept 6: Bias Inheritance from Source Material**
*Why needed:* Curricula are not neutral; they reflect state-approved narratives that may omit, revise, or frame events controversially.
*Quick check:* Perform a bias audit on sampled QA pairs with subject matter experts to identify potential historical or political leanings.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Curriculum Parser -> Query Generator Agent -> Response Generator Agent (with readability & language constraints) -> LLM-Judge Filter -> Optional Human Review -> Final KCaQA Dataset

**Critical Path:** Parsing curriculum LOs -> LO-to-query transformation -> multilingual response generation with readability control -> quality filtering.

**Design Tradeoffs:** 
- *Automation vs. Nuance:* Full automation enables scale but can miss subtle cultural cues or propagate curriculum biases without detection.
- *Multilingual Flexibility vs. Framing Fidelity:* Generating in multiple languages increases accessibility but may subtly shift framing toward perspectives dominant in the target language's cultural sphere.
- *Readability Metrics vs. True Comprehension:* Automated readability scores are a proxy; they do not guarantee pedagogical appropriateness or actual learner comprehension.

**Failure Signatures:**
- Localization altering contested terminology (e.g., using term preferred in target language's region over source culture's standard).
- Omission of key historical details in simpler readability tiers.
- LLM-judge giving high scores to fluent but culturally superficial or inaccurate responses.
- Generated questions that are too generic or fail to capture the LO's specific cultural context.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Cross-Curriculum Replication:** Run CuCu on a national curriculum from a linguistically/culturally diverse nation (e.g., India) to test robustness beyond a homogeneous context like South Korea.
2. **Readability Validation Study:** Present tiered QA pairs to human speakers at corresponding education levels and measure perceived appropriateness vs. automated scores.
3. **Controlled Terminology Comparison:** For a fixed set of culture-specific queries on contested topics, generate responses in all four target languages and systematically compare terminology choices and factual framing against a Korean-cultural gold standard.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can CuCu be adapted for nations where the national curriculum does not uniformly reflect the diversity of cultural, ethnic, or linguistic groups within the country?
- **Basis:** The authors explicitly note that their approach "faces challenges when applied to nation-state in multiple geocultural regions with varying cultural contexts" (Limitation, p.5). They also discuss in Appendix B.2 that a nation-state may not follow a single uniform curriculum.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates CuCu on South Korea, a relatively homogeneous nation-state; no experiments on multicultural contexts are presented.
- **What evidence:** Apply CuCu to a country with known cultural diversity (e.g., India, Canada) by using multiple regional curricula or incorporating supplementary sources, then evaluate coverage of cultural groups and alignment with local norms through quantitative metrics and human evaluation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent does the target language of the generated QA pairs alter the cultural framing and factual accuracy regarding contested issues (e.g., territorial names, historical events)?
- **Basis:** The authors observed in human inspection that "Japanese responses sometimes shift from Korean-standard terminology to expressions preferred in Japan" and adopt Japan-aligned terminology for disputed topics (Section 4.2). They also mention in Appendix B.2 that constraining outputs to a specific language may inadvertently shift framing toward perspectives prevalent where that language is widely used.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper includes only anecdotal observations; systematic analysis across languages and topics is lacking.
- **What evidence:** Generate KCaQA-like data for multiple target languages for the same set of culture-specific queries, then compare responses for consistency in terminology and coverage of contested facts using both LLM judges and human experts familiar with the Korean stance.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does reliance on national curricula introduce or amplify biases (e.g., political, historical revisionism) in the generated QA data, and how can such biases be detected and mitigated?
- **Basis:** The authors explicitly caution that "CuCu may inherit and potentially amplify any bias and revisionism latent in the curriculum without a thorough review" (Broader Impact) and note that curricula may propagate unresolved controversies (p.5). However, no bias detection or mitigation is implemented.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not measure the extent of bias in the generated data or provide methods to address it.
- **What evidence:** Conduct a bias audit of KCaQA by having subject matter experts identify potential biases in a sample of QA pairs. Experiment with interventions such as curriculum source diversification, multi-perspective alignment, or post-generation filtering to reduce bias.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the choice of foundation LLM (e.g., GPT, Qwen, DeepSeek) affect the cultural fidelity and naturalness of the generated QA pairs in CuCu?
- **Basis:** The paper uses multiple LLMs for response generation but does not compare their outputs or analyze model-specific effects. The assumption that all LLMs produce similarly high-quality culture-aware content is untested.
- **Why unresolved:** The study reports aggregated quality scores across all models but no per-model breakdown, making it unclear whether some models are better suited for cultural QA generation.
- **What evidence:** Run CuCu with different candidate LLMs on a fixed set of queries, evaluate the resulting responses along cultural appropriateness and language use dimensions using consistent metrics, and perform statistical comparisons to identify top-performing models.

## Limitations
- Generalizability is unproven beyond South Korea's standardized curriculum and specific language pairs; applicability to decentralized or culturally diverse educational systems is uncertain.
- The evaluation relies heavily on LLM-as-a-judge and unspecified human evaluation, which may not capture deep cultural nuances or true pedagogical value.
- The framework may systematically alter contested terminology or omit details when generating for certain target languages, a known but unresolved issue.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-cultural replication:** Apply the CuCu framework to at least two other national curricula (e.g., from a non-Asian country and one with a decentralized education system) to test methodological robustness and output consistency.
2. **Human-centric readability validation:** Conduct a study with target-language speakers at different education levels to judge the actual appropriateness of the generated QA pairs for the "basic, intermediate, advanced" tiers, comparing against automated metrics.
3. **Qualitative error analysis:** Perform a structured audit on the subset of QA pairs flagged for "altered terminology" or "omitted historical details" to categorize error types, assess their frequency and cultural significance, and relate them back to specific steps in the multi-agent pipeline.
