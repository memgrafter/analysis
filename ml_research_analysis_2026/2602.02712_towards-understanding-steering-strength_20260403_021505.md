# Towards Understanding Steering Strength

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02712
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02712
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The paper provides a theoretical characterization of activation steering in LLMs, showing token probabilities exhibit a non‑monotonic “bump” with α, concept probability follows a tanh‑shaped curve, and cross‑entropy increases quadratically near α=0.

## Executive Summary
Activation steering—adjusting model activations to influence behavior—is widely used but its dependence on steering strength α lacks a theoretical foundation. This paper analyzes α using a simplified Unconstrained Features Model with a difference‑of‑means steering vector. The authors derive three main results: (1) token‑level probability changes are non‑monotonic, forming a “bump” where target‑concept tokens peak later than off‑target ones; (2) aggregated concept probabilities evolve in a tanh‑like sigmoid, with the target concept increasing and others decreasing or reverting; (3) cross‑entropy rises quadratically around α=0, implying any non‑zero steering degrades local performance. Empirical validation across 11 LLMs confirms these patterns and additionally shows that for large α the steered logits converge to an input‑independent limit. The work offers the first formal treatment of steering strength and guides the selection of α to balance efficacy and distortion.

## Method Summary
The authors empirically test their theoretical predictions on 11 LLMs (GPT‑2 variants, Gemma 3, Qwen 3, Llama 2, Mistral). For each model they collect two prompt sets—positive examples of a target concept and negatives (either contrastive opposites or random)—and extract residual‑stream activations at a middle layer. The steering vector v is the difference of mean activations between sets. During inference they add αv to every token’s residual at that layer and sweep α over a wide range. Three families of metrics are measured: (i) token‑level probability shifts for a fixed context; (ii) concept prevalence by sampling completions and judging with an LLM; (iii) cross‑entropy on a large held‑out corpus (FineWeb). The observed token‑probability bumps, sigmoidal concept curves, and U‑shaped cross‑entropy changes align with theory.

## Key Results
- **Non‑monotonic token probabilities** Δp(α): for most tokens, probability first increases then decreases with α, forming a “bump”; target‑concept tokens reach their peak later than off‑target tokens.
- **Tanh‑shaped concept curves** Δp(C|α): the target concept’s prevalence rises sigmoidally; other concepts either decrease monotonically or revert to their original value at large |α|.
- **Quadratic local cross‑entropy** ΔCE(α): expansion around α=0 yields a positive quadratic term, so any non‑zero α immediately increases loss; at large |α| the loss plateaus.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The next‑token probability increase Δp(z,α) for most tokens exhibits a non‑monotonic “bump”: it rises with α, peaks, then falls, with target‑concept tokens peaking later than off‑concept tokens.
- **Mechanism:** Steering adds αv to the context embedding, yielding an additive shift αΔℓ in the logits. The steered token probability becomes σ_z(fα(cj)) = p(z|cj)·exp(αM(z)) / ∑ exp(αM(z′)), where M(z) is the log‑odds from the positive/negative sets. The derivative Δp'(α) = σ_z(fα(cj))[M(z) − E_{σ(fα(cj))}[M(Z)]]. The expected log‑odds E[M] increases strictly with α because its derivative is a variance term. For any token with M(z) not equal to the global min or max, the derivative changes sign exactly once, giving a peak. Target tokens have M(z)>0, off‑targets M(z)≤0, so the expectation crosses the target’s M(z) later.
- **Core assumption:** Assumption 2 (perfect UFM training) and Assumption 1 (on‑concept probability a_z > off‑concept b_z), plus the difference‑of‑means construction.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Theorem 3.3 (Section 3.1) formalizes the bump; proof uses Lemma B.5 and monotonicity of E[M] (Eq. 10). Corpus discusses empirical bumps but without quantification.
- **Break condition:** The sign separation M(z)>0 ⇔ z∈T fails if off‑concept tokens have higher conditional probability, destroying the peak ordering. In real transformers, layer normalization and residual connections can flatten the bump for large α (Prop. 4.1).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The mean concept probability increase Δp(C|α) follows a tanh‑shaped curve: target concept C=T increases sigmoidally; other concepts decrease sigmoidally or revert.
- **Mechanism:** Summing token dynamics over a concept yields d/dα F_C(α) = F_C(α)(1−F_C(α))·(µ_C(α)−µ_C∁(α)). This integrates to a logistic (sigmoid) when µ_C−µ_C∁ ≠ 0. For target T, M(z)>0 for all tokens in T and ≤0 otherwise, so µ_T−µ_T∁ > 0, giving a monotonic increase. For a concept C whose tokens all have M(z) below all others, the difference is negative, yielding decrease. If C contains no extremal M‑tokens, the limit as |α|→∞ is −∑_{z∈C} p(z|cj) (Theorem 3.6).
- **Core assumption:** Same as Mechanism 1, plus disjoint concepts and smoothness of µ_C(α).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Theorem 3.6 (Section 3.2). Von Rütte et al. report a tanh trend for concept presence, consistent.
- **Break condition:** If the concept includes tokens with extremal log‑odds (C ∩ ({M}∪{M}) ≠ ∅), large‑α saturation deviates from simple tanh. Sampling‑based concept metrics can mask underlying Δp(C|α) if probabilities remain too low to be sampled.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The cross‑entropy change ΔCE(α) is locally quadratic around α=0 with positive coefficient, meaning any non‑zero steering immediately degrades global performance.
- **Mechanism:** Expand ΔCE(α) via Taylor series: ΔCE(α) = ΔCE(0) + ΔCE'(0)α + ½ΔCE''(0)α² + o(α²). Using the cross‑entropy expression and Lemma B.5, we get ΔCE'(α) = ∑_j π_j (E[M]_{α,j} − E[M]_{0,j}). At α=0 the distributions coincide, so ΔCE'(0)=0. The second derivative is ΔCE''(α) = ∑_j π_j Var_{σ(fα(cj))}(M(Z)), which at α=0 equals a non‑negative sum of variances of M(Z) under the unsteered token distributions (Theorem 3.8). Hence ΔCE(α) ≈ (c/2)α² near zero, c>0.
- **Core assumption:** Assumption 2 (perfect model fit) and distinct contexts with mixture weights π_j.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Theorem 3.8 (Section 3.3). Von Rütte et al. empirically observed a quadratic trend; the theorem clarifies it is only local.
- **Break condition:** Quadratic holds only near zero. If the model is not perfectly trained, first‑order terms might appear. For large α, normalization causes a plateau (Prop. 4.1), contradicting continued quadratic growth.

## Foundational Learning

### Steering Vector (Difference‑of‑Means)
- **Why needed:** It is the computed direction that, when added to activations, induces the desired behavior. All theoretical results center on its linear effect.
- **Quick check question:** If the positive set P contains prompts that inadvertently mix the target concept with others, how would the steering vector v change, and how might that affect the bump pattern?

### Unconstrained Features Model (UFM)
- **Why needed:** It mathematically isolates the steering effect by treating embeddings as free variables, enabling exact characterisation of optimal solutions and clean analysis.
- **Quick check question:** How does the UFM abstraction differ from a real transformer, and which omitted components (e.g., LayerNorm) become important in the large‑α regime (Prop. 4.1)?

### Log‑Odds M(z)
- **Why needed:** This scalar determines the sign of derivatives and the ordering of token peaks; it is the fundamental quantity in all three main results.
- **Quick check question:** According to Lemma B.4, what does M(z)>0 imply about token membership, and why does this imply target tokens peak later than off‑targets?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Collect P/N -> Extract residual at layer ℓ -> Compute v = µP - µN -> Add αv to residuals -> Measure token probabilities, concept prevalence, cross-entropy.

### Critical path
(a) Ensure P/N are high quality and diverse (500+ generations). (b) Pick a middle layer (e.g., 12/32) for strong steering without collapse. (c) Sweep α from negative to positive values, including zero and both signs. (d) First verify token‑level Δp shows the expected bump before aggregating to concepts or cross‑entropy.

### Design tradeoffs
- Contrastive vs random N: Contrastive gives cleaner separation but requires a defined opposite concept; random negatives are simpler but may dilute v.
- Steering scope: All positions yields stronger behavioral change but more distortion; last‑token steering is more targeted but may need larger α.
- Normalized v: Some studies normalize v to decouple α from its norm; this paper uses raw difference‑of‑means, linking α directly to the original scale.

### Failure signatures
- Absence of bump: P/N sets do not encode the concept cleanly or steering at an ineffective layer.
- Non‑monotonic concept probability: Poor judge reliability, extremely abstract concept, or v misaligned with the true concept direction.
- Cross‑entropy decreasing at small α: model not perfectly trained (violating Assumption 2) or an implementation error (e.g., forgetting residual connections).

### First 3 experiments
1. Train a 1‑layer GPT on the synthetic dataset of Section 2.1, compute v analytically, sweep α, plot Δp(z,α) for several tokens – the bump should be clear.
2. On a real LLM (e.g., GPT‑2 medium), steer a middle layer for a concrete concept like “evil”. For a fixed prompt, compute Δp(α) for the top tokens at α=200; verify target tokens bump and off‑targets peak earlier (Figure 6).
3. Compute ΔCE(α) on a large held‑out corpus (e.g., FineWeb) across α ∈ [−50,50]. Fit a quadratic near zero; confirm positive coefficient and plateaus for large α (Figure 7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do the predicted effects of steering strength (non‑monotonic token probability shifts, sigmoidal concept prevalence, quadratic local cross‑entropy) change when input contexts contain tokens from multiple concepts, i.e., mixed‑concept contexts?
- **Basis:** The authors state in the conclusion that “narrowing the theory/practice gap (e.g., mixed‑concept contexts)” is an important direction for future work.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical analysis assumes each distinct training context belongs entirely to a single concept, which simplifies the sign‑separation property of the log‑odds M(z). Mixed‑concept contexts could violate this assumption and potentially alter the sign pattern, leading to different behavior of the steered model.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A formal extension of the Unconstrained Features Model to accommodate mixed‑concept contexts and derive new closed‑form expressions; or systematic empirical validation on varied prompts to establish whether the observed curves generalize or require correction.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Do the core theoretical findings carry over to alternative steering methods such as using sparse autoencoders (SAE) or other steering vector constructions beyond the difference of means?
- **Basis:** The authors list “extending the analysis to other steering methods (e.g., SAE)” as a specific item of future research.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper’s analysis is tailored to the difference‑of‑means steering vector and relies on its specific relationship with the trained model logits. Other methods (e.g., SAE‑based) introduce different geometries and may not preserve the sign‑separation of log‑odds or the neat representation of logits.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a theoretical framework for SAE‑based steering within a similar simplified model (e.g., a single‑layer linear decoder with sparse features) and prove analogous theorems; or conduct extensive experiments comparing SAE‑based steering on modern LLMs against the predicted patterns.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can one derive a principled, possibly analytic, rule for selecting the steering strength α that automatically balances efficacy (target concept expression) against distortion (cross‑entropy increase), thereby formalizing the observed “steering sweet spot”?
- **Basis:** The authors call for “developing principled prompt‑adaptive rules for choosing α by characterizing the steering ‘sweet spot’ suggested by our results.”
- **Why unresolved:** Although the paper identifies a sweet spot region where target concept probability rises while off‑target tokens have not yet collapsed, it does not provide a method to pick α optimally across different models, layers, or prompts. A general rule is missing.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis that links the derivatives of the relevant curves (e.g., where the margin M(z) crosses the mean log‑odds) to the sweet spot boundaries; or a validated algorithm that estimates the sweet spot from limited data and consistently outperforms manual or grid search.

## Limitations
- UFM ignores LayerNorm, residual connections, and nonlinearities; this may distort large‑α behavior.
- Assumptions of perfect training and clean concept separation rarely hold fully in real models.
- Empirical validation used a limited set of concepts and fixed prompts; exact reproduction details are not fully provided.

## Confidence
- Token probability bump: Medium
- Concept tanh curve: Medium
- Quadratic CE rise: High

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation study:** Compare a synthetic transformer (with/without LayerNorm/residual) to UFM; measure deviations in bump shape and large‑α flattening.
2. **Concept separation test:** For many concepts, compute M(z) histograms; quantify how often sign separation holds and correlate with tanh curve fidelity.
3. **Second‑derivative validation:** Numerically estimate ΔCE''(0) across models and compare to predicted Var[M(Z)]; test scaling with model size.
