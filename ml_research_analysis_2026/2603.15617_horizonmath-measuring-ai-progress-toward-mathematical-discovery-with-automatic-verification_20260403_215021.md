# HorizonMath: Measuring AI Progress Toward Mathematical Discovery with Automatic Verification
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15617
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15617
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Frontier models solve <10% of 101 unsolved research problems; only GPT‑5.4 Pro produces two solutions that beat known results (Thin‑Triangle Kakeya area reduced by 4.93%, diagonal Ramsey constant lowered from 3.7992 to 3.6961).

## Executive Summary
HorizonMath introduces a benchmark of 101 unsolved mathematical problems designed to evaluate AI systems on genuine discovery. The benchmark exploits the generator–verifier gap: finding solutions is mathematically hard, but verifying a candidate solution is automatic via high‑precision numeric comparison, improvement over published baselines, or deterministic construction checks. Testing GPT‑5.4 Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Claude Opus 4.6 reveals a stark capability gap. GPT‑5.4 Pro solves 7 problems (7% solve rate), including two problems previously unsolved in the literature, while the other models solve only 3 (3%). These two improvements constitute potential novel mathematical discoveries pending independent expert verification.

## Method Summary
The framework curates problems from unsolved research literature to avoid data contamination. Each problem requires an explicit output (e.g., a symbolic expression, a construction) that is objectively verifiable by a fast, deterministic procedure. Proposed solutions are filtered by an LLM‑based compliance checker to forbid numerical hacks (e.g., hard‑coded constants, iterative root‑finding). Admissible solutions are executed in a sandbox and evaluated by one of three verifiers: (1) high‑precision numeric comparison against a pre‑computed reference (for closed‑form constants), (2) direct computation of an objective metric to check improvement over a baseline (for optimization), or (3) validation of structural properties (for existence constructions). The pipeline is open‑source to support community contributions and replication.

## Key Results
- GPT‑5.4 Pro solves 7 of 101 problems (5 calibration + 2 unsolved); Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.6 solve 3 each (all calibration).
- For the Thin‑Triangle Kakeya problem, GPT‑5.4 Pro finds 128 intercepts yielding union area 0.10915, improving the prior best 0.11481 by 4.93%.
- For the diagonal Ramsey upper bound constant, GPT‑5.4 Pro produces a quintic polynomial reducing the constant from 3.7992 to 3.6961.
- All reported successes passed automated checks but are labeled "pending expert review" because numeric verification is not a formal proof.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Automated verification using high‑precision numeric comparison and deterministic construction checks provides scalable, objective evaluation for unsolved problems without human intervention.
- **Mechanism:** For closed‑form constant discovery, the model outputs a symbolic expression; the framework evaluates it with `mpmath` at 100+ decimal places and compares to a pre‑computed reference. For optimization tasks, it executes the candidate construction and computes the objective metric to compare with the best‑known bound. For existence constructions, deterministic validators check all required structural properties. These routines are computationally efficient (low‑order polynomial time) compared to the difficulty of discovering a solution.
- **Core assumption:** The generator–verifier gap holds: candidate solutions are hard to generate but easy to verify automatically.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the benchmark targets problems where "discovery is hard, requiring meaningful mathematical insight, but verification is computationally efficient and simple." Section 3.1 specifies that each problem must have an answer "objectively verifiable by a simple deterministic computational procedure."
- **Break condition:** If verification for a problem class requires expensive computations (e.g., high‑dimensional integrals, NP‑hard checks), the automated pipeline becomes a bottleneck or loses objectivity.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Problem selection from unsolved research literature ensures immunity to data contamination, so any correct solution implies genuine reasoning and potential novel mathematical discovery.
- **Mechanism:** The curation process uses AI‑driven search and manual review to identify problems that are open and have known baselines or numerical references. Since the exact solution is unknown, it cannot appear in model training data, preventing memorization. Calibration problems (solvability 0) are included to gauge baseline capabilities without contamination risk.
- **Core assumption:** The unsolved status of the chosen problems is accurate and stable; model training corpora do not contain their exact solutions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "Because the solutions are unknown, they do not exist in any training corpus, and any correct solution produced by a model would therefore signal genuine reasoning ability and autonomous discovery." Section 3.2 describes the AI‑driven search and manual verification to ensure problems are open.
- **Break condition:** If a problem becomes solved and its solution enters widely used training data (e.g., appears in a published paper), the contamination immunity degrades and the problem loses its benchmark value.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** An LLM‑based compliance checker enforces admissibility criteria, filtering out solutions that rely on forbidden computational tricks and thereby preserving the integrity of the generator‑verifier gap.
- **Mechanism:** Before evaluation, the proposed Python code is parsed and screened by an LLM that detects prohibited operations (infinite series, numerical integration, root‑finding, hard‑coded constants, etc.). Only solutions built from allowed operations (rationals, elementary/special functions, explicit discrete constructions) proceed to the sandbox. This prevents models from using brute‑force numeric methods to match reference values.
- **Core assumption:** The compliance checker reliably identifies disallowed operations with high precision and low false rejection rates.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.1 describes the compliance checker that "inspects the proposed solution for forbidden operations such as numerical root‑finding, quadrature, and hard‑coded constants." Section 3.4 details the allowed operation set.
- **Break condition:** If the compliance checker becomes too permissive, models could exploit loopholes (e.g., very high‑precision numeric tricks). If too strict, it might reject innovative but valid approaches that stretch the allowed operation set.

## Foundational Learning
- **Generator–verifier gap:** Problems must be hard to solve but easy to verify automatically. *Why needed:* It is the foundational principle enabling automatic evaluation of unsolved problems; understanding this gap explains why HorizonMath can scale without human experts. *Quick check:* What properties make a problem suitable for automatic verification in HorizonMath? (Answer: The problem must have a concrete answer that is easy to check computationally, while finding that answer is mathematically hard.)
- **High‑precision numerical evaluation with `mpmath`:** Many benchmark problems compare symbolic expressions to high‑precision reference values. *Why needed:* Accurate arbitrary‑precision arithmetic is crucial to avoid false positives/negatives due to rounding errors. *Quick check:* Why is matching 20 decimal digits considered strong but not absolute evidence of a correct closed form? (Answer: A numerically close but incorrect expression could coincidentally match up to the displayed precision; a formal proof is still required for certainty.)
- **Admissibility and compliance checking:** Solutions must follow a restricted format (e.g., finite symbolic combinations) to avoid brute‑force numeric search. *Why needed:* To ensure solutions follow the intended symbolic or constructive format rather than relying on brute‑force numeric search, maintaining the benchmark’s focus on reasoning rather than computation. *Quick check:* List two forbidden operations in proposed solutions and explain why they are disallowed. (Example: infinite series and numerical integration are forbidden because they circumvent the need for a true closed form and could be used to approximate the reference value.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Problem definitions (registry) -> Model interface (API call) -> Compliance checker (LLM‑based filter) -> Sandbox execution (isolated Python) -> Evaluation engine (router: numeric comparator / benchmark scorer / construction validator) -> Result aggregator (statistics). Curation pipeline runs in parallel to update the problem set.
- **Critical path:** 1. Obtain model output (`proposed_solution`). 2. Run compliance check; reject if violations. 3. Execute code in sandbox. 4. Apply verification routine based on problem’s evaluation mode. 5. Record result and score.
- **Design tradeoffs:** LLM‑based compliance vs. hand‑coded rules (LLM offers semantic flexibility but is brittle and costly); broad special‑function allowance vs. minimal core (accommodates diverse math but risks obscure functions); numeric verification vs. formal proof (numeric matching is a strong heuristic pending expert review); open‑source release vs. controlled access (openness invites contributions but may lead to low‑quality additions).
- **Failure signatures:** Compliance checker passes a solution that uses hidden iterative solvers or pre‑computed tables (semantic bypass); reference values computed with insufficient precision cause false failures; sandbox lacks required libraries or has security weaknesses; models time out or produce malformed code; validator for optimization problems does not exactly match the published baseline metric.
- **First 3 experiments:** 1. **Calibration run:** Evaluate GPT‑5.4 Pro on all solvability 0 problems to verify pipeline accepts known solutions and establishes a human baseline. 2. **Adversarial compliance test:** Submit a solution that hard‑codes the reference constant for a closed‑form problem; confirm the compliance checker rejects it. 3. **New‑problem contribution:** Add a simple new problem (e.g., “Compute the exact value of ζ(3)”) with a valid validator, push to the open‑source repo, and run an end‑to‑end evaluation with a baseline model.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How reliable is the LLM‑based compliance checker at filtering inadmissible solutions? The paper acknowledges it is imperfect and may accept subtle loopholes or reject valid ones, but provides no quantitative error analysis.
- **Open Question 2:** Do the GPT‑5.4 Pro solutions for the Thin‑Triangle Kakeya and Ramsey constant problems indeed constitute mathematically valid improvements? They are "pending expert review"; automated checks are not formal proofs.
- **Open Question 3:** Can AI systems effectively solve mathematical problems that require formal proof verification? The benchmark currently excludes such problems; extending it to include proof‑based verification with systems like Lean is a key future direction.
- **Open Question 4:** How often do solutions that pass high‑precision numeric comparison turn out to be incorrect exact conjectures? The paper notes that matching 20 digits does not formally prove correctness; the false positive rate among numeric matches is unknown.

## Limitations
- Automated verification (e.g., 20‑digit numeric match) is a heuristic, not a formal proof; coincidental numeric matches could pass.
- Contamination immunity relies on problems staying unsolved and out of training data; future publication of solutions would retroactively invalidate the benchmark’s assumption.
- The LLM‑based compliance checker is a novel, under‑validated component; its effectiveness against semantic bypasses and false rejections is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Generator‑verifier gap design & implementation:** High. The framework is clearly specified, uses standard tools (`mpmath`), and is validated on calibration problems.
- **Contamination‑free benchmark:** Medium. Curation seems rigorous, but the claim about training data is intrinsically unobservable and susceptible to future events.
- **Novel mathematical discovery claims (Kakeya & Ramsey improvements):** Low to Medium. The numeric improvements are specific and plausible, but they are provisional discoveries pending independent expert human verification. Confidence is in the pipeline’s correctness, not the absolute correctness or significance of the solutions.
