# Unveiling Hidden Convexity in Deep Learning: a Sparse Signal Processing Perspective

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.23831
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23831
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: For two-layer ReLU networks with weight decay, training is equivalent to a convex group Lasso problem over hyperplane arrangement patterns, enabling global optimality and polynomial-time complexity for fixed input dimension; extends to deeper parallel networks with zero duality gap.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the fundamental challenge of non-convex optimization in training ReLU neural networks by introducing convex reformulations that recast learning as a convex optimization problem. Drawing on sparse signal processing models like group Lasso, the authors show that for a two-layer ReLU network with weight decay (L2 regularization), the training objective is exactly equivalent to a convex group Lasso problem defined over hyperplane arrangement patterns. This equivalence guarantees global optimality and implies polynomial-time solvability when the input dimension is fixed. The approach extends to deeper parallel networks—multiple two-layer subnetworks whose outputs are summed—where a zero duality gap is achieved. Geometric interpretations via zonotopes and geometric algebra provide further insights into network structure. Empirical results on autoregressive tasks (stock forecasting, ECG prediction) demonstrate that convex training achieves lower training loss and higher test accuracy than stochastic gradient descent (SGD), with consistent performance across initialiations, offering practical benefits for signal processing applications.

## Method Summary
The core method transforms the non-convex training of a two-layer ReLU network with L2 regularization into a convex group Lasso problem. This is accomplished by analyzing the network’s piecewise linear behavior through hyperplane arrangements induced by ReLU activations. The weight decay term induces sparsity in the dual variables, yielding a group Lasso penalty over coefficients associated with each activation pattern. For deeper parallel networks—where independent two-layer subnetworks process the input and their outputs are summed—the formulation extends seamlessly with a zero duality gap. The resulting convex problems are solved via proximal algorithms tailored to the group Lasso structure. Geometrically, the network’s representational capacity is interpreted using zonotopes (Minkowski sums of line segments) and geometric algebra, which elucidate the relationship between weights and activation regions.

## Key Results
- Convex equivalence: Two-layer ReLU networks with weight decay train via an exact convex group Lasso reformulation.
- Polynomial complexity: For fixed input dimension, the convex problem is solvable in polynomial time despite an exponential number of hyperplane patterns.
- Zero duality gap: Deeper parallel ReLU networks admit a convex formulation with no duality gap.
- Empirical benefits: On autoregressive tasks, convex training outperforms SGD in training loss and test accuracy, with robustness to initialization.
- Geometric insights: Zonotope and geometric algebra interpretations provide alternative perspectives on network structure and activation patterns.

## Why This Works
The convexification is enabled by weight decay (L2 regularization), which induces a sparsity structure in the dual problem that precisely matches a group Lasso penalty over hyperplane arrangement patterns. ReLU networks are piecewise linear; their input space is partitioned into regions by hyperplanes defined by the weights. Each region corresponds to a constant activation pattern and a linear subnetwork. By conceptually enumerating these patterns, the training objective becomes a sum of squared errors over linear models, one per pattern, with a group Lasso penalty on the coefficients for each pattern. This penalty arises naturally from the weight decay term via Lagrange duality, yielding a convex problem that exactly recovers the original non-convex optimum under the specified conditions. For parallel deeper networks, the summation of independent subnetworks preserves convexity, resulting in zero duality gap.

   *Why needed:* Transforms intractable non-convex training into a globally solvable convex problem.  
   *Quick check:* Verify the reformulated objective is convex (e.g., Hessian positive semidefinite) and that strong duality holds (no duality gap).

2. **Group Lasso**  
   *Why needed:* Provides sparsity over groups of coefficients (hyperplane patterns), mirroring weight decay’s effect.  
   *Quick check:* Confirm penalty is a sum of ℓ2 norms over groups; inspect dual variables for group-wise sparsity patterns.

   *Why needed:* Captures the piecewise linear nature of ReLU networks by partitioning input space.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input data -> Two-layer ReLU network with weight decay -> Hyperplane arrangement enumeration (conceptual) -> Convex group Lasso formulation -> Proximal algorithm (e.g., ADMM) -> Global optimal weights.

Critical path: The duality transformation that maps the non-convex network training to a convex problem over pattern-specific coefficients, relying on weight decay to produce a group Lasso structure.

Design tradeoffs:
- Enumerating all hyperplane patterns yields exponential dependence on input dimension d; practical implementations use approximations or fixed-d regimes.
- Omitting biases simplifies convexification but may reduce model expressiveness.
- Parallel deep networks achieve zero duality gap but require subnetworks to be independent and summed, which is not standard.
- Convex solvers may have high computational cost despite polynomial guarantees; tradeoff between exactness and scalability.

Failure signatures:
- Non-zero duality gap for standard (non-parallel) deep networks, indicating suboptimality of convex relaxation.
- Memory explosion when input dimension d grows due to pattern enumeration.
- Performance degradation if biases are included without further modifications.
- Wall-clock time significantly higher than SGD on large datasets, despite better objective values.

2. Scalability stress test: Measure runtime and memory of the convex solver as the number of hidden units increases (with fixed input dimension), verifying polynomial scaling.
3. Test extension to deeper parallel networks: train a three-layer parallel architecture (two two-layer subnetworks summed) and confirm zero duality gap and improved performance over non-parallel deeper networks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Can exact convex formulations be derived for standard (non-parallel) deep ReLU networks and networks with non-homogeneous activations, achieving zero duality gap?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that parallel deep networks achieve zero duality gap, but standard networks do not, and extending to modern activations is noted as future work in the conclusion.
- Why unresolved: The convex duality approach for standard networks yields a non-zero duality gap, preventing exact equivalence to the non-convex training problem.
- What evidence would resolve it: Derivation of a convex program with strong duality for standard architectures or proof of inherent limitations under certain conditions.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can the exponential dependence on input dimension d in the number of hyperplane arrangements be mitigated for convex neural network training in high-dimensional settings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The computational complexity of the convex formulation is proportional to (n choose d)^d, which is exponential in d, as discussed in the computational complexity section.
- Why unresolved: Enumerating all hyperplane patterns becomes intractable for high d, and while randomized sampling reduces complexity, it may not preserve exact equivalence.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of approximation algorithms with provable bounds or alternative convex representations that avoid explicit pattern enumeration.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can specialized convex optimization solvers for neural network training achieve wall-clock time efficiency comparable to or better than stochastic gradient descent on large-scale datasets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Simulations show convex training achieves better objective values but rely on custom proximal algorithms; general convex solvers like MOSEK are slower, and scalability to large datasets is not fully established.
- Why unresolved: The convex formulations involve large-scale group Lasso problems with many features (hyperplane patterns), and optimized solvers for such structures are underexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementation and benchmarking of tailored convex solvers (e.g., stochastic proximal methods) on standard large-scale datasets, demonstrating competitive or superior runtime performance.

## Limitations
- Convex reformulation size explodes with many hidden units; practical scalability unknown despite polynomial-time claim for fixed d.
- Biases are likely omitted; their inclusion could break the convex equivalence.
- Extension to deeper parallel networks is highly restrictive and not applicable to standard deep architectures.
- Empirical evaluation limited to autoregressive tasks; no image or audio benchmarks.
- No wall-clock time comparison with SGD; convex solvers may be substantially slower.
- Experimental protocol lacks details on hyperparameter tuning, random seeds, and statistical tests.
