# How LLMs Cite and Why It Matters: A Cross-Model Audit of Reference Fabrication in AI-Assisted Academic Writing and Methods to Detect Phantom Citations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03299
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03299
- **Reference Count:** 26
- **Primary Result:** LLMs hallucinate citations at rates between 11.4% and 56.8%, with prompt design significantly influencing fabrication; effective detection is possible via multi-model consensus, within-prompt repetition, and a lightweight bibliographic feature classifier.

## Executive Summary
This study presents a large-scale audit of citation hallucination across ten commercial LLMs in four academic domains. By generating and verifying 69,557 citations against three scholarly databases, it establishes that citation fabrication is primarily prompt-induced, not an intrinsic generative tendency. Hallucination rates vary widely by model and are higher for prompts framing "recent advances" versus "seminal works." The research identifies two highly accurate filtering heuristics—multi-model consensus (≥3 agreeing models) and within-prompt repetition (≥2 replications)—and develops a lightweight classifier using only bibliographic string features (AUC 0.876) that can pre-screen citations without external database queries. The findings provide a empirical foundation for understanding and mitigating phantom citations in AI-assisted academic writing.

## Method Summary
The study employed a controlled audit across 10 LLMs, generating responses to 303 unique prompts per model (4 domains × 2 temporal framings × 3 replications, plus an unprompted control). A two-stage parsing pipeline extracted structured citation strings (authors, title, journal, year, DOI). Verification was conducted via sequential queries to CrossRef, OpenAlex, and Semantic Scholar, using a fuzzy match score (title, author, year) to label citations as `confirmed` (score ≥80), `probable` (65–79), or `hallucinated` (<65). A separate search-augmented LLM adjudicated a stratified sample to estimate pipeline precision/recall. For detection methods, the analysis evaluated the accuracy of consensus (citations repeated across ≥3 models) and repetition (same citation generated ≥2 times for the same prompt). A gradient-boosted machine (GBM) classifier was trained on 27 features derived from the citation string itself (e.g., author count, year, journal presence) and evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation and leave-one-model-out (LOMO) generalization.

## Key Results
- Hallucination rates varied significantly by model, ranging from 11.4% to 56.8% (using the conservative confirmed threshold).
- The unprompted control condition produced zero parseable citations, establishing fabrication as prompt-induced.
- Multi-model consensus (≥3 agreeing models) achieved 95.6% citation accuracy. Within-prompt repetition (≥2 replications) achieved 88.9% accuracy.
- A lightweight classifier using only bibliographic string features achieved an AUC of 0.876 (5-fold CV) and 0.834 in LOMO generalization, with key signatures including fewer authors and shorter author strings in hallucinations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Citation hallucination is primarily *prompt-induced* rather than an intrinsic generative tendency of LLMs.
- **Mechanism:** Models only generate structured, formal citation strings when explicitly prompted. The unprompted control produced zero parseable citations. Prompting triggers a mode where the model attempts to retrieve and format bibliographic information from its fragmented parametric memory, which can fail and produce plausible but non-existent citations.
- **Core assumption:** The model's training data contains incomplete or inconsistent associations for scholarly references. Forced citation synthesis from these fragments increases fabrication risk.
- **Evidence anchors:** Unprompted condition results (zero citations); parser conservatism is critical.
- **Break condition:** Observation of formal, verifiable citations in a neutral topic explanation prompt would falsify pure prompt-dependence.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Real citations exhibit *stochastic stability* across models and replications, while hallucinated citations are *stochastically variable*, enabling filtering via consensus and repetition.
- **Mechanism:** A real publication is a concrete anchor in training data, leading to a high probability of multiple independent generation events producing the exact same citation string. A hallucination is a novel string sampled from a distribution over plausible formats; its generation is high-entropy and unlikely to recur identically.
- **Core assumption:** Real references create a strong probability attractor in the model's distribution; fabrications lack this anchor.
- **Evidence anchors:** Accuracy of multi-model consensus (95.6%) and within-prompt repetition (88.9%); match rates rise sharply with citation frequency (16.5% for unique titles vs. 95.6% for ≥3 models).
- **Break condition:** Comparable verification rates for single-occurrence and multi-occurrence citations would invalidate the stability filter.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Hallucinated citations carry *detectable structural biases* in their bibliographic string features, allowing pre-screening without external database queries.
- **Mechanism:** Fabrication leads to systematic simplifications: fewer listed authors, shorter author strings, rare use of "et al.," and temporal skew (overrepresentation of recent years). The model optimizes for plausibility over accuracy, favoring simpler, more generic, or more recent-sounding metadata.
- **Core assumption:** The generative process for a hallucinated citation differs consistently from recalling a real citation, manifesting in surface-level lexical and numerical properties.
- **Evidence anchors:** Classifier AUC of 0.876 (CV) and 0.834 (LOMO); feature importance rankings (author count, author string length, year recency).
- **Break condition:** A failure of the classifier to generalize in LOMO (e.g., mean AUC near 0.5 for a held-out model) would indicate model-specific artifacts, not generalizable hallucination signatures.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: LLM Training Data Biases**
  - *Why needed here:* The observed domain effect (NLP/AI lowest hallucination) and open-access amplification directly stem from the composition and accessibility of texts in the pre-training corpus. Understanding that models reflect their training data's coverage patterns explains why hallucination rates vary by field.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might models hallucinate less on topics well-covered by arXiv preprints compared to subscription-engineering journals?

- **Concept: Prompt Engineering & Frame Effects**
  - *Why needed here:* The "recent" vs. "seminal" framing experiment isolates how prompt wording modulates hallucination. This demonstrates that output is not just a function of model parameters but of the specific query formulation.
  - *Quick check question:* How does asking for "recent advances" increase hallucination risk compared to asking for "seminal works," based on training data recency?

- **Concept: Statistical Validation & Thresholding**
  - *Why needed here:* The verification pipeline (confidence scores, confirmed/probable/hallucinated thresholds) and classifier evaluation (AUC, LOMO) rely on understanding trade-offs in precision/recall and the meaning of operational thresholds in empirical measurement.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the difference in interpretation between the "confirmed" (score ≥80) and "probable" (65-79) hallucination rate estimates, and why might the confirmed rate be a conservative upper bound?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Prompt Engine -> Response Parser -> Verification Resolver -> (Optional) Classifier Pre-Screen -> Labeled Dataset
  1. Prompt Engine: Generates 303 unique prompts per model (domain × framing × replication × control). Input: topic lists. Output: API calls.
  2. Response Parser: Two-stage regex/normalization pipeline. Input: raw LLM text. Output: structured citations (authors, title, journal, year, DOI).
  3. Verification Resolver: Sequential API queries to CrossRef → OpenAlex → Semantic Scholar. Computes fuzzy match score (title/author/year). Output: `confirmed`/`probable`/`hallucinated` label + metadata.
  4. Validation Subsystem: Uses a separate search-augmented LLM (GPT-4.1-mini) to adjudicate a stratified sample. Output: precision/recall estimates for the resolver.
  5. Feature Extractor & Classifier: Computes 27 string-level features from the citation. Trains/tests GBM model. Output: hallucination probability score.
- **Critical path:** Prompt Generation → Model API Call → Parsing → Verification Resolver → (Optional) Classifier Pre-Screen → Labeled Dataset. The unprompted control is a branch that never reaches the Resolver.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Verification threshold (80 vs. 65): Higher threshold (80) gives higher precision but increases false negatives (conservative hallucination estimate). Lower threshold (65) increases recall but admits more probable real citations as "real."
  - Filter selection: Multi-model consensus (95.6% acc) costs ~3x API calls and requires multi-account coordination. Repetition filtering (88.9% acc) costs 3x calls but is single-model. Classifier pre-screen (AUC 0.876) costs virtually nothing but is probabilistic.
  - Coverage vs. Cost: The resolver's 3-source matching maximizes recall but adds latency (~1.2s/citation). A single-source check would be faster but miss many valid citations.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Parser overloading on unprompted: If the parser begins extracting citations from the control condition, it violates the core finding of prompt-dependence. The parser for controls must be exceptionally conservative.
  - Resolver domain failure: A sudden drop in match rate for a specific domain (e.g., structural engineering) could indicate poor coverage in one of the three scholarly databases for that field's venues.
  - Classifier concept drift: A large performance drop in LOMO for a held-out model (like `kimi-k2.5` with AUC 0.672) signals that model's hallucination patterns are qualitatively different, requiring model-specific tuning.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Unprompted Control Replication: Run the exact unprompted condition (System Prompt: "Do NOT provide a literature review...") for a new model (e.g., `claude-3.7-sonnet`). Verify parser returns *zero* citations. If any are returned, investigate parser false positives.
  2. Framing Effect Isolation: For a single high-hallucination model (e.g., `haiku-4.5`), run only the "recent" and "seminal" prompts for one domain (e.g., NLP). Compute hallucination rate difference. Expect ~20% gap confirming the framing effect.
  3. Classifier Sanity Check: Extract the top 5 features (author count, author string length, year recency, et al. presence, author initial diversity) from 100 new hallucinated vs. real citations from the dataset. Manually verify the direction of the difference matches the paper's report (e.g., hallucinations have *lower* author count).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific mechanism caused Anthropic's Haiku-4.5 to regress in citation accuracy relative to Haiku-3.5?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 7.2 discusses the counterintuitive regression and proposes three non-exclusive explanations (safety-accuracy tradeoff, training data filtering, lack of citation-specific evaluation) but states "we cannot adjudicate between these explanations with the data available."
- **Why unresolved:** The study design compares model outputs but does not isolate training data composition, alignment objectives, or development evaluation practices. The observed regression is correlational without causal identification.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of Haiku-3.5 vs. 4.5 across (a) pre-training data sources and filtering policies, (b) alignment fine-tuning datasets and objectives, and (c) internal evaluation benchmarks. Ablation experiments where only one factor is varied would pinpoint the cause.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does the proportion of academic literature in pre-training data quantitatively affect citation hallucination rates across model families?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 7.1 hypothesizes that models with "a larger share of indexed academic literature" in training data have lower hallucination, noting the correlation with open-access overrepresentation in low-hallucinating models. However, it acknowledges "it does not establish causation."
- **Why unresolved:** The study compares models with unknown training data compositions; the observed correlation could be confounded by other factors (e.g., alignment, architecture). No model in the study had its training data composition systematically varied.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training controlled experiments where identical model architectures are trained on corpora with systematically varied percentages of scholarly text (while holding other factors constant), then measuring citation hallucination under standardized prompts. Alternatively, retrospective analysis of proprietary models with disclosed training data breakdowns, controlling for model size and alignment.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems and frontier reasoning models (e.g., Claude Opus, o3, DeepSeek-R1) reduce citation hallucination compared to unaugmented generative models?
- **Basis in paper:** The Limitations section explicitly states the study evaluated "unaugmented text-generation models" and excluded "reasoning-specialized models" and "very large frontier systems," noting that these "may rely on different internal deliberation policies... and are expected to exhibit different error modes." This creates a gap in understanding real-world deployment scenarios where RAG or advanced reasoning is common.
- **Why unresolved:** No empirical data was collected on RAG or frontier models; the conclusions are limited to mid-tier generative models. The effectiveness of proposed heuristics (consensus, repetition) on such systems is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the audit protocol with state-of-the-art RAG implementations (querying scholarly databases) and frontier models that use test-time compute or specialized reasoning. Compare hallucination rates, error types, and heuristic efficacy against the baseline generative models studied.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What are the precise boundary conditions—beyond "recent vs. seminal"—under which prompt framing induces citation hallucination?
- **Basis in paper:** The study tested only one prompt manipulation (temporal framing). Section 4.2 notes this effect but states "the field lacks... attempts to manipulate the prompting frame" beyond this. The paper does not explore other prompt dimensions (e.g., specificity, confidence language, instruction clarity).
- **Why unresolved:** The factorial design was limited to domain × framing × replication. Other potentially critical prompt features (e.g., "cite only papers you are certain exist," explicit uncertainty penalties, format constraints) were not varied. Thus, the scope of "prompt-induced" hallucination is underspecified.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic study using a full factorial or fractional factorial design across multiple prompt dimensions (temporal, specificity, confidence qualifiers, instruction strength, etc.) with multiple models. Measuring hallucination rates across this multidimensional prompt space would identify which features most reduce fabrication.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How accurate is the three-source verification pipeline across different scholarly domains and metadata quality regimes, and how does uncertainty in verification affect reported hallucination rates?
- **Basis in paper:** The Limitations section notes the pipeline is an "operational classifier" with a 10.7% false-negative recovery rate in the hallucinated stratum, and suggests "future iterations should strengthen calibration by... targeted expert adjudication." The current thresholds (score ≥80) are arbitrary and may yield conservative upper-bound estimates.
- **Why unresolved:** The automated validation used a web-search model (GPT-4.1-mini) as proxy for ground truth, not human experts. Ambiguous cases (e.g., preprints, non-indexed venues, non-English titles) likely have higher error rates that vary by domain. The 10.7% false-negative estimate is global and may not generalize.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Human expert adjudication on a stratified sample of citations across all four domains, especially near-confidence-threshold cases (scores 65–79) and domain-specific edge cases (e.g., engineering conference proceedings, non-English biomedical journals). This would yield domain-specific precision/recall for the pipeline, allowing calibrated uncertainty intervals on hallucination rates.

## Limitations
- The verification pipeline relies on automated fuzzy matching to external databases, which may have domain-specific coverage gaps and introduces estimation uncertainty (e.g., 10.7% false-negative rate in the hallucinated stratum).
- The study evaluated only unaugmented generative models; findings may not generalize to retrieval-augmented (RAG) or frontier reasoning models, which are common in real-world academic writing tools.
- The classifier's leave-one-model-out (LOMO) performance dropped significantly for one model (`kimi-k2.5`, AUC 0.672), indicating potential model-specific hallucination patterns that may limit generalization.
