# Evaluating Anomaly Detectors for Simulated Highly Imbalanced Industrial Classification Problems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.00005  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00005  
- **Reference count:** 39  
- **Primary result:** The best detector type depends on the number of faulty training examples: unsupervised (kNN/LOF) for <20, semi-supervised (XGBOD) and supervised (SVM/CatBoost) for 30–50+, with semi-supervised showing advantages only in 10D; adding more healthy samples yields insignificant gains.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates 14 anomaly detection algorithms on a synthetic industrial dataset with extreme class imbalance. The data are generated from a controlled “TvS” distribution in 2D and 10D, with training sizes ranging from 1k to 10k and anomaly rates from 0.05% to 20%. Performance is measured via AUCROC and false‐positive/false‐negative rates at a 1% FPR target. The results reveal a clear transition: when fewer than 20 anomalies are available, unsupervised density‐based methods (kNN, LOF) excel; around 30–50 anomalies, semi‐supervised (XGBOD) and supervised (SVM, CatBoost) methods achieve large improvements, particularly in higher dimensions. Additional healthy examples do not substantially boost performance. Generalization error worsens on smaller datasets, and selecting models via validation tends to overestimate test performance—AUCROC proves more stable than FNR for this purpose. These findings provide practical guidance for choosing anomaly detectors under severe label scarcity.

## Method Summary
The authors create a synthetic dataset using the “TvS” distribution: healthy points follow a Gaussian mixture forming a three‑mode cluster, while faulty points approximate a uniform hypersphere via a 200‑cluster Gaussian mixture. Two scenarios are considered: S1 (2D) and S2 (10D). Training sets of sizes 1k, 2k, 5k, and 10k are generated with a primary anomaly rate of 0.5% (expanded to 0.05%–20% for some experiments). A fixed test set of 40,960 samples with 50% anomalies is held out. Each of the 14 detectors (unsupervised, semi‑supervised, supervised) undergoes 5‑fold cross‑validation on the training data for hyperparameter selection, with AUCROC as the selection criterion. The best configuration is retrained on the full training set and applied to the test set. To compute FPR/FNR at a 1% target, an empirical threshold derived from the validation scores’ healthy distribution is used. All experiments are repeated ~100 times with different random seeds to ensure robust estimates.

## Key Results
- Unsupervised detectors (kNN, LOF) dominate when fewer than 20 faulty training examples are available; semi‑supervised (XGBOD) and supervised (SVM, CatBoost) methods outperform around 30–50 anomalies.
- Semi‑supervised methods show significant improvements over both unsupervised and supervised approaches only in higher‑dimensional (10D) settings, not in 2D.
- Generalization error increases as training size decreases, and validation‑based detector selection overestimates test performance; AUCROC is more consistent than FNR for model selection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1:** For extremely low numbers of anomalous training examples (<20), unsupervised density‑based detectors (kNN, LOF) achieve superior performance over supervised and semi‑supervised approaches.  
*Mechanism:* They model the distribution of healthy data only, which is abundant and can be reliably estimated. Supervised methods require enough positive examples to learn a decision boundary and suffer from overfitting when anomalies are scarce. Semi‑supervised methods also rely on labeled anomalies for the final classifier and are thus limited by the same sample‑size issue.  
*Core assumption:* The healthy distribution is relatively stable and can be captured without labels; anomalies are sparse and do not form dense clusters that would fool density estimators.  
*Evidence:* Abstract and Table VI/Figure 13 show unsupervised dominance below 20 anomalies.  
*Break condition:* When anomalies exceed ~30–50 or if they form dense clusters, supervised/semi‑supervised methods begin to outperform.

**Mechanism 2:** Semi‑supervised detectors (e.g., XGBOD) yield significant performance improvements over both unsupervised and fully supervised methods specifically in higher‑dimensional feature spaces (e.g., 10D) when a moderate number of labeled anomalies (30–50) are available.  
*Mechanism:* XGBOD first computes unsupervised anomaly scores from multiple base detectors, then uses a supervised gradient‑boosting model (XGBoost) to combine these scores. The ensemble exploits complementary strengths while adapting to the specific anomaly distribution using the limited labels. In higher dimensions, raw features become less discriminative, so the combined scores provide a richer representation. In 2D the base detectors already separate well, leaving little room for improvement.  
*Core assumption:* Base detectors produce diverse scores; the supervised learner has enough positives to avoid overfitting.  
*Evidence:* Abstract and Table VI/Figure 13 show XGBOD topping rankings in 10D but not in 2D.  
*Break condition:* If base detectors are highly correlated or labeled anomalies are too few, the combination may not help; also, in very low‑dimensional separable problems the added complexity could degrade performance.

**Mechanism 3:** Generalization error (the discrepancy between validation and test performance) increases as training dataset size decreases, and selecting the best detector via validation tends to overestimate test performance; AUCROC is a more stable metric for this purpose than FNR.  
*Mechanism:* Smaller datasets lead to higher model variance, so validation scores fluctuate more. Picking the best model based on validation introduces a “winner’s curse” – the chosen model appears better by chance, and this optimism carries over to the test set. FNR, which depends on the few anomalies, is particularly noisy; AUCROC aggregates across thresholds and is less sensitive to class imbalance and anomaly count.  
*Core assumption:* Validation and test sets are drawn from the same distribution; variance decreases with more data.  
*Evidence:* Abstract, Section V‑B, Figure 14, Tables VIII‑IX show larger MSE for FNR and asymmetric overestimation for FNR‑based selection.  
*Break condition:* With very large training sets, variance decreases and selection bias becomes negligible; using an independent model‑selection set can also mitigate the overestimation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Class Imbalance:** The problem deals with anomaly rates as low as 0.05%, making standard accuracy misleading. Metrics like AUCROC and control of false rates are essential.  
  *Quick check:* If a classifier predicts all samples as healthy on a dataset with 0.1% anomalies, what is its accuracy and why is that not informative about detection capability?
- **Cross‑Validation for Hyperparameter Tuning:** Used to select hyperparameters for each detector and estimate validation performance.  
  *Quick check:* In 5‑fold cross‑validation, how is the training/validation split performed and why is it beneficial compared to a single train‑test split?
- **Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and AUC:** Primary performance measure evaluates trade‑off between TPR and FPR across thresholds and is insensitive to class imbalance.  
  *Quick check:* How is AUCROC calculated from pairwise comparisons of anomaly and healthy scores, and why does a value of 0.5 indicate random guessing?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Synthetic Data Generator -> Dataset Splitting -> Detector Implementations -> Hyperparameter Selection -> Training & Inference -> Metrics Computation -> Generalization Analysis -> Monte Carlo Repetition

**Critical path (steps to reproduce core findings):**  
1. Set simulation parameters (scenario S1 or S2, total training size, desired number of anomalies).  
2. Generate training data from the TvS distribution with specified healthy/faulty counts.  
3. Perform 5‑fold CV on training data: for each split, train each detector with hyperparameter candidates, compute validation AUCROC, average over folds to select best hyperparameters.  
4. Retrain the detector on the entire training set using selected hyperparameters.  
5. Apply the trained detector to the fixed test set to obtain anomaly scores.  
6. Compute test AUCROC.  
7. Pool validation scores from all CV folds; apply the simple anomaly predictor to both pooled validation and test scores to obtain FPR/FNR at target 1% FPR.  
8. Store all metrics; repeat for many random seeds.  
9. Aggregate results (average ranks, percentile bounds, statistical tests) as in the paper’s tables/figures.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Synthetic vs. real data:* TvS distribution allows precise control but may not capture all complexities of real industrial data.  
- *Fixed target FPR = 1%:* Aligns with industrial tolerances but restricts analysis to a single operating point.  
- *Empirical CDF/ICDF for thresholding:* Standardizes binary decisions but can be noisy with very few anomalies.  
- *Pre‑defined hyperparameter grids:* Ensures fairness and limits computational cost, but might miss optimal values outside the grid.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High variance in metric values across Monte Carlo repeats, especially for small training sizes.  
- Detectors failing to converge or producing degenerate scores (e.g., all identical) due to inappropriate hyperparameters.  
- Validation AUCROC substantially higher than test AUCROC for many detectors, indicating optimistic bias from model selection.  
- Extremely wide confidence intervals for FNR, reflecting unreliable estimation given few anomalies.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Recreate Figure 13:** Fix total training size (e.g., 5000), vary the number of faulty examples via anomaly rate, and plot average maximum AUCROC per detector category (US, SS, FS) to reveal the unsupervised‑to‑supervised transition.  
2. **Recreate Table VI for scenarios S1 and S2:** Keep anomaly rate at 0.5% and vary total training size (1k, 2k, 5k, 10k). Compare detector rankings to see the impact of dimensionality (e.g., XGBOD’s rise in 10D vs. 2D).  
3. **Generalization assessment:** For each detector and training size, compute the mean absolute difference between validation and test AUCROC over 100 Monte‑Carlo repeats and plot as in Figure 14. Confirm that error decreases with larger datasets and that FNR differences are larger and more asymmetric.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** How well do the findings on detector performance transitions (unsupervised to semi‑supervised/supervised) generalize to real‑world industrial datasets with non‑hyper‑spherical anomaly distributions and potentially different feature correlations?  
- **Basis in paper:** The study uses a specific synthetic “TvS” distribution with a hyper‑spherical anomaly structure and Gaussian mixtures for healthy data. The authors acknowledge this is a “problem‑agnostic simulated dataset” but note real‑world defects can follow “distributions that are difficult to model.” The clear performance thresholds observed (e.g., ~30‑50 anomalies) are tied to this controlled distribution’s separability and structure.  
- **Why unresolved:** The controlled synthetic environment eliminates many real‑world confounders (e.g., sensor noise patterns, multi‑modal healthy states, temporal dependencies, non‑Gaussian feature relationships). The transition points could shift or vanish with different underlying data geometries.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A follow‑up study benchmarking the same set of detectors on a diverse suite of *real* industrial anomaly datasets (e.g., from Table I) with known class imbalance and verified labels. Performance should be analyzed similarly (by number of fault examples, dimensionality) to see if the same categorical transitions hold. Alternatively, generating new synthetic datasets with fundamentally different anomaly topologies (e.g., manifold‑based, clustered, local outliers) and repeating the core experiment would test the robustness of the identified thresholds.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** What is the underlying reason for the observed performance drop and overfitting tendency when selecting detectors via validation, particularly for models with higher parameter counts (e.g., deep learning, XGBOD)?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly states: “the process of selecting the detector frequently leads to a performance drop… This could possibly be due to the cross‑validation setup but the general process of applying a decision on which model to use, is itself part of the model creation process, and may contribute to over fitting.” It also notes that deep learning methods like FTTransformer and DeepSAD appear in the top validation performers but not in the top test performers.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies the phenomenon and a plausible cause (overfitting to the validation set via the model selection process), but does not disentangle the contributions. Is the drop primarily due to (a) the classic “double‑dipping” problem of using the same validation set for hyperparameter tuning *and* model selection, (b) an inherent instability/variance in the estimators for small‑sample, high‑imbalance scenarios, or (c) a fundamental mismatch between the validation data generation process and the test data that becomes acute for complex models?  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled ablation study. For example: 1) Compare the validation‑test gap when using a completely held‑out “model selection” set versus the current cross‑validation approach. 2) Analyze the variance in validation AUCROC scores across the five CV folds for different model classes; higher variance would indicate instability. 3) Apply statistical learning theory (e.g., VC dimension) to estimate the expected generalization gap for each model class given the training size and compare it to the observed gap. This would isolate whether the issue is methodological (selection procedure) or intrinsic to the model complexity.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** Does the finding that “additional healthy examples offer insignificant benefits” hold for all anomaly distribution types, or is it specific to the balanced hyperspherical anomaly distribution used?  
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state: “Our findings reveal that the best detector is highly dependant on the total number of faulty examples in the training dataset, with additional healthy examples offering insignificant benefits in most cases.” This is supported by Figure 13 and the associated text, where varying the anomaly rate (thus changing the healthy‑to‑faulty ratio) at a fixed number of fault examples showed few statistically significant changes.  
- **Why unresolved:** The tested distribution (TvS) has anomalies forming a contiguous, roughly uniform hypersphere shell. Real‑world anomalies can be rare, isolated, or clustered. If anomalies are very sparse and localized, the *density* of healthy samples covering the normal manifold might be more critical for accurately modeling the boundary. The conclusion might reverse if anomalies are extremely localized or if the healthy distribution is highly complex/multi‑modal.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Repeat the core experiment (varying healthy sample size at fixed fault counts) using alternative synthetic anomaly distributions: 1) **Local outliers**: Anomalies are generated as isolated points far from any healthy cluster. 2) **Clustered anomalies**: Anomalies form their own tight clusters separate from the healthy data. 3) **Novel patterns**: Anomalies lie in low‑density regions of the healthy manifold. Measuring the change in performance (especially FNR at fixed FPR) as healthy samples increase would reveal if the “insignificant benefit” finding is distribution‑dependent.

**Open Question 4**  
- **Question:** What is the practical, cost‑sensitive impact of the observed shift in optimal detector category (unsupervised vs. semi‑supervised vs. supervised) as a function of available fault examples, when operational costs for false positives and false negatives are highly asymmetric?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper evaluates detectors primarily via AUCROC and FNR/FPR at a fixed 1% FPR target, based on the premise that “setting the target error rate based on FPR is much more reliable” due to few fault examples. Section I‑A3 discusses how industrial applications prioritize FPR or FNR based on downstream costs (waste vs. safety). However, the main analysis ranks detectors by maximum AUCROC, not by a utility function that weights the two error types.  
- **Why unresolved:** The “best” detector for minimizing AUCROC error may not be the best for minimizing *expected monetary loss* or *risk* if the cost of a false negative (e.g., a defective product causing a recall) is 1000× the cost of a false positive (scrapping a good product). The transition from unsupervised to supervised methods might occur at a different number of fault examples if the cost matrix is skewed. The paper’s guidance (“use unsupervised when fault data is very scarce”) is based on overall discrimination power, not cost‑sensitive optimization.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re‑analyze all simulation results using a cost‑weighted metric (e.g., Total Cost = C_FP × FP + C_FN × FN) for a range of plausible cost ratios. Plot the “optimal” detector (minimizing expected cost) as a function of (number of fault examples, cost ratio). This would provide actionable guidance that directly incorporates the application‑specific risk preferences mentioned in the introduction. It could also reveal that for very high C_FN/C_FP ratios, a conservative unsupervised method might be preferred even with 50+ fault examples, contrary to the AUCROC‑based ranking.

**Open Question 5**  
- **Question:** How does the “curse of dimensionality” specifically affect the comparative advantage of semi‑supervised methods over supervised methods, as observed in the 10D vs. 2D results?  
- **Basis in paper:** A key finding is: “While semi‑supervised methods do not show significant benefits with only two features, the improvements are evident at ten features.” The authors suggest this is related to the “larger feature spaces” where semi‑supervised methods “tend to perform better than the supervised methods.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not empirically dissect *why* semi‑supervised methods gain an advantage. Possibilities include: 1) **Regularization:** The unsupervised component (e.g., in XGBOD using kNN/LOF outputs) provides a form of representation learning or manifold regularization that is more beneficial in higher dimensions where supervised models overfit more easily. 2) **Signal‑to‑Noise:** In higher dimensions, the raw features may be noisier; the semi‑supervised pre‑processing might act as a filter. 3) **Hypersphere Geometry:** The specific anomaly distribution (hypersphere) might interact with dimensionality in a way that unsupervised density estimators capture structure that linear/kernel‑based supervised methods miss. The paper does not test which mechanism dominates.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct an ablation where the semi‑supervised methods (e.g., XGBOD) are replaced with a supervised model trained on the *same* unsupervised feature representations (e.g., the distance‑to‑kNN neighbors or LOF scores used as input features). If performance remains high, it supports the “representation learning” hypothesis. Additionally, measuring the effective dimensionality or intrinsic dimension of the healthy/anomaly manifolds for the 2D and 10D cases, and correlating that with the performance gap, could test if it’s a general dimensionality effect or specific to the distribution’s geometry.

## Limitations
- **Data generation specifics:** The exact construction of the faulty hypersphere (200‑cluster Gaussian mixture) is not fully detailed; reliance on reference [8] or reverse engineering introduces reproducibility risk.  
- **Library and seed details:** Versions, random seeds, and handling of non‑deterministic algorithms are not specified, potentially causing minor performance deviations.  
- **Synthetic domain gap:** The clean TvS distribution may not reflect real‑world industrial noise and concept drift, limiting external validity.

## Confidence
- **Detector transition with anomaly count:** High  
- **Semi‑supervised benefit only in higher dimensions:** Medium  
- **Generalization error increases with smaller datasets; AUCROC more stable than FNR:** High
