# To Words and Beyond: Probing Large Language Models for Sentence-Level Psycholinguistic Norms of Memorability and Reading Times

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12105
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12105
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on small datasets enables LLMs to predict sentence-level psycholinguistic norms (memorability, reading times) with strong correlations (up to R² = 0.59), far surpassing zero-shot prompting and baseline predictors.

## Executive Summary
This study examines whether large language models (LLMs) can capture human-like sentence-level psycholinguistic norms—specifically memorability and reading times—which require integrating multi-word context. The authors compare zero-shot/few-shot prompting to supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on hundreds of examples across three LLMs (GPT-4o-mini, Gemma-3-27b, Qwen3-32b). Model predictions are evaluated against four human behavioral datasets: word memorability (2,109 words), sentence memorability (2,500 sentences), self-paced reading times (Natural Stories), and eye-tracking reading times (OneStop). Results show SFT yields substantial improvements over interpretable baselines (e.g., word frequency, length) and static embeddings, while zero-shot performance remains inconsistent. The findings indicate that LLMs contain relevant information for these tasks, but explicit alignment via SFT is necessary to unlock it.

## Method Summary
The study tests three LLMs on predicting four psycholinguistic norms. For each norm, the models are evaluated in zero-shot/few-shot prompting mode (no weight updates) and after supervised fine-tuning on small labeled datasets (hundreds of examples). Predictions are compared to human ratings using R², with baselines including simple linear models on linguistic features (e.g., word frequency, length) and pretrained word/sentence embeddings. The tasks cover both word- and sentence-level memorability, as well as reading times from self-paced and eye-tracking corpora, all in English.

## Key Results
- SFT strongly outperforms baselines: up to R² = 0.59 for word memorability (baseline 0.28), 0.49 for sentence memorability (baseline 0.32), 0.21 for self-paced reading (baseline 0.08), and 0.57 for eye-tracking (GPT-4o fine-tuned).
- Zero-shot/few-shot prompting shows mixed utility: modest correlations for eye-tracking (R² 0.27–0.35) but negligible for memorability tasks.
- Fine-tuned models surpass both interpretable baselines and static embeddings across all four tasks, demonstrating the value of task-specific adaptation even with limited training data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success of SFT suggests that LLMs can be aligned to human psycholinguistic norms through direct regression on small labeled datasets. However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear: it is uncertain whether models learn to encode latent cognitive variables or simply capture surface-level statistical patterns. The poor zero-shot performance indicates that such norms are not readily accessible in the pre-trained knowledge of LLMs without explicit tuning, implying that the pre-training objective may not naturally develop these specific predictive abilities.

## Foundational Learning
- **Psycholinguistic Norms**: Behavioral measures (e.g., memorability, reading times) quantifying human language processing. *Why needed*: Provide ground truth for evaluating whether AI models simulate human cognition. *Quick check*: Are these collected from controlled experiments? Yes.
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: Adapting a pre-trained model to a specific task using labeled data. *Why needed*: To directly optimize the model for predicting continuous human ratings. *Quick check*: Does it involve gradient updates? Yes.
- **Zero/Few-Shot Prompting**: Eliciting model outputs via textual prompts without weight changes. *Why needed*: To test inherent model knowledge of psycholinguistic variables. *Quick check*: Can the model generate numeric predictions from a prompt? Yes.
- **Regression Evaluation**: Measuring alignment between predictions and human norms using metrics like R². *Why needed*: Quantifies how well the model replicates human behavior. *Quick check*: Is R² bounded between 0 and 1? Yes.
- **Baseline Predictors**: Simple models (e.g., linear regression on word frequency, length) used as reference. *Why needed*: To determine if LLMs add predictive power beyond trivial features. *Quick check*: Do they use hand-crafted features? Yes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input sentence -> Tokenizer -> Token IDs -> Embedding Lookup -> Positional Encoding -> Transformer Blocks (multiple layers of Multi-Head Attention + Feed-Forward Network) -> Final Hidden State -> (SFT) Linear Regression Head -> Predicted Norm Value / (Zero-shot) Generate text in response to prompt -> Extract number.

**Critical path**:  
- **SFT pipeline**: Tokenization → embedding → transformer encoding (extract representation, e.g., last token) → regression head → continuous prediction.  
- **Zero-shot pipeline**: Prompt construction (e.g., “Memorability of ‘...’:”) → model generation → parse numeric output.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Model size*: Larger models (e.g., 32B) have greater capacity but higher compute cost; smaller models are efficient but may underfit.  
- *SFT vs prompting*: SFT yields better alignment but requires labeled data; zero-shot is flexible but unreliable.  
- *Context length*: Limits maximum sentence length; long sentences may be truncated, losing information.

**Failure signatures**:  
- *Overfitting*: SFT performance drops sharply on out-of-distribution sentences or genres.  
- *Underfitting*: Zero-shot prompts produce incoherent or non-numeric outputs.  
- *Bias*: Predictions reflect training data biases (e.g., memorability of frequent words) rather than genuine cognitive cues.

**First experiments**:  
1. Reproduce SFT on word memorability using GPT-4o-mini, confirming R² > 0.28 baseline.  
2. Compare zero-shot vs SFT performance on sentence memorability across all three models, verifying SFT superiority.  
3. Test generalization by evaluating a fine-tuned model on a held-out genre (e.g., using a subset of OneStop not seen during training) to measure performance drop.

## Limitations
- **Task framing uncertainty**: It is unclear whether regression-based prediction of norms truly simulates human cognition or exploits superficial correlations.
- **Generalizability**: Evaluations are restricted to English and specific corpora (Natural Stories, OneStop); transfer to other languages or norm types is unknown.
- **Methodological transparency**: The absence of pre-registration and limited details on SFT hyperparameters/prompt design raises reproducibility concerns.

## Confidence
- **Claim**: Supervised fine-tuning yields significantly better alignment with human psycholinguistic norms than zero-shot prompting. **Confidence**: High (consistent large-effect results across tasks and models).  
- **Claim**: Zero-shot/few-shot prompting is generally unreliable for predicting memorability and reading times. **Confidence**: Medium (mixed results; eye-tracking shows some promise, but memorability does not).  
- **Claim**: Large language models inherently encode useful latent information about sentence-level psycholinguistic features. **Confidence**: Low (inferred from SFT success; without probing internal representations, this remains speculative).

## Next Checks
1. **Expand model spectrum & control for training data**: Replicate SFT results with additional model families (e.g., Llama, Mistral, Claude) and test on entirely held-out norm sets from different corpora to ensure gains are not due to memorization of the small fine-tuning sets.
2. **Probe internal representations**: Use diagnostic classifiers or representational similarity analysis (RSA) on penultimate layer embeddings of fine-tuned models to directly test whether they encode human-rated norm values independently of the regression head, comparing these internal correlations to final output correlations.
3. **Cross-linguistic replication**: Conduct identical SFT pipelines on non-English psycholinguistic norm datasets (e.g., German, Mandarin) to determine if alignment is language-specific; include evaluation on multilingual models (e.g., mBERT, XLM-R) to control for training data effects.
