# Draft-Conditioned Constrained Decoding for Structured Generation in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.03305  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03305  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Draft-Conditioned Constrained Decoding (DCCD) improves strict structured accuracy by up to 24.0 percentage points across multiple benchmarks, enables compositions of smaller models (e.g., 1.5B+1.5B) to outperform a single 14B model, and scales effectively with additional test‑time compute.

## Executive Summary
Structured generation that requires exact machine‑parseable formats (JSON, grammars) is essential for LLM deployment but suffers from the “projection tax” during constrained decoding, which repeatedly renormalizes low‑probability valid tokens and distorts the distribution. This paper introduces Draft‑Conditioned Constrained Decoding (DCCD), a training‑free two‑step inference method: first generate an unconstrained draft that captures the semantic plan, then perform constrained decoding conditioned on this draft. Conditioning increases the feasible mass (total probability on valid tokens) before projection, thereby reducing distortion while preserving constraint guarantees. Evaluations on GSM8K, MATH500, GSM‑Symbolic, and FOLIO across model scales (1B–14B) show DCCD improves strict structured accuracy by up to 24.0 percentage points, yields better accuracy per parameter (e.g., a 1.5B+1.5B pair beats a 14B single model), and scales more effectively with additional test‑time compute (best‑of‑K drafts).

## Method Summary
DCCD operates in two stages. **Stage 1 (Draft generation):** sample K unconstrained drafts y^(k) from the model given the prompt x, without applying any structural constraints. These drafts are free to explore semantic reasoning, such as chain‑of‑thought. **Stage 2 (Draft‑conditioned constrained decoding):** for each draft, run standard token‑by‑token constrained decoding (masking invalid tokens and renormalizing) but with the model conditioned on the concatenation of the prompt and the draft (x, y^(k)). At each step, compute the feasible mass – the sum of probabilities over the set of valid next‑tokens – and accumulate its logarithm as a score S^(k). After generating all K candidate outputs, select the draft with the highest S^(k) (or the first if K=1). The approach is training‑free, works with any constraint oracle (e.g., JSON schema, grammar), and can use the same model for both stages or a smaller “projector” model. The cumulative log feasible mass serves as a cheap proxy for the KL distortion incurred during constrained decoding, guiding test‑time compute toward promising reasoning traces.

## Key Results
- DCCD raises strict structured accuracy substantially: e.g., on GSM8K with a 1B model, accuracy jumps from 15.2% to 39.0% (+23.8 pp); similarly, up to +24.0 pp on other benchmarks.
- Parameter efficiency: a composition of two 1.5B models (draft + projector) outperforms a single 14B model using standard constrained decoding, demonstrating that DCCD extracts more performance per parameter.
- Test‑time scaling: accuracy increases more steeply with the number of drafts K compared to standard constrained decoding, and gains do not saturate quickly, making effective use of additional compute.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Conditioning Increases Feasible Mass
The unconstrained draft y provides a semantic plan that, when prepended to the context, shifts the model’s next‑token distribution so that the total probability assigned to structurally valid tokens (the feasible mass α̃(ĥ_t)) is significantly larger than without conditioning. This reduces the severity of each renormalization step. Evidence: Abstract states “Conditioning increases feasible mass for valid continuations, reducing distortion”; Section 4.1 formalizes α(ht;d) ≫ α(ht).

### Mechanism 2: Reduced Cumulative Projection Tax
Constrained decoding incurs a cumulative “projection tax” equal to the sum of per‑token KL divergences, which biases the generation toward prefixes with high feasible mass and often hurts semantic correctness. DCCD lowers this tax by increasing α̃ at each step, making log 1/α̃ smaller. Evidence: Section 3.1 derives the tax as E[∑ log 1/α(ht)]; Section 4.2 shows DCCD aims for α̃(ĥ_t) ≫ α(ht) along the realized trajectory, directly reducing the tax.

### Mechanism 3: Parameter‑Efficient Composition & Test‑Time Scaling
Because Stage 2 primarily performs structured realization (a less demanding task), the projector model can be smaller than the draft model or even a separate lightweight model. This separation allows a pair of modest‑sized models to surpass a single large model. Moreover, sampling multiple drafts and selecting the one with highest cumulative feasible mass leverages test‑time compute effectively; the slope of accuracy vs. K is steeper for DCCD than for standard CD. Evidence: Abstract and Section 5.2 present parameter‑efficiency figures (Fig 5) and test‑time scaling (Fig 6).

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: KL divergence as a divergence measure**  
*Why needed:* DCCD’s analysis frames constrained decoding as a reverse‑KL projection; understanding KL divergence clarifies why renormalization distorts distributions and how the projection tax accumulates.  
*Quick check:* If you project distribution p onto a constrained set by minimizing KL(q ∥ p) (reverse‑KL), what does that imply about the support of q relative to p? How does a small feasible mass α relate to this KL value?

**Concept 2: Autoregressive generation & token‑level masking**  
*Why needed:* Constrained decoding operates token‑by‑token; masking and renormalization at step t affect not only token t but also all future distributions because the context changes. Grasping this sequential dependency is key to understanding the “projection tax”.  
*Quick check:* In an autoregressive model, how does masking and renormalizing at step t affect the distribution over tokens at step t+1, t+2, …?

**Concept 3: Feasible mass (α(ht))**  
*Why needed:* Feasible mass is the total probability the model assigns to syntactically valid next‑tokens given the current prefix. It directly determines the per‑step KL penalty; increasing it is DCCD’s core objective.  
*Quick check:* Given a prefix ht and a constraint set A(ht), how would you compute α(ht) from the model’s next‑token logits? What does it mean if α(ht)=0.1 versus α(ht)=0.9?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Prompt Input (x) → Draft Model (p_draft) → Drafts y^(k) → Draft Sampler & Selector (scores S^(k)) → selects best y^(k*)  
(x, y^(k*)) → Projector Model (p_proj) + Constraint Oracle (A(ht)) → Constrained Decoder → Output z^(k*)

**Critical path:**  
1. Receive input x.  
2. Generate K drafts: for k=1..K, y^(k) ∼ p_draft(·|x).  
3. For each draft: initialize z=∅; for t=1..T: compute logits from p_proj(x, y^(k), z_{<t}); obtain valid token set A(current prefix); mask invalid tokens, renormalize, sample/greedy decode next token z_t; update S^(k) += log(Σ_{valid} softmax(logits)).  
4. Choose k* = argmax_k S^(k) (or k=1 if K=1).  
5. Return z^(k*).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Draft quality vs. latency: Better drafts increase feasible mass but cost more in Stage 1.  
- Model sizing: Projector can be smaller than draft, but if too small it may fail to realize the structure even with a good draft.  
- Number of drafts (K): Larger K improves chance of finding a high‑feasible‑mass draft but scales cost linearly.  
- Selection criterion: Cumulative log feasible mass is cheap and correlates with success but is not a perfect proxy.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. **Draft misalignment:** Drafts lack correct semantic plan; conditioning fails to boost feasible mass on the right path.  
2. **Constraint violation despite draft:** Projector assigns near‑zero probability to required syntax tokens even when conditioned, causing forced errors.  
3. **Selection criterion failure:** High S^(k) for a draft that ultimately yields invalid/incorrect output, due to early high feasible mass but later failure.  
4. **Model mismatch:** Overly weak draft or projector model breaks the synergy.

3. Test‑time scaling: For a fixed model (e.g., Qwen2.5‑1.5B), run DCCD and CD with K ∈ {1,3,5,7,9,11,13} on GSM8K and MATH500. Plot accuracy vs. K; verify DCCD’s slope is steeper and final accuracy higher.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What draft model characteristics (capacity, prompting) yield the best accuracy–latency trade‑off in DCCD?** The paper notes that draft and projector may be the same or the projector smaller, and shows small‑large pairings work, but lacks systematic study of the design space.  
2. **Can DCCD generalize to more complex structural constraints with long‑range dependencies (e.g., indentation‑sensitive languages, cross‑field validation)?** The current evaluation covers JSON schemas, expression grammars, and FOL grammars, where constraints are token‑local. More expressive constraints may break the feasible‑mass assumption.  
3. **Is there a theoretical bound linking the increase in feasible mass from draft conditioning to the reduction in projection tax and ultimately to higher structured accuracy?** The analysis shows conditioning increases feasible mass and that the tax is ∑ log 1/α̃, but no quantitative relationship between α̃ improvement and accuracy is proved.

## Limitations
- Constraint oracle implementation (e.g., XGrammar token masks, incremental parsing) is not fully specified, making exact baseline reproduction difficult.  
- Critical hyperparameters (temperature, top_p, draft length, number of beams) are not reported, leaving ambiguity in replicating results.  
- The draft selection criterion (cumulative log feasible mass) is a heuristic proxy; its correlation with final correctness is not thoroughly validated across tasks.
