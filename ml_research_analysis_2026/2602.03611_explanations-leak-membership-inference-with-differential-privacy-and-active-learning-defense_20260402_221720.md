# Explanations Leak: Membership Inference with Differential Privacy and Active Learning Defense

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03611
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03611
- Reference count: 36
- Primary result: Counterfactual explanations (CFs) in MLaaS significantly strengthen membership inference attacks (MIAs), increasing attack accuracy by up to 20–40 percentage points across privacy budgets; a defense integrating differential privacy (DP) with active learning (AL) limits memorization and reduces exposed data, yet CFs still drastically erode micro-level privacy even under strong DP (ε=1), while CF quality metrics remain stable under DP.

## Executive Summary
Counterfactual explanations (CFs) provided by machine learning-as-a-service (MLaaS) APIs expose side information that can be exploited to enhance membership inference attacks (MIAs). This paper demonstrates that CFs amplify MIA accuracy by 20–40 percentage points across a range of differential privacy (DP) budgets. To mitigate this, the authors propose a defense combining DP with active learning (AL) – AL-Guided-DP – which reduces the number of training records exposed and limits memorization. While AL-Guided-DP preserves predictive performance comparable to DP-only training while using fewer samples, CFs continue to undermine true member concealment even under strict DP (ε=1). Moreover, CF quality (proximity, sparsity) remains stable under DP, revealing a tension between transparency and privacy. The study underscores that explanation APIs, even when backed by DP-trained models, pose significant privacy risks that demand integrated defenses.

## Method Summary
The study investigates the privacy impact of counterfactual explanations in MLaaS. Using shadow-based membership inference attacks, the authors show that CFs provide a signal of distance to the decision boundary, which correlates with training membership and boosts attack accuracy. They evaluate several defense strategies: (1) standard DP-SGD, (2) active learning (AL) to select a small informative training subset, and (3) a hybrid AL-Guided-DP that applies DP-SGD to the AL-selected subset. Experiments on two datasets (EEG, In-Location) compare predictive performance, MIA success (overall and at the individual record level), and CF quality. Results indicate that AL-Guided-DP often matches DP-only utility while exposing fewer records, but CFs still severely compromise micro-privacy. The work concludes that while DP and AL reduce memorization, CFs remain a potent side channel, urging further research into privacy-preserving explanation methods.

## Key Results
- CFs increase MIA accuracy by 20–40 percentage points across various DP budgets (ε).
- AL-Guided-DP maintains utility comparable to DP-only while using fewer training samples (e.g., 10% for In-Location), but CFs drastically reduce true member concealment even at ε=1.
- CF quality metrics (proximity, sparsity) remain stable under DP, indicating that explanations retain their technical properties despite formal privacy guarantees.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Counterfactual Explanations Amplify Membership Inference
Exposing CFs via MLaaS APIs strengthens shadow-based MIAs because CFs approximate an instance’s distance to the decision boundary. Training samples are typically farther from the boundary (higher confidence); CFs reveal minimal feature changes needed to flip the prediction, thus correlating with that distance. An attacker collects a shadow dataset containing model outputs and CFs, trains shadow models, and learns a membership classifier that exploits this signal. Core assumption: confidence (or boundary distance) differs systematically between members and non-members. Evidence: [abstract] and [section III-A] state that CFs reveal distance to the decision boundary and can be exploited by the intuition that training samples are more confidently classified. Break condition: if CF generation adds excessive noise or boundary complexity nullifies the distance-confidence correlation, the attack advantage diminishes.

### Mechanism 2: Differential Privacy Limits Memorization
DP-SGD provides formal (ε,δ)-DP guarantees by clipping per-sample gradients and adding calibrated Gaussian noise. This bounds the influence of any single record on model parameters, preventing overfitting to individual training points and thus reducing membership leakage. Core assumption: MIAs primarily exploit memorization; reducing memorization reduces attack success. Evidence: [abstract] and [section III-B] describe DP-SGD as limiting memorization and providing instance-level privacy. Break condition: if ε is too large (weak privacy) or the model is highly overparameterized, memorization may persist; side information like CFs can also circumvent DP.

### Mechanism 3: Active Learning Complements DP
Integrating AL with DP (AL-Guided-DP) constructs a smaller, high-utility training subset. AL selects the most informative unlabeled samples, reducing the number of exposed records. Training with DP on this subset limits the attack surface while potentially preserving utility better than DP on the full dataset (because fewer samples allow larger noise relative to data size). Core assumption: a strategically chosen smaller training set reduces vulnerable members and can achieve comparable performance. Evidence: [abstract] and [section V-A] show Only-AL uses only 10% of In-Location data and matches baseline; AL-Guided-DP achieves similar accuracy to Only-DP but with fewer samples (e.g., EEG: ~75% vs 100%). Break condition: if AL selection is unrepresentative, utility suffers; if DP noise dominates, sample reduction yields negligible privacy gain.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:
Raw dataset D -> (AL selector) -> informative subset S -> DP-SGD training -> target model f_θ and CF explainer E (trained on S) -> Deployment: MLaaS API returns (f_θ(x), E(x)) on query. Attacker: queries API to collect shadow dataset {E(x_i), f_θ(x_i)} -> trains shadow models f_s -> trains attack model g on shadow outputs (and optionally CFs) -> predicts membership. Evaluation: compute MIA accuracy, micro/macro defended ratios, predictive performance, CF quality metrics.

Critical path:
1. Start with raw dataset D.
2. If using AL, run AL loop to select subset S.
3. Train target model with DP-SGD on S (if using DP) or without.
4. Train CF explainer on S.
5. Deploy model and explainer as MLaaS.
6. Attacker queries API to collect shadow dataset (with/without CFs).
7. Train shadow models and attack model.
8. Evaluate attack success across ε and CF availability.
9. Measure CF quality and predictive performance.

Design tradeoffs:
- Privacy vs Utility: Lower ε increases privacy but degrades model accuracy.
- Privacy vs Explainability: CFs remain stable under DP, so explainability is preserved, but privacy erodes when CFs are released.
- Data exposure: AL reduces number of records used in training, limiting the pool of members, but may require more data under DP to maintain utility (AL-Guided-DP sometimes selects larger subsets than Only-AL when combined with DP).
- Defense choice: AL-Guided-DP generally outperforms DP-Post-AL in utility but may expose more records due to larger selected subsets.

Failure signatures:
- High MIA accuracy (>60%) even with low ε when CFs are enabled.
- Significant drop in predictive performance (>10%) compared to baseline when using AL-Guided-DP.
- CF quality metrics (proximity, sparsity) degrade sharply under DP (contrary to paper's finding of stability).
- AL selecting a very small subset (<5%) causing model underfitting.
- DP noise clipping norm too low causing training instability.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline MIA without CFs: Train Baseline model, perform MIA to establish attack baseline; then with CFs to quantify amplification (reproduce Fig. 2 leftmost bars).
2. Defense comparison: Train with Only-DP, Only-AL, DP-Post-AL, AL-Guided-DP across ε values; measure predictive performance (Table I) and MIA success (Fig. 2) to identify best trade-off.
3. CF quality and micro/macro protection: For each defense, compute CF proximity and sparsity (Fig. 4) and micro/macro defended ratios (Fig. 3) to assess whether DP stabilizes explanations and whether macro metrics mask micro-level vulnerability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What is the end-user utility of counterfactual explanations under differential privacy in high-stakes domains? The paper calls for empirical user studies to assess whether DP-stable explanations retain interpretability and decision-support value.
2. Can counterfactual explanation algorithms be inherently privacy-preserving to limit membership leakage without relying solely on model-level defenses like DP? The paper suggests exploring modifications to CF generation itself.
3. Do the findings on CF-amplified MIAs and DP-AL defense effectiveness generalize beyond deep neural networks to other model families and data types? The study was limited to DNNs on two datasets.
4. Beyond membership inference, do counterfactual explanations enable other privacy attacks (e.g., attribute inference, model extraction), and can DP-AL defenses mitigate these risks? The scope was confined to shadow-based MIA.

## Limitations
- CF amplification mechanism: The correlation between CF properties (e.g., proximity) and confidence for members vs. non-members is not formally proven and may vary with model architecture or data distribution.
- Micro-privacy erosion under DP: It remains unclear why CFs drastically reduce true member concealment even at ε=1 – whether due to CFs' inherent stability under DP or side information bypassing DP's gradient-level protection.
- AL-Guided-DP subset dynamics: AL-Guided-DP sometimes selects more samples than Only-AL (e.g., EEG: ~75% vs 10%), contradicting the "fewer samples" claim; the interaction between DP noise and AL’s informativeness criterion needs clarification.

## Confidence
- CFs amplify MIAs: High (strong empirical evidence across ε).
- DP reduces memorization/leakage: Medium (theoretical foundation solid, but CFs still erode micro-privacy under DP, suggesting insufficiency).
- AL-Guided-DP reduces exposed data while maintaining utility: Medium (utility often maintained, but subset size increases under DP in some cases, undermining the advantage).

## Next Checks
1. Reproduce CF amplification across at least two additional datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10, Adult Income) and two CF methods (e.g., Wachter, generative approaches) to test consistency of the 20–40 pp gain.
2. Analyze CF distributions under DP: For ε∈{0.1, 1, 10}, compute statistical differences (e.g., KL divergence) in CF proximity/sparsity between members and non-members to understand why micro-privacy fails despite formal DP guarantees.
3. Audit AL-Guided-DP selection: Compare the informativeness scores (e.g., entropy, margin) of samples selected by Only-AL vs. AL-Guided-DP across ε; check whether DP noise forces AL to pick redundant or boundary-near samples, increasing subset size.
