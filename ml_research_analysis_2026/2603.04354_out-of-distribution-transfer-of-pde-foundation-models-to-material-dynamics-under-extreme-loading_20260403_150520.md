# Out-of-distribution transfer of PDE foundation models to material dynamics under extreme loading

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04354
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04354
- **Reference count:** 9
- **Primary result:** Out-of-distribution transfer of fluid-pretrained PDE foundation models to extreme-loading material dynamics shows regime-dependent performance: MORPH-Ti superior on shock-driven interfaces (MSE 0.055 vs 0.139), POSEIDON-T slightly better on dynamic fracture (0.037 vs 0.041); pretraining improves sample efficiency mainly in low-data regimes but gains are modest compared to fluid benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the transferability of two pretrained PDE foundation models, POSEIDON and MORPH, to extreme-loading material dynamics scenarios involving shock-driven multi-material interfaces (PLI) and dynamic fracture (FRAC). The models are evaluated on terminal-state prediction, mapping initial conditions to final states without intermediate supervision. Results indicate that pretraining yields modest out-of-distribution gains, with MORPH-Ti performing better on PLI and POSEIDON-T holding a slight edge on FRAC. Data scaling experiments reveal that pretraining primarily benefits low-data regimes, with diminishing returns as in-domain data increases. The findings suggest that fluid-centric pretraining alone provides limited transfer to discontinuity-dominated mechanics and highlight the need for incorporating extreme-loading data into future pretraining corpora.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate transfer learning for PDE foundation models on two out-of-distribution tasks: shock-driven multi-material interface dynamics (PLI) and dynamic fracture (FRAC). Both tasks involve predicting the final state from the initial snapshot (terminal-state prediction) without intermediate supervision. The PLI dataset comprises 5,293 simulations of density fields at 1×1120×400 resolution, while the FRAC dataset uses damage fields at 1×128×128 from ~200k tungsten simulations. The models under study are POSEIDON-T (21M parameters) and MORPH-Ti (7M parameters), both transformer-based architectures pretrained on fluid-centric PDEs. Fine-tuning uses AdamW with a warmup-cosine schedule, early stopping, and RevIN normalization. Inputs are preprocessed by channel replication to 4 channels; PLI inputs are downsampled to 128×128 for POSEIDON but kept at native resolution for MORPH. Evaluation metric is test MSE on native-resolution states. The key experimental variable is the training set size, comparing fine-tuning from pretrained checkpoints against training from random initialization.

## Key Results
- MORPH-Ti outperforms POSEIDON-T on PLI (test MSE 0.055 vs 0.139).
- POSEIDON-T has a modest advantage on FRAC (test MSE 0.037 vs 0.041).
- Pretraining improves sample efficiency mainly when in-domain data is limited; gains diminish with larger training sets.
- Transfer gains are modest relative to fluid-centric benchmarks, suggesting fluid-pretraining alone is insufficient for extreme-loading regimes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Fluid-centric pretraining provides a generalizable representation that improves sample efficiency primarily in low-data regimes for OOD transfer to discontinuity-dominated dynamics.
- **Mechanism:** Pretraining on smooth, fluid-centric PDEs teaches the model foundational operator approximation skills (e.g., learning to map initial conditions to future states via learned latent dynamics) and spatial feature extraction via attention/convolutional backbones. When fine-tuned on a new, OOD regime with limited data, this general skill amortizes learning; the model converges faster and generalizes better than random initialization because it starts from a useful parameter basin rather than a flat prior.
- **Core assumption:** The pretrained feature hierarchy (e.g., multiscale patches in scOT, field fusion in MORPH) captures transferable spatial correlations and temporal dependencies, even if the *exact* physics (shocks/fracture) differ from fluids.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...pretraining improves sample efficiency primarily in low-data regimes, but the benefit diminishes with increased in-domain data."
  - [section 3.3] "Pretraining is most beneficial in the low-data regime... the performance gap between fine-tuning and training from scratch shrinks, indicating that more in-domain data reduces the benefit of pretraining."
  - [corpus] Weak direct support; corpus contains fracture-specific papers (e.g., "A Foundation Model for Material Fracture Prediction") but no direct evidence on *why* fluid-pretrained models transfer.
- **Break condition:** If the OOD shift involves fundamentally different singularities (e.g., fracture tip dynamics vs. shock fronts) not represented in any form in the fluid pretraining distribution, the transferred representations may be irrelevant or harmful ("negative transfer" observed for MORPH on FRAC).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The relative performance regime-dependence (MORPH > POSEIDON on PLI; POSEIDON > MORPH on FRAC) stems from architecture-specific inductive biases interacting with discontinuity geometry and dynamics.
- **Mechanism:** MORPH's component-wise convolutions and field-wise cross-attention may better handle multi-material interface dynamics (PLI) by explicitly processing scalar/vector fields and fusing them, aiding in tracking material boundaries under shock-driven mixing. POSEIDON's hierarchical multiscale SwinV2 transformer may better capture localized, sharp features (e.g., fracture tips, damage concentration) via its windowed attention and patch merging, which can adapt to sparse, high-contrast patterns in FRAC.
- **Core assumption:** Architectural design choices (convolution vs. attention, patch size, field handling) differentially encode inductive biases for discontinuity topology (evolving interfaces vs. branching cracks).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2] Descriptions of MORPH's "component-wise convolutions" and "field-wise cross-attention" vs. POSEIDON's "SwinV2 blocks with shifted-window self-attention" and "multiscale token resolutions."
  - [section 3.2] "On PLI, MORPH-Ti significantly outperforms POSEIDON-T... On FRAC, POSEIDON-T achieves a modest advantage over MORPH-Ti."
  - [figures 1,2] Error maps show MORPH errors more on coarse interface shape in PLI; POSEIDON errors concentrated near crack tips/ branches in FRAC.
- **Break condition:** Changing the data preprocessing (e.g., channel replication for POSEIDON) or patch size could alter these regime advantages, indicating the observed superiority is not purely intrinsic to the architecture but tied to implementation choices matched to the dataset's native resolution and field structure.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Terminal-state prediction (first-frame → final-frame) as a long-horizon operator learning task reveals that pretrained models struggle with accumulating error over very long rollouts implicitly, as they are not required to generate intermediate states.
- **Mechanism:** The task eliminates explicit autoregressive error propagation, forcing the model to learn a direct, compressed mapping from initial condition to terminal state. This bypasses one major failure mode of PDE surrogates (accumulating error step-by-step) but instead tests the model's ability to infer the integrated effect of long-time dynamics (e.g., shock paths, crack coalescence) in a single forward pass. Pretraining on shorter, fluid-centric trajectories may not have taught this specific "integrated effect" mapping, limiting transfer.
- **Core assumption:** The terminal-state loss function (MSE on final frame) implicitly measures the model's capacity to learn a coarse-grained, long-time operator without relying on intermediate supervision, which may differ in inductive bias from models trained on step-wise prediction.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 1] "We formulate the downstream task as terminal-state prediction, i.e., learning a long-horizon map that predicts the final state directly from the first snapshot without intermediate supervision."
  - [section 2.2] Both models are adapted for "terminal-state prediction by conditioning on the final lead time" (POSEIDON) or fine-tuned for the same.
  - [abstract] The paper explicitly contrasts their focus on terminal-state prediction with "most downstream evaluations [that] emphasize autoregressive rollouts."
- **Break condition:** If intermediate states were provided as supervision during fine-tuning (e.g., using a multi-step loss), the relative performance and transfer benefits might change significantly, as the model would then be trained to handle error accumulation explicitly.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Operator Learning (Solution Operator Approximation)**
  - Why needed here: The downstream task is a mapping from initial condition *a* to terminal state *u(T)*, i.e., learning an operator *G: a → u(T)*. Both PDE-FMs are pretrained as operator learners, meaning they are designed to approximate PDE solution maps for a family of equations and initial conditions.
  - Quick check question: Why is learning an operator (mapping between function spaces) more suitable for OOD transfer than learning a specific solution trajectory for a fixed PDE/IC?
- **Concept: Distribution Shift & Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization**
  - Why needed here: The pretraining corpora (fluid-centric: Navier-Stokes, Euler) and downstream tasks (PLI/FRAC: shock/fracture-dominated) represent a significant shift in the underlying PDE family, solution regularity (smooth vs. discontinuous), and dominant physics. Evaluating "transfer" explicitly requires quantifying performance degradation/gain relative to training from scratch under this shift.
  - Quick check question: What specific aspects of the PLI/FRAC datasets constitute an OOD shift relative to PDEGym/PDEBench pretraining datasets (e.g., solution smoothness, number of fields, physics)?
- **Concept: Sample Efficiency & Data-Level Scaling**
  - Why needed here: The key metric for transfer utility is whether pretraining reduces the amount of in-domain data needed to achieve a target performance. The experiments vary training-set size to measure the gap between fine-tuning and random initialization.
  - Quick check question: How would you interpret a plot where the "fine-tune vs. scratch" performance gap closes as training-set size increases? What does that imply about the role of pretrained representations vs. in-domain data?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
- Input Processor: Handles heterogeneous input shapes/channels (e.g., PLI: 1×1120×400; FRAC: 1×128×128). For POSEIDON, channel replication to 4 channels and bilinear downsampling to 128×128. For MORPH, native-resolution processing with configurable patch size (8×8 or 4×4).
- Tokenization/Patch embedding: Splits spatial field into patches and projects to latent tokens. POSEIDON uses 4×4 patches; MORPH allows configurable patch size.
- Core backbone: POSEIDON: Hierarchical SwinV2 blocks with shifted-window self-attention and patch merging/expansion. MORPH: Component-wise convolutions → field-wise cross-attention → axial spatial-temporal attention.
- Temporal conditioning: POSEIDON uses time-conditioned layer normalization (affine parameters as function of lead time *t*). MORPH likely uses lead-time in a conditioning channel or similar.
- Head: Projects final token representations back to output spatial grid (native resolution via upsampling for POSEIDON after prediction).
- Adapters: MORPH includes LoRA layers for parameter-efficient fine-tuning (not explicitly used here, but available).

**Critical path:**  
Input → Preprocessing (resize/replicate channels) → Patch embedding → Backbone processing (spatial mixing + temporal conditioning) → Output head → Postprocessing (resize to native grid) → MSE loss vs. terminal frame.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Patch size vs. discontinuity resolution: Smaller patches (4×4) can better localize sharp features (crack tips) but increase token count/compute; larger patches (8×8) may smooth over thin discontinuities. MORPH experiments tested both.
- Fixed vs. flexible input resolution: POSEIDON's fixed 128×128 expectation forces downsampling, potentially losing fine interface/fracture detail. MORPH's native-resolution processing avoids this but requires careful patch-size tuning to manage token count.
- Inductive bias: Convolutions (MORPH) impose local bias, beneficial for material interfaces; attention (POSEIDON) offers global context, potentially helpful for fracture patterns influenced by remote boundaries.

**Failure signatures:**
- PLI: Errors concentrated along material interfaces (misplaced jets, incorrect mixing) and shock fronts (smearing).
- FRAC: Errors at crack tips (failure to capture sharp tip propagation), branch junctions (coalescence errors), and thin damage ligaments (disappearance or thickening).
- General: Inability to predict correct *number* of distinct fragments/cracks; failure to capture path-dependent sensitivity to initial perturbations (late-time morphology mismatch).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What limits the modest (~2×) transfer gains from fluid-pretrained PDE foundation models to extreme-loading regimes? The paper hypothesizes a distribution mismatch but does not isolate whether the bottleneck lies in pretraining data (lack of shocks/fractures), model architecture (smoothness bias), or pretraining objectives. Resolving would require systematic experiments varying pretraining data composition, modifying architectural inductive biases, and measuring transfer on PLI/FRAC.
- **Open Question 2:** Why does MORPH significantly outperform POSEIDON on shock-driven interface dynamics (PLI) while POSEIDON holds a modest advantage on fracture evolution (FRAC)? No ablation or diagnostic study identifies which components drive the contrasting strengths. Swapping modules between models or systematically varying patch sizes and evaluating on both regimes could reveal key factors.
- **Open Question 3:** Does incorporating extreme-loading datasets (such as PLI and FRAC) into the pretraining corpus substantially improve transfer performance on shock- and fracture-dominated tasks? The suggestion remains speculative; experiments pretraining on a mixture of fluid-centric (e.g., PDEBench, PDEGym) and extreme-loading datasets are needed.
- **Open Question 4:** Can continual learning strategies effectively integrate new extreme-loading regimes into existing fluid-pretrained PDE foundation models without catastrophic forgetting of fluid dynamics? The paper does not explore continual learning; evidence would come from implementing continual learning protocols and evaluating on both old and new tasks.

## Limitations
- **Uncertainties:** Exact training hyperparameters (warmup steps, early-stopping patience delta), dataset split random seeds, MORPH patch size selection (4×4 vs 8×8), and RevIN implementation details (per-sample vs global statistics, upsampling method) remain unspecified, potentially affecting reproducibility.
- **Confidence levels:** Claims about sample efficiency gains (Medium), architecture-specific regime advantages (Medium), and terminal-state task limitations (Medium) are only moderately supported.
- **Risk:** The modest transfer gains observed could be sensitive to implementation choices not reported, limiting generalizability.
