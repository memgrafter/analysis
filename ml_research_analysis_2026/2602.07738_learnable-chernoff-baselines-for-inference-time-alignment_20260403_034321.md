# Learnable Chernoff Baselines for Inference-Time Alignment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07738
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07738
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Learnable Chernoff Baselines (LCBs) enable efficient inference-time alignment from KL-regularized reward models by using adaptive rejection sampling with learned baselines, achieving 7–12.5× fewer model queries (continuous) and 20–40% fewer than Best-of-N (discrete) while matching ideal rejection sampling performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Learnable Chernoff Baselines (LCBs), a method for efficient inference-time alignment of generative models with reward functions. LCBs address the problem of sampling from a KL-regularized, reward-aligned target distribution using only black-box access to a pretrained model. The core innovation is to replace the naive, fixed upper bound in rejection sampling with state- and timestep-dependent baselines learned to satisfy a high-probCert nof bound (via Chernoff). This reduces the expected number of proposals per step while controlling total variation error. The method works by exponentially tilting the pretrained model's transition kernels using learned soft-value functions, then applying adaptive rejection sampling with learned baselines. Theoretical analysis links TV error to baseline approximation quality. Empirically, on a Gaussian mixture and the LLaDA-8B diffusion model, LCBs match ideal rejection sampling with significantly fewer model queries and outperform Best-of-N in discrete settings, demonstrating a practical compute-reward tradeoff.

## Method Summary
The method proceeds in three offline stages. First, soft-value functions *v̂*<sub>t</sub>(*x*<sub>t</sub>) ≈ log E[exp(*r*(*x*<sub>0</sub>)/α) | *x*<sub>t</sub>] are learned by regression on pretraining trajectories. Second, for each timestep *t*, a baseline *B*<sub>t+1</sub>(*x*<sub>t+1</sub>) = *b*<sub>t+1</sub>(*x*<sub>t+1</sub>) + τ is learned to upper-bound *v̂*<sub>t</sub>(*x*<sub>t</sub>) with high probability over *x*<sub>t</sub> ~ *p*<sub>pre</sub><sub>t</sub>(· | *x*<sub>t+1</sub>). The parameters (λ, *b*) are selected by minimizing an objective *J*<sub>t</sub> derived from a Chernoff bound, which trades off baseline tightness against proposal complexity. Crucially, baselines are trained in a mutually compatible, particle-based loop (Algorithm 3) where the training distribution for *b*<sub>t</sub> is the marginal *q̂*<sub>t+1</sub> induced by already-learned later baselines. Third, at inference, Algorithm 2 runs backward induction: from *x*<sub>T</sub>, propose *x*<sub>t</sub> from the pretrained kernel and accept with probability min{1, exp(*v̂*<sub>t</sub>(*x*<sub>t</sub>) – *B*<sub>t+1</sub>(*x*<sub>t+1</sub>))}.

## Key Results
- LCBs achieve total variation error comparable to ideal rejection sampling (Algorithm 1) while using 7–12.5× fewer model queries in continuous Gaussian mixture experiments.
- For discrete text generation with LLaDA-8B, LCBs match or exceed the average reward of Best-of-N with 20–40% fewer proposals and approach ideal rejection sampling with 25–87% fewer proposals.
- Theoretical bounds (Lemma 4.2, Theorem 4.8) show TV error is controlled by the Chernoff-type objective *J*<sub>t</sub> and the soft-value approximation error, with explicit dependence on the confidence parameter δ.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Exponential Tilt Unwrapping of Aligned Distribution
The KL-regularized reward alignment objective yields a target distribution *p*<sup>*</sup>(*x*<sub>0</sub>) ∝ exp(*r*(*x*<sub>0</sub>)/α)*p*<sub>pre</sub>(*x*<sub>0</sub>). For a Markovian pretrained model (e.g., diffusion), this can be exactly generated by a reverse-time Markov chain that exponentially tilts each transition kernel: *p*<sup>*</sup><sub>*t*</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*</sub> | *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub>) ∝ exp(*v*<sub>*t*</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>)/α)*p*<sub>pre</sub><sub>*t*</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*</sub> | *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub>), where *v*<sub>*t*</sub> is a soft-value function. This transforms the alignment problem into sampling from a sequence of tilted kernels given estimates {*v̂*<sub>*t*</sub>}. The mechanism relies on the pretrained model being Markovian and the reward being bounded.

### Mechanism 2: Chernoff-Certified Adaptive Rejection Sampling
Naive rejection sampling from each tilted kernel would use a fixed upper bound (e.g., *B* = 1/α), leading to inefficiency. LCBs replace this with adaptive baselines *B*<sub>*t*+1</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*+1</sub>) learned to satisfy a joint Chernoff condition: Pr<sub>*x*<sub>*t*+1</sub>∼*q̂*<sub>*t*+1</sub>,*x*<sub>*t*</sub>∼*p*<sub>pre</sub><sub>*t</sub>(·|*x*<sub>*t*+1</sub>)[*v̂*<sub>*t*</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>) > *B*<sub>*t*+1</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*+1</sub>)] ≤ δ. The baseline is parametrized as *b*<sub>*t*+1</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*+1</sub>) + τ, where τ is chosen via a Chernoff bound. The pair (λ, *b*) is selected to minimize an objective *J*<sub>*t*</sub> that upper-binds both the TV error and the expected number of proposals. This yields tighter, state-dependent envelopes, reducing rejections while controlling error.

### Mechanism 3: Mutually Compatible Baselines via Particle-based Training
To ensure theoretical guarantees compose across timesteps, baselines must be trained on data from the *actual* sampling distribution *q̂* that will be used at inference, not the pretrained model distribution. This "mutual compatibility" is achieved by a particle-based scheme (Algorithm 3): start with particles at *T*, then for *t* = *T* down to 1, (i) generate proposals from the pretrained kernel for each particle state, (ii) train *b*<sub>*t*</sub> on these pairs to minimize empirical *Ĵ*<sub>*t*</sub>, (iii) update particles using rejection sampling with the new baseline. This sequentially builds marginals *q̂*<sub>*t*</sub> used to train earlier baselines, aligning training and inference distributions.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Exponential Tilting of Probability Kernels
**Why needed:** The aligned distribution is expressed as an exponential tilt of the pretrained model's joint distribution. LCBs operate on the tilted kernels *p̂*<sub>*t*</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*</sub> | *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub>) ∝ exp(*v̂*<sub>*t*</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>))*p*<sub>pre</sub><sub>*t*</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*</sub> | *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub>), which are the ideal (but costly) sampling targets.
**Quick check:** Given *p*(*x*) ∝ exp(*f*(*x*))*q*(*x*), the acceptance probability for standard rejection sampling using proposals from *q* is min{1, exp(*f*(*x*) – sup *f*)}.

### Concept: Rejection Sampling Theory and Total Variation Bounds
**Why needed:** Correctness guarantees (TV distance between the LCB sampler *q̂* and the ideal tilted sampler *p̂*) are proven by bounding the error per timestep introduced by the approximate rejection step (Lemma D.4, D.7). These bounds rely on classical rejection sampling analysis.
**Quick check:** In rejection sampling with proposal *q*, target *p*, and envelope *B* (accept if *U* ≤ *p*(*x*)/(*q*(*x*)*B*)), the TV distance to *p* is bounded by *q*(*F*) + *p*(*F*), where *F* = {*x* : *p*(*x*) > *q*(*x*)*B*}.

### Concept: Chernoff Bounds and Moment Generating Functions
**Why needed:** The LCB objective *J*<sub>*t*</sub> and its TV/proposal bounds (Lemma 4.2) arise from applying a Chernoff bound to the exceedance probability of *v̂* – *b*. The MGF terms E[e<sup>λ(*v̂*–*b*)</sup>] and E[e<sup>λ(*b*–*v̂*)</sup>] directly control baseline tightness and proposal complexity.
**Quick check:** To ensure Pr[*Y* > *b* + τ] ≤ δ given *b* and λ, Chernoff suggests τ = (1/λ)[log E[e<sup>λ*Y*</sup>] + log(1/δ)], assuming *b* ≤ *Y* a.s. or using E[e<sup>λ*Y*</sup>] directly.

### Concept: Sub-Gaussian Concentration for Gaussian Conditionals
**Why needed:** In the Gaussian mixture example, the TV bound simplifies because *v*<sub>*t*</sub>(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>) conditional on *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub> is sub-Gaussian. This yields an explicit bound where the sub-Gaussian parameter σ<sub>*t*</sub> is O(β<sub>*t*</sub> + ˜β<sub>*t*</sub>), linking sampling error to forward/reverse diffusion variances.
**Quick check:** If *X* | *Y* ~ N(*μ*(*Y*), Σ) and *f* is *L*-Lipschitz, *f*(*X*) | *Y* is sub-Gaussian with parameter proportional to *L*√(∥Σ∥) because Lipschitz functions of Gaussians are sub-Gaussian.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Pretrained Model (*p*<sub>pre</sub>) -> Soft-Value Estimator (*v̂*<sub>t</sub>) -> Baseline Network (*b*<sub>t</sub>) -> LCB Sampler (Algorithm 2)

### Critical path
1. **Offline Pretraining:** Gather *n* trajectories from *p*<sub>pre</sub>. Train *v̂*<sub>t</sub> for all *t* via regression on exp(*r*(*x*<sub>0</sub>)/α).
2. **Baseline Learning (Particle Loop):** Initialize *m* particles at *T*. For *t* = *T* down to 1: (a) For each particle state *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>, draw a proposal *y*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup> ~ *p*<sub>pre</sub><sub>*t*</sub>(· | *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>). (b) Train *b*<sub>*t*</sub> on pairs {(*x*<sub>*t*+1</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>, *y*<sub>*t*</sub><sup>(*i*)</sup>)} by minimizing empirical *Ĵ*<sub>*t*</sub>. (c) Update particles by rejection sampling with baseline *B*<sub>*t*</sub> = *b*<sub>*t</sub> + τ.
3. **Inference:** Freeze *v̂*, *b*, τ. Run Algorithm 2 repeatedly to generate aligned samples.

### Design tradeoffs
- **δ (baseline level):** Smaller δ ⇒ tighter baseline ⇒ lower TV but higher expected proposals. TV scales ~ δ exp(*O*(1/√δ)) in sub-Gaussian regimes. Paper uses δ=0.1.
- **λ (Chernoff exponent):** Larger λ ⇒ potentially smaller τ for fixed δ, but increases estimation variance in *J*. Paper sets Λ=12 (Gaussian) or searches λ∈[1,6] (LLaDA).
- **Hypothesis class *H* for *b*<sub>t</sub>:** Larger capacity reduces approximation error *ϵ*<sub>0</sub> but requires more particles *m* for uniform convergence. For LLaDA, a RoBERTa backbone is used.
- **Particles *m* in training:** Larger *m* improves mutual compatibility. Required *m* ≍ complexity(*H*)/*ϵ*<sub>0</sub><sup>2</sup>. Used *m*=7000 (Gaussian) and *m*=350 (LLaDA, with pre-training to mitigate small *m*).

### Failure signatures
- **High TV despite low δ:** Likely poor *v̂* estimation. Diagnostic: check MSE of *v̂* on held-out trajectories.
- **No proposal count reduction:** Baselines too loose (*B*<sub>t+1</sub> ≈ *B*). Inspect learned *b* values; may need smaller δ or λ, or more flexible *b*.
- **Particle training instability:** Particle collapse/diversity loss. Mitigation: increase *m*, add noise to proposals, or pre-train *b* on *p*<sub>pre</sub> data (as done for LLaDA).
- **Baseline coverage violation:** Even if joint Chernoff holds, some *x*<sub>t+1</sub> may have high local exceedance. Check high-probability coverage (Appendix I); if ζ (fraction of bad states) large, use more expressive *b* or larger *m*.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Gaussian Mixture TV Match:** Run LCB with δ=0.1. Compare histograms of final *x*<sub>0</sub> to exact rejection sampling and true *p*<sup>*</sup>. Compute TV numerically. Vary δ to see tradeoff: smaller δ increases proposal count but may not visibly improve alignment beyond a point.
2. **LLaDA Alignment and Efficiency:** For a fixed prompt (e.g., "Write a creative story..."), compare LCB (various α) vs. Best-of-N and Rejection Sampling on average reward and "perfect sample" rate (reward=1). Measure average proposals per sample. Confirm LCB achieves comparable reward to BoN with 20-40% fewer proposals and similar to RS with 25-87% fewer. Also check text quality metrics (e.g., sentence count, non-standard words).
3. **Baseline Tightness Visualization:** For guided trajectories from LLaDA, plot *v̂*<sub>t</sub>(*x*<sub>t</sub>) and baseline *B*<sub>t+1</sub>(*x*<sub>t+1</sub>) over timesteps. The baseline line should be a tight upper bound (within O(1)) of the value line. Large gaps imply δ too small or poor *b* training; baseline below value indicates coverage violation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can LCBs be extended to handle prompt-conditioned rewards, values, and baselines for more flexible downstream tasks?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Conclusion states: "Future empirical directions include prompt-conditioned rewards, values and baselines, and to use these trained modules across many downstream tasks."
**Why unresolved:** Current formulation assumes a fixed reward; conditioning on prompts would enable task-specific alignment without retraining baselines per prompt.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis of conditional LCBs and empirical validation on diverse prompt-conditioned tasks (e.g., text generation with varying constraints).

### Open Question 2
**Question:** To what extent can the soft-value approximation error be reduced, and what are the practical limits of LCB performance given imperfect value estimates?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Conclusion notes: "LCBs are limited by soft-value approximation, which is an active area of study in the literature." Theorem 3.1 bounds TV error via L2 error of value estimates.
**Why unresolved:** Paper provides a bound but does not characterize practical impact of value approximation on final alignment quality for complex models like LLMs.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic empirical studies varying value function accuracy (e.g., via more data or better architectures) and measuring resulting TV error or reward metrics on benchmarks.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What theoretical guarantees exist for LCBs in discrete state-space diffusion models beyond the Gaussian mixture toy example?
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] Analysis primarily uses Gaussian mixtures; empirical results include discrete LLMs; paper acknowledges need for theoretical guarantees for sampling distributions but does not extend them to discrete spaces.
**Why unresolved:** Analysis relies on sub-Gaussianity and coverage assumptions that may not hold for discrete models; bounds may not directly transfer.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Extend theoretical framework to discrete Markov kernels, e.g., using concentration inequalities for discrete distributions, and verify assumptions on real discrete models.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How can the dual-use risks of LCBs be mitigated, given their ability to amplify rare harmful behaviors by targeting low-support regions of the pretrained model?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Impact Statement warns: "our experiments suggest the method can efficiently amplify regions of low model support, which could be misused to amplify rare harmful behaviors."
**Why unresolved:** Paper does not propose safeguards against adversarial reward specification; alignment methods require careful reward design to avoid reward hacking.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Development of certification methods for reward functions, empirical red-teaming to identify failure modes, and integration with safety filters or constrained optimization.

## Limitations
- Mutual compatibility: Baseline training relies on particle loops; simplified LLaDA training may violate theory.
- Soft-value errors: *v̂*<sub>t</sub> approximation quality not stress-tested; poor *v̂* inflates TV.
- Markov assumption: Not applicable to non-Markov generators; proposal cost may remain high for large outputs.
- Hyperparameter choices (δ, λ) are fixed; robustness unclear.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical framework (exponential tilt, TV bounds): High** (proofs solid under assumptions).
- **Empirical efficiency (7–12.5× speedup, 20–40% vs BoN): Medium** (limited validation scenarios, single seed).
- **Generalization to other models (e.g., autoregressive): Low** (no experiments; potential obstacles in *v̂*<sub>t</sub> design).
