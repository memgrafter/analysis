# CEI: A Benchmark for Evaluating Pragmatic Reasoning in Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09993
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09993
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Top language model accuracy is 25.0%, far below human majority agreement (54.3%), with no improvement from chain-of-thought or few-shot prompting.

## Executive Summary
The CEI benchmark is introduced to evaluate pragmatic reasoning in language models, specifically the ability to infer intended emotional meaning from ambiguous utterances within social contexts that have explicit power relations. It comprises 300 expert-authored scenarios covering five pragmatic subtypes (sarcasm, mixed signals, strategic politeness, passive aggression, deflection) and three power configurations, annotated by three trained raters using Plutchik’s emotions and VAD ratings. Evaluation of seven LLMs reveals a substantial performance gap compared to humans, a reversed difficulty pattern across subtypes, and no benefit from advanced prompting—highlighting a critical deficiency in models' social-pragmatic competence.

## Method Summary
The task requires models to select the correct speaker emotion from Plutchik’s eight categories given a scenario context, speaker/listener roles, and an ambiguous utterance. The dataset is systematically constructed to balance subtype and power-relation variables. Annotations from three raters per scenario undergo a multi-stage quality control pipeline that resolves clear errors but preserves genuine multi-label ambiguity. The resulting gold label is a majority vote. Models are evaluated in zero-shot, chain-of-thought (5-step reasoning), and few-shot (3 exemplars) settings via API calls. Out-of-vocabulary model outputs are mapped to Plutchik categories using NRC Emotion Lexicon adjacency. Primary metrics are exact-match accuracy and macro-F1, with breakdowns by subtype and power relation.

## Key Results
- Best model (Llama-3.1-70B) achieves only 25.0% accuracy, substantially below human majority agreement (54.3%).
- Neither chain-of-thought nor few-shot prompting yields meaningful performance gains.
- Models perform worst on sarcasm (15.2%), where human agreement is highest, and best on strategic politeness (25.5%), revealing a dissociation from human error patterns.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Expert Authorship for Balanced Phenomenon Coverage
- **Claim:** Expert-authored scenarios enable controlled, balanced coverage of pragmatic subtypes and power relations, isolating the target inference challenge from naturalistic noise.
- **Mechanism:** Researchers design each scenario to explicitly encode a specific pragmatic subtype and power configuration, ensuring the dataset tests disambiguation across the strategic space of indirect speech. This contrasts with crowd-sourced data, which may overrepresent common patterns and miss rarer configurations.
- **Core assumption:** Pragmatic reasoning can be decomposed into discrete subtypes with distinct contextual demands; expert control generates items that uniformly tax these demands without confounding variables.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "300 expert-authored scenarios across five pragmatic subtypes... and three power configurations." [section 3.1] "Expert authoring ensures balanced coverage... which naturalistic data cannot guarantee."
- **Break condition:** If naturalistic conversations reveal pragmatic subtypes or power-stratified strategies not captured by the expert taxonomy, the benchmark's external validity diminishes.

### Mechanism 2: Treating Disagreement as Informative
- **Claim:** Treating multi-annotator disagreement as informative rather than noisy yields a gold standard that reflects the inherent ambiguity of pragmatic inference.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline preserves all individual labels and uses expert adjudication only to resolve clear errors, not to force consensus. Majority-vote gold labels, combined with released per-annotator data, allow evaluation against a distribution of plausible human judgments.
- **Core assumption:** Pragmatic utterances often admit multiple valid interpretations; a model's output should be compared to the full human judgment distribution, not a single "correct" label that may suppress legitimate ambiguity.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Inter-annotator agreement... is low but expected." [section 5.5] "Valence coherence in split scenarios... 40.4% of three-way splits had complete valence agreement, suggesting systematic ambiguity beneath categorical disagreement."
- **Break condition:** If future behavioral experiments show model predictions aligning with minority human judgments are systematically "wrong," the decision to treat disagreement as feature may need revision.

### Mechanism 3: Quality Control for Reliable Annotation under Low Agreement
- **Claim:** A rigorous quality-control pipeline combining automated checks with expert adjudication ensures annotation integrity even when inter-rater agreement is inherently low.
- **Mechanism:** A four-stage pipeline filters schema errors and statistically anomalous responses, then flags low-agreement scenarios for expert review. Adjudication focuses on identifying clear errors (e.g., misreading context) rather than imposing a single interpretation, preserving genuine ambiguity.
- **Core assumption:** Low Fleiss’ κ does not necessarily indicate poor annotation quality; it can reflect task difficulty. The pipeline distinguishes between error-driven disagreement (to be corrected) and ambiguity-driven disagreement (to be preserved).
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.2] "Quality control pipeline... Level 3: Agreement analysis... scenarios with three-way disagreement... flagged for Level 4 review." [section 5.2] "Fair agreement... is expected: pragmatic utterances support multiple valid readings."
- **Break condition:** If the adjudication process is found to systematically favor certain interpretations (e.g., more negative emotions), the gold labels may be biased despite low κ.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Pragmatic reasoning
  - **Why needed:** The core task is inferring speaker emotion from indirect speech; without understanding how context, power, and social norms shape meaning beyond literal words, the benchmark's purpose is missed.
  - **Quick check:** If a subordinate says "I’ll handle it" with a sigh after being assigned extra work, what factors might make this sarcasm, strategic politeness, or passive aggression? How would you decide?
- **Concept:** Emotion taxonomies (Plutchik & VAD)
  - **Why needed:** Models must map ambiguous utterances to a fixed 8-class label set and dimensional ratings; understanding the structure (e.g., wheel adjacency) and difference between categorical vs. continuous representation is essential for interpreting results.
  - **Quick check:** How would you distinguish "anger" from "surprise" in a deflection scenario? Could valence/arousal ratings help even if the category is disputed?
- **Concept:** Inter-annotator agreement as a difficulty signal
  - **Why needed:** Human disagreement is central to CEI's design; learners must grasp why low κ is expected and informative, not a sign of bad data. This shifts evaluation from binary correctness to alignment with a distribution of human judgments.
  - **Quick check:** If three annotators give three different emotions for the same utterance, does that mean the annotation failed? What might it instead indicate about the scenario?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Dataset (300 scenarios with context, roles, utterance) -> Annotations (3 per scenario: Plutchik emotion + VAD + confidence) -> Quality-control outputs (flag logs, adjudication notes) -> Evaluation metrics (accuracy, macro-F1, subtype/power breakdowns) -> Prompt templates (zero-shot, CoT, few-shot) -> OOV harmonization mapping.
- **Critical path:** Load stratified splits -> iterate scenarios -> for each model/prompt mode: generate prediction -> map OOV labels -> compare to gold (majority vote) -> aggregate by subtype/power -> compute metrics -> analyze confusion matrices and VAD consistency.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Expert authorship vs. scale (300 items allow deep validation but limit diversity); multi-annotator labels preserve ambiguity but complicate metric design (majority vote vs. distributional scoring); explicit power relations enable targeted analysis but create imbalanced subgroups (72% peer).
- **Failure signatures:** Model accuracy near random (12.5%) across all subtypes; no improvement with CoT/few-shot; high OOV rate (>5%) indicating poor instruction following; subtype pattern opposite to human κ (e.g., worst on sarcasm where humans agree most); VAD-category inconsistency (e.g., "joy" with negative valence).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce zero-shot baselines: Run the provided script for all 7 models on the test split, confirm best accuracy ~25% and that no model exceeds human majority (54%). Check parse failure rates per model.
  2. Subtype stress test: Isolate each pragmatic subtype (60 scenarios) and compute per-subtype accuracy for your model. Compare to Table 9: does your model show the same difficulty ordering (sarcasm hardest, strategic politeness easiest)?
  3. Disagreement analysis: For scenarios with three-way human splits (31.3% of data), compute model accuracy. If model still performs poorly, it suggests failure on inherently ambiguous items; if model does well, it may be exploiting patterns humans miss. Also check valence polarity agreement in model predictions vs. human splits.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Does the low inter-annotator agreement on CEI primarily reflect genuine pragmatic ambiguity or limited annotator diversity and expertise?
  - **Basis:** The authors acknowledge all 15 annotators are undergraduate students from a single institution and state this is the most important future direction. They argue low κ is expected due to task difficulty but cannot rule out annotator inexperience or cultural homogeneity.
  - **Why unresolved:** The annotation pool lacks variation in professional training and cultural background. Without comparative data from diverse rater groups, we cannot disentangle inherent task ambiguity from annotator-specific effects.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-annotate with a demographically and professionally diverse group (e.g., cross-cultural samples, expert pragmatists). Compare agreement statistics; if κ rises substantially with experts, current scores overestimate task ambiguity.

- **Question:** To what extent do expert-authored scenarios generalize to naturalistic, spontaneous conversations where pragmatic cues are noisier and culturally variable?
  - **Basis:** The paper notes the dataset is "researcher-authored rather than drawn from naturalistic conversation" as a deliberate choice and acknowledges it may miss patterns from spontaneous interaction.
  - **Why unresolved:** CEI’s controlled design isolates phenomena but may lack ecological validity. Performance on curated scenarios may not predict success in real-world settings with disfluencies and cultural idioms.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Collect a naturalistic parallel dataset (e.g., transcribed dialogues from workplace meetings) annotated with the same CEI schema. Test whether models trained/evaluated on CEI transfer to natural data.

- **Question:** Why do language models show a reversed difficulty pattern compared to humans (worst on sarcasm, best on deflection), and what capabilities are they missing?
  - **Basis:** The paper highlights this dissociation: "sarcasm is the subtype where humans agree most yet models perform worst," while "deflection shows the lowest human agreement but middling model performance." They call this a key finding suggesting qualitatively different processing failures.
  - **Why unresolved:** The paper speculates sarcasm requires sensitivity to tonal incongruity and shared expectations, while deflection may rely on simpler sequential patterns. Without targeted probing, we do not know which specific contextual integration models fail at for each subtype.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct ablation studies that systematically vary contextual elements (remove power relations, simplify descriptions) and measure performance drops per subtype. Use probing classifiers on model internal states to see if sarcasm-related features are encoded but not utilized.

- **Question:** Can dimensional VAD ratings serve as a more reliable and informative continuous evaluation target than categorical emotion labels for pragmatic reasoning?
  - **Basis (inferred):** The paper collects VAD ratings and notes higher consistency on valence (ICC=0.40) than on categorical labels (κ=0.06–0.25). They observe that even in three-way categorical splits, annotators often agree on valence polarity and suggest VAD prediction could be a complementary target.
  - **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether models can predict VAD more accurately than categories, or whether VAD errors correlate with pragmatic failure types. The paper does not report model performance on VAD regression.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Run baseline models on the VAD annotation task (regression). Compare model-human correlation and error patterns to those from categorical classification. If models achieve higher VAD correlation, it would validate VAD as a more robust metric.

- **Question:** Does fine-tuning on CEI data improve pragmatic reasoning in downstream social AI tasks, or does the dataset's size and synthetic nature limit transfer?
  - **Basis:** The authors state CEI can enable research on "whether fine-tuning on CEI data improves performance on adjacent tasks such as SocialIQa or GoEmotions" and note the dataset is small (300 scenarios) and synthetic.
  - **Why unresolved:** It is unknown whether CEI’s expert-curated, multi-annotator data provides a better learning signal than larger crowdsourced benchmarks. Transfer could be limited by dataset scale, synthetic language, or narrow subtype coverage.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform supervised fine-tuning on CEI (using all annotations for soft-label training) and evaluate zero-shot transfer to related benchmarks. Compare to models fine-tuned on larger in-domain datasets. Measure whether CEI-finetuned models show subtype-specific improvements that generalize.

## Limitations
- **External validity:** 300 expert-authored scenarios may miss naturalistic pragmatic diversity and cultural/idiomatic variations; generalization beyond controlled settings is unproven.
- **Annotation philosophy:** Treating low inter-annotator agreement as a feature rather than noise is a conceptual choice; it remains debatable whether model alignment with majority vote is correct if genuine ambiguity exists.
- **Technical reproducibility:** Some evaluated models (e.g., GPT-5-mini, Gemini 2.5 Flash) are unreleased or internal, forcing substitutions that may alter results. The OOV mapping rule is underspecified beyond the released mapping table.

## Confidence
- **Expert-authored design & balanced subtype/power coverage:** **High** – Well-documented methodology, clear contrast with prior corpora.
- **Low agreement as inherent to pragmatic inference:** **Medium** – Supported by valence coherence analysis, but depends on theoretical stance about “correct” interpretation.
- **Quality-control pipeline distinguishes error vs. ambiguity:** **Medium** – Logically sound, but adjudication bias cannot be fully ruled out without inter-adjudicator agreement stats.
- **Performance gap & dissociation patterns:** **High** – Results are reproducible from provided scripts; statistical significance and consistency across models are clear.

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate subtype difficulty ordering under controlled model swaps:** Run the exact pipeline with substitute models (e.g., GPT-4o for GPT-5-mini) and verify whether the relative performance pattern (sarcasm hardest, strategic politeness easiest) holds. *Outcome:* Pattern reversal would challenge the robustness of the dissociation claim.
2. **Human–model ambiguity comparison on three-way splits:** For the 31.3% of scenarios with complete human disagreement, compute model accuracy and its VAD rating variance. *Outcome:* If models show high confidence (low VAD variance) on these items, it suggests they miss genuine ambiguity; if they match human distributional spread, it supports alignment.
3. **OOV mapping rule audit:** Generate novel OOV labels from models (e.g., “anticipation”, “frustration”) and apply the unreleased mapping algorithm. Cross-check with the released mapping table and NRC wheel adjacency to reverse-engineer the rule. *Outcome:* Inconsistent mapping would invalidate cross-model comparisons if OOV rates differ significantly.
