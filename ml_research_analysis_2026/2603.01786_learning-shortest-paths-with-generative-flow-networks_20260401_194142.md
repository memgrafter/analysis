# Learning Shortest Paths with Generative Flow Networks
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01786
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01786
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** Non‑acyclic GFlowNets with flow regularization learn policies that traverse exclusively shortest paths; exact optimal solutions on Swap puzzle and competitive results on Rubik's Cube with improved runtime.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of finding shortest paths in large discrete graphs, such as the Cayley graphs underlying Rubik's Cube. It proposes a novel framework based on non‑acyclic Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets) augmented with flow regularization. Theoretically, minimizing the expected trajectory length of the backward policy is shown to concentrate all probability mass on shortest paths between an initial state and terminal states. The authors validate their approach on synthetic Swap puzzles and 2×2×2 and 3×3×3 Rubik's Cubes, achieving exact shortest paths for Swap and competitive results on Rubik's Cube, notably using a drastically smaller beam width than baselines and reducing runtime per configuration. This work demonstrates the feasibility of using GFlowNets for optimal pathfinding in combinatorial spaces.

## Method Summary
Any unweighted shortest‑path problem is reformulated as a non‑acyclic GFlowNet environment. Given an original graph \(G\) with goal vertex \(v_g\), the construction reverses all edges, sets the initial state \(s_0 = v_g\), and adds a sink state \(s_f\) with transitions from every other state. Rewards are uniform (\(R(s)=1\)). A neural network with separate policy heads \(P_F\) (forward) and \(P_B\) (backward) is trained using a regularized trajectory balance loss: for sampled partial trajectories from \(P_F\), the loss combines the standard TB term with an additional term \(\lambda / P_F(s_f|s_i)\) for each prefix state, which encourages minimization of expected trajectory length. After training, beam search over \(P_B\) yields short (theoretically optimal) paths from any state to the goal.

## Key Results
- Exact shortest paths obtained on the Swap puzzle (n=15,20) across all test instances.
- On 2×2×2 Rubik's Cube, the method achieves optimal average solution length (10.64) with beam width 2, representing a 16× reduction in beam size compared to the state‑of‑the‑art baseline.
- On 3×3×3 Rubik's Cube, the method produces shorter solutions than the baseline at small beam widths and reduces per‑configuration runtime from 6.19 s to 1.74 s.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Minimizing the expected trajectory length `E[nτ]` of a non‑acyclic GFlowNet's backward policy `PB` is mathematically equivalent to concentrating all probability mass on shortest paths between the initial state `s0` and terminal states `X`.
- **Mechanism:** The proof constructs a lower bound (Lemma 3.2): `E[nτ] ≥ Σ_{x∈X} PB(x|sf) ℓ(x)`, where `ℓ(x)` is the shortest path length. It then shows a specific `PB` (Lemma 3.3) that achieves this bound by always moving along shortest paths (`par(s)` selects a predecessor on a shortest path). Theorem 3.4 concludes that any `PB` achieving the minimum must have `P(τ)=0` for any trajectory `τ` with length `nτ > ℓ(x)`.
- **Core assumption:** `PB` satisfies `E[nτ] < ∞` (Assumption 3.1), ensuring `PB` defines a valid probability distribution over trajectories and that the random walk from `sf` absorbs at `s0` with probability 1.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We prove that, if the total flow is minimized, forward and backward policies traverse the environment graph exclusively along shortest paths..."
  - [section 3.1] Theorem 3.4 and its proof, particularly the combination of Lemma 3.2 (lower bound) and Lemma 3.3 (achievability).
  - [corpus] Weak direct support; neighbor papers (e.g., "Revisiting Non‑Acyclic GFlowNets") discuss non‑acyclic extensions but do not establish this specific shortest‑path equivalence.
- **Break condition:** If `E[nτ]` cannot be minimized (e.g., due to poor optimization or incorrect `λ`), `PB` will assign non‑zero probability to longer, non‑shortest paths.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Adding a regularization term `λ * Fθ(s)` (state flow) to the training loss (e.g., Trajectory Balance) encourages the learned `PB` to minimize `E[nτ]`, thereby inducing shortest‑path behavior.
- **Mechanism:** In the constructed environment (Section 3.2), the state flow `F(s)` is proportional to `R(s)/PF(sf|s)` (Equation derived in Section 3.3). Regularizing with `λFθ(s)` penalizes high flow (i.e., frequent visitation) in non‑terminal states. Since minimizing total flow `Σ F(s)` is equivalent to minimizing `E[nτ]` (Morozov et al., 2025), this regularization pushes the model toward the `PB` with the smallest `E[nτ]`, which Theorem 3.4 shows must travel only on shortest paths.
- **Core assumption:** The regularization coefficient `λ` is chosen such that it influences optimization without destroying the ability to satisfy the core GFlowNet consistency conditions (reward matching and trajectory balance). The parameterization `F(s) = R(s)/PF(sf|s)` holds given the environment's structure (every non‑`sf` state has a terminal transition).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.1] "A simple practical approach... involves adding a regularizer `λFθ(s)` to the utilized loss."
  - [section 3.3.2] "To add state flow regularization... one can note that... the flow can be expressed as `F(s) = R(s)/PF(sf|s)`." and Equation (8) defining `LregTB`.
  - [section 4.3] Experimental ablation showing `λ` critically affects solution quality; too small yields longer paths, too large causes failure to find valid paths.
- **Break condition:** If `λ` is too large, the model prioritizes minimizing flow over satisfying reward matching/trajectory balance, failing to learn a valid `PB` (Figure 3). If `λ=0`, the model may not minimize `E[nτ]` and will learn longer paths.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Any shortest‑path problem on an arbitrary unweighted graph `G=(V,E)` with goal `vg` can be solved by training a non‑acyclic GFlowNet on a derived environment `G̃` where:
  1. States `S = V ∪ {sf}`, initial state `s0 = vg`.
  2. Transitions `Ẽ` are reversed edges from `G` plus `(s→sf)` for all `s≠sf`.
  3. Rewards `R(s)=1` for all `s∈V` (uniform).
- **Mechanism:** Reversing edges makes `PB`'s backward moves in `G̃` correspond to forward moves toward `vg` in the original `G`. Since `s0=vg` has no outgoing edges in `G̃` (by removing `vg`'s outgoing edges from `G`), `PB` starting from any state `s` must reach `s0` via reversed edges—a path that is a shortest path *to* `vg` in the original graph *from* `s`. Minimizing `E[nτ]` for this `PB` (Theorem 3.4) ensures it traverses only these shortest paths. The uniform reward makes the optimal `E[nτ]` equal the average shortest‑path length to `vg`.
- **Core assumption:** The original graph `G` is finite, unweighted, and every vertex has a path to `vg`. The construction correctly reverses the graph and adds a sink.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] "The core idea of our approach is to slightly modify the graph `G` to define such a non‑acyclic GFlowNet environment..." and the bullet‑point construction steps.
  - [section 3.2] "Therefore, training a non‑acyclic GFlowNet that minimizes its expected trajectory length will result in a model that solves the pathfinding task in the graph `G`."
  - [section 4.1] Experimental validation on Swap puzzle (a Cayley graph of `S_n`) and Rubik's Cube (Cayley graph of the puzzle group) confirms the construction works for large, structured graphs.
- **Break condition:** If the graph `G` is weighted, the construction as described (unweighted reversal) does not directly apply; the shortest paths would need to account for edge costs. If `G` has vertices with no path to `vg`, Assumption 2.1 fails and the GFlowNet environment is invalid.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Non‑Acyclic GFlowNets & Flow Regularization**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper builds directly on the theory of GFlowNets in cyclic environments (Morozov et al., 2025). Understanding how policies `PF`/`PB` induce a trajectory distribution, the role of state flows `F(s)`, and how flow regularization minimizes `E[nτ]` is essential to grasp the core mechanism.
  - *Quick check question:* Given a GFlowNet environment where every non‑sink state has a transition to `sf`, why is `F(s) = R(s)/PF(sf|s)` a valid flow parameterization?
- **Concept: Graph Theory for Pathfinding**
  - *Why needed here:* The reduction (Mechanism 3) relies on graph concepts: vertices, edges, shortest path length `ℓ(v)`, and Cayley graphs for Rubik's Cube. Misunderstanding the graph transformation (reversing edges, setting `s0=vg`) would break the mapping.
  - *Quick check question:* In the constructed GFlowNet environment for a graph `G`, what does a trajectory sampled via `PB` from a state `s` (corresponding to vertex `v`) represent in the original graph `G`?
- **Concept: Beam Search as Inference‑Time Heuristic**
  - *Why needed here:* The learned `PB` is approximate. Beam search (Section 3.4) is used at test‑time to approximate the trajectory with highest probability under `PB`, which for a near‑optimal `PB` should be a shortest path. Understanding its role (vs. greedy `argmax`) and the cost (beam width `W`) is key to interpreting results.
  - *Quick check question:* Why does increasing beam width `W` in this framework tend to produce shorter solutions, and what is the computational trade‑off?

## Architecture Onboarding
Environment Builder → Neural Model → Trajectory Sampler → Loss Calculator → Optimizer → Beam Search → Solutions

**Critical path:** The environment builder takes the original graph G and produces the reversed G̃ with sink sf and uniform reward. The neural model (MLP with separate PF and PB heads) is initialized. During training, the trajectory sampler draws partial trajectories from PF (with stop action masked). For each prefix, the loss calculator computes the regularized trajectory balance loss `LregTB = LTB + λ / PF(sf|si)`. The optimizer (AdamW with gradient clipping) updates the neural model. After convergence, the beam search module uses the trained PB to explore high‑probability paths from test states to the goal, producing final solutions.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- `Nmax` (training trajectory horizon): Too small limits exploration of distant states; too large increases training cost. Chosen empirically per domain (e.g., 12 for 2×2×2, 24 for 3×3×3).
- `λ` (flow regularization strength): Controls push to minimize `E[nτ]`. High `λ` risks breaking consistency; low `λ` yields suboptimal paths. Requires careful tuning (e.g., 1e‑3 on Swap, 1e‑2 on 2×2×2, 5e‑7 on 3×3×3).
- `W` (beam width): Larger `W` improves solution quality (better approximation of optimal PB trajectory) but linearly increases test‑time compute.

**Failure signatures:**
- Training loss diverges or `PF(sf|s)` collapses to zero everywhere: likely `λ` too high or architectural issue.
- Model finds valid paths but lengths significantly above optimal: `λ` too low, or insufficient capacity/training steps.
- Greedy (`W=1`) fails while beam (`W>1`) succeeds: PB not peaked enough; beam compensates.
- `PF` during training never reaches non‑`s0` states: `Nmax` too small or sampling/reward misconfiguration.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Swap Puzzle (n=15):** Verify theory on a simple Cayley graph; test if greedy/beam achieves average length equal to optimal inversion count; tune `λ` (1e‑3).
2. **2×2×2 Rubik's Cube:** Test on ~3.6M‑state graph; compare against CayleyPy baseline; expect optimal solutions (avg 10.64) with tiny beam (`W=2`); tune `λ` (~1e‑2).
3. **3×3×3 Rubik's Cube:** Stress test on ~4.3×10¹⁹‑state graph; expect competitive lengths; verify runtime advantage (1.74 s vs 6.19 s); tune `λ` very small (5e‑7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the GFlowNet framework extend to weighted shortest path problems with non‑uniform edge costs?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion calls for extending "to weighted graphs and cost‑sensitive settings."
- **Why unresolved:** The current theory relies on minimizing expected step count, which is equivalent to shortest paths only for unit weights; weighted shortest paths require minimizing total edge weight instead.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Modify the flow objective to incorporate edge weights, prove a weighted analogue of Theorem 3.4, and test on benchmarks like weighted grid‑worlds or puzzles with variable move costs.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does the method scale to extremely large graphs, such as 4×4×4 Rubik's Cube puzzles with ~10⁴⁵ states?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion identifies exploration of "scalability to extremely large graphs" as key future work.
- **Why unresolved:** Experiments were limited to 2×2×2 and 3×3×3 cubes; larger instances expose generalization limits due to minimal training coverage and potential approximation errors.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train on 4×4×4 and larger cubes, measure solve rates and optimality gaps versus training budget, and compare with state‑of‑the‑art heuristic‑based methods.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does trajectory balance loss outperform detailed balance for training shortest‑path GFlowNets?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state they "leave the examination of this phenomenon for further study" regarding TB's more efficient credit assignment.
- **Why unresolved:** The empirical advantage is noted but not explained; understanding could yield better training algorithms for GFlowNets in pathfinding and other domains.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare gradient statistics, convergence curves, and sensitivity to hyperparameters; theoretically analyze how each loss propagates reward information through trajectories.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can entropy‑regularized Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) improve test‑time solution quality compared to beam search?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper mentions MCTS as "an interesting direction for future research" for test‑time search with GFlowNets.
- **Why unresolved:** Only beam search was evaluated; MCTS might better balance exploration and exploitation under uncertainty in the backward policy, potentially finding shorter paths.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement entropy‑regularized MCTS using the trained backward policy, benchmark on Rubik's Cube and permutation puzzles, and compare solution lengths, success rates, and computational cost against beam search.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees require exact minimization of `E[nτ]` and hold only for finite, unweighted graphs where every state can reach the goal; approximate training may not achieve global optimum.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity: flow regularization coefficient `λ` and trajectory horizon `Nmax` need domain‑specific tuning; no general selection principle is provided.
- Evaluation is narrow, covering only two families of permutation puzzles; generalization to other discrete graphs (weighted, directed, real‑world planning) remains unknown.

## Confidence
- Theoretical claim (minimizing `E[nτ]` forces shortest‑path‑only policies): **High**
- Empirical success on Swap and Rubik's Cube: **Medium**
- Runtime efficiency versus CayleyPy baseline: **Medium**
