# Does the Question Really Matter? Training-Free Data Selection for Vision-Language SFT

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09715
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09715
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: CVS selects 10–15% of data to achieve >103% average relative performance (ARP), outperforming full-data training (ARP=100).

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses a critical issue in visual instruction tuning: many multimodal samples can be solved via linguistic shortcuts without genuine cross-modal reasoning, degrading model performance. The authors propose Conditional Verdict Shift (CVS), a training-free data selection method. CVS uses a frozen vision-language model to measure how much the inclusion of a question changes the model's assessment of an answer's validity given an image. Samples where the question substantially alters the judgment are prioritized as they require true vision-language joint reasoning, while semantically inconsistent or noisy samples are filtered out. On the Vision-Flan dataset, using only 10% or 15% of CVS-selected data achieves average relative performance (ARP) of 103.5% and 104.8%, respectively, outperforming full-data training (ARP=100). On The Cauldron dataset, CVS reduces computational cost by 17.3% and 44.4% compared to state-of-the-art methods COINCIDE and XMAS, while maintaining robust performance. The method is training-free, efficient, and demonstrates consistent effectiveness across diverse datasets and evaluation setups.

## Method Summary
CVS is a training-free data selection method for vision-language SFT. It employs a frozen VLLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct) to evaluate each sample (I, Q, A) under two conditions: with the question (full context) and without the question (prior context, only image and answer). The model outputs probabilities for YES/NO to the query “Is the answer correct?”. CVS computes the logarithmic ratio of YES probabilities: CVS_YES = log(P(YES|I,Q,A) / P(YES|I,A)). A positive shift indicates the question provides non-redundant information gain. Samples are filtered to retain those with CVS_YES > 0 and CVS_NO < 0 (ensuring semantic consistency). From the filtered set, the samples with the lowest CVS_YES values are selected, as these represent “hard positives” where the question modestly increases confidence, implying genuine joint reasoning. The selected subset (e.g., 10–15% of the original pool) is then used to fine-tune a target VLM (e.g., LLaVA-1.5-7B) via LoRA. The entire selection process requires only inference with the frozen evaluator, no additional training.

## Key Results
- On Vision-Flan, 10% CVS-selected data yields ARP 103.5%, and 15% yields 104.8%, both surpassing full-data training (ARP=100).
- On The Cauldron, CVS reduces computational cost by 17.3% vs. COINCIDE and 44.4% vs. XMAS while maintaining robust performance.
- The method is training-free, efficient, and effective across diverse datasets and evaluation setups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Conditional shift as information gain**  
CVS identifies samples requiring genuine vision-language joint reasoning by measuring how much the question modulates the model’s judgment on answer validity. For a sample (I, Q, A), CVS computes CVS_YES = log(P(YES|I,Q,A) / P(YES|I,A)). A significant positive shift indicates the question provides non-redundant information gain, meaning the answer’s validity cannot be assessed from the image alone and requires joint reasoning. The core assumption is that a frozen, capable VLLM’s zero-shot judgment on answer validity conditional on an image provides a stable baseline. Evidence: Section 3.3.1 defines the shift as quantifying information gain. Break condition: If the evaluator is weak or miscalibrated such that P(YES|I,A) depends on linguistic patterns, the shift won’t isolate the question’s contribution.

**Mechanism 2: Low CVS_YES prioritizes hard positives**  
Selecting samples with low positive CVS_YES (near the decision boundary) prioritizes “hard positives” that yield stronger learning signals for joint reasoning than easy, high-confidence samples. High CVS_YES suggests strong linguistic shortcuts or common-sense priors between Q and A, allowing the model to answer correctly while largely ignoring I. Low CVS_YES indicates the model’s confidence in the answer’s correctness is modestly increased by the question, implying the visual evidence I is essential but the reasoning is non-trivial, forcing explicit integration of both modalities during training. Evidence: Section 3.3.3 states that lower CVS_YES reflects non-trivial inference; Section 5.1 shows Low strategy consistently outperforms High and random, with High performing significantly worse than random. Break condition: If the data pool contains too few samples where visual context is genuinely necessary, the Low strategy may select noisy or ambiguous samples, harming performance (e.g., at 5%).

**Mechanism 3: Visual anchoring is critical**  
The visual anchoring term P(YES|I,A) (keeping the image in the denominator) is critical to distinguish samples requiring vision-language reasoning from those with purely textual or noisy signals. Using P(YES|A) as a baseline would reflect mutual information between (I,Q) and textual prior of A, corrupting selection toward low-correlation or low-quality samples. The visual baseline ensures low CVS_YES correctly implies the question improves confidence in the correct answer in a non-trivial manner, requiring feature alignment grounded in visual priors. Evidence: Section 5.4 ablation shows CVS without visual anchoring underperforms random by >10 percentage points. Break condition: If the visual modality is extremely noisy or the task is inherently text-heavy, the visual baseline may be too weak, reducing discriminative power.

## Foundational Learning
- **Sample Value Function V(s)**: Why needed: CVS operationalizes the abstract goal of maximizing learning signal. V(s) is not a scalar but a pair of conditional shifts and a selection predicate that instantiate “vision-language joint reasoning utility.” Quick check: Given (I,Q,A), if P(YES|I,Q,A) and P(YES|I,A) are both high (CVS_YES near zero), what does this imply about the sample’s value, and why might it still be selected if CVS_NO < 0?
- **Frozen Evaluator as a Zero-Shot Discriminator**: Why needed: The entire method depends on using a single frozen VLLM to evaluate answer validity in two contexts, avoiding costly proxy training. Understanding that the model’s output distribution over {YES, NO} can be treated as a calibrated belief about propositional validity is key. Quick check: Why is using the same frozen model for both conditional probabilities crucial, compared to using two different models or a specially trained evaluator?
- **Less is More Regime from Distributional Data Selection Theory**: Why needed: Section 3.1 frames VIT training as an interaction between a Generator and an Evaluator. The paper’s results (outperforming full-data training with 10–15% data) are a practical instance of this regime, where a strong evaluator filters data to align the training distribution better with the true task distribution. ## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Pool -> Frozen Evaluator VLLM -> CVS Scorer -> Alignment Filter -> Subset Selector -> Target Model Trainer

**Critical path:** For each sample in pool -> Evaluate with frozen VLLM (2 inferences per sample) -> Compute CVS scores -> Apply C_align filter -> Rank remaining by low CVS_YES -> Select top K% -> Train target model.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Evaluator choice vs. Cost: Stronger, larger evaluators improve selection quality but increase inference cost.
- Threshold rigidity: Using a hard threshold of 0 for C_align and ranking by CVS_YES is simple and effective; tuning per-dataset could help but is not necessary.
- Computational vs. Clustering Methods: CVS trades high memory/compute of clustering methods for repeated inference; more efficient on GPU-hours but potentially slower wall-clock.
- Generality vs. Specialization: CVS aims for general joint reasoning utility; may be less optimal than task-specific selectors when structural redundancy is the sole noise source.

**Failure signatures:**
1. Evaluator miscalibration: If the frozen VLLM is poor at judging answer validity given an image (P(YES|I,A) is noisy), all CVS scores are meaningless.
2. Absence of linguistic shortcuts: If the dataset is already clean and all samples genuinely require vision, CVS_YES distribution may have little variance, making ranking arbitrary.
3. Severe visual degradation: If images are low quality such that P(YES|I,A) is uniformly random, the conditional shift cannot isolate Q’s contribution.
4. Task distribution shift: The selected subset may over-represent tasks where CVS scores vary most, potentially under-representing tasks with medium-difficulty reasoning.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Reproduce main result on Vision-Flan: Implement CVS with Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, select 10% and 15% via Low strategy after C_align, fine-tune LLaVA-1.5-7B, and evaluate on 12 benchmarks; expect ARP ~103.5 and 104.8 vs. full-data ARP=100.
2. Test evaluator robustness: Repeat experiment 1 but swap evaluator to InternVL3-8B or Qwen2.5-VL-3B; expect consistent outperformance of random, with larger evaluator giving a small boost.
3. Validate visual anchoring necessity: Ablate by computing CVS_YES using P(YES|A) instead of P(YES|I,A) as denominator; apply same Low selection and train; expect significant performance drop vs. random and standard CVS.

## Limitations
- Unspecified details (prompt templates, LoRA hyperparameters) may cause implementation drift.
- CVS relies on a strong, well-calibrated evaluator; weak evaluators degrade selection quality.
- The “Low” strategy fails at very low data budgets (e.g., 5%), indicating cold-start issues.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Info gain via conditional shift): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (Low CVS_YES = hard positives): High
- Mechanism 3 (Visual anchoring necessity): High
