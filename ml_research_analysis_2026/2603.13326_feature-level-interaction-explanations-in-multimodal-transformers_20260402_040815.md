# Feature-level Interaction Explanations in Multimodal Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.13326  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13326  
- **Reference count**: 25  
- **Primary result**: FL-I2MoE improves task performance (+7.5 micro-F1 on MM-IMDb, +5.8 accuracy on ENRICO) and yields more faithful cross-modal interaction explanations.

## Executive Summary
Multimodal Transformers lack fine-grained explanations of how different modalities interact at the feature level. This paper introduces Feature-level I2MoE (FL-I2MoE), a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer applied to token and patch sequences from frozen modality encoders. FL-I2MoE explicitly separates unique, synergistic, and redundant evidence via dedicated experts and a reweighting network. During inference, expert-wise importance maps are obtained by combining gradient signals with attention rollout (Grad×AttnRoll). Candidate feature pairs are then evaluated using Monte Carlo estimates of the Shapley Interaction Index (for synergy) and a redundancy-gap score (for redundancy). Experiments on MM-IMDb, ENRICO, and MMHS150K show that FL-I2MoE produces more concentrated importance patterns (larger performance drops under masking) and outperforms dense Transformers and the original I2MoE. Pair-level masking of top-ranked interactions leads to significantly larger performance degradation than random masking, confirming the causal relevance of identified pairs. Additionally, higher expert-importance bins correlate with stronger measured interactions, aligning the metrics.

## Method Summary
FL-I2MoE processes inputs through frozen modality encoders (e.g., CLIP ViT for images, RoBERTa for text) to obtain token/patch sequences. These sequences are concatenated, projected to a common dimension, and fed into a fusion Transformer. The joint representation is then dispatched to three expert networks—uniqueness, synergy, and redundancy—each producing a class logit. A reweighting network generates non‑negative mixture weights, and the final prediction is the weighted sum of expert logits. Training uses standard cross‑entropy loss plus an interaction loss that encourages experts to specialize in their designated roles. For explanation, Grad×AttnRoll (gradient × attention rollout) is computed per expert to produce token‑wise importance scores. From the top‑ρ% important features, all cross‑modal pairs are considered as candidates. The Shapley Interaction Index (synergy) and redundancy‑gap (redundancy) are estimated via Monte Carlo sampling over random masked coalitions. Pairs are ranked by these scores and their relevance is validated through masking experiments.

## Key Results
- FL-I2MoE yields more concentrated importance patterns than a dense Transformer: masking top‑K% features identified by expert attribution causes larger performance drops.
- It achieves significant task improvements: e.g., +7.5 micro‑F1 on MM‑IMDb and +5.8 accuracy on ENRICO over the dense baseline and original I2MoE.
- Pair‑level masking of top‑ranked synergistic or redundant pairs leads to significantly larger degradation than random masking, confirming the causal relevance of the detected interactions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
FL-I2MoE’s effectiveness stems from three interconnected mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1: Structural separation of interaction types via MoE.**  
The model uses a Mixture‑of‑Experts layer with three dedicated experts (uniqueness, synergy, redundancy) trained with an interaction loss. This forces each expert to compute a distinct type of cross‑modal evidence. The reweighting network combines the expert outputs into the final prediction. Evidence includes the expert‑wise importance maps (Fig. 3) and the fact that FL‑I2MoE outperforms baselines, indicating useful specialization.

**Mechanism 2: Expert‑wise Grad×AttnRoll provides faithful attribution.**  
For each expert, gradients of the true‑class logit are multiplied by attention rollout aggregated across fusion layers, yielding token‑wise importance specific to that expert’s role. Masking features according to these scores causes a larger performance drop than random masking or masking in a dense Transformer, demonstrating that the scores highlight causally relevant features (Section 4.2, Fig. 3).

**Mechanism 3: Monte Carlo interaction metrics reliably identify synergistic and redundant pairs.**  
After selecting candidate features from the importance maps, cross‑modal pairs are evaluated using Monte Carlo approximations: Shapley Interaction Index (SII) for synergy and a redundancy‑gap score for redundancy. Positive SII indicates that the pair’s joint effect exceeds the sum of individual effects; high redundancy‑gap indicates that one feature alone often suffices. Ranking pairs by these scores yields larger performance drops when those pairs are masked (Table 1). Moreover, higher importance bins correlate with higher interaction scores (Fig. 4), validating the metrics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)**  
  *Why needed*: Core architecture that separates unique, synergistic, and redundant evidence into distinct computational pathways.  
  *Quick check*: How does the reweighting network ensure each expert contributes, and what role does the interaction loss play in shaping specializations?

- **Shapley Interaction Index (SII) & Partial Information Decomposition (PID)**  
  *Why needed*: Provide formal definitions of synergy and redundancy used to compute interaction metrics.  
  *Quick check*: What is the mathematical difference between a positive SII score and a high redundancy‑gap score, and what does each imply about the modalities’ joint contribution?

- **Feature Attribution in Transformers (Grad×AttnRoll, Attention Rollout)**  
  *Why needed*: Produces per‑expert importance maps that identify crucial tokens/patches for each interaction type.  
  *Quick check*: Why might Grad×AttnRoll be more faithful than pure attention rollout or integrated gradients in this multimodal MoE setup?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Input1 -> Encoder1 -> H1; Input2 -> Encoder2 -> H2; Concat(H1, H2) -> Project -> Fusion Transformer -> Joint representation R; R -> Reweighting network W -> weights λ; R -> Expert_Uniqueness -> logit_u; R -> Expert_Synergy -> logit_s; R -> Expert_Redundancy -> logit_r; Final prediction = λ_u·logit_u + λ_s·logit_s + λ_r·logit_r. Inference: For each expert, compute Grad×AttnRoll on its logit w.r.t. input features to get importance; select top‑ρ% features as candidates; estimate SII (synergy) or redundancy‑gap (redundancy) for each cross‑modal pair via Monte Carlo masked evaluations.

**Critical path**  
Data → frozen encoders → concatenated joint sequence → fusion Transformer → [reweighting network + three experts] → weighted sum logits → loss (cross‑entropy + interaction loss).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Frozen encoders isolate the interaction module and reduce compute but prevent modality‑specific adaptation.  
- Restricting candidate pairs to top‑ρ% important features makes pairwise interaction estimation tractable but may miss some interactions.  
- Increasing Monte Carlo samples improves metric stability but raises computational cost quadratically.

**Failure signatures**  
- Poor expert specialization: experts show similar importance maps and masking performance; SII/redundancy‑gap fail to distinguish importance bins.  
- Unfaithful attribution: Grad×AttnRoll masking drops nearly match random masking.  
- Misaligned interactions: masking top‑ranked synergistic/redundant pairs yields no significant gain over random masking.  
- Training instability: high variance in task metrics across seeds, indicating interaction loss not reliably inducing specialization.

**First three experiments**  
1. Replicate top‑K% feature masking per expert on all datasets; confirm FL‑I2MoE’s performance drop exceeds random and dense Transformer baselines.  
2. For synergy and redundancy experts, split top‑30% features into bins and verify monotonic increase in mean SII/redundancy‑gap across bins using Monte Carlo samples.  
3. Mask top 5% pairs ranked by interaction scores and compare degradation to masking an equal number of random pairs; expect significantly larger drop for interaction‑ranked pairs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Efficiency of Monte Carlo estimation** – How can the computational cost of estimating SII and redundancy‑gap be reduced while maintaining accuracy? The authors note future work will improve Monte Carlo efficiency.  
2. **Extension to >2 modalities and higher‑order interactions** – Can FL‑I2MoE be generalized to three or more modalities and capture interactions beyond pairs? The paper suggests this as a direction.  
3. **Robustness to modality noise/missingness** – How stable are the identified synergistic and redundant pairs when one modality is corrupted or absent? The authors list robustness tests under noise and missingness as future work.  
4. **Impact of attribution method choice** – How does using an alternative attribution method (e.g., integrated gradients) instead of Grad×AttnRoll affect the discovered interactions? The paper relies solely on Grad×AttnRoll.

## Limitations
- The exact formulation and weighting of the interaction loss (L_int) from the original I2MoE are not provided, hindering exact replication.  
- Key training hyperparameters (learning rate schedule, batch size, early stopping) are omitted.  
- No statistical significance testing accompanies the reported performance gains, leaving uncertainty about the robustness of improvements.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Expert separation via MoE): Medium  
- Mechanism 2 (Grad×AttnRoll faithfulness): Medium  
- Mechanism 3 (Monte Carlo interaction metrics): Medium
