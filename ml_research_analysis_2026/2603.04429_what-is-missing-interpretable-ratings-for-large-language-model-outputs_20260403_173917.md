# What Is Missing: Interpretable Ratings for Large Language Model Outputs
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.04429
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04429
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** WIM reduces tied pairwise ratings from 42.78% to 2.00%, increases average rating delta by 47.82%, cuts DPO training loss by 2.95×, and yields a non‑significant 3.79% relative win‑rate gain on a downstream task.

## Executive Summary
Current preference learning for large language models relies on subjective numerical ratings that serve as poor proxies for quality and often produce many tied comparisons. The authors introduce the What Is Missing (WIM) rating system, where a judge writes natural‑language feedback describing omissions in a response; the cosine similarity between the response and critique embeddings becomes a continuous scalar rating. WIM dramatically reduces rating ties (from 42.78% to 2.00%) and increases rating separation (47.82% higher average delta) compared to a 1–10 numerical scale. When fed into Online DPO, WIM lowers training loss by a factor of 2.95× and modestly improves downstream win‑rate (52.0% vs. 50.1%). Crucially, each scalar rating remains directly traceable to its critique, enabling qualitative debugging of model outputs.

## Method Summary
WIM replaces numerical scoring with a two‑step pipeline. First, given a prompt, the actor LLM generates n candidate responses. A judge (human or LLM) reviews each response and, instead of assigning a number, writes a brief “what is missing” critique. Both the response and critique are embedded using a sentence encoder (e.g., all‑mpnet‑base‑v2). The WIM rating is the cosine similarity of these embeddings, ranging from -1 to 1; a missing‑free output (identical critique) yields a rating of 1. Optionally, the WIM score can be mixed with a traditional numerical rating via reward = (1–ζ)·R + ζ·WIM. The n responses are then ranked by their final scores, and the top‑ranked response is treated as chosen while lower‑ranked ones become rejected. These pairs train the actor via Online DPO (or a similar preference algorithm) with a LoRA‑adapted model. Key hyperparameters include β=0.1, temperature 0.9, batch size 64, and training on multiple GPUs for ~200 hours. The system is agnostic to the underlying preference learner and embedding model, but performance hinges on consistent judge behavior and encoder sensitivity.

## Key Results
- Tied pairwise ratings dropped from 42.78% (numerical) to 2.00% with WIM; average rating delta increased by 47.82%.
- Under Online DPO, WIM reduced training loss by a factor of 2.95× compared to the numerical baseline.
- On a downstream evaluation, WIM achieved a 52.0% win‑rate vs. 50.1% for numerical ratings—a 3.79% relative improvement that was not statistically significant.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Reduction of Ties via Continuous‑Like Scores
- **Claim:** WIM reduces the frequency of tied pairwise ratings compared to discrete numerical scales.  
- **Mechanism:** Instead of picking an integer from a small set, the judge produces a free‑form textual critique. The cosine similarity between the output and critique embeddings yields a scalar in a continuous‑like range. Small semantic differences lead to distinct similarity values, making exact ties extremely unlikely.  
- **Core assumption:** The embedding model captures meaningful semantic differences such that cosine similarity correlates with “missingness”.  
- **Evidence:** The paper reports ties decreasing from 42.78% to 2.00% and a 47.82% rise in average delta. Histograms (Figures 3, 4) show a spread of scores.  
- **Break condition:** If critiques become formulaic or the embedder cannot resolve subtle differences, the score distribution may recluster, increasing ties.

### Mechanism 2: Interpretability via Critiqued Ratings
- **Claim:** Each WIM rating is directly traceable to a natural‑language critique, enabling qualitative debugging.  
- **Mechanism:** The scalar is computed from a preserved critique text. Inspecting the critique reveals exactly what the judge considered missing, providing actionable feedback for model developers.  
- **Core assumption:** Judges can produce concise, relevant critiques that reflect genuine deficiencies in the response.  
- **Evidence:** The abstract and Section A.4 case studies show critiques like “missing key point about X” that explain the rating.  
- **Break condition:** Judges that ignore instructions (e.g., output “wim”) render the rating opaque; consistent instruction‑following is required.

### Mechanism 3: Stronger Learning Signal for Preference Optimization
- **Claim:** WIM’s higher rating deltas and lower tie rate produce a more informative gradient for DPO.  
- **Mechanism:** DPO’s loss depends on the reward advantage between chosen and rejected responses. Larger rating separations yield larger advantages, leading to larger gradients when the model’s implicit reward predictions are inaccurate. With fewer ties, almost every pair contributes a non‑zero advantage, avoiding uninformative updates.  
- **Core assumption:** A larger numeric gap between chosen and rejected ratings translates to a more useful signal for policy improvement.  
- **Evidence:** Training loss decreased by 2.95× under WIM, and reward advantage trajectories changed more significantly over time (Figure 5).  
- **Break condition:** If the embedding‑based scores do not correlate with true preference quality (e.g., critiques are orthogonal), the larger deltas may mislead the optimizer. Non‑stationary judges (Changing Judge) also harm performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cosine similarity in embedding space:** Measures directional alignment between two text vectors. *Why needed:* It is the core computation for turning a critique into a WIM score. *Quick check:* Why does a perfectly comprehensive output (nothing missing) receive a WIM score of 1? Because the critique would be identical to the output, yielding cosine similarity 1.  
- **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):** A method that avoids training a separate reward model by directly optimizing the policy with a binary cross‑entropy loss on pairwise preferences. *Why needed:* WIM ratings are used as implicit rewards in DPO; the gradient magnitude depends on the reward difference between chosen and rejected responses. *Quick check:* What happens to the DPO gradient if the chosen and rejected responses have nearly identical implicit rewards? The σ term in the gradient (Equation 7) becomes near 0, yielding a vanishing update.  
- **Sentence embedding models (e.g., all‑mpnet‑base‑v2):** Encode variable‑length text into fixed‑dimensional vectors that capture semantics. *Why needed:* They convert raw output and critique texts into the vectors used for cosine similarity. *Quick check:* What property might cause two semantically similar but lexically different critiques about the same missing point to yield a high similarity score? The model’s training objective encourages mapping of semantically equivalent sentences to nearby vectors regardless of surface wording.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Base LLM (Actor) → Judge → Sentence Encoder → Scoring Module → Preference Sorter → Preference Trainer

**Critical Path:**  
The judge and the sentence encoder must be applied consistently across all n responses for a given prompt. Any variability in judge behavior or encoder implementation across responses can invalidate the relative ratings and corrupt the preference pairs used for training.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- **Judge type:** A Fixed Judge (frozen reference model) gives a stable critique distribution but may not adapt to the actor’s improving capabilities. A Changing Judge (the current actor) yields critiques aligned with the latest model but introduces non‑stationarity, often hurting optimization.  
- **Mixing coefficient ζ:** ζ=0 uses pure numerical ratings; ζ=1 uses pure WIM. Intermediate values blend signals. Higher ζ leverages continuity but risks poor performance if critique quality degrades.  
- **Embedding model choice:** More powerful encoders (e.g., all‑mpnet‑base‑v2) detect subtle differences, producing a finely graded score distribution. Weaker encoders may collapse scores, increasing ties and diminishing the WIM advantage. They also differ in computational cost.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- **Clumped score distribution:** If the histogram of WIM ratings resembles that of numerical scales (few distinct values), the encoder or judge is not providing fine‑grained distinctions.  
- **High tie rate after WIM:** >10% ties suggests critiques are failing to differentiate outputs or the embedding model is insensitive.  
- **Judge instruction‑following errors:** Outputs like “wim” instead of a critique break interpretability; spot‑checking feedback is necessary.  
- **Unstable reward advantage:** When using a Changing Judge, the advantage trajectory may be erratic or even decrease, indicating non‑stationary targets.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **Core metric replication:** Run Online DPO on UltraFeedback‑prompt with WIM (Fixed Judge) and with numerical ratings. Confirm reductions in ties (42.78%→2.00%), increase in delta (+47.82%), and a 2.95× drop in training loss. Compare reward advantage curves.  
2. **Judge consistency check:** For a fixed set of prompts, obtain multiple independent WIM rankings from the same judge (different random seeds). Compute inter‑ranking agreement (e.g., Kendall’s τ) and compare to agreement from numerical ratings; expect WIM to show higher consistency due to finer granularity.  
3. **Embedding ablation:** Substitute the default encoder with a weaker alternative (all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2). Measure the impact on tie rate, delta distribution, and final downstream win‑rate to quantify the encoder’s influence on WIM’s effectiveness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Limitations of WIM with LLM judges:** What are the key failure modes when using LLMs as judges, and how sensitive are results to prompt engineering and the judge’s instruction‑following abilities? The paper mentions this as a next step but provides only anecdotal evidence (e.g., a judge outputting “wim”) without systematic study.  
2. **Generality across preference algorithms:** Does WIM benefit preference learning methods other than Online DPO, such as PPO or GRPO? These algorithms involve reward model training or different update rules; the impact of continuous‑like ratings is unknown.  
3. **Comparison between human and LLM judges:** How do human‑produced WIM critiques differ in quality, consistency, and interpretability from those generated by LLM judges? Human feedback may introduce nuance or bias that affects the resulting model.  
4. **Fixed vs. Changing Judge performance gap:** Why does the Fixed Judge configuration outperform the Changing Judge, and how can the latter be stabilized? The paper hypothesizes non‑stationarity but offers no empirical analysis of feedback dynamics or remedies like lagged judges.

## Limitations
- **Judge critique quality:** Invalid or generic feedback (e.g., “wim”) can break interpretability and increase ties.
- **Embedding sensitivity:** WIM’s advantage depends on using a high‑quality sentence encoder; weaker models reduce score granularity and may increase ties.
- **Modest downstream impact:** The reported win‑rate gain (3.79%) is not statistically significant, raising doubts about practical benefit.
- **Scope limited:** Experiments were restricted to UltraFeedback‑prompt and Llama‑3‑8B‑Instruct; generalizability to other datasets and model scales is unknown.

## Confidence
- Reduced ties & increased delta: **High** (empirically robust across paper’s experiments)  
- Interpretable ratings: **Medium** (depends on judge consistency; break conditions observed)  
- Stronger DPO signal: **Medium** (large loss reduction but downstream win‑rate not significant; needs external validation)  
- Fixed Judge superiority: **High** (explicit experimental comparison shows Changing Judge underperforms)

## Next Checks
1. **Statistical robustness:** Replicate the core metrics (ties, delta, loss reduction) across multiple random seeds and report variance and statistical significance.  
2. **Cross‑task and judge generalizability:** Test WIM on other preference datasets (e.g., HH‑RLHF, CodeFeed) and with diverse judges (GPT‑4, Claude, humans) to verify tie reduction and win‑rate improvements hold.  
3. **Embedding model ablation:** Systematically vary the sentence encoder (e.g., MiniLM, OpenAI embeddings) to quantify its effect on WIM’s distribution properties and downstream performance.
