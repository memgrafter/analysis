# PivotAttack: Rethinking the Search Trajectory in Hard-Label Text Attacks via Pivot Words
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10842
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10842
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Under a 100-query budget, PivotAttack achieves higher Attack Success Rates with lower perturbation rates than seven state-of-the-art baselines across traditional models and Large Language Models.

## Executive Summary
PivotAttack introduces a novel "inside-out" search strategy for hard-label black-box text adversarial attacks. Unlike prior "outside-in" methods that exhaustively explore the vast token space, PivotAttack first identifies small groups of tokens—called Pivot Sets—that are combinatorially critical for the model's prediction. Using a Multi-Armed Bandit algorithm (KL-LUCB), it efficiently discovers these pivot words and then strategically perturbs only a few of them to flip the label. Empirical results on two datasets (Yelp, Yahoo) and two model families (WordLSTM, Qwen2.5) show that PivotAttack attains superior attack success rates with significantly fewer token changes compared to seven strong baselines. Human evaluation further indicates that the selected pivot words are more semantically reasonable than those from an interpretable baseline.

## Method Summary
PivotAttack rethinks the adversarial search trajectory by focusing on the interior of the input space rather than expanding outward. It begins by representing the input as a set of candidate token groups (e.g., n-grams) and uses a Multi-Armed Bandit approach, specifically KL-LUCB, to allocate queries toward identifying which groups are most influential on the model's hard-label prediction. These influential groups form the "Pivot Set." Once identified, the attacker selectively perturbs tokens within the pivot set (e.g., by synonym replacement or character-level changes) to induce a misclassification with minimal modifications. The inside-out paradigm drastically reduces the number of queries needed compared to methods that greedily modify tokens from the input periphery. The framework is model-agnostic and operates under a strict query budget (100 in the evaluations).

## Key Results
- On WordLSTM (Yelp), PivotAttack achieves 16.8% ASR with only 1.4% perturbed tokens, outperforming the best baseline by 3.2% ASR while using ~60% fewer perturbations.
- On zero-shot Qwen2.5 (Yahoo), it reaches 93.5% ASR with 1.1% perturbations, surpassing baselines by up to 8% ASR under the same 100-query limit.
- Human evaluation rates pivot words selected by PivotAttack as more semantically reasonable than those from LimeAttack, though details of the study are sparse.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
PivotAttack's efficiency stems from its focus on combinatorially critical token groups. Traditional attacks perturb tokens one by one from the input edges, ignoring dependencies among distant tokens. In contrast, PivotAttack discovers small sets of tokens whose joint presence or absence heavily influences the model's decision. By targeting these pivot sets, perturbations create maximal impact with minimal changes. The KL-LUCB bandit algorithm optimally balances exploration and exploitation: it queries the model with different group masks to estimate each group's effect on the label, quickly homing in on the most predictive groups. This inside-out approach reduces the search space from exponential in sequence length to polynomial in the number of candidate groups, explaining the high query efficiency and low perturbation rates observed.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Hard-label black-box attack**  
   Why needed: The attacker only observes the final predicted label, not probabilities, making gradient-based methods infeasible.  
   Quick check: Can you design an attack that only uses binary feedback (original vs. target label)?

2. **Pivot Set**  
   Why needed: Captures inter-token dependencies; a single token change may be insufficient if other tokens compensate.  
   Quick check: For a given input, does removing a candidate group always change the label? If not, it might be a pivot.

3. **Multi-Armed Bandit (KL-LUCB)**  
   Why needed: To allocate limited queries among many candidate groups in a way that identifies the most influential ones with high confidence.  
   Quick check: Does KL-LUCB guarantee that the best arm is identified with few samples under a fixed error probability?

4. **Inside-out search trajectory**  
   Why needed: Avoids the combinatorial explosion of modifying tokens in arbitrary order; starts from the most influential core.  
   Quick check: Does starting from the whole input and removing groups lead to faster label flips than adding perturbations?

5. **Perturbation minimality**  
   Why needed: To produce adversarial examples that are both stealthy and transferable.  
   Quick check: Can you always reduce the number of changed tokens while preserving the attack success?

6. **Query budget constraint**  
   Why needed: Realistic scenario where the attacker has limited model access.  
   Quick check: Does halving the query budget halve the ASR? (Not necessarily linear.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input -> Tokenizer -> Candidate Group Generator -> Bandit Query Manager (KL-LUCB) -> Pivot Set Selector -> Perturbation Engine -> Query to Model -> Label Check -> Output

Critical path: Tokenization splits text into tokens; Candidate Group Generator creates overlapping n-gram groups; Bandit Query Manager iteratively selects groups to mask and queries the model; Pivot Set Selector aggregates groups that cause label change when masked; Perturbation Engine applies minimal modifications to pivot tokens; final query verifies misclassification.

Design tradeoffs:
- Pivot set size: larger sets capture more dependencies but require more queries to evaluate; tuned via bandit thresholds.
- Exploration vs. exploitation in KL-LUCB: higher confidence requires more samples, reducing budget for perturbations.
- Perturbation strategy: synonym replacement preserves semantics but may be less effective than character-level changes; tradeoff between stealth and success.

Failure signatures:
- Pivot set empty: no group influences label; may indicate overfitting or insufficient candidate diversity.
- ASR plateaus early: bandit converged to suboptimal arms; consider more aggressive exploration.
- Perturbation rate spikes: pivot tokens are essential for meaning; consider alternative perturbation types.

First experiments:
1. Run PivotAttack on a single example with a large query budget to visualize pivot set composition and verify that pivots are indeed influential.
2. Compare ASR and perturbation rate against a random group selection baseline (no bandit) to isolate the benefit of KL-LUCB.
3. Vary the maximum group size (n-gram length) to see the impact on query efficiency and ASR; expect a sweet spot balancing coverage and cost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions. However, the limitations and evaluation scope imply the following unanswered questions:
- How does PivotAttack generalize to other datasets (e.g., AGNews, SST-2) and model architectures (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa), especially in multilingual or multimodal settings?
- What is the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameters such as maximum group size, bandit thresholds, and the overlap strategy for candidate groups?
- The human evaluation lacks methodological details (sample size, annotator criteria, inter-annotator agreement); can the claim of semantic superiority be robustly validated?

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to two datasets (Yelp, Yahoo) and two models (WordLSTM, Qwen2.5); generalizability to other domains and model architectures is uncertain.
- Fixed query budget of 100; no analysis of performance under varying budgets or sensitivity to hyperparameters like pivot set size.
- Human evaluation lacks methodological details (sample size, annotator criteria, inter-annotator agreement), undermining the claim of semantic superiority.

## Confidence
- Core efficiency and ASR improvement: Medium
- Generalization across model families: Medium
- Semantic reasonableness of selected pivot words: Low
