# Domain-informed explainable boosting machines for trustworthy lateral spread predictions
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17175
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17175
- Reference count: 4
- Primary result: Corrected non-physical EBM relationships via domain-informed shape function edits, yielding physically consistent predictions with a small accuracy drop (test accuracy: 0.80 → 0.75; AUC: 0.88 → 0.80).

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the issue of Explainable Boosting Machines (EBMs) learning non-physical relationships from biased data when predicting earthquake-induced lateral spreading. The authors develop a domain-informed framework that manually edits the EBM's shape functions: univariate functions are replaced with monotonic sigmoids (or step functions) fitted to physically plausible regions, and bivariate interactions are synthesized from these modified univariates with selective replacement of bins that deviate significantly from the synthesized pattern. Applied to the 2011 Christchurch dataset, the method successfully eliminated counterintuitive trends (e.g., shallow groundwater decreasing spreading likelihood) and improved physical consistency of both global and local explanations. However, this came with a modest reduction in predictive performance (test accuracy decreased from 0.80 to 0.75; AUC from 0.88 to 0.80), mainly due to increased false positives. The work demonstrates a trade-off between trustworthiness and accuracy and highlights the potential for incorporating domain knowledge into interpretable models.

## Method Summary
The method starts by training a baseline EBM on the Christchurch earthquake lateral spreading dataset (7,291 sites, 5 features) using interpretML with default hyperparameters. Non-physical relationships in the learned univariate shape functions (e.g., negative contribution for shallow groundwater) are identified via manual inspection or SHAP analysis. To correct them, the authors manually select "trusted" data ranges where the physical trend is believed to hold, then fit constrained monotonic functions (sigmoids or step functions) to the shape function values sampled in those ranges, replacing the entire univariate term. For bivariate interactions, they construct a synthesized function by simply adding the two modified univariate functions, rescaling it to match the original interaction's score range, and then selectively replacing bins in the 30×30 interaction matrix where the range-normalized absolute error exceeds a threshold (ε=40%). The modified EBM, with these edited shape functions, is evaluated on test data and compared to the original.

## Key Results
- Non-physical relationships in the original EBM (e.g., shallow groundwater decreasing lateral spreading likelihood) were successfully corrected through domain-informed shape function edits.
- The modified EBM showed improved physical consistency in global and local explanations, aligning with domain expectations.
- Predictive performance decreased modestly: test accuracy dropped from 0.80 to 0.75, and AUC from 0.88 to 0.80, primarily due to increased false positives.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Detecting non-physical relationships**  
Non-physical relationships (e.g., shallow groundwater decreasing likelihood) can be identified by directly inspecting the EBM's univariate and bivariate shape functions, which expose each feature's contribution. Deviations from domain expectations (e.g., negative scores for shallow groundwater) are flagged manually or via SHAP aggregation. This relies on the core assumption that training data bias (e.g., sparse samples in certain ranges) causes the model to learn statistical artifacts rather than true physics. Evidence is provided in the paper's abstract, Section 3.1, and 3.2.

**Mechanism 2: Constraining univariate functions to monotonic trends**  
For features with known monotonic influence (e.g., groundwater depth, PGA), the original shape function is replaced by a monotonic sigmoid fitted only to the "trustworthy" region (where data is sufficient and physics consistent). The sigmoid form enforces monotonicity and saturation. The core assumption is that the trustworthy region is correctly identifiable and the sigmoid adequately captures the true relationship. For non-monotonic features like distance-to-river, a step function is used instead. Evidence is in Section 3.3.1.

**Mechanism 3: Selective correction of bivariate interactions**  
Bivariate interactions are synthesized by adding the two modified univariate functions (f_syn = f'_i + f'_j) and rescaling to the original interaction's score range. Then, for each bin in the 30×30 matrix, if the range-normalized absolute error Δ_rng = |f_syn - f_original| / (f_max - f_min) exceeds a threshold (ε=40%), the original value is replaced by the synthesized one. This preserves legitimate data-driven patterns while removing unphysical regions. The core assumption is that the synthesized function represents physical consistency and that high-discrepancy bins are artifacts. Evidence is in Section 3.3.2 and 4.1.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: Explainable Boosting Machines (EBMs) as Generalized Additive Models (GAMs)**  
- *Why needed:* EBMs are the core modeling framework; their additive structure (score = intercept + Σ univariate + Σ bivariate functions) is essential to understand how modifications propagate.  
- *Quick check:* If an EBM’s univariate function for PGA is monotonically decreasing, what does that imply about its learned relationship to lateral spreading, and why might that be non-physical? (Answer: It implies higher PGA reduces predicted likelihood, contradicting physics that stronger shaking increases spreading.)

**Concept 2: Shape function interpretability and centering**  
- *Why needed:* Shape functions are the directly editable objects in an EBM; their centering (zero mean over the training data) ensures the intercept captures baseline probability and avoids redundancy when terms are added. Modifications must preserve this identifiability.  
- *Quick check:* When replacing an original shape function with a fitted monotonic curve, what must be done to ensure the overall model score remains calibrated? (Answer: The new function should be centered (zero mean) relative to the training data distribution, often by adjusting the intercept accordingly.)

**Concept 3: Domain knowledge as monotonicity/threshold constraints**  
- *Why needed:* The correction method encodes expert physical principles (e.g., "shallower groundwater increases likelihood") as mathematical constraints (monotonic sigmoid, step function) that override data-driven artifacts.  
- *Quick check:* For feature `L` (distance to river), why is a step function more appropriate than a sigmoid, and how does that reflect domain knowledge? (Answer: Because the physical effect is threshold-like: spreading likelihood drops sharply beyond a certain distance, not gradually. A step function captures this discontinuity.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Original EBM (trained) → Univariate editor (select trust region, fit sigmoid/step, replace) → Bivariate synthesizer (synthesize from edited univariates, rescale, selective replacement) → Hybrid model (modified shape functions) → Evaluation metrics

**Critical path**  
Data → Train original EBM → Inspect shape functions → Identify untrusted regions → Fit constrained univariates → Synthesize bivariates → Apply thresholded replacement → Evaluate modified EBM

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Physical consistency vs. accuracy: Enforcing constraints discards some valid nonlinear patterns, increasing false positives.
- Preservation vs. correction: The range-based threshold ε balances between retaining learned interactions and removing unphysical bins; relative error is sensitive near zero.
- Scope of modification: Only features with clear physical influence are edited; opaque but influential features are left unmodified.

**Failure signatures**  
1. Unphysical trends remain in modified univariates (e.g., sigmoid fitted incorrectly due to misidentified trust region).
2. Overwritten bivariate interactions cause accuracy collapse (e.g., false positive rate > 50%).
3. Synthesized bivariate `f_syn` is itself unphysical if modified univariates are wrong.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Reproduce original non-physical patterns: Train EBM on Christchurch data; plot GWD and GWD–PGA functions; verify shallow GWD negative score and high-PGA+shallow-GWD negative region.
2. Univariate modification only: Apply sigmoid to GWD/PGA, step to L; evaluate change in global feature importance and SHAP monotonicity.
3. Bivariate threshold sensitivity: For GWD–PGA, sweep ε (range-based) from 10% to 60%; measure percentage of bins replaced and corresponding test AUC/F1; select ε=40% as compromise.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can domain knowledge be systematically incorporated into a trained EBM to correct non-physical learned relationships? The paper's proposed framework requires manual selection of trustworthy regions and is limited to a few features; an automated procedure is needed.
2. What trade-offs emerge between predictive accuracy and physical consistency when imposing such constraints? The study reports a modest accuracy drop for the Christchurch dataset, but the consistency and magnitude across other contexts are unknown.
3. How can the identification of unphysical regions in EBM shape functions be automated? Manual selection hinders scalability; an objective detection method is required.
4. What systematic approach can be used to select the threshold ε for selective replacement in bivariate interaction functions? The choice of 40% is arbitrary; a principled optimization method is needed.

## Limitations
- Manual definition of "trusted regions" for univariate functions is subjective, hindering reproducibility and scalability.
- The binning strategy for bivariate interaction matrices (30×30) is not fully described, making exact reproduction of corrections challenging.
- The chosen error threshold ε=40% for selective bivariate replacement lacks justification and sensitivity analysis; its impact on different features is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Non-physical relationships in original EBM**: High confidence (directly visible in shape functions and SHAP).
- **Domain-informed correction yields physically consistent models**: Medium confidence (depends on subjective trusted regions and threshold ε).
- **Performance trade-off (4–5% accuracy drop)**: Medium confidence (specific numbers reported, but implementation details may cause variation).
