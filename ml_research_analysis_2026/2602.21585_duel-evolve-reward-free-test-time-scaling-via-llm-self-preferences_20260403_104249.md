# Duel-Evolve: Reward-Free Test-Time Scaling via LLM Self-Preferences

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21585
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21585
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Duel-Evolve achieves 94% accuracy on MathBench and 37.4% on LiveCodeBench without external rewards, outperforming baselines by up to 20 percentage points.

## Executive Summary
Duel-Evolve tackles the problem of optimizing large language model (LLM) outputs at test time when no external scalar reward is available. It uses the LLM itself as a judge to provide pairwise preferences among candidate solutions, aggregates these noisy comparisons via a Bayesian Bradley–Terry model to obtain uncertainty-aware utility estimates, and employs Double Thompson Sampling to efficiently allocate comparison effort and guide generation. On the MathBench benchmark, it reaches 94% accuracy, surpassing the strongest baseline by 20 percentage points; on LiveCodeBench, it achieves 37.4% accuracy, an improvement of over 12 percentage points compared to iterative methods. The method demonstrates effective test-time scaling solely from self-preferences, requiring no reward model or ground-truth labels.

## Method Summary
Duel-Evolve is an evolutionary algorithm that operates without external rewards. It maintains a pool of candidate solutions. At each generation, it fits a Bayesian Bradley–Terry model to all pairwise comparisons collected so far, yielding Gaussian posterior summaries (mean µ and variance σ) for each candidate’s utility. Using Double Thompson Sampling, it selects pairs of candidates to compare, focusing effort on those that could plausibly be optimal. The LLM judge evaluates each pair, and the outcomes update the posterior. To generate new candidates, the algorithm selects a parent set from the survivor pool based on a mix of high posterior means and high uncertainty, then prompts the LLM generator with the query and the annotated parents (y, µ) to produce offspring. The pool is pruned using confidence bounds to keep it tractable. The process repeats until a comparison budget is exhausted, and the candidate with the highest posterior mean is returned.

## Key Results
- On MathBench, Duel-Evolve achieves 94% accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline by 20 percentage points.
- On LiveCodeBench, it reaches 37.4% accuracy, improving over comparable iterative methods by over 12 percentage points.
- The method requires no reward model or ground-truth labels, demonstrating effective test-time scaling solely from self-preferences.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Duel-Evolve aggregates noisy pairwise comparisons into a global utility posterior that estimates candidate quality with uncertainty.
- **Mechanism:** It fits a Bayesian Bradley–Terry model to all observed duels, placing independent Gaussian priors on latent utilities and computing a MAP estimate via L‑BFGS. A diagonal Hessian gives a Laplace approximation, yielding per-candidate Gaussian summaries (mean µ, variance σ²).
- **Core assumption:** The Bradley–Terry likelihood correctly models the judge’s noisy preferences as a function of utility differences, and the posterior is unimodal so Laplace approximation is accurate.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “aggregates these noisy candidate comparisons via a Bayesian Bradley‑Terry model, yielding uncertainty‑aware estimates of candidate quality.”
  - [section 2.2] Describes the Bradley–Terry likelihood and posterior.
  - [section 2.3.1] Details the MAP + Laplace approximation implementation.
  - [corpus] Neighboring works address pairwise LLM evaluation and dueling bandits, but Duel‑Evolve’s specific Bayesian aggregation over a growing pool is novel.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM judge exhibits systematic biases (e.g., favoring verbose solutions) or the pairwise noise is not logistic, the posterior will be misspecified and may misrank candidates. Additionally, if the candidate pool is too small or unrepresentative of the true solution space, the posterior over the pool may not reflect the global optimum.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Duel‑Evolve focuses the limited comparison budget on candidates plausibly optimal by using Double Thompson Sampling (DTS) to select duel pairs.
- **Mechanism:** At each round, it approximates the maximizer probability p*(y) = P(y = y* | D) by first computing posterior summaries (µ, σ) for all candidates in the survivor set. It then independently samples two candidates from a distribution proportional to p*(y) (via Thompson‑sampling from their Gaussian posteriors and taking the argmax over a sample of utilities) and pits them against each other. The winner updates the posterior.
- **Core assumption:** The posterior p*(y) accurately identifies which candidates are likely optimal, and sampling according to this probability efficiently balances exploration and exploitation in a finite‑budget setting.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “uses Double Thompson Sampling to allocate comparison effort and guide generation.”
  - [section 2.2] Defines p*(y) and the idealized DTS algorithm.
  - [section 2.3.3] Explains the approximation: Thompson‑sample duels from a pruned survivor set.
  - [corpus] DTS is a standard dueling‑bandits algorithm (Wu & Liu, 2016); Duel‑Evolve adapts it to a growing candidate pool, which is not covered in the corpus.
- **Break condition:** If the posterior is overly diffuse (e.g., too few comparisons) or mis‑calibrated, Thompson sampling may waste comparisons on low-quality candidates. Aggressive pruning of the survivor set could discard the true optimal candidate before it receives enough comparisons, causing permanent misranking.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Duel‑Evolve generates improved candidates by conditioning the LLM generator on a small set of high‑utility parents annotated with their posterior means.
- **Mechanism:** In each generation, it selects a parent set Aₜ from the survivor set via a mixture: Thompson‑sampled top scorers (exploitation) and high‑uncertainty recent arrivals (exploration). The generator LLM is prompted with the query x and pairs (yᵢ, µᵢ) for each parent yᵢ ∈ Aₜ. The LLM infers the relationship between solution structure and utility and proposes new candidates in the same space.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM can perform effective in‑context learning from the annotated parents, producing variants that inherit desirable properties while avoiding flaws; the posterior means provide a useful scalar signal for this conditioning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “conditioning the generator LLM on a small set of high scoring parents along with their estimated posterior utilities to propose new candidates.”
  - [section 2.3.2] Describes the approximation of p*ₜ via LLM‑conditioned proposals.
  - [section 2.3.3] Details the parent selection (Thompson/recency mix) and generation step.
  - [corpus] Corpus includes LLM‑based evolutionary methods, but none combine Bayesian posterior annotation with LLM conditioning.
- **Break condition:** If the parent set is homogeneous (all very similar) or consistently suboptimal, the LLM may not discover novel improvements. Inaccurate posterior means (e.g., from a poorly fit model) could mislead the generator, causing it to reinforce incorrect patterns. The LLM’s own limitations (e.g., lack of creativity) also bound improvement.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Dueling bandits  
  - **Why needed here:** Provides the theoretical framework for optimization from pairwise comparisons, defining the goal (identify the best arm) and exploration‑exploitation trade‑offs. Duel‑Evolve adapts the DTS algorithm to a growing, combinatorially large arm set.  
  - **Quick check question:** How does the dueling‑bandits setting differ from the standard multi‑armed bandit problem, and why is pairwise feedback often easier to elicit?
- **Concept:** Bradley–Terry model  
  - **Why needed here:** It is the likelihood used to aggregate noisy pairwise preferences into a global utility scale. Understanding its parameters (θ) and the logistic link is essential to grasp how Duel‑Evolve computes posterior means and variances.  
  - **Quick check question:** What is the mathematical form of the Bradley–Terry model, and what identifiability issue does the Gaussian prior resolve?
- **Concept:** Bayesian inference with Laplace approximation  
  - **Why needed here:** Duel‑Evolve must maintain a posterior over utilities as comparisons arrive, but exact Bayesian updating is intractable over a growing pool. The Laplace approximation gives a computationally cheap way to obtain Gaussian posterior summaries.  
  - **Quick check question:** What are the assumptions behind Laplace approximation (e.g., unimodality, curvature), and when might it fail?
- **Concept:** Thompson Sampling (and Double Thompson Sampling)  
  - **Why needed here:** It drives the selection of both duel pairs and parents, turning posterior uncertainty into efficient exploration. Double Thompson Sampling specifically targets the probability that an arm is optimal.  
  - **Quick check question:** In dueling bandits, how does Double Thompson Sampling differ from standard Thompson Sampling, and what problem does it solve?
- **Concept:** Evolutionary search with LLM conditioning  
  - **Why needed here:** Duel‑Evolve uses the LLM both as judge and generator. Understanding how to prompt the LLM to produce new candidates conditioned on scored parents is key to exploiting semantic structure in the discrete space.  
  - **Quick check question:** Why is it beneficial to condition the generator on posterior‑annotated parents rather than just on a set of high‑scoring solutions without uncertainty information?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Candidate pool (Eₜ) -> Comparison history (Dₜ) -> Posterior inference (µ, σ) -> Survivor set (Sₜ). Evaluation selector picks duel pairs from Sₜ -> Judge LLM updates Dₜ. Evolution selector chooses parents Aₜ from Sₜ -> Generator LLM creates children added to Eₜ. Pruning module updates Sₜ from Eₜ.
- **Critical path (per generation):**
  1. **Update:** Fit Bradley–Terry MAP (L‑BFGS) on Eₜ with Dₜ → obtain (µ, σ) via Laplace.
  2. **Evaluate:** For a fixed budget, select pairs from Sₜ via Double Thompson Sampling, invoke judge LLM (order‑consistent), append outcomes to Dₜ.
  3. **Evolve:** Pick parent set Aₜ from Sₜ (mix of Thompson‑sampled top scorers and high‑uncertainty recent arrivals), call generator LLM with (x, {(yᵢ, µᵢ)}) to produce B children, add to Eₜ.
  4. **Prune:** Update Sₜ for next round by discarding candidates whose upper confidence bound is below the best lower bound.
  5. Repeat steps 1‑4 until total budget exhausted; return argmax₍y∈Eₜ₎ µ.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Finite pool vs. full space:** Restricting inference to generated candidates is tractable but risks missing global optimum if the pool is insufficiently explored. Pool growth rate and pruning balance coverage vs. noise.
  - **Laplace approximation:** Fast but assumes unimodality and diagonal posterior; may be inaccurate if correlations are strong or posterior is multi‑modal.
  - **Survivor set pruning:** Concentrates comparisons on promising candidates but can prematurely discard candidates that would improve with more data or after children are added. Confidence‑width hyperparameter controls aggressiveness.
  - **Parent selection mix:** Thompson‑sampled top scorers exploit; including high‑uncertainty recent arrivals explores new regions. Mixture ratio affects convergence speed and diversity.
  - **Order‑consistent judging:** Two LLM calls per pair reduce position bias but double judge cost; acceptable if judge is cheap relative to generation.
  - **Temperature:** Higher temperature increases diversity but may produce more invalid or low‑quality solutions; tuned per task (e.g., 0.7 for MathBench, 1.2 for LiveCodeBench).
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Stagnant µ:** Posterior means plateau across generations, indicating search is not improving.
  - **Persistent high σ:** Many candidates retain large uncertainty despite many comparisons, suggesting inconsistent judge or insufficient duels per candidate.
  - **Low judge consistency:** Order‑consistent agreement rate falls below expected (e.g., <70%), indicating the LLM judge is unreliable for the task.
  - **Pool diversity collapse:** Many candidates become near‑duplicates (high string or AST similarity), risking premature convergence.
  - **Degenerate generation:** New children consistently have lower µ than their parents, suggesting conditioning may be failing or the generator is stuck.
  - **Budget exhaustion without convergence:** Total comparisons/generations used but no candidate reaches high confidence.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Synthetic sanity check:** Implement Duel‑Evolve on a tiny binary‑string problem (e.g., length 4, fitness = number of 1s). Verify posterior correctly ranks candidates, DTS concentrates comparisons on the optimum, and algorithm finds global optimum within a few generations.
  2. **Judge reliability probe:** Sample 100 random candidate pairs from the actual task (MathBench or LiveCodeBench), run order‑consistent judging, and compute concordance rate. If below ~75%, consider using a stronger judge model or majority voting across multiple judges.
  3. **Scaling curve on a subset:** Select 10 problems from MathBench. Run Duel‑Evolve with increasing generation budgets (e.g., 5, 10, 20, 40) and plot accuracy vs. total comparisons. Confirm monotonic improvement and identify diminishing returns; use this to set budgets for full‑dataset runs.

## Limitations
- Judge reliability: The method hinges on LLM pairwise preferences; systematic biases or low inter-order agreement could severely degrade posterior quality. The paper does not report judge consistency metrics.
- Posterior approximation: The Laplace approximation assumes a unimodal, Gaussian-like posterior. Misspecification (e.g., multi-modal utilities due to diverse solution types) or strong correlations between candidates could make (µ, σ) misleading.
- Finite pool limitation: Optimization is restricted to the generated candidate pool. If the evolutionary process fails to include the true optimal solution in the pool—due to limited generation diversity or aggressive pruning—the final output cannot be optimal.

## Next Checks
1. Judge consistency probe: On a subset of problems, compute order-consistent agreement rate for the LLM judge. If <75%, test alternative judges or majority voting to establish a reliability threshold.
2. Synthetic ground‑truth test: Run Duel‑Evolve on a small fully known problem (e.g., maximizing a hidden binary‑string fitness). Validate that the posterior mean ranking converges to the true global optimum and that DTS comparison allocation matches theoretical efficiency.
3. Posterior calibration check: Compare the Laplace‑predicted candidate variances against empirical outcome frequencies (e.g., do candidates with high σ indeed show higher win‑rate volatility?). Mis‑calibration would undermine Thompson Sampling’s exploration‑exploitation balance.
