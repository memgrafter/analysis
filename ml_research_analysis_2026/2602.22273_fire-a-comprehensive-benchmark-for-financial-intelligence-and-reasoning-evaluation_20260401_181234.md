# FIRE: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Financial Intelligence and Reasoning Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22273
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22273
- **Reference count:** 9
- **Primary result:** FIRE introduces a benchmark with 14,000+ financial qualification exam questions and 3,000 scenario-based questions, plus a rubric-trained scoring model that achieves a ScoreDiff of 0.755 for open-ended evaluation.

## Executive Summary
FIRE addresses the lack of robust evaluation for financial LLMs by combining a comprehensive set of certification exam questions and a two-dimensional scenario matrix covering eight sectors and four functional pillars. It introduces a rubric-based scoring system for open-ended tasks, trained via SFT and RLVR, which aligns closely with human judgments (ScoreDiff 0.755). Results show models excel in theoretical exams (e.g., XuanYuan 4.0: 92.15%) but lag in practical scenarios (e.g., ~77–79%), revealing a critical gap between rote knowledge and operational reasoning.

## Method Summary
The benchmark compiles over 14,000 multiple-choice questions from 14 professional certification exams (CFA, CPA, etc.) to assess theoretical knowledge. For practical evaluation, it constructs an 8×4 business matrix (sectors × functional pillars) populated with 3,000 scenario questions: 1,000 closed-form with reference answers (exact-match scoring) and 2,000 open-ended with custom rubrics. A dedicated scoring model based on Qwen3-32B is trained using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with reverse chain-of-thought data, followed by reinforcement learning with a DAPO variant to ensure faithful rubric adherence. The pipeline yields consistent, human-aligned scores for complex financial reasoning tasks.

## Key Results
- High exam accuracy: XuanYuan 4.0 achieves 92.15% average across qualification exams.
- Lower scenario performance: GPT 5.2 scores 77.46% and XuanYuan 4.0 scores 79.08% on the practical scenario set.
- Rubric-trained scoring model attains a ScoreDiff of 0.755, indicating strong alignment with human judgments on open-ended tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Rubric-Trained Scoring Model for Consistency
- **Claim:** The rubric-trained scoring model mitigates instability in open-ended financial task evaluation by replacing holistic LLM-as-a-judge with structured, fine-grained, question-specific criteria.
- **Mechanism:** A multi-stage pipeline generates per-question rubrics via collaborative multi-model synthesis and expert optimization, creating explicit, domain-grounded scoring dimensions. A dedicated model is then trained via SFT (with reverse CoT) and RLVR to predict scores and justifications against these rubrics.
- **Core assumption:** Decomposing complex financial judgment into observable, standardized criteria aligns automated scoring with expert reasoning better than a single monolithic evaluator.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...a rubric-trained scoring model using SFT and RLVR ensures consistent assessment. The scoring model attains a ScoreDiff of 0.755, demonstrating strong alignment with human judgments." [section 2.4] Details the rubric generation pipeline and scoring model training. Appendix A elaborates on the reverse CoT and RLVR process.
- **Break condition:** If rubric generation fails to capture critical, task-specific financial nuances (e.g., regulatory intent), or if the scoring model cannot generalize the rubric logic to novel responses, alignment degrades.

### Mechanism 2: Two-Dimensional Matrix for Comprehensive Coverage
- **Claim:** The evaluation matrix (8 sectors × 4 functional pillars) systematically ensures comprehensive coverage of financial business scenarios, preventing domain blind spots inherent in prior coarse benchmarks.
- **Mechanism:** The matrix decomposes the financial value chain into essential vertical sectors (Banking, Insurance, etc.) and horizontal capabilities (Insight & Decision, Product & Marketing, etc.). Scenario questions are explicitly populated into matrix cells, forcing representation across all domain‑competence intersections.
- **Core assumption:** Financial LLM proficiency is multi‑dimensional; mastery requires competence across specific business contexts *and* functional tasks, not just general financial knowledge.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...a systematic evaluation matrix that categorizes complex financial domains and ensures coverage of essential subdomains and business activities...collect 3,000 financial scenario questions..." [section 2.2] Presents the full matrix table (Table 2) and states: "This framework provides a rigorous decomposition of the tasks AI systems must master within specific scenarios."
- **Break condition:** If key real‑world financial workflows (e.g., post‑investment management in Trust sector) are omitted from the matrix, or if scenario questions cannot be unambiguously mapped to single cells, coverage claims weaken.

### Mechanism 3: Decoupling Theoretical and Practical Skills
- **Claim:** The decoupling between high exam scores and lower practical scenario scores exposes a fundamental gap: current LLMs acquire theoretical financial knowledge but fail to operationalize it in complex, multi‑step business contexts.
- **Mechanism:** The qualification exam set (14,000+ questions) tests rote knowledge and standardized problem-solving via multiple‑choice. The scenario set (3,000 questions) requires synthesis, judgment, and domain‑specific constraints (e.g., compliance, risk) in open‑ended or decision formats. The performance differential (e.g., XuanYuan 4.0: 92.15% avg exam vs. 79.08% scenarios) quantifies this translation failure.
- **Core assumption:** Theoretical knowledge and practical operational intelligence constitute distinct, non‑interchangeable capabilities; excellence in one does not guarantee competence in the other.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Models achieve high exam accuracy (XuanYuan 4.0: 92.15% average) but lower practical scores (GPT 5.2: 77.46%, XuanYuan 4.0: 79.08% on scenarios)." [section 4.2] "These findings indicate that current models perform exceptionally well on financial qualification exams... However, this success does not translate to real‑world financial scenarios, where proficiency remains limited."
- **Break condition:** If scenario questions inadvertently test only surface‑level knowledge or if exam questions contain leakage from scenario training data, the performance gap may be an artifact of test design rather than a true capability gap.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Financial Certification Exam Structure
**Why needed here:** The qualification dataset's validity depends on understanding how professional exams (CFA, CPA, FRM, etc.) test knowledge—typically via multiple‑choice questions covering standardized curricula, ethics, and regulations. This shapes expectations for "theoretical knowledge" assessment.
**Quick check question:** Given a CFA ethics question on fiduciary duty, what key regulatory principle is being tested, and how might that differ from applying that principle in a client suitability scenario?

### Concept: Business Evaluation Matrix Decomposition
**Why needed here:** The 8×4 matrix is the backbone of scenario coverage. Engineers must understand how "Banking‑Corporate Finance‑Product Design" differs from "Insurance‑Life Health‑Risk & Compliance" to correctly place or validate new tasks.
**Quick check question:** For a task about designing a robo‑advisory portfolio for young clients, identify the correct sector, business line, and functional pillar. What ambiguities might arise?

### Concept: Rubric-Based vs. Reference‑Answer Evaluation
**Why needed here:** The benchmark uses two evaluation protocols: exact‑match for closed‑form questions and rubric‑scoring for open‑ended ones. Mixing these or misapplying the rubric model to reference‑answer tasks invalidates results.
**Quick check question:** Why is an exact‑match metric inappropriate for a task like "draft a compliance memo explaining a new AML regulation"? What would a rubric dimension for this task look like?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
- **Qualification Dataset → Evaluation Harness** (closed‑form scoring)
- **Scenario Matrix → Closed Subset → Evaluation Harness** (exact‑match)
- **Scenario Matrix → Open Subset → Rubric Generation → Scoring Model → Evaluation Harness** (rubric scoring)

### Critical Path
1. Data curation  
2. Scenario assignment to matrix  
3. Rubric generation for open tasks  
4. Scoring model training  
5. Benchmark execution  
6. Results aggregation by sector/pillar

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Matrix granularity:** 32 cells provide coverage but risk sparse data per cell vs. coarser aggregation losing nuance.
- **Rubric specificity:** Per‑question rubrics increase validity but require massive expert effort; generic rubrics are scalable but less precise.
- **Scoring model size:** Larger models (e.g., Qwen3‑32B) may align better with human judgment but increase evaluation cost.

### Failure Signatures
- **High ScoreDiff (>0.9) on open‑ended tasks** indicates scoring model degradation.
- **Large performance variance within a matrix cell** suggests poor scenario curation or rubric misalignment.
- **Exam scores near 100% but scenario scores <60%** indicates the model has memorized exam patterns but lacks operational reasoning.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Sanity check:** Run a baseline model (e.g., GPT‑OSS‑120B) on the qualification set; expect ~70–85% average. If significantly lower, debug data loading or prompt format.
2. **Closed‑scenario pilot:** Evaluate on a 100‑question subset of closed‑form scenarios; verify exact‑match pipeline, especially JSON extraction. Investigate any anomalies.
3. **Scoring model calibration:** Apply the scoring model to 50 pre‑scored open‑ended examples; compute ScoreDiff. If >0.85, inspect rubrics for ambiguity or output format violations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Why do LLMs with high accuracy on financial qualification exams (e.g., >90%) exhibit a significant performance drop on practical, scenario‑based financial tasks (e.g., ~77–79%)?
- **Open Question 2:** What specific characteristics of certain financial sectors (e.g., FinTech, Trust) lead to consistently lower model performance compared to others (e.g., Fund Management), as shown in Table 4?
- **Open Question 3:** Do the problem‑wise rubrics and the trained scoring model (ScoreDiff 0.755) fully capture the nuanced judgment criteria used by human financial experts in open‑ended evaluations, or are there latent evaluation dimensions not accounted for?
- **Open Question 4:** How transferable and robust is the rubric‑trained scoring model when applied to financial scenario questions outside the FIRE benchmark’s specific structure and content, such as novel regulatory changes or emerging FinTech innovations?

## Limitations
- Rubric generation process is underspecified, hindering exact replication of scenario evaluation.
- Scoring model training hyperparameters (e.g., RLVR settings) are omitted, preventing faithful reproduction of the scoring component.
- The human test set used to compute ScoreDiff (size, distribution) is undisclosed, raising uncertainty about the reliability of the claimed alignment.

## Confidence
- Rubric‑trained scoring alignment with human judgments: **Medium**
- Completeness of the 8×4 business matrix coverage: **Medium**
- Existence of a gap between theoretical exam and practical scenario performance: **High**
- Validity of decoupling exams and scenarios to isolate distinct capabilities: **Medium**
