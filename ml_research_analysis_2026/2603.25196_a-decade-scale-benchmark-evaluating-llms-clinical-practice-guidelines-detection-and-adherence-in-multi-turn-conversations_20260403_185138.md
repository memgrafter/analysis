# A Decade-Scale Benchmark Evaluating LLMs' Clinical Practice Guidelines Detection and Adherence in Multi-turn Conversations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv: 2603.25196
- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25196
- References: 40
- Primary result: LLMs demonstrate a significant "know-do gap": content detection rates are high (71.1%–89.6%) but adherence rates are substantially lower (21.8%–63.2%), with title grounding particularly poor (3.6%–29.7%).

## Executive Summary
This study introduces CPGBench, the first decade-scale benchmark for evaluating large language models (LLMs) on clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) in multi-turn conversations. The benchmark comprises 3,418 CPG documents spanning 24 specialties and 32,155 recommendations. Eight leading LLMs were assessed on two tasks: guideline detection (recognizing recommendation content and its source) and adherence (correctly applying the recommendation within the dialogue context). Results reveal a pronounced gap between knowing guideline content and applying it: detection rates range from 71.1% to 89.6%, while adherence rates drop to 21.8%–63.2%. Title grounding—identifying the exact guideline document—is especially deficient (3.6%–29.7%). Human evaluation with 56 clinicians confirms the automated Judge-LLM pipeline’s validity (Cohen’s κ 0.62–0.84). The findings underscore serious reliability concerns for deploying LLMs in real-world clinical settings, particularly for safety-critical recommendations.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed CPGBench through a multi-stage pipeline. First, they crawled authoritative medical society websites to collect CPG documents published from 2015 onward, filtering to retain only true guidelines using GPT‑4o. This yielded 3,418 documents. Next, GPT‑4o extracted structured data from each document (title, date, institute, specialty) and parsed individual recommendations, capturing strength, context, action, and goal. For each recommendation, DeepSeek‑R1 generated a multi‑turn clinical conversation in which the guideline was embedded via a special `<recommendation id>` tag placed in an assistant turn. Conversations were formatted as JSON lists and required to end with `[END]`. The dataset was then split for two evaluation tasks: for detection, all tags were removed; for adherence, the conversation was truncated just before the first assistant turn containing the tag. The tested LLMs produced responses to these prompts. Finally, GPT‑4o acted as a Judge‑LLM, scoring responses for content detection (match to recommendation), title grounding (exact title match), and adherence (correct application). Human clinicians validated the extraction, conversation quality, and judge reliability, yielding Cohen’s κ scores between 0.62 and 0.84.

## Key Results
- Content detection rates across models: 71.1% – 89.6%
- Adherence rates: 21.8% – 63.2%
- Title grounding rates: 3.6% – 29.7%
- Human evaluation confirms Judge‑LLM agreement (Cohen’s κ 0.62–0.84)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: High content detection via pre‑training recall**  
LLMs achieve high detection because CPG text appears in their pre‑training corpora, enabling pattern matching without deep reasoning. The model recognizes familiar phrasing and terminology when a guideline topic is mentioned, essentially performing a lexical/semantic retrieval task. This relies on the assumption that public CPGs are well represented in web‑scale training data. If a guideline is very new, niche, or from a region with limited English/web presence, detection would drop sharply, indicating reliance on memorized text rather than on‑the‑fly reasoning.

**Mechanism 2: Low adherence due to contextual application gap**  
Adherence requires maintaining multi‑turn dialogue state, matching the patient’s scenario to the guideline’s context and population, selecting the correct action, and formulating an appropriate response. Failures can occur at any step. The large gap between detection and adherence shows that recognizing guideline text does not equip the model to apply it correctly in a dynamic conversational setting. If adherence rates approached detection rates for all models and specialties, the task would be primarily recall; the observed gap confirms the need for complex contextual reasoning.

**Mechanism 3: Extremely low title grounding reveals citation hallucination**  
Models fail to retrieve the exact document title associated with a recommendation, often generating plausible‑sounding names or defaulting to common sources (e.g., WHO, NICE). This indicates a weak link between a factoid and its bibliographic provenance in the model’s parametric knowledge. Title grounding is a stricter test of precise retrieval than content detection. If models were prompted to output titles verbatim from a known set, grounding rates would rise dramatically, showing the failure is in recall precision, not comprehension.

## Foundational Learning
- **Clinical Reasoning vs. Information Retrieval**  
  *Why needed*: The benchmark distinguishes knowing a guideline (retrieval) from applying it in context (reasoning). High retrieval scores do not imply clinical competence.  
  *Quick check*: If a model detects a hypertension guideline but fails to apply it to a patient with renal comorbidity, is this a retrieval failure or a reasoning failure?

- **Multi‑Turn Dialogue State Tracking**  
  *Why needed*: Adherence evaluation uses truncated conversations; the model must use preceding dialogue (symptoms, history, concerns) to ground its response. Without robust state tracking, application fails.  
  *Quick check*: In a 5‑turn conversation about diabetes management, what specific pieces of information from turns 1‑3 must the model correctly use in turn 4 to adhere to a guideline about insulin initiation?

- **Source Grounding and Hallucination**  
  *Why needed*: Title grounding measures the ability to cite a precise source, a critical safety feature. Low rates indicate a systemic tendency to hallucinate references.  
  *Quick check*: If a model correctly applies a vaccination guideline but cites the wrong document title, what are the potential risks?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text flow):**  
Corpus Builder (download/filter CPGs) -> Structured Extractor (parse recommendations) -> Conversation Generator (DeepSeek‑R1 creates multi‑turn dialogues) -> Dataset Transformer (create detection and adherence variants) -> Judge‑LLM Evaluator (GPT‑4o scores responses) -> Human Validation Pipeline (kappa checks).

**Critical path:**  
Document Collection → Filtering (CPG quality) → Extraction (structured data) → Conversation Generation → Transformation (detection/adherence sets) → Model Inference → Judge‑LLM Scoring → Human Validation. A failure in extraction quality propagates to all downstream tasks.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Synthetic vs. Real Conversations*: Synthetic generation allows massive scale but may lack full clinical nuance; real dialogues are scarce and ethically complex.  
- *Automated vs. Human Evaluation*: Judge‑LLM enables scale but has leniency/strictness biases; human evaluation is gold‑standard but costly (used only for validation).  
- *Generalist vs. Specialist Models*: Medical‑specific models sometimes underperform larger general models, suggesting domain fine‑tuning hasn’t fully bridged the know‑do gap.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High detection + low adherence → Failure in contextual application/reasoning.  
- Low title grounding across all models → Systemic issue with source attribution.  
- Specialty‑specific adherence dips (e.g., radiology, thoracic surgery) → Potential data or reasoning gaps in those domains.  
- Lower adherence on safety‑critical subset → Models less reliable on high‑stakes recommendations.  
- Judge‑LLM scores > human scores (more lenient) → Automated scores may overestimate capability.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replication Run**: Execute the full pipeline on 1% of the data (322 recommendations) with a single model (e.g., GPT‑4o) to validate end‑to‑end functionality, checking extraction quality, conversation generation format, and Judge‑LLM output structure.  
2. **Specialty Stratification Test**: For one high‑performing (e.g., preventive medicine) and one low‑performing (e.g., thoracic surgery) specialty, run all 8 models on 50 samples each. Plot detection vs. adherence gaps to confirm specialty‑level variation.  
3. **Safety‑Critical Subset Drill**: Filter the 6,632 safety‑critical recommendations. Run one model (e.g., GPT‑4o) and compare its detection/adherence rates on this subset vs. the full set to verify the “worse on critical” pattern. Manually inspect 10 failure cases to hypothesize reasons.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What are the specific cognitive or architectural limitations causing the persistent "know‑do gap"?**  
   The benchmark quantifies the gap but does not diagnose its root causes, which could include insufficient multi‑turn reasoning, poor contextual tracking, or training data biases. Resolution requires ablation studies or interpretability analyses isolating factors like conversation history length or reasoning depth.

2. **How can LLMs be improved to achieve reliable title grounding?**  
   Title grounding rates are extremely low (3.6%–29.7%), reflecting a systemic citation hallucination. The problem may stem from models not associating recommendation content with source metadata during pre‑training or lacking source retrieval mechanisms. Evidence from citation‑augmented training or retrieval‑augmented generation could guide solutions.

3. **Why do adherence capabilities vary significantly across medical specialties?**  
   Certain specialties (e.g., radiology, thoracic surgery) show systematically lower adherence. The descriptive analysis does not investigate whether this stems from guideline complexity, jargon, data representation, or conversational query nature. Error analysis and domain‑specific fine‑tuning studies are needed.

4. **Are lower adherence rates on the safety‑critical subset indicative of a fundamental inability to handle high‑stakes reasoning?**  
   The safety‑critical subset (6,632 recommendations) shows further drops in adherence, but it is unclear if models truly fail on safety‑critical reasoning or if the evaluation is too narrow. Human evaluation on this subset and case studies are required to distinguish.

5. **To what extent does the use of synthetic conversations overestimate or underestimate real‑world performance?**  
   Synthetic dialogues may be simpler or follow patterns that exploit model strengths, while real conversations involve more ambiguity. The paper acknowledges this limitation but provides no comparison. Parallel evaluation on real de‑identified clinical encounters is needed.

## Limitations
- The exact CPG corpus (3,418 documents) is unreleased; crawling queries, depth, and exclusion rules are unspecified, making exact replication impossible without the original snapshot.
- Model versions and decoding parameters (temperature, top_p, max_tokens) are not detailed, potentially affecting generation and evaluation outcomes.
- The definition and filtering criteria for the safety‑critical subset are opaque; reproducing the exact 6,632‑recommendation subset requires the same automated prompt and its deterministic output.

## Confidence
- Know‑do gap (detection vs. adherence): **High** (consistent across all eight models, supported by human validation).
- Title grounding deficiency: **High** (all models struggle; Judge‑LLM agreement acceptable; systematic citation hallucination).
- Pre‑training recall explains high detection: **Medium** (indirect evidence; no decisive test with never‑seen guidelines).

## Next Checks
1. **New‑CPG probe**: Sample 50 CPGs published after the training cut‑off of evaluated models and measure detection/adherence. A sharp drop would confirm reliance on pre‑training.
2. **Truncation sensitivity**: For 100 long CPGs, extract recommendations under three truncation regimes (first 100K tokens, last 100K tokens, middle 100K tokens) and compare extraction completeness to assess fragility.
3. **Human title audit**: Have clinicians independently judge title correctness on 200 model outputs scored by Judge‑LLM; compute human–automation agreement and calibrate title grounding rates for systematic Judge bias.
