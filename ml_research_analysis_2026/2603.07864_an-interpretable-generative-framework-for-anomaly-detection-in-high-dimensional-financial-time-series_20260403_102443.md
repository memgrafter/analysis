# An Interpretable Generative Framework for Anomaly Detection in High-Dimensional Financial Time Series

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.07864
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07864
- **Reference count**: 8
- **Primary result**: State-of-the-art unsupervised anomaly detection in high-dimensional financial time series, achieving F1 0.624 on synthetic anomalies and F1 0.772 on market regime shifts.

## Executive Summary
ReGEN-TAD is a novel framework for unsupervised anomaly detection in high-dimensional financial time series. It employs a generative backbone that jointly forecasts and reconstructs rolling windows using convolutional, transformer, and LSTM layers. Anomalies are detected by aggregating six complementary instability scores (forecast error, reconstruction loss, latent distortion, etc.) via robust standardization and weighted averaging, followed by calibration. The approach also offers factor-level attribution to interpret anomalies. Extensive experiments on synthetic and real financial data demonstrate superior performance over baselines, with high precision and low false positive rates. The framework effectively identifies known market crises without fine-tuning, balancing accuracy, robustness, and interpretability.

## Method Summary
ReGEN-TAD addresses the challenge of detecting anomalies in multivariate financial time series where both temporal dependencies and cross-sectional relationships evolve. The method processes data as overlapping rolling windows of past observations (length L) and corresponding future forecasts (horizon H). A deep generative model, comprising temporal convolutions, positional encoding, and parallel transformer and bidirectional LSTM pathways, maps each window to a latent representation. This representation is used to produce both a forecast of the future block and a reconstruction of the past window. A refinement network adjusts the forecasts, and a composite loss combining forecast and reconstruction errors with L2 regularization guides training. To ensure model robustness, a purification stage iteratively removes windows with high reconstruction error (above a quantile threshold) before final training, limiting removal to 30%. For anomaly scoring, six component signals are derived: refined forecast error, reconstruction error, k-nearest neighbor distance in latent space, latent residual dynamics, Mahalanobis distance of residuals, and residual dispersion. Each component is robustly standardized using median and interquartile range statistics computed on a calibration set, then aggregated via a weighted sum (weights summing to the number of components). The aggregated score is smoothed with an exponentially weighted moving average. A threshold for flagging anomalies is set based on a predefined false positive rate (e.g., α=0.05) using the calibration data. Finally, factor-level attribution is achieved by computing, for each anomalous window, the product of standardized asset deviations and gradient-based sensitivity of the anomaly score with respect to those deviations, highlighting which assets or sectors drove the detection.

## Key Results
- On synthetic anomalies, ReGEN-TAD achieved an F1-score of 0.624, surpassing the next best baseline (0.595).
- On real market regime shifts, it attained F1 0.772, precision 0.968, and a false positive rate of 0.002.
- Attribution experiments recovered injected sector shocks with match ratios up to 0.69, and the model automatically flagged historical crises (2008, COVID-19) without fine-tuning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Anomalies are detected by aggregating multiple, complementary instability signals rather than relying on a single residual. The generative backbone produces forecasts and reconstructions for each rolling window. Six component scores are computed from these outputs, robustly standardized (using median and IQR), and combined via weighted averaging into a unified anomaly score. This multi-signal approach increases robustness across heterogeneous anomaly types, as different anomalies manifest in different components. Robust standardization prevents any single component from dominating due to outliers, and the weighted sum allows flexibility. Additionally, the purification step reduces contamination of the training data, ensuring the model learns normal patterns. The factor attribution further enhances interpretability by linking anomalies to specific assets through gradient-based sensitivity.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Rolling Window Processing**  
   *Why needed*: Captures local temporal dynamics and cross-sectional dependencies, enabling the model to learn from sequential data in a supervised manner.  
   *Quick check*: Verify that sliding a window of length L over the series yields tensors X_t ∈ ℝ^{L×p} and F_t ∈ ℝ^{H×p} with correct alignment.

2. **Generative Backbone (Conv + Transformer + LSTM)**  
   *Why needed*: Convolutions extract short-term patterns, transformers capture long-range dependencies via self-attention, and LSTM models sequential dependencies; together they provide a powerful representation for forecasting and reconstruction.  
   *Quick check*: Pass a sample batch through the backbone; ensure output shapes for forecast (H×p), reconstruction (L×p), and latent (128-d) are as expected.

3. **Purification via Reconstruction Error**  
   *Why needed*: Removes windows that are themselves anomalous from the training set, preventing the model from learning to generate anomalies.  
   *Quick check*: After purification, check that the proportion of removed windows is ≤30% and that the distribution of reconstruction loss on remaining data is approximately normal (e.g., via histogram).

4. **Multi-Signal Ensemble Scoring**  
   *Why needed*: Different anomaly types (e.g., volatility spikes, mean shifts) affect different aspects of model behavior; combining diverse signals improves detection robustness.  
   *Quick check*: Compute all six component scores for a few windows; confirm they show variation and are not perfectly correlated (e.g., pairwise correlation <0.9).

5. **Robust Standardization & Calibration**  
   *Why needed*: Scaling scores using median and IQR makes the aggregation resilient to outliers; calibration sets the anomaly threshold to a controlled false positive rate.  
   *Quick check*: On a held-out clean set, the empirical FPR should be close to the nominal α (e.g., 0.05) after thresholding.

6. **Factor-Level Attribution**  
   *Why needed*: Provides interpretability by identifying which assets or sectors contributed most to an anomaly, aiding root cause analysis.  
   *Quick check*: For an injected sector shock, the top-ranked assets by contribution should predominantly belong to the shocked sector; compute a match ratio.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Raw data → Rolling window extraction → Generative backbone (Temporal Conv → Positional encoding → Parallel Transformer & BiLSTM → Latent z_t) → Forecast head & Reconstruction head → Refinement network → Outputs (Forecast F̂_t, Reconstruction X̂_t, z_t). From these, six parallel scorers compute component signals → Robust standardization (using calibration stats) → Weighted averaging (weights sum to M=6) → EWMA smoothing (span 5) → Final anomaly score. Attribution branch: from anomaly score back through computational graph to compute gradients w.r.t. asset deviations (Δ_j) and combine with Δ_j to yield factor contributions C_j.

**Critical Path**  
1. Data ingestion and rolling window creation.  
2. Model training on purified data using composite loss.  
3. Computation of calibration statistics (median, IQR) for each component score.  
4. Scoring of test windows: forward pass to obtain outputs, compute six scores, standardize, aggregate, smooth.  
5. Thresholding to flag anomalies.  
6. For flagged windows, run attribution to identify contributing factors.  
Dependencies: Calibration stats must be computed before scoring test data; purification must precede final training; attribution requires the trained model and anomaly score.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Model capacity vs. computational cost*: The hybrid Conv-Transformer-LSTM backbone increases representational power but also runtime and memory; simpler alternatives (e.g., pure LSTM) may be faster but less accurate.  
- *Purification strictness*: A higher quantile threshold q removes more potential anomalies, yielding cleaner training data but risks discarding useful normal patterns if set too aggressively; the 30% removal cap mitigates excessive loss.  
- *Fixed vs. adaptive ensemble weights*: Fixed weights (or equal averaging) are simple and stable, but may not optimize detection across diverse anomaly types; learning weights could improve performance but risks overfitting and adds complexity.

**Failure Signatures**  
- *Elevated false positive rate*: Significantly higher than α on clean validation data suggests mis-calibration (e.g., non-robust standardization if scores have zero variance) or insufficient purification.  
- *Poor attribution quality*: Low match ratio in controlled experiments indicates unstable gradients or that the anomaly signal is not well-localized to specific assets; could be due to window length or aggregation smoothing.  
- *Training instability*: Reconstruction or forecast loss fails to converge, possibly due to poor hyperparameters, excessive contamination, or architectural mismatches.

**First Experiments**  
1. *Reconstruction sanity check*: Train the generative backbone on clean synthetic data; verify that reconstruction loss decreases and forecasts exhibit plausible dynamics (e.g., similar autocorrelation to true series).  
2. *Purification impact*: Compare anomaly detection F1 on synthetic data with and without the purification stage, contaminating training data with known anomalies; expect purification to improve robustness.  
3. *Component ablation*: Systematically remove each of the six component scores and re-evaluate detection performance; identify which signals contribute most to overall performance and whether any are redundant.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why does attribution for transient jump-burst anomalies yield much lower recovery than for sustained sector disturbances? Possible causes include gradient sensitivity to short-lived shocks, rolling window length, or dilution from aggregating multiple signals.
2. Can the ensemble aggregation weights be learned in a data-driven, adaptive manner, rather than fixed, to optimize detection across heterogeneous anomaly types? The paper currently uses manually specified or equal weights.
3. How does computational complexity scale with cross-sectional dimension p and forecast horizon H, and what are practical limits for real-time deployment? Only aggregate runtime for moderate dimensions (p≈100) is reported, with no theoretical analysis.
4. Could the purification stage be enhanced by using forecast-based diagnostics (e.g., forecast error) instead of solely reconstruction loss, to better handle contaminated windows that exhibit predictive anomalies?

## Limitations
- The exact synthetic data generation parameters and full training hyperparameters (batch size, epochs, stopping criteria) are not disclosed, making exact replication difficult without the authors' code.
- The gradient-based attribution method lacks validation against alternative feature importance techniques, raising concerns about the stability and reliability of factor contributions.
- Performance evaluations are limited to financial datasets with up to p=150 assets; scalability to higher dimensions or longer horizons remains unproven.

## Confidence
- **SOTA performance on tested financial datasets**: Medium. Results are numerically specific and benchmarked, but external validation is absent.
- **Robustness via multi-signal aggregation**: Medium. The design is theoretically sound, but the individual contribution of each signal is not disentangled empirically.
- **Interpretable factor-level attribution recovers true drivers**: Low. Gradient-based explanations are noisy and not compared to established methods; match ratios, while positive, do not guarantee reliability.

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate synthetic benchmark**: Following the paper's methodological description (e.g., AR process dimensionality, rolling window sizes), generate a synthetic dataset with injected sector shocks. Train ReGEN-TAD and report whether F1 approximates 0.624 within a reasonable tolerance.
2. **Gradient sanity check for attribution**: For a set of clean and anomalous windows, compute Γ_j (gradient of anomaly score w.r.t. asset deviations) and compare to permutation importance (shuffle each asset's series). Low correlation would indicate unstable Γ_j.
3. **Calibration robustness test**: Apply the trained model to a prolonged period of confirmed normal market activity (e.g., a tranquil 2-year stretch). Verify that the empirical false positive rate matches the calibrated α=0.05 threshold, and check for drift over time.
