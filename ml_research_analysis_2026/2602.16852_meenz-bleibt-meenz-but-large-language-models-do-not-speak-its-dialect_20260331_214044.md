# Meenz bleibt Meenz, but Large Language Models Do Not Speak Its Dialect

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.16852
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16852
*   **Reference count:** 0
*   **Primary result:** State-of-the-art open-source LLMs achieve near-zero accuracy (<10%) on Meenzerisch word-definition tasks, while the same models perform near human-level (~87%) on English.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the first digital dataset for Meenzerisch, an endangered German dialect, comprising 2,351 word-meaning pairs extracted from a historical dictionary. It evaluates multiple large language models on two lexical tasks: generating Standard German definitions for Meenzerisch words and vice versa. Results show catastrophic failure across all models, with the best scoring under 6.3% on definition generation and 1.5% on word generation. Even with few-shot learning and automatically derived linguistic rule injection, performance remains below 10%. In stark contrast, models excel on identical English tasks, highlighting a severe deficiency for low-resource dialects. The study concludes that current LLMs lack fundamental representational knowledge of Meenzerisch due to data scarcity, phonological divergence, and missing cultural context.

## Method Summary
The study employs a two-pipeline approach. First, a **dataset creation pipeline** digitized a 1966 physical dictionary for Meenzerisch via scanning, OCR, manual correction, and rule-based word extraction, yielding 2,351 curated pairs split into 250 train, 250 dev, and 1,851 test examples. Second, an **evaluation pipeline** tested various open-source LLMs (Llama, GPT-OSS, Qwen, etc.) using zero-shot, few-shot, and rule-injected prompting on the two tasks, with accuracy measured by an LLM-as-a-judge (for definitions) and exact string match (for words). No model fine-tuning was performed; only inference-time interventions were applied.

## Key Results
*   Best model (Llama-3.3 70B) achieved only **6.27%** accuracy on definition generation and **1.51%** on word generation.
*   Interventions provided minimal gains: edit-distance few-shot reached **9.24%** (definitions), rule injection **8.37%** (definitions); no condition exceeded 10%.
*   The same models achieved **86.89%** average accuracy on an English dictionary benchmark, demonstrating a massive performance gap for the low-resource dialect.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Catastrophic Data Scarcity
*   **Claim:** LLMs fail primarily because Meenzerisch is absent from pretraining corpora.
*   **Mechanism:** Models rely on statistical patterns from vast text data. As an endangered dialect with minimal digital presence, Meenzerisch is not represented, forcing models to guess or rely on superficial Standard German similarities.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Paper notes no prior NLP research on Meenzerisch; dataset is the first digital resource; neighbor papers focus on other German dialects.
*   **Break condition:** Including significant, high-quality Meenzerish text in continued pretraining or fine-tuning.

### Mechanism 2: Systematic Phonological & Morphological Divergence
*   **Claim:** Consistent dialect-specific transformations (e.g., "ch"→"sch", "-en"→"-ele") create a surface-form gap LLMs cannot bridge.
*   **Mechanism:** These patterns are not dominant in pretraining data, so models fail to induce them from few examples. Rule extraction and injection yielded small gains, confirming the gap.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.1 details sound laws; Section 7.2 shows rule injection improved definition accuracy from 6.27% to 8.37%.
*   **Break condition:** Explicit training on parallel data demonstrating these transformations.

### Mechanism 3: Lack of Cultural & Contextual Grounding
*   **Claim:** Many Meenzerisch terms are culture-bound (e.g., carnival, local idioms), requiring socio-cultural schemas absent from LLM training.
*   **Mechanism:** Without exposure to authentic cultural contexts, models cannot accurately map between dialect words and definitions, as the link is not purely lexical.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.1 highlights dialect's role in carnival and local life; dataset includes culturally specific entries like "Mauldabbezierer".
*   **Break condition:** Training on extensive text using Meenzerisch within authentic cultural contexts (e.g., carnival speeches, local folklore).

## Foundational Learning
*   **Low-Resource Language Modeling:** Understanding data sparsity and standard techniques (few-shot, augmentation) is essential to interpret why near-zero performance persists and why interventions yield minimal gains. *Quick check:* Why does scaling from 4B to 70B parameters yield only marginal gains for Meenzerisch but >85% on English?
*   **Dialectology & Sociolinguistics:** Treating Meenzerisch as a systematic, rule-governed variant with specific sociolinguistic status is crucial; misinterpreting it as "noisy Standard German" invalidates analysis. *Quick check:* How do French/Yiddish influences create unpredictable lexical items not derivable from Standard German morphology?
*   **Information Retrieval Evaluation Metrics:** The study uses LLM-as-a-judge (semantic equivalence) and exact string match (orthographic precision). Understanding their properties is key to interpreting the accuracy gap between tasks. *Quick check:* Why is exact string match stricter than the judge metric, and how does this contribute to the performance gap?

## Architecture Onboarding
Dataset Creation Pipeline: Scanning -> OCR -> Manual Correction -> Rule-based Word Extraction -> LLM-based Definition Extraction/cleaning. Evaluation Pipeline: Input (word/definition) -> LLM Inference (various models) -> Output Prediction -> Evaluation (LLM judge/string match) -> Statistics. Dependencies: Dataset artifacts feed evaluation; same LLMs used for cleaning and testing.

**Critical Path:** Obtain dialect dictionary -> Digitize and clean -> Split data -> Run at least one LLM with baseline prompt -> Evaluate. Innovation is revealing catastrophic baseline failure.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   Semi-automatic vs. Full Manual Curation: Chose semi-auto for scalability but incurred 6% major error rate.
*   LLM-as-a-Judge vs. Human Evaluation: Used scalable judge (validated 92% on "equal" cases) vs. costly human evaluation.
*   Greedy Decoding: Used for fair comparison but misses potential diversity that could include correct answers.

**Failure Signatures:**
*   Primary: Test accuracies near zero (4.24% avg definition, 0.56% avg word) vs. English >80%, indicating representational gap.
*   Secondary: Rule injection and few-shot yield <3% absolute gains, failing to break 10%, indicating generalizations not learnable from provided data.
*   Dataset Signal: 12% minor / 6% major error rate in dataset cannot explain near-zero model performance.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1.  Baseline Zero-Shot Evaluation: Feed single word/definition via prompts; measure accuracy via judge or string match. Goal: Establish catastrophic starting point.
2.  Few-Shot In-Context Learning: For Llama-3.3 70B, prepend *k* examples (random or edit-distance selected) to prompt. Goal: Test if models learn from context with similar surface forms.
3.  Automatic Rule Injection: Prepend DeepSeek-R1 generated rule summary to zero-shot prompt. Goal: Test if explicit linguistic transformations compensate for missing inductive bias.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
*   **Question:** What additional language resources (beyond word–definition pairs) would most effectively improve LLM performance on Meenzerisch, and how should they be structured?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 9 states "additional language resources will need to be created" but does not specify types.
*   **Why unresolved:** Optimal resource type (parallel sentences, morphological analyzers, etc.) and scale remain unknown.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments comparing resource categories on Meenzerisch tasks, measuring gains per category.

### Open Question 2
*   **Question:** Can transfer learning from closely related German dialects (e.g., Rhine-Franconian) successfully adapt LLMs to Meenzerisch, or do its unique historical influences (French, Yiddish) create an irreducible gap?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 9 suggests leveraging other dialect pairs but notes Meenzerisch's distinct influences.
*   **Why unresolved:** No experiments test cross-dialect transfer for this dialect; contact phenomena may limit transferability.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluating dialect-adapted LLMs fine-tuned on related dialects on Meenzerisch tasks, analyzing transferred features.

### Open Question 3
*   **Question:** How do LLMs perform on sentence-level Meenzerisch tasks (e.g., translation, sentiment), and does word-level failure fully predict sentence-level challenges?
*   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Evaluation limited to word-level (Section 9), sentence-level work is future.
*   **Why unresolved:** Word-level tasks test lexical knowledge; sentences involve syntax/context. Failure at word level may not preclude sentence-level success.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Building a sentence-level Meenzerisch benchmark and evaluating LLMs on tasks like translation, comparing to word-level.

### Open Question 4
*   **Question:** Why does rule injection improve definition generation but not word generation, and what modeling approaches (e.g., constrained decoding) could better capture dialectal word forms?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Table 7: rule injection increases definition accuracy (6.27%→8.37%) but slightly decreases word generation (1.03%→0.76%).
*   **Why unresolved:** Word generation may require phonological/morphological generation beyond surface rules; LLM may understand semantics but not produce correct orthography.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyzing word generation error types; testing interventions like phonetic embeddings or rule-based post-processing.

### Open Question 5
*   **Question:** What computationally efficient methods (e.g., PEFT, RAG) can significantly improve performance without massive compute, addressing cost inequity?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 9 argues against sole reliance on increased compute to avoid burdening underrepresented cultures.
*   **Why unresolved:** Only prompting and rule injection tested (<10% gains); PEFT/RAG not explored.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparing PEFT (LoRA) and RAG using the Meenzerisch dataset against full fine-tuning and prompting, measuring accuracy/compute trade-offs.

## Limitations
*   **Dataset small & outdated:** 2,351 pairs from 1966 dictionary risks non-contemporary vocabulary; semi-automatic digitization introduced 6% major errors.
*   **Evaluation constraints:** LLM-as-a-judge validated on only 100 samples; exact-match metric is orthographically brittle; no scalable human evaluation.
*   **Scope limited:** Only prompting evaluated, no fine-tuning; tasks restricted to word-level lexicon, not sentence-level language use.

## Confidence
*   **Data scarcity cause:** **High** – Near-zero performance across models aligns with dialect's absence from pretraining.
*   **Phonological divergence cause:** **Medium** – Modest rule-injection gains indicate surface transformations matter but are insufficient.
*   **Cultural grounding cause:** **Low** – Relies on anecdotal/intuitive evidence; no experiment isolates cultural knowledge from lexical mapping.

## Next Checks

2.  **Extended fine-tuning:** Augment training set with contemporary Meenzerisch texts (carnival speeches, social media); fine-tune a baseline LLM to test if breaking 10% requires more in-domain data.
