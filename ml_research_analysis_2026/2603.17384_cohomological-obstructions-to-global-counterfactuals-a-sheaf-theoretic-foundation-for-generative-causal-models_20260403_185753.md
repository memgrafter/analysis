# Cohomological Obstructions to Global Counterfactuals: A Sheaf-Theoretic Foundation for Generative Causal Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.17384
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17384
- **Reference count**: 4
- **Primary result**: The paper introduces a sheaf-theoretic framework for causal models that uses entropic Wasserstein flows to compute globally coherent counterfactuals, avoiding deterministic singularities via topological obstructions (H¹≠0) and providing a Topological Causal Score for discovery.

## Executive Summary
Continuous generative causal models often fail to produce globally coherent counterfactuals when causal graphs contain topological obstructions such as cycles or hidden confounders, leading to deterministic singularities termed "manifold tearing". This work formalizes structural causal models as cellular sheaves over Wasserstein spaces, defining cohomological obstructions via non-trivial first cohomology (H¹≠0). To ensure tractability and avoid singularities, the authors introduce entropic regularization and derive the Entropic Wasserstein Causal Sheaf Laplacian—a system of coupled Fokker‑Planck equations. A key technical innovation is the entropic pullback lemma, which links the first variation of pushforward measures to the Vector‑Jacobian Product (VJP). Combined with the Implicit Function Theorem on Sinkhorn optimality, this enables O(1)-memory reverse-mode gradients independent of iteration depth. Theoretically, the paper proves that unregularized flows suffer finite‑time collapse due to negative synthetic Ricci curvature. Empirical evaluation on a 2D vector field simulation with engineered conflict shows a 64.53% reduction in causal Dirichlet energy without singularities. On PBMC scRNA‑seq counterfactuals, the method achieves successful "entropic tunneling" across the transcriptomic void, while deterministic baselines produce biologically impossible chimeras. Finally, the Topological Causal Score—defined as residual energy at harmonic equilibrium—empirically isolates spurious edges with a 3.79× energy gap versus the true graph, demonstrating a sensitive detector for topology‑aware causal discovery.

## Method Summary
The core method, **Entropic Sheaf Flow**, treats each node's counterfactual distribution as a stalk in a cellular sheaf over Wasserstein spaces. Causal edges are encoded as restriction maps (pushforward operators). The objective is to minimize an Entropic Causal Dirichlet Energy \(E^\varepsilon(\mu)\) over the joint measure. The Wasserstein gradient flow yields a coupled system of McKean‑Vlasov SPDEs (the Entropic Wasserstein Causal Sheaf Laplacian). For each node, the drift consists of two parts: (i) a forward contribution from parents, given by the gradient of the Sinkhorn dual potential \(f^{( \varepsilon)}\) evaluated on the node's particles; (ii) a backward contribution from children, given by the Vector‑Jacobian Product of the causal mechanism \(\Phi\) multiplied by the gradient of the Sinkhorn dual potential \(g^{( \varepsilon)}\) evaluated at the transported particles. The backward gradient \(\nabla_y g^{( \varepsilon)}\) is obtained via the Implicit Function Theorem applied to the Sinkhorn optimality conditions, which avoids unrolling the Sinkhorn iterations and thus uses O(1) memory regardless of iteration depth. The flow is discretized with a Langevin integrator: particles are updated as \(X_i \leftarrow X_i - \eta \cdot \text{drift}_i + \sqrt{2\varepsilon \eta} \cdot \xi\), where \(\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\). The entropic regularization \(\varepsilon > 0\) acts as a diffusion coefficient, preventing finite‑time singularities (manifold tearing) that occur in the unregularized case due to negative synthetic Ricci curvature.

## Key Results
- On a 2D simulation with a topologically conflicting graph (A→B→C and A→C), the entropic sheaf flow reduces the Entropic Causal Dirichlet Energy by **64.53%** without encountering singularities, while deterministic baselines collapse.
- For PBMC scRNA‑seq counterfactuals (intervention from T‑cells to monocytes), the method successfully performs **entropic tunneling** across a low‑density transcriptomic void, producing biologically plausible results; deterministic generative models yield nonsensical chimeras.
- The **Topological Causal Score** (minimum harmonic energy) reveals a **3.79×** larger value for a spurious graph (adding an extra edge) compared to the true graph, demonstrating its sensitivity to topological errors in causal discovery.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Sheaf‑theoretic obstruction (H¹≠0)
- **Claim**: Modeling SCMs as cellular sheaves over Wasserstein spaces provides a rigorous algebraic‑topological definition of cohomological obstructions (H¹≠0) that precisely capture global counterfactual incoherence due to topological conflicts.
- **Mechanism**: Each node's counterfactual distribution is a stalk in the Wasserstein space. Causal edges are restriction maps (pushforwards). The coboundary operator measures local discrepancies between a node's distribution and the pushforward from its parents. Non‑trivial H¹ means there exists a cycle of discrepancies that cannot be resolved by any global section, i.e., no joint distribution can simultaneously satisfy all edge constraints.
- **Core assumption**: The causal graph topology (cycles, hidden confounders) can induce non‑commuting pushforwards that create a "topological friction" irreducible by local adjustments.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - Abstract: "We formalize structural causal models as cellular sheaves over Wasserstein spaces, providing a strict algebraic topological definition of cohomological obstructions (H¹≠0) in measure spaces."
  - Section 4: Assigns stalks as nonlinear Wasserstein spaces \(P^2(M_v)\) and restriction maps as pushforward operators \(\Phi_{uv\#}\).
  - The neighbor paper "HOLOGRAPH" uses sheaf theory for LLM priors, but this work focuses on measure‑theoretic obstructions.
- **Break condition**: If the causal graph is a DAG with invertible, compatible structural equations and no hidden confounders, then H¹=0 (exact complex) and the obstruction vanishes. However, any conflicting cycle or hidden confounder can make H¹ non‑zero.

### Mechanism 2: Entropic regularization prevents finite‑time collapse
- **Claim**: Adding an entropy regularizer (\(\varepsilon>0\)) to the Wasserstein distance transforms the deterministic, potentially explosive gradient flow into a well‑posed stochastic partial differential equation that avoids manifold tearing.
- **Mechanism**: The unregularized Causal Dirichlet Energy \(E^0\) can have negative synthetic Ricci curvature when H¹≠0 (Theorem 11), causing the Wasserstein gradient flow to develop gradient blow‑up (finite‑time singularity). The entropic term \(\varepsilon \mathcal{H}(\mu)\) adds a strictly parabolic diffusion term \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2} \Delta \mu_i\) to the Fokker‑Planck equation (Eq. 9), acting like a heat equation to smooth density spikes and prevent Jacobian collapse.
- **Core assumption**: The entropic regularizer is small enough to preserve the topological gradient signal but large enough to dominate near singularities.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "To ensure computational tractability and avoid deterministic singularities (which we define as manifold tearing), we introduce entropic regularization..."
  - Section 7, Theorem 12: Under \(\kappa_{\text{global}}<0\), the Jacobian determinant collapses to zero in finite time \(T_{\text{tear}} < \infty\) for \(\varepsilon=0\).
  - Equation (9) includes the thermal diffusion \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2} \Delta \mu_i\).
- **Break condition**: If \(\varepsilon\) is too small (\(\varepsilon\to0\)), diffusion vanishes and tearing may occur. If \(\varepsilon\) is too large, topological signal is washed out. An optimal Pareto balance exists (experiments suggest \(\varepsilon\in[0.1,5.0]\)).

### Mechanism 3: O(1)‑memory gradients via IFT on Sinkhorn
- **Claim**: By treating the Sinkhorn dual potentials as the root of an implicit function, the gradient of the topological stress with respect to the potential can be computed via a single linear solve, avoiding O(L) memory overhead from unrolling Sinkhorn iterations.
- **Mechanism**: The Sinkhorn algorithm iteratively solves for dual potentials \(f^{( \varepsilon)}, g^{( \varepsilon)}\) such that optimality conditions \(F(f,g)=0\) hold. The gradient \(\nabla_y g^{( \varepsilon)}\) needed for the entropic pullback lemma (Lemma 4) is the derivative of an implicit function. Applying the Implicit Function Theorem to \(F=0\) yields a linear system for the gradient that does not depend on the number of iterations \(L\), only on the final coupling matrix.
- **Core assumption**: The Jacobian \(\partial F/\partial g\) is invertible at the solution (holds for \(\varepsilon>0\) due to strong convexity) and the solution is unique.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "By integrating this with the Implicit Function Theorem (IFT) on Sinkhorn optimality, we establish a direct algorithmic bridge to automatic differentiation (VJP), achieving O(1)-memory reverse‑mode gradients strictly independent of the iteration horizon."
  - Section 9: The IFT yields a steady‑state gradient \(\nabla_y g^{( \varepsilon)}(y)\) via a single linear solve independent of \(L\).
  - Algorithm 1 steps 14–16: Solve Sinkhorn, then compute \(\nabla_y g^{( \varepsilon)}\) via IFT.
- **Break condition**: As \(\varepsilon\to0\), the coupling matrix becomes nearly singular, the Jacobian ill‑conditioned, and the linear solve unstable. If Sinkhorn fails to converge, IFT does not apply.

## Foundational Learning

1. **Wasserstein Geometry and Otto Calculus**
   - **Why needed**: The entire framework treats probability measures as points on a manifold and uses the Wasserstein metric to define gradients and flows. Without understanding the tangent space as \(L^2(\mu)\) gradients and the continuity equation, the derivation of the Sheaf Laplacian (Eq. 9) will be opaque.
   - **Quick check**: Given a curve \(\mu_t\) in \(P^2(M)\) driven by velocity field \(v_t\) in the continuity equation \(\partial_t \mu_t + \nabla\cdot(v_t \mu_t)=0\), what is the expression for the Wasserstein gradient of the functional \(E(\mu)=\int f(x) d\mu(x)\)?

2. **Cellular Sheaf Theory**
   - **Why needed**: The core innovation is lifting SCMs to sheaves. Understanding stalks (local probability spaces), restriction maps (pushforwards), and the coboundary operator is essential to grasp the definition of cohomological obstructions (H¹≠0) and the Dirichlet energy (Eq. 3).
   - **Quick check**: For a directed graph edge \(e=(u,v)\) with sheaf \(\mathcal{F}\), what is the 1‑cochain \((\delta \mu)_e\) in terms of the stalks and restriction map?

3. **Entropic Optimal Transport and Sinkhorn Algorithm**
   - **Why needed**: The Sinkhorn divergence replaces the vanilla Wasserstein distance to make computations tractable, and the dual potentials \(f^{( \varepsilon)}, g^{( \varepsilon)}\) appear in the Sheaf Laplacian. The entropic pullback lemma (Lemma 4) relies on properties of the Sinkhorn dual.
   - **Quick check**: What is the Sinkhorn dual formulation of \(W_{2,\varepsilon}^2(\mu,\nu)\)? What are the optimality conditions (Sinkhorn equations)?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
SheafGraph – (graph structure and mechanisms) → WassersteinSpace – (pushforward, Sinkhorn) → CausalSheafLaplacian – (drift assembly) → IFTLinearSolver – (∇g via linear solve) → LangevinIntegrator – (particle update). The loop repeats per iteration.

**Critical path** (per iteration)  
1. For each edge \((u,v)\), compute pushforward \(\rho_{u\to v} = \Phi_{uv\#} \mu_u\).  
2. For each node \(i\) and parent \(p\): compute Sinkhorn between \(\mu_i\) and \(\rho_{p\to i}\) to obtain \(f_{i\leftarrow p}^{( \varepsilon)}\). Add \(\nabla f_{i\leftarrow p}\) (evaluated on samples from \(\mu_i\)) to drift\(_i\).  
3. For each node \(i\) and child \(c\): compute Sinkhorn between \(\rho_{i\to c}\) and \(\mu_c\) to obtain \(g_{c\leftarrow i}^{( \varepsilon)}\). Use IFTLinearSolver to get \(\nabla_y g_{c\leftarrow i}\) at points \(y = \Phi_{ic}(X_i)\). Compute VJP \(= J_{\Phi_{ic}}^\top \nabla_y g\) and add to drift\(_i\).  
4. Update particles: \(X_i \leftarrow X_i - \eta \cdot \text{drift}_i + \sqrt{2\varepsilon\eta} \cdot \xi\).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Entropic regularization \(\varepsilon\)**: Too small (\(\varepsilon\to0\)) risks manifold tearing and IFT instability; too large washes out topological signal. Empirically \(\varepsilon\in[0.1,5.0]\).  
- **Particle count \(N\)**: Larger \(N\) reduces sampling error but increases \(O(N^2)\) memory for coupling matrices.  
- **Sinkhorn tolerance**: Early stopping introduces error in drift; too strict increases compute.

**Failure signatures**  
- Energy increases over steps → time step \(\eta\) too large or IFT gradient inaccurate.  
- Exploding particle variance → \(\varepsilon\) too small or numerical instability in drift.  
- IFT linear solver fails to converge → \(\varepsilon\) too small (ill‑conditioned) or Sinkhorn not at optimality.  
- "Biological chimeras" in scRNA‑seq → entropic tunneling failed; consider increasing \(\varepsilon\) or Langevin steps.

**First 3 experiments** (validation suite)  
1. **2D conflict replication**: Use exact parameters from Section 10 (N=500, drifts, \(\varepsilon=0.1\), T=500). Verify E\(^{\varepsilon}\) reduction >64.53% and monitor maximum Jacobian eigenvalue to ensure no singularity (should remain bounded).  
2. **scRNA‑seq tunneling**: Run on PBMC data (15D PCA) as in Section 11. Check that the counterfactual trajectory from T‑cells to monocytes avoids the low‑density void and lands in the monocyte cluster. Quantify density along the path to confirm tunneling.  
3. **Topological Causal Score isolation**: Build synthetic graphs: true (A→B→C) and spurious (add A→C). Use identical mechanisms and initialization (N=200). Run flow to convergence; compute final E\(^{\varepsilon}\). Expect spurious/true ratio >3.5× (targeting reported 3.79×).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1: Extending to higher‑order topological features**  
- **Question**: How can the Entropic Wasserstein Causal Sheaf framework be extended to higher‑order topological features (2‑cells, hyperedges) to capture synergistic confounding and H² obstructions?  
- **Basis in paper**: The Conclusion’s Future Directions states: "extending the causal simplicial complex to include 2‑cells (faces) will allow us to investigate H² cohomological obstructions and synergistic confounding in causal hypergraphs (a formal mathematical roadmap is provided in Appendix B)."  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper develops theory only for 1‑dimensional graphs (edges). Generalization to 2‑cells, higher coboundary operators, and corresponding dynamics is left as future work, with only a sketch in Appendix B. Concrete definitions and PDEs for synergistic mechanisms are missing.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A rigorous extension introducing 2‑stalks, a well‑defined \(d_1: C^1\to C^2\), and an "Entropic Sheaf Laplacian" for 2‑forms. Validation on synthetic causal hypergraph data where pairwise mechanisms conflict with ternary interactions, showing that H² obstruction predicts failure of standard flows.

**Open Question 2: Finite‑sample guarantees for Topological Causal Score**  
- **Question**: What are the precise finite‑sample statistical guarantees (e.g., convergence rates, distributional robustness) for the Topological Causal Score when used for structure learning, and how do graph Betti numbers affect sample complexity?  
- **Basis in paper**: The Conclusion and Appendix C mention: "as preliminary shown in Appendix C, the sample complexity of learning these counterfactual mappings is strictly governed by the Betti numbers of the causal graph, formalizing a topological PAC‑learning paradigm."  
- **Why unresolved**: Appendix C contains only an inequality (Equation 42) without derivation, proof, or discussion of assumptions. Dependence on \(\beta_1\) (first Betti number) and other topological invariants is not quantified, limiting practical use with finite data.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A full statistical theory deriving finite‑sample error bounds for the estimated harmonic energy under i.i.d. sampling and various noise models, including concentration inequalities for the empirical sheaf Laplacian. Simulations showing how increasing \(\beta_1\) inflates required sample size, confirming Equation 42.

**Open Question 3: Adaptive selection of entropic regularization \(\varepsilon\)**  
- **Question**: How should \(\varepsilon\) be chosen theoretically or adaptively to simultaneously ensure topological resolution (avoiding manifold tearing) and guarantee numerical stability of the IFT‑based gradient computation?  
- **Basis in paper**: Section 9.2 discusses the trade‑off: as \(\varepsilon\to0\), the coupling matrix becomes nearly singular, causing the Hessian condition number to scale as \(\exp(\text{diam}/\varepsilon)\). The authors heuristically set \(\varepsilon\in[0.1,5.0]\) for experiments.  
- **Why unresolved**: No principled method for selecting \(\varepsilon\) is provided. The relationship between \(\varepsilon\), graph topology (e.g., H¹ gap), and IFT condition number is unquantified. Adaptive scheduling (e.g., annealing \(\varepsilon\) during flow) is not explored.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Deriving a bound linking \(\varepsilon\) to the minimum singular value of the Sinkhorn coupling matrix or Hessian in terms of graph invariants. An adaptive algorithm that monitors residual energy or harmonic norm to adjust \(\varepsilon\), validated by showing automatic selection matches or exceeds heuristic choices on a range of frustrated graphs.

**Open Question 4: Scalability to large graphs and high‑dimensional spaces**  
- **Question**: Can the Entropic Sheaf Flow be scaled to large causal graphs (e.g., hundreds of nodes) with high‑dimensional state spaces while preserving theoretical properties and computational tractability?  
- **Basis in paper**: The empirical evaluation uses a 3‑node graph (2D) and a scRNA‑seq dataset with 15 PCA dimensions. No experiments beyond a few nodes are reported.  
- **Why unresolved**: Asymptotic analysis with respect to graph size is missing. Time complexity per iteration is not given in terms of \(|V|\) and \(|E|\). For dense graphs, the \(O(N^2)\) per edge coupling could become prohibitive. Numerical stability of IFT for large, possibly poorly conditioned systems is not investigated.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Benchmarking on synthetic large‑scale causal discovery datasets (e.g., Erdős‑Rényi DAGs with hundreds of nodes) measuring run time, memory, and convergence. Development of approximate schemes (e.g., sparse Sinkhorn, low‑rank coupling) or distributed solvers to maintain scalability.

**Open Question 5: Well‑posedness on non‑compact spaces and under weaker assumptions**  
- **Question**: Under what broader assumptions (e.g., non‑compact state spaces, non‑Lipschitz mechanisms, continuous‑time limits) does the Entropic Sheaf Flow guarantee global existence and non‑explosion, particularly with topological obstructions?  
- **Basis in paper**: The proof of the Topological Frustration Inequality (Lemma 6) and energy dissipation (Theorem 10) rely on Assumption 1: compact state spaces \(M^v\) and bi‑Lipschitz mechanisms \(\Phi\). Remark 1 softens bi‑Lipschitz via entropic regularization but retains compactness.  
- **Why unresolved**: Many real‑world generative models operate on unbounded spaces (e.g., real‑valued data without clipping). The well‑posedness of the coupled McKean‑Vlasov SPDE (Equation 9) on non‑compact \(P^2(M^v)\) is not addressed. Possibility of explosion or loss of absolute continuity when harmonic flow drives measures towards infinity remains open.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extending AGS gradient flow theory to unbounded settings by establishing Lyapunov functions (e.g., weighted second moments). Proving existence of global weak solutions under weaker moment conditions and numerically verifying stability for non‑compact spaces.

## Limitations
- **IFT gradient implementation underspecified**: The precise linear system for \(\nabla_y g^{( \varepsilon)}\) (differentiating Sinkhorn KKT conditions) is not fully detailed; a naive autodiff unrolling violates the O(1) memory claim and may introduce truncation bias.
- **PBMC counterfactual pipeline incomplete**: The paper omits how causal mechanisms \(\Phi\) are learned from observational scRNA‑seq data; without this, the "entropic tunneling" result cannot be independently verified.
- **Sensitivity to entropic regularization \(\varepsilon\)**: No principled method for selecting \(\varepsilon\) is provided; the heuristic range [0.1,5.0] may not generalize, and adaptive scheduling is unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Sheaf obstruction formalism (H¹≠0)**: High – Mathematically defined via cellular sheaves over Wasserstein spaces, with clear break conditions.
- **Entropic regularization preventing finite‑time collapse**: High – Theorem 12 proves tear inevitability for \(\varepsilon=0\) under negative synthetic Ricci curvature; diffusion term makes the SPDE well‑posed.
- **O(1) memory via IFT on Sinkhorn**: Medium – Relies on strong convexity and uniqueness; practical IFT implementation is underspecified and may suffer from ill‑conditioning as \(\varepsilon\to0\).
