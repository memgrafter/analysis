# On Calibration of Large Language Models: From Response To Capability
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13540
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13540
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Introduces capability calibration for query-level confidence estimation and demonstrates its utility for pass@k prediction and inference budget allocation; linear probing offers a cost-effective in-domain solution though cross-domain generalization remains limited.

## Executive Summary
Existing LLM confidence estimation typically assesses the correctness of individual sampled responses (response calibration). However, due to stochastic decoding, such estimates conflate the model's inherent ability with random variation and thus fail to reflect true capability. This paper formally defines capability calibration as the expected accuracy of a response drawn from the model's output distribution for a given query. The authors show that response calibration and capability calibration are theoretically distinct, with capability calibration providing a query-level sufficient statistic for applications such as pass@k prediction and compute budget allocation. They evaluate several estimation methods—linear probing of internal activations, verbalized confidence, P(True), and response consistency—across multiple models and datasets. Results indicate that linear probing achieves the best tradeoff: it incurs almost no extra inference cost (less than decoding a single token) and yields the lowest Brier scores on in-domain data, while its performance degrades on out-of-domain tasks. Capability-calibrated confidence derived from probing or verbalized estimates improves solve rates in budget allocation and enables accurate pass@k simulation from a single confidence score.

## Method Summary
The paper compares four approaches to estimate capability-calibrated confidence: linear probing, verbalized confidence, P(True), and response consistency. Linear probing trains a linear model on mean-pooled activations from the last token of each transformer layer, supervised by a ground-truth expected accuracy estimated from many samples. Verbalized confidence and P(True) query the LLM directly via tailored prompts to elicit a probability or binary self-assessment. Response consistency samples multiple outputs and takes the majority vote fraction. Experiments involve three LLMs (Olmo-3-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B, gpt-oss-20b) and seven datasets spanning question answering, mathematics, and multi-subject benchmarks.

Ground-truth expected accuracy for training and evaluation is obtained by averaging correctness over 100 stochastic samples per query. Primary evaluation uses the Brier score (mean squared difference between estimated confidence and ground truth). The utility of capability confidence is demonstrated in two applications: (1) pass@k prediction, where a single confidence score simulates the success rate of k samples; (2) inference budget allocation, where a greedy algorithm distributes compute based on confidence to maximize total solves. ## Key Results
- The work formally distinguishes response calibration (conditioned on a specific output) from capability calibration (conditioned on the query and model), showing that the latter captures the model's true expected accuracy.
- Capability-calibrated confidence serves as a sufficient statistic for pass@k, enabling accurate prediction of the success rate from a single score; probing-based estimates achieve lower mean squared error than response-calibrated oracles.
- In inference budget allocation, capability confidence (from probing or verbalized estimates) improves the number of solved queries over uniform allocation.
- Linear probing on model activations yields the best cost-performance tradeoff—lowest Brier scores with minimal extra inference cost—in in-domain settings, but suffers from poor out-of-domain generalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Removing Stochasticity Bias via Expected Accuracy
The key insight is that response calibration is penalized by the inherent stochasticity of the LLM’s output distribution: even a model with high expected accuracy can produce a low-confidence estimate for a particular sampled output due to random variation. Capability calibration targets the expected accuracy \( p = \mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}\sim f_\theta(\cdot|x)}[C(x,\hat{y})] \), which removes this variance. Theorem 2 decomposes the Brier loss of capability calibration as the expected response calibration loss minus the variance of correctness under the model distribution: \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}} = \mathbb{E}[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{resp}} ] - \text{Var}(C(x,\hat{y})) \). This decomposition isolates irreducible uncertainty, allowing a predictor to focus on the mean. Empirically, as the number of samples \( k \) in pass@k increases, the error of response calibration grows because a single output provides a noisy proxy for \( p \), whereas capability calibration error remains stable (Table 3). The distinction collapses only when the model is deterministic or perfectly correct/incorrect.

### Mechanism 2: Linear Probing for Cost-Effective Query-Level Confidence
A linear probe trained on mean-pooled activations from multiple transformer layers offers an effective and cheap way to estimate capability. The probe learns a linear mapping from the model’s internal representations (which encode uncertainty cues) to a scalar confidence. Because activations are already computed during a standard forward pass, the probe adds negligible overhead—far less than generating even one token. Mean-pooling across layers aggregates complementary signals. In cost-performance plots (Figures 3,4), probing consistently outperforms random baselines and often rivals more expensive methods (e.g., response consistency) while residing in the low-cost regime. This makes it the most practical choice for in-domain deployment. However, the linear relationship may not hold across domains, as evidenced by sharp Brier score increases on out-of-distribution datasets (Table 2).

### Mechanism 3: Capability-Calibrated Confidence as a Sufficient Statistic for Pass@k
Under the assumption of i.i.d. samples, the probability that at least one of \( k \) draws is correct is \( 1-(1-p)^k \), where \( p \) is the query-level expected accuracy. A confidence estimator that directly predicts \( p \) therefore constitutes a sufficient statistic for the entire pass@k curve, enabling simulation without generating multiple responses. This is not true for response calibration, which conditions on a specific output and thus is only a noisy indicator of \( p \). Empirically, an oracle with access to the true \( p \) achieves near-zero MSE in pass@k prediction, whereas an oracle response-calibrated estimator shows increasing MSE with \( k \) (Table 3). Similarly, in budget allocation, using capability confidence in a greedy gain formula yields higher solve rates than uniform sampling.

## Foundational Learning
### Calibration metrics (Brier score, Expected Calibration Error)
- **Why needed here**: The paper evaluates confidence estimators primarily using Brier score. Understanding why Brier score is chosen over ECE is essential because Brier is a strictly proper scoring rule that penalizes both mis calibration and confidence, while ECE is a bagged measure that may obscure per‑query errors.
- **Quick check question**: Why does the paper use Brier score instead of ECE for capability calibration?

### Stochastic decoding in LLMs (temperature sampling, top-p)
- **Why needed here**: The entire premise hinges on LLMs being stochastic; deterministic decoding would collapse the two calibration targets. Knowledge of how temperature and top-p affect the output distribution’s variance is required to understand when capability calibration is most relevant.
- **Quick check question**: How does increasing temperature generally affect the variance of model outputs?

### Linear probing of neural networks
- **Why needed here**: The proposed best-performing method trains linear probes on model activations. Understanding that probing is a white‑box technique that uses frozen representations is crucial, as it underpins the low inference overhead claim.
- **Quick check question**: Why is probing considered lower-cost than methods that require additional forward passes beyond the standard generation?

### Pass@k metric
- **Why needed here**: The main application is predicting pass@k from a single confidence estimate. Pass@k measures the probability that at least one of k samples solves the problem. The Bernoulli relationship \( 1-(1-p)^k \) is key to the simulation.
- **Quick check question**: Given a query-level expected accuracy \( p=0.6 \), what is the expected pass@4?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
LLM engine -> Confidence estimator module -> Application modules (Pass@k simulator, Budget allocator). The Repeat sampler generates multiple responses per query to estimate ground-truth expected accuracy during training and evaluation, feeding into the confidence estimator’s supervision and into metric computation.

### Critical path
1. For each incoming query \( x_i \), obtain a confidence estimate \( p_i \) using the chosen estimator (probe, verbalized, etc.).
2. Compute the marginal gain for adding one more sample: \( gain_i = p_i (1-p_i)^{k_i} \), where \( k_i \) is the number of samples already allocated to query \( i \).
3. Greedily assign the next compute unit (i.e., one additional sample) to the query with the highest gain until the total budget \( N \times B \) is exhausted.
4. After allocation, generate the assigned number of samples per query and return the best (correct) answer if any.

### Design tradeoffs
- Cost vs accuracy: Response consistency yields accurate confidence but multiplies inference cost by \( k_c \) (number of samples). Linear probe adds almost no extra cost (activations reused) but may not generalize. Verbalized/P(True) are mid‑cost and work with APIs, but performance varies across models.
- White‑box vs black‑box: Probing requires open‑weight models and access to internal activations; other methods can be applied with closed APIs.
- In‑domain vs out‑of‑domain: Training a probe on a specific dataset gives best in‑distribution Brier scores, but performance degrades on task types not seen during training; mixing training data from related domains can modestly improve generalization but not fully.

### Failure signatures
- High Brier score on the target domain, indicating poor calibration.
- For probing: a large drop in calibration performance when moving from the training distribution to a held‑out domain.
- In budget allocation: the success rate curve falls below that of uniform allocation, meaning the confidence estimates are misleading.
- Verbalized confidence consistently worse than random baseline on certain models/datasets.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Improving capability calibration performance** – Current methods achieve only moderate Brier scores; no approach consistently approaches perfect calibration. Developing novel estimators (e.g., advanced probing architectures, post‑hoc calibration) that lower Brier while maintaining low cost remains open.
2. **Enhancing cross‑domain generalization** – Probes trained on one domain (e.g., mathematics) perform poorly on others (e.g., MMLU). Strategies such as cross‑domain training, domain adaptation, or meta‑learning are needed to produce universally calibrated confidence.
3. **Extending to downstream applications** – The paper demonstrates pass@k prediction and budget allocation, but capability calibration could be useful for selective prediction, active learning, model routing, human‑AI collaboration, and trustworthy AI. Concrete implementations and evaluations in these settings are future work.
4. **Achieving reliable black‑box calibration** – White‑box probing attains strong in‑domain results, but many LLMs are only accessible via APIs. Effective black‑box confidence estimators that match probing performance without internal access are an important challenge.

## Limitations
- **I.i.d. sample assumption**: pass@k derivation assumes independent and identically distributed draws; real LLM outputs may exhibit serial correlation, breaking the Bernoulli model and inflating prediction error.
- **Probing out‑of‑domain failure**: Linear probes suffer large Brier score increases on datasets not seen during training, limiting their utility without retraining.
- **Verbalized confidence inconsistency**: Elicited probabilities and P(True) scores vary widely across models and tasks, sometimes performing worse than random, making them unreliable, especially for black‑box APIs.

## Confidence
- Theoretical distinction between response and capability calibration: **High** confidence.
- Linear probing provides best cost‑performance tradeoff in‑domain: **Medium** confidence (based on empirical results that may not generalize).
- Capability‑calibrated confidence improves pass@k prediction and budget allocation: **Medium** confidence (supported by simulations but dependent on estimator quality).
- Probes generalize poorly across domains: **High** confidence (consistent out‑of‑domain degradation in Table 2).
