# MieDB-100k: A Comprehensive Dataset for Medical Image Editing

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09587  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09587  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Fine-tuning OmniGen2 on MieDB-100k improves Perception DICE from 0.248 to 0.831 and P-ACC from 0.065 to 0.737, achieving state-of-the-art results across all task perspectives and strong generalization to unseen clinical tasks.

## Executive Summary
MieDB-100k is a large-scale dataset of 112k triplets (input image, text instruction, edited output) designed for text-guided medical image editing. It covers 10 imaging modalities and 69 edit targets, organizing tasks into Perception (mask overlay), Modification (lesion edits), and Transformation (low-level edits). The dataset is constructed via a hybrid pipeline combining modality-specific expert inpainting models, rule-based synthesis, and manual quality checks. Fine-tuning the OmniGen2 model on this data yields dramatic performance gains, establishing a new state-of-the-art and demonstrating robust generalization to previously unseen clinical scenarios.

## Method Summary
Data construction follows a four-stage hybrid pipeline. Stage I trains modality-specific expert inpainting models (e.g., FLUX.1-Fill) to synthesize plausible healthy tissue for lesion removal. Stage II uses deterministic rule-based methods (e.g., alpha-blending masks) to generate perception and transformation pairs. Stage III applies rejection sampling with segmentation models (nnUNet) or VLMs to filter low-quality outputs. Stage IV involves manual quality checks and prompt rephrasing to ensure clinical coherence. This approach balances scalability with fidelity, producing diverse, high-quality editing triplets across a wide range of medical contexts.

## Key Results
- Perception metrics improve dramatically: DICE from 0.248 to 0.831; Perception Accuracy (P-ACC) from 0.065 to 0.737.
- OmniGen2-MIE achieves state-of-the-art performance across all task perspectives, outperforming both open-source and proprietary models (e.g., Nano Banana Pro).
- The model generalizes effectively to unseen clinical tasks, such as editing bone metastasis and adapting to internet-sourced images.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hybrid Curation Pipeline Ensures Fidelity and Scale
The hybrid pipeline combines modality-specific expert models, rule-based synthesis, and manual checks to produce clinically plausible edits at scale. Expert models (e.g., FLUX.1-Fill) generate realistic inpainting within learned distributions for specific modalities, while rule-based methods provide deterministic, scalable outputs for structured tasks. Manual quality checks act as a final filter. Core assumption: expert models can handle modality-specific nuances, and rule-based methods avoid hallucinations for well-defined operations. Evidence includes the pipeline description in the abstract and detailed stages in section 3.2. Break condition: if expert models hallucinate on rare pathologies or rule-based artifacts persist, clinical fidelity may drop, requiring more manual intervention.

### Mechanism 2: Unified Editing Paradigm Bridges Understanding and Generation
Categorizing tasks into Perception, Modification, and Transformation forces the model to jointly learn medical knowledge (segmentation via mask overlay) and generative skills (inpainting, low-level edits). Perception tasks provide grounding that prevents semantically incorrect modifications, while transformation tasks refine generation quality. Joint training leverages cross-task synergies, leading to overall performance gains. Core assumption: medical understanding (e.g., anatomy localization) is a prerequisite for safe and accurate generation. Evidence: ablation study (section 4.4) shows full-task training outperforms single-perspective models. Break condition: if task distributions are imbalanced or perception knowledge does not transfer to modification, joint training could harm individual perspectives.

### Mechanism 3: Modality and Target Diversity Promotes Robust Generalization
Exposure to 10 modalities (CT, MRI, Ultrasound, etc.) and 69 edit targets forces the model to learn invariant editing operations rather than dataset-specific biases. This diversity encourages the model to separate editing semantics from modality-specific appearance, improving out-of-distribution (OOD) performance. Core assumption: diverse training data yields robust feature representations applicable to unseen clinical scenarios. Evidence: strong OOD results on bone metastasis (section 4.5) and internet-sourced images (section E.3). Break condition: if diversity includes noisy/irrelevant samples or unseen tasks involve fundamentally different imaging physics, generalization may fail.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Source Repositories (MSD, ISIC, etc.) → Modality-Specific Expert Inpainting Models → Rejection Sampling (nnUNet/VLM filters) → Triplet Pairing → Post-processing (prompt rephrasing, quality checks).  
**Critical path:** For Modification tasks, the essential flow is: lesion image → mask generation → expert inpainting → rejection sampling → triplet pairing. Any failure in inpainting quality or filtering propagates to final data fidelity.  
**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Scalability vs. fidelity: Rule-based and expert methods scale but risk hallucinations; manual checks ensure quality but limit volume.  
- Modality-specific vs. unified: Separate experts per modality improve accuracy but increase maintenance; a unified model might generalize worse.  
- Automation vs. control: Fully synthetic data is scalable but may miss clinical nuance; real image pairs are rare and often misaligned.  
**Failure signatures:**  
- Data level: inpainting artifacts (texture mismatches), poor mask reconstruction (low DICE), background inconsistencies in transformation pairs.  
- Model level: semantic confusion (editing wrong structures), intensity discontinuities, boundary blending failures (see Figure 12).  
- Evaluation: low P-ACC indicating lack of medical knowledge, high variance in multi-round generation (Table 6).  
**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Benchmark existing models on the MieDB-100k test split using per-perspective metrics (DICE for perception, VLM rubric for modification, PSNR/SSIM for transformation).  
2. Ablation study: Train OmniGen2 on individual perspectives (P-only, M-only, T-only) versus the full dataset to quantify cross-task synergy.  
3. Generalization test: Evaluate the fine-tuned model on OOD targets (e.g., bone metastasis) not seen during modification training, using rubric scoring and preference ranking.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can MieDB-100k be expanded to include underrepresented medical imaging modalities and rare clinical findings to improve model generalizability?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state a primary limitation is "the inherent difficulty of capturing ALL possible medical imaging modalities, and the relative scarcity of data for rare clinical cases."
- **Why unresolved:** The current 10 modalities and 69 targets still leave many clinical scenarios unaddressed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic data collection with quality assurance, followed by benchmarking that demonstrates improved performance on previously under-represented tasks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How reliable are Vision-Language Model (VLM) based automatic scoring metrics for medical image editing, and can they be made consistent with clinical expert evaluation?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors use a VLM rubric to score Modification tasks, acknowledging the challenge of automating reliable assessment and attempting to mitigate "potential critic hallucinations," yet the true reliability remains untested against human experts.
- **Why unresolved:** VLMs may lack specialized medical knowledge, leading to biased or inaccurate quality estimates that could misguide model development.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large-scale comparison of VLM scores with clinician ratings across diverse edit samples, coupled with calibration or fine-tuning of VLM evaluators to match expert judgment.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What are the scaling properties of MieDB-100k—does increasing the dataset size beyond 112k samples yield continuous performance improvements, and are there modality-specific saturation points?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors emphasize "the necessity of scaling up medical editing data" and note that stronger OOD performance of Nano Banana Pro may stem from its massive-scale training data.
- **Why unresolved:** The current dataset size is fixed; the impact of further scaling on various tasks and modalities remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train models on progressively larger subsets or augmented versions of MieDB-100k, measuring performance trends to establish scaling curves and identify any diminishing returns or modality-specific limits.

## Limitations
- Hybrid pipeline fidelity: risk of expert model hallucinations on rare pathologies and rule-based synthesis artifacts; manual quality checks are limited in scope.
- Evaluation metrics: reliance on DICE, PSNR, and an unvalidated VLM rubric may not capture true clinical quality or diagnostic utility.
- Generalization evidence: OOD validation covers only a few targets (e.g., bone metastasis) and internet-sourced images; dataset imbalances could bias results.

## Confidence
- Hybrid pipeline scalability & fidelity: **Medium** (supported by internal description but lacking external clinical audits).
- Unified editing paradigm synergy: **High** (ablation demonstrates clear performance gains across all perspectives).
- Diversity-driven generalization: **Medium** (positive OOD outcomes, yet limited diversity in test scenarios and potential biases remain).
