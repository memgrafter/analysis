# Design and evaluation of an agentic workflow for crisis-related synthetic tweet datasets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.13625
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13625
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The agentic workflow generates synthetic crisis tweets with a 20–50% pass rate after three feedback rounds, producing datasets up to ~58k tweets that closely mirror real tweet characteristics and achieve near-real performance on downstream tasks like geolocalization and damage prediction.

## Executive Summary
The increasing inaccessibility of real Twitter data due to API restrictions severely limits AI development for crisis informatics. Existing annotated datasets are small, event-specific, and costly. This paper presents an agentic workflow that iteratively synthesizes crisis-related tweets. A generator LLM creates tweets conditioned on target labels (location, damage level). An evaluator checks three heuristics: location keyword presence, damage level consistency via kNN on embeddings, and textual diversity via self-BLEU. An augmenter converts failures into structured feedback appended to the next prompt, enabling in-context refinement. Tested on six earthquake events, after three rounds the workflow produced synthetic datasets ranging from 2,547 to 58,495 tweets, with 20–50% passing all checks. The synthetic data closely matches real tweet length distributions and hashtag usage. Downstream AI performance on geolocalization (73.85–87.96% synthetic vs 32.85–76.48% real) and damage prediction (28.60–56.56% synthetic vs 32.16–64.59% real) approached that on real datasets, demonstrating a scalable, flexible alternative to manual annotation.

## Method Summary
The workflow comprises three core components in an iterative loop. A generator LLM produces candidate tweets based on a prompt containing target labels (derived from existing datasets or simulators). An evaluator applies three automated checks: (1) location presence (keyword matching), (2) damage level correctness (kNN similarity between tweet embedding and reference embeddings of real labeled tweets), and (3) diversity (self-BLEU). An augmenter processes failures by converting them into natural language feedback (e.g., "add a location name") and appends it to the prompt for the next round. This in-context learning loop refines outputs without model weight updates. After a fixed number of rounds (e.g., three), all passing tweets are aggregated into the final synthetic dataset.

## Key Results
- After three feedback rounds, 20–50% of generated tweets passed all checks, yielding synthetic datasets from 2,547 to 58,495 tweets across six earthquake events.
- Synthetic datasets closely mirror real tweet characteristics, including length distributions and hashtag usage patterns.
- Downstream AI performance on geolocalization (73.85–87.96% synthetic vs 32.85–76.48% real) and damage prediction (28.60–56.56% synthetic vs 32.16–64.59% real) approached that on real datasets; higher LLM temperature increased diversity and acceptance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism relies on iterative in-context refinement. The generator LLM produces tweets based on a prompt containing target labels and, in subsequent rounds, feedback from previous failures. The evaluator’s heuristic checks provide immediate, automated signals about compliance with location, damage, and diversity constraints. The augmenter translates failures into actionable feedback (e.g., "include a location"). This closed loop progressively aligns the generator’s outputs with desired properties without retraining. Embedding-based similarity (kNN) for damage assessment ties synthetic tweets to the semantic distribution of real crisis tweets, while self-BLEU prevents mode collapse. Because feedback is incorporated via the prompt, the process remains flexible and computationally efficient per iteration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Large Language Model (LLM) Text Generation**: Why needed: To produce fluent, contextually appropriate tweet text conditioned on specific labels. Quick check: Can the LLM follow instructions to include required elements in a single sentence?  
- **k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN) Embedding Similarity**: Why needed: To assess semantic alignment of damage level by comparing tweet embeddings to real labeled references. Quick check: Does a kNN classifier on real embeddings achieve high accuracy on a held-out set?  
- **Self-BLEU Diversity Metric**: Why needed: To quantify lexical diversity within a batch; lower scores indicate broader expression coverage. Quick check: Is the average self-BLEU below a threshold (e.g., 0.4) after each round?  
- **Iterative In-Context Learning (Agentic Workflow)**: Why needed: To refine outputs without model updates, using feedback to steer generation. Quick check: Does the pass rate increase with each feedback round?  
- **Synthetic Data Evaluation via Downstream Tasks**: Why needed: To verify practical utility of synthetic data for AI systems. Quick check: Does a model trained on synthetic data match performance of one trained on real data on a real test set?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Target Label Provider -> Generator LLM -> Evaluator -> Augmenter -> (back to Generator LLM)

**Critical path**:  
1. Start with target label vectors.  
2. Generator produces a batch of tweets.  
3. Evaluator checks each tweet against the three heuristics.  
4. Augmenter generates feedback for failed tweets; passing tweets are saved.  
5. Feedback is aggregated and appended to the prompt for the next generation round.  
6. Loop repeats for a fixed number of rounds.  
7. Final output: union of all passing tweets.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Speed vs quality: Rule-based heuristics (keyword matching, fixed kNN, self-BLEU) are fast but less accurate; more sophisticated evaluators (e.g., LLM prompting) could improve fidelity at higher cost.  
- Pass rate vs dataset size: Stricter heuristics yield higher quality but lower yield; batch size and round count must balance cost and desired size.  
- Heuristic simplicity vs adaptability: Current kNN depends on a fixed reference set; changing crisis types may require new references.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Location failures: No recognized location keyword → generator may ignore location constraint.  
- High self-BLEU: Near-duplicate tweets → mode collapse; feedback may need to emphasize diversity.  
- Low kNN confidence: Synthetic embeddings cluster incorrectly → reference set or embedding model may need adjustment.

**First experiments**:  
1. **Temperature sweep**: Run with LLM temperatures {0.2, 0.5, 0.8} and compare pass rates, diversity, and downstream performance to find an optimal balance.  
2. **Heuristic substitution**: Replace kNN with an LLM-based zero-shot classifier (e.g., "Does this tweet indicate severe damage?") and compare label accuracy (via human eval) and pass rates.  
3. **Round scaling**: Test with 1, 3, and 5 feedback rounds to observe marginal gains in pass rate and dataset size; plot cost-benefit curves.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can the generation of target label vectors be fully automated in the absence of existing labeled crisis datasets or crisis simulators?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors identify that the current workflow "requires target labels to guide the generation process, which assumes a certain level of prior knowledge about the crisis" and that these labels are currently derived from "existing real tweet datasets" or "crisis simulators."
- **Why unresolved**: The reliance on pre-existing labeled data or external simulators limits the workflow's applicability to novel crises or contexts where such resources are unavailable, creating a barrier to truly on-demand synthetic dataset generation.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A demonstrable method for automatically generating plausible and useful target label distributions (e.g., via LLM-based scenario generation, unsupervised clustering of unlabeled social media, or generative modeling of crisis impacts) that yields synthetic datasets effective for downstream AI evaluation tasks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can the damage level correctness check be reliably implemented without depending on reference embeddings from a fixed, labeled dataset?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors note the limitation that "the damage level correctness requires reference embeddings to evaluate the damage level of a synthetic tweet" and suggest this could be addressed by "a small, reliable, and general labeled dataset."
- **Why unresolved**: The dependence on a curated reference dataset reintroduces the original annotation bottleneck and may reduce the workflow's adaptability to new damage taxonomies or crisis types not covered by the reference set.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An evaluation of alternative compliance checks, such as using zero-shot or few-shot prompting with a capable LLM as the evaluator, or developing embedding-based similarity measures that do not require a fixed labeled corpus, showing comparable or better correlation with human judgment of damage level.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What is the quantitative trade-off between computational efficiency (speed, cost) and the quality (fidelity, utility) of the resulting synthetic tweet dataset when modifying the agentic workflow's components?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state they "implemented the generator, evaluator, and augmenter to prioritize the speed of the workflow" but acknowledge this "may have reduced output quality," proposing improvements like "a more capable LLM" or "an LLM-based compliance evaluator."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not systematically measure how specific changes (e.g., LLM size/evaluator sophistication) impact the cost-quality frontier, leaving users without guidance on optimal configurations for different resource constraints.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A controlled study varying generator and evaluator model scales, along with feedback round counts, while measuring dataset quality via multi-dimensional metrics (e.g., label accuracy, linguistic diversity, AI system evaluation correlation) and computational cost (e.g., API calls, latency, monetary expense).

## Limitations
- Over-reliance on simplistic heuristics (location keyword, damage kNN, self-BLEU) may compromise label fidelity and realism.
- Synthetic data evaluation omitted many crucial dimensions (temporal dynamics, user behavior, network, semantics), limiting understanding of data fidelity.
- Downstream task comparisons lack rigorous experimental design (e.g., model training details, statistical tests), undermining confidence in the reported utility.

## Confidence
- **Workflow compliance** (heuristic pass rate improvement): High  
- **Data fidelity** (length distribution, hashtag usage similarity): High  
- **Downstream utility** (geolocalization and damage prediction performance): Medium  

## Next Checks
1. Human evaluation: Annotate a random sample of synthetic tweets for location, damage level, and realism; compare against real tweet baselines to validate heuristic labels.
2. Comprehensive feature analysis: Extract temporal, user, network, and linguistic attributes from synthetic and real datasets; apply statistical tests (Kolmogorov–Smirnov, effect sizes) to quantify distributional gaps.
3. Transfer learning experiment: Train geolocalization and damage prediction models exclusively on synthetic data, evaluate on real test data; use multiple random seeds and statistical significance testing to assess realistic generalization.
