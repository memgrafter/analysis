# RAPID: Risk of Attribute Prediction-Induced Disclosure in Synthetic Microdata

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09235
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09235
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** RAPID quantifies attribute-inference risk by simulating an attacker who trains a predictive model solely on released synthetic data and applies it to original quasi-identifiers; on UCI Adult (binary income), ~72% of records exceeded the default risk threshold.

## Executive Summary
RAPID introduces a metric to assess inferential disclosure risk in synthetic microdata, shifting focus from traditional identity-matching threats to the more realistic scenario where an attacker predicts sensitive attributes from quasi-identifiers. The method trains an attacker model on the released synthetic data, applies it to the original quasi-identifiers, and evaluates prediction quality relative to a baseline (e.g., marginal class frequencies). Simulations and real-data experiments (UCI Adult) demonstrate that RAPID scales with the strength of dependence between quasi-identifiers and the sensitive attribute. In the Adult example, a substantial majority of records were flagged as at-risk, highlighting significant vulnerability even when classical identity disclosure measures might be low. The resulting proportion offers a bounded, interpretable summary that complements utility diagnostics for informed release decisions.

## Method Summary
RAPID simulates a realistic attacker with access only to released synthetic data and target individuals’ quasi-identifiers. The custodian uses the original data to compute risk: a predictive model (e.g., random forest) is trained on the synthetic quasi-identifiers and sensitive attribute; this model predicts on the original quasi-identifiers. For categorical sensitive attributes, each record receives a normalized gain score: r_i = (g_i - b_i)/(1 - b_i), where g_i is the predicted probability for the true class and b_i is the original marginal frequency of that class. A record is at risk if r_i exceeds a threshold τ (default 0.3). For continuous sensitive attributes, risk is based on relative prediction error e_i; a record is at risk if e_i < ε (default 10%). The final RAPID metric is the proportion of original records flagged as at-risk.

## Key Results
- RAPID increases monotonically with the strength of dependence between quasi-identifiers and the sensitive attribute in controlled simulations.
- On the UCI Adult dataset (predicting binary income from quasi-identifiers), approximately 72% of records had normalized gain exceeding τ=0.3, indicating high attribute-inference vulnerability.
- The metric yields an interpretable, bounded proportion of at-risk individuals and can be combined with utility measures to evaluate privacy-utility trade-offs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** RAPID quantifies attribute-inference risk by simulating a realistic attacker who trains a predictive model solely on released synthetic data and applies it to original quasi-identifiers.  
**Mechanism:** The custodian uses the original data to simulate the attack: fit a model on synthetic data (Z^s), predict on original X_Q, and compare predictions to true y. The difference between model performance and a baseline (e.g., marginal class frequencies) indicates information leakage.  
**Core assumption:** The attacker has access only to the released synthetic data and quasi-identifiers of target individuals; no auxiliary real data for validation.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "An adversary trains a predictive model solely on the released synthetic data and applies it to real individuals’ quasi-identifiers."  
- [section 2.1] "Let the original microdata be denoted by Z = [X_Q, X_U, y] ... The attacker trains a predictive model M on (X_Q^s, y^s) and applies this model to X_Q to produce predictions ŷ."

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** For categorical sensitive attributes, RAPID uses a baseline-normalized confidence score that measures how much more confident the attacker is about the true class than would be expected from class prevalence alone.  
**Mechanism:** For each record i, compute g_i = Pr(ŷ_i = y_i | x_i) from the attacker model, baseline b_i = empirical frequency of class y_i in original data, then normalized gain r_i = (g_i - b_i)/(1 - b_i). A record is at risk if r_i > τ.  
**Core assumption:** The baseline b_i computed from original data reflects the attacker’s prior knowledge without quasi-identifiers. Using original marginals (rather than synthetic) ensures consistent baseline across synthesizers.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "For categorical attributes, it uses a baseline-normalized confidence score comparing predicted probabilities to marginal class frequencies."  
- [section 2.2] "We compute a normalized gain score, r_i = (g_i - b_i)/(1 - b_i) that measures the improvement in prediction confidence over baseline, normalized by the maximum possible improvement."

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** For continuous sensitive attributes, RAPID defines risk as the proportion of records where the attacker’s prediction falls within a specified relative error tolerance, providing a direct measure of predictive accuracy that maps to disclosure.  
**Mechanism:** Compute prediction error e_i (e.g., symmetric relative error). Flag record as at risk if e_i < ε. RAPID_cont = (1/n) Σ I(e_i < ε).  
**Core assumption:** A prediction within a small relative error ε constitutes a successful attribute inference; the threshold ε reflects the tolerable precision for the sensitive attribute.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "For continuous attributes, it uses the proportion of records where predictions fall within a specified relative error tolerance."  
- [section 2.3] "When the confidential attribute y is continuous, we assess whether the model prediction ŷ_i is sufficiently close to the true value y_i ... A record i is considered at risk if at-risk_i = I(e_i < ε)."

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept:** Inferential disclosure  
  **Why needed here:** RAPID redefines disclosure risk from identity matching to attribute inference; understanding that publishing data can enable accurate prediction of sensitive attributes is fundamental.  
  **Quick check question:** "If an attacker can predict a person’s income from their age and education using released data, does that count as a disclosure even if no identity match is made?"

- **Concept:** Baseline normalization  
  **Why needed here:** Raw prediction accuracy can be misleading due to class imbalance; normalized gain isolates the information contributed by quasi-identifiers.  
  **Quick check question:** "Why is comparing the attacker’s confidence to the marginal class frequency better than just looking at classification accuracy?"

- **Concept:** Threshold-based risk summarization  
  **Why needed here:** Converting continuous risk scores into binary flags (via τ or ε) yields an interpretable proportion that can be compared across datasets and aligned with policy.  
  **Quick check question:** "How does choosing a higher τ (e.g., 0.7 vs 0.3) change the interpretation of RAPID’s risk estimate?"

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Original Data (Z: [X_Q, y]) & Synthetic Data (Z^s: [X_Q^s, y^s]) -> Attacker Model Training (M on X_Q^s, y^s) -> Prediction on Original X_Q -> Scoring (r_i for categorical, e_i for continuous) -> Aggregation (mean of risk flags = RAPID). Optional: bootstrap for uncertainty.
- **Critical path:** The minimal viable evaluation follows: train attacker on synthetic → predict on original QIs → compute per-record scores → aggregate to RAPID. Any failure in model training or prediction invalidates the risk estimate.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Attacker model choice: stronger models (RF, GBM) may reveal higher risk but are less interpretable; simpler models (logistic) are conservative but may underestimate risk.  
  - Threshold selection: τ/ε must balance sensitivity vs specificity; defaults (τ=0.3, ε=10%) are starting points but require calibration to policy context.  
  - Baseline source: using original marginals vs synthetic marginals; original ensures consistency but assumes known population prevalences.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Attacker model performance close to baseline (r_i ≈ 0) despite strong synthetic utility may indicate that synthetic data failed to preserve quasi-identifier → sensitive attribute relationships → false sense of privacy.  
  - Extremely high RAPID (≈1) suggests synthetic data may be overfitting or reproducing original records too closely (potential memorization).  
  - Large variability across bootstrap replicates or synthesizer folds indicates unstable risk estimates; may require more data or different synthesis method.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Toy validation: Use a small simulated dataset with known dependency strength (e.g., κ parameter) to verify that RAPID increases monotonically with dependency. Run with both categorical and continuous sensitive attributes.  
  2. Threshold sensitivity: Compute RAPID across a grid of τ (categorical) or ε (continuous) values to produce a threshold curve. Identify inflection points where risk drops sharply; use to justify threshold choice for a given policy.  
  3. Synthesizer comparison: Apply RAPID to synthetic data generated by at least two different methods (e.g., CART vs. generative model) using k-fold cross-validation. Compare mean RAPID and confidence intervals to determine which method offers better privacy-utility trade-off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can RAPID be extended to assess simultaneous inference risk for multiple sensitive attributes, rather than evaluating them separately? (Section 6.2 notes current formulation assesses one attribute at a time; multi-attribute extensions are future work.)
- What data-driven methods can determine the thresholds τ (categorical) and ε (continuous) in a principled way, reducing reliance on arbitrary expert judgment? (Paper acknowledges lack of universal guidance; permutation tests are suggested but not developed.)
- How can RAPID be combined with utility measures into a unified decision framework for synthetic data release? (Section 6.3 calls for integration in unified risk-utility dashboards.)
- Can RAPID be adapted for longitudinal data, where individuals have repeated observations, and how should within-person correlation be incorporated into the risk model? (Conclusion mentions application to longitudinal data as a natural direction requiring adaptation for temporal dependence.)

## Limitations
- Synthetic generation parameters (CART settings, m) and the precise quasi-identifier set are not fully reported, preventing exact replication of the Adult results.
- Attacker model hyperparameters (mtry, node size, calibration) are unspecified; risk estimates may be sensitive to model capacity and choice.
- Threshold defaults (τ=0.3, ε=10%) appear arbitrary, and multi-class handling lacks validation; extreme class imbalance can destabilize normalized gain.

## Confidence
- Attacker simulation realism: Medium (formal threat model is sound, but assumption of no auxiliary data may not hold in practice)
- Baseline-normalized confidence: High (mathematically sound and isolates predictive contribution of quasi-identifiers)
- Threshold-based aggregation: Medium (proportion is interpretable but threshold-dependent and may require policy calibration)
