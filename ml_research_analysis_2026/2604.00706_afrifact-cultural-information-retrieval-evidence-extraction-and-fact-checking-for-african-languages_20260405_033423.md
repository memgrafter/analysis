# AfrIFact: Cultural Information Retrieval, Evidence Extraction and Fact Checking for African Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00706
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00706
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art multilingual embedding models exhibit a 50–70% drop in cross-lingual retrieval nDCG@10 for African languages; open-source LLMs achieve near-random (~33%) zero-shot fact-checking accuracy, but few-shot prompting and QLoRA fine-tuning yield significant improvements.

## Executive Summary
AfrIFact presents the first end-to-end multilingual fact-checking benchmark for ten African languages and English, focused on healthcare and culture-news domains. The dataset includes over 18,000 human-annotated claims with evidence spans. Evaluation reveals critical deficiencies: embedding models fail in cross-lingual retrieval, and LLMs struggle with zero-shot verification even when provided evidence. However, targeted few-shot prompting and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) substantially improve performance, demonstrating that tailored training on small-scale, culturally relevant data can enhance multilingual fact-verification capabilities.

## Method Summary
The study constructs a unified corpus from AfriDocMT (health), Wikipedia, and XL-Sum (culture-news) and annotates claims with gold evidence spans across 11 languages. The benchmark evaluates three sequential stages: Information Retrieval (IR), where a query in any language must retrieve relevant documents from a multilingual pool; Evidence Extraction (EE), which ranks sentences within a retrieved document; and Claim Verification (CV), where an LLM classifies a claim as SUPPORTS, REFUTES, or NOT_ENOUGH_INFORMATION. IR and EE use embedding models (e.g., AfriE5, Qwen3-Embedding) with cosine similarity ranking; CV uses LLMs (e.g., AfriqueQwen-14B) in zero-shot, few-shot, and QLoRA-fine-tuned configurations. Metrics are nDCG@10 for IR, nDCG@3 for EE, and accuracy for CV, with experiments isolating monolingual vs. multilingual retrieval and the impact of providing evidence spans.

## Key Results
- Cross-lingual retrieval nDCG@10 for African languages drops 50–70% when relevant documents exist in other languages compared to monolingual settings.
- Cultural/news content retrieval significantly outperforms healthcare content across embedding models.
- LLMs perform near-random (~33% accuracy) in zero-shot fact-checking on African languages; providing evidence spans yields inconsistent improvements. Few-shot prompting improves accuracy by up to 43%, and QLoRA fine-tuning adds up to 26% absolute gain.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** State-of-the-art multilingual embedding models exhibit severe deficiencies in cross-lingual retrieval for African languages, with nDCG@10 dropping 50–70% when relevant documents exist in other languages compared to monolingual settings.
- **Mechanism:** The models' embedding spaces fail to align semantically equivalent content across typologically diverse, low-resource African languages and English. Retrieval performance collapses because the model cannot effectively rank a relevant document in a non-query language above irrelevant documents in the query language when both are present in the corpus.
- **Core assumption:** Embedding models trained on predominantly high-resource language data develop a "monolingual bias," where similarity metrics are optimized for within-language matching and do not generalize to the morphological and syntactic diversity of African languages without explicit cross-lingual training.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Evaluation shows that state-of-the-art embedding models exhibit severe cross-lingual retrieval deficiencies, with nDCG@10 scores dropping 50–70% when relevant documents exist in other languages compared to monolingual settings."
  - [section 5.1.1] Analysis on AfrIFact-Health showing large gaps between "mono" and solid bars in Figure 3, quantifying the drop.
  - [corpus] Table 11 (Appendix C) compares monolingual vs. multilingual retrieval settings; e.g., for multilingual-e5-large, average African language nDCG@10 drops from 0.424 (monolingual eval on multilingual corpus) to 0.149 (standard multilingual eval) on Health data.
- **Break condition:** If a model maintains <10% performance gap between monolingual and multilingual retrieval settings on this benchmark, the mechanism of "severe cross-lingual deficiency" is falsified for that model.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Cultural and news content is retrieved more effectively than healthcare content across embedding models, indicating domain-specific challenges in low-resource language retrieval.
- **Mechanism:** The linguistic and topical distribution of pretraining data for embedding models likely contains more cognitively and culturally salient information (e.g., news, politics, famous people) for African languages than specialized, technical health terminology. This creates a retrieval bias where models have stronger latent representations for general cultural/news concepts.
- **Core assumption:** The scarcity of digitized, high-quality parallel healthcare corpora for African languages in the pretraining data of these models leads to weaker cross-lingual alignment for domain-specific medical terminology compared to more commonly discussed cultural/news topics.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Cultural/news content is retrieved more effectively than healthcare content."
  - [section 5.1.2] "Comparing the scores, we found that embedding models have different behavior across domains. where e5-based embeddings... are better on AfrIFact-Culture-News, and Qwen3-based embeddings are better on AfrIFact-Health." This shows a relative domain gap.
  - [corpus] Tables 4 and 5 show consistently higher nDCG scores for Culture-News vs. Health across almost all models and languages (e.g., AfriE5-Large-instruct: 0.490 vs 0.222 avg on multilingual Health corpus; 0.521 vs 0.490 avg on Culture-News evidence extraction).
- **Break condition:** If a model's performance difference between the two domains (Health vs. Culture-News) is negligible (e.g., <0.05 nDCG difference) across a majority of African languages, the mechanism of "domain-specific difficulty gradient" is weak for that model.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Open-source LLMs lack robust multilingual fact-verification capabilities in African languages in zero-shot settings, and providing gold evidence spans does not consistently improve accuracy, indicating models default to parametric knowledge over grounded evidence.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs pretrained on predominantly English and high-resource language data have underdeveloped internal representations for reasoning about factuality in African languages. Furthermore, their training objective (next-token prediction) does not incentivize strict adherence to provided evidence, leading to hallucination or reliance on parametric memory even when evidence is present.
- **Core assumption:** The fact-verification capability in low-resource languages is bottlenecked by both linguistic understanding and the model's propensity for "evidence grounding"—the ability to suppress internal knowledge and base a judgment solely on a given text span.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "LLMs lack robust multilingual fact-verification capabilities in African languages... Providing evidence does not consistently improve fact-checking accuracy."
  - [section 5.3] "Zero-shot fact verification remains extremely challenging... near-random fact checking capability." and "Providing evidence does not consistently improve fact-checking accuracy... suggesting that models often rely more on their internal knowledge."
  - [corpus] Figure 5 (confusion matrices) shows that with evidence, models increase "NOT_ENOUGH_INFORMATION" predictions (being conservative) but do not systematically increase "SUPPORTS" accuracy, indicating a shift in prediction distribution rather than clear grounding.
- **Break condition:** If a model achieves >70% zero-shot accuracy on African languages *and* shows a consistent >15% accuracy boost when provided gold evidence across both domains, the mechanism of "evidence underutilization due to parametric bias" is overcome.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Cross-lingual Semantic Alignment**
  - **Why needed here:** AfrIFact's core finding is the failure of current models to map a claim and its relevant evidence across languages. A learner must understand that this is not a translation problem but an embedding space geometry problem—the model must place a sentence in Language A and its semantically equivalent in Language B closer in vector space than to any unrelated sentence in either language.
  - **Quick check question:** If you have a Swahili claim and a parallel English evidence document, why might an embedding model still fail to retrieve the English document when querying in Swahili?

- **Concept: Domain-Specific Information Retrieval Challenges**
  - **Why needed here:** The 20-30% performance gap between Culture-News and Health domains shows that retrieval difficulty is not uniform. A learner must grasp that domain terminology, text structure (e.g., clinical vs. narrative), and pretraining data distribution create distinct "retrieval landscapes" even for the same model.
  - **Quick check question:** What characteristics of health-related text (vs. cultural news) might make it harder for a general-purpose embedding model to match across languages?

- **Concept: Evidence Grounding vs. Parametric Recall in LLMs**
  - **Why needed here:** The fact-checking results reveal a key behavioral pattern: models often ignore provided evidence. A learner must differentiate between a model's *ability to recall facts* (from training) and its *ability to verify a fact against a specific source* (a grounded reasoning task). The former is measured in zero-shot accuracy; the latter is measured by the delta when evidence is provided.
  - **Quick check question:** In Table 15, why does AfriqueQwen-14B's accuracy jump from ~34% (0-shot, no evidence) to ~77% (3-shot, with evidence) on Health data? Is this purely "learning" from examples, or is it learning to use the evidence?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Multilingual Corpus:** Unified pool of documents from Health (AfriDocMT), Culture-News (Wikipedia + XL-Sum), and English sources. (~18k documents).
  2. **Retrieval Engine:** Consumes a claim (in any of 11 languages) and the corpus, outputs top-k document IDs. Uses sentence embeddings from models like `multilingual-e5-large` or `AfriE5-Large-instruct`.
  3. **Evidence Extraction Module:** A pass over a *single* retrieved document. Ranks its sentences by similarity to the claim to select top-3 evidence spans.
  4. **Fact-Verification Classifier:** LLM (e.g., AfriqueQwen-14B) prompted to classify (SUPPORTS/REFUTES/NEI) given a claim and either just the document or document+selected evidence spans.
  5. **Evaluation Harness:** `lm-eval` framework computes nDCG@10 (retrieval/evidence) and accuracy (verification).

- **Critical path:** The performance bottleneck cascade is: **Retrieval → Evidence Extraction → Verification**. A failure at **retrieval** (not finding the correct document) guarantees failure downstream. Even with correct retrieval, **evidence extraction** must find the *exact* supporting sentence. Finally, the **verifier** must correctly interpret that evidence. The paper shows retrieval is the weakest link for African languages.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Monolingual vs. Multilingual Corpus:** A smaller, monolingual corpus boosts performance but lacks cross-lingual realism. The paper uses a large multilingual corpus to expose model weaknesses.
  - **Evidence Span Length:** Fixed to sentence-level. Longer spans might provide more context but increase noise; shorter spans risk missing crucial context. This is a pragmatic simplification for annotation.
  - **Few-shot vs. Fine-tuning:** Few-shot prompting (3 examples) gives large gains (~43%) but is inference-costly and prompt-sensitive. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) gives further gains (~26%) but requires training compute and risks overfitting on small dataset.

- **Failure signatures:**
  1. **Retrieval:** nDCG@10 for African languages < 0.2 on the full multilingual corpus (Table 4). Especially low scores for Oromo, Twi, Wolof.
  2. **Evidence Extraction:** Large gap (>0.3 nDCG@3) between English and African language scores on Health domain (Table 5). Indicates failure to identify salient sentences in non-English docs.
  3. **Verification:** Near-random (~33%) zero-shot accuracy on African languages (Figure 4, Table 15). If with-evidence performance is similar to without-evidence, the model is not grounding.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce Core Retrieval Gap:** Run `multilingual-e5-large` on the AfrIFact-Health test set in both monolingual and multilingual corpus settings (as in Figure 3). Expect ~50-70% drop in nDCG@10 for African languages. Validate the primary finding.
  2. **Isolate Domain Effect:** Compare average nDCG@10 for Culture-News vs. Health queries using the same model (e.g., AfriE5-Large-instruct) on the full corpus. Expect ~0.1-0.2 higher score for Culture-News. Tests Mechanism 2.
  3. **Evidence Grounding Diagnostic:** For AfriqueQwen-14B, evaluate 0-shot accuracy with and without gold evidence spans on a small sample (e.g., 100 claims per language). Calculate the "evidence delta." Expect a small or negative delta for most languages, confirming evidence underutilization (Mechanism 3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What linguistic or representational factors underlie the 50–70% nDCG@10 drop in cross-lingual information retrieval for African languages compared to monolingual settings?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ1 directly investigates embedding model effectiveness in IR, with results showing severe performance degradation when relevant documents exist in other languages (Section 2, Figure 3, Table 4).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper quantifies the gap but does not deeply analyze causes, such as inter-lingual embedding alignment issues, morphological-syntactic divergences, or training data language imbalance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Cross-lingual embedding space visualization, fine-grained error analysis by language pair, ablation studies testing impact of morphological complexity and corpus size.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why is evidence extraction significantly less effective for the health domain than for culture-news across African languages, and what domain-specific challenges drive this gap?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ2 examines evidence extraction precision, with results showing a larger English–African language gap in health (e.g., Swahili 0.52 vs. English 0.79 nDCG@3) than in culture-news (Section 5.2, Table 5).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper notes the disparity but does not systematically explore how characteristics of health documents (e.g., terminology density, structured content) impact evidence localization or annotator difficulty in that domain.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare lexical diversity, sentence length, and entity density between health and cultural documents; analyze annotation error patterns; investigate domain adaptation pretraining effects.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does providing annotated evidence spans not consistently improve fact-checking accuracy for LLMs in zero- and few-shot settings, even when retrieval is accurate?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ3 evaluates LLM fact-verification with and without evidence, finding marginal or inconsistent gains from evidence across models (Section 5.3, Figure 4, Table 6).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper observes the phenomenon but does not unpack its mechanism, such as whether models rely on internal knowledge, the difficulty of matching evidence to claims, or prompt engineering limitations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Error analysis distinguishing "evidence neglect" from "evidence misinterpretation"; ablation studies varying evidence span length and position; measuring model attention distribution over evidence.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the translation-based construction of the health split (from English to African languages) impact downstream fact-checking performance, and to what extent do translation artifacts propagate as noise?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The health dataset relies on manual translation of English claims and documents (Section 3.1, Figure 2), with quality checked via COMET scores, but the effect on multilingual fact-checking is not evaluated.
- **Why unresolved:** Translation quality may introduce semantic shifts or cultural mismatches, but the paper does not quantify their cascading impact on IR, evidence extraction, or verification tasks.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare performance on native African-language health documents (if available) vs. translated versions; controlled experiments on subsets with varying translation quality; human evaluation of claim cultural appropriateness post-translation.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What are the error propagation patterns when integrating IR, evidence extraction, and fact-checking into an end-to-end pipeline for African languages, and how do they differ from isolated task performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Limitations state that each step is evaluated standalone, and future work should explore end-to-end pipelines (Section 6). Current results show gaps in each stage, but joint behavior is unknown.
- **Why unresolved:** Modular evaluation may mask error accumulation (e.g., retrieval failure cascading to verification), and cross-task optimization remains unstudied.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Build full pipeline and measure impact of errors at each stage on final accuracy; compare isolated vs. cascaded settings; design multi-task learning or joint optimization approaches.

## Limitations
- Missing fine-tuning hyperparameters (learning rate, optimizer) hinder exact replication of QLoRA results.
- Few-shot example selection strategy (random vs. curated) and exact prompt templates are not fully detailed.
- The dataset covers only 10 African languages and two domains, limiting generalizability.
- The health data is translation-based, potentially introducing artifacts not present in native-language documents.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Cross-lingual gap):** High. Consistent 50–70% nDCG@10 drop across models and languages (Tables 4, 11; Figure 3).
- **Mechanism 2 (Domain gap):** Medium. Culture-News > Health in retrieval is clear, but magnitude varies and may reflect pretraining bias rather than intrinsic domain difficulty.
- **Mechanism 3 (Evidence underutilization):** Medium. Zero-shot ~33% accuracy and limited evidence boost are robust, but fine-tuning mitigates the issue, suggesting the bias is addressable.
