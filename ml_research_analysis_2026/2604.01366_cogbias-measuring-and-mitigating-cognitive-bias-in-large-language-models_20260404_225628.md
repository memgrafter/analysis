# CogBias: Measuring and Mitigating Cognitive Bias in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01366  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01366  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Introduces LLM CogBias benchmark; finds cognitive biases linearly separable (up to 99.8% probe accuracy); achieves 26–32% bias reduction via activation steering with moderate capability preservation.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether cognitive biases in large language models (LLMs) are represented in internal activations and can be mitigated. The authors introduce **LLM CogBias**, a benchmark covering four bias families (Judgment, Information Processing, Social, Response) and evaluate three state-of-the-art instruct models. They find systematic biases across all families. Using **contrastive linear probing**, they demonstrate that bias signals are linearly separable, achieving high classification accuracy. They then apply **activation steering**—subtracting a bias direction from hidden states—and achieve a significant reduction in biased outputs (26–32%) while largely preserving performance on downstream tasks. The effectiveness of steering varies by bias family: Judgment and Information Processing biases require strong early-layer intervention, while Social and Response biases respond to gentler mid-layer steering. Despite the bias directions being nearly orthogonal across models, steering effectiveness correlates across architectures, suggesting a shared functional organization.

## Method Summary
The study employs a three-stage pipeline. First, **behavioral profiling** uses the LLM CogBias benchmark (5,300 instances across four families) to quantify baseline bias in three models. Second, **contrastive linear probing** extracts residual-stream activations from paired bias-salient and debias-neutralizing prompts, trains logistic regression probes per layer, and evaluates classification accuracy to test linear separability. Third, **activation steering** computes a mean-difference bias vector (`v_bias = mean(activations_bias) – mean(activations_control)`), normalizes it, and modifies hidden states during generation (`h' = h – α·v_bias`). A grid search over steering strength `α` and target layers identifies Pareto-optimal configurations that maximize bias reduction while retaining at least 50% capability on a 25-benchmark suite.

## Key Results
- Bias signals are linearly separable in activation space, with mean probe accuracy exceeding 93% across all four families; peak accuracy reaches 99.8%.
- Activation steering reduces bias scores by 26–32% with minimal capability loss (e.g., <5% drop on most downstream tasks), though excessive `α` causes severe degradation in some models.
- Optimal steering parameters cluster into two groups: Judgment and Information Processing biases (D1/D2) require strong early-layer intervention (`α`~1.8–2.2), while Social and Response biases (D3/D4) yield to gentle mid-layer steering (`α`~0.1).
- Bias directions are nearly orthogonal across models (mean cosine similarity 0.01), yet steering effectiveness correlates significantly (r=0.621, p<0.001), implying shared functional organization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Cognitive biases in LLMs are encoded as linearly separable directions in activation space.
- **Mechanism:** For each bias family, train a logistic regression probe on hidden states from bias-salient vs. debias-neutralizing prompts; high test accuracy indicates the bias signal can be captured by a linear classifier.
- **Core assumption:** Biases produce a consistent shift in the model’s internal representation that is distinguishable from non‑biased states along a linear direction.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "Using contrastive linear probing, we show that these biases are encoded as linearly separable directions, achieving up to 99.8% accuracy."
  - Section 5: Figure 1 and Table 17 report >93% mean probe accuracy across all four families under the contrastive design.
  - Corpus: Adjacent work probes LLM representations, but specific linear separability claims remain tied to this study.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A contrastive prompt design isolates genuine bias encoding from instruction‑detection artifacts.
- **Mechanism:** Both conditions explicitly mention the heuristic mechanism; only the policy toward using it differs. This equalizes instruction content while preserving the bias manipulation.
- **Core assumption:** Non‑contrastive probing’s near‑perfect early‑layer accuracy stemmed from classifiers detecting the debiasing prefix, not bias.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Section 5.2: Non‑contrastive probing yields >99% accuracy at layer 0; contrastive probing reduces off‑diagonal cosine similarity and concentrates signal in middle layers (Figure 4, Table 17).
  - Abstract: "Using contrastive linear probing…" confirms this design as foundational.
  - Corpus: Limited evidence; adjacent work often uses direct bias prompts without contrastive control.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Activation steering by subtracting a scaled bias direction reduces biased behavior with moderate capability cost.
- **Mechanism:** Compute mean difference vector `v_bias`; during generation, modify hidden states: `h' = h – α·v_bias`. Choose `α` and target layer via Pareto analysis.
- **Core assumption:** The mean difference points toward a region of activation space associated with biased reasoning; moving opposite to it encourages unbiased outputs.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "achieving 26–32% reduction in bias score while preserving downstream capability."
  - Section 6.2: Figure 2 shows dose‑response curves; Table 5 lists optimal `α` and layer; two‑cluster structure (early‑vs‑mid‑layer) emerges.
  - Section 6.2, paragraph 3: Qwen shows severe degradation for D1/D2 (`α`=1.8–2.2) but near‑baseline for D3/D4 (`α`=0.1).

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  LLM CogBias benchmark datasets -> Probing pipeline (activation extraction, contrastive labeling, logistic regression) -> Steering engine (mean-difference vector extraction, intervention `h' = h – α·v`) -> Evaluation suite (family-specific bias scores + 25 downstream benchmarks)

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Prepare contrastive prompts from each dataset (matched bias-salient and debias-neutralizing versions).  
  2. Run forward passes to collect residual‑stream activations at the final token for all layers.  
  3. Train per‑layer logistic regression probes on train splits (80%); identify optimal layer (peak accuracy).  
  4. Compute `v_bias` at optimal layer using mean difference over train data; normalize to unit norm.  
  5. Generate responses with modified hidden states over an `α`–layer grid; compute bias score.  
  6. Evaluate capability on downstream benchmarks; perform Pareto optimization (≥50% retention).  
  7. Validate with robustness controls (random/orthogonal directions, cross‑family transfer).

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Contrastive vs. non‑contrastive:** Contrastive eliminates instruction-detection confounds but requires carefully matched prompts.  
  - **Steering strength `α`:** Larger `α` reduces bias more but risks capability loss; optimal `α` differs across models and bias families.  
  - **Layer selection:** Steering effectiveness does not always align with probe‑optimal layer; empirical sweep needed.  
  - **Direction source:** Mean difference (used for steering) vs. linear probe weights (used for evaluation); mean difference is closed‑form but may capture noise.  
  - **Token application:** Blanket application (all tokens) is simpler than position‑selective, but may cause unnecessary disruption.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Probe accuracy >99% at layer 0 → instruction‑detection confound (non‑contrastive design).  
  - Steering causes >20 pp drop on downstream benchmarks (e.g., Qwen D1/D2).  
  - Trajectory AUC ~0.5 → separation failure, possibly due to wrong layer/`α`.  
  - Cross‑model transfer accuracy ~50% → directions are model‑specific; cannot reuse vectors.  
  - Random direction controls produce identical bias reduction → effect may be general perturbation, not specific bias removal.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Behavioral profiling (RQ1):** Evaluate three LLMs on vanilla prompts; compute family‑specific bias metrics. Confirms systematic biases.  
  2. **Contrastive probing (RQ2):** Train/evaluate linear probes on contrastive data; report layer‑wise accuracy and cross‑model transfer. Confirms linear separability.  
  3. **Activation steering (RQ3):** Apply mean‑difference steering; measure bias reduction and downstream capability; perform Pareto analysis to select optimal `α`/layer.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Question 1
What is the mechanistic explanation for the observed two-cluster steering effectiveness, where judgment/information processing biases require strong early-layer intervention while social/response biases yield to gentle mid-layer steering?  
**Basis:** The paper observes the pattern and links it to dual-process theory but does not provide a causal, representational-level explanation.  
**Why unresolved:** It remains unknown whether this reflects fundamental differences in how bias mechanisms are implemented in the network’s architecture and training dynamics.  
**Resolving evidence:** Controlled probing tracking bias signal evolution across layers, combined with ablation studies targeting early vs. mid-layer functional roles (e.g., layer-wise lesioning). Correlating with chain-of-thought analysis could reveal computational nature.

### Question 2
Despite near-orthogonal bias representations across models (mean cosine similarity 0.01), why does steering show correlated effectiveness (r=0.621)? Does this indicate a shared, higher-level functional organization for debiasing that transcends geometric similarity?  
**Basis:** The paper notes the surprising correlation but does not investigate the shared functional organization.  
**Why unresolved:** It is unknown whether correlation arises from common training data distributions, universal learning algorithms, or convergent feature learning that manifests in geometrically different but functionally similar directions.  
**Resolving evidence:** Analyze training data for common statistical patterns; test if steering vectors project onto a shared low-dimensional subspace; evaluate steering on a wider range of architectures to see if correlation holds and what moderates it.

### Question 3
To what extent are the identified bias directions causally responsible for biased behavior, rather than merely correlated with it? Could the activation steering effects be due to general signal disruption rather than targeted removal of a bias-specific computation?  
**Basis:** Robustness tests show random/orthogonal directions achieve similar bias reduction (though slightly less), suggesting a broad perturbation basin.  
**Why unresolved:** The design is correlational; subtracting a vector might debias by interfering with precise computation, noisy signal, or unrelated activations.  
**Resolving evidence:** Causal intervention methods (e.g., ROME, MEMIT) to directly test if modifying the specific activation pattern alters downstream bias without affecting unrelated computations. Compare against a "null" intervention adding an equal-magnitude but structured vector.

### Question 4
How do the bias representations and their responsiveness to steering generalize to reasoning-optimized models (e.g., o1, DeepSeek-R1) that employ chain-of-thought or reinforcement learning for reasoning?  
**Basis:** The conclusion lists extending to reasoning models as a future direction; current models are standard instruct models.  
**Why unresolved:** Reasoning models may develop different internal strategies (e.g., explicit verification steps) that could alter how biases are encoded or how interventions affect them.  
**Resolving evidence:** Repeat the probing and steering pipeline on reasoning-optimized models. Compare layer-wise bias separability and optimal steering parameters to the current study. Measure the capability-bias tradeoff curve to see if it improves due to robust reasoning pathways.

### Question 5
What is the relationship between the model-specific failure of cross-model transfer of bias directions and the architectural or training differences that cause it? Can we identify invariant "bias features" preserved across models despite geometric dissimilarity?  
**Basis:** Cross-model transfer fails (49.7%), indicating model-specific representations, yet steering effectiveness correlates functionally.  
**Why unresolved:** Unclear whether differences stem from tokenization, layer normalization, positional embeddings, or higher-level representational schemes.  
**Resolving evidence:** Align activation spaces across models using SVCCA or PROCRUSTES to see if a linear transformation aligns bias directions. Ablate/modify architectural components (e.g., RoPE, layer norms) in controlled training runs to isolate divergence causes. Probe for invariant vs. model-specific correlates.

## Limitations
- **Prompt and split irreproducibility:** Contrastive prompt templates and exact dataset splits (80/20) are unreleased; wording variations could alter bias elicitation and probe results.
- **Underspecified hyperparameters:** Probe regularization strength `C`, solver details, and steering direction normalization are not fully specified, risking reproducibility deviations.
- **Incomplete evaluation details:** The 25 downstream benchmarks and scoring harness are not provided, hindering exact verification of capability preservation claims.

## Confidence
- **Linear separability of bias signals:** Medium (high probe accuracies reported, but residual confounds possible if early layers still encode task cues).
- **Steering reduces bias while preserving capability:** Medium (dose-response curves are convincing, but exact `α` and layer choices are sensitive to implementation).
- **Two‑cluster steering pattern:** Low‑Medium (observed empirically but lacks statistical clustering support; may be artifact of specific models/families).
- **Near‑orthogonal directions across models but correlated steering effectiveness:** Medium (cross‑model transfer at chance and significant correlation provide evidence, but interpretation remains uncertain).

## Next Checks
1. **Probe robustness:** Sweep regularization `C` ∈ {0.1, 1.0, 10.0} and solvers (`saga`, `lbfgs`) across 5 random seeds; ensure early‑layer accuracy ≤55% and mean test accuracy >90%.
2. **Direction specificity:** For each model‑family pair, generate 10 random unit vectors and 10 vectors orthogonal to the learned direction. At optimal `α` and layer, compute bias reduction for each control. Perform one‑sided paired t‑test (α=0.05) comparing learned direction to each control set; expect p<0.01.
3. **Cluster generalization:** Add two additional bias families (e.g., anchoring, framing) and test on a fourth model (e.g., GPT‑4). Compute steering effectiveness vectors (reduction % across layers) and apply hierarchical clustering (k=2). Silhouette score should exceed 0.5 to confirm two robust clusters.
