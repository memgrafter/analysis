# Residuals-based Offline Reinforcement Learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.01378
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01378
- **Reference count**: 19
- **Primary result**: Residuals-based DQN achieves higher average testing rewards than a baseline without residuals on stochastic CartPole, with performance improving as the offline dataset size increases.

## Executive Summary
Offline reinforcement learning (RL) aims to learn optimal policies from fixed datasets without environment interaction. A major challenge is distribution shift, where the learning algorithm encounters state-action pairs not well‑represented in the data. This paper proposes a residuals‑based framework that mitigates this issue by generating synthetic trajectories. Specifically, a transition model is learned from the offline data, and empirical residuals (differences between observed next states and model predictions) are sampled and added to the model’s point predictions to create new next states. This process expands the effective coverage of the state‑action space. The authors define a residuals‑based Bellman operator and prove it is a contraction mapping, leading to asymptotic optimality and finite‑sample guarantees under standard assumptions. An algorithm, residuals‑based DQN, is implemented and evaluated on a stochastic CartPole environment. Results show that the residuals‑based approach outperforms a baseline that uses only the transition model’s point predictions, and that performance improves with larger offline datasets.

## Method Summary
The method comprises three steps: (1) train a transition model (e.g., a neural network) on the offline dataset using supervised learning to predict next states; (2) compute empirical residuals for each transition as the difference between the observed next state and the model’s prediction; (3) during Q‑learning, generate synthetic transitions by sampling a residual uniformly from the bank, adding it to the model’s prediction, and projecting onto the state space. These synthetic transitions are stored in a replay buffer and used to train a Q‑network with DQN updates. By augmenting the data with residuals, the algorithm effectively explores beyond the original dataset, reducing distribution shift.

## Key Results
- The residuals‑based Bellman operator is a γ‑contraction, ensuring convergence of value iteration.
- Theoretical analysis provides asymptotic optimality and finite‑sample exponential error bounds under Lipschitz and uniform convergence assumptions.
- Empirical evaluation on stochastic CartPole demonstrates that the residuals‑based DQN yields higher average testing reward than a baseline without residuals, and its performance increases with the size of the offline dataset.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The framework mitigates distribution shift and limited coverage by generating synthetic trajectories that expand the effective state‑action space beyond the offline dataset.
- **Mechanism:** Empirical residuals (differences between observed next states and model predictions) are sampled and added to the transition model’s point predictions to create new next states. This simulates plausible alternative outcomes, effectively augmenting the data distribution.
- **Core assumption:** The empirical residuals accurately represent the true transition error distribution, and the state space projection Π_S is non‑expansive (e.g., clipping in bounded spaces).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “generate trajectories by sampling residuals and adding them to the point prediction” and addresses “restrictive data coverage”.
  - [section III‑A]: Definition of empirical transition kernel ˆP_N using residuals and projection.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors highlight distribution shift as a key challenge in offline RL (e.g., MICRO abstract: “distribution shift”).
- **Break condition:** If the offline dataset lacks diversity, the residual set may not capture the true error distribution, leading to simulated transitions that are unrealistic or Out‑of‑Distribution (OOD) in ways the policy cannot handle.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The residuals‑based Bellman operator is a contraction mapping, ensuring that value iteration converges to a unique fixed point.
- **Mechanism:** The operator uses an empirical transition kernel that is a uniform mixture over deterministic next states (predicted state plus a residual). The supremum‑norm contraction follows from the standard Bellman operator’s contraction property, as the empirical kernel is a finite average of deterministic kernels, and the min operator over actions is non‑expansive.
- **Core assumption:** The discount factor γ < 1 and the immediate cost function c(s,a) is bounded. The projection Π_S is 1‑Lipschitz.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section III‑A, Theorem 1]: Proof that ˆT_N is a γ‑contraction.
  - The proof relies on the fact that for each residual scenario, the update is a deterministic Bellman update, and averaging preserves the contraction factor γ.
- **Break condition:** If the state space is non‑convex and the projection Π_S is not uniquely defined or not non‑expansive, the contraction property may fail. Also, if γ is close to 1, the contraction constant approaches 1, slowing convergence.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Under Lipschitz and uniform convergence conditions, the fixed point of the residuals‑based operator converges asymptotically to the true optimal Q‑function and enjoys finite‑sample exponential guarantees.
- **Mechanism:** The error bound ∥ ˆQ_N − Q⋆ ∥_∞ is decomposed into (i) approximation error of the empirical operator relative to a full‑information operator (using true residuals) and (ii) the error of the full‑information operator relative to the true Bellman operator. Lemma 1 links the first error to the average residual discrepancy, and Theorem 2/3 combine this with regression consistency and LLN assumptions.
- **Core assumption:** Assumptions 1–7 (Lipschitz cost/transition, uniform consistency of regression estimator, uniform LLN for V⋆ under true residuals, exponential bounds for regression errors, and exponential bound for full‑information fixed point deviation). State and action spaces must be compact for uniform convergence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section III‑B, Theorem 2]: Asymptotic optimality (∥ ˆQ_N − Q⋆ ∥_∞ → 0 in probability).
  - [section III‑B, Theorem 3]: Finite‑sample bound P(∥ ˆQ_N − Q⋆ ∥_∞ > η) ≤ Γ(η) exp(−γ(N,η)).
- **Break condition:** If any assumption fails (e.g., non‑Lipschitz transition, non‑compact state/action spaces, regression estimator not uniformly consistent), the theoretical guarantees do not hold. In practice, this may manifest as no convergence or large optimality gaps even with large N.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Transition Model (ˆf_N) → Residual Bank → Residuals‑based Simulator → Replay Buffer → Q‑network → Target Network → Action Selection

### Critical Path
1. Train ˆf_N on offline dataset D_N using supervised learning (mean squared error).
2. Compute residuals for each transition in D_N and store in Residual Bank.
3. Initialize replay buffer D (empty) and Q‑network.
4. For each episode: start from a state (e.g., from D_N or random), then for each step:
   - Select action a_t via ϵ‑greedy from Q.
   - Sample residual from bank, compute s_{t+1} = Π_S( ˆf_N(s_t,a_t) + residual).
   - Store (s_t,a_t,c(s_t,a_t),s_{t+1}) in D.
   - Sample minibatch from D, compute targets y = c + γ min_{a'} ˆQ(s',a'; ˆθ).
   - Perform gradient step on θ to minimize (y − Q(s,a;θ))².
   - Periodically update target network ˆQ ← Q.
5. After training, deploy policy π(s) = argmin_a Q(s,a;θ) (or ϵ‑greedy with ϵ=0).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Regression model capacity**: A high‑capacity model (e.g., deep net) may overfit to noise in D_N, leading to residuals that are mostly noise and poor generalization. A low‑capacity model may underfit, causing biased residuals. Use validation on held‑out D_N to choose model size.
- **Residual sampling strategy**: Uniform sampling assumes residuals are i.i.d. and stationary. If the true error distribution is heteroscedastic (depends on s,a), uniform sampling may misrepresent. Consider stratified sampling by state‑action region if dataset is large.
- **Projection Π_S**: Must be explicitly defined for the environment. For continuous bounded states, clipping is common. For non‑Euclidean spaces (e.g., angles), a proper metric projection is needed. The projection affects the Lipschitz property assumed in theory.
- **Replay buffer composition**: Buffer initially empty, filled solely from simulator. If simulator generates very OOD states early, Q‑network may diverge. Consider seeding buffer with real transitions from D_N to anchor early training.

### Failure Signatures
- **Training reward improves but testing reward degrades**: The simulator may be generating transitions that are not aligned with true dynamics, causing the policy to overfit to a biased model. Check regression error on held‑out data and visualize sampled next states.
- **Q‑values explode or become NaN**: Likely due to large TD errors from simulator generating extreme states/rewards. Caused by poor regression model or residuals from out‑of‑support regions. Reduce learning rate, clip TD errors, or limit residual magnitude (e.g., only use residuals from in‑distribution data).
- **Policy collapses to a single action**: May indicate the simulator is not providing sufficient exploration or the Q‑network is too shallow. Verify that residuals provide variability in next states; if not, the residual bank may be homogeneous (small dataset or overfitted regression).
- **No performance gain with more data**: If offline dataset is very small (< 500 transitions), the residual bank may not capture the error distribution well. The theoretical guarantees require N large enough for uniform consistency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Distributional robustness extension
- **Question**: How can the residuals‑based offline RL framework be extended to incorporate distributional robustness to handle ambiguity in the learned transition dynamics?
- **Basis**: Explicitly stated in the conclusion as a future direction.
- **Why unresolved**: Current method relies on empirical residuals from a single learned model, which may not capture full uncertainty when the dataset is limited or biased.
- **What would resolve**: Develop a distributionally robust Bellman operator that replaces the empirical kernel with an ambiguity set; prove asymptotic optimality and finite‑sample guarantees; empirically validate on environments with limited coverage or model misspecification.

### Continuous action spaces
- **Question**: Can the residuals‑based framework be effectively extended to environments with continuous action spaces, and what algorithmic modifications are required?
- **Basis**: The proposed algorithm is a residuals‑based DQN, which only supports discrete actions; the paper claims general applicability but provides no solution.
- **Why unresolved**: Many real‑world problems involve continuous control; without an extension (e.g., actor‑critic), the framework’s generality remains unsubstantiated.
- **What would resolve**: Design an offline RL algorithm combining residual‑based synthetic transitions with a continuous‑action policy parameterization (e.g., deterministic policy gradient) and evaluate on standard benchmarks like MuJoCo.

### Nonlinear function approximation
- **Question**: What are the convergence and sample‑complexity guarantees for the residuals‑based deep Q‑learning algorithm when using nonlinear function approximation (e.g., neural networks)?
- **Basis**: Theoretical analysis assumes a tabular setting; Algorithm 1 uses neural networks, introducing approximation and optimization errors not covered.
- **Why unresolved**: The gap between theory and practice raises doubts about convergence and how factors like network capacity and model error interact.
- **What would resolve**: Extend theory to include function approximation error (e.g., using recent off‑policy Q‑learning analyses) and derive explicit bounds in terms of network complexity and transition‑model quality; correlate with empirical performance.

### Benchmarking on standard suites
- **Question**: How does the performance of residuals‑based offline RL compare with state‑of‑the‑art offline RL algorithms on standard benchmark suites such as D4RL?
- **Basis**: Evaluation limited to one stochastic CartPole environment and only a simple baseline; no comparison with established methods like CQL, IQL, BCQ.
- **Why unresolved**: Without benchmarking, it’s unclear whether the approach offers practical advantages in returns, robustness, or data efficiency; positive CartPole results may not generalize.
- **What would resolve**: Comprehensive experiments on D4RL across diverse tasks (e.g., halfcheetah, walker2d, kitchen) with varying dataset qualities, reporting normalized scores, stability, sensitivity; identify scenarios where residuals help most.

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (compact state/action spaces, Lipschitz transitions, non‑expansive projection) may not hold in many practical problems, and the projection Π_S is not concretely defined.
- Hyperparameters (transition model architecture, DQN learning rates, batch size, target update frequency, replay buffer size) are unspecified, hindering reproducibility.
- Experimental validation is limited to a single stochastic CartPole environment, lacking comparisons to standard offline RL benchmarks like D4RL.
