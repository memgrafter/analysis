# Projective Psychological Assessment of Large Multimodal Models Using Thematic Apperception Tests

## Executive Summary
This study applies the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) and SCORS-G scoring to evaluate personality-like functioning in Large Multimodal Models (LMMs). LMMs serve dual roles: as subject models generating narratives in response to TAT images, and as evaluator models scoring those narratives. The pipeline identifies a subset of evaluator models (e.g., Claude 3.7/3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4.1, GPT-5) that achieve high agreement with human experts (MAE 0.59–0.66, Spearman ρ=0.60–0.68), providing a scalable scoring mechanism. Subject models consistently exhibit high scores on cognitive dimensions (Understanding Social Causality, Complexity of Representations, Identity/Coherence of Self) and lower scores on affective-relational dimensions, particularly Experience and Management of Aggressive Impulses. Performance improves with model size and recency within families, suggesting that more advanced multimodal reasoning yields richer narrative output aligned with human-scored criteria.

## Method Summary
The study used seven standardized TAT images and three semantically equivalent prompt variants. Each subject model (SM) generated 63 stories (7 images × 3 prompts × 3 repetitions). Evaluator models (EMs) scored each story on eight SCORS-G dimensions, with three repetitions. Candidate EM subsets were exhaustively searched to maximize inter-rater reliability (Krippendorff's α) under constraints (family balance, minimum models). The selected EM ensemble was validated against a human-scored benchmark of 90 stories using mean absolute error (MAE) and Spearman correlation. Validated EMs achieved MAE 0.59–0.66 and ρ=0.60–0.68. SM scores were aggregated across stimuli and repetitions, and dimension means were compared across models and families, with statistical tests for image and instruction effects.

## Key Results
- Selected evaluator models (Claude 3.7/3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4.1, GPT-5) achieve high agreement with human experts (MAE 0.59–0.66, Spearman ρ=0.60–0.68).
- Subject models universally score highest on cognitive dimensions (Understanding Social Causality, Complexity of Representations, Identity/Coherence of Self; ~6.2–6.4/7) and lowest on affective-relational dimensions, especially Experience and Management of Aggressive Impulses (~4.9/7).
- Performance systematically improves with model size and recency within families (e.g., GPT-5 > GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 > 3.5; LLaMA family scores lower).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The study's validity hinges on three key mechanisms:

1. **Evaluator model reliability as a proxy for human scoring:** By exhaustively searching for EM subsets that maximize inter-rater reliability (Krippendorff's α) on a human benchmark, the pipeline identifies models whose scores are consistent with trained raters. High agreement metrics (MAE 0.59–0.66, Spearman ρ=0.60–0.68) indicate that these EMs can serve as scalable raters for TAT narratives, provided their responses generalize beyond the benchmark.

2. **Stable, differentiable personality-like profiles in LMMs:** SMs produce a robust pattern across images and prompts: cognitive dimensions score notably higher (~6.2–6.4) than affective-relational ones (~4.9). This is interpreted as reflecting the models' strength in representing social causality and self-coherence, coupled with avoidance of conflictual or emotionally charged content—possibly due to safety alignment. The consistency of this gap across diverse models suggests a trait-like signature, though the psychological validity of such signatures in non-conscious systems remains contested.

3. **Scaling with model capability:** Within each family, larger and more recent models achieve higher SCORS-G scores. For example, GPT-5 outperforms GPT-4.1 and GPT-5-mini; Claude 3.7 outperforms Claude 3.5. This aligns with known scaling trends in multimodal reasoning and alignment, implying that as models become more sophisticated, their narratives better approximate the complexity expected by human raters.

Potential break conditions include: EM agreement not generalizing to a holdout benchmark; SM patterns being dominated by instruction or image order effects (rather than stable traits); scaling trends reversing without architectural changes.

## Foundational Learning
Several concepts underpin the methodology:

- **Projective Psychological Testing (TAT):** The TAT uses ambiguous images to elicit narratives that project internal psychological structures. This open-ended approach avoids direct questioning and is theorized to reveal unconscious processes, unlike structured inventories such as the Big Five.
  *Why needed:* The entire assessment framework relies on TAT's premise that narrative content reflects underlying psychological functioning; understanding this projection assumption is crucial for interpreting results.
  *Quick check:* What is the core theoretical assumption of the TAT, and how does it differ from a structured inventory like the Big Five?

- **SCORS-G Dimensional Scoring:** SCORS-G provides eight dimensions (e.g., COM, AGG, ICS) that codify cognitive-representational and affective-relational aspects of narratives. It was developed for human clinical assessment.
  *Why needed:* These dimensions operationalize "personality-like functioning" and allow comparison across models; distinguishing cognitive vs. affective domains is essential to interpret the reported patterns.
  *Quick check:* Which SCORS-G dimensions are most affected by social desirability bias, and why might that be the case?

- **Large Multimodal Model (LMM) Architecture:** LMMs combine visual encoders and language decoders to process images and generate text. This architecture determines capacity for visual understanding and narrative coherence.
  *Why needed:* SMs must interpret TAT images; EMs must process image+story pairs. Architectural differences influence generative and scoring capabilities.
  *Quick check:* How does a typical LMM (e.g., GPT-4V) combine visual and textual information to generate a story for a TAT image?

## Architecture Onboarding
The pipeline consists of nine main components flowing as follows:

TAT stimulus set & instruction variants → Generative engine (SM pool) → generated stories → Scoring engine (EM pool) → raw EM ratings → EM selection module → validated EM ensemble → Analysis module.

The human benchmark feeds into the Validation module, which compares EM ensemble scores to human scores. The critical path is the **EM selection and validation**; if the selected EMs fail to achieve high agreement with humans, the SM scores lack external validity. 

Key design tradeoffs include:
- **Automation vs. nuance:** Using EMs enables large-scale scoring but sacrifices the contextual sensitivity of clinical experts.
- **Breadth vs. depth:** Seven TAT images allow testing many models but may miss dimension-specific sensitivities captured by the full 31-card set.
- **Ensemble diversity vs. performance:** The family-balance constraint in EM selection avoids bias but may exclude top-performing single-family subsets.

Failure signatures to monitor:
- EM validation: MAE > 0.75 or Spearman ρ < 0.55 on the benchmark; low ICC across EM repetitions.
- SM pattern: Absence of a consistent cognitive>affective gap; high score variability (wide CIs) within a model.
- Scaling trend: Reversal within a family (e.g., newer model scores lower than predecessor) without a known architectural change.

**First 3 experiments to run:**
1. **EM Benchmark Replication:** Execute the full EM selection and validation on the 92-story SCORS-G benchmark to reproduce the top EM subset (claude-3.7/3.5, gpt-4.1, gpt-5) and their MAE/Spearman values.
2. **SM Baseline Consistency:** For a top SM (e.g., gpt-4.1), generate all 63 stories, score with the validated EM ensemble, and compute: (a) dimension means, (b) 95% CIs across repetitions, (c) instruction effect p-values. Confirm the cognitive>affective pattern and narrow CIs.
3. **Aggression Probe:** For image 3BM (high aggression ambiguity) vs. image 12M (neutral), perform qualitative analysis of AGG-related content. Correlate manual coding of explicit aggression with EM AGG scores to test whether EMs capture aggression indirectly.

## Limitations
- The validity of applying SCORS-G (a human clinical tool) to LMM narratives is unproven; it assumes "personality-like" functioning in models and relies on the contested projection premise of TAT for non-conscious systems.
- EM selection optimizes inter-rater reliability on a small benchmark (n=90), but high agreement among EMs does not guarantee alignment with clinical judgment or generalizability to novel SM outputs; LLM psychometric scoring is known to be context-sensitive.
- Observed SM patterns may reflect training artifacts (e.g., safety tuning) or superficial keyword compliance rather than stable trait-like differences; qualitative analysis in the paper is illustrative, not systematic.

## Confidence
- **Evaluator model reliability:** Supported by strong internal metrics on the benchmark, but external validity to clinical practice and novel SM outputs is unproven (High-Medium).
- **SM dimensional pattern:** Statistically consistent cognitive>affective gap across models, but psychological interpretation is inferential and may reflect artifacts (Low-Medium).
- **Scaling trends:** Clear within-family progressions reported, but cross-family comparisons confounded by architectural differences; trend may not hold for future architectures (Medium).

## Next Checks
1. **Benchmark Replication & Holdout Test:** Re-run the EM selection exactly, then apply the selected EM ensemble to a different human-scored TAT holdout set (e.g., 30 stories). Report MAE/Spearman to test generalizability.
2. **Instruction/Image Dissection:** For a top SM, run a 2-way ANOVA on all SCORS-G scores (7 images × 3 instructions × 3 reps). Report effect sizes (η²) for image vs. instruction. Large image effects indicate trait-like stability; large instruction effects indicate prompt sensitivity.
3. **Aggression Content Audit:** Manually code narratives from image 3BM for explicit aggressive acts/intent (binary). Correlate coding with EM AGG scores. Low correlation suggests EMs rely on indirect markers, questioning ecological validity.
