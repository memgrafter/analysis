# Time Series Reasoning via Process-Verifiable Thinking Data Synthesis and Scheduling for Tailored LLM Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07830
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07830
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** VeriTime enables small (3B‑4B) LLMs to match or surpass larger proprietary models on time series reasoning, with improvements up to 102.15% and a 71% token reduction.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) struggle with complex multi‑step reasoning on time series data due to insufficient process‑level supervision, inefficient data usage, and reinforcement learning approaches that disregard intermediate reasoning steps. This paper presents VeriTime, a comprehensive framework that addresses these gaps through three integrated components. First, TSRgen automatically constructs TSRBench, a multimodal time series‑text dataset annotated with process‑verifiable chain‑of‑thought (CoT) following a six‑step TS‑tailored reasoning process. Second, a difficulty‑aware data scheduling mechanism allocates samples between supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) based on task taxonomy and model performance on a warm‑up model. Third, a two‑stage RL fine‑tuning strategy employs Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with multi‑objective rewards that jointly assess final answer accuracy and the validity of critical intermediate reasoning steps.  

Experiments across scenario‑based and knowledge‑based tasks demonstrate the effectiveness of VeriTime. For Qwen2.5‑3B and Qwen3‑4B models, it yields average improvements of 102.15% and 14.67% on scenario tasks, and 23.58% to 49.79% on knowledge tasks. Compact 3B‑4B models achieve performance parity or superiority against larger proprietary LLMs. Additionally, the TS‑tailored CoT reduces inference token count by 71%, yielding significant efficiency gains. Ablations confirm the critical role of process‑level rewards and adaptive data scheduling.

## Method Summary
VeriTime's training pipeline begins with TSRgen, which synthesizes the TSRBench dataset. Using a powerful teacher LLM (DeepSeek‑R1), TSRgen generates chain‑of‑thought (CoT) reasoning for each time series sample according to a predefined six‑step process tailored to temporal dynamics: Task Intent, Key Patterns, Analysis, Preliminary Answer, Reflection, and Summary. From these CoTs, rule‑based extractors produce process‑verifiable labels (e.g., identified task type, critical patterns) that can be used for fine‑grained supervision. The resulting TSRBench covers seven tasks, split into scenario‑based (easier, synthetic) and knowledge‑based (real‑world, complex) examples.  

Training proceeds in two stages after a brief warm‑up SFT on 10% of the data. A warmed‑up model evaluates all scenario samples; those solved correctly are deemed "normal" and assigned to Stage 1 SFT, while incorrect scenario samples and all knowledge samples are considered "hard" and allocated to Stage 2 RL. Stage 1 fine‑tunes the model on the normal subset to establish a solid reasoning prior. Stage 2 applies GRPO to the hard subset with a composite reward: structural (format, length), hard answer correctness (+5 for correct, –2 for incorrect), and four process rewards corresponding to the intermediate steps (intent alignment, pattern relevance, answer alignment, and verification step). The model is trained with LoRA (rank 32) for efficiency; hyperparameters include SFT learning rate 2e‑4, RL learning rate 5e‑6, group size 4, and temperature 0.8.

## Key Results
- VeriTime boosts Qwen2.5‑3B and Qwen3‑4B by up to 102.15% on scenario‑based reasoning and 23.58%–49.79% on knowledge‑based tasks.
- Compact 3B‑4B models achieve parity or superiority over larger proprietary LLMs.
- The TS‑tailored CoT reduces inference token count by 71% on average.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Synthesizing time series‑text datasets with **process‑verifiable Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) annotations** (TSRBench) provides structured intermediate steps that are aligned with time series properties, enabling LLMs to learn interpretable multi‑step reasoning trajectories.
- **Mechanism:** The TSRgen pipeline creates reasoning data via a predefined six‑step TS‑tailored thinking process (Task Intent → Key Patterns → Analysis → Preliminary Answer → Reflection → Summary). Each step’s output is annotated with verifiable labels (e.g., task intent, critical patterns) that can be supervised during training, moving beyond outcome‑only supervision.
- **Core assumption:** Effective time series reasoning requires explicit, validated intermediate reasoning steps that align with temporal dynamics (e.g., trends, thresholds), rather than relying on latent pattern recognition in end‑to‑end training.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "TSRgen... constructs a multimodal dataset with process‑verifiable annotations... integrates valid reasoning trajectories."
  - [section 2.1] "TSRgen constructs a TS‑tailored reasoning process with six logically ordered steps... verifiable process‑level labels from the reasoning chains."
  - [corpus] Strong alignment with neighboring work on CoT fine‑tuning (e.g., "Eliciting Chain‑of‑Thought Reasoning for Time Series Analysis using Reinforcement Learning").
- **Break condition:** If intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., pattern selection) consistently misalign with ground‑truth verifiable labels, the process supervision fails to teach valid temporal reasoning, leading to performance degradation despite correct final answers.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A **difficulty‑aware data scheduling strategy** that allocates samples between supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) based on model performance and task taxonomy improves training efficiency and final reasoning capability.
- **Mechanism:** Samples are stratified into "normal" (easier, learnable via SFT) and "hard" (challenging, requiring RL exploration) using a warm‑up model. Scenario‑based tasks typically get more SFT allocation, while knowledge‑based tasks (real‑world, complex) get more RL exposure.
- **Core assumption:** Different time series reasoning tasks have varying difficulty profiles; RL is computationally expensive and most effective when targeted at samples where SFT plateaus, avoiding wasted computation on easily learnable patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "data scheduling mechanism that stratifies samples by difficulty and task type for targeted training."
  - [section 2.2] "We devise a data scheduling mechanism... dynamically identify learnable and representative samples... based on task difficulty and model performance."
  - [corpus] Related to data selection strategies (e.g., "Eliciting Chain‑of‑Thought...", "Innate Reasoning is Not Enough").
- **Break condition:** If the difficulty classifier (based on warm‑up model accuracy) mislabels sample difficulty (e.g., marking a conceptually hard sample as "normal"), appropriate learning signals are not applied, causing either SFT overfitting on hard samples or insufficient RL on truly hard ones.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A **two‑stage RL fine‑tuning with fine‑grained, multi‑objective rewards** that jointly evaluate final answer accuracy and the validity of key intermediate reasoning steps (process rewards) stabilizes and enhances time series reasoning performance.
- **Mechanism:** After warm‑up SFT, RL (using GRPO) is applied with rewards that include: (a) hard reward for final answer correctness (+5/–2), (b) process rewards for critical intermediate steps (e.g., correct task intent, key pattern identification, answer alignment), and (c) structural rewards for output format and length. This guides the model to maintain logical coherence while optimizing for accuracy.
- **Core assumption:** Optimizing solely for final answer accuracy ("outcome supervision") is insufficient for complex multi‑step tasks; explicit supervision of intermediate reasoning steps is necessary to develop robust, interpretable, and generalizable time series reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "two‑stage RL fine‑tuning strategy using GRPO with fine‑grained, multi‑objective rewards that jointly evaluate final answer accuracy and the validity of key intermediate reasoning steps."
  - [section 2.3] "To enhance LLM time series reasoning capabilities, it is essential to ensure correct final predictions while optimizing the intermediate reasoning trajectory... four process‑level rewards aligned with the TS‑tailored reasoning steps."
  - [section 3.4] Ablation results show removing process rewards (especially critical pattern reward) causes significant performance drops.
  - [corpus] Connects to RL for reasoning literature (e.g., "Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning in Large Language Models").
- **Break condition:** If reward components are poorly calibrated (e.g., process rewards too weak relative to hard reward), the model may ignore intermediate logical validity and resort to "reward hacking" or superficial pattern matching to maximize final answer reward.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Process Supervision vs. Outcome Supervision**
  - *Why needed here:* VeriTime's core innovation is using process‑verifiable labels for intermediate steps. Understanding this distinction is crucial to grasp why standard SFT/RL on final answers is insufficient for complex time series reasoning.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might a model produce a correct final answer via flawed reasoning, and why is process supervision necessary even in such cases?
- **Concept: Task Taxonomy in Time Series Reasoning**
  - *Why needed here:* The data scheduling mechanism relies on categorizing tasks into scenario‑based (synthetic, simpler) and knowledge‑based (real‑world, harder). This classification dictates the SFT/RL allocation strategy.
  - *Quick check question:* How does the nature of "knowledge‑based" tasks (e.g., medical ECG analysis) inherently make them more suitable for RL‑focused training compared to scenario‑based tasks?
- **Concept: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**
  - *Why needed here:* VeriTime uses GRPO for RL fine‑tuning. Understanding its advantage‑normalization across sampled groups (no separate value network) is key to why it provides stable optimization for this multi‑reward setting.
  - *Quick check question:* In GRPO, why is normalizing advantages within a group of rollouts for the same input helpful, especially when using multiple sparse reward signals (format, process, hard)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** TSRgen Pipeline: Data Source → Task Filtering → TS‑tailored CoT Generation → Cross‑LLM Validation → Process Label Extraction → TSRBench. VeriTime Trainer: Input (TSRBench) → Warm‑up SFT (10% random) → Difficulty Stratification (via warmed‑up model) → Stage 1: SFT on "normal" samples → Stage 2: RL (GRPO) on "hard" samples with Multi‑Objective Rewards.
- **Critical path:** The end‑to‑end flow is not linear but has a critical dependency: **Quality of TSRBench → Effectiveness of Difficulty Stratification → Success of Targeted RL**. Poor data synthesis undermines all subsequent steps.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *TS‑tailored CoT complexity vs. token efficiency:* The 6‑step CoT improves reasoning interpretability but increases token count. VeriTime mitigates this by penalizing excess length in rewards, achieving 71% average token reduction vs. base model.
  - *SFT vs. RL allocation:* Over‑investing in RL on medium‑difficulty scenario tasks causes overfitting/instability; under‑investing RL on knowledge‑based tasks limits performance ceiling. The model‑guided allocation balances this.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low cross‑model validation scores (<4.0) on DeepSeek‑R1's reasoning trajectories indicate flawed TSRBench data.
  - Model‑guided stratification assigns >70% of scenario tasks to RL, indicating the warm‑up SFT failed to learn basic patterns.
  - Ablation dropping *critical pattern reward* causes >18% average drop, showing failure to learn essential temporal feature identification.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Validate TS‑tailored CoT quality:** Run cross‑model evaluation (using Qwen3‑235B/GPT‑5/Gemini‑2.5) on 100 random TSRBench samples per task type, scoring the five dimensions (Intent Alignment, Pattern Relevance, etc.). Must achieve >4.5 average on first two dimensions.
  2. **Test data scheduling:** Compare model‑guided allocation against random selection and full RL on scenario‑based tasks. Expect model‑guided to outperform full RL (avoids overfitting) and random (better RL data selection).
  3. **Ablate reward components:** Sequentially remove structural, hard, and process rewards (especially rpattern) in RL training. Expect largest drops when removing hard or rpattern rewards, confirming their necessity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can TSRBench be expanded to cover a broader range of real‑world domains and more complex, long‑horizon time series scenarios to improve generalizability?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state in the Limitations (Appendix E): "Given the scarcity of high‑quality reasoning datasets in the time series domain, future work could focus on expanding TSRBench to cover a broader range of domains and more complex scenarios."
- **Why unresolved:** The current TSRBench focuses on seven tasks across synthetic and limited real‑world sources. Its coverage of domain diversity (e.g., finance, climate, astronomy) and scenario complexity (e.g., multi‑variate causal chains, very long sequences) is constrained, leaving unknown how well models trained on it perform on unseen, more complex real‑world time series reasoning problems.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A large‑scale expansion of TSRBench with contributions from multiple domain experts, including more challenging tasks (e.g., multi‑step causal interventions, irregularly sampled data), followed by rigorous evaluation of VeriTime's transfer performance on these new tasks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the optimal weighting and interaction scheme for the multi‑objective reward components (structural, hard, process) across different types of time series reasoning tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** Ablation studies (Figure 4) show that removing different process reward components (e.g., critical pattern, alignment) causes varying levels of performance drop across tasks (e.g., inferential calculation is more sensitive to alignment reward). The reward design uses a uniform average (Eq. 15) without task‑specific tuning.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates that different process rewards are more crucial for certain tasks (e.g., pattern reward for anomaly detection), but the fixed, uniform aggregation may not be optimal. The trade‑off between encouraging detailed, valid reasoning vs. over‑optimizing for specific intermediate steps is not theoretically or empirically resolved.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic study varying the relative coefficients (λ_fmt, λ_len, λ_hard, λ_process) on a validation set of diverse TSRBench tasks, potentially using automated reward tuning (e.g., Bayesian optimization) to find task‑specific or universally optimal weighting that maximizes final accuracy and reasoning quality.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is the proposed difficulty‑aware data scheduling strategy (model‑guided selection for scenario‑based, full RL for knowledge‑based) generally applicable to other time series reasoning tasks and model architectures, or is it overly tailored to the specific characteristics of TSRBench and Qwen models?
- **Basis in paper:** The strategy is justified by empirical observations: scenario‑based tasks benefit more from SFT on "normal" samples, while knowledge‑based tasks need more RL exposure (Section 3.5). The authors attribute this to task difficulty and pattern complexity.
- **Why unresolved:** The strategy's success is demonstrated on two model families (Qwen2.5/3B, Qwen3/4B) and a specific dataset split. It is unclear if this dichotomy (SFT‑heavy vs. RL‑heavy) generalizes to other model scales (e.g., >10B), different architectures (e.g., decoder‑only vs. encoder‑decoder), or other time series benchmarks with different task distributions. The principled "difficulty hierarchy" is based on task taxonomy and a single warm‑up model assessment.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the scheduling study on a wider range of LLMs (e.g., Llama, Mistral, GPT‑series), different time series datasets (e.g., MTBench, TSAIA), and possibly defining difficulty via alternative metrics (e.g., sample entropy, concept drift). A meta‑analysis of which task characteristics (synthetic vs. real, sequence length, domain specificity) predict the optimal SFT/RL allocation ratio.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How reliable and unbiased is the cross‑model validation process used to verify the quality of DeepSeek‑R1's reasoning trajectories in TSRBench?
- **Basis in paper:** Cross‑model validation (Appendix C.2) uses Qwen3‑235B, GPT‑5, and Gemini‑2.5 as judges. The results show high scores but with notable variation: GPT‑5 gives lower "Logical Coherence" and "Robustness" scores on knowledge‑based tasks (~3.9) compared to Qwen3‑235B (~4.2). The rubric is defined, but inter‑annotator agreement among LLM judges is not quantified (e.g., Krippendorff's alpha).
- **Why unresolved:** The validation process assumes the judge models are capable, unbiased evaluators of time series reasoning. However, LLM judges can exhibit model‑specific biases, misunderstanding of time series concepts, or sensitivity to prompt phrasing. The reported scores may reflect the judges' own capabilities and biases rather than an objective ground truth of reasoning quality. This threatens the validity of the process‑verifiable labels used for RL training.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting a human evaluation study where domain experts (time series analysts) score a subset of the same reasoning trajectories. Comparing human scores to the LLM‑judge scores would quantify bias/agreement. Additionally, analyzing the specific cases where judges disagree (e.g., low logical coherence scores) to identify systematic failure modes in the teacher model's reasoning.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** To what extent do the efficiency gains (reduced token count) from the TS‑tailored CoT compromise the richness, interpretability, or error‑diagnosability of the reasoning process compared to more verbose, unconstrained reasoning?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper highlights a 71% average reduction in inference token count (Table 4) as a major benefit of the structured CoT. The TS‑tailored CoT explicitly constrains reasoning to six steps.
- **Why unresolved:** While efficiency is improved, the paper does not analyze whether the compressed reasoning output captures all relevant nuances of the time series data or whether it suppresses potentially valuable exploratory reasoning that could aid in debugging model errors or providing richer explanations to users. The trade‑off between conciseness and explanatory power is not measured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A user study or expert evaluation comparing explanations from VeriTime (structured CoT) vs. a baseline model with unconstrained reasoning (e.g., base LLM with standard CoT prompting) on criteria like: completeness of analysis, ease of identifying reasoning errors, and overall helpfulness for decision‑making. Additionally, analyzing failure cases where VeriTime's concise reasoning might miss critical patterns that a more verbose approach would catch.

## Limitations
- Replication is hindered by undisclosed details (e.g., fuzzy matching thresholds for process rewards, rule‑based label extraction logic, access to the DeepSeek‑R1 teacher model, and exact keyword lists/train‑test splits).
- Generalizability is limited to Qwen models and a specific task split; performance on heterogeneous time series data (multivariate, irregular sampling, diverse domains) remains unknown.
- The claimed 71% token reduction may not hold for all task distributions or larger models, and the unreported warm‑up SFT accuracy distribution (which drives scheduling) raises reliability concerns.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Process‑verifiable TSRBench):** Medium
- **Mechanism 2 (Difficulty‑aware scheduling):** Medium
- **Mechanism 3 (Two‑stage RL with multi‑objective rewards):** Medium‑Low

## Next Checks
1. **TSRBench quality stress test:** Recompute cross‑model validation scores on a fresh 200‑sample subset using GPT‑4o and Claude‑3.5 Sonnet. Require >90% of samples to score >4.0 on *Intent Alignment* and *Pattern Relevance*; if not, data synthesis pipeline robustness is in question.
2. **Data scheduling ablation:** Train with three partitioning strategies on scenario tasks: (a) model‑guided as proposed, (b) random 50/50 SFT/RL split, (c) all SFT. Compare final accuracy and RL stability (loss variance). A >5% drop for (b)/(c) confirms the necessity of the proposed scheduling; no drop suggests the claim is overstated.
3. **Reward calibration sensitivity:** Perform a grid search over process reward weights (0.1–1.0) while fixing hard reward at +5/–2. Monitor final accuracy and average response length. If accuracy peaks at weight = 0 (i.e., process rewards hurt performance), the multi‑objective design is fragile; if accuracy remains stable or improves across weights, the design is robust.
