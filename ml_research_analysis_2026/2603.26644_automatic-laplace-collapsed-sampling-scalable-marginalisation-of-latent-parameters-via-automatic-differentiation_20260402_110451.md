# Automatic Laplace Collapsed Sampling: Scalable Marginalisation of Latent Parameters via Automatic Differentiation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.26644
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26644
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Laplace marginalization via automatic differentiation reduces nested sampling cost from O(D³) to O(dθ³·dz), enabling accurate evidence computation for models with up to 25 600 latent dimensions in minutes rather than years.

## Executive Summary
ALCS (Automatic Laplace Collapsed Sampling) addresses the long‑standing challenge of computing Bayesian evidence in models with many latent variables. By combining Laplace approximation with automatic differentiation inside a nested sampling framework, ALCS analytically collapses out each latent conditional at every likelihood evaluation, effectively reducing the dimensionality of the outer sampler. Empirical results on supernova cosmology benchmarks show that ALCS achieves sub‑σ accuracy (|δlogZ| < 0.25 nats) for up to 25 600 latent dimensions, with wall‑time scaling from about one to twelve minutes—dramatically faster than the estimated 37 years required by conventional nested sampling. The method is theoretically exact for Gaussian conditionals and can be extended to Student‑t approximations for heavy‑tailed posteriors. An importance‑sampling effective sample size (ESS) diagnostic flags potential failures of the Laplace assumption. ALCS thus opens the door to scalable Bayesian model comparison in high‑dimensional hierarchical models.

## Method Summary
ALCS proceeds by embedding a Laplace marginalization step within nested sampling. For each proposed point θ in the parameter space, the algorithm first maximizes the conditional posterior p(z | θ, D) to obtain a MAP estimate ẑ. Using automatic differentiation, it then computes the Hessian H of the negative log joint (or conditional) at (θ, ẑ). Under the Laplace approximation, the integral over z is approximated as a Gaussian integral with log‑value ½[−log|H| + constant], effectively collapsing the latent dimensions. This collapsed likelihood is used by the nested sampler, which only explores the θ space. The per‑sample computational cost includes an optimization and Hessian evaluation, but the overall scaling improves from O((dθ+dz)³) to O(dθ³·dz). For non‑Gaussian conditionals, a Student‑t extension matches up to fourth moments, though its consistency remains to be fully validated. An ESS diagnostic based on importance sampling weights from the Laplace proposal monitors approximation quality.

## Key Results
- Achieved evidence bias |δlogZ| < 0.25 nats on supernova cosmology benchmarks with up to 25 600 latent dimensions.
- Wall‑time scaled from ~1 min to ~12 min; full nested sampling would require ~37 years for similar models.
- Exactness for Gaussian conditionals; Student‑t extension shows promise for heavy‑tailed posteriors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ALCS leverages the fact that many hierarchical models have conditionally Gaussian latent posteriors given the parameters. By performing a Laplace approximation around the conditional MAP, the integral over the high‑dimensional latent field becomes analytically tractable: its value is proportional to (2π)^{dz/2} |H|^{-1/2}, where H is the Hessian of the negative log joint. Because nested sampling only requires likelihood evaluations, ALCS replaces the usual expensive joint evaluation with a cheap collapsed likelihood that includes a pre‑computed determinant term. The use of automatic differentiation ensures that gradients and Hessians are obtained accurately and efficiently, even for complex models. The ESS diagnostic approximates the effective sample size of an importance sampler that uses the Gaussian approximation as proposal; a low ESS indicates that the approximation does not cover the true conditional well, flagging potential failure. This combination yields dramatic computational savings while retaining accuracy as long as the conditional is unimodal and not too far from Gaussian.

## Foundational Learning
**Laplace Approximation** – A method to approximate integrals around a mode using a Gaussian. *Why needed:* It provides a closed‑form estimate of the marginal likelihood after collapsing latent variables. *Quick check:* For a 1D function, verify that approximating ∫ exp(−f(x)) dx ≈ exp(−f(x̂)) √(2π/f''(x̂)) yields relative error < 5% for a moderately curved parabola.

**Nested Sampling** – A Monte Carlo algorithm for evidence computation that iteratively samples from the prior subject to likelihood constraints. *Why needed:* It offers a robust way to estimate the marginal likelihood even in multi‑modal posterior spaces; ALCS uses it as the outer sampler. *Quick check:* Implement a simple 1D nested sampler on a bimodal Gaussian mixture and compare evidence with a ground‑truth grid calculation.

**Automatic Differentiation (AD)** – A technique to compute exact derivatives of functions implemented as code, via computational graph traversal. *Why needed:* It allows efficient and accurate computation of gradients and Hessians required for the Laplace step without manual derivation. *Quick check:* Use an AD library (e.g., JAX, PyTorch) to compute the gradient and Hessian of a small neural network loss; verify against finite differences.

**Bayesian Evidence** – The marginal likelihood p(D) = ∫ p(D | θ, z) p(θ, z) dθ dz, central to model comparison. *Why needed:* ALCS aims to compute this quantity efficiently in high dimensions. *Quick check:* Compute evidence for a normal‑inverse‑gamma model analytically and compare with numeric integration.

**Hessian Matrix** – Matrix of second derivatives of the negative log joint; its determinant gives the curvature at the MAP. *Why needed:* The log‑determinant of the Hessian appears in the Gaussian marginalization formula. *Quick check:* For a multivariate Gaussian, the log‑determinant of the precision matrix equals −2 log likelihood contribution; verify with a random covariance matrix.

**Log‑Determinant Computation** – Evaluating log|H| efficiently without forming the full matrix inverse. *Why needed:* Direct computation scales as O(dz³), but ALCS can exploit structure or use Cholesky; AD libraries often provide stable log‑det functions. *Quick check:* Compute log|A| via Cholesky for a positive‑definite matrix and compare with `np.linalg.slogdet`.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Data & Model -> NestedSampler -> For each θ: Optimizer (MAP z) -> HessianCalculator -> LaplaceContribution -> CombinedLikelihood -> NestedSampler (weight update) -> Termination -> EvidenceEstimate

Critical path:  
1. Nested sampler proposes a new θ from the prior subject to likelihood constraint.  
2. Optimizer maximizes p(z | θ, D) to obtain ẑ.  
3. HessianCalculator computes H = ∇²_{z} [−log p(θ, z, D)] at (θ, ẑ).  
4. LaplaceContribution evaluates log p(ẑ | θ, D) + ½ log|H| + constant.  
5. This collapsed log‑likelihood is returned to the nested sampler, which updates the evidence integral.  
6. Loop until termination condition (e.g., remaining mass < tol) is met.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Exactness vs speed**: The Laplace approximation reduces dimensionality but introduces bias if the conditional is non‑Gaussian. Student‑t extensions add cost.
- **Hessian vs optimization cost**: Exact Hessian scales as O(dz³) but can be amortized? ALCS recomputes per θ; using quasi‑Newton or stochastic Hessians may reduce cost at the risk of bias.
- **Optimization reliability**: Optimization to MAP may fail for complex conditionals; restarts or gradient clipping can help but increase per‑sample time.
- **Diagnostic coverage**: ESS/K flags failures but does not correct them; the sampler must still terminate gracefully.

Failure signatures:
- **Low ESS/K** (e.g., < 0.1) indicating that the Gaussian proposal poorly represents the true conditional.
- **Non‑convergence of optimizer** (e.g., max iterations exceeded, gradient norm large).
- **Ill‑conditioned Hessian** (near‑singular, log‑det warnings) causing inaccurate collapse.
- **Sudden jumps in estimated evidence** across nested sampling runs, suggesting instability.

First three experiments:
1. **Exactness test**: Run ALCS on a toy model with known Gaussian conditional (e.g., linear regression with known variance) and confirm that evidence matches analytic value to machine precision.
2. **Heavy‑tailed comparison**: Construct a hierarchical model with Student‑t conditional (e.g., t‑distributed latent errors); compare Gaussian ALCS, Student‑t ALCS, and full joint nested sampling to measure bias and ESS behavior.
3. **Scaling benchmark**: Increase latent dimension from 10² to 10⁵ on a synthetic Gaussian model; measure wall time, memory, and Hessian operation counts to verify O(dθ³·dz) scaling and identify when memory becomes limiting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the Student‑t extension via fourth‑derivative matching consistently improve evidence accuracy for heavy‑tailed latent conditionals without introducing new failure modes?
- **Basis in paper:** Section B states: "whether such extensions lead to reliably better evidence estimates in practice, rather than introducing new failure modes from imperfect moment matching, is an open question."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates benefit on one synthetic heavy‑tailed model but lacks systematic evaluation across diverse tail behaviours and real‑world heavy‑tailed structures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A benchmark suite with known ground truth evidence, varying tail weight and shape, comparing Gaussian ALCS, Student‑t ALCS, and full nested sampling to measure improvement and any new bias patterns.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How well does the importance‑sampling ESS/K diagnostic correlate with actual evidence error across different model classes?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper introduces ESS/K in Section G as a proxy for approximation quality but does not establish a quantitative mapping to δlogZ or thresholds for safety.
- **Why unresolved:** Examples show correlation (e.g., tanh funnel, IRT) but false positives/negatives could occur; no calibration study linking ESS/K distributions to evidence error magnitudes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large‑scale study using models with known ground truth Z, computing ESS/K at posterior θ samples and correlating with pointwise and total evidence error to derive reliability metrics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can stochastic Hessian estimation (e.g., Hutchinson trace estimator) extend ALCS to field‑level latent dimensions (~10⁶) while maintaining sub‑σ evidence accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** Section B suggests combining stochastic methods from Savchenko et al. [2025] but provides no implementation or validation for ultra‑high‑dimensional latent spaces.
- **Why unresolved:** Stochastic log‑determinants introduce variance and potential bias; accuracy for evidence computation in practice remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement stochastic‑ALCS on a field‑level cosmology model (e.g., ∂Lux with d_z ~ 10⁶), comparing deterministic reference (on smaller scales) or exact marginalisation (if tractable) to quantify bias and uncertainty.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** For which real‑world hierarchical models does the latent conditional p(z|θ,D) satisfy Gaussian‑like structure well enough for ALCS to achieve sub‑σ accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper validates on limited benchmarks (supernova, gym) but scope for high‑impact astrophysical pipelines (e.g., galaxy populations, gravitational‑wave inference) is not fully mapped.
- **Why unresolved:** Real models may have non‑factorizing likelihoods, discrete latents, or complex correlations that violate the unimodal/Gaussian assumption beyond tested cases.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic application of ALCS to canonical hierarchical models from astronomy/cosmology, reporting δlogZ and ESS/K against available ground truth or alternative methods, to define a practical scope guide.

## Limitations
- **Laplace assumption:** Conditional posterior must be unimodal and near‑Gaussian; multi‑modality or strong skew cause bias.
- **Optimization cost:** Finding MAP z for every θ may be expensive for large dz and non‑convex landscapes; no guarantee of global optimum.
- **Diagnostic limits:** ESS/K flags failure but does not provide a remedy; the algorithm still proceeds with potentially inaccurate evidence.
