# Priority-Aware Shapley Value

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.09326
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09326
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Introduces Priority-Aware Shapley Value that unifies hard precedence constraints (via DAG) and soft priority weights, satisfying natural axioms, and demonstrates improved credit allocation and robustness via priority sweeping on real-world tasks.

## Executive Summary
The paper proposes the Priority-Aware Shapley Value (PASV) to address limitations of traditional Shapley values when players have precedence constraints (e.g., data lineage) or trust/risk priorities. PASV defines a random-order value where the distribution over linear extensions of a given DAG is biased by player-specific weights λ. It is uniquely characterized by axioms combining Precedence Random Order Values with Weight Proportionality and Equal-Weight Uniformity. For computation, an adjacent-swap Metropolis-Hastings sampler is introduced, enabling scalable estimation on general DAGs. Experiments on data valuation (MNIST/CIFAR10) and feature attribution (Census Income) show PASV yields structure-faithful credit allocation and that priority sweeping identifies robust versus unstable features.

## Method Summary
PASV operates on a set of players with a directed acyclic graph (DAG) encoding hard precedence constraints and a positive weight vector λ representing soft priorities. The value is the expected marginal contribution of a player under a probability distribution p(⪯,λ) over linear extensions π of the DAG. This distribution factorizes along the positions of the permutation, assigning probability proportional to the product of λ of the current player and the number of maximal elements at that step, normalized by the sum of λ over the current maximal set. This construction yields a Shapley-type value that respects the DAG and downweights/upweights players. Exact computation requires summing over all linear extensions, which is infeasible for large DAGs. The authors therefore develop an MCMC sampler: start from a valid linear extension, repeatedly propose swaps of adjacent incomparable players and accept with Metropolis-Hastings probability that depends only on the maximal sets before and after the swap. The marginal contribution for each sampled permutation is computed with a utility oracle, and the average approximates PASV. The method recovers Precedence Shapley (uniform λ) and Weighted Shapley (ordered-partition DAG) as special cases.

## Key Results
- PASV unifies precedence and priority within a single random-order framework, satisfying natural axioms.
- On MNIST/CIFAR10 data valuation with a complex lineage DAG, PASV allocated credit more faithfully to the data generation structure compared to PSV, WSV, and SV; increasing λ for duplicated/poisoned providers effectively suppressed their contributions.
- In Census Income feature attribution, PASV showed that the DAG structure significantly changes feature attributions and that priority sweeping serves as a sensitivity analysis tool, highlighting features with stable contributions across weight variations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1  
**Claim:** PASV defines a single probability distribution over valid linear extensions of a DAG that simultaneously respects hard precedence constraints and biases orders according to player-specific soft priorities.  
**Mechanism:** For a DAG (poset) ⪯ and positive weights λ, the unnormalized probability of a linear extension π is  
p(⪯,λ)(π) ∝ ∏_{t=1}^n λ_{π_t} · |max⪯(S_t)| / (∑_{k∈max⪯(S_t)} λ_k),  
where S_t = {π_1,…,π_t} and max⪯(S_t) are the maximal elements of S_t. This product weights each permutation based on the λ of its elements and the number of maximal elements in each prefix. When all λ are equal, |max⪯(S_t)| cancels the denominator factor, making all valid π equally likely (reducing to PSV). When the DAG is an ordered partition, the distribution exactly matches the Weighted Shapley value.  
**Core assumption:** The DAG correctly encodes all necessary precedence constraints; λ are positive and interpretable as soft priorities.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "unifies hard precedence constraints (via a DAG) and soft priority weights (λ) within a random-order framework" and "recovers Precedence Shapley and Weighted Shapley as special cases".  
- [section] Equation (4) defines p(⪯,λ)(π); Propositions 3.1 and 3.2 prove reductions.  
- [corpus] Neighboring works (e.g., FW-Shapley, Shapley Sets) address weighting or grouping but not combined hard/soft priority, underscoring PASV’s novelty.  
**Break condition:** If the DAG is misspecified (e.g., missing edges), PASV may allocate credit ignoring true dependencies. Extreme λ can effectively add edges (Section 3.4), but these derived edges never create cycles or remove existing ones; if cycle creation occurred, the DAG would be invalid and the method inapplicable.

### Mechanism 2  
**Claim:** PASV is the unique Shapley-type value that satisfies natural axioms for precedence and priority, providing a theoretically grounded extension.  
**Mechanism:** Starting from Precedence Random Order Values (PROV)—values representable as expectations over some distribution on linear extensions satisfying Efficiency, Linearity, Null player, Monotonicity, and Maximal support—the authors restrict to distributions with State-Choice Factorization (SCF). Then adding Weight Proportionality (WP: probabilities among admissible players proportional to λ) and Equal-Weight Uniformity (EWU: uniform when all λ equal) singles out the PASV distribution (Theorem 3.12).  
**Core assumption:** The SCF factorization is an appropriate structural constraint; the axioms collectively capture fairness desiderata for precedence-respecting, weight-aware attribution.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "uniquely characterized by natural axioms".  
- [section] Section 3.3 defines PROV, SCF, WP, EWU; Theorem 3.12 gives the axiomatization.  
- [corpus] Corpus shows broad interest in principled Shapley extensions, but none combine these axioms; PASV fills this gap.  
**Break condition:** If one rejects SCF (e.g., desires a non-factorizing distribution), the characterization does not hold and other PROVs may satisfy WP/EWU. Also, the Maximal Support axiom might be too strong in settings where non-maximal players legitimately contribute (e.g., cooperative games with synergy).

### Mechanism 3  
**Claim:** PASV can be scaled to large, general DAGs via an efficient Metropolis-Hastings sampler that avoids enumerating all linear extensions.  
**Mechanism:** The sampler (Algorithm 1) starts from any linear extension (e.g., via Kahn’s algorithm). At each step, it picks a random adjacent pair; if the two players are incomparable, it proposes swapping them. The Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability α = min(1, p(π’)/p(π)) is computed using Lemma 4.1, which expresses the ratio as  
(p(π’)/p(π)) = (∑_{i∈M_k} λ_i / |M_k|) / (∑_{j∈M’_k} λ_j / |M’_k|),  
where M_k and M’_k are the maximal sets at position k before and after the swap. This local computation makes each iteration efficient.  
**Core assumption:** The Markov chain is ergodic and mixes sufficiently fast; the adjacent-swap proposal set is rich enough to connect all linear extensions for the given DAG.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "employs an adjacent-swap Metropolis-Hastings sampler for scalable Monte Carlo estimation on general DAGs".  
- [section] Section 4 details Algorithm 1 and Lemma 4.1; experiments use NMC=10,000 with burn-in and thinning.  
- [corpus] Neighbors like FW-Shapley also rely on MCMC for weighted Shapley, indicating MCMC is a standard scalability tool in this space.  
**Break condition:** The chain may mix slowly on large or highly constrained DAGs, leading to high autocorrelation and biased estimates if burn-in is insufficient or sample size too small. The swap proposal requires incomparable adjacent pairs; if the DAG is almost total order, the chain may become stuck or require many steps to move.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept 1: Shapley Value and Random-Order Interpretation  
**Why needed here:** PASV is a Shapley-type value; understanding the classical Shapley value (and its equivalence to averaging marginal contributions over uniform random orders) is essential to see how PASV modifies the order distribution to incorporate precedence and priority.  
**Quick check question:** Write the Shapley value formula both in coalition-sum form and as an expectation over permutations. Why does the random-order view facilitate extensions like PASV?

### Concept 2: Partial Orders and Linear Extensions  
**Why needed here:** Hard precedence constraints are modeled as a DAG, which defines a poset. PASV’s distribution is supported only on linear extensions (permutations respecting the DAG). Without understanding linear extensions, one cannot grasp what constitutes a valid order or how the distribution is constrained.  
**Quick check question:** Given a DAG, what is a linear extension? How can you test if a given permutation is a valid linear extension? How does the number of linear extensions grow with n?

### Concept 3: Markov Chain Monte Carlo (Metropolis-Hastings)  
**Why needed here:** Exact computation of PASV requires summing over all linear extensions, which is infeasible for realistic n. The paper uses MCMC to approximate the expectation, so implementing PASV requires grasping how to design a Markov chain over the space of linear extensions and how to compute acceptance ratios.  
**Quick check question:** In Metropolis-Hastings, what is the acceptance probability and why does it ensure the stationary distribution? How does the adjacent-swap proposal respect the DAG constraints?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
- DAG input (adjacency list/matrix) defines the poset ⪯.
- Weight vector λ (positive scalars per player) encodes soft priorities.
- Utility oracle U(S) evaluates subset performance (e.g., model accuracy).
- Linear extension sampler implements Algorithm 1 (adjacent-swap M-H) to draw π ∼ p(⪯,λ).
- Marginal contribution estimator computes U(S ∪ {i}) – U(S) for each sampled π.
- Aggregator averages marginal contributions across samples to estimate PASV.
- Priority sweeping module varies λ_i while fixing others to assess sensitivity.

**Critical path:**  
1. Validate DAG (acyclic, matches domain precedence).
2. Set baseline λ (often uniform λ_i=1 to recover PSV).
3. Run MCMC sampler: choose N_MC, burn-in B, thinning τ; monitor mixing if possible.
4. Estimate values: collect marginal contributions, average; verify efficiency (sum equals U([n])).
5. Priority sweep: loop over players/groups, adjust λ, repeat estimation, track changes.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- MCMC vs exact: Exact enumeration only for n ≤ ~12; MCMC introduces approximation error. Trade sample count (time) vs variance.
- λ scaling strategy: Paper uses λ = b^c (c type indicator). Choosing meaningful types requires domain knowledge.
- Burn-in and thinning: Too aggressive thinning reduces effective sample size; too little burn-in risks bias. Diagnostics (trace plots, ESS) help but often omitted; defaults B=100k, τ=10k are used.
- Swap proposal space: In dense DAGs, fraction of incomparable adjacent pairs may be low, slowing mixing. Non-adjacent swaps could help but complicate ratio.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High variance across seeds: large standard deviation of value estimates across repeated MCMC runs; indicates insufficient samples or poor mixing.
- Efficiency violation: sum of estimated PASV differs significantly from U([n]); suggests sampler not converged or utility inconsistency.
- Priority sweep anomalies: values change erratically with λ; could be estimator instability or genuine non-monotonicity. Compare with theoretical limits (Section 3.4).
- DAG errors: input DAG contains cycles or is not transitively closed; sampler may fail or produce invalid permutations. Preprocess to ensure validity.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Synthetic enumeration sanity check: Use a tiny DAG (4 nodes with a few edges) and utility U(S)=∑_{i∈S} i. Compute exact PASV by enumerating all linear extensions. Compare with MCMC estimates to validate sampler and ratio computation.  
2. Verification of theoretical limits: For a moderate DAG, pick a maximal player i. Set λ_i → ∞ (large b) and verify PASV values converge to those from PSV on DAG with added edges from all other maximal players to i (Proposition 3.13). Repeat for ordered partition to test Proposition 3.14.  
3. Recovery of PSV and WSV: With non-ordered-partition DAG, set λ constant and confirm PASV matches PSV. With ordered partition DAG and non-constant λ, confirm PASV matches WSV. Ensures correct inheritance of special cases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: For a given DAG and player i, what are necessary and sufficient conditions for λ_i→∞ to induce a deterministic ordering where i is placed as late as possible (i.e., adding implicit precedence from all other maximal elements to i)?
- Basis: [explicit] Section 3.4 states extreme weights are not always equivalent to adding DAG edges, shows a counterexample, and gives only sufficient conditions (i globally maximal, ordered partition).
- Why unresolved: No general characterization exists for arbitrary posets.
- Evidence: A complete theorem describing the limiting distribution (e.g., via the set of linear extensions where i appears after all challengers) and a constructive method to obtain the "effective DAG" in the limit.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the mixing time of the adjacent-swap Metropolis-Hastings sampler for pPASV on general DAGs, and how does it depend on the DAG structure and the priority weights λ?
- Basis: [inferred] Section 4 proposes the sampler but provides no theoretical convergence analysis; experiments use heuristics for burn-in/thinning.
- Why unresolved: Without guarantees, it is unclear whether the estimator scales to large problems or extreme λ.
- Evidence: Rigorous bounds on the spectral gap or conductance for classes of DAGs, combined with empirical autocorrelation studies across diverse instances.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Under what conditions on the utility function U and the precedence DAG is the PASV value of a player i monotonic in its weight λ_i?
- Basis: [inferred] Section 5.2 shows non-monotone sweeps (Figure 12) and offers an informal explanation about complementarity vs. redundancy but no formal theory.
- Why unresolved: Monotonicity would simplify interpretation of priority sweeping and help identify stable attributions.
- Evidence: A characterization linking monotonicity to properties like supermodularity of U or specific DAG structures; proof for common utility classes (e.g., additive, coverage functions) and counterexamples otherwise.

## Limitations
- The method assumes a correctly specified DAG and positive, interpretable λ; real-world precedence or trust relationships may be noisy or cyclic.
- MCMC scalability depends on mixing speed, which is not guaranteed for dense or complex DAGs; slow mixing could undermine practical utility.
- Experimental validation uses specific synthetic data generation protocols and k-NN utilities; results may not generalize to other model utilities or data distributions.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Unified Distribution): High confidence (explicit definition, correct reductions).
- Mechanism 2 (Axiomatic Uniqueness): Medium confidence (depends on acceptance of SCF and Maximal Support axioms).
- Mechanism 3 (Scalable Sampling): Medium confidence (sampler defined, but mixing not rigorously quantified).
- Experimental Results: Medium confidence (domain-specific utilities; causal link relies on chosen λ sweeps and may be sensitive to utility noise).
