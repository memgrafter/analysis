# Conspiracy Frame: a Semiotically-Driven Approach for Conspiracy Theories Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21368
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21368
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** 70B LLMs outperform 8B on conspiracy detection (F-score +3.1, recall +3.8); FrameNet-based frame hints do not significantly improve over few-shot prompting; LLMs under-represent abstract frames such as CALENDRIC_UNIT.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the binary-label limitation in computational conspiracy theory detection by introducing the Conspiracy Frame—a fine-grained semantic representation with five elements (plan, secret, in-group, out-group, call-to-action). This schema guided the creation of the Con.Fra. corpus, a span-annotated collection of 2,577 Telegram messages. In-context learning experiments with Llama 3.3 (8B and 70B) show that larger models consistently improve classification and span detection (F-score +3.1, recall +3.8). Injecting FrameNet frame hints into prompts yields no clear performance gain over standard few-shot learning. Out-of-domain evaluation via human ELO ratings confirms the superiority of larger models. Qualitative mapping of spans to FrameNet frames reveals recurring semantic patterns (e.g., POLITICAL_LOCALES, STATEMENT) but also shows LLMs under-detect abstract frames like CALENDRIC_UNIT, highlighting a gap between narrative structure and generalizable semantic awareness.

## Method Summary
The study defines the Conspiracy Frame to capture the semiotic structure of conspiratorial narratives. Researchers collected Telegram messages and produced the Con.Fra. corpus, where annotators labeled each message (conspiracy yes/no) and marked text spans for the five frame elements. LLM experiments used Llama 3.3 in 8B and 70B parameter sizes, comparing zero-shot, few-shot, and frame-guided prompting (which incorporates FrameNet frame names). Models were evaluated on classification and span detection using precision, recall, and F-score. Out-of-domain performance was assessed through human pairwise comparisons converted to ELO ratings. Additionally, annotated spans were lemmatized and mapped to FrameNet frames to analyze semantic patterns across the dataset.

## Key Results
- Larger models (70B) significantly outperform smaller ones (8B) on in-domain tasks (classification F-score +3.1, recall +3.8) and dominate out-of-domain comparisons (998/1000 ELO wins).
- Frame-guided prompting (with FrameNet hints) does not provide statistically significant improvements over standard few-shot prompting for classification or span detection.
- LLMs exhibit a notable under-detection of abstract semantic frames (e.g., CALENDRIC_UNIT) compared to human annotations, suggesting a limitation in capturing temporal and abstract aspects of conspiracy narratives.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Structural Narrative Representation Improves Generalizability
The Conspiracy Frame decomposes conspiratorial discourse into five semantic elements (plan, secret, in-group, out-group, call-to-action), forcing models to identify narrative roles instead of surface topic markers. This structural approach assumes that conspiracy narratives share a stable formal framework regardless of topic. Evidence from the Con.Fra. annotation shows moderate inter-annotator agreement (κ mean 0.41 for classification, up to 0.808 for plan spans), supporting feasibility. A break condition occurs when a message lacks core elements (plan/secret), causing the frame to be incomplete and detection to fail.

### Mechanism 2: Model Capacity Enables Complex Narrative Understanding
Larger language models (70B parameters) better capture long-range dependencies and implicit relationships between frame elements due to richer internal representations. This scaling assumption is validated by consistent performance gains: 70B models outperform 8B by 3.1 F-score and 3.8 recall points in classification, and they achieve 998 wins out of 1000 out-of-domain ELO comparisons. Smaller models exhibit degraded recall on span detection, especially for non-core or abstract elements.

### Mechanism 3: FrameNet Alignment Reveals Patterns but Prompt Injection Is Not Discriminative
Mapping spans to FrameNet frames (via lemmatization) uncovers recurring high-level semantic templates (e.g., POLITICAL_LOCALES, STATEMENT). However, providing these frame names as hints in few-shot prompts does not improve performance over standard few-shot learning, likely because the mapping is noisy and models already implicitly capture similar patterns. LLMs also under-detect abstract frames like CALENDRIC_UNIT, indicating a limitation.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Data Collector → Annotation Pipeline → FrameNet Mapper → LLM Experiment Engine → Evaluation Module → Qualitative Analyzer.

**Critical path:**  
Telegram data collection → human annotation (2,577 messages) → lexical mapping to FrameNet → LLM prompting → automated metrics + human ELO → frame frequency analysis.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Lexical mapping simplicity vs. ambiguity: Lemmatization + heuristics reduces noise but cannot resolve polysemy (e.g., "fire").
- Frame-guided prompt complexity vs. utility: Adding FrameNet frame names increases prompt length but yields no clear gain, suggesting redundancy or poor framing.
- Span annotation granularity vs. agreement: Five-element scheme captures nuance but core/non-core distinction and overlapping spans challenge consistency (in-group κ=0.633 lowest among spans).

**Failure signatures:**
- Low pairwise Cohen’s κ on binary conspiracy labels (0.23) in out-of-domain evaluation, indicating domain shift makes even human judgment unreliable.
- LLMs under-represent abstract frames (e.g., CALENDRIC_UNIT) in qualitative analysis, showing gap in temporal reasoning.
- Frame-guided prompts produce more complete but sometimes over-inclusive spans; few-shot is more precise but fragmentary.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. In-domain classification: Compare 8B vs 70B Llama 3.3 under zero/few/frame-guided prompts to validate scaling hypothesis and prompt strategy effects.
2. Span detection F1: Evaluate all frame elements separately to see which are hardest (e.g., secret, in-group).
3. Out-of-domain ELO: Human pairwise preference between frame-guided and few-shot outputs on unseen Telegram channels to test generalizability and frame hint utility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1: Can fine-tuning or pre-training with FrameNet-derived knowledge improve conspiracy detection?**  
- **Basis:** The authors conclude that future work will explore deeper integration via fine-tuning or knowledge graphs because current frame-guided prompting does not lead to clear increase of performance.  
- **Why unresolved:** Frame-guided prompting experiments showed no clear gain; the hypothetical benefit of frame semantics remains unproven in a training context.  
- **Resolving evidence:** A comparative study where LLMs are fine-tuned or pre-trained on a knowledge graph encoding FrameNet relations and the Conspiracy Frame structure, then evaluated on classification and span detection against a strong few-shot baseline.

**Open Question 2: How does lexical-based FrameNet mapping affect observed frame distributions?**  
- **Basis:** Limitations state lexical approach cannot resolve lexical ambiguity and more sophisticated integration is needed.  
- **Why unresolved:** Mapping used lemma matching without contextual disambiguation, potentially conflating senses and misaligning spans, skewing qualitative analysis (e.g., under-representation of CALENDRIC_UNIT).  
- **Resolving evidence:** Re-run alignment using context-sensitive methods (e.g., contextualized embeddings) and compare frame distributions to original mapping; a significant shift for abstract frames would confirm the limitation.

**Open Question 3: Why are core elements (plan, secret) harder for LLMs to detect?**  
- **Basis:** Results show zero-shot performed worst on secret and call-to-action; frame-guided improved in-group; error analysis noted issues with span completeness and structural precision, especially for abstract plan/secret versus concrete entities.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies performance gaps but does not analyze underlying linguistic or representational reasons; these elements are often implicit, nominalized, or distributed.  
- **Resolving evidence:** A fine-grained error analysis categorizing plan/secret errors by linguistic features (implicitness, syntactic complexity, lexical diversity); controlled experiments testing prompt augmentation with definitions or models with enhanced coreference capabilities.

**Open Question 4: How generalizable is the Conspiracy Frame schema across contexts and platforms?**  
- **Basis:** Out-of-domain evaluation yielded Cohen’s Kappa = 0.23 for binary classification, much lower than corpus annotation agreement; authors note possible frame criteria inconsistencies.  
- **Why unresolved:** The frame was designed and annotated on Telegram data; the drop in agreement suggests the schema may not consistently capture CT manifestations in different contexts.  
- **Resolving evidence:** A multi-site annotation study applying the Conspiracy Frame to content from diverse platforms (Twitter/X, Facebook, forums) and cultural/linguistic contexts, measuring inter-annotator agreement and conducting qualitative interviews to identify context-dependent vs. stable elements.

## Limitations
- Single-source (Telegram) corpus limits generalizability; out-of-domain inter-annotator agreement drops to κ = 0.23.
- FrameNet alignment relies on lemmatization heuristics that ignore polysemy, potentially skewing semantic pattern analysis.
- LLMs systematically under-detect abstract frames (e.g., CALENDRIC_UNIT), indicating a gap in capturing temporal and abstract narrative aspects.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1** (narrative structure improves detection): Medium — theoretical grounding strong, but annotation inconsistencies and break conditions weaken robustness.
- **Claim 2** (model scaling benefits): High — consistent quantitative gains across classification (F-score +3.1, recall +3.8) and out-of-domain ELO (998/1000 wins).
- **Claim 3** (FrameNet alignment reveals patterns, prompt injection ineffective): Medium — FrameNet mapping shows recurring frames, but prompt method may be poorly specified; abstract frame gap persists.

## Next Checks
- **Cross-platform validation:** Apply the Con.Fra. annotation schema to Twitter/Reddit conspiracy datasets to test domain portability of frame elements.
- **Refine FrameNet alignment:** Integrate word sense disambiguation (e.g., BERT-based WSD) into the lexical mapping pipeline to resolve polysemy.
- **Abstract frame probing:** Use diagnostic classifiers on LLM hidden states during span detection to isolate reasons for under-detection of temporal/abstract frames (e.g., CALENDRIC_UNIT).
