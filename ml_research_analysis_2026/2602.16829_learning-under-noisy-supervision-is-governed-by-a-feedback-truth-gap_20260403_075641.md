# Learning under noisy supervision is governed by a feedback-truth gap
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16829
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16829
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Under noisy supervision, learners inevitably favor feedback over truth, creating a feedback‑truth gap whose magnitude and effects depend on the system’s regulatory capacity.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the feedback–truth gap, a universal measure of the discrepancy between what a learner tracks from noisy feedback and the true underlying state. A two‑timescale analytical model shows the gap arises inevitably when feedback integration outpaces truth evaluation, vanishing only when the two rates match. This phenomenon is demonstrated across three distinct systems: dense neural networks trained on noisy labels, human probabilistic reversal learning, and human EEG during reward‑based learning. Crucially, the gap’s consequences are polarity‑dependent: in unregulated systems (dense networks) larger gaps harm generalization, while in regulated humans larger gaps predict faster behavioral recovery. Architectural interventions (sparse‑residual bottlenecks) can causally suppress the gap but introduce a capacity‑memorization trade‑off. The work recasts noisy supervision as a timescale‑mismatch problem with broad implications for machine learning and cognitive science.

## Method Summary
The study combines a two‑timescale theoretical model with empirical experiments in three domains. The model posits fast and slow exponential moving averages that track noisy feedback and truth, respectively; the gap is their difference. In machine learning, dense and sparse‑residual neural networks were trained on tabular datasets with symmetric label noise (20–40%). The gap was quantified as the rolling difference between training and validation accuracy (summarized by AUGnorm and T*). Human reversal learning data (N=292) were analyzed by aligning trials to reward‑schedule reversals and comparing feedback‑aligned (received reward) and truth‑aligned (objectively correct option) choice accuracies; gap metrics were AUGpos and T*. For EEG (N=25), trial‑wise features were extracted after preprocessing, and separate logistic decoders predicted feedback valence and pre‑feedback expectation; the neural gap was the difference in decoder probabilities, summarized by neural AUGpos. Across all systems, the gap’s dynamics, magnitude, and relationship to outcomes were compared, and the effect of architectural bottlenecks (sparsity parameter α) was tested in neural networks.

## Key Results
- The feedback–truth gap is universal: it emerges in dense neural networks, human reversal behavior, and EEG signals whenever feedback integration is faster than truth evaluation.
- The gap’s sign flips: larger gaps predict worse generalization in unregulated networks (ρ = –0.74) but faster recovery in humans (ρ = +0.20).
- Architectural regulation via sparse‑residual bottlenecks monotonically suppresses the gap (Spearman ρ = 1.0 across α), yet excessive suppression reduces test accuracy, revealing a capacity‑memorization trade‑off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The feedback–truth gap stems from a fundamental timescale separation. A two‑timescale learner maintains two exponential moving averages: a fast channel that quickly incorporates noisy feedback (rate α_fast) and a slow channel that tracks the true state more cautiously (rate α_slow). Because α_fast > α_slow, the fast channel responds first to changes, overshooting the truth before the slow channel catches up, creating a transient positive gap that decays only as the slow channel aligns. This gap is mathematically inevitable when the rates differ and persists as long as noisy supervision exists.

Whether the gap is beneficial or detrimental hinges on the system’s regulatory capacity. In unregulated systems like dense neural networks, the gap accumulates as memorization of noise, impairing generalization. In regulated systems like humans, active cognitive or neural processes compress the gap after its peak, turning the initial over‑commitment into a catalyst for faster realignment with truth. Architectural interventions, such as sparse‑residual bottlenecks, adjust the effective capacity of the learner, thereby tuning the timescale ratio and suppressing the gap, albeit at the risk of underfitting if too restrictive.

Thus, the gap’s existence is guaranteed by timescale mismatch, but its functional significance is entirely shaped by downstream regulation.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Noisy supervision**  
*Why needed:* The entire framework assumes feedback labels can be incorrect; without noise the gap would be negligible or even beneficial.  
*Quick check:* When a model is trained with 40% symmetric label noise, what pattern emerges between training and validation accuracy over epochs?  

**Concept: Timescale separation**  
*Why needed:* The two‑timescale model relies on distinct integration speeds for feedback and truth; equal rates would eliminate the gap.  
*Quick check:* If a learner uses learning rates α_fast = 0.1 and α_slow = 0.05, after a sudden reversal will the fast channel’s estimate be closer to the new state than the slow channel’s? Why?  

**Concept: Memorization vs. generalization**  
*Why needed:* In machine learning, a growing gap indicates memorization of noise, which harms generalization. Understanding this trade‑off is essential for interpreting gap metrics.  
*Quick check:* How does the AUGnorm metric capture memorization pressure, and why would a higher AUGnorm correlate with poorer validation performance in dense networks?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Two‑timescale theoretical model → machine‑learning pipeline (dense/sparse‑residual networks with noisy labels) + human behavioral reversal learning + human EEG task with decoders → unified feedback‑truth gap metrics (AUGnorm/T* or AUGpos).  
**Critical path:** For ML: set noise level, train model, compute rolling train‑minus‑validation accuracy, extract AUGnorm/T*. For human reversal: identify reversal trials, compute 8‑trial rolling accuracy for truth and feedback, align, compute AUGpos/T*. For EEG: preprocess EEG, extract features, train logistic decoders for feedback valence and pre‑feedback expectation, compute neural gap and AUGpos.  
**Design tradeoffs:** Choice of gap metric (AUGpos vs T*); noise level (higher noise amplifies gap); bottleneck strength α (higher sparsity reduces gap but may underfit); truth definition (objective labels vs operational expectation).  
**Failure signatures:** In ML: AUGnorm grows steadily across epochs → memorization; sparse‑residual gap near zero → suppression but check for underfitting (low test accuracy). In human reversal: non‑positive AUGpos → mis‑aligned truth or reversal detection error. In EEG: chance‑level decoders (AUROC ≈ 0.5) → inadequate features or missing expectation labels.  
**First 3 experiments:** (1) Train dense networks on five tabular datasets with 40% label noise and confirm emerging gap (Extended Data Fig. 1). (2) Apply sparse‑residual bottleneck across α values and demonstrate graded gap suppression and test‑accuracy peak (Fig. 4). (3) Analyze human reversal data (N=292) to show universal positive AUGpos and its correlation with faster recovery (Fig. 3b).

## Limitations
- Human reversal analysis depends on reconstructing "truth" from known reward probabilities and reversal times; misalignment risks circular gap computation.
- EEG neural gap requires valid pre‑feedback confidence labels; if absent or noisy, the expectation decoder fails.
- Sparse‑residual architecture details (expander‑graph connectivity) are underspecified, potentially leading to replication drift across implementations.
