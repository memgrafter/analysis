# Step-Wise Refusal Dynamics in Autoregressive and Diffusion Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02600  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02600  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Diffusion models' iterative remasking enables recovery from harmful intermediate states; the Step-Wise Refusal Internal (SRI) signal identifies incomplete internal recovery; SRI Guard, an anomaly detector, matches or outperforms existing jailbreak defenses while reducing inference overhead by over 100×.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how sampling mechanisms in autoregressive (AR) and diffusion language models (DLMs) shape refusal behavior and jailbreak robustness. It shows that diffusion sampling’s iterative remasking allows recovery from harmful intermediate states—a structural advantage over AR decoding, which locks prefixes. To analyze this, the authors introduce the Step-Wise Refusal Internal (SRI) signal, derived from internal activations across generation steps, which captures refusal dynamics and identifies harmful outputs as cases of incomplete internal recovery not observable at the text level. Using SRI, they build a lightweight anomaly detector (SRI Guard) that matches or outperforms existing jailbreak defenses in refusal rate and attack success while reducing inference overhead by over 100×. Empirical results demonstrate that DLMs achieve higher harmful remasking rates (up to 96%) and internal recovery rates than AR models across multiple attacks.

## Method Summary
The SRI signal is computed at each generation step `t` (up to T=32) by extracting last-layer hidden activations of all current tokens, applying mean pooling to obtain a step embedding `ϕₜ`, and measuring cosine distances to precomputed harmless and harmful prototype centers `µₜ^harmless` and `µₜ^harmful`. The logit `ℓₜ = (log(d_harmfulₜ+ϵ) - log(d_harmlessₜ+ϵ)) / τ` (with τ=0.1) is passed through a sigmoid to yield `σₜ ∈ [0,1]`, forming the SRI trajectory `{σₜ}ᵀ`. For detection, an MLP autoencoder (~3000 parameters) is trained on SRI signals from harmless prompts; reconstruction error serves as an anomaly score, with a threshold set at the 99% quantile of errors on held-out harmless validation data. The evaluation harness unifies response generation, defense application, and metric computation (Refusal Rate, Attack Success Rate, False Positives) using a refusal dictionary and an LLM judge.

## Key Results
- Diffusion models achieve higher harmful remasking rates (up to 96%) and internal recovery rates than autoregressive models across multiple jailbreak attacks.
- The SRI signal captures step‑wise refusal dynamics and distinguishes harmful outputs (incomplete internal recovery) from harmless and explicitly refused responses.
- SRI Guard, a lightweight anomaly detector (∼3000 parameters), matches or exceeds the refusal robustness of existing defenses (e.g., LlamaGuard) while reducing inference overhead by over 100×.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Diffusion sampling’s iterative remasking enables recovery from harmful intermediate states.**  
In diffusion models, previously generated tokens are not fixed; they can be revised in subsequent steps via remasking. If harmful content appears at an intermediate step, the model can overwrite it later, leading to a safe final output. AR decoding lacks this revision capability due to its left‑to‑right, prefix‑locked nature. The core assumption is that harmfulness is prefix‑monotone. *Evidence anchors:* abstract; Section 3.1 (Proposition 3.1 formalizes structural asymmetry); corpus context limited. *Break condition:* Harmful intermediate states are never revised (HRR ~0) or revisions do not persist to final output (FRR ~0).

**Mechanism 2: The sampling strategy itself is an independent factor shaping jailbreak robustness.**  
Using identical model weights but different sampling strategies (AR vs. native diffusion) leads to measurable differences in Refusal Rate (RR) and Attack Success Rate (ASR). The diffusion process inherently provides more opportunities to correct unsafe trajectories. *Evidence anchors:* abstract; Section 3.4 (Table 2 shows fixed‑weight switch to diffusion improves RR and lowers ASR); corpus limited. *Break condition:* Fixed‑model robustness does not change when switching sampling strategies.

**Mechanism 3: Harmful generations evade text‑level refusals due to incomplete internal recovery, detectable via SRI.**  
The SRI signal, derived from step‑wise internal activations, captures the trajectory of internal refusal alignment. Harmless and explicitly refused responses show stable, low‑variance SRI. Jailbreak‑induced harmful outputs show volatile, non‑stationary signals because they fail to fully transition to a refusal‑aligned internal state, even if the final text appears safe. *Evidence anchors:* abstract; Sections 4.1 & 5.3 (SRI construction, Figures 2, 5, volatility analysis); corpus weak. *Break condition:* SRI signals for harmful and harmless are not separable (AUROC ~0.5) or temporal volatility does not correlate with harmful outcomes.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
SRI Computation Pipeline: Prompt → Model generation (T steps) → Extract last‑layer activations per step → Mean pooling → Compute cosine distances to harmless/harmful prototype centers → Log‑ratio + sigmoid → SRI signal `{σₜ}ᵀ`.  
SRI Guard Detector: SRI signals → MLP autoencoder (2 encoder, 2 decoder layers) → Reconstruction error → Anomaly score → Threshold decision.  
Evaluation Harness: Unified protocol for generating responses, applying defenses, and computing RR/ASR/FP using refusal dictionary and judge LLM.

**Critical path:**  
Data flow begins with harmless/harmful anchor datasets (preprocessing to compute prototype centers). During inference, for each prompt, SRI signal is computed on‑the‑fly over generation steps. SRI Guard scores the SRI trajectory; if the anomaly score exceeds the threshold, the response is flagged. Final metrics (RR, ASR, FP) are computed based on flagged responses and judge evaluations.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Layer choice: Last layer yields strongest SRI separation; earlier layers weaker. Deeper layers may be more semantically rich but noisier.  
- Temporal vs. static: Step‑wise SRI improves detection but requires storing intermediate activations (memory cost).  
- Threshold calibration: 99% quantile of harmless validation errors controls false positives but may miss subtle jailbreaks; adjust based on risk tolerance.  
- Judge reliance: RR/ASR depend on LLM judge or keyword dictionary, which may disagree with human judgment.

**Failure signatures:**  
- SRI Guard high false positives when harmless training set does not cover true benign manifold.  
- Low Internal Recovery Rate (IRR) in diffusion models despite high Harmful Remasking Rate (HRR) indicates revisions don’t persist; SRI may still flag but detection relies on volatility.  
- For some models (e.g., Dream), SRI signals for harmless and harmful are less separable, reducing AUROC and detection reliability.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate Figure 3 IRR: Compute Internal Recovery Rate for AR vs. diffusion models across thresholds λᵣ; confirm diffusion shows higher IRR.  
2. SRI Signal Visualization: Generate SRI trajectories for harmless, refused, and jailbroken responses; verify the incomplete internal recovery pattern (volatile, non‑stationary SRI) in harmful cases.  
3. SRI Guard Benchmark: Train the autoencoder on harmless prompts; evaluate on jailbreak test set; compare RR, ASR, FP, and overhead against baselines (PPL filter, LlamaGuard, Self‑Examine).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can the SRI framework generalize to generative architectures beyond autoregressive and diffusion models (e.g., non‑autoregressive transformers, recurrent neural networks, encoder‑decoder models with iterative decoding)?
- Beyond jailbreak attacks and refusal behavior, what other safety failure modes (bias, privacy violations, sycophancy, prompt injection) manifest as incomplete internal recovery in SRI space, and can SRI‑based detection be generalized to these cases?
- Can sampling strategies be designed that use step‑wise SRI signals during generation to proactively steer the model away from unsafe trajectories, rather than using SRI only for post‑hoc detection?
- Can we establish a theoretical framework—probabilistic, geometric, or information‑theoretic—that explains the observed differences in step‑wise safety dynamics between AR and diffusion sampling, and predicts when incomplete internal recovery is likely to occur?

## Limitations
- Unknown token handling in diffusion mean‑pooling (whether masked tokens are included) directly impacts SRI computation.
- Autoencoder architecture hyperparameters (layer sizes, optimizer, learning‑rate schedule) are unspecified, risking performance deviation.
- No official code or resources are provided, forcing reverse‑engineering of preprocessing and evaluation protocols.
