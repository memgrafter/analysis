# Buffered AUC maximization for scoring systems via mixed-integer optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.05544
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05544
- **Reference count**: 17
- **Primary result**: The proposed bAUC-Integer method achieves higher testing AUC than L1/Elastic-Net regularization, forward/backward selection, and a bAUC variant without integer constraints on three of four datasets.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a mixed-integer linear optimization (MILO) framework for constructing interpretable scoring systems—linear classifiers with small integer coefficients—that directly maximize buffered AUC (bAUC), a concave lower bound on the true AUC. The formulation incorporates group sparsity constraints to limit the number of questions (groups of binary features), ensuring simplicity. By transforming the non-convex bAUC objective into an equivalent MILO via auxiliary variables for the positive part, the method solvable with standard solvers. Computational experiments on four binary classification datasets show that bAUC-Integer outperforms several baseline methods in testing AUC on three datasets (surgery, mushroom, adult), with computation times ranging from 10 to 112 seconds. The resulting score tables align with domain knowledge, demonstrating practical utility.

## Method Summary
The core method formulates a MILO problem where the objective minimizes the sum of auxiliary variables representing the positive part in the bAUC definition, plus an L1-regularization term (λ₁ = 0.005). Integer constraints enforce discrete coefficients, while binary group selection variables and linear constraints (e.g., -M zₛ ≤ wⱼ ≤ M zⱼ for j in group s) enforce sparsity at the group level, with ∑zₛ ≤ θ limiting the number of questions. The model is solved with Gurobi on a subsample of 300 training instances to manage the O(n₊n₋) pairwise terms. After obtaining integer weights, the intercept is estimated via maximum likelihood on the same subsample. Baselines include regularized logistic regression (L1, Elastic-Net) tuned to match θ, forward/backward stepwise selection, and a bAUC-Rounding variant that first solves a continuous bAUC problem then rounds coefficients.

## Key Results
- bAUC-Integer achieved average testing AUC of 0.619, 0.987, and 0.856 on surgery, mushroom, and adult datasets, respectively, surpassing all baselines.
- On the bank dataset (imbalanced, 11.3% positives), conventional methods performed better, likely due to subsampling and class imbalance.
- Computation times ranged from 10 to 112 seconds, indicating feasibility for offline model development.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Transforming the non-convex, discontinuous bAUC maximization objective into a solvable MILO problem via auxiliary variables.
- **Mechanism:** The positive part function `[·]+` in the bAUC definition is linearized by introducing continuous non-negative variables `v_{iℓ}` with constraints `v_{iℓ} ≥ wᵀ(x_ℓ - x_i) + 1`. This replaces the minimization of a sum of positive parts with a linear objective and linear constraints.
- **Core assumption:** The variable substitution `w ← γw̃` and setting `γ=1` is valid for the bAUC minimization problem, as justified by positive homogeneity (Norton & Uryasev, 2019).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "transformed into an equivalent MILO using auxiliary variables for the positive part and linear constraints."
  - [section 2.4]: Detailed derivation from Eq. (8a) to Eq. (9b), introducing `v` to model `[wᵀ(x_ℓ - x_i) + 1]+`.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct support; neighboring papers focus on MIO for sparse regression, not bAUC-specific transformations.
- **Break condition:** The linearization relies on `γ` being positive and the equivalence holding for the transformed problem. If the underlying bAUC theory (Norton & Uryasev, 2019) fails for the given data distribution or classifier family, the MILO may not correctly maximize the true bAUC bound.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Enforcing interpretability by limiting the number of *questions* (groups of features) via group sparsity constraints, rather than individual features.
- **Mechanism:** Binary group selection variables `z_s` are introduced. Constraint `-M z_s ≤ w_j ≤ M z_s` for `j ∈ J_s` forces all coefficients in an unselected group (`z_s=0`) to zero. The cardinality constraint `∑ z_s ≤ θ` caps the number of questions.
- **Core assumption:** A "question" corresponds to a logically grouped set of binary features (e.g., all dummies for "Age"). The scoring system's interpretability is primarily determined by the number of such questions, not the total number of binary features.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "subject to a group sparsity constraint for limiting the number of questions."
  - [section 2.2]: Defines group partition `J_s` and logical implication `z_s=0 ⇒ w_j=0` via linear constraints `-M z_s ≤ w_j ≤ M z_s`.
  - [corpus]: Weak; neighboring papers (e.g., "Optimal Cross-Validation for Sparse Linear Regression") address feature selection but not group-level constraints for question limiting.
- **Break condition:** If the pre-defined grouping of features into questions does not align with human-understandable concepts, the resulting "question-limited" model may still be unintelligible. The constraint also assumes `M` is sufficiently large to not artificially limit coefficients within selected groups.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Achieving superior predictive AUC by directly optimizing bAUC under integer coefficient constraints, compared to post-hoc rounding of continuous solutions.
- **Mechanism:** The MILO explicitly enforces `w ∈ ℤ^p`. This joint optimization over discrete coefficients and the bAUC objective avoids suboptimality from rounding a continuous solution (bAUC-Rounding), which can violate the optimality conditions of the bAUC surrogate.
- **Core assumption:** The discrete optimization landscape, when searched directly via MILO, can find integer coefficient vectors that yield higher bAUC (and thus potentially higher true AUC) than any integer rounding of a continuous optimizer's output. The concave bAUC surrogate is sufficiently aligned with AUC.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "achieves higher testing AUC than L1/Elastic-Net regularization, forward/backward selection, and a bAUC variant without integer constraints."
  - [section 3.3]: Tables 3a,b,d show bAUC-Integer often outperforms bAUC-Rounding and other baselines in average AUC.
  - [corpus]: Weak; neighboring papers on MIO for interpretable models (e.g., "Responsible Machine Learning via Mixed-Integer Optimization") do not compare integer-constrained vs. rounded AUC maximization.
- **Break condition:** The computational burden of MILO may force subsampling (300 instances), which can degrade performance, especially on imbalanced datasets (as seen with the bank dataset). If the optimal continuous solution is already near-integer, the benefit of joint optimization diminishes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Mixed-Integer Linear Optimization (MILO)**
  - *Why needed:* The entire framework is an MILO problem (9). Understanding variables (continuous `v`, integer `w`, binary `z`), linear constraints, and solver behavior is essential to grasp how the model is trained and why it's computationally challenging.
  - *Quick check:* What is the effect on the feasible region and solution time of changing an integer variable `w_j` to a continuous variable?

- **Concept: Buffered AUC (bAUC) as a Surrogate for AUC**
  - *Why needed:* bAUC is the optimization target. Knowing it's a concave, computable lower bound on the true AUC (which is non-convex and discontinuous) explains why direct maximization is tractable via MILO.
  - *Quick check:* How does the positive part function `[x]+` in the bAUC formula create a convex surrogate for the indicator function `1_{x≥0}` used in empirical AUC?

- **Concept: Group Sparsity in Feature Selection**
  - *Why needed:* It operationalizes the constraint "limit the number of questions." Understanding how dummy variables from a single categorical feature are grouped is key to designing interpretable scorecards.
  - *Quick check:* If a categorical variable "Color" with levels {Red, Blue, Green} is one-hot encoded into three binary features, what group sparsity constraint ensures the entire "Color" question is either included or excluded?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Preprocessor -> MILO Formulator -> Solver Interface -> Post-processor
- **Critical path:** Data → Grouping → MILO Formulation → Solve → Extract w → Fit w₀ → Score Table. The group definition must precede formulation; solver success is a hard dependency.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Accuracy vs. Time: Larger θ or M increases search space and time but may yield better scores. Subsampling (n=300) trades data size for solvability, risking performance on small/imbalanced sets.
  - Integer vs. Continuous: Integer constraints (w∈ℤ) increase solve time but avoid rounding suboptimality. The bAUC-Rounding baseline is a faster approximation.
  - Group vs. Feature Sparsity: Group sparsity directly controls question count but may force inclusion of irrelevant feature dummies if the group is selected.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Solver timeout/infeasibility: Often with large p, q, M, or θ. Signal: Gurobi returns non-optimal status. Mitigation: tighten bounds, reduce θ, or increase subsample size gradually.
  - Poor testing AUC vs. baselines: Especially on imbalanced/large datasets (bank). Signal: bAUC-Integer AUC << baselines. Likely cause: subsampling bias or bAUC surrogate mismatch.
  - Uninterpretable score signs: Coefficients counter to domain knowledge (e.g., "smoking" negative for mortality). Signal: clinical/domain expert review rejects model. Check: feature grouping, data preprocessing, or over-regularization (λ₁).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Sanity Check:** Run bAUC-Integer on a tiny, separable dataset (e.g., XOR-like small set). Verify solution time is low (<1s) and score table matches expected signs. Confirm solver finds a feasible integer solution.
  2. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** On a medium dataset (surgery), vary θ ∈ {2,4,6} and M ∈ {1,2}. Plot testing AUC vs. time. Expected: AUC increases with θ/M (until overfitting), time increases non-linearly.
  3. **Baseline Comparison Replication:** Replicate Table 3a (surgery, M=2, θ=6). Ensure bAUC-Integer AUC > bAUC-Rounding and other baselines. If not, check: (a) random seed for train/test split, (b) MIO solver tolerance, (c) λ₁ value (paper uses 0.005).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can we design more efficient algorithms (e.g., cutting-plane methods, decomposition) to solve the bAUC maximization MILO problem for large datasets without subsampling?
  - *Basis in paper:* The conclusion states: "Future research directions include developing efficient algorithms for solving the bAUC maximization problem on large datasets. While we reduced the solution time for the optimization problem by sampling a small subset of data instances, using all data instances will further improve the predictive performance and robustness of our method."
  - *Why unresolved:* The current approach relies on subsampling 300 instances for larger datasets to keep computation times acceptable (10–112 seconds). However, subsampling may sacrifice predictive performance and robustness, especially on imbalanced data. Efficient algorithms that handle full datasets are needed to fully realize the potential of bAUC maximization.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Development and implementation of novel MIO algorithms (e.g., tailored cutting planes, column generation, or parallel decomposition) that can solve the MILO problem to optimality or near-optimality for datasets with tens of thousands of instances within reasonable time (e.g., a few minutes). Computational experiments comparing the new algorithms with the current subsampling approach on large datasets (like bank and adult) would demonstrate feasibility and performance gains.

- **Open Question 2:** Can we develop an exact MIO formulation that directly maximizes AUC (instead of bAUC) for scoring systems, and what are the computational and theoretical challenges?
  - *Basis in paper:* The conclusion says: "Another direction for future research is to design an exact solution method that directly maximizes AUC of scoring systems. Although direct maximization of AUC is known to be an extremely difficult problem, it will lead to further improvements in the predictive accuracy of scoring systems."
  - *Why unresolved:* Direct AUC maximization is non-convex and discontinuous, making it intractable for MIO. The current method uses bAUC as a concave lower bound surrogate, but it may not achieve the true AUC optimum. An exact method would avoid this surrogate but faces significant computational hurdles.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* A new MIO formulation that exactly maximizes AUC, perhaps by using creative linearization techniques for the indicator function, or a branch-and-bound approach tailored to the AUC objective. Validation would involve solving small instances to optimality and comparing the achieved AUC with that of bAUC-based solutions, showing improvement (or lack thereof) and computational trade-offs.

- **Open Question 3:** Does the use of subsampling in the bAUC maximization MILO approach degrade performance on imbalanced datasets, and how can we adapt the framework to handle class imbalance without subsampling?
  - *Basis in paper:* The results show that on the bank dataset (11.3% positive class), conventional methods outperformed bAUC-Integer and bAUC-Rounding. The authors speculate that this is due to class imbalance and the smaller training set (300 sampled instances) used for MILO. This suggests that subsampling may disproportionately hurt performance on imbalanced data.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper does not systematically investigate the impact of subsampling on imbalanced datasets. It is unclear whether the underperformance is solely due to subsampling or also due to the bAUC objective itself. Moreover, no alternative handling of imbalance (e.g., weighted bAUC, stratified sampling, or cost-sensitive formulations) is explored.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Experiments varying the sample size (e.g., using all data, or different sample sizes) on imbalanced datasets (like bank) to measure the effect on AUC. Also, incorporating class weights or balanced sampling strategies into the MILO formulation could be tested. Comparing these variants would clarify the cause of underperformance and suggest improvements.

- **Open Question 4:** What is the typical gap between bAUC and the true AUC for scoring systems obtained by maximizing bAUC, and does maximizing bAUC reliably lead to high AUC in practice?
  - *Basis in paper:* The method maximizes bAUC, a lower bound on AUC, but the paper does not report the actual AUC of the solutions relative to the bAUC value. It only reports testing AUC. Without knowing the training bAUC and its relation to test AUC, it's unclear how much opportunity for improvement exists by directly maximizing AUC.
  - *Why unresolved:* The authors claim bAUC is the tightest concave lower bound, but the tightness may vary across datasets and model sparsity constraints. If the gap is large, then direct AUC maximization might yield significantly better classifiers. The paper does not analyze this gap.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* For the trained models, compute the training bAUC value and compare it to the training AUC (empirical) and testing AUC. This would show how much bAUC underestimates AUC and whether bAUC optimization translates to high AUC. Additionally, one could compute the maximum possible AUC (or an upper bound) for scoring systems with given sparsity constraints to see how close bAUC-Integer solutions are to the theoretical optimum.

## Limitations
- Preprocessing ambiguity: exact tertile thresholds, top category selections, and missing-value handling for each dataset are unspecified, impeding exact replication.
- Experimental randomness: seeds for train/test splits, subsampling, and stepwise validation splits are not reported, introducing unreproducible variance.
- Subsampling concerns: the consistent use of 300 training instances for bAUC methods (vs. full sets for baselines) may bias results, particularly on imbalanced/large datasets like bank.
