# Multilevel Determinants of Overweight and Obesity Among U.S. Children Aged 10-17: Comparative Evaluation of Statistical and Machine Learning Approaches Using the 2021 National Survey of Children's Health

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.20303  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20303  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: No single model was uniformly superior; logistic regression and gradient boosting offered the best balance of discrimination and calibration, deep learning modestly improved recall, and persistent performance disparities across race and poverty groups were observed across all algorithms.

## Executive Summary
This study compares statistical, machine learning, and deep learning approaches to predict overweight/obesity in U.S. children aged 10–17 using the 2021 National Survey of Children’s Health (NSCH). Multilevel predictors spanning behavior, household, and neighborhood domains were evaluated. All models achieved similar predictive performance (AUC 0.66–0.79), with logistic regression and gradient boosting showing the most stable trade-off between discrimination and calibration. Deep learning models improved recall but degraded calibration. Performance gaps by race and poverty level were consistent across all algorithms, suggesting that data quality and equity issues—not algorithmic choice—are the primary constraints. Increasing model complexity provided limited gains over simpler methods.

## Method Summary
The analysis used the 2021 NSCH public-use data, filtering to 18,792 children aged 10–17 with complete information. The outcome was a binary indicator of overweight/obese versus normal weight (underweight excluded). Predictors included individual behaviors (physical activity, screen time, diet, sleep), household factors (parental weight concern, education, poverty, adverse experiences), neighborhood characteristics (support, safety, amenities), and demographics. Preprocessing entailed recoding survey-specific missing values to NaN, one-hot encoding categorical variables, standardizing continuous predictors using training statistics, and applying a single imputation method to produce a uniform completed dataset. Data were split into training, validation, and test sets (exact ratios not reported); validation guided early stopping for deep learning models. Models evaluated: logistic regression (ℓ2 regularized), random forest, gradient boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, multilayer perceptron, and TabNet. Hyperparameters reflected common configurations with no aggressive tuning. Performance on the held-out test set was assessed via AUC, accuracy, precision, recall, F1, Brier score, and stratified fairness metrics by sex, race, poverty, and metropolitan status.

## Key Results
- All models yielded AUC between 0.66 and 0.79; logistic regression (≈0.79) and gradient boosting were competitive and most stable across evaluation metrics.
- Deep learning (MLP, TabNet) increased recall and F1 relative to simpler models but suffered worse calibration (higher Brier scores).
- Performance disparities by race and poverty persisted across every algorithm, indicating structural data limitations rather than algorithm-specific bias.
- Feature importance was convergent across methods, highlighting parental weight concern, physical activity, and poverty as robust multilevel determinants.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Increased model complexity offers limited predictive gains over simpler methods for this survey-based obesity prediction task.
- **Mechanism**: The predictive signal in the NSCH data is constrained by measurement error, categorical thresholds, and unmeasured confounding, creating a performance ceiling that complex models cannot surpass. Simpler models like logistic regression achieve comparable AUC because they are less prone to overfitting noisy, high-dimensional survey data.
- **Core assumption**: The relationship between predictors and outcome is sufficiently linear or low-order interactive that flexibility from ensembles/deep learning yields diminishing returns.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Increasing model complexity offered limited gains over simpler methods"
  - [section 4.1] Table 2 shows logistic regression (AUC 0.788) matches or exceeds most complex models; learning curve (Fig 3) indicates stable performance without overfitting.
  - [corpus] Weak direct corpus support; related papers (e.g., arXiv:2512.22758) also find ML modestly improves over regression for obesity, but this paper’s comparative suite is unique.
- **Break condition**: If feature engineering introduces strong nonlinearities or interactions not captured by current predictors, or if objective clinical measures replace survey-reported BMI.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Persistent performance disparities across race and poverty groups are driven by structural data limitations, not algorithmic bias.
- **Mechanism**: Systematic under-representation, measurement inequity (e.g., culturally biased survey items), and socioeconomic confounding in the source data create subgroup-specific noise floors. All models inherit these disparities because they learn from the same biased distribution; changing the algorithm cannot resolve upstream data inequities.
- **Core assumption**: Subgroup performance variance is primarily a function of data support (sample size, feature variance) rather than model inductive biases.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Persistent performance disparities across race and poverty groups were observed across all algorithms."
  - [section 5] Tables 6–7 show race/poverty gaps persist across logistic regression, boosting, and deep learning; no model uniformly improves disadvantaged group calibration.
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; neighboring papers on housing instability (arXiv:2409.06011) also find social determinants drive outcomes, implying data structure dominates.
- **Break condition**: If re-sampling or fairness-constrained training significantly reduces gaps without degrading overall performance, algorithm choice may matter more.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Feature importance convergence across modeling approaches indicates a robust multilevel determinant structure.
- **Mechanism**: Despite different modeling assumptions (linear vs. tree-based vs. attention), variables like parental weight concern, physical activity, and poverty consistently rank highly. This convergence suggests these factors have strong, relatively stable associations with outcome across analytic frameworks, pointing to real underlying multilevel etiology (behavioral, household, community).
- **Core assumption**: Importance metrics from different models (coefficients, permutation, attention) reflect overlapping, though not identical, aspects of true predictor relevance.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Predictors consistently span behavioral, household, and neighborhood domains."
  - [section 4.2] Table 3 shows WgtConcn_21, AdultEduc_21, SC_RACE_R, PhysAct_21 appear in top-10 across logistic regression, LightGBM, TabNet.
  - [corpus] Weak; neighboring studies (arXiv:2602.09079) also identify multilevel factors but do not compare model convergence.
- **Break condition**: If SHAP/LIME analyses reveal highly discordant feature rankings between interpretable models, the apparent convergence may be an artifact of dominant linear effects.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
NSCH 2021 CSV → pandas DataFrame → Preprocessing (missing value recoding → one-hot encoding of categoricals → standardization of continuous variables) → Train/validation/test split → Model training (sklearn: LogisticRegression, RandomForest; XGBoost; LightGBM; PyTorch: MLP, TabNet) → Evaluation (AUC, precision, recall, F1, Brier score, group‑conditional metrics) → Interpretability (coefficients, permutation importance, feature masks)

**Critical path**: Preprocessing consistency → reproducible train/test split → hyperparameter stability (no aggressive tuning) → cross‑group fairness computation.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Simplicity (LR) vs. flexibility (boosting/DL): LR provides interpretable coefficients but may miss nonlinearities; DL improves recall but degrades calibration.
- Global vs. local interpretability: LR/LightGBM offer global importance; TabNet’s per‑step attention is more granular but less stable.
- Performance vs. equity: Boosting/DL improve sensitivity but do not fix race/poverty gaps; fairness requires data‑level intervention.

**Failure signatures**:
- TabNet: Very low AUC (<0.70), high Brier, unstable subgroup metrics → architecture mismatch with sparse, heterogeneous survey data.
- All models: Large calibration drop in low‑income or minority subgroups → indicates data representation/measurement issues.
- Logistic regression: Near‑zero coefficients for key behavioral variables → may indicate insufficient nonlinear capture or suppressor effects.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline replication**: Train logistic regression with ℓ2 penalty on preprocessed data; report AUC, Brier, and cross‑validated stability (Fig 3). Verify coefficient signs align with literature (e.g., physical activity negative).
2. **Model comparison**: Train RF, GradientBoosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, MLP (3 hidden layers, dropout 0.2), TabNet (n_steps=3). Compare Table 2 metrics; note which improves recall without catastrophic calibration loss.
3. **Subgroup stress test**: For each model, compute race‑ and poverty‑stratified AUC/Recall/Brier (Tables 6–7). Identify groups where all models fail (e.g., American Indian/Alaska Native, <100% FPL); flag as data‑poor strata.

## Limitations
- Unspecified methodology details: train/validation/test split ratios, hyperparameters, imputation technique, categorical encoding, and sampling weight usage.
- NSCH data constraints: parent‑reported BMI, categorical behavior coding, cross‑sectional design, and omitted variables limit causal interpretation and predictive ceiling.
- Generalizability limited to 2021 U.S. children; may not extend to clinical or non‑U.S. settings.
