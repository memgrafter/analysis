# A Deep Dive into Scaling RL for Code Generation with Synthetic Data and Curricula

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.24202
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24202
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Multi-turn synthetic problem generation increases valid yield ~4-fold and improves code RL performance across models via environment diversity and difficulty-optimized curricula.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates scaling reinforcement learning (RL) for code generation, identifying data diversity and structure as primary bottlenecks over mere volume. The authors introduce a multi-turn synthetic data pipeline where a teacher model iteratively refines problems based on student performance summaries, without teacher fine-tuning. This approach yields a fourfold increase in valid synthetic problems and naturally creates easy-medium-hard stepping stones. Augmenting real code data with such synthetic tasks consistently boosts in-domain code performance (LiveCodeBench) across three model families and often enhances out-of-domain math reasoning. Key insights include: training on medium-difficulty questions provides the best balance, reverse curricula starting from medium improve stability, and distributing the data budget across multiple RL environments (Induction, Abduction, Deduction, Fuzzing) enhances generalization by increasing task structural diversity.

## Method Summary
The method comprises two main stages: synthetic data generation and RL training. For generation, a teacher model (GPT-OSS 120B) starts from a seed code snippet and produces an initial problem. A student model (same architecture, low reasoning) attempts the problem multiple times (M=32), and the pass rate along with representative solutions is summarized and fed back to the teacher, which mutates the problem to target a desired difficulty (e.g., medium). This loop repeats up to six turns, without updating the teacher's weights. After generation, problems are filtered (pass rate ∈ [0.01, 0.97], no formatting errors, I/O diversity) and deduplicated. For RL training, the student policy is updated using asynchronous Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on a mix of real contest problems (25K or 81K) and synthetic problems (~20K). Hyperparameters include learning rates around 6×10⁻⁸, group size G=8, temperature 1.0 during training, and higher clipping (ε_low=0.2, ε_high=0.25) to prevent entropy collapse. Difficulty bins (easy, medium, hard) are defined by student pass rates, and curriculum schedules (classic, reverse, soft) control sampling weights over time.

## Key Results
- Multi-turn iterative refinement increases valid synthetic problem yield approximately fourfold compared to single-turn generation and produces natural difficulty stepping stones.
- Augmenting real code RL data with synthetic problems from diverse environments consistently improves in-domain code generation (LiveCodeBench pass@1/pass@10) across Llama3.1-8B Instruct, Qwen3-8B Base, and Qwen2.5-32B, and often enhances out-of-domain math (Math500, AIME2024).
- Training on medium-difficulty questions yields the best balance between convergence speed and final performance; reverse curricula starting from medium improve training stability. Distributing the data budget across multiple synthetic environments further boosts generalization, highlighting environment diversity as a key scaling axis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Multi-turn iterative refinement by a teacher, conditioned on student performance summaries, significantly increases the yield of valid synthetic problems and naturally produces stepping stones across difficulty levels.  
**Mechanism:** The teacher starts from a seed snippet and generates an initial problem. The student attempts the problem many times (M=8/32). The pass rate and representative solutions are summarized and fed back to the teacher, which mutates the problem to target a desired difficulty (e.g., medium if pass rate was high). This loop repeats for up to 6 turns, without updating the teacher’s weights. Each turn corrects formatting errors, improves solvability, and adjusts complexity, yielding a chain of related problems that serve as intermediate challenges.  
**Core assumption:** The teacher model can accurately interpret pass rate statistics and generate mutations that meaningfully adjust difficulty while maintaining valid problem structure.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "multi-turn synthetic data pipeline... increases valid problem yield ~4-fold and naturally produces easy-medium-hard stepping stones."  
- [section 3.3] describes the iterative generation loop, pass rate computation, and progression strategy.  
- [corpus] Includes works on iterative diverse data generation, supporting the diversity principle.  
**Break condition:** If the teacher’s in‑context reasoning fails to align mutations with the target difficulty (e.g., due to noisy pass rate from small M or model limitations), the chain may not provide useful stepping stones and yield gain diminishes.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Augmenting real code RL data with synthetic problems from diverse environments improves in‑domain performance and often out‑of‑domain generalization, because structural diversity of tasks mitigates overfitting and encourages a more robust policy.  
**Mechanism:** Synthetic problems are generated in four RL environments (Induction, Abduction, Deduction, Fuzzing), each with distinct input‑output structures and reward definitions. Seeds are drawn from random code snippets (starcoderdata) or real contest solutions, ensuring a broad distribution. Mixing these with real contest problems during RL training exposes the model to varied problem‑solving modes, preventing it from exploiting superficial patterns in any single environment.  
**Core assumption:** Skills transferred from diverse synthetic tasks generalize to target domains (code contests, math reasoning). The synthetic tasks are sufficiently high‑quality (solvable, well‑formed) to be useful.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "augmenting real code data with such synthetic problems consistently improves in‑domain code performance... and often enhances out‑of‑domain math."  
- [section 4.1.1] presents training curves showing gains.  
- [corpus] Works on synthetic problem diversity highlight that diversity improves generalization.  
**Break condition:** If synthetic generation produces a skewed distribution (e.g., too many easy problems) or low‑quality tasks, the RL signal may degrade, harming performance.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The difficulty distribution of RL problems and the curriculum schedule critically shape optimization dynamics; training on medium‑difficulty questions or using reverse curricula (from medium towards easier) yields more stable and generalizable learning than classic easy‑to‑hard or uniform sampling.  
**Mechanism:** In RL with binary rewards, easy problems yield frequent positive gradients leading to rapid entropy collapse, while hard problems provide sparse signals. A curriculum that starts with medium‑level tasks maintains exploration and provides a denser learning signal than hard tasks. Reverse scheduling (starting from medium and gradually including easier problems) prevents the policy from overfitting to easy patterns early on. The paper shows that training exclusively on medium splits balances convergence speed and final performance, and that medium‑start reverse curricula improve stability.  
**Core assumption:** Pass rate measured on the student model reliably reflects true problem difficulty; curriculum hyperparameters (transition points, sampling probabilities) can be chosen to optimize learning.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "Training on medium‑difficulty questions yields the best balance; reverse curricula starting from medium improve stability."  
- [section 4.3] compares RL on easy‑only, medium‑only, hard‑only, showing medium best.  
- [section 4.4] compares classic, reverse, and uniform curricula.  
- [corpus] Includes papers on difficulty‑aware RL discussing similar trade‑offs.  
**Break condition:** If difficulty bins are poorly defined or student pass rates are miscalibrated, the curriculum may not match actual learner capabilities, causing either stagnation (too hard) or overfitting (too easy).

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Teacher model (GPT-OSS 120B) -> generates/mutates problems; Student model (same architecture, low reasoning) -> solves problems and provides pass rates/solutions; Environments (Induction, Abduction, Deduction, Fuzzing) -> define templates and rewards; Seed corpus (real solved contest problems, starcoderdata snippets) -> inspiration for generation; Synthetic data pipeline -> orchestrates multi‑turn loops, filtering, deduplication; RL trainer (asynchronous GRPO) -> consumes real+synthetic mixes and updates student policy; Evaluation suite (LiveCodeBench, Math500, AIME2024) -> monitors performance.

### Critical path
1. Select seed snippet from corpus.
2. Teacher generates first problem (turn 1) according to environment rules.
3. Student attempts problem M times; compute pass rate and gather example solutions.
4. Teacher receives previous problem + summary, produces next mutation (turn 2+); repeat up to 6 turns.
5. After final turn, validate problem (syntax, executability, diversity) and deduplicate.
6. Add problem to synthetic dataset; combine with real data to form RL training mix.
7. RL trainer samples batches, computes GRPO updates, repeats for N steps.
8. Periodically evaluate on benchmark sets.

### Design tradeoffs
- *Multi‑turn depth:* More turns improve chain coherence but increase compute per seed; diminishing returns after ~6 turns.
- *Seed diversity vs relevance:* Random snippets give high diversity but may produce less grounded tasks; real seeds yield more relevant puzzles but narrower coverage.
- *Difficulty binning:* Narrow bins give clean splits but fewer samples; broad bins retain data but blur difficulty boundaries.
- *Curriculum schedule:* Hard transitions (abrupt switches) can cause reward shock; soft mixing maintains gradient stability but may dilute signal. Reverse curricula need careful starting difficulty to avoid early sparsity.
- *Environment count:* More environments increase structural diversity but may spread the data budget too thin, requiring more seeds per environment to maintain quality.

### Failure signatures
- *Low valid yield:* <10% of generated problems pass filtering; suggests teacher mutation ineffective or environment constraints too strict.
- *RL stagnation on medium/hard splits:* In‑domain pass@1 plateaus early while easy split dominates; indicates overfitting to easy tasks or curriculum mis‑alignment.
- *High variance across seeds:* Especially with hard‑start reverse curricula; signals exploration instability due to sparse rewards.
- *In‑domain pass@1 drop with multi‑environment:* Might indicate interference between environment‑specific dynamics; could require separate policy heads or more training steps.
- *Synthetic‑only collapse on out‑of‑domain benchmarks:* Lack of transfer suggests synthetic tasks are too narrow or not grounded; increase seed diversity or adjust difficulty distribution.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Multi‑turn vs single‑turn validation:** Quantify valid problem yield after filtering for single‑turn (independent) and multi‑turn (chain) generation; demonstrate ~4× improvement with multi‑turn.
2. **Synthetic augmentation benefit:** Train three model families (Llama3.1‑8B Instruct, Qwen3‑8B Base, Qwen2.5‑32B) on 25K real problems vs 25K real + 20K synthetic; show consistent gains in LCB pass@1/pass@10 and Math500/AIME2024 improvements.
3. **Difficulty and curriculum impact:** Compare exclusive training on easy‑only, medium‑only, hard‑only synthetic sets; then evaluate classic (easy→medium→hard), soft‑transition, and reverse (medium→easy) curricula. Report convergence speed and final scores on LCB splits to confirm medium‑difficulty preference and reverse curriculum stability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Does the integration of stepping stones (via multi-turn synthetic generation) consistently improve RL generalization across diverse curriculum schedules, and what is the optimal curriculum design to maximize their benefit?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly inquire whether stepping stones improve downstream generalization and study how curriculum interacts with them (Sec 4.2). They find marginal gains under hard curricula but not under soft/uniform, indicating dependence on scheduling.  
**Why unresolved:** The benefit of stepping stones was inconsistent; it varied with curriculum type and showed no significant improvement on many benchmarks. The underlying reasons—such as how mixing difficulties interferes with gradient updates—remain unclear.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments varying curriculum parameters (e.g., transition sharpness, exposure ratios) across multiple model families and synthetic datasets, with analysis of learning dynamics (e.g., reward distributions, solve rates per difficulty) to identify conditions where stepping stones yield stable gains.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Why do medium-difficulty problems yield the best balance for RL training, and how does this relate to reward density, generalization, and avoidance of overfitting?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors ask how difficulty influences training dynamics (Sec 1). Their results show medium-difficulty training achieves comparable or better final performance than easy or hard, with easier tasks leading to overfitting and harder ones to sparse rewards (Sec 4.3).  
**Why unresolved:** The paper presents empirical trends but does not dissect the mechanistic reasons—e.g., whether medium problems provide optimal signal-to-noise ratio, or how they shape exploration vs. exploitation. The connection to out-of-domain generalization is also not fully explained.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled ablations measuring reward density, policy entropy, and gradient magnitudes across difficulties; probing generalization to new domains with datasets that independently vary difficulty and concept coverage; possibly theoretical analysis of how difficulty distributions affect policy optimization under binary rewards.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can closing the teacher-student loop—where the teacher model is dynamically updated based on student performance—further enhance the quality of synthetic data and RL outcomes compared to the current decoupled, in-context adaptation?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The pipeline currently uses a fixed teacher (GPT-OSS 120B) that adapts in-context without gradient updates, and the authors note: “We expect further performance gains from having the teacher in the loop and learning from the mistakes of the student on-the-go” (Sec 3.3 and Discussion).  
**Why unresolved:** No experiments were conducted with an adaptive teacher; thus, the potential magnitude and nature of gains (e.g., better difficulty calibration, reduced invalid generations) remain speculative.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a variant where the teacher is fine-tuned (or prompted with aggregated student metrics) during data generation, compare the resulting synthetic datasets (e.g., validity rates, difficulty distributions) and downstream RL performance against the static teacher baseline; analyze whether the teacher learns to generate more targeted or diverse problems.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How can we explicitly control the difficulty distribution of synthetic data to prevent overfitting on easy problems and ensure a balanced coverage that supports robust learning across all difficulty levels?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors observe that synthetic datasets (without difficulty filtering) are dominated by easy questions, leading to overfitting on LCB easy splits and poorer medium/hard performance (Sec 4.1.2, Fig 7, Fig 17). They suggest explicit curation is needed but do not explore methods.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper highlights the problem but does not test difficulty-curation strategies (e.g., target pass-rate ranges, stratified sampling) or their impact on RL convergence and generalization. The interaction between dataset difficulty distribution and curriculum design is not systematically studied.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Generate synthetic datasets with controlled difficulty distributions (e.g., uniform, medium-biased, inverse difficulty) and train RL models with appropriate curricula; measure performance across difficulty splits and out-of-domain benchmarks to identify optimal distribution shapes and sampling schedules.

## Limitations
- Exact teacher model (GPT-OSS 120B) and prompt templates are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
- Filtering criteria (e.g., "formatting errors", "I/O diversity") and deduplication methods are not concretely specified.
- Asynchronous GRPO implementation details are delegated to external work, making precise training dynamics unclear.
