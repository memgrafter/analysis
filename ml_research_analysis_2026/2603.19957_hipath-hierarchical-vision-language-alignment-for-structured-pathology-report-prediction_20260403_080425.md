# HiPath: Hierarchical Vision-Language Alignment for Structured Pathology Report Prediction

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19957
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19957
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: HiPath achieves 68.9% strict slot accuracy and 74.7% clinically acceptable accuracy at 97.3% safety on 749K Chinese pathology cases, outperforming all baselines.

## Executive Summary
HiPath introduces a novel vision-language model for structured pathology report generation, addressing the gap in existing pathology VLMs that focus on flat labels or free text. By leveraging three lightweight trainable modules on frozen UNI2 and Qwen3 backbones, HiPath effectively aligns multi-image inputs with a fixed vocabulary of 304 diagnostic terms. Trained on a large dataset of 749K Chinese cases, it achieves high accuracy and safety, and demonstrates robust cross-hospital generalization. This work sets a new direction for clinically reliable automated pathology reporting.

## Method Summary
HiPath employs a hierarchical approach to process multiple pathology images (ROIs) and generate structured reports. The Hierarchical Patch Aggregator (HiPA) encodes multi-image inputs by aggregating patches at multiple scales. The Hierarchical Contrastive Learning (HiCL) module uses optimal transport to align visual features with text embeddings from a frozen LLM (Qwen3), enabling cross-modal correspondence. Finally, the Slot-based Masked Diagnosis Prediction (Slot-MDP) module predicts a set of diagnostic slots from a fixed vocabulary, ensuring structured and clinically interpretable outputs. All three modules are lightweight and trained on top of frozen pre-trained backbones, minimizing computational cost.

## Key Results
- Strict slot accuracy: 68.9%, clinically acceptable accuracy: 74.7%, safety: 97.3% on in-hospital test set.
- Outperforms all compared baselines on structured report prediction.
- Cross-hospital evaluation shows only a 3.4 percentage-point drop in strict accuracy while maintaining 97.1% safety.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
HiPath's effectiveness stems from its alignment with clinical workflow and technical design choices. The hierarchical patch aggregator captures both local details and global context across multiple ROIs, mimicking pathologist review. Optimal transport-based contrastive learning (HiCL) handles variable-sized image and text feature sets, creating a robust cross-modal alignment without requiring fine-tuning the large language backbone. Slot-based prediction restricts outputs to a clinically validated vocabulary, improving safety and interpretability. The use of frozen backbones preserves general knowledge while the lightweight adapters allow efficient specialization to pathology.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: ROI images -> UNI2 patch embeddings -> HiPA -> Image feature set -> HiCL (with term embeddings from Qwen3) -> Aligned features -> Slot-MDP -> Slot predictions

Critical path: 1) Extract patches from input ROIs. 2) Encode patches with frozen UNI2. 3) Aggregate patches hierarchically via HiPA to produce a variable-sized image feature set. 4) Obtain embeddings for the 304-term vocabulary from frozen Qwen3. 5) Apply HiCL with Sinkhorn optimal transport to align image features and term embeddings. 6) Use Slot-MDP to predict presence/absence of each slot based on aligned features.

Design tradeoffs: Lightweight modules vs. performance ceiling; frozen backbones vs. adaptability; optimal transport vs. simpler alignment; structured slots vs. free-text expressiveness; ROI-based vs. whole-slide processing.

Failure signatures: Incorrect slot predictions for rare or out-of-vocabulary terms; safety failures when nearest-neighbor assignment mismatches; performance drop on unseen staining/scanner types; hierarchical aggregation losing fine details.

First experiments: 1) Ablate HiPA by replacing with average pooling. 2) Replace Sinkhorn OT in HiCL with softmax attention. 3) Evaluate out-of-vocabulary handling by injecting synthetic rare terms and comparing nearest-neighbor vs. rejection.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Does HiPath maintain its performance and safety across a broader, multi-centre validation involving hospitals from diverse geographical regions and healthcare systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "the current evaluation covers three hospitals in one region (broader multi-centre validation is needed)" (Conclusion).
- Why unresolved: The study is limited to three hospitals in one region of China, which may not capture variability in staining protocols, scanner models, patient demographics, and reporting practices worldwide.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluate HiPath on an international multi-centre dataset spanning multiple countries and healthcare systems, reporting per-site strict accuracy, clinically acceptable accuracy, and safety rates.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can the HiPath framework be extended to whole-slide images (WSI) instead of pathologist-selected ROIs, and what is the impact on accuracy, safety, and computational cost?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "the model operates on pathologist-selected ROIs rather than whole-slide images" (Conclusion).
- Why unresolved: Using ROIs reduces computational load but assumes pathologist prior selection; deployment on WSI would remove this assumption but may require architectural adjustments and more memory.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implement HiPath on gigapixel WSI using a hierarchical patch aggregation strategy (e.g., nested transformers) and compare performance against ROI-based HiPath on the same cases, measuring both metrics and inference time.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the current nearest-neighbor assignment for terms outside the 304-term vocabulary affect clinical safety, and would an explicit rejection or unknown token improve reliability?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "cases outside the 304-term vocabulary are assigned the nearest term rather than rejected" (Conclusion).
- Why unresolved: Assigning the nearest term may produce clinically unsafe predictions for rare conditions not in the vocabulary, as safety boundaries are defined only for known terms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Introduce an out-of-vocabulary detection mechanism (e.g., confidence thresholding) and measure safety metrics on held-out cases with rare terms, comparing to baseline nearest-neighbor assignment.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does HiPath compare in accuracy, safety, and parameter efficiency to a generative VLM that fine-tunes the LLM backbone with constrained decoding over the same vocabulary?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "we do not compare against a generative baseline (e.g., a visual-adapter-augmented Qwen3 with constrained decoding over the same vocabulary). Such a comparison would require fine-tuning the LLM backbone, which substantially exceeds the 15M parameter budget of our framework; we consider this an important direction for future work" (Results/Discussion).
- Why unresolved: The work focuses on lightweight training; generative approaches may offer better language grounding but at higher computational cost, and the trade-off is not quantified.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implement a generative variant with a visual adapter and fine-tune Qwen3 on the same structured prediction task, then compare metrics while controlling for parameter count or compute budget.

### Open Question 5
- Question: Do alternative soft-assignment mechanisms (e.g., softmax attention, differentiable Hungarian matching) for cross-modal alignment outperform Sinkhorn optimal transport in the HiCL module?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "while we motivate Sinkhorn OT by its ability to handle unequal-sized sets differentiably, a systematic comparison against alternative soft-assignment strategies (e.g., softmax attention) is not included" (Results/Discussion).
- Why unresolved: The choice of OT may affect alignment quality; other methods could be more effective or efficient but are unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Replace Sinkhorn OT with alternative differentiable assignment methods in HiCL, train identical models under the same conditions, and compare retrieval metrics (R@1) and downstream slot prediction accuracy.

## Limitations
- Dataset limited to three Chinese hospitals; generalization to other populations and settings uncertain.
- Key metrics (strict accuracy, clinically acceptable accuracy, safety) are not explicitly defined, hindering reproducibility.
- No ablation studies to validate the contribution of individual modules (HiPA, HiCL, Slot-MDP).
