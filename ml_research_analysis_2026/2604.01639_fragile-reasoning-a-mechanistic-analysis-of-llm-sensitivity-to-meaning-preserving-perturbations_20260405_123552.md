# Fragile Reasoning: A Mechanistic Analysis of LLM Sensitivity to Meaning-Preserving Perturbations
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.01639
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01639
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: LLMs (7B–8B) exhibit 28.8%–45.1% answer-flip rates on GSM8K when faced with meaning-preserving perturbations, with number format changes being more disruptive than name substitutions.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why large language models (LLMs) often produce incorrect answers even when the input question is semantically unchanged—only rephrased or with irrelevant details altered. The authors evaluate three 7B–8B instruction-tuned models (Mistral-7B, Llama-3-8B, Qwen2.5-7B) on a dataset of 677 paired GSM8K math problems, where each pair consists of an original and a perturbed version (via name substitution or number format paraphrasing). They find substantial answer-flip rates across all models, revealing a fragility in mathematical reasoning. To understand the internal causes, they introduce the Mechanistic Perturbation Diagnostics (MPD) framework, which combines interpretability tools—logit lens, activation patching, component ablation—and a new metric, the Cascading Amplification Index (CAI), to trace when and how computation diverges. The analysis uncovers three architecture-specific failure modes (localized, distributed, entangled) that strongly predict how repairable the errors are via targeted interventions.

## Method Summary
The study first constructs a perturbation dataset by applying two transformation rules to GSM8K: (1) name substitution replaces characters with random alternatives; (2) number format paraphrasing converts expressions like "$5" to "5 dollars". For each model, paired greedy decoding yields original and perturbed answers; a "flip" occurs when the original is correct but the perturbed is not. The core diagnostic pipeline, MPD, is then applied to flipped samples. Logit lens identifies the earliest layer where top-1 predictions diverge. Activation patching replaces hidden states at divergence positions with clean (correct) activations to test recoverability per layer. Component ablation zeros out either the attention or MLP sublayer output to attribute failure to specific mechanisms. CAI quantifies whether layer-wise Jensen-Shannon divergence between original and perturbed logit lens distributions amplifies (high CAI) or self-corrects (low CAI). Finally, targeted repair experiments—steering vectors and layer fine-tuning—attempt to correct failures based on the failure mode taxonomy.

## Key Results
- All models show high flip rates: Mistral-7B (45.1%), Llama-3-8B (33.5%), Qwen2.5-7B (28.8%); number format perturbations cause more flips than name substitutions.
- Failure modes differ by architecture: Llama-3 is *localized* (71.7% patching recovery), Mistral is *distributed* (5.0% recovery, attention-dominant), Qwen is *entangled* (0% recovery, MLP-dominant).
- CAI predicts answer flips better than first divergence layer for Mistral (AUC 0.679 vs. 0.579) and Llama-3 (0.593 vs. 0.540). Repair recovers 12.2% of localized failures (Llama-3) but only 5–7% of distributed/entangled ones.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper explains LLM sensitivity through three key mechanisms:

1. **MPD framework triangulation**: By combining logit lens (first divergence), activation patching (recoverability), and component ablation (attention/MLP attribution), the framework diagnoses whether a failure is localized, distributed, or entangled. This works because perturbations cause measurable divergences in hidden states or logits that correlate with answer flips. The approach assumes failures manifest along the critical computation path. *Break condition*: If all layers/ components show near-zero patching recovery (as in Qwen), the failure may be irreparable.

2. **Cascading Amplification Index (CAI)**: CAI measures how Jensen-Shannon divergence between original and perturbed logit lens distributions evolves across layers after the first divergence. High CAI (near 1) indicates error amplification; low CAI (near 0) indicates self-correction. This captures non-monotonic propagation, improving failure prediction over merely the first divergence layer. *Break condition*: For models where divergence is transient or occurs very late (e.g., Qwen), first divergence may be more predictive.

3. **Architecture-specific failure modes**: The taxonomy arises from patterns in patching recovery and ablation: Localized (high recovery, no sublayer bias) suggests modular reasoning with a narrow bottleneck; Distributed (very low recovery, attention-dominant) points to widespread but attention-mediated disruption; Entangled (zero recovery, MLP-dominant) indicates deep feed-forward entanglement. These profiles predict repair difficulty: localized failures respond to targeted interventions; others do not.

## Foundational Learning
Key concepts required to understand the paper:

- **Transformer residual stream & layer-wise computation**: Each layer's output is a residual of previous layers, enabling logit lens and activation patching. *Why needed*: These diagnostics access and manipulate hidden states at specific layers to trace divergence. *Quick check*: If you patch the residual stream at layer *l*, which subsequent layers' computations are directly affected, and why might effects propagate non-locally?

- **Attention vs. MLP sublayer roles**: Attention mixes token information; MLPs transform per-token features. Component ablation distinguishes failures due to routing errors (attention) or feature corruption (MLP). *Why needed*: This distinction underlies the failure taxonomy. *Quick check*: How might an attention-dominant failure pattern differ computationally from an MLP-dominant one, and what would each imply about the model's reasoning strategy?

- **Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD)**: A symmetric, bounded measure of distributional difference. Used in CAI to compare layer-wise logit lens projections. *Why needed*: JSD tracks divergence amplification across layers in a bounded scale, making trends interpretable. *Quick check*: Why use JSD instead of KL divergence for comparing layer-wise logit distributions in this context?

## Architecture Onboarding
Understanding the model architecture and diagnostic approach:

- **Component map**: Input tokens → Embedding → [Transformer block (LayerNorm → Attention → Residual → LayerNorm → MLP → Residual)]_L → Final LayerNorm → Unembedding → Logits.

- **Critical path**: The sequence of layers from input embedding to the final answer token position. Perturbations induce divergence detectable via logit lens; activation patching tests whether intervening at specific layers or tokens restores correct computation.

- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - *Localization vs. distribution*: Llama-3’s failures cluster in few layers (easy to patch), Mistral’s are spread (harder), reflecting architectural differences in redundancy.
  - *Component dominance*: Qwen’s MLP-dominant failures suggest stronger reliance on feed-forward feature extraction, making it sensitive to input format changes.
  - *CAI vs. first divergence*: CAI captures error cascade dynamics; a model may diverge early but self-correct (low CAI) or diverge late and amplify (high CAI).

- **Failure signatures**:
  - *Localized (Llama-3)*: High patching recovery (>50%), narrow bottleneck layers, moderate flip rate (33.5%).
  - *Distributed (Mistral)*: Very low recovery (<10%), attention-dominant ablation, highest flip rate (45.1%).
  - *Entangled (Qwen)*: Zero recovery, MLP-dominant ablation, earliest mean first divergence (17.2), lowest flip rate (28.8%).

- **First 3 experiments to replicate**:
  1. **Reproduce flip rates**: Run paired greedy decoding on 677 GSM8K problem pairs (original vs. perturbed) for each model; compute overall and per-type flip rates.
  2. **Apply logit lens**: For all pairs, compute per-layer top-1 predictions via linear projection of hidden states; record first divergence layer where predictions differ; compare distributions for flipped vs. stable samples.
  3. **Activation patching**: For 60 flipped samples per model, patch hidden states at the divergence positions with clean activations for each layer; compute layer-wise recovery rate to identify bottlenecks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability**: Do the identified failure modes persist in larger models (>10B parameters) or across other architectures (e.g., MoE)? Current evidence is limited to 7B–8B decoder-only models.
2. **Generalization**: Do the same modes and CAI predictive power hold for other reasoning domains (commonsense, logical deduction) and other perturbation types (e.g., irrelevant context, syntactic changes)? The study only examined GSM8K math reasoning with two perturbation types.
3. **Synergistic interventions**: Can combining multiple taxonomy-guided repair methods (e.g., steering vectors and layer fine-tuning) yield higher recovery rates, especially for distributed and entangled failures? Individual methods achieved only 5–12% recovery for non-localized cases.

## Limitations
- Perturbation construction details (name substitution pool, number formatting rules) are underspecified, hindering exact replication.
- The study is restricted to three 7B–8B instruction-tuned models; findings may not generalize to other scales or architectures (e.g., MoE).
- MPD relies on approximate logit lens and would benefit from direct circuit verification (e.g., sparse autoencoders).

## Confidence
- **MPD framework validity**: Medium. Triangulation successfully separates failure profiles within the studied setup, but generalizability remains unproven.
- **CAI as failure predictor**: Medium. Shows improved AUC for two models but gains are modest; risk of overfitting to GSM8K.
- **Architecture-specific taxonomy**: Medium. Clear empirical separation among similar-sized decoder-only models; may not hold for other architectures or scales.

## Next Checks
1. **Broader model sweep**: Apply the full MPD pipeline to models of varying sizes (1B–70B) and types (e.g., Mixture-of-Experts) on the same perturbation dataset to test robustness of flip rates, CAI, and the taxonomy.
2. **Cross-task CAI validation**: Replicate CAI analysis on a different reasoning benchmark (e.g., MATH, LogiQA) to assess its failure-prediction power compared to baseline uncertainty metrics.
3. **Fine-grained circuit verification**: Use automated circuit discovery (e.g., sparse autoencoders) on a stratified sample of failures to confirm the localized/distributed/entangled labels and pinpoint specific attention heads/MLPs.
