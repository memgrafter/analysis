# Fast Conformal Prediction using Conditional Interquantile Intervals

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.02769
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02769
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Introduces Conformal Interquantile Regression (CIR) and CIR+, which achieve near-CHR interval widths with >100× speedup and better adaptation to skewed distributions than CQR.

## Executive Summary
Conformal regression provides distribution-free predictive intervals with guaranteed coverage, but existing methods face trade-offs. Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR) uses only two quantiles, leading to symmetric intervals that poorly fit skewed conditional distributions. Conformal Histogram Regression (CHR) builds fine-grained response space histograms for near-optimal intervals but suffers high computational cost, especially with large calibration sets. This paper introduces Conformal Interquantile Regression (CIR) and its enhancement CIR+, which merge many interquantile intervals from a quantile regression model to efficiently construct near-minimal prediction sets. CIR+ further refines intervals via a width-based rule, yielding additional narrowness.

Theoretically, both guarantee finite-sample marginal coverage and, under unimodality and model consistency, asymptotically achieve conditional coverage with expected interval length approaching the optimum. Empirical evaluations on synthetic and real datasets demonstrate that CIR and CIR+ match CHR's interval widths while being over 100 times faster, and they consistently outperform CQR on skewed data, striking a favorable balance between accuracy and efficiency.

## Method Summary
CIR and CIR+ operate within the split conformal framework. Given a dataset split into training, calibration, and test sets, a base quantile regression model is trained on the training data to predict \(T\) equiprobable quantiles (e.g., \(T=100\)). For any input \(x\), these quantiles define \(T\) adjacent interquantile intervals. During calibration, for each calibration point \((x_i, y_i)\), the algorithm greedily merges consecutive intervals starting from the narrowest until \(y_i\) is covered. The number of intervals merged, \(k_i\), serves as the conformity score for CIR; CIR+ enriches this score with a fractional component based on the scaled width of the last included interval, yielding \(s_i = k_i-1 + \epsilon_i\). The \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of these scores across calibration points determines a threshold. For a test point \(x\), CIR outputs the union of the \(\lfloor\text{threshold}\rfloor\) or \(\lceil\text{threshold}\rceil\) narrowest intervals (according to the rule), while CIR+ uses a conditional inclusion based on the scaled length of the candidate interval relative to the fractional part. This approach avoids explicit binning of the response space and leverages the conditional density information embedded in the quantile model. Both methods are agnostic to the base quantile estimator and come with theoretical guarantees: under exchangeability, they achieve exact marginal coverage; with a consistent quantile model and unimodal conditional distributions, they asymptotically attain conditional coverage and near-optimal interval width.

## Key Results
- CIR and CIR+ achieve comparable widths to CHR (e.g., on 5000-sample synthetic data: CHR 3.43, CIR 3.76, CIR+ 3.63) while reducing calibration/prediction time by over 100× (CHR ~103 s, CIR ~0.66 s, CIR+ ~0.86 s).
- They consistently outperform CQR on skewed data, demonstrating better adaptation to asymmetric conditional distributions.
- Both methods provide guaranteed marginal coverage and, under unimodality, asymptotically achieve conditional coverage with near-optimal expected interval length.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** CIR efficiently constructs near-minimal intervals by merging interquantile intervals from narrowest upward, avoiding explicit histogram binning.
- **Mechanism:** Train a multi-output quantile regression model to predict \(T\) equiprobable quantiles, defining \(T\) interquantile intervals. For each calibration point, find the smallest number of consecutive intervals (starting from the narrowest) that cover the observed response; this count is the conformity score. The \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of these scores determines how many intervals to merge for test points.
- **Core assumption:** The quantile model provides reasonably accurate estimates so that each narrow interquantile interval has approximately uniform probability mass \(1/T\).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "CIR uses a quantile regression model to estimate many interquantile intervals, then merges consecutive intervals from the narrowest upward until covering the response." Section 3: "CIR eliminates explicit response space binning by directly estimating the conditional probability... using a multi-output quantile regression model with equiprobable quantiles."
- **Break condition:** If the quantile regression model is severely miscalibrated (e.g., crossing quantiles, poor tail estimation), the "narrowest" intervals may not correspond to high-density regions, leading to over- or under-coverage.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** CIR+ narrows intervals by discarding the last merged interval if its scaled width is below a data-driven threshold, refining the conformity score ranking.
- **Mechanism:** Beyond counting intervals (\(k\)), CIR+ adds a fractional component based on the smallest adjacent interval length when expanding coverage. This creates a finer-grained conformity score \(s = k-1 + \epsilon\), allowing more precise thresholding at rank \(r_\alpha\). During prediction, it conditionally includes or excludes the \(\lfloor s \rfloor\)-th interval based on that interval's scaled length vs. the fractional part.
- **Core assumption:** The length of the interquantile interval containing the true response is correlated with local conditional density; shorter intervals indicate higher density and thus can be optionally omitted without sacrificing coverage.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "CIR+ adds a width-based rule to possibly exclude the last merged interval, yielding narrower sets." Section 3.2: "Rather than simply increasing from \(k-1\) to \(k\)... we add a rescaled length of the newly included interval—specifically, the adjacent interquantile interval with the smaller size."
- **Break condition:** In multimodal distributions where optimal sets are non-contiguous, the greedy merging from narrowest upward may still produce suboptimal single intervals regardless of the width rule. The unimodality assumption in theory (Thm 4.2) supports this mechanism.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Both methods guarantee marginal coverage and (under consistency + unimodality) asymptotically achieve conditional coverage with near-optimal expected interval length.
- **Mechanism:** The conformity scores are exchangeable; selecting the \(r_\alpha\)-th smallest among calibration scores ensures coverage via standard conformal argument (Thm 4.1). As \(T \to \infty\) and quantiles are consistent, the merged interval approximates the shortest interval containing \((1-\alpha)\) probability mass (Eq. 2), because the greedy merge from narrowest intervals up accumulates the highest-density regions first under unimodality.
- **Core assumption:** (1) Exchangeability of data; (2) Consistency of the quantile regression model (each interval has true probability \(1/T\)); (3) Unimodality of \(P(Y|X)\) so that the shortest covering set is a single contiguous interval.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4, Theorem 4.1: "If \((X_i, Y_i)\)... are exchangeable... satisfies \(P[Y_i \in C_{\alpha}^{\text{bk}}(X_i)] \ge 1-\alpha\)." Theorem 4.2: Under i.i.d., consistency, unimodality, \(b_k \to \lceil T(1-\alpha) \rceil\) and conditional coverage holds asymptotically.
- **Break condition:** Violation of exchangeability (e.g., temporal drift) breaks finite-sample coverage. Without unimodality, the greedy contiguous merge may not yield the shortest set; the paper notes this in Remark and suggests non-unimodal extension via ranking by length (Appendix C), but theoretical guarantees for that case are not provided.

## Foundational Learning
- **Conformal prediction's split (inductive) framework and exchangeability**
  - *Why needed here:* Guarantees finite-sample marginal coverage by using a held-out calibration set to set a nonconformity score threshold. All methods (CIR, CIR+, CHR, CQR) rely on this.
  - *Quick check:* If the calibration and test data are not exchangeable, what happens to the coverage guarantee?
- **Quantile regression for estimating conditional distribution**
  - *Why needed here:* Provides the interquantile intervals that encode the estimated conditional distribution shape. Accuracy directly impacts interval efficiency.
  - *Quick check:* What is the key distinction between using many interquantile intervals (CIR) and only two (CQR) for conformalization?
- **Greedy merging of ordered intervals to approximate highest posterior density set**
  - *Why needed here:* Under unimodality, merging from the narrowest intervals upward accumulates the mode region first, yielding near-shortest covering intervals without binning the response space.
  - *Quick check:* Why does merging from narrowest intervals (rather than, say, from lowest quantile upward) help adapt to skewness?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** The pipeline consists of four components: (1) Base quantile model: any black-box regressor (e.g., neural net, random forest) trained on the training set to predict \(T\) conditional quantiles; (2) Calibration scorer: for each calibration point, iteratively merges adjacent interquantile intervals from shortest upward until the response is covered, recording either the count \(k\) (CIR) or the fractional score \(s\) (CIR+); (3) Threshold selector: computes the rank \(r_\alpha = \lceil (1-\alpha)(1+|I_{\text{cal}}|) \rceil\) and takes the \(r_\alpha\)-th smallest conformity score from calibration; (4) Prediction assembler: for a test point, starts with \(\lfloor s \rfloor\) or \(k\) intervals and, in CIR+, conditionally includes/excludes the last interval based on its scaled length relative to the fractional part.

**Critical path:** Model training → quantile evaluation on calibration/test → conformity score computation → threshold selection → interval construction.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- CIR vs CIR+: CIR+ may yield ~5-10% narrower intervals at ~30% extra calibration/prediction time (computing interval lengths), but both are ~100× faster than CHR.  
- \(T\) (number of quantiles): Larger \(T\) improves approximation to optimal intervals but increases memory/computation; diminishing returns beyond \(T \approx 100\) (experiments use \(T=100\)).

**Failure signatures:**  
- Tied conformity scores in CIR causing conservative intervals (coverage > \(1-\alpha\) on average).  
- Poor quantile model (crossing quantiles, high bias) leading to non-monotonic interquantile intervals; empirical stability with RF defaults but risk remains.  
- Extreme skewness/multimodality: May still outperform CQR but not necessarily optimal disjoint sets; the non-unimodal extension (Appendix C) is untested.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic calibration test:** With known distribution, verify that CIR/CIR+ intervals adapt to skewness (asymmetric intervals) while CQR remains symmetric; check that interval widths approach CHR's.  
2. **Computational benchmark:** Measure calibration+prediction time for CHR vs CIR/CIR+ on increasing \(|I|\); confirm O(|I|) vs O(|I| log |I|?) scaling report (Table 1 shows CHR time grows sharply with |I| while CIR/CIR+ remain ~1 s).  
3. **Coverage validation:** On multiple datasets, verify marginal coverage ~90% (theoretically guaranteed) and that conditional coverage (worst-slab) is comparable to CHR, especially under high skewness (Table 2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the theoretical guarantees of CIR and CIR+ be extended to dependent data, such as time series, while maintaining valid coverage?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states: "Our future work will focus on extending these frameworks to time series data, preserving its theoretical guarantees while expanding its applications."
- **Why unresolved:** The current theory assumes exchangeability, which fails for temporally dependent data. No modifications for dependence are proposed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive finite-sample marginal coverage bounds under mixing conditions, and validate on time series benchmarks with known dependency structures.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do CIR and CIR+ perform on multi-modal \(P_{Y|X}\), and can the methods be adapted to produce disjoint optimal intervals when needed?
- **Basis in paper:** The remark after Theorem 4.2 notes non-unimodal cases may require multiple disjoint intervals, but methods assume unimodality for optimality and all experiments use unimodal/skewed data.
- **Why unresolved:** Empirical evaluation is limited; theoretical optimality (Theorem 4.2) requires unimodality. Performance on truly multi-modal distributions is unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Test on synthetic data with controlled multi-modality, modify CIR/CIR+ to rank all possible disjoint interval sets, and compare coverage and width against oracles.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How should the number of interquantile intervals \(T\) be chosen to balance approximation error and computational cost in practice?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper fixes \(T=100\) without justification, and theory requires \(T \to \infty\) asymptotically but offers no finite-sample guidance.
- **Why unresolved:** No analysis of \(T\)'s effect on interval width or coverage error; arbitrary choice may cause inefficiency or inaccuracy.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Study varying \(T\) across datasets, derive theoretical trade-offs, and propose a data-driven selection method (e.g., validation-based tuning).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive is CIR+ to the rescaling constant \(c\) in the conformity score (Eq. 12), and can \(c\) be set adaptively?
- **Basis in paper:** Eq. (12) defines \(e_k(x)\) with an arbitrary constant \(c \in (0,1)\), but no analysis or tuning of \(c\) is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** \(c\) affects ranking of tied scores and thus interval selection; its impact on coverage and width is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirically evaluate performance across \(c\) values on diverse datasets, and analyze theoretically how \(c\) influences conformity score distribution.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on unimodal conditional distributions; performance on multimodal data lacks formal support.
- Assumption of exchangeability; vulnerability to distribution shift (e.g., temporal drift) is unexplored.
- Sensitivity to the quantile regression model's calibration: crossing quantiles or poor tail estimates can break the merging logic.

## Confidence
- **Coverage guarantees (marginal & asymptotic conditional):** High
- **Computational efficiency vs CHR:** High
- **Adaptation to skewed distributions (outperforming CQR):** Medium

- **Greedy merging approximating shortest covering intervals:** Medium
