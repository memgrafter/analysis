# Step-resolved data attribution for looped transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10097
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10097
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: SDI decomposes TracIn into per‑step influence, scaling with negligible error and massive memory savings, and uncovers latent reasoning patterns.

## Executive Summary
Looped transformers apply a shared body recurrently to perform latent reasoning, but existing attribution methods like TracIn yield only an aggregated influence score, obscuring *when* during the recurrence a training example affects the computation. This paper introduces Step‑Decomposed Influence (SDI), which extends TracIn by unrolling the recurrent computation graph to attribute influence to each loop iteration. To make SDI practical at scale, the authors implement a sketch‑during‑backpropagation algorithm using TensorSketch, which never materializes per‑example gradients. Experiments on GPT‑style models (up to 135M parameters) and algorithmic tasks show SDI scales excellently: with sketch dimension m=2048, relative SDI error is 3.9% and TracIn error 2.2%, while reducing memory use by ~1000× and adding only 2.55s overhead per checkpoint. SDI accurately recovers a four‑state finite‑state circuit in parity, reveals that harder Sudoku puzzles exert influence later in the recurrence, and uncovers geometric growth of influence in a 328M‑parameter chat model, suggesting implicit loop‑step counters. Conservation holds to 10⁻⁷ and error scales as O(1/√m).

## Method Summary
SDI builds on TracIn’s gradient‑based influence but adapts to weight‑tied recurrent transformers. The core insight (Proposition 1) is that the total gradient of the body parameters equals the sum of per‑step contributions ϕₜ, where for a linear layer ϕₜ = Σⱼ δₜ,ⱼ ⊗ aₜ,ⱼ (δₜ,ⱼ is the loss derivative w.r.t. the layer’s output at step t, aₜ,ⱼ the corresponding activation). SDI defines the step‑localized influence Iₜ(z_train, z_test) as the dot product between the total training gradient (summed over steps) and the test gradient at step t, scaled by learning rates and summed across checkpoints. This decomposition exactly recovers standard TracIn when summing over t.

To achieve scalability, the authors implement sketch‑during‑backprop: during the backward pass, for each step t, intermediate signals δₜ,ⱼ and aₜ,ⱼ are captured. For matrix parameters, TensorSketch (two independent CountSketches with FFT‑based convolution) compresses the outer product δₜ,ⱼ ⊗ aₜ,ⱼ into a vector of length m; for vector parameters, CountSketch directly sketches δₜ,ⱼ. These per‑step sketches are accumulated into buffers. After the backward pass, the total sketched gradient is obtained by summing per‑step sketches. This streaming approach never materializes full per‑example gradients, reducing memory by ~1000×. The sketching is linear and unbiased; the error in the dot product scales as O(1/√m). Conservation holds because the total gradient sketch equals the sum of per‑step sketches.

## Key Results
- SDI with sketch dimension m=2048 achieves relative error of 3.9% (SDI) and 2.2% (TracIn) while reducing memory by ~1000× and adding only 2.55s overhead per checkpoint.
- Conservation error is ~10⁻⁷ and error scales as O(1/√m), confirmed empirically.
- Empirical findings: SDI recovers a four‑state finite‑state circuit in parity tasks; harder Sudoku puzzles exert influence later in the recurrence; a 328M‑parameter chat model shows geometric growth of influence across steps, suggesting an implicit loop‑step counter.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

#### Mechanism 1: Unrolling for Per‑Step Decomposition
The claim: SDI decomposes TracIn into a τ‑length influence trajectory. Mechanism: By unrolling the recurrent computation via backpropagation through time, the total gradient with respect to body parameters is expressed as a sum of per‑step contributions ϕₜ (Proposition 1). SDIₜ is defined as the dot product of the total training gradient (summed over steps) with the test‑step gradient ϕₜ (Equation 5). Summing over all steps exactly recovers TracIn (Equation 6). Core assumption: The model body is weight‑tied and applied repeatedly, so the same function is reused and gradients separate cleanly across iterations. Evidence anchors: abstract, Section 3 (Proposition 1, Definition 1), and corpus papers on weight‑tied recurrence. Break condition: If body parameters are not weight‑tied, the decomposition loses its interpretation.

#### Mechanism 2: Scalable Sketching On‑the‑Fly
The claim: SDI achieves scalability by computing sketched per‑example gradients during backpropagation using TensorSketch, avoiding materialization of full per‑example gradients. Mechanism: During backward, for each step t, capture δₜ,ⱼ (loss derivative) and aₜ,ⱼ (activation). For matrix parameters, TensorSketch computes a sketch of the outer product δₜ,ⱼ ⊗ aₜ,ⱼ in O(d + d′ + m log m) time; for vector parameters, CountSketch is applied directly. These per‑step sketches are summed over tokens to form sketched ϕₜ. For training examples, the total sketched gradient is the sum of per‑step sketches; for test examples, per‑step sketches are retained. This streaming approach never stores full per‑example gradients. Core assumption: The per‑example gradient of a linear layer factorizes as a sum of outer products (Proposition 1). Evidence: abstract, Section 3 Algorithm 1, Section 4 results (memory reduction ~1000×, overhead ~2.55s/checkpoint). Break condition: If body operations include non‑linearities that do not yield outer‑product sums (e.g., complex attention beyond linear projections), the TensorSketch approach may need modification.

#### Mechanism 3: Unbiasedness, Conservation, and Error Scaling
The claim: The sketched SDI estimator is unbiased, conserves the total TracIn sum, and its error scales as O(1/√m) with sketch dimension m. Mechanism: CountSketch and TensorSketch are linear random embeddings that preserve inner products in expectation. Lemma 1 shows that for each step, the sketched dot product has expectation equal to the true dot product and variance bounded by (4/m² + 6/m)‖g‖₂²‖p‖₂². Summing across checkpoints preserves unbiasedness. Conservation holds because the total gradient is exactly the sum of per‑step gradients; in the sketched case, error accumulates linearly but remains unbiased. Core assumption: Sketching primitives use sufficiently independent hash functions (2‑wise for CountSketch, 4‑wise for TensorSketch). Evidence: abstract (conservation 10⁻⁷, error O(1/√m)), Section 3 Lemma 1, Section 4 Figure 4. Break condition: Weak hash independence or extreme gradient norm distributions can increase error or bias.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**
Inputs (training/test examples, checkpoints) → LoopedTransformer → BackwardHooks (capture δₜ, aₜ) → SketchingModule (CountSketch/TensorSketch) → Aggregation (sum over steps) → InfluenceAccumulator (dot products) → Outputs (SDI tensor, TracIn matrix).

**Critical Path**
1. Forward pass on a batch (train or test).
2. Backward pass: for t = 1..τ, backward hooks on each body parameter capture δₜ,ⱼ and aₜ,ⱼ; the sketching module computes per‑step sketched gradient ϕₜ for that parameter and accumulates into a per‑step buffer.
3. After processing all τ steps: sum the per‑step buffers to obtain the total sketched gradient for training examples (retain only total); for test examples, retain all per‑step buffers.
4. Repeat steps 1–3 for all checkpoints, accumulating SDI scores as ηₖ * (total_train_sketch · test_step_sketchₜ) into the SDI tensor.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- **Sketch dimension m**: Larger m reduces error (O(1/√m)) but increases memory and compute; paper uses m=2048 for ~4% error.
- **Batch size**: Larger batches amortize overhead but increase peak memory (∝ batch_size × τ × num_param_tensors × m).
- **Checkpoint count**: More checkpoints improve TracIn fidelity but increase total compute; typical usage may use only the final checkpoint.
- **Parameter selection**: Only body parameters (weight‑tied block) are decomposed; read‑in/out parameters are excluded from per‑step attribution.
- **Truncated BPTT**: If training used truncated BPTT (k < τ), full‑horizon SDI requires recomputation with full BPTT or accepts zero influence for early steps beyond k.

**Failure Signatures**
- **Zero early SDI**: Likely due to truncated BPTT (expected if analysis horizon > training truncation length).
- **Memory overflow**: Reduce batch size or sketch dimension m; consider activation recomputation.
- **High SDI error**: Increase m; verify hash function independence (2‑wise/4‑wise).
- **Slow runtime**: Ensure FFT‑based TensorSketch is optimized; use larger batch to hide FFT overhead.
- **Non‑conservation (sum SDI ≠ TracIn)**: Indicates bug in sketch accumulation or per‑step extraction; check numerical precision (paper reports ~10⁻⁷).

**First 3 Experiments**
1. **Scalability validation**: Run SDI on a looped GPT (135M parameters, τ=32) with varying sketch dimensions m. Verify O(1/√m) error scaling, ~1000× memory reduction vs. full gradients, and ~2.5s overhead per checkpoint.
2. **Mechanistic hypothesis on parity**: Train a looped transformer on the parity task with a length curriculum. Compute SDI from the training set to a length‑40 alternating pattern probe. Expect a sawtooth SDI trajectory with period 4 and peak at the readout step. Corroborate with PCA of answer‑token hidden states showing a 4‑state cycle; build a discrete‑state proxy (k‑means + lookup) and achieve ~100% accuracy on alternating sequences, including out‑of‑distribution lengths.
3. **Influence timing on Sudoku**: Train on SATNet Sudoku puzzles with randomized loop horizon τ (mean 32). Measure board accuracy as a function of test‑time loops for puzzles of varying difficulty (number of missing cells). Compute SDI energy curves for test puzzles binned by difficulty. Expect harder puzzles to maintain higher SDI energy deeper into the recurrence, mirroring their greater sensitivity to additional computation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1: Geometric growth of influence in chat models**
- **Basis**: The paper observes exponential influence increase across steps in a 328M‑parameter chat model, hypothesizing an implicit loop‑step counter.
- **Unresolved**: The underlying mechanism (e.g., positional encoding, state accumulation) is unknown, and it’s unclear whether this phenomenon is universal across architectures and tasks.
- **Evidence needed**: Systematic SDI analysis across diverse looped transformers and reasoning tasks; mechanistic probes to isolate which latent dimensions encode step count.

**Open Question 2: Difficulty‑driven influence timing in natural language**
- **Basis**: In Sudoku, harder puzzles allocate more influence to later loop steps, aligning with prior work linking example difficulty to deeper layers.
- **Unresolved**: It is unknown whether this pattern holds for natural language reasoning tasks, which are more heterogeneous.
- **Evidence needed**: Compute SDI trajectories on language benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, MATH) stratified by difficulty metrics (solution length, grade level); test if harder problems show increased late‑step influence.

**Open Question 3: Adaptation to variable loop counts and truncated BPTT**
- **Basis**: Training with randomized depths or truncated BPTT distorts step‑wise attribution; full‑horizon SDI is expensive for long recurrences.
- **Unresolved**: No method exists to align or normalize step indices across variable unroll lengths; estimators for partial unrolls are lacking.
- **Evidence needed**: Develop algorithms for canonical unrolling (e.g., maximum horizon with interleaved gradients) or variance‑reduced estimators for partial unrolls; validate on models trained with randomized depths.

**Open Question 4: Extension to RLHF and reinforcement learning**
- **Basis**: Future work proposes extending SDI to preference‑based alignment and RL with verifiable rewards.
- **Unresolved**: Current SDI is defined for supervised next‑token losses; gradient structures for ranking or RL losses differ fundamentally; step‑wise influence may need reformulation.
- **Evidence needed**: Derive step‑decomposed estimators for preference losses (DPO, IPO) or RL losses (policy gradients); apply to aligned models and check if step‑wise patterns align with qualitative failure modes (e.g., early instruction‑following vs. late refinement).

## Limitations
- Decomposition assumes weight‑tying and linear parameters; extensions to LayerNorm or non‑tied bodies are not addressed.
- Sketching implementation details (hash independence, FFT) are underspecified, potentially affecting error bounds and reproducibility.
- Validation is limited to small models (≤135M) and synthetic tasks; generalization to large language models and natural data remains open.

## Confidence
- **Decomposition (exact per‑step TracIn)**: High — rigorous under assumptions.
- **Scalability (1000× memory reduction)**: Medium — empirical on small models only.
- **Insights (step‑resolved findings)**: Medium — compelling but on limited benchmarks.
