# The Necessity of Setting Temperature in LLM-as-a-Judge

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.28304
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28304
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: Lower temperatures (e.g., 0.01) yield near-perfect consistency (~1.0) and negligible error rates (~0.00), while higher temperatures (e.g., 3.0) drastically reduce consistency (as low as 0.57) and increase error rates (up to 0.49).

## Executive Summary
This paper studies the effect of decoding temperature on LLM-as-a-judge evaluation quality. Through over 480,000 inference runs across multiple models, datasets, and prompt strategies, the authors find that temperature strongly influences consistency and error rates. Low temperatures produce near-perfect consistency and virtually no parse errors, whereas high temperatures (e.g., 3.0) cause consistency to drop to as low as 0.57 and error rates to rise to 0.49. Causal analysis confirms a direct negative effect on consistency (ATE -0.10 to -0.17) and a positive effect on errors (ATE +0.01 to +0.09), ranking temperature as a top factor. The findings underscore that temperature is a critical configuration that must be deliberately set, not left at default, in LLM judge systems.

## Method Summary
The study uses two open-weight LLMs (Qwen3-Next-80B, gemma-3-27b-it) as judges across three paradigms (pairwise comparison, single-answer grading, reference-guided grading) and two prompt styles (Direct, Chain-of-Thought). Datasets include MT-Bench Human Judgments (350 questions) and MMLU-Pro (150 questions), with GPT-5.4 serving as a proxy gold standard for MMLU-Pro. Temperature values [0.01, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0] are tested, with 10 random seeds per condition and fixed top_p=0.95, max_length=2048. Each run yields outcomes: Agreement Rate (AGR) against reference, Consistency (CON) across seeds, and Error Rate (ERR) from parse failures. The total of 480,000 inferences enables correlation, causal (binary treatment T≥1.5 vs T<1.5 via AIPW), and feature importance (SHAP) analysis. Infrastructure uses vLLM with async batching.

## Key Results
- Lower temperatures (0.01) achieve near-perfect consistency (~1.0) and negligible parse errors (~0.00).
- Higher temperatures (3.0) reduce consistency to as low as 0.57 and increase error rates to up to 0.49.
- Temperature exhibits a strong negative causal effect on consistency (ATE -0.10 to -0.17) and positive effect on error (ATE +0.01 to +0.09), and ranks among the top factors affecting judgment stability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
- **Mechanism 1 (Output stochasticity)**: Temperature rescales logits, flattening the token probability distribution. This increases variability across repeated inferences with the same input, lowering judgment consistency. Evidence: consistency drops sharply with higher temperatures; temperature is the top SHAP feature for consistency.
- **Mechanism 2 (Format adherence)**: Higher temperature encourages more exploratory, human-like generation, causing the model to deviate from strict output formats (e.g., JSON), leading to parse errors. Evidence: error rates climb dramatically at high temperatures; case studies show models produce detailed explanations that fail JSON validation.
- **Mechanism 3 (Prompt moderation)**: Chain-of-Thought prompts amplify temperature’s negative effects because longer generation sequences compound variability and error risk. Evidence: SHAP shows prompt variant is a top feature for error rate; group ATEs reveal larger temperature effects under CoT.

## Foundational Learning
- **Causal Average Treatment Effect (ATE)**: Estimates the average difference in an outcome between treated and control groups while adjusting for confounders. *Why needed:* Isolates temperature’s direct effect from other factors (model, prompt). *Quick check:* What does ATE estimate in the binary treatment (T≥1.5 vs T<1.5)?
- **LLM Decoding Temperature**: A sampling parameter that rescales logits before softmax, controlling the sharpness of the output distribution (higher T = flatter). *Why needed:* Temperature is the independent variable; its mathematical role explains output distribution changes. *Quick check:* How does T=0.01 versus T=3.0 change the softmax distribution for identical logits?
- **Evaluation Metrics for LLM Judges**: Metrics such as Agreement Rate (AGR), Consistency (CON), and Error Rate (ERR) quantify judge performance. *Why needed:* These define the outcomes studied. *Quick check:* What specific condition triggers an “error rate” penalty in the pairwise comparison paradigm?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Judge Models -> Datasets -> Inference Engine (vLLM) -> Prompt Templates -> Metrics Pipeline -> Causal Analysis (AIPW, SHAP)
- **Critical path**:
  1. Prepare datasets (sampling 500 items with reference judgments).
  2. For each combination of model, paradigm, prompt variant, temperature, seed, run inference.
  3. Parse outputs into verdicts; compute AGR, CON, ERR.
  4. Aggregate metrics and perform statistical/causal analysis (correlations, ATE, SHAP).
  5. Conduct case studies on high/low temperature samples.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Cost vs. power: 480k runs ensure statistical power but are computationally expensive; tradeoffs with number of seeds or sample size.
  - Temperature range: [0.01, 3.0] balances breadth and compute; extremes (0.0, >4.0) omitted.
  - Proxy gold standard: Using GPT-5.4 for MMLU-Pro may introduce bias if GPT models share temperature sensitivities.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Parse error surge: At T≥2.0, error rates exceed 25% for some models under CoT.
  - Consistency collapse: CON falling below 0.7 (e.g., Qwen3-Next-80B at T=3.0) indicates unreliable repeated evaluations.
  - SHAP inconsistency: Temperature’s feature importance rank varies across metrics, signaling task-dependent dominance.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Replicate Table 1 core: Run one model (e.g., gemma-3-27b-it) on pairwise comparison with direct prompt for T∈{0.01, 1.0, 3.0} (10 seeds) to verify the consistency/error trends.
  2. Parse error diagnosis: At T=3.0 with CoT, manually inspect 50 error outputs; classify failure modes (JSON syntax, missing key, extra text) and quantify distribution.
  3. ATE sanity check: On a small subset (e.g., 1000 samples), compute unadjusted mean difference in CON between low/high T and compare to the paper’s AIPW ATE to validate the causal estimator setup.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Temperature effects on judge biases**: How temperature influences specific biases (e.g., position, length) remains uninvestigated. Future work should measure bias metrics across the full temperature spectrum.
- **Generalizability to closed-source models**: Findings are limited to open-weight models; closed-source APIs (GPT-4, Claude) may behave differently due to opaque implementations.
- **Interaction with few-shot prompting**: The combined effect of temperature and few-shot exemplars on judging stability and accuracy is unknown and warrants factorial experiments.

## Limitations
- Missing prompt details for MMLU-Pro candidate generation and opaque AIPW implementation hinder full replication.
- Potential bias from GPT-5.4 as proxy judge and limited model/dataset coverage reduce generalizability.
- Error rate metric may penalize valid non-JSON outputs; temperature effects at extremes (0.0, >4.0) are unexplored.

## Confidence
- Lower temperatures yield near-perfect consistency and negligible errors: High
- Higher temperatures reduce consistency and increase errors: High
- CoT prompts amplify temperature’s negative impact: Medium
- Temperature ranks among top factors affecting judgment stability: High
- Deliberate temperature setting is non‑trivial and necessary: High
