# France or Spain or Germany or France: A Neural Account of Non-Redundant Redundant Disjunctions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23547
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23547
- Reference count: 4
- Primary result: Formally redundant disjunctions become acceptable when each repeated item is bound to a distinct meaning; large neural models (>400M parameters) replicate human behavioral patterns via context-specific token binding and induction heads.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the puzzling acceptability of formally redundant disjunctions like “France or Spain or Germany or France” in natural language. Behavioral experiments show that humans generate the repeated item at 72.3% in critical contexts where each occurrence is bound to a distinct meaning (e.g., “the philosophy program in France or the mathematics program in Spain or the physics program in Germany or France”) versus only 28.2% in control simple-list contexts. The authors test a range of pre-trained language models (from 70M to 6.9B parameters) and find that models with more than ~400M parameters replicate the human pattern, while smaller models do not.  
The neural account proposes three mechanisms: (1) Self-attention dynamically binds context-specific information to each token occurrence, creating distinct internal representations for repeated words. (2) Induction heads—specialized attention heads for pattern matching—selectively amplify the signal from context-licensed repetitions, enabling the model to copy the appropriate token. (3) This process is order-sensitive: attention and copying are strongest when the surface order of the second disjunction matches between context and completion. Causal activation patching confirms that intervening with the context-bound representation of a repeated token shifts the model’s prediction toward the associated suffix. The work links formal semantic intuitions about non-redundant redundancy to mechanistic properties of transformer language models.

## Method Summary
The study combines human behavioral data with systematic testing of pre-trained transformer language models (Pythia 70M–6.9B, GPT-2 124M–1.5B, Llama2-7B). Stimuli are sentence fragments with either a critical disjunctive structure (two disjunctions sharing a repeated entity bound to distinct modifiers) or a control simple list. Participants on Prolific completed the sentence by choosing the final entity; for models, the next-token distribution was computed and the argmax taken over the set of entity names. Experiment 2 employed activation patching: for a base sentence with ambiguous completion, the activation of a specific repeated token from a source sentence (where the context unambiguous determines a suffix) was inserted into the base model at various layers, and the resulting change in suffix probabilities measured the causal effect of the context-bound representation. Experiment 3 analyzed induction heads (the top-9 identified in Llama2-7B) by averaging their attention weights on the repeated tokens across three conditions: all-match (both disjunction orders match), second-disjunction-match (only the second matches), and control. The results reveal a clear size threshold and order-sensitive attention patterns in induction heads.

## Key Results
- Humans and large LMs (>400M parameters) produce the repeated item at ~72% in critical contexts vs ~28% in controls; smaller models (<400M) show no such distinction.
- Activation patching across middle layers causally shifts the model’s continuation toward the context-appropriate suffix (avg. +20 percentage points), confirming that context-bound token representations drive the decision.
- Induction heads attend selectively to the context-licensed repeated token, with attention strongest when the second disjunction’s order matches between context and completion; this attention pattern mirrors the behavioral ordering effect.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Language models encode context-specific meaning (e.g., “philosophy program” vs. “mathematics program”) into the internal representation of repeated lexical items like “France.”
- **Mechanism:** Through self-attention across the context sentence (S1), the model’s intermediate layer activations for each occurrence of a repeated token aggregate distinct contextual cues, creating non-identical vector representations for the same word in different positions.
- **Core assumption:** Token embeddings in autoregressive transformers are not static; they are dynamically recomputed at each layer based on the entire preceding sequence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “models learn to bind contextually relevant information to repeated lexical items.”
  - [section Experiment 2] Activation patching causally demonstrates that intervening with the context-bound representation of one “France” shifts the model’s prediction toward the associated suffix (e.g., “for physics”).
  - [corpus] Weak: neighbor papers focus on unrelated “non-redundant” applications (e.g., radiomics, clause learning); no direct corpus support for this neural binding mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If the context (S1) lacks disjunctive structure with distinct modifiers (i.e., control condition), the model fails to bind distinct meanings to repeated tokens, leading to suppression of repetition in completions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Transformer “induction heads” selectively attend to the context-bound representations of repeated tokens to enable copying when the repetition is context-licensed.
- **Mechanism:** Specific attention heads (identified via prior work) detect surface pattern matches between earlier and later tokens. They amplify the signal from contextually appropriate instances (e.g., the “France” associated with “mathematics program”) to bias the model toward copying that token.
- **Core assumption:** Induction heads are a general, reusable circuit motif in transformers that supports in-context learning and pattern repetition.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Transformer induction heads selectively attend to these context-licensed representations.”
  - [section Experiment 3] Attention pattern analysis shows induction heads strongly focus on the second disjunction’s repeated token in the all-match critical condition but not in controls.
  - [corpus] Weak: neighbors do not address induction heads or attention pattern analysis in language models.
- **Break condition:** In models below ~400M parameters, induction heads are absent or ineffectual, resulting in poor replication of the human pattern (e.g., “dumb copying” in controls).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The efficacy of induction heads is order-sensitive; attention and copying are strongest when the surface order of the second disjunction (e.g., “Germany or France”) matches exactly between context and completion.
- **Mechanism:** Induction heads perform local sequence matching. The attention weights depend on the precise token order; a permutation (e.g., “France or Germany”) reduces the match quality and thus the attention paid to the licensed representation.
- **Core assumption:** Induction heads implement a shallow, position-dependent pattern-matching algorithm rather than a fully abstract, order-invariant reasoning over meaning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “attention strongest when the second disjunction’s order matches between context and completion.”
  - [section Experiment 1b & 3] Behavioral and neural data show the second disjunction order is the most influential factor, especially in smaller models; attention patterns align with this behavioral gradient.
  - [corpus] Weak: no neighboring work identifies ordering sensitivity in disjunction repetition tasks.
- **Break condition:** Permuting the order of the second disjunction (ZX → XZ) dramatically reduces repetition rates and induction head attention, even when the overall logical structure is preserved.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Transformer self-attention as context aggregator**
  - *Why needed here:* Understanding how a token’s representation evolves across layers is essential to grasp contextual binding. The model must integrate earlier disjuncts into later ones.
  - *Quick check question:* How does the query-key-value mechanism allow a token’s representation at layer *l* to depend on all previous tokens?
- **Concept: Induction heads (pattern-matching attention heads)**
  - *Why needed here:* They are the proposed workhorse for implementing the copy/suppress decision. Recognizing their signature behavior (previous token → current token) is key to interpreting the attention analyses.
  - *Quick check question:* What experimental signature (e.g., attention pattern, causal effect) identifies an induction head, and why is it relevant to the completion task?
- **Concept: Causal mediation analysis via activation patching**
  - *Why needed here:* This method tests whether a representation *causally* contributes to an output, moving beyond correlation. It’s used to verify that context-bound representations drive the repetition decision.
  - *Quick check question:* In a patching experiment, what does it mean if patching a “France” activation from a *for math* context increases the probability of the *for math* suffix on a different input?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input tokens → token embeddings → multiple transformer blocks (each: layer norm, self-attention, MLP) → output logits. Key components for this phenomenon: attention heads in middle layers (especially induction heads), residual stream representations of disjunct tokens.
- **Critical path:** 1) S1 tokens are processed; context-bound representations for “France” (in first and second disjunctions) are formed. 2) When processing the final “France” in S2, induction heads attend back to the earlier “France” tokens in S1. 3) The attention pattern weights the context-bound representation of the *matching-order* “France,” biasing the output layer to repeat it.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Model scale (~400M+ parameters) seems necessary for robust induction heads; single-token entity names (vs. multi-token) are critical for clean induction; context length must encompass both disjunctions.
- **Failure signatures:** 
  - Small models: high repetition in control conditions (lack of suppression), low repetition in critical conditions (failure to bind/copy).
  - Ordering effects: disproportionate drop in performance when second disjunction order mismatches, indicating brittle pattern matching.
  - No contextual differentiation: activation patching shows no causal effect between context and completion representations.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Behavioral replication:** Test human vs. model generation rates on critical (structured disjunction) vs. control (simple list) stimuli to establish the core effect.
  2. **Causal binding test:** Use activation patching to show that the representation of a repeated token in S1 causally influences the model’s prediction for the corresponding contextual suffix in S2.
  3. **Induction head analysis:** Identify known induction heads; measure their attention weights on repeated tokens across order-manipulated conditions to link neural activity to behavioral ordering effects.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do language model representations of repeated disjuncts encode something specifically analogous to Mandelkern's formal "possibility conjuncts" (i.e., are the contextual distinctions modal in nature), or is the differentiation merely general contextual binding?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "It remains to be shown whether the LM representations encode something more specifically analogous to Mandelkern’s possibility conjuncts: not just that 'France' carries different contextual information in different positions, but that this information has the character of possibility relative to a contextually determined domain."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper establishes that context is bound into token representations but does not test whether this binding has the specific modal, possibility-oriented structure posited by the formal semantic analysis. The neural mechanism could be a more general contextual differencing.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A targeted analysis probing LM representations (e.g., via linear probes or controlled generation tasks) to see if the contextual information attached to the repeated noun correlates with or activates representations of possibility/modality distinct from other contextual features.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Do humans genuinely lack ordering sensitivity for non-redundant disjunctions, or was the null effect in the Prolific study due to noise and insufficient power?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "While it is possible that humans genuinely do not show ordering effects, we also think it plausible that Prolific participants were working quickly and that the data is noisy... lab-based studies with more controlled conditions may well yield ordering effects."
- **Why unresolved:** The human data showed a numerical but non-significant trend opposite to LMs (better performance with first disjunction mismatch). The authors suspect the online crowdsourcing setup was underpowered/noisy, leaving the genuine human sensitivity to surface order an open empirical question.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A well-powered, pre-registered laboratory experiment using the same stimuli with careful response time control and potentially different response modalities (e.g., forced-choice, eye-tracking) to detect subtle ordering preferences.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is the neural mechanism of context-bound repeated items and induction head deployment generalizable beyond disjunction to other linguistic constructions requiring context-sensitive repetition (e.g., anaphora, ellipsis, repeated adjectives)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "We do not claim that disjunction is the only construction that can support this kind of binding... we hypothesize that the underlying neural mechanism is more general: repeated lexical items become contextually bound, and induction heads can exploit surface pattern match to copy when that repetition is licensed by the context."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper presents disjunction as a "particularly neat case" and a "clean testbed," but the proposed mechanism is claimed to be more general. This hypothesis is not directly tested on other constructions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the core experimental and mechanistic (activation patching, induction head analysis) paradigm on a variety of constructions where repetition is licensed or suppressed by context (e.g., "She adopted a dog and then a cat, and finally the dog again" vs. a non-licensed control).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What specific architectural or training factors cause the ~400M parameter threshold for reliable performance on this task, and what does the failure mode of sub-400M models look like mechanistically?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The results show a clear scaling trend where models under ~400M parameters (e.g., Pythia-70M, GPT-2 small) perform poorly on the critical vs. control distinction and show different ordering sensitivity patterns. The paper notes the scaling boundary but does not investigate the underlying cause.
- **Why unresolved:** The correlation with scale is established, but the paper does not analyze *why* smaller models fail. Possible reasons include insufficient capacity for contextual binding, underdeveloped induction heads, or poor disjunction representation. The specific deficit is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comparative mechanistic study across model sizes (e.g., analyzing induction head emergence, context-token representation quality, or circuit-level differences) to pinpoint which components or representational properties change at the threshold and how those changes causally affect the task.

## Limitations
- Original stimulus items are unavailable; newly generated items may introduce variability and alter effect sizes.
- Identification of induction heads relies on an external source (Feucht et al., 2025), which hinders faithful reproduction.
- The ~400M parameter cutoff for reliable performance is based on a limited set of models; its generality across architectures is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Behavioral replication** (critical > control, ordering effects in models): High
- **Contextual binding via activation patching**: Medium
- **Induction head involvement**: Low–Medium
- **Order-sensitivity mechanism**: Medium
