# Optimizing Language Models for Crosslingual Knowledge Consistency
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04678
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04678
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** DCO improves crosslingual consistency (RankC) by up to +13.1% on MMMLU while preserving or improving accuracy, especially in non‑English languages.

## Executive Summary
Multilingual large language models often produce inconsistent answers to equivalent questions across languages, undermining reliability. This paper introduces Direct Consistency Optimization (DCO), a reinforcement learning framework that aligns model response distributions across languages without requiring explicit reward models or human preference data. DCO uses a structured reward derived from the model’s own likelihoods of answering translated versions of prompts, encouraging consistent preference rankings. Experiments on nine multilingual LLMs and three datasets covering 26 languages show that DCO significantly improves crosslingual consistency (RankC) by up to +13.1% on MMMLU compared to baselines. The method maintains or improves accuracy, particularly for non‑English languages, demonstrates strong out‑of‑domain generalization, and allows controllable bilingual alignment via directional hyperparameters. DCO also complements supervised approaches like DPO when gold labels are available, offering a robust and efficient solution for multilingual knowledge consistency.

## Method Summary
DCO targets crosslingual knowledge inconsistency, where the same query receives different answers in different languages. The objective is to maximize crosslingual consistency measured by RankC (overlap of top‑ranked answer candidates) while preserving accuracy. DCO operates in the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) paradigm but avoids explicit reward modeling and online sampling. It defines a reward \(r_{\text{ALIGN}}\) for a prompt‑answer pair \((x,y)\) in language \(L_1\) as \(\gamma_1 \log \pi_{\text{REF}}(\tau^2(y) \mid \tau^2(x))\), where \(\tau^2\) translates to \(L_2\); symmetrically for \(L_2\). Here \(\pi_{\text{REF}}\) is the reference model and \(\gamma_1\gamma_2=\beta^2\) ensures consistency. The optimal policy under this reward takes a product‑of‑experts form, coupling the distributions in both languages. DCO implements this via a DPO‑inspired loss: given parallel prompt‑response pairs \((x_1,y_1)\leftrightarrow(x_2,y_2)\), a reward estimator \(b_\theta\) is trained so that the difference \(b_\theta(x_1,y^w_1)-b_\theta(x_1,y^l_1)\) matches the translation‑based difference from the other language. Minimizing this pairwise absolute loss recovers the desired reward structure. Training uses AdamW with \(\beta=1\), default \(\gamma_1=\gamma_2=1\), and requires a dataset of parallel pairs across languages. Evaluation on MMMLU (14 languages), XCSQA (16 languages), and BMLAMA (17 languages) confirms large RankC gains over SFT, CALM, and DPO (without gold preferences), with improved accuracy in low‑resource languages.

## Key Results
- DCO improves crosslingual consistency (RankC) by up to +13.1% on MMMLU compared to baselines.
- Accuracy is preserved or improved, particularly for non‑English languages.
- DCO shows strong out‑of‑domain generalization and effective bilingual alignment with controllable direction hyperparameters; it also complements supervised DPO when gold labels are available.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A reward function based on translation likelihoods promotes crosslingual consistency.
- **Mechanism:** For a prompt in language \(L_1\), the reward is the log‑likelihood that the base model would assign to the same answer when the prompt and answer are translated into \(L_2\) (and vice versa). This encourages the model to rank candidate answers similarly regardless of language.
- **Core assumption:** Consistent knowledge manifests as consistent preference rankings across languages, even if absolute likelihoods differ.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "proposes a reinforcement learning framework with a structured reward derived directly from the model's own likelihoods"
  - [section 4.2] Formal reward definition in Eq. (8), using \(\pi_{\text{REF}}(\tau^2(y) \mid \tau^2(x))\) for \(L_1\).
  - [corpus] Weak: Corpus papers study crosslingual inconsistency but do not evaluate this specific reward design.
- **Break condition:** Does not guarantee exact probability matching; if translation quality is poor, the reward signal becomes noisy.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The optimal policy under this reward takes a product‑of‑experts form that mathematically enforces consistency under a hyperparameter constraint.
- **Mechanism:** The policy \(\pi^\star(y|x)\) is proportional to \(\pi_{\text{REF}}(y|x) \cdot \pi_{\text{REF}}(\tau_j(y) \mid \tau_j(x))^{\gamma_j/\beta}\), coupling the distributions.
- **Core assumption:** The weighted product of likelihoods from two languages will have aligned rankings if the weights satisfy \(\gamma_1\gamma_2 = \beta^2\).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.2] Derivation to Eq. (9a,9b) and Lemma 1 proving consistency when \(\gamma_1\gamma_2 = \beta^2\).
  - [section 4.2] Generalization to N languages with matrix \(\Gamma\) and rank condition.
  - [corpus] Weak: Related work investigates factors behind inconsistency but not this algebraic solution.
- **Break condition:** If \(\gamma_1\gamma_2 \neq \beta^2\), the optimal policy is not theoretically guaranteed to be consistent.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Direct Consistency Optimization (DCO) avoids explicit reward modeling and online sampling by adapting a DPO‑style loss to the consistency objective.
- **Mechanism:** Given parallel prompt‑response pairs \((x_1,y_1)\sim(x_2,y_2)\), it trains a reward estimator \(b_\theta\) such that the difference \(b_\theta(x_1,y^w_1)-b_\theta(x_1,y^l_1)\) matches the difference of translation log‑likelihoods from the other language. Minimizing this loss recovers a reward equivalent to \(r_{\text{ALIGN}}\).
- **Core assumption:** Randomly paired responses within parallel pairs provide a sufficient signal to align the Bradley–Terry model with the translation‑based reward differences.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.3] Loss function in Eq. (10) and Lemma 2 showing \(b_\theta\) converges to structure in Eq. (11).
  - [abstract] "DCO, a DPO‑inspired method that requires no explicit reward model"
  - [corpus] Weak: No direct evidence; corpus contains DPO adaptations for other tasks but not this exact formulation.
- **Break condition:** Requires a dataset of parallel prompt‑response pairs; performance degrades if such aligned data is scarce or translations are low‑quality.

## Foundational Learning
### Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
- **Why needed:** DCO builds on the RLHF framework (maximizing reward under KL constraint) and directly adapts the DPO variant, which bypasses reward modeling. Understanding the standard RLHF pipeline and DPO’s reparameterization is essential to see how DCO modifies the reward shape.
- **Quick check:** How does DPO transform the constrained RL objective into a pairwise loss without training a separate reward model?

### Crosslingual Knowledge Consistency Metrics (RankC)
- **Why needed:** The paper’s core goal is to improve RankC, which measures overlap in top‑ranked candidate answers across languages. Designing a reward to optimize this metric requires understanding what RankC captures (ordered agreement) versus simple accuracy.
- **Quick check:** Why is RankC preferred over accuracy to evaluate crosslingual consistency, and how does it weight top‑ranked candidates?

### Product‑of‑Experts Models
- **Why needed:** The optimal policy \(\pi^\star\) is derived as a product of the base model’s likelihood in the source language and the likelihood for the translated version from the other language. This ensemble mechanism is central to the theoretical guarantee.
- **Quick check:** In the product \(\pi_{\text{REF}}(y|x) \cdot \pi_{\text{REF}}(\tau_j(y) \mid \tau_j(x))^{\gamma_j/\beta}\), what role does each expert play, and how do the exponents affect the final distribution’s consistency?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Parallel data \(\rightarrow\) base model \(\pi_{\text{REF}}\) computes log‑probabilities \(\rightarrow\) translation functions \(\tau\) derive parallel targets \(\rightarrow\) DCO loss calculator aligns reward differences \(\rightarrow\) policy \(\pi_\theta\) updates; hyperparameters (\(\beta,\gamma\)) control the KL‑constraint and alignment strength.

### Critical path
1. Prepare parallel dataset \(D_\parallel\): tuples \((x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2)\) where \((x_1,y_1)\leftrightarrow(x_2,\tau^2(y_1))\) are translations.
2. For each batch, compute base model log‑probabilities for all candidates in both languages.
3. Randomly pair answers within each parallel pair to form \((y^w, y^l)\).
4. Compute \(d_\theta(x,y^w)-d_\theta(x,y^l)\) where \(d_\theta = \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{REF}}(y|x)}\).
5. Compute target differences using translation log‑likelihoods scaled by \(\gamma\).
6. Minimize the sum of absolute differences (Eq. 10) with respect to \(\theta\).
7. Evaluate RankC and accuracy on held‑out parallel test sets.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **γ ratio vs. fidelity:** Increasing \(\gamma\) for one language anchors it to its base distribution, allowing the other to adapt more. This trades stability in the high‑resource language for improvement in the low‑resource one.
- **Parallel data quality:** DCO assumes clean translational equivalence; noise in \(\tau\) or parallel pairs misleads the reward signal.
- **Computational cost:** DCO avoids online sampling but requires forward passes for multiple languages per batch, roughly doubling compute versus monolingual fine‑tuning.

### Failure Signatures
- **Accuracy collapse in high‑resource language:** Often when its \(\gamma\) is too small relative to the other language, causing excessive deviation from \(\pi_{\text{REF}}\).
- **No RankC gain:** If \(\gamma_1\gamma_2 \neq \beta^2\) (e.g., implementation bug) or translation quality is very poor.
- **Overfitting to training domain:** If \(D_\parallel\) is narrow, out‑of‑domain RankC may not improve; monitor validation splits.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Bilateral sanity check:** Align English–Spanish on a small MMMLU subset with default \(\gamma_1=\gamma_2=1\), \(\beta=1\). Verify RankC improves (>2%) and both languages’ accuracy do not drop drastically.
2. **Baseline comparison:** Run DCO, DPO (using gold labels as preferences), and SFT on the same multilingual training set. Confirm DCO achieves comparable RankC to DPO without using gold labels, and surpasses SFT/CALM.
3. **Direction control test:** For a low‑resource language (e.g., Swahili), vary \((\gamma_1,\gamma_2) \in \{(10,0.1),(1,1),(0.1,10)\}\). Expect EN‑stable (10,0.1) to yield smallest EN accuracy drop and largest SW gain; SW‑stable (0.1,10) to harm EN accuracy. ## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can DCO's structured reward be generalized to enforce consistency beyond crosslingual knowledge, such as across paraphrases or modalities?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states the training objective "can naturally be extended to other forms of consistency" (e.g., paraphrastic or multimodal), but no experiments validate this.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper exclusively evaluates crosslingual consistency on QA datasets; the feasibility, reward design, and metrics for non‑linguistic consistency remain unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement DCO on paraphrase‑robustness benchmarks (e.g., PAWS) or multimodal tasks (e.g., text‑image consistency), define appropriate consistency metrics, and demonstrate improvements over baselines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does DCO remain effective in full on‑policy reinforcement learning for open‑ended generation, or is its benefit confined to offline, probing‑style evaluation?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6.5 describes a "pilot experiment" with online DPO on MMMLU and GSM8K, explicitly noting it is "intentionally limited" and "leaves a more comprehensive investigation for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The pilot uses small batches (32), few models, and no hyperparameter tuning; scalability, sample efficiency, and performance stability in realistic RL settings are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct large‑scale on‑policy RL experiments with diverse LLMs and open‑ended tasks (e.g., creative writing, code generation), varying batch sizes and training steps, and compare convergence, final performance, and consistency against standard RLHF methods.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How robust is DCO to imperfect or noisy parallel translations, given its reliance on accurate translational mappings \(\tau\)?
- **Basis in paper:** DCO assumes the existence of precise translational mappings (Def. 1) and uses translation‑based log‑likelihoods as reward; real‑world parallel data often contain errors or ambiguities.
- **Why unresolved:** All experiments use curated human translations (MMMLU, XCSQA, BMLAMA); no stress‑testing with machine‑translated or synthetically corrupted data is performed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Introduce controlled noise (e.g., back‑translation errors, word dropout) into parallel training pairs, measure degradation in RankC and accuracy, and analyze whether DCO exhibits resilience or requires denoising mechanisms.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What are the scalability limits of DCO for aligning many (\(N > 12\)) languages due to the \(\text{rank}(\Gamma)=1\) constraint on hyperparameters?
- **Basis in paper:** Lemma 3 provides a sufficient condition (\(\text{rank}(\Gamma)=1\)) for consistency across N languages, introducing \((N^2-N)\) hyperparameters; experiments only go up to 12 languages, and the condition may become difficult to satisfy as N grows.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not explore failure modes when the rank condition is violated with many languages; hyperparameter search space explodes quadratically, potentially making consistent alignment intractable.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically increase N (e.g., 15, 20 languages) in alignment experiments, attempt to optimize \(\gamma_{ij}\) to satisfy \(\text{rank}(\Gamma)=1\) (e.g., via grid search or gradient‑based methods), and record at which N consistency gains plateau or collapse.

## Limitations
- **Translation pipeline:** Unknown MT system and quality control; poor \(\tau\) directly corrupts reward signal.
- **Implementation details:** Prompt templates, candidate formatting, and exact training schedule (steps/batch size) are unreported.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Theoretical guarantee requires \(\gamma_1\gamma_2=\beta^2\) exactly; robustness to approximate equality is untested.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical guarantee** (\(\gamma_1\gamma_2=\beta^2\) enforces consistency): **High**
- **Empirical RankC improvements** (+13.1% on MMMLU): **Medium**
- **Accuracy preservation/improvement** in low‑resource languages: **Low**
