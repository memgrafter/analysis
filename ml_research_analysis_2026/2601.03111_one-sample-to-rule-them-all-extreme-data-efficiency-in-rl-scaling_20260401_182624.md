# One Sample to Rule Them All: Extreme Data Efficiency in RL Scaling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03111
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03111
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: A single engineered "Synthetic Prime" math sample, when used for RL training, achieves superior cross-domain reasoning performance (30.8% average) compared to comprehensive RL with over 1,000 samples (25.0%) on Qwen2.5-7B-base.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the prevailing assumption that reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models requires thousands of high-quality samples to improve reasoning. It introduces "polymath learning," where a single strategically designed math reasoning sample—especially one integrating multidisciplinary elements and strong foundational math skills—elicits broad reasoning gains across physics, chemistry, biology, and general benchmarks. Using GRPO reinforcement learning on Qwen2.5-7B-base, the authors show that an engineered synthetic sample outperforms training with large-scale sample sets. The findings suggest that sample quality, multidisciplinary design, and coverage of core skills like algebra and precalculus are more critical than data volume, proposing a paradigm shift toward sample engineering in RL-based model post-training.

## Method Summary
The method uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), an outcome-based RL algorithm with 0-1 exact-match rewards, trained on a single repeated problem-answer pair. The training sample is either a natural math problem selected via LIMR score (~0.6) or a synthetic "Synthetic Prime" sample engineered to integrate biology, chemistry, and physics while emphasizing algebra and precalculus. The batch size is 128 (all identical prompts), with 16 responses generated per prompt, a learning rate of 1e-6, and training for ~140 steps. The model’s pretrained checkpoint (Qwen2.5-7B-base) is fine-tuned without KL penalties or format rewards. Evaluation uses greedy decoding (temperature 0) on a suite of reasoning benchmarks: MATH500, AIME, MinervaMath, GPQA-Diamond, SciBench, MMLU-Pro, and SuperGPQA.

## Key Results
- The synthetic "Synthetic Prime" sample achieves a 30.8% average score across reasoning benchmarks, surpassing the 25.0% score of LIMR-selected 1k-sample comprehensive training.
- Polymath samples with high algebra/precalculus skill coverage show stronger cross-domain transfer; synthetic multidisciplinary samples outperform natural math-only samples.
- Training on a single polymath sample induces more frequent self-verification behaviors (re-evaluation, code use) compared to comprehensive learning, though the direct causal link to performance remains unclear.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Foundational Skill Activation**
A single high-quality math sample activates transferable foundational reasoning skills (algebra, precalculus) that generalize to other domains. The RL training reinforces core mathematical operations and problem-solving heuristics common across STEM fields. The sample's emphasis on algebra/precalculus acts as a "priming" signal. Core assumption: the model's pretraining already contains latent knowledge of these skills; RL surfaces and strengthens them. Evidence: polymath samples with high prevalences of these skills demonstrate stronger performance; synthetic specialist samples with multidisciplinary backgrounds span a broader skill range than math-specialized samples. Break condition: if the model lacks pretraining in these foundational skills, the single sample will not transfer.

**Mechanism 2: Multidisciplinary Integration**
Synthetic multidisciplinary samples force integration of multiple knowledge domains, creating richer internal representations that support cross-domain reasoning. By combining concepts from biology, chemistry, and physics into one problem, the model learns to switch contexts and apply diverse principles. Core assumption: the model's architecture can support such integration; the synthetic sample's complexity is within capacity. Evidence: the Synthetic Prime includes biology (DNA sequence), chemistry (hydrogen bonds, enthalpy), and physics (photons, energy) and shows particular strength in physics and chemistry. Break condition: if the sample is too complex or domain knowledge is missing, it may cause confusion or overfitting.

**Mechanism 3: Self-Verification Behavior Emergence**
The single-sample RL regime induces self-verification behaviors (re-evaluating, coding verification) that improve reasoning robustness. The model learns to incorporate internal checks during training on a complex sample, which becomes a habit applied to other problems. Core assumption: self-verification is learnable through RL with verifiable rewards. Evidence: polymath learning demonstrates more frequent self-verification than comprehensive learning; different polymath samples show distinct verification preferences by domain. Break condition: if the reward function does not incentivize verification or the sample doesn’t require it, this pattern may not emerge.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR)**
Why needed: The approach uses GRPO, which relies solely on outcome-based rewards (0-1) to train the model without a critic model, simplifying implementation and avoiding reward hacking.
Quick check: What are the advantages of outcome rewards over process rewards? They avoid reward hacking and simplify implementation (see Section 3 and Shao et al., 2024).

**Concept: Skill Identification and Salient Math Skills**
Why needed: To select or synthesize optimal samples, one must identify which math skills (algebra, precalculus, etc.) are present in a problem. This drives sample engineering and correlates with cross-domain performance.
Quick check: How do you determine if a problem requires algebra vs. geometry skills? Analyze solution steps for operations like equation solving, function manipulation, or trigonometric calculations (see Section 6.2 and Table 7).

**Concept: Cross-Domain Transfer of Reasoning**
Why needed: The core claim is that training on a math sample improves non-math domains. Understanding transfer mechanisms helps in designing samples and interpreting results.
Quick check: Why might a math sample improve biology reasoning? Both require logical deduction, quantitative analysis, and systematic problem-solving (see Section 6.1 showing gains in agronomy).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:
Pretrained base model (Qwen2.5-7B-base) -> Sample Preparation (Skill identification pipeline & Sample selection/synthesis module) -> GRPO Trainer (single-sample batch, 0-1 reward) -> Evaluation suite (MATH500, GPQA-Diamond, SciBench, MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA)

**Critical path**:
a. Acquire or synthesize a polymath sample with high algebra/precalculus skill coverage.
b. Configure GRPO: learning rate 1e-6, batch size 128, num_generations 16, max_new_tokens 2048.
c. Train for ~140 steps, monitoring mean reward; validate on multidisciplinary sets for saturation.
d. Evaluate on full suite; compare against 0-shot and comprehensive learning baselines.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Natural vs synthetic: Natural samples are authentic but may lack multidisciplinary integration; synthetic samples allow precise skill control but risk being unnatural.
- Sample selection: LIMR scores identify learnable samples, but low scores (~0.6) are needed for polymath effect; high scores lead to math overfitting.
- Reward granularity: 0-1 reward works best; adding format or KL penalties harms performance.

**Failure signatures**:
- Overfitting to math: High MATH500 but low cross-domain scores indicates sample is too domain-specific.
- No transfer: Performance similar to 0-shot across non-math benchmarks suggests sample lacks foundational skills or multidisciplinary elements.
- Training instability: Loss divergence or reward collapse may indicate too high learning rate or insufficiently informative reward.
- Incorrect batch construction: If prompts in a batch are not all identical, the experiment becomes multi-sample, invalidating the one-shot premise.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Replicate baseline: Train with a single natural math sample from each category (algebra, prealgebra, etc.) and measure cross-domain performance to confirm polymath effect and identify best natural category.
2. Synthetic sample test: Use the provided Synthetic Prime sample (Table 2) and compare against natural samples and comprehensive LIMR training; verify the 30.8% vs 25.0% gap.
3. Skill-coverage ablation: For a set of samples, count algebra/precalculus skills and correlate with average cross-domain performance to validate the skill-spectrum hypothesis (Figure 2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does effectiveness vary when scaling the number of distinct polymath samples? Does a single optimal sample consistently outperform multiple samples, or are there diminishing returns or synergistic effects?
2. Can the skill-based synthesis approach be automated and scaled to generate a diverse corpus of polymath samples with guaranteed coverage of foundational math skills?
3. What is the relationship between self-verification behaviors and reasoning performance? Do certain patterns correlate with gains in specific domains?
4. Does one-shot polymath learning remain effective for multiple-choice question formats, which often require different reasoning strategies?
5. Can non-mathematical reasoning problems (e.g., philosophy, law) serve as effective polymath samples, or is the benefit specific to math-derived skills?

## Limitations
- Exact reward matching logic (string equality vs. numeric equivalence) unspecified, risking noisy gradients.
- Batch construction ambiguous: whether the single sample is repeated to fill the batch or mixed with other data remains unclear.
- LIMR score computation details (preliminary training steps, averaging window, category selection) omitted.
- Synthetic sample construction pipeline (model versions, prompts, candidate pool size, selection criterion) not described.
- Evidence limited to Qwen2.5-7B-base; transferability to other architectures or scales is untested.
- Evaluation restricted to STEM reasoning benchmarks; broader domain coverage absent.

## Confidence
- Polymath learning (single math sample) matches/exceeds thousands-sample RL: Medium
- Foundational math skills (algebra/precalculus) are the primary transfer mechanism: Medium
- Synthetic multidisciplinary samples outperform natural ones: Low
- One-sample RL induces self-verification behavior: Low

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture replication**: Apply the same GRPO-OneSample pipeline to at least two other model families (e.g., Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B) and compare cross-domain performance to Qwen2.5-7B.
2. **Reward robustness ablation**: Train with variations of the outcome reward (numeric tolerance, multiple-choice letter normalization, partial credit) and measure changes in cross-domain gain and self-verification frequency.
3. **Skill-coverage gradient analysis**: Generate a suite of synthetic samples with systematically varied algebra/precalculus prevalence (e.g., 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%) and correlate their cross-domain scores to directly test the skill-spectrum hypothesis.
