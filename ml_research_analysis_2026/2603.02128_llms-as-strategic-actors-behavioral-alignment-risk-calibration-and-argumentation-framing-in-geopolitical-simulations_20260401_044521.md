# LLMs as Strategic Actors: Behavioral Alignment, Risk Calibration, and Argumentation Framing in Geopolitical Simulations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02128  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02128  
- **Reference count:** 25  
- **Primary result:** LLMs show moderate initial alignment with humans (micro-F1 0.25–0.54) that declines by Round 2 (0.16–0.33), escalate action severity significantly across all models, and produce predominantly cooperative justifications (Strategic Stability & De-escalation in 34–38% of cases).

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates six large language models (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, Mistral, Qwen) against human participants in four structured geopolitical simulations. Models selected actions from predefined menus and generated justifications. Results reveal a pattern of temporal divergence: alignment with human decisions was moderate in the first round but dropped sharply in the second. Concurrently, all models escalated the severity of their chosen actions, suggesting a risk recalibration driven by narrative cues. The explanations were dominated by a normative‑cooperative framing centered on strategic stability and de‑escalation, clustering the models in a cooperative region of an IR‑grounded ideological space with minimal adversarial reasoning. These findings highlight the need to understand how LLMs handle extended strategic reasoning and how their inherent biases shape simulated geopolitical behavior.

## Method Summary
The study employed a controlled geopolitical simulation platform where six large language models (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, Mistral, Qwen) and human participants played the role of national decision‑makers. In each of four scenarios, actors selected actions from predefined menus across two decision rounds. The menu items carried instructor‑assigned severity levels (Low to Extreme). Models generated free‑text justifications for their choices, which were subsequently annotated with primary and secondary frames using an IR‑grounded taxonomy via GPT‑4o. Behavioral outcomes included alignment with human choices (micro‑F1, macro‑F1), escalation in action severity, and framing distributions. Lexical and ideological space analyses complemented the quantitative assessments.

## Key Results
- **Alignment decay:** Round 1 micro‑F1 scores ranged 0.25–0.54 (Gemini highest), dropping to 0.16–0.33 in Round 2, indicating growing divergence over time.
- **Severity escalation:** All models significantly increased action severity between rounds (Mann–Whitney U, p_fdr < 0.001, effect sizes r = 0.45–0.76), with Mistral escalating most and Qwen least.
- **Cooperative framing:** Explanations were dominated by a normative‑cooperative frame; “Strategic Stability & De‑escalation” was primary in 34–38% of cases, and all models clustered in the cooperative region of an IR‑grounded ideological space.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Divergence in action alignment over simulation rounds stems from differential strategic updating as contextual complexity accumulates.  
- **Mechanism:** In early rounds, models rely on shared, high‑probability heuristics derived from training data, yielding moderate alignment with humans. As rounds progress and scenarios evolve, models apply these heuristics inconsistently to new context, amplifying idiosyncratic interpretations of geopolitical dynamics.  
- **Core assumption:** Initial round alignment reflects common default patterns; temporal divergence reveals model‑specific reasoning pathways under uncertainty.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] “Models approximate human decision patterns in base simulation rounds but diverge over time, displaying distinct behavioural profiles and strategy updates.”  
  [section 4.1] Micro‑F1 scores drop from Round 1 (0.25–0.54) to Round 2 (0.16–0.33) for all models.  
  [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., *Noise, Adaptation, and Strategy*) echo findings on fidelity decay in dynamic decision environments, but no direct replication.  
- **Break condition:** If alignment remained stable or improved in Round 2, the mechanism would be unsupported; instead, systemic decline confirms differential updating.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Systematic escalation in action severity across rounds is a risk recalibration response to perceived scenario intensification, not a random artifact.  
- **Mechanism:** Models interpret briefing updates and inter‑round results as signals of mounting crisis, prompting a shift toward higher‑severity actions as a default “hardening” strategy. This manifests as a rightward shift in severity distributions (Low→Extreme) independent of decision domain.  
- **Core assumption:** LLMs lack stable risk preferences; instead, they adjust severity based on narrative cues about escalation, leading to uniform upward pressure.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] “All models significantly escalated action severity between rounds… with Mistral showing the strongest escalation and Qwen the weakest.”  
  [section 4.2] Mann–Whitney U tests confirm significant severity increases (p_fdr < 0.001, r = 0.45–0.76) for all models.  
  [section 4.2] Kruskal–Wallis shows no domain effect; temporal effect dominates.  
- **Break condition:** No significant severity change between rounds, or escalation varying systematically by domain without temporal consistency, would refute this mechanism.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Dominance of normative‑cooperative framing (e.g., Strategic Stability & De‑escalation) arises from an IR‑grounded bias toward conflict‑avoidance in model‑generated justifications, limiting representation of adversarial reasoning.  
- **Mechanism:** Training objectives and safety fine‑tuning systematically penalize outputs that could be interpreted as endorsing aggression or power politics. When generating justifications for selected actions, models default to frames emphasizing stability, coordination, and risk mitigation—even when chosen actions are escalatory.  
- **Core assumption:** The framing taxonomy reveals a latent normative constraint: models rationalize choices through cooperative discourse regardless of action severity.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] “Explanations… exhibit a strong normative‑cooperative framing centered on stability, coordination, and risk mitigation, with limited adversarial reasoning.”  
  [section 5] Primary frame “Strategic Stability & De‑escalation” appears in 34–38% of cases; all models cluster in the normative‑cooperative region of the 2D ideological projection.  
  [corpus] Related work (e.g., *Mapping geopolitical bias*) finds similar cooperative biases in LLMs, but not specifically in constrained simulation justifications.  
- **Break condition:** Emergence of a primary adversarial or realist frame (e.g., deterrence via coercion) in a substantial fraction (>20%) of explanations would challenge the mechanism.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Geopolitical simulation structure
- **Why needed:** Understanding the experimental design—multi‑round, role‑based, menu‑constrained decisions—is essential to interpret behavioral metrics (alignment, severity) correctly.  
- **Quick check:** How does a predefined action menu with instructor‑assigned severity levels shape the measurable outcomes of escalation and alignment?

### Concept: International Relations (IR) framing taxonomy
- **Why needed:** The study’s framing analysis maps justifications to IR theories (realism, liberalism, constructivism). Without this mapping, “cooperative” vs. “strategic” distinctions lack theoretical grounding.  
- **Quick check:** Why would “Multilateralism & Cooperation” be classified as normative/cooperative, while “Strategic Stability & De‑escalation” straddles normative and strategic orientations?

### Concept: Behavioral alignment metrics in sequential decision tasks
- **Why needed:** Micro‑F1 (exact match) and macro‑F1 (category‑balanced) quantify model‑human similarity. Their divergence across rounds signals different update patterns—critical for assessing simulation fidelity.  
- **Quick check:** What does a drop in micro‑F1 but stable macro‑F1 across rounds imply about the nature of divergence (e.g., specific action mismatch vs. category‑level drift)?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Prompt → Action Selector → Explanation Generator → Annotation Pipeline → Scoring Module

### Critical path
Prompt → Action Selection → Explanation Generation → Frame Annotation → Aggregation & Comparison (against human data). Timing depends on model latency; batch processing recommended to maintain identical prompt conditions across models.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Constrained action menu vs. open‑ended action:** Menu ensures comparability but may force unnatural choices; open‑ended would allow richer strategy but breaks alignment comparability.  
- **Automated framing annotation (GPT‑4o) vs. human coding:** Scalable but introduces validator bias (human review only on subset).  
- **Single‑role simulation (model plays all actors) vs. multi‑agent interaction:** Simpler but omits inter‑actor dynamics; multi‑agent would require complex state tracking.

### Failure signatures
- **Alignment decay without escalation:** Models diverge from humans but maintain severity—suggests heuristic drift without crisis interpretation.  
- **Framing inconsistency:** High lexical variability (low TF–IDF similarity) within a model across simulations—indicates unstable justificatory logic.  
- **Domain‑agnostic severity:** No variation across Economic/Security/Political—suggests models ignore domain‑specific risk norms.  
- **Corpus mismatch:** If neighbor papers show human‑like escalation in synthetic tasks but not in real‑simulation replication, the failure is ecological validity.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Reproduce alignment trajectories:** Re‑run Round 1 and Round 2 with same six models; confirm micro‑F1 drop matches Table 3. If not, check prompt fidelity (Appendix A.0.1) and action menu parsing.  
2. **Isolate severity calibration:** Run a minimal simulation with only one round, then inject a “crisis update” narrative. Measure severity shift; should replicate escalation pattern if mechanism holds.  
3. **Frame bias stress test:** Add an adversarial actor prompt (e.g., “You are a hardline realist prioritizing power projection”). Re‑annotate explanations; expect shift toward strategic frames. Failure to shift indicates strong normative lock‑in.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **How do LLMs perform in geopolitical simulations featuring high competitive tension or explicit zero‑sum dynamics compared to the more cooperative scenarios studied here?**  
   *Basis:* The authors state: “Future work should examine more competitive or high‑uncertainty scenarios… to better understand how AI agents shape strategic reasoning.”  
   *Why unresolved:* The current study used scenarios where cooperation and de‑escalation were plausible strategies. It is unknown if models retain their normative‑cooperative framing under conditions that more strongly incentivize adversarial or power‑balancing moves.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate the study with simulations designed to create clear winner‑take‑all stakes or where adversarial actions are the dominant rational choice. Measure if action alignment, escalation patterns, and framing distributions shift toward realism/coercion.

2. **What causes the observed temporal divergence in action alignment between LLMs and humans in later simulation rounds?**  
   *Basis:* Results show alignment declines sharply from Round 1 to Round 2 for all models. The authors note this suggests “extended strategic reasoning amplifies differences.”  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper establishes the phenomenon but does not diagnose its cause. Potential drivers include: differential handling of accumulating narrative context, divergent long‑horizon planning, or model‑specific updating heuristics that deviate from human group learning.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments manipulating the presentation of prior rounds’ outcomes (e.g., summarizing vs. providing full history) and measuring the impact on round‑2 alignment. Process‑tracing analyses of chain‑of‑thought could reveal if models misinterpret prior context or apply inconsistent strategic rules.

3. **To what extent are the observed behavioral and framing patterns (cooperative bias, escalation) robust to variations in prompting, role‑playing instructions, or the structure of the action menu?**  
   *Basis:* The study uses a highly structured prompt (Appendix A.0.1) and fixed action menus. The strong normative framing may be an artifact of the prompt’s “experienced geopolitical intelligence analyst” role and the limited, often cooperative, action options provided.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper does not conduct ablation studies on prompt or action‑space design. The cooperative bias might emerge from the prompt’s implicit norms or from the absence of sufficiently aggressive menu options.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically vary prompt framing (e.g., instructing the model to “maximize national interest” vs. “promote stability”) and the severity range/availability of actions. Test if cooperative framing persists or if adversarial actions/frames increase with more coercive menu options or role instructions.

4. **Can the ideological clustering of LLMs in the normative‑cooperative region be shifted via fine‑tuning or few‑shot examples toward more realist or adversarial strategic reasoning?**  
   *Basis:* The discussion notes the shared normative orientation “may limit their ability to represent the full spectrum of geopolitical reasoning” and calls for exploring this limitation.  
   *Why unresolved:* The study evaluates models out‑of‑the‑box. It is unknown if the observed framing is a fixed, inherent bias or a malleable output that can be redirected with targeted training data or in‑context examples featuring realist/deterrence logic.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct fine‑tuning experiments on a subset of models using a dataset of historical or expert geopolitical justifications framed through realist theory. Evaluate if post‑fine‑tuned models show increased use of Strategic Stability & De‑escalation frames *as active deterrence* (vs. passive de‑escalation) and incorporate more Reputation Signaling and Power Balancing.

5. **How do model‑specific architectures, training objectives, and safety fine‑tuning regimes correlate with the distinct behavioral profiles (escalation magnitude, lexical signatures) observed?**  
   *Basis:* The results show clear model‑specific differences (e.g., Mistral’s strong escalation, Claude’s lengthy institutional focus, Qwen’s geographic tokens). However, the paper does not link these to model properties, treating them as a “black box.”  
   *Why unresolved:* The study compares models but does not analyze whether differences stem from model size, training data composition, or RLHF/rlaif processes. Understanding this is key to predicting model behavior in strategic settings.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Correlate the behavioral metrics (alignment, escalation effect size, frame distributions) with known model characteristics (parameter count, training data cutoff, presence/type of safety fine‑tuning). If model cards or internal reports are unavailable, proxy analyses using open‑source model families (e.g., varying only safety tuning) could isolate the effect of alignment procedures on strategic behavior.

## Limitations
- Small model and human sample sizes limit statistical power and generalizability.  
- Single‑agent simulation design omits inter‑agent dynamics and feedback loops.  
- Instructor‑assigned severity levels lack reported reliability metrics.  
- Automated frame labeling by GPT‑4o was not validated by human coders.
