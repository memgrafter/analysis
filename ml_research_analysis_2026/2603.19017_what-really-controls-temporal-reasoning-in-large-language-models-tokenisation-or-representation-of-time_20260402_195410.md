# What Really Controls Temporal Reasoning in Large Language Models: Tokenisation or Representation of Time?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19017
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19017
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: For high-resource languages (English, German, Chinese), temporal reasoning performance is limited by the internal geometry of time representations; for low-resource languages (Arabic, Hausa), it is bottlenecked by poor tokenisation (high date fragmentation).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the primary bottleneck for multilingual temporal reasoning in large language models (LLMs): tokenisation quality or the internal representation of time. Using a new benchmark, MultiTempBench, covering 15,000 examples across five languages and three calendar systems, the authors measure two key factors: the multilingual Date Fragmentation Ratio (mDFR) to quantify how poorly dates are tokenised, and temporal linearity (probe R²) to assess how linearly time is organised in the model's hidden states. A crossed mixed-effects regression on 20 LLMs reveals a resource-dependent split: in low-resource languages, higher mDFR (more fragmentation) strongly predicts lower accuracy, while in high-resource languages, greater temporal linearity is the stronger predictor of success.

## Method Summary
The study introduces MultiTempBench, a multilingual benchmark for date arithmetic, time zone conversion, and temporal relations across Gregorian, Hijri, and Chinese Lunar calendars. For each model and language, the authors compute the multilingual Date Fragmentation Ratio (mDFR), which measures tokenisation deviation from a semantically-preserving baseline. They also extract hidden states for dates spanning 1990–2024, average them per year, and train linear probes to predict the scalar year, month, and day, using the probe's R² as a metric of temporal linearity. Model accuracy is evaluated zero-shot with GPT-4o as a judge. The core analysis uses a crossed mixed-effects logistic regression with fixed effects for z-scored mDFR, linearity, resource level (high/low), and their three-way interaction, plus random intercepts for question and model.

## Key Results
- Accuracy varies sharply by language: Hausa (16.6%) vs. English (54.3%).
- mDFR ranges from 0.50 (German) to 0.78 (Hausa), indicating severe fragmentation in low-resource languages.
- In low-resource languages (Arabic, Hausa), higher mDFR correlates strongly with lower accuracy (e.g., Hausa date arithmetic r = -0.97).
- In high-resource languages (English, German, Chinese), higher temporal linearity (probe R²) correlates strongly with higher accuracy (e.g., English r = 0.77).
- The crossed mixed-effects regression confirms a significant three-way interaction (mDFR × linearity × resource; β = 0.016, p = 0.021). Fragmentation is a dominant negative predictor in low-resource regimes (β = -0.126, p < 0.001), while linearity is a dominant positive predictor in high-resource regimes (β = 0.087, p < 0.001).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1 (Surface Tokenization Bottleneck)
- **Claim**: For low-resource languages, poor tokenisation quality (high fragmentation) directly impedes the model’s ability to access discrete calendar components (year, month, day), leading to lower accuracy.
- **Mechanism**: Subword tokenizers (e.g., BPE) split date strings into semantically opaque sub-tokens, destroying boundaries between components. When digits, month names, and delimiters are fragmented, the model must reconstruct these units from unstable substrings, a task that fails more frequently in languages with less training data.
- **Core assumption**: The model relies on token boundaries to identify and manipulate calendar primitives; if these boundaries are inconsistent or destructive, downstream reasoning collapses.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: "in low-resource languages (Arabic, Hausa), higher mDFR (greater fragmentation) strongly predicts lower accuracy (e.g., Hausa date arithmetic r = −0.97)". Section 6.2: "Fragmentation arises from digit splitting… These effects are amplified in low-resource settings… date fragments are a major bottleneck for temporal reasoning in low-resource languages."

### Mechanism 2 (Internal Temporal Geometry Enabler)
- **Claim**: For high-resource languages, once components are accessible, performance depends on whether the model embeds time in an approximately linear, ordered subspace that supports arithmetic-like updates.
- **Mechanism**: The model’s hidden states encode temporal values (e.g., years) such that a linear probe can decode the scalar with high R², indicating an ordered 1D axis. This geometry allows the model to perform operations (e.g., addition) by moving along the direction.
- **Core assumption**: Linear decodability of temporal attributes from hidden states is necessary (or at least strongly facilitative) for correct date arithmetic and conversion; nonlinear or entangled representations hinder computation.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: "temporal linearity (probe R²) is the stronger predictor in high-resource regimes (e.g., English r = 0.77)". Section 6.3: "temporal linearity is strongly correlated with accuracy in English (r=0.77) and Chinese (r=0.75)… ordered temporal geometry is a key driver of high performance when it emerges."

### Mechanism 3 (Resource-Dependent Interaction)
- **Claim**: The dominant control mechanism shifts from fragmentation (low-resource) to linearity (high-resource) due to an interaction between language resource level and the two factors.
- **Mechanism**: Resource level moderates the effect of each factor. In low-resource regimes, sparse data prevents the model from learning to reconstruct fragmented tokens, so fragmentation dominates. In high-resource regimes, frequent exposure yields robust component recognition, making internal geometry the limiting factor.
- **Core assumption**: Language resource level (proxy: training data size) determines whether the primary failure is at the input interface (tokenization) or within the model’s representational space.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: "The regression confirms the three-way interaction (mDFR × linearity × resource) is significant (β = 0.016, p = 0.021), with fragmentation dominant in low-resource regimes (β = −0.126, p < 0.001) and linearity in high-resource regimes (β = 0.087, p < 0.001)". Section 6.4: "Most importantly, the three-way interaction… is significant… In low-resource languages… higher fragmentation strongly predicts lower accuracy… In high-resource languages… temporal linearity is instead the stronger predictor."

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Subword Tokenization & Fragmentation**
  - **Why needed**: To understand mDFR—how BPE/byte-level tokenizers split date strings into subwords, potentially breaking semantic units (e.g., “2024” → “2|0|3|4”). Fragmentation is measured by mDFR, which penalizes root splitting, delimiter loss, token count inflation, and distributional divergence.
  - **Quick check**: If a date “10 October 2024” is tokenized as `["1", "0", "October", "2", "0", "2", "4"]`, what components are fragmented and how might that hurt arithmetic?
- **Concept: Linear Probing for Geometric Representations**
  - **Why needed**: To measure temporal linearity—the degree to which temporal values (years) are organized along an approximate 1D axis in hidden states. A high probe R² means a linear readout can decode the year, suggesting an ordered geometry suitable for computation.
  - **Quick check**: What does it imply if a linear probe trained to decode the year from layer-10 hidden states achieves R² = 0.65?
- **Concept: Crossed Mixed-Effects Regression**
  - **Why needed**: The paper’s key analysis uses this model to predict per-question correctness while accounting for random variability across questions (item difficulty) and models (baseline ability). Fixed effects test mDFR, linearity, resource, and their interactions.
  - **Quick check**: Why include random intercepts for `question` and `model` in the regression? What would happen if they were omitted?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: MultiTempBench -> mDFR Calculator / Geometry Prober -> Accuracy Metric -> Crossed Mixed-Effects Regression -> Findings
- **Critical path**:
  1. Generate or obtain multilingual date data (MultiTempBench).
  2. For each model/language/format, compute mDFR by comparing model tokenization to semantic baseline.
  3. For each model/language, extract hidden states for dates (e.g., “The date is <date>”), average over sampled dates per year, train linear probes for Year/Month/Day, record R².
  4. Collect per-question model predictions (zero-shot), grade via LLM-as-judge.
  5. Fit crossed mixed-effects model: correct ~ mDFR_z * linearity_z * resource + (1|question) + (1|model).
  6. Interpret fixed-effect coefficients and interactions; plot resource-stratified correlations.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Zero-shot evaluation**: Avoids confounds from prompt engineering or fine-tuning but may understate model potential; findings generalize to basic competence, not optimized use.
  - **LLM-as-judge**: Scalable multilingual grading but introduces judge bias/noise; mitigated by human validation (87% agreement).
  - **Probe-centric analysis**: Linear probes are interpretable but may miss nonlinear structures; the paper acknowledges this limitation.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Low-resource languages (e.g., Hausa)**: High mDFR (>0.7) + low accuracy (<20%). PCA (Figure 8) shows non-linear year trajectories.
  - **High-resource languages (e.g., English)**: Low mDFR (~0.5) but accuracy varies (40-70%) correlated with probe R²; if probe R² for Year is <0.45, expect poor performance.
  - **All languages**: If a model’s tokenizer splits digits into single tokens (e.g., “2024” → “2|0|2|4”), mDFR will be high; if hidden states for consecutive years do not form a coherent path in PCA, linearity is low.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Replicate mDFR computation**: For a given model (e.g., Gemma 3 4B) and language (e.g., Arabic), tokenize all date strings from MultiTempBench, compare to semantic baseline (year|month|day), calculate F components, apply learned weights (α = [0.2015, 0.1932, 0.1053, 0.5000]). Verify that Hijri formats have higher mDFR than Gregorian.
  2. **Run temporal geometry probes**: For the same model/language, extract layer-10 hidden states for years 1990–2024 (5 random dates per year), train a linear regressor to predict year from the mean hidden state, compute R². Compare across languages; expect Hausa R² < 0.3, English R² > 0.6.
  3. **Test resource-dependent prediction**: Using the provided predictions (or generate your own for a subset), fit a logistic mixed-effects model with `mDFR`, `linearity`, `resource` (code: 1=EN/DE/ZH, 0=AR/HA), and `mDFR*linearity*resource` interaction. Check that the three-way interaction is positive and significant (β ≈ 0.016, p < 0.05), indicating that the effect of fragmentation changes with resource level when linearity is considered.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Does the resource-dependent bottleneck pattern (tokenization vs. linearity) generalize beyond the five languages and three calendar systems studied?
  - *Basis*: The authors explicitly state that their low-resource regime is represented by only two languages (Arabic and Hausa), so claims are "suggestive rather than fully general." They also note the language split is coarse and additional languages are needed to test across other typological profiles, scripts, and calendar traditions.
  - *Why unresolved*: The current benchmark covers a limited set of languages and calendars; without broader validation, it's unclear if the observed split is universal or specific to the selected languages/calendars.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Replicating the study with a larger, more diverse set of languages (especially from different script families and resource levels) and additional calendar systems (e.g., Hindu, Persian) using the same mDFR and probing methodology.
- **Open Question 2**: What is the causal direction between date tokenization quality, internal temporal geometry, and performance? Does improving tokenization directly enhance linearity and accuracy, or are they independent outcomes of training data and architecture?
  - *Basis*: The authors acknowledge their mechanistic analyses remain "correlational and probe-centric, leaving open causal questions about tokenizer design, training data, decoding dynamics, and non-linear representational structure."
  - *Why unresolved*: The crossed mixed-effects regression shows associations but cannot prove that fragmentation causes low accuracy in low-resource settings, or that linearity enables high accuracy in high-resource settings. Intervention studies are needed.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Controlled experiments where models are fine-tuned or trained with modified tokenizers (e.g., enforced date-preserving tokenization) or data augmentation targeting temporal expressions, then measuring concurrent changes in mDFR, temporal linearity, and task accuracy across resource levels.
- **Open Question 3**: What architectural or training factors lead some high-resource models to develop stronger temporal linearity (higher probe R²) in their representations?
  - *Basis*: The paper finds that temporal linearity is the strongest predictor of performance in high-resource languages, but it does not explain *why* models differ in their linearity scores (e.g., GPT-4o vs. Llama 3.1). This suggests an underlying factor related to model design or data that shapes the geometry of time.
  - *Why unresolved*: The analysis correlates linearity with accuracy but does not investigate the determinants of linearity itself. Factors like model scale, objective function, tokenizer vocabulary composition, or the frequency of temporal expressions in training data could be responsible.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: A follow-up study correlating model architectural choices (e.g., positional encoding, activation functions), pretraining data composition (temporal expression density), and tokenizer properties (e.g., merge rules affecting date tokens) with measured temporal linearity across a wide model zoo.
- **Open Question 4**: Can targeted interventions (e.g., fine-tuning, prompt engineering, or external tool use) mitigate the identified bottlenecks—especially the tokenization bottleneck in low-resource languages?
  - *Basis*: The limitations note that evaluation was in a zero-shot setting "without fine-tuning, chain-of-thought demonstrations, or external knowledge," which may understate performance. The authors also mention that linearity gaps "may be more tractable through targeted interventions."
  - *Why unresolved*: It is unknown whether the fragmentation bottleneck in Hausa/Arabic is fixable without retraining the tokenizer or if high linearity in high-resource languages can be further improved or transferred. Practically, this determines whether solutions lie in data/prompting or fundamental model changes.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Experiments applying techniques like few-shot prompting with explicit date formatting, fine-tuning on temporal tasks, incorporating date-specific adapters, or using calculators/calendar APIs, then measuring whether these reduce the impact of mDFR in low-resource settings or boost linearity-driven accuracy in high-resource settings.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability**: Findings are based on five languages (EN, DE, ZH, AR, HA); mid-resource languages (e.g., FR, ES, SW) are absent, so the claimed resource-dependent split may not hold across a continuous spectrum.
- **Causal inference**: The analysis is correlational; unmeasured confounders (model scale, pretraining data diversity) could simultaneously influence token fragmentation, internal geometry, and accuracy.
- **Metric sensitivity**: mDFR weights were learned from an undisclosed human rating process; inter-rater reliability is unknown. Linear probing might fail to capture nonlinear temporal structures that nonetheless support reasoning.

## Confidence
- **Fragmentation bottleneck in low-resource**: Medium – very strong negative correlations (e.g., Hausa r = –0.97) but limited to two languages; no experimental manipulation of tokenization.
- **Internal linearity in high-resource**: Medium – robust positive probe-accuracy link (e.g., EN r = 0.77), yet probe R² may be an incomplete proxy for computational geometry.
- **Three-way interaction**: Low – effect size is small (β = 0.016) with a borderline p-value (p = 0.021); binary resource categorization reduces statistical power and risks overfitting.

## Next Checks
1. **Tokenization intervention**: For Arabic and Hausa models, replace subword tokenization with a date-aware tokenizer that preserves year/month/day boundaries; if accuracy rises substantially, this confirms fragmentation as a causal bottleneck.
2. **Representation manipulation**: For high-resource models, fine-tune with objectives that explicitly encourage linear temporal organization (e.g., year distance regression); test whether increases in probe R² lead to higher accuracy.
3. **Expand language coverage**: Add 6–8 mid-resource languages across diverse scripts (e.g., Russian, Thai, Swahili) to assess whether the dominance of fragmentation vs. linearity changes monotonically with resource level or reveals a threshold effect.
