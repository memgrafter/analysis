# DARC: Disagreement-Aware Alignment via Risk-Constrained Decoding

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.08145
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08145
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: DARC introduces an inference-time reranking method that maximizes a KL-robust satisfaction objective to balance average reward and tail risk (disagreement), reducing proxy risk and improving risk-adjusted metrics while maintaining competitive average performance.

## Executive Summary
Large language models often suffer from brittleness in alignment due to heterogeneous human preferences. DARC addresses this by treating disagreement among annotators as a risk to be controlled during decoding. It uses a distributionally robust optimization (DRO) foundation to derive an entropic risk objective and applies simple risk controls—either capping or penalizing the entropic risk premium—to explicitly budget disagreement without retraining. By leveraging multiple annotator scores or perturbation-based proxies, DARC balances mean quality against tail risk across standard benchmarks like MT-Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0, achieving lower reward standard deviation and higher CVaR while preserving average reward. Human evaluations confirm higher satisfaction and lower cross-rater variance compared to baselines. The method also complements robust training approaches.

## Method Summary
DARC operates at inference time given a pre-trained language model and a candidate set of responses for each prompt. It estimates per-candidate reward and a disagreement proxy (either via multiple reward model scores or via input perturbations). Using a KL-robust optimization framework, it defines an objective that maximizes a lower bound on expected satisfaction under worst-case distributional shifts that increase disagreement. The optimization reduces to a tractable reranking rule: either cap the entropic risk premium or add a penalty proportional to the estimated variance. This yields a risk-constrained decoding strategy that selects responses optimizing a trade-off between mean reward and tail risk. The approach requires no model retraining and only moderate computational overhead, and its risk budget is tuned on a held‑out set.

## Key Results
- DARC reduces the standard deviation of reward scores (proxy for risk) across candidates while maintaining or slightly improving average reward on MT‑Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0.
- It improves risk‑adjusted metrics such as CVaR10% (Conditional Value at Risk at 10% tail) compared to Best‑of‑K and other inference‑time baselines.
- Gains are concentrated on prompts with high annotator disagreement, and human evaluation shows higher satisfaction and lower cross-rater variance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that preference heterogeneity can be modeled as distributional ambiguity around the observed reward estimate. By adopting a KL‑divergence ambiguity set in DRO, the worst‑case expected reward under the set has a closed‑form lower bound that depends on both the mean reward and the entropy of the candidate distribution. This leads to an objective that penalizes high dispersion (disagreement). DARC operationalizes this by estimating per‑candidate variance via multiple scorers or perturbations, then applying a soft‑min operator with a tunable risk budget to balance mean utility against tail risk. The method effectively hedges against scenarios where the true human preference distribution deviates from the observed average, thus producing outputs that are less likely to be rejected by a diverse set of annotators.

## Foundational Learning
**Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO)**
- *Why needed*: Provides a mathematically principled way to optimize under uncertainty about the true data distribution, directly addressing preference heterogeneity.
- *Quick check*: Can you derive the closed‑form worst‑case expectation for an KL‑ball around a nominal distribution? (Answer: It involves subtracting a scaled log‑partition function.)

**Entropic Risk Premium**
- *Why needed*: Serves as a tractable surrogate for tail risk in the DRO framework, enabling simple risk controls.
- *Quick check*: How does adding an entropic risk penalty affect the ranking of candidates with equal mean but different variance? (Answer: It favors lower variance.)

**KL-Divergence as Ambiguity Set**
- *Why needed*: Yields a convex optimization problem with a measured dependence on both mean and entropy, linking to risk‑sensitivity.
- *Quick check*: What property of KL‑DRO leads to the mean–dispersion surrogate? (Answer: Taylor expansion of the log‑partition function.)

**Disagreement-Aware Alignment**
- *Why needed*: Moves beyond average reward to explicitly manage conflicting human preferences.
- *Quick check*: Why is minimizing standard deviation of reward scores across candidates a proxy for reducing cross‑annotator disagreement? (Answer: High variance indicates polarized preferences.)

**Risk‑Constrained Decoding**
- *Why needed*: Enables inference‑time adaptation without costly retraining, leveraging existing models.
- *Quick check*: What is the difference between capping and penalizing the entropic risk premium? (Answer: Capping imposes a hard budget; penalizing uses a tunable weight.)

**Soft‑Min Aggregation**
- *Why needed*: Provides a smooth approximation to worst‑case aggregation over multiple scorers, facilitating gradient‑free optimization.
- *Quick check*: How does the temperature parameter affect the soft‑min’s conservatism? (Lower temperature → closer to true min, more conservative.)

## Architecture Onboarding
DARC is an inference‑time reranker; it sits on top of any candidate generation process (e.g., beam search, best‑of‑N). The main components and data flow are:

Candidate Generator -> Scorer(s) -> Disagreement Proxy Estimation -> Risk‑Aware Reranker -> Final Selection

1. **Candidate Generator**: Produces a set of candidate responses for a given prompt (e.g., via sampling or beam search).  
2. **Scorer(s)**: Computes reward scores for each candidate. This can be one or multiple reward models (or a single model with perturbation to estimate variance).  
3. **Disagreement Proxy Estimation**: From the scores, computes a measure of dispersion (e.g., standard deviation across scorers or across perturbed copies).  
4. **Risk‑Aware Reranker**: Applies the DARC objective: for each candidate, calculates a risk‑adjusted utility = mean reward – λ·entropic_risk_premium (or caps the premium). The candidate with highest adjusted utility is selected.  
5. **Final Selection**: The chosen response is returned to the user.

**Critical Path**: The risk‑adjusted score must be computed for every candidate, so the bottleneck is scoring and proxy estimation. The reranking itself is negligible.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- *Risk budget (λ or cap)*: Larger λ gives more protection against disagreement but may lower average reward. Requires validation set tuning.
- *Number of scorers/perturbations*: More scorers improve proxy reliability but increase compute. Perturbation‑based proxies trade off fidelity for efficiency.
- *Capping vs. penalizing*: Capping enforces a strict bound on risk premium; penalizing offers smoother trade‑offs.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Overly conservative selections (low mean reward) when risk budget is too high or proxy overestimates disagreement.
- Degeneration to random or low‑quality outputs if the candidate set does not contain any moderate‑risk, high‑mean options (coverage problem).
- Instability across runs if proxy variance is noisy; may require score smoothing or ensemble.

**First Experiments**:
1. Replicate main results: Run DARC on MT‑Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0 with Best‑of‑N candidate generation, compare mean, std, and CVaR against baselines.
2. Ablation on risk control: Vary the risk budget parameter and observe the trade‑off curve between average reward and proxy risk; identify sweet spots.
3. Efficiency profiling: Measure wall‑clock time for different numbers of scorers/perturbations and candidate pool sizes; determine practical overhead.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Beyond treating all annotators as equally noisy, how can DARC be extended to perform user- or group-conditional risk control (e.g., explicitly protecting minority preference groups) during decoding?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion states "Future work includes... user/group-conditional risk control" and mentions related work on group-robust objectives, but notes they rely on access to group structure.
- Why unresolved: The current DARC framework aggregates disagreements into a single scalar risk proxy and does not incorporate explicit group identifiers or conditional robustness guarantees.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extensions to DARC that condition risk budgets on inferred or provided group membership, validated on multi-group preference benchmarks with known annotator demographics or preference clusters.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Is the KL-divergence local neighborhood in the DRO characterization the most appropriate robustness set for preference heterogeneity, or do other f-divergences or ambiguity sets (e.g., Wasserstein) yield materially different and superior selection rules?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The method derives from KL-robustness and χ²-DRO, and acknowledges the choice yields a "mean–dispersion" surrogate via Taylor expansion. It is not claimed that KL is the only or universal choice.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not compare different DRO ambiguity sets or f-divergences for decoding, and the trade-offs between tractability, conservatism, and alignment with human disagreement remain unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study comparing decoding rules derived from different DRO sets (e.g., total variation, Wasserstein) against human disagreement metrics and tail performance on diverse alignment benchmarks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the theoretical relationship between the perturbation-based disagreement proxy ˆσ and the true underlying variance of human satisfaction? Can a valid confidence interval for the true human disagreement be constructed from proxy samples?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper treats the proxy as a "scalable screening signal" and validates it via correlation and overlap diagnostics (Figure 4). It does not provide an error bound on how well ˆσ estimates true human σ.
- Why unresolved: The proxy is justified empirically by ranking correspondence, but its absolute calibration and the effect of estimation error on downstream risk control are not quantified.
- What evidence would resolve it: A calibration study where, for a sample of prompts, true human disagreement (from multi-rater ratings) is regressed against proxy ˆσ, along with prediction intervals; then DARC’s sensitivity to proxy miscalibration is analyzed.

### Open Question 4
- Question: When scorer families are used to hedge against reward model shift, does the soft-min aggregation (Equation 14) with a fixed τ risk budget optimally balance worst-case scorer protection against average utility, or can a learned or adaptive aggregation scheme improve robustness without sacrificing mean performance?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 4.3 proposes the soft-min operator with a risk budget and gives a variational interpretation, but calls the parameters (γ, τ) "deployment knobs" to be tuned on a held-out set.
- Why unresolved: The optimality of the soft-min aggregation for the specific goal of hedging scorer shift under preference heterogeneity is not characterized. Adaptive γ(·) depending on prompt-level scorer agreement could potentially outperform a fixed γ.
- What evidence would resolve it: An extension that learns γ(s) per prompt based on scorer diversity metrics, tested on multi-scorer robustness benchmarks and compared against the static γ baseline for CVaR and Tradeoff improvements.

### Open Question 5
- Question: Can the finite-candidate assumption be relaxed? How does DARC perform when the candidate set is generated stochastically on the fly (e.g., in a best-of-N with dynamic budget), and what theoretical guarantees exist for interactive decoding where candidates are not pre-sampled?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper's guarantees (Section 3) and all experiments condition on a fixed, pre-generated candidate set Y(s). The conclusion notes "limitations include a finite candidate pool."
- Why unresolved: Best-of-N, Poisson sampling, and other inference-time methods often adjust the pool size dynamically. The guarantee and risk-control logic may change if the selection process itself affects the candidate distribution.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical extension of the LCB/DRO view to sequential or adaptive candidate generation, and empirical evaluation of DARC in dynamic best-of-N settings where N is not fixed.

## Limitations
- Evaluation limited to MT‑Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0; broader benchmark coverage needed.
- Disagreement estimation relies on multiple annotator scores or perturbation proxies; its validity as a true human disagreement measure remains uncertain.
- Human evaluation lacks details on sample size, demographics, and statistical tests, limiting confidence in reported satisfaction and variance reductions.
- No analysis of computational overhead or hyperparameter sensitivity, making deployment practicality unclear.

## Confidence
- Theoretical DRO derivation: High
- Empirical risk/reward balance (lower std, higher CVaR, similar mean): Medium
- Focus on high‑disagreement prompts: Medium
- Human preference gains (higher satisfaction, lower cross‑rater variance): Medium
- Complementarity with robust training: Low
