# More Test-Time Compute Can Hurt: Overestimation Bias in LLM Beam Search

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.15377  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15377  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Beam search with a noisy scorer suffers from overestimation bias that grows with beam width; beyond an optimal width \hat{k} performance degrades, and \hat{k} depends exponentially on the scorer's signal-to-noise ratio.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether increasing beam width in large language model (LLM) reasoning always improves output quality. Using Extreme Value Theory, it shows that selecting the best candidate from a pool of scores subject to noise introduces a systematic overestimation bias that increases with the number of candidates. The authors derive a theoretical maximum useful beam width, \hat{k}, beyond which performance declines; this threshold depends exponentially on the scorer's signal-to-noise ratio (Δ/σ)^2, where Δ is the quality gap between correct and incorrect paths and σ is the scorer's noise level. Experiments with three 7B parameter models on 5,975 questions across ten domains compare two scoring functions: perplexity (high noise) and a process reward model (PRM, low noise). Results show that perplexity yields \hat{k}=1, meaning no beam width offers benefit and performance can drop by up to 5.4 points; PRM yields \hat{k}≥4 with improvements up to 8.9 points. The study concludes that more test-time compute can actually hurt if the scorer is insufficiently accurate, and proposes diagnostic indicators (e.g., score margins, pilot runs) to guide beam width selection.

## Method Summary
The methodology combines theoretical analysis with large-scale empirical evaluation. Theoretically, the authors model scorer noise as Gaussian and apply Extreme Value Theory to the distribution of the maximum score across a beam of candidates. They derive an expression for the overestimation bias and identify the optimal beam width \hat{k} where the marginal benefit turns negative, showing that \hat{k} grows exponentially with the scorer's signal‑to‑noise ratio (Δ/σ)^2. Empirically, they evaluate three 7B LLMs on a diverse set of 5,975 reasoning questions from ten domains. They generate multiple candidate solutions per question (using sampling) and rescore them with either perplexity (a high‑noise metric) or a process reward model (PRM, a low‑noise metric). They then measure accuracy as a function of beam width, compare the empirical optimal width with theoretical predictions, and quantify the performance differences.

## Key Results
- Beam search with a noisy scorer exhibits an overestimation bias that grows with beam width; beyond an optimal width \hat{k}, accuracy degrades.
- The optimal beam width \hat{k} depends exponentially on the scorer's signal‑to‑noise ratio (Δ/σ)^2.
- Using perplexity (high noise) scoring, \hat{k}=1 (no benefit from any beam) and performance can drop by up to 5.4 points; using PRM (low noise), \hat{k}≥4 with gains up to 8.9 points.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism is the overestimation bias arising from selecting the maximum score among many noisy estimates. When each candidate's true quality is obscured by independent Gaussian noise, the expected maximum score is inflated relative to the true maximum quality. This inflation increases with the number of candidates (beam width). At small widths, the benefit of exploring more candidates—i.e., the chance of including a high‑quality solution—outweighs the bias. However, as width grows, the bias accumulates faster than the marginal gain, eventually causing net harm. The signal‑to‑noise ratio (Δ/σ)^2 quantifies how well the scorer distinguishes correct from incorrect paths: a higher SNR means the bias is smaller relative to the true quality gap, allowing a larger beam before the turning point. Thus, the optimal beam width \hat{k} is determined by the trade‑off between coverage (finding a good candidate) and overestimation bias, leading to the exponential dependence on SNR.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Candidate Generation (LLM sampling) → Scorer (perplexity/PRM) → Beam Selector (max‑score picker) → Final Output

**Critical path**: The pipeline's performance hinges on the scorer's noise: each candidate's score = true quality + independent Gaussian noise (approx.). The selector's output quality is the maximum of these noisy scores. The bias emerges at the selection step, and its magnitude depends on beam width and noise variance.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Beam width (k)*: Larger k increases coverage (chance of including a high‑quality candidate) but also increases computational cost and overestimation bias.  
- *Scorer accuracy*: A more accurate (low‑noise) scorer permits larger k and yields higher gains, but may be more expensive to run (e.g., PRM vs. perplexity).  
- *Candidate generation*: Using a diverse set of candidates (e.g., high temperature) can increase the effective quality gap, but may also increase scorer noise if the scorer is sensitive.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Performance peaks at some intermediate beam width and then declines as k increases, particularly with noisy scorers.  
- With extremely noisy scorers, even k=1 (no beam) is optimal; any beam leads to degradation.  
- The turning point (\hat{k}) is predictable from the SNR: lower SNR leads to smaller optimal k.

**First experiments**:  
1. **Baseline comparison**: For a fixed model and task, evaluate accuracy at beam widths {1,2,4,8,16} using a high‑noise scorer (perplexity). Expect no improvement or degradation as k increases.  
2. **Low‑noise contrast**: Repeat the same with a low‑noise scorer (e.g., PRM). Expect accuracy to increase up to an optimal k (≥4) and then possibly decline.  
3. **SNR estimation**: Compute the margin between the top two scores on a validation set; use it as a proxy for Δ/σ. Verify that the experimental optimum aligns with the predicted exponential relationship between \hat{k} and the margin.

## Limitations
- The study is limited to three 7B parameter models and a single dataset of 5,975 questions across ten domains, raising concerns about generalizability to other model scales, architectures, or tasks.  
- The theoretical analysis assumes independent Gaussian noise, which may not fully capture the complex, correlated noise present in real LLM scorers.  
- The proposed diagnostic indicators (score margins, pilot runs) lack systematic empirical validation; their reliability across diverse settings remains uncertain.
