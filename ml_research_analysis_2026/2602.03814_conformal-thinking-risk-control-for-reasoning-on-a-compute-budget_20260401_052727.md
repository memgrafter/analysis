# Conformal Thinking: Risk Control for Reasoning on a Compute Budget

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03814
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03814
- Reference count: 39
- Primary result: Dual-threshold early stopping with distribution-free risk control achieves user-specified error bounds and reduces token usage on unsolvable reasoning problems.

## Executive Summary
Large language models often generate lengthy step-by-step reasoning, incurring significant computational cost. Adaptive early stopping can save tokens but risks loss of accuracy; existing heuristic approaches lack statistical guarantees. This paper introduces a dual-threshold mechanism: an upper threshold halts when the model’s confidence exceeds a preset level (controlling false positive risk), while a novel parametric lower threshold halts when confidence falls below a time-dependent curve (controlling false negative risk on unsolvable problems). Thresholds are calibrated via distribution-free risk control, adding a finite-sample UCB correction to ensure test risk stays below a user-specified ϵ with high probability; among feasible signal‑threshold pairs, the one minimizing an efficiency loss is selected. On reasoning benchmarks (AIME, DeepScaleR, GPQA, MathVision), calibrated thresholds consistently meet risk targets, unlike naive cross‑validation which often exceeds budgets. The lower threshold reduces token consumption on unsolvable instances by over 30% in some settings, and ensembling multiple uncertainty signals further improves the accuracy–compute trade‑off, with gains magnifying as the proportion of unsolvable problems increases.

## Method Summary
The method defines a scalar uncertainty signal s̃_t extracted from each reasoning chunk (e.g., length‑normalized log‑likelihood, entropy, or a probe predictor). Two stopping thresholds govern early exit: the upper threshold λ⁺ triggers when s̃_t ≥ λ⁺; the lower threshold λ⁻(t;c) = σ(c(ω_t - B/2)) triggers when s̃_t dips below this time‑dependent curve (ω_t tokens used, B total budget). At inference, the stopping time τ = min{τ⁺, τ⁻, T}. Calibration uses a validation set to search a grid of (λ⁺, c) for each signal, computing empirical false positive risk (Eq. 6), false negative risk (Eq. 7), and efficiency losses (Eq. 8–9). A UCB term √(log(1/δ)/(2n)) is added to empirical risk to form a high‑probability upper bound gRisk. Candidates with gRisk ≤ ϵ are feasible; among them, the one minimizing average efficiency loss is chosen. Dual‑threshold calibration may fix λ⁺ for a target ϵ⁺ then search c for ϵ⁻, ensuring λ⁻(t) ≤ λ⁺, or jointly optimize both. This yields statistically guaranteed risk control while optimizing token usage.

## Key Results
- Calibrated dual thresholds achieve test false positive and false negative risks at or below user‑specified ϵ across multiple reasoning benchmarks, whereas naive cross‑validation often exceeds budgets.
- The lower threshold reduces token consumption on unsolvable problems substantially (by >30% in some settings) without increasing risk.
- Ensembling multiple uncertainty signals (confidence, probe, EAT) improves the accuracy–compute trade‑off; gains grow with the proportion of unsolvable instances.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** An upper threshold halts reasoning when confidence exceeds a preset level to bound the false positive error rate (incorrect answers from premature stopping).
- **Mechanism:** Monitor a transformed confidence signal ˜s_t; exit at τ⁺ = min{t: ˜s_t ≥ λ⁺}. The threshold λ⁺ is calibrated so that the empirical false positive loss (Eq. 6) on a validation set, adjusted with a UCB term, stays below user‑specified ϵ with high probability.
- **Core assumption:** The confidence signal (e.g., normalized log‑likelihood of a forced answer) is a monotonically increasing proxy for answer correctness during successful reasoning traces.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "upper threshold halts when confidence exceeds a level (controls false positive risk)"
  - [section 4.2]: Defines τ⁺ and links λ⁺ to false positive risk control via Eq. (6).
  - [corpus]: Neighbors explore confidence‑based exit (e.g., ThinkBrake, Entropy After /Think) but do not establish statistical risk control; this paper’s novelty is the principled calibration of λ⁺.
- **Break condition:** If confidence does not reliably increase with correctness (e.g., due to distribution shift or model miscalibration), the upper threshold may terminate too early or too late, violating the risk guarantee.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A parametric lower threshold halts reasoning when progress is insufficient, preemptively stopping on likely unsolvable instances to reduce wasted tokens, while bounding false negative loss (missed correct answers from early exit).
- **Mechanism:** Define a time‑dependent boundary λ⁻(t; c) = σ(c(ω_t - B/2)), where ω_t is tokens used, B is max budget, and σ is sigmoid. Exit at τ⁻ = min{t: ˜s_t < λ⁻(t; c)}. The shape parameter c is calibrated to control false negative loss (Eq. 7) via validation set adjustment.
- **Core assumption:** On unsolvable problems, the confidence signal exhibits prolonged low values or flat trajectories; a decreasing threshold function allows early exit when improvement stalls.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "novel parametric lower threshold halts when confidence falls below a time‑dependent curve (controls false negative risk for unsolvable instances)"
  - [section 4.2]: Derives Eq. (12) and explains the design intuition; Figure 2 visualizes trajectories triggering the lower threshold.
  - [corpus]: Corpus lacks direct precursors—neighbors focus solely on upper‑threshold exit. This mechanism is presented as a novel contribution.
- **Break condition:** The lower threshold’s efficacy relies on signal separation between solvable and unsolvable instances. If solvable problems also show slow initial progress (e.g., due to difficulty), the lower threshold may cause premature exit, increasing false negative loss beyond the controlled risk.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Distribution‑free risk control with a finite‑sample correction (UCB) maps a user‑specified risk tolerance ϵ to threshold parameters (λ⁺, c) that guarantee test risk ≤ ϵ with high probability, while selecting the most efficient signal‑threshold pair.
- **Mechanism:** For each candidate signal s and threshold λ, compute empirical risk dRisk(V, s, λ) on validation set V. Apply gRisk(V, s, λ) = dRisk(V, s, λ) + √(log(1/δ)/(2n)) to form an upper confidence bound. Feasible pairs satisfy gRisk ≤ ϵ; among them, pick the one minimizing efficiency loss (Eq. 8/9).
- **Core assumption:** The per‑instance loss is bounded in [0,1]; the validation set is i.i.d. from the test distribution; the UCB correction (Hoeffding‑based) controls for selection over a finite candidate grid.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "calibrate thresholds via distribution‑free risk control using a validation set, adding a finite‑sample correction (UCB) to ensure test risk stays below user‑specified ϵ with high probability"
  - [section 4.3]: Algorithm 1 formalizes the grid search and UCB adjustment; Figure 4 empirically shows UCB maintains test risk ≤ target, unlike naive.
  - [corpus]: Work like SAFE‑KD uses conformal risk control for early‑exit in vision; this paper adapts the framework to reasoning LLMs with dual thresholds and efficiency loss.
- **Break condition:** The guarantee depends on bounded losses and i.i.d. validation data. Under distribution shift (e.g., length or dataset shift), the finite‑sample correction may be insufficient, leading to test risk violation as shown in ablation studies (Figures 8–9). The union bound over many candidates also weakens if the grid is extremely large.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**  
  Inputs: user risk tolerances (ϵ⁺, ϵ⁻), validation set V with gold answers, max token budget B, candidate uncertainty signals S.  
  Core modules: (1) Signal extractor – computes s̃_t per chunk; (2) Threshold tuner – grid‑searches (λ⁺, c) with UCB‑adjusted risk and efficiency loss; (3) Dual‑threshold executor – at inference, computes τ = min{τ⁺, τ⁻, T}.  
  Outputs: selected (signal, λ⁺, c) and resulting token usage/accuracy trade‑off.

- **Critical path**  
  1. Precompute validation scores: for each (s, λ), simulate early stopping on V, compute dRisk (false positive/negative) and dLosseff (efficiency).  
  2. Apply UCB: gRisk = dRisk + √(log(1/δ)/(2n)).  
  3. Filter candidates with gRisk ≤ ϵ; pick the one minimizing dLosseff.  
  4. At test time, run reasoning with selected s and thresholds; exit at first hitting time τ.

- **Design tradeoffs**  
  - Signal choice vs. transfer: Probe signals (trained on AIME) may be more discriminative but less generalizable across domains than model‑native signals (EAT, confidence).  
  - Lower threshold shape c: Steeper curves (large |c|) exit very early on slow progress but risk false negatives; flatter curves are more lenient but may waste tokens.  
  - Dual‑threshold interaction: Lower threshold is bounded above by λ⁺ to avoid intersection; this coupling may limit flexibility unless jointly optimized.

- **Failure signatures**  
  - Naive calibration overfitting: Validation risk below ϵ but test risk exceeds it, especially with small n.  
  - Length shift degradation: Lower threshold risk control fails when validation and test reasoning lengths differ significantly.  
  - Dataset shift: Signal distributions change (e.g., Math vs. Science); UCB helps but test risk may still creep up.  
  - No feasible candidate: All signal‑threshold pairs exceed ϵ even after UCB.

- **First 3 experiments**  
  1. Risk control validation: For a range of ϵ values, calibrate thresholds with naive vs. UCB; plot test risk against target ϵ. Expect UCB points to lie below y=x line, naive to scatter above.  
  2. Ensemble efficiency gain: For each ϵ, compare token usage vs. accuracy when selecting from multiple signals vs. using any single signal. Expect ensemble to dominate.  
  3. Lower threshold utility: On mixtures of solvable/unsolvable datasets with varying ratios, compare upper‑only vs. lower+upper curves. Expect lower+upper to shift left (fewer tokens) as unsolvable proportion increases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How to jointly calibrate upper and lower thresholds efficiently while preserving risk guarantees?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note joint search yields stronger guarantees but significant overhead (Sec. 4.4).
- **Why unresolved:** Sequential calibration risks violating the risk constraint; exhaustive joint search is computationally infeasible.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design a scalable algorithm with theoretical risk bounds and demonstrate reduced calibration runtime on benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the lower threshold’s risk control be made robust to reasoning length distribution shifts?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The ablation shows false negative risk exceeds target under length shift (Fig. 8).
- **Why unresolved:** The current λ⁻(t;c) assumes a fixed token‑progress relationship; distribution shift breaks this assumption.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Propose length‑normalized or adaptive lower thresholds and show controlled risk across varied length distributions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How critical is separation between solvable and unsolvable uncertainty distributions for the dual‑threshold guarantee?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states: “if the uncertainty signals are well separated … unlikely to happen” (Sec. 4.4).
- **Why unresolved:** This assumption is not evaluated; poor separation could degrade both false positive and negative risks.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Quantify signal separation (e.g., ROC) across datasets and correlate with risk control performance; develop methods to improve separation.

## Limitations
- Threshold calibration details unspecified: grid boundaries and step sizes for λ⁺ and c, total budget B, and validation set size choices.
- Ground truth labeling ambiguity: operational definition of solvable vs. unsolvable, answer extraction and matching criteria, identification of first‑correct step.
- Implementation intricacies: efficiency loss calculation for lower threshold and its interaction with upper threshold; joint vs. sequential calibration procedure; decoding parameters and chunking consistency.
