# Dynamic Symmetric Point Tracking: Tackling Non-ideal Reference in Analog In-memory Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21321  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21321  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A dynamic symmetric point tracking algorithm (RIDER) eliminates costly pre-calibration, reducing pulse complexity and improving accuracy under device non-idealities.

## Executive Summary
Analog in-memory training suffers from weight drift toward a device-specific symmetric point (SP) due to update asymmetry. Existing static calibration methods require many pulse updates, with cost scaling inversely with device precision. This paper introduces RIDER, an algorithm that dynamically estimates the SP during training via a moving average over the device’s update sequence, supported by theoretical guarantees of O(1/√K) convergence and pulse complexity O(δ⁻²). An enhanced version, E‑RIDER, adds chopping and filtering to accelerate tracking and reduce programming overhead. Evaluations on LeNet‑5/MNIST, FCN/MNIST, and ResNet‑18/CIFAR‑100 show E‑RIDER achieving significantly higher test accuracy (e.g., 93.75% vs. 72.62% under nonzero SP offset) and lower total pulse cost than static methods, especially for high‑precision devices.

## Method Summary
RIDER replaces static SP calibration with online estimation. During training, it maintains a moving average of the weight updates applied to each device, using this as an estimate of the symmetric point. The algorithm adjusts subsequent updates relative to this dynamic estimate, mitigating drift without pre‑programming. E‑RIDER enhances this by incorporating chopping (periodic reset of the average) to avoid bias accumulation and filtering (e.g., low‑pass) to smooth noise, speeding up SP acquisition and reducing the number of programming pulses required.

## Key Results
- E‑RIDER achieves 93.75% test accuracy on ResNet‑18/CIFAR‑100 under nonzero SP offset, versus 72.62% for static calibration.
- Theoretical pulse complexity reduced to O(δ⁻²) from O(δ⁻²+δ⁻¹Δwmin⁻¹) for two‑stage approaches.
- Benefits are most pronounced for high‑precision devices, with consistent accuracy gains and pulse savings across LeNet‑5, FCN, and ResNet‑18 benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Update asymmetry in analog memories causes weights to drift toward a fixed symmetric point (SP) specific to each device. Static calibration determines this SP beforehand via many pulse updates, but this cost grows with precision. RIDER’s moving average directly tracks the SP online from the actual update sequence, exploiting the fact that over many updates, the average drift converges to the SP. The O(1/√K) convergence ensures the estimate error shrinks with training steps. E‑RIDER’s chopping prevents long‑term bias from systematic errors, while filtering attenuates high‑frequency noise, allowing faster and more stable SP tracking, which in turn reduces the corrective pulses needed and maintains weight accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Update Asymmetry:** Why needed? Explains the root cause of weight drift; asymmetrical conductance changes push weights toward an equilibrium. Quick check: How would you model asymmetric up/down pulse responses in a resistive memory? Answer: As two distinct nonlinear conductance change functions per pulse, typically with different magnitudes and saturation behaviors depending on the current state.
- **Symmetric Point (SP):** Why needed? The stable equilibrium where net update vanishes; critical to understand device-specific drift. Quick check: What device parameters (e.g., switching nonlinearity) determine the SP value? Answer: The SP is defined where the average of the asymmetric update functions equals zero, determined by parameters such as the switching threshold, nonlinearity exponent, and any intrinsic device asymmetries.
- **Pulse‑based Weight Updates:** Why needed? Analog weights are programmed via pulses; precision tied to pulse count. Quick check: How does the number of pulses relate to minimum weight resolution Δw? Answer: The weight resolution Δw is inversely proportional to the number of programming pulses; more pulses enable finer weight increments.
- **Moving Average Estimation:** Why needed? Provides a low‑complexity online estimate of SP without separate calibration. Quick check: What is the trade‑off between averaging window size and tracking latency? Answer: A larger window reduces estimation variance but increases lag in tracking the true SP; a smaller window reduces lag but yields a noisier estimate.
- **Convergence Analysis (O(1/√K)):** Why needed? Guarantees on SP estimate error inform training stability. Quick check: Why does the error decay as 1/√K rather than exponentially? Answer: Because the SP estimate is an average of independent (or weakly dependent) random updates; the central limit theorem gives a Monte Carlo convergence rate of 1/√K, unlike exponential convergence which requires contraction mappings.
- **Chopping and Filtering:** Why needed? Digital signal techniques to accelerate convergence and suppress noise in the SP estimate. Quick check: How does chopping frequency affect bias versus variance in tracking? Answer: Higher chopping frequency reduces bias from accumulated systematic drift but increases variance due to frequent resets; lower frequency increases bias but reduces variance.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Analog Memory Array -> Update Circuitry -> Symmetric Point (SP) Tracker (Moving Average) -> Training Controller -> Loss Calculation -> Weight Update Decision  
Feedback: SP Tracker influences Update Circuitry to adjust pulse polarity/magnitude.

**Critical path:** During each training iteration, after gradient computation, weight updates are applied via pulses; the SP Tracker updates its estimate from these updates; the Training Controller uses the SP estimate to modify subsequent update commands; this loop repeats until convergence.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Accuracy vs. pulse cost: finer SP tracking (longer average) improves weight fidelity but increases pulses.  
- Tracking speed vs. stability: aggressive filtering/chopping speeds convergence but may introduce oscillation or bias.  
- Hardware overhead: digital logic for moving average, chopping, and filtering adds area/power, potentially offsetting analog savings.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Systematic SP estimation error → persistent weight offset → reduced model accuracy.  
- Excessive chopping → high‑frequency jitter in SP estimate → unstable training.  
- Inadequate filtering → noisy SP estimate → over‑compensation and divergence.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Single‑device SP tracking test: inject known SP offset, run RIDER/E‑RIDER, measure estimate error vs. step count.  
2. End‑to‑end training on MNIST with synthetic device asymmetry: compare test accuracy and pulse count of E‑RIDER vs. static calibration.  
3. Pulse budget analysis: train a small network to target loss, tally total programming pulses for each method, assess overhead of digital post‑processing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper identifies the following open questions for future investigation:
- How does RIDER/E‑RIDER scale to larger architectures (e.g., transformers) and more complex datasets?
- What is the full-system hardware overhead (energy, latency, area) of implementing the digital tracking logic?
- What are the optimal precision operating points (4‑bit to 8‑bit) that balance SP‑tracking accuracy, convergence speed, and pulse efficiency?

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes ideal device behavior; real‑world cycle‑to‑cycle variability, temperature drift, and read‑disturb may degrade convergence.
- Experiments are limited to LeNet‑5, FCN, and ResNet‑18 on MNIST/CIFAR‑100; generalization to larger models (e.g., transformers) and complex datasets is untested.
- Pulse cost reductions ignore digital overhead of moving averages and filtering; net system‑level gains remain uncertain.

## Confidence
- Weight drift to a symmetric point is a fundamental analog memory issue: **High**  
- Static calibration pulse count scales as Δw⁻¹: **Medium** (theoretical, sparse empirical)  
- RIDER convergence O(1/√K) and pulse complexity: **Medium** (proof under idealized models)  
- E‑RIDER improves accuracy and reduces pulses: **Medium** (validated on three tasks only)
