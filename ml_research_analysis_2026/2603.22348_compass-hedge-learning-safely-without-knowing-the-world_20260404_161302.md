# COMPASS-Hedge: Learning Safely Without Knowing the World
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22348
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22348
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Solves the long-standing trilemma in online learning: simultaneously achieving minimax-optimal O(√T) adversarial regret, instance-optimal O(1/Δ) stochastic regret, and O(log T) comparator regret relative to a safe baseline, all without requiring parameter tuning or prior knowledge of the environment type.

## Executive Summary
COMPASS-Hedge introduces a novel online learning algorithm that resolves a fundamental trilemma: attaining worst-case optimality, instance-dependent optimality, and strong safety guarantees (logarithmic regret against a designated safe comparator) within a single, parameter-free method. The core innovation lies in an adaptive doubling trick that self-adjusts a regret budget based on observed losses, combined with phase-based aggression that cautiously increases reliance on an aggressive Hedge learner only after empirically demonstrating the safe baseline is significantly suboptimal. This approach eliminates the need for any prior knowledge of the environment (adversarial vs. stochastic, horizon, or gap sizes) while maintaining rigorous guarantees under a controlled adaptivity condition.

## Method Summary
COMPASS-Hedge operates in stages with dynamically scaled budgets. Within each stage, it maintains a phase index that controls a mixing coefficient α. The algorithm plays a convex combination: μ_t = α·H_t + (1−α)·μ_c, where H_t is an aggressive Hedge (FTRL) expert distribution and μ_c is the safe comparator. The stage budget bR_s starts small and doubles only when the observed Hedge regret exceeds the current budget. The phase increases (raising α) only after the accumulated regret against μ_c exceeds twice the current budget, thereby "banking" surplus before becoming more aggressive. This design ensures that the comparator regret remains tightly bounded (O(log T)) while still allowing the Hedge component to achieve optimal rates in both stochastic and adversarial regimes.

## Key Results
- Minimax-optimal O(√T) adversarial regret.
- Instance-optimal O(1/Δ) stochastic regret under a unique optimal arm.
- O(log T) comparator regret against a fixed safe baseline μ_c.
- No parameter tuning or prior knowledge of environment type or horizon required.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The adaptive doubling trick autonomously scales the regret budget to be tight in both stochastic and adversarial regimes without parameter tuning.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm maintains an estimator for the pseudo-regret budget bR_s. It starts small (e.g., 2) and doubles it only when the observed cumulative regret of the Hedge learner against the best arm (R_hedge) exceeds the current budget. This creates a self-correcting loop: in stochastic settings, R_hedge is low (O(1/Δ)), so the budget stays small, enabling fast convergence; in adversarial settings, R_hedge grows (O(√T)), forcing the budget to double logarithmically to cover the linear-in-T worst-case.
- **Core assumption:** The observed losses serve as a valid proxy for the underlying regret scale. This requires that the gap between the observed (pseudo-)regret and the expected regret is tightly controlled (Lemma 3.1/C.2).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "...an adaptive doubling trick that scales the regret budget based on observed losses..."
  - [section 4.1]: "We maintain a dynamic estimator of the pseudo-regret and double it only when the cumulative observed loss exceeds the current threshold... This induces a self-correcting feedback loop..."
  - [corpus]: The mechanism aligns with the "Doubling Tricks" principle from the corpus (A.6) but innovates by coupling it to regret estimation rather than a fixed geometric schedule.
- **Break condition:** If the controlled adaptivity (A1) is violated, the adversary could cause large fluctuations in the observed losses to trigger unnecessary budget doublings in a stochastic regime, or suppress the observed regret to keep the budget inadequate in an adversarial regime, degrading the Worst-Case guarantee to Ω(T).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Rigorously bounding the gap between expected regret (theoretical objective) and pseudo-regret (stopping rule trigger) enables correct phase exits and budget scaling across both stochastic and adaptive adversarial models.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm’s internal decisions (phase exit, stage reset) depend on realized cumulative losses. However, regret guarantees are stated for expected quantities. A concentration argument bridges this: under A1 (controlled adaptivity) and bounded variance, the martingale difference sequence of centered losses concentrates, making the realized worst-case pseudo-regret a high-probability upper bound on the expected worst-case regret. In stochastic settings with a unique optimal arm (S2), the gap vanishes (o(1)).
- **Core assumption:** The environment's adaptivity (via A1) and stochasticity (via S1,S2) ensure the martingale has bounded increments/variance and the optimal arm is identifiable, enabling concentration.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "...rigorously bounds the difference between expected and pseudo-regret using martingale concentration..."
  - [section 3.2.1 & Lemma 3.1/C.2]: Formalizes conditions (A1) and proves G(I) = O(√|I|) in adversarial and o(1) in stochastic settings.
  - [corpus]: The approach relates to the "bridging realized and expected regret" technique in Best-of-Both-Worlds analysis (e.g., Mourtada & Gaïffas [42]), but extends it to the adaptive adversary model (A1).
- **Break condition:** Violation of the conditional variance bound (included in VI definition) or the uniqueness of the optimal arm (S2). Without uniqueness, the gap can be Ω(√T) asymptotically (Remark 3.4), making the pseudo-regret an unreliable proxy for instance-optimal rates.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Comparator-aware mixing with phase-based aggression ensures O(1) baseline safety without sacrificing optimal rates, by only increasing reliance on the aggressive Hedge learner when the safe comparator μ_c is empirically proven suboptimal.
- **Mechanism:** In each phase k, a fixed mixing coefficient α_k = min(1, 2^{k-1}/bR_s) sets μ_t = α_k · H_t + (1-α_k) · μ_c. The phase only advances (k increases, α increases) when the accumulated regret against μ_c exceeds 2·bR_s. This creates a "ratchet": advancing phases "banks" negative regret (surplus) against μ_c (Lemma 4.3/C.4), while staying in a phase caps the positive comparator regret to O(1) (Lemma 4.2/C.3). The aggressive Hedge learner H_t operates with a decaying learning rate (η_t ~ 1/√t), preserving its optimality guarantees.
- **Core assumption:** The phase exit condition using observed losses correctly signals when the expected regret against μ_c is large enough to justify increased aggression. This depends on the concentration bound from Mechanism 2.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "...phase-based aggression and comparator-aware mixing..."
  - [section 4.1]: "We only exit a phase... if we verify that the comparator μ_c is significantly suboptimal... This 'Phase-Budgeted' approach ensures that we never incur large regret against μ_c unless we have already banked significant gains against the environment."
  - [corpus]: Directly corresponds to the "Phased Aggregation" concept in the related work (Even-Dar et al. [23], Müller et al. [43]) but integrates it with the adaptive budget from Mechanism 1.
- **Break condition:** If the adversary can manipulate the observed regrets to trigger phase exits prematurely (e.g., by briefly making μ_c look bad), the algorithm might increase α too early, leading to Ω(T) comparator regret before the true safety budget is "banked". The controlled adaptivity (A1) prevents such rapid manipulation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Pseudo-Regret vs. Expected Regret**
  - Why needed here: The algorithm's logic (budget doubling, phase exits) uses realized pseudo-regret (sum of observed loss differences). Theoretical guarantees, however, are for expected regret. The analysis hinges on proving the realized pseudo-regret is a high-probability upper bound on the expected one.
  - Quick check: Why is max_µ E[∑ ⟨c_t, μ_t − μ⟩] (expected) generally less than E[max_µ ∑ ⟨c_t, μ_t − μ⟩] (pseudo-regret)? (Answer: Jensen's inequality; the max operator is convex.)
- **Concept: Adaptive Doubling / Restart Tricks**
  - Why needed here: To be "parameter-free" and "horizon-free," the algorithm cannot use a fixed budget or learning rate schedule tied to T. The doubling trick self-adjusts the budget (bR_s) to the environment's difficulty, enabling the single algorithm to handle both O(√T) and O(1/Δ) regimes.
  - Quick check: What is the key pitfall of naive doubling tricks (e.g., restarting every 2^s rounds) that COMPASS-Hedge avoids? (Answer: They reset accumulated statistics/regret, destroying instance-dependent progress and preventing O(1) comparator guarantees.)
- **Concept: Best-of-Both-Worlds (BOBW) Paradigm**
  - Why needed here: COMPASS-Hedge extends BOBW from two worlds (adversarial/stochastic) to three by adding a baseline safety world. Understanding the BOBW goal (same algorithm, optimal rates in both regimes) is prerequisite to seeing the "trilemma."
  - Quick check: In standard BOBW bandit algorithms (e.g., Tsallis-INF), what changes between the stochastic and adversarial regimes? (Answer: The implicit exploration bonus or regularizer adapts based on observed variance/gaps.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Budget Estimator -> Phase Manager -> Hedge Engine -> Comparator Mixer
- **Critical path:** Compute R_hedge & R_comp from losses -> Check stage condition: if R_hedge > bR_s, call Budget Estimator to double bR_s, reset k, H; else check phase condition: if R_comp > 2·bR_s and α_k < 1, call Phase Manager to increment k and update α; else update Hedge weights.
- **Design tradeoffs:** The mixing coefficient α_k is the key tension: low α = safety (high 1−α) but slow learning; high α = aggression but risk of large comparator regret. The doubling budget bR_s acts as a "budget for aggression." The algorithm trades off the logarithmic overhead (from log T stages/phases) against achieving the three-world guarantees. If the overhead is too high, asymptotic constants worsen.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. Stuck in safe mode: If bR_s never grows (e.g., due to a bug in R_hedge calculation), α never increases → algorithm reduces to playing μ_c with O(1) safety but linear optimal regret.
  2. Premature aggression: If phase exit condition R_comp > 2·bR_s is checked incorrectly (e.g., using stale bR_s), the algorithm may increase α before sufficient "banked" regret, leading to high comparator regret.
  3. Budget explosion: If bR_s doubles erroneously on noise (e.g., R_hedge miscalculation), number of stages S becomes Ω(T), merging logarithmic overhead into linear regret.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Unit test: Doubling logic. Simulate a pure adversarial sequence (e.g., constant losses). Verify bR_s doubles exactly log2(T) times and final bR_s is Θ(T).
  2. Unit test: Phase ratchet. Run in a stochastic setting with a unique optimal expert. Verify that for early phases where μ_c is suboptimal, R_comp becomes negative (banking surplus) and total R_comp over T is O(log T).
  3. Integration test: Regret separation. Run on a pre-defined mixed sequence: first 1000 steps stochastic (gap Δ=0.1), next 1000 steps adversarial (linear pattern). Verify pseudo-regret curve: O(1/Δ) in first segment, transitions to O(√T) only after the regime change, while R(μ_c) remains flat throughout.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Are the extra logarithmic factors in the bounds inherent to any algorithm that attains all three desiderata (minimax, instance-optimal, constant comparator regret), or are they artifacts of the current analysis?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the Conclusion, the authors state: “A natural next step is to tighten the remaining polylogarithmic factors and to understand the fundamental ‘price of protection’...”
- **Why unresolved:** The proof relies on a hierarchical geometric scaling that introduces log T terms; it is unknown whether a tighter analysis or a lower bound proves these factors unavoidable.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A matching lower bound showing Ω(log T) overhead for comparator regret, or a refined algorithm/analysis that removes the logarithmic dependence while preserving all three guarantees.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can COMPASS-Hedge be extended to settings beyond full-information experts (e.g., linear bandits, structured action spaces, reinforcement learning) while maintaining best-of-three-worlds guarantees?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work: “More broadly, extending comparator-safe adaptivity beyond expert problems—to linear bandits, structured action spaces, and reinforcement learning—is a promising direction.”
- **Why unresolved:** The algorithm’s phase-based mixing and doubling trick exploit the finite-armed expert structure; generalization to richer decision sets introduces exploration-exploitation and safety coupling challenges.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A novel algorithm that adapts the core ideas (adaptive pseudo-regret scaling, comparator-aware mixing) to one of these settings with rigorous (near-)optimal regret and safety bounds.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the assumption of a unique optimal arm (Assumption S2) fundamentally limit the stochastic regime, and can it be relaxed?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Remark 3.4: “when multiple arms are optimal, ... the discrepancy between these metrics can remain asymptotically non-negligible ... addressing this regime would require techniques fundamentally different ... we highlight the relaxation of this assumption as a major open question.”
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis hinges on the pseudo-regret gap vanishing due to a unique optimum; with ties, the identifier of the “best” arm oscillates, breaking the connection between observed and expected regret.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An algorithm that still achieves instance-optimal O(1/Δ) or even O(log T) rates despite multiple optimal experts, perhaps via variance-aware or elimination-style techniques adapted to the safety framework.

## Limitations
- Ambiguities in reproduction details (exact definition of Oracle comparator, sign in loss transform, Hedge learning-rate schedule, handling of zero IQR) may hinder faithful replication.
- Theoretical assumptions (controlled adaptivity A1, unique optimal arm S2) are non-trivial; violations can degrade guarantees (e.g., Ω(T) comparator regret or loss of instance-optimal rates).
- Practical claims rely on experiments on a single financial dataset (S&P 500, 2015–2024) that may not fully represent the spectrum of adversarial, stochastic, and mixed regimes.

- Practical efficacy (S&P experiment): Medium
