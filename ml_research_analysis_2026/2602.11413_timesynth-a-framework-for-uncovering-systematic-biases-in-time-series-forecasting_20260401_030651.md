# TimeSynth: A Framework for Uncovering Systematic Biases in Time Series Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11413
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11413
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Linear models systematically collapse to simple oscillations or global averages on complex synthetic signals, while nonlinear models (CNNs, Transformers) maintain adaptive performance, revealing biased evaluations that previously overstated linear model capabilities.

## Executive Summary
TimeSynth introduces a synthetic time series framework designed to expose systematic biases in forecasting evaluations, particularly the erroneous claim that linear models can match nonlinear architectures. By generating controlled signals (Drift Harmonic, SPM-Harmonic, DPM-Harmonic) with parameters fitted from real data and enforcing independent train/test splits, the framework prevents data leakage and enables targeted experiments under clean, noisy, and distribution-shifted conditions. Results demonstrate that linear models fail to capture signal complexity, defaulting to overly simplistic patterns, whereas nonlinear models adapt more effectively. The study statistically confirms these biases via mixed-effects models, highlighting the need for more rigorous evaluation protocols.

## Method Summary
TimeSynth synthesizes time series signals by fitting parametric models (incorporating drift, harmonics, and modulation) to real-world data, then generating train and test sets independently to avoid leakage. The evaluation protocol measures model performance across multiple axes: amplitude error (MAE), frequency error, and phase error. Experiments probe three conditions: clean signals, signals with additive noise, and signals under distribution shift (e.g., changed drift or modulation parameters). This controlled design isolates model behavior from confounding factors present in real-world benchmarks.

## Key Results
- Linear models consistently collapse to predicting simple oscillations or global averages, yielding deceptively stable but poor MAE across complex signals.
- Nonlinear models (especially CNNs and Transformers) adapt to modulated signals, while MLPs lag, showing architecture-specific strengths.
- Under noise, Drift Harmonic signals degrade most; under distribution shift, linear models remain insensitive (due to collapse), while nonlinear models show adaptive but variable errors, revealing a stability-fidelity trade-off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The framework works by constructing signals with known, tunable generative factors (drift, harmonic, modulation) that challenge models in specific ways. Linear models lack the capacity to capture nonlinear interactions and phase relationships inherent in modulated signals, causing them to regress to the mean or simple periodic defaults. Nonlinear models with sufficient depth (CNNs) or attention (Transformers) can disentangle these factors. Independent train/test splits prevent inadvertent leakage of signal parameters, ensuring that performance reflects true generalization. Controlled noise and shift manipulations further expose model robustness and adaptation limits.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
SignalParameterFitter -> SignalGenerator -> IndependentDataSplitter -> ModelTrainer -> MultiMetricEvaluator -> MixedEffectsAnalyzer

Critical path:
1. Fit signal parameters (drift, harmonic, modulation) from real data.
2. Generate train and test signals with independent parameter draws.
3. Train candidate models (linear, CNN, Transformer, MLP) on train set.
4. Evaluate on test set using MAE, frequency error, phase error.
5. Fit mixed-effects model to assess systematic biases across signal families and model types.

Design tradeoffs:
- Realism vs. Controllability: Fitting from real data improves realism but may not capture all complexities; pure synthetic signals offer full control but may lack nuance.
- Noise/Shift Granularity: Adding noise or shifting parameters isolates effects but may not reflect real-world shift distributions.
- Metric Diversity: Multiple metrics (amplitude, frequency, phase) give nuanced view but complicate interpretation.

Failure signatures:
- Linear models: MAE plateauing at high values, predicted signals resembling simple sine waves or horizontal lines regardless of input complexity.
- CNNs/Transformers: Performance degrades gracefully with noise/shift; MLPs show intermediate collapse on complex modulation.
- Mixed-effects: Significant random effects for model type and signal family, indicating systematic biases.

First three experiments:
1. Train linear (ARIMA, Ridge) and nonlinear (CNN, Transformer) models on clean Drift/SPM/DPM-Harmonic signals; compare MAE.
2. Introduce increasing levels of Gaussian noise to signals; measure MAE degradation per model.
3. Apply distribution shift by changing modulation frequency in test set; compare frequency and phase errors.

## Limitations
- Synthetic signals may not capture all real-world complexities (e.g., long-range dependencies, rare events), limiting generalizability.
- Mixed-effects models rely on correctly specified random effects; unmeasured confounders could bias statistical conclusions.
- Findings might be sensitive to the specific set of real data used for fitting signal parameters.

## Confidence
- Synthetic framework validity: **High** – independent splits prevent leakage; controlled parameters.
- Linear model collapse: **Medium** – consistent across conditions but may not generalize beyond fitted signal families.
- Nonlinear model superiority: **Medium** – CNNs/Transformers adapt best; MLP lag might be tuning/architecture-specific.
- Noise/distribution shift responses: **Low** – patterns observed but real-world shift distributions may differ.
- Statistical bias confirmation: **Medium** – mixed-effects support structural issues but depend on synthetic design.
