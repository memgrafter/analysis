# Time-adaptive functional Gaussian Process regression

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.21144
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21144
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: Proposes a time-adaptive empirical Bayes functional GP regression on manifolds via spectral truncation, achieving computational efficiency and predictive accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Functional Gaussian Process (GP) regression for spatiotemporal data on manifolds, such as the sphere, is computationally demanding due to infinite-dimensional function spaces and long-range temporal dependencies. This paper introduces an Empirical Bayes framework that leverages the invariance of the covariance kernel under manifold isometries. By diagonalizing the spatial covariance operator with eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, the problem reduces to pointwise operations on a time-varying angular spectrum. Adaptive truncation of the spectral expansion (logarithmic or power-law) is chosen based on functional sample size and smoothness, balancing computational cost and approximation error.

Simulations using spherical data from two Gneiting-class covariance subfamilies show that for larger functional sample sizes logarithmic truncation yields lower Empirical Mean Quadratic Errors (EMQEs), while for small sample sizes with high spatial singularity a power-law truncation performs better; for smoother processes logarithmic truncation remains optimal. Five‑fold cross‑validation on a synthetic solar radiation task yields larger out-of-sample errors than in-sample EMQEs, indicating robust generalization without overfitting. The method thus provides an effective trade‑off between scalability and accuracy for functional GP regression on manifolds.

## Method Summary
The method assumes spatiotemporal observations on a compact Riemannian manifold (e.g., the unit sphere) with a spatially isotropic covariance kernel from the restricted Gneiting class. Functional data are projected onto the eigenfunctions of the Laplace‑Beltrami operator to obtain angular coefficients. For each time point, hyperparameters \((\theta(t),\sigma(t))\) are estimated via Empirical Bayes by maximizing a marginal likelihood that is approximated using Monte Carlo integration over \(R\) replicates in the truncated spectral domain (order \(T_R\)). The posterior mean functional predictor is reconstructed by weighting the observed coefficients by the ratio of the estimated angular spectrum to the total spectrum plus noise variance. The truncation order \(T_R\) is chosen adaptively: logarithmic in the functional sample size \(T\) for larger \(T\) or smoother processes, and power‑law in \(T\) for small \(T\) or spatially rough processes.

## Key Results
- For larger functional sample sizes, logarithmic truncation yields lower EMQEs; for small sample sizes with high spatial singularity, power‑law truncation gives better performance; for smoother spatial processes logarithmic truncation remains optimal.
- Five‑fold cross‑validation on a synthetic solar radiation regression task shows out-of-sample errors exceed in-sample EMQEs, demonstrating robust generalization without overfitting.
- The approach effectively balances computational cost and predictive accuracy through adaptive spectral truncation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The method reduces computational complexity by diagonalizing the infinite-dimensional covariance operator via the eigenfunctions of the Laplace‑Beltrami operator, exploiting the invariance of the spatiotemporal covariance kernel under the manifold's isometry group.
- **Mechanism:** The covariance operator is decomposed spectrally using the eigenbasis \(\{S_{n,j}\}\) of the Laplace‑Beltrami operator on the manifold \(M_d\). This transforms the inverse covariance operator calculation (the main computational bottleneck) into pointwise operations on the time‑varying angular spectrum \(B_n(t, \theta(t))\) in the \(l^2\) sense, avoiding direct matrix inversion in function space.
- **Core assumption:** The manifold \(M_d\) is a connected, compact two‑point homogeneous space (e.g., a sphere), ensuring the eigenfunctions form a complete orthonormal system and the covariance kernel is spatially isotropic and stationary.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "exploiting the invariance property of covariance kernels under the group of isometries of the manifold. The identification of these measures with infinite‑product Gaussian measures is then obtained via the eigenfunctions of the Laplace–Beltrami operator."
  - [section 2.2] "This result is based on the following diagonal expansion of a spatially invariant spatiotemporal covariance kernel \(C_{M_d}\)... in terms of the eigenfunctions... of the Laplace–Beltrami operator \(\Delta_d\)"
  - [corpus] The corpus confirms the centrality of manifold eigenfunction spectral methods in contemporary GP regression on manifolds (e.g., "Intrinsic Gaussian Process Regression Modeling for Manifold-valued Response Variable", "Implicit Manifold Gaussian Process Regression").
- **Break condition:** If the covariance kernel is not invariant under the manifold isometry group or the manifold geometry is not two‑point homogeneous, the spectral decomposition via Laplace‑Beltrami eigenfunctions may not diagonalize the operator, invalidating the computational shortcut.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Computational cost and predictive accuracy are balanced by adaptively truncating the infinite‑dimensional angular spectrum using a scheme (logarithmic or power‑law) determined by the functional sample size and spatial smoothness properties.
- **Mechanism:** The infinite sum over eigenfunctions in the Karhunen‑Loève expansion is truncated at a finite order \(T_R\). The truncation scheme is chosen based on the functional sample size \(T\) and the spatial regularity of the underlying process: logarithmic (\(T_R \sim \log(T)\)) for large \(T\) or smooth processes; power‑law (\(T_R \sim \lfloor T^\rho \rfloor\)) for small \(T\) or processes with high spatial singularity.
- **Core assumption:** The angular spectrum \(B_n(t, \theta(t))\) decays sufficiently fast with increasing eigenvalue index \(n\), so that a finite truncation captures most of the signal variance. The rate of decay is influenced by the smoothness parameters (e.g., \(\gamma, \nu, \varpi\) in the Gneiting subfamilies).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4] "For larger functional sample sizes, a logarithmic truncation scheme yields lower Empirical Mean Quadratic Errors (EMQEs). For small sample sizes with high spatial singularity, a power‑law truncation provides better performance."
  - [section 4.1] "When increasing the functional sample size, under this sparse spatial scenario, leads to an important asymptotic improvement... Note that this increment in the number of spherical nodes is reflected on a slightly better performance at higher resolution levels."
  - [abstract] "The involved time‑varying angular spectra constitute the key tool for dimension reduction in the implementation of this regression approach, adopting a suitable truncation scheme depending on the functional sample size."
- **Break condition:** If the angular spectrum decays too slowly (e.g., due to very rough spatial fields or long‑range temporal dependence not captured by the kernel family), any practically feasible truncation order \(T_R\) will omit significant signal variance, leading to high bias.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The Empirical Bayes (EB) framework provides a computationally feasible way to estimate time‑varying hyperparameters \((\theta(t), \sigma(t))\) by maximizing the marginal likelihood computed in the truncated spectral domain via Monte Carlo integration.
- **Mechanism:** For each time \(t\), the marginal likelihood of the functional data \(Y_t\) is expressed as an infinite product (via Theorem 1 and Lemma 1) over the truncated angular spectrum. This product is approximated using \(R\) Monte Carlo replicates of the functional GP and observations. The ML‑II estimates \((\hat{\theta}(t), \hat{\sigma}(t))\) are obtained numerically by maximizing this approximate marginal likelihood.
- **Core assumption:** The Monte Carlo integration over \(R\) replicates provides a sufficiently accurate approximation of the true marginal likelihood for optimization. The truncation error from Mechanism 2 is small enough that the ML‑II estimates converge to their infinite‑dimensional analogs.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1] "In practice, for each \(t\in\mathcal{T}\), the integral... is computed in the time‑varying angular spectral domain (7), applying Theorem 1 and Lemma 1. In the simulation study... this integral is approximated in terms of a truncated version of the time‑varying angular spectrum via Monte Carlo numerical integration."
  - [section 4] "Beyond the minimum threshold \(R=200\), Monte Carlo numerical integration produces suitable approximations... This fact can also be checked... where a better performance is obtained than at the center‑left."
  - [section 5] "Time‑adaptive Empirical Bayes is implemented in terms of the training samples" for 5‑fold cross‑validation.
- **Break condition:** If \(R\) is too small, the Monte Carlo estimate of the marginal likelihood has high variance, leading to unstable or biased ML‑II estimates. If the truncation order \(T_R\) is too low for the given \(R\), the bias from truncation dominates.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Separable Hilbert Space Gaussian Measures (\(L^2(M_d, d\nu)\))
  - **Why needed here:** The functional GP and its posterior are defined as probability measures on an infinite‑dimensional function space. The paper uses the \(l^2\) identification (Theorem 1) to represent these measures as infinite products of 1D Gaussians, enabling computation.
  - **Quick check:** Why is the identification of a Gaussian measure on \(L^2(S^2)\) with an infinite product measure on \(\mathbb{R}^\infty\) crucial for implementing GP regression on the sphere？

- **Concept:** Laplace‑Beltrami Operator Eigenfunctions
  - **Why needed here:** They provide a canonical, geometry‑aware orthonormal basis for \(L^2(M_d)\) that diagonalizes any spatially isotropic covariance kernel on the manifold. This is the core spectral decomposition enabling dimension reduction.
  - **Quick check:** How do the eigenfunctions of the Laplace‑Beltrami operator differ from using a generic basis (e.g., Fourier series on \([0,1]^2\) projected to the sphere) for functional data on \(S^2\)？

- **Concept:** Truncation Schemes (Logarithmic vs. Power‑law)
  - **Why needed here:** They determine the finite‑dimensional approximation of the infinite‑dimensional problem. The choice directly trades off computational cost (size of \(T_R\)) against approximation error (bias/variance), and must adapt to data properties.
  - **Quick check:** According to the simulation, what characteristics of the data and model would lead you to choose a power‑law truncation (\(T_R = \lfloor T^\rho \rfloor\)) over a logarithmic one (\(T_R \approx \log(T)\))？

- **Concept:** Gneiting Class Covariances for Manifolds
  - **Why needed here:** This parametric family provides spatially isotropic, temporally stationary covariance models on \(\mathbb{R}^3\) that, when restricted to the sphere \(S^2(1)\), yield valid spatiotemporal processes on the manifold. Their parameters control spatial smoothness (\(\gamma, \nu, \varpi\)) and temporal memory (\(\alpha, \beta\)).
  - **Quick check:** In the Gneiting class restriction to the sphere (Equation 15), what do the parameters \(\gamma\) (in \(\phi\)) and \(\alpha\) (in \(\psi\)) physically represent about the spatiotemporal field？

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Input Handler:** Accepts spatiotemporal observations \(Y_t(x)\) sampled at discrete times \(t \in \mathcal{T}_T\) and spatial locations \(x \in \{grid\ on\ M_d\}\). Handles manifold coordinate conversions (e.g., lat/lon to 3D Cartesian on \(S^2\)).
  2. **Spectral Preprocessor:** Computes (or loads) the eigenfunctions \(\{S_{n,j}\}\) and eigenvalues \(\{\lambda_n\}\) of the Laplace‑Beltrami operator for the specific manifold \(M_d\). This is a one‑time offline cost for a given geometry.
  3. **Projector:** Projects the spatial functional observations onto the eigenbasis to obtain the time‑varying angular coefficients \(Y_{n,j}(t)\). Involves numerical integration over the spatial discretization.
  4. **Truncation Module:** Given functional sample size \(T\) and spatial discretization size \(N\), selects truncation order \(T_R\) via scheme (logarithmic \(\lfloor\log(T)\rfloor\) or power‑law \(\lfloor T^\rho \rfloor\)). \(\rho\) is a hyperparameter (e.g., \(1/2.45\) in experiments).
  5. **EB Optimizer:** For each time \(t\) (or in a sliding window), approximates the marginal likelihood \(p(Y_t | \theta(t), \sigma(t))\) using \(R\) Monte Carlo replicates from the prior GP (generated in spectral domain). Maximizes to obtain \((\hat{\theta}(t), \hat{\sigma}(t))\).
  6. **Posterior Computer:** Computes posterior mean coefficients \(E[Z_{n,j}(t) | Y_t]\) and posterior spectral variances \(\lambda_n(posterior)\) using formulas (10) with \((\hat{\theta}(t), \hat{\sigma}(t))\) and \(\hat{\sigma}(t)\). Reconstructs the functional posterior mean via inverse transform \(\sum_{n,j} E[Z_{n,j}] S_{n,j}(x)\).
  7. **Evaluator:** Computes time‑varying EMQEs \(\|Z_{true}(t) - E[Z|Y]_t\|^2_{L^2}\) or cross‑validation errors.
- **Critical path:** Spectral Preprocessor → Projector → (Truncation + EB Optimizer) → Posterior Computer. The EB Optimizer is the most computationally intensive, requiring \(O(R \cdot T_R \cdot T)\) operations per time step for the Monte Carlo integration.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Truncation order (\(T_R\)): higher reduces bias but increases EB optimization and reconstruction cost. Monte Carlo replicates (\(R\)): higher reduces variance of likelihood estimate but linearly increases EB cost. Spatial discretization (\(N\)): higher reduces projection error but increases inner product cost; for smooth processes, too high \(N\) with low \(T\) can cause multicollinearity.
- **Failure signatures:** Poor posterior correlation reconstruction (high discrepancy between theoretical and estimated \(B_n(t)\)) indicates \(T_R\) too low or \(R\) too small. Spatial edge effects in posterior mean maps indicate insufficient spatial sampling \(N\). Elevated EMQEs at time series start/end may indicate poor EB initialization or unmodeled non‑stationarity. Cross‑validation >> in‑sample EMQE suggests overfitting due to aggressive truncation or insufficient \(R\).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Truncation Scheme Validation:** Fix \(T=300, N=150, M=50, R=400\). Compare EMQEs for \(T_R = \lfloor\log(T)\rfloor\) vs. \(T_R = \lfloor T^{1/2.45}\rfloor\) under Subfamily 1 (high spatial singularity). Expect power‑law to win for small \(T\).
  2. **Smoothness Sensitivity:** Fix \(T=500, N=50, T_R=\log(T), M=100, R=400\). Compare EMQEs for Subfamily 1 (rough) vs. Subfamily 2 (smooth). Expect both to perform well with logarithmic truncation, but Subfamily 2 may suffer if \(T_R\) is too high (multicollinearity).
  3. **Monte Carlo Robustness:** Fix \(T=300, N=150, T_R=\log(T)\). Vary \(R\) in \(\{50,200,400,800\}\) and stabilize EMQE curve. Expect significant improvement from \(R=50\) to \(R=200\), then diminishing returns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What are the asymptotic consistency properties of the posterior predictor under the proposed truncation schemes (logarithmic and power‑law) as the functional sample size increases, and how can time‑adaptive credibility regions be constructed based on these truncations？
- **Basis in paper:** In Section 6, the authors state: "Consistency of the posterior predictor can be analyzed in terms of suitable truncation schemes, depending on the functional sample size. This asymptotic analysis will be undertaken in subsequent work, being applied to the definition of time‑adaptive credibility regions in an infinite‑dimensional tight Gaussian measure framework."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper illustrates finite sample performance via simulations but does not provide theoretical consistency analysis or methods for credibility regions, which are mentioned as future work.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical proof of convergence of the posterior to the true functional parameter under given conditions on truncation order, sample size, and manifold properties. Empirical studies on coverage probabilities of constructed credibility intervals.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Under what conditions on spatial smoothness, temporal dependence, and functional sample size does one truncation scheme (logarithmic vs. power‑law) outperform the other in terms of prediction error, and can this be theoretically predicted？
- **Basis in paper:** The simulation study in Section 4 shows that for large functional sample sizes logarithmic truncation yields lower EMQEs, while for small sample sizes with high spatial singularity, power‑law truncation performs better. However, no theoretical explanation is provided for this switch.
- **Why unresolved:** The choice of truncation scheme is based on empirical observations, but the underlying reasons related to eigenvalue decay rates of the Laplace‑Beltrami operator or covariance kernels are not analyzed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis linking truncation error to eigenvalue spectra and sample size, possibly through bounds on bias‑variance trade‑offs. Simulation studies varying smoothness parameters and sample sizes to confirm theoretical predictions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does the proposed method extend to Riemannian manifolds that are not two‑point homogeneous, and what modifications are needed to the spectral decomposition and invariance assumptions？
- **Basis in paper:** The paper restricts to connected and compact two‑point homogeneous spaces (like spheres) for which the Laplace‑Beltrami eigenfunctions provide a spherical harmonic basis. This assumption is crucial for the invariance property and spectral diagonalization.
- **Why unresolved:** Many manifolds of interest (e.g., general surfaces, graphs) do not have such symmetric properties, so the current approach may not directly apply.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a general framework using manifold learning or approximate eigenfunction expansions for non‑symmetric manifolds. Testing on synthetic data on manifolds like tori or irregular surfaces to assess performance loss.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive are the ML‑II hyperparameter estimates and subsequent posterior predictions to the choice of prior distributions for the hyperparameters, given that the Empirical Bayes approach relies on these priors？
- **Basis in paper:** In Section 4, the authors specify informative priors for hyperparameters based on an exploratory simulation study, but robustness to prior mis‑specification is not investigated.
- **Why unresolved:** In Empirical Bayes, the choice of hyperparameter prior can affect ML estimates, especially with small samples. The paper does not discuss sensitivity analysis.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulation studies with different prior specifications (e.g., vague vs. informative, misspecified) to see impact on prediction errors. Theoretical results on prior robustness in the infinite‑dimensional setting.

## Limitations
- Eigenfunction details (normalization, multiplicities) omitted, hindering exact implementation.
- Simulation parameters not fully reported; exact reproduction impossible.
- Hyperparameter prior support unspecified; EB estimates may be sensitive.
- Assumes spatial isotropy and manifold isometries; generalizability uncertain.
- Adaptive truncation (log vs power) lacks theoretical justification.
- Cross‑validation errors exceed in‑sample EMQE; optimism bias not analyzed.
- Scalability to large \(N,T\) unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Spectral diagonalization:** High (mathematically solid for two‑point homogeneous manifolds).
- **Adaptive truncation:** Medium (empirical support, no general theory).
- **EB hyperparameter accuracy:** Medium (stable for \(R\ge200\), sensitivity to \(R/T_R\) not fully mapped).
- **Generalization to other manifolds:** Low (only sphere validated).
