# Directional Routing in Transformers
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.14923
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14923
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** Introduces directional routing, a lightweight coordination mechanism that suppresses irrelevant feature directions in attention heads, achieving 31–56% perplexity reduction on held-out data while making individual heads functionally redundant.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles cross-domain interference in transformer models by introducing directional routing, a parameter-efficient method that learns to suppress task-irrelevant components in attention head outputs. Each head is equipped with fixed unit direction vectors, and a shared router (a small MLP) produces per-input weights that control how much each direction is subtracted. The result is a model that becomes highly dependent on the router—disabling routing collapses factual recall and induction performance—while individual heads can be ablated with negligible effect. The model self-organizes into two regimes: early layers adapt routing to the input domain, and late layers apply fixed syntactic pruning. Although directional routing yields substantial perplexity gains (31–56% relative reduction), it does not improve multiple-choice benchmark accuracy, highlighting a separation between likelihood improvement and downstream capability. The findings underscore the importance of coordination mechanisms in managing shared representations and reveal a surprising redundancy of individual attention heads when such coordination is present.

## Method Summary
The proposed architecture augments each attention head with K=4 learnable unit-norm direction vectors in the head’s output space. A shared router per layer takes a mean-pooled representation of the residual stream and, via a 4-layer MLP, produces H×K sigmoid weights that scale the projection of each head’s output onto its directions. The directional suppression operation modifies the head output: \( o_h' = o_h - \sum_k r_{h,k} (o_h \cdot d_{h,k}) d_{h,k} \). This is applied after attention, before concatenation and the residual connection. The full model adds only 3.9% parameters (16.2M) and minimal FLOPs (<0.02%), though mean-pooling introduces a sequential dependency that increases wall-clock time (~13.7% at sequence length 1024). Training uses standard language modeling objectives without auxiliary losses.

## Key Results
- Disabling routing reduces factual recall probabilities to near zero across all eight test prompts and drops induction accuracy from 93.4% to 0.0%, while ablating individual attention heads has negligible effect.
- The model self-organizes without explicit pressure: early layers show domain-adaptive routing (high variance across domains), while late layers apply fixed syntactic pruning (low variance, directions correspond to punctuation, articles, conjunctions).
- Layer-wise knockout reveals Layer 9 as most critical; disabling its routing increases perplexity by +42.6. Overall, routing reduces perplexity by 31–56% relative to an identical baseline, but no improvement is observed on seven multiple-choice benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
#### Mechanism 1: Mitigating cross-domain interference
- **Claim:** Directional routing reduces interference from other domains by selectively suppressing irrelevant feature components in attention outputs.
- **Mechanism:** Each head learns K unit direction vectors \( d_{h,k} \). The router produces weights \( r_{h,k} \). The head output is modified by subtracting projections along these directions: \( o_h' = o_h - \sum_k r_{h,k} (o_h \cdot d_{h,k}) d_{h,k} \). This removes components associated with other domains.
- **Core assumption:** Multi-domain training causes features from different domains to coexist in head activations, creating noise for any given input.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the aim to manage cross-domain interference. Section 8 explains directional routing as a mechanism for managing this interference. Corpus context: prior work notes domain-specific head behavior but lacks suppression mechanisms.
- **Break condition:** If the model is trained on a single, homogeneous domain, the routing mechanism may not develop meaningful direction vectors or may have negligible impact.

#### Mechanism 2: Router as irreplaceable coordinator
- **Claim:** The router acts as an essential coordination layer, making individual attention heads functionally redundant.
- **Mechanism:** By globally controlling suppression across all heads, the router allows the model to distribute computational roles; no single head’s output is essential because the router can reweight or compensate via other heads.
- **Core assumption:** The model can achieve target functionality through multiple head-output combinations when a coordinator modulates them.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3: disabling routing collapses factual recall/induction, while knocking out individual heads has negligible effect; induction accuracy drops from 93.4% to 0.0% with routing OFF but remains 92.5% after ablating the three identified induction heads. This contradicts standard findings that specific induction heads are crucial.
- **Break condition:** If the router’s capacity is too limited (e.g., too few directions or shallow MLP), it may fail to coordinate effectively, making individual heads necessary again.

#### Mechanism 3: Self-organized layer regimes
- **Claim:** Routing spontaneously organizes layers into two regimes—domain-adaptive early layers and syntactic-pruning late layers—optimizing the trade-off between flexibility and noise reduction.
- **Mechanism:** Early layers receive domain-sensitive routing weights (high variance) to tailor suppression to the input type; late layers apply near-constant suppression (low variance) targeting predictable syntactic features, cleaning up final predictions.
- **Core assumption:** Different processing stages benefit from different routing strategies: early semantic discrimination, late syntactic refinement.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4: "The model self-organizes, without explicit pressure, into two regimes..." Section 6.1: Late-layer directions map to punctuation, articles, conjunctions; early layers show domain-sensitive variance (e.g., L0H6 suppresses code vs factual differently).
- **Break condition:** With limited training data or shallow depth, the two-regime pattern may not emerge; layers might adopt similar routing behaviors.

## Foundational Learning
#### Attention Head Redundancy
- **Why needed:** The paper’s finding that heads are interchangeable builds on prior observations that many heads can be pruned without catastrophic loss (Voita et al., 2019). Understanding this baseline helps interpret the extreme case where all heads are redundant due to routing.
- **Quick check:** In a standard transformer, what typically happens if you ablate the highest-attention head in a known circuit (e.g., an induction head)?

#### Cross-Domain Interference and Superposition
- **Why needed:** Routing’s purpose is to manage interference. Superposition (Elhage et al., 2022) describes how models pack more features than dimensions, causing overlapping activations. This provides a theoretical underpinning for why interference occurs.
- **Quick check:** If a model trained on both math problems and stories represents a math token, what mixed signals might appear in its activations?

#### Perplexity vs. Downstream Accuracy
- **Why needed:** The paper reports large perplexity reductions but no consistent gains on multiple-choice benchmarks. This distinction is crucial for interpreting efficiency results.
- **Quick check:** Why might a model that assigns higher probability to in-distribution tokens still perform poorly on a question-answering task?

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component Map
Token embeddings → Residual stream x_i → (per layer): Mean-pool{x_i} → Router MLP → weights r_h,k → For each head: directional suppression (o_h' = o_h - Σ_k r_h,k (o_h·d_h,k) d_h,k) → Concatenate heads → Residual add & LayerNorm → Feed-forward → next layer → Final layer norm → LM head → logits.

#### Critical Path
1. Token embedding produces initial residual vectors.
2. For each layer:
   a. Compute multi-head attention → raw head outputs o_h.
   b. Mean-pool the current residual stream to form router input.
   c. Router MLP produces H×K suppression weights r_h,k.
   d. Apply directional suppression to each head output to get o_h'.
   e. Concatenate suppressed head outputs, add to residual, apply LayerNorm.
   f. Pass through feed-forward network.
3. After final layer, apply LayerNorm and linear LM head to produce logits.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- **Parameter overhead:** +3.9% (16.2M), acceptable.
- **FLOPs:** +0.02% (router MLP cheap), but mean-pool creates wall-clock overhead (~13.7% at seqlen 1024) due to sequential dependency.
- **Mean-pool bottleneck:** Router discards positional info; benefit decays with token position. Per-token routing improves representation but hurt optimization at small scale.
- **Suppression risk:** Misclassified domains may lead to suppression of needed features; training from scratch avoids this by learning aligned directions and router jointly.
- **Interceptability:** Directions are interpretable via vocabulary projection; coarse categories only.

#### Failure Signatures
- Sudden PPL spike when disabling routing in any layer (especially L9).
- Factual recall/induction probabilities collapse to near zero when routing weights zeroed.
- PCA of routing weight vectors shows no domain clustering.
- Router outputs near-constant (all r_h,k ≈ 0.5 or 1) across diverse inputs.
- Per-head routing variance near zero in all layers (no adaptive behavior).

#### First 3 Experiments
1. **Routing ablation**: Set all r_h,k = 0 (or fixed w). Expect factual recall and induction probabilities to drop sharply vs. normal.
2. **Head redundancy check**: Identify candidate induction heads (e.g., via activation patching) and ablate them individually. Accuracy should decrease minimally (<5%), confirming no single head is essential.
3. **Domain sensitivity test**: Collect passages from distinct domains (code, math, prose). Compute routing weight vectors at early layers. Expect clear separation via nearest-centroid classification; swapping routing between domains should increase loss by >5% relative.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Per-token routing scalability** – Does per-token routing improve performance at larger scales (400M+) despite underperforming relative to mean-pooling at 26M parameters? The authors note the 26M model with per-token routing gave worse perplexity (+19%) but state it's unknown at larger scale.
2. **Parameter vs. data matching** – Is the reported 31–56% perplexity reduction attributable to routing or to the inductive bias of allocating 3.9% additional parameters under severe data scarcity? They call for a data-matched comparison (same tokens, extra parameters given to FFN instead of routing).
3. **Multiple-choice benchmark impact** – Does directional routing improve performance on multiple-choice benchmarks or other capability measures beyond standard perplexity when evaluated on a broader suite? They report no improvement on seven benchmarks and speculate routing sharpens predictions without adding knowledge.
4. **Universality of irreplaceable router** – Is the "coordination mechanism is irreplaceable; the components it coordinates are not" pattern universal across all circuit types, or specific to factual recall and induction? They only tested these two circuits.
5. **Scaling of self-organized regimes** – Does the early (adaptive) vs. late (fixed) regime architecture persist at 1B+ parameters, and is Layer 9's criticality stable? They identify this structure in a 433M model but call for validation at larger scales.

## Limitations
- Insufficient training details (learning rate, batch size, warmup) hinder exact replication.
- Reported gains are limited to perplexity and narrow factual/induction tasks; no improvement on multiple-choice benchmarks questions broader utility.
- The router’s mean-pool discards positional information; its impact on long sequences or downstream tasks remains untested.

## Confidence
- **Routing mitigates cross-domain interference:** High – supported by strong ablation and domain-variance analyses.
- **Router is irreplaceable & individual heads are redundant:** Medium – head ablations show no essential heads, but pattern may be specific to the routing architecture or training mixture.
- **Self-organized early (adaptive) vs. late (fixed) regimes:** Low – based on observed correlations; causal evidence lacking.
- **Perplexity reductions and Layer 9 criticality:** High – consistent layer-knockout results across models.

## Next Checks
1. **Router ablation robustness** – Train three independent seeded models; fully disable routing (set all r_h,k = 0). Factual recall and induction accuracy should collapse to near-zero, confirming the claimed dependence.
2. **Alternative coordinator test** – Replace the MLP router with a simple attention-based gating module that also uses mean-pooled context. If perplexity benefits disappear, the specific MLP router is essential, not just any adaptive coordination.
3. **Domain generalization** – Evaluate the final routed model on held-out domains (e.g., biomedical abstracts, legal text) without fine-tuning. Measure whether the routing-induced perplexity reduction transfers or is confined to the training mixture.
