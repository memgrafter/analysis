# Diagnosing Causal Reasoning in Vision-Language Models via Structured Relevance Graphs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.20878
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20878
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: VLCG-augmented prompting improves causal attribution by 6.6% and causal inference by 5.8% with negligible change in QA accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Evaluations of large vision-language models (LVLMs) typically measure only final answer accuracy, failing to distinguish whether a correct answer arises from genuine causal reasoning or from superficial correlations. This paper introduces Vision-Language Causal Graphs (VLCGs) – structured, query‑conditioned representations of causally relevant objects, attributes, relations, and assumptions – and the ViLCaR benchmark, which defines three diagnostic tasks: Causal Attribution (CA), Causal Inference (CI), and Question Answering (QA). Using graph‑aligned metrics, the authors assess whether LVLM failures stem from misidentifying relevant information or from an inherent lack of reasoning capacity.

Experiments with Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B show that augmenting prompts with VLCGs significantly improves CA (0.458 → 0.488, +6.6%) and CI (0.652 → 0.690, +5.8%), while QA accuracy remains almost unchanged (0.763 → 0.768). This decoupling demonstrates that LVLMs can produce correct answers without proper relevance identification, indicating that current causal reasoning limitations are due to insufficient structural guidance rather than a lack of reasoning ability.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes VLCGs to explicitly encode query‑specific causal relevance. The ViLCaR benchmark (~12.5k instances) is constructed by filtering existing VQA and VCR datasets for questions requiring associative, interventional, or counterfactual reasoning. For each (image, question) pair, a pipeline generates a preliminary VLCG using an LVLM, then validates each node/edge with vision models (object detectors, CLIP‑Score) and prunes non‑essential elements via iterative LLM self‑play, followed by optional human validation. The resulting gold VLCGs serve as ground truth for evaluation. The study tests Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B under three prompting conditions – zero‑shot, standard in‑context learning (ICL), and VLCG‑augmented – and measures (a) CA via semantic similarity between model output and graph elements, (b) CI via an LLM judge comparing reasoning against gold assumptions, and (c) QA accuracy against gold answers. No model training is performed; the focus is on diagnosing reasoning via structured guidance.

## Key Results
- CA increased from 0.458 to 0.488 (+6.6% relative improvement)
- CI increased from 0.652 to 0.690 (+5.8% relative improvement)
- QA accuracy changed minimally from 0.763 to 0.768

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** VLCG‑augmented prompting improves causal attribution and inference by acting as a structured relevance prior that guides the model’s attention to causally meaningful visual features.
- **Mechanism:** The VLCG explicitly lists objects, attributes, relations, and scene‑grounded assumptions relevant to the query. When injected into the prompt, it constrains the model’s reasoning to these elements, reducing reliance on spurious correlations present in zero‑shot or few‑shot settings.
- **Core assumption:** LVLMs possess latent causal reasoning capacity but fail to identify the correct relevant features without explicit structural guidance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "injecting structured relevance information significantly improves attribution and inference consistency compared to zero‑shot and standard in‑context learning"
  - [section 6] "VLCG‑augmented prompting improves both Causal Attribution and Causal Inference. CA increases from 0.458 to 0.488 (+6.6% relative improvement), while CI improves from 0.652 to 0.690 (+5.8% relative improvement)."
  - [corpus] Neighbor "Where do Large Vision‑Language Models Look at when Answering Questions?" highlights that LVLMs' visual attention is underexplored, indirectly supporting that unstructured prompts may lead to suboptimal feature selection.
- **Break condition:** If the VLCG contains hallucinated or incorrect nodes/edges (e.g., due to generation errors), the relevance prior may misguide the model, potentially degrading performance on CA/CI and QA.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** VLCGs enhance causal inference by explicitly encoding scene‑grounded assumptions that bridge perceptual evidence and causal conclusions.
- **Mechanism:** The VLCG includes directed dependencies and explicit assumptions (e.g., cultural or contextual knowledge) required to link visual attributes to the answer. These assumptions fill gaps that pure perception cannot resolve, enabling the model to construct a coherent causal chain.
- **Core assumption:** Models lack implicit access to such background assumptions and need them to be stated to integrate them into reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.1] "VLCGs incorporate scene‑grounded assumptions following the structural view of causality. These assumptions encode implicit cultural or contextual knowledge that cannot be directly inferred from visual perception alone."
  - [section 6.2] Qualitative example shows that without VLCG, the model fails to identify role attributes (server/customer) and the associated assumption, leading to incorrect reasoning; with VLCG, it succeeds.
  - [corpus] Assumption: The broader literature (e.g., neighbor "Making medical vision‑language models think causally...") indicates that LVLMs often rely on correlations, suggesting they do not spontaneously generate such assumptions.
- **Break condition:** If the encoded assumptions are incorrect or overly specific (e.g., culturally biased), the model may derive invalid inferences from correct perceptual facts.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The VLCG‑based benchmark disentangles relevance identification from answer correctness, enabling diagnosis of whether LVLM failures stem from misidentifying causally relevant information.
- **Mechanism:** By evaluating Causal Attribution (CA) separately from Question Answering (QA) and adding Causal Inference (CI), the benchmark isolates three stages: selecting relevant variables, composing a valid causal mechanism, and predicting the answer. This reveals cases where models achieve high QA accuracy via spurious correlations while failing CA/CI.
- **Core assumption:** Final answer accuracy alone is an insufficient proxy for genuine causal reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Existing evaluations primarily assess the correctness of the answers, making it unclear whether failures arise from limited reasoning capability or from misidentifying causally relevant information."
  - [section 6.1] "Despite gains in CA and CI, VQA accuracy remains nearly constant (0.763 → 0.768). This decoupling indicates that correct answers can be obtained even when relevance identification is imperfect."
  - [corpus] Neighbor "CausalVLBench" also critiques accuracy‑only evaluation, showing community recognition of this limitation.
- **Break condition:** The disentanglement relies on accurate graph alignment metrics (for CA and CI); if these metrics are noisy or biased, the diagnosis may misattribute failures.

## Foundational Learning

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Dataset Pipeline → VLCG Generator → Grounding Validator → Minimal Causal Pruner → Evaluation Modules

**Critical path:**  
1. Filter raw VQA/VCR questions for causal reasoning types.  
2. Generate preliminary VLCG for each (image, question) using an LVLM.  
3. Validate graph elements against vision models (object detection, CLIP‑Score).  
4. Prune graph to minimal sufficient set via LLM self‑play.  
5. Perform human validation (optional but used).  
6. Split into train/val/test sets.  
7. For each experiment, implement prompting strategy (zero‑shot, ICL, VLCG‑augmented).  
8. Compute CA, CI, QA using corresponding metrics.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- LLM‑based graph generation scales but may hallucinate; grounding mitigates but may discard valid elements vs. costly human annotation.  
- Semantic similarity for CA is cheap but approximate; LLM judge for CI is more accurate but expensive/stochastic.  
- Graph injection format unspecified; engineers must experiment with linearized, JSON, or natural language.  
- Pruning aggressiveness tradeoff: simpler graphs vs. risk of removing necessary elements.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low node retention after grounding indicates poor initial generation or mismatched vision models.  
- High variance in CA/CI across embedding choices or judges suggests metric instability.  
- CA/CI improvements accompanied by QA drop may signal misleading VLCG assumptions.  
- Standard ICL showing improvement would counter paper’s claim and hint dataset artifacts.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline reproduction: Verify that on ViLCaR test set, VLCG‑augmented prompting improves CA and CI while QA stays near constant, for Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B.  
2. Ablation of VLCG components: Remove graph elements (objects only, objects+attributes, without assumptions) to measure contributions to CA/CI gains.  
3. Cross‑model generalization: Apply VLCG‑augmented prompting to other LVLMs (e.g., LLaVA‑1.5‑13B, InstructBLIP‑7B) to test if benefits generalize.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can the graph‑aligned metrics (CA and CI) be refined to reduce reliance on approximate semantic alignment and provide more faithful assessments of causal reasoning?  
  *Basis in paper*: [explicit] The conclusion states that metrics rely on approximate semantic alignment and future work may refine them through LLM‑as‑a‑judge or human‑in‑the‑loop validation.  
  *Why unresolved*: Current CA uses heuristic similarity scores combining BERT, W2V, and unigram overlap, while CI uses an LLM‑based evaluator; both may not perfectly capture true causal structure and could be biased by the evaluator's limitations.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Comparative studies using multiple LLM judges or extensive human evaluations to validate metric scores against ground‑truth causal graphs, measuring correlation and agreement to identify the most reliable alignment approach.

- **Open Question 2**: To what extent do the improvements from VLCG‑augmented prompting generalize across diverse LVLM architectures and model scales?  
  *Basis in paper*: [inferred] Experiments were conducted only on Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B; the abstract mentions "state‑of‑the‑art LVLMs" but results are from a single model.  
  *Why unresolved*: Without testing on other models (e.g., LLaVA, BLIP, GPT‑4V), it is unclear if VLCG benefits are model‑specific, architecture‑dependent, or universal across LVLMs.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Benchmarking multiple LVLMs from different families and parameter scales on ViLCaR with VLCG prompting, reporting CA/CI gains and statistical significance to assess generalizability.

- **Open Question 3**: Why does QA accuracy show minimal improvement despite gains in causal attribution and inference, and what does this decoupling reveal about current VQA benchmarks?  
  *Basis in paper*: [explicit] Results show QA accuracy nearly unchanged (0.763 → 0.768) while CA and CI improved, and the discussion highlights that correct answers can be obtained without proper relevance identification.  
  *Why unresolved*: The decoupling suggests VQA benchmarks may not require genuine causal reasoning, but the underlying reasons (e.g., shortcut learning, task design biases) are not deeply investigated in the paper.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Fine‑grained error analysis comparing instances where QA is correct but CA/CI are low to identify common spurious cues; redesigning VQA tasks to force causal reasoning and measuring correlation between QA and CA/CI.

- **Open Question 4**: How can the inherent subjectivity in human annotations of causal relevance (as seen in ViLCaR's quality control) be mitigated to establish more objective ground‑truth VLCGs?  
  *Basis in paper*: [inferred] Human validation showed disagreement rates below 15% and near‑equal distribution between "agree" and "strongly agree", highlighting subjectivity in causal judgment.  
  *Why unresolved*: Causal relevance can be ambiguous even for humans, affecting benchmark reliability; without consensus, model evaluation may be inconsistent and limit the benchmark's diagnostic power.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Developing clearer annotation guidelines with concrete examples and edge cases, followed by re‑annotation to measure reduced inter‑annotator disagreement; or using probabilistic annotations to capture uncertainty in causal relevance.

## Limitations
- Undisclosed prompt templates, grounding thresholds, and pruning details hinder faithful reproduction.
- Graph‑aligned metrics (CA via semantic similarity, CI via LLM judge) may be noisy or biased.
- Experiments limited to a single LVLM (Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B); generalizability to other architectures is unknown.

## Confidence
- Core empirical claim (VLCG improves CA/CI, QA decoupled): Medium
- Diagnosis interpretation (failures stem from relevance misidentification): Medium
- Benchmark disentanglement (separate CA/CI/QA tasks): Medium
