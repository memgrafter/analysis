# Synthetic Data Generation for Training Diversified Commonsense Reasoning Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18361
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18361
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A synthetic data pipeline (CommonSyn) that improves both quality and diversity for generative commonsense reasoning across multiple LLMs, outperforming human-annotated data and gradient-based selection methods.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality diverse training data for generative commonsense reasoning (GCR). It introduces **CommonSyn**, a synthetic dataset generated through a two-stage process: first, expanding concept sets from CommonGen via an LLM using 2-hop ConceptNet relations; second, generating candidate sentences with three complementary prompting strategies and selecting samples via local (intra-set) and global (dataset-wide) diversity scoring. Models fine-tuned on CommonSyn achieve superior quality–diversity trade-offs on 11 LLMs, demonstrating consistent gains over vanilla models and those trained on the original human-annotated CommonGen. The approach generalizes to other GCR tasks without catastrophic forgetting and is more computationally efficient than gradient-based data selection.

## Method Summary
CommonSyn is created via a three-module pipeline. First, the **Concept Expansion Module** uses a strong LLM (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) to take two random “anchor” concepts from a CommonGen seed set and add 1–3 “bridge” concepts, guided by 2-hop ConceptNet relations to ensure coherence. Second, the **Candidate Generation Module** applies three prompting strategies to each expanded set: `Ddyn` (dynamic few-shot) for high quality, `Dms` (multi-sentence few-shot) for lexical diversity, and `DCoT` (chain-of-thought) for semantic depth. Outputs are filtered for keyword coverage and length. Third, the **Two-Stage Selection Module** filters by a quality score `Q(y)≥4` (from Gemini-2.5-Flash) and selects top-8 sentences per set by local diversity (`1 - mean pairwise cosine` using SimCSE). From this pool, it ranks all samples by a joint score `S(s) = Q(s) + D_global(s)`, where `D_global(s)` measures uniqueness against the entire pool. The top 83,184 samples form the final CommonSyn dataset for supervised fine-tuning.

## Key Results
- Models fine-tuned on CommonSyn achieve a better **quality–diversity Pareto frontier** than those trained on CommonGen or other synthetic baselines across 11 LLMs.
- CommonSyn significantly improves both **quality** (Win-Tie rate, Coverage) and **diversity** (Self-CosSim, Vendi score) simultaneously, countering the typical trade-off.
- The method generalizes to other GCR tasks (e.g., ComVE, α-NLG) without catastrophic forgetting and outperforms computationally expensive gradient-based data selection techniques.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: 2-Seed Expansion Creates Novel, Coherent Concept Sets
- **Claim:** Expanding seed concept pairs from CommonGen using an LLM (guided by 2-hop ConceptNet relations) creates novel, coherent concept sets with broader coverage than human annotations or direct generation.
- **Mechanism:** Two randomly selected concepts from a CommonGen seed act as "anchors." An LLM is prompted to add 1–3 bridge concepts, forming a new set. This leverages the observation that 50% of CommonGen concept sets are fully connected within 2-hops, ensuring semantic coherence while exploring new combinations.
- **Core assumption:** Concepts within 2-hops in ConceptNet can form a plausible, single-scenario event suitable for one sentence.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "first expanding concept sets from CommonGen using an LLM"; [section 3.2] describes the anchor and bridge process; [table 6] shows "2-Seed Expansion" yields the highest number of unique concepts (4,260) and maintains a high unseen concept-triple rate (75.28%).
- **Break condition:** If the LLM frequently generates concepts that break the 2-hop coherence constraint, novel sets become implausible, reducing downstream quality.

### Mechanism 2: Complementary Prompting Strategies Populate Quality–Diversity Space
- **Claim:** Generating candidate sentences via three distinct prompting strategies (Dynamic Few-Shot, Multi-sentence Few-shot, Chain-of-Thought) populates different regions of the quality–diversity (Q-D) space, providing a diverse candidate pool.
- **Mechanism:** Each strategy biases generation differently: `Ddyn` favors high quality but low diversity (mode collapse); `Dms` boosts lexical/syntactic diversity but may reduce quality; `DCoT` encourages semantic depth via reasoning, improving coverage. Their union creates a pool where individual weaknesses are compensated by other strategies' strengths.
- **Core assumption:** Different prompting paradigms induce qualitatively different generation distributions in Q-D space, and their union is a superset of high-potential candidates.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "generating sentences with three prompting strategies"; [section 3.3] describes each strategy's observed Q-D characteristics; [table 3] shows `Ddyn` highest Win-Tie, `Dms` highest diversity, `DCoT` highest Coverage; pooled candidates underperform, necessitating selection.
- **Break condition:** If one strategy's outputs consistently dominate the others' high-Q-D samples, the pool's diversity becomes limited, reducing the effectiveness of subsequent selection.

### Mechanism 3: Two-Stage Selection Balances Local and Global Diversity
- **Claim:** A two-stage selection process—first maximizing local diversity per concept set, then ranking globally by a joint quality + global diversity score—constructs a dataset that improves both quality and diversity simultaneously.
- **Mechanism:** Stage 1: For each concept set's candidate pool, filter by a quality threshold (LLM score) and select top-`k` sentences by *local* diversity (low intra-set cosine similarity). Stage 2: From all locally selected sentences, compute each sample's *global* diversity (low similarity to all other samples) and rank by `S(s) = Q(s) + D_global(s)`. This balances intra-set variety with dataset-wide semantic coverage.
- **Core assumption:** Maximizing diversity at both granularities (per-concept-set and cross-dataset) is necessary and sufficient for a model to learn to generate diverse *and* high-quality outputs; a simple sum score effectively trades off the two.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "selecting samples with local (intra-set) and global (dataset-wide) diversity scoring"; [section 3.4] defines `D_local`, `D_global`, and `S(s)`; [table 7] shows `CommonSyn` outperforms gradient-based `Q&G-based` in quality (47.3 vs. 45.7 Win-Tie) while matching diversity.
- **Break condition:** If local selection discards too many high-quality but semantically central samples, or if global ranking overemphasizes marginal diversity, the dataset's Q-D balance degrades.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Generative Commonsense Reasoning (GCR)**
  - **Why needed:** The paper's goal is to improve models on GCR tasks (e.g., CommonGen), which require generating a *set* of sentences from concept sets that are individually plausible (quality) and collectively varied (diversity). Understanding this dual objective is prerequisite.
  - **Quick check:** What distinguishes a "diversified" GCR model from one that just generates plausible sentences?

- **Concept: Quality–Diversity Trade-off in NLG**
  - **Why needed:** The core contribution is a method that *jointly* improves both axes, countering the typical tension where boosting one harms the other. Evaluating success requires understanding metrics like Win-Tie/Coverage (quality) and Self-CosSim/Vendi (diversity).
  - **Quick check:** Why might a model that generates highly diverse outputs (low Self-CosSim) still perform poorly on the GCR task?

- **Concept: Pitfalls of Synthetic Data (Model Collapse, Bias)**
  - **Why needed:** The method is designed to avoid common synthetic data failures: low structural diversity, style bias, and model collapse. The two-stage selection aims to produce data that teaches diverse reasoning, not just surface diversity.
  - **Quick check:** How could a synthetic dataset that appears diverse still cause a model to "forget" general reasoning skills (catastrophic forgetting)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Seed Concepts -> 2-Seed Expansion (LLM) -> Expanded Sets -> Candidate Generation (3 Prompting Strategies) -> Filtered Candidate Pool -> Local Selection (Quality filter + Local Diversity top-k) -> Global Ranking (by S(s) = Q + D_global) -> CommonSyn Dataset
- **Critical path:** Seed Concepts from CommonGen → Expansion to novel triples → Generation of multiple candidates per set via three prompts → Per-set local diversity selection → Cross-set global ranking → Final dataset → Fine-tune target LLM.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Local vs. Global Diversity:** Maximizing intra-set variety might reduce cross-set semantic coverage, and vice versa. The joint score `S(s)` is a heuristic balance.
  - **Quality Scorer Cost:** Using a powerful LLM (Gemini-2.5-Flash) for per-sentence scoring is expensive but more reliable than perplexity or rule-based filters.
  - **Computational Efficiency:** Embedding-based diversity (SimCSE) is cheaper than gradient-based clustering (Jung et al., 2025), which requires forward/backward passes on a proxy model.
  - **Dataset Size vs. Concept Set Count:** Fixed total samples can be distributed across more concept sets (higher scenario diversity) or more sentences per set (higher intra-set diversity). Table 10 shows stable performance across 5k–20k unique sets, suggesting efficient use of concept variety.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Low Quality:** High diversity but poor Win-Tie/Coverage indicates selection failed to enforce plausibility (e.g., `Dms` pooled alone).
  - **Low Diversity:** High quality but high Self-CosSim/S-BLEU indicates mode collapse (e.g., `Ddyn` alone).
  - **Concept Omission:** Sentences missing input keywords (filter failure).
  - **Catastrophic Forgetting:** On downstream tasks (e.g., ComVE, α-NLG), quality drops despite good CommonGen metrics, indicating overfitting to the synthetic distribution.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replicate Concept Expansion:** Take 1,000 CommonGen seed sets, apply 2-seed expansion (with `Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct`). Measure (a) unique concepts generated, (b) % of expanded sets where all concepts are within 2-hops in ConceptNet, (c) human plausibility rating of a sample.
  2. **Validate Prompting Strategies:** For 500 expanded sets, generate candidates via `Ddyn`, `Dms`, `DCoT` (N=4 each). Compute per-set average quality (LLM score) and diversity (pairwise cosine). Confirm `Ddyn` high-quality/low-diversity, `Dms` low-quality/high-diversity, `DCoT` moderate/high coverage.
  3. **Implement Selection Pipeline:** On the pooled candidates from Exp. 2, apply: (a) local filter (Q>=4), (b) top-8 local diversity select per set, (c) global ranking by `Q + D_global`. Compare final set's aggregate Q-D metrics against baselines (random select, quality-only, diversity-only) using a small proxy model fine-tune (e.g., `Llama-3.1-8B` for 1 epoch). Expect `CommonSyn` pipeline to achieve the best Pareto frontier.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the CommonSyn generation pipeline be extended to multilingual settings, and what modifications are needed to maintain the quality–diversity trade-off in languages with different morphological richness and commonsense knowledge resources?
- **Basis:** The Limitations section explicitly states the dataset is restricted to English due to scarce high-quality human reference benchmarks in other languages, and that extending to multilingual settings is a desirable direction.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper provides no methodology or experiments for multilingual adaptation. Reliance on ConceptNet (primarily English) and English-only benchmarks creates a barrier. It is unknown whether the two-stage selection and prompting strategies would transfer to languages with richer morphology or differing commonsense structures.
- **Needed evidence:** A replication study creating CommonSyn-style datasets for at least two typologically diverse languages (e.g., Russian, Japanese), using appropriate multilingual concept graphs and benchmarks. Fine-tune LLMs on these and measure the quality–diversity Pareto frontier against baselines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent are the reported quality–diversity improvements and generalization capabilities of CommonSyn-dependent on the specific choice of automated evaluator (GPT-4o) for both data scoring and final Win-Tie assessment, and would alternative strong LLM judges alter the conclusions?
- **Basis:** The Limitations acknowledge potential intrinsic biases in using GPT-4o as an automated evaluator and state that verifying findings with other comparable or superior LLMs remains an important future direction. GPT-4o is used as the quality scorer and pairwise judge.
- **Why unresolved:** The entire data selection strategy and primary evaluation metric are contingent on GPT-4o's judgments. If GPT-4o has systematic biases in scoring "commonness" or "concept coverage," the selected dataset and measured improvements could be an artifact. No evaluator robustness analysis is conducted.
- **Needed evidence:** A cross-evaluator study where CommonSyn is re-created using different LLM scorers (e.g., Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Gemini-2.0-Flash). Re-evaluate final model outputs from CommonSyn-finetuned models using multiple LLM judges. The stability of the quality–diversity Pareto improvements and selection strategy rankings across evaluators must be shown.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the observed "catastrophic forgetting" when fine-tuning on human CommonGen data, and its mitigation by CommonSyn, stem primarily from differences in dataset *style* (e.g., descriptive elaboration vs. concise action-centric sentences) versus differences in *commonsense knowledge coverage*, and can the effect be isolated?
- **Basis:** The paper shows CommonGen-finetuned models suffer severe quality drops on unseen GCR tasks while CommonSyn does not. A case study suggests CommonGen models generate stylistic over-elaboration or hallucinations. The 2-Seed Expansion is designed to create "event-centric" concept sets, possibly influencing sentence style differently. However, the paper does not disentangle style from knowledge.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper attributes poor generalization to "format overfitting" but lacks controlled experiments. It is possible that CommonGen's human-authored sentences cover a narrower set of commonsense *relations* (e.g., more static descriptive scenes) compared to the dynamic, action-chain scenarios from expansion. The improvement could be due to broader relational coverage, better stylistic alignment, or both.
- **Needed evidence:** A controlled ablation where the *style* of CommonGen sentences is artificially altered (e.g., paraphrased to be more concise/action-oriented) and the *knowledge graph coverage* is measured (e.g., diversity of ConceptNet relation types). Fine-tune models on these modified versions and test on downstream tasks. If style-change alone recovers generalization, it points to format; if knowledge-coverage change is key, it points to semantics.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the fundamental reason that a computationally efficient, embedding-based global diversity selection (using 1−average cosine similarity) outperforms gradient-based data selection (Jung et al., 2025) on the quality–diversity trade-off for GCR, despite gradients being theoretically model-aware?
- **Basis:** The paper directly compares its two-stage selection (quality score + global embedding diversity) against a gradient-based method ("Prismatic Synthesis" / Q&G-based). Table 7 and Figure 2 show CommonSyn achieves higher quality at matched diversity. The paper states gradient methods are "computationally expensive" and "model-dependent."
- **Why unresolved:** While computational cost is cited, the *fundamental reason* for the performance gap is not analyzed. Is it that cosine similarity of SimCSE embeddings better captures *semantic* diversity relevant to commonsense scenarios, whereas gradient clustering captures *influence* diversity which may not correlate with semantic variety? Or does the global ranking function `S(s)=Q(s)+D_global(s)` implicitly optimize for a different objective than minimizing gradient-based sparsity? The paper does not probe *why* gradients underperform for this specific task.
- **Needed evidence:** An analysis correlating different diversity metrics (embedding-based Self-CosSim, Vendi score, gradient-based cluster entropy) with downstream model quality–diversity trade-offs across multiple tasks. Ablating the components of `S(s)` (using only `D_global`, only `Q`, or a weighted combination) and measuring the resulting model's performance would clarify the role of the joint score. Comparing the *type* of diversity promoted by gradients vs. embeddings by visualizing selected samples would provide insight.

## Limitations
- Synthetic data quality hinges on Qwen-72B and Gemini-2.5; their biases may transfer to the final dataset and models.
- SimCSE-based diversity may miss subtle semantic nuances relevant to commonsense reasoning.
- The global O(N²) selection step scales poorly for very large candidate pools.
- The 2‑seed expansion’s 2‑hop assumption lacks direct human plausibility validation.
- Ethical risks (e.g., amplification of social biases in ConceptNet) are not examined.

## Confidence
- **Claim A (2‑seed expansion produces coherent, high‑coverage sets):** Medium. Table 6 reports unique concepts and unseen triples, but human plausibility is not measured.
- **Claim B (Three prompts yield complementary Q‑D regions):** High. Table 3 demonstrates distinct profiles; naive pooling underperforms, confirming the need for selection.
- **Claim C (Two‑stage selection balances Q‑D and improves models):** High. Consistent gains across 11 LLMs (Tables 3, 7, Figure 2) and ablations validate the mechanism.
- **Claim D (CommonSyn mitigates catastrophic forgetting):** Medium. Table 4 shows improved generalization, but the exact cause (style vs. knowledge) is unresolved (Open Question 3).
