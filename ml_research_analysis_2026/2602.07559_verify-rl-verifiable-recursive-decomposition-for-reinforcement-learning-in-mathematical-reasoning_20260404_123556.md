# VERIFY-RL: Verifiable Recursive Decomposition for Reinforcement Learning in Mathematical Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07559
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07559
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: 100% verification‑by‑construction (vs 78.4% heuristic); D5 accuracy +112% relative (32%→68%); overall +40% relative improvement.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement Learning (RL) for mathematical reasoning often relies on heuristic decomposition to create subproblems, but these can be invalid and harm training. VERIFY-RL introduces a rule‑based decomposition framework for symbolic differentiation, using three automatically verifiable properties—strictly decreasing structural complexity, solution containment, and formal rule derivation—to guarantee valid parent‑child relationships by construction. As a result, every generated subproblem is provably easier and contributes directly to solving its parent. On challenging D5 problems, VERIFY-RL more than doubles accuracy (from 32% to 68%), a 112% relative gain, and achieves a 40% relative improvement overall. Ablation studies confirm that all three verification properties are essential, with the easiness property (V1) contributing the largest benefit.

## Method Summary
VERIFY-RL constructs a verified curriculum for RL training through a pipeline of symbolic operations. First, a problem generator creates differentiable expressions of varying nesting depth (δ). The decomposition engine then recursively applies calculus rules (chain, product, sum) to break each target problem into child subproblems. Every parent‑child pair is checked against three verification properties: V1 ensures the child’s nesting depth is strictly lower; V2 confirms the child’s derivative appears as a factor or term in the parent’s derivative; V3 verifies the child is a structural subtree and the relation matches a calculus rule. Only verified nodes are retained. A curriculum builder topologically sorts all unique subproblems by ascending δ, creating a staged schedule (e.g., introduce D1–D2 first, then D3, D4, D5). Finally, an RL trainer (using GRPO or PPO) trains a language model on this ordered curriculum with a simple reward: 1.0 for correct derivative and 0.1 for proper `\boxed{}` formatting. This pipeline guarantees that every training example is a valid, easier subproblem, enabling stable curriculum learning.

## Key Results
- VERIFY-RL achieves **100% verification‑by‑construction**, compared to **78.4%** for heuristic decomposition methods.
- On the hardest difficulty level (D5), accuracy jumps from **32% to 68%**, a **112% relative increase**.
- Across all levels, the method yields a **~40% relative improvement** in overall accuracy.
- Ablations show that removing V1 (easiness) causes the largest drop (~17 points), followed by V2 (~10 points) and V3 (~5 points).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Strictly Decreasing Complexity
**Claim**: Child problems have strictly lower structural complexity than parents, ensuring a proper curriculum order.  
**Mechanism**: The nesting depth δ counts function applications; calculus rules (e.g., chain rule) reduce depth by exactly 1 because the inner function becomes the child. Theorem 1 proves δ(parent)=1+δ(child).  
**Core assumption**: Nesting depth correlates with human‑perceived difficulty and model solvability.  
**Evidence**: The abstract notes subproblems are “genuinely easier.” Table 2 shows depth decreasing from D4 to D1. Heuristic methods fail V1 in 5.8% of cases.  
**Break condition**: δ(child) ≥ δ(parent) invalidates V1 and disrupts curriculum ordering.

### Mechanism 2: Solution Containment
**Claim**: The child’s solution appears as a factor or term in the parent’s solution, enabling direct knowledge transfer.  
**Mechanism**: Calculus rules structurally embed the child’s derivative. For chain rule: d/dx[f(g(x))] = f’(g(x))·g’(x), so σ(child)=g’(x) is a multiplicative factor.  
**Core assumption**: Solving subproblems yields reusable components for the parent.  
**Evidence**: Definition 5 and Theorem 1 prove σ(parent) contains σ(child). Removing V2 degrades accuracy by 10.4 points (Section 6.2).  
**Break condition**: If σ(child) is not a factor/term (e.g., due to algebraic manipulation errors), V2 fails and training on that child provides no transfer benefit.

### Mechanism 3: Formal Rule Derivation
**Claim**: Parent‑child relationships derive from formal calculus rules, ensuring mathematical grounding.  
**Mechanism**: Decomposition operators (chain, product, sum) are applied only when the parent expression matches the rule’s structural template (e.g., f(g(x)) for chain). This is verified via pattern matching.  
**Core assumption**: Human‑curated calculus rules capture all valid decomposition patterns; heuristics can produce spurious relationships.  
**Evidence**: Definition 6 (V3) and Theorem 1‑3 show structural containment (c ⊑ p). Heuristic methods fail V3 in 8.7% of cases; VERIFY‑RL achieves 100% by construction.  
**Break condition**: If the decomposition does not match any rule template (e.g., arbitrary “easier” variants), V3 fails, risking learning invalid patterns.

## Foundational Learning
- **Symbolic Differentiation Rules**: Why needed: These rules define the only valid ways to break down a derivative problem; they are the basis for rule‑based decomposition.  
  *Quick check*: Given `d/dx[sin(x^2 * cos(x))]`, which rule applies first and what is the immediate child subproblem?  
- **Nesting Depth as Complexity Measure**: Why needed: V1 requires a formal, automatable difficulty metric; nesting depth (δ) counts function applications to quantify structural complexity.  
  *Quick check*: Which has higher nesting depth: `d/dx[exp(sin(x))]` or `d/dx[sin(x) + cos(x)]`?  
- **Expression Trees and Subtree Relationships**: Why needed: V3 (structural relatedness) and V2 (solution containment) rely on syntactic containment; the child’s expression tree must be a subtree of the parent’s.  
  *Quick check*: Is `x^2` a structural subtree of the expression tree for `d/dx[log(x^2 + 1)]`?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Problem Generator → Decomposition Engine → Verifier (V1,V2,V3) → Curriculum Builder → RL Trainer  

**Critical path**: Decompose → Verify (V1‑V3) → Build Curriculum → RL Training. Any verification failure triggers re‑decomposition.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Rule‑based vs Heuristic*: Guarantees (100% valid) vs flexibility (may miss valid decompositions).  
- *Depth vs Curriculum size*: Full depth gives best gains but expands the curriculum exponentially.  
- *Reward complexity*: Simple base reward (accuracy+format) suffices; adding process‑based rewards yields only ~+1 point.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Non‑zero verification failure rate indicates non‑rule‑based decomposition.  
- Sudden token‑usage spikes at specific difficulties (e.g., D4) suggest model confusion from invalid subproblems.  
- Frequent chain‑rule factor omission (38.1% of errors, Table 10) shows inadequate learning of rule application.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Main training**: Train Qwen3‑1.5B with GRPO on the verified curriculum; compare accuracy per difficulty to a baseline.  
2. **Ablation – Verification properties**: Remove V1, V2, or V3 individually and retrain; expect largest drop for V1.  
3. **Verification success rate comparison**: Apply heuristic (LADDER‑style) decomposition to the same targets; compute joint V1‑V3 pass rate (expected ~78.4% vs 100%).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to other mathematical domains?**  
   *Basis*: The “Limitations and Future Work” explicitly mentions extending VERIFY‑RL to integration, differential equations, and proofs.  
   *Why unresolved*: The framework is only evaluated on symbolic differentiation; no empirical or theoretical analysis exists for other domains.  
   *What evidence*: Implement VERIFY‑RL in another domain with formal rule systems, demonstrate automatic V1‑V3 satisfaction, and show training gains.

2. **Scaling to larger models and deeper problems?**  
   *Basis*: Limitations note experiments used models ≤3B and a maximum depth D5; decomposition grows exponentially.  
   *Why unresolved*: Interaction between model scale, decomposition depth, and verification success is unexplored.  
   *What evidence*: Train larger models (7B, 13B, 70B) on the same curriculum and measure accuracy across D1–D5; check if verification‑by‑construction holds for deeper trees.

3. **Generalization to non‑mathematical reasoning domains?**  
   *Basis*: The conclusion suggests the principle may generalize to any domain with compositional structure.  
   *Why unresolved*: No experiments outside mathematics; formal verification of decomposition might be harder.  
   *What evidence*: Identify a domain with a formal, compositional rule system (e.g., compiler syntax translations, logical inference), define V1‑V3 analogs, implement rule‑based decomposition, and compare to heuristics.

4. **Causes of training interference during curriculum transitions?**  
   *Basis*: Table 4 and text describe reward dips, KL spikes, and temporary regressions when new levels are introduced.  
   *Why unresolved*: No mechanistic analysis (e.g., forgetting, optimization instability).  
   *What evidence*: Vary pacing of curriculum introduction and monitor metrics (gradient norms, parameter changes, CCA) to pinpoint the source of dips/spikes.

5. **Improving heuristic decomposition to approach 100% verification?**  
   *Basis*: The paper contrasts VERIFY‑RL’s 100% with LADDER’s 78.4% verification, but does not attempt to bridge the gap.  
   *Why unresolved*: It is unknown whether the heuristic gap can be narrowed and whether that yields proportional performance gains.  
   *What evidence*: Enhance heuristic methods (better prompting, verifier‑guided filtering) to increase verification rate, then measure the impact on RL training accuracy and model the verification‑accuracy relationship.

## Limitations
- The exact set of 50 target problems is unknown; random generation may alter curriculum distribution and results.
- Curriculum sampling distribution within each stage is unspecified (assumed uniform), potentially affecting training stability.
- Verification property V2 relies on SymPy equivalence checks that can fail for algebraically equivalent expressions, leading to false failures.
- The study is confined to symbolic differentiation; generalizability to other domains remains unproven.
