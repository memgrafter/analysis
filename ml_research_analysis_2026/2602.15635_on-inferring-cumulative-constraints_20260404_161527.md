# On inferring cumulative constraints

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15635
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15635
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** A preprocessing technique that infers new cumulative constraints from existing ones via cover discovery and lifting reduces solving time by up to an order of magnitude on favorable RCPSP/RCPSP‑max instances and discovers 25 new lower bounds.

## Executive Summary
Constraint programming models for resource-constrained scheduling often propagate cumulative constraints independently, missing interactions across resources that cause severe slowdown. This paper introduces a method to infer additional cumulative constraints at preprocessing by representing tasks as occupancy vectors, exposing a polyhedral structure. It enumerates "covers" (task subsets that cannot run in parallel) and strengthens their inequalities via lifting, then injects the resulting constraints into the model. On standard RCPSP and RCPSP/max benchmarks, the approach yields substantial improvements: solving time drops by up to a factor of ten on many instances, lower bounds improve consistently, and it proves optimality for previously unsolved instances or finds better solutions, including eight bounds obtained directly from inferred constraint capacity limits without any search.

## Method Summary
The method takes a constraint programming model with cumulative constraints (∑ rᵢ⟨sᵢ|dᵢ⟩ ≤ C) and transforms it at the root node. First, each task’s start time and duration are encoded as a binary occupancy vector ⟨sᵢ|dᵢ⟩, turning the cumulative constraint into a linear inequality over a 0–1 polyhedron P(A,b). Second, it enumerates candidate covers—subsets of tasks whose total resource use exceeds capacity for some resource—using efficient pairwise and grouping heuristics, ranking them by a capacity bound L(r,d,C)=∑dᵢ/C. Third, for each selected cover, a lifting procedure strengthens the initial cover inequality x(C) ≤ |C|–1 by computing maximal coefficients for other tasks via integer subproblems, yielding a valid inequality that translates back to a new cumulative constraint. Finally, the strongest inferred constraints (up to N_out=5) are added to the model before search begins. The preprocessing is governed by parameters: up to N_cover=100 covers are enumerated, and lifting is performed greedily in order of increasing task duration.

## Key Results
- Solving time reduced by up to an order of magnitude on many RCPSP/RCPSP‑max instances; dual integrals (lower bound growth) improve consistently across benchmark suites.
- Discovers 25 new lower bounds and five new best known solutions on standard benchmarks (UBO100, UBO200, UBO1000, PSPLIB).
- Eight lower bounds are obtained directly from the capacity bounds of inferred constraints without any search, demonstrating the strength of the polyhedral perspective.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Reformulating cumulative constraints as linear inequalities over occupancy vectors exposes a polyhedral structure that allows generating valid inequalities via standard integer programming techniques.  
**Mechanism:** Each task’s start time sᵢ and duration dᵢ are represented by a binary occupancy vector ⟨sᵢ|dᵢ⟩(t) indicating resource occupation at time t. The cumulative constraint ∑ rᵢ⟨sᵢ|dᵢ⟩ ≤ C becomes a linear inequality ∑ rᵢ ⟨sᵢ|dᵢ⟩(t) ≤ C for all t. This maps the scheduling model to a 0–1 polyhedron P(A,b) where rows of A encode resource usage patterns and b holds capacities. Lemma 7 shows that any valid inequality (π,π₀) for P(A,b) implies a new cumulative constraint ∑ πᵢ⟨sᵢ|dᵢ⟩ ≤ π₀.  
**Core assumption:** Occupancy vectors correctly capture temporal overlap and resource consumption, and the polyhedron P(A,b) faithfully represents all feasible assignments under the original cumulative constraints.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states the interpretation as linear inequalities over occupancy vectors; [Section 3] defines occupancy vectors (Definition 6), proves Lemma 7, and Example 9 demonstrates inference; [corpus] neighboring papers address online constrained optimization and bandit feedback, not this polyhedral correspondence.  
**Break condition:** If tasks have variable resource consumptions or durations (e.g., multi‑mode RCPSP), the occupancy‑vector representation becomes nonlinear, and the polyhedral equivalence breaks down.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Efficiently enumerating cover sets (task subsets that cannot run in parallel) provides seed inequalities that are then strengthened via lifting to capture multi‑resource interactions.  
**Mechanism:** A cover C satisfies ∑_{i∈C} aᵢ > b for at least one original cumulative constraint (∑ aᵢ⟨sᵢ|dᵢ⟩ ≤ b). The paper enumerates short covers (pairs/triples via pairwise sums and threshold checks) and long covers (uniform groups by resource consumption), each prioritized by the capacity bound L(r,d,C)=∑dᵢ/C. These covers serve as starting points for lifting.  
**Core assumption:** The most promising covers for bound improvement are those with high capacity bound (large total duration relative to their cover inequality’s right‑hand side).  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] mentions discovering covers; [Section 4] details Algorithm 1: short covers via O(n²) pairwise checks and O(bn²) ternary completion; long covers via grouping by resource value v and selecting top‑k longest/shortest tasks; capacity bound L is used for ranking.  
**Break condition:** If covers are not indicative of multi‑resource conflict (e.g., in sparse disjointness graphs), enumerating many covers yields little pruning, and preprocessing overhead dominates without benefit.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Lifting initial cover inequalities with respect to the polyhedron P(A,b) strengthens them by computing maximal coefficients for previously uncensored variables, yielding valid cumulative constraints that prune more effectively than the original covers.  
**Mechanism:** Starting from the cover inequality x(C) ≤ |C|–1, the lifting procedure iteratively adds one task i at a time (chosen as the shortest remaining) and computes πᵢ = π₀ – v∗, where v∗ is the optimal value of the lifting subproblem: maximize ∑_{c∈C} π_c x_c subject to ∑_{c∈C} a_{j,c} x_c ≤ bⱼ for all j and x_c ∈ {0,1}. This computes the largest πᵢ such that the inequality remains valid. The process repeats until all variables are either lifted or skipped (if already covered by a previously added constraint).  
**Core assumption:** Lifting in the order of increasing duration (shortest first) yields tighter inequalities for the scheduling context, as shorter tasks contribute more to potential overlap in a fixed horizon.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] mentions strengthening cover inequalities with lifting; [Section 2] provides the general lifting procedure (Algorithm 2 description) and Example 5 showing improvement; [Section 4] details Algorithm 2 with skipping and dominance checks.  
**Break condition:** If the lifting subproblems are too expensive to solve (NP‑hard in general), the approach may scale poorly for large cover sizes or many resources; the paper mitigates this via skipping and early termination.

## Architecture Onboarding
Preprocessor -> Cover Generator (Algorithm 1) -> Lifting Engine (Algorithm 2) -> CP Solver (e.g., Pumpkin or CP‑SAT)

**Critical path:** Cover enumeration → lifting per cover → constraint selection (Top K by capacity bound) → injection into model → CP solver execution. Lifting is the bottleneck; the skipping condition in Algorithm 2 avoids redundant lifting for subsets already covered by stronger constraints.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Preprocessing cost vs. bound improvement: Parameters N_cover (≤100) and N_out (≤5) limit covers considered and constraints added. Smaller values reduce overhead but may miss beneficial constraints.  
- Cover selection metric: Capacity bound L(r,d,C) prioritizes constraints that tighten makespan lower bounds, but may overlook constraints that prune search more effectively (e.g., instance #40 in UBO50 where disjointness graph sparsity hurt performance).  
- Constraint dominance: Discarding inferred constraints that are component‑wise dominated by existing model constraints avoids redundancy.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High preprocessing time with little search improvement: Indicates generated constraints are too weak or dominated; lifting time ratio is a red flag (Figure 1).  
- Degraded primal or dual integral: Added constraints overly restrict the search space without contributing to bound tightening (Figure 2a shows some primal integral degradation).  
- Poor constraint selection: When the best inferred constraint has large capacity (>8), it often fails to improve search (Figure 3). Selection based solely on individual constraint quality may miss global conflict coverage (Appendix B analysis of UBO50 #40).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Preprocessing overhead benchmark:** Run Algorithms 1–2 with varying N_cover and N_out on a set of instances; measure preprocessing time and ensure it is typically a few seconds (Figure 4) to avoid outweighing search gains.  
2. **Dual integral comparison:** Against baseline without lifting, compute dual integrals across benchmark suites (Figure 2b). A successful run shows lower dual integral (faster bound tightening).  
3. **Constraint validation on a favorable instance:** Pick an instance where lifting yields a new lower bound without search (e.g., UBO200 #3 in Table 4). Verify the bound equals the capacity bound L of an inferred constraint and that the constraint is not dominated.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Dynamic integration during search:** How can inferred cumulative constraints be integrated during search (e.g., at restarts) while ensuring they remain valid after backtracking? The paper notes that a constraint lifted from a conflicting assignment does not necessarily propagate after backtracking, proposing tighter coupling with the search loop as future work.
2. **Extension to multi‑mode RCPSP:** Can the lifting approach be extended to multi‑mode RCPSP, where task durations and resource consumptions are decision variables, without prohibitive overhead? The current model assumes fixed ri and di; variable demands/durations introduce nonlinearities that break the polyhedral equivalence.
3. **Comparison with UNL:** Does the lifting approach fully subsume the "Unite and Lead" (UNL) method, or are there structural problem features where UNL’s dynamic, search‑based clique discovery provides strictly stronger inferences? The paper shows UNL outperforms lifting on some instances (e.g., UBO50 #40) but lacks formal characterization of the gap.
4. **Primal vs. dual impact:** Why does the inferred constraint set consistently improve dual integrals (lower bounds) but show more variable, often weaker, impact on primal integrals (solution finding)? The paper speculates that stronger constraints tighten the search space but do not directly guide the solver toward high‑quality solutions; the exact mechanism linking constraint properties to primal search efficiency is not analyzed.
5. **Constraint selection heuristic:** Is the current heuristic for selecting which lifted constraints to add (based on capacity bound L) optimal, or does it systematically miss high‑impact constraints? The UBO50 #40 failure case suggests prioritizing total duration over coverage diversity may be suboptimal; no alternative selection strategy is evaluated.

## Limitations
- The lifting uses a greedy shortest‑first heuristic and may miss tighter inequalities, especially for large cover sets.
- Preprocessing overhead can dominate total runtime on large or sparse instances; some cases (e.g., UBO50 #40) show no benefit or degradation.
- The occupancy‑vector representation assumes fixed task durations and resource demands; it does not extend to multi‑mode RCPSP or time‑varying capacities without losing the polyhedral equivalence.

## Confidence
- **Polyhedral mapping ( occupancy vectors ↔ valid inequalities ):** High – supported by Lemma 7.
- **Cover enumeration + lifting:** Medium – empirical evidence is strong on many benchmarks, but heuristic lifting and occasional failures limit certainty.
- **Performance gains on RCPSP/RCPSP‑max:** Medium‑High – consistent dual‑integral improvements and numerous new bounds support the claim, though generalizability beyond these families remains to be proven.

## Next Checks
1. **Solver‑independent replication:** Execute the full pipeline with CP‑SAT alone on the entire RCPSP and RCPSP‑max benchmark suites, using Ncover=100 and Nout=5. Compare solving times, dual integrals, and new bounds to the paper’s reported values.
2. **Cover‑ranking ablation:** Replace the capacity‑bound L with alternative ranking functions—(a) total resource demand of the cover, (b) estimated constraint violation from random schedules—and measure effects on preprocessing cost, number of added constraints, and final solving performance.
3. **Extension to multi‑mode scheduling:** Adapt the occupancy‑vector encoding to handle mode choices (e.g., via auxiliary binaries) and test on a subset of multi‑mode RCPSP instances. Verify that inferred constraints remain valid and assess net benefit on solving time or bound quality.
