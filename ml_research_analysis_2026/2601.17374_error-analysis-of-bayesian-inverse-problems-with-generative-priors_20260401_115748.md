# Error Analysis of Bayesian Inverse Problems with Generative Priors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17374
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17374
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The Wasserstein-1 error between the approximate posterior (using a generative prior) and the true posterior is bounded by a data-dependent stability constant times the Wasserstein-2 error of the prior approximation, plus higher-order bias and stochastic terms; the prior error decays as \(N^{-1/d}\) with high probability.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies Bayesian inverse problems where the prior distribution over unknown parameters is replaced by a generative model (e.g., a GAN) learned from data. The authors treat the generative model as a deterministic transport map from a latent reference distribution to the parameter space and analyze how errors in this prior affect the posterior. Their approach combines perturbation theory for Bayesian posteriors with optimal transport, deriving a bound on the Wasserstein-1 distance between the approximate and true posteriors.

The main theoretical result shows that this posterior error is at most a data‑dependent stability constant (arising from the likelihood’s regularity) times the Wasserstein-2 distance between the true prior and the approximate prior, plus additional bias and stochastic terms. Under standard assumptions, the prior error decays as \(N^{-1/d}\) with high probability, where \(N\) is the number of training samples. The theory is supported by 2D numerical experiments and a high‑dimensional PDE inversion using an MNIST‑based prior, demonstrating that generative priors allow simple MCMC to capture multi‑modal posteriors.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two main stages: training a generative prior and quantifying the resulting posterior error.

First, a generative model (typically a Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty) is trained to approximate the true prior \(\mu\). A fixed reference distribution \(\eta\) (e.g., standard Gaussian) on a latent space \(\mathbb{R}^k\) is chosen. The generator \(bT\) is learned by minimizing the squared Wasserstein-2 distance between the pushforward \(bT\#\eta\) and an empirical prior \(\mu_N\) obtained from a dataset. The trained generator defines the approximate prior \(b\mu = bT\#\eta\).

Second, the error in the Bayesian posterior is analyzed. Starting from Bayes’ rule, the authors apply a perturbation framework based on integral probability metrics. They prove that if the likelihood potential \(\Phi(u;y)\) is locally Lipschitz and satisfies integrability conditions, then the Wasserstein-1 distance between the true posterior \(\nu\) and the approximate posterior \(b\nu\) is bounded by a constant \(C_{\text{stab}}(y)\) times \(W_2(\mu, b\mu)\), plus higher‑order terms (Theorem 2.2). The prior error itself decomposes into an approximation bias (the minimal \(L_2(\eta)\) distance between the best transport in the model class and the true transport) and a stochastic error due to finite training samples. Under moment conditions and with a sufficiently rich model class, the stochastic error decays as \(N^{-1/d}\) with high probability. For unbounded priors, a trimming argument yields analogous bounds with an extra tail term. Consequently, the posterior \(W_1\) error inherits the same convergence rate as the prior \(W_2\) error, up to the data‑dependent constant. For inference, preconditioned Crank–Nicolson (pCN) MCMC is run in the latent space to sample from the latent posterior, and samples are pushed forward via \(bT\) to obtain approximate parameter posteriors.

## Key Results
- **Perturbation bound:** \(W_1(\nu, b\nu) \le C_{\text{stab}}(y)\, W_2(\mu, b\mu) + \text{higher‑order bias and stochastic terms}\) (Theorem 2.2).
- **Prior error rate:** With high probability, \(W_2(\mu, b\mu_N) \le \inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}} \|T - T^\dagger\|_{L_2(\eta)} + O(N^{-1/d})\) (Theorems 3.13 and 3.19), where \(N\) is the number of training samples.
- **Empirical validation:** 2D benchmark experiments confirm that the posterior \(W_1\) distance is indeed controlled by the prior \(W_2\) distance. A high‑dimensional Darcy flow inverse problem, using an MNIST‑trained generative prior, shows that a simple MCMC sampler in the latent space can effectively sample multi‑modal posteriors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Perturbation bound linking posterior \(W_1\) to prior \(W_2\)
The authors establish that the Wasserstein‑1 distance between the true posterior \(\nu\) and the approximate posterior \(b\nu\) is bounded by a stability constant \(C_{\text{stab}}(y)\) multiplied by the Wasserstein‑2 distance between the true prior \(\mu\) and the approximate prior \(b\mu\), plus higher‑order terms. The proof uses perturbation analysis with integral probability metrics. For a cost \(c_y\) derived from the likelihood’s local Lipschitz constant \(\ell\), they first bound \(D_{c_y}(\nu, b\nu)\) in terms of \(D_{c_y}(\mu, b\mu)\) (Proposition 2.1). Then, by choosing \(c(u,v)=\|u-v\|\) and relating \(D_{c_y}\) to \(W_2\) via an optimal coupling and a Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, they obtain Theorem 2.2: \(W_1(\nu, b\nu) \le C_{\text{stab}}(y) W_2(\mu, b\mu)\). The constant involves the likelihood’s functions \(f\), \(g\), \(h\) and depends on the observed data \(y\). The key assumptions are that the likelihood \(\Phi\) belongs to a function class \(\mathcal{F}(f,g,h,\ell,\mu)\) requiring local Lipschitz continuity and that \(f\), \(h\) are integrable with respect to \(\mu\) (and \(b\mu\)). Violations (e.g., discrete likelihoods) can make \(C_{\text{stab}}(y)\) infinite.

### Mechanism 2: Decomposition of prior \(W_2\) error
The Wasserstein‑2 error of the generative prior, trained by minimizing \(W_2\) against an empirical prior \(\mu_N\), decomposes into an approximation bias and a stochastic error. Let \(bT_N\) be the trained generator and \(bT^\dagger\) the best‑in‑class transport in the model class \(\mathcal{T}\). By optimality,
\[
W_2(bT_N\#\eta, \mu) \le \inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}} \|T - T^\dagger\|_{L_2(\eta)} + 2\,W_2(\mu_N, \mu).
\]
Here, \(\inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}} \|T - T^\dagger\|_{L_2(\eta)}\) captures the approximation bias (how well the model class can represent the true transport \(T^\dagger\) such that \(\mu = T^\dagger\#\eta\)), while \(W_2(\mu_N, \mu)\) is the stochastic error due to finite samples. Under a finite \(q\)-th moment condition (\(q > 2d/(d-2)\)) and for dimension \(d\ge4\), the expected squared stochastic error satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[W_2^2(\mu_N,\mu)] \le C N^{-2/d}\) (Fournier & Guillin, 2015). Markov’s inequality then yields a high‑probability bound of order \(N^{-1/d}\). Thus, with enough training data, the stochastic error decays at this rate, while the approximation bias depends solely on the expressivity of the generator architecture.

### Mechanism 3: Controlling the stability constant and high‑probability posterior bounds
The stability constant \(C_{\text{stab}}(y)\) depends on quantities like \(\|h(\cdot,y)\|_{L^1(b\mu)}\), which involve the approximate prior. To obtain a posterior error bound that holds with high probability, the authors show that when the prior \(W_2\) error is small and the likelihood terms \(h\) and \(\varphi\) are Lipschitz, these norms stay close to their counterparts under the true prior (Lemma 3.11). They also bound fluctuations of the coupling integral appearing in \(C_{\text{stab}}\) (Lemma 3.12). For bounded parameter spaces, combining these with the prior error decay yields Theorem 3.13: a high‑probability bound on \(W_1(\nu, b\nu)\) that incorporates the approximation bias, the stochastic \(N^{-1/d}\) term, and a small trimming error. For unbounded spaces, they introduce a truncation radius \(r\) and control the tail contribution via a moment condition, leading to Theorem 3.19 with an extra \((2/r)\,\nu(\|\cdot\|^2)\) term. Overall, under Lipschitz and moment assumptions, the posterior \(W_1\) error inherits the same convergence rate as the prior \(W_2\) error, up to a data‑dependent constant. This explains why, in practice, the generative prior’s accuracy directly determines posterior accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
### Bayesian inverse problems
**Concept:** In a Bayesian inverse problem, an unknown parameter \(u\) is modeled as a random variable with prior measure \(\mu\). Observations \(y\) are related to \(u\) through a forward model and noise, encoded in a likelihood potential \(\Phi(u;y)\). Bayes’ rule yields the posterior \(\nu\) with density \(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(u) = \frac{\exp(-\Phi(u;y))}{Z(y)}\), where \(Z(y)\) is the normalizing constant (evidence).

**Why needed:** The paper’s entire analysis focuses on replacing the true prior \(\mu\) with a learned generative prior \(b\mu\). Understanding the Bayesian framework is essential to see where the generative model fits and how its error propagates to the posterior.

**Quick check:** *What is the purpose of the normalizing constant \(Z(y)\)?*  
→ It ensures the posterior is a properly normalized probability measure (total mass 1) and plays the role of the marginal likelihood for model comparison.

### Optimal transport and Wasserstein distances
**Concept:** Optimal transport seeks a coupling (or map) that moves one probability distribution to another at minimal total cost, often taken as \(\|u-u'\|^p\). The resulting minimal cost is the \(p\)-Wasserstein distance. For \(p=1\) it equals the dual of bounded Lipschitz functions; for \(p=2\) it has a geometric structure and is frequently used with quadratic cost.

**Why needed:** The paper uses \(W_2\) to measure prior error (aligning with the training objective of Wasserstein GANs) and \(W_1\) to measure posterior error (arising naturally from the perturbation analysis). The central bound links these two distances, making optimal transport theory indispensable.

**Quick check:** *Why might the authors choose \(W_2\) for the prior error but \(W_1\) for the posterior error?*  
→ \(W_2\) matches the Wasserstein loss minimized during training and yields a simple bound via the optimal transport map; the perturbation bound naturally leads to \(W_1\) for the posterior because it relies on the dual formulation with Lipschitz functions and the structure of Bayes’ rule.

### Generative models as transport maps
**Concept:** A generative model defines a deterministic map \(T\) from a latent space (with reference distribution \(\eta\), e.g., standard Gaussian) to the data space, inducing the pushforward measure \(T\#\eta\) as the approximate data distribution. Examples include GANs, normalizing flows, and diffusion models.

**Why needed:** Treating the generative model as a transport map allows the application of optimal‑transport theory to bound the prior approximation error \(W_2(b\mu,\mu)\). Moreover, the training objective (minimizing a Wasserstein distance) directly yields a statistical guarantee on the prior error.

**Quick check:** *What is a key difference between a GAN and a normalizing flow in the transport map view?*  
→ Normalizing flows provide an explicit, invertible map with tractable log‑density, enabling exact likelihood computation; GANs typically only allow sampling from \(T\#\eta\) and do not provide a tractable density, requiring a discriminator to approximate the Wasserstein distance.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
(Dataset μ_N, Reference η) -> Train Generator bT -> Approximate prior bμ -> Likelihood Φ -> Latent posterior -> MCMC sampler -> Posterior bν -> Error analysis -> Validation

### Critical path
1. **Problem definition:** Identify unknown parameter \(u\), forward model, and true prior \(\mu\) (available only via a dataset \(\mu_N\)).
2. **Choose latent dimension \(k\)** and reference distribution \(\eta\) (e.g., \(\mathcal{N}(0,I_k)\)).
3. **Train generator \(bT\):** Solve \(\min_T W_2(T\#\eta, \mu_N)\) using a Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty; optionally employ sequential training to improve accuracy.
4. **Quantify prior error:** Given the training sample size \(N\), model class \(\mathcal{T}\), and moment conditions, apply Theorem 3.5 (or 3.8/3.19) to obtain a high‑probability bound on \(W_2(\mu, b\mu)\).
5. **Estimate stability constant:** Verify that the likelihood satisfies local Lipschitz and integrability conditions; bound \(C_{\text{stab}}(y)\) using Lemma 3.11 (for bounded \(U\)) or Lemma 3.18 (for unbounded \(U\) with trimming radius \(r\)).
6. **Combine:** Conclude that \(W_1(b\nu, \nu) \le C'(y) \bigl( \inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}} \|T - T^\dagger\|_{L_2(\eta)} + N^{-1/d} + \text{tail term} \bigr)\) with high probability.
7. **Posterior sampling:** Run pCN MCMC in the latent space (Algorithm 4.1) to sample from the latent posterior, then push forward through \(bT\) to obtain samples from \(b\nu\). For low‑dimensional problems, importance reweighting may be used.
8. **Validation:** Compute Wasserstein distances against the true posterior (if known) or assess mixing diagnostics (e.g., ESS, autocorrelation) for high‑dimensional cases.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Generator architecture:** GANs (e.g., WGAN‑gp) are flexible but can suffer from mode collapse or training instability; normalizing flows guarantee exact likelihoods but require invertible architectures and may be less expressive in high dimensions.
- **Latent dimension \(k\):** Smaller \(k\) reduces the effective sample‑complexity rate (since \(N^{-1/k}\)) but introduces approximation bias if the data manifold is intrinsically high‑dimensional. Must balance computational cost and expressivity.
- **Sample size \(N\):** Larger \(N\) reduces the stochastic error at a slow rate \(N^{-1/d}\); collecting large representative datasets may be prohibitive, especially for high‑dimensional priors.
- **Network capacity:** Deeper/wider networks decrease approximation bias (smaller \(\inf_T \|T-T^\dagger\|\)) but make optimization harder and increase computational load; also may exacerbate GAN training issues.
- **Likelihood smoothness:** A smoother (more locally Lipschitz) likelihood yields a smaller stability constant \(C_{\text{stab}}\). Discontinuous or steep likelihoods can cause the bound to blow up.
- **Truncation radius \(r\) (unbounded case):** Larger \(r\) reduces the tail error \((2/r)\nu(\|\cdot\|^2)\) but increases the constant; one must choose \(r\) so that \(\mu(\|u\|^2 \le r)\) is large while keeping \(r\) moderate.

### Failure signatures
- **Prior \(W_2\) does not decay with \(N\):** Indicates that approximation bias dominates; possible remedies include increasing network capacity, changing architecture, or improving training (e.g., more sequential steps). Could also signal heavy‑tailed prior violating moment conditions.
- **Posterior \(W_1\) much larger than prior \(W_2\):** Suggests \(C_{\text{stab}}(y)\) is large; check whether the observed data \(y\) is improbable under the prior (small evidence) or whether the likelihood lacks local Lipschitz continuity.
- **MCMC in latent space mixes poorly:** May be due to a highly concentrated posterior (low noise‑to‑signal ratio) requiring smaller pCN step size, or the generative prior may not capture relevant posterior modes; increasing latent samples or switching to a more advanced sampler may help.
- **Training instability (oscillating losses, mode collapse):** Indicates failure to accurately approximate the prior in \(W_2\); remedies include enforcing Lipschitz via gradient penalties, using spectral normalization, or adjusting the adversarial training schedule.
- **Constants from theorems diverge numerically:** Likely violation of assumptions – e.g., \(h(\cdot,y)\) not bounded/integrable, infinite prior moments, or poor local Lipschitz constant; consider trimming or verifying assumptions.

### First experiments
1. **2D benchmark suite:** Three synthetic distributions (Swissroll, Pinwheel, Checkerboard) are used to train a WGAN‑gp with varying sample sizes \(N\), network widths, and training epochs. Prior \(W_2\) and posterior \(W_1\) are computed via reweighting (exact posteriors exist). Results consistently show that posterior error tracks prior error, validating Theorem 2.2. Log‑log slopes indicate sub‑optimal convergence, likely due to imperfections in the WGAN’s approximation of \(W_2\).
2. **Darcy flow PDE inverse problem:** A high‑dimensional (784‑dim) log‑permeability field is inferred from noisy pressure measurements. The prior is the empirical distribution of MNIST digits. A WGAN‑gp trained on MNIST provides the generative prior. Posterior sampling uses pCN in the 64‑dim latent space. With 20% noise the posterior is clearly multimodal (digits 3,8,2,5 appear), showing that a simple MCMC can traverse complex posteriors thanks to the learned prior. With 10% noise the posterior concentrates near the true digit, illustrating noise‑driven adaptation.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity and scaling:** Systematic variation of \(N\), generator width, and training epochs (Figures 4.2–4.4) serves as an ablation study. The experiments quantify how prior \(W_2\) and posterior \(W_1\) errors depend on these choices, providing practical guidance for tuning generative models in Bayesian inverse problems and illustrating the theoretical dependency on \(N\) and model capacity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Infinite‑dimensional parameter spaces:** The analysis is restricted to finite‑dimensional spaces \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\). Extending the results to infinite‑dimensional function spaces (e.g., for PDE‑constrained problems) is unclear because empirical Wasserstein rates depend on \(d\) and require different tools (e.g., metric entropy).  
   *Basis:* The conclusion states that most results do not readily extend to infinite dimensions.  
   *Why unresolved:* The rate \(N^{-1/d}\) and finite‑dimensional concentration inequalities would need to be replaced by dimension‑free or regularity‑dependent rates.

2. **Data‑independent or expectation bounds:** Current bounds contain constants that depend explicitly on the observed data \(y\) and may deteriorate for low‑likelihood data. Obtaining high‑probability bounds over \(y\) or bounds in expectation with respect to the data distribution would be more robust.  
   *Basis:* The authors note that data‑dependent constants are a limitation and suggest extending to expectation bounds.  
   *Why unresolved:* The stability constant \(C_{\text{stab}}(y)\) involves quantities like \(\|h(\cdot,y)\|_{L^1(b\mu)}\), which can become arbitrarily large for tail \(y\); analyzing its distribution under the prior predictive is non‑trivial.

3. **Generative models beyond minimum Wasserstein training:** The theory assumes the prior is learned by minimizing a Wasserstein distance, but many practical generative models (e.g., VAEs, standard GANs, diffusion models) use different divergences (KL, Jensen‑Shannon, score matching). Extending the error analysis to these more common training objectives is an open problem.  
   *Basis:* The conclusion remarks that minimum Wasserstein analysis is removed from implementation and suggests analyzing metrics more closely tied to implementable models.  
   *Why unresolved:* The perturbation bound relies on Wasserstein distances; translating it to other metrics requires developing analogous stability constants and proving that training errors in those metrics control the relevant Wasserstein errors or directly the posterior error.

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (local Lipschitz likelihood, integrability of auxiliary functions, finite prior moments, and rich enough model class) may not hold for real‑world inverse problems (e.g., discrete data, heavy‑tailed priors, strong nonlinearities), limiting applicability.
- The statistical rate \(N^{-1/d}\) decays extremely quickly with latent dimension \(d\); in practice, approximation bias, suboptimal training, or insufficiently large \(N\) may dominate long before the stochastic term becomes negligible.
- The data‑dependent stability constant \(C_{\text{stab}}(y)\) can blow up when the observed data are improbable under the prior (small evidence), making the bounds potentially vacuous in tail regimes.
- Experimental validation is limited to a few 2D synthetic benchmarks and a single high‑dimensional PDE example with an MNIST prior; broader evaluation across diverse problem families is needed to confirm the theory’s general utility.

## Confidence
- **Theorem 2.2** (perturbation bound \(W_1 \le C_{\text{stab}} W_2\)): **High** – rigorous derivation under clear assumptions.
- **Prior error rate** (\(W_2 = O(N^{-1/d})\) with high probability): **Medium** – the rate follows from empirical Wasserstein theory, but actual attainment depends on model capacity and training success.
- **Empirical results** (posterior error tracking, MCMC mixing): **Medium** – experiments support the claims but are not exhaustive and rely on a specific linear forward model and WGAN‑gp generative prior.

## Next Checks
1. **Robustness to likelihood irregularities:** Test the bound with non‑Lipschitz likelihoods (e.g., Poisson or binomial noise) to study how the stability constant degrades; this could motivate extending the theory to weaker regularity conditions.
2. **Empirical scaling study:** Systematically vary latent dimension \(d\) and sample size \(N\) across multiple generative architectures (WGAN, normalizing flow, diffusion) to measure the actual decay of \(W_2(\mu, b\mu)\) and compare with the theoretical \(N^{-1/d}\) rate; this will clarify when the rate is achievable in practice.
3. **Nonlinear high‑dimensional inversion:** Apply the framework to a nonlinear inverse problem (e.g., Bayesian inversion for a nonlinear elliptic PDE) to assess whether the posterior error still tracks the prior error and whether MCMC mixing remains effective without additional tuning.
