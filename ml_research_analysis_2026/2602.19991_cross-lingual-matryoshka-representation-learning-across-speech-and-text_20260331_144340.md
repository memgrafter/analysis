# Cross-lingual Matryoshka Representation Learning across Speech and Text

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19991
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19991
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** The Late-Fusion model achieves state-of-the-art retrieval on the Kallaama-Retrieval-Eval benchmark with nDCG@5 of 69.85, outperforming a 10× larger text-only model (57.98) and an ASR+text pipeline (57.09), while also reaching 96.11 F1 on a speech intent detection task.

## Executive Summary
Low-resource languages face two key barriers: a scarcity of written content in local languages and a mismatch between spoken language and text‑based retrieval systems. This paper addresses both for Wolof speakers by enabling speech queries in Wolof to retrieve relevant French text documents. The proposed solution is a bilingual speech‑text Matryoshka representation model trained via late modality fusion. A frozen cross‑lingual text embedding LLM (Qwen3‑0.6B‑Embed) is combined with a Wolof HuBERT speech encoder and trained on a large synthetic dataset of speech‑transcribed, machine‑translated, and LLM‑generated document pairs using instruction‑following prompts. On the new Kallaama‑Retrieval‑Eval benchmark, the Late‑Fusion model achieves SOTA nDCG@5 of 69.85, significantly surpassing baselines including a much larger text‑only model and a pipelined ASR+text approach. It also generalizes strongly to an unseen speech intent detection task, obtaining 96.11 F1 in a 16‑shot setting. Analysis shows that the learned representations concentrate information in the leading components of the highest Matryoshka dimension, enabling efficient compression with minimal accuracy loss—a crucial property for deploying retrieval in low‑resource environments.

## Method Summary
The method unifies a frozen multilingual text LLM with a separate speech encoder through a late‑fusion architecture. First, a scalable synthetic data pipeline is constructed: raw Wolof speech is filtered, diarized, and transcribed using a Wolof speech language model; transcriptions are translated to French; an LLM (Gemini‑2.5‑Flash) generates diverse French documents (stories, dialogues, blogposts) conditioned on the translation, yielding ~1.17M speech‑document pairs with instruction prompts. The model uses a frozen HuBERT encoder to extract speech features, which are downsampled by a CNN and linearly projected to the LLM’s embedding dimension. The projected speech sequence is concatenated with task instruction tokens (e.g., `<retrieve document>`) and fed into the frozen Qwen3‑0.6B‑Embed LLM. The final token’s state serves as the query embedding; document embeddings are produced analogously from the same prompt plus the French document. Both embeddings are Matryoshka representations, trained with a joint InfoNCE contrastive loss over multiple fixed dimensions (128, 256, 512, 1024). Only the CNN and projection matrix are trained, preserving the LLM’s cross‑lingual knowledge while avoiding catastrophic forgetting. This design yields high retrieval accuracy and supports efficient inference by slicing embeddings to lower dimensions on demand.

## Key Results
- SOTA retrieval on Kallaama‑Retrieval‑Eval: nDCG@5 = 69.85, beating NLLB‑LLM2Vec (57.98) and an ASR+text pipeline (57.09).
- Strong generalization to speech intent detection: 96.11 F1‑score on WolBanking77 in a 16‑shot setting.
- Information concentration: Higher Matryoshka dimensions (e.g., 1024) exhibit a low effective rank—most energy captured by a small subset of components—enabling compression to smaller `d` with limited loss.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness stems from three key factors:

**1. Late modality fusion with a frozen text LLM:** Speech features are projected and merged with instruction tokens before entering the LLM’s transformer layers. This leverages the LLM’s deep, cross‑lingual semantic representations to align speech and text within a unified space. Keeping the LLM frozen preserves its pre‑trained knowledge and avoids forgetting. Evidence: Section 3.2 details the pipeline; Tables 1 & 2 show Late‑Fusion outperforms Dual‑encoder and pipelined baselines.

**2. Matryoshka Representation Learning for efficient compression:** Joint training over multiple dimensions forces the highest dimension (e.g., 1024) to concentrate information in its leading components, as seen in the energy accumulation curves (Section 5.2, Figure 4). This low‑rank property allows slicing to smaller `d` (e.g., 128, 256) with manageable accuracy trade‑offs, enabling faster retrieval and reduced storage. Section 5.3 demonstrates practical speed‑memory gains.

**3. Large‑scale synthetic data curation:** A pipeline that filters raw Wolof speech, transcribes, translates, and uses an LLM to generate diverse French documents provides abundant training pairs despite the scarcity of authentic speech‑document data. The model trained on this synthetic corpus generalizes to natural test queries, evidenced by its SOTA on Kallaama. The core assumption—that synthetic documents are semantically close enough to real ones—holds for this language pair. Evidence: Sections 2.1, 4.1; Kallaama benchmark includes natural speech and documents.

Break conditions include misalignment of the LLM’s cross‑lingual space for other language pairs, loss of critical information when slicing very low dimensions, and systematic biases in synthetic data that diverge from the real distribution.

## Foundational Learning
- **Contrastive Learning with In‑Batch Negatives (InfoNCE Loss):** The training objective for retrieval. It pulls embeddings of matching (query, document) pairs together while pushing apart all other documents in the batch. *Why needed:* It provides a scalable, differentiable signal for learning aligned representations without pre‑defined negative pools. *Quick check:* What is a key disadvantage of using in‑batch negatives compared to a large, pre‑defined negative pool?

- **Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL):** A technique that optimizes a single model for multiple fixed embedding dimensions simultaneously (e.g., 128, 256, 512, 1024) using a summed loss, enabling slicing at inference for cost‑accuracy trade‑offs. *Why needed:* It allows efficient deployment by exploiting the often low‑rank nature of learned representations. *Quick check:* Why does training a single model for multiple dimensions potentially hurt the optimality of the smallest dimension relative to training a dedicated small model?

- **Late vs. Early/Dual Modality Fusion:** Late fusion combines speech and prompt tokens before the LLM, leveraging the LLM’s contextualization; Dual (early) fusion encodes each modality separately and merges at the representation level. *Why needed:* To align speech with cross‑lingual text while respecting instruction‑following prompts. *Quick check:* What architectural limitation of Dual‑encoder approaches does Late‑Fusion avoid, especially regarding prompt processing and semantic depth?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
1. Input: Wolof speech waveform.  
2. Speech Encoder: Frozen Wolof HuBERT (12‑layer). Output: sequence of 768‑dim hidden states per ~20 ms frame.  
3. Feature Aggregation & Projection: CNN (downsamples sequence by 2) + trainable linear projection `W` (maps to LLM embedding dim, e.g., 1024). Only this module is trained.  
4. Prompt Injection: Task instruction token embeddings (e.g., `<retrieve document>`) are concatenated with the projected speech sequence *before* the LLM.  
5. Text LLM Backbone: Frozen Qwen3‑0.6B‑Embedding (Transformer). Processes the fused sequence. Final token’s state is the query embedding.  
6. Matryoshka Output Head: Final layer projects to the highest dimension (1024). During training/inference, slicing `[:, :d]` yields embeddings for any `d` in {128, 256, 512, 1024}.  
7. Document Encoder: Same frozen Qwen3‑0.6B‑Embedding processes French text prompt + document. Final token is document embedding.  
8. Loss: Joint InfoNCE over all `d` in the MRL set; in‑batch negatives.

**Critical Path:**  
Speech → HuBERT → CNN + `W` → [Prompt tokens + Speech tokens] → Frozen LLM → [Slice to `d`] → InfoNCE loss.  
Parallel path for documents: French document → Prompt + LLM → Slice → InfoNCE.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- *Frozen LLM vs. full fine‑tuning:* Frozen preserves cross‑lingual knowledge and compute efficiency, but limits adaptation to the retrieval task.
- *Late‑Fusion vs. Dual‑encoder:* Late‑Fusion leverages LLM depth and supports instructional prompts; Dual is lighter but loses semantic capacity and underperforms (Section 4.1).
- *Synthetic vs. real data:* Synthetic scaling enables training in low‑resource settings but may not capture the full diversity and noise of authentic documents; real data is authentic but scarce.

**Failure Signatures:**
1. Dual‑encoder models consistently underperform across all dimensions, indicating insufficient representational capacity for semantic retrieval.
2. Poor performance on Fleurs‑Retrieval‑Eval correlates with low‑quality speech (hesitations, low volume); higher Matryoshka dimensions (1024) are more robust.
3. Sharp drop at smallest Matryoshka dimension (`d=128`) reveals capacity limits; rank analysis shows documents remain effectively full‑rank at that scale.

**First Experiments:**
1. **Text‑only baseline:** Fine‑tune the frozen Qwen3‑0.6B‑Embed on the text‑only portion of the synthetic dataset (1.17 M pairs). Validate retrieval on a held‑out text query set (target nDCG@5 > 50).  
2. **Late‑Fusion training:** Add frozen HuBERT + CNN + projection to the text‑tuned LLM and train on the instruction‑following speech‑text dataset. Expect improvement over a pipelined ASR+text baseline on a held‑out speech validation set.  
3. **Matryoshka property verification:** Evaluate the trained Late‑Fusion model on Kallaama for all dimensions {128, 256, 512, 1024}. Expect monotonic nDCG gains with diminishing returns and confirm speed/memory savings for smaller `d`.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to other language pairs?** The study focuses solely on Wolof‑French; it remains unclear how the approach transfers to other low‑resource languages with different phonologies or writing systems.
2. **Impact of synthetic data on robustness?** The reliance on ASR/MT‑generated data may limit performance on natural speech and documents, and many languages lack functional upstream systems.
3. **Why lower MRL dimensions underperform?** Despite higher dimensions exhibiting low rank (concentrated information), lower slices show poor retrieval, suggesting suboptimal compression behavior.
4. **Can alternative compression improve trade‑offs?** The authors propose dynamic structured sparsity as a more flexible alternative to fixed Matryoshka slicing, but it has not been evaluated.

## Limitations
- Synthetic document distribution may not fully match the diversity and noise of real‑world French documents encountered by Wolof speakers.
- The pipeline depends on a functional Wolof ASR and MT system; languages without such tools cannot replicate the approach.
- Model performance degrades on low‑quality speech inputs (e.g., hesitations, background noise), as seen on the Fleurs dataset.
- The low‑rank property of Matryoshka representations is only analyzed for retrieval; its generality to other tasks is unknown.
- Critical hyperparameters (CNN downsampler details, projection matrix) are unspecified, hindering exact reproduction.

## Confidence
- **Late‑fusion with frozen LLM:** *High* for Wolof‑French retrieval (SOTA validated on Kallaama); *Medium* for cross‑lingual generalization (no evidence beyond this pair).
- **Matryoshka compression via concentration:** *Medium* (observed on retrieval; may not generalize to other tasks or languages).
- **Synthetic data pipeline scalability:** *Medium* (generalizes to natural test set but long‑tail biases and ASR‑quality dependencies remain uncertain).

## Next Checks
1. Evaluate the Late‑Fusion architecture on at least two additional low‑resource language pairs (e.g., Hausa‑French, Swahili‑English) with comparable synthetic data pipelines.
2. Fine‑tune on a hybrid dataset combining synthetic data with a small authentic human‑annotated speech‑document set (e.g., 10 k pairs) and measure performance improvement on Kallaama.
3. Perform singular value decomposition (SVD) on the output embeddings across all Matryoshka dimensions for both retrieval and intent detection tasks to quantify effective rank and validate the claimed concentration property.
