# Asymmetric Prompt Weighting for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11128
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11128
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Asymmetric prompt weighting improves from-scratch RL Pass@1 by ~6% (e.g., 0.80 vs 0.74 on TinyZero) but shows no benefit in post‑SFT regimes.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) for large language models, where standard group‑based policy gradient methods (e.g., GRPO) assign symmetric weights to prompts that downplay those with very low or very high success probabilities. This can suppress useful gradient signals from prompts the model currently fails completely. The authors propose asymmetric weighting functions (Linear‑R, Sqrt‑R, Plateau‑R) that upweight prompts with low empirical success rate, even when no completions are correct.

Theoretical analysis models the learning dynamics and shows that, under an effective time reflecting the higher cost of sampling successes on hard prompts, the Sqrt‑R weighting minimizes the time to improve low‑success prompts. Empirical evaluations on from‑scratch RL (TinyZero countdown task, GSM8K) show that asymmetric methods boost final Pass@1 by about 6% relative to symmetric baselines. In contrast, when starting from a supervised fine‑tuned model (MATH, DAPO‑math), the benefit disappears, indicating that asymmetric weighting is most valuable in the early stages of RL training.

## Method Summary
**Algorithm:** The method modifies group‑based policy gradient algorithms (GRPO, RLOO) by applying an asymmetric prompt‑level weight to the advantage estimates. For each prompt in a batch, *M* completions are generated and binary rewards are computed. The empirical success probability $\hat{\rho} = (\#\text{ correct})/M$ is calculated. Each rollout receives an advantage $A_i = \omega(\hat{\rho})\cdot(r_i - \hat{\rho})$, where the weight function $\omega$ is either Linear‑R ($1/\hat{\rho}$), Sqrt‑R ($1/(\hat{\rho}\sqrt{1-\hat{\rho}})$), or Plateau‑R (constant for $\hat{\rho}<0.5$, then $1/\sqrt{\hat{\rho}(1-\hat{\rho})}$). These weights diverge as $\hat{\rho}\to0$, but the formulas remain finite when taking limits (e.g., Linear‑R yields $A_i = -1$ for incorrect completions when $\hat{\rho}=0$). The policy gradient loss aggregates the log‑probabilities weighted by $A_i$, without a KL penalty, using a single gradient step per batch.

**Theory:** The success probability $\rho$ evolves according to an ordinary differential equation $d\rho/dt \propto \rho(1-\rho)\,\omega(\rho)$. To account for the fact that sampling a successful rollout is harder when $\rho$ is small, the authors introduce an effective time $\tau$ with $d\tau = dt/\rho$. Under a fixed budget constraint $\int \omega\,d\rho \le 1$, minimizing the effective time to reach a target $\rho^*$ leads to an optimal weight $\omega(\rho) \propto 1/(\rho\sqrt{1-\rho})$, which corresponds to Sqrt‑R. This optimality holds when the cost per update is dominated by rollouts, especially in the low‑$\rho$ regime.

## Key Results
- **From‑scratch RL (TinyZero, GSM8K):** Asymmetric weightings (Linear‑R, Sqrt‑R, Plateau‑R) improved final Pass@1 by ~6%, e.g., ~0.80 vs ~0.74 for GRPO.
- **Post‑SFT RL (MATH, DAPO‑math):** No meaningful performance difference between asymmetric and symmetric weightings.
- **Theoretical guarantee:** Under a fixed update budget, asymmetric weights minimize the effective time needed to raise success probability from a low initial value, particularly when informative rollouts are scarce.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Asymmetric prompt weighting improves learning by providing stronger gradient signals for prompts with low empirical success probability, including those with zero successes.  
**Mechanism:** The weight function $\omega(\rho)$ increases as $\rho$ decreases. For Linear‑R, $\omega(\rho)=1/\rho$ yields per‑response advantage $A_i = (r_i - \rho)/\rho$. For incorrect completions ($r_i=0$) this gives $A_i = -1$ even when $\rho\to0$, while correct completions receive a large positive advantage when $\rho$ is small. The effective weight on the gradient difference becomes $1-\rho$, staying near 1 for hard prompts. Thus, prompts with $\rho=0$ contribute a non‑zero gradient.  
**Core assumption:** The gradient directions for hard prompts are informative; the model can learn from the large negative advantages.  
**Evidence:** Empirical results in Section 4.1 (TinyZero) and 4.2 (GSM8K) show asymmetric methods achieving higher final Pass@1.  
**Break condition:** If the model's initial accuracy is already high (post‑SFT), few prompts have low $\rho$, so upweighting has minimal effect. Excessive weight on extremely hard prompts may also cause instability.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Asymmetric weighting (Sqrt‑R) minimizes the effective time to improve low‑success prompts when the cost of obtaining successes scales as $1/\rho$.  
**Mechanism:** The success probability $\rho$ follows an ODE $d\rho/dt \propto \rho(1-\rho)\,\omega(\rho)$. In the low‑$\rho$ regime, obtaining a successful rollout is expensive; effective time $\tau$ accumulates with factor $1/\rho$ ($d\tau = dt/\rho$). Under a budget constraint $\int \omega\,d\rho \le 1$, the weight minimizing $\tau$ to reach a target $\rho^*$ is $\omega(\rho) \propto 1/(\rho\sqrt{1-\rho})$ (Sqrt‑R). This upweights low $\rho$ to accelerate convergence.  
**Core assumption:** The dominant computational cost is the number of rollouts, which scales as $1/\rho$ because the probability of seeing at least one success among $M$ rollouts is $\approx M\rho$.  
**Evidence:** Proposition 2 in Section 5.3 formally proves Sqrt‑R optimal for effective time minimization.  
**Break condition:** Optimality relies on the ODE approximation and the $1/\rho$ cost scaling; if rollout cost does not depend strongly on $\rho$, symmetric GRPO becomes optimal for regular time.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** In from‑scratch RL, asymmetric weightings achieve about 6% higher final Pass@1 compared to symmetric baselines.  
**Mechanism:** By upweighting prompts with low $\rho$, the algorithm extracts usable gradient from many prompts that would otherwise be downweighted to near zero. In TinyZero, Linear‑R and Plateau‑R reached ~0.80 Pass@1 while GRPO plateaued around ~0.74. The continuous gradient from hard prompts prevents saturation and pushes the policy to eventually solve them.  
**Core assumption:** The model has sufficient capacity to eventually solve the low‑success prompts, and the additional gradient does not destabilize training.  
**Break condition:** The advantage disappears in post‑SFT settings where starting accuracy is moderate and few prompts have very low $\rho$. If the dataset predominantly consists of unsolvable prompts, upweighting may cause negative transfer.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Prompt Weight Calculator → Advantage Composer → Policy Gradient Loss → Rollout Buffer → Hyperparameter Controller  

**Component descriptions:**  
- **Prompt Weight Calculator:** Computes $\omega(\hat{\rho})$ from the per‑prompt empirical success rate; may include a floor (e.g., $\min_\rho$) to avoid numerical issues.  
- **Advantage Composer:** For each rollout, computes $A_i = \omega(\hat{\rho})\cdot(r_i - \hat{\rho})$. Handles edge case $\hat{\rho}=0$ by using limit formulas (e.g., Linear‑R gives $A_i = -1$ for wrong responses).  
- **Policy Gradient Loss:** Aggregates $L = -\frac{1}{BM}\sum_i A_i \log \pi_\theta(y_i|x)$. May optionally add KL penalty or gradient norm clipping.  
- **Rollout Buffer:** Stores prompts, completions, rewards, and log‑probabilities for the current batch.  
- **Hyperparameter Controller:** Manages choice among weighting functions and related hyperparameters (learning rate, $\min_\rho$).  

**Critical path:**  
1. Sample a batch of $B$ prompts.  
2. Generate $M$ completions per prompt using current policy.  
3. Compute binary reward for each completion.  
4. For each prompt, compute $\hat{\rho} = (\#\text{ correct})/M$.  
5. Compute prompt weight $\omega(\hat{\rho})$.  
6. For each completion, compute advantage $A_i = \omega(\hat{\rho})\cdot(r_i - \hat{\rho})$.  
7. Compute policy gradient loss using advantages and log‑probabilities.  
8. Backpropagate and update model parameters.  
9. Repeat.  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Weight function:* Linear‑R is aggressive but high variance for tiny $\hat{\rho}$; Sqrt‑R is smoother; Plateau‑R (constant for $\hat{\rho}<0.5$) offers robustness. Choose based on dataset difficulty and stability needs.  
- *Handling $\hat{\rho}=0$:* Must define finite weight; common approaches: set a minimum $\hat{\rho}$ (e.g., $1/M$) or use continuous formulas that give finite advantages (Linear‑R yields $A_i=-1$). Implementation should avoid division by zero while preserving upweighting.  
- *Rollout count $M$:* Larger $M$ gives finer‑grained $\hat{\rho}$ but increases cost. With small $M$, weight functions should be discretized accordingly.  
- *KL regularization:* The paper uses no KL, but in practice a KL penalty may be needed; its strength interacts with asymmetric weights.  
- *Warmup:* For datasets with many zero‑success prompts initially, a warmup with symmetric weighting can stabilize training (as in GSM8K).  

**Failure signatures:**  
- Exploding gradients: If $\omega(\rho)$ becomes huge for very small $\rho$ (no floor), advantages can explode, causing divergence.  
- No improvement: If most prompts already have high $\rho$ (>0.5), asymmetric weights behave like symmetric ones; switching back to GRPO may be simpler.  
- Degradation on hard prompts: Over‑upweighting prompts that are unsolvable with current capacity may cause negative transfer; monitor per‑prompt success distributions.  
- Implementation bug: Miscomputing $\hat{\rho}$ (e.g., token‑level instead of completion‑level) or applying weight before subtracting $\rho$ can nullify the effect.  

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce TinyZero: Train Qwen2.5‑3B base on countdown task with Linear‑R vs GRPO; expect Pass@1 ~0.8 vs ~0.74 after ~500 steps.  
2. Reproduce GSM8K: Train Llama‑3.1‑8B base on GSM8K with Plateau‑R; verify slight improvement over GRPO across batch sizes.  
3. Post‑SFT MATH: Train Llama‑3.2‑3B‑instruct on MATH with Linear‑R and GRPO; confirm minimal difference, illustrating the break condition.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to non‑binary verifiable rewards:** How can asymmetric prompt weighting be extended to tasks with multi‑level or continuous rewards, and would it improve performance? The current analysis assumes binary outcomes.  
2. **Adaptive rollout allocation:** Does adaptively allocating more rollouts to low‑success‑probability prompts ($\rho\approx0$) reduce wall‑clock time, as suggested by the effective‑time model? The theory indicates a $1/\rho$ cost scaling, but practical gains are unexplored.  
3. **Scaling to larger models and datasets:** Does the advantage of asymmetric weighting persist for >10B parameter models and on diverse, large‑scale benchmarks?  
4. **Difficulty distribution determinants:** What property of the prompt difficulty distribution determines whether asymmetric weighting yields a measurable benefit? The paper hypothesizes that from‑scratch RL with many difficult prompts benefits, but the precise threshold or statistic is unclear.

## Limitations
- Undisclosed TinyZero test set and evaluation scripts risk replication fidelity.  
- Incomplete reporting of RLOO/Plateau‑R learning rates may lead to hardware‑specific optimal values and hinder exact reproduction.  
- Benefits have only been demonstrated on models up to 3B parameters and math/reasoning datasets; generalizability to larger models and other task domains remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- **From‑scratch RL improvement:** High (consistent ~6% gain across TinyZero and GSM8K).  
- **Post‑SFT neutrality:** Medium (observed on two datasets; needs broader validation).  
- **Optimality of Sqrt‑R under effective time:** High (theorem under clear assumptions).
