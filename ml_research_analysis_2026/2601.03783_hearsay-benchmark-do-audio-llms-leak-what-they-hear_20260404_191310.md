# HearSay Benchmark: Do Audio LLMs Leak What They Hear?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03783
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03783
- **Reference Count**: 35
- **Primary Result**: Audio LLMs can accurately infer sensitive attributes (e.g., gender up to 92.89% accuracy) from voiceprints alone, with near-zero refusal rates, indicating that privacy leakage is inherent to audio pre‑training and current safety mechanisms are insufficient.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces HearSay, a benchmark of over 22,000 real‑world audio clips with verified labels for eight sensitive speaker attributes (e.g., gender, age, income). It evaluates 13 state‑of‑the‑art Audio Large Language Models (ALLMs) in zero‑shot settings. The findings reveal significant privacy leakage: models predict physiological traits like gender with up to 92.89% accuracy and social attributes like income up to 61.19%. Alarmingly, most models show near‑zero refusal rates for privacy‑intruding queries, and Chain‑of‑Thought reasoning amplifies risks, e.g., boosting accent inference accuracy by 22.1%. The study concludes that privacy leakage stems from the audio pre‑training objective itself and calls for audio‑level safety alignments such as encoder‑stage de‑identification.

## Method Summary
The HearSay dataset combines public lecture audio with existing corpora (NISP, VocalSound, Age‑VoxCeleb), yielding 22,064 clips with factual labels for eight sensitive attributes. Evaluation uses a standardized “Forensic Vocal Profiler” system prompt instructing models to answer based solely on acoustic features. Three input conditions are tested: With‑Audio (original audio), Transcribed‑Text (ASR transcript only), and empty audio (for prior bias). Metrics include Inference Accuracy Rate (IAR), Answer Refusal Rate (ARR), and Blind Bias Rate (BBR). The automated judge (GPT‑4o‑mini) classifies responses. Additional experiments examine Chain‑of‑Thought prompts and a simple textual safety defense. No model fine‑tuning is performed; all assessments are zero‑shot.

## Key Results
- ALLMs achieve high accuracy on sensitive attributes: gender up to 92.89%, age 83.55%, income 61.19%.
- Most models exhibit near‑zero ARR (<5%) for privacy‑intruding queries, indicating a failure of safety alignment.
- Chain‑of‑Thought reasoning amplifies leakage, e.g., Qwen3‑Omni‑Flash shows +22.1% on accent inference and +10.8% on income.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Pre‑training on large‑scale audio corpora causes ALLMs to implicitly encode sensitive speaker attributes in their internal representations, enabling inference from voiceprints alone.
- **Mechanism**: The audio encoder learns to map acoustic features (pitch, timbre, resonance) into embeddings. Because many private traits correlate with these acoustic properties, the embeddings retain signals about gender, age, health, etc. The LLM decoder can then access and verbalize these signals when prompted.
- **Core assumption**: Acoustic features are statistically predictive of private traits, and the pre‑training objective does not filter out such correlations.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract]: “privacy leakage is an inherent property of audio pre‑training.”  
  - [section 2.2]: “the models may have inadvertently encoded latent correlations between vocal nuances and sensitive speaker information during their foundational training phase.”  
  - [corpus]: Direct evidence for this mechanism is drawn solely from the target paper; neighboring works examine trustworthiness but not voiceprint leakage.
- **Break condition**: If pre‑training data is curated to decouple acoustic features from demographic correlates (e.g., via adversarial debiasing) or if the audio encoder is replaced with a privacy‑preserving feature extractor, the leakage could be reduced or eliminated.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Existing safety mechanisms in ALLMs fail to recognize acoustic privacy intrusions as harmful, resulting in near‑zero refusal rates for attribute‑probing queries.
- **Mechanism**: Safety alignment for ALLMs is typically adapted from text‑based LLMs using textual harm detection. Acoustic privacy threats lack explicit textual red flags; the model may perceive the query as a legitimate question about the audio content. Consequently, the refusal classifier does not trigger. Open‑source models also often lack robust safety fine‑tuning.
- **Core assumption**: Safety policies and training data do not sufficiently cover voiceprint‑based inference attacks.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract]: “most models exhibit near‑zero refusal rates for privacy‑intruding queries.”  
  - [section 4.2]: Open‑source models maintain near‑zero ARR for physiological traits, while GPT‑4o‑Audio shows 100% ARR on Weight.  
  - [section 4.5]: Lightweight prompt defense improves ARR for some models but not for Gender, indicating inherent misalignment.  
  - [corpus]: Support is primarily observational within the paper; neighboring works explore backdoors, not refusal failures.
- **Break condition**: If safety training explicitly includes acoustic privacy examples (e.g., demonstrations refusing to answer based on voice characteristics) and the model learns to generalize this refusal to unseen acoustic privacy queries, ARR could increase. Alternatively, a separate acoustic‑modality safety module could detect and block such queries.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Chain‑of‑Thought reasoning amplifies privacy leakage in capable ALLMs by enabling the model to articulate and combine subtle acoustic cues that are not utilized in direct answer mode.
- **Mechanism**: CoT prompts force the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps, encouraging it to attend to fine‑grained acoustic details (e.g., “high pitch, clear articulation”) and associate them with attribute categories. This stepwise introspection may activate latent knowledge about voice‑trait correlations that the model otherwise does not surface when giving a concise answer.
- **Core assumption**: The model possesses richer acoustic‑attribute associations than it typically uses, and CoT provides a scaffold to retrieve them.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract]: “Chain‑of‑Thought reasoning amplifies risks, increasing accent inference accuracy by 22.1%.”  
  - [section 4.3]: Qwen3‑Omni‑Flash shows +22.1% on Accent, +10.8% on Income, +7.0% on Education under CoT; weaker models show degradation, suggesting capability as a prerequisite.  
  - [corpus]: Neighbors discuss joint audio‑speech reasoning but not CoT‑induced privacy amplification; support is internal to the paper.
- **Break condition**: If the model is explicitly trained to suppress such reasoning (e.g., by penalizing detailed explanations of sensitive attributes) or if CoT is disabled or constrained to avoid discussing acoustic cues, the amplification effect could be mitigated. Models with limited capacity may not benefit from CoT at all.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
- Data curation pipeline: profiling agent → audio extractor → human verification → external data integrator  
- Benchmark engine: model inference service → automated judge → metric calculator  
- Experiment harness: prompt templates → configuration manager → results aggregator  
- Analysis suite: visualization scripts → statistical tests → case‑study generator  

### Critical Path
1. Raw audio + source metadata → profiling → initial label set.  
2. Audio segmentation → alignment with verified labels.  
3. Dataset split (train/test) → stored as structured files.  
4. For each model: iterate over test samples, collect responses using base prompt.  
5. Run judgment on responses → store correctness & refusal flags.  
6. Compute IAR/ARR/BBR → generate tables/figures.  
7. Repeat with CoT and defense prompts.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Real‑world audio authenticity vs. scalability: Using public lectures ensures genuine voiceprints but limits control over demographic distribution; synthetic augmentation could increase diversity but risks artifacts.  
- Automated judge speed vs. cost: GPT‑4o‑mini offers consistent evaluation at scale but introduces API cost and potential bias; human evaluation is reliable but infeasible for 22k clips.  
- Prompt simplicity vs. model capability: Base prompt works across models, but CoT requires more capable models; using CoT on weak models may cause degradation.  
- Open‑source vs. closed models: Evaluating proprietary models requires API access with rate limits; open‑source models need local compute but offer reproducibility.

### Failure Signatures
- Label‑audio mismatch: sample where the ground‑truth attribute does not correspond to the speaker (e.g., multiple speakers); detectable by spot‑checking 1% of pairs.  
- Judge inconsistency: same response gets different judgments on reruns; monitor by duplicating a small set and measuring agreement.  
- Prompt injection failure: model ignores instruction and answers based on semantics; check by validating that transcribed‑text condition yields lower IAR than audio (Figure 3).  
- Resource exhaustion: GPU OOM on large models; monitor memory usage during batch inference.  
- API drift: model updates change behavior; pin model versions and log used IDs.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Baseline replication: Run all 13 models with the base profiling prompt on the full test set, compute IAR and ARR tables, and verify that results match the paper’s Table 1 within ±2% margin.  
2. CoT amplification test: For the top‑3 highest‑IAR models (e.g., Qwen2.5‑Omni‑7B, MiniCPM‑o‑2.6, GPT‑4o‑Audio), repeat inference with the CoT prompt and measure ΔIAR on Accent, Income, Education; confirm significant boosts (≥5%) on at least two attributes.  
3. Prompt defense efficacy: Apply the provided safety‑note prompt to six representative models (three open‑source, three closed) and measure change in ARR; expect substantial improvements on non‑Gender attributes and persistent low ARR on Gender.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can encoder‑stage acoustic de‑identification techniques (e.g., voice anonymization, feature perturbation) effectively mitigate privacy leakage in ALLMs without severely degrading core audio understanding performance?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state in the Conclusion and Limitation that “native acoustic safety alignments, such as de‑identification at the encoder level” are urgently needed and that their defense exploration was “primarily focused on textual prompt engineering,” leaving audio‑level defenses uninvestigated.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper demonstrates that current text‑based safety mechanisms are insufficient, especially for physiological traits. It does not evaluate whether modifying the acoustic feature space itself—before it reaches the LLM—can break the voiceprint‑to‑attribute correlation while preserving utility for legitimate tasks.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A systematic study training or fine‑tuning the audio encoder to produce privacy‑preserving embeddings (e.g., via adversarial training or differential privacy) followed by evaluation on the HearSay benchmark and downstream audio tasks (e.g., ASR, translation) to measure the privacy‑utility trade‑off.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What causes the stark divergence in Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) effects across models and attributes (e.g., risk amplification for complex social traits vs. performance degradation for intuitive traits like gender)? Is this due to model architecture differences, training data composition, or inherent limitations in the models’ acoustic reasoning modules?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Obs 3 and Obs 4 detail that CoT harms performance in models like Kimi‑Audio and Qwen2.5‑Omni, while significantly boosting accuracy (up to +22.1%) for Qwen3‑Omni‑Flash on abstract attributes like Accent.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies this phenomenon but does not diagnose its root cause. Understanding this is critical for predicting which future, more capable models will pose the greatest privacy risks and whether certain reasoning patterns should be restricted.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Ablation studies analyzing the linguistic vs. acoustic content in CoT rationales across models. Correlation with model size, pre‑training data diversity, or architectural details (e.g., modality fusion strategy). Probing the audio encoder’s representations during CoT to see if “over‑thinking” leads to attention mis‑allocation or hallucination on non‑semantic features.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: To what extent do the privacy leakage findings generalize to non‑English, multilingual speech? Does the model’s pre‑training exposure to diverse accents and languages create new privacy vectors (e.g., native‑language identification from L2 speech) or alter the leakage profiles for social attributes?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The Limitation section explicitly notes the dataset is “English‑centric” and that “expanding to multilingual corpora is essential.” The paper’s findings are thus based on a monolingual acoustic landscape, yet real‑world ALLMs are trained on vastly multilingual data.
- **Why unresolved**: Acoustic cues for attributes like accent, education, or social stratum are language‑specific. Voiceprint correlations learned from English lectures may not transfer or may manifest differently for speakers of other languages. Models may also leak information about a speaker’s native language from their L2 accent.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Curating HearSay‑style benchmarks for other major language families (e.g., Mandarin, Arabic, Spanish) with culturally relevant sensitive attributes. Evaluating the same set of ALLMs to compare leakage rates, attribute hierarchies, and CoT effects across languages. Analyzing whether multilingual models exhibit cross‑lingual transfer of privacy vulnerabilities.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Why are physiological attributes (especially Gender) so persistently vulnerable to inference, showing near‑zero refusal rates even under defense prompts and from capable models? Is this leakage fundamentally different (e.g., more acoustically deterministic) than leakage for social attributes like Income or Education?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Obs 1 and Obs 8 highlight that Gender inference averages 92.89% IAR with near‑zero ARR for open‑source models, and prompt‑based defense is “largely ineffective” against Gender, which hovers near 0% ARR.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper shows the symptom but not the underlying cause. Gender from voice is often considered a more “biophysical” cue than social class. If this reflects a deeper, less corrigible acoustic‑attribute mapping in the pre‑training data, it suggests a fundamental limit of safety alignment via textual supervision.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Analyzing the audio encoder’s feature space (e.g., via t‑SNE or linear probes) to see if gender forms a more separable cluster than other attributes. Auditing pre‑training data mixtures to estimate the prevalence and strength of gender‑voice correlations. Testing if specialized, gender‑focused adversarial attacks on the encoder can successfully obscure gender cues without affecting other attributes.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: For abstract social attributes (Income, Social Stratum, Education) where weaker models show “prior dominance” (high BBR), is the high IAR of stronger models (e.g., Qwen3‑Omni‑Flash on Income at 61.19%) a sign of true acoustic inference or the model leveraging residual semantic/situational cues from the audio (e.g., background noise, topic) that were not fully eliminated?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper uses a “Transcribed‑Text” control setting to remove semantic cues (Figure 3), showing a precipitous drop in IAR for many attributes. However, Obs 5 notes that capable models leverage acoustic evidence to correct priors. A gap remains: the “With‑Audio” setting still contains non‑voiceprint acoustic context (e.g., recording venue, topic).
- **Why unresolved**: The benchmark’s goal is “solely from voiceprints,” but isolating pure paralinguistic features from all other acoustic context is extremely challenging. The residual performance on social attributes after text removal could still be driven by contextual sounds (e.g., a lecture hall vs. a factory floor) that correlate with social attributes, not fundamental voice properties.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Designing an even stricter control: using synthesized speech with identical semantic content and recording conditions, varying only the speaker’s biological/demographic voice. Alternatively, using “voice‑converted” audio where a speaker’s linguistic content is delivered with another speaker’s voiceprint. If leakage persists, it confirms true voiceprint inference; if it vanishes, it points to contextual leakage.

## Limitations
- **Dataset Access Uncertainty**: The full HearSay dataset requires approved access; without it, exact replication at scale is blocked.
- **Evaluation Artifacts**: Metrics rely on GPT‑4o‑mini as judge; its alignment and consistency across diverse responses are unverified.
- **Model Version Drift**: Exact model endpoints/parameters used in the paper are unspecified; results may vary with API updates or open‑source re‑releases.

## Confidence
- **Inherent Encoding Mechanism**: High (strong internal evidence and alignment with known audio encoder biases)
- **Safety Mechanism Failure**: Medium (observational refusal rates are clear, but external validation of safety training gaps is limited)
- **CoT Amplification**: Medium (results are capability‑bound and model‑specific; generalizability beyond tested models is uncertain)
