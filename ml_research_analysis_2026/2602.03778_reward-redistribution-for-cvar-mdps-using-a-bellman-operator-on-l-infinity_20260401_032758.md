# Reward Redistribution for CVaR MDPs using a Bellman Operator on L-infinity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03778
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03778
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Reformulates static CVaR control via a transformed Bellman operator on an augmented state, enabling dense per-step rewards and provable convergence of value iteration and Q-learning in the tabular setting.

## Executive Summary
Infinite-horizon static CVaR control suffers from a lack of Bellman recursion, which yields sparse rewards and degenerate fixed points, hindering reinforcement learning. This paper addresses the problem by reformulating the CVaR objective: an auxiliary variable **z** tracks the discounted cumulative return, and a transformed per-step reward **z⁻ – (r(s,a)+z)⁻** is introduced. The resulting augmented MDP admits a standard Bellman operator that contracts in **L∞** on bounded functions. Discretizing the continuous **z** uniformly yields lower and upper bounding operators with approximation error scaling linearly with the grid resolution **Δ** and the range **r_γ = r_max/(1-γ)**. Theoretically, the fixed point of the transformed operator corresponds to the optimal CVaR value; value iteration converges; and a block-update Q-learning algorithm converges almost surely under standard Robbins-Monro and exploration conditions. Empirically, on a stochastic gridworld, learned policies exhibit the expected risk-safety trade-offs: lower **α** (higher risk aversion) produces thinner return tails and fewer crater entries. The discretization gap between upper and lower bounds vanishes as the grid is refined.

## Method Summary
The method begins with a standard MDP (**S, A, P, r**) where rewards are bounded by **r_max**. For static CVaR, the objective is to optimize the **α**-quantile of the discounted return distribution. The authors introduce an augmented state variable **z ∈ Z** representing the current discounted return value. The transformation yields a dense reward: **˜r(s,z,a) = z⁻ – (r(s,a)+z)⁻**. The Bellman operator on the augmented space is defined as  
**(T¯v)(s,z) = max_a { ˜r(s,z,a) + γ E_{s'∼P}[ ¯v(s', p((r(s,a)+z)/γ) ] }**,  
where **p** clamps **z** to **[-r_γ, r_γ]** and **r_γ = r_max/(1-γ)**. This operator is a contraction on the space of bounded functions on **S×Z**.

For implementation, the continuous auxiliary variable **z** is discretized uniformly into a finite grid **Z_Δ**. Rounding functions **l_Δ** (floor) and **u_Δ** (ceil) define approximate operators **T^l** and **T^u** that, respectively, lower and upper bound the true **T** when rewards are non-positive. These operators enable a tractable finite MDP for which either value iteration (Algorithm 1) or a model-free Q-learning (Algorithm 2) can be applied. After convergence, the optimal initial budget **z*_α** is selected by maximizing a CVaR-related objective **J(z)**. The resulting policy maps each **(s,z)** to the greedy action with respect to the learned **Q**-function. During execution, the agent starts at **(s₀, z*_α)**, selects actions greedily, receives rewards, and updates **z** via the clipping projection **p**.

## Key Results
- **Theoretical**: The transformed operator’s fixed point equals the optimal CVaR value; value iteration converges geometrically due to **L∞**-contraction; Q-learning converges almost surely under Robbins-Monro step-sizes and sufficient exploration, even with the block-update scheme that reuses a single transition for all **z**-levels.
- **Empirical**: In a stochastic gridworld, the learned policies correctly trade off risk and safety: decreasing **α** (higher risk aversion) leads to return distributions with thinner left tails and fewer entries into crater (penalty) cells. The upper and lower value estimates from discretization converge as the grid resolution increases, confirming the theoretical error bound **O(Δ)**.
- **Algorithmic**: The block-update Q-learning reuses each environment transition for all **z**-bins, greatly improving data efficiency in the tabular setting while maintaining asymptotic convergence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The static CVaR objective is non-Markovian because it involves a quantile over the entire return distribution. By augmenting the state with the current discounted return **z**, the problem becomes Markovian: the future depends only on **(s,z)**. The transformed reward **˜r(s,z,a) = z⁻ – (r(s,a)+z)⁻** is constructed so that the sum of **˜r** over an infinite trajectory equals the CVaR objective up to an additive constant. Consequently, the standard Bellman optimality principle applies, yielding an operator that is a contraction in the sup norm because the reward and value functions are bounded (by **±r_γ/(1-γ)**). This contraction guarantees unique fixed points and convergence of value iteration.

For implementation, the continuous **z** is approximated via uniform discretization. The floor and ceil rounding operators **l_Δ, u_Δ** are monotone and preserve the ordering of the transformed rewards when rewards are non-positive, which ensures that the approximate operators **T^l** and **T^u** provide, respectively, lower and upper bounds on the true **T**. The approximation error between **T^l** (or **T^u**) and **T** is proportional to **Δ** and the range **r_γ**, and propagates through the value iteration with a factor **1/(1-γ)**. Thus the gap between the bounds scales linearly with **Δ**. The block-update in Q-learning exploits the fact that a single environment transition provides useful information for all **z**-levels simultaneously, improving sample complexity without affecting the asymptotic convergence, provided the step-sizes satisfy **∑β_n = ∞, ∑β_n^2 < ∞** and all state-action-augmented pairs are visited infinitely often.

## Foundational Learning
**CVaR**: Conditional Value at Risk measures the expected outcome in the worst **α** fraction of scenarios; it is the risk criterion optimized. *Why needed*: The paper optimizes α-CVaR; understanding its definition and properties is essential. *Quick check*: Given a random variable **X** and **α∈(0,1]**, compute **CVaR_α(X)**.
**Bellman Operator**: Defines the dynamic programming update for expected cumulative rewards; core of value-based RL. *Why needed*: The transformed operator builds on the standard Bellman framework. *Quick check*: Write the Bellman equation for a discounted MDP.
**L∞-Contraction**: Contraction in the supremum norm ensures unique solution and geometric convergence. *Why needed*: The paper’s convergence proofs rely on showing the transformed operator is an **L∞**-contraction. *Quick check*: Show that for any bounded functions **f,g**, **|T f – T g|_∞ ≤ γ |f – g|_∞** if **T** is a **γ**-contraction.
**State Augmentation**: Technique to handle non-Markovian objectives by augmenting the state with relevant memory. *Why needed*: The CVaR objective is path‑dependent; augmentation with **z** makes it Markovian. *Quick check*: Describe how to augment a MDP to optimize an objective that depends on the sum of rewards.
**Uniform Discretization**: Approximation of continuous spaces by finite grids. *Why needed*: The augmented variable **z** is continuous; discretization makes the problem tabular. *Quick check*: Derive the approximation error of the supremum norm when replacing a continuous variable by its nearest grid point.
**Robbins-Monro Conditions**: Step-size conditions for stochastic approximation convergence. *Why needed*: Q-learning uses decreasing step-sizes; these conditions guarantee almost-sure convergence. *Quick check*: State the conditions on **β_n** for convergence of a stochastic iterative algorithm.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
MDP (S, A, P, r) → Augmented State (z) → Transformed Bellman Operator T → Discretization (uniform grid Z_Δ) → Approximate Operators T^l, T^u → Learning Algorithms (Value Iteration / Q-learning) → Q-function approximation → Outer Optimization (select z*_α) → Policy π(s,z)=argmax_a Q(s,z,a) → Execution (environment interaction, z-update)

**Critical path**: The entire pipeline hinges on the correctness of the augmented state and transformed reward. Discretization follows; the choice of grid size **Δ** directly impacts the approximation quality and computational load. Learning (VI or QL) must accurately estimate **Q**-values on the finite augmented MDP. The outer optimization and policy execution are straightforward post-processing but depend on the learned **Q**.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Grid resolution Δ*: Finer grids approximate the continuous **z** more accurately (error **O(Δ)**) but increase the state space size **|S|×|Z_Δ|**, raising memory and time costs. Coarse grids are cheaper but may yield suboptimal CVaR.
- *Block updates in Q-learning*: Each observed transition updates **Q(s_k, z, a_k)** for all **z** simultaneously. This maximizes data efficiency because the environment step provides information about every level of **z**. However, updates for distant **z**’s may have high variance due to the recursive Bellman backup, potentially slowing convergence. The step-size must decay slowly enough to average out this variance.
- *Step-size schedule*: The Robbins-Monro conditions require **∑β_n = ∞** and **∑β_n^2 < ∞**. A common choice **β(n)=κ/(1+λ n)** balances exploration and stability. Too small a minimum step-size **κ_min** can freeze learning before optimality; too large a decay **λ** risks violating the second condition.
- *Non-positive reward assumption*: The theoretical bound for the lower/upper operators uses the concavity of **min(0, x+r)** when **r≤0**. If rewards can be positive, this monotonicity may fail, and the bounding operators could lose their ordering, invalidating the error analysis. The paper suggests a modification for non-negative rewards but leaves mixed signs open.

**Failure signatures**:
- In value iteration, divergence or lack of contraction indicates an implementation error (e.g., incorrect transformed reward, improper clipping or rounding).
- In Q-learning, if the maximum **Q**-values drift upward without bound or the learning curve becomes erratic, check step-size decay or exploration coverage; insufficient visits to **(s,z,a)** pairs lead to biased estimates.
- The gap between the upper and lower bound solutions should shrink as **Δ** decreases. If the gap remains large even with very fine grids, verify that the rounding functions are applied correctly and that the clipping projection **p** is used.
- If the learned policies do not exhibit the expected dependence on **α** (e.g., higher **α** still yields conservative behavior), it may signal an error in the outer optimization (**J(z)** computation) or in the **z**-update during execution.

**First three experiments**:
1. **Contraction test**: Randomly initialize two bounded functions **v,w** on **S×Z_Δ**. Compute **T¯v** and **T¯w** using the true (continuous) operator (or a sufficiently fine discretization). Measure **||T¯v – T¯w||_∞** and **||v – w||_∞**; the ratio should be ≤ **γ** (0.9) in all cases. Repeat for many random pairs.
2. **Discretization convergence**: Run value iteration on the gridworld using both **T^l** and **T^u** for several grid resolutions (e.g., **Δ** corresponding to 1000, 2500, 5000, 10000, 20000 bins). After convergence, record the maximum difference between the upper and lower **Q**-estimates. Plot this gap versus **Δ**; expect linear decrease, confirming Theorem 7.
3. **Q-learning replication**: Implement Algorithm 2 with the specified hyperparameters (**M=75000** episodes, max steps 150, ε-greedy decay from 1 to 0.1, step-size **β(n)=max(10⁻⁴, 1/(1+0.01 n)**). Train 10 independent seeds. For each risk level **α ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 0.9}**, evaluate the policy over 10,000 rollouts, computing empirical CVaR, return distribution percentiles, and number of crater entries. Compare results to the value iteration solution and verify that lower **α** yields fewer crater entries and thinner left tails, as reported.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability to function approximation**: Can the transformed Bellman operator be integrated with deep neural networks or other function approximators while maintaining convergence guarantees and sample efficiency in large or continuous state/action spaces? The current work is limited to tabular MDPs.
2. **Extension to general rewards**: The theoretical development assumes non-positive rewards (Assumption 1). How can the approach be adapted to environments with both positive and negative rewards without losing the bounding properties and optimality guarantees?
3. **Adaptive discretization of the augmented state**: Uniform grids scale poorly. Could an adaptive or learned discretization of the **z**-variable concentrate resolution where it matters most, reducing approximation error and computational cost?
4. **Benchmarking against modern risk-sensitive RL**: The empirical evaluation is restricted to a simple gridworld. How does the proposed method compare with contemporary algorithms (e.g., distributional RL with CVaR optimization, nested CVaR approaches) in terms of sample efficiency, stability, and achieved risk-safety trade-offs on more challenging benchmarks (e.g., finance, autonomous driving)?
5. **Finite-time analysis of block-update Q-learning**: The asymptotic convergence proof for Q-learning leaves open finite-time rates and sample complexity. Specifically, how does the number of **z**-levels affect the learning speed and variance? Can theoretical bounds be derived for the block-update scheme?

## Limitations
- **Underspecified experimental environment**: The stochastic gridworld lacks concrete definitions: the exact coordinates of crater cells, the start (**S**) and goal (**G**) positions, the set of safe states used for training resets, and a complete transition probability model (the slip probabilities do not sum to 1). This prevents exact replication of the reported empirical results.
- **Tabular restriction**: The algorithms and convergence proofs assume a finite state-action space and an explicit tabular representation. The paper does not address scaling to large or continuous domains, which typically require function approximation.
- **Reward sign assumption**: The analysis critically depends on Assumption 1 that rewards are non‑positive. While the authors mention a possible modification for non‑negative rewards, the treatment of mixed‑sign rewards is left as an open question, limiting the method’s general applicability.
