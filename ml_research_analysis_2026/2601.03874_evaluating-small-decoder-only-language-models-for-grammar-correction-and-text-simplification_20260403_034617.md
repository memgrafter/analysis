# Evaluating Small Decoder-Only Language Models for Grammar Correction and Text Simplification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03874
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03874
- **Reference count**: 5
- **Primary result**: Small decoder-only language models (SLMs) significantly underperform large language models (LLMs) in grammar correction and text simplification, with large gaps in accuracy and higher hallucination rates.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether small decoder-only language models (SLMs) can efficiently replace large language models (LLMs) for grammar correction and text simplification. SLMs were fine-tuned on task-specific datasets (BEA-2019 for grammar, WikiAuto for simplification) and evaluated on standard benchmarks (JFLEG, ASSET) using metrics such as GLEU, M2, SARI, and named-entity hallucination rates. Results show SLMs substantially underperformed LLMs: for grammar correction, the best SLM achieved a GLEU of ~25 compared to GPT-3.5 Turbo's ~75; for simplification, SLMs approached LLMs in SARI (~55 vs. ~59) but failed to compress text effectively and hallucinated more frequently. Fine-tuning often degraded performance and increased hallucinations. Cascading multiple SLMs did not yield consistent improvements. The findings indicate that current SLMs are not yet viable alternatives to LLMs for high-quality rewriting due to deficiencies in accuracy, meaning preservation, and reliability.

## Method Summary
The experimental pipeline involved fine-tuning several small decoder-only language models (GPT-2, GPT-Neo, Gemma 2B, TinyLlama) on domain-specific datasets: BEA-2019 for grammar correction and WikiAuto for text simplification. Models were trained using the causal language modeling objective, with inputs formatted as prompts (e.g., "Correct this text: [source] | Corrected: [target]") and optimized with AdamW, mixed precision, and gradient checkpointing. Evaluation was performed on test sets JFLEG (grammar) and ASSET (simplification) using automatic metrics: GLEU and M2 for grammar, SARI along with compression ratio and named-entity hallucination rate (NER) for simplification. The authors also explored cascading multiple SLMs sequentially and compared all results against zero-shot LLM baselines (GPT-3.5, GPT-4) using identical prompts. Inference used greedy decoding with token limits to reduce hallucinations.

## Key Results
- SLMs lagged far behind LLMs in grammar correction (best GLEU ~25 vs. GPT-3.5 ~75) and, while nearing LLMs in simplification SARI (~55 vs. ~59), they failed to achieve text compression and hallucinated more.
- Task-specific fine-tuning often harmed performance, increasing named-entity hallucinations and sometimes lowering SARI.
- Cascading multiple SLMs did not consistently improve output quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Fine-tuning adapts pre-trained SLMs to task-specific distributions.
Fine-tuning on task-specific data improves SLM performance by aligning the model's output distribution with the target task through weight updates via maximum likelihood estimation. This relies on the assumption that the pre-trained model possesses transferable linguistic knowledge that can be specialized without catastrophic forgetting. Evidence includes the paper's fine-tuning procedures (Section 4.3) and comparisons between fine-tuned and baseline models (Section 6). However, if the task dataset is small or mismatched, fine-tuning may cause overfitting, as indicated by increased hallucinations and inconsistent metric improvements.

### Mechanism 2: Cascading multiple SLMs enables iterative refinement.
Applying multiple SLMs sequentially can cumulatively correct errors by allowing each subsequent model to focus on residual errors from the previous one. This assumes that errors made by one model are independently correctable by another with similar capabilities. The paper evaluated cascading but found no significant gains (Sections 4.5, 6.1.2, 6.2.2). Cascading may propagate or amplify errors if models share similar failure modes, explaining the lack of improvement.

### Mechanism 3: Decoder-only generation suffices for rewriting tasks.
Decoder-only models can perform grammar correction and simplification by generating the target text autoregressively, conditioned on the source text, without a separate encoder. The causal language modeling objective is used, assuming it can capture the source-to-target transformation given sufficient context. The paper focuses on decoder-only SLMs (Section 2.2) and uses them in causal LM mode (Section 4.2). However, for tasks requiring heavy editing or holistic input reasoning, the lack of bidirectional context may lead to poor accuracy and meaning loss, as observed in the grammar results.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input datasets (BEA-2019, WikiAuto) -> Model selection (SLMs: GPT-2, GPT-Neo, Gemma, TinyLlama; LLMs: GPT-3.5, GPT-4) -> Training (fine-tuning for SLMs using AdamW, mixed precision, gradient checkpointing) -> Inference (greedy decoding, optional cascading) -> Evaluation (GLEU/M2 for grammar; SARI/NER/FRE for simplification) -> Result scores

### Critical path
1. Load pre-trained SLM and tokenizer.
2. Prepare dataset in prompt format (e.g., "Correct this text: [input] | Corrected: [target]").
3. Fine-tune using causal LM objective on training set.
4. Evaluate on test set: generate predictions, compute metrics.
5. For cascading: feed predictions of first model as input to second model, then evaluate final output.
6. Compare with LLM baselines via API calls using same prompts.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Model size vs. efficiency**: Smaller SLMs are computationally cheaper but yield lower performance; even 2B-parameter SLMs lag behind >10B LLMs.
- **Fine-tuning vs. zero-shot**: Fine-tuning adapts to the task but risks overfitting and hallucinations; the paper found fine-tuning often degraded performance.
- **Cascading vs. single-pass**: Cascading adds computational overhead and did not reliably improve quality.
- **Decoding strategy**: Greedy decoding reduces hallucinations but may limit diversity; sampling increased hallucinations during validation.

### Failure signatures
- **Grammar correction**: Very low GLEU (<30) and M2 F0.5 (<20) compared to LLMs (~75 GLEU, ~51 F0.5).
- **Simplification**: High compression ratio >1 (indicating decompression) and low SARI relative to LLMs.
- **Hallucination**: Named-entity rate >50% for fine-tuned SLMs vs. <10% for LLMs.
- **Fine-tuning impact**: Increase in NER from baseline to fine-tuned, and drop in SARI for simplification.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Single-pass evaluation**: Run each SLM (baseline and fine-tuned) on JFLEG and ASSET, compute GLEU/M2/SARI/FRE/NER. Compare to LLM baselines.
2. **Cascading evaluation**: For selected SLMs, run 2-stage and 3-stage cascades, evaluate metrics. Compare cascaded to single-pass results.
3. **Decoding ablation**: During validation, test temperature settings (e.g., 0.7 vs. 0) to gauge hallucination impact; final evaluation uses greedy decoding.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does applying multiple small decoder-only language models sequentially (cascading) improve grammar correction or text simplification quality beyond single-pass inference?**
   - *Basis*: Introduction explicitly states this as an open question.
   - *Why unresolved*: Experiments showed no significant improvements from cascading (Figures 3, 4, 8–10), but the paper did not analyze causal reasons such as error propagation, model similarity, or diminishing returns.
   - *Resolution*: Controlled experiments varying model diversity in cascades, error analysis tracking mistake types across stages, and theoretical modeling of error accumulation in sequential small-model pipelines.

2. **Would intermediate-sized decoder-only models (e.g., 3–10B parameters) offer a more favorable accuracy-efficiency trade-off than the tested small (≤2B) or large (GPT) models?**
   - *Basis*: Conclusion recommends looking at mid-sized models as they may perform better while still being smaller than LLMs.
   - *Why unresolved*: The study only evaluated small (≤2B) and large (GPT-3.5/4) models; the performance scaling curve within the intermediate range remains unexplored.
   - *Resolution*: Systematic evaluation of models across a gradient of sizes (e.g., 3B, 7B, 10B) on the same benchmarks, measuring key metrics (GLEU, SARI, NER) against inference cost and memory footprint.

3. **What specific modifications to pre-training or fine-tuning methodologies can enable small decoder-only models to achieve competitive performance with large language models on rewriting tasks while maintaining low hallucination rates?**
   - *Basis*: Abstract and conclusion state that further advances in training are required for SLMs to close the performance gap and that substantial modeling and training advances are still needed.
   - *Why unresolved*: Only standard fine-tuning was tested; alternative approaches (e.g., reinforced editing, constrained decoding, auxiliary objectives) were not explored, and fine-tuning often increased hallucinations.
   - *Resolution*: Comparative studies of novel training objectives (e.g., contrastive learning for meaning preservation), decoding constraints (e.g., copy mechanisms), or data augmentation strategies targeted at reducing hallucinations in small models.

4. **Why does task-specific fine-tuning of small decoder-only models frequently degrade performance compared to their zero-shot baselines for grammar correction and simplification?**
   - *Basis*: Results show fine-tuned SLMs often scored worse than baselines (e.g., Figure 5 SARI, Figures 11–12 NER); Discussion notes fine-tuning was "overall harmful" and caused overfitting.
   - *Why unresolved*: The paper did not investigate underlying mechanisms (e.g., catastrophic forgetting, distribution shift in fine-tuning data, optimization instability), only reporting outcome metrics.
   - *Resolution*: Analysis of fine-tuning dynamics (loss curves, gradient norms), ablation studies on data quantity/quality, and comparative probing of representations before/after fine-tuning to identify which linguistic capabilities are lost or corrupted.

5. **Is named-entity hallucination rate (NER) a sufficient measure of content fabrication in grammar correction and simplification, or do SLMs exhibit other forms of hallucinations (e.g., factual errors, structural changes) that NER misses?**
   - *Basis*: Methodology uses only NER for hallucinations, but Discussion notes SLMs added "extra text" and failed compression, suggesting non-entity hallucinations may be prevalent and unmeasured.
   - *Why unresolved*: NER captures only entity-level additions; other hallucination types (e.g., altering predicate-argument structure, adding unsupported clauses) were not quantified, possibly underreporting reliability issues.
   - *Resolution*: Development of a comprehensive hallucination taxonomy for rewriting tasks, manual error analysis of SLM outputs beyond named entities, and correlation studies between NER and other hallucination indicators (e.g., semantic role violations, negation flips).

## Limitations
- The mechanism behind fine-tuning's inconsistent benefits (sometimes harming performance) is uncertain; the paper did not investigate whether it stems from small fine-tuning datasets, model capacity limits, or objective mismatch.
- The conclusion that decoder-only architecture is inherently limiting for rewriting tasks lacks a controlled comparison with alternative architectures (e.g., encoder-decoder models).

## Confidence
- **Core claim (SLMs underperform LLMs)**: High confidence.
- **Mechanism behind fine-tuning's inconsistent benefits**: Medium confidence.
- **Broader conclusion about decoder-only architecture being inherently limiting**: Low confidence.

## Next Checks
1. Fine-tuning protocol ablation: Systematically vary fine-tuning data size, learning rate, and use of adapter layers to isolate conditions that reduce hallucinations while improving task metrics.
2. Architecture contrast: Replicate the evaluation using a small encoder-decoder model (e.g., T5-small) on the same tasks to directly test if the decoder-only design is the primary bottleneck.
3. Error analysis expansion: Perform fine-grained human evaluation on a subset of SLM outputs to categorize error types (e.g., meaning alteration, over-correction, poor compression) beyond NER-based hallucination counts.
