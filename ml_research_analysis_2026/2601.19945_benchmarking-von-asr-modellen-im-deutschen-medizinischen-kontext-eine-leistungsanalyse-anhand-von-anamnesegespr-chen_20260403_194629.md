# Benchmarking von ASR-Modellen im deutschen medizinischen Kontext: Eine Leistungsanalyse anhand von Anamnesegesprächen

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.19945
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19945
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: AssemblyAI Universal (commercial) 3.0% WER; best open-weight (Mistral Voxtral Small) 7.1% WER.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the absence of German medical ASR benchmarks, especially for dialects, by introducing the curated Med-De-Anamnese dataset. It comprises four simulated doctor-patient conversations covering standard cases, specific medical terminology, non-native accents, and strong regional dialects. Twenty-nine ASR models—including open-source (Whisper, Wav2Vec2, Voxtral) and commercial APIs (AssemblyAI, Deepgram)—were evaluated using WER, CER, BLEU, and speaker-attributed WER. AssemblyAI Universal achieved the lowest overall WER (3.0%), while Mistral Voxtral Small led among open-weights (7.1%). Performance deteriorated sharply on dialect-heavy and terminology-dense scenarios, with some models exceeding 20% WER. Commercial APIs consistently offered integrated speaker diarization, whereas open-source solutions required external modules. The paper concludes with proposals for hybrid evaluation, domain-specific metrics, and semantic extraction to enhance clinical utility.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed the Med-De-Anamnese corpus from publicly available YouTube dialogues, simulating anamnesis conversations across four scenarios: baseline, medical terminology, non-native accent, and strong regional dialect. Ground truth transcripts were normalized (numbers spelled out, punctuation standardized) and segmented with speaker labels. For evaluation, 29 ASR systems were run with standardized preprocessing (16 kHz resampling, peak normalization), greedy decoding, and postprocessing (lowercasing, punctuation removal). Metrics computed were Word Error Rate (WER), Character Error Rate (CER), BLEU, and speaker-attributed WER (SA-WER) when diarization was available. No model fine‑training was performed; the study is a pure inference benchmark.

## Key Results
- AssemblyAI Universal achieved the best overall WER of 3.0%.
- Among open-weight models, Mistral Voxtral Small reached 7.1% WER.
- Performance degraded significantly on dialect-heavy and terminology-dense scenarios, with some models exceeding 20% WER.
- Commercial APIs provided integrated speaker diarization; open-source models required separate modules.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Robustness from diverse pretraining and architecture
**Claim:** Models with diverse multilingual pretraining and robust architectural inductive biases (e.g., Conformer) exhibit lower error variance across acoustically and lexically challenging medical scenarios (dialects, terminology).  
**Mechanism:** Large-scale pretraining on varied speech data—including accented and domain-diverse content—builds acoustic representations and lexical priors that generalize to unseen medical dialects and compound terminology. The Conformer architecture’s local‑global attention may better model long‑range dependencies in fluent speech and handle spectral variations.  
**Core assumption:** Architectural design (Conformer vs. Transformer) and exposure to phonetically diverse data during pretraining are primary determinants of robustness, not just parameter count.  
**Evidence:**  
- [abstract]: “AssemblyAI Universal… achieved a WER of 3.0%… performance significantly degraded on dialect-heavy and terminology-dense scenarios” implies robustness from architecture/training.  
- [section 4.2]: “Hochperformante Modelle wie AssemblyAI Universal… demonstrieren hier eine bemerkenswerte Invarianz gegenüber diesen Störfaktoren” directly attributes invariance to model design.  
- [corpus]: Weak correlation; neighboring papers not directly relevant (industrial activity recognition, fairness), no ASR/medical evidence.  
**Break condition:** If a model’s pretraining corpus lacks >10% non-native accented speech or domain‑specific jargon, its WER increases by >5% absolute on dialect/terminology scenarios relative to baseline.

### Mechanism 2: Integrated speaker diarization reduces error propagation
**Claim:** End‑to‑end integrated speaker diarization in commercial APIs yields lower speaker‑attributed WER (SA‑WER) than open‑source pipeline approaches (ASR + separate diarization module).  
**Mechanism:** Joint optimization of transcription and speaker separation during training/inference aligns speaker labels with word timestamps more accurately. Pipeline approaches propagate errors: ASR errors distort speaker embeddings, and diarization errors misassign correct words.  
**Core assumption:** Speaker identity and lexical content are interdependent signals; modeling them jointly reduces error propagation.  
**Evidence:**  
- [abstract]: “Commercial APIs consistently provided integrated speaker diarization, whereas open‑source solutions required additional modules.”  
- [section 4.3]: “Nur die kommerziellen Modelle attributierte Transkripte direkt liefern… AssemblyAI… konsistentesten Ergebnisse” shows commercial advantage.  
- [corpus]: Weak; neighboring papers do not cover diarization or ASR pipeline comparisons.  
**Break condition:** When diarization is decoupled from ASR (e.g., Whisper + Pyannote), SA‑WER degrades by >3% absolute relative to the best integrated commercial system on the same audio.

### Mechanism 3: Domain‑specific lexical coverage drives errors
**Claim:** Domain‑specific lexical coverage (medical terminology density) is a primary error driver; models without explicit exposure to similar jargon suffer disproportionate WER increases on terminology‑dense scenarios.  
**Mechanism:** Medical terms (Latin/Greek compounds, e.g., “Divertikulitis”) have low in‑vocabulary rates in general ASR models, leading to substitution/insertion errors. Acoustic models trained on conversational speech lack priors for rare word forms.  
**Core assumption:** Vocabulary mismatch between pretraining distribution and test domain causes lexical decoding errors that dominate WER in terminology‑heavy segments.  
**Evidence:**  
- [abstract]: “Performance significantly degraded on… terminology‑dense scenarios, with some models exceeding 20% WER.”  
- [section 2.1]: Scenario 2 (“Unterbauchschmerzen… spezifischerer internistischer Fachterminologie”) explicitly designed to test terminology.  
- [section 4.2]: Heatmap (Abb. 2) shows Divertikulitis case has highest average WER across models, confirming terminology as a stressor.  
**Break condition:** If medical term token frequency in test segment exceeds 5%, WER for models not fine‑tuned on medical speech increases non‑linearly (e.g., >2× baseline WER).

## Foundational Learning
### Word Error Rate (WER) and Character Error Rate (CER)
**Why needed:** Primary metrics for transcription accuracy; WER measures word‑level substitutions/deletions/insertions, CER captures morphological errors in German compounds (e.g., “Unterbauchschmerzen”).  
**Quick check:** Why might CER be more forgiving than WER for compound medical terms, and when would this masking hide clinically significant errors?

### Speaker Diarization
**Why needed:** Medical dialogues require attributing statements to doctor vs. patient for accurate clinical notes; SA‑WER combines transcription and attribution accuracy.  
**Quick check:** In a pipeline approach, what error modes (e.g., laughter, overlapping speech) most severely impact subsequent diarization after ASR?

### Domain Shift in ASR
**Why needed:** General ASR models face domain shift from conversational speech to medical dialogues (terminology, accents, speech patterns); understanding shift informs model selection/fine‑tuning.  
**Quick check:** How would you design a synthetic data generation pipeline to simulate regional dialects and medical jargon for fine‑tuning?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Audio (Med-De-Anamnese) -> Preprocessing (16 kHz resample, peak norm) -> ASR model inference (29 systems) -> Postprocessing (lowercase, strip punctuation) -> Metrics (WER, CER, BLEU, SA‑WER) -> Analysis

### Critical path
1. Corpus ingestion: Load audio + transcripts; verify segmentation by scenario.  
2. Standardized inference: Apply consistent preprocessing/decoding across all models.  
3. Metric computation: Align hypotheses to references (edit distance); for SA‑WER, also match speaker IDs.  
4. Analysis: Aggregate results by model/scenario; generate heatmaps and stability plots.

### Design tradeoffs
- Accuracy vs. Latency: Commercial APIs optimize for accuracy with large models; open‑source (e.g., Whisper‑tiny) trades accuracy for speed—critical for real‑time clinical use.  
- Open vs. Closed: Open‑weights (Voxtral) allow on‑premise deployment (DSGVO compliance) but require diarization integration; commercial APIs offer turnkey diarization but risk data sovereignty.  
- Generalist vs. Specialist: Whisper’s multilingual pretraining gives broad coverage but may underfit medical jargon; domain‑finetuned models (Wav2Vec2 DE) specialize but risk overfitting to training accents.

### Failure signatures
- High WER (>20%) on Divertikulitis scenario → poor medical terminology coverage.  
- High WER on “Starker Akzent” scenario → weak acoustic modeling for dialects/background noise.  
- SA‑WER much higher than WER → diarization errors (speaker swaps, missed overlaps).  
- High variance across audio samples (Fig. 3 outliers) → model instability; avoid for safety‑critical documentation.

### First 3 experiments
1. Baseline replication: Run AssemblyAI Universal and Whisper Large‑v3 on all four scenarios; verify WERs match reported (3.0% and 12.6%).  
2. Dialect stress test: Isolate “Starker Akzent” audio; measure WER variance across top 7 models to confirm robustness hierarchy.  
3. Diarization pipeline penalty: Use open‑source WhisperX (ASR) + Pyannote (diarization) vs. AssemblyAI integrated; compute SA‑WER difference to quantify pipeline cost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Hybrid evaluation framework:** How can a hybrid evaluation combining LLM‑as‑a‑Judge and human evaluation systematically quantify the discrepancy between automatic metrics (e.g., WER) and actual clinical information loss? The paper notes LLMs may be overly strict; human evaluation for clinical relevance remains unimplemented.  
2. **Domain‑specific error metrics:** To what extent do domain‑specific metrics (e.g., Keyword‑specific WER for medical terminology, Human‑centered WER for readability) provide a more meaningful assessment than standard WER? The study uses general metrics but identifies significant errors on jargon/dialects; kWER/hWER are proposed but not defined or validated.  
3. **Semantic extraction:** Can agent systems reliably extract structured clinical data (e.g., FHIR‑standardized entities) directly from stable ASR transcripts without extensive fine‑tuning, and what is the error propagation from ASR to structured extraction? The paper shows top models maintain semantic integrity (via BLEU/LLM), but structured extraction from transcripts remains an unbuilt system; error accumulation is unknown.  
4. **Domain‑specific fine‑tuning:** Does fine‑tuning on German medical speech significantly improve performance on dialect‑heavy and terminology‑dense conversations compared to out‑of‑the‑box models? The study tests only generalist models; it is unknown whether domain adaptation reduces errors in challenging sub‑scenarios.  
5. **Generalization to real clinical environments:** How well do models trained/evaluated on the simulated Med‑De‑Anamnese dataset (sourced from YouTube) generalize to real‑world clinical conversations with spontaneous speech, overlapping talk, and background noise? The dataset is simulated and lacks real clinical data, potentially limiting ecological validity.

## Limitations
- Dataset not publicly available (only YouTube URLs provided), and ground truth transcripts/speaker segmentation are inaccessible, hindering replication.  
- The corpus contains only four simulated conversations, insufficient to represent the diversity of German medical speech (dialects, terminology, accents).  
- Diarization details for open‑source models (e.g., choice of external module, parameters) are not specified, preventing faithful reproduction of speaker‑attributed results.  
- No model outputs or per‑sample error analysis are provided, limiting diagnostic capabilities.

## Confidence
- Dataset validity: Low  
- Commercial SOTA (AssemblyAI 3.0% WER): Medium  
- Integrated diarization advantage: Low  
- Degradation on dialects/terminology: Medium
