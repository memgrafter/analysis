# Do Language Models Know Theo Has a Wife? Investigating the Proviso Problem

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08358  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08358  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Language models mimic human unconditional interpretations of presuppositions via shallow position-based heuristics, yielding near-perfect accuracy on original conditionals but catastrophic drops when trigger semantics are altered.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the *proviso problem* in presupposition projection—the discrepancy between theoretical predictions and human intuitions about when a presupposition survives under a conditional. The authors reformulate the problem as a Natural Language Inference (NLI) task and construct a dataset (CONFER/PROVISER) of 8,500 conditional sentence pairs with controlled structural, lexical-semantic, and contextual variations. They evaluate RoBERTa, DeBERTa, LLaMA, and Gemma, finding that fine-tuned models align with human judgments (near-perfect accuracy) but not with theory (0% accuracy). However, when the semantic content of triggers is manipulated (e.g., replacing “his wife” with “John’s friend’s garment”), accuracy plummets to below 4% for some models, revealing reliance on surface position rather than meaning. Explainability analyses (Integrated Gradients, attention) confirm that models assign stable importance to trigger tokens even when their semantics are broken, with RoBERTa robustly highlighting triggers while DeBERTa achieves high accuracy with minimal trigger focus. The findings suggest that models adopt human-like behavior through brittle heuristics rather than genuine pragmatic reasoning.

## Method Summary
The authors create four evaluation subsets (original, structural variants, lexical-semantic variants, contextual variants) based on the CONFER dataset, containing ~8,500 NLI pairs each. The premise is a conditional sentence with a presupposition trigger (e.g., possessive pronoun, “again”) in the consequent; the hypothesis is the presupposition. Models include RoBERTa-large-MNLI, DeBERTa-large-MNLI, Llama-3.2-1B, and Gemma-3-1B. After fine-tuning on CONFER’s train set (1 epoch, LR=5e-5), they measure classification accuracy against human and theory labels. They also compute Integrated Gradients (trigger IG ratio) and, for the contextual subset, attention metrics between annotated spans (K1→K2, K2→special token). Zero-shot evaluations are performed for baseline comparison.

## Key Results
- Models achieve near-perfect accuracy on original conditionals, matching human judgments but yielding 0% accuracy on theory-based predictions.
- Semantic manipulation of triggers (especially “again” in independent conditionals) reduces accuracy to below 4%, despite stable Integrated Gradients attribution to the trigger tokens.
- DeBERTa attains high accuracy with minimal trigger IG (<1), while RoBERTa maintains robust trigger focus; both diverge in generalization to semantically modified examples.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Models align with human judgments on the proviso problem by relying on the surface-level position and presence of presupposition triggers rather than semantic or pragmatic reasoning.  
**Mechanism:** During fine-tuning, models learn to map the structural pattern of a conditional containing a trigger (e.g., “his wife”, “again”) to an “Entailment” label for the corresponding presupposition hypothesis. This creates a brittle heuristic: the model’s prediction is primarily driven by detecting the trigger’s position in the consequent, not by evaluating the logical relationship between the antecedent and the presupposition.  
**Core assumption:** The CONFER training data contains consistent co-occurrence patterns between specific trigger positions and the “Entailment” label, which models exploit as a shortcut.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Near-perfect accuracy on original examples but 0% on theory predictions ([abstract], [section 5.3]).  
- High accuracy on unmodified examples but catastrophic failure when trigger semantics are altered, showing reliance on structural position over semantic content ([section 5.3]).  
**Break condition:** The heuristic fails when the trigger’s semantic relationship is broken (e.g., replacing “his wetsuit” with “John’s friend’s garment”), causing accuracy to drop below 4% for some models on IND‑again variants.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Fine-tuning on CONFER can cause models to overfit to spurious correlations present in the training distribution, harming generalization to logically similar but contextually modified examples.  
**Mechanism:** The CONFER training set likely contains examples where a semantically related antecedent paired with the “again” trigger was annotated as “Neutral” due to superset relationships. Models internalize the pattern “related context + `again` = often Neutral”. When evaluated on modified IND‑again examples where only the contextual phrase is made unrelated (but the trigger and hypothesis remain the same), the model incorrectly applies this learned spurious correlation and predicts “Neutral”.  
**Core assumption:** The dataset contains systematic label patterns tied to superficial features (like context‑trigger semantic relatedness) that do not reflect the core pragmatic reasoning task.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Zero‑shot models achieved >80% accuracy on these same examples (except Gemma), while fine‑tuned models dropped to 5–15% on modified IND‑again, indicating overfitting from CONFER fine‑tuning ([section 5.4]).  
- Only IND‑again modifications produced consistent, significant changes across all four models, the same subset where accuracy decreased ([section 5.4]).  
**Break condition:** Overfitting is exposed when performance on modified IND‑again plummets compared to both zero‑shot (~80%) and other modified trigger types (DEP, IND‑poss).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Different model architectures exhibit distinct strategies for processing presupposition triggers, which are revealed through explainability metrics even when classification accuracy is high.  
**Mechanism:** RoBERTa shows robust, high Integrated Gradients (IG) attribution to the trigger token across all modifications, indicating a consistent, trigger‑centric processing strategy. DeBERTa achieves near‑perfect accuracy but assigns minimal IG to the trigger (<1) and shows low K1→K2 attention, suggesting it uses a different, less trigger‑dependent heuristic that is more fragile to semantic changes (0% on IND‑again). The mechanism is architecture‑specific attention/distribution learning.  
**Core assumption:** Model inductive biases (e.g., attention mechanism design, pre‑training objectives) determine which features become leveraged for solving the task, with some leading to more robust but less “human‑like” (in terms of IG patterns) solutions.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- RoBERTa indicated the highest trigger IG ratios, indicating strong focus on presupposition triggers regardless of syntactic complexity; DeBERTa showed lower trigger IG ratios (<1) across all examples ([section 5.2]).  
- RoBERTa maintained high IG ratios regardless of semantic fit, while DeBERTa showed consistently low values; DeBERTa achieves identical accuracy while putting minimal importance on the trigger ([section 5.3]).  
**Break condition:** DeBERTa’s strategy fails completely on semantically modified triggers (0% accuracy on IND‑again), while RoBERTa’s more robust trigger‑focus maintains some accuracy (~20‑50%). The break is not in accuracy parity but in semantic generalization.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Presupposition Projection & the Proviso Problem**  
   Why needed: The entire study revolves around this core pragmatic phenomenon. Understanding the theoretical prediction (conditional projection) versus human intuition (unconditional accommodation) is essential to grasp why the NLI task is constructed with conflicting “human” and “theory” labels.  
   Quick check: Given “If Theo hates sonnets, so does his wife,” what does formal semantics predict as the presupposition, and what do human speakers typically assume?

2. **Natural Language Inference (NLI) Task Formulation**  
   Why needed: The paper reformulates a pragmatic problem into a standard NLI framework. Understanding entailment versus neutrality in NLI is crucial for seeing how the “proviso problem” becomes a testable classification task.  
   Quick check: For the premise “If A, B” and hypothesis “p” (presupposition of B), when should the NLI label be “Entailment” versus “Neutral” according to theory versus human judgment?

3. **Explainability Methods (Integrated Gradients, Attention Analysis)**  
   Why needed: Accuracy alone is insufficient; the paper’s core argument depends on *how* models arrive at predictions. IG and attention metrics are the tools used to probe whether models use trigger semantics or shallow heuristics.  
   Quick check: What does a high Integrated Gradients score for a specific token indicate about the model’s decision‑making process?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Dataset (CONFER + PROVISER subsets) → Models (RoBERTa, DeBERTa, LLaMA, Gemma) → Fine‑tuning (1 epoch, LR=5e‑5) → Evaluation (accuracy, IG, attention) → Analysis (statistics, correlation)

**Critical path:**  
1. Prepare dataset: Load CONFER base, generate modified examples for subsets 2–4 according to prescribed rules (conjunction/disjunction/belief embedding, WordNet/ConceptNet substitutions, context relation flips).  
2. Fine‑tune each model for 1 epoch on CONFER train set (LR=5e‑5, AdamW). Save checkpoints.  
3. For each model and each test subset:  
   a. Get classification predictions; compute accuracy vs. human and theory labels.  
   b. Run explainability: Compute Integrated Gradients (Captum, zero baseline); derive trigger IG ratio. For subset 4, extract attention metrics (K1→K2, K2→special).  
   c. Aggregate metrics by sentence type (DEP, IND‑poss, IND‑again, and their -mod variants).  
4. Perform paired t‑tests (FDR‑corrected) to compare original vs. modified metrics.  
5. Correlate accuracy drops with shifts in explainability metrics.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Accuracy vs. interpretability: High accuracy (e.g., DeBERTa) can coexist with low trigger attribution, indicating a non‑semantic heuristic. RoBERTa’s higher trigger attribution correlates with better (though still poor) performance on semantic breaks.  
- Task format: Using NLI makes the problem computationally testable but may not fully capture presupposition processing in generative contexts or broader discourse.  
- Variation scope: Focusing on two trigger types (“again”, possessives) and one conditional form (“If A, B”) controls variables but limits generalizability.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Catastrophic drop on semantic modification: Accuracy on subset 3 (lexical substitution) falls to ~0% for IND‑again variants, despite near‑perfect accuracy on original IND‑again.  
2. Stable trigger IG despite semantic change: High trigger IG ratio remains statistically unchanged (p>0.05) when trigger semantics are broken (subset 3), confirming reliance on position/identity.  
3. Context sensitivity only for IND‑again: Significant attention shifts (K1→K2, K2→special) and accuracy drops occur *only* for modified IND‑again in subset 4, indicating overfitting to a specific training pattern.  
4. Negative IG values: Observed for LLaMA/Gemma on some variants, indicating the trigger’s presence actively suppresses the “Entailment” prediction.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Zero‑shot baseline:** Evaluate all four models on subsets 1–4 without fine‑tuning. Establish whether pre‑trained knowledge aligns with human (high acc) or theory (0% acc) judgments. Expected: RoBERTa/DeBERTa perfect on originals; LLaMA/Gemma lower.  
2. **Structural variation test (subset 2):** Fine‑tune on CONFER, test on conjunction/disjunction/belief‑embedding variants. Hypothesis: Accuracy remains high (models ignore structure), but IG analysis reveals whether trigger focus is robust or shifts. Expected: RoBERTa’s trigger IG stable; DeBERTa’s low.  
3. **Semantic manipulation test (subset 3):** Fine‑tune on CONFER, test on trigger lexical substitutions (related → somewhat → unrelated). Core break test. Expected: Massive accuracy drop for IND‑again (all models), moderate for IND‑poss, slight for DEP. IG ratios should not significantly change between original and unrelated variants.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Do language models rely on the same shallow pattern‑matching heuristics when processing other pragmatic phenomena like scalar implicature or conventional implicature?**  
  The authors suggest extending the diagnostic approach to other pragmatic phenomena; it remains unknown whether heuristic reliance generalizes.
- **How do language models perform on presupposition projection when evaluated with a broader range of presupposition triggers beyond possessive pronouns and “again”?**  
  The dataset includes only two trigger types, omitting others (factive verbs, aspectual verbs, clefts). Generalizability is unclear.
- **Do the findings about heuristic reliance in NLI tasks generalize to generative settings where models must produce or reason about presuppositions in free text?**  
  The NLI format may not reflect processing in generation or other downstream tasks; generation‑based evaluations are needed.
- **Is the difference in trigger importance attribution between models (e.g., RoBERTa’s high vs. DeBERTa’s low IG ratios) primarily due to architectural differences or variations in training data and objectives?**  
  The paper compares off‑the‑shelf models without ablations, leaving the source of differing IG patterns speculative.

## Limitations
- Focus limited to two triggers (“again”, possessives) and one conditional (“If A, B”), restricting generalizability.
- NLI simplification may not capture full presupposition dynamics in discourse or generation.
- Explainability methods (IG, attention) are indirect and sensitive to implementation choices.
- Overfitting claim is inferential; alternative explanations (e.g., catastrophic interference) not fully excluded.
- Dataset biases (lexical, annotator) remain uncharacterized.

## Confidence
- **Human alignment (vs. theory):** High – near‑perfect accuracy on original examples, 0% on theory.
- **Shallow heuristic reliance:** High – drastic drops on semantic modifications, stable IG ratios.
- **Fine‑tuning overfitting:** Medium – zero‑shot vs. fine‑tuned contrast suggestive but not conclusive.
- **Architectural strategies:** Medium – distinct IG/attention patterns observed, causality not proven.

## Next Checks
1. Test additional triggers and conditionals (e.g., factive verbs, other embeddings) to assess the generality of heuristic reliance.
2. Obtain human judgments on the modified subsets (especially lexically substituted and context‑flipped items) to benchmark model failures against human performance.
3. Retrain models on a balanced version of CONFER that removes the spurious “related antecedent + again → Neutral” pattern to confirm the overfitting hypothesis.
