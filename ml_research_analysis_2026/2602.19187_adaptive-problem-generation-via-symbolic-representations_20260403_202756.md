# Adaptive Problem Generation via Symbolic Representations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19187
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19187
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Adaptive symbolic data generation (Opt-Sym) yields consistent accuracy gains (e.g., +3.18% for Qwen2.5-1.5B with PPO) over seed-only and natural-language baselines on multiple math benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a closed-loop framework for generating training data to improve small language models on mathematical reasoning via reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR). The core innovation lies in converting seed math problems into symbolic representations (SymPy/SMT code), modifying them through an optimized prompt whose parameters are adjusted based on student model performance, and rendering the modified symbolic problems back to natural language. Experiments with Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B models demonstrate that training on the adaptively generated data (Opt-Sym) consistently outperforms both seed-only training and training on data generated through naive or non-adaptive natural-language modification. Gains are attributed to increased data diversity, precise structural control, and tailored problem difficulty.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins by parsing seed math problems (e.g., from GSM8K, MATH) into symbolic code using SymPy (primary) or SMT-LIB (secondary). A generator model (GPT-5-mini) then modifies this code using a learned prompt `p_Sym_opt`. Prompt optimization is performed iteratively in a closed loop: for batches of symbolic seed problems, `p_Sym_opt` produces variants; these are rendered to natural language, solved symbolically for ground-truth answers, and presented to the student model; the student’s accuracy on the batch defines a loss (balancing difficulty and quality guardrails) that is backpropagated through TextGrad to update `p_Sym_opt`. After optimization, a final dataset is generated, filtered via a GPT-5-mini validator to ensure answer correctness, and combined with seed data to train the student model using RLVR (PPO or GRPO) with binary correctness rewards.

## Key Results
- For Qwen2.5-1.5B, Opt-Sym achieves average benchmark accuracy of 54.32% (PPO) and 54.71% (GRPO) vs. 51.14% and 51.40% for seed-only (+3.18% and +3.31%).
- With additional MATH seeds, Opt-Sym+MATH reaches 56.18% vs. 52.37% for seed+MATH (+3.81%).
- Generated data exhibits higher semantic diversity (average pairwise cosine distance 0.37 vs. 0.07 for Opt-NL) and increased difficulty (student pre-training accuracy lower than on seed data).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Symbolic representations enable precise structural control and automatic verification
- **Claim:** Operating on SymPy/SMT code allows targeted manipulation of mathematical features (operations, depth, constraints) and reliable solution generation, decoupling structure from language.
- **Core assumption:** Formal intermediates are more suitable than free-text for algorithmic modification and verification of mathematical properties.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2 explicitly lists benefits of symbolic abstraction; Section 4.3 reports diversity metric (0.37 vs. 0.07) supporting abstract variation.
- **Break condition:** If the symbolic formalism fails to capture the semantics of seed problems (e.g., due to ambiguity), control and verification degrade.

### Mechanism 2: Closed-loop prompt optimization tailors difficulty to the student’s evolving capabilities
- **Claim:** Using the student model’s incorrect answers as a signal, `p_Sym_opt` is refined to generate problems near the “zone of proximal development”—challenging but solvable.
- **Core assumption:** Student error patterns provide a dense, valid training signal for adjusting problem difficulty.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.4 defines loss `L_sym` with difficulty criterion; Figure 4 shows generated problems are harder (lower pre-training accuracy); Table 1 shows Opt-Sym outperforms non-adaptive baselines.
- **Break condition:** Noisy or biased student errors may cause overfitting to spurious signals, producing degenerate problems.

### Mechanism 3: Decoupling mathematical generation from linguistic realization increases diversity and reduces confounding
- **Claim:** Separate stages—symbolic modification and NL rendering—allow structural complexity to vary independently of narrative context, improving robustness.
- **Core assumption:** Mathematical reasoning ability is somewhat independent of specific lexical framing; varied contexts prevent overfitting to surface patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2 decoupling claim; Section 4.3 qualitative examples (e.g., “clips” → “candies,” “flour”); Opt-Sym shows more context changes than Opt-NL.
- **Break condition:** If the renderer introduces unnatural or inconsistent mappings, the diversity benefit could be offset by semantic noise.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Seed Parser -> Symbolic Modifier (M_gen + p_Sym_opt) -> NL Renderer (M_gen + p_render) -> Solution Generator/Solver -> Validator -> Prompt Optimizer (TextGrad) -> Student RLVR Trainer (VERL). Iterative loop exists between Symbolic Modifier and Prompt Optimizer.

**Critical path:** Seed Data -> Parse to SymPy -> (Iterative: p_opt modifies SymPy -> Render to NL -> Student predicts -> Evaluate accuracy -> TextGrad updates p_opt) -> Generate final dataset -> Filter -> RLVR train student model.

**Design tradeoffs:** Symbolic generation offers control/diversity but depends on high-quality parsing/solving and may yield unnatural renderings; NL generation is simpler but less controllable. Closed-loop adaptation increases compute cost versus open-loop generation. Use of proprietary GPT-5-mini for generation/filtering creates reproducibility and cost barriers.

**Failure signatures:** Low solution validity rate (many unsolvable/malformed problems). Diversity collapse (cosine distance of generated variants close to seed). No performance gain on benchmarks (generated data misaligned). Overfitting to student idiosyncrasies (good on generated distribution but poor generalization).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Establish baseline: Train student on seed data only; measure benchmark accuracy.  
2. Compare generation spaces: Train on Base-NL vs. Base-Sym (simple modifications without prompt optimization) to isolate symbolic space effect.  
3. Validate loop closure: Run one iteration of prompt optimization (Opt-Sym); train on that data; compare against Base-Sym and seed to confirm adaptation benefit.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the symbolic framework be extended to handle ambiguous or complex real-world reasoning beyond structured math problems?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The approach relies on converting problems into symbolic representation, limiting applicability to problems allowing reliable formalization; ambiguous language, implicit constraints, or complex reasoning may be difficult.
- **Why unresolved:** Demonstrated only on well-defined math tasks; extension to domains with ambiguity is untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the framework to non-mathematical domains with formal representations (e.g., logic puzzles, planning tasks) and report success/failure cases.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent does the student model’s initial error distribution affect the quality of adaptively generated problems, and how can overfitting to idiosyncratic patterns be mitigated?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Prompt optimization relies on student output; idiosyncratic error patterns may cause overfitting rather than genuine difficulty.
- **Why unresolved:** No investigation into how different student initial skills influence generated problem distribution; no mitigation techniques proposed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments varying student model initial performance (e.g., pre-trained vs. fine-tuned) and analyzing diversity/challenge of generated problems; explore regularization in prompt loss.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What are the performance implications of relying on proprietary LLMs like GPT-5-mini for generation and filtering, and how might the approach be adapted for fully reproducible, open-source alternatives?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Acknowledges concerns over reproducibility, cost, and potential bounding of student improvement by GPT-5-mini capabilities.
- **Why unresolved:** Dependency on a specific commercial model raises questions about cost, availability, and upper bounds; alternatives unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate pipeline using open-source models (e.g., Llama, Mistral) for generation/filtering; compare data quality and student performance to proprietary-based results.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How do different RL algorithms (beyond PPO/GRPO) interact with the adaptive generation loop, and does this impact the efficiency of difficulty adaptation?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Paper compares PPO and GRPO but does not explore other algorithms or synergy with adaptive generation; success depends on accurate difficulty evaluation via student performance, which could be algorithm-sensitive.
- **Why unresolved:** Methodology focuses on two algorithms; generalizability unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train student models with alternative RL algorithms (e.g., TRPO, A2C) using same generated data; measure convergence speed, final accuracy, and alignment with target difficulty.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What specific properties of SymPy representations (vs. SMT-LIB or direct NL modification) make them more effective for generating natural and solvable word problems, and can this be generalized to other formal languages?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] SymPy outperforms SMT-LIB and NL baselines; SMT-LIB’s syntax leads to unnatural problems due to low-level details, but precise factors (abstraction level, rendering ease) not deeply analyzed.
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding why SymPy works better could inform design of future symbolic systems and portability to other domains (physics, logic).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments manipulating SymPy representations (abstraction, constraints) and measuring naturalness scores and student solvability; extend to other formalisms (e.g., Prolog) and compare.

## Limitations
- **Proprietary dependency:** Heavy reliance on GPT-5-mini for generation and filtering hinders reproducibility and may bound student improvement.
- **Incomplete specifications:** Exact validation filter prompts and TextGrad hyperparameters (optimizer, learning rate) are not provided, leading to implementation variance.
- **Overfitting risk:** Adaptive loop uses student error signals that can be noisy or model-specific, potentially generating problems that exploit quirks rather than general skills.

## Confidence
- **Symbolic control & verification:** High (directly supported by diversity metrics and section claims).
- **Adaptive difficulty loop:** Medium (evidence from gains and pre-training difficulty, but depends on error signal quality).
- **Decoupled diversity:** Medium (supported by examples and metrics, but rendering quality unquantified).
