# Position: Spectral GNNs Are Neither Spectral Nor Superior for Node Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19091  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19091  
- **Reference count:** 37  
- **Primary result:** Spectral GNNs do not meaningfully use graph spectra nor consistently outperform spatial MPNNs; their reported effectiveness stems from mathematical equivalence to MPNNs or implementation bugs.

## Executive Summary
This position paper fundamentally challenges the theoretical and empirical justification for Spectral Graph Neural Networks (Spectral GNNs) in node classification. The authors prove two core flaws: graph Laplacian eigenvectors do not satisfy the necessary properties to form a Fourier basis for general graph signals, and the polynomial approximation narrative is mathematically invalid because an (n-1)-degree polynomial can exactly interpolate any spectral filter on an n-node graph. Consequently, the low-/high-pass filtering behavior attributed to models like GCN arises solely from message-passing dynamics, not spectral formulations. Case studies on directed spectral models (MagNet, HoloNet) reveal that their reported strong performance depends critically on implementation errors that reduce them to simpler spatial MPNNs; when corrected, accuracy weakens significantly. The overarching conclusion is that competitive results from spectral GNNs are better explained by their hidden equivalence to MPNNs or by inconsistencies in implementation and evaluation.

## Method Summary
The paper combines rigorous theoretical analysis with targeted empirical case studies. Theoretically, it disproves the validity of using Laplacian eigenvectors as a Fourier basis and exposes the triviality of polynomial interpolation in finite graphs. Empirically, it dissects two prominent directed spectral GNNs—MagNet and HoloNet—by reverse-engineering their implementations, identifying specific bugs (incorrect recurrence, first-order-only aggregation), and quantifying the performance impact of normalization choices and parameter mixing. Corrected versions and ablations are benchmarked on datasets like Telegram, Chameleon, and Squirrel to isolate the sources of reported gains.

## Key Results
- Graph Laplacian eigenvectors do not constitute a valid Fourier basis for graph signals on general graphs, undermining the spectral foundation of these models.
- The justification for polynomial filters as "approximations" of ideal spectral responses is invalid; a degree-(n-1) polynomial can exactly match any filter, blurring the line between spectral and spatial methods.
- The superior accuracy of MagNet (95.6% on Telegram) and HoloNet on heterophilic graphs is attributable to implementation bugs that effectively simplify them to standard MPNNs (e.g., GraphSAGE with GCN-style normalization); corrected versions show substantially weaker performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Polynomial filters in spectral GNNs are mathematically identical to spatial message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) with bounded receptive fields. A polynomial filter \(h(L) = \sum_{k=0}^K \alpha_k L^k\) applied to features \(X\) yields \(\sum_{k=0}^K \alpha_k L^k X\). Each term \(L^k X\) aggregates information from nodes exactly \(k\) hops away, which is the precise operation of a \(K\)-layer MPNN. This equivalence holds for any polynomial filter applied to a graph matrix (e.g., normalized Laplacian or adjacency), so long as the matrix encodes connectivity such that its \(k\)-th power represents \(k\)-step walks. The spectral perspective adds no computational or representational advantage beyond the spatial aggregation already captured by MPNNs.

### Mechanism 2
The low-pass and high-pass filtering behaviors credited to spectral GNNs (e.g., GCN) originate from message-passing dynamics, not from properties of the graph Fourier transform. Viewing message passing as a discrete-time, shift-invariant process along hop depth \(n\) with update \(X^{(n+1)} = S X^{(n)}\), operators like \(I + S\) (self-loop plus one-hop aggregation) naturally smooth features (low-pass), while \(I - S\) emphasizes differences (high-pass). This hop-domain viewpoint directly characterizes filtering behavior through simple linear operations on propagation depth, paralleling classical LTI systems but without requiring eigen-decomposition. Nonlinear activations may modulate the response, but the linear core retains this filtering property.

### Mechanism 3
The strong empirical performance of directed spectral GNNs like MagNet and HoloNet is primarily a byproduct of implementation bugs that collapse them into simpler spatial MPNNs, not their intended spectral mechanisms. In MagNet, an incorrect Chebyshev recurrence (using \(-cA\) instead of the proper Hermitian Laplacian) and a complex phase construction yield an effective operator equivalent to GraphSAGE with GCN-style symmetric normalization. In HoloNet, a coding error in `ComplexFaberConv` restricts it to first-order propagations, making it merely a weighted sum of \(A X W\) and \(A^\top X W\) terms. These simplifications transform the models into standard MPNNs, explaining their competitive results. When implementations are corrected to match the described spectral algorithms, performance drops significantly, confirming that the spectral mechanisms themselves offer no advantage.

## Foundational Learning
- **Graph Fourier Transform and Spectral Graph Theory:** The paper contests the assumption that Laplacian eigenvectors can serve as a Fourier basis for graph signals. A valid Fourier basis must support a convolution theorem and have well-defined frequency components; Laplacian eigenvectors fail these for general graphs due to non-orthogonality in the signal space and sensitivity to graph perturbations.  
  *Quick check:* What key properties define a Fourier basis, and why do graph Laplacian eigenvectors fall short for arbitrary graphs?

- **Polynomial Approximation in Spectral GNNs:** The "approximation" argument for polynomial filters is shown to be trivial because, on a graph with \(n\) nodes and distinct eigenvalues, a degree-(n-1) polynomial can exactly interpolate any target spectral response. This means low-order polynomial filters do not inherently approximate a frequency response; they simply implement multi-hop spatial aggregations whose "spectral" interpretation is coincidental.  
  *Quick check:* Why does a degree-(n-1) polynomial trivially interpolate any filter, and how does this exactness relate to the number of message-passing hops?

- **Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs):** MPNNs provide the spatial framework that subsumes polynomial-based spectral GNNs. A \(K\)-layer MPNN aggregates features from \(K\)-hop neighborhoods through iterative neighborhood mixing, which algebraically matches a \(K\)-th order polynomial of the adjacency or Laplacian. Understanding MPNNs clarifies that spectral GNNs are not fundamentally different but offer a less transparent, more computationally costly viewpoint.  
  *Quick check:* How does a \(K\)-layer MPNN aggregate information, and what is the precise relationship between the polynomial order \(K\) and the number of MPNN layers?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
- GCN: raw adjacency (A) -> add self-loop (I) -> compute \(\tilde{D}^{-1/2} \tilde{A} \tilde{D}^{-1/2}\) -> multiply with features and weights (\( \hat{A} H W \))
- MagNet: symmetrized adjacency (cA) -> flawed Chebyshev recurrence -> complex phase matrix multiplication -> real/imaginary aggregation
- HoloNet: adjacency (A) and transpose (A⊤) -> ComplexFaberConv (buggy: only first-order AXW and A⊤XW terms) -> learned coefficient mixing -> output

Critical path:  
A typical spectral GNN layer computes a polynomial of the graph matrix (e.g., Laplacian) applied to node features: \( \sum_{k=0}^K \alpha_k L^k X \). Expanding \(L^k X\) reveals it sums contributions from nodes exactly \(k\) hops away. Thus, the entire operation is a weighted, multi-hop spatial aggregation—exactly a \(K\)-layer MPNN followed by a linear combination of the intermediate representations. No spectral decomposition is actually performed during inference; the computation is purely spatial.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Spectral vs. Spatial:** Polynomial spectral GNNs offer no representational benefit over spatial MPNNs but incur overhead from eigen-decomposition (if used) and obscure interpretation. The spatial view is simpler, scales to large graphs, and aligns with actual computation.
- **Directed graphs:** Spectral approaches attempt to use Hermitian Laplacians to handle directionality, adding complex arithmetic and recurrence complexity. Spatial MPNNs with edge-wise operations (e.g., Dir-GNN) handle direction naturally and more efficiently.
- **Normalization:** Choice of normalization dramatically affects performance. GCN-style symmetric normalization (\(\hat{D}^{-1/2}\hat{A}\hat{D}^{-1/2}\)) acts as a low-pass smoother and hurts accuracy on heterophilic graphs; row normalization (\(\hat{D}^{-1}\hat{A}\)) preserves degree information and often performs better.

Failure signatures:
- Claims of "spectral filtering" paired with low-order polynomial layers (K ≤ 3) likely indicate a hidden MPNN, as higher-order spectral filters would require explicit eigenvector use.
- Directed spectral models reporting unusually high gains on heterophilic datasets may suffer from implementation bugs (e.g., incorrect recurrence, first-order-only aggregation) or mismatched normalization (using symmetric normalization on an asymmetric graph).
- If increasing polynomial order (i.e., number of MPNN layers) does not improve or even degrades accuracy, it suggests the model is already capturing useful spatial locality and higher orders cause over-smoothing, not a spectral limitation.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Equivalence demonstration:** Implement a 1-layer GCN and a 1-layer MPNN that both compute \((I + \hat{A}) X W\). Verify identical outputs on a small graph by feeding random features and comparing element-wise. This establishes that the spectral polynomial is just a spatial aggregation.
2. **MagNet reduction and normalization ablation:** Reproduce MagNet's reported 95.6% accuracy on Telegram using the flawed Chebyshev recurrence (Equation 7) and symmetric normalization. Then switch to row normalization and observe the accuracy drop to ~38%, confirming the model's dependence on a specific MPNN-like normalization rather than any spectral property.
3. **HoloNet code audit and correction:** Inspect the `ComplexFaberConv` implementation from the original HoloNet codebase to confirm it only uses first-order propagations \(A X W\) and \(A^\top X W\). Then modify it to perform intended multi-order aggregation (e.g., via Chebyshev recursion) and compare accuracy on Chameleon/Squirrel. The buggy version's gains should diminish or disappear.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Can any spectral GNN architecture that uses polynomial filters on the graph Laplacian be exactly expressed as a spatial message-passing neural network (MPNN)? The paper demonstrates this reduction for GCN, MagNet, and HoloNet, but a general proof (or counterexample) for all polynomial-based spectral GNNs is lacking. Without such a theorem, it remains possible that some spectral designs yield unique capabilities beyond finite-hop MPNNs.

### Open Question 2
How does the known instability of Laplacian eigenvectors under small graph perturbations affect the robustness and generalization of spectral GNNs relative to spatial MPNNs? The paper highlights eigenvector sensitivity theoretically but provides no empirical measurement of how often this instability occurs in real-world graphs or how it translates into accuracy differences under structural noise. Quantifying this effect would clarify whether spectral methods are practically fragile.

### Open Question 3
Can a theoretically sound alternative to spectral GNNs be built on hop-domain filtering—that is, using shift-invariant operators on propagation depth—while retaining signal-processing intuition and avoiding Laplacian-eigenvector pitfalls? The paper sketches a hop-domain view where message-passing steps act as a "time" axis, but it stops short of defining a general class of hop-invariant operators, characterizing their expressive power, or providing a practical instantiation that surpasses existing MPNNs. A formal framework and empirical validation are needed.

## Limitations
- The theoretical critique assumes distinct Laplacian eigenvalues; repeated eigenvalues could affect the polynomial interpolation argument and the validity of the Fourier basis claims in degenerate graphs.
- The bug analysis focuses on specific implementations of MagNet and HoloNet; other codebases or later versions might have corrected these issues, potentially restoring some spectral behavior.
- The paper's conclusions are derived from node classification tasks; implications for other graph learning problems (e.g., link prediction, graph classification) remain unexplored and may differ.

## Confidence
- **Fourier basis invalidity:** Medium (strong mathematical reasoning, but edge cases with special graph structures might behave differently).
- **Polynomial approximation flaw:** Medium (rigorous for distinct eigenvalues; the practical impact on low-order filters is indirect but compelling).
- **Empirical bug attribution:** High (concrete code errors documented, normalization effects reproducible, and corrected models show performance drops).

## Next Checks
1. **MagNet normalization ablation:** On the Telegram dataset, run the flawed MagNet implementation with symmetric normalization vs. row normalization. Expect a large accuracy gap (∼95% vs. ∼38%) to confirm the model's reliance on GCN-style smoothing rather than spectral filtering.
2. **HoloNet code audit and multi-order enforcement:** Examine the `ComplexFaberConv` layer to verify it only computes first-order propagations. Then modify it to use proper multi-order aggregation (e.g., via Chebyshev polynomials) and re-evaluate on Chameleon/Squirrel. The performance should degrade relative to the buggy version, indicating the gains were accidental.
3. **Polynomial depth scaling:** Train GCNs (or general polynomial GNNs) with increasing number of layers (K) on heterophilic benchmark graphs (e.g., Squirrel). If accuracy plateaus or declines beyond K=2, it supports the claim that higher-order spectral filters provide no spatial benefit and that low-order MPNNs are sufficient.
