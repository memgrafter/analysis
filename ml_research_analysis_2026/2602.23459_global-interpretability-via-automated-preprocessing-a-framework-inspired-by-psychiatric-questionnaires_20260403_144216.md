# Global Interpretability via Automated Preprocessing: A Framework Inspired by Psychiatric Questionnaires

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23459
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23459
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: REFINE matches nonlinear predictive accuracy while providing globally interpretable linear coefficients, superior temporal structure preservation, and faster runtime.

## Executive Summary
Psychiatric questionnaires yield noisy, weakly predictive data. While flexible nonlinear models improve accuracy, their lack of global interpretability hinders clinical trust. We propose REFINE, a two‑stage framework that separates preprocessing from prediction. First, a nonlinear preprocessor—trained with future measurements as supervision—stabilizes baseline items while preserving item‑wise alignment, leveraging longitudinal redundancy. Second, an exactly linear decoder maps the stabilized items to future outcomes, producing a globally interpretable coefficient matrix. Theoretically, REFINE is Bayes‑optimal and uniquely identified under minimal criteria. Across three longitudinal psychiatric datasets (NAPLES‑3, STAR*D, ABCD), REFINE attains predictive performance comparable to state‑of‑the‑art nonlinear models in forward correlation, substantially outperforms other interpretable approaches in backward correlation and cosine similarity, and runs faster. Ablations confirm that nonlinear preprocessing and matrix inversion are essential for optimal accuracy and interpretability.

## Method Summary
REFINE operates in two distinct phases. In the preprocessing phase, a flexible nonlinear model (e.g., a neural network) is trained to predict future item responses from baseline items, using the future measurements as target variables. This induces the preprocessor to denoise and stabilize the baseline representation while maintaining alignment with the original item space, thanks to the longitudinal redundancy of the questionnaires. The trained preprocessor is then applied to all baseline items to produce a set of stabilized features. In the second phase, a linear decoder is derived by solving a matrix equation that maps these stabilized features to the outcomes of interest; this solution is exact (via matrix inversion) given the preprocessed features and outcomes. The resulting linear model is globally interpretable through its coefficient matrix, which directly quantifies each item’s contribution to predicted outcomes. The decoupled design allows the nonlinear preprocessor to capture complex noise structures without sacrificing the simplicity and transparency of a linear predictor, and it enables theoretical guarantees under mild assumptions.

## Key Results
- REFINE matches or exceeds the predictive performance (forward correlation) of nonlinear baselines on all three datasets.
- It substantially outperforms other interpretable models in backward correlation (preservation of temporal structure) and cosine similarity (item alignment).
- REFINE runs faster than compared nonlinear methods.
- Ablations demonstrate that removing nonlinear preprocessing or replacing matrix inversion with iterative solvers degrades both accuracy and interpretability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The key insight is to use future measurements as a supervision signal for preprocessing. Because the same items are administered repeatedly over time, future item values contain information about the latent, less noisy constructs that also influence baseline responses. By training a nonlinear model to predict these future values, the preprocessor learns to map noisy baseline items into a stabilized latent space that preserves item‑wise semantics. This stabilized space is then amenable to a simple linear mapping to outcomes, yielding an interpretable coefficient matrix without the accuracy loss typically associated with linear models. The two‑stage design also separates the concerns: the preprocessor handles noise reduction, while the decoder provides direct, global explanations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Longitudinal Redundancy**: Repeated measurements of the same items across time provide a natural source of supervision. *Why needed*: Enables denoising without external labels. *Quick check*: Compute correlation between baseline and follow‑up items.
- **Nonlinear Preprocessing**: Flexible models capture complex transformations of noisy inputs. *Why needed*: Linear methods cannot remove nonlinear noise patterns. *Quick check*: Does a neural net significantly improve prediction of future items compared to linear regression?
- **Linear Decoding**: A linear model on preprocessed features yields globally interpretable coefficients. *Why needed*: Direct mapping between items and outcomes fosters clinical trust. *Quick check*: After preprocessing, does a linear model fit outcomes well (high R²)?
- **Matrix Inversion**: Provides an exact, closed‑form solution for the decoder when the preprocessed feature matrix is full rank. *Why needed*: Avoids iterative optimization and guarantees uniqueness under identification conditions. *Quick check*: Is the feature matrix after preprocessing invertible (non‑zero determinant)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Baseline Items -> Nonlinear Preprocessor (trained with Future Items) -> Stabilized Items -> Linear Decoder (via matrix inversion) -> Outcome Predictions

**Critical Path**: 1) Collect baseline items and corresponding future items for training. 2) Train the nonlinear preprocessor to minimize future item prediction error. 3) Apply the preprocessor to baseline items to obtain stabilized features. 4) Compute the linear decoder by solving ZᵀW = Yᵀ (matrix inversion). 5) For new subjects, transform baseline items with the preprocessor, then apply the linear decoder to predict outcomes.

**Design Tradeoffs**: Nonlinear preprocessor adds capacity to denoise but risks overfitting; using future data as supervision requires longitudinal data; linear decoder guarantees interpretability but may underfit if the preprocessor fails to capture all relevant structure; matrix inversion assumes no multicollinearity; the two‑stage approach is not end‑to‑end optimized but ensures transparency.

**Failure Signatures**: Preprocessor distorts item alignment (checked via reconstruction error); stabilized features become rank‑deficient (matrix inversion fails); poor generalization when training and test epochs differ; decoder coefficients unstable across cross‑validation folds; overfitting of preprocessor (train loss << validation loss).

**First Experiments**: 1) Replicate main result on NAPLS‑3: compare forward correlation of REFINE to a nonlinear baseline. 2) Ablation: replace nonlinear preprocessor with identity and measure drop in backward correlation and cosine similarity. 3) Clinical validity: visualize top decoder coefficients and verify they correspond to known risk factors for psychosis (e.g., unusual thought content).

## Limitations
- Evaluation is confined to three psychiatric cohorts; generalizability to other domains remains untested.
- Theoretical identification relies on minimal criteria that may be violated by high missingness or non‑stationary data distributions.
- Novel interpretability metrics (backward correlation, cosine similarity) have not been validated against clinical expertise or decision‑making impact.
- The preprocessor requires future measurements, limiting applicability in strictly prospective settings.

## Confidence
- Theoretical guarantees (Bayes‑optimality, unique identification): **High**
- Empirical effectiveness (matching nonlinear baselines in forward correlation, superiority in backward correlation/cosine similarity, speed): **Medium**
- Ablation results (necessity of nonlinear preprocessing and matrix inversion): **High**

## Next Checks
1. Test REFINE on diverse non‑psychiatric longitudinal data (e.g., electronic health records, climate time series) to assess domain transferability.
2. Conduct a clinician user study to evaluate whether decoder coefficients align with clinical reasoning and improve trust/decisions compared to black‑box models.
3. Compare REFINE against a broader set of interpretable models, including inherently interpretable neural networks and SHAP‑based linear surrogates, using sparsity and stability metrics.
