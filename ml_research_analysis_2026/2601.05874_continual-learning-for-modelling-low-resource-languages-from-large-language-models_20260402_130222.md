# Continual-learning for Modelling Low-Resource Languages from Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05874
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05874
- **Reference count:** 20
- **Primary result:** A continual learning framework with a shared replay adapter and POS-based code-switching during experience replay substantially reduces catastrophic forgetting when adapting large language models to low-resource languages, achieving average cross-lingual accuracy retention improvements of 5–8% over no replay and outperforming LWF and EWC with only ~2% parameter increase.

## Executive Summary
Sequential fine-tuning of large language models for low-resource languages leads to catastrophic forgetting of previously acquired linguistic knowledge. This paper introduces a parameter-efficient continual learning approach that freezes the multilingual encoder and trains per-language adapters alongside a single shared replay adapter. During training, experience replay is periodically triggered: English data (the high-resource pivot) is transformed via part-of-speech (POS)-guided code-switching into a target language from the set of already learned languages, and only the replay adapter is updated on this synthetic data. This isolates the consolidation of past knowledge and preserves language-specific representations. Evaluated on intent classification (MTOP), question answering (PAXQA), and visual QA (XGQA) across 13 languages, the method significantly improves average accuracy retention compared to no replay, random code-switching, and standard continual learning techniques like Learning without Forgetting and Elastic Weight Consolidation.

## Method Summary
The core architecture uses a frozen XLM-RoBERTa Base encoder. For each new language in the sequence, a lightweight bottleneck adapter is inserted into each transformer layer. A single shared replay adapter is also added, trained throughout. Training proceeds sequentially: on the current language’s data, both the language-specific adapter and the replay adapter are updated. Every *f* batches (e.g., 10) and after the first language, a replay phase occurs: a batch from the stored English dataset is selected, tokens of a chosen POS category (e.g., adjectives) are replaced with equivalents from a randomly chosen previously seen language using a bilingual lexicon (derived from MUSE embeddings), creating a syntactically valid code-switched example. Only the replay adapter is updated on this data. The approach requires storing only the English data and bilingual lexicons, adds ~2% parameters, and avoids modifying the frozen backbone.

## Key Results
- On MTOP, the proposed method achieves average accuracy of 90.25–92.38% across language sequences, versus 84.49% for the no-replay baseline.
- It consistently outperforms random code-switching, Learning without Forgetting, and Elastic Weight Consolidation on all three tasks.
- Ablations confirm the necessity of both the shared replay adapter and POS-guided code-switching; removing either causes significant performance degradation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Shared Replay Adapter Decouples Replay Optimization
- **Claim:** A shared replay adapter isolates the consolidation of past knowledge, preventing interference with language-specific representations.
- **Mechanism:** During replay phases, only the replay adapter is updated using synthetic code-switched data from the pivot language (English). This confines weight changes to a dedicated module, leaving language-specific adapter weights untouched and preserving their integrity.
- **Core assumption:** The replay adapter has sufficient capacity to encode cross-lingual, task-relevant knowledge without overwriting language-specific features.
- **Evidence:** 
  - Abstract: "a shared replay adapter... maintains strong cross-lingual generalization... with minimal increase (~2%) in the overall parameter footprint."
  - Section 2.1: "During replay phases, only the parameters of the replay adapter are updated, preserving the integrity of previously learned representations while mitigating catastrophic forgetting."
  - Table 4 ablation shows that removing the replay adapter degrades performance.

### Mechanism 2: POS-Guided Code-Switching Strengthens Structural Understanding
- **Claim:** POS-based token substitution creates syntactically valid code-switched sentences that provide a stronger learning signal for cross-lingual transfer than random substitution.
- **Mechanism:** Tokens of a selected POS category (e.g., adjectives) are replaced with their equivalents in a target language using a bilingual lexicon, preserving the syntactic frame (e.g., noun-verb order). This forces the model to rely on language-agnostic structural patterns rather than surface lexical matches.
- **Core assumption:** Syntactic structure is more transferable across languages than lexical semantics; maintaining grammaticality during code-switching yields better replay.
- **Evidence:** 
  - Abstract: "POS-based code-switching... preserves syntactic structures during experience replay."
  - Section 2.2: "POS-based code switch replacement also preserves the original meaning and context of the sentence... The code mixed sentence still sounds natural."
  - Section 3.6: "POS-based code switch consistently outperforms random code switch across all evaluated language sequences."

### Mechanism 3: Frozen Encoder with Lightweight Adapters Balances Plasticity and Stability
- **Claim:** Freezing the encoder backbone and training only small adapters limits interference and enables parameter-efficient continual learning.
- **Mechanism:** The multilingual encoder (270M parameters) remains fixed. Language-specific adapters and the shared replay adapter (together ~2% of base model) are updated. This confines changes to low-rank matrices, reducing catastrophic forgetting while preserving the encoder’s general multilingual knowledge.
- **Core assumption:** The frozen encoder already possesses sufficient universal linguistic knowledge; specialization can be achieved via adapters.
- **Evidence:** 
  - Section 2.1: "Throughout multilingual continual learning, the encoder backbone remains frozen, while both the language-specific adapters and the shared replay adapter are jointly optimised."
  - Section 3.7: The method outperforms LWF and EWC, which modify shared weights or rely on distillation.
  - Parameter efficiency: only ~2% increase in total parameters.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) via Adapters**
  - **Why needed:** The method uses lightweight bottleneck adapters inserted into transformer layers to adapt the frozen encoder. Only these small modules are updated, enabling efficiency and reducing forgetting.
  - **Quick check:** If the base model has 270M parameters and total increase is ~2%, how many parameters are added? (~5.4M).

- **Concept: Experience Replay in Continual Learning**
  - **Why needed:** Replay mitigates catastrophic forgetting by revisiting past knowledge. Here, it uses synthetically code-switched data from a stored pivot language, avoiding raw data storage.
  - **Quick check:** How does this replay differ from classic RL replay? (Synthetic data transformation; focused on updating a specific adapter, not the whole network).

- **Concept: Code-Switching as Structured Data Augmentation**
  - **Why needed:** Code-switching creates syntactically valid multilingual examples for replay, helping the model learn language-agnostic structural patterns.
  - **Quick check:** Why is POS-guided replacement better than random? (Preserves syntactic frames, providing a stronger transfer signal).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Frozen Encoder (XLM-RoBERTa Base) -> Language-Specific Adapter (A_l) AND Shared Replay Adapter (A_replay) -> Task Head.
  Code-Switch Generator: takes English batch and target language (from previously seen) -> code-switched batch.

- **Critical path:**
  1. Initialize frozen encoder, A_replay, and A_l1 for English.
  2. Train on English: update A_l1 and A_replay on every batch.
  3. For each subsequent language l_t:
     - Initialize A_l_t.
     - For each batch:
        - If batch count mod 10 == 0 and t>1: trigger replay. Sample English batch, code-switch to random previously seen language, update only A_replay.
        - Else: update both A_l_t and A_replay on current batch.
  4. Repeat until all languages trained. Evaluate on all seen languages.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Pros: Minimal parameters (~2%), isolates forgetting mitigation, syntactically grounded replay.
  - Cons: Requires high-resource pivot language; sensitive to POS tagger quality and bilingual lexicon; hyperparameters (replay frequency f, code-switch ratio ρ) need tuning.
  - vs. Alternatives: Avoids expensive Fisher computation (EWC) or logit distillation (LWF); does not need all languages simultaneously (joint training).

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Sudden drop in accuracy for early languages after later training → insufficient replay or replay adapter capacity overflow.
  - Code-switched sentences unnatural → lexicon gaps or POS errors.
  - No improvement over no-replay baseline → replay frequency too low or code-switch ratio too small.
  - Ablation: removing replay adapter causes performance drop.

- **First 3 experiments to try:**
  1. **Baseline forgetting:** Train sequentially on 3 languages with no replay. Expect significant accuracy drop for first two languages.
  2. **Replay adapter ablation:** Use POS-CS but remove the replay adapter (update language adapters during replay). Compare to full method.
  3. **POS category selection:** For a fixed sequence, vary the POS category used for code-switching (e.g., adjectives vs. nouns vs. random). Confirm that POS-based outperforms random and that adjective often best on MTOP.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Why do adjectives as a POS category yield the highest accuracy in code-switching replay?** Empirically observed but lacking linguistic justification.
- **Can other PEFT methods (e.g., Prefix Tuning, QLoRA) match or surpass adapters for multilingual continual learning?** Only adapters were evaluated.
- **How does machine translation quality affect the effectiveness of POS-based code-switching?** The study used NLLB translations for low-resource languages, but the impact of translation artifacts is unquantified.

## Limitations
- Bilingual lexicon construction details (alignment, filtering) are underspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Optimal POS category for code-switching appears sequence-dependent; it is unclear whether it was selected via validation.
- Evaluation limited to ≤6 languages; replay adapter capacity effects for longer sequences are unknown.

## Confidence
- **Effectiveness** (the method improves retention): Medium-High (multi-dataset evidence, but reproducibility concerns and limited sequence length).
- **Shared replay adapter mechanism** (the adapter isolation helps): Medium (ablation suggests benefit, but bottleneck for many languages untested).
- **POS-guided code-switching superiority** (over random CS): Medium (consistent gains, but sensitive to POS tagger quality and POS selection).

## Next Checks
1. **Lexicon & POS replication:** Implement the MUSE alignment pipeline and generate dictionaries for all 13 languages. Measure token-switch coverage and POS tagger accuracy. Produce fluent code-switched examples.
2. **Scalability test:** Train on 10-language sequences with the proposed method and compare average accuracy to no-replay baseline. Observe for replay adapter saturation.
3. **Replay adapter ablation:** Disable the shared replay adapter and perform replay by updating language-specific adapters directly. Measure forgetting relative to full method to isolate the adapter’s role.
