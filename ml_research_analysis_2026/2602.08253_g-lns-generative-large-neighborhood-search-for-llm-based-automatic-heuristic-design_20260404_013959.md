# G-LNS: Generative Large Neighborhood Search for LLM-Based Automatic Heuristic Design

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08253  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08253  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** G-LNS significantly outperforms state-of-the-art LLM-based AHD methods and classical solvers like LKH-3 and OR-Tools on TSP/CVRP benchmarks, achieving near-optimal gaps (1.10%–1.31% on TSP100–200) with 5× fewer queries.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces G-LNS, a generative evolutionary framework that co-evolves destroy and repair operators for Large Neighborhood Search using large language models. It tackles the structural bottleneck in LLM-based Automated Heuristic Design, where prior methods cannot escape deep local optima in complex combinatorial problems. By maintaining dual populations, a synergy matrix to capture operator coupling, and an adaptive cooperative evaluation, G-LNS discovers high-performing LNS heuristics. On TSP and CVRP benchmarks, it achieves near-optimal solution gaps (1.10%–1.31% on TSP100–200) and outperforms both classical solvers and existing LLM-based AHD approaches, while using far fewer LLM queries and converging faster.

## Method Summary
G-LNS searches the space of LNS operators via a coevolutionary process. Two small populations (size 5) store destroy and repair operators. Evaluation runs multiple episodes of adaptive LNS: in each, operators are selected proportionally to adaptive weights, applied in pairs, and rewards update both the weights and a synergy matrix S that records pairwise performance. After every few generations, the worst operators (by global fitness) are pruned. New operators are generated by a large language model using three strategies: mutation on a single parent, homogeneous crossover on two parents of the same type, or synergistic joint crossover that inherits from a high-synergy destroy-repair pair. The framework is trained for 200 generations, after which the best operator pair is used to solve test instances. This approach combines the generative power of LLMs with evolutionary pressures that explicitly account for operator interactions, yielding effective LNS heuristics with minimal query budget.

## Key Results
- On TSP100–200, G-LNS achieves gaps of 1.10%–1.31%, while prior LLM-based AHD methods exceed 10% and classical LKH-3 is within 0.1% but with more queries.  
- On CVRP100–200, G-LNS attains gaps near 0% and superior solution quality to OR-Tools, with faster convergence (≈70 s vs 320 s for OR-Tools).  
- The discovered operators generalize robustly, reducing gaps to 2.8% on TSPLib and 15.9% on CVRPLib Set F, far below baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Co-evolving destroy and repair as coupled pairs**  
G-LNS evolves destroy and repair operators together in tightly linked pairs rather than independently. The evaluation engine always applies a destroy operator from one population together with a repair operator from the other, recording their joint reward. The evolutionary operators include synergistic joint crossover, which selects a high-synergy parent pair and breeds a new destroy-repair couple that inherits traits from both. This ensures that the discovered operators are complementary: the destroy operator creates a challenging subproblem that the repair operator can efficiently reconstruct. The core assumption is that LNS performance is a function of the interaction between destroy and repair, not just their standalone quality. Ablating synergistic crossover (w/o Syn.) degrades performance on TSP50 from 0.37% to 1.24% gap, confirming the importance of coupling. If synergy estimates become too noisy (due to few evaluation episodes), the matrix may misidentify complementary pairs, leading to ineffective couplings.

**Mechanism 2: Synergy matrix guides recombination**  
The synergy matrix S accumulates rewards for each destroy-repair pair across evaluation episodes. At the end of a management phase, the pair with the highest synergy score is chosen as parents for synergistic joint crossover. This allows successful couplings to be propagated to offspring, effectively “remembering” which combinations work well. The assumption is that high accumulated synergy indicates structural complementarity that can be inherited. The matrix size is N×N (N=5), trivial to store. Ablating synergy (w/o Syn.) consistently yields worse results across TSP and CVRP benchmarks, demonstrating the matrix’s role. However, early high-reward pairs could dominate, reducing diversity and causing premature convergence; periodic resets of S and small N mitigate this risk.

**Mechanism 3: Adaptive weight updates concentrate sampling**  
During each evaluation episode, operators are selected by roulette wheel based on adaptive weights W_d and W_r. After applying a pair (d_i, r_j), their weights are updated via w ← λw + (1−λ)σ, biasing future selections toward operators with recent high rewards. This focuses computational effort on promising operators while maintaining some inertia. The assumption is that recent performance better predicts utility in the current search stage than long-term averages. Ablating adaptivity (w/o Adapt.) increases gaps (e.g., TSP50 0.95% vs. 0.37%), and sensitivity analysis shows balanced λ=0.5 and reward set {1.5,1.2,0.8,0.1} yields the best trade‑off. Too high λ slows adaptation; too low λ overreacts to noise.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Dual Populations (𝓟𝒹 and 𝓟𝓇) store destroy and repair operators. The Evaluation Engine runs K=10 episodes of adaptive LNS, each with T=100 iterations, using adaptive weights W to select operator pairs, compute rewards σ, and update Global Fitness F (per operator) and Synergy Matrix S (N×N). The Evolution Controller periodically ranks operators by F, prunes the bottom M=2 from each population, resets F and S, and invokes the LLM Interface to generate new operators via mutation (m1/m2), homogeneous crossover (c1), or synergistic joint crossover (c2). The new operators replace the pruned ones. Key metrics: F (size N each), S (N×N), W (size N each). The LLM backbone used is DeepSeek‑V3.2.

**Critical Path**  
1. Initialise 𝓟𝒹 and 𝓟𝓇 with seed operators (e.g., Random Removal, Greedy Insertion) and LLM‑generated operators (i1/i2). Set F=0, S=0, W=1.  
2. For generation g=1 to G_max=200:  
   a. Run K evaluation episodes, each performing T inner LNS iterations with adaptive weight updates, accumulating F and S.  
   b. If g mod K == 0: prune bottom M operators from each pool based on F; reset F and S; use LLM to generate new operators (sampling from {m1,m2,c1,c2}) until pools return to size N; validate and add.  
3. Return the best destroy‑repair pair (typically highest synergy) and its best-found solution x*.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Population size N=5 balances evaluation cost and diversity; small N risks premature convergence, mitigated by joint crossover and periodic metric resets.  
- Synergy matrix S scales as N²; with N=5 it is negligible, but larger N would require more samples for reliable estimates and more storage.  
- Adaptive weights (λ=0.5) improve sample efficiency by focusing on recent successes but may reduce exploration if λ is too low or too high.  
- Using an LLM as a variation operator reduces the number of required generations (200 vs. 1000) but incurs high token costs; the trade‑off is between API expense and algorithmic performance.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Stagnation: No improvement in best fitness over many generations.  
- High validation failure rate: Many LLM-generated operators fail syntax/IO checks, indicating prompt issues.  
- Diversity collapse: Synergy matrix dominated by a few pairs; populations become homogenized.  
- Training‑test gap: Excellent performance on random held‑outs but poor on TSPLib/CVRPLib, suggesting overfitting.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. Ablate synergistic joint crossover (c2): Run G-LNS without c2 (only mutation and c1) and compare performance to full version.  
2. Vary destruction ratio ε: Train operators with ε∈{0.1,0.2,0.3} and evaluate on test sets to assess adaptation to disruption magnitude.  
3. Cross‑size generalization: Train on TSP50, evaluate on TSP10/20/100/200 and TSPLib; compare with baselines like EoH‑S.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1: Extension to multi‑objective optimization**  
  The paper explicitly states future work will extend G-LNS to multi‑objective settings. The current framework is single‑objective; multi‑objective optimization requires new evaluation metrics, selection mechanisms, and operator designs to handle trade‑offs. Resolution would involve implementing a multi‑objective variant (e.g., bi‑objective TSP), comparing the achieved Pareto front against state‑of‑the‑art multi‑objective metaheuristics, and analyzing diversity and convergence.

- **Open Question 2: Transfer to non‑routing combinatorial problems**  
  The conclusion mentions investigating broader applicability beyond routing. G‑LNS has only been tested on TSP, CVRP, and OVRP; other domains (scheduling, bin packing) have different constraint structures and solution representations, which may require fundamentally different operator logic. Evidence would be applying G‑LNS to several non‑routing COPs (e.g., job shop scheduling, knapsack) and comparing against hand‑crafted heuristics and other AHD methods.

- **Open Question 3: Impact of LLM choice**  
  Inferred from exclusive use of DeepSeek‑V3.2 with no comparisons. Different LLMs vary in code generation, reasoning, and instruction following, potentially affecting evolutionary dynamics and operator quality. Resolution: re‑run the pipeline with multiple LLMs (GPT‑4o, Claude 3, Llama 3) on the same tasks, compare operator diversity, fitness distributions, convergence speeds, and failure rates.

- **Open Question 4: Theoretical relationship of synergy matrix to joint performance**  
  The synergy matrix is used heuristically; its predictive power and correlation with long‑term co‑evolution success are not analyzed. Understanding this could improve selection strategies and reduce overhead. Evidence: statistical analysis of matrix evolution (e.g., correlation between early scores and final best‑pair performance) and building a predictive model of synergy from operator features.

- **Open Question 5: Scalability to very large instances**  
  The largest experiments are on 200‑node instances; real‑world problems often have thousands of nodes. Computational cost per iteration grows, and LLM‑generated operators may not scale gracefully. The paper does not address scalability. Evidence: evaluate G‑LNS on large‑scale TSPLib/CVRPLib (N≥1000), measure solution quality, runtime, memory; investigate modifications like hierarchical decomposition or adaptive ratios.

## Limitations
- Unreported random seeds for training/test instance generation lead to variability in reported gaps and hamper reproducibility.  
- Final operator selection criterion (e.g., highest synergy vs. fitness) is not explicitly stated, creating ambiguity in the final solution choice.  
- LLM prompt templates are only partially described; missing details may affect operator diversity and code validity.
