# From Specification to Architecture: A Theory Compiler for Knowledge-Guided Machine Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.14369
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14369
- Reference count: 39
- Primary result: A conceptual framework for automatically compiling formal domain theories into neural architectures with provable consistency, defining soundness/completeness and identifying key open problems.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the Theory Compiler, a system to automate translating formal domain theories into neural network architectures. It tackles the manual, error-prone process in scientific machine learning by ensuring architectures are consistent with domain theories by construction. Contributions are primarily conceptual: formal definitions of soundness and completeness, identification of three foundational open problems, and a falsifiable conjecture about improved generalization, without empirical validation.

## Method Summary
The Theory Compiler pipeline comprises three stages: (1) a theory parser that extracts a typed intermediate representation from a formal domain theory and verifies well-formedness; (2) an architecture synthesizer that applies compilation rules to map theory primitives to architectural modules, composing them according to relational structure; (3) a certificate generator that produces a formal soundness proof. Soundness guarantees the architecture's function space is a subset of theory-consistent hypotheses, while completeness is the reverse inclusion. The approach relies on a typed theory language and correct compositional rules, which remain open challenges.

## Key Results
- Formal definitions of soundness and completeness for theory-guided architecture synthesis.
- Identification of three foundational open problems: universal decidable theory language, compositional correctness for interacting primitives, and scalable verification certificates.
- Conjecture that provably constrained architectures achieve superior generalization with reduced data requirements.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism rests on three pillars: First, automated parsing via a typed intermediate representation ensures accurate interpretation of domain theories and validates input before synthesis. Second, compilation rules directly map theory primitives (e.g., symmetry groups) to architectural modules (e.g., equivariant layers) that enforce constraints by construction, avoiding reliance on regularization. Third, soundness certificates provide formal, machine-checkable guarantees that the synthesized architecture adheres to the theory. However, these assume all primitives have known architectural realizations and that verification is feasible, which may fail for complex, interacting constraints.

## Foundational Learning
- **Domain Theory (`T = (Σ, Φ, R)`)**  
  Why needed: It is the formal input; misinterpreting signature, primitives, or relations leads to incorrect compilation.  
  Quick check: For a theory with symmetry group `φ_G` and conservation law `φ_C`, what must `R` specify about their interaction to ensure correct composition?
- **Soundness vs. Completeness (`A(T) ⊆ H(T)` vs. `H(T) ⊆ A(T)`)**  
  Why needed: These define correctness guarantees; soundness ensures no violation, completeness ensures expressivity. Confusing them misguides expectations.  
  Quick check: If an architecture is sound but not complete for `T`, what does this imply about its hypothesis space?
- **Compositional Correctness (`κ(T1 ∧ T2) = κ(T1) ⊗ κ(T2)`)**  
  Why needed: Critical for multi-primitive theories; failure means naive module stacking may produce unsound architectures.  
  Quick check: Why might combining an equivariant layer and a conservation layer naively violate constraints for `T = φ_G ∧ φ_C`?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Domain Theory -> Parser -> Synthesizer -> Certificate Generator -> Verified Model
- **Critical path:** Theory specification → Parser (type-check) → Synthesizer (apply `κ` and compose) → Certificate generator → Verified model.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Expressivity vs. Decidability: Richer theory languages may make type-checking undecidable.
  - Generality vs. Performance: Universal compiler may yield less optimized architectures than domain-specific ones.
  - Certificate cost: Proof generation for complex compositions may be computationally expensive.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Parser rejects valid theory due to restrictive type system.
  - Synthesizer produces unsound architecture from incorrect composition rules.
  - Certificate generation fails for non-trivial verification conditions.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Implement `κ` for a single primitive (e.g., symmetry), prove soundness/completeness, and compare generalization on a benchmark.
  2. For a theory with two interacting primitives (e.g., symmetry and conservation), test if the composed architecture satisfies constraints that a naive stack violates.
  3. Use a simple DSL to run the full pipeline, measure success rate of certificate generation, proof size, and time cost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper identifies three foundational open problems:
1. Universal theory language: A decidable, expressive language for domain theories.
2. Compositional correctness: A general rule for correctly composing modules from multiple primitives.
3. Verification certificates: Scalable generation of machine-checkable proofs for complex architectures.

## Limitations
- No empirical validation; the generalization conjecture remains untested.
- The three open problems are unsolved, blocking practical implementation.
- Absence of a prototype compiler; feasibility and costs are unexamined.

## Confidence
- Automated parsing reduces error and enables reuse: Medium
- Compilation rules enforce constraints by construction: Low
- Soundness certificates provide formal guarantees: Medium-Low

## Next Checks
1. Build a prototype for a single-primitive theory, verify soundness/completeness, and benchmark against unconstrained models.
2. Stress-test compositional correctness by comparing the proposed composition rule against naive stacking for interacting primitives.
3. Generate soundness certificates for minimal theories to assess proof feasibility, size, and computational cost.
