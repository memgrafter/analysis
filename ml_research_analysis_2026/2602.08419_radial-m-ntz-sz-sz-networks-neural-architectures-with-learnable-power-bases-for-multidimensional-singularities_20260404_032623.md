# Radial Müntz-Szász Networks: Neural Architectures with Learnable Power Bases for Multidimensional Singularities

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08419  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08419  
- Reference count: 10  
- Primary result: RMN achieves 1.5–51× lower RMSE than MLPs and 10–100× lower than SIREN on radial singularity benchmarks while using only 27 parameters.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Radial Müntz-Szász Networks (RMN), a neural architecture specifically designed to approximate radial singular functions such as \(1/r\) and \(\log r\). A central theoretical contribution is the proof that any \(C^2\) radial and additively separable function must be quadratic, revealing a fundamental limitation of coordinate‑separable networks like MLPs. RMN circumvents this by directly representing fields as linear combinations of learnable radial power laws \(r^\mu\) (including negative exponents) together with a limit‑stable logarithm primitive. This basis is provably dense for suitable exponent sequences and yields closed‑form gradient and Laplacian expressions, enabling stable physics‑informed learning on punctured domains. Across ten 2D/3D benchmarks, RMN drastically outperforms MLPs and SIREN in accuracy while using orders of magnitude fewer parameters. Extensions handle angular dependence (RMN‑Angular) and multiple sources (RMN‑MC), and the learned exponents provide direct interpretability of singularity orders.

## Method Summary
RMN constructs a scalar field \(\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^K a_k r^{\mu_k} + c_0 \psi_{\log}(r) + b_0\), where \(r = \|\mathbf{x}\|\) and the domain excludes a ball around the singularity (i.e., \(r \ge \epsilon\)). The exponents \(\mu_k\) are learnable, ordered, and confined to a prescribed interval via a differentiable parameterization (softplus gaps). The logarithmic primitive \(\psi_{\log}(r) = (r^\mu-1)/\mu\) converges to \(\log r\) as \(\mu\to0\); numerically it switches to a truncated Taylor expansion for small \(|\mu|\) to avoid cancellation errors. Closed‑form formulas for \(\nabla\phi\) and \(\Delta\phi\) are derived, allowing stable computation of PDE residuals without automatic differentiation near singularities. For angularly varying targets, spherical harmonics are added (RMN‑Angular). For multiple sources, RMN‑MC introduces learnable centers with a residual‑based initialization scheme. Training uses full‑batch Adam on uniformly sampled points in the punctured domain, minimizing mean squared error.

## Key Results
- RMN reduces RMSE by 1.5–51× compared to MLPs and 10–100× compared to SIREN on ten radial singularity benchmarks (2D/3D), with only 27 parameters versus ~33k (MLP) and ~8.6k (SIREN).  
- Learned exponents accurately recover physical singularity orders (e.g., \(\mu \approx -1\) for \(1/r\), \(\mu_{\log} \approx 0\) for \(\log r\)), offering interpretability.  
- Extensions: RMN‑Angular yields ~10× lower RMSE than RMN‑Direct on crack‑tip fields; RMN‑MC recovers multiple centers to <\(10^{-4}\) error with residual initialization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Coordinate‑Separability Obstruction
- **Claim:** Any \(C^2\) radial and additively separable function must be quadratic.  
- **Explanation:** For \(f(\mathbf{x}) = h(\|\mathbf{x}\|)\) to be expressible as \(\sum_i g_i(x_i)\), all mixed second partial derivatives vanish. This forces \(h(r)\) to satisfy a differential equation whose only \(C^2\) solutions are quadratic polynomials \(h(r)=cr^2+b\). Consequently, coordinate‑separable architectures (MLPs, coordinate‑wise MSN) cannot efficiently approximate non‑quadratic radial singularities like \(\log r\) or \(1/r\); they resort to axis‑aligned diamond‑shaped artifacts. (Theorem 14)

### Mechanism 2: Direct Radial Power Basis
- **Claim:** RMN directly parameterizes radial power laws \(r^\mu\) (including negative \(\mu\)) and a log‑primitive to exactly capture singularities.  
- **Explanation:** The hypothesis space \(\sum_k a_k r^{\mu_k} + c_0\psi_{\log}(r)\) with learnable \(\mu_k\) and \(\psi_{\log}(r) = (r^\mu-1)/\mu\) (converging to \(\log r\) as \(\mu\to0\)) is dense in appropriate radial function spaces (extended Müntz‑Szász theorem). Negative exponents enable inverse‑power singularities; the stabilized log‑primitive handles logarithmic behavior without requiring \(\mu=0\) exactly. (Definitions 21, 22)

### Mechanism 3: Stable Derivatives for Physics‑Informed Learning
- **Claim:** Closed‑form spatial derivatives enable stable PDE residual computation on punctured domains.  
- **Explanation:** For \(\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_k a_k r^{\mu_k} + c_0\psi_{\log}(r)\), we have \(\nabla\phi = \bigl(\sum_k a_k\mu_k r^{\mu_k-2} + c_0 r^{\mu_{\log}-2}\bigr)\mathbf{x}\) and \(\Delta\phi = \sum_k a_k\mu_k(\mu_k+d-2) r^{\mu_k-2} + c_0(\mu_{\log}+d-2) r^{\mu_{\log}-2}\). These expressions avoid numerical cancellation near \(r=0\) and yield finite residuals on punctured domains (where \(r\ge\epsilon\)). (Proposition 23)

## Foundational Learning

### Radial Functions and Their Calculus
- **Why needed:** RMN operates on the radius \(r=\|\mathbf{x}\|\); derivatives of \(r^\mu\) are essential for designing the network and computing PDE residuals.  
  \(\nabla(r^\mu)=\mu r^{\mu-2}\mathbf{x}\), \(\Delta(r^\mu)=\mu(\mu+d-2)r^{\mu-2}\).  
- **Quick check:** Why is \(\Delta(r^\mu)\) proportional to \(r^{\mu-2}\), and what does the factor \((\mu+d-2)\) imply for harmonicity? (Answer: When \(\mu=0\) or \(\mu=2-d\), the Laplacian vanishes, i.e., \(r^\mu\) is harmonic.)

### Müntz‑Szász Theorem and Density
- **Why needed:** The classical Müntz‑Szász theorem (positive exponents only) shows that power‑law bases are dense in \(C[0,1]\). RMN extends this to negative exponents and radial \(L^2\) spaces (Theorem 7) to guarantee that singularities can be approximated with appropriate exponent sequences.  
- **Quick check:** How does the bilateral Müntz condition \(\sum 1/\mu_k^+ = \infty\) and \(\sum 1/|\mu_k^-| = \infty\) ensure density in radial \(L^2\)? (It generalizes the classical condition to include both positive and negative exponents.)

### Punctured Domains and Integrability
- **Why needed:** Singularities like \(r^{-1}\) are not \(L^2\) on the full unit ball; training and evaluation must occur on \(\Omega_\epsilon = \{\mathbf{x}: \|\mathbf{x}\|\ge\epsilon\}\) to keep mean‑squared error well‑defined. Proposition 3 gives the integrability threshold \(\alpha > -d/p\) for \(r^\alpha \in L^p\).  
- **Quick check:** Is \(r^{-1}\) in \(L^2(B_1)\) for \(d=2\)? For \(d=3\)? (Answer: \(d=2\): no (integral diverges); \(d=3\): yes (integral converges).)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Input \(\mathbf{x}\) → radius \(r = \|\mathbf{x}\|\) → compute power basis \(\{r^{\mu_k}\}\) and \(\psi_{\log}(r)\) → linear combination \(\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_k a_k r^{\mu_k} + c_0\psi_{\log}(r) + b_0\)

### Critical path
- **Exponent parameterization:** Raw parameters \(s_k\) → positive gaps \(\delta_k = \text{softplus}(s_k)+\epsilon\) → cumulative sums \(\sigma_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \delta_i\) → normalized \(u_k = \sigma_k/\sigma_K\) → mapped to \(\mu_k = \mu_{\min}+(\mu_{\max}-\mu_{\min})u_k\). This ensures ordered exponents within \([\mu_{\min},\mu_{\max}]\) and enables differentiable gradients.  
- **Log‑primitive stabilization:** Use \(\psi_{\log}(r) = (r^\mu-1)/\mu\) for \(|\mu| > \epsilon_{\log}\); for small \(|\mu|\) use a Taylor expansion (e.g., up to \(\mathcal{O}(\mu^3)\)) to avoid catastrophic cancellation.  
- **Derivative formulas:** Precompute constants \(\mu_k\) and \(\mu_{\log}\); then \(\nabla\phi = \bigl(\sum_k a_k\mu_k r^{\mu_k-2} + c_0 r^{\mu_{\log}-2}\bigr)\mathbf{x}\) and \(\Delta\phi = \sum_k a_k\mu_k(\mu_k+d-2) r^{\mu_k-2} + c_0(\mu_{\log}+d-2) r^{\mu_{\log}-2}\).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Parameter count vs. expressivity:** Increasing the number of exponents \(K\) yields diminishing returns; the paper uses \(K=12\) (27 parameters total).  
- **Negative exponents:** Essential for representing inverse‑power singularities; setting \(\mu_{\min}\) too high (e.g., \(\mu_{\min} > -1\)) prevents exact representation of \(1/r\).  
- **Angular dependence:** Add spherical harmonics only when the target exhibits angular variation; otherwise RMN‑Direct is simpler and more stable.  
- **Multi‑source:** RMN‑MC introduces learnable centers; it is sensitive to initialization and becomes unstable with many sources (\(J \ge 3\)).

### Failure signatures
- **Smooth non‑radial targets:** RMN‑Direct fails dramatically (e.g., \(\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)\) yields RMSE ~0.48 vs MLP ~0.008) due to radial basis mismatch.  
- **Multi‑source with many centers:** RMN‑MC shows high variance in center recovery and test RMSE when \(J \ge 3\).  
- **Unstabilized log‑primitive:** Direct use of \((r^\mu-1)/\mu\) for \(\mu \approx 0\) causes numerical overflow/underflow.  
- **Domain includes singularity:** Training without excluding \(r=0\) leads to unstable gradients and divergence.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Single‑center singularities:** Train RMN‑Direct on 2D \(\log r\) and \(r^{-1}\) using 10,000 uniformly sampled points in the punctured disk \(0.01 \le r \le 1\). Verify test RMSE \(4.85\times10^{-3}\) (log) and \(\approx1.2\times10^{-3}\) (1/r); check that learned exponents converge to \(\mu_{\log}\approx0\) and \(\mu\approx -1\).  
2. **Angular field (crack‑tip):** Train RMN‑Angular on \(r^{1/2}\cos(\theta/2)\) in a disk. Compare RMN‑Direct (fails, RMSE \(\approx0.227\)) vs RMN‑Angular (succeeds, RMSE \(\approx0.024\)) with ~51 parameters.  
3. **Multi‑source recovery:** Train RMN‑MC on a 2D mixture of two logarithmic sources at distinct centers. With residual‑based initialization, report center recovery error \(<10^{-4}\) and low RMSE. Stress‑test by increasing to three sources; observe significant variance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Non‑radial singularities** – How can RMN handle intrinsically anisotropic or edge/corner singularities? The radial bias is insufficient; new inductive biases are needed.  
2. **Scalability of RMN‑MC** – How to improve robustness and accuracy when the number of singularity centers \(J\) is large or unknown? Current residual initialization helps but fails for \(J \ge 3\) in moderate dimensions.  
3. **Higher‑dimensional scaling** – Can RMN be efficiently applied to \(d > 3\)? Sampling and optimization costs may become prohibitive; no evaluation beyond 3D exists.  
4. **PINN integration** – How well does RMN perform within physics‑informed neural network frameworks for forward/inverse PDE problems with singularities? Preliminary studies show trade‑offs in accuracy versus stability.  
5. **Hybrid architectures** – Can RMN be combined with MLPs to handle mixed smooth‑singular fields? Simple convex mixtures destabilize exponent learning; a structured gating or partitioning mechanism is required.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility gaps:** Random seeds, exact uniform sampling method, and code version are not fully specified, hindering exact replication of reported RMSE values.  
- **Extension brittleness:** RMN‑Angular and RMN‑MC exhibit high variance and sensitivity to hyperparameters; performance degrades as problem complexity (angular modes, number of sources) increases.  
- **Scope constraints:** RMN assumes radial singularities and fails on smooth anisotropic functions; it cannot represent non‑radial targets.

## Confidence
- Theoretical obstruction (Theorem 14): **High** – Rigorous proof; structural argument is sound.  
- RMN basis density (negative exponents + log‑primitive): **High** – Formal extension of Müntz‑Szász theorem with stability proof.  
- Empirical superiority on radial benchmarks: **Medium** – Results are compelling but depend on unreported seeds and sampling details.  
- Extensions (RMN‑Angular, RMN‑MC): **Low** – Performance degrades with complexity; hyperparameter tuning is not standardized.  
- Exponent interpretability: **Medium** – Learned exponents match expected singularities in many cases, but training stability is not guaranteed.
