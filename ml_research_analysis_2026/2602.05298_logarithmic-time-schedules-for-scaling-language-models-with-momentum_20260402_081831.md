# Logarithmic-time Schedules for Scaling Language Models with Momentum

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05298
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05298
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ADANA optimizer reduces compute by up to 40% relative to tuned AdamW for training large language models (45M–2.6B parameters), with gains increasing at larger scales.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ADANA, an optimizer that leverages logarithmic-time schedules for momentum and weight decay combined with a damped Nesterov-style update to improve training efficiency of large language models. The design is motivated by the power-law structure of language data, which suggests that effective learning rates should decay logarithmically across spectral directions. The key hyperparameter κ (spectral dimension) is found to be transferable across model scales. Experiments on transformer models from 45M to 2.6B parameters trained on FineWeb data show that ADANA achieves up to 40% compute savings compared to a carefully tuned AdamW baseline, with the advantage growing at larger scales. Robust variants—Dana-MK4 (with SNR clipping) and Dana-Star (with sparse-gradient correction)—further improve stability without sacrificing performance.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates ADANA and its variants against AdamW, Muon, and AdEMAMix on a scaling ladder of GPT-3-like Enoki models (45M–2.6B) and Qwen3 models up to 2.6B. All models are trained in the compute-optimal regime (Chinchilla scaling: tokens N = 20 × total parameters D) on the FineWeb dataset, using a sequence length of 2048 and a global batch size of 32 (65,536 tokens per step). Training uses bfloat16 forward/backward passes, float32 optimizer states, gradient clipping (0.5), and a cosine learning rate decay with 2% warmup to a peak γ* that is obtained by a two-stage sweep per optimizer/scale; the best peak LRs follow a power law γ*(P) = a·(b+P)^{-d}.

ADANA’s core innovations are: (1) momentum parameters β₁(t) and β₂(t) follow a log-time schedule β(t) = 1−δ/(δ+t) with δ=8; (2) weight decay λ(t) decays as ω/(T/10+t) with ω=4; (3) update scaling α(t) = (1+t)^{1−κ} with κ=0.85, providing damped Nesterov acceleration. Variants add SNR clipping (clipsnr=2) and a τ estimator for sparse gradients. Baselines include AdamW with constant β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95 and independent weight decay, Muon (with orthogonalization on 2D parameters), and AdEMAMix (with long/short momentum mixing). Compute multipliers (efficiency gains) are estimated by fitting a broken power law to validation loss vs. compute across model scales.

## Key Results
- ADANA achieves up to 40% compute efficiency relative to tuned AdamW on models up to 2.6B, with the advantage increasing with scale.
- The damped Nesterov component with κ≈0.85 transfers across Enoki and Qwen3 architectures and all tested scales.
- Robust variants (Dana-MK4, Dana-Star, Dana-Star-MK4) mitigate instability from sparse gradients and spectral sensitivity without noticeable performance loss.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Language data typically exhibits a power-law covariance spectrum, meaning that the eigenvalues of the Hessian (or feature correlations) decay as a power law. This heavy-tailed structure implies that a uniform learning rate allocates too much capacity to a few dominant directions and too little to the long tail. ADANA’s log-time schedules for momentum and weight decay naturally allocate a decreasing effective step size that matches the spectral density: β(t) ≈ 1 − δ/t (for large t) gives an effective memory length logarithmic in t, which gracefully handles the infinitely many directions. The damping factor α(t) = (1+t)^{1−κ} prevents the momentum buffer from growing too fast (which would cause instability) while still providing acceleration; κ is tied to the spectral exponent (κ ≈ 1/(2ρ)) and is found to be around 0.85 across datasets and scales, making it transferable. The decaying weight decay λ(t) = ω/(T/10+t) ensures that regularization does not vanish prematurely, which is crucial for large models. The hardened variants address practical failure modes: SNR clipping rescales updates based on local signal-to-noise ratio to stabilize training under spectral heterogeneity, and the τ estimator corrects for sparse gradients that can otherwise break the second-moment estimation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Power-law data covariance**  
  Why needed: Language data’s covariance matrix has a heavy-tailed spectrum, motivating adaptive learning rates that decay logarithmically across directions.  
  Quick check: Compute the eigenvalues of the covariance of a batch of token embeddings; verify λ_i ∝ i^{-ρ} for some ρ.

- **Logarithmic-time momentum schedule**  
  Why needed: Matching the effective step size to the power-law spectrum requires a memory that grows only logarithmically with time, preventing overfitting to large gradients.  
  Quick check: The running average β(t) = 1−δ/(δ+t) yields an effective memory trace ~ log(t)/δ.

- **Damped Nesterov acceleration**  
  Why needed: Pure momentum (β near 1) can cause divergence when gradients are sparse or noisy; damping via α(t) = (1+t)^{1−κ} limits the momentum buffer’s growth while retaining acceleration benefits.  
  Quick check: Monitor the magnitude of the momentum buffer over training; it should grow roughly as t^{1−κ}.

- **Spectral dimension (κ)**  
  Why needed: κ captures the tail exponent of the covariance spectrum (κ ≈ 1/(2ρ)) and is the primary hyperparameter controlling damping; it transfers across model sizes and datasets.  
  Quick check: Estimate the power-law exponent ρ from activation covariance spectra; κ should be close to 1/(2ρ).

- **Compute-optimal scaling (Chinchilla)**  
  Why needed: The number of training tokens N = 20·D determines the time horizon T for each model size, which is required to schedule the decaying weight decay and damping correctly.  
  Quick check: Train small models at different data scales and fit a scaling law; verify loss follows a broken power law in compute.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Power-law assumption → Hyperparameters (δ, κ, ω) → Scheduler (β1(t), β2(t), λ(t), α(t)) → ADANA update (Adam-style with damped Nesterov) → Optional: SNR clipping / τ correction (in variants) → Parameter update

Critical path (per optimization step):
1. Compute gradient g_t from minibatch.
2. Update momentum buffers: m_t = β1(t)·m_{t-1} + (1−β1(t))·g_t, v_t = β2(t)·v_{t-1} + (1−β2(t))·g_t².
3. Compute damped update: u_t = α(t) · (m_t / (√v_t + ε)).
4. Apply weight decay: θ_t = θ_{t-1} − η_t·(u_t + λ(t)·θ_{t-1}).
   (In practice, AdamW-style separation: weight decay applied directly to parameters.)
5. For variants: (a) Dana-MK4: clip u_t by |u_t| ≤ clipsnr·√v_t; (b) Dana-Star: adjust using τ estimator for sparse gradients.

Design tradeoffs:
- δ (log momentum decay): Larger δ yields slower momentum buildup; too small (<4) may cause instability, too large (>16) slows adaptation. Default 8 works well.
- κ (damping exponent): Lower κ (<0.8) increases damping (safer but slower); higher κ (>0.9) risks divergence. Empirically κ≈0.85 is robust across scales.
- Weight-decay schedule: λ(t)=ω/(T/10+t) outperforms constant or pure ω/t at large scales; ω controls final regularization strength.
- Batch size: Schedules assume batch size 32; larger batches may require scaling the damping factor α̃ (see Figure 24) because noise reduction changes the effective learning rate.

Failure signatures:
- Early loss spikes or NaNs: often due to κ too high, insufficient damping, or β schedules too aggressive. Solution: lower κ or increase δ.
- Divergence in embedding or output layers with sparse gradients: fixed by Dana-Star’s τ estimator.
- Over-regularization (loss decreases too slowly): likely weight decay λ(t) too large; reduce ω.
- Sensitivity to κ across layers: indicates spectral heterogeneity; consider using κ per layer or SNR clipping (Dana-MK4).

Exactly 3 first experiments:
1. Implement vanilla ADANA with defaults (δ=8, κ=0.85, ω=4) on a 45M Enoki model. Train to compute-optimal horizon (N=20·D) and compare final validation loss to a tuned AdamW baseline using the same LR schedule. Compute multiplier should be near 1 (baseline) or better.
2. Ablate the damping parameter κ on a medium scale (e.g., 400M). Train with κ ∈ {0.75,0.8,0.85,0.9} and compare stability and final loss. Expect κ≈0.85 to yield lowest loss without spikes.
3. Compare weight-decay schedules on the same medium scale: constant λ (optimized via sweep), λ=ω/t, and λ=ω/(T/10+t). The decaying schedule with T/10 offset should win.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Muon + log-time scheduling?** The paper speculates that applying logarithmic-time momentum and weight-decay schedules to Muon (which uses orthogonalization) could yield further gains, but this remains unexplored. Muon’s stability may interact differently with the damping factor.
2. **What determines κ’s optimality and transferability?** Although κ≈0.85 works across scales, the theoretical explanation for why a single κ is optimal for different model sizes and datasets is open. The PLRF theory suggests κ matches the spectral exponent of the data covariance, but the precise relationship and its invariance to architecture are not fully understood.
3. **How does ADANA scale to larger batch regimes?** Preliminary results suggest the damping parameter α̃ should scale with batch size B (α̃ ∝ B^{0.33}) for batches 32–512, but the scaling law beyond 512 is unknown. Very large batches may require different adjustments to maintain the compute advantage.

## Limitations
- Transferability across model families (e.g., Llama, Mamba) and tasks beyond language modeling has not been validated.
- Compute multiplier estimates rely on fitting broken power laws to a limited number of scales; the absence of confidence intervals or significance tests undermines the robustness of the reported up-to-40% savings.
- Implementation details such as the elementwise attention output gating used for Qwen3 may impact scaling and could be specific to the experimental setup.

## Confidence
- **ADANA reduces compute by up to 40% vs AdamW** (scaling experiments on two architectures up to 2.6B): *Medium*.
- **Log-time schedules and damped Nesterov are key design elements** (ablations isolate contributions): *High*.
- **κ ≈ 0.85 transfers across scales** (consistent across Enoki scales): *Medium* (cross-architecture transfer less certain).
- **Robust variants (Dana-MK4, Dana-Star) improve stability** (qualitative improvements noted): *Medium*.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture replication:** Implement ADANA on at least one additional architecture (e.g., Llama) and dataset (e.g., The Pile) to verify compute savings and κ transferability.
2. **Statistical significance test:** For each optimizer and scale, run ≥3 random seeds; use bootstrapping or Bayesian fitting to obtain credible intervals for compute multipliers and assess whether gains are statistically meaningful.
3. **Ablation on damping and weight-decay schedules:** On a large model (≥2.6B), systematically vary κ ∈ {0.75,0.8,0.85,0.9} and weight-decay forms (constant, ω/t, ω/(T/10+t)). Also test batch sizes up to at least 4096 to determine the correct α̃(B) scaling and identify any stability limits.
