# iAgentBench: Benchmarking Sensemaking Capabilities of Information-Seeking Agents on High-Traffic Topics
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04656
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04656
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** iAgentBench reveals a significant performance gap in cross-theme sensemaking even with retrieval augmentation (RAG), showing that evidence access alone is insufficient for integrating information across multiple thematic communities and their explicit linkages.

## Executive Summary
Existing open-domain QA benchmarks often fail to measure true cross-source sensemaking because answers can be found in a single retrieved passage. To address this, iAgentBench introduces a dynamic benchmark that constructs questions requiring the integration of evidence across multiple thematic communities and the connector relations between them. It uses real-time, high-traffic attention signals from GDELT to seed topics, retrieves a query-specific web corpus, structures it into a "story graph" with clustered themes and cross-community connectors, and generates questions whose answers depend on at least two communities and one connector. Experiments with four LLMs show that retrieval improves accuracy but a substantial gap remains, and agentic self-reflection (Reflexion) yields mixed results, highlighting the need to evaluate evidence integration reliability, not just access.

## Method Summary
iAgentBench builds instances through a multi-stage pipeline. First, it selects time-indexed, high-traffic seed queries from the GDELT Global Knowledge Graph using a scoring function for salience, diversity, and temporal specificity. For each seed, it retrieves a web corpus via SearxNG. An LLM extracts entities and claim-like relations to build a graph, which is partitioned into thematic communities via Leiden clustering. Cross-community edges are identified as "connectors." Communities are summarized and assigned roles (Core, Bridge, Satellite) based on graph centrality. QA pairs are generated only from packets containing 2–3 linked communities and their connectors, with strict constraints: the generated question must require at least one finding from two distinct communities and at least one connector. A panel of LLM judges validates each instance against eight objective criteria, including necessity tests. The final instance releases all auditable artifacts (corpus, graph, communities, connectors, judge decisions) to support contamination checks and failure diagnosis.

## Key Results
- Retrieval-augmentation (RAG) consistently improves accuracy over base models on iAgentBench, but a significant performance gap persists, indicating that access to relevant documents is not sufficient for cross-theme sensemaking.
- Agentic self-reflection (Reflexion) produces mixed gains, improving some models while degrading others on iAgentBench, contrasting with more uniform improvements on simpler benchmarks like SimpleQA and HotpotQA.
- The magnitude of RAG gains on iAgentBench is moderate (+0.064 to +0.176), suggesting the benchmark successfully isolates evidence integration challenges from pure retrieval.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Graph-Constrained Question Generation
- **Claim:** iAgentBench forces cross-theme evidence integration by structuring retrieved content into a "story graph" with explicitly defined thematic communities and connector relations, then generating questions that *necessarily* depend on multiple communities and at least one connector.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline clusters retrieved documents into communities (themes) via Leiden clustering. It then identifies "connector" edges—relational assertions that cross community boundaries. QA generation is constrained to produce questions where the answer requires at least one finding from two distinct communities and at least one connector relation linking them. The LLM judge enforces necessity: removing all findings from any one required community or all cited connectors must make the question unanswerable.
- **Core assumption:** A graph representation with community-level abstraction and explicit cross-community links is a valid and tractable proxy for the cognitive work of sensemaking—integrating dispersed evidence across facets of a topic.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "constructs questions requiring integration of evidence across multiple themes and their explicit linkages." [section 3.2] "partition G(q) into communities... edges are connector if they cross community boundaries." [section 3.4.3] "necessity test: if you remove ALL cited connector texts, the question becomes unanswerable."
- **Break condition:** If community detection fails to produce coherent, separable themes or cross-community connectors are too sparse/noisy, generated questions may not require genuine cross-theme synthesis.

### Mechanism 2: Grounding in Real-Time Attention Signals
- **Claim:** Grounding seed topics in real-time, high-traffic attention signals (GDELT) ensures benchmark instances reflect *naturalistic* information needs that users actually seek, making the sensemaking task ecologically valid.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline samples candidate seed queries from GDELT's Global Knowledge Graph, which aggregates news media mentions. Candidates are scored for salience, diversity, and temporal specificity. This yields topics actively discussed in a recent window. Subsequent retrieval creates a query-conditioned web corpus mirroring what an agent would retrieve for a current user query.
- **Core assumption:** Topics with high, recent public attention are more likely to correspond to genuine, complex information-seeking scenarios where users need to synthesize evidence from multiple angles.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "draws seed topics from real-world attention signals." [section 3.1] "derive seed topics from public attention signals... GDELT... as a proxy for what events and entities are receiving attention." [section 5] "iAgentBench targets realistic, high-traffic topics drawn from attention signals."
- **Break condition:** If the attention signal is biased toward certain event types or the scoring formula poorly balances salience vs. diversity, the topic distribution may not generalize to all sensemaking intents.

### Mechanism 3: Auditable Artifacts for Failure Diagnosis
- **Claim:** Releasing intermediate auditable artifacts (community cards, connectors, judge decisions) enables precise failure diagnosis, distinguishing whether a model's poor performance is due to *retrieval failure* (missing key themes/connectors) or *synthesis failure* (present but not integrated).
- **Mechanism:** For each instance, the pipeline releases the query-conditioned corpus, the story graph's communities with summaries and findings, the specific connector relations, and the LLM judge panel's binary flags and reasoning. An evaluator can examine a failure: if required communities/connectors are absent from the retrieved context, it's a retrieval issue; if present but the answer is wrong, it's a synthesis/integration issue.
- **Core assumption:** Diagnostic granularity beyond aggregate accuracy is necessary to advance sensemaking systems. Understanding *why* a system fails directs remediation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "each instance is released with traceable evidence and auditable intermediate artifacts that support contamination checks and enable fine-grained diagnosis of failures in retrieval versus synthesis." [section 4] "instances can be regenerated... because the retrieved corpora are recorded, the benchmark supports contamination checks and controlled re-runs." [section 5] "failures can be analyzed as breakdowns in evidence access, evidence integration, or multi-step stability."
- **Break condition:** If released artifacts are incomplete or misaligned (e.g., judge incorrectly certified an instance), diagnosis becomes unreliable. Reliance on LLM judges introduces propagation of biases/hallucinations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Graph-based Corpus Representation (Story Graph)**
  - **Why needed here:** iAgentBench does not treat the retrieved corpus as a flat bag of documents. It structures it into a hypergraph of assertions (edges) between entities (nodes), then clusters nodes into thematic communities. This structure is the substrate from which cross-theme questions are derived.
  - **Quick check:** How does representing the corpus as a graph of claim-like edges (rather than isolated passages) enable the construction of questions that require linking disparate pieces of information?
- **Concept: Cross-Document Sensemaking vs. Multi-Hop QA**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper distinguishes sensemaking (integrating evidence across themes, tracking causal links, resolving dependencies) from multi-hop QA (chaining evidence across hops, often snippet-stitching). iAgentBench is designed to target the former.
  - **Quick check:** According to Edge et al. [4] and this paper, why is a multi-hop question on HotpotQA potentially still answerable without true cross-theme synthesis?
- **Concept: Dynamic Benchmarking & Contamination Mitigation**
  - **Why needed here:** Static benchmarks suffer from data contamination as models train on web corpora. iAgentBench is "dynamic": seed topics are time-indexed from GDELT, and the entire pipeline can be re-run for new time windows, generating fresh instances grounded in *current* web evidence.
  - **Quick check:** What specific design choices in iAgentBench (beyond just using a fresh data source) make it a *dynamic* benchmark suitable for evaluating retrieval-augmented and agentic systems over time?

## Architecture Onboarding
Seed Sourcing -> Scoring & Selection -> Corpus Retrieval -> Graph Extraction -> Community Detection -> Community Reporting -> Meta-graph & Roles -> Connector Selection -> Packet Construction -> QA Generation -> LLM-as-Judge Verification -> Instance Compilation
- **Critical path:** Seed Selection → Retrieval → Graph Build (Extraction + Community Detection) → Role Assignment → Packet Build → QA Gen → Judge Filter. A failure in community detection or judge consensus breaks the pipeline.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Cost vs. Dynamism:* Dynamic regeneration is expensive (retrieval + LLM calls) but prevents contamination and keeps topics fresh. Static datasets are cheap but age quickly.
  - *Precision vs. Recall in QA Gen:* Heavily constrained generation yields high-precision, clean instances but may reject many plausible candidate QAs, limiting dataset size.
  - *LLM Reliance:* The pipeline critically depends on LLMs for graph extraction, community reporting, QA generation, and judging. Hallucinations or biases at any stage propagate.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low ΔRAG on iAgentBench but high on SimpleQA: Indicates benchmark is successfully measuring synthesis, not just retrieval.
  - Negative ΔRefl (Reflexion worse than RAG): Suggests multi-step reasoning introduces drift/over-correction on complex integration tasks.
  - High judge rejection rate: May indicate poor packet quality (e.g., insufficiently distinct communities or weak connectors), or overly strict judge prompts.
  - Low inter-judge agreement: Threatens validity of necessity tests; requires audit of judge prompts or move to human verification subset.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline vs. RAG Sanity Check:** Run Base and RAG on SimpleQA, HotpotQA, and a small iAgentBench sample. Confirm retrieval helps on all, but gap remains largest on iAgentBench, establishing face validity.
  2. **Artifact Audit Analysis:** Manually inspect 100 retained iAgentBench instances. Verify (a) questions are natural, (b) answer genuinely requires ≥2 communities + ≥1 connector, (c) supporting findings/connectors are correct and sufficient. Estimate "false positive" rate of judge filtering.
  3. **Component Ablation (Retrieval Quality):** Run RAG with *oracle* retrieval (providing the exact retrieved corpus used in instance construction) vs. standard search. If oracle RAG closes the gap on iAgentBench, it confirms the bottleneck is *retrieval of the right themes/connectors*, not synthesis per se. If gap persists, synthesis is the primary bottleneck.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why does retrieval (RAG) provide only moderate gains on iAgentBench compared to SimpleQA, and what specific evidence integration failures persist despite access to relevant documents?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state that "retrieval alone does not reliably resolve these questions" and iAgentBench shows a "meaningful gap under RAG," indicating that access to evidence is insufficient for cross-theme sensemaking. They emphasize the need to "evaluate evidence use, not just evidence access."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper diagnoses that the bottleneck shifts from retrieval to integration but does not isolate *which* integration capabilities are missing (e.g., tracking causal links across themes, resolving dependencies, synthesizing contradictory information). Without this granularity, it is unclear whether failures stem from reasoning depth, graph comprehension, or answer formatting constraints.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Fine-grained error analysis using the released artifacts (supporting findings, connectors, judge decisions) to categorize integration failures (e.g., missing a connector, mis-weighting community evidence, inability to combine partial facts). Human studies comparing model vs. human integration strategies on the same packets could identify specific reasoning gaps.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does agentic self-reflection (Reflexion) yield inconsistent results—improving some models while degrading others—on iAgentBench, and what makes certain models more robust to iterative refinement in cross-theme sensemaking?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The experiments show "Reflexion yields mixed results, improving some models while degrading others," and this pattern is "especially salient for iAgentBench." The authors conclude that multi-step reasoning can introduce "drift or over-correction," underscoring the need to evaluate "reliability of evidence use."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not investigate *why* Reflexion helps or harms. Possibilities include: (a) models overfitting to the specific artifact format during reflection, (b) reflection prompts amplifying retrieval errors, or (c) varying capacities to maintain cross-community consistency across turns. Without probing these mechanisms, it is unclear how to design stable agentic pipelines for sensemaking.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled ablations of Reflexion’s components (e.g., number of reflection steps, type of feedback provided) across models, analyzed via the judge’s flags (e.g., whether failures increase due to "evidence drift" or "non-unique answers"). Logging intermediate reasoning states could reveal if reflection introduces hallucinated connectors or misallocates community dependencies.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do design choices in the graph construction pipeline (e.g., community detection algorithm, connector selection heuristics, community role definitions) affect the difficulty and validity of iAgentBench instances?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The pipeline uses Leiden clustering, influence scoring (Eq. 4), and heuristic role assignment (Core/Bridge/Satellite). These are implementation-specific and not validated against alternative graph-theoretic approaches. The assumption that "connector relations" and "community roles" naturally map to sensemaking difficulty is untested.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not measure sensitivity to these choices. Different community detection algorithms could yield overlapping or fragmented themes, altering the necessity of cross-community links. Heuristic thresholds (e.g., `k_core = max{5, 0.3 |C(q)|}`) may bias instance distribution. Without robustness checks, benchmark scores may reflect pipeline artifacts rather than intrinsic sensemaking capability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-building a subset of instances with alternative graph methods (e.g., Louvain clustering, different edge-weighting schemes) and measuring correlation in model performance. Human evaluation of whether generated questions remain cross-theme dependent under different graph constructions. Analysis of failure modes when connector thresholds are varied.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do the five defined intent patterns (`explainer`, `connection`, `trigger`, `consequence`, `stake`) capture the full spectrum of real-world sensemaking queries on high-traffic topics, and are there latent intent classes not represented?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors define five intent patterns during generation and record `intent_pattern` per instance. They state these capture "common ways users seek understanding." However, the set is small and manually curated.
- **Why unreolved:** The patterns are derived from author intuition and may miss nuances (e.g., comparative sensemaking: "How does X differ from Y in terms of impact?"; contingency: "Under what conditions did Z occur?"). The paper does not analyze the distribution of intent patterns in natural search logs versus their synthetic set. If benchmark performance varies significantly by intent, this could bias model development toward the covered patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Mining real user queries from high-traffic topics (e.g., GDELT-aligned search logs) and clustering them to discover emergent intent classes. Comparing iAgentBench’s pattern distribution against this natural distribution. Evaluating whether models show intent-specific failure patterns that suggest overlooked query types.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How effective are the released audit artifacts (e.g., `supporting_findings`, `supporting_connectors`, `judge_decisions`) in practice for diagnosing contamination and distinguishing retrieval vs. synthesis failures in dynamic evaluations?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper emphasizes auditability: "each instance is released with traceable evidence and auditable intermediate artifacts that support contamination checks and enable fine-grained diagnosis." The judge’s structured output includes necessity flags.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not demonstrate a systematic methodology for using these artifacts in contamination detection or failure diagnosis. It is unclear how reliably the `judge_decisions` (from LLM-as-a-Judge) align with human judgments on necessity tests, or how often artifacts reveal contamination (e.g., if a model’s retrieved documents inadvertently include benchmark instances). Without validation, the claimed auditability remains theoretical.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Human evaluation of a sample of instances to verify that the judge’s `*_necessity_flag` decisions match expert assessments. Applying the artifacts in a controlled contamination experiment (e.g., injecting known benchmark answers into retrieved corpora) to measure detection accuracy. Publishing case studies of how researchers have used the artifacts to debug model failures.

## Limitations
- **Parameter & Configuration Opaqueness:** Key pipeline hyperparameters (GDELT scoring weights, community role thresholds), LLM model versions/prompts for extraction/generation/judging, and SearxNG retrieval settings are unreported, hindering faithful replication and sensitivity analysis.
- **LLM-as-Judge Reliability:** The entire QA validation relies on an LLM judge panel; inconsistencies or biases in these judges could compromise instance validity and the diagnostic utility of the released artifacts.
- **Topic Domain Bias:** Seed topics are drawn from GDELT news-centric attention signals, which may over-represent conflict, politics, or breaking events, limiting generalizability to other sensemaking domains (e.g., scientific, technical, historical analysis).
