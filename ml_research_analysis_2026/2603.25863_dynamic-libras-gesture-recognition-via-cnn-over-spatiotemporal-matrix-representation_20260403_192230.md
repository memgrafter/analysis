# Dynamic LIBRAS Gesture Recognition via CNN over Spatiotemporal Matrix Representation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25863
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25863
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** Achieves 95% accuracy under low-light and 92% under normal lighting for 11‑class LIBRAS gesture recognition on a single‑user dataset, enabling real‑time home‑automation control.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a lightweight system for dynamic Brazilian Sign Language (LIBRAS) gesture recognition aimed at controlling home automation devices. Hand landmarks are extracted using MediaPipe, then arranged into a fixed‑size 90×21 spatiotemporal matrix that encodes 30 frames of (x, y, z) coordinates. A standard 2D CNN classifies the matrix, avoiding recurrent architectures. A sliding window with triplicated frames ensures continuous, real‑time inference. On a single‑user dataset the method achieves 95% accuracy in low light and 92% in normal lighting, demonstrating effectiveness for both static and dynamic gestures on general‑purpose hardware. Generalization to multiple users and embedded deployment remain open challenges.

## Method Summary
The pipeline starts with MediaPipe Hand Landmarker to obtain 21 skeletal keypoints (x, y, z) per frame. For each gesture instance, 30 consecutive frames are collected. These frames are transformed into a 90×21 matrix: rows 1‑30 contain x‑coordinates of all frames, rows 31‑60 the y‑coordinates, and rows 61‑90 the z‑coordinates. Each matrix is independently min‑max normalized to [0, 255] and treated as a grayscale image. A lightweight CNN (three 3×3 convolutional layers with 33, 64, 64 filters, max‑pooling, and two dense layers) is trained to classify the images into 11 LIBRAS gestures. During live operation, a FIFO buffer of length 30 is updated; each newly received frame is inserted three times. When the buffer fills, the matrix is built and classified; if the prediction confidence exceeds 0.98, the gesture is emitted and the buffer is cleared. This sliding‑window strategy allows the static CNN to handle variable‑length dynamics without recurrence.

## Key Results
- 95% accuracy under low‑light and 92% under normal lighting on a single‑user test set (100 samples per condition).
- Successfully recognizes both static and dynamic LIBRAS gestures using a purely feed‑forward CNN.
- Real‑time performance on general‑purpose CPU hardware; model size ~25,000 parameters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** A 90×21 spatiotemporal matrix constructed from MediaPipe hand keypoints enables effective classification of both static and dynamic LIBRAS gestures using a standard 2D CNN.  
**Mechanism:** For 30 frames, the 21 keypoints’ x, y, z values are vertically concatenated: first 30 rows (x), next 30 (y), final 30 (z). This turns the temporal sequence into a single image where temporal evolution appears as vertical texture patterns. The CNN learns spatial patterns from this matrix that correspond to specific gestures.  
**Core assumption:** Gesture‑discriminative information is preserved and becomes linearly separable by this axis‑wise concatenation and per‑matrix min‑max normalization, such that temporal dynamics are encoded as stable 2D variations.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section III.C describe the matrix construction and show visual patterns for static vs. dynamic gestures (Figure 4). Corpus support is weak; neighboring works use different skeleton‑to‑image encodings but do not evaluate this exact format.  
**Break condition:** If two gestures produce nearly identical vertical textures in the matrix (e.g., similar velocity profiles but different semantics), the CNN lacks discriminative features.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A sliding window with temporal frame triplication (n=3) enables continuous, real‑time inference for dynamic gestures without requiring recurrent architectures.  
**Mechanism:** Each new camera frame is added three times to a FIFO buffer of length 30. A full buffer yields a 90×21 matrix for classification. After classification, the three oldest entries are removed per new frame, creating high overlap between consecutive windows. Triplication effectively slows the perceived temporal sampling, allowing faster gestures to be fully captured within the 30‑entry window.  
**Core assumption:** The gesture’s discriminative temporal pattern is robust to the artificial redundancy, and the 98% confidence threshold plus buffer reset after detection prevents duplicate triggers from overlapping windows.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions triplication enables real‑time inference without recurrence. Section III.E details the algorithm. Neighboring papers combine MediaPipe with LSTMs, supporting the claimed avoidance of recurrent complexity, though triplication itself is not directly validated.  
**Break condition:** If a gesture’s critical temporal segment is shorter than the effective resolution of the triplicated window, it may be misrepresented or missed. Subset gestures (e.g., “I” inside “J”) may trigger prematurely.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** A lightweight CNN (~25,000 parameters) trained on normalized 90×21 matrices generalizes adequately to unseen lighting conditions for the specific 11‑class LIBRAS home‑control vocabulary.  
**Mechanism:** The CNN (3 conv + 2 dense layers with ReLU/softmax) learns texture classifiers for the matrix patterns. Min‑max normalization to [0,255] per matrix provides invariance to absolute hand size and distance. The small parameter count reduces overfitting on a single‑user dataset and enables CPU inference.  
**Core assumption:** Feature hierarchy learned from one user’s normalized skeletal patterns transfers to other users’ anatomies and to varying lighting (which only affects MediaPipe’s upstream extraction, not the normalized matrix).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports 95% low‑light and 92% normal lighting accuracy. Section III.D describes architecture and regularization; training/validation accuracy reaches 100%/99.55% on the single‑user set. Section IV presents test results but notes single‑user limitation. Indirect support from Alves et al. [5] shows CNN‑based skeletal image classification can work across users with a different representation.  
**Break condition:** If MediaPipe fails significantly in new lighting/user conditions (occlusion, non‑standard hand sizes), the downstream matrix and CNN receive corrupted inputs, causing failure regardless of CNN quality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Skeletal Keypoint Representation** – MediaPipe provides 21 (x,y,z) joints per frame, abstracting raw pixels. The entire pipeline depends on these keypoints as a stable, appearance‑invariant hand description. *Quick check:* If a finger joint is occluded and MediaPipe outputs a low‑confidence position, how might that manifest in the 90×21 matrix, and what could the CNN learn?
- **2D CNN for Non‑Image Data** – The matrix of concatenated coordinate sequences can be treated as a 2D “image” where patterns (vertical lines, gradients, changes) encode gesture dynamics. *Quick check:* What visual texture in the matrix would correspond to a fast, linear hand movement versus a slow, rotational movement?
- **Sliding Window for Temporal Segmentation** – Dynamic gestures have variable duration. The sliding window with overlap and triplication is a heuristic to capture arbitrary‑length gestures within a fixed‑input model. *Quick check:* What is the effective temporal duration (in seconds) of one 30‑entry buffer at 30 fps when n=3, and what is the temporal stride between consecutive classifications?

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component Map
Camera stream -> MediaPipe Hand Landmarker -> Buffer Manager (FIFO, length=30, each frame triplicated) -> (if full) Matrix Builder (90×21, min‑max normalize) -> CNN Classifier -> Decision Logic (confidence > 0.98?) -> Gesture output & Buffer clear -> Home automation trigger.

#### Critical Path
Frame -> MediaPipe -> Valid keypoints? -> Push/Buffer management -> (if full) Matrix build -> CNN inference -> Confidence > threshold? -> Action & buffer clear. Any keypoint extraction failure halts the path.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- Simplicity vs. temporal precision: static CNN + triplication is simpler than LSTM/3D‑CNN but sacrifices fine‑grained temporal alignment.
- Single‑user performance vs. generalization: near‑perfect training/validation on one user likely overfits to one anatomical style, risking poor cross‑user performance.
- Hardware: CPU‑only inference feasible due to small model, but MediaPipe CPU latency (~17 ms) may become a bottleneck at high frame rates.

#### Failure Signatures
- MediaPipe failure: no hand detected or low‑confidence keypoints -> buffer does not fill -> no classification; sporadic bad keypoints create noisy matrix patterns.
- CNN misclassification: false positives (e.g., “C” vs. neutral) or false negatives due to unseen pose variations or missing matrix patterns.
- Sliding window artifact: premature recognition of a subset gesture (e.g., detecting “I” during “J”) if buffer clears incorrectly.

#### First 3 Experiments
1. **Validate Matrix Sanity:** Manually capture a known static (“A”) and dynamic (“toggle”) gesture. Visualize the resulting 90×21 matrix; confirm static yields uniform vertical bands and dynamic shows clear vertical variation. Ensure column alignment matches MediaPipe’s 21‑point schema.
2. **Tune Sliding Window Parameter `n`:** Test n=1,2,3,4. Measure detection rate for fast vs. slow executions, latency between gesture completion and detection, and incidence of subset‑gesture errors. Optimize for target hardware’s average frame interval.
3. **Lighting & Confidence Threshold Stress Test:** Collect 20 samples per gesture under expected worst‑case lighting (dim room, backlight). Run inference with current model, plot confidence distribution for correct vs. incorrect samples, and adjust the 98% threshold if false positives/negatives are unbalanced. If accuracy collapses, plan to augment training data with similar lighting conditions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **User diversity generalization:** How does the model’s accuracy generalize across a diverse population of LIBRAS signers with varying anatomical proportions, expression rhythms, and signing styles? The paper admits that all results are from a single user and calls for systematic experiments with greater user diversity.
2. **Optimal triplication factor `n`:** What is the optimal `n` in the sliding window heuristic for balancing temporal resolution, gesture capture completeness, and latency in real‑time deployment? The paper adopts n=3 without quantifying the trade‑off.
3. **Embedded hardware performance:** Can the lightweight 2D CNN model maintain accuracy and real‑time performance when deployed on resource‑constrained embedded hardware (e.g., Raspberry Pi, microcontroller) typical of IoT home automation systems? The paper lists this as future work.
4. **Comparison with alternative temporal models:** How does the spatiotemporal matrix representation compare to 3D CNNs, CNN+LSTM, or optical flow in terms of accuracy, robustness, and computational cost for dynamic LIBRAS gestures? The paper contrasts its approach with recurrent networks but lacks a direct benchmark.
5. **Lighting robustness and data augmentation:** To what extent is performance dependent on specific, consistent lighting conditions, and can robustness be improved through data augmentation or architecture modifications? The paper only tests two lighting conditions and suggests incorporating variable lighting as an explicit training variable.

## Limitations
- **Single‑user evaluation:** All reported results (95%/92%) are from tests with one user, limiting claims about generalization across anatomies and signing styles.
- **Sliding window implementation details underspecified:** Exact buffer state‑machine logic and timing relationship between frame triplication and real‑time frame rate are not fully described, hindering precise replication.
- **Upstream dependency on MediaPipe:** The entire pipeline depends on MediaPipe’s hand detection quality; performance under occlusion, extreme angles, or atypical hand anatomies is unexamined.

## Confidence
- **Matrix representation effectiveness:** Medium – Detailed in paper and visualized, but break conditions (indistinguishable patterns) not stress‑tested across diverse gestures/users.
- **Triplication‑enabled real‑time inference:** Low – Mechanism described, but no ablation studies prove triplication is optimal versus other windowing/overlap strategies.
- **Lighting‑invariant CNN generalization:** Medium‑Low – Results from single‑user data; lighting effects may be confounded with MediaPipe’s own lighting sensitivity.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑user matrix pattern sanity check:** Capture the same 11 gestures from 3+ new users. Visualize 90×21 matrices to confirm that key vertical textural patterns (static vs. dynamic) are preserved across anatomies and that columns (keypoints) remain aligned.
2. **Sliding window parameter ablation:** Systematically vary triplication factor `n` (1–5) and buffer size (20–40 frames). Measure detection latency, false positives from subset gestures (e.g., “I” during “J”), and completion rate for fast/slow executions. Identify optimal `n` for your hardware’s fps.
3. **Lighting & user stress test with threshold tuning:** Collect 30+ samples per class from 3+ users under low‑light (<50 lux) and challenging lighting (backlight, colored light). Run inference, plot confidence distributions, and adjust the 98% threshold to balance false positives/negatives. Report per‑user accuracy to reveal generalization gaps.
