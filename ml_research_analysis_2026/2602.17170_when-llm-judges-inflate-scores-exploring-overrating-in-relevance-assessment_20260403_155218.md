# When LLM Judges Inflate Scores: Exploring Overrating in Relevance Assessment
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17170
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17170
- **Reference Count:** 28
- **Primary Result:** Small open-weight LLMs exhibit systematic overrating in relevance assessment, inflating scores for non-relevant passages and showing extreme overconfidence, challenging their suitability as human assessor replacements.

## Executive Summary
This paper systematically investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can reliably serve as judges for document relevance, a role traditionally performed by humans. Using the TREC Deep Learning 2019/2020 tracks, the authors evaluated four small open-weight models (Llama-3.2-3B, Gemma-3-4B, Mistral-7B, Qwen-3-8B) under pointwise (graded/binary scoring) and pairwise comparison paradigms. They found pervasive label inflation—models consistently assigned higher relevance scores than human judgments—coupled with extreme overconfidence in their (often incorrect) assessments. Pairwise evaluations revealed high tie rates, suggesting model indecision rather than wrong preferences. Controlled experiments showed sensitivity to superficial lexical cues, like query-term insertion, which could trigger perfect relevance labels for irrelevant passages. The findings indicate a systematic bias in LLM-based evaluation, not random noise, raising significant concerns about their deployment as automated assessors.

## Method Summary
The study employs a robust empirical design centered on the TREC DL 2019 and 2020 passage retrieval datasets, which include human relevance judgments (qrels). Four small, open-weight instruction-tuned LLMs (~3B–8B parameters) are prompted in two evaluation paradigms: pointwise (directly assigning a graded or binary relevance score to a passage given a query) and pairwise (choosing which of two passages is more relevant). The core analysis compares LLM-generated labels and confidences against the TREC qrels to measure bias (mean difference), overrate ratios (percentage of non-relevant passages labeled relevant), and agreement (Cohen’s κ). To probe mechanisms, controlled experiments manipulate passage length and inject lexical/semantic cues into non-relevant passages to observe scoring behavior.

## Key Results
- **Pervasive label inflation in pointwise grading:** Overrate ratios reached up to 66.51% for graded pointwise scoring, with mean bias (LLM score minus human score) as high as 0.895.
- **Extreme model overconfidence:** Across both correct and incorrect judgments, models expressed confidence exceeding 95%, indicating poor calibration.
- **High pairwise tie rates with correct non-tie preferences:** Tie rates peaked at 45.44%, but when excluding ties, accuracy for preferring the more relevant passage exceeded 85%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The systematic overrating stems from LLMs' over-reliance on superficial lexical overlap and their inherent tendency toward high-confidence outputs, even when incorrect. In pointwise settings, the presence of query terms in a passage—a shallow lexical cue—is sufficient for the model to assign a perfectly relevant label, regardless of semantic utility. This suggests the models are matching patterns from their instruction tuning (where relevance often correlates with keyword presence) rather than performing a nuanced, human-like assessment. The high confidence values are a byproduct of the models' next-token prediction objective, which produces highly certain probability distributions even for ambiguous tasks, decoupling confidence from actual accuracy.

- **LLM calibration:** The alignment between a model's stated confidence and its actual correctness. *Why needed:* To interpret the "overconfidence" finding. *Quick check:* Explain the difference between a calibrated and overconfident model.
- **Pairwise comparison vs. pointwise scoring:** Two fundamental evaluation paradigms. *Why needed:* The paper contrasts their failure modes (ties vs. inflation). *Quick check:* Describe how a pairwise prompt differs from a pointwise one.
- **Lexical matching vs. semantic understanding:** Surface form overlap vs. meaning-based relevance. *Why needed:* To understand the lexical cue experiment results. *Quick check:* Give an example where lexical matching fails for semantic relevance.
- **Cohen’s κ:** A metric for inter-rater agreement that accounts for chance. *Why needed:* Used to quantify LLM-human agreement. *Quick check:* What does a κ of 0.6 indicate?
- **Instruction tuning:** The fine-tuning process that teaches models to follow prompts. *Why needed:* The observed biases likely originate from this data. *Quick check:* How does instruction tuning data for "relevance" differ from actual retrieval assessment?

## Architecture Onboarding
The system is a single LLM acting as a judge, guided by a natural language prompt.

**Component Map:**  
User Query + Candidate Passage -> Prompt Template -> LLM Inference -> Text Output (Score/Label + Confidence) -> Parser -> Numeric Judgment

**Critical Path:** Prompt engineering is the dominant factor. The prompt structure (e.g., "Rate relevance on a scale of 0-3") dictates the task formulation. The LLM’s internal representation of "relevance," formed during instruction tuning, then processes the query-passage pair. Finally, the free-form output is parsed to extract the score and confidence.

**Design Tradeoffs:** Prompt simplicity vs. control. Simple prompts are easy to implement but prone to superficial heuristics (e.g., keyword matching). More complex chain-of-thought prompts might improve reasoning but increase variability and cost. Using confidence scores from the model's output probabilities is convenient but notoriously uncalibrated for open-ended tasks.

**Failure Signatures:** 1) Score inflation for passages containing query terms but lacking substantive content. 2) Near-perfect confidence (>95%) regardless of difficulty or correctness. 3) High tie rates in pairwise comparisons, indicating an inability to discriminate subtle relevance differences. 4) Sensitivity to passage length—simply expanding a passage with irrelevant text can increase its score.

**First Three Experiments:** 1) **Prompt variation test:** Run the pointwise task with 5+ different prompt formulations (e.g., with/without examples, different scale descriptions) to measure score stability. 2) **Passage length ablation:** Systematically append random, irrelevant sentences to passages and measure the change in pointwise scores. 3) **Lexical cue isolation:** Create a non-relevant passage and insert the exact query terms into it. Compare the score against a semantically relevant but lexically non-matching passage to isolate the lexical effect.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability scope:** Findings are limited to small (~3B–8B) open-weight models evaluated on English passage retrieval (TREC DL); performance for larger proprietary models, encoder-decoder architectures, or other domains (code, dialogue) is unknown.
- **Overconfidence nuance:** The reported ">95% confidence" metric may mask calibration issues across strata of difficulty or correctness; the distribution of confidence values is not explored.
- **Pairwise tie ambiguity:** High tie rates could stem from model indecision or true symmetry in preference; the experiment design does not disentangle these. Prompt variation effects on tie rates were minimally tested.

## Next Checks
1. **Scale & architecture test:** Replicate the core pointwise/pairwise experiments on proprietary models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) and larger open-weight models (>50B parameters), including encoder-decoder variants (e.g., T5).
2. **Cross-domain replication:** Evaluate the same models on non-English retrieval benchmarks (e.g., CLEF), code retrieval tasks (e.g., CodeXGLUE), and multi-modal retrieval to test domain robustness.
3. **Human correlation depth:** Compare LLM pairwise tie rates and confidence distributions against a multi-human assessor dataset to see if LLM "indecision" mirrors human disagreement patterns, not just against a single qrels standard.
