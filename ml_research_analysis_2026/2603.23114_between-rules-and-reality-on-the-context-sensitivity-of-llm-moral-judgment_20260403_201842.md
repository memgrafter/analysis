# Between Rules and Reality: On the Context Sensitivity of LLM Moral Judgment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23114  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23114  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** LLMs exhibit systematic context-sensitive moral judgment, shifting toward rule violations under consequentialist, emotional, or relational variations; activation steering can reliably modulate this sensitivity with minor capability trade-offs (1–3% on MMLU).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) adjust moral decisions when faced with contextual variations in ethical dilemmas. To address a lack of systematic evaluation, the authors construct *Contextual MoralChoice*, a dataset of 302 moral dilemmas augmented with consequentialist, emotional, and relational contextual changes. Evaluating 22 diverse LLMs reveals that nearly all models increase their selection of rule-violating actions under these variations, with mean contextual preference shifts (CPS) between 0.05 and 0.15. Compared to a human survey (N=132), LLMs are most influenced by different variation types—humans show largest shifts for relational/emotional factors, while most models are most sensitive to consequentialist changes. Crucially, a model’s baseline rule adherence (measured by marginal action likelihood) does not predict the magnitude of its contextual sensitivity. Finally, activation steering—extracting and applying contrastive vectors from model activations—reliably suppresses or induces contextual shifts, with only modest performance degradation on general benchmarks. The work highlights that LLM morality is not static but dynamically shaped by contextual cues, and that this sensitivity can be controlled via targeted interventions.

## Method Summary
The study builds on the MoralChoice dataset by generating three systematic contextual variations (consequentialist, emotional, relational) for 302 high-ambiguity base scenarios, resulting in 669 variant instances. For each scenario-variation pair, 22 LLMs (4B to 671B parameters) are prompted using three semantically equivalent templates (A/B comparison, single-choice, repeated question) with two action orders and 10 sampling repeats (60 total per instance). Model outputs are mapped to actions via rule-based matching and an LLM classifier. Two key metrics are computed: Marginal Action Likelihood (MAL), which averages the probability of the preferred action across equivalent prompts to isolate moral preference from template bias; and Contextual Preference Shift (CPS), defined as MAL(variation) minus MAL(base), quantifying sensitivity to contextual changes. Activation steering is implemented by extracting contrastive vectors from the residual stream at a selected layer (for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, middle layers 14–22 are optimal), weighted by the behavioral shift each training example contributes. The steering vector is applied during inference with a coefficient α, renormalized, and its effect on CPS is measured. Off-target capabilities are evaluated on MMLU, HellaSwag, and ETHICS to assess trade-offs. Human data from a survey (N=132) provides baseline comparisons for base agreement and CPS distributions.

## Key Results
- All 22 evaluated models show positive CPS across variation types (mean CPS 0.05–0.15), indicating a consistent increase in rule-violating choices when contextual factors are made salient.
- LLMs’ sensitivity patterns differ from humans: humans are most triggered by relational/emotional variations, while most models show largest shifts for consequentialist changes. Furthermore, a model’s base MAL (rule adherence) does not predict its CPS magnitude, suggesting orthogonal dimensions of moral behavior.
- Activation steering on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct reliably modulates CPS: subtracting the steering vector from contextual variants suppresses shifts, while adding it to base scenarios induces shifts, with minimal capability loss (1–3% drop on MMLU at |α| ≤ 2).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
- **Mechanism 1:** Adding consequentialist, emotional, or relational contextual variations systematically increases LLMs’ probability of choosing rule-violating actions. By modifying scenario descriptions to highlight specific morally relevant factors (e.g., explicit outcomes, emotional language, relational proximity), the model’s internal representation is biased toward actions that violate deontological rules but align with utilitarian, empathetic, or parochial considerations. Evidence: Abstract and Section 5.3 report positive CPS across nearly all models; Fig. 3 and Tab. 3 show significant shifts.
- **Mechanism 2:** A model’s baseline rule adherence (MAL) does not predict the magnitude of its contextual sensitivity (CPS). The contextual shift operates as a constant offset regardless of base preference, implying that sensitivity is governed by mechanisms separate from base morality. Evidence: Section 5.4 and Fig. 4 show parallel regression lines with slope near 1.0, indicating a predictable degree of sensitivity independent of baseline MAL.
- **Mechanism 3:** Activation steering can reliably increase or decrease an LLM’s contextual sensitivity. Contrastive activation addition computes a direction in activation space that encodes the difference between base and contextual variant representations; interpolating along this direction modulates the probability shift. Evidence: Section 6.2 (Fig. 5) shows CPS modulation across α values; weighted vector extraction (Section 6.1) denoises the signal; linear representation hypothesis supports the approach.

## Foundational Learning
- **Marginal Action Likelihood (MAL):** The average probability of the model’s preferred action across semantically equivalent prompt templates (e.g., A/B, single-choice). *Why needed:* Removes artifacts from template sensitivity to isolate moral preference. *Quick check:* How does marginalization over prompt forms improve reliability compared to a single template?
- **Contextual Preference Shift (CPS):** The difference in MAL between a contextual variant and its base scenario. *Why needed:* Isolates the causal effect of a specific contextual change. *Quick check:* What does a positive CPS value (e.g., 0.1) indicate about the model’s judgment under that variation?
- **Activation Steering:** An inference-time intervention that adds a computed direction (steering vector) to a model’s activations at a specific layer to alter its behavior. *Why needed:* Provides non-destructive control over contextual sensitivity without retraining. *Quick check:* What role does the behavioral weight play in computing the steering vector?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Base Scenarios -> Variation Generation (consequentialist, emotional, relational) -> Prompt Templates (A/B, Compare, Repeat) -> Model Sampling (60 instances per scenario-variation) -> Response Mapping (rule-based + LLM classifier) -> MAL/CPS Computation -> Steering Vector Extraction (weighted contrastive activations) -> Steering Application (layer l, coefficient α) -> Off-target Benchmarking (MMLU, HellaSwag, ETHICS).

**Critical path:** 1. Load dataset and model in isolated sessions. 2. For each scenario-variation, generate all 60 prompts and sample responses (T=1, no reasoning). 3. Map responses to actions; compute MAL for base and variants, then CPS. 4. For steering: split core scenarios (108 with all three variations) 70/30; extract steering vectors from training set (A/B format only, final prompt token activations, weighted by ΔP); select layer via linear probe accuracy; apply vectors to test set with swept α; recompute CPS. 5. Evaluate off-target benchmarks to measure degradation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Prompt format:* A/B yields most robust steering vectors but may lack nuance; Compare/Repeat offer diversity but higher noise (Sec. F.2 ablation).  
- *Weighted vs. unweighted vectors:* Weighting by behavioral shift denoises but requires reliable probability estimates; unweighted is simpler but noisier.  
- *Steering layer selection:* Middle layers (e.g., 14–22 for Llama-3.1-8B) often provide best probe accuracy; deep layers may capture high-level concepts but risk overfitting (validated via cross-validation, Fig. 11).

**Failure signatures:**  
- High refusal/invalid rates (>5% in Fig. 6) indicate misalignment or safety overrides contaminating CPS.  
- High boundary mass (BM0.1 > 0.3) signals model indecisiveness, making CPS noisy (Sec. E.3).  
- Steering-induced performance drop >5% on off-target benchmarks suggests overfitting or destructive intervention (Sec. F.4).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce base MAL distribution for a single model (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B) to validate the evaluation pipeline and prompt formatting/response mapping.  
2. Compute CPS for one variation (e.g., consequentialist) across all models; verify significant positive shifts via bootstrap confidence intervals (Tab. 3).  
3. Extract and apply a steering vector for that variation on held-out scenarios; sweep α from -5 to 5 and observe CPS modulation (Fig. 5); confirm linear mixed-effects model slope significance (Tab. 16).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **What is the appropriate degree of contextual sensitivity for LLMs' moral judgments?** The paper notes that whether context sensitivity is desirable remains an open normative question. No established calibration exists for alignment.
- **Are LLMs' contextual moral judgments robust to morally irrelevant perturbations?** The evaluation lacks controls for invariance under irrelevant changes (e.g., synonym swaps, reordering), so observed shifts might partly reflect brittleness rather than true moral processing.
- **How does reasoning scaffolding (e.g., chain-of-thought) affect contextual moral sensitivity?** Models were evaluated under direct-response conditions; enabling reasoning could alter sensitivity magnitude or direction.
- **Does activation steering generalize to control contextual sensitivity across diverse LLMs?** Steering was only tested on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct; findings may not transfer to other architectures or scales.
- **Are the observed patterns of contextual moral sensitivity consistent across languages and cultures?** The dataset and human survey are English-only; cultural-linguistic framing likely influences moral heuristics and cross-linguistic generalizability is unknown.

## Limitations
- **Dataset availability:** The Contextual MoralChoice dataset is not publicly confirmed; regenerating variations with a different model may alter wording and CPS values, requiring exact original scenarios for faithful replication.
- **Prompt and mapping underspecification:** Exact A/B/Compare/Repeat template texts are not fully provided; minor phrasing differences can shift MAL distributions. The LLM classifier used for response mapping is also unreported, risking systematic bias.
- **Steering specifics and generalizability:** Layer selection and token position for activation extraction lack full detail; steering was validated only on one model, limiting applicability to other LLMs.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Contextual variations (consequentialist, emotional, relational) systematically increase rule-violating choices (CPS > 0). **Confidence:** Medium – Direct evidence from 22 models (Fig. 3, Tab. 3), but reasoning was disabled, possibly altering natural sensitivity patterns.
- **Claim:** A model’s baseline rule adherence (MAL) does not predict its contextual sensitivity (CPS). **Confidence:** Medium-High – Strong visual and regression evidence (Fig. 4) showing parallel trends, though extreme floor effects could weaken the claim for very low-MAL models.
- **Claim:** Activation steering reliably modulates contextual sensitivity with modest capability trade-offs. **Confidence:** Medium – Validated on Llama-3.1-8B only; transferability and precise parameter choices (layer, token, weighting) remain uncertain.
