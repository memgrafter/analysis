# Left-right asymmetry in predicting brain activity from LLMs' representations emerges with their formal linguistic competence

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12811  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12811  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Left-right asymmetry in LLM-to-brain prediction emerges concurrently with formal linguistic competence around 10B training tokens.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the relationship between the development of large language models (LLMs) and the hemispheric asymmetry observed when using their representations to predict human brain activity. Using the OLMo-2 7B model at multiple training checkpoints and fMRI data from English participants, the authors compute hemisphere-specific brain scores and track their evolution alongside performance on various benchmarks. They find a sharp phase transition in left-right asymmetry near 10 billion tokens, which coincides with improvements on formal linguistic tasks (BLiMP, Zorro, acceptability) but not on arithmetic, Dyck, or functional tasks. The pattern replicates with Pythia models and French fMRI data, suggesting the asymmetry specifically reflects the acquisition of formal linguistic competence (grammatical pattern knowledge).

## Method Summary
The analysis employed OLMo-2 7B checkpoints across training, encoding naturalistic text stimuli that participants had viewed during fMRI scanning. For each checkpoint, activations from model layers were extracted and used as features to train separate ridge regression probes to predict fMRI voxel activity for the left and right hemispheres. The resulting prediction accuracies (brain scores) were aggregated over whole hemispheres. Asymmetry was quantified as the difference between left and right hemisphere scores. In parallel, the same checkpoints were evaluated on a battery of benchmarks covering formal linguistic (BLiMP, Zorro, acceptability), formal non-linguistic (arithmetic, Dyck), and functional (ARC, Hellaswag) skills. Sigmoidal curves were fitted to all trajectories to compare transition points and slopes.

## Key Results
- A sharp phase transition in left-right asymmetry appears around 10B training tokens.  
- The asymmetry transition aligns with performance gains on formal linguistic benchmarks but not on arithmetic, Dyck, or functional tasks.  
- Sigmoid fits show minimal Euclidean distance between asymmetry and formal linguistic metrics in transition point (x₀) and slope (β); the relationship replicates across Pythia models and French data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The authors propose that the emergence of left-right asymmetry in brain-LLM alignment is tied to the acquisition of formal linguistic competence—knowledge of grammatical patterns and rules. This suggests a reorganisation of model representations that mirrors the functional hemispheric specialization in the human brain, where the left hemisphere is more engaged in fine-grained syntactic processing. The exact causal or computational mechanism remains unspecified; the evidence is correlational, based on co-occurring developmental trajectories.

## Foundational Learning
- **Brain score**: Correlation between LLM layer activations and fMRI responses; needed to quantify model-brain alignment; quick check: compute Pearson r for held-out stimuli.  
- **Hemisphere asymmetry**: Difference between left and right hemisphere brain scores; needed to test lateralization; quick check: subtract right from left scores at each checkpoint.  
- **Formal linguistic competence**: Knowledge of grammatical structures, measured by tasks like BLiMP and Zorro; needed to isolate specific capabilities; quick check: evaluate model on acceptability judgments or grammaticality classification.  
- **Phase transition**: Sudden improvement during training; needed to pinpoint emergence; quick check: fit sigmoid to metric trajectory and locate inflection point.  
- **Training checkpoint**: Model state after processing a fixed number of tokens; needed to track development; quick check: load weights corresponding to token count and compute metrics.  
- **Sigmoid fitting**: Nonlinear regression capturing S-shaped growth; needed to compare trajectories; quick check: estimate parameters x₀ and β using least squares.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Input text -> OLMo-2 transformer -> Layer activations -> Ridge regression probe (trained per hemisphere) -> Predicted fMRI activity -> Brain score (per hemisphere) -> Asymmetry index  
Benchmark evaluation: Input -> Model -> Task-specific head -> Accuracy

**Critical path**  
1. Obtain or train LLM checkpoints at varying token counts.  
2. For each checkpoint, encode fMRI stimuli, extract activations.  
3. Train separate linear probes to predict left/right hemisphere activity.  
4. Evaluate probes on held-out data to compute brain scores.  
5. Calculate asymmetry (left minus right).  
6. Run model on benchmark suites to gather skill measures.  
7. Fit sigmoids to all metrics and compare parameters.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Aggregating over entire hemispheres masks potential regional differences; a voxelwise analysis would be more sensitive but computationally heavier.  
- Choice of layer(s) for activations influences alignment; the paper likely used a single middle layer for simplicity.  
- fMRI’s low temporal resolution may miss rapid neural dynamics; electrophysiology could complement.  
- Sparse checkpoint sampling risks missing nuances of the transition.

**Failure signatures**  
- Near-zero brain scores across checkpoints indicate no alignment.  
- Asymmetry trajectory remains flat, contradicting the main finding.  
- Benchmarks improve smoothly without a counterpart jump in asymmetry.  
- Replication with a new model or language fails to reproduce the parameter similarity.

**First experiments**
1. Plot left vs. right hemisphere brain scores across checkpoints to visualise asymmetry emergence.  
2. Fit sigmoids to asymmetry and each benchmark; compute Euclidean distances between (x₀, β) pairs to identify closest match.  
3. Replicate the entire pipeline with an alternative model family (e.g., Pythia) and a different language (e.g., French) to test generalisability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
How do semantic manipulations (e.g., altering word order, removing words) affect the left-right asymmetry in brain-LLM alignment?  
*Basis:* The authors note that Kauf et al. found brain scores sensitive to semantic changes and suggest investigating asymmetry.  
*Why unresolved:* No controlled semantic variations were included; analysis used naturalistic fMRI data.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Apply Kauf et al.’s minimal-pair manipulations to predict brain activity separately in left/right hemispheres across training checkpoints and compare effect sizes.

**Open Question 2**  
Do distinct brain regions within the hemispheres show different developmental trajectories of alignment with LLM representations?  
*Basis:* The Discussion calls for finer-grained evaluation beyond whole hemispheres.  
*Why unresolved:* Hemisphere-wide averaging obscures regional heterogeneity.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Compute voxelwise or ROI-based brain scores (e.g., IFG, STG) across training and test whether transitions occur at different steps for different regions.

**Open Question 3**  
What underlies the slight continued increase in left-right asymmetry after the phase transition tied to formal linguistic competence?  
*Basis:* The authors observe a residual asymmetry growth not matched by their functional benchmarks and call for investigation of “more sophisticated linguistic capabilities.”  
*Why unresolved:* No benchmark captured this late-stage change.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Develop or identify benchmarks that gradually improve post-formal competence (e.g., discourse-level tasks, pragmatic inference) and test alignment with the residual asymmetry; or analyse qualitative generation improvements.

**Open Question 4**  
Is the co-emergence of left-right asymmetry and formal linguistic competence universal across languages with diverse grammatical structures?  
*Basis:* Replication in French (typologically close to English) is encouraging but insufficient to claim universality.  
*Why unresolved:* No evidence from typologically distant languages (e.g., agglutinative, polysynthetic).  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct similar experiments with LLMs trained on multilingual data and fMRI from speakers of diverse languages, using language-specific formal competence benchmarks.

## Limitations
- The analysis is correlational; co-occurrence does not prove causation.  
- Used only two model families (OLMo-2, Pythia) and two languages (English, French), limiting generalizability across architectures and language families.  
- Reliance on fMRI (low temporal resolution) and a narrow set of formal tasks may overlook relevant competencies; sparse checkpoint sampling adds estimation uncertainty.

## Confidence
- Asymmetry emergence reflects formal linguistic competence: **Medium**  
- Phase transition occurs around 10B tokens: **Medium**  
- Specificity to linguistic (vs. non-linguistic) tasks: **Medium**  
- Cross-linguistic generalization (English→French): **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. Test additional LLM families (e.g., LLaMA, GPT-NeoX, Falcon) across comparable training scales to verify consistency of the asymmetry transition and its relation to formal linguistic benchmarks.  
2. Expand multilingual analysis to typologically diverse languages (e.g., Japanese, Turkish, Arabic) using comparable neuroimaging data to assess generalizability.  
3. Perform causal interventions: selectively ablate or fine-tune model components hypothesized to encode grammatical knowledge, then measure changes in hemisphere-specific brain-prediction scores.
