# Who Judges the Judge? Evaluating LLM-as-a-Judge for French Medical open-ended QA

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04033
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04033
- **Reference count:** 13
- **Primary result:** LLM judges for French medical QA show generator-dependent bias; domain-adapted models (MedGemma-27B) achieve highest expert alignment (~60% F1); SFT+GRPO on Phi-3.5-mini boosts accuracy from 47% to 71.4% and reduces bias.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates large language models (LLMs) as automatic judges for semantic equivalence in French medical open-ended question answering, aiming to reduce costly expert annotations. It finds that judge performance varies substantially depending on which LLM generated the answer being evaluated, revealing a generator-dependent bias. Domain-adapted biomedical models (e.g., MedGemma-27B) and large general-purpose models (Qwen-80B) align best with expert judgments (~60% F1). A compact model (Phi-3.5-mini) is lightweightly adapted via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on only 184 expert-labeled instances, significantly improving accuracy (47% → 71.4%) and reducing bias. Statistical tests confirm GRPO's gains, suggesting that carefully adapted small models can support scalable evaluation in low-resource medical settings, though generator-aware design remains essential.

## Method Summary
The study constructs a French medical QA dataset from S-EDITION (100 training questions) and MediQAl (100 evaluation questions), each with clinician reference answers. Five LLMs generate candidate answers per question. Experts annotate binary semantic equivalence (covers essential medical info, allowing paraphrasing). The training set is expanded to 184 instances via answer swapping and paraphrasing augmentation. LLM judges—including closed-access (GPT-5.1, Gemini-2.5-Pro), general-purpose (Qwen-80B), biomedical (MedGemma-27B), and compact (Phi-3.5-mini)—are evaluated using a fixed English prompt. Phi-3.5-mini is aligned: first SFT for 5 epochs on 166 instances (early stopping on validation loss), then GRPO for 2 epochs on 184 instances with a reward combining correctness (+1/-1) and format compliance (+0.5 for exact "0" or "1"). Evaluation computes accuracy, F1, Pearson correlation against expert labels, with per-generator analysis to measure bias.

## Key Results
- LLM judges exhibit substantial sensitivity to the answer-generating model, with F1 scores varying significantly across generators (e.g., Phi-3.5-mini's F1 ranges from 24.7% to 66.2%).
- Domain-adapted MedGemma-27B and large Qwen-80B achieve highest alignment with experts (F1 ~60%, Pearson ~40.7), outperforming larger generalists like GPT-5.1.
- SFT+GRPO on Phi-3.5-mini raises accuracy from 47% to 71.4% and F1 from 52.6% to 57.1%, while reducing performance variance across generators; McNemar test shows significance (p<0.001).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Generator-Dependent Judge Sensitivity
- **Claim:** LLM-as-a-Judge performance in French medical OEQA is sensitive to the answer-generating model, meaning a judge's reliability varies depending on which LLM produced the answer.
- **Mechanism:** Different LLM generators produce answers with distinct stylistic and structural characteristics (e.g., verbosity, phrasing). An LLM judge encodes biases aligned with these surface patterns rather than purely medical semantics, e.g., over-rejecting equivalence for concise, fine-tuned models.
- **Core assumption:** The judge's decision boundary is influenced by features correlated with the generator's style.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "LLM judges exhibit substantial sensitivity to the answer-generating model"; [section 4.2] Figure 2 and Table 9 show large F1 variations across five generators; [corpus] neighboring papers on LLM judge biases support plausibility.

### Mechanism 2: Domain Adaptation Advantage
- **Claim:** Domain-adapted biomedical LLMs (e.g., MedGemma-27B) achieve higher alignment with expert medical judgments than larger general-purpose models.
- **Mechanism:** Medical pretraining exposes the model to domain-specific terminology and clinical reasoning, allowing better assessment of clinical validity and semantic equivalence, reducing reliance on superficial cues.
- **Core assumption:** Alignment improves through continued pretraining on biomedical corpora beyond general instruction tuning.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Domain-adapted models (MedGemma-27B) and large general-purpose models (Qwen-80B) achieve the highest alignment"; [section 4.3] MedGemma-27B tops F1 (60.5%) and Pearson (40.67), surpassing GPT-5.1 and Gemini-2.5-Pro.

### Mechanism 3: SFT+GRPO for Compact Models
- **Claim:** Lightweight adaptation of a compact model via SFT followed by GRPO with limited expert data substantially improves judging accuracy and reduces generator-dependent bias.
- **Mechanism:** SFT teaches the binary task and output format; GRPO refines the policy using a reward combining correctness and format adherence, stabilizing learning and correcting biases (e.g., over-predicting equivalence) more effectively than SFT alone.
- **Core assumption:** Reinforcement-based alignment with a simple, well-shaped reward improves discriminative boundaries beyond imitation learning, even with minimal supervision.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Lightweight adaptation... significantly improves metrics: accuracy rises from 47% to 71.4%"; [section 4.4] Table 1 shows GRPO-Phi-3.5-mini achieves 71.4% accuracy; McNemar test (Table 3) significant (p<0.001); Figure 3 shows reduced variance; [section D] ablation (Table 7) shows GRPO after SFT convergence yields best F1 (0.571).

## Foundational Learning
- **Binary Semantic Equivalence Judgment**  
  *Why needed:* The evaluation is framed as binary classification (equivalent vs. not), simplifying annotation but defining correctness as covering essential medical info while allowing paraphrasing/minor omissions.  
  *Quick check:* If an answer is clinically accurate but omits a non-essential detail from the reference, should it be labeled equivalent? (Yes, per section 3.1.)

- **Generator-Aware Evaluation Design**  
  *Why needed:* Judge performance is not invariant to the answer-generating model; evaluation protocols must account for biases linked to generator style (e.g., verbosity).  
  *Quick check:* In Table 9, why does Phi-3.5-mini have near-100% recall but low precision? (It massively over-predicts equivalence, a bias varying by generator.)

- **GRPO Reward Formulation for Judging**  
  *Why needed:* GRPO uses a reward combining correctness (+1/-1) and format compliance (+0.5 for exact "0"/"1") to shape the model for stable, correct binary output in low-data regimes.  
  *Quick check:* What happens if reward only uses correctness? Model might output explanations or non-binary text, breaking the pipeline.

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Pipeline -> Judge Implementation -> Evaluation Loop

**Critical path:**
1. Ensure strict separation between alignment training data (184 instances) and evaluation data (500 instances) to avoid overfitting to generator-specific patterns.
2. Use the same binary equivalence prompt for humans and LLMs (Appendix B.2) to ensure conceptual consistency.
3. After SFT, wait for validation loss plateau before starting GRPO (Appendix D shows this yields best performance).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- SFT vs. GRPO: SFT is faster/more stable but may not fully correct biases; GRPO requires more compute/tuning but improves accuracy and reduces generator sensitivity.
- Model size: Small models (Phi-3.5-mini) are accessible but base versions can be highly biased; adaptation is essential.
- Annotation scheme: Binary equivalence is simple for experts but misses nuanced errors (incompleteness, safety), limiting real-world applicability.

**Failure signatures:**
- Judge outputs non-binary text (e.g., explanations, "maybe") → violates format, likely due to insufficient GRPO or poor reward shaping.
- Performance collapses on specific generators (e.g., very low F1 on SFT-Qwen) → indicates unmitigated generator bias; check if GRPO was applied after SFT convergence.
- High recall, very low precision (like base Phi-3.5) → model over-predicts equivalence; needs stronger negative examples or reward penalty for false positives.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replicate baseline bias: Run base Phi-3.5-mini on the 500-instance evaluation set. Compute confusion matrix and F1 per generator. Expect high recall (~98%) and low accuracy (~47%).
2. SFT-only alignment: Fine-tune Phi-3.5-mini on the 166 SFT instances for 5 epochs with early stopping on validation loss. Evaluate on test set. Expect modest F1 improvement to ~54%.
3. GRPO after SFT convergence: Take the SFT model from experiment 2 and apply GRPO for 2 epochs using the 184-instance reward-labeled set. Validate that McNemar test shows significant accuracy gain over base and SFT models. Check that F1 per generator variance decreases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What are the root causes of generator-dependent biases in LLM judges for medical OEQA, and how can they be mitigated? The study identifies bias but does not investigate underlying mechanisms (e.g., verbosity, style, model family effects) or propose debiasing strategies.
- Can lightweight alignment methods (e.g., SFT+GRPO) scale effectively with larger, more diverse medical datasets and complex annotation schemas? The adaptation experiments use limited data (184 instances) and a binary task; it is unclear if gains generalize to larger scales or nuanced clinical error types.
- Do binary semantic equivalence judgments sufficiently capture clinically relevant errors, or are richer, multi-faceted evaluation schemas required for medical OEQA? Binary labels may overlook partial correctness, misleading information, or clinical safety issues critical in real-world QA.
- How well do findings on LLM-as-a-Judge generalize beyond French medical OEQA to other languages, domains, and cross-cultural clinical contexts? The study is confined to French medical terminology and practices; linguistic and cultural variations may affect judge reliability.

## Limitations
- Single-clinician annotation for the evaluation set (90% agreement on a 10-item subset) limits ground-truth reliability; no multi-rater statistics reported.
- Anonymized generator models (`SFT-LLaMA-13B-Chat`, `SFT-Qwen-4B`) prevent exact replication of answer distributions, affecting bias analysis.
- GRPO implementation details (e.g., KL penalty coefficient, advantage normalization) are absent; defaults from `trl` library may not match original, risking replication divergence.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Judge sensitivity to generator):** High confidence. Directly supported by per-generator F1 variance in Figure 2/Table 9 and statistical analysis.
- **Claim 2 (Domain adaptation advantage):** Medium confidence. MedGemma-27B achieves top metrics, but comparison limited to single dataset; no ablation on pretraining corpus specifics.
- **Claim 3 (SFT+GRPO efficacy for small models):** Medium confidence. Gains statistically significant (McNemar p<0.001), but GRPO hyperparameters not fully specified; replication may diverge.

## Next Checks
- **Generator-invariance stress test:** Retrain Phi-3.5-mini with SFT+GRPO on answers from substituted models (e.g., MedInjection-FR); measure if performance gains and bias reduction persist across shifted distributions.
- **GRPO ablation:** Compare SFT+GRPO against SFT+PPO and SFT-only with identical data/compute; isolate GRPO’s contribution using matched hyperparameter sweeps.
- **Annotation robustness:** Re-annotate a 100-instance subset with 2+ French clinician experts; compute inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ) and reassess judge alignment against consensus labels.
