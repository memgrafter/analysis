# CIRCLE: A Framework for Evaluating AI from a Real-World Lens

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.24055
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24055
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** A six-stage lifecycle framework that translates stakeholder concerns into measurable constructs, integrating field testing, red teaming, and longitudinal studies to produce systematic, context-sensitive evidence for AI governance.

## Executive Summary
CIRCLE is a framework designed to address the "reality gap" in AI evaluation, where current practices focus on model capabilities rather than real-world outcomes for non-technical stakeholders. It operationalizes the Validation phase of TEVV through a structured, six-stage lifecycle. The core method is a construct-centered approach: it first elicits and systematizes stakeholder priorities into named, abstract constructs (e.g., "over-reliance"), then operationalizes these into specific, observable metrics. By integrating disparate evaluation methods (field testing, red teaming, longitudinal studies) into a single traceable pipeline, CIRCLE aims to produce "systematic knowledge"—evidence that is comparable across deployment sites yet sensitive to local context—enabling governance based on materialized downstream effects.

## Method Summary
The framework prescribes an iterative six-stage pipeline: 1) **Contextualize** (elicit stakeholder concerns into a context brief and named constructs), 2) **Identify** (operationalize constructs into an evaluation design plan with indicators, methods, and sampling), 3) **Represent** (execute real-world trials to collect execution data), 4) **Compare** (analyze data to map behaviors to constructs and produce a findings synthesis), 5) **Learn** (facilitate stakeholder interpretation to generate actionable insights), and 6) **Extend** (design a continuous, multi-layer monitoring plan). It treats contextual heterogeneity as a primary signal, using strategies like stratified sampling to include non-users and link every metric back to a stakeholder-relevant outcome, thereby strengthening multiple forms of measurement validity.

## Key Results
- Produces "systematic knowledge"—evidence comparable across deployment sites but sensitive to local context—by integrating field testing, red teaming, and longitudinal studies into a single, traceable pipeline.
- Strengthens core forms of measurement validity (ecological, construct, consequential, criterion, internal) by design, through deliberate choices that treat contextual variation as informative.
- Enables governance based on materialized downstream effects and socio-technical outcomes rather than solely on theoretical model capabilities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Stakeholder-Construct Translation
- **Claim:** CIRCLE bridges the "reality gap" by systematically translating stakeholder concerns into measurable evaluation constructs.
- **Mechanism:** Stage 1 ("Context specification") elicits priorities from non-technical stakeholders (e.g., educators) and synthesizes them into abstract constructs (e.g., "over-reliance"). Stage 2 ("Evaluation design") then operationalizes these into specific, observable indicators and metrics, creating a direct link between qualitative concerns and quantitative evidence.
- **Core Assumption:** Stakeholder-articulated priorities, when properly systematized, define the valid scope of an AI system's evaluation. Ignoring this external perspective leads to evaluations with poor ecological and consequential validity.
- **Evidence Anchors:**
  - [abstract] "CIRCLE operationalizes the Validation phase of TEVV... by formalizing the translation of stakeholder concerns outside the stack into measurable signals."
  - [section 2.1] "The lifecycle's first stage builds contextual awareness by eliciting what matters most to stakeholders... This collected information is then synthesized into an evaluation construct..."
- **Break Condition:** If stakeholder elicitation fails to surface latent concerns or produces conflicting priorities that cannot be systematized, the foundational construct becomes invalid, undermining the pipeline.

### Mechanism 2: Integrated Pipeline for Systematic Knowledge
- **Claim:** CIRCLE produces "systematic knowledge" by integrating disparate evaluation activities into a single, traceable, iterative lifecycle.
- **Mechanism:** The six-stage architecture forces a sequential, documented handoff of deliverables (e.g., context brief → evaluation plan → execution data → synthesis report). This creates an auditable chain of evidence, allowing insights from longitudinal monitoring (Stage 6) to feed back and refine original constructs (Stage 1). It coordinates methods typically run in isolation.
- **Core Assumption:** Fragmented, one-off evaluations cannot provide comparable evidence across sites or track higher-order effects over time. A unified pipeline is necessary to link causal mechanisms to materialized outcomes.
- **Evidence Anchors:**
  - [abstract] "By integrating methods such as field testing, red teaming, and longitudinal studies into a coordinated pipeline, CIRCLE produces 'systematic knowledge'—evidence that is comparable across sites yet sensitive to local context."
  - [section 2] "It brings a traceable pipeline to current ad-hoc testing paradigms where every stage generates its own documented output, feeding directly into the next stage."
- **Break Condition:** If any stage's deliverable is poorly documented or fails to connect to the next stage's input (e.g., analysis metrics not tied to the original constructs), the traceable chain breaks, reverting to siloed evidence.

### Mechanism 3: Validity Through Contextual Heterogeneity
- **Claim:** CIRCLE strengthens multiple forms of measurement validity by treating contextual heterogeneity as a primary signal, not noise.
- **Mechanism:** The framework's design (Stage 2 method selection, Stage 3 stratified sampling, Stage 6 multi-layer monitoring) deliberately captures and analyzes how different user groups, settings, and time periods adapt to the AI system. By analyzing this variance, it assesses whether performance generalizes meaningfully (criterion/consequential validity) and whether outcomes reflect the intended construct (construct validity) in diverse real-world conditions (ecological validity).
- **Core Assumption:** Contextual differences are not experimental noise but the critical variable determining real-world success or failure. Evaluating only under optimized, homogenous conditions provides misleading inferences.
- **Evidence Anchors:**
  - [section 2] "CIRCLE leverages context as the primary signal of system behavior... CIRCLE strengthens... validity by treating contextual heterogeneity as a primary signal."
  - [section 3] "CIRCLE enhances ecological validity... It strengthens construct validity... Criterion validity is supported... Consequential validity is improved..."
- **Break Condition:** If evaluation design (Stage 2) uses sampling or scenarios that reify the same narrow contexts as model benchmarking (e.g., only recruiting expert users), the mechanism fails, and validity threats persist.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Construct Operationalization**
  - **Why needed:** The entire CIRCLE lifecycle depends on turning abstract, stakeholder-defined ideas (e.g., "fairness," "over-reliance") into specific, observable, and measurable indicators. Without this, evaluation remains vague and non-actionable.
  - **Quick Check:** Given a stakeholder concern like "the AI might erode critical thinking," what would be one concrete, observable behavior in a classroom that could serve as an indicator for this construct?
- **Concept: Socio-Technical System Thinking**
  - **Why needed:** CIRCLE evaluates an *AI system*—a combination of model, interface, organizational process, and user practices—not just a model in isolation. Understanding that outcomes emerge from interactions between people, technology, and context is essential for designing valid scenarios (Stage 2) and interpreting results (Stage 4).
  - **Quick Check:** In an EdTech vignette, why would measuring only the chatbot's answer accuracy (a model metric) be insufficient to assess the "over-reliance" construct?
- **Concept: Validity Types in Contextual Measurement**
  - **Why needed:** CIRCLE's core argument is that current evaluations fail on multiple validity fronts (ecological, construct, consequential). Understanding these terms is crucial to grasp *why* its design choices (like longitudinal studies or stratified sampling) are necessary improvements.
  - **Quick Check:** What is the difference between *ecological validity* (realism of setting) and *consequential validity* (link to meaningful outcomes), and which CIRCLE stage most directly addresses each?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Contextualize -> Identify -> Represent -> Compare -> Learn -> Extend. The central "Construct" flows through all stages, and the "Context Brief" seeds the design.
- **Critical path:** A project must successfully complete Stage 1 (construct definition) to inform Stage 2 (design). Stage 2's design dictates Stage 3's data collection, which is necessary for Stage 4's analysis. Insights (Stage 5) are meaningless without prior analysis. Monitoring (Stage 6) relies on established constructs and metrics. The earliest point of failure is Stage 1.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Breadth vs. Depth:** Stage 3 sampling strategy. A large, diverse sample improves generalizability but increases cost and complexity.
  - **Precision vs. Safety:** Stage 2/4 scenario design. Highly realistic proxy scenarios yield more ecologically valid data but risk exposing participants to harm.
  - **Automation vs. Interpretation:** Stage 6 monitoring. Fully automated signal tracking is efficient but may miss emergent, nuanced risks requiring human contextual judgment.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. **Vague Constructs:** Stage 1 output lists broad concepts ("bias," "safety") without specific, operational definitions.
  2. **Metric-Construct Misalignment:** Stage 4 analysis reports standard model metrics (e.g., F1 score) that cannot be traced back to any stakeholder concern in the Context Brief.
  3. **Context-Blind Monitoring:** Stage 6 monitors only system performance drift (e.g., latency, error rates) without tracking shifts in the socio-technical context (e.g., new user policies, changed workflows).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Construct Definition Pilot:** Run a mini Stage 1 with 3-5 representative stakeholders. Goal: Produce a single, clear, and contested-free construct definition (e.g., "task completion time increase due to AI interface friction"). Test if the team can derive 2-3 observable indicators.
  2. **Scenario-Construct Alignment Test:** Using the construct from Exp 1, design one evaluation scenario (Stage 2) and one data collection protocol. Run it with 2-3 pilot participants (Stage 3). Goal: Successfully observe and code at least one of the predefined indicators in the raw data. Validate that the scenario actually surfaces the intended behavior.
  3. **Traceability Audit:** Take outputs from Exp 1 (Context Brief) and Exp 2 (raw data, initial codes). Manually attempt to reconstruct the logical chain: Does every coded behavior map to an observable indicator? Does every indicator map to the named construct? Does the construct map to a stakeholder priority? Document breaks in this chain.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the capture of contextual detail be effectively scaled while preserving the depth needed for valid measurement?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Future Work" section states a need to "reduce the friction of capturing contextual detail at scale, for example by developing validated user simulators and other tooling grounded in empirical lifecycle data."
- **Why unresolved:** The framework identifies context as the primary signal but acknowledges that current methods (workshops, field trials) are inherently slow and resource-intensive, creating a barrier to widespread adoption and comparative analysis across many settings.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** The development and empirical validation of tools (e.g., user simulators, LLM-augmented elicitation protocols) that can reliably generate or synthesize context-rich data streams comparable to those from human-centered stages, with demonstrated fidelity to real-world stakeholder priorities.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can conflicts between divergent stakeholder priorities be systematically resolved during construct operationalization?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The framework centers translating "stakeholder-articulated priorities" into constructs (Stage 1) but does not address scenarios where different stakeholder groups (e.g., developers vs. end-users vs. regulators) hold incompatible or zero-sum concerns about system impacts.
- **Why unresolved:** Without a method for negotiation or prioritization, the process risks either ignoring critical perspectives or producing intractable disputes that stall evaluation design, undermining the goal of producing actionable evidence for governance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A documented protocol or decision heuristic for construct prioritization, tested in multi-stakeholder workshops, that yields shared, measurable constructs while transparently accounting for power asymmetries and value trade-offs.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What governance and incentive structures are necessary for organizations to adopt lifecycle-based evaluation over isolated, model-centric testing?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Future Work" section calls for "shifts in institutional incentives so that deployment-level evidence is valued alongside model documentation."
- **Why unresolved:** CIRCLE requires significant upfront investment in stakeholder engagement, interdisciplinary teams, and longitudinal tracking, which conflict with common performance metrics (e.g., release velocity, benchmark scores) and resource allocations in typical AI development workflows.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Case studies or policy frameworks demonstrating successful integration of CIRCLE stages into existing MLOps or DevOps pipelines, with documented metrics (e.g., ROI, risk reduction, compliance) that justify the additional overhead to senior leadership and investors.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can operationalized constructs and scenarios be standardized to enable valid cross-context comparison without losing local sensitivity?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Future Work" section proposes "shared libraries of operationalized constructs" to make evaluations "more interoperable across settings," while the core framework aims to produce evidence "comparable across sites yet sensitive to local context."
- **Why unresolved:** There is a tension between standardization (needed for comparison) and contextualization (needed for validity). The framework describes the output (context briefs, constructs) but not the meta-structures or metadata schemas that would allow different organizations to align their constructs while preserving contextual nuances.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A pilot library of cross-domain constructs (e.g., "over-reliance," "equity impact") with defined dimensions, observable indicators, and scenario templates, tested across multiple deployments to measure both comparability of outcomes and fidelity to local stakeholder concerns.

## Limitations
- The framework's validity is currently theoretical; no empirical validation studies demonstrate its application, reliability, or comparative effectiveness against existing TEVV or audit approaches.
- Specific tooling for construct operationalization (e.g., rubrics, annotation schemas, user simulators) is absent, blocking faithful reproduction and scalable implementation.
- Scalability mechanisms for resource-intensive stages (interdisciplinary teams, human-subjects protocols, longitudinal tracking) in large-scale or multi-site deployments are underspecified.

## Confidence
- **Stakeholder-Construct Translation (Mechanism 1):** **Medium**. The Stage 1/2 process is clearly defined in-concept but lacks tested rubrics or case studies proving consistent, unbiased operationalization.
- **Integrated Pipeline Production of "Systematic Knowledge" (Mechanism 2):** **Medium**. The sequential architecture is specified, but its claimed advantage over siloed methods is an assertion, not yet demonstrated with comparative evidence.
- **Enhanced Validity & Real-World Outcomes (Core Outcome):** **Low**. The argument links design choices to validity types persuasively, but the ultimate outcome—governance based on materialized effects—remains a prospective claim without empirical backing.
