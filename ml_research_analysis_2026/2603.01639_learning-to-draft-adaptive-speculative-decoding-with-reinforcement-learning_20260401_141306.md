# Learning to Draft: Adaptive Speculative Decoding with Reinforcement Learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01639  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01639  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Achieves 2.24×–4.32× speedup over autoregressive decoding and outperforms Eagle3 by up to 36.4% on greedy decoding while maintaining robustness in high-temperature scenarios.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces LTD, a framework that reformulates speculative decoding as a reinforcement learning (RL) environment to jointly optimize draft tree depth and verification size for maximum throughput. Two co-adaptive policies—a depth policy and a size policy—are trained with proximal policy optimization (PPO) using a reward directly based on accepted tokens per total wall-clock time. Through iterative co-adaptation, the policies learn to coordinate drafting and verification dynamically. Evaluated on five large language models across four tasks, LTD substantially outperforms prior dynamic speculative decoding methods, especially under high-temperature sampling where others degrade.

## Method Summary
LTD frames each draft-and-verify cycle as an RL step. The state includes draft depth, context length, and token probabilities from the draft tree. The depth policy (binary actions: continue/stop) decides after each expansion whether to halt drafting. If halted, the size policy (discrete actions: verification size V) selects how many tokens to verify. The reward is throughput: accepted tokens divided by the sum of draft and verification wall-clock times. Policies are first pretrained independently (depth policy with fixed V=60; size policy with uniformly sampled depths) then undergo iterative co-adaptation: alternately freezing one policy while updating the other, enabling them to adjust to each other’s evolving strategies. This direct optimization of throughput, rather than acceptance length, avoids common suboptimal trade-offs.

## Key Results
- **Speedup ratios:** 2.24×–4.32× over vanilla autoregressive decoding across five LLMs.
- **SOTA comparison:** Outperforms Eagle3 by up to 36.4% on greedy decoding.
- **Robustness:** Maintains stable speedup in high-temperature scenarios where other dynamic methods fail.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Framing speculative decoding as a reinforcement learning (RL) environment with a throughput-based reward directly aligns the optimization objective with true inference speedup.
- **Mechanism:** The environment defines state, actions (depth stop/continue, verification size), and a reward λ<sub>c</sub> = L<sub>A</sub>/(T<sub>draft</sub>+T<sub>verify</sub>). PPO updates both depth and size policies to maximize expected cumulative reward.
- **Core assumption:** Maximizing throughput per cycle properly balances generating more candidates against the time spent drafting and verifying.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "directly optimizes for throughput of each draft-and-verify cycle" and "formulate the problem as a reinforcement learning environment."
  - [section 3.1] Formal throughput definition and decomposition into latency components.
  - [section 4] Details of state representation, action spaces, and PPO objective with throughput reward.
  - [corpus] Prior methods (e.g., PEARL, Ouroboros) optimize acceptance length; LTD’s novelty is the explicit throughput reward.
- **Break condition:** If the latency model (T<sub>draft</sub>, T<sub>verify</sub>) is inaccurate due to hardware variability or batching, the reward signal may misguide policy updates, reducing gains.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Co-adaptive training of depth and size policies allows them to learn coordinated strategies that jointly optimize drafting and verification phases.
- **Mechanism:** After independent pretraining, policies undergo iterative rounds where one is frozen while the other updates based on the frozen partner’s dynamic decisions. This mutual adaptation avoids isolated optimization.
- **Core assumption:** The optimal draft depth and verification size are interdependent; each policy must anticipate the other’s behavior to maximize throughput.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "two co-adaptive policies to dynamically coordinate the draft and verification phases."
  - [section 4] "train these two policies iteratively, which allows them to co-adapt to each other’s evolving strategies."
  - [section 6.1] Figure 4 and Tables 7–8 show progressive speedup improvements across iterations.
  - [corpus] Methods like Disco or SpecDec++ adjust only depth or size independently, missing synergistic gains.
- **Break condition:** If one policy converges to a suboptimal fixed strategy early, the other may overfit to it during iterative training, preventing a globally optimal joint policy.

### Mechanism 3
- **Mechanism:** Including draft depth (D) and context length (L) in the state makes policies aware of time implications, avoiding the "acceptance length trap" of prior work.
- **Core assumption:** Draft time scales linearly with depth, and verification time scales with verification size; thus these features are sufficient proxies for latency without explicit timing measurements.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1] Figure 2 (Left) empirically shows linear draft time vs. depth and step-wise verification time vs. size.
  - [section 4] State vector includes D and L for latency, and token probabilities for acceptance prediction.
  - [table 2] Throughput reward outperforms acceptance-length and time-cost rewards, confirming explicit time balance matters.
  - [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., C2T, Gammatune) use similar features but optimize for acceptance length, not throughput.
- **Break condition:** If hardware or model architecture changes cause non-linear time scaling (e.g., saturation at large V), the static feature mapping may fail to capture true costs.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** State Encoder -> Depth Policy (π<sub>D</sub>) -> Size Policy (π<sub>V</sub>) -> PPO Trainer, with Reward Calculator measuring wall-clock time and computing throughput.

**Critical path:**
1. After target model emits a token, initialize draft tree with that token as root.
2. **Depth policy loop:** After each expansion step, use current state to decide CONTINUE/STOP.
3. If STOP, pass full draft tree to **size policy** to select verification size V.
4. Execute verification: target model processes V tokens, determines acceptance length L<sub>A</sub>.
5. Measure T<sub>draft</sub>, T<sub>verify</sub>, compute reward λ<sub>c</sub> = L<sub>A</sub>/(T<sub>draft</sub> + T<sub>verify</sub>).
6. Store transition (s, a, r) in PPO buffer; update policies after sufficient steps.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Depth policy latency:** Single-layer MLP minimizes per-step overhead but may limit capacity; trade-off between decision speed and policy expressiveness.
- **Action granularity:** Size policy uses discrete V (20–240 in steps of 20); coarser actions reduce training complexity but may miss optimal points.
- **Discount factor γ:** High γ (0.999) for depth policy encourages long-term exploration of deeper trees but may slow convergence.

**Failure signatures:**
- **High τ but low speedup:** Size policy consistently proposes too-large V, over-optimizing acceptance length at cost of verification time (check mean V distribution).
- **Policy collapse:** Depth policy always chooses STOP at minimal depth (e.g., D=1) → no acceleration; inspect action entropy.
- **Training instability:** Reward variance spikes during iterative rounds; monitor loss curves for divergence.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Ablation of policies:** Remove depth or size policy (Table 4) to measure each component’s contribution to speedup.
2. **Iterative training analysis:** Run zero to three rounds of alternating optimization; track validation speedup per iteration (Figure 4, Tables 7–8).
3. **Reward comparison:** Train size policy with acceptance-length, time-cost, and throughput rewards; compare final speedup (Table 2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How does LTD's performance correlate with diverse sampling temperatures beyond 1.0, and what is the temperature threshold where robustness degrades?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper claims robustness in "high-temperature scenarios" and demonstrates stable speedup at temperature=1.0 (Section 5.2, Table 5), but no results are presented for higher temperatures (e.g., >1.0) that are used in creative or diverse generation tasks.  
- **Why unresolved:** Without empirical data beyond temperature=1.0, it is unclear whether the throughput-based reward remains effective as token distributions become more stochastic, or if the policies overfit to greedy/low-temperature patterns.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic evaluation of LTD across a wider temperature range (e.g., 0.8, 1.2, 1.5) on multiple datasets to quantify speedup variance and identify breakdown points, potentially comparing against other dynamic methods' failure modes.

**Open Question 2:** Does the iterative co-adaptation training guarantee convergence to a globally optimal joint policy, or are there model-specific local optima that limit further improvements?  
- **Basis in paper:** The iterative optimization yields benefits for DeepSeek-Distill-LLaMA 8B across three rounds but saturates earlier for Llama3-8B (Section 6.1, Figure 4). The paper does not explain this divergence or provide a convergence criterion.  
- **Why unresolved:** The alternating optimization lacks theoretical guarantees; empirical results suggest model-dependent convergence behavior, raising questions about whether a universal iteration count exists or if certain models require tailored co-adaptation schedules.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train with more iterations across diverse models, analyze policy reward landscapes and mutual influences (e.g., depth policy's impact on size policy's action distribution), and possibly introduce convergence metrics like policy distance.

**Open Question 3:** How transferable are the policies trained on a single hardware setup (NVIDIA A100) to different GPU architectures or system-level conditions (e.g., memory bandwidth, concurrent requests)?  
- **Basis in paper:** All experiments use a single A100 GPU with single-request decoding (Section 5.1). The throughput reward directly incorporates wall-clock time, which is hardware-dependent, yet the paper assumes learned policies generalize across deployment environments.  
- **Why unresolved:** Draft and verification latencies scale differently across hardware; e.g., verification may be more memory-bound on some GPUs. Policies optimized on A100 might misallocate resources on other systems, reducing speedup.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the entire training and evaluation pipeline on at least two other GPU architectures (e.g., H100, T4) or in batched inference settings to measure speedup degradation and need for system-specific fine-tuning.

**Open Question 4:** Could enriching the depth policy's observation space (currently minimal) with additional contextual features, such as entropy or intermediate hidden states, improve its decision-making without prohibitive latency overhead?  
- **Basis in paper:** The depth policy deliberately uses a lightweight input (token probabilities at last layer, depth, length) to minimize latency (Section 4, Appendix A.2). Other dynamic methods (e.g., SpecDec++, Disco) incorporate entropy or hidden states (Table 3). The paper's ablation (Table 5, Appendix A.4) only tested the size policy with extra features.  
- **Why unresolved:** The trade-off between decision accuracy and computational overhead is not fully explored for the depth policy, which executes more frequently. It is unknown whether richer signals could reduce premature stopping or over-expansion in complex contexts.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a controlled study where the depth policy's observation space is systematically expanded (adding entropy, layer-wise probabilities, etc.), measuring both the change in speedup and the added decision latency per step.

## Limitations
- **Draft model ambiguity:** Exact architecture and weights of Eagle3 draft models are not provided; they must be sourced externally and may vary across target LLMs.
- **State representation details:** The "probability of tokens at the last layer" is ambiguous—likely the log-probabilities of the W frontier tokens after the most recent expansion, but exact vector ordering/size is unspecified. Token selection for verification while preserving a valid tree structure is also not described.
- **Hardware-dependent timing:** Reward uses wall-clock time; without identical hardware (NVIDIA A100) and batching/kernel settings, measured times may differ, affecting training dynamics and generalization.
- **Training data narrowness:** Training on HumanEval (code) risks overfitting; generalization to dialogue/QA is unproven.
- **Iterative schedule unclear:** The paper states "two rounds" but reports Iter0–Iter3, implying three optimization steps; the precise sequence of freezing/training is unclear.
- **Latency scaling assumptions:** The linear time scaling with depth and size may not hold universally (e.g., saturation effects at large V), limiting applicability.

## Confidence
- **Overall speedup (2.24×–4.32×, +36.4% vs. Eagle3):** Medium (evaluation breadth strong but reproducibility hindered by missing details).
- **Mechanism 1 (throughput-based RL reward):** High (reward design aligns with objective but sensitive to timing accuracy).
- **Mechanism 2 (co-adaptive policies):** Medium (iterative gains shown, yet convergence stability not guaranteed).
- **Mechanism 3 (state with depth/context length):** Medium (ablation supports necessity, but linear latency assumption may limit generalizability).
