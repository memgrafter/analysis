# Reward Shaping for Inference-Time Alignment: A Stackelberg Game Perspective

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02572  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02572  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Stackelberg Reward Shaping (SRS) improves inference-time alignment, boosting average reward on HH‑RLHF and SHP, achieving >66% GPT‑4 win‑tie rates, and incurring negligible overhead (2.69e‑4 s/token).

## Executive Summary
Directly using preference‑trained reward models under KL regularization for LLM alignment inherits biases from the base policy and often requires reward amplification, which can lead to reward‑hacking suboptimalities. This work addresses the problem by framing reward design as a Stackelberg game: a leader (reward provider) commits to a reward function, and a follower (LLM) best‑responds via KL‑regularized optimization. Theoretical analysis reveals an optimal threshold‑structured reward per prompt—responses above a threshold receive maximum reward, others minimum—where the threshold equals the expected utility under the equilibrium policy. Practically, the threshold is approximated using Monte Carlo samples from the base policy, and a soft sigmoid‑shaped reward is adopted for tractability and robustness. Integrating this Stackelberg Reward Shaping (SRS) into inference‑time alignment methods (CD, ARGS) yields consistent improvements on HH‑RLHF and SHP benchmarks with Qwen3‑8B and Llama3‑8B, while remaining computationally efficient and mitigating reward hacking.

## Method Summary
The authors model reward design as a Stackelberg game. The leader (reward designer) selects a reward function, anticipating the follower’s (LLM’s) best response under a KL‑regularized optimization objective. They prove that the optimal reward takes a threshold form: for each prompt, responses whose true reward exceeds a certain threshold are assigned the highest possible reward, while others receive the lowest. This threshold coincides with the expected utility achieved by the resulting policy. To make the approach practical, they approximate the threshold using Monte Carlo samples drawn from the base policy. Then, instead of a hard threshold, they apply a smooth sigmoid function to the raw reward values, preserving the threshold’s intent while ensuring differentiability and robustness. The resulting Stackelberg Reward Shaping (SRS) can be wrapped around existing inference‑time alignment techniques such as Contrastive Decoding (CD) and ARGS, modifying the reward signal fed into the KL‑regularized optimization.

## Key Results
- SRS consistently increases average reward on the HH‑RLHF and SHP benchmarks using Qwen3‑8B and Llama3‑8B models.
- GPT‑4 evaluations show win‑tie rates exceeding 66% against all baseline alignment methods.
- Inference overhead is negligible (2.69e‑4 seconds per token for ARGS), and SRS effectively mitigates reward hacking associated with unbounded reward scaling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Stackelberg formulation ensures the reward is optimized with full awareness of the LLM’s subsequent best response, avoiding the implicit bias transfer that occurs when directly using preference‑trained reward models. The threshold structure decouples the reward from the base policy’s distortions: by calibrating the threshold to the expected utility under the equilibrium policy, the LLM is incentivized to produce responses that genuinely improve alignment rather than merely exploiting reward quirks. The soft sigmoid approximation retains this calibration while providing smooth gradients for stable optimization, and the Monte Carlo estimate from the base policy gives a practical way to compute the threshold without full reinforcement‑learning training. Together, these elements yield a reward signal that guides the LLM toward higher‑quality responses with minimal computational overhead.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Base Policy → Monte Carlo Sampling → Threshold Computation → Sigmoid Reward Shaping → KL‑Regularized Optimization → Aligned Policy  

**Critical path:** The threshold computation step relies on Monte Carlo samples from the base policy to estimate the expected utility; this threshold then parametrizes the sigmoid reward shaping, which directly influences the KL‑regularized optimization that produces the final aligned policy.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Hard threshold (optimal but non‑differentiable) vs. soft sigmoid (tractable but slightly suboptimal).  
- Number of Monte Carlo samples (higher accuracy vs. additional compute).  
- Integration point—as a wrapper around existing inference‑time methods—preserves compatibility but may limit deeper architectural changes.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Inaccurate threshold due to insufficient or biased Monte Carlo samples (e.g., under‑sampling low‑probability responses) leading to mis‑calibrated rewards.  
- Overly flat sigmoid (large temperature) failing to create a meaningful distinction above/below threshold, reducing alignment effectiveness.  
- Excessive Monte Carlo overhead eroding the claimed efficiency gains.

## Limitations
- The theoretical threshold result assumes a deterministic reward commitment and a unique LLM best response, while real reward models are stochastic and possibly non‑unique.  
- Monte Carlo threshold estimation may be biased if the base policy under‑samples low‑probability but important responses.  
- The soft sigmoid approximation deviates from the optimal threshold, potentially leaving some alignment gains unrealized.
