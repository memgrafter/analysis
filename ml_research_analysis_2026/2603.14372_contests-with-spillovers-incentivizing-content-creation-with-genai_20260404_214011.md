# Contests with Spillovers: Incentivizing Content Creation with GenAI

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.14372
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14372
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The Provisional Allocation mechanism family guarantees a unique Pareto‑dominant pure Nash equilibrium and admits approximation algorithms (e.g., Greedy Cost Selection) that achieve near‑optimal social welfare on random interaction graphs.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles incentive design in GenAI content platforms where creators' efforts generate positive spillovers. Standard contests become unstable because spillovers create strategic complementarities. The authors introduce the Provisional Allocation (PRA) family, assigning each creator a fixed share of attention scaled by their quality. PRA mechanisms eliminate strategic uncertainty and yield a unique Pareto‑dominant pure Nash equilibrium. While optimizing social welfare over PRA is NP‑hard and inapproximable in general, the paper identifies tractable cases: a (1+β)‑approximation for β‑bounded spillovers, optimality for tree‑structured spillovers, and a linearithmic‑time Greedy Cost Selection (GCS) algorithm that attains a (1−O(N⁻¹/⁴))‑approximation on Erdős–Rényi random graphs with high probability. Simulations corroborate the strong practical performance of GCS relative to a naïve equal‑allocation baseline.

## Method Summary
The core method is the Provisional Allocation mechanism: the designer chooses weights p_i (∑ p_i ≤ 1), and each creator i’s utility is U_i = p_i·Q_i(x) − (1/N)·c_i·x_i, where Q_i(x) = (1/N)·x_i·(q_i + Σ_{j≠i} r_ij·g_ij·x_j). This induces a potential game with a greatest pure Nash equilibrium reachable via best‑response dynamics. For welfare optimization, the authors prove NP‑hardness and develop approximation algorithms for structured scenarios: a (1+β)‑approximation for β‑bounded spillovers, an exact algorithm for trees, and Greedy Cost Selection (GCS) for random graphs. GCS sorts players by ascending cost, finds the largest feasible prefix k such that p_i = c_i / (q_i + Σ_{j in prefix} r_ij·g_ij) satisfy ∑ p_i ≤ 1, and returns these p_i. The paper’s simulations generate random instances (varying N, r, q_star), compute the greatest PNE for GCS and an Equal Allocation baseline, and report average social welfare SW = (1/N) Σ_i x_i·(q_i + Σ_j r_ij·g_ij·x_j) and number of active players K(x).

## Key Results
- **Unique stable equilibrium**: PRA mechanisms guarantee a unique Pareto‑dominant pure Nash equilibrium, unlike standard contests that may be unstable.
- **Complexity barriers**: Optimizing social welfare over PRA is NP‑hard and inapproximable within a constant factor in general.
- **Efficient approximation**: Greedy Cost Selection runs in O(N²) time and achieves a (1−O(N⁻¹/⁴))‑approximation to the optimal PRA on random interaction graphs with high probability. Simulations confirm GCS significantly outperforms equal allocation across various parameters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
PRA’s fixed shares decouple the allocation problem from strategic interactions: each creator’s best response depends only on others’ efforts through the quality term A_i = q_i + Σ r_ij·g_ij·x_j, which exhibits increasing differences in x. This supermodularity guarantees the existence of a greatest pure Nash equilibrium that Pareto dominates all other equilibria. The Pareto dominance stems from the potential function aligning with social welfare up to a constant factor. Greedy Cost Selection constructs a feasible allocation by incentivizing the lowest‑cost creators, approximating the optimal PRA. On random graphs, independent sampling of spillover coefficients yields concentration, allowing a constant‑factor approximation despite worst‑case intractability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Provisional Allocation (PRA)**: Mechanism assigning each creator a fixed share p_i scaled by quality. *Why needed*: Converts the contest into a potential game with a unique, efficient equilibrium. *Quick check*: Are p_i functions of other players’ actions? No, they are set once by the designer.
- **Pareto‑dominant PNE**: The equilibrium that Pareto dominates all others. *Why needed*: Refines the set of equilibria to the socially best outcome. *Quick check*: In PRA, which PNE is selected by iterated best response from all‑effort? The greatest (Pareto‑dominant) one.
- **Greedy Cost Selection (GCS)**: Approximate algorithm sorting by cost and selecting a prefix of low‑cost players; sets p_i = c_i / A_i where A_i is i’s quality if all selected players exert effort. *Why needed*: Provides an O(N²) approximation for welfare‑optimal PRA on random graphs. *Quick check*: What does A_i include? q_i plus sum of g_ij from other selected players (with edge indicators r_ij).
- **Spillover effects**: Positive externalities where one creator’s effort increases others’ quality. *Why needed*: Models network effects in content ecosystems. *Quick check*: Is the effect directed from j to i? Yes, via r_ij·g_ij·x_j.
- **Random interaction graphs**: Erdős–Rényi directed graphs where edge (i,j) exists with probability r and spillover g_ij uniform. *Why needed*: Enables average‑case analysis and concentration. *Quick check*: Expected indegree per player is r(N−1).
- **Supermodular game**: Game with increasing differences in players’ actions. *Why needed*: Guarantees existence and convergence of best‑response dynamics. *Quick check*: Does PRA’s utility satisfy increasing differences? Yes, because Q_i is sum of others’ efforts with positive coefficients.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
RandomGraph -> Mechanism (GCS/EqualAlloc) -> PNESolver -> Metrics

**Critical path**:  
1. Generate random instance (q_i, c_i, r_ij, g_ij).  
2. Run chosen allocation algorithm to obtain p.  
3. Compute greatest PNE via iterated best‑response starting from all‑effort.  
4. Evaluate social welfare SW = (1/N) Σ_i x_i·A_i and active player count K = Σ_i x_i.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Complexity*: GCS takes O(N²) due to repeated A_i computation; exhaustive search is infeasible. Equal Allocation is O(N) but yields much lower welfare.  
- *Stability vs. optimality*: PRA ensures a unique Pareto‑dominant PNE; GCS inherits this while approximating the optimal PRA.  
- *Model assumptions*: Theoretical guarantees rely on random graphs; real‑world networks may violate these assumptions, potentially degrading performance.

**Failure signatures**:  
- GCS returns no feasible prefix (all p_i=0) even for N=1, indicating a bug in the feasibility condition or sorting.  
- Best‑response dynamics cycles or fails to terminate, breaking monotonic decrease from all‑effort.  
- Social Welfare scales as N² or far deviates from theory, suggesting missing (1/N) factor or incorrect A_i.

**Exactly 3 first experiments**:  
1. **Symmetric instance test**: Verify GCS analytically; e.g., identical players (q_i=1, g_ij=1, r_ij=1, c_i=0.2) should result in all players active and p_i = 0.2/N? Actually compute: A_i = 1 + (k−1); p_i = 0.2 / k; feasibility sum = k·0.2/k = 0.2 ≤ 1, so k=N. Confirm via code.
2. **Brute‑force optimal for small N**: For N≤6, enumerate all possible p vectors (or use LP) to compute exact optimal SW, then compare GCS’s SW to verify the approximation gap.
3. **Replication of Figure 1 scaling**: Fix q_star=1, vary r∈{0.2,0.5,0.8} and N∈{100,…,1000}, generate 1000 instances per configuration, plot mean SW±3σ for GCS and Equal Allocation, and check alignment with N·(q_star r)^{3/2} and K≈r·q_star·N.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Assumption relaxation**: How do the stability and welfare guarantees change when Assumption 1 (monotonic spillovers) does not hold?
- **Multi‑dimensional objectives**: How can platform revenue, fairness, or diversification be incorporated into the PRA framework without sacrificing too much welfare?
- **Endogenous attention**: What if total user attention depends on the ecosystem’s aggregated quality rather than being fixed?
- **Fairness and diversity**: How to prevent dominance by a small set of highly connected or high‑quality creators while preserving efficiency?
- **Robustness to realistic networks**: How do approximation algorithms like Greedy Cost Selection perform on non‑Erdős–Rényi graphs (e.g., clustered, power‑law)?

## Limitations
- **Theoretical–simulation discrepancy**: The paper cites `N(q r)^{3/2}` from Theorem 13, but Theorem 13 actually states `SW = 4N(q_star r)^3`. The mismatch raises questions about the reference used in figures.
- **Missing experimental details**: Random seed, exact number of instances per configuration, and error bar computation are unspecified, hindering exact figure replication.
- **Best‑response dynamics ambiguity**: The update order (synchronous vs. asynchronous) is not described; different orders could affect convergence speed but not final greatest PNE, though this should be verified.

## Confidence
- *Pareto‑dominant PNE existence*: Medium (depends on structural assumptions not fully explored beyond the model).  
- *NP‑hardness and inapproximability*: High (standard reductions).  
- *GCS near‑optimality on random graphs*: Medium (high‑probability guarantee, but constants and small‑N behavior uncertain).  
- *Simulation performance*: Medium (trend matches, but exact numbers vary with randomness).
