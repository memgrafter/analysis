# SpokenUS: A Spoken User Simulator for Task-Oriented Dialogue
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.16783
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16783
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: SpokenUS achieves goal alignment of 0.82 with ~1K hours of speech, human MOS 4.06, surpassing all baselines including larger omni models.

## Executive Summary
Task-oriented dialogue (TOD) systems require robust training and evaluation, yet existing spoken user simulators often fail to capture natural human behaviors such as proactive barge-in, disfluent speech, and emotional prosody. This paper presents SpokenUS, a spoken user simulator grounded in TOD, and SpokenTOD, a large-scale dataset of 52,390 dialogues augmented with cross-turn slots, barge-in events, disfluencies, and emotional annotations. SpokenUS employs a dedicated turn-taking head for proactive interruption with human-like reaction times (~240 ms), a two-stage training pipeline that transfers TOD knowledge from text-only data, and a structured transcript representation for controllable generation of spoken behaviors. With only ~1,000 hours of speech, SpokenUS achieves a goal alignment of 0.82 and a Human Mean Opinion Score of 4.06, outperforming all baselines including much larger omni models trained on ~20M hours. Its natural timing and slot disclosure patterns cause substantial performance drops in downstream agents, demonstrating its effectiveness for training robust spoken dialogue systems.

## Method Summary
SpokenUS extends the Qwen2.5-3B language model with special tokens for emotions and disfluencies, and processes audio in 6,561 discrete token vocabulary at 25 Hz. The simulator operates through three sequential modes. **Listening** mode: a turn‑taking head monitors each incoming assistant speech token and outputs per‑token probabilities for listen, barge‑in, or turn‑end; these are aggregated over a 6‑token sliding window (~240 ms) to decide whether to interrupt. **Pre‑scripting** mode: upon detecting a turn boundary, the text head generates a structured transcript delimited by `<BOT>` and `<EOT>`, prefixed with an emotion token and interleaved with disfluency markers (`[FP]`, `[COR]`, etc.). **Speaking** mode: the speech head autoregressively generates speech tokens conditioned on the transcript, which are then converted to waveforms by a Conditional Flow Matching model followed by a HiFi‑GAN vocoder. A zero‑shot speaker module selects voice characteristics from a pool of 542 speakers, and a goal conditioner injects the user’s task goal (domains, intents, slots) at each step.

Training proceeds in two stages. **Stage 1** uses the full SpokenTOD corpus (both user and assistant turns) in a text‑only setting to acquire TOD policies. **Stage 2** focuses on user turns only, jointly optimizing three losses: text generation (cross‑entropy), speech token prediction (cross‑entropy), and turn‑taking classification (cross‑entropy). This multi‑task approach ensures that the model retains goal‑directed behavior while learning to produce natural spoken phenomena. The model converges after 12k optimization steps with a total of ~1,034 hours of speech, demonstrating data efficiency compared to models pretrained on tens of thousands of hours.

## Key Results
- Goal alignment (GA) reaches **0.82** with only ~1K hours of speech, matching models trained on ~20M hours.
- Human Mean Opinion Score (MOS) for naturalness is **4.06**, exceeding all baseline systems including larger omni models.
- The simulator’s realistic barge‑in, disfluency, and gradual slot disclosure cause **substantial performance degradation** in dialogue agents trained on synthetic data, confirming its value for robustness evaluation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The dedicated turn-taking head with streaming token aggregation enables realistic proactive interruption (barge-in) at human-like reaction times.
- **Mechanism:** The turn-taking head processes each incoming assistant speech token, producing per-token probabilities for three classes (listen, barge-in, turn-end). These are aggregated using linear-recency weighting over a 6-token sliding window (~240ms at 25 Hz), mimicking human reaction time for turn-taking. A barge-in prediction fires an immediate interruption, while turn-end indicates yielding the floor.
- **Core assumption:** Barge-in decisions can be learned from token-level contextual patterns with a fixed latency window that matches average human response time (~240ms). The model must distinguish partial assistant speech that contains errors/insufficiency (barge-in) from complete utterances (turn-end).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "SpokenUS, a spoken user simulator grounded in TOD with a dedicated architecture for barge-in"
  - [section 3.2] "Listening Mode. The turn-taking head continuously monitors incoming assistant speech to determine when the user should speak... agregates them by computing a weighted sum of per-token probabilities"
  - [section 6.1] Turn-end detection 66.0% accuracy, barge-in 58.6% accuracy on test set with 240ms window
- **Break condition:** If the 240ms window proves insufficient for semantic understanding of assistant utterance content, or if the model cannot learn to differentiate barge-in triggers from turn-end cues in the token stream, resulting in >25% confused predictions between barge-in and turn-end.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Explicit pre-scripting with structured transcripts containing emotion and disfluency tokens enables controllable, interpretable generation of natural spoken behaviors while maintaining task coherence.
- **Mechanism:** Upon detecting a turn boundary, the text head generates a transcript delimited by special tokens (<BOT>, <EOT>), prefixed with an emotion token and interleaved with disfluency tokens ([FP], [COR], etc.). This transcript then conditions speech token generation in Speaking Mode, ensuring prosodic and hesitation patterns align with the annotated behaviors. The explicit transcript layer makes behavior generation transparent and traceable.
- **Core assumption:** Spoken user behaviors can be effectively modeled as discrete text-level annotations that map reliably to acoustic realization via a conditional speech generation model. The transcript serves as a reliable intermediate representation that separates content planning from acoustic realization.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] "Pre-scripting Mode... generates a structured transcript delimited by <BOT> and <EOT>. As illustrated... the transcript begins with an emotion token... with interleaved disfluency tokens"
  - [section 6.2] "SpokenUS closely follows the human pattern, exhibiting a steady increase in slot coverage throughout the dialogue rather than front-loading them"
  - [table 3] SpokenUS achieves Human MOS 4.06, exceeding all baselines including larger omni models
- **Break condition:** If the special tokens fail to consistently elicit appropriate prosodic/disfluent patterns in the synthesized speech, or if the transcript generation loses goal-directed coherence (e.g., slot values omitted or corrupted during text generation), causing goal coverage to drop below random baselines.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A two-stage training pipeline—first acquiring TOD knowledge from text-only dialogues, then joint multi-task training on speech behaviors—enables effective spoken user simulation with modest spoken data (~1K hours), rivaling models pretrained on ~20M hours.
- **Mechanism:** Stage 1 trains on full text-only TOD dialogues (both user and assistant turns) to learn goal-directed response generation. Stage 2 performs multi-task training on user turns only, combining text generation, speech token generation, and turn-taking classification losses. This staged approach transfers strong text-based TOD knowledge before introducing the complexities of spoken behavior generation.
- **Core assumption:** Task-oriented dialogue knowledge can be effectively acquired from text corpora and transferred to the spoken domain with limited spoken data, while the explicit multi-task objective prevents catastrophic forgetting of goals during speech behavioralization.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1] "Stage 1, the model is trained on full text-only SpokenTOD dialogues... to acquire TOD knowledge. Stage 2, all three heads are jointly trained"
  - [abstract] "SpokenUS achieves comparable goal coverage to significantly larger models while substantially outperforming all baselines in Human MOS"
  - [table 3] GA 0.82 with ~1K hours vs Qwen3-Omni-30B GA 0.93 with ~20M hours
- **Break condition:** If the text-to-speech transfer gap cannot be bridged—i.e., goals degrade significantly when adding speech training (GA drop >0.3 from Stage 1 to Stage 2), or if multi-task objectives conflict causing tradeoffs (e.g., high goal coverage but low MOS, or vice versa).

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Task-Oriented Dialogue (TOD) Goal Structure**  
- **Why needed here:** SpokenUS operates from a structured user goal G = (T, S) where S contains domain, intent, and slot-value pairs. Understanding this representation is essential to grasp what "goal coverage" means and why gradual slot disclosure matters.  
- **Quick check question:** Given a goal with constraints (area, price) and requests (phone, postcode), which should appear earlier in a natural conversation?

**Concept: Turn-taking and Barge-in Taxonomy**  
- **Why needed here:** The simulator's core innovation is proactive interruption. Understanding the three barge-in types (error recovery, clarification, efficiency) and their triggers is necessary to interpret turn-taking performance and failure modes.  
- **Quick check question:** What distinguishes an error recovery barge-in from an efficiency barge-in in terms of assistant utterance state?

**Concept: Discrete Speech Tokenization**  
- **Why needed here:** SpokenUS uses 6,561 discrete speech tokens at 25 Hz. This differs from continuous waveforms or mel-spectrograms. Token frame rate directly impacts the 240ms turn-taking window size (6 tokens).  
- **Quick check question:** Why 6 tokens at 25 Hz equals ~240ms, and why this matters for turn-taking latency?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Assistant speech token stream (6,561 vocab, 25 Hz) -> Turn‑taking Head (3‑class classifier with linear‑recency aggregation over 6 tokens) -> Text Head (generates `<BOT>`→emotion token→disfluency‑marked text→`<EOT>`) -> Speech Head (autoregressive token generation conditioned on transcript, `<BOA>`→speech tokens→`<EOA>`) -> Vocoder (Conditional Flow Matching + HiFi‑GAN) -> Audio output (24 kHz). Parallel conditioning: Goal Conditioner embeds user goal at each turn; Speaker Module provides zero‑shot voice cloning from 542‑speaker pool.

**Critical path:** The processing pipeline proceeds as:
1. Streaming ingestion: Assistant audio → speech tokenizer → stream to turn‑taking head.
2. Turn decision: Aggregate per-token predictions over 6‑token window; if non‑listen predicted, record trigger point.
3. Transcript generation: Feed history + current context to text head; generate until `<EOT>`.
4. Speech synthesis: Condition speech head on transcript + speaker reference; generate until `<EOA>`.
5. Audio output: CFM → HiFi‑GAN → waveform, with emotion conditioning throughout.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Sequential modes vs unified architecture:** Three separate modes simplify training and debugging but introduce pipeline latency. A fully parallel end‑to‑end design could reduce latency but would require joint training with complex credit assignment.
- **Special token annotations vs implicit generation:** Explicit `[FP]`, `[COR]` tokens provide precise control and interpretability but rely on perfect annotation during training. Implicit prosody prediction from emotion alone would be simpler but less controllable.
- **Small model footprint vs capability ceiling:** 3B parameters enable efficient deployment but may limit complex goal tracking. Larger omni models (30B) achieve higher GA but at massive compute cost and lower naturalness.
- **Synthetic data augmentation vs authenticity:** SpokenTOD’s pipeline scales infinitely but may miss natural acoustic variability. Incorporating SpokenWOZ real recordings helps but the majority is synthetic.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Turn-taking degradation:** >15% confused rate between barge‑in and turn‑end (Table 4: 16.4% confused for turn‑end) indicates poor decision making.
- **Behavioral unnaturalness:** Speaker similarity scores >0.95 across turns (Table 3: humans ~0.84‑0.88, SpokenUS 0.93‑0.92) indicate overly uniform voice; >0.95 suggests insufficient prosodic variation.
- **Goal disclosure timing:** Slot coverage curve that plateaus by turn 3‑4 (like omni models in Figure 3) indicates failure to mimic gradual human disclosure.
- **WER spikes:** Cascaded WER >15% (Table 3) suggests speech intelligibility issues, often from misplaced disfluencies or emotional prosody distorting clarity.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Turn‑taking boundary ablation:** Process 100 assistant turns with known barge‑in/turn‑end labels. Vary the aggregation window (3, 6, 9 tokens) and measure precision/recall. Confirm the 6‑token ~240ms window is optimal.
2. **Cross‑slot continuity test:** Generate dialogues with multi‑turn phone numbers (e.g., "555‑" then "1234"). Verify the simulator maintains the partial slot value in internal state and self‑corrects if needed, rather than restarting.
3. **Emotion‑prosody validation:** For identical text with different emotion tokens, extract pitch contour and energy envelope using Librosa. Confirm systematic variation (e.g., angry → higher energy, fearful → higher pitch variance) matching the keyword mapping in Table 6.

## Limitations
- Dataset availability: SpokenTOD is not publicly released; reproduction requires reverse‑engineering the augmentation pipeline and speaker pool.
- Turn‑taking optimization: Thresholds (T_T=0.45, T_B=0.05) were selected on a dev set and may not generalize; barge‑in accuracy is modest (58.6%).
- Synthetic data bias: Over 90% of SpokenTOD is synthetic speech, which may limit acoustic diversity, leading to high speaker similarity (0.93–0.92 vs human 0.84–0.88) and potential overfitting.

## Confidence
- Barge‑in architecture (Mechanism 1): Medium
- Explicit pre‑scripting (Mechanism 2): High
- Two‑stage training pipeline (Mechanism 3): High

## Next Checks
1. **Aggregation window ablation:** Process 100 assistant turns with known barge‑in/turn‑end labels; test window sizes (3, 6, 9 tokens); compute F1; expect optimal performance at 6 tokens (~240 ms).
2. **Cross‑slot state retention:** Generate dialogues that split a slot value across turns (e.g., “555‑” then “1234”); measure whether the simulator correctly continues the value without restarting; target >95% correct continuation.
3. **Emotion‑prosody mapping:** Synthesize identical sentences with each emotion token; extract pitch and energy features; verify systematic variation matching the emotion keyword mapping; require effect size >0.5 (Cohen’s d).
