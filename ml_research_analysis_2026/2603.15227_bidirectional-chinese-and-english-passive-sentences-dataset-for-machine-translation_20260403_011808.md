# Bidirectional Chinese and English Passive Sentences Dataset for Machine Translation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15227
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15227
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Machine translation models over-preserve the source voice when translating passive constructions, failing to adapt to target-language norms, with English-to-Chinese translation showing partial awareness of Chinese passive constraints.

## Executive Summary
The study investigates how machine translation (MT) systems handle passive voice, a construction that differs significantly between English (frequent, neutral) and Chinese (rare, often negative-connoted). The authors construct a large parallel dataset of 73,965 sentences with automatic annotation of translation strategies, supplemented by a manually verified test set. Evaluation of various NMT and LLM models reveals that MT systems are heavily influenced by the syntactic voice of the source sentence, often producing a passive in the target language even when that clashes with target-language norms. Models demonstrate some knowledge of Chinese passive usage constraints, leading to better voice consistency in English-to-Chinese translation than the reverse. Commercial NMT systems achieve higher scores on automatic metrics (BLEU, chrF++, COMET), while large language models exhibit greater diversity in translation strategies, highlighting a trade-off between metric optimization and linguistic flexibility.

## Method Summary
The authors built a bidirectional dataset by extracting candidate passive sentences from five existing parallel corpora using heuristic rules (e.g., Chinese *bei*, English *be* + past participle). For each sentence, they applied dependency parsing (spaCy for English, LTP for Chinese) and rule-based matchers to automatically label source-side voice and target-side translation strategies (e.g., Chinese *bei* passive, *ba* resultative, topic sentence). The automatic annotation was validated on a held-out test set of 450 sentences per translation direction and register, corrected by human experts. The dataset was used to evaluate several MT systems (open-source NMT, commercial APIs, and LLMs). Model outputs were re-annotated with the same rule-set, and voice/structure consistency with human translations was measured, alongside standard automatic evaluation metrics. This pipeline enabled quantitative analysis of how well models respect cross-linguistic passive norms and explore alternative strategies.

## Key Results
- A parallel dataset of 73,965 sentences with automatic strategy annotation and a manually verified test set was created, enabling fine-grained analysis of passive translation.
- MT models consistently over-preserve the source voice: for English *be* passives translated to Chinese, models produce Chinese passives at 26.7–40.3% rates, far exceeding the ~8.9% passive rate in original Chinese text.
- Models show directional asymmetry: they achieve higher voice consistency in English-to-Chinese translation, suggesting partial acquisition of Chinese passive constraints; in the reverse direction, source anchoring dominates.
- Commercial NMT systems (Google, DeepL) score higher on BLEU/chrF++/COMET, while LLMs (GPT series) generate more diverse translation strategies, though not always human-like.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** MT models are directly "anchored" to the syntactic voice of the source sentence, overriding the statistical and pragmatic norms of the target language.
- **Mechanism:** The model's decoding process strongly conditions on explicit source-side syntax (the presence of a passive construction like *be* + V-en or *bei*). This creates a high prior for producing a target-side passive, even when the target language norm would dictate an active construction. The model's internal representation prioritizes literal syntactic transfer over learning the broader distributional patterns of voice in the target language.
- **Core assumption:** NMT architectures develop a brittle shortcut where source-side form heavily dictates target-side form, rather than learning the functional equivalence between source pragmatic intent and target linguistic realization.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "models are more influenced by the voice of the source text rather than the general voice usage of the source language, and therefore tend to maintain the passive voice when translating a passive in either direction."
  - Section 4.2, Tables 5 & 6: For EN→ZH, 43.2% (OPUS) and 40.3% (NLLB) of model translations used Chinese passives for *be* passive source, far exceeding the ~8.9% passive rate in original Chinese text. For ZH→EN, models produce English passives for Chinese *bei* sources at rates (~73.8%) similar to the 65% passive rate in the *source* English texts, not the lower rate in original Chinese.
  - The dataset construction identifies source-side passives first, making it a perfect probe for this "preservation" behavior.
- **Break condition:** Models would consistently produce active voice for English *be* passives when translating to Chinese for neutral/positive contexts, matching human translator distributions shown in Table 5. This is not observed.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Models possess partial, directionally asymmetric knowledge of Chinese passive constraints (low frequency, negative connotation), which improves voice consistency in EN→ZH translation but not in ZH→EN.
- **Mechanism:** The training data imbues models with a coarse-grained association: Chinese passive markers (*bei*, etc.) co-occur more frequently with negative semantics. When translating *from* English into Chinese, this association can sometimes override the "preserve source syntax" mechanism, leading to more human-like voice choices. When translating *from* Chinese into English, the target language (English) has no such strong constraint, so the preservation mechanism dominates unchecked.
- **Core assumption:** The model's knowledge of target-language distributional facts is fragile and context-dependent, only surfacing when the source language does not provide a conflicting strong syntactic signal.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "models demonstrate some knowledge of the low frequency and predominantly negative context of Chinese passives, leading to higher voice consistency with human translators in English-to-Chinese translation than in Chinese-to-English translation."
  - Section 4.2: For EN(be)→ZH, human passive rate is 8.9% (Table 5, target text), while models produce 26.7%/40.3%—still high due to source influence, but closer to human than the inverse direction. Figure 1 shows consistently higher voice consistency rates for all models in EN→ZH across registers.
  - Section 4.4, Example 2: A model (DeepL-N) uses a *bei* passive for a neutral/descriptive English sentence, ignoring the context where a human used an active *ba* resultative.
- **Break condition:** Models would show equally high voice consistency in both directions, or show low consistency in EN→ZH as well. The observed asymmetry is the key evidence for this partial, direction-sensitive knowledge.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Commercial NMT systems and LLMs differ in their exploration of the solution space for passive translation; commercial systems optimize for high metric scores through conservative, high-frequency choices, while LLMs exhibit greater lexical/structural diversity, sometimes at the cost of metric scores.
- **Mechanism:** Commercial NMT models are likely trained and tuned explicitly for high performance on automatic metrics (BLEU, chrF++) which reward lexical and n-gram overlap with reference translations. This incentivizes producing statistically safe, high-frequency translations. LLMs, trained on broader objectives (next-token prediction), generate from a distribution that can more easily sample lower-probability, diverse structures when prompted for translation, even if those structures are less frequent in the specific training parallel corpus.
- **Core assumption:** Metric optimization leads to mode collapse in the output distribution, suppressing diverse but valid linguistic strategies. LLM's decoding strategy (often higher temperature) and broader training data expose a wider range of possible translations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "Commercial NMT models scored higher in metric evaluations, but LLMs showed a better ability to use diverse alternative translations."
  - Section 4.3, Table 7: "Label div." and "Strategy div." columns; commercial NMT (Google, DeepL) show high diversity counts, but GPT-5 often matches or exceeds this. More telling is the type of diversity: DeepL-N used 19 different constructions for EN→ZH, and case studies show LLMs using *get* passives appropriately. The metric scores (BLEU/chrF++) are higher for commercial NMT (e.g., ZH→EN BLEU: Google 22.9 > GPT-5 15.5).
  - Section 4.4, Example 3: Human uses *get* passive ("get struck down") for dynamic negative event. GPT-5 uses "being overthrown" (neutral *be*). This shows LLMs *can* access diverse forms, but selection isn't always human-like. Commercial NMT may not even generate *get* as a candidate.
- **Break condition:** If commercial NMT models began to match LLMs on diversity metrics without sacrificing automatic metric scores, or if LLMs consistently outperformed NMT on both metrics and human-like strategy selection.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Passive Voice Disparity (Cross-Linguistic)
- **Why needed here:** The entire study hinges on the fundamental linguistic fact that passive constructions are not cross-linguistically equivalent in form, frequency, or pragmatics (English: neutral/frequent; Chinese: rare/negative). Without this, the observed MT behavior is just a neutral translation task.
- **Quick check question:** "If an English sentence with a neutral *be* passive (e.g., 'The report was published') is translated to Chinese, why would a human translator very likely avoid a *bei* passive structure?"

### Concept: Dependency Parsing & Automatic Strategy Annotation
- **Why needed here:** The dataset's scale (73k sentences) necessitated automatic annotation of translation strategies. Understanding how rules based on dependency labels map to linguistic categories is core to trusting the dataset's labels and the subsequent analysis.
- **Quick check question:** "For an English sentence annotated as a passive, what two dependency label conditions must be met according to the rules in Table 4?" (Conditions: auxiliary token with `auxpass` dependency and a main verb with `VBN` tag, often with an agent introduced by a `prep_by` phrase.)

### Concept: Automatic MT Evaluation Metrics vs. Fine-Grained Linguistic Evaluation
- **Why needed here:** The paper's key critique is that high BLEU/chrF++/COMET scores do not guarantee appropriate use of linguistic strategies. A model can get a high score by consistently outputting a single, frequent translation strategy that matches the reference's words but not its pragmatic intent.
- **Quick check question:** "Why might a model that perfectly translates 'The gas was compressed' as '气体被压缩' (using *bei*) score well on BLEU but still be considered a poor translation for a neutral scientific context?" (Because Chinese avoids *bei* for neutral/positive events; using it introduces an unintended negative connotation.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Acquisition & Filtering → Automatic Annotation Engine → Test Set Creation & Manual Validation → Model Inference Pipeline → Re-Annotation & Metric Computation → Analysis Layer

**Critical path:** Data Extraction → Rule Development & Pilot Annotation → Full Automatic Annotation → Manual Test Set Validation → Model Inference → Re-Annotation → Metric Calculation & Consistency Analysis. The validation step is the critical quality gate; errors here propagate to all conclusions.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Automatic vs. Manual Annotation:** Chose rules over full manual for scale, accepting some noise. Tradeoff: efficiency vs. label precision (addressed by test set).
- **Metric Choice:** Used standard corpus metrics and COMET. Tradeoff: these capture fluency/adequacy but not specific strategy appropriateness.
- **Model Selection:** Compared open-source, commercial NMT, and LLMs. Tradeoff: different architectures/training objectives make direct comparison noisy but reveal behavioral trends.
- **Dataset Balance:** Unbalanced (more *be* than *bei* passives) due to source corpora. Tradeoff: reflects real-world frequency but limits symmetric analysis.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Annotation Failure:** High `N/A` rate in model outputs for Chinese strategy labels indicates parsing failure or model outputting novel structures not in rule-set.
- **Metric-Strategy Mismatch:** High BLEU score but low voice/structure consistency (e.g., a model always using *bei* for EN→ZH would get decent BLEU if references sometimes used *bei*, but low consistency).
- **Directional Anomaly:** If EN→ZH consistency were lower than ZH→EN, it would contradict the core finding about partial knowledge of Chinese constraints.
- **Register Skew:** If Literature consistency were *high*, it would contradict the finding that literary texts are harder.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Dataset Sanity Check:** Run the annotation pipeline on the *source human translations* within the dataset. Verify that the strategy distributions match published linguistic facts (e.g., ~90% of Chinese originals are active, ~65% of English originals are passive).
2. **Baseline Model Test:** Translate the 450-sentence test set with a strong baseline (e.g., Helsinki-NLP). Re-annotate. Calculate its voice consistency. Expect: High passive preservation rate, lower structure consistency for EN→ZH, and lower consistency for Literature.
3. **Rule-Set Lim Probe:** Manually inspect 50 model outputs from the test set where the automatic annotation labeled the model output as `N/A` (active) for an EN→ZH *be* passive source. Determine if this is because: a) model used a non-rule structure, b) model made a genuine active translation error, or c) rule failed to catch a valid structure. This quantifies annotation error rate and reveals missed strategy categories.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the imbalance in passive sentence frequencies between Chinese and English in the dataset affect the generalizability of MT model performance evaluation?
- **Basis in paper:** The dataset contains 16,850 Chinese *bei* passives and 57,115 English *be* passives, reflecting the eightfold higher frequency of passives in English.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper evaluates models on this imbalanced data but does not analyze whether performance differences between directions are confounded by the raw count disparity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled experiment with a balanced subset (equal passive occurrences per language) comparing model consistency and strategy use across directions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why do standard automatic evaluation metrics fail to correlate with models' ability to use diverse, human-like translation strategies for passives?
- **Basis in paper:** The results show commercial NMT models score higher on metrics, while LLMs exhibit greater strategy diversity; authors state evaluation metrics cannot always reflect ability to make human-like choices.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies the dissociation but does not investigate which specific metric limitations cause this gap or how to design better metrics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A metric error analysis correlating human judgments of strategy adequacy with metric scores per sentence, identifying linguistic features that metrics currently fail to capture.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What specific linguistic or pragmatic knowledge gaps cause MT models to underuse alternative active structures (e.g., topic sentences, causatives) when translating English passives into Chinese, compared to human translators?
- **Basis in paper:** Table 5 shows models use far fewer topic sentences, causatives, and resultatives than human translations for EN(be)→ZH. Authors suggest models may struggle with paraphrasing an action into a statement and subject-object switch, but root cause is unknown.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether this stems from insufficient training data on these structures, lack of explicit pragmatic constraints, or architectural biases.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Fine-grained error analysis of model outputs where humans used diverse active structures, probing model representations to see if they recognize pragmatic triggers.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why is literary text the most challenging register for models to match human voice and structure choices in Chinese-to-English passive translation, and does this reflect broader difficulties with genre-specific translation norms?
- **Basis in paper:** Figure 1 shows the lowest consistency rates for literature in ZH(bei)→EN, with greatest discrepancy between voice and structure consistency; human translators use more diverse alternative translations for passives in literary texts.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze what makes literary Chinese passives distinct (e.g., stylistic ambiguity, classical constructions) or whether models lack exposure to such domain-specific transformations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A qualitative study of literary passive examples where models failed, categorizing errors by literary device and testing if fine-tuning on literary parallel data improves performance.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can large language models be effectively leveraged to automate and improve the annotation of translation strategies for passive structures, reducing reliance on error-prone rule-based systems?
- **Basis in paper:** Authors state future work will incorporate LLMs to better locate and categorize passive structures; current automatic annotation uses rule-based spaCy/LTP with acknowledged errors.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only uses LLMs as translation systems, not as annotators. It is unknown if LLMs can reliably distinguish subtle strategy categories.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An annotation experiment comparing LLM-based labeling against human annotations on the test set, measuring inter-annotator agreement and error patterns.

## Limitations
- Automatic strategy annotation relies on rule-based dependency matching, which may mislabel rare or novel constructions and impact analysis.
- Manual validation is limited to 450 sentences per direction, potentially missing distributional nuances across different registers.
- The dataset inherits frequency imbalances from source corpora (more English passives than Chinese), which could skew conclusions about model behavior.

## Confidence
- **Over-preservation of source voice:** High confidence (robust evidence across multiple models and registers).
- **Directional asymmetry in Chinese passive knowledge:** Medium confidence (observed pattern is clear but limited by test set size and possible annotation noise).
- **Commercial NMT vs. LLM diversity/metric trade-off:** Medium confidence (differences are evident but confounded by varying architectures and prompts).

## Next Checks
- **Manual re-annotation of N/A outputs:** Randomly sample 200 model outputs per direction labeled active by automatic rules; have experts determine true status to quantify misclassification rate.
- **Source-signal ablation experiment:** Fine-tune a baseline NMT model with a control token indicating source voice; measure if over-preservation is reduced, directly testing the anchoring hypothesis.
- **Cross-domain generalization test:** Evaluate models on a held-out passive-focused dataset from a different domain (e.g., biomedical) to see if observed patterns hold outside the original corpora.
