# DEAF: A Benchmark for Diagnostic Evaluation of Acoustic Faithfulness in Audio Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18048  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18048  
- Reference count: 11  
- Primary result: DEAF reveals pervasive text dominance in audio language models: Acoustic Robustness Score (ARS) falls to near zero under dual interference for emotion and background tasks, while speaker identity retains moderate robustness (34-44%), and all models show a gap between acoustic sensitivity and robustness.

## Executive Summary
The DEAF benchmark is introduced to diagnose whether audio language models genuinely use acoustic signals or default to textual cues. It constructs over 2,700 conflict stimuli across three acoustic dimensions (emotion, background, speaker identity) with controlled semantic–acoustic mismatches. The evaluation framework employs three levels of textual interference: conflict audio only (L1), neutral audio with a misleading textual prompt (L2), and conflict audio with a reinforcing prompt (L3). Models are assessed zero-shot via open-ended responses judged by an LLM, and metrics such as Acoustic Robustness Score (ARS) combine accuracy and acoustic sensitivity. Testing on seven state-of-the-art models uncovers a systematic bias toward text: under dual interference (L3), ARS for emotion and background collapses to near zero, while speaker identity shows modest robustness (34-44%). All models exhibit high acoustic sensitivity (detecting the acoustic flip) but low robustness (acting on it), indicating they perceive acoustic changes but disregard them in favor of textual evidence. GPT-4o-Audio performs worst, with Qwen3-Omni and Gemini 2.5 Flash relatively stronger yet still vulnerable to combined pressure.

## Method Summary
DEAF creates conflict audio by manipulating acoustic properties while preserving or altering textual content. For emotion (ESC), 104 sentences are synthesized with four prosodic styles using TTS, yielding matched and mismatched emotion–text pairs. For background (BSC), neutral TTS speech is mixed with environmental noises at five signal-to-noise ratios, generating within- and cross-scene conflicts. For speaker identity (SIC), 82 sentences are spoken with four voice profiles, introducing gender/age mismatches. For each stimulus, models receive a textual query (e.g., “What emotion does the speaker express?”) under three interference conditions: L1 uses only the conflict audio; L2 presents a separate neutral audio and a misleading prompt that describes the opposite acoustic property; L3 combines the conflict audio with a prompt reinforcing the wrong acoustic label. Open-ended responses are collected from each model and classified by an LLM judge (DeepSeek-R1) into “Correct”, “Trap”, or “Other”. Primary metrics are Acoustic Robustness Score (ARS = harmonic mean of Accuracy and Acoustic Sensitivity Score) and Acoustic Sensitivity Score (ASS), with fine-grained background discrimination measured by EDI. Evaluation is conducted zero-shot across seven audio MLLMs, with audio resampled to 16 kHz mono and results averaged over three runs.

## Key Results
- Under dual interference (L3), ARS for emotion and background collapses to near zero, while speaker identity maintains moderate robustness (34-44%).
- All models exhibit a gap: acoustic sensitivity (ASS) is high, but robustness (ARS) is low, showing they detect acoustic flips yet ignore them in favor of textual cues.
- Model rankings: GPT-4o-Audio shows extreme text dominance; Qwen3-Omni and Gemini 2.5 Flash perform relatively better but still fail under combined pressure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DEAF effectively diagnoses acoustic faithfulness because it isolates acoustic evidence from textual evidence through carefully constructed conflicts and then systematically escalates textual pressure. The three-level design allows separating models that merely detect acoustic changes (shown by performance at L1) from those that consistently ground their reasoning in acoustics even when contradicted by text (shown by ARS at L2/L3). The use of an LLM-as-judge for open-ended responses captures nuanced reasoning beyond forced-choice, while the ARS metric directly quantifies robustness by combining correctness with sensitivity to the acoustic flip. By varying the acoustic dimension (emotion, background, speaker), the benchmark also reveals which types of acoustic information are more resistant to text dominance.

## Foundational Learning
**Acoustic–Semantic Conflict Generation**  
- *Why needed*: To create stimuli where the acoustic signal and textual content (either in audio or prompt) provide opposing cues, enabling a clean test of modality reliance.  
- *Quick check*: Verify that an audio labeled “happy” is spoken with angry prosody; the speech transcript may remain “I am happy”.

**Textual Interference Levels (L1–L3)**  
- *Why needed*: To gradually increase textual influence and observe at which point models switch from acoustic to textual reasoning.  
- *Quick check*: At L2, a neutral audio is described with a misleading prompt; confirm the model follows the prompt instead of the silent audio.

**Acoustic Robustness Score (ARS)**  
- *Why needed*: A single metric that captures both accuracy and adherence to acoustic evidence, distinguishing models that are accurate only when cues align from those that remain acoustic-grounded under conflict.  
- *Quick check*: A model that always answers correctly when acoustic/text align but flips when conflict exists will have high ASS but low ARS.

**LLM-as-Judge Evaluation**  
- *Why needed*: To scale classification of open-ended model outputs into correct/trap/other without manual annotation.  
- *Quick check*: Use DeepSeek‑R1 to label a few known responses; ensure it matches human labels.

**Sensitivity–Robustness Gap**  
- *Why needed*: To highlight that models may internally sense acoustic changes (high ASS) yet fail to incorporate that perception into reasoning (low ARS).  
- *Quick check*: Compare ASS and ARS across levels; a large gap indicates distrust of acoustics.

**Zero-Shot Assessment**  
- *Why needed*: To evaluate inherent capabilities of models without task-specific fine‑tuning, reflecting real‑world deployment.  
- *Quick check*: Run models without any exemplars; ensure no adaptation to DEAF format.

## Architecture Onboarding
The DEAF evaluation pipeline consists of the following components connected in sequence:

Stimulus Generator -> Audio Preprocessor -> Model Inference (API/Local) -> Response Collector -> LLM Judge -> Metrics Aggregator

**Critical Path**: Generating conflict stimuli → preparing audio at 16 kHz mono → feeding audio and prompt to model → capturing open-ended response → classifying via LLM judge → computing ARS/ASS/EDI.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- *Stimulus recreation*: proprietary TTS (ElevenLabs) yields high quality but limits reproducibility; open‑source alternatives increase accessibility but may affect acoustic properties.
- *Model access*: API models provide convenience but risk version drift; local models ensure control but require hardware.
- *LLM judge*: automated scoring scales but may inherit biases; human validation improves reliability but is costly.
- *Open‑ended vs forced‑choice*: open‑ended reveals natural reasoning but introduces noise; forced‑choice gives cleaner metrics but may not reflect real usage.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Matched‑condition accuracy significantly below 100% indicates stimulus or inference issues.
- LLM judge shows low agreement (κ < 0.8) with human labels on a validation set.
- ARS values deviate by >5% from reported numbers after controlling for model version.
- Large variance across three runs suggests nondeterministic inference (e.g., temperature > 0).

**First Experiments**:
1. **Sanity Check on Matched Stimuli**: Run all models on a small set of acoustically and textually aligned examples (e.g., ESC happy audio with happy label). Expected: accuracy >95%. Failure suggests pipeline errors.
2. **Judge Validation**: Sample 100 responses across conflict types, annotate manually, compute Cohen’s κ with DeepSeek‑R1. If κ < 0.85, iterate on judge prompt or consider majority vote over multiple LLMs.
3. **Text Interference Gradient**: Evaluate a representative model (e.g., Qwen2‑Audio) across L1, L2, L3 for one acoustic dimension (emotion). Expected pattern: high accuracy at L1, drop at L2, near‑zero ARS at L3. Deviations may indicate incorrect stimulus generation or prompt formatting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can pretraining objectives or architectural modifications to audio encoders (e.g., paralinguistic-focused contrastive losses) close the perception–trust gap, where models sense acoustic variation but fail to act on it?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors conclude: "Future work should investigate whether paralinguistic pretraining objectives, alternative audio encoder architectures, or inference-time grounding mechanisms can close the gap between acoustic perception and acoustic-grounded reasoning."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper diagnoses the gap via metrics (ASS vs. ARS) but does not test any interventions. No ablations on encoder pretraining or architecture are conducted.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Train or fine-tune models with specific paralinguistic objectives (e.g., predicting emotion/speaker traits from acoustics alone) and re-evaluate on DEAF; compare ARS improvements against baseline models.

#### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What architectural or training factors cause extreme text dominance (e.g., GPT-4o-Audio’s near-zero ARS) vs. relative robustness (e.g., Qwen3-Omni)? Is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) amplifying sycophancy?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors note: "GPT-4o-Audio presents an extreme case... consistent with strong RLHF-induced sycophancy" and "Gemini 3 Flash underperforms its predecessor... may reflect an alignment tax."
- **Why unresolved**: These are speculative correlations based on model lineage; no controlled experiments isolate the impact of alignment training, instruction tuning, or encoder design.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Ablate alignment/instruction-tuning steps on open-source models; compare attention distributions or acoustic feature utilization using probes or intervention studies.

#### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Does text dominance generalize to languages with different lexical–prosodic mappings or to acoustic dimensions beyond the three tested (emotion, background, speaker identity), such as temporal structure or multi-speaker interactions?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] DEAF covers only English TTS and three acoustic dimensions; the limitations state: "it covers only a limited set of audio phenomena... leaving out... temporal reasoning, multi-speaker interaction, and complex acoustic scenes."
- **Why unresolved**: DEAF’s design is language- and dimension-specific; no experiments test cross-linguistic or broader acoustic validity.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extend DEAF to multilingual stimuli and additional conflict types (e.g., tempo vs. semantic pacing, overlapping speakers); evaluate whether the same text-dominance patterns hold.

#### Open Question 4
- **Question**: How does reliance on synthetically generated conflict stimuli (TTS + noise mixing) affect conclusions about real-world model behavior? Would natural, human-produced incongruent speech yield the same text-dominance effects?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Limitations: "most stimuli are generated through TTS and controlled audio synthesis pipelines... may not fully capture the variability and noise characteristics of real-world speech."
- **Why unresolved**: All conflict samples are synthetic; natural speech has more variability in prosody, recording conditions, and semantic–acoustic incongruence that could interact differently with models.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Collect and evaluate a dataset of real human speech with natural acoustic–semantic conflicts (e.g., sarcastic conversations, background noise in field recordings) and compare ARS patterns to DEAF.

#### Open Question 5
- **Question**: To what extent does the LLM-as-judge evaluation protocol introduce bias in measuring acoustic robustness, and how do model responses to open-ended questions versus constrained multiple-choice formats differ?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors acknowledge: "The evaluation also relies on an LLM-as-Judge protocol... may introduce bias in ambiguous cases without large-scale human verification." All evaluations are open-ended.
- **Why unresolved**: No human evaluation is provided to validate the LLM judge; open-ended responses might allow models to hedge or refuse in ways that bias the trap/correct/other classification.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct human evaluation on a subset of responses to measure agreement with the LLM judge; run parallel experiments with forced-choice (multiple-choice) questions to see if text dominance patterns persist under more constrained output.

## Limitations
- Dataset unreleased → reconstruction fidelity uncertain; exact TTS voices, SNR levels, and sentence scripts are not publicly available.
- LLM-as-judge evaluation lacks human validation; potential bias in response classification could affect ARS/ASS rankings.
- Model version drift (especially for API-based models) and ambiguous open-source checkpoints may lead to inconsistencies with reported numbers.

## Confidence
- Text dominance pervasive: Medium
- ARS collapse for emotion/background: Medium-Low
- Speaker robustness (34-44%): Medium
- Sensitivity-robustness gap: Low
- Model rankings (GPT-4o-Audio weakest): Medium

## Next Checks
1. Pilot reconstruction: Create 200 conflict clips (ESC/BSC) using open-source TTS and DEMAND; verify baseline model accuracy on matched audio exceeds 95%.
2. Judge validation: Sample 200 model responses, obtain human correctness labels, compute Cohen’s κ with DeepSeek‑R1 classifications; if κ < 0.85, refine judge prompt or adopt an ensemble of LLMs.
3. Version audit: Log exact model IDs/endpoints and run a 10% stratified sample; recompute ARS and compare with paper; if discrepancy >5%, note version drift and adjust interpretation.
