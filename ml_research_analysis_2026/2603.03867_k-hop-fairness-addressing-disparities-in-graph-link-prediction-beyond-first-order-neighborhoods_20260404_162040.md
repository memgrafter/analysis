# k-hop Fairness: Addressing Disparities in Graph Link Prediction Beyond First-Order Neighborhoods
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03867  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03867  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Introduces k-hop fairness to assess disparities within sensitive groups' neighborhoods at multiple graph distances, revealing that standard LP models reproduce structural biases across hops and proposing effective post-processing mitigation.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses fairness in graph link prediction, arguing that dyadic fairness—which only considers inter-group edges—fails to capture disparities within sensitive groups' own neighborhoods. It introduces k-hop fairness, conditioning fairness assessment on graph distance to measure exposure and bias at each hop. Key contributions include formal definitions of k-hop group exposure φ(k), fairness gap NF(k), and structural bias NB(k), along with pre-processing (graph rewiring) and post-processing (prediction adjustment) mitigation methods. Experiments on real-world graphs reveal that standard LP models exhibit biases at multiple hops, that mitigating bias at one hop affects others due to topological interdependence, and that the proposed post-processing can significantly reduce targeted NF(k) with minimal AUC trade-offs while leaving dyadic fairness metrics largely unaffected.

## Method Summary
The methodology extends fairness evaluation beyond dyadic interactions to k-hop neighborhoods. For a given hop distance k, group exposure φ(k) averages the proportion of links from nodes in a sensitive group to other groups within exactly k steps. The fairness gap NF(k) measures deviation from equal exposure, while structural bias NB(k) captures inherent topological homophily at that scale. Mitigation includes pre-processing: iteratively adding edges that most reduce NB(k) via differentiable approximations of k-hop indicators. Post-processing adjusts link prediction probabilities only for node pairs at exact distance k in the training graph, using a masked optimization that leaves other hops unaffected. Base models (Node2Vec, GCN, GraphSAGE) are trained on standard link prediction tasks; evaluation compares NF(k) reductions and AUC across methods and existing fair LP baselines.

## Key Results
- Standard LP models reproduce structural biases at multiple k-hop distances, with highest bias often at k=1 but significant at k=2 and k=3 as well.
- Pre-processing edge addition shows interdependent effects: reducing NB(k) at one hop correlates positively or negatively with changes at other hops, indicating dataset-specific propagation.
- Post-processing achieves substantial NF(k) reductions for targeted hops with controlled AUC trade-offs; notably, at k=1 there is no performance loss, at k=2 modest trade-offs, and dyadic fairness metrics (ΔDP, ΔEO) remain largely unchanged.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Dyadic fairness obscures segregation by focusing on edge types without considering *where* inter-group edges occur in the graph, potentially reinforcing disparities within groups.  
**Mechanism:** Dyadic fairness aggregates over all node pairs without conditioning on graph distance ω(k)(v). This weights contributions by structural centrality, letting well-connected bridge nodes dominate the metric while disconnected nodes remain invisible. The k-hop framework instead groups contributions by exact distance k and averages equally over nodes in V(k)_s, ensuring each node’s neighborhood diversity counts equally.  
**Core assumption:** Fairness should protect information access for all nodes in a sensitive group, not just increase overall inter-group edge counts that may benefit already-privileged nodes.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “dyadic fairness overlooks potential disparities within the sensitive groups themselves”  
- [section 3.1] Figure 1 example showing dyadic fairness promoting inter-group edges within diverse clusters while leaving segregated clusters untouched  
- [corpus] Strong neighbor FMR (0.48–0.59) on TopoFair and Breaking the Dyadic Barrier, showing active research on topological bias in LP fairness  
**Break condition:** If all nodes in a group have similar connectivity patterns (no central bridges), the ω(k)(v) weighting effect vanishes and dyadic metrics align with k-hop fairness.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Structural biases at different k-hops are interdependent; mitigating NB(k) at one hop propagates to other hops, either reinforcing or compensating changes in those scales.  
**Mechanism:** When edges are added/removed during rewiring, the shortest-path distances between many node pairs shift simultaneously. An edge that shortens paths for k=3 may also create new 2-hop connections or eliminate 4-hop ones. The correlation measurements (Figure 3) show these dependencies are dataset-specific and can be positive or negative.  
**Core assumption:** Graph topology changes affect multiple neighborhood scales non-locally because shortest-path distances are globally coupled.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 4.2] Edge addition process that tracks NB(k') for k' ≠ targeted k  
- [section 6.2] Figure 3 heatmaps and correlation coefficients demonstrating significant interdependence across hops  
- [corpus] Networked Inequality paper explores GNN link prediction biases, implying structural effects cascade through network  
**Break condition:** In graphs where neighborhoods are perfectly modular (disconnected components), changing edges within one component affects only that component’s internal distances, decoupling hops across components.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Post-processing mitigation can target NF(k) independently for each k because it modifies only predictions for node pairs at exact distance k in the *training* graph, which are disjoint sets for different k.  
**Mechanism:** The post-processing loss ℓ(k)_post uses mask ˜A(k) to apply adjustments U only to k-hop pairs. Since a node pair (u,v) at distance k in the training graph does not belong to any ˜A(k') for k' ≠ k, optimizing NF(k) leaves NF(k') completely unchanged. This contrasts with pre-processing where changing the graph structure affects all ˜A(k') simultaneously.  
**Core assumption:** The training graph’s distance structure is fixed during post-processing; we only adjust probabilities for pairs already at distance k, not altering which pairs are considered k-hop.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 6.3] “By construction, node pairs at exact distance k in the training graph do not contribute to NF(k′) for k′ ≠ k, enabling controlled and modular trade-offs across multiple hops.”  
- [section 4.2] “ ˜A(k) is independent of optimization variable U”  
- [corpus] FairACE and FairMILE target degree fairness locally but not across multiple hops; this work gives formal guarantee of independence.  
**Break condition:** If the training graph is very sparse such that many node pairs are not connected at any meaningful k, then some NF(k) may be undefined or trivial, reducing the need for independent targeting.

## Foundational Learning
- **k-hop neighborhood and graph distance**  
  *Why needed:* The entire k-hop fairness framework conditions on σ(v,v′)=k. Understanding how neighborhoods expand with k is fundamental to interpreting NF(k) and NB(k) values.  
  *Quick check:* For a given node v, does V(k)_s include nodes that are reachable by *exactly* k shortest-path steps? What happens if v has no k-hop neighbors?
  
- **Assortativity and homophily**  
  *Why needed:* Homophily is the structural bias source. NB(k) quantifies assortative mixing *at specific distances*, extending global assortativity to a multi-scale view.  
  *Quick check:* If assortativity a is low (heterophily), would you expect NB(1) to be low? What about NB(2)?
  
- **Dyadic fairness metrics (DP, EO)**  
  *Why needed:* These are the baselines that k-hop fairness critiques and extends. Understanding their edge-centric aggregation is necessary to see what k-hop fixes.  
  *Quick check:* In the dyadic ∆DP definition, what do S and S′ represent? How does this differ from k-hop group exposure ϕ(k)?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Distance calculator -> Metric engine -> Pre-processor/Post-processor (two separate paths)

### Critical path
1. Compute all ˜A(k) for k in K (set of hops where ≥50% nodes have non-empty neighborhood)
2. Evaluate baseline NB(k) on raw graph → choose target k’s (top 3 biased)
3. For pre-processing: run edge-addition or rewiring optimization with ℓ(k)_pre
4. For post-processing: train base LP model → compute P → run optimization with ℓ(k)_post
5. Evaluate NF(k) on test edges and compute AUC

### Design tradeoffs
- Pre-processing affects all downstream models but requires differentiable k-hop computation (matrix powers), which is O(k n²) per iteration and may not scale to huge graphs.
- Post-processing is model-agnostic and cheap to apply after training, but only adjusts predictions for pairs present in training graph’s k-hop matrix; cannot fix bias for k where training graph sparse.
- α regularization: higher α preserves AUC but reduces fairness gain; need grid search per dataset/k.

### Failure signatures
- Pre-processing: NB(k) does not decrease after many iterations → edge addition heuristic stuck in local minima.
- Post-processing: NF(k) reduction minimal even with high α → training graph’s k-hop set too small to adjust meaningfully; or α too high.
- High variance across random seeds: indicates unstable optimization or training split sensitivity; increase α or reduce learning rate.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline correlation:** For each dataset, compute NB(k) and NF(k) for 3 standard LP models (N2V, GCN, SAGE). Verify hops with highest NB(k) have highest NF(k) gap.
2. **Pre-processing interdependence:** For each target k, run edge addition (α=0) for 100 iterations, record NB(k') for all k'. Compute Pearson correlation between NB(k) trajectory and each NB(k'). Expect both positive and negative correlations.
3. **Post-processing trade-off curves:** For each target k, run post-processing with α in [1e-6, 1e-2] for base model. Plot AUC vs NF(k). Check that k=1 gives no AUC loss (since test edges are 1-hop?), k=2 shows modest trade-off, higher k sometimes improves AUC slightly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability of exact distances:** How can the k-hop fairness framework be extended to large-scale graphs where exact shortest-path distances are computationally prohibitive? The paper notes reliance on exact distances limits scalability and suggests approximate estimators but leaves this uninvestigated.
2. **Incorporating node features:** How can node feature information be incorporated into the k-hop fairness definitions and mitigation strategies to move beyond purely structural considerations? The approach is intentionally structural, whereas real-world graphs often include node features, suggesting a complementary direction.
3. **Joint multi-hop pre-processing:** How can we design pre-processing graph modification methods that simultaneously reduce structural bias at multiple k-hop distances, avoiding adverse side-effects where reducing bias at one hop increases it at another? Pre-processing experiments targeted one hop at a time and showed unpredictable inter-hop dependencies, lacking a systematic multi-objective approach.
4. **Theoretical basis for inter-hop correlations:** What underlying graph properties determine the direction (reinforcing vs. compensatory) and magnitude of inter-hop correlations in structural bias when the graph is modified? The paper reports strong correlations but provides no theoretical explanation for why some modifications propagate positively while others have opposite effects.

## Limitations
- Results on only 5 small/medium graphs; scalability to large networks unclear.
- Post-processing relies on fixed training-graph distances; hops with sparse training representation may be unaddressed.
- Nodes without k-hop neighbors are excluded (≥50% rule), potentially ignoring isolated individuals.
- Key hyperparameters (β for smoothing, α for trade-off, iteration count) are not systematically justified.

## Confidence
- Dyadic fairness masks within-group gaps: **Medium**
- Bias interdependence across hops: **High**
- Post-processing independence: **High**
- NF(k) reduction with AUC trade-off: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Scalability test:** Apply methods to a >100k-node graph; measure time/memory and check if interdependence heatmaps (Fig. 3) hold.
2. **Joint multi-hop optimization:** Extend post-processing to minimize a combined loss (e.g., ∑_{k∈K} NF(k)) and evaluate AUC/fairness trade-offs compared to single-hop.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** For each dataset/target k, perform a dense grid search over α (20 values) and β (5 values); report stability of NF(k) reduction and AUC loss.
