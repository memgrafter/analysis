# On the Rate of Convergence of GD in Non-linear Neural Networks: An Adversarial Robustness Perspective

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.02095
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02095
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Gradient Descent on a minimal two-neuron ReLU network, after neuron specialization, converges to the optimal robustness margin at a slow rate of Θ(1/ln(t)), a fundamental bottleneck for non-linear models.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the convergence rate of Gradient Descent (GD) when training a depth-2 width-2 ReLU network for adversarial robustness. Using a two-point dataset, the authors show that while gradient flow converges quickly to the optimal robustness margin, GD's discrete dynamics face an intrinsic slowdown after neuron specialization. Specifically, once the network's neurons specialize to different data points, the decision boundary approaches the optimum at a rate of Θ(1/ln(t)). This logarithmic rate is proven tight and holds for almost all initializations under small step sizes. Empirical simulations confirm the theory and often reveal even slower practical rates, indicating a severe computational barrier to achieving robustness via standard GD.

## Method Summary
The study focuses on a minimal two-neuron ReLU network with exponential loss, analyzing GD dynamics on a synthetic two-point dataset. The authors first establish that gradient flow (continuous-time GD) converges to the max-margin solution, implying robustness. They then examine discrete GD, proving that once the loss drops below 0.5, neurons specialize: each becomes active on only one data point. This specialization induces an invariant activation pattern, simplifying the dynamics to explicit update rules for weights and biases. By solving these recursions, they derive that the decision boundary's distance to the optimum decays as 1/ln(t). The analysis relies on small step sizes and shows that the rate is tight and initialization-dependent. Simulations on random initializations (including He initialization) validate the asymptotic rate and highlight that a non-negligible fraction of runs suffer from large constants, making convergence impractically slow.

## Key Results
- After neuron specialization, GD's convergence rate for the robustness margin is Θ(1/ln(t)), fundamentally slower than polynomial or exponential rates.
- The rate is tight and holds for almost all initializations; the constant in the 1/ln(t) decay depends on the initial weight sum and can be large (e.g., >10% of runs have |b₂−b₁|>1 under He initialization).
- Empirical simulations often show even worse rates due to finite-time effects, confirming a severe computational barrier for robustness in non-linear networks via basic GD.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
We present the core mechanisms behind the slow convergence after specialization.

**Mechanism 1: Imbalanced updates due to neuron specialization.**  
After loss L(θ)<0.5, neuron 1 specializes on x₂ and neuron 2 on x₁. Their update rules involve distinct exponential terms: w₁ and b₁ update via exp(−w₁−b₁), while w₂ and b₂ update via exp(w₂−b₂). This causes w₁ to increase logarithmically and w₂ to decrease logarithmically, but the difference w₁−w₂ grows only as ln(t) while the bias difference b₂−b₁ converges to a constant. Since the decision boundary x⋆ = (b₂−b₁)/(w₁−w₂), its convergence rate is Θ(1/ln(t)).

**Mechanism 2: Invariant activation pattern locks GD into a slow manifold.**  
Once neurons specialize (their breakpoints β₁∈(−1,1), β₂∈(−1,1)), the ReLU subgradients become fixed (0 or 1) for all subsequent iterations under small step size. This makes the GD update direction constant, preventing escape from the basin. The weights and biases evolve monotically, but the imbalance from initialization persists, causing the slow logarithmic approach.

**Mechanism 3: Equilibrium condition yields logarithmic decay.**  
GD reaches an equilibrium where b₂−b₁ and w₁+w₂ converge to the same constant ((v₁(0)+u₂(0))/2). Meanwhile, w₁∼(1/2)ln(t) and w₂∼−(1/2)ln(t), so w₁−w₂ = Θ(ln(t)). Hence x⋆(t) = (b₂−b₁)/(w₁−w₂) ∼ constant / ln(t). The numerator's fast convergence and the denominator's logarithmic growth together produce the tight rate.

The analysis assumes L(θ)<0.5 (ensuring correct classification and specialization) and a small step size to maintain the activation pattern. If the pattern changes (e.g., a neuron deactivates), the dynamics may shift, but under the assumptions specialization is inevitable and invariant.

## Foundational Learning
These concepts underpin the paper's analysis.

- **Gradient Descent (GD)**  
  *Why needed*: The paper analyzes discrete-time GD updates, contrasting with gradient flow; the update rule determines the dynamics.  
  *Quick check*: Write the GD update for parameter θ with loss L(θ) and step size η: θ_{t+1} = θ_t - η ∇L(θ_t).

- **ReLU Activation and Subgradients**  
  *Why needed*: ReLU non-linearity creates piecewise-linear dynamics; subgradients (0 or 1) dictate which terms appear in the gradient and thus the update rules.  
  *Quick check*: For neuron output [w·x+b]+, what is the subgradient w.r.t. w when w·x+b > 0, =0, <0? (Answer: 1 if >0, 0 if ≤0, undefined at 0 but typically 0 or 1.)

- **Adversarial Robustness Margin**  
  *Why needed*: The paper's goal is to maximize the robustness margin γ(θ), the minimum distance from data points to the decision boundary; convergence analysis targets this quantity.  
  *Quick check*: For the two-point dataset, how is γ(θ) defined? (Answer: γ(θ) = min_i |Φ(θ;x_i)| / ||∇_x Φ(θ;x_i)||, but in this 1D setting with linear pieces, it reduces to the distance from each x_i to the decision boundary x⋆(θ).)

- **Implicit Bias**  
  *Why needed*: GD on homogeneous networks often exhibits implicit bias toward certain solutions (e.g., max-margin); understanding GF's bias (Theorem 2.2) sets the expectation that GD should also converge to robust solutions, albeit slowly.  
  *Quick check*: What does Theorem 2.2 say? (Answer: Gradient flow on homogeneous networks with exponential loss converges to the max-margin solution in the normalized parameter space.)

- **Neuron Specialization**  
  *Why needed*: Specialization is the phase where each neuron becomes responsible for one data point, triggering the simplified dynamics that lead to the logarithmic rate.  
  *Quick check*: What condition on L(θ) ensures neurons have specialized? (Answer: L(θ) < 0.5 ensures correct classification and that each neuron is active on exactly one data point; see Lemma B.2.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (plain text chain):
Data (x₁=-1,y₁=-1; x₂=1,y₂=1) -> Model: two-neuron ReLU network Φ(θ;x)=[w₁x+b₁]+ - [w₂x+b₂]+ -> Loss: L(θ)=½ exp(Φ(θ;-1)) + ½ exp(-Φ(θ;1)) -> Optimizer: GD with step size η -> Dynamics: after specialization, updates follow Eqs (9-12); weights w₁↑, w₂↓, biases stabilize -> Decision boundary x⋆(t) = (b₂−b₁)/(w₁−w₂) -> Convergence rate Θ(1/ln(t)).

**Critical path**:
1. Initialize θ(0) (e.g., He: w₁,w₂~N(0,2), b₁=b₂=0).
2. Run GD until L(θ)<0.5 (correct classification).
3. Neurons specialize: neuron 1 active near x₂ (β₁∈(-1,1)), neuron 2 active near x₁ (β₂∈(-1,1)).
4. Activation pattern becomes invariant; GD updates simplify to monotonic recursions.
5. Equilibrium reached: b₂−b₁ → (w₁(0)+w₂(0))/2, w₁+w₂ → (v₁(0)+u₂(0))/2.
6. Decision boundary x⋆(t) → 0 at rate Θ(1/ln(t)).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Step size η: Smaller η maintains the invariant activation pattern but requires more iterations to reach specialization; η>0.5 may break monotonicity and cause deactivations.
- Initialization: He initialization often yields small |w₁(0)+w₂(0)|? Actually, the constant in the numerator depends on v₁(0)+u₂(0) which can be large; large initial imbalance worsens the constant factor in the 1/ln(t) decay.
- Loss choice: Exponential loss yields clean analytical forms; other losses (e.g., logistic) may alter constants but likely preserve logarithmic rate.

**Failure signatures**:
- Margin error γ∗−γ(θ(t)) decays as 1/ln(t), not polynomially/exponentially.
- Under He initialization, with probability ~10%, |b₂−b₁| exceeds 1 at equilibrium, requiring >10⁴ iterations for modest margin improvement.
- Empirically, rates can be worse than 1/ln(t) due to transient non-monotonicities not captured in asymptotic analysis.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Reproduce Theorem 5.1**: For random initializations satisfying L(θ(0))<0.5, run GD to 10⁶ steps. After t=10⁴, plot γ∗−γ(t) vs t on log-log and fit to C/ln(t); expect high R².
2. **Test Corollary 5.3**: Using He initialization (w₁,w₂~N(0,2), b₁=b₂=0), run 10,000 GD trials (η=0.05). Compute limₜ |b₂−b₁| after stabilization. Compare histogram to theoretical PDF (Eq 15); verify ~10% of runs have |b₂−b₁|>1.
3. **Compare GD vs GF**: On same initialization, simulate gradient flow (ODE solver) and GD. Plot x⋆(t) vs iterations/time. GF should converge exponentially fast, while GD shows slow logarithmic decay.

## Limitations
- Analysis restricted to a minimal two-neuron ReLU network with exponential loss on two data points; generalization to deeper/wider networks is not proven.
- Assumes small step size and persistent neuron specialization; if activation pattern changes, the derived rate may not hold.
- The constant factor in the 1/ln(t) rate depends on initialization and can be large in practice, and empirical rates may be even slower due to finite-time effects.

## Confidence
- **Rate tightness (Θ(1/ln(t)))**: High  
- **Universality over initializations (except measure-zero set)**: High  
- **Empirical confirmation of the rate**: Medium (simulations described but not independently verified)  
- **Generalization to broader non-linear networks**: Low

3. **Width scaling**: Train a 3-neuron ReLU network (same output structure) on the two-point data. Measure decision boundary x⋆(t) convergence. Compare decay to 1/ln(t); if still logarithmic, it suggests the phenomenon is not width-specific.
