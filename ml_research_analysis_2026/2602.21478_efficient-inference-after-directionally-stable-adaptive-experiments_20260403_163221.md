# Efficient Inference after Directionally Stable Adaptive Experiments

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21478
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21478
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Directional stability—a target‑specific condition—makes the canonical gradient a martingale with stabilized quadratic variation, enabling asymptotic normality and semiparametric efficiency for the one‑step estimator without propensity weighting; verified for LinUCB.

## Executive Summary
Adaptive data collection (e.g., contextual bandits) invalidates classical inference because the sampling distribution depends on past observations. Existing stability methods demand full‑matrix stabilization of the empirical design, which is unrealistic for regret‑minimizing policies that explore anisotropically. This paper introduces *directional stability*, a much weaker requirement that only the design along the direction of the scalar estimand must stabilize. Under this condition, the canonical gradient becomes a martingale difference sequence whose predictable quadratic variation converges, yielding a martingale central limit theorem. Consequently, the one‑step estimator—constructed by plugging in consistent estimates of the outcome model and Riesz representer—achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound without any modification (e.g., propensity weighting). The authors verify directional stability for the LinUCB algorithm under a sufficiently large exploration bonus and sub‑linear feature dimension, providing the first efficiency guarantee for a regular scalar target under this standard bandit algorithm.

## Method Summary
We consider a linear reward model \(Y_t = \phi_t^\top \beta_0 + \varepsilon_t\) with homoskedastic noise \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_t|Z_t)=\sigma^2\) and target \(\Psi = \nu^\top \beta_0\). Given an adaptive policy (LinUCB) that collects \(T\) samples, computation proceeds:

1. Precompute the empirical design \(\widehat{\Sigma}_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \phi_t\phi_t^\top\) and cross‑covariance \(\widehat{\Sigma}_{T,ZY} = \frac{1}{T}\sum \phi_t Y_t\).
2. Estimate the outcome model via ridge‑regularized least squares: \(\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda_h} = (\widehat{\Sigma}_T + \lambda_h I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}_{T,ZY}\), \(\widehat{h}(z)=\phi(z)^\top \widehat{\beta}_{\lambda_h}\).
3. Estimate the Riesz representer for the linear functional: \(\widehat{\alpha}_{\lambda_\alpha}(z) = \nu^\top (\widehat{\Sigma}_T + \lambda_\alpha I)^{-1}\phi(z)\).
4. Form the one‑step estimator
   \[
   \widehat{\Psi} = \nu^\top \widehat{\beta}_{\lambda_h} + \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \widehat{\alpha}_{\lambda_\alpha}(Z_t)\bigl(Y_t - \widehat{h}(Z_t)\bigr).
   \]
5. Estimate the asymptotic variance as \(\widehat{\sigma}^2 \cdot \nu^\top (\widehat{\Sigma}_T+\lambda_\alpha I)^{-2}\nu / T\) (with \(\widehat{\sigma}^2\) from residuals) to construct confidence intervals.

The key insight: directional stability—stabilization of the empirical design only along the \(\nu\)-direction—suffices for the martingale CLT and thus for asymptotic normality and efficiency of \(\widehat{\Psi}\).

## Key Results
- **Directional stability** replaces full‑matrix stabilization, enabling inference for anisotropic exploration strategies like LinUCB.
- The **canonical gradient** \(D^*_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \alpha_T(Z_t)(Y_t - h_T(Z_t))\) is a martingale; under directional stabilization its predictable quadratic variation converges, yielding asymptotic normality.
- The **one‑step estimator** attains the semiparametric efficiency bound; no explicit correction for adaptivity (e.g., propensity weighting) is required. This is the first efficiency guarantee for LinUCB under a regular scalar target.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** The canonical gradient is a martingale difference sequence. Directional stability (Definition 1) ensures that the empirical design’s quadratic form along the target direction converges to a deterministic limit: \(\| \alpha_T - \widetilde{\alpha}_T \|_{L_2(P_T)} \to 0\), where \(\widetilde{\alpha}_T(z) = \nu^\top \widetilde{\Sigma}_T^{-1}\phi(z)\) and \(\widetilde{\Sigma}_T\) is a stabilizing matrix. This convergence implies that the predictable quadratic variation \(\langle D^* \rangle_T\) stabilizes. Together with a Lindeberg condition, a martingale central limit theorem gives \(D^*_T \overset{d}{\to} \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 \nu^\top \widetilde{\Sigma}_T^{-1}\nu)\).

**Mechanism 2:** The one‑step estimator’s influence function equals the canonical gradient up to a remainder \(R_T\) from a von Mises expansion. If the nuisance estimators \(\widehat{\alpha}\) and \(\widehat{h}\) converge fast enough—\(\| \widehat{\alpha} - \alpha_T \|_{L_2} = o_P(1)\), \(\| \widehat{h} - h_T \|_{L_2} = o_P(1)\), and the bias term \(\nu^\top \widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta_0) = o_P(1/\sqrt{T})\)—then \(R_T = o_P(1/\sqrt{T})\). Because the canonical gradient lies in the tangent space and achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound, the convolution theorem (Theorem 4) implies that the one‑step estimator is asymptotically efficient; no additional variance remains.

**Mechanism 3:** For LinUCB with linear rewards, directional stability holds if the exploration bonus \(\gamma_T\) is large enough (e.g., \(\gamma_T \gtrsim d_T^2(\sigma\sqrt{d_T} + \log\log T)\)) and \(d_T = o(T)\). The empirical design decomposes into a low‑rank “expert direction” (spanned by \(\beta_0\)) and an orthogonal residual. Optimism forces frequent sampling near the top eigenvector of the true signal, while orthogonal directions accumulate data more slowly. By tuning \(\gamma_T\), both the parallel and perpendicular components of the Riesz representer error are controlled, yielding \(\| \widehat{\alpha} - \widetilde{\alpha} \|_{L_2} = o_P(1)\) when scaled by the effective standard deviation. This verifies directional stability for LinUCB and establishes efficiency.

## Foundational Learning
- **Semiparametric efficiency and the canonical gradient:** The canonical gradient (influence function) encodes the pathwise derivative of a functional and determines the minimum asymptotic variance among regular estimators. It is central to the claim that the one‑step estimator is efficient. *Quick check:* What is the Riesz representer, and how does it determine the variance of any regular estimator for a linear functional?
- **Martingale CLT with stabilization of conditional quadratic variation:** In adaptive experiments, the score is a martingale. Asymptotic normality follows from convergence of its predictable quadratic variation, not from independent increments. Directional stability provides exactly this convergence along the needed direction. *Quick check:* Why does convergence of the predictable quadratic variation suffice for a martingale CLT, and how is directional stability used to establish it?
- **Stability in adaptive experiments (full vs. directional):** Full stability requires the entire empirical design matrix to converge, which is too strong for regret‑minimizing bandits that allocate data anisotropically. Directional stability exploits that the target is scalar, needing stabilization only along \(\nu\). *Quick check:* In what way does LinUCB’s sampling rule lead to anisotropic information accumulation, making full stability unlikely but directional stability plausible?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** LinUCB data collection → compute empirical design \(\widehat{\Sigma}_T\) and cross‑covariance → ridge‑regularized outcome model \(\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda_h}\) → Riesz representer estimate \(\widehat{\alpha}_{\lambda_\alpha}\) → one‑step estimator \(\widehat{\Psi}\) → variance estimation → confidence intervals.

**Critical path:** \(\widehat{\Sigma}_T\) must be computed first; it is used both for \(\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda_h}\) and \(\widehat{\alpha}_{\lambda_\alpha}\). \(\widehat{h}(Z_t)\) is needed for the augmentation term. Matrix inversion (via ridge) is the numerically sensitive step.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Regularization (\(\lambda_h,\lambda_\alpha\)): Larger values reduce variance but add bias. Theory often uses \(\lambda_\alpha = 1/T\) and similarly decaying \(\lambda_h\); in practice one may tune while preserving rates.
- Exploration bonus (\(\gamma_T\)): Must scale at least as \(d_T^2(\sigma\sqrt{d_T} + \log\log T)\) to guarantee directional stability. Too small \(\gamma\) leads to anisotropic information gaps; too large increases regret.
- Feature dimension growth: Assumes \(d_T = o(T)\). Super‑linear growth requires stronger regularization and controlling the effective dimension \(\text{deff}(\lambda,T)\).

**Failure signatures:**
- Coverage of nominal 95% CIs drops below 95% as \(T\) increases, indicating violation of directional stability or CLT conditions.
- Numerical instability in inverting \(\widehat{\Sigma}_T\) even with ridge; may require larger \(\lambda\) or smaller \(d_T\).
- Estimated Riesz \(\widehat{\alpha}\) has very large norm, infling variance.
- Large discrepancy between \(\widehat{\alpha}\) and the theoretical \(\widetilde{\alpha}\), especially in the orthogonal subspace, suggests insufficient exploration (\(\gamma_T\) too small).
- Heavy‑tailed or heteroscedastic noise invalidates sub‑Gaussian homoscedastic assumptions.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Directional stability verification:** Simulate LinUCB with varying \(\gamma_T\) (small vs. large). Compute scaled error \(\text{err}_T = \| \widehat{\alpha} - \widetilde{\alpha} \|_{L_2}/\widetilde{\sigma}_T\) and check its decay as \(T\) grows. Observe coverage of the one‑step CI for \(\Psi\).
2. **i.i.d. benchmark:** Run the same target under independent random design (uniform arms). The one‑step estimator should achieve nominal coverage; compare MSE to the adaptive case.
3. **Break condition test:** Set \(\gamma_T\) too small (e.g., \(\gamma_T=1\)) or let \(d_T\) grow nearly linearly with \(T\). Expect coverage to drop dramatically; monitor the remainder term in the von Mises expansion to become non‑negligible relative to \(1/\sqrt{T}\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Misspecification and nonparametric M‑estimands:** Can the analysis be extended when the linear reward model is misspecified and the target is a nonparametric M‑estimand? The current theory assumes correct specification and homoscedastic noise.
2. **General regularization schedules:** Is it possible to generalize the asymptotic results to arbitrary ridge regularization, where the effective dimension \(\text{deff}(\lambda,T)\) governs complexity rather than the specific \(\sqrt{d_T/T}\) rates? The current proof relies on delicate bounds tailored to \(\lambda = 1/T\).
3. **Debiased inference after model selection:** How to perform debiased inference in adaptive experiments when the model is selected from the data? Combining sample splitting/cross‑fitting with the one‑step estimator under adaptive sampling remains open.

## Limitations
- **Large exploration bonus:** The sufficient condition for directional stability in LinUCB requires \(\gamma_T \gtrsim d_T^2(\sigma\sqrt{d_T} + \log\log T)\), which can be conservative and impact regret.
- **Noise and homoscedasticity assumptions:** The martingale CLT hinges on sub‑Gaussian, homoskedastic noise. Violations (heavy tails, heteroscedasticity) may break asymptotic normality.
- **Nuisance rate dependence:** Efficiency relies on fast convergence of \(\widehat{\alpha}\) and \(\widehat{h}\) (e.g., \(d_T = o(T)\) and appropriate regularization). In finite samples, especially with moderate \(T\) and growing \(d_T\), the remainder may be non‑negligible.

## Confidence
- **High:** The theoretical link between directional stability and the martingale central limit theorem is solid and well‑established.
- **Medium:** The one‑step efficiency claim depends on nuisance convergence rates; while plausible, empirical validation across diverse settings is needed.
- **Medium:** The directional stability proof for LinUCB is technical and uses strong assumptions (e.g., large \(\gamma_T\)), making the result somewhat fragile to violations.

## Next Checks
1. **Reproduce directional stability for LinUCB:** Vary \(\gamma_T\) and \(d_T\). Compute \(\text{err}_T\) and empirical coverage of 95% intervals from the one‑step estimator. Confirm \(\text{err}_T \to 0\) and coverage \(\to 0.95\) when \(\gamma_T\) meets the theoretical bound.
2. **Test robustness to non‑Gaussian noise:** Replace Gaussian rewards with heavy‑tailed (e.g., \(t_5\)) or heteroscedastic distributions. Check whether coverage remains nominal or deteriorates, assessing sensitivity to Lindeberg and homoscedasticity assumptions.
3. **Benchmark against doubly robust (AIPW) estimator:** Under LinUCB sampling, compare bias, variance, and coverage of the one‑step estimator to an AIPW‑type estimator that uses explicit propensity scores. This validates the claim that no propensity weighting is needed and clarifies any finite‑sample efficiency trade‑offs.
