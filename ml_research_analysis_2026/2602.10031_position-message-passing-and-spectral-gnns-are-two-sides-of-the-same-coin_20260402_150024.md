# Position: Message-passing and spectral GNNs are two sides of the same coin

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10031
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10031
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Under natural assumptions (aggregation GSO, continuous filters), message‑passing and spectral GNNs are equally expressive, matching the 1‑Weisfeiler–Leman test.

## Executive Summary
This paper dissolves the apparent rivalry between message‑passing (spatial) and spectral graph neural networks. It shows that both are simply different parametrizations of permutation‑equivariant operators on graph signals. When the graph shift operator (GSO) is an “aggregation” type (e.g., adjacency or Laplacian) and spectral filters are continuous (hence approximable by polynomials), the two families achieve identical expressive power: they distinguish exactly the same graphs as the 1‑Weisfeiler–Leman test. The authors then argue that each viewpoint brings unique strengths: MPNNs naturally encode discrete structure, logical expressivity, and algorithmic alignment, while spectral GNNs provide a principled basis for analyzing smoothing (oversmoothing), long‑range bottlenecks (oversquashing), and community structure. The synthesis advocates unified frameworks to accelerate progress.

## Method Summary
The core contribution is a rigorous equivalence between the two architectures. An MPNN layer using sum aggregation,  
`x_i' = φ(x_i, Σ_{j∈N(i)} κ(x_j))`,  
can be exactly simulated by a polynomial spectral GNN layer,  
`X' = Σ_{k=0}^K A^k X C_k + b`,  
followed by a nonlinearity; conversely, any such spectral layer unrolls as message passing. The assumptions are: (1) the GSO is an **aggregation GSO** (implementable by a finite‑depth sum‑aggregation MPNN – holds for common choices like A, L, L_sym) and (2) spectral filters are continuous functions of eigenvalues. Under these conditions, the authors prove both families have separation power at most that of 1‑WL, and with the adjacency GSO they achieve exactly 1‑WL. The methodology includes explicit construction of conversions (Lemma 5, Proposition 12), expressive‑power analysis via the 1‑WL hierarchy, and discussion of complementary analytical tools (discrete vs. spectral). The work is primarily theoretical, with full proofs in the appendix.

## Key Results
- MPNNs and spectral GNNs with continuous filters on an aggregation GSO are equally expressive; both match the 1‑Weisfeiler–Leman test.
- The equivalence fails for non‑aggregation GSOs (e.g., encoding global identifiers) or graphs with unbounded spectra.
- MPNNs excel at discrete‑structure reasoning and directed graphs; spectral GNNs provide direct knobs for smoothing, oversquashing, and community sensitivity via filter design and eigenvalue analysis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper’s thesis rests on three pillars:

1. **Parametrization equivalence** – Both architectures compute permutation‑equivariant functions on graph signals. With an aggregation GSO, polynomial spectral filters coincide with certain MPNNs, and continuous filters are uniformly approximable by polynomials, yielding identical expressive power (1‑WL). The equivalence breaks if the GSO is non‑aggregation or its spectrum is unbounded across graph sizes.

2. **Discrete‑structure primacy of MPNNs** – MPNNs process neighborhoods as multisets and mirror the iterative refinement of 1‑WL and first‑order logic. This connection yields a clear expressivity hierarchy (e.g., *k*-WL, subgraph counts) and facilitates extensions to directed graphs and large‑scale deployment, aligning naturally with combinatorial algorithms.

3. **Spectral perspective for smoothing and bottlenecks** – In spectral GNNs, the frequency response \(h(\lambda)\) explicitly controls which spectral components are preserved. Oversmoothing is seen as geometric spectral contraction (\(\|X^{(t)}\| \le \gamma^t \|X\|\)), oversquashing is linked to algebraic connectivity (\(\lambda_2\)) and conductance, and community structure corresponds to low‑frequency eigenspaces. This view provides principled tools for designing and diagnosing these phenomena (e.g., heat kernels, resolvents).

## Foundational Learning
**Graph Shift Operator (GSO) & Aggregation GSO**  
*Why needed:* Determines whether a spectral GNN can be implemented by an MPNN; central to the expressivity equivalence.  
*Quick check:* Can \(Df\) (degree matrix times signal) be realized by a finite‑depth sum‑aggregation MPNN? (Yes; compute degrees first, then multiply – see Example 10.)

**Permutation Equivariance & Invariance**  
*Why needed:* Both paradigms are defined by symmetry constraints; MPNNs respect node permutations, spectral GNNs respect eigenbasis invariance.  
*Quick check:* Why is \(X \mapsto V H(\Lambda) V^\top X\) permutation‑equivariant? It is invariant to the choice of eigenbasis \(V\) for a fixed GSO due to the spectral theorem.

**1‑Weisfeiler–Leman (1‑WL) Test**  
*Why needed:* The canonical expressivity benchmark; the paper equates both paradigms to 1‑WL under the aggregation GSO assumption.  
*Quick check:* Give a pair of non‑isomorphic graphs that 1‑WL cannot distinguish. (e.g., two strongly regular SRG(16,6,2,2) graphs.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Graph + features -> Core: either MPNN stack (message + update) or spectral GNN stack (polynomial/continuous filter on GSO) -> [Optional: positional encodings] -> Readout (global pooling + MLP)

**Critical path**
1. Diagnose task: discrete structure/logic → favor MPNN; smoothing/bottleneck/community → favor spectral.
2. If spectral, choose an aggregation GSO (A, L, L_sym) to preserve 1‑WL equivalence.
3. Decide on positional encodings; if using spectral PEs, ensure stability (sign‑/basis‑invariance).
4. Select architecture: for discrete tasks use MPNN‑based models (GIN, subgraph GNNs); for spectral control use spectral GNNs or deep polynomial MPNNs.
5. Validate with diagnostics: oversmoothing (Dirichlet energy decay), oversquashing (spectral gap, effective resistance), expressivity (separate 1‑WL graphs).

**Design tradeoffs**
- MPNNs: simpler, linear scaling, natural for directed graphs. Spectral GNNs: explicit frequency control but often require eigendecomposition (mitigated by polynomials).
- Deep MPNNs oversmooth implicitly; spectral filters allow precise smoothing rates via \(h(\lambda)\) (e.g., heat kernel).
- Polynomial spectral GNNs of degree \(K\) have receptive field \(K\), similar to a \(K\)‑layer MPNN; rational/nonlinear filters may capture biases beyond MPNNs but are under‑explored.

**Failure signatures**
- Oversmoothing: rapid decay of Dirichlet energy or node feature variance.
- Oversquashing: poor long‑range performance despite depth; diagnosed by small \(\lambda_2\) or high effective resistance.
- Spectral instability (with SPEs): performance sensitive to eigenvector sign flips or small perturbations.
- Expressivity ceiling: inability to distinguish graphs separable by 1‑WL; indicates insufficient depth or non‑aggregation GSO.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Expressivity equivalence:** Train a 1‑WL‑maximal MPNN (GIN) and a spectral GNN with aggregation GSO & polynomial degree ≥ 1 on EXP/CLOG datasets. Both should achieve ~100% accuracy.
2. **Oversmoothing diagnosis:** Train deep (≥ 20‑layer) MPNN and spectral GNN with low‑pass filter (e.g., \(h(\lambda)=e^{-\tau\lambda}\)) on a node classification task; monitor Dirichlet energy per layer.
3. **Positional encoding stability:** Use a Transformer with raw Laplacian eigenvectors as PEs; evaluate on original graphs, sign‑flipped eigenvectors, and slightly perturbed graphs; measure accuracy drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Invariant and universally approximating spectral GNNs?** Can we design spectral GNN layers that are both eigenbasis‑invariant and universally approximating? Current designs sacrifice one property for the other.
2. **Benchmarks for isolating spectral phenomena?** A standardized suite with controlled GSOs (varying λ₂, community strength) is needed to determine real‑world advantages of each viewpoint.
3. **Unifying stability analyses?** Spectral (exponential) and MPNN (linear) stability bounds appear different; a unified framework linking depth, filter type, and perturbation growth is lacking.
4. **Stable permutation‑equivariant SPEs?** Raw eigenvectors are neither stable nor sign‑invariant. While invariant constructions exist, their interaction with different backbones and generalization impact requires systematic study.
5. **Beyond polynomials: rational or nonlinear spectral filters?** Filters like Cayley or ARMA may better capture long‑range or multi‑scale biases, but comparative evidence against strong MPNN baselines is missing.

## Limitations
- The equivalence hinges on the GSO being an aggregation operator; many useful spectral operators (e.g., personalized PageRank, diffusion matrices) may not satisfy this, limiting the scope.
- The analysis assumes undirected graphs with real symmetric GSOs; extending to directed graphs (magnetic Laplacians) remains an open challenge.
- Uniform approximation guarantees lack explicit degree bounds; for large graphs high‑degree polynomials may be inefficient and numerically unstable.
- The claimed complementary strengths are qualitative; no systematic guidance exists on when to prefer one viewpoint or how to combine them.
- A critical position paper (“Spectral GNNs Are Neither Spectral Nor Superior”) questions whether spectral methods truly offer advantages, highlighting ongoing debate.

## Confidence
- Equivalence of parametrizations (under aggregation GSO): **High**
- Discrete‑structure primacy of MPNNs: **Medium**
- Spectral tools for smoothing/bottleneck analysis: **Medium**
