# Model Capability Dominates: Inference-Time Optimization Lessons from AIMO 3

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.27844
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27844
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Model capability dominates inference-time optimization; high-temperature sampling already yields near-zero error correlation and no decorrelation strategy improves over baseline.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether inference-time techniques can mitigate correlated errors in LLM ensembles for mathematical reasoning. The authors propose the Diverse Prompt Mixer, which assigns distinct reasoning strategies to different voters in a majority voting setup, aiming to decorrelate errors. In a rigorous evaluation on 50 IMO-level problems using three GPT-family models and over 23 experiments under a strict 5‑hour GPU budget, every intervention failed to improve overall accuracy. The study finds that sampling at high temperature (T=1.0) already produces near‑zero or negative pairwise error correlations, leaving no headroom for further decorrelation. Moreover, a 17‑point performance gap between the best model and its rivals dwarfs any gains from optimization, underscoring the paramount importance of base model capability.

## Method Summary
The authors test the Diverse Prompt Mixer on a suite of 50 challenging mathematical problems. For each problem, they generate multiple solution attempts from a single LLM using different prompts that encode structured reasoning strategies (e.g., “small cases first”, “work backwards”). The attempts are aggregated via majority voting. The experimental matrix varies the distribution of strategies (equal mix, isolated strategies, code‑first variant) and sampling temperature, measuring both per‑attempt accuracy and pairwise error correlation. All experiments are constrained to a total of 5 GPU hours, forcing careful allocation across three GPT models (including gpt‑oss‑120b) and numerous prompt configurations.

## Key Results
- High‑temperature sampling (T=1.0) yields a mean error correlation ρ ≈ -0.113, effectively eliminating positive correlation between voter mistakes.
- All prompt‑diversity configurations degrade per‑attempt accuracy relative to the baseline; the monotonic drop from baseline 39.7/50 to as low as 36–38/50 demonstrates that any decorrelation gain is outweighed by accuracy loss.
- The 17‑point capability gap between gpt‑oss‑120b (39.7) and the next‑best models (23–26) is an order of magnitude larger than any improvement seen from inference‑time optimization, confirming that model capability is the dominant factor.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The near‑zero/negative correlation at high temperature arises because independent stochastic sampling naturally diversifies the error patterns of the model; each sample explores a different region of the probability space, reducing the likelihood that multiple samples fail on the same subproblem. Consequently, majority voting already operates near the theoretical limit of decorrelation, leaving minimal room for a prompt‑based diversity scheme to further reduce correlation. Moreover, the Diverse Prompt Mixer introduces prompts that are suboptimal for the model’s native reasoning style, reducing per‑sample accuracy. Since the ensemble’s expected score is roughly the product of individual accuracy and the correlation structure, any accuracy drop outweighs marginal correlation improvements. Finally, the stark capability gap indicates that when the base model is weak, its fundamental limitations (e.g., mathematical knowledge, reasoning depth) cannot be overcome by inference‑time tricks; a more capable model simply produces fewer errors to begin with, making ensembles less beneficial.

## Foundational Learning
- **Majority Voting**: An ensemble technique that aggregates multiple model outputs via plurality to reduce variance and correct isolated mistakes. *Why needed*: Improves reliability without retraining. *Quick check*: For three binary predictions, output the majority label.
- **Error Correlation**: The tendency of multiple model outputs to err on the same inputs; high correlation diminishes ensemble gains. *Why needed*: Quantifies the limit of voting effectiveness. *Quick check*: Compute Pearson correlation of error indicators across samples.
- **Temperature Sampling**: A softmax temperature parameter controlling output randomness; higher T increases diversity but may reduce individual accuracy. *Why needed*: Balances exploration and exploitation. *Quick check*: Set T=1.0 in generation API and observe varied outputs.
- **Prompt Engineering**: Crafting input prompts to steer model behavior; different strategies (e.g., “think step by step”) can affect solution paths. *Why needed*: Unlocks latent reasoning capabilities. *Quick check*: Compare model responses to “Solve directly” vs. “List cases then solve”.
- **Capability Gap**: Performance difference between model architectures or sizes; a larger gap means that improvements from inference‑time methods are relatively small. *Why needed*: Prioritizes investment in better base models. *Quick check*: Compare accuracy scores of two models on the same benchmark.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Problem -> Prompt Mixer (assigns strategy) -> LLM (multiple parallel calls) -> Solution Generation -> Voting Aggregator -> Final Answer  
**Critical path**: Input problem is dispatched to several inference workers, each receiving a distinct prompt; each worker samples a solution; solutions are parsed for correctness; a majority vote selects the final answer.  
**Design tradeoffs**: 
- Accuracy vs. Diversity: More diverse prompts can harm per‑sample correctness but might lower correlation; net benefit depends on the tradeoff.
- Temperature vs. Reliability: Higher T increases diversity and reduces correlation but also lowers individual accuracy.
- Computational cost: More samples improve voting but consume GPU time; the 5‑hour budget forced tradeoffs in the number of configurations tested.  
**Failure signatures**: 
- Monotonic accuracy drop when introducing prompt diversity, even when correlation decreases.
- Lack of correlation headroom at T=1.0, indicating that stochastic sampling alone already maximizes diversity for that model.  
**First experiments**:
1. Baseline: High‑temperature (T=1.0) sampling with a single generic prompt, measuring accuracy and correlation.
2. Equal strategy mix: Distribute three reasoning strategies uniformly across voters and evaluate ensemble performance.
3. Isolated strategies: Test each reasoning strategy separately to isolate its impact on individual accuracy and pairwise correlation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly identify any open questions for future research.

## Limitations
- Model scope limited to three GPT‑family models; findings may not generalize to other architectures (Claude, Gemini, open‑source variants).
- Prompt diversity method used a fixed small set of reasoning strategies; unexplored prompt spaces or adaptive strategy selection might behave differently.
- Evaluation restricted to 50 IMO‑level mathematics problems, raising questions about transfer to other reasoning domains such as logic or science.

## Confidence
- Intervention degradation: High confidence (consistent across 23+ experiments)
- High‑τ decorrelation: High confidence (directly measured)
- Model capability dominance: High confidence (17‑point gap)
- Generalization beyond IMO: Low confidence (single benchmark)

## Next Checks
1. Cross‑architecture replication: Test the same Diverse Prompt Mixer on Claude 3.5, Gemini 1.5, and Llama‑3‑70B to see if capability dominance persists.
2. Alternative decorrelation approaches: Replace prompt diversity with heterogeneous model mixtures (e.g., combining code‑specialized and math‑specialized models) or temperature annealing schedules; measure correlation‑accuracy trade‑offs.
3. Broader benchmark evaluation: Run baseline and best prompt‑mix configurations on MATH, GPQA, and TheoremQA to assess whether the “no headroom” finding holds across domains.
