# SAGEO Arena: A Realistic Environment for Evaluating Search-Augmented Generative Engine Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12187
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12187
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** SAGEO Arena introduces a realistic end-to-end benchmark and shows that a stage-aware optimization strategy improves retrieval Hit Rate by +28% and citation rate by +1.01 over baseline.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents SAGEO Arena, a benchmark for evaluating Search-Augmented Generative Engine Optimization (SAGEO) that preserves structural web document information (title, meta description, headings, schema) and integrates a full retrieval-reranking-generation pipeline over a 170k-document corpus. Experiments reveal that optimizing body text—the focus of prior work—harms retrieval by an average of -16% Hit Rate, while optimizing structural fields alone improves retrieval by +22%. However, naive combinations of optimizations fail due to stage-specific trade-offs. A stage-aware method that tailors content to each stage’s needs achieves the strongest overall performance, demonstrating that effective SAGEO requires joint, stage-specific optimization and that structural information is critical.

## Method Summary
SAGEO Arena evaluates SAGEO strategies using a corpus of 170k web documents with intact structural fields. The pipeline comprises BM25 retrieval, a cross-encoder reranker, and an LLM generator. The authors define eight base optimization strategies (e.g., adding statistics, simplifying language) and combine them in various ways (All-in-One, selective, stage-aware). Visibility is measured per stage via metrics like Hit Rate, rank, and citation rate. The stage-aware approach designs prompts to produce content specifically for retrieval (emphasizing structural fields), reranking (answer-first structure), and generation (substantive body), based on observed patterns of what each stage favors.

## Key Results
- Structural optimization alone boosts retrieval Hit Rate by +22% and indirectly benefits later stages by increasing candidate documents.
- Body text optimization alone reduces retrieval Hit Rate by -16% on average, often causing documents to exit the candidate pool.
- A stage-aware SAGEO method improves retrieval Hit Rate by +28% and citation rate by +1.01 over baseline, outperforming naive combinations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Each pipeline stage prioritizes distinct document qualities: retrieval (BM25) relies on lexical overlap in structural fields, while generation depends on substantive body content. Structural fields provide dense, query-relevant terms that improve lexical matching without incurring BM25’s length normalization penalty. Body text optimization often introduces less common vocabulary and expands length, harming retrieval. The stage-aware method aligns content with these signals: it enriches structural fields for retrieval, ensures answers appear early for reranking, and maintains body depth for generation. This stage-specific tailoring avoids cross-stage interference and yields net gains.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Query -> Retriever (BM25) -> Reranker (Cross-encoder) -> Generator (LLM) -> Answer with citations. Corpus contains structured fields (title, meta description, headings, schema, body).
- **Critical path:** A user query triggers retrieval of top-k documents via BM25; these are reordered by the reranker; the generator selects citations from reranked documents and produces a final answer.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Lexical (BM25) vs. dense retrieval: BM25 is interpretable and sensitive to keyword overlap but penalizes document length and requires field weighting.
  - Field separation vs. merging: Keeping structural fields separate enables stage-specific tuning but increases complexity.
  - Model choice: Different LLMs (GPT vs. LLaMA vs. Qwen) show biases toward retrieval or generation, affecting strategy effectiveness.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Retrieval: Document not in top-k (low Hit Rate), often after body expansion.
  - Reranking: Rank drop despite retrieval, linked to answer placement or scope expansion.
  - Generation: Missing citations or low-quality answers when body lacks substantive content.
- **Exactly 3 first experiments:**
  1. Optimize only title, only meta description, and only headings separately; measure retrieval Hit Rate to identify the most influential structural field.
  2. Apply body-text-only optimization; compare retrieval Hit Rate and citation rate against baseline to confirm degradation at retrieval and any generation benefit.
  3. Implement the paper’s stage-aware prompt and compare against an All-in-One (naive combination) strategy on overall metrics (retrieval Hit Rate, citation rate).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### 1. How do different structural field types (title, meta description, headings, schema) individually and combinatorially influence visibility at each stage (retrieval, reranking, generation) across diverse domains?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper preserves structural information as separate components and finds that optimizing structural information significantly improves retrieval (+22% Hit Rate) and benefits later stages. However, it analyzes structural information as an aggregated scope rather than dissecting the contribution of each field.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper states that merging fields would obscure distinct signals and that search engines index them separately, but the evaluation combines all structural fields. It is unclear which specific fields drive retrieval gains versus generation benefits, and whether certain fields (e.g., schema vs. headings) have opposing effects on later stages.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation experiments isolating each structural field during optimization, measuring stage-level visibility metrics per field, and analyzing interaction effects across domains and query types.

### 2. What underlying characteristics of user queries (e.g., specificity, domain, intent) make them more or less susceptible to retrieval failure when body text is optimized, and how can SAGEO strategies be made robust to query paraphrasing and abstraction?
- **Basis in paper:** Body text optimization consistently degrades retrieval (average -16% Hit Rate), and the paper's query variation analysis shows all variations further harm performance, with abstraction causing the most severe declines due to documents falling out of the candidate pool.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies lexical mismatch as the cause (optimized body text uses less common vocabulary), but does not characterize which query attributes (e.g., length, keyword density, domain terminology) exacerbate this mismatch. It also notes structural optimization only partially mitigates the issue.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlational analysis between query features (lexical diversity, term frequency, domain specificity) and retrieval drop rates after body optimization; testing whether query expansion techniques during indexing could buffer against paraphrasing; developing optimization prompts that explicitly preserve or diversify vocabulary based on predicted query distribution.

### 3. Why does the reranking stage remain a persistent bottleneck across all SAGEO strategies, and what specific content or structural attributes cause documents to gain or lose rank during reranking beyond the two case studies presented?
- **Basis in paper:** All strategies show consistent reranking degradation (average rank drop ~0.94) regardless of optimization scope. Case studies reveal that reranking favors content that directly addresses the query and places answers early, while penalizing scope expansion and answer displacement. However, these are based on two examples.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze the reranker’s decision-making at scale (e.g., through attribution or error analysis). The exact trade-offs between relevance, coherence, and positional bias are not quantified. It is unclear whether reranking failures stem from subtle semantic shifts introduced by optimization, length changes, or interference from structural field contents.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large-scale analysis of reranker scores and feature importance (e.g., using SHAP or attention visualization) comparing optimized vs. original documents; controlled experiments varying answer placement, paragraph coherence, and content expansion while holding other factors constant; studying the reranker’s sensitivity to document length and field composition.

### 4. How does the choice of backbone LLM for the generator (and potentially the reranker) fundamentally alter the effectiveness and stage-level trade-offs of SAGEO strategies, and what model-specific optimization guidelines emerge?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper compares three backbone models (GPT-5-mini, LLaMA-3.3-70B, Qwen3-80B) and finds LLaMA excels at retrieval due to keyword-dense short outputs but fails at reranking and generation, while GPT dominates downstream stages. This indicates model-specific biases.
- **Why unresolved:** The experiment only applies the All-in-One strategy and does not decompose why different models respond differently to the same optimization. It is unknown whether model architecture, training data, or inference parameters drive these disparities.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic study of model behaviors across multiple optimization strategies, probing models for their citation preferences (e.g., via controlled A/B tests on document snippets), and analyzing how model size, training objectives, and retrieval augmentation methods affect stage-level outcomes.

### 5. To what extent can stage-aware SAGEO (as proposed) be automated or learned, rather than hand-crafted with heuristic principles, and can such a method generalize across domains and query distributions without per-domain tuning?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper introduces a manual stage-aware strategy combining four principles (entity mirroring, fluent language, concrete evidence, keyword reinforcement) that achieves the best overall performance. However, it is prompt-engineered based on observed patterns.
- **Why unresolved:** The hand-crafted approach may not capture all relevant factors or scale to unseen domains. The paper shows domain-specific variations (e.g., shopping domain responds poorly), but the stage-aware prompt does not adapt to domain. It is unclear whether a learned policy (e.g., via reinforcement learning that optimizes for end-to-end visibility) could surpass the heuristic method.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing a reinforcement learning or supervised learning framework that takes document, query, and pipeline state as input and outputs optimization actions, trained to maximize a multi-stage reward (e.g., retrieval hit + rerank rank + citation). Evaluating such a learned method against the heuristic across held-out domains and query types.

### 6. What is the causal impact of structural information on retrieval and reranking in generative search pipelines, independent of its effect on generation, and does its benefit stem primarily from lexical overlap or from semantic disambiguation?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper shows structural optimization improves retrieval Hit Rate (+22%) and also benefits reranking/generation indirectly by increasing the number of documents that pass retrieval. However, it speculates that structural information provides dense query-relevant terms for BM25.
- **Why unresolved:** The retriever is BM25, a lexical method, so the benefit could be purely lexical. But the reranker (a cross-encoder) also shows improvement, suggesting semantic factors. It is unclear whether structural fields help resolve entity ambiguities, establish document credibility, or provide concise summaries that rerankers prefer.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where structural fields are optimized for lexical overlap only (e.g., keyword stuffing) versus semantic richness (e.g., adding entity definitions), measuring impact on lexical vs. semantic retrievers (e.g., BM25 vs. dense retriever) and rerankers; analyzing the correlation between structural field content and reranker confidence scores.

### 7. How do commercial, production-grade generative search engines (e.g., Bing Copilot, Google AI Overviews) differ from the SAGEO Arena pipeline in their handling of structural information and document ranking, and would the paper's findings generalize to such systems?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper cites commercial guidelines and prior work suggesting that engines like Bing and Google leverage structural context. However, SAGEO Arena uses a specific BM25 + cross-encoder + LLM pipeline that may not capture proprietary ranking signals, weighting schemes, or generation mechanisms.
- **Why unresolved:** The benchmark is designed to be reproducible and modular, but it does not claim to replicate any specific commercial engine. Without validation against real engines, it is uncertain whether optimizations effective in SAGEO Arena (e.g., structural field enrichment) would translate to production environments.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Running a subset of optimized documents through actual commercial generative search APIs (where feasible) and measuring visibility changes; collaborating with engine providers to compare SAGEO Arena's ranking decisions with theirs; incorporating known commercial ranking features (e.g., freshness, domain authority) into the arena.

### 8. What is the optimal balance between document length and information density for SAGEO, given that body text expansion harms retrieval (length normalization in BM25) but may benefit generation (more content to cite)?
- **Basis in paper:** AutoGEO's body expansion causes severe retrieval degradation (-36% Hit Rate, -22.35 rank change). The paper notes that BM25 applies length normalization favoring shorter documents. However, generation benefits from substantive body text, as citations primarily come from body content.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not systematically vary document length or measure the trade-off curve. It is unknown whether moderate expansion could improve generation without crippling retrieval, or whether structural optimization can compensate for length penalties. The optimal length may also depend on domain.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experimentally controlling document length (e.g., truncating or expanding body text while keeping meaning constant) and measuring multi-stage visibility; analyzing the interaction between length and structural field richness; developing length-aware optimization that adjusts expansion based on retriever characteristics.

### 9. Are there diminishing returns or conflict points when combining multiple SAGEO strategies (e.g., adding statistics vs. simplifying language), and can a portfolio approach that dynamically selects strategies per document/query improve overall performance?
- **Basis in paper:** The All-in-One strategy (combining all eight base strategies) does not yield consistent gains and sometimes underperforms individual strategies. Selective combinations still show retrieval rank degradation. The paper suggests naive pairing cannot benefit all stages.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not explore why combinations fail—whether strategies interfere (e.g., adding technical terms conflicts with easy language) or whether certain strategies are synergistic only in specific contexts. A portfolio that selects strategies based on document attributes or query type might avoid negative interactions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large-scale combinatorial analysis measuring pairwise and higher-order interactions of strategies across document features (e.g., initial readability, domain, length); building a classifier to predict which strategies will be beneficial for a given document/query pair; evaluating a dynamic strategy selection mechanism against fixed combinations.

However, it speculates that structural information provides dense query-relevant terms for BM25.
- **Why unresolved:** The retriever is BM25, a lexical method, so the benefit could be purely lexical. But the reranker (a cross-encoder) also shows improvement, suggesting semantic factors. ## Limitations
- **Corpus representativeness:** The 170k-document corpus may not capture the full diversity, scale, or dynamic nature of the live web, limiting generalizability of retrieval dynamics.
- **Pipeline & model specificity:** Findings are tied to the specific retrieval (BM25), reranking, and generation models; trade-offs may differ with alternative architectures (e.g., dense retrieval, different LLMs).
- **Novelty & external validation:** The benchmark is new with no citations yet; independent replication and comparison against other SAGEO benchmarks are absent.

## Confidence
- **Benchmark Design & Need:** Medium.
- **Stage-Specific Optimization Findings (Trade-offs):** High (within study).
- **Generalizability of "Stage-Aware" Superiority:** Medium.
