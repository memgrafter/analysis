# What Do Claim Verification Datasets Actually Test? A Reasoning Trace Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01657
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01657
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** Analysis of 24,100 examples across nine datasets reveals that direct evidence extraction dominates reasoning demands (49.8%), while complex patterns like multi-sentence synthesis and numerical reasoning are severely under-represented, meaning high benchmark scores primarily reflect retrieval-plus-entailment ability rather than robust reasoning.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates what reasoning skills are actually measured by popular claim verification benchmarks. By generating and analyzing structured reasoning traces for over 24,000 examples from nine datasets, the authors identify six distinct reasoning patterns. They find that the most common pattern, direct evidence extraction (nearly 50% of cases), involves matching a claim to a verbatim or slightly paraphrased text span, creating a shortcut where high accuracy can be achieved without deeper synthesis or numerical computation. Training a compact verifier exposes domain-specific failure modes: lexical overlap bias in general claims, overcautiousness in scientific claims, and arithmetic failures in mathematical claims. The core conclusion is that prevailing aggregate benchmarks like LLMAggreFact are dominated by surface-level retrieval and entailment, potentially masking a lack of genuine reasoning capability and calling for more diverse, challenging evaluation suites.

## Method Summary
The analysis uses GPT-4o-mini to generate zero-shot step-by-step reasoning traces for 30.4K examples from nine datasets aggregated in LLMAggreFact. Traces where the model's generated label mismatched the dataset's ground truth are filtered out, leaving 24.1K accepted traces. Human annotators review a stratified sample of 1,000 traces to inductively develop a six-category reasoning pattern taxonomy (A-F). This taxonomy is used to annotate the full filtered set, quantifying pattern distribution per dataset and overall. To diagnose failure modes, the authors fine-tune a 4-bit quantized Gemma3 1B model (ClaimTrace) on the accepted traces to produce its own structured reasoning and labels. This compact verifier is then evaluated on held-out data from three domains (general, scientific, mathematical), and its errors are categorized into domain-specific taxonomies (five for general/science, six for math) to reveal distinct error profiles.

## Key Results
- Direct evidence extraction (Pattern A) is the dominant reasoning pattern, covering 49.8% of all analyzed examples, while multi-sentence synthesis (Pattern D) and numerical reasoning (Pattern E/F) are severely under-represented.
- Dataset-level reasoning pattern distributions vary starkly: some benchmarks (e.g., FEVER) are almost entirely lexical matching, while others (e.g., SciFact, COVID-Fact) require multi-sentence synthesis in nearly 50% of cases.
- A compact 1B-parameter verifier exhibits domain-specific error profiles: lexical overlap bias in general-domain claims, overcautiousness (requiring explicit evidence) in scientific claims, and arithmetic reasoning failures in mathematical claims.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Benchmark scores primarily test retrieval-plus-entailment, not complex reasoning.
*Claim:* Current claim verification benchmarks primarily test surface-level retrieval and textual entailment rather than complex reasoning.
*Mechanism:* The paper demonstrates that the majority of reasoning traces (49.8%) follow a "Direct evidence extraction & matching" pattern, where models locate and match text spans verbatim or with minor paraphrasing. This creates a shortcut where high accuracy can be achieved without multi-hop synthesis, numerical computation, or handling of nuance.
*Core assumption:* The reasoning traces generated by GPT-4o-mini are a valid proxy for the reasoning demands inherent in the benchmark datasets themselves.
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] "find that direct evidence extraction dominates (49.8% of cases)"; [section 4.3] Figure 2 shows the dominance of Pattern A across the aggregated dataset.
*Break condition:* If a new benchmark exhibits a majority pattern distribution other than direct extraction (e.g., >50% synthesis or step-by-step verification), the mechanism that high scores reflect primarily retrieval-plus-entailment would be weakened for that benchmark.

### Mechanism 2: Verifier failure modes are domain-specific, requiring tailored mitigation.
*Claim:* The failure modes of verification systems are highly domain-specific, requiring tailored mitigation strategies.
*Mechanism:* By training a compact verifier (ClaimTrace) and analyzing its errors across general, scientific, and mathematical domains, the study reveals distinct error profiles: lexical overlap bias dominates general claims, overcautiousness dominates scientific claims, and arithmetic reasoning failures dominate mathematical claims.
*Core assumption:* The error profile of a small, specialized model (1B parameters) is indicative of the fundamental reasoning gaps that exist across model architectures for these domain types.
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] "error profiles vary dramatically by domain -- general-domain verification is dominated by lexical overlap bias, scientific verification by overcautiousness, and mathematical verification by arithmetic reasoning failures"; [section 6.3] Figure 4 quantitatively shows the shift in dominant error types across SciFact, LLMAggreFact, and GSMClaims.
*Break condition:* If a single model architecture or training technique were shown to simultaneously eliminate the top error across all three domains (e.g., solving lexical bias also resolves overcautiousness and arithmetic errors), the claim of domain-specific failure profiles would be challenged.

### Mechanism 3: High aggregate scores mask critical reasoning deficits.
*Claim:* High benchmark scores on existing aggregate datasets are potentially misleading indicators of robust, general-purpose claim verification capability.
*Mechanism:* Since most test instances in LLMAggreFact require only direct evidence extraction, a system optimized for this pattern will score highly. However, such a system will fail on claims requiring the under-tested patterns (synthesis, numerical reasoning). The paper shows that even a compact verifier fails catastrophically on a mathematical reasoning benchmark, revealing a capability gap masked by general-domain scores.
*Core assumption:* The LLMAggreFact development set is a representative sample of the reasoning demands of its nine constituent benchmarks, and the generated traces accurately classify those demands.
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] "Our findings suggest that high benchmark scores primarily reflect retrieval-plus-entailment ability"; [section 7, Finding 1] "The dominance of direct evidence extraction (Pattern A) across LLMAggreFact means that a model achieving high balanced accuracy... is primarily demonstrating its ability to locate and match relevant text spans."
*Break condition:* If a system achieving state-of-the-art on LLMAggreFact also achieved >90% accuracy on a dedicated, balanced benchmark for multi-sentence synthesis and numerical reasoning (without specific fine-tuning on those tasks), this mechanism would be falsified.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Reasoning Pattern Taxonomy (A-F)**
  *Why needed here:* To move beyond aggregate accuracy and diagnose *how* a system arrives at a verification decision. This is the core analytical lens of the paper.
  *Quick check question:* For a given claim-document pair, what is the single most dominant reasoning step (e.g., direct quote, synthesis across two sentences, unit conversion) that justifies the label? Can you identify it without considering the final label?

- **Concept: Domain-Specific Error Taxonomy**
  *Why needed here:* To understand that "improving accuracy" is not a monolithic goal; the necessary intervention (e.g., reduce lexical bias vs. improve arithmetic) depends entirely on the claim domain.
  *Quick check question:* For a scientific claim labeled NOT SUPPORTED, what are the two most likely distinct root causes: 1) the verifier missed a negated statement, or 2) it rejected because a specific numeric value wasn't verbatim in the text?

- **Concept: Benchmark Analysis via Synthetic Traces**
  *Why needed here:* The paper's methodological contribution: using a capable LLM (GPT-4o-mini) to generate scalable, structured reasoning traces for mass analysis, avoiding costly human annotation for every example.
  *Quick check question:* What is a key limitation of using model-generated traces for this analysis (hint: see Limitations section), and how might it bias the discovered pattern distribution?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Trace Generator (GPT-4o-mini) -> Filter & Sample -> Pattern Taxonomy & Coder (human) -> Compact Verifier (ClaimTrace, Gemma3 1B) -> Error Analyzer.

**Critical path:** Raw datasets -> Trace Generation -> Filtering -> (A) Human analysis for taxonomy OR (B) Training ClaimTrace -> Error analysis on new domains. Path (A) defines the "what," Path (B) diagnoses the "why" of failures.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Trace Generator Choice:** Using a powerful, cost-effective model (GPT-4o-mini) vs. a more deliberate reasoning model (DeepSeek R1). Tradeoff: conciseness/analyizability vs. potential depth/verbosity of reasoning.
- **Filtering Threshold:** Filtering on label agreement with original dataset ensures trace relevance to the benchmark's intent but discards 21% of examples where the generator's reasoning might be correct but the original label is noisy or ambiguous.
- **Verifier Capacity:** Using a tiny 1B model (ClaimTrace) makes error analysis clearer (failures are less likely to be "smart" but wrong) but limits the absolute performance ceiling and may not expose failure modes of larger, more capable models.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Pattern-level:** A dataset where >80% of traces are Pattern A (Direct Extraction) is vulnerable to lexical matching shortcuts.
- **Model-level:** For a general-domain verifier, a high rate of `Lexical Overlap Bias` errors indicates it is not learning true entailment. For a scientific verifier, a high rate of `Overcautiousness` suggests it is overly reliant on explicit mentions and poor at integrative understanding.
- **Systemic:** If your compact verifier's error distribution on a target domain closely matches the pattern distribution of the training traces (e.g., low synthesis errors because training data had low synthesis), your model is likely just reflecting dataset bias.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Reproduce Pattern Distribution:** Generate traces for a held-out sample of your target benchmark (e.g., 1,000 examples) using the same prompt. Manually code 200 to estimate the % of Pattern A vs. D. If Pattern A is >70%, the benchmark is likely dominated by retrieval.
2. **Train & Diagnose a Compact Verifier:** Fine-tune a small model (e.g., 1B-3B) on the *accepted* traces from your target domain(s). Evaluate it on a challenging, out-of-distribution set known to require synthesis (e.g., AggreFact-CNN from the paper). Measure the `Insufficient Aggregation` error rate.
3. **Cross-Domain Transfer Test:** Take your compact verifier trained *only* on general-domain traces (LLMAggreFact) and evaluate it directly on SciFact and GSMClaims without further training. Document the spike in `Overcautiousness` (SciFact) and `Arithmetic Reasoning` (GSMClaims) errors. This confirms domain-specific failure modes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** To what extent do the identified reasoning pattern distributions (e.g., dominance of direct extraction) generalize when using different LLMs for trace generation, beyond GPT-4o-mini? *Basis:* The limitations section explicitly states: "Our analysis relies on reasoning traces from a single generator (GPT-4o-mini); different models may produce traces emphasizing different strategies." *Why unresolved:* The taxonomy and quantitative findings are tied to one model’s zero-shot trace generation. Without comparative traces from models with varying capabilities, it is unclear whether the observed pattern dominance is an artifact of the generator or a true property of the benchmarks. *What evidence would resolve it:* Generate and analyze traces for the same 24K examples using a diverse set of LLMs. Compare the distribution of reasoning patterns across generators.
- **Open Question 2:** Do claim verification datasets beyond the nine aggregated in LLMAggreFact exhibit substantially different reasoning demands, particularly for under-represented patterns like multi-sentence synthesis and numerical reasoning? *Basis:* The limitations note: "The analysis is limited to the nine constituent datasets aggregated in LLMAggreFact." *Why unresolved:* LLMAggreFact may not capture the full spectrum of claim verification tasks. If newer or domain-specific datasets inherently require more complex reasoning, the conclusion that benchmarks primarily test retrieval+entailment may be overstated. *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically apply the same reasoning trace generation and pattern annotation protocol to a broad set of recently released or specialized claim verification benchmarks.
- **Open Question 3:** What specific architectural or training modifications to compact verifiers can most effectively mitigate the domain-specific error profiles identified (lexical overlap bias in general, overcautiousness in science, arithmetic failures in math)? *Basis:* The findings state: "Error profiles vary dramatically by domain… no single mitigation strategy suffices." *Why unresolved:* The error analysis diagnoses the problem but does not evaluate solutions. It remains unknown whether targeted training data, modular architectures, or external tools can reduce these errors without degrading performance on other patterns. *What evidence would resolve it:* Design and train verifier variants that explicitly address each dominant error type (e.g., contrastive learning to reduce lexical bias, confidence calibration for overcautiousness, integrated symbolic modules for arithmetic). Evaluate them on held-out sets from each domain.
- **Open Question 4:** What is an effective and reliable fine-grained annotation scheme (e.g., partial support labels) for claim verification that accurately reflects nuanced reasoning, and how does it impact benchmark discriminativity? *Basis:* The recommendations include: "finer-grained labels such as Partial support to capture the nuanced nature of real-world claim verification." *Why unresolved:* While suggested, no concrete schema is proposed or tested. The field lacks consensus on what "partial support" means operationally. Without a standardized scheme, adopting it may introduce more annotation inconsistency than insight. *What evidence would resolve it:* Propose multiple candidate schemas based on the reasoning pattern taxonomy. Conduct controlled annotation studies measuring inter-annotator agreement and benchmark discriminativity when using these schemas versus binary labels.
- **Open Question 5:** Can adversarial or synthetic data generation techniques produce claim-evidence pairs that reliably evoke under-tested reasoning patterns (multi-sentence synthesis, numerical reasoning) without sacrificing dataset validity or introducing artifacts? *Basis:* The first recommendation is: "adversarial data mining that specifically targets lexical overlap to reduce surface-matching shortcuts," and the second/third recommend "inclusion of more multi-hop claims… and dedicated numerical reasoning components." *Why unresolved:* The paper identifies the gap but does not demonstrate a methodology for creating such examples. Adversarial generation risks producing unrealistic claims, while synthetic construction may oversimplify. *What evidence would resolve it:* Develop and release a benchmark extension or new dataset constructed via adversarial methods and controlled synthesis. Show via human evaluation that these examples are valid and that state-of-the-art verifiers fail on them specifically due to the targeted reasoning deficit.

## Limitations
- The analysis relies on reasoning traces from a single LLM (GPT-4o-mini); different generators may produce traces with different strategy emphases, biasing the pattern distribution.
- Manual reasoning pattern coding for the taxonomy development lacks reported inter-annotator agreement, introducing potential subjectivity in the categorization.
- The GSMClaims mathematical benchmark construction from GSM8K is insufficiently described, risking unnatural claim-evidence pairs that may inflate or distort arithmetic reasoning errors.

## Confidence
- *Direct evidence extraction dominates*: **Medium** (large-scale estimate relies on LLM traces and an unverified automated labeling procedure).
- *Domain-specific error profiles*: **Medium** (consistent across three domains but based on a single small verifier).
- *High scores reflect retrieval-plus-entailment*: **Medium** (inferred from pattern distribution and error analysis; assumes LLMAggreFact is representative).
