# Benchmarking Temporal Web3 Intelligence: Lessons from the FinSurvival 2025 Challenge

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.23159
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23159
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** A domain-aware, hierarchical feature engineering approach with XGBoost survival models achieved a mean Concordance Index of 0.914 across 16 DeFi user behavior prediction tasks, significantly outperforming generic deep learning.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the FinSurvival 2025 Challenge, the first reproducible benchmark for temporal analytics in Web3 that explicitly handles censoring and non-stationarity. Constructed from 21.8 million Aave v3 transactions, the benchmark defines 16 survival prediction tasks (e.g., time to liquidation). The core finding is that models relying on extensive, domain-specific hierarchical feature engineering to reconstruct latent user states and market context dramatically outperform end-to-end deep learning approaches. The winning solution's success demonstrates that for long-horizon, non-stationary Web3 behavior modeling, explicit temporal state representation is critical, and tree-based survival models aligned with ranking-based metrics provide a robust foundation.

## Method Summary
The study built a temporal survival benchmark from Aave v3 on Polygon data, framing user behavior transitions (e.g., deposit to borrow) as time-to-event prediction problems with right-censoring. A strict temporal split trained models on data from March 2022–July 2024 and tested on July 2024–August 2025 to prevent leakage and test generalization under regime shift. The primary evaluation metric was the Concordance Index (C-index), which measures the model's ability to correctly rank risk scores for comparable user pairs. Competitors were provided with a base set of 90 features but encouraged to engineer their own. The top-performing approach involved creating over 10,000 features through hierarchical aggregation (user, market, transaction) across multiple temporal windows, followed by training an XGBoost model with a survival objective (AFT or Cox) for each task.

## Key Results
*   The winning solution achieved a **mean C-index of 0.914** across all 16 tasks, a substantial margin over other teams and baseline deep survival models.
*   **Domain-aware hierarchical feature engineering** was the primary performance driver, enabling the reconstruction of latent user states (e.g., risk tolerance) from sparse transaction streams.
*   **Tree-based survival models (XGBoost)** that directly optimize ranking consistency under censoring aligned perfectly with the C-index metric and proved more stable than deep learning alternatives on this non-stationary data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Domain-aware hierarchical feature engineering reconstructs latent user states and market context from sparse transaction streams, enabling models to capture long-term behavioral trajectories under censoring.
*   **Mechanism:** The winning solution generated >10,000 features via multi-level aggregation (user, market, transaction) with temporal windows and decay, explicitly encoding cumulative exposure and cross-market dependencies. This externalizes the inference of latent variables (e.g., risk tolerance) that end-to-end models must learn from irregular, sparse events.
*   **Core assumption:** Latent user states are sufficiently approximated by historical behavioral aggregates when aligned with contemporaneous market conditions.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract]: "domain-aware, hierarchical feature engineering (generating over 10,000 features) with XGBoost-based survival models substantially outperformed generic end-to-end deep learning approaches."
    *   [section 4.2.1]: "DeFi user behavior is highly stateful but sparsely observed... Hierarchical feature construction effectively externalizes this inference process by encoding cumulative exposure, rolling statistics, and cross-market dependencies as explicit covariates."

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Tree-based survival models (XGBoost AFT/Cox) directly optimize ranking-based metrics (C-index) and naturally handle heterogeneous, non-linear interactions between temporal features, avoiding instability in risk ordering.
*   **Mechanism:** XGBoost’s survival objectives maximize concordance under censoring during training, aligning perfectly with the evaluation metric. This contrasts with deep survival models that optimize partial likelihood but require careful regularization to stabilize risk rankings on sparse, non-stationary data.
*   **Core assumption:** The C-index is an appropriate measure of temporal predictive value for long-horizon, censorship-heavy tasks.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 3.1]: "The C-index measures whether the model’s predictions correctly order comparable pairs... This metric is robust to censoring... This formulation reflects a general pattern in temporal web analytics where outcomes are sparse, delayed, or censored."
    *   [section 4.2.1]: "Survival objectives in tree-based models such as AFT and Cox variants of XGBoost naturally align with the concordance-based evaluation metric."

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Strict temporal train-test splits force models to learn temporal dynamics and avoid leakage from future market regimes, exposing non-stationarity as the primary generalization challenge.
*   **Mechanism:** By training on data up to July 2024 and testing on subsequent data, the benchmark prevents models from exploiting static patterns that do not persist. This penalizes end-to-end deep models that tend to overfit historical noise, while feature-engineered approaches with market-normalized statistics generalize better.
*   **Core assumption:** Future protocol and market conditions will exhibit sufficient continuity with the past for temporal generalization to be meaningful, yet enough drift to require explicit temporal modeling.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 3.3]: "We chose a temporal split to avoid leakage in the test set... it would be possible for the model to learn market trends from the training data that it would not be able to learn in a real deployment."
    *   [section 4.2.1]: "The use of a strict temporal train-test split amplifies the impact of non-stationarity."

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Survival Analysis with Right-Censoring**
    *   **Why needed here:** The tasks predict time-to-event (e.g., liquidation) but many users have no outcome by the observation window’s end. Ignoring censored instances would bias the dataset, as survival times for these users are only known to exceed the window.
    *   **Quick check question:** How would model training be affected if you discarded all censored user instances instead of using a survival loss?
*   **Concept: Temporal Non-Stationarity in Financial Systems**
    *   **Why needed here:** DeFi user behavior and protocol parameters evolve due to market cycles, governance changes, and competitor launches. Models must not assume identically distributed samples over time.
    *   **Quick check question:** What artifacts in a randomly shuffled train-test split (vs. temporal split) would falsely inflate model performance?
*   **Concept: Feature Engineering for Stateful, Sparse Event Streams**
    *   **Why needed here:** On-chain transactions are discrete, irregular, and lack explicit state labels. Raw sequences are insufficient; engineered features (e.g., rolling 30-day borrow sum, time-since-last-deposit) approximate continuous state variables like liquidity risk or engagement.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why might a recurrent neural network (RNN) struggle to learn user risk states from raw transaction sequences compared to a feature-based approach?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Data Ingestion (Raw Transactions) -> Feature Engineering Module (Hierarchical Aggregation) -> Model Training (XGBoost Survival) -> Evaluation Pipeline (Temporal C-index)
*   **Critical path:** Feature engineering -> model training -> evaluation. Feature construction is the bottleneck and primary performance driver; errors here cascade.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Feature complexity:** Provided 90 features (accessible) vs. custom 10,000+ (high performance, high maintenance).
    *   **Model choice:** XGBoost (robust, aligned with C-index) vs. deep survival models (flexible but unstable under non-stationarity).
    *   **Temporal granularity:** Aggregation window sizes (e.g., 7d vs. 30d) trade sensitivity vs. noise.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Consistently low C-index on `*→Liquidated` tasks → missing market volatility or leverage features.
    *   High dev-set but low test-set performance → temporal leakage in feature computation (e.g., using future price data).
    *   Model sensitivity to minor feature changes → overfitting due to insufficient regularization in XGBoost.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Baseline replication:** Train XGBoost Cox on the provided 90 features with default hyperparameters; expect C-index ~0.75–0.80.
    2.  **Simple temporal features:** Add rolling statistics (e.g., 7-day average borrow amount, time-since-last transaction) to the provided set; expect modest gain (~0.02–0.03 C-index).
    3.  **Hierarchical reconstruction attempt:** Implement a simplified version of the winning approach: per-user aggregates over 30/90-day windows, plus market-wide TVL and asset price percentiles. Validate no leakage by ensuring all features are strictly pre-index. Expect significant gain if done correctly (~0.85+ C-index).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** To what extent do the performance advantages of hierarchical feature engineering generalize beyond Aave v3 on Polygon to other DeFi protocols with different mechanics?
    *   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper identifies the focus on a single protocol family as a key limitation, noting it may bias models toward specific liquidation rules and incentive structures.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Protocols vary in design (e.g., collateralization, oracle usage, tokenomics), which could alter the relevance and construction of temporal aggregates like user history features.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Multi-protocol benchmark datasets with standardized survival tasks, comparing feature engineering effectiveness and model transferability across heterogeneous DeFi systems.
*   **Open Question 2:** Can off-chain data (e.g., social sentiment, governance events, macroeconomic news) improve survival predictions in DeFi, and what methods ensure its temporally faithful integration?
    *   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations section explicitly cites the absence of off-chain influences such as user sentiment and governance decisions as a gap.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Off-chain signals are noisy, temporally misaligned with on-chain events, and their predictive value for time-to-event outcomes remains untested in survival models.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Curated datasets with precise timestamps linking off-chain events to on-chain user actions, evaluated through fusion techniques that respect censoring and non-stationarity.
*   **Open Question 3:** Is the Concordance Index sufficient for evaluating survival models in temporal Web3 benchmarks, or should complementary metrics (e.g., calibration, time-specific error) be adopted?
    *   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states evaluation is limited to C-index, which emphasizes ranking but does not assess calibration or absolute time prediction accuracy.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Risk-sensitive applications (e.g., liquidation prevention) require both relative risk ordering and accurate time estimates; over-reliance on C-index may hide critical deficiencies.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative experiments showing model rankings under C-index versus calibration/time-error metrics, coupled with case studies on downstream decision quality.

## Limitations
*   **Opaque winning feature pipeline:** The exact definitions, temporal windows, and logic for the 10,000+ features used by the winning team are not disclosed, blocking precise replication.
*   **Single-protocol validation:** Findings are based solely on Aave v3 on Polygon; generalization to other DeFi protocols or blockchain ecosystems is unverified.
*   **Two-stage training opacity:** The precise hyperparameter tuning and split between global and task-specific stages for the XGBoost AFT model are not detailed.

## Confidence
*   **Feature Engineering Superiority:** **High Confidence.** The massive performance gap on a rigorous temporal benchmark strongly supports the claim, even without the full feature specification.
*   **Tree Model Alignment with C-index:** **Medium Confidence.** The theoretical alignment is sound, but the relative contribution vs. feature quality is not cleanly isolated in results.
*   **Temporal Split as Key Stress Test:** **High Confidence.** The methodological choice is explicitly justified and standard for time-series evaluation to prevent leakage.
*   **Generalization to Other Web3 Contexts:** **Low Confidence.** This is an inferred claim; external validity requires testing on different ecosystems, which the paper does not do.

## Next Checks
1.  **Feature Audit & Leakage Test:** Replicate the provided 90-base-feature baseline. Then, implement a candidate set of hierarchical features (e.g., user 30/90-day rolling sums). Rigorously audit all code to ensure **zero future data leakage** by confirming every feature value at index time *t* uses only data from *< t*.
2.  **Ablation Study on Feature Granularity:** Train models with incrementally more sophisticated feature groups (base -> simple rolling -> full hierarchical). Measure the marginal C-index gain per group to quantify the contribution of "state reconstruction."
3.  **Stress Test on a New Temporal Split:** Re-run the best reproduced model on a **different, later temporal split** (e.g., train: Mar 2022–Jan 2025, test: Jan 2025–Aug 2025). A significant drop in C-index would confirm sensitivity to regime change; stable performance would suggest overfitting to the original test period.
