# Robustness, Cost, and Attack-Surface Concentration in Phishing Detection
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19204
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19204
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** Phishing detectors achieve near-perfect held-out accuracy but are fragile under adversarial feature manipulation, with evasion cost bounded by a small set of low-cost surface features and formal invariance across model architectures.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies adversarial evasion in phishing detection using a cost-aware framework. Evaluated on the UCI Phishing Websites benchmark, all tested classifiers (Logistic Regression, Random Forests, GBDT, XGBoost) achieve high AUC (≥0.979) under static evaluation but exhibit low median minimal evasion cost (MEC) of 2 under budgeted monotone feature edits. Over 80% of successful minimal-cost evasions concentrate on three low-cost surface features, yielding high robustness concentration index (RCI) values. A formal cost-floor bound shows that when a nontrivial fraction of phishing instances admit single-feature evasion at minimal cost c_min, no classifier can raise the median MEC above c_min without altering the feature representation or cost model. Under strict cost schedules that block cheap infrastructure upgrades, infeasible mass (instances with MEC = ∞) rises to 17–19% for ensemble models, but the median MEC among evadable instances remains unchanged. The findings indicate that adversarial robustness in this setting is governed by feature economics rather than model complexity.

## Method Summary
The authors train classifiers on a 75% stratified split of the UCI Phishing Websites dataset (30 ternary features). For each phishing instance correctly detected in the 25% test split (conditioning set P0), they compute minimal evasion cost (MEC) via uniform-cost search over the discrete feature graph under a monotone edit constraint (only increasing feature values toward legitimate). The cost schedule assigns nonnegative costs to each admissible edit; two schedules are studied: base (reflecting typical attacker effort) and strict (making infrastructure upgrades costly). From the MEC distribution, they derive survival curves S(B), robustness concentration index (RCI_k, fraction of edits on top-k features), and FirstTop1 (fraction whose first edit is on the most common feature). The cost-floor bound (Proposition 3.1) is proven and empirically validated. Experiments vary feature subsets (removing surface features), cost schedules, and model architectures to test robustness concentration and the invariance of median MEC.

## Key Results
- All classifiers achieve AUC ≥ 0.979 on held-out test data, but median MEC converges to 2 under the base cost schedule.
- Over 80% of successful minimal-cost evasions concentrate on three low-cost surface features (RCI₃ ≥ 0.78 across all models).
- The cost-floor bound holds: median MEC cannot exceed c_min = 1 without changing the feature representation or cost model, regardless of classifier architecture.
- Under the strict cost schedule (blocking cheap infrastructure edits), ensemble models exhibit 17–19% infeasible mass, while the median MEC among evadable instances remains 2.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Cost-floor bound invariance.**  
If a nontrivial fraction α of correctly detected phishing instances admit a single-feature edit at minimal cost c_min, then Pr(MEC ≤ c_min) ≥ α (Proposition 3.1). Consequently, the median MEC cannot exceed c_min without altering the feature set or cost schedule. This is observed empirically: with c_min = 1 (e.g., changing SSLfinal_State from -1 to 0), median MEC = 2 across all classifiers, consistent with the bound. The invariance fails only if (a) all c_min features are removed, (b) c_min is increased via cost rescheduling, or (c) α = 0.

**Mechanism 2: Attack surface concentration.**  
Heterogeneous edit costs and feature importance cause shortest-path search to preferentially traverse features with low cost and high influence on the decision boundary. As a result, minimal-cost evasion traces concentrate on a few surface features; over 80% of evasions use only three features (RCI₃ ≥ 0.78). Concentration persists even when surface feature costs are scaled up (λ_surf = 2, 4) but dissipates if those features are removed or costs are equalized, confirming the role of cost asymmetry.

**Mechanism 3: Infeasible mass without median shift.**  
When a dominant low-cost transition (e.g., SSLfinal_State upgrade) serves as a primary bottleneck for many instances, blocking it (cost → ∞) renders a fraction of instances unevadable (infeasible mass increases). However, for evadable instances, the next-cheapest path remains unchanged, so the conditional median MEC stays the same. This effect requires a truly singular bottleneck; if the attack surface is diffuse, blocking one path may shift median MEC upward.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Classifier (f) -> Feature Extractor -> Cost Schedule -> Attacker Model (Monotone Edits) -> MEC Calculator (Uniform-Cost Search) -> Diagnostics (MEC, RCI, Survival)

Critical path:
1. Train classifier on 75% stratified split of UCI Phishing Websites dataset.
2. Identify P0 = {x in 25% test split: f(x) = -1} (correctly detected phishing).
3. For each x ∈ P0 (or uniform sample of size 300), run uniform-cost search up to B_max = 18 to compute MEC(x).
4. Aggregate MEC values across P0: median, quartiles, survival function S(B) at integer B.
5. For instances with finite MEC, extract canonical minimal-cost trace (via priority queue tie-breaking), count edits per feature (N_j) and first-edited feature.
6. Compute RCI_k (fraction of edits on top-k features) and FirstTop1.
7. Compute cost-floor: α_cmin = fraction of P0 with MEC ≤ c_min (c_min = min transition cost = 1).
8. Count infeasible mass: instances with MEC = ∞.

Design tradeoffs:
- Monotone edits only: simplifies attacker action space and aligns with short campaign lifetimes, but underestimates capability (non-monotone attacks could be cheaper).
- Ternary feature representation: enables discrete state transitions but may oversimplify continuous website characteristics.
- Fixed 0.5 decision threshold: ignores model confidence; using a margin could alter MEC distribution.
- B_max = 18: covers most evasions but is arbitrary; higher budgets might reveal longer paths.
- Conditioning on P0: isolates post-detection evasion but ignores false negatives (already evaded without cost).

Failure signatures:
- High median MEC (≫ c_min) would contradict the cost-floor mechanism; paper finds median MEC = 2 (consistent with c_min=1).
- Low RCI₃ (e.g., <0.3) would indicate diffuse attack surface; paper finds RCI₃ >0.8 (concentrated).
- Large gap between exact MEC and query-limited greedy search would mean attackers need many queries to find minimal paths; gaps are small (≤0.08 at B=2).
- Removing surface features does not increase median MEC: would suggest other features provide equally cheap paths, possibly weakening cost-floor claim.

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate Table 5 for GBDT on Full/base and Full/strict: verify median MEC = 2, survival curves, and infeasible mass (0% in base, 17-19% in strict).
2. Compute RCI₃ and FirstTop1 for Full/base vs RA-8/base: observe concentration increase (RCI₃ 0.89→1.00, FirstTop1 0.37→0.99).
3. Surface cost scaling (λ_surf = 2,4) on Full/base: confirm median MEC scales to 4 and 8 while RCI₃ remains >0.82, validating cost-floor regime (Table 8).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do non-monotone feature edits (e.g., anti-feature injection, extractor-level manipulation) quantitatively affect the cost floor and robustness concentration index in phishing detection? The current threat model assumes only monotone sanitization edits, providing a conservative lower bound; non-monotone actions enlarge the feasible set and may lower MEC.
2. Does the observed architecture-independent robustness convergence (median MEC ≈ 2) hold when evaluated on modern phishing datasets that incorporate updated features like certificate transparency logs, visual similarity, and JavaScript behavioral fingerprints? The UCI Phishing Websites benchmark is dated, and quantitative transfer requires re-validation.
3. How can the dimensionless cost units in the schedule be empirically calibrated to real-world attacker budgets, such as monetary costs or operational time constraints, to enhance threat modeling accuracy? The schedule represents operational friction; translating to market-level budgets remains an open empirical problem.

## Limitations
- **Single dataset:** All experiments use the UCI Phishing Websites dataset with 30 ternary features; generalizability to other feature sets or continuous representations is unknown.
- **Attacker model assumptions:** Assumes monotone edits only, exact cost knowledge, and no model inference; real attackers may use non-monotone changes, approximate costs, or adaptive queries.
- **Cost schedule subjectivity:** Base and strict cost assignments are manually defined without empirical grounding in real attacker effort.

## Confidence
- **Cost-floor bound invariance:** High (formal proof with supporting experiments; generalizability beyond tested setting uncertain).
- **Attack surface concentration:** High (consistently observed across all classifiers on the dataset; external validity limited).
- **Infeasible mass increase without median MEC change:** Medium (empirical evidence strong but conditional on a dominant bottleneck feature; may not hold if attack surface is diffuse).
- **Feature economics dominating model complexity:** Medium (based on comparative results across conventional models; lacks evidence from deep architectures or other domains).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-dataset replication:** Apply the full framework (MEC, RCI, cost-floor analysis) to at least two additional phishing datasets (e.g., PhishTank, OpenPhish) with different feature engineering to test persistence of concentration and cost-floor phenomena.
2. **Non-monotone attack extension:** Modify the attacker model to permit both increasing and decreasing feature values (within cost constraints) and recompute MEC distributions. Evaluate whether the cost-floor bound still holds and whether concentration remains pronounced.
3. **Deep model evaluation:** Train and evaluate state-of-the-art deep phishing detectors (e.g., CNN/RNN on raw URLs or HTML) under the same cost schedules. Compare their MEC distributions and RCI to conventional models to directly test the claim that model complexity does not govern robustness.
