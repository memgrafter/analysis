# Open, to What End? A Capability-Theoretic Perspective on Open Search
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14584
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14584
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Open search initiatives that focus solely on releasing open artifacts (code, data) without addressing the varying conversion factors (resources, power, governance) across actors will fail to deliver equitable capabilities, particularly for marginalized communities; true openness requires opening social infrastructure like compute, labor, and governance to enable co-construction.

## Executive Summary
This paper argues that the open search movement risks co-option and failure by measuring success through the openness of components (e.g., code, data) rather than the practical capabilities those openings afford different actors. Drawing on Amartya Sen’s Capability Approach, the authors reframe openness around the *functionings* (critique, copy, co-construct) it enables for specific stakeholders pursuing well-being goals (e.g., sovereignty, justice). A central finding is that identical open commodities yield sharply divergent capabilities due to differences in actors’ circumstances (resources, skills, power). Therefore, merely open-sourcing code or releasing public data is insufficient. To achieve justice-oriented outcomes, open search must deliberately open associated social infrastructure—including compute access, labor pools, and governance structures—to empower marginalized communities to co-construct systems, not just copy them.

## Method Summary
The research applies a theoretical framework (Sen’s Capability Approach) to critique and reconceptualize “open search.” The method involves: (1) defining target well-being goals (e.g., epistemic justice, digital sovereignty); (2) deriving necessary *functionings* (the actionable freedoms to critique, copy, or co-construct a search system) for those goals; (3) inventorying relevant *commodities* (artifacts like code, data, models, but also compute, labor, and governance mechanisms); and (4) analyzing how an actor’s unique *conversion factors* (personal, social, environmental circumstances) transform available commodities into realized capabilities. This lens exposes how identical “open” artifacts can produce inequitable outcomes, as resource-rich actors convert commodities into powerful functionings while marginalized actors cannot. The synthesis is normative, proposing that equitable open search requires intentionally opening the social infrastructure that serves as key conversion factors.

## Key Results
- Openness must be evaluated by the capabilities (critique, copy, co-construct) it affords diverse actors, not by the mere availability of open components.
- Identical open commodities generate divergent capabilities across actors due to vastly different conversion factors (resources, power, skills); thus, open-source licenses or public datasets alone are insufficient for equity.
- For justice-oriented well-being goals (e.g., epistemic justice), the functioning of *co-construction*—the ability to modify the platform itself—is indispensable; copying alone cannot repair representational harms embedded in hegemonic systems.
- Achieving equitable capabilities requires opening social infrastructure (compute, labor, governance) to lower conversion barriers for marginalized communities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The capability-theoretic reframing works by shifting the unit of analysis from artifacts to human/collective agency. It reveals that “open” is not a property of an object but a relation between an artifact, its surrounding infrastructure, and an actor’s circumstances.

**Mechanism:** The framework maps a chain: *Commodities* (code, data, compute, labor, governance) + *Conversion Factors* (actor’s resources, skills, social context, power) → *Capabilities* (real freedom to achieve *functionings* like critique, copy, co-construct) → *Well-being Goals* (sovereignty, justice). Openness is successful only if it moves actors along this chain toward their defined well-beings. The mechanism highlights that opening only technical artifacts (code/data) leaves critical conversion factors (e.g., expensive compute, decision-making power) untouched, allowing powerful actors to capture benefits while marginalized groups remain capability-deprived. True open search must therefore open the social infrastructure that serves as a conversion factor.

**Core Assumption:** Capabilities are context-dependent; an actor’s personal, social, and environmental characteristics profoundly mediate whether an open commodity translates into an actionable freedom. Equity requires adjusting these conversion factors, not just donating artifacts.

**Break Condition:** If an initiative provides open commodities but target actors lack necessary conversion factors (e.g., no compute budget, no governance seat, no technical labor), the desired functionings—especially co-construction—remain unachievable. The initiative then risks open-washing and reinforcing existing power asymmetries.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Well-being Goal Registry -> Functionings Catalog -> Commodities Inventory -> Actor Profiler -> Capability Evaluator -> Gap Analyzer -> Decision Support Dashboard

**Critical Path:**
1. Stakeholder alignment: Define and rank well-being goals.
2. Functioning derivation: Translate each goal into necessary, measurable functionings (critique, copy, co-construct).
3. Commodity audit: Assess current openness of the target system’s artifacts and infrastructure.
4. Actor segmentation: Identify and profile representative actor groups (e.g., community NGOs, startups, academics).
5. Capability evaluation: For each actor, estimate capability scores (0-1) per functioning based on commodities and conversion factors.
6. Gap identification: Compare capability scores against thresholds required by selected well-being goals; pinpoint missing commodities or conversion factors.
7. Action planning: Prioritize which components (e.g., compute credits, labor access, governance seats) to open to close capability gaps.
8. Iterate: Re-evaluate capabilities after openings to assess impact.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- Granularity of access: Full source code vs. API-only. Full source enables deep critique/co-construction but increases security risks and maintenance burden for the provider.
- Licensing: Permissive licenses (e.g., Apache 2.0) lower adoption barriers for copying and co-construction, while restrictive licenses (e.g., non-commercial) can block commercial deployment or modification, undermining certain functionings.
- Resource allocation: Opening compute or labor pools requires sustained subsidy and management, conflicting with pure “hands-off” open-source models but is necessary for equitable capability building.

**Failure Signatures:**
- Capability gap persists despite commodity openness (e.g., code is open but actors lack compute to run it).
- Powerful corporations capture the vast majority of open commodities (e.g., using open data to train proprietary models) while marginalized groups see negligible benefit.
- Open-washing: Artifacts are nominally open but with restrictive terms, poor documentation, or de facto access barriers (e.g., code available but binaries gated).
- Governance capture: Decision-making bodies remain controlled by original maintainers, structurally blocking community co-construction attempts.
- Metric failure: Success is defined by vanity metrics (stars, downloads) rather than actual capability improvements for target actors.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. Comparative case study: Apply the framework to evaluate the Open Web Index (EU) versus Google Custom Search JSON API. Profile two actor groups (a university research lab, a small local journalism nonprofit) and measure capability differences for the functionings of critique, copy, and co-construct.
2. Resource constraint simulation: Build a simulation with synthetic actor profiles varying in compute budget, technical skill, and network. Test interventions: (a) open code only, (b) code + free compute credits, (c) code + compute + labor pool access. Measure the percentage of resource-poor actors achieving the co-construction functioning.
3. Participatory governance pilot: Collaborate with a marginalized community organization to co-design a governance module (e.g., voting on content moderation rules) for an open search prototype. Conduct pre/post surveys on perceived capability to influence the system and track actual changes in platform decisions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can we measure the capabilities (critique, copy, co-construct) enabled by open search for different actors? The paper proposes the capability lens but offers no operationalization or standardized metrics, leaving projects vulnerable to open-washing.
- **Open Question 2:** What openness configurations (which commodities, how opened) are needed to enable marginalized communities to co-construct search systems, rather than merely copying them? The paper identifies the need to open social infrastructure but gives no specific design guidance.
- **Open Question 3:** How can open search initiatives be governed to resist co-option by powerful actors and ensure equitable capability distribution across actor groups? The paper warns of co-option but does not propose concrete governance models.

## Limitations
- The framework provides no validated quantitative metrics or assessment tools to measure and compare capability levels across actors in practice.
- It lacks empirical case studies demonstrating successful “opening” of social infrastructure (governance, labor) that enabled marginalized co-construction; the argument remains largely theoretical.
- The necessity of the *co-construction* functioning for all justice-oriented goals is asserted but not empirically tested; for some well-being goals, copying might suffice.

## Confidence
- **High:** The theoretical argument that openness must be evaluated via capabilities (not just commodities) is sound and well-grounded in Sen’s Capability Approach.
- **Medium:** The claim that co-construction is a *necessary* functioning for justice is logically compelling but empirically undertested; its universal necessity across contexts is uncertain.
- **Medium:** The assertion that identical open commodities yield divergent capabilities is empirically plausible and theoretically consistent, but requires comparative actor profiling studies for robust validation.
- **Low:** Proposals for opening social infrastructure (compute, labor, governance) are suggestive but lack concrete, replicable design patterns or evidence of real-world feasibility.
