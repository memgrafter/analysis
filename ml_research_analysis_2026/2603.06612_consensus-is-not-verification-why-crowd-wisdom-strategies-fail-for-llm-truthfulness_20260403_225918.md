# Consensus is Not Verification: Why Crowd Wisdom Strategies Fail for LLM Truthfulness
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.06612
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06612
- **Reference count**: 19
- **Primary result**: Scaling inference compute via crowd aggregation (e.g., majority voting) fails to improve language model truthfulness in domains lacking external verifiers.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether scaling inference compute through crowd aggregation (such as majority voting, confidence-weighted voting, and the Surprisingly Popular method) can enhance the truthfulness of large language models in settings where no external ground truth is available. Testing five aggregation methods across four binary benchmarks and five models, the authors find no consistent accuracy gains compared to single-sample baselines, even when increasing compute by a factor of 25. The core reason is that model errors are strongly correlated—both within a single model across samples and across different models—due to shared training data and inductive biases. This correlation violates the independence assumption underlying the “wisdom of crowds” effect, causing shared misconceptions to be amplified rather than cancelled. Moreover, confidence and surprise signals, which might serve as internal verifiers, are found to track expected consensus rather than actual correctness. A negative control using random string prompts confirms that correlated biases persist even without any factual ground truth. The study delineates a key boundary: aggregation can scale truthfulness only when an external verifier (e.g., human labels, execution) exists; in unverifiable domains, it systematically degrades performance.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluated five aggregation rules—majority vote, highest confidence, confidence-weighted, prediction-weighted, and Surprisingly Popular—on four binary question-answering benchmarks (Com2Sense, HLE, BoolQ, Predict-the-Future) using five instruction-tuned language models (Gemma-3-4B, GPT-OSS-20B, GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-32B, Qwen3-235B). For each model and question, they sampled 25 responses at two temperatures (0.7, 1.0), extracting three response types: a direct binary answer, a confidence score (0–100), and a predicted vote share for each option. These were aggregated per question according to each rule and compared to ground truth to compute accuracy. To diagnose error patterns, they measured inter-model agreement (Cohen’s κ), vote entropy, temperature flip rates, and confidence calibration. A random-string prompt condition served as a negative control to assess correlation without ground truth, and a forecasting benchmark (Predict-the-Future) tested performance on events resolved after model cutoffs. The main comparison was between aggregated accuracy and the average accuracy of single samples from the same models.

## Key Results
- No aggregation method produced consistent accuracy improvements over single-sample baselines on verifier-absent benchmarks, even with 25× compute (5 models × 5 samples each).
- Errors are highly correlated both within and across model families (Cohen’s κ > 0.3 on random strings), violating the independence assumption needed for crowd wisdom.
- Confidence and surprise signals fail to track correctness; they instead reflect expected consensus, rendering confidence-weighted voting ineffective.
- Aggregation only shows benefits when an external verifier is present; in unverifiable settings it amplifies shared misconceptions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper identifies three interconnected mechanisms explaining why crowd aggregation fails to improve truthfulness in unverifiable domains:

1. **Correlated Errors**: Aggregation fails because language model errors are strongly correlated, violating the independence assumption required for crowd wisdom. *Mechanism*: Models trained on overlapping corpora and optimized for similar objectives develop shared priors and blind spots, causing them to converge on the same incorrect answers when uncertain. *Core assumption*: Crowd wisdom assumes errors are independent; correlated errors amplify rather than cancel. *Evidence*: [abstract], [section 4.2] show errors are highly correlated both within and across model families. *Break condition*: If errors were independent (e.g., from models with disjoint training data and objectives), aggregation could improve accuracy.

2. **Confidence Misalignment**: Confidence and surprise signals track expected consensus rather than correctness, making them poor verifiers. *Mechanism*: Self-reported confidence and predicted vote shares are influenced by training objectives that reward common outputs (sycophancy) and by models' ability to predict collective behavior, not by epistemic accuracy. *Core assumption*: Using internal signals as proxies for truth assumes they correlate with correctness; misalignment breaks this. *Evidence*: [abstract], [section 4.1] show confidence does not track correctness and surprise signals do not reliably track truth. *Break condition*: If confidence were well-calibrated and surprise gaps consistently pointed to truth, these signals could aid aggregation.

3. **Social vs. Truth Prediction**: Models are better at social prediction (forecasting what others will say) than at truth verification, creating a separation that invalidates aggregation. *Mechanism*: Models can anticipate the distribution of responses across a crowd (due to shared priors) but cannot internally verify correctness; thus, signals based on consensus reflect social prediction, not truth. *Core assumption*: Aggregation relies on the crowd's answers containing a truth signal; if the crowd is systematically wrong and models cannot detect that, aggregation fails. *Evidence*: [abstract], [section 4.4] show models predict collective opinion substantially better than they predict correctness. *Break condition*: If models could reliably predict which answers are correct (not just popular), aggregation might work.

## Foundational Learning
- **Independence of errors in ensemble methods**: Needed to understand why aggregation fails; crowd wisdom theory requires errors to be independent for improvement. *Quick check*: If two models always give the same wrong answer on a question, does majority voting improve accuracy over a single model? - **Confidence calibration in language models**: Needed to interpret confidence-weighted voting; poorly calibrated confidence cannot distinguish correct from incorrect answers. *Quick check*: What does it mean for a model's confidence to be 'calibrated'? Give an example of mis-calibration in LLMs.
- **Social prediction vs. truth verification**: Needed to grasp the paper's disentanglement of models' ability to predict consensus versus correctness. *Quick check*: Can a model's prediction of how other models will vote be used as a proxy for the correct answer? Under what conditions might this succeed or fail?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Inputs (benchmarks, model cards) -> Generators (sample answer, confidence, predicted votes) -> Parsers (extract structured outputs) -> Aggregators (apply five voting rules) -> Evaluators (compute accuracy, correlation, calibration) -> Controls (random strings, forecasting benchmark).

**Critical path**: 1. Load benchmark splits and verify binary format. 2. For each model and temperature (0.7, 1.0), generate 25 responses per question for all three response types. 3. Parse responses into structured data; log parsing failures. 4. Apply each aggregation method to compute per-question aggregated answers. 5. Compare aggregated answers to ground truth to compute accuracy. 6. Compute diagnostic metrics: cross-model agreement, confidence-accuracy alignment, surprise gap, temperature stability. 7. Repeat for all benchmarks and ensemble configurations.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- Binary questions simplify aggregation but limit generalizability to open-ended generation.
- Forced-choice ensures discrete votes but may not reflect natural output distributions.
- Moderate-high temperatures (0.7, 1.0) are used to induce diversity; however flip rates remain low, indicating limited diversity.
- 25 samples per model balance cost and diversity; more samples may not yield independent errors.
- Model selection uses open-source models; proprietary models could behave differently.

**Failure signatures**:
- >5% unclear responses indicate parsing issues or model non-compliance.
- Cohen’s κ on random strings near zero would contradict the paper’s finding of correlated biases.
- Majority voting improving >5% absolute on verifier-absent benchmarks would contradict the main result.
- Strong confidence calibration (accuracy ≈ confidence) would be unexpected given the paper.
- Temperature flip rate >10% might indicate successful diversity induction, unlike the paper’s low rates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Does incorporating external grounding mechanisms (retrieval, tool use, execution, or human feedback) enable aggregation methods to improve truthfulness in domains lacking an external verifier?** The study only examined pure polling without any external grounding; it remains unknown whether such interventions can break error correlation or provide a reliable verification signal.
- **Can engineering genuine epistemic diversity—through disjoint training data, objectives, or architectural changes—sufficiently reduce correlated errors to make crowd aggregation beneficial?** The paper tested models with overlapping training and similar architectures, finding strong error correlation. It did not systematically vary diversity; the minimum diversity needed for aggregation gains is unknown.
- **Can learning explicit verifiers from externally labeled evidence allow inference-time scaling of truthfulness without requiring a verifier at test time?** The study focused on aggregation rules that rely solely on internal signals (agreement, confidence, predicted popularity). The potential of a learned verifier (trained offline) to replace external verification during inference was not explored.
- **Do the failure of aggregation methods extend to open-ended generation tasks where answer spaces are not constrained to a finite set of options?** All experiments used forced binary choices; open-ended generation introduces additional factors such as answer variation, potential for implicit verification, and different forms of agreement measurement. The correlation structure and effectiveness of aggregation could differ.

## Limitations
- **Predict-the-Future dataset**: Unreleased; exact construction and sourcing are unspecified, limiting exact replication.
- **Model specifications**: Exact checkpoints, quantization, and context windows are not detailed; using open-source variants may yield variance.
- **Benchmark scope**: Binary, forced-choice questions only; findings may not generalize to open-ended generation tasks.

## Confidence
- **Correlated errors (Mechanism 1)**: Medium (strong in-paper evidence but replication gaps reduce certainty).
- **Confidence misalignment (Mechanism 2)**: High (direct results and known calibration issues support this robustly).
- **Social vs. truth prediction (Mechanism 3)**: Medium (novel disentanglement but requires cross-validation with broader model crowds).

## Next Checks
1. **Correlation persistence**: Compute Cohen’s κ on random-string prompts across all model pairs; expect κ > 0.3 to confirm correlated biases without ground truth.
2. **Independence test**: Use models with disjoint training data (e.g., different corpora/objectives) and re-evaluate majority voting; independence should yield accuracy gains.
3. **Boundary verification**: Introduce an external verifier (e.g., human labels) for a subset of "unverifiable" benchmarks; aggregation should then improve accuracy, testing the paper's claimed boundary condition.
