# AI Act Evaluation Benchmark: An Open, Transparent, and Reproducible Evaluation Dataset for NLP and RAG Systems

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.09435
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09435
- **Reference count:** 20
- **Primary result:** Demonstration RAG system achieves F1-scores of 0.87 (prohibited) and 0.85 (high-risk), with lower performance on ambiguous limited (0.65) and minimal (0.45) risk categories.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the absence of open, machine-readable resources for evaluating NLP and RAG systems against the EU AI Act, where compliance checks are currently manual and error-prone. The authors construct a dataset of 339 AI system scenarios, covering all four risk levels, by using a human-derived rule-based flowchart of the AI Act's classification logic to prompt an LLM. The scenarios and associated QA pairs are generated in structured JSON format, providing a benchmark for automated compliance evaluation. A demonstration RAG system shows strong performance on clearly defined prohibited and high-risk categories but struggles with the more ambiguously defined limited and minimal risk levels, underscoring both the dataset's potential and the inherent challenges in regulatory interpretation.

## Method Summary
The core method involves creating a flowchart that encodes the AI Act's risk classification logic based on legal interpretation. This flowchart is used to prompt the proprietary LLM `gpt-oss-120b` to generate diverse, document-grounded descriptions of hypothetical AI systems. The output is constrained to structured JSON, specifying the system's purpose, inputs, outputs, and risk category. Corresponding question-answer pairs are also generated for classification tasks. The resulting dataset is validated through limited human review to ensure basic alignment with the AI Act's text and logical consistency with the flowchart.

## Key Results
- Dataset contains 339 scenarios spanning prohibited, high-risk, limited, and minimal AI risk categories.
- Demonstration RAG system achieves F1-scores of 0.87 (prohibited) and 0.85 (high-risk) on risk-level classification.
- Performance drops significantly on ambiguous categories: F1 0.65 (limited) and 0.45 (minimal).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The rule-based flowchart acts as a structured, logical proxy for the AI Act's legal provisions, translating open-ended legal text into a series of binary decisions. Prompting an LLM with this flowchart ensures that generated scenarios are systematically grounded in specific regulatory criteria rather than free-form invention. This mechanistically bridges the gap between legal documents and concrete examples, enabling the creation of a dataset that can be used to train and evaluate systems designed to reason about compliance.

## Foundational Learning
1. **EU AI Act Risk Taxonomy**  
   - *Why needed*: To correctly map AI systems to legal obligations based on defined risk levels.  
   - *Quick check*: List the four risk categories defined by the AI Act (prohibited, high-risk, limited, minimal).  
2. **Rule-Based Regulatory Extraction**  
   - *Why needed*: To convert ambiguous legal language into executable, deterministic logic for consistent scenario generation.  
   - *Quick check*: Identify two specific conditions from the AI Act that would classify an AI system as "high-risk".  
3. **LLM-Driven Synthetic Data Generation**  
   - *Why needed*: To scale the production of diverse, realistic compliance scenarios without exhaustive manual authoring.  
   - *Quick check*: Prompt an LLM to generate a one-sentence description of a "prohibited" AI system per the AI Act.  
4. **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for Compliance**  
   - *Why needed*: To ensure model predictions are anchored in specific regulatory text, reducing hallucination and improving auditability.  
   - *Quick check*: For a given AI system description, retrieve the most relevant AI Act article concerning high-risk obligations.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Decision Flowchart -> LLM (gpt-oss-120b) -> JSON Scenarios/QA -> RAG System (Retriever + Classifier LLM)

**Critical Path:**  
Design flowchart based on AI Act -> Craft prompting strategy -> Generate & validate scenarios -> Build RAG (index articles, implement retrieval) -> Train/classify on generated QA -> Analyze performance by risk level.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Used a proprietary, high-capability LLM for generation quality vs. full reproducibility and cost.
- Relied on a single human-derived flowchart for consistency vs. potential loss of interpretive diversity.
- Generated a moderate-sized, controlled dataset vs. attempting full coverage of all possible AI systems.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Drastic performance drop on "limited" and "minimal" risk categories.
- Inconsistent JSON formatting or logically incoherent scenarios.
- Low inter-annotator agreement if multiple experts review the flowchart logic.

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. Replicate dataset generation using an open-weight LLM (e.g., Llama 3.1 405B) and compare scenario diversity, adherence to flowchart logic, and JSON validity.
2. Perform an inter-annotator agreement study where legal experts independently apply the flowchart to a sample of AI system descriptions to quantify subjectivity in the classification logic.
3. Implement a RAG system with hybrid retrieval (dense + sparse) and chain-of-thought prompting, then evaluate specifically on the "limited" and "minimal" risk QA pairs to isolate the impact of retrieval and reasoning strategies.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the AI Act dataset be validated for legal accuracy and ground truth alignment in a semi-automated, scalable manner?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state that validation with legal experts is required and propose a semi-supervised framework to reduce constant expert supervision.
- **Why unresolved:** The current dataset was generated by LLMs without systematic legal validation; potential misalignments with the AI Act could compromise its reliability for compliance evaluation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A validation study where legal experts annotate a representative sample of scenarios, measure inter-annotator agreement, and develop automated consistency checks against AI Act provisions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What techniques (e.g., prompt engineering, retrieval strategies, model architecture) most effectively improve classification performance on the ambiguous limited and minimal risk categories in the EU AI Act?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors identify that the context from AI Act articles is insufficient for predicting limited/minimal risk and list potential improvements (query rewriting, chain-of-thought, fine-grained chunking) as outside the scope of their work.
- **Why unresolved:** The baseline RAG system achieved low F1-scores for limited (0.65) and minimal (0.45) risk levels, indicating poor model comprehension due to vague definitions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments comparing various RAG enhancements on the dataset’s ambiguous categories, with statistical analysis to determine which methods yield significant performance gains.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can the dataset generation methodology be extended to incorporate evolving regulatory materials (e.g., amendments, guidelines) to maintain long-term relevance?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note that the regulatory landscape is constantly evolving and that incorporating corrections and additional material is a prominent future direction.
- **Why unresolved:** The dataset is static and based on a fixed version of the AI Act; future updates may render scenarios outdated, limiting their usefulness for ongoing compliance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A pipeline that automatically ingests new regulatory texts, updates the underlying decision flows, regenerates scenarios, and provides versioning with changelogs to track modifications over time.

## Limitations
- Dataset generation relies on a proprietary, non-public LLM (gpt-oss-120b), introducing opacity and hindering full reproducibility.
- The human-derived rule-based flowchart may embed subjective interpretations of the AI Act's legally ambiguous criteria.
- Evaluation is limited to a single demonstration RAG system on a moderate-sized dataset (339 scenarios), restricting generalizability of performance claims.

## Confidence
- *Utility as a benchmarking resource*: **Medium** – Structured format and coverage are strengths, but generation dependency reduces trust in edge-case diversity.
- *Reported classification performance (F1 >0.85 for clear categories)*: **High** – Consistent with typical RAG performance on well-defined tasks.
- *Handling of ambiguous risk categories (limited/minimal)*: **Low** – Lower scores likely reflect dataset/definition challenges more than system capability.
- *Full reproducibility of dataset construction*: **Low** – Barred by use of a closed model.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate dataset generation using open-weight LLMs (e.g., Llama 3.1 405B, Mixtral) to assess consistency and identify model-specific biases.
2. Conduct inter-annotator agreement study on the rule-based flowchart with legal experts to quantify subjectivity in classification logic.
3. Expand stress-test evaluation with additional, independent RAG architectures (e.g., hybrid retrieval, fine-tuned smaller models) on the ambiguous risk categories to isolate dataset vs. method limitations.
