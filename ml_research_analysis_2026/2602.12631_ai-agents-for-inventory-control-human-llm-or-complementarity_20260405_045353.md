# AI Agents for Inventory Control: Human-LLM-OR Complementarity
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12631
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12631
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: OR→LLM yields 0.538 normalized reward (Gemini 3 Flash), a 21% improvement over OR (0.445); human-AI collaboration (Mode B) achieves 0.534, significantly outperforming other modes.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates complementarity between operations research (OR) heuristics, large language model (LLM) agents, and humans in the context of multi-period inventory control. Using the InventoryBench benchmark—comprising 1,320 synthetic and real instances—the authors compare standalone OR and LLM methods against two hybrid architectures (OR→LLM and LLM→OR) and three human-AI collaboration modes. They find that combining OR and LLM improves performance: OR→LLM achieves a normalized reward of 0.538 with Gemini 3 Flash, a 21% improvement over OR alone (0.445). Disaggregated results show LLMs excel under stochastic lead times and demand shifts, while OR heuristics are superior in deterministic settings. In a classroom experiment with 69 participants, Mode B (OR→LLM→Human) attains a normalized reward of 0.534, significantly higher than Mode A (0.466), Mode C (0.464), and automated OR→LLM (0.482). The authors formalize individual-level complementarity and prove a distribution-free lower bound on the fraction of individuals who benefit; applied to their data, at least 20.3% of participants experience positive complementarity (95% CI: 14.7%-32.9%). Population-level teams also outperform the better of human or AI alone by 10.2%.

## Method Summary
The study addresses the multi-period inventory control problem with stochastic demand, lead times (including lost orders when lead times are infinite), and per-period profit maximization. The evaluation uses InventoryBench, a benchmark with 1,320 instances: 720 synthetic scenarios spanning 10 demand pattern families (stationary, shifts, trends, etc.), three lead-time regimes (L=0, L=4, stochastic with lost orders), and three critical fractiles (ρ=0.50, 0.80, 0.95); plus 600 real-world instances from H&M, each with 47 test weeks and 5 weeks of historical demand, filtered for stable prices and positive sales. Four automated methods are implemented: (1) OR baseline (data-driven capped base-stock policy), (2) LLM agent (no OR input), (3) OR→LLM (LLM overrides OR recommendation), and (4) LLM→OR (LLM provides demand/lead-time estimates to OR). LLMs tested include Gemini 3 Flash, Grok 4.1 Fast, and GPT-5 Mini, with per-period calls using a fixed system prompt and user message containing carry-over insights and current state. The human experiment deploys a web-based game where 69 participants each manage three inventory instances under one of three modes: Mode A (OR recommendation only), Mode B (OR→LLM→Human, showing LLM reasoning), or Mode C (autonomous OR+LLM with human strategic guidance every 4 periods). Performance is measured by normalized reward (profit divided by maximal possible profit, capped at zero).

## Key Results
- OR→LLM achieves a normalized reward of 0.538 with Gemini 3 Flash, a 21% improvement over OR alone (0.445).
- In the human experiment, Mode B (OR→LLM→Human) yields 0.534 normalized reward, significantly exceeding Mode A (0.466), Mode C (0.464), and automated OR→LLM (0.482) (p<0.001).
- At least 20.3% of individuals experience positive complementarity (95% CI: 14.7%-32.9%), and population-level teams outperform the best solo performer by 10.2%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Combining OR heuristics with LLM agents creates a complementary division of labor where OR provides computational precision for stable conditions and LLMs provide adaptive contextual reasoning for non-stationary environments.
- **Mechanism**: The OR pipeline (data-driven capped base-stock policy) calculates precise order quantities based on estimated demand distributions. The LLM agent receives this OR recommendation and overrides it when it detects mismatches between the OR's stationary assumptions and observed context (e.g., demand shifts, supply disruptions, seasonality from product descriptions). The LLM's override decision integrates world knowledge and pattern recognition from textual context.
- **Core assumption**: OR heuristics are mathematically optimal under their modeling assumptions (stationary demand, known lead times) but brittle to violations; LLMs can detect and adapt to assumption violations using unstructured context.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "LLMs excel at handling stochastic lead times and demand shifts, while OR excels for deterministic lead times."
  - [section 4.3]: Performance disaggregation shows LLM→OR (LLM forecasts, OR calculates) dominates under deterministic lead times, while OR→LLM (LLM overrides OR) dominates under stochastic lead times where OR fails due to lost orders.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers focus on reinforcement learning or forecasting rather than explicit OR-LLM complementarity.
- **Break condition**: When the LLM overfits to noise in stationary settings (Example 2), OR alone outperforms LLM-based methods. The LLM's pattern-seeking can create false regime shifts, degrading performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Human judgment adds value to the OR→LLM pipeline by providing oversight that catches LLM failures, particularly in anomaly detection and sustained pattern recognition, creating true individual-level complementarity.
- **Mechanism**: In Mode B (OR→LLM→Human), humans see both the OR recommendation and the LLM's reasoning. Humans override the LLM when they detect errors the LLM missed (e.g., persistent lost orders, mis-calibrated responses to demand trends). The human acts as a "co-pilot" using the same contextual reasoning channel as the LLM but with different failure modes, leading to synergistic coverage of blind spots.
- **Core assumption**: Human and LLM contextual reasoning are correlated but imperfectly aligned; humans can identify when the LLM's interpretation of context is flawed.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 5.2.2]: Mode B significantly outperforms OR→LLM alone (p=0.000009), demonstrating human-added value.
  - [section 6.3]: Analysis of human deviations from LLM recommendations in Mode B and human guidance in Mode C shows humans intervene on the same dimensions where LLMs initially excel over OR—detecting demand trends, seasonality, and lost orders.
  - [section 6.2]: Lower bound analysis shows at least 20.3% of individuals experience positive complementarity, indicating genuine synergy beyond selection effects.
- **Break condition**: If the LLM achieves near-perfect contextual reasoning, human overrides may degrade performance. The experiment's design (LLM not perfect) is necessary for human value-add.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The measurement framework using distribution-free bounds distinguishes population-level complementarity (team outperforms best solo) from individual-level complementarity (specific individuals benefit beyond their solo or AI solo performance), avoiding selection-effect confounds.
- **Mechanism**: Since each participant experiences only one condition, direct within-person complementarity (CE_i) is unobservable. Theorem 1 provides a lower bound on P(CE_i > 0) by comparing the empirical CDFs of collaborative performance (from Mode B participants) and the best-solo benchmark (from Mode A participants combined with fixed AI performance). The maximum vertical gap where the best-solo CDF lies above the collaborative CDF gives a tight lower bound.
- **Core assumption**: The best-solo benchmark for an individual can be approximated by taking the maximum of that individual's solo performance (if observed in Mode A) and the fixed AI performance on the same instance. This ignores potential individual-AI interaction effects in the solo benchmark but is necessary due to missing data.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 6.1]: Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 define the bound mathematically.
  - [section 6.2]: Empirical application yields at least 20.3% of individuals benefit (95% CI: 14.7%-32.9%), rejecting the pure selection hypothesis.
  - [corpus]: Weak; neighboring papers on human-AI complementarity (Vaccaro et al. 2024, Hemmer et al. 2025) inform the conceptual framework but not the specific bound.
- **Break condition**: If collaborative performance distribution is a simple mixture of solo and AI performances (weak participants follow AI, strong ignore it), the bound may estimate zero even if some true complementarity exists. The bound is conservative.

## Foundational Learning
### Multi-period inventory control with lead times and lost sales
**Why needed**: The paper's problem formulation defines the sequential decision-making environment: orders placed in period t arrive after stochastic lead times ℓ_t; unmet demand is lost; profit depends on sales minus holding costs. Understanding this dynamic is essential to grasp why OR heuristics (base-stock policies) are used and where they fail.
**Quick check**: If lead time is 2 periods and an order is placed in period 3, in which period will it first be available to satisfy demand, and what happens if the lead time is infinite (lost order)?

### Capped base-stock policy (OR heuristic)
**Why needed**: This is the OR baseline. It estimates demand mean/σ over the lead time horizon, sets a base-stock level B_t = μ̂_t + z*σ̂_t using the critical fractile, and orders q_t = max(0, min(B_t - IP_t, cap)). The cap prevents overordering during high-demand periods. Understanding this reveals OR's structural assumptions (stationarity, iid, known lead time) and its computational precision.
**Quick check**: How does the cap term in the order quantity formula protect against unstable inventory flow when lead times are positive?

### Human-AI complementarity metrics (population vs. individual level)
**Why needed**: The paper's key contribution is proving complementarity exists at both levels. Population-level CE is E[ f_H+AI - max(f_H, f_AI) ]. Individual-level CE_i is per-person analog. The distinction matters because positive population CE can arise from selection alone (weak follow AI, strong ignore it). The bound on P(CE_i > 0) addresses this.
**Quick check**: Why does E[max(f_H, f_AI)] ≠ max(E[f_H], E[f_AI]) imply population-level complementarity does not guarantee individual-level benefit?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
1. Environment Simulator: Generates inventory instances (synthetic/real) with demand sequences, lead times, and contextual text (product descriptions, dates). Tracks state: on-hand inventory, in-transit orders, realized demand, profit calculation.
2. OR Baseline Module: Implements data-driven capped base-stock policy (Appendix A). Takes historical demand, calculates μ̂, σ̂, base-stock B_t = μ̂_t + z*σ̂_t using the critical fractile, and orders q_t = max(0, min(B_t - IP_t, cap)). The cap prevents overordering during high-demand periods.
3. LLM Agent: Stateless per-period decision loop. System prompt fixed (game mechanics, OR explanation, reasoning checklist, JSON output). User message per period: carry-over insights + current observation (I_t, in-transit, history) + OR recommendation. Returns order quantity, rationale, optional carry-over insight update.
4. Human-in-the-loop Interface (Web Game): Displays product description, state, parameters, AI recommendation (varies by mode), charts. Modes: A (OR→Human), B (OR→LLM→Human shows LLM reasoning), C (OR+LLM autonomous, human strategic guidance every 4 periods). Records decisions, timestamps, guidance text.
5. Evaluation & Metrics: Normalized reward = max(reward / (p * total demand), 0). Implicit critical fractile = fraction of periods with excess inventory (vs. stockout). Statistical tests: OLS with subject/instance fixed effects; bootstrap for complementarity bounds.

### Critical path
For OR→LLM (best-performing): Simulator → OR Baseline (computes B_t, μ̂, σ̂, IP_t, cap) → LLM Agent (receives OR stats + context + carry-over insights, overrides if detects violation) → Order decision → Simulator (updates state, calculates reward). For human-in-the-loop: Simulator → OR Baseline → LLM Agent (internal) → Human sees OR+LLM output → Human final decision → Simulator.

### Design tradeoffs
- **LLM reasoning vs. OR precision**: OR→LLM lets LLM override OR's precise calculation, risking overfitting; LLM→OR lets LLM only influence forecasts, preserving OR's calculation discipline. Performance depends on lead time determinism (Fig 5).
- **Human decision authority**: Mode B (LLM recommendation + reasoning) outperforms Mode C (LLM autonomous + periodic guidance). Placing LLM as advisory tool rather than controller leverages human oversight without burdening them with every decision.
- **Context window vs. memory**: LLM uses carry-over insights (concise memos) instead of full history to stay within context limits. Tradeoff: insights must be conservative and evidence-backed; missing subtle patterns.

### Failure signatures
1. **LLM overfitting to noise** (Example 2, stationary IID): LLM interprets random high-demand draws as regime shifts, increasing forecast variance unnecessarily. Evidenced by OR outperforming LLM on stationary patterns (Table 2, p01).
2. **Lost order detection failure** (Example 1): OR counts pipeline inventory that never arrives; LLM may or may not detect based on reasoning. Under stochastic lead times, OR→LLM excels because LLM can write off lost orders; OR→OR fails.
3. **Human override misalignment**: In Mode B, humans sometimes override correctly (catch lost orders) but may also override LLM's correct adaptations, reducing performance. The net positive average indicates correct overrides dominate.
4. **Calibration decay**: Methods with more LLM influence show flatter response to critical fractile ρ (Table 3). LLM-only and OR→LLM have narrow implicit fractile ranges (0.028, 0.069), indicating LLMs under-adjust to cost structure changes compared to OR.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Component isolation benchmark**: Run OR alone, LLM alone (no OR input), and OR→LLM on a small set of deterministic lead time instances with known demand patterns (e.g., stationary, step change). Verify baseline performance matches paper claims and understand failure modes (LLM overfitting on stationary, detection on step change).
2. **Lead time sensitivity test**: Create a suite where stochastic lead time probability varies (0%, 25%, 50%, 100% lost orders). Measure performance delta between OR→LLM and LLM→OR. Expect OR→LLM advantage to grow with lost-order prevalence due to LLM's anomaly detection.
3. **Human-AI mode A/B comparison**: Implement the three collaboration modes in the web game with 10-15 pilot participants (not full 69). Verify Mode B > Mode A statistically in this pilot. Check interface usability: Do participants understand OR and LLM recommendations? Does the LLM reasoning field change their decisions?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question**: How should LLM-based agents be integrated with OR heuristics in inventory control pipelines to maximize performance across diverse environments?
**Basis in paper**: The abstract notes: "it remains unclear how best to incorporate LLM-based methods into traditional decision-making pipelines."
**Why unresolved**: The paper compares OR→LLM and LLM→OR but leaves open whether other hybrid architectures (e.g., iterative refinement, parallel ensembles) could further improve robustness or adaptability.
**What evidence would resolve it**: A systematic evaluation of multiple integration architectures across a broader spectrum of inventory settings (e.g., multi-echelon, non-stationary costs) with explicit analysis of when each pattern excels.

### Open Question 2
**Question**: Can human-AI collaboration modes emphasizing high-level strategic guidance (Mode C) reach performance comparable to decision-support modes (Mode B), and what interface designs enable this?
**Basis in paper**: Subsection 5.2.1 acknowledges: "this comparison is technology- and interface-dependent: stronger models, better mechanisms for translating human intent... could narrow or potentially reverse the gap between Modes C and B."
**Why unresolved**: Mode B outperformed Mode C in the experiment, but the gap may stem from suboptimal guidance frequency/structure rather than a fundamental limitation of strategic oversight.
**What evidence would resolve it**: A follow-up experiment manipulating guidance modality (e.g., parameter tweaks vs. free text), frequency, and AI responsiveness, measuring effects on normalized reward and human trust.

### Open Question 3
**Question**: How can individual-level human-AI complementarity be directly measured without within-subject comparisons, and what factors predict which individuals benefit from collaboration?
**Basis in paper**: The paper's theoretical framework provides a distribution-free lower bound on the fraction of individuals with positive complementarity, because each participant is observed in only one condition, preventing direct computation of CE_i.
**Why unresolved**: Without identifying which individuals truly benefit, we cannot personalize collaboration systems or understand psychological/ skill-based drivers of synergy.
**What evidence would resolve it**: Deploy a cross-over study where the same individuals work both with and without AI on matched scenarios, or develop causal inference methods (e.g., instrumental variables) to estimate individual complementarity from one-shot data.

## Limitations
- LLM API parameters (temperature, top_p) not fully specified, risking non-deterministic runs.
- Human experiment protocol (recruitment, incentives, pre-registration) missing, raising selection bias concerns.
- Real H&M data filtering thresholds (e.g., "stable prices") not justified, limiting external validity.

## Confidence
- **Core automated results** (OR→LLM improvement, lead-time interactions): High confidence.
- **Human-added value and individual-level complementarity bound**: Medium confidence.
- **Mechanism claims** (LLM detects assumption violations, human catches LLM errors): Medium confidence.
