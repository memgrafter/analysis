# MTI: A Behavior-Based Temperament Profiling System for AI Agents
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.02145
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02145
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** Introduces the Model Temperament Index (MTI), a behavior-based, two-stage measurement system that profiles AI agent temperament across four independent axes (Reactivity, Compliance, Sociality, Resilience) and finds temperament is independent of model size (1.7B–9B) and selectively shifted by RLHF.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the Model Temperament Index (MTI) to address the lack of standardized measurement for behavioral dispositions in AI agents. Critiquing the use of human personality scales and the framing of behavioral variation as a defect, MTI uses agent-level, behavior-based protocols to separate disposition from capability. Profiling ten small language models (1.7B–9B parameters), it finds that the four temperament axes are largely independent among instruction-tuned models, that within-axis facets can be fully dissociated (e.g., Compliance formal vs. stance), and that RLHF selectively shifts certain axes while creating new facet differentiations. Temperament scores show no correlation with model size, validating MTI as a measure of disposition rather than capability.

## Method Summary
MTI profiles an AI agent (Core model + Shell configuration) using a two-stage design per axis. Stage 1 verifies the model possesses the basic capability required for the temperament task. Only models passing Stage 1 proceed to Stage 2, where behavior is measured under conditions that pit the instruction against the model's default tendencies (e.g., opinion pressure, context shifts, stress escalation). Disposition scores are derived solely from Stage 2 performance, ensuring variation reflects choice, not incapacity. Scoring is fully automated via deterministic scripts (keyword counts, heuristics, flip rates). A canonical agent configuration (temperature=0, minimal system prompt) is used to isolate Core-level temperament.

## Key Results
- The four MTI axes (Reactivity, Compliance, Sociality, Resilience) are largely independent among instruction-tuned models (all |r| < 0.42).
- Within-axis facets can be fully independent; e.g., Compliance formal and stance facets show r = 0.002 in an aligned model.
- RLHF selectively shifts axis scores (affecting Reactivity, Compliance, Resilience but not Sociality) and creates within-axis facet differentiation absent in the base model.
- Temperament is independent of model size across the 1.7B–9B parameter range.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** MTI’s two-stage design isolates temperament from capability by requiring a capability verification before measuring disposition under conflict.
- **Mechanism:** For each axis, Stage 1 confirms the model can perform the target behavior (e.g., follow a formatting constraint). Only models passing Stage 1 proceed to Stage 2, where behavior is measured in conditions that pit instruction against default tendencies (e.g., multi-turn opinion pressure). The disposition score is derived solely from Stage 2 performance. This ensures that observed variation reflects choice, not incapacity.
- **Core assumption:** Capability is a necessary but not sufficient condition for observing temperament; without ability, behavior is constrained by incapacity rather than disposition.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "using structured examination protocols with a two-stage design that separates capability from disposition."
  - [section 3.3.3] "Stage 1 (capability check) verifies that the model can perform the target behavior; only Stage 2 data... contributes to the temperament score."
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers do not directly address this mechanism; evidence is internal to MTI design.
- **Break condition:** If many models fail capability checks for an axis, the battery may be measuring capability rather than temperament. If Stage 2 performance correlates with Stage 1 success, separation is incomplete.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The four MTI axes measure behaviorally independent dimensions of temperament, as evidenced by low correlations among instruction-tuned models.
- **Mechanism:** Each axis targets a distinct behavioral domain: Reactivity (sensitivity to neutral environmental changes), Compliance (alignment with explicit instructions under conflict), Sociality (spontaneous relational resource allocation), Resilience (performance under negative stress). Protocols are designed to avoid overlap (e.g., Reactivity uses uninstructed context changes, Compliance uses explicit instructions; Resilience uses negatively valenced stress, Reactivity uses neutral changes). Facet structures within axes are empirically validated to ensure each axis captures a unified construct.
- **Core assumption:** Behavioral tendencies in these four domains are not inherently correlated; any observed correlation reflects a latent factor or methodological artifact.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "the four axes are largely independent among instruction-tuned models (all |r| < 0.42)."
  - [section 5.2] Correlation matrices for n=9 instruction-tuned models show all |r| < 0.42, none significant.
  - [section 3.5] Confound management table explains how design choices prevent overlap.
- **Break condition:** If future samples with more diverse models show strong cross-axis correlations (|r| > 0.5), the independence claim fails, suggesting either non-orthogonal axes or a common underlying factor (e.g., general cooperativeness).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** RLHF (alignment training) reshapes temperament selectively and structurally: it shifts some axes (Reactivity, Compliance, Resilience) but leaves Sociality stable, and creates within-axis facet differentiation absent in base models.
- **Mechanism:** Alignment modifies the model’s Core weights to optimize for helpfulness/harmlessness, changing behavioral dispositions: reduces Reactivity (more consistent outputs), increases Compliance (more instruction-following), increases cognitive Resilience (better handling overload/ambiguity) but may slightly reduce adversarial Resilience (more engagement with false premises). RLHF also introduces internal structure—e.g., Compliance splits into independent formal and stance facets in aligned models, whereas base models show higher correlation between them.
- **Core assumption:** RLHF objectives specifically target certain behavioral dimensions (e.g., instruction following, engagement) but not others (e.g., social relational tendencies), leading to selective changes. The emergence of facet independence suggests alignment creates new behavioral dissociations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "RLHF selectively shifts axis scores and creates within-axis facet differentiation absent in the unaligned base model."
  - [section 5.5] llama3.1 instruct vs. base: large changes in R, C, Re; minimal change in S (∆ = -0.02). Facet correlations: Compliance D/B r=0.002 (instruct) vs. r=0.285 (base).
  - [section 5.3.4] "The base model amplifies all facet correlations... RLHF creates within-axis facet differentiation."
- **Break condition:** If additional instruct/base pairs show Sociality changes under RLHF, selectivity claim fails. If facet independence is not replicated in other aligned models, the structural effect may be model-specific.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Temperament vs. Personality in AI
  - **Why needed:** The paper deliberately avoids borrowing human personality frameworks, arguing temperament better fits AI because it captures constitutionally based dispositions (rooted in model weights/architecture) rather than socially learned behaviors. This distinction justifies behavior-based measurement and AI-native dimensions.
  - **Quick check:** Why is self-report personality assessment considered invalid for measuring AI temperament?
- **Concept:** Agent-Level Measurement (Core + Shell)
  - **Why needed:** MTI measures the deployed agent (Core+Shell composite), not just the bare model. Baseline uses a minimal Shell to isolate Core-level temperament. This is critical for interpreting profiles and comparing models across deployment configurations.
  - **Quick check:** If you change the system prompt (Shell), how should that affect an MTI profile according to the paper?
- **Concept:** Two-Stage Design for Capability-Disposition Separation
  - **Why needed:** This is the core methodological innovation that allows MTI to measure temperament rather than capability. Without it, scores would confound what a model can do with what it tends to do.
  - **Quick check:** In the Compliance battery, what is the purpose of Condition D?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Axes (R, C, S, Re) -> Facets (e.g., formal/stance, cognitive/adversarial) -> Batteries (scenario conditions per axis) -> Scoring scripts (automated rules) -> Report (type code + continuous scores)
- **Critical path:**
  1. Prepare model with minimal Shell (temperature=0, default system prompt).
  2. Run full battery (~1930 runs) across all scenario conditions.
  3. Apply scoring scripts to compute deltas, flip rates, PM scores, etc.
  4. Assemble axis scores (0–1) and facet breakdowns.
  5. Map scores to type codes using provisional thresholds.
  6. Validate: check capability check pass rates, scoring errors, and cross-axis correlations (should be low for instruct models).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Behavior-based vs. efficiency: ~1930 runs per model are costly but ensure behavioral ground truth.
  - Automated scoring vs. nuance: Scripts ensure reproducibility but may miss subtle social cues; human validation planned.
  - Provisional thresholds: Type code cutoffs derived from current sample; may not generalize.
  - Facet completeness: Sociality Facet A (Agent↔Agent) lacks independent MTI-native measurement and relies on external game data.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High capability check failure rates → battery too hard or model deficient.
  - Unexpectedly high cross-axis correlations → confounds or outlier model (e.g., base model).
  - Scoring script crashes → response format unexpected (e.g., chain-of-thought in deepseek-r1).
  - All models scoring near extremes → battery not discriminative for the tested family.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate the paper's 10-model study: verify axis independence (|r|<0.42), Compliance facet independence (r≈0), and size independence.
  2. RLHF effect: profile an instruct/base pair (e.g., llama3.1) to confirm Sociality stability vs. shifts in other axes, plus base model’s undifferentiated facets.
  3. Compliance-Resilience paradox: verify that stance compliance (flip rate) and adversarial resilience (PM_C) are uncorrelated; identify models at diagonals.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Is the hypothesized stability of Sociality under RLHF a general property, or does it vary across model families and alignment methods?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper finds Sociality changed minimally (Δ=0.02) for llama3.1 and states this is a "single-pair observation" that is "hypothesis-generating, not confirmed" and requires "multi-family replication" (§5.5, §6.2, §7.2).
- **Why unresolved:** The conclusion about Sociality's resistance to alignment training rests on one instruct/base pair (llama3.1). Different pretraining datasets, RLHF algorithms (DPO, Constitutional AI), or model architectures may yield different outcomes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Measure MTI profiles for instruct and base versions from at least 3-4 additional model families (e.g., from Mistral, Google, Cohere). A consistent pattern of near-zero Sociality Δ across families would support the hypothesis; systematic variation would refute it.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent do runtime configuration changes (Shell variation) alter an agent's MTI temperament profile?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly states it does not measure Shell effects and that "How does a custom system prompt change an MTI profile?" is a planned extension involving a "Shell × Core factorial design" (§2.3, §7.2). All reported profiles use a single minimal Shell.
- **Why unresolved:** The framework defines the Agent as Core+Shell, but the current empirical work isolates Core effects. The practical utility of MTI for deployment depends on understanding how common interventions (system prompts, temperature adjustments, tool augmentation) shift scores.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a factorial experiment: measure multiple agents from the same Core under systematically varied Shells (different system prompts, temperatures, tool configurations). Quantify the proportion of variance in each MTI axis attributable to Core vs. Shell.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the Facet A (Agent↔Agent) dimension of Sociality represent a distinct temperament trait from Facet H (Agent↔Human), and how should it be measured directly?
- **Basis in paper:** The current Sociality score reflects only Facet H. The paper acknowledges Facet A is underdeveloped, relying on "partial extraction from LxM game data" and calls its independent measurement a "significant limitation" and a "priority" (§5.3.5, §7.1, §7.2).
- **Why unresolved:** Preliminary exploratory data (n=4) suggest Facet H and cooperation/bluff rates in games are not strongly correlated, hinting at dissociation. However, Facet A lacks a dedicated, validated MTI-native battery. This gap hinders understanding of social temperament in multi-agent deployments.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design and validate a standalone MTI battery for Facet A (e.g., structured multi-agent negotiation, coalition formation, or resource-sharing tasks without human participants). Demonstrate that scores from this battery are distinct from Facet H scores via factor analysis and show predictive validity for outcomes in pure multi-agent environments.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do the fully automated, heuristic-based scoring methods (e.g., keyword counting, rule-based stance detection) produce valid and reliable facet scores, particularly for nuanced constructs like Sociality?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes all scoring was automated with "no human raters or LLM-as-judge" (§4.3) and that this introduces limitations: heuristics may miss nuances for Sociality, and reliance on model self-report for two measures (Reactivity-B Likert, Sociality H2) creates "tension with the behavior-based measurement principle" (§6.6). No human validation was performed.
- **Why unresolved:** While reproducibility is a strength, the validity of the operational definitions (e.g., counting "empathy markers" for Sociality) against ground-truth human judgment is unknown. The r=0.971 validation for Reactivity-B Likert is against another automated metric, not behavior.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a human validation study where a subset of model responses across all batteries is rated by trained human judges on the intended constructs (e.g., "degree of relational investment"). Compute agreement (e.g., ICC) between human scores and automated MTI scores. Low agreement would necessitate refined operational definitions or hybrid scoring.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Do MTI temperament profiles and their core findings (axis independence, size independence, RLHF effects) generalize to frontier-scale models (e.g., >50B parameters) and closed-source APIs?
- **Basis in paper:** The study is limited to open-weight SLMs (1.7B–9B). The paper acknowledges "whether MTI profiles generalize to frontier models... is unknown" and lists "Large-model measurement via API" as a key future work item (§7.1, §7.2). Key findings like size independence are demonstrated only within this narrow range.
- **Why unresolved:** Frontier models differ not just in size but in training data scale, algorithm sophistication, and safety fine-tuning intensity. Their behavioral dispositions could be qualitatively different. The observed facet dissociations and RLHF structural effects might not hold at scale.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Run the full MTI battery on a diverse set of 5-10 frontier models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude 3, Gemini 1.5) via their APIs. Test if the four axes remain largely independent, if size correlations emerge beyond the SLM range, and if RLHF-like alignment still produces the same pattern of facet differentiation and Sociality stability observed in the SLM sample.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability:** Findings rely on a narrow, proprietary model sample (6 orgs, 1.7B–9B). Unknown if axes remain independent in larger/more diverse models or different architectures.
- **Scoring Validity:** Automated heuristics (e.g., stance detection, keyword-based Sociality) are not fully specified or externally validated. Risk of measurement error, especially for nuanced social behaviors.
- **Construct Validity:** The two-stage capability-disposition separation is logical but lacks a definitive "gold standard" test. Break conditions (e.g., high cap-check failures) were not observed but could emerge with harder batteries or weaker models.

## Confidence
- **Axis Independence:** Medium. Observed in their sample (|r|<0.42), but base model inclusion distorts correlations. Requires testing on broader instruct-model sets.
- **RLHF Selectivity & Facet Differentiation:** Low to Medium. Based on one instruct/base pair (llama3.1). Not replicated across other alignment methods or model families.
- **Capability-Disposition Separation:** Medium. Mechanically sound, but empirical validation depends on the capability check difficulty and Stage 2 protocol sensitivity—both untested independently.
- **Size Independence:** Medium. Within 1.7B–9B range, but unknown beyond. Could be confounded by training data or methodologies correlated with size.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-Sample Validation:** Profile at least 5 new instruction-tuned models (from different developers, >10B params) to test if axis independence (|r|<0.5) and facet dissociations hold.
2. **Human-Scoring Benchmark:** For a subset of responses (e.g., 100 per axis), have human raters score the targeted dispositional behaviors. Compare human-derived scores with MTI automated scores to estimate heuristic validity, especially for Sociality and nuanced Compliance stances.
3. **Shell Sensitivity Stress Test:** Run MTI with multiple system prompts (Shells) of varying neutrality/alignment on the same Core model. According to the agent-level measurement principle, temperament scores should be stable across Shells unless the Shell itself alters core decision processes. Measure score variance.
