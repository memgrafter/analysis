# Semantically Labelled Automata for Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning with LTL Instructions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06746
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06746
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: SEMLTL achieves near-optimal success rates (~98%) on finite tasks and superior performance on infinite tasks, scaling to large LTL specifications (hundreds of states) where baselines fail.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles multi-task reinforcement learning where tasks are specified by Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) formulas, requiring a single policy to generalize to arbitrary instructions. The core innovation is the use of semantically labelled limit-deterministic Büchi automata (LDBAs) derived from modern semantic translations. Each automaton state carries rich, structured information (main and breakpoint formulae), enabling efficient on-the-fly construction and extraction of expressive task embeddings. These embeddings condition a multi-headed reinforcement learning policy that handles ε-actions inherent in LDBAs. Experiments across diverse domains and complex task families show state-of-the-art results: near-optimal success on finite tasks, higher acceptance state visits on infinite tasks, and scalability to large specifications (hundreds of LDBA states) by constructing only a small fraction online, whereas prior methods fail. The approach supports full LTL and avoids myopic decomposition pitfalls.

## Method Summary
The method begins by converting an LTL formula into a limit-deterministic Büchi automaton (LDBA) through a semantic translation. Unlike standard automata, each state in this LDBA is annotated with two types of semantic formulae: the main formula representing the original LTL constraint, and breakpoint formulae that capture progress toward satisfaction. This labeling allows the system to compute the automaton on-the-fly during execution, expanding only the reachable portion rather than building the entire product upfront. From the current automaton state, a structured task embedding is extracted that encodes the semantic context. A reinforcement learning policy with a multi-headed architecture then conditions on this embedding; the heads correspond to possible ε-actions (non-deterministic transitions) and allow the policy to select appropriate actions even when the automaton makes internal jumps. This design enables a single policy to generalize across many LTL tasks while handling the non-determinism and partial observability introduced by the automaton.

## Key Results
- SEMLTL achieves near-optimal success rates (~98%) on finite-horizon tasks across multiple environments.
- On infinite-horizon tasks, SEMLTL visits accepting states more frequently than baseline methods.
- For large LTL specifications (hundreds of LDBA states), SEMLTL constructs only a small fraction of the full automaton on-the-fly and scales successfully where DEEPLTL times out.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The semantic labelling provides rich, structured information at each automaton state (main and breakpoint formulae), which yields expressive task embeddings that capture global task context. This avoids the myopia of decomposition-based methods that break tasks into subgoals without considering overall dependencies. On-the-fly construction ensures computational feasibility by expanding only reachable states, drastically reducing memory and time for large formulas. The multi-headed policy architecture natively handles ε-actions from the LDBA’s limit-determinism, allowing the agent to follow automaton transitions without ad-hoc smoothing. Together, these elements enable effective generalization: the embeddings give the policy a nuanced understanding of the current task phase, while the online approach keeps overhead manageable even for complex LTL instructions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)**: Formal language for specifying time-bound goals (e.g., "eventually A and always B"). *Why needed*: Provides precise, compositional task specifications. *Quick check*: Can you write an LTL formula for "visit A infinitely often while avoiding B"?
- **Büchi Automata**: Automata that accept infinite runs; used to model LTL semantics. *Why needed*: Turns logical formulas into state machines that can guide decision-making. *Quick check*: What does an accepting run in a Büchi automaton represent?
- **Limit-Deterministic Büchi Automata (LDBA)**: A subclass where from any state, all but one outgoing transition are deterministic; ε-transitions model non-determinism. *Why needed*: Balances expressiveness (captures full LTL) with efficient on-the-fly exploration. *Quick check*: How does limit-determinism differ from full determinism in practice?
- **Semantic Labeling**: Attaching logical formulae (main and breakpoint) to automaton states. *Why needed*: Encodes the meaning of each state, enabling structured embeddings rather than arbitrary IDs. *Quick check*: What information do main and breakpoint formulae provide at a given state?
- **Task Embeddings**: Vector representations derived from semantic labels that condition the policy. *Why needed*: Allows a single network to parameterize many tasks by providing task-specific context. *Quick check*: How are embeddings constructed from the semantic formulae, and why is this more effective than one-hot state encodings?
- **Multi-headed Policy**: Reinforcement learning policy with separate heads for each possible ε-action. *Why needed*: Handles the non-deterministic branching of LDBAs without averaging over actions, preserving semantics. *Quick check*: What problem would arise if the policy treated ε-actions as standard actions?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: LTL Formula -> Semantic Translator -> LDBA (with semantic labels) -> On-the-fly Builder -> Embedding Extractor -> Multi-headed Policy Network -> Actions

**Critical Path**: An LTL formula is translated into a semantically labelled LDBA. During interaction with the environment, the on-the-fly builder expands the LDBA as new states become reachable. At each step, the current automaton state’s semantic labels are transformed into an embedding. The policy network, conditioned on this embedding and environment observation, selects an action via its appropriate ε-head (if applicable) and updates the automaton state accordingly.

**Design Tradeoffs**: On-the-fly construction trades upfront computation for potential online latency; semantic richness improves embedding quality but increases extraction cost; multi-headed handling of ε-actions adds architectural complexity but is necessary for correctness with LDBAs. The design prioritizes generalization and scalability over minimal inference time.

**Failure Signatures**: 
- Automaton construction times out or exhausts memory when formulas are excessively large or complex.
- Embeddings fail to distinguish semantically distinct automaton states, leading to policy confusion.
- Policy learns to ignore embeddings (e.g., by always selecting the same head), collapsing to a single-task solution.

**First Three Experiments**:
1. Test SEMLTL on a set of simple LTL formulas (e.g., "F a", "G (a -> F b)") in a gridworld to verify basic functionality and embedding extraction.
2. Measure the growth of online LDBA construction (number of states expanded, time per step) as the input LTL formula size increases systematically.
3. Ablate the semantic labels by replacing them with random or one-hot state encodings, and compare success rates to the full method on a medium-complexity task.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Are the semantic embeddings expressive enough to overcome the myopia of decomposition-based methods?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors explicitly pose this as RQ1 in the Experiments section, noting that decomposition methods can suffer from myopic solutions and asking if their embeddings overcome this.
- Why unresolved: While the paper provides an illustrative example (conveyor belt) showing SEMLTL avoids myopia, this is limited to one environment. The question remains to what extent the embeddings consistently capture global context across diverse tasks and whether they generalize to new environments without retraining.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comprehensive experiments across multiple environments and task families where decomposition is known to fail, coupled with ablation studies isolating the contribution of each embedding feature. Additionally, theoretical guarantees relating the semantic labeling to task similarity would strengthen the claim.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can SEMLTL successfully learn generalist policies for zero-shot executing arbitrary LTL instructions?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors list this as RQ2 in the Experiments section, investigating whether a single policy can generalize to unseen LTL tasks without retraining.
- Why unresolved: The paper reports strong performance on held-out test specifications from several families. However, "arbitrary LTL instructions" is an aspirational goal; the experiments cover a broad but finite set of formula patterns. Zero-shot generalization to completely out-of-distribution LTL structures (e.g., highly nested or with rare operators) remains unevaluated. Also, the policy relies on semantic labeling, which might not capture all nuances of very complex specs.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluation on an exhaustive suite of LTL formulas, including worst-case examples, and testing in unseen environments. Analysis of the policy's failure modes on tasks it cannot zero-shot execute would clarify boundaries. Perhaps incorporating more expressive embedding mechanisms could extend coverage.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does the approach successfully scale to complex specifications where previous methods fail?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors pose this as RQ3 in the Experiments section, highlighting scalability as a key advantage over prior work.
- Why unresolved: The results show SEMLTL handles specifications with hundreds of LDBA states by constructing only a small fraction online, while DEEPLTL times out. But scalability is measured against a specific baseline (DEEPLTL) and within a time limit. There may be more demanding specs (e.g., with numerous atomic propositions leading to larger alphabets, or extremely long formulas) where on-the-fly construction and embedding become computationally burdensome or lose discriminative power. Also, the claim of scaling to "complex specifications" needs to be precisely defined and tested against a broader set of metrics (memory, sample complexity).
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic scaling experiments varying the size (number of atomic propositions, nesting depth, length) of LTL formulas, measuring both computational efficiency (time, memory) and policy performance. Comparing against additional baselines and perhaps theoretical scaling bounds. Also, real-world deployment on large-scale tasks would provide practical validation.

## Limitations
- Robustness to stochastic dynamics and noisy LTL specifications is not examined.
- Computational overhead of on-the-fly automaton construction and embedding extraction remains unclear; scalability may not translate to low inference latency.
- Dependence on the specific semantic translation used has not been ablated; alternative translations could affect performance.
- Evaluation covers a limited subset of LTL operators and task families; generalizability to arbitrary LTL formulas is uncertain.
- No theoretical guarantees (e.g., sample complexity, convergence) are provided.
