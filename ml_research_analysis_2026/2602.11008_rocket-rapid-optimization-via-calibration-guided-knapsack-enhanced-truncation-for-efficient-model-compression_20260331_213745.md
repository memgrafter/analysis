# ROCKET: Rapid Optimization via Calibration-guided Knapsack Enhanced Truncation for Efficient Model Compression

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11008
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11008
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: ROCKET achieves state-of-the-art post-training compression, retaining over 90% accuracy at 30% compression without fine-tuning; after light fine-tuning, a compressed Qwen3-14B (to 8B) nearly matches the original Qwen3-8B.

## Executive Summary
ROCKET introduces a novel two-phase approach for post-training compression of large language models (LLMs). It formulates layer-wise compression as a multi-choice knapsack problem solved via dynamic programming to optimally allocate a global parameter budget, minimizing total reconstruction error while respecting per-layer fidelity constraints. The per-layer compression itself uses a calibration-guided, single-step sparse matrix factorization in a whitened activation space, avoiding iterative optimization. Across multiple LLMs, ROCKET significantly outperforms prior methods like SVD-LLM and CoSpaDi, retaining over 90% of original accuracy at 30% compression without any fine-tuning. After fine-tuning with only 30 million tokens, a ROCKET-compressed Qwen3-14B (to 8B parameters) nearly matches the performance of the original Qwen3-8B. The method generalizes to vision-language and speech models and is environmentally favorable, consuming over 100x less energy than CoSpaDi.

## Method Summary
ROCKET consists of profiling and allocation phases. During profiling, for each linear layer a small calibration set computes a whitening transform (Cholesky factor of activation covariance). In the whitened space, eigen-decomposition yields a dictionary; the coefficient matrix is sparsified using a fused importance metric that combines entry magnitude with sensitivity in the original space. A closed-form ridge-regularized least-squares refines the dictionary given the fixed sparsity pattern. Each candidate configuration (varying rank and sparsity) yields a cost (parameter count) and reconstruction error (relative Frobenius). In the allocation phase, these profiles are fed to a constrained multi-choice knapsack solver via dynamic programming, which selects one candidate per layer to minimize total error under a global budget and per-layer error caps. The final compressed model is represented as a product of two matrices (U, V) for efficient inference.

## Key Results
- ROCKET significantly outperforms SVD-LLM and CoSpaDi on Qwen3-8B and Llama3-8B across 20–50% compression, achieving higher average zero-shot accuracy and lower perplexity.
- At 30% compression, ROCKET retains over 90% of the original model's accuracy without any fine-tuning.
- After fine-tuning with only 30M tokens, a ROCKET-compressed Qwen3-14B (to 8B parameters) nearly matches the original Qwen3-8B performance.
- The approach generalizes to vision-language (Qwen3-4B-VL) and speech (VibeVoice) models and uses over 100x less energy than CoSpaDi.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Optimal per-layer compression under a global parameter budget is achievable by formulating it as a multi-choice knapsack problem (MCKP) and solving via dynamic programming, minimizing total reconstruction error while respecting per-layer fidelity constraints.
- **Mechanism:** For each layer, a discrete set of (compression ratio, sparsity) candidates is profiled, recording cost (parameter count) and error (relative Frobenius). The MCKP selects one candidate per layer to minimize summed error subject to total cost ≤ budget and per-layer error ≤ α·reference. Dynamic programming efficiently finds the global optimum.
- **Core assumption:** Reconstruction error per layer is accurately estimable from a small calibration set and additive across layers; the discretized candidate space sufficiently explores the Pareto frontier of accuracy vs. compression.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "formulates layer-wise compression allocation as a multi-choice knapsack problem, selecting the optimal compression level for each layer to minimize total reconstruction error while adhering to a target model size." [section 3.2] Details the constrained MCKP formulation, profiling, and dynamic programming solution (Algorithm 1 steps 15–29). [corpus] ROCKET differentiates from uniform (SVD-LLM) or trainable (Dobi-SVD, ARA) allocation methods by its post-training, optimization-based approach.
- **Break condition:** If the number of compressible components is very large (e.g., MoE models with many experts), the combinatorial state space may become intractable for dynamic programming.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Calibration-guided structured sparsification of the coefficient matrix in a whitened activation space, using a fused importance metric balancing whitened-space optimality and original-space directional sensitivity, preserves reconstruction fidelity while enabling union-of-subspaces flexibility.
- **Mechanism:** After eigen-decomposition in the whitened space (WL = BΛB⊤), compute coefficient matrix C = B⊤WL. Importance per entry is |cij| · ‖L⁻¹bi‖^λ (λ=0.5). Column-wise hard thresholding retains top-s entries per column, followed by global reactivation to hit exact sparsity target.
- **Core assumption:** A small calibration set suffices to estimate the whitening transform L and that the fused importance metric approximates the true impact on reconstruction error in the original weight space.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "sparsifies weight coefficients based on activation-weights sensitivity." [section 3.1] Derives whitening, eigen-decomposition, importance scores (Eq. 5), and two-stage sparsification. [corpus] Related works like CoSpaDi use iterative K-SVD/OMP; ROCKET replaces this with a single-step, importance-thresholding approach, avoiding alternating minimization.
- **Break condition:** If calibration data is highly distributionally mismatched to deployment data, the whitening transform L and importance scores may become unrepresentative, degrading reconstruction.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Relaxing the orthonormality constraint on the dictionary matrix after sparsification via a closed-form ridge-regularized least-squares update yields a better reconstruction without iterative optimization or backpropagation.
- **Mechanism:** Given sparse coefficient matrix C_sparse, solve D_final = argmin_D ‖WL – DC_sparse‖_F² + μ‖D‖_F². This Cholesky-based closed-form solution optimally fits the whitened weight matrix under the fixed sparsity pattern.
- **Core assumption:** The sparsity pattern determined by importance scoring is sufficiently good that a linear least-squares refinement suffices; further iterations (as in dictionary learning) are unnecessary.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "updates the dictionary in closed form via least squares bypassing iterative optimization, sparse coding, or backpropagation entirely." [section 3.1] Equation (7) and surrounding text describe the ridge-regularized least-squares step. [corpus] Contrasts with iterative methods like K-SVD used in CoSpaDi.
- **Break condition:** If the initial sparsity pattern is poor (e.g., due to bad importance estimates), the least-squares update cannot recover lost information; joint optimization of sparsity pattern and dictionary might be needed.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Low-rank matrix approximation (SVD)**
  - Why needed: ROCKET builds on truncated SVD in the whitened space (WL ≈ BC) as a baseline union-of-subspaces representation before sparsification.
  - Quick check: Why does operating in the whitened activation space simplify the reconstruction objective?
- **Concept: Structured sparsification**
  - Why needed: Sparsity is applied column-wise to the coefficient matrix C, enabling a sparse UV factorization that preserves hardware-friendly matrix multiplication during inference.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Calibration set X → Compute whitening L = chol(X⊤X) → For each layer: Compute WL = L W; eigen-decompose WLW⊤ to get B, C; compute fused importance; sparsify C to C_sparse; solve D_final via ridge-LS; record (cost, error) → After profiling all layers, run constrained MCKP via DP to select optimal (rank, sparsity) per layer → Apply selected configurations: U = L⁻¹ D_final, V = C_sparse → Compressed model.
- **Critical path:** Calibration set → whitening transform → layer profiling (eigen-decomp + sparsification + LS + error recording) → DP allocation → final factorization. Profiling is parallelizable across layers; DP is sequential but cheap.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Speed vs. accuracy: Single-step factorization (ROCKET) vs. iterative dictionary learning (CoSpaDi). Allocation: Global optimal MCKP vs. uniform (SVD-LLM) or learned (ARA). Calibration data: Size and source affect L and importance; ROCKET is robust but not invariant.
- **Failure signatures:** High reconstruction error localized to a few layers (check per-layer error profiles). DP solver fails to find feasible solution (per-layer error caps too strict; increase α). Inference slowdown despite sparsity (non-optimal sparsity pattern; adjust sparsification).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Main comparison: Evaluate ROCKET vs. SVD-LLM and CoSpaDi on Qwen3-8B/Llama3-8B at 20–50% compression, measuring zero-shot accuracy and perplexity (Table 1, Fig 2).
  2. Ablation: Remove knapsack allocation (uniform) or sparsification (SVD-LLM) to quantify contributions (Table 7); test alternative error metrics (Table 6).
  3. Generalization: Apply to vision-language (Qwen3-4B-VL) and speech (VibeVoice) models at 20% compression (Tables 4, 5).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the multi-choice knapsack allocation in ROCKET be scaled to efficiently handle models with a vast number of compressible components, such as Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, where the combinatorial explosion of compression options renders dynamic programming intractable?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The conclusion explicitly states that "the dynamic programming solution ... is hard to scale to architectures with a very large number of compressible components such as modern Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models ... scalable alternatives are left for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The current DP approach’s state space grows with both the number of layers and the number of candidate compression options per layer; for MoE models with dozens or hundreds of experts per block, this becomes combinatorially prohibitive.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A novel allocation algorithm with sub-quadratic or near-linear complexity in the number of components, demonstrated by successfully compressing MoE models at various sparsity levels while maintaining accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can sparsity patterns be jointly learned during the fine-tuning phase instead of being fixed after the training-free compression, thereby surpassing the performance of the current fixed-pattern healing approach?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The conclusion identifies that "our healing experiments assume a fixed sparsity pattern determined during the training-free compression phase, which is sub-optimal. Jointly learning adaptive sparsity patterns during fine-tuning may yield further improvements and is a direction we intend to explore."
- **Why unresolved:** The current method freezes the sparsity mask during fine-tuning, which prevents the model from discovering more optimal weight structures specific to the downstream task; allowing the mask to evolve could yield better reconstructions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A differentiable sparsity learning mechanism (e.g., using continuous relaxations and regularization) integrated into fine-tuning, showing statistically significant accuracy gains over fixed-pattern healing on standard benchmarks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the impact of the per-layer error cap parameter α on compression performance across different architectures and compression targets, and can α be automatically tuned to optimize the trade-off between global budget and local fidelity?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The method section notes that "In all experiments, we set α=α_min. Further per-model tuning of α may yield improved performance," implying that the chosen minimal feasible α may not be optimal.
- **Why unresolved:** Using α_min guarantees a feasible solution but might be overly restrictive, potentially preventing the allocation from favoring layers where higher error is less detrimental; systematic tuning could improve results.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comprehensive evaluation varying α for multiple models and compression ratios, revealing a consistent trend or an automatic selection rule based on model statistics (e.g., layer sensitivity distributions).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive is ROCKET’s compression quality to the size of the calibration dataset, and what is the minimal number of samples required to achieve near-optimal reconstruction error and downstream performance?
- **Basis:** [inferred] The method relies on a small calibration set (256 sequences) to compute the whitening transform and importance scores, but the paper does not analyze the effect of calibration set size—this is an unstated assumption.
- **Why unresolved:** Too few samples may fail to capture the true activation distribution, leading to a poorly estimated basis and importance metrics; too many increase overhead. The sweet spot remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments varying calibration size (e.g., 32, 128, 512, 1024 samples) across multiple models, measuring reconstruction error and zero-shot accuracy to determine robustness and minimal requirements.

## Limitations
- **Scalability to MoE models:** Dynamic programming becomes intractable for architectures with many compressible components (e.g., Mixture-of-Experts), as the state space explodes.
- **Calibration data sensitivity:** Compression quality depends on the representativeness of the small calibration set; distribution mismatch can degrade reconstruction.
- **Fixed sparsity pattern during fine-tuning:** The healing phase freezes the sparsity mask, which may be suboptimal compared to joint sparsity learning.

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** Core empirical claims on LLM compression (e.g., >90% accuracy retention at 30% compression, superiority over SVD-LLM and CoSpaDi) are well-supported by detailed benchmarks and ablations.
- **Medium confidence:** Generalization to vision-language and speech models, as validation is limited to a few models.
- **Low confidence:** Environmental claims (100x less energy than CoSpaDi) due to unspecified measurement methodology.
- **High confidence:** Theoretical formulation of MCKP and closed-form dictionary update is sound.
