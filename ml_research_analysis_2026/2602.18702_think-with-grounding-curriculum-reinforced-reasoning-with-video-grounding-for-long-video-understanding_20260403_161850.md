# Think with Grounding: Curriculum Reinforced Reasoning with Video Grounding for Long Video Understanding

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.18702
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18702
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Video-TwG achieves significant accuracy gains of +7.0, +5.3, and +7.1 on Video-MME, LongVideoBench, and MLVU under low-resolution settings, while reducing redundant grounding actions by 15.7%.

## Executive Summary
Long video understanding suffers from hallucinations when models reason over fixed, coarse-grained context due to temporal redundancy. Video-TwG introduces a "Think-with-Grounding" paradigm where a video LLM dynamically performs on-demand video grounding during multi-turn reasoning, selectively zooming into question-relevant clips. A two-stage reinforced curriculum first trains on short-video GQA data with grounding labels, then generalizes to diverse unlabeled QA data. The TwG-GRPO algorithm uses fine-grained grounding rewards for labeled data and a self-confirmed pseudo-reward for unlabeled data, gated by answer accuracy. Trained on the TwG-51K dataset, Video-TwG (based on Qwen2.5-VL-7B) consistently outperforms strong baselines on three major benchmarks and demonstrates improved efficiency. This work highlights the promise of active, grounded reasoning for both effective and efficient long-video understanding.

## Method Summary
Video-TwG reformulates long-video question answering as an interactive process where the model interleaves thinking steps with grounding actions. The LLM policy decides at each turn whether to generate a thought or to ground by selecting a temporal clip from a pre-sampled coarse set of candidates. When grounding is chosen, the model extracts fine-grained frames from the selected clip and incorporates them into its state. Training employs a two-stage curriculum: stage 1 uses short videos with explicit grounding annotations to bootstrap the grounding policy via fine-grained rewards that measure temporal overlap with ground-truth segments; stage 2 scales to long videos and diverse QA data without grounding annotations, using a self-confirmed pseudo-reward. This pseudo-reward penalizes the policy if the model, after grounding, cannot produce a correct answer in a self-evaluation step, but the grounding-related reward is only granted if the final answer is correct—an accuracy-gating mechanism to avoid optimization conflicts. The TwG-GRPO algorithm optimizes the policy with a group-relative objective, and the entire system is trained on the constructed TwG-51K dataset.

## Key Results
- Consistently outperforms strong baselines on Video-MME, LongVideoBench, and MLVU, with improvements of +7.0, +5.3, and +7.1 overall accuracy under low-resolution settings.
- Reduces redundant grounding actions by 15.7% and improves the quality of selected clips compared to prior approaches.
- Based on Qwen2.5-VL-7B and trained solely on the TwG-51K dataset, demonstrating the effectiveness of the curriculum and reward design.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Think-with-Grounding paradigm addresses the core challenge of temporal redundancy by allowing the model to actively seek evidence rather than digesting the entire video. This dynamic selection focuses computation on relevant moments and mitigates hallucination. The two-stage curriculum is crucial: starting with short, cleanly annotated GQA data provides a stable signal for the grounding action, preventing it from collapsing early; this learned grounding skill then transfers to long videos where annotations are sparse. The accuracy-gated reward ensures that grounding actions are only reinforced when they contribute to a correct final answer, aligning the grounding policy with the ultimate goal and preventing reward hacking (e.g., selecting easy clips that incidentally yield correct answers). The self-confirmed pseudo-reward leverages the model's own reasoning to estimate the utility of a grounding step, providing a scalable signal for unlabeled data; while not perfect, it suffices to guide the policy when combined with the accuracy gate. Finally, GRPO stabilizes training by evaluating actions relative to a batch, which is particularly important given the sparse and delayed rewards in multi-turn reasoning. Together, these components yield both higher accuracy and greater efficiency.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Video → Coarse Frame Sampler (64 frames) → LLM Policy Network (action head) → Action: {Think, Ground(Clip_i)} → if Ground: Fine Frame Extractor (16 frames from Clip_i) → LLM state update → repeat → Answer generation

**Critical Path**  
During a typical inference run, the video is first uniformly sampled to 64 coarse frames. The LLM, conditioned on the question and current state, outputs a thought or a grounding action. If grounding is selected, the model picks one of the coarse clips, extracts 16 fine-grained frames within that temporal segment, and feeds them back to the LLM. This loop continues until the model emits a final answer. The accuracy gating is applied only during training, not inference.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Coarse vs. fine granularity*: More coarse frames provide broader context but increase redundancy; more fine frames yield detail but raise computational cost. The fixed 64/16 split balances this but may not adapt to question complexity.
- *Accuracy gating*: Aligns grounding rewards with final correctness but risks discouraging exploratory grounding that only pays off after multiple steps.
- *Pseudo-reward vs. human annotation*: Enables scalability to unlabeled data but introduces potential bias from the model's own self-assessment.

**Failure Signatures**  
- *Vanishing grounding*: The policy ignores the grounding action entirely, relying solely on the initial coarse frames; this occurs if the curriculum is skipped or rewards are poorly shaped.
- *Irrelevant clip selection*: The model grounds on temporally incoherent or semantically unrelated segments, leading to wrong answers and inefficient token usage.
- *Over-penalization*: The pseudo-reward may penalize a grounding action that is actually necessary but does not immediately yield a correct answer, causing the policy to avoid necessary exploration.
- *Redundant grounding*: Selecting overlapping or multiple similar clips without gaining new information, indicating a lack of progress in the reasoning loop.

**First Experiments**  
1. *Curriculum necessity*: Train without the short-video stage (skip stage 1) and measure grounding action frequency and final accuracy; expect grounding to collapse and performance to drop.
2. *Accuracy gating impact*: Remove the gating (apply grounding rewards even for incorrect final answers) and compare QA accuracy and grounding behavior; anticipate poorer final performance and possible reward hacking.
3. *Pseudo-reward calibration*: Compute correlation between R_pseudo scores and human-annotated relevance of the last grounded clip on a small validation set of unlabeled long videos; assess whether the self-assessment aligns with true grounding utility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can Video-TwG be extended to incorporate fine-grained process reward modeling beyond trajectory-level optimization, as suggested for future work?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Conclusion states: "In future work, we plan to go beyond trajectory-level optimization by developing fine-grained process reward modeling and single-step training under more complex constraints, aiming to build more powerful and efficient long video reasoning models."
- Why unresolved: The current TwG-GRPO uses trajectory-level rewards. Fine-grained, step-by-step process rewards could provide more granular supervision for intermediate grounding actions, potentially improving grounding quality and reasoning stability, but the design and integration of such rewards remain unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing and integrating a step-level reward model (e.g., trained on human-annotated reasoning traces or via auxiliary models) that provides immediate feedback on the quality of each `` and `<ground>` action, then demonstrating consistent improvements on long-video benchmarks through ablation studies.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent does the proposed Self-Confirmed Pseudo Reward (`R_pseudo`) reliably approximate grounding quality on unlabeled general QA data, and in what scenarios does it fail?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper introduces `R_pseudo` to replace `R_grounding` for unlabeled data, using the model itself to judge if the last grounded clip is sufficient. However, the paper notes the difficulty in distinguishing helpful from useless grounding actions, and the pseudo-reward is a penalty-based heuristic. Its correlation with true grounding utility (e.g., human judgment or oracle grounding) is not quantified beyond indirect QA performance gains.
- Why unresolved: The self-confirmation mechanism may suffer from the model's own biases or hallucinations. A model might produce a correct answer from an irrelevant clip by chance or due to prior knowledge, leading to a false positive (low penalty) for bad grounding. Conversely, it might penalize useful grounding if the model fails to answer correctly from that clip alone due to complexity. The failure modes and robustness across diverse video domains are not analyzed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct a detailed analysis on a held-out set of unlabeled general QA samples where, besides the final answer, human annotators also label the relevance of the *last grounded clip* to the question. Correlate `R_pseudo` scores with human relevance judgments. Stress-test with cases where the correct answer requires multi-step grounding (beyond the last clip) to see if `R_pseudo` misguides.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does the accuracy-gated mechanism (which only applies grounding rewards if the final answer is correct) inadvertently discourage necessary but risky exploratory grounding that could lead to correct answers after multiple steps?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 3.5 describes the accuracy-gated mechanism: "the grounding-related reward is awarded only if the trajectory leads to a correct final answer to avoid the optimization conflict." The ablation (Fig. 5, Tab. 3) shows that removing it leads to lower final accuracy, indicating a conflict.
- Why unresolved: The gate prioritizes answer correctness but may suppress grounding actions that are initially unproductive but are part of a longer, correct reasoning chain. For complex questions requiring multiple grounding steps to gather evidence, an early grounding action might not immediately yield a correct final answer but is essential for later steps. The current reward structure might train the model to avoid such "low-reward" early grounding, potentially harming performance on questions needing sequential evidence gathering.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyze the distribution of grounding actions across the turn sequence for correct vs. incorrect final answers. Specifically, measure the frequency and quality of *first-turn* grounding actions in successful multi-turn trajectories. If successful multi-turn reasoning often begins with an "unrewarded" grounding step (because the final answer comes later), the gating might be suboptimal. An experiment where grounding rewards are provided independently of final answer correctness (with appropriate regularization) could test this.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How critical is the availability of high-quality short-video GQA data (with grounding labels) for the cold-start stage of the curriculum, and can the curriculum be replaced or bootstrapped without such labeled data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Two-stage Reinforced Curriculum Strategy (Section 3.4) explicitly uses "short video GQA data with grounding labels" for stage 1 as a "cold start." Ablation (Fig. 4) shows that skipping stage 1 causes the grounding action to vanish quickly during stage 2.
- Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the necessity of stage 1 but does not probe its data requirements. It is unclear how much labeled short GQA data is needed, whether the quality of grounding labels matters, or if alternative cold-start strategies (e.g., using synthetic grounding hints, prompting, or different unlabeled data with heuristic rewards) could achieve similar stabilization. This limits applicability to domains where such labeled short GQA data is scarce or expensive.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct controlled experiments varying the size and source (e.g., synthetic vs. human-annotated) of stage 1 GQA data. Compare against ablations where stage 1 uses unlabeled short video QA with only the `R_pseudo` reward or with imitation learning from a small set of supervised grounding trajectories. Measure grounding action retention and final accuracy in stage 2.

### Open Question 5
- Question: Is the fixed multi-grained video representation (coarse 64 frames, fine 16 frames) optimal for all video lengths and question types, or would an adaptive, question-driven selection of frame counts and granularity improve efficiency and performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The method uses static parameters for coarse (`F_coarse=64`) and fine (`F_fine=16`) frame sampling during training and inference (Section 4.1). The grounding action selects a clip from the coarse frame indices. The design assumes a fixed trade-off between global context and local detail. However, the paper's motivation is to avoid redundancy; the optimal amount of "zoom-in" detail likely varies by question (e.g., a global activity question might need less fine detail than a subtle object attribute question). The current system does not adapt the fine-grained frame count (`F_fine`) or token budget per clip based on the uncertainty or nature of the question.
- Why unresolved: A one-size-fits-all representation might over-process simple questions (wasting compute) or under-process complex ones. The grounding reward encourages selecting the *right clip* but not the *right amount of detail* within that clip. The efficiency gains (15.7% reduction in redundant groundings) might improve further if the model could also decide *how many* fine frames to extract based on the thinking process.
- What evidence would resolve it: Modify the action space to allow variable `F_fine` (e.g., choosing from a discrete set {8, 16, 32}) or a continuous budget, with rewards adjusted for computational cost. Compare performance and average token usage against the fixed baseline, especially analyzing subsets of questions by type (e.g., "attribute" vs. "activity" questions) to see if adaptive granularity yields benefits.

## Limitations
- The claim of reduced hallucinations relies on benchmark-specific qualitative assessments and lacks a standardized metric.
- Generalization to unlabeled long videos via the self-confirmed pseudo-reward assumes that the model's self-assessment correlates with true grounding utility; this correlation is not quantified and may fail on out-of-distribution content.
- The construction details and potential biases of the TwG-51K dataset are not thoroughly described, which hinders reproducibility and raises questions about dataset representativeness.
