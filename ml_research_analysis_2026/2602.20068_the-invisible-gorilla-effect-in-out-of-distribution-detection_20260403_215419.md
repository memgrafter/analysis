# The Invisible Gorilla Effect in Out-of-distribution Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20068
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20068
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** OOD detection performance drops significantly when artefact colour differs from the model's region of interest (ROI); e.g., Mahalanobis Score AUROC 31.5% higher for red ink (similar) vs. black (dissimilar) on a skin lesion classifier.

## Executive Summary
The paper reveals the "Invisible Gorilla Effect" in out-of-distribution (OOD) detection: detection accuracy for near-OOD artefacts improves when the artefact colour resembles the model's region of interest (ROI). Through large-scale experiments on medical (ISIC, CheXpert) and industrial (MVTec-AD) datasets, evaluating 40 OOD methods across 3795 hyperparameters, they find feature-based methods suffer larger performance drops (mean 7.1 percentage points) for colour-dissimilar artefacts compared to confidence-based methods (1.5 pp). Mechanistically, PCA shows colour-sensitive latent directions align with high-variance components; removing a 5D nuisance subspace nearly eliminates the gap for Mahalanobis. The effect is architecture-agnostic, confirmed with colour-swapped counterfactuals, and highlights a critical yet previously overlooked failure mode.

## Method Summary
The authors train primary classifiers (ResNet18, VGG16, ViT-B/32) on artefact-free in-distribution data from ISIC (skin lesions), CheXpert (chest X-rays), and MVTec-AD (industrial tools). They then evaluate 40 OOD detection methods on test sets containing synthetic artefacts (e.g., coloured ink patches) whose colour is either similar or dissimilar to the ROI's mean colour. The ROI is segmented using SAM; artefact colour similarity is determined by Euclidean distance between mean RGB values. Extensive hyperparameter sweeps (3795 configurations) across 7 benchmarks yield robust measurements of AUROC, AURC, and FPR@TPR80. To probe the mechanism, they compute PCA on latent features from a hidden layer, define colour-sensitivity scores for each principal component, and demonstrate that high-variance directions are colour-sensitive. A mitigation projects features orthogonal to a 5-dimensional nuisance subspace before applying feature-based methods, which dramatically reduces the performance gap.

## Key Results
- Feature-based OOD methods show a mean AUROC drop of 7.1 percentage points for colour-dissimilar vs. similar artefacts; confidence-based methods drop only 1.5 pp.
- For a skin lesion classifier, Mahalanobis Score achieves 31.5% higher AUROC for red ink (similar to skin lesion colour) than for black ink (dissimilar).
- Removing a 5D nuisance subspace identified via PCA shrinks Mahalanobis's AUROC gap from 13.4 pp to 1.7 pp on the ISIC ink benchmark, nearly eliminating the effect.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Feature-based OOD methods under-penalize colour-shifted OOD artefacts because their scoring functions (e.g., Mahalanobis distance) downweight high-variance latent directions, and colour variation aligns with these directions.
- **Mechanism:** The latent space of DNNs contains directions of high variance that often capture low-level, dataset-specific statistics like colour. When an OOD artefact induces feature shifts along these high-variance directions, feature-based detectors that normalise by the covariance assign lower anomaly scores because the shifted features still fall within the "normal" high-variance bounds. Thus, dissimilar-colour artefacts, which provoke larger shifts along these nuisance directions, may appear less anomalous than similar-colour artefacts that shift in lower-variance, more task-relevant directions.
- **Core assumption:** The primary model's latent space contains separable nuisance subspaces (e.g., colour) that are high-variance and can be identified via PCA on in-distribution (ID) features.
- **Evidence anchors:** PCA revealed colour-sensitive latent directions align with high-variance components; projecting out a 5D nuisance subspace reduced Mahalanobis's gap from 13.4 to 1.7 pp. Spearman correlation ρ=0.47 between log PCA variance and colour sensitivity.
- **Break condition:** If the OOD artefact's colour variation projects primarily onto low-variance latent directions, the effect may reverse. Methods that do not normalise by feature covariance are less affected.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Artefacts visually similar to the model's region of interest (ROI) are more salient to the model's attention, leading to more distinctive feature representations that are easier for OOD detectors to identify.
- **Mechanism:** DNNs trained for a primary task learn to focus on the ROI. When an OOD artefact shares visual properties (e.g., colour) with the ROI, it activates similar feature pathways, potentially creating a more coherent and less "blended" anomalous signal. Conversely, dissimilar-colour artefacts may be partially suppressed or integrated into background feature statistics, making their overall feature distribution closer to ID data and thus harder to detect.
- **Core assumption:** The model's ROI is stable and can be identified (e.g., via saliency maps); artefacts that resemble the ROI elicit stronger, more discriminative activation patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:** Models trained on counterfactuals with hypointense hearts showed reversed performance: better detection of hypointense artefacts than hyperintense ones. Consistent AUROC drops across methods when artefact colour differs from ROI mean RGB.
- **Break condition:** If the model relies on contextual features rather than the ROI (Clever Hans effect), the effect may not hold. Very large artefacts (<10% image area rule in ISIC) are detected regardless of colour, breaking the pattern.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The effect persists across architectures and is not a dataset artifact; it reflects a fundamental bias in how models encode colour in latent space.
- **Mechanism:** Colour-swapped counterfactuals rule out the possibility that the effect is due to confounding correlations in the original datasets. By synthetically altering artefact colours while keeping shape/texture constant, the authors isolate colour as the causal factor. The consistent drop across ResNet18, VGG16, and ViT-B/32 suggests the mechanism is architecture-agnostic, likely tied to how early convolutional or attention layers process colour.
- **Core assumption:** Colour is an independent, trainable nuisance factor that affects latent representations similarly across architectures.
- **Evidence anchors:** Colour-swapped counterfactuals ruled out dataset bias. Results hold across three architectures. The effect is observed in multiple medical and industrial benchmarks.
- **Break condition:** If the primary task itself is colour-dependent, the effect may be masked or amplified. For datasets where ROI colour variance is minimal, the effect may be negligible.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: OOD Detection Taxonomy**  
- **Why needed here:** The paper distinguishes between feature-based, confidence-based, ad-hoc, and external methods. Understanding this taxonomy is crucial because the Invisible Gorilla Effect disproportionately impacts feature-based methods (mean AUROC drop 7.1 pp vs. 1.5 pp for confidence-based).  
- **Quick check question:** Why do feature-based methods like Mahalanobis Score show larger performance drops than confidence-based methods like MCP when artefact colour differs from the ROI?

**Concept: PCA for Latent Space Analysis**  
- **Why needed here:** The authors use PCA to identify high-variance latent directions that align with colour sensitivity. This is key to diagnosing the mechanism and designing the subspace projection mitigation.  
- **Quick check question:** How does computing the Spearman correlation between log PCA eigenvalues (λk) and colour sensitivity scores (Ik) reveal the nuisance subspace?

**Concept: Region of Interest (ROI) Definition**  
- **Why needed here:** The entire effect hinges on what the model attends to. The ROI is operationalised via saliency maps and SAM segmentation. Misidentifying the ROI would invalidate the similar/dissimilar colour grouping.  
- **Quick check question:** How was the model's ROI validated for the ISIC skin lesion classifier, and why was this step necessary?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Primary DNN (ResNet18/VGG16/ViT-B/32) -> Feature extractor (hidden layer) -> OOD detector (e.g., Mahalanobis Score). Supporting modules: SAM segmentation (ROI and artefact masks), PCA module (latent decomposition), Nuisance subspace projector (orthogonal projection matrix), Evaluation metrics (AUROC, AURC, FPR@TPR80).

**Critical path:**  
1. Train primary model on artefact-free ID data.  
2. For ID training set, extract features from a chosen hidden layer.  
3. Apply PCA to ID features to obtain principal components (PCs) and eigenvalues.  
4. For each PC, compute colour sensitivity Ik by projecting OOD artefacts (with known colour labels) and measuring discriminative power.  
5. Select top-k PCs with highest Ik as nuisance subspace U.  
6. For test OOD samples, project features orthogonal to U: F⊥ = (I - UU⊤)F.  
7. Apply OOD scoring function to projected features.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **k selection:** Too small (k<5) may not capture all nuisance variance; too large (k>10) risks removing task-relevant structure.  
- **Layer selection:** The effect varies across layers; must evaluate multiple layers to avoid cherry-picking.  
- **ROI segmentation accuracy:** SAM prompts require human annotation; imprecise masks will misestimate ROI mean RGB.  
- **Projection vs. ID performance:** Subspace projection might degrade ID accuracy if task-relevant features lie in the nuisance subspace.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **No AUROC drop** for dissimilar artefacts (e.g., DDPM-MSE): suggests method is insensitive to latent colour variation.  
- **Negative AUROC drop** (e.g., DICE under heavy augmentation): indicates augmentation or projection harmed detection of similar artefacts.  
- **Low correlation** between logλk and Ik (ρ near 0): nuisance variance not in high-variance directions, so projection may not help.  
- **High variance** across hyperparameters: results may not generalise without exhaustive search.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Benchmark replication:** On ISIC, train ResNet18 without artefacts, evaluate Mahalanobis on red vs. black ink. Expect ~31.5% AUROC improvement for red (similar) vs. black (dissimilar).  
2. **PCA validation:** Compute PCA on layer1.1 features; plot logλk vs. Ik for red/black artefacts. Expect positive Spearman correlation (ρ≈0.47).  
3. **Mitigation test:** Apply nuisance subspace projection (k=5) to features before Mahalanobis scoring. Expect gap reduction from ~13 pp to <2 pp on ISIC ink benchmark.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** Why do feature-based OOD detection methods exhibit larger performance drops when OOD artefact color differs from the model's ROI compared to confidence-based methods?  
- **Basis in paper:** Results in Table 2 show feature-based methods have a mean AUROC drop of 7.1 pp versus 1.5 pp for confidence-based methods. PCA analysis in Section 3.3 indicates color-sensitive latent directions align with high-variance components, hypothesized to cause under-penalization of dissimilar artefacts.  
- **Why unresolved:** The hypothesis relies on correlation (Spearman ρ=0.47) and projection experiments; direct causal links across diverse methods and datasets are lacking.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments isolating variance alignment, e.g., manipulating latent space properties or designing feature-based methods that explicitly decouple high-variance color signals.

**Open Question 2:** Does the Invisible Gorilla Effect extend to other forms of visual similarity beyond color, such as texture, brightness, or shape similarity to the ROI?  
- **Basis in paper:** The paper focuses on color as a controlled, practical factor but defines the effect broadly as "visual similarity" to ROI.  
- **Why unresolved:** The study systematically varies only color; other attributes may independently bias detection or interact with color, limiting understanding of the effect's scope.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic benchmarks where multiple visual attributes (e.g., texture, luminance) are varied orthogonally across OOD artefacts, evaluating detection performance across methods.

**Open Question 3:** Can the nuisance subspace projection mitigation strategy be generalized to improve robustness against the Invisible Gorilla Effect for all feature-based OOD detection methods and across diverse domains?  
- **Basis in paper:** Subspace projection reduced the AUROC gap for Mahalanobis and FeatureNorm on ISIC/MVTec but failed for XOOD-M, and was tested on only two datasets.  
- **Why unresolved:** Effectiveness appears method-specific and domain-limited; broader validation is needed for practical deployment in varied settings (e.g., natural images, different architectures).  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large-scale evaluation of projection across 40+ OOD methods, multiple domains (medical, industrial, natural), and primary model architectures, with analysis of failure cases.

## Limitations
- **ROI mask availability:** Exact masks for skin lesions (ISIC) and industrial tools (MVTec-AD) are unreleased, risking inconsistent colour-similarity labeling. SAM-based segmentation prompts may introduce variability.
- **Hyperparameter opacity:** Full search spaces and best configurations for 40 OOD methods (3795 settings) are undocumented, hindering exact replication of reported AUROC gaps.
- **Implementation gaps:** Training code, feature extraction layers, PCA computation, and subspace projection are unreleased, requiring inference that may deviate from authors' pipeline.

## Confidence
- **Existence of the Invisible Gorilla Effect (colour–ROI similarity impacts OOD detection):** High
- **Mechanism via high-variance nuisance directions:** Medium
- **Mitigation via nuisance subspace projection:** High
- **Architecture-agnostic nature:** High
