# EPIC-EuroParl-UdS: Information-Theoretic Perspectives on Translation and Interpreting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09785
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09785
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Provides a merged, annotated English-German parliamentary speech corpus (EPIC-EuroParl-UdS) with word-level surprisal and alignment, enabling cross-modal information-theoretic analysis and demonstrating in a case study that base (non-fine-tuned) surprisal models better predict filler particle occurrence in interpreting.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the EPIC-EuroParl-UdS corpus, a resource that combines and updates spoken (EPIC-UdS) and written (EuroParl-UdS) English-German parliamentary speech data. The corpus addresses a gap in resources for information-theoretic studies of translation and interpreting by providing corrected metadata, balanced translation directions, and novel annotations including word alignment and word-level surprisal values from both base and fine-tuned GPT-2 and machine translation models. An application study uses these annotations to predict filler particle (e.g., *euh*, *hum*) occurrence in interpreting via mixed-effects logistic regression, finding that models using base (off-the-shelf) surprisal provide a better fit than those using fine-tuned surprisal. The resource is designed to support research comparing written and spoken modes, investigating translationese, and decomposing production difficulty into comprehension, formulation, and transfer components.

## Method Summary
The study’s core method is a case analysis using the newly created EPIC-EuroParl-UdS corpus. The corpus construction involved merging and cleaning existing written and spoken parliamentary datasets, removing document overlap to prevent bias, and balancing translation directions in the written component. Key annotations were added: (1) word-level surprisal for every token, computed from base and fine-tuned versions of a monolingual GPT-2 model and a direction-specific machine translation model, and (2) automatic word alignment between source and target segments. For the filler particle (FP) prediction application, the analysis used mixed-effects logistic regression on the spoken subcorpus. The dependent variable was a binary indicator of whether a target word was immediately preceded by an FP. Fixed-effect predictors were z-scored surprisal values (next-word and segment-average) from the four model types, intended to capture local/global aspects of comprehension (source surprisal), formulation (target surprisal), and transfer (MT surprisal). Random intercepts for speaker and document accounted for data hierarchy. Models were fit separately for each language direction (DE-EN, EN-DE) and model type (base vs. fine-tuned), with fit compared via Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and discrimination via C-score.

## Key Results
- Base surprisal models (using off-the-shelf GPT-2/MT) fit the FP prediction data better than fine-tuned models, as indicated by lower AIC (e.g., DE-EN: 11359 vs. 11473) and higher C-score (e.g., DE-EN: 0.699 vs. 0.686).
- Target next-word surprisal has a strong positive effect on FP probability, indicating that local formulation difficulty increases disfluency.
- Average source surprisal across a segment has a positive effect, suggesting that global comprehension difficulty promotes filler use.
- Average target surprisal has a negative effect, which may reflect a compensatory strategy or planning benefit.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Word-level surprisal annotations derived from language and translation models operationalize cognitive effort during translation and interpreting production.
- **Mechanism:** Surprisal (S(w) = -log₂ P(w|context)) quantifies the unexpectedness of a word given its left context, with higher values indicating greater processing difficulty. By annotating every token in parallel source-target segments with surprisal from base/fine-tuned GPT-2 (monolingual fluency) and MT models (cross-lingual transfer), the corpus provides a continuous, context-sensitive measure of effort at the point of production or comprehension. The core assumption is that these model-based surprisal estimates are valid proxies for allocational processing effort in human language production.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract mentions providing word-level surprisal from base and fine-tuned models; Section 2 defines surprisal and its links to cognitive effort; Section 3.4 details computation; the corpus `vertical` format includes `srp_base_gpt2`, `srp_ft_gpt2`, `srp_base_mt`, `srp_ft_mt` fields.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The corpus’s balanced, mode-separated structure with removed overlap allows for isolating the effects of modality (spoken vs. written) from text type (original vs. translated) in information-theoretic analyses.
- **Mechanism:** By construction, the written subcorpus is balanced in translation direction, and overlapping documents appearing in both modes are removed from the written component. This prevents confounds: comparisons between written originals and translations are not skewed by spoken-origin material, and cross-modal comparisons use non-overlapping documents. The design assumes linguistic variation from modality (e.g., higher entropy in speech) can be separated from translationese when document overlap is controlled and direction is balanced.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states the resource supports comparing written and spoken modes; Section 3.1 describes overlap removal and direction balancing; the corpus includes `mode` (`SP`/`WR`) and `ttype` (`ORG`/`TRG`) fields with non-overlapping `doc_id`s.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The inclusion of word alignment and multi-model surprisal allows decomposing production difficulty in interpreting into comprehension (source surprisal), formulation (target surprisal), and transfer (MT surprisal) components.
- **Mechanism:** Word alignment links source and target tokens. This linkage permits: assigning source surprisal to the comprehended input that triggered a target word; computing target surprisal on the interpreter’s output; and computing MT surprisal on the target word conditioned on the full aligned source segment (simulating a full-context translation task). Differences between these measures model distinct cognitive sub-processes.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract lists word alignment and multi-model surprisal as new annotations; Section 3.4 describes the AWESoME-inspired alignment method; Section 4.1 explicitly uses this decomposition with source-related and MT surprisal predictors; the corpus `vertical` format includes `aligned_word_id` to map tokens.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Information-Theoretic Measures (Surprisal, Entropy)
- **Why needed here:** The entire corpus is built around surprisal as a key variable. Users must understand that surprisal is a *local*, context-dependent measure (for a word) versus entropy, a *global* measure of overall uncertainty in a model/distribution.
- **Quick check:** If a word has a surprisal of 5 bits, what does this imply about its probability given the left context? How would the average surprisal over a segment relate to the segment's cross-entropy?

### Concept: Parallel Corpus Design & Translationese
- **Why needed here:** The corpus is a tool for translation and interpreting studies. Users need to distinguish *parallel* (source-target pairs) from *comparable* (originals vs. translations in same language) designs, and understand "translationese" as systematic deviance from non-translated text. The corpus structure (`lpair`, `ttype`, `mode`) directly supports these designs.
- **Quick check:** To study whether English translations from German are more explicit than English originals, which `ttype` values would you compare in the `written` subcorpus? What is a potential confound if you accidentally include `spoken` data?

### Concept: Mixed-Effects Logistic Regression
- **Why needed here:** The case study uses this model to account for non-independence (words nested in segments, speakers, documents). Replicating or extending the analysis requires understanding the need for random effects (`(1|speaker_id)`, `(1|doc_id)`) to avoid inflated significance.
- **Quick check:** Why is a standard logistic regression inadequate for modeling FP occurrence across all target words? What would violating the independence assumption likely do to the p-values of your fixed effects?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:
Raw Sources (EuroParl XML + audio transcripts) -> Preprocessing Pipeline (text extraction, sentence alignment, metadata standardization, overlap removal, punctuation addition) -> Linguistic Annotation Layer (Stanza: tokenization, POS, lemma, deps; special handling for FPs and multiword tokens) -> Information-Theoretic Annotation Layer (surprisal computation from GPT-2/MT; word alignment via BERT-based method) -> Integration & Formatting (merge annotations into canonical `word_id`; generate `vertical`, `long`, `wide` views; split written data into `train`/`test`) -> Output (distributed `.tsv.gz` tables).

Critical path: The most fragile and sequence-dependent step is **surprisal annotation alignment**. It depends on correct Stanza tokenization, model input formatting that preserves surface forms, and post-processing rules for ~3% tokenization mismatches. Failure here propagates to all surprisal-based analyses. The `raw_seg` field helps diagnose misalignments.

Design tradeoffs:
1. **Tokenization:** Used Stanza UD tokenization as canonical but fed *surface* forms to LMs to avoid probability inflation. This creates a multi-row `word_id` structure for multiword tokens. Tradeoff: Complexity vs. accuracy of surprisal estimation.
2. **Surprisal context:** Used segment-bounded context (`<|bos|>` + left context up to 150 tokens) vs. sliding window. Tradeoff: Standardized conditioning vs. potential boundary effects.
3. **Alignment threshold:** Fixed softmax score threshold (0.01) from AWESoME. Tradeoff: Prevents noise from low-confidence links but may miss valid divergences, especially in spoken data (high % unaligned).

Failure signatures:
- **High N/A surprisal rate:** In `vertical`, `srp_*` = N/A for FPs, expanded multiword tokens, and ~3% of segments with tokenization mismatches. **Action:** Filter N/A or investigate `raw_seg` and `misc`.
- **Unaligned source words (`aligned_word = 'NA'`):** Expected, especially in spoken (35%). **Action:** Exclude these rows for source surprisal analysis, reducing sample size.
- **Directional imbalance in train/test:** The `train` sets for DE-EN and EN-DE are balanced by subsampling. **Action:** Use the provided `train` split for fair comparisons.
- **FP handling:** FPs are removed for surprisal computation but present in `vertical` (N/A). Clean versions exclude them entirely. **Action:** Be explicit whether FPs are included in token counts but excluded from surprisal calculations.

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate FP Prediction: Use spoken `vertical` files. Prepare dataset: filter `ttype=="tgt"`, create binary FP label (previous token in `["euh","hum","hm"]`), retrieve aligned source surprisal (average if multiple alignments), compute segment-average surprisals, z-score predictors, drop N/As. Fit mixed-effects logistic regression with `FP_occurrence ~ nxtwS_tgt + nxtwS_src + nxtwS_mt + AvS_tgt + AvS_src + AvS_mt + (1|speaker_id) + (1|doc_id)` separately for each `lpair` and surprisal type (base vs. fine-tuned). Compare AIC and C-score.
2. Explore Accuracy-Fluency Trade-off: For written test set, plot target `srp_base_mt` (x) vs. `srp_base_gpt2` (y) per word. Fit a GAM to test for nonlinearity. Replicate finding that negative correlation holds only for low-surprisal words.
3. Cross-modal Translationese Comparison: Compute "translatedness" score: `(srp_base_gpt2_target - srp_base_gpt2_original)` for comparable language (e.g., EN target from DE-EN vs. EN original in written). Compare this gap between `written` and `spoken` modes. Does the information-theoretic gap differ?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Alternative model architectures:** How do surprisal estimates from models like multilingual LLMs (LLaMA) compare to the provided GPT-2/MT surprisals in capturing processing effort and predicting linguistic variation? The paper plans to incorporate such models in future updates.
- **Time-aligned source surprisal:** How does using incremental source surprisal (reflecting the interpreter’s real-time input) change relationships between comprehension difficulty and filler particles, compared to full-segment source surprisal? The current analysis uses full-segment surprisal, not accounting for the interpreter’s limited auditory context.
- **Unexplained directional exception in spoken interpreting:** Why does transfer difficulty (MT surprisal) fail to predict translatedness in spoken DE-EN interpreting, contrary to its strong role in other settings? This exception is noted but not investigated.
- **Nonlinear accuracy-fluency trade-off constraints:** What specific production constraints cause the observed nonlinear breakdown of the accuracy-fluency trade-off at high transfer difficulty, and how does this relate to cognitive load in interpreting versus translation? The paper suggests inherent production constraints but does not test specific factors.

## Limitations
- **Surprisal validity gap:** The assumption that model surprisal proxies human processing effort is theoretically grounded but not validated on this specific parliamentary speech data; high surprisal may not consistently predict disfluencies here.
- **Alignment noise in spoken data:** Automatic word alignment yields 35% unaligned source tokens in interpreting, likely biasing source–surprisal assignments and reducing sample size for analyses relying on alignment.
- **Residual confounding:** Despite overlap removal, unmeasured factors (topic, speaker ideology, interpreter experience) may covary with modality and text type, threatening causal claims about modality effects.

## Confidence
- **High:** The corpus exists, is publicly available, and its construction (metadata correction, direction balancing, overlap removal) is transparently documented; provided annotations are reproducible from the described pipeline.
- **Medium:**
  - *Mechanism 1 (Surprisal as effort proxy):* Theoretically grounded but model-specific validity is uncertain; transformer surprisal may not align with incremental human processing.
  - *Mechanism 2 (Isolated modality/translation effects):* Design is rigorous but cannot guarantee orthogonality of all confounders.
  - *Mechanism 3 (Decomposition via alignment):* Alignment method is sound but performance drops in spoken data; MT surprisal as a "transfer" norm may not reflect interpreter constraints.
  - *FP prediction results:* Directional patterns are plausible, but exact model specification and significance tests are irreproducible without further details on random effects and multi-alignment handling.
- **Low:** Not applicable.

## Next Checks
1. **Human–surprisal correlation pilot:** For a balanced subset of 50 spoken segments, collect behavioral indices (e.g., disfluency duration, word onset latency) and compute correlation with provided `srp_base_gpt2`/`srp_base_mt`. Expected: moderate positive *r* (0.3–0.5) for `srp_base_gpt2`; near-zero would falsify the proxy assumption.
2. **Confounding audit in cross–modal comparison:** Replicate the translationese analysis adding `topic_id`, `speaker_gender`, and `speaker_party` as fixed effects in a mixed model predicting `srp_base_gpt2` difference. Expected: modality effect remains; if it disappears, original claims are compromised.
3. **Alignment quality manual evaluation:** Randomly sample 200 target words from the spoken test set. Have annotators judge correctness of `aligned_word_id`. Compute Cohen’s κ and F1. Threshold: if κ < 0.6 or F1 < 0.7, re-run analyses relying on `nxtwS_src` with alignment-quality filters.
