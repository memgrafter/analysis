# DIET: Learning to Distill Dataset Continually for Recommender Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24958  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24958  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Reduces training data to 1–2% while preserving full-data training performance trends, cutting model iteration cost by up to 60× and generalizing robustly across diverse recommender architectures.

## Executive Summary
Retraining large-scale recommender models on full historical streaming data for architecture iteration is prohibitively expensive. This paper formulates the problem as **streaming dataset distillation**: creating a compact, evolving synthetic dataset that faithfully approximates full-data training dynamics over time. The proposed method, DIET, constructs an evolving boundary memory by initializing synthetic samples from task-conditioned influential interactions and continually fusing them with aligned historical memory. Optimization uses an influence-guided bidirectional addressing mechanism within a bi-level framework to selectively update active memory units against hard real targets. Results show that DIET achieves high compression (1–2%) with performance comparable to full-data training and strong cross-architecture generalization.

## Method Summary
DIET operates in two phases per streaming stage. **Phase 1 (Memory Initialization & Maintenance)**: For a new data block \( D_t \), task-conditioned influential samples are selected via EL2N scoring on the teacher model checkpoint \( \phi_t \). These real samples are converted to embedding–soft label pairs to form a new synthetic core set \( S_t \). Historical synthetic data from previous stages \( D_{1:t-1}^{syn} \) are scored by influence alignment with \( S_t \), and the top-aligned subset is fused with \( S_t \) to form the stage’s initialized synthetic dataset \( D_t^{syn} \). **Phase 2 (Optimization)**: A bi-level loop refines \( D_t^{syn} \). A bidirectional addressor uses influence scores to identify a hard target set \( B_t^{hard} \) of real samples and an active synthetic memory \( M_t^{active} \). An inner-loop trains a proxy model on \( M_t^{active} \); an outer-loop meta-update via random-truncated BPTT (RaT-BPTT) adjusts \( D_t^{syn} \) to minimize loss on \( B_t^{hard} \). After processing all stages, the aggregated synthetic dataset \( D_{1:T}^{syn} \) is used to train candidate models with a warmup protocol.

## Key Results
- Compresses training data to 1–2% while matching full-data AUC on datasets like KuaiRand.
- Cuts model iteration cost by up to 60×.
- Distilled data generalizes across multiple recommender architectures (DCN, DCNv2, FinalNet, FinalMLP, WuKong).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Influential Sample Initialization
- **Claim:** Distilled samples initialized from task-conditioned influential interactions (via EL2N scoring) capture training-critical signals and form an effective optimization starting point in high-dimensional, sparse recommender data.
- **Mechanism:** For each label combination \( c \) in block \( D_t \), samples are ranked by EL2N score (Eq. 6). Samples in the upper-middle range per label group are selected to form a balanced boundary candidate set \( S_t \). Real samples are converted to embedding–soft label pairs \((e, \hat{y})\) using teacher \( \phi_t \) to create initial synthetic \( D_t^{syn} \).
- **Core assumption:** Training influence is highly concentrated; a small subset of samples disproportionately shapes the parameter update trajectory. EL2N is a practical proxy for this influence.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "initializes synthetic samples from task-conditioned influential interactions (via EL2N scoring)"; [section 4.1.1] "we identify task-conditioned influential samples... we use it as a practical proxy for identifying training-influential samples".
- **Break condition:** If the data stream’s label distributions shift dramatically, the fixed “upper-middle” EL2N percentile selection may miss emerging critical patterns, causing initialization to fail.

### Mechanism 2: Influence-Guided Bidirectional Memory Addressing
- **Claim:** An influence-guided bidirectional memory addressing mechanism selectively updates synthetic memory units against hard real targets, ensuring stable and efficient continual updates without redundant or conflicting signals.
- **Mechanism:** For each real sample \( z \) in batch \( B_t \), \( Deficiency(z) = \sum_{x \in D_t^{syn}} A_t(x, z) \). Samples with smallest deficiency form hard target set \( B_t^{hard} \). For each synthetic \( x \), \( Resp(x) = \sum_{z \in B_t^{hard}} A_t(x, z) \); samples with largest response form active memory \( M_t^{active} \). Only active units are updated in the inner loop.
- **Core assumption:** Not all synthetic samples equally contribute to reducing loss on current hard examples; selective updating prevents optimization instability.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "influence-guided bidirectional addressing mechanism... to selectively update active memory units against hard real targets"; [section 4.2.1] "Bidirectional memory addressing... identifies which real samples are most difficult... and which synthetic units are most responsible for representing them".
- **Break condition:** If gradient alignment \( A_t(x, z) \) becomes unreliable (e.g., due to non-convex landscapes or sparse gradients), selection of \( B_t^{hard} \) and \( M_t^{active} \) may misalign, leading to unstable distillation.

### Mechanism 3: Continual Memory Fusion
- **Claim:** Continual memory fusion (retraining aligned historical synthetic samples with newly initialized ones) preserves cumulative training effects across stages, preventing temporal drift away from historically important decision boundaries.
- **Mechanism:** After selecting new boundary samples \( S_t \) from \( D_t \), historical synthetic samples from \( D_{1:t-1}^{syn} \) are scored by influence weight \( \alpha_i = A((e_i, \hat{y}_i), S_t) \). Top-scoring historical samples form \( eD_{1:t-1}^{syn} \). Stage \( t \) memory is \( D_t^{syn} = S_t \cup eD_{1:t-1}^{syn} \).
- **Core assumption:** The decision boundary evolves cumulatively; synthetic data must retain historically influential information to remain aligned with long-term training dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "continually fuses them with aligned historical memory"; [section 4.1.2] "the decision boundary... evolves cumulatively... we model synthetic data as a continually evolving boundary memory".
- **Break condition:** If historical data distributions become irrelevant (e.g., concept drift), retaining aligned historical samples may introduce outdated signals, preventing adaptation to new patterns.

## Foundational Learning
- **Dataset Distillation (Bi-level Optimization):** Why needed here: DIET’s core is a bi-level loop where synthetic data is optimized to mimic full-data training dynamics. Understanding inner/outer loop structure (Eqs. 4–5, Algorithm 2) is essential.  
  *Quick check:* What is the purpose of the inner-loop \( K \)-step update \( \phi^t(D_t^{syn}) = Opt_K(\phi^{t-1}, D_t^{syn}) \) and how does it differ from traditional training?
- **Continual Learning Paradigm for Recommenders:** Why needed here: The problem is defined over chronologically split data blocks \( D_{1:T} \) where models are updated incrementally. This staging drives the need for evolving synthetic sets.  
  *Quick check:* Why can’t a static distilled dataset (from a fixed snapshot) suffice under streaming data? What changes between stages \( t-1 \) and \( t \)?
- **Influence Estimation via Gradient Alignment:** Why needed here: DIET uses \( A_t(x, z) = \langle \nabla \ell(w^t, z), \nabla \ell(w^t, x) \rangle \) to measure how much synthetic sample \( x \) explains real sample \( z \)’s gradient. This underpins both memory fusion and addressing.  
  *Quick check:* How does the first-order Taylor expansion justify using gradient inner product as influence (Eq. 14)? What are the limitations of this approximation?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Streaming data -> Stage t block D_t -> Teacher φ_t -> Compute EL2N per label group -> Select S_t -> Convert S_t -> New synthetic cores -> Score historical D_syn vs S_t via A -> Select aligned subset -> Fuse -> D_t^syn (initialized) -> Bidirectional addressor -> Select B_hard^t, M_active^t -> Inner-loop train proxy θ on M_active^t -> Trajectory {θ^m} -> Meta-objective L_real(θ^M, B_hard^t) -> RaT-BPTT -> Update D_t^syn -> Iterate -> Final D_t^syn -> Aggregate D_1:T^syn -> Distilled dataset for downstream training.

**Critical path**  
1. Streaming data -> Stage t block D_t  
2. Teacher φ_t -> Compute EL2N per label group -> Select S_t  
3. Convert S_t -> New synthetic cores  
4. Score historical D_syn vs S_t via A -> Select aligned subset  
5. Fuse -> D_t^syn (initialized)  
6. Addressing: Sample B_t -> Compute Deficiency/Resp -> B_hard, M_active  
7. Inner loop: Train proxy θ on M_active -> Trajectory {θ^m}  
8. Meta-objective: L_real(θ^M, B_hard) -> RaT-BPTT -> Update D_t^syn  
9. Iterate steps 6–8 -> Final D_t^syn  
10. Aggregate D_1:T^syn -> Distilled dataset for downstream training

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Compression ratio vs. fidelity: Lower ratios risk losing boundary signals; higher ratios increase storage/overhead.  
- Reference model capacity vs. generality: Stronger φ_t yields better synthetic data but may overfit to its inductive bias.  
- Active memory size |M_active|: Larger |M_active| improves updates but raises inner-loop cost; DIET uses truncated window K_rat to bound this.  
- Hard target ratio: Fixed at 0.1; too small misrepresents D_t, too large increases meta-noise.

**Failure signatures**  
- Temporal misalignment: Performance drops when increasing stage number T, as fusion fails to preserve relevant historical signals (see Taobao ablation in Table 4 without MF).  
- Optimization instability: Divergence or oscillation if active memory selection (MS) is ablated (Table 4 variant ✗✓✗).  
- Long-tail neglect: On severely imbalanced datasets, EL2N-based initialization may bias toward majority behaviors if label-conditioning is insufficient.  
- Memory explosion: If historical fusion is unrestrained, |D_t^syn| grows linearly with t, defeating compression.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Compression ratio sweep (Fig. 3): Vary distilled data size (1e-4 to 0.05) and measure candidate model (WuKong) AUC. Shows diminishing returns and reference model capacity effects.  
2. Ablation study (Table 4): Remove Memory Fusion (MF), Target Selection (TS), Memory Selection (MS) individually. Isolates component contributions; MF critical for long sequences (Taobao), MS key for stability (KuaiRand).  
3. Cross-architecture generalization (Table 3): Train DCN as teacher, evaluate on DCN, DCNv2, FinalNet, FinalMLP, WuKong. Tests whether distilled data transfer across architectures; DIET consistently matches/nears full-data AUC across all.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the accuracy of the first-order Taylor approximation for influence scores (Equation 15) degrade over multi-stage continual distillation, given that the inner-loop optimization involves many gradient steps and synthetic data may diverge significantly from the teacher's trajectory?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper derives the influence score using a first-order Taylor expansion (Section 4.2.1) and employs random truncation (RaT-BPTT) to limit backpropagation depth for efficiency (Section 4.2.2, Algorithm 2). The validity of the approximation beyond one step is not analyzed.
- **Why unresolved:** The approximation error could accumulate across stages, leading to misidentification of hard targets and active memory units, thereby degrading distillation quality over time. The paper does not measure the gap between the approximated influence and true influence (e.g., via leave-one-out) or study how this gap correlates with final performance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical studies comparing approximated vs. true influence at different inner-loop steps and stages; ablation experiments using higher-order influence estimates; correlation analysis between approximation error and performance drop on downstream tasks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the continual memory fusion mechanism (Section 4.1.2) effectively prevent the accumulation of outdated or misaligned synthetic samples in the evolving boundary memory over many stages, especially under significant temporal distribution shifts?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper proposes retaining historical synthetic samples based on an alignment estimation function A(·) (Equation 10). However, it does not analyze how the composition of the memory changes over time or whether the alignment criterion remains robust as the teacher model evolves and data distributions drift.
- **Why unresolved:** In a long streaming scenario, previously influential samples may become less relevant or even misleading if user preferences or item catalogs shift. The alignment estimation might fail to detect such staleness, causing incremental degradation of the memory's fidelity to current training dynamics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal analysis of memory content (e.g., label distribution, feature embeddings, task coverage) across stages; controlled experiments with synthetic distribution shifts to measure memory drift; performance tracking over many stages with and without periodic memory resetting or rejuvenation; evaluation of alignment scores over time.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the theoretical and practical lower bound on compression ratio for streaming dataset distillation in recommender systems, and how does this bound depend on data sparsity, model capacity, and the degree of temporal non-stationarity?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper reports successful distillation at 1–2% compression (Abstract, Section 5) and notes diminishing returns beyond 0.3% on Tmall (Section 5.3.3). However, it does not explore extremely low ratios (e.g., <0.1%) or characterize failure modes.
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding the minimal viable compression is crucial for extreme-scale applications. The bound likely varies with dataset characteristics—e.g., sparser data or faster concept drift may require more samples to preserve dynamics. The paper lacks a systematic study to derive such guidelines.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comprehensive experiments across datasets with varying sparsity and temporal dynamics, testing compression ratios down to 0.01%; failure analysis identifying when performance sharply declines (e.g., coverage of rare behaviors, long-tail items); theoretical bounds relating compression to mutual information or gradient diversity.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent does DIET-distilled data generalize across fundamentally different recommendation paradigms, such as sequential models (e.g., SASRec) or generative recommendation models (e.g., LLM-based recommenders), beyond the pairwise CTR prediction models evaluated in the paper?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper demonstrates cross-architecture generalization among CTR models (DCN, DCNv2, FinalNet, FinalMLP, WuKong) in Table 3 and Figure 3. However, all these models share a similar pointwise prediction architecture and training objective. The paper does not test generalization to sequential or generative paradigms, which have distinct training dynamics (e.g., next-item prediction, autoregressive generation).
- **Why unresolved:** The distillation process optimizes synthetic data to mimic the training trajectory of a specific teacher model. Paradigms with different objective functions, attention mechanisms, or sequence modeling capabilities might require different training signals. It is unclear whether the distilled embedding-soft label pairs capture paradigm-invariant knowledge or are biased toward the teacher's inductive biases.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the distilled dataset from a CTR teacher to train sequential recommenders (e.g., SASRec, BERT4Rec) and generative recommenders (e.g., RecGen, LLM-based models); compare performance against full-data training and selection-based baselines; analyze whether the distilled data maintains temporal or sequential patterns necessary for these paradigms.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity & unspecified defaults:** Core bi-level optimization parameters (inner-loop steps M, outer iterations K, meta-learning rate β, RaT-BPTT window K_rat, selection ratios) are unreported, making performance claims dependent on unknown settings.
- **Scalability of influence estimation:** The mechanism for computing gradient alignment A(x, z) across large, sparse historical memories is not detailed; its computational complexity may impede practical deployment on very large streams.
- **Theoretical guarantees absent:** Key assumptions (EL2N as proxy for influence; gradient alignment accuracy under concept drift) lack formal justification; approximation error accumulation across stages is unanalyzed.
