# Samasāmayik: A Parallel Dataset for Hindi-Sanskrit Machine Translation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24307
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24307
- **Reference Count:** 5
- **Primary Result:** Fine-tuning on the new Samasāmayik corpus yields large gains (up to +10 BLEU for Hindi→Sanskrit, +23 BLEU for Sanskrit→Hindi) on an in-domain test set, but performance drops on external benchmarks, highlighting domain specificity.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces Samasāmayik, a new parallel corpus of 92,196 contemporary Hindi-Sanskrit sentence pairs sourced from modern materials like spoken tutorials, children’s magazines, and radio broadcasts. The authors address a critical gap: the lack of modern, non-classical Hindi-Sanskrit parallel data for machine translation. By fine-tuning three state-of-the-art multilingual models (ByT5, NLLB, IndicTrans-v2) on this dataset, they achieve substantial improvements on a dedicated in-domain test set, with BLEU score increases of up to 10 and 23 points for the two translation directions. However, these gains do not fully transfer to existing external benchmarks (IN22, Flores-200), underscoring the domain-specific nature of the new resource and the challenge of generalizing beyond contemporary prose.

## Method Summary
The research constructs and evaluates the Samasāmayik corpus. Data was curated from four contemporary Hindi sources (Chandamama, Mann Ki Baat, Spoken Tutorials, NIOS), aligned into parallel Hindi-Sanskrit sentences. This novel dataset (92,196 pairs) is split into 90,016 training and 1,839 test pairs. For baseline comparison, Hindi-Sanskrit pairs (79,977 sentences) are extracted from the existing BPCC corpus via English pivoting. Three pre-trained models—ByT5-base, NLLB-200 Distilled 1.3B, and IndicTransV2—are fine-tuned separately on both the Samasāmayik training data and the BPCC data. Each model is trained for 3 epochs using AdamW with model-specific learning rates and a batch size of 8 on 2-4 NVIDIA A6000 GPUs. Performance is evaluated using BLEU, chrF++, and WER on three test sets: the in-domain Samasāmayik test set, IN22, and Flores-200 DevTest.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuning on Samasāmayik leads to very large improvements on the in-domain test set compared to the BPCC baseline, with gains of ~10 BLEU for Hindi→Sanskrit (e.g., NLLB: 15.83 vs 5.90) and over 20 BLEU for Sanskrit→Hindi (e.g., IndicTrans: 23.49 vs 1.98).
- On external benchmarks (IN22, Flores-200), models fine-tuned on Samasāmayik perform comparably to or only slightly better than BPCC-trained models, indicating limited cross-domain generalization.
- Similarity analysis confirms the novelty of Samasāmayik, showing low lexical overlap (17% Jaccard) and semantic distance (0.515 average max cosine similarity) with the BPCC corpus.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism is **domain-matching through targeted data curation**. The prior BPCC corpus, derived from classical and bilingual texts, suffered from a severe domain mismatch with contemporary Hindi prose. Samasāmayik directly bridges this gap by providing parallel data in the target domain (modern spoken/written Hindi). Fine-tuning a powerful pre-trained multilingual model (which already possesses basic Sanskrit knowledge) on this relevant data allows it to adapt its representations and decoding to the specific vocabulary, style, and syntactic patterns of modern Hindi-Sanskrit translation. The large gains on the in-domain test set confirm that the primary bottleneck was the absence of contemporary parallel examples, not the capacity of the models themselves. The drop on external benchmarks further validates that the improvement is specifically due to learning the new domain, not acquiring general translation skill.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Parallel Corpus Curation:** The process of collecting, cleaning, and aligning sentence pairs across languages. **Why needed:** Machine translation models require large quantities of high-quality, aligned text; without it, supervised training is impossible. **Quick check:** Verify alignment quality by sampling pairs and checking for semantic equivalence and sentence boundary accuracy.
*   **Domain Adaptation:** Adjusting a model trained on one data distribution to perform well on a different, target distribution. **Why needed:** Real-world translation needs vary (news, literature, conversation); a model trained on one domain (e.g., Wikipedia) fails on another (e.g., social media). **Quick check:** Evaluate a model on both its training domain and a new target domain; a performance drop indicates adaptation is needed.
*   **Fine-tuning:** Taking a pre-trained model and continuing training on a smaller, task-specific dataset. **Why needed:** Pre-training provides general language knowledge; fine-tuning efficiently specializes it for a specific task (e.g., Hindi-Sanskrit MT) without full retraining. **Quick check:** Compare zero-shot performance of the base model to its performance after a few epochs of task-specific training.
*   **Multilingual Model Transfer:** Using a single model trained on many languages to perform a task for a low-resource language pair. **Why needed:** Training a model from scratch for Hindi-Sanskrit is infeasible due to data scarcity; a multilingual model (e.g., NLLB) already encodes cross-lingual knowledge that can be adapted. **Quick check:** Test the base model’s zero-shot translation quality between the language pair before fine-tuning.
*   **Evaluation Metric Awareness (BLEU/chrF++/WER):** Understanding the strengths and weaknesses of automatic metrics. **Why needed:** BLEU correlates with lexical n-gram match; chrF++ better handles morphological richness (critical for Sanskrit); WER penalizes insertion/deletion errors. **Quick check:** Analyze error types in model outputs; does a high BLEU but low chrF++ indicate poor handling of word inflections?
*   **Lexical & Semantic Similarity Analysis:** Quantifying the relationship between datasets (e.g., Jaccard index, cosine similarity of embeddings). **Why needed:** To diagnose whether a model's failure on a test set is due to domain shift or fundamental task incapacity. **Quick check:** Compute vocabulary overlap between training and test sets; high overlap suggests domain adaptation, not basic competence, is the issue.

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component Map:** Raw Sources (Chandamama, etc.) -> OCR/Text Extraction -> Alignment Pipeline -> Samasāmayik Corpus -> Tokenizer (SentencePiece/Byte) -> Pre-trained Model (ByT5/NLLB/IndicTrans) -> Fine-tuning (AdamW) -> Evaluation (SacreBLEU)
*   **Critical Path:** Data Curation & Alignment -> Corpus Statistics -> Model Fine-tuning -> In-Domain Evaluation -> Cross-Domain Evaluation.
*   **Design Tradeoffs:** 
    *   **Pre-trained Model Choice:** ByT5 (byte-level, robust to OOV) vs. NLLB/IndicTrans (subword, efficient, multilingual). Tradeoff: vocabulary coverage vs. parameter efficiency and existing multilingual knowledge.
    *   **Data Source Selection:** Contemporary sources ensure relevance but may be noisy (OCR errors, informal language). Tradeoff: domain purity vs. data cleanliness and size.
    *   **Evaluation Focus:** In-domain vs. external benchmarks. Tradeoff: measuring practical utility for a specific use case (contemporary translation) vs. generalizability and comparison to prior work.
*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   **Domain Overfitting:** Very high in-domain scores combined with very low scores on IN22/Flores, indicating the model has not learned generalizable Hindi-Sanskrit mapping.
    *   **Vocabulary Gaps:** Higher OOV rates for Sanskrit in test sets for subword models (NLLB, IndicTrans) compared to ByT5, potentially manifesting as lower chrF++ relative to BLEU.
    *   **Alignment Artifacts:** Poor quality in the parallel corpus (misaligned sentences, translationese) would limit the upper bound of fine-tuning performance, visible as a ceiling in in-domain scores even after many epochs.
*   **First Three Experiments:**
    1.  **Vocabulary Coverage Analysis:** For each tokenizer, compute the percentage of tokens in the Samasāmayik test set that are out-of-vocabulary (OOV) relative to the training vocabulary. Correlate OOV rates with performance drops on specific test sets.
    2.  **Learning Curve Analysis:** Fine-tune each model on varying subsets of Samasāmayik (e.g., 10%, 25%, 50%, 100%). Plot BLEU/chrF++ on the in-domain test set vs. training data size to identify saturation points and potential data efficiency.
    3.  **Error Analysis:** Manually inspect 100 error sentences from the best-performing model (e.g., IndicTrans) on the in-domain test set. Categorize errors: lexical choice, grammar/morphology (Sanskrit case/verb), word order, or hallucination. This diagnoses whether the remaining errors are linguistic or data-limited.

## Limitations
- The raw data sourcing and preprocessing pipeline for Samasāmayik (OCR outputs, alignment scripts, original URLs for Chandamama, Mann Ki Baat, etc.) are not released, preventing full replication from raw inputs.
- The method for selecting the 1,839-sentence in-domain test set is unspecified; if a predefined split is not released, exact test set recreation is impossible.
- The BPCC baseline extraction process (specific corpus version, alignment tool) is not detailed, introducing variability in the baseline comparison.
- The dataset covers only contemporary prose; the approach does not address translation of classical Sanskrit literature or scripture.
- The in-domain test set is relatively small (1,839 pairs), so reported BLEU/chrF++ scores may have non-negligible variance.
