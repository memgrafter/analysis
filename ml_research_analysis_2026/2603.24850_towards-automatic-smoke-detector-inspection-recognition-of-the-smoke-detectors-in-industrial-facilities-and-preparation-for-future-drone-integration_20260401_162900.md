# Towards automatic smoke detector inspection: Recognition of the smoke detectors in industrial facilities and preparation for future drone integration

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.24850
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24850
- **Reference Count**: 33
- **Primary Result**: YOLOv11n achieved the best average mAP@0.5 of 0.884 on challenging test sets using a mixed real/synthetic training strategy with real validation, and ran at ~6.4 FPS on a Raspberry Pi 5.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of automating visual inspections of smoke detectors in industrial facilities, where detectors are often placed in hard-to-reach locations. It proposes using drone-mounted cameras and object detection models to locate detectors autonomously. To overcome the scarcity of real training images, the authors generate semi-synthetic data by compositing rendered sensor images onto real backgrounds. Through extensive experiments comparing YOLOv11, SSD, and RT-DETRv2, they find that a mixed training strategy (real and synthetic images) validated on real data yields the best performance. The smallest YOLO variant, YOLOv11n, provides the optimal balance of accuracy and speed, achieving real-time inference on a Raspberry Pi 5 and demonstrating a viable path toward embedded drone deployment.

## Method Summary
The study compares three modern object detection architectures (YOLOv11, SSD, RT-DETRv2) across various model sizes. The core methodology involves training these models using different data mixture strategies: training and validating solely on real images (R-R), training on synthetic and validating on real (G-R), training on both and validating on synthetic (G-G), and the proposed mixed approach where training combines real and synthetic images while validation uses only real data (M-R). The synthetic dataset (3,840 images) is created by inserting rendered 3D models of smoke detectors into the upper frame of real background images from industrial sites, with adjustments for brightness and blur. Models are evaluated on two test sets: a normal set of real images and a difficult set containing blurry, partially occluded, or poorly lit instances. Inference speed is benchmarked on a Raspberry Pi 5 after converting models to ONNX/TensorRT format.

## Key Results
- The YOLOv11n model achieved the highest average mAP@0.5 score of **0.884** across the normal and difficult test sets.
- The optimal training strategy was **Mixed real and generated data with real validation (M-R)** for YOLO and RT-DETR, while SSD performed best with the real-only (R-R) strategy.
- YOLOv11n achieved sufficient deployment speed at approximately **6.4 FPS** on a Raspberry Pi 5, confirming its suitability for low-speed drone navigation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Mixed real and semi-synthetic training data with real validation (M-R strategy) yields the best generalization for limited-shot industrial inspection tasks.
- **Mechanism**: Semi-synthetic data expands the training distribution without requiring hazardous field collection. Real validation anchors learning to the target domain, preventing overfit to synthetic artifacts.
- **Core assumption**: Generated data preserves key visual characteristics (shape, texture) of real sensors while backgrounds vary realistically; the real validation set must represent operational conditions.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - M-R achieved the highest average test mAP for YOLOv11m (0.827) and RT-DETRv2-M (0.803), outperforming R-R (0.811, 0.762) in Table IV.
  - G-G experiments resulted in severely degraded test mAP (~0.2), showing the failure mode of synthetic-only validation.
- **Break condition**: If the generation pipeline introduces unrealistic lighting, shadows, or blending artifacts, domain shift occurs, degrading performance. Real validation mitigates this but cannot fully compensate for gross inconsistencies.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Smaller model variants (YOLOv11n, RT-DETRv2-S) exhibit better sample efficiency and robustness to distribution shifts (e.g., motion blur, partial occlusion) than larger variants when training data is limited.
- **Mechanism**: Reduced parameter count lowers model capacity, limiting overfitting on small datasets. Smaller models also have fewer spatial resolution reductions, preserving details of small/incomplete objects common in drone imagery.
- **Core assumption**: Industrial smoke detectors exhibit high intra-class similarity with minimal visual variation; complex models overfit to background clutter or dataset-specific quirks.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - YOLOv11n achieved the highest test mAP (0.884) and the smallest normal/difficult performance gap (0.112), while YOLOv11x had a gap of 0.130.
  - Section V.B explicitly states "smaller N and S models work better with the limited available dataset and under blur and sub-optimal illumination."
  - YOLOv11n's deployment speed (~6.4 FPS on Pi 5) aligns with the computational efficiency of smaller models.
- **Break condition**: If the dataset scale increases significantly (>10k real images), larger variants may catch up as increased capacity becomes beneficial and overfitting risk decreases.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Transformer-based detectors (RT-DETRv2) are less data-efficient than convolutional ones (YOLO) for this single-class, low-data regime but benefit more from added synthetic data due to global context sensitivity.
- **Mechanism**: Transformers lack convolutional inductive biases (locality, translation invariance), requiring more data to learn basic geometric patterns. Once a baseline of real data coverage exists, their self-attention may better utilize additional synthetic data to learn context.
- **Core assumption**: Smoke detector detection is primarily a localized pattern-matching task; transformers' global context offers diminishing returns when objects are small and consistently positioned in the upper frame.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - RT-DETRv2-M's mAP improved by +0.14 (from R-R to M-R) vs. YOLOv11's +0.02 improvement, indicating greater sensitivity to the mixed training data.
  - RT-DETRv2-L's poor performance on Raspberry Pi 5 (0.265 FPS) highlights a practical deployment limitation despite potential data efficiency gains.
- **Break condition**: If the task shifts to multi-class detection requiring complex contextual reasoning (e.g., detecting smoke, flame, and hazard signs simultaneously), transformers may surpass convolutives given sufficient data.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: mAP@0.5 and robustness metrics  
  *Why needed*: The paper's core results hinge on mAP@0.5 scores across normal and difficult test sets. Understanding this metric (mean average precision at 50% IoU) is essential to interpret model comparisons and the importance of the "Test Diff" (gap between normal/difficult performance).  
  *Quick check*: A model with high mAP@0.5 on normal test but low on difficult test indicates poor robustness to real-world distortions like blur or occlusion. A small Test Diff indicates the model generalizes well to challenging conditions.

- **Concept**: Semi-synthetic data generation and domain adaptation  
  *Why needed*: The paper's central innovation is using generated data to overcome real-data collection barriers. This requires understanding associated risks (domain shift, overfitting) and best practices (real validation, careful blending).  
  *Quick check*: If synthetic training data is created by pasting rendered sensors onto real backgrounds with Gaussian blur, potential failure modes include unrealistic lighting/shadows on the pasted object or visible seams at the blending boundary. Such artifacts can cause the model to learn spurious correlations that fail on real test data.

- **Concept**: Embedded inference trade-offs (FPS, memory, power)  
  *Why needed*: The deployment constraint is a resource-limited Raspberry Pi. Inference time directly affects drone navigation safety and stability. Model selection must balance accuracy and latency.  
  *Quick check*: YOLOv11n achieves 6.4 FPS on a Pi 5. For a drone moving at low speed (e.g., 1 m/s) with a camera field of view covering detectors at ~5m distance, this frame rate may be sufficient if the pipeline latency is stable. However, sustained operation must consider thermal throttling and battery drain.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Drone camera -> ROS2 ImageMsg -> Preprocessing (resize/normalize) -> YOLOv11n (TensorRT/ONNX) -> NMS -> BBox + confidence -> ROS2 DetectionMsg -> Navigation/logic

**Critical path**  
The total inference pipeline latency on Raspberry Pi 5 is ~157.1 ms (preprocessing 3.1 ms + model inference 153.5 ms + postprocessing 0.5 ms). This determines the maximum sustainable drone velocity for stable detection. To ensure safe navigation, this latency should be under 200 ms.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Model size vs. accuracy**: YOLOv11n (1.7M params) offers the best accuracy-speed tradeoff. Larger YOLO variants overfit the limited data; SSD-VGG16 is too slow (1179 ms) for real-time use.
- **Synthetic data usage**: The M-R strategy adds ~3k synthetic images to 1k real, which benefits YOLO/RT-DETR but harms SSD. This model-specific response requires validation on real data to avoid overfitting.
- **Augmentation policy**: Default augmentations (e.g., YOLO's RandAugment) outperformed custom motion-blur augmentations. Over-augmentation harms performance on limited datasets.

**Failure signatures**  
1. **Overfit to synthetic domain**: High validation mAP (>0.95) but low test mAP (<0.5), as seen in G-G experiments. **Diagnostic**: Monitor divergence between training/validation loss and test mAP.
2. **Poor robustness to blur/partial views**: Large gap (>0.2 mAP) between Test-N and Test-D. **Diagnostic**: Visualize false negatives on the difficult test set to confirm missed occluded/blurry detectors.
3. **Inference bottleneck**: FPS < 2 on Pi 5 (observed with SSD-VGG16, RT-DETRv2). **Diagnostic**: Profile the inference node with `time` command; check CPU/GPU utilization.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Baseline training strategy comparison**: Train YOLOv11m, SSD-MobileNetV3, RT-DETRv2-M on the five dataset splits (R-R, R-G, G-G, G-R, M-R). Evaluate on both Test-N and Test-D. Expected: M-R wins for YOLO/RT-DETR, R-R for SSD.
2. **Model variant sweep**: For each detector's best strategy (M-R for YOLO/RT-DETR, R-R for SSD), test all size variants (YOLO:n/s/m/x; RT-DETR:S/M/L; SSD:VGG16/MobileNetV3). Expected: YOLOv11n leads in accuracy and speed.
3. **Augmentation ablation**: Take the best variant from Experiment 2 (YOLOv11n, SSD-VGG16, RT-DETRv2-L). Retrain with custom Albumentations (MotionBlur, Defocus) vs. default augmentations. Expected: custom augmentations degrade or provide minimal gain.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: What architectural factors cause object detectors to respond differently to mixed synthetic-real training data, and how can we predict the optimal data mix for a given detector?  
  *Basis*: The experiments show M-R benefits YOLOv11 and RT-DETRv2, while R-R is best for SSD, but no underlying explanation is provided.  
  *Why unresolved*: The paper does not analyze model-specific properties (e.g., convolution vs. transformer, capacity) that drive these differences, limiting generalizability.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Systematic studies varying model components (backbone, attention) with controlled synthetic data ratios, measuring generalization gaps and feature distribution alignment to derive predictive rules.

- **Open Question 2**: Why do augmentations simulating drone motion blur and defocus degrade detection performance across all models, and what augmentation principles ensure robustness for aerial inspection without harming clean-image accuracy?  
  *Basis*: Custom augmentations (MotionBlur, Defocus) worsened mAP scores compared to defaults, especially on difficult test sets.  
  *Why unresolved*: Hypotheses like overfitting are speculative; no diagnostic analysis identifies which augmentations are detrimental or how they interact with model architectures.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Per-augmentation ablation with visual explanations (e.g., Grad-CAM) to observe distortion effects on feature learning, combined with sensitivity analysis across model types.

- **Open Question 3**: How can object detectors be optimized for diverse embedded drone platforms, balancing accuracy, speed, and power consumption beyond current Raspberry Pi benchmarks?  
  *Basis*: Evaluation is limited to Raspberry Pi 4/5; transformer models are too slow; no exploration of accelerators, compression, or system-level constraints.  
  *Why unresolved*: No exploration of hardware accelerators (e.g., Jetson, Coral), model compression, or thermal/power constraints leaves gaps for real-world deployment.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Comprehensive benchmarking of quantized/pruned models on multiple edge devices under simulated flight, measuring latency, accuracy, and energy use to define hardware-model co-design trade-offs.

## Limitations
- **Synthetic data generation details** (blur kernel sizes, blending algorithms) are not fully documented, risking replication gaps and potential domain shift artifacts.
- **Augmentation parameters** (e.g., RandAugment magnitude) rely on library defaults, making the finding that custom augmentations harm performance potentially specific to the chosen settings.
- **Generalizability** to other industrial settings or sensor types is uncertain, as the corpus shows weak thematic alignment and no direct citations, indicating this approach is novel and unvalidated externally.
- **Deployment evaluation** omits sustained operation factors like thermal throttling, battery impact, and communication latency with flight controllers.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (M-R strategy superiority)**: **Medium**. Supported by consistent test mAP lifts in Table IV, but hinges on the specific semi-synthetic pipeline and real validation set representativeness. Lack of external validation reduces confidence.
- **Mechanism 2 (smaller models' robustness)**: **High**. Strong empirical evidence: YOLOv11n leads in accuracy and has the smallest normal/difficult gap. Deployment results (6.4 FPS) align with capacity-robustness trade-offs.
- **Mechanism 3 (transformers' data efficiency)**: **Medium**. RT-DETR's larger mAP gain from M-R supports the claim, but its lower baseline accuracy and poor Pi 5 performance (0.265 FPS) limit practical utility. The break condition remains untested.

## Next Checks
1. **Domain shift quantification**: Compute Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) or t-SNE features between the synthetic training set and the real test sets to verify that the semi-synthetic data does not introduce a high-distribution shift. A high FID (>50) would contradict the M-R strategy's premise.
2. **External dataset generalization**: Test the trained YOLOv11n (M-R) on an independent industrial inspection benchmark. A drop in mAP@0.5 >0.15 would indicate overfitting to the paper's specific data conditions.
3. **Sustained embedded inference stress test**: Run the YOLOv11n model on a Raspberry Pi 5 for 60+ minutes of continuous inference under representative thermal conditions (e.g., enclosed case). Monitor FPS drift, CPU temperature (>70°C throttling), and power consumption. A >20% FPS drop from the initial 6.4 FPS would challenge deployment sufficiency.
