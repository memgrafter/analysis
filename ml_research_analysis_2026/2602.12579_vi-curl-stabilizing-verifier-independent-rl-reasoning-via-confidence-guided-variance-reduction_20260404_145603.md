# VI-CuRL: Stabilizing Verifier-Independent RL Reasoning via Confidence-Guided Variance Reduction

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12579
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12579
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: VI-CuRL stabilizes verifier-independent RL via confidence-guided curriculum, reducing gradient variance 20-45% and matching oracle-verified performance without ground-truth signal.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning for large language model reasoning often fails in verifier-free settings due to high gradient variance. VI-CuRL introduces a verifier-independent curriculum that prioritizes high-confidence training samples—using the model’s own token-level entropy as a proxy for difficulty—to reduce both action and problem variance. The method employs a dynamic quantile threshold during training, ensuring asymptotic unbiasedness while trading temporary bias for stability. Extensive experiments on six math and logic benchmarks show that VI-CuRL stabilizes learning and consistently outperforms verifier-independent baselines, achieving up to 20-45% variance reduction and matching oracle-verified performance without any ground-truth signal.

## Method Summary
VI-CuRL extends Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a confidence-based curriculum. For each training step, it first computes a confidence score for each prompt by sampling rollouts from a frozen behavior policy and calculating the length-normalized average negative token entropy. A target retention rate βt (annealed from 0.2 to 1.0) determines a dynamic quantile threshold: only prompts with confidence above this threshold are retained. The GRPO loss for retained prompts is scaled by 1/βt to maintain unbiasedness in expectation. This selective training reduces both action variance (within-prompt sampling noise) and problem variance (heterogeneity across prompts), stabilizing early learning while asymptotically converging to the full-distribution objective.

## Key Results
- VI-CuRL reduces gradient variance by 20-45% during early training compared to standard GRPO.
- It prevents training collapse in verifier-independent settings and consistently outperforms baselines across six challenging math and logic benchmarks.
- Performance matches that of oracle-verified methods despite using no ground-truth reward signal, demonstrating the effectiveness of the confidence-guided curriculum.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Variance Decomposition via Curriculum Selection
VI-CuRL stabilizes verifier-independent RL by decomposing and reducing the gradient estimator’s variance into two components: *action variance* (sampling variance within a prompt) and *problem variance* (heterogeneity across prompts). By filtering for high-confidence samples (low entropy), it selects a more homogeneous subset of problems where both variance components are smaller. A dynamic quantile threshold enforces a target retention rate βt, and retained gradients are scaled by 1/βt to maintain asymptotic unbiasedness. The core assumption is that token-level entropy correlates with difficulty and gradient homogeneity. Evidence includes formal variance decomposition (Theorem 4.3) and empirical validation (Figure 3). The approach breaks down if confidence becomes uncorrelated with true difficulty, e.g., due to overconfidence on wrong chains.

### Mechanism 2: Intrinsic Confidence as Curriculum Signal
The method uses the model’s own token-level entropy as an intrinsic confidence score. For a prompt x, confidence c(x) = 1 − (1/T Σ_t H(π_θold(|s_t))/log|V|), where H is token entropy normalized by vocabulary size. This score is computed once per prompt using a frozen behavior policy π_θold. During training, prompts are ranked by c(x), and a quantile threshold τt retains the top βt fraction. This creates a curriculum that progressively includes harder (lower-confidence) problems as βt anneals. The assumption is that low entropy indicates simpler, more correct reasoning paths. Evidence: Definition 2.1, Figure 4 showing higher pass rates for kept samples. Failure if entropy does not correlate with true difficulty (e.g., hallucinations cause high confidence on wrong solutions).

### Mechanism 3: Dynamic Quantile Thresholding for Unbiased Convergence
Instead of a fixed threshold, the schedule specifies a target retention rate βt (e.g., linearly annealed). For each batch, τt is set to the (1-βt)-quantile of the batch’s confidence scores, ensuring exactly βt fraction of prompts are kept. The surrogate objective re-weights the standard GRPO loss by w_t(x)/βt. Theorem 4.1 proves that the bias between the full objective L(θ) and the curriculum objective L_t(θ) is bounded by 2L_max(1-βt), guaranteeing convergence as βt → 1. The bounded loss assumption (|ℓ| ≤ L_max) holds with clipped PPO and bounded rewards. The break condition is annealing too fast (insufficient learning on easy distribution) or too slow (not enough variance reduction early).

## Architecture Onboarding
Confidence Scorer -> Curriculum Scheduler -> GRPO Trainer
- **Component map:**
  1. Confidence Scorer: Computes c(x) for each prompt by sampling rollouts from π_θold and calculating length-normalized average negative token entropy. Runs once per outer loop iteration.
  2. Curriculum Scheduler: Takes batch confidence scores and βt, computes dynamic quantile threshold τt and binary mask w_j for each prompt (retain if c≥τt).
  3. GRPO Trainer: Computes rewards, advantages, and standard loss ℓ(θ; x, y¹ᴳ). VI-CuRL multiplies per-prompt loss by w_j/βt before backpropagation.
  4. Retention Rate Annealer: Updates βt over training steps (e.g., linear from 0.2 to 1.0).

- **Critical path:** Outer loop: Sample batch → Compute confidence → Get βt → Compute τt & masks → Inner loop: For K epochs, sample minibatch → Compute GRPO loss ℓ → Scale by w/βt → Accumulate gradient → Update θ. Confidence computation must precede first gradient update.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - βt schedule: Aggressive annealing risks bias on hard problems; slow annealing reduces early stability benefits. Linear is simple but may not be optimal.
  - Confidence metric: Normalized negative entropy is cheap but may not perfectly correlate with true difficulty. More accurate proxies (self-consistency) are more expensive.
  - Stopping gradient on advantages & masks: Crucial to avoid feedback loops where policy games the confidence score.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Training collapse: Pass@1 drops near 0 early; indicates variance not reduced (βt too high or confidence ineffective).
  - Suboptimal plateau: Final performance below baseline or oracle; suggests bias remained too large or curriculum filtered useful signals.
  - Wild oscillations: High variance persists; βt schedule inadequate or confidence not sorting well.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Correlation sanity check**: For an early checkpoint, compute c(x) vs. oracle pass rate per prompt; expect monotonic increase (higher confidence → higher pass rate).
  2. **Variance reduction validation**: At low βt, measure action and problem variance for kept subset vs. random subset of equal size; expect significant reduction (>30%).
  3. **Ablation of annealing schedule**: Train with constant low βt (e.g., 0.2) vs. annealed schedule; constant should show stability but poor final performance, annealed should recover, confirming trade-off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to other RL domains**: The method is only evaluated on math/logic benchmarks. It is unclear if VI-CuRL works on non-reasoning tasks like Atari, robotics, or code generation. Evidence needed: Application to standard RL benchmarks and comparison against baselines.
2. **Reliability of token entropy as confidence proxy**: The correlation between token entropy and true difficulty/gradient homogeneity may vary across models and tasks. If entropy fails to separate easy from hard problems, the curriculum may mis-prioritize. Evidence needed: Measure entropy–correctness correlation across multiple datasets and model scales; compare with alternative confidence measures.
3. **Early bias effect on hard problems**: Although the objective is asymptotically unbiased, early bias might cause convergence to a suboptimal region, particularly for hardest problems. Without targeted evaluation on hard subsets, it is unclear if VI-CuRL incurs a cost. Evidence needed: Evaluate final policy on problems dropped during training; compare with no-curriculum baseline.
4. **Empirical validation of variance decay assumptions**: Theorem 4.5 assumes decay rates σ²_g,t ≤ a₁β^{1+α} and V_prob,t ≤ a₂β^{1+α}. It is not verified whether these hold in practice or how sensitive variance reduction is to violations. Evidence needed: Compute variance components as a function of βt across domains; fit decay rates; test scenarios with random or anti-correlated confidence.
5. **Compatibility with other RL algorithms**: VI-CuRL is implemented on GRPO; the theoretical analysis is specific to GRPO. It is unknown if similar benefits and guarantees hold for PPO, DPO, or ReLLa. Evidence needed: Implement masking on standard PPO and measure stability/variance; extend theory to other algorithms.

## Limitations
- Confidence signal validity: Token entropy may not reliably correlate with true problem difficulty; risk of overconfidence on flawed reasoning chains.
- Dataset dependence: Results rely on DeepScaleR; transfer to other domains (code, factual QA) unverified.
- Implementation gaps: Exact hyperparameters (outer steps, inner epochs), quantile computation scope, and confidence timing remain unspecified, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- Variance reduction & stabilization: High (theoretical decomposition + empirical variance plots)
- Entropy as difficulty proxy: Medium (supported by pass-rate correlation in Fig. 4, but no out-of-distribution tests)
- Asymptotic unbiasedness: High (Theorem 4.1 under bounded loss assumption)
- Generalization to benchmarks: Medium (strong results on 6 math/logic sets, but narrow domain)
