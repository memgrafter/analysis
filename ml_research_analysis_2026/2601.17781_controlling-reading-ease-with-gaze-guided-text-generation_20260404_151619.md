# Controlling Reading Ease with Gaze-Guided Text Generation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17781
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17781
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: Integrating a gaze model that predicts first-pass reading time (FPRT) into language model decoding steers output readability, achieving directional control over human reading times.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a method to control text readability by embedding a gaze model that predicts human first-pass reading time into the decoding process of an off‑the‑shelf language model. The gaze model, fine‑tuned on eye‑tracking data (EMTeC), estimates the cognitive effort for each word given context. During generation, the language model’s token probabilities are combined with a scaled gaze score, biasing the output toward longer (harder) or shorter (easier) reading times. An eye‑tracking study with native and non‑native English speakers confirmed significant, directional changes in actual reading times and perceived difficulty. However, analysis showed the effect is largely mediated by low‑level lexical features, and the method works better for increasing than decreasing difficulty.

## Method Summary
First, a GPT‑2 model (124M) is fine‑tuned on the EMTeC eye‑tracking corpus to predict normalized first‑pass reading time (FPRT) per word, achieving an R² of 0.355 on held‑out data. For generation, an instruction‑tuned Llama 3.2 3B model is used. At each decoding step, the top‑k (k=8) token candidates from the language model are evaluated. For each candidate sequence, the gaze model sums its predicted FPRT values across all tokens to produce a gaze score. The final score for a candidate is: token log‑probability from the LM plus a scaled gaze score (gaze_weight × normalized gaze_score). Beam search (beam size 8) selects the highest‑scoring candidate, emits its first token, and repeats. By varying the gaze_weight (e.g., −2, 0, +2), the generated text is steered toward easier or harder reading.

## Key Results
- Generated texts with positive (+2) and negative (−2) gaze weights yield significant, directional changes in first‑pass reading times compared to neutral (0) outputs (p<0.001) for both native (L1) and non‑native (L2) readers.
- Perceived difficulty ratings increase with higher gaze weights, with a sharper effect for L2 speakers.
- The gaze model’s predictions correlate moderately with observed reading times (r≈0.55–0.59) and explain 35.5% of variance on held‑out eye‑tracking data.
- Lexical features (word length, prevalence) largely mediate the reading time differences, indicating the gaze model primarily captures low‑level processing effort.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A fine‑tuned gaze model can predict human first‑pass reading time (FPRT) for a given text sequence, serving as a differentiable proxy for low‑level lexical processing effort.
- **Mechanism:** The gaze model (a fine‑tuned GPT‑2) is trained on the EMTeC corpus to predict a normalized FPRT value for each word based on its preceding context. The sum of these predictions across a candidate text sequence yields the “gaze score”.
- **Core assumption:** Human FPRT on a word is sufficiently predictable from preceding textual context and correlates with lexical difficulty (e.g., word length, prevalence), allowing a unidirectional language model to approximate cognitive effort.
- **Evidence anchors:** Training on EMTeC (R²=0.355), correlation of r≈0.55–0.59 in the eye‑tracking study, best performance on low‑prevalence words.
- **Break condition:** Tokenization mismatch between gaze model and LM leads to unreliable scores for subwords; mitigated by beam search but not fully resolved.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Adjusting a scalar “gaze weight” during decoding steers an off‑the‑shelf language model’s outputs toward sequences that elicit longer (harder) or shorter (easier) reading times in human readers.
- **Mechanism:** During beam search, each candidate sequence’s token log‑probability from the LM is combined with the gaze score: total score = token score + (gaze_weight * gaze_score). Positive weight favors higher predicted FPRT (harder), negative favors lower (easier).
- **Core assumption:** Aggregated, linearly combined gaze scores during decoding meaningfully influence the LM’s lexical and syntactic choices to alter human reading behavior.
- **Evidence anchors:** Eye‑tracking results show significant directional changes in FPRT aligned with weight (p<0.001) for both L1 and L2; lexical analysis shows expected trends in word length and frequency.
- **Break condition:** Extreme gaze weights (|weight|>3) cause degenerate, repetitive outputs; effectiveness is asymmetric (increasing difficulty works better than decreasing).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The observed changes in reading time are primarily mediated by changes in low‑level lexical features (word length, prevalence) introduced by the gaze‑guided generation, rather than deeper syntactic or discourse‑level alterations.
- **Mechanism:** Optimizing for FPRT automatically selects words with specific length and frequency properties; these lexical shifts drive the reading time differences.
- **Core assumption:** First‑pass reading time is a predominantly lexically‑mediated measure; controlling for lexical features will attenuate the gaze weight effect.
- **Evidence anchors:** Linear mixed‑effects models: adding word length and prevalence as predictors renders the gaze weight effect largely insignificant for L1 readers and reduces it for L2 (Figure 5).
- **Break condition:** If a gaze model trained on a measure sensitive to syntactic processing (e.g., go‑past time) were used, the mediation pattern might differ; indeed, preliminary experiments with other measures failed.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Lexical Processing Effort**
  - **Why needed here:** The method relies on the principle that certain lexical properties (e.g., word length, familiarity) directly impact early‑stage cognitive processing during reading, measurable via first‑pass fixation duration. This psycholinguistic principle is what the gaze model exploits.
  - **Quick check question:** What are two word‑level features that strongly predict longer first‑pass reading times, and what cognitive concept do they reflect?

- **Concept: Autoregressive Decoding & Beam Search**
  - **Why needed here:** The integration requires modifying the token probability distribution during left‑to‑right generation. Understanding standard decoding (greedy, beam search) is necessary to grasp how an external “gaze score” is interpolated at each step to influence future token selection.
  - **Quick check question:** In beam search, how is the final sequence selected from multiple candidate sequences, and where in this process is the gaze score added in this paper’s method?

- **Concept: Eye‑Tracking Metrics (FPRT vs. Others)**
  - **Why needed here:** The choice of First‑Pass Reading Time (FPRT) as the target prediction fundamentally constrains what the model learns (lexical vs. syntactic effects). Knowing the difference between FPRT, gaze duration, go‑past time, and total reading time is key to understanding the method’s capabilities and limitations.
  - **Quick check question:** What specific cognitive processes is First‑Pass Reading Time (FPRT) most commonly associated with, and why might it be less sensitive to sentence‑level syntactic complexity?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
User Prompt → Language Model (LM) → Candidate Expansion (beam search) → Gaze Model → Gaze Score Aggregation → Scoring (LM log‑prob + gaze_weight * gaze_score) → Beam Selection → Output Token

**Critical path:**  
1. Prompt → LM generates top‑k candidate next‑token sequences.  
2. Each candidate → Gaze model predicts word‑level FPRTs → Sum → Gaze score.  
3. Combine LM score + (gaze_weight × gaze_score) for each candidate.  
4. Select best candidate via beam search. Output its first token.  
5. Append token to prompt, return to step 1.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- LM vs. Gaze Model Size/Speed: Larger LM ensures fluency but slows iteration; smaller gaze model is fast but less accurate (R²=0.355).  
- Gaze Weight Magnitude: Small weights (±1‑2) have subtle effects; large weights (±3+) cause degenerate output (repetition, incoherence). Requires empirical tuning.  
- Tokenization Alignment: Gaze model (GPT‑2) and LM (Llama) have different tokenizers; mismatches cause unreliable gaze scores for subwords. Beam search partly mitigates but not perfectly; shared tokenizer ideal.  
- Metric Selection: FPRT is easy to predict with a unidirectional LM but captures mainly lexical effects. Measures sensitive to syntax (e.g., go‑past time) could enable syntactic control but are harder to predict accurately.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Repetitive degeneracy at high absolute gaze weights (|weight|>3), indicated by nonsensical, repetitive outputs.  
- Lexical obfuscation without coherence change: reading times change due to word length/frequency but semantic and syntactic complexity remains similar.  
- Gaze model prediction out‑of‑distribution for L2 readers, potentially leading to less effective control or unnaturalness.  
- Subword token mismatch artifacts in early generation steps causing abrupt trajectory changes.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate Gaze Model Training:** Use the EMTeC corpus to fine‑tune GPT‑2 to predict normalized FPRT. Validate performance (R² ~0.35) on held‑out test set.  
2. **Minimal Integration Test:** Integrate the trained gaze model with a small LM (e.g., GPT‑2 Small) using the scoring formula. Generate 100 sequences for a fixed prompt with gaze weights −2, 0, +2. Inspect outputs and compute lexical statistics (avg word length, type‑token ratio) to confirm monotonic increase in lexical complexity with positive weight.  
3. **Controlled Human Evaluation Simulation:** Using outputs from Exp 2, compute the aggregate gaze score (sum of predicted FPRT) for each sequence. Perform a one‑way ANOVA to confirm the gaze score significantly differs across weight conditions in the expected direction, validating the steering signal before human studies.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can alternative eye‑tracking measures (e.g., regressions, go‑past time) better capture syntactic and discourse‑level complexity for text simplification, and can they be reliably predicted with modern generative models?**  
   The paper notes that FPRT primarily captures lexical effects, while measures like rereading time are associated with syntactic processing but are harder to predict with a unidirectional LM. Preliminary experiments with other metrics performed poorly, leaving open whether richer metrics can be used for higher‑level control.

2. **How effective and personalized is gaze‑guided text generation for specific user groups with distinct reading profiles (e.g., individuals with dyslexia, low literacy, or specific language impairments)?**  
   The evaluation only included native and non‑native English speakers; no data from accessibility‑focused user groups was used. Personalized models would require collecting eye‑tracking data from such groups and evaluating tailored generation.

3. **Beyond lexical prevalence and word length, what specific linguistic or cognitive factors cause the residual increase in first‑pass reading time for L2 readers exposed to texts generated with a positive gaze weight (+2)?**  
   The analysis showed that after controlling for lexical features, the effect of positive weight remains significant for L2 readers but not L1. The additional difficulty could stem from unknown words, syntactic complexity, or discourse issues, but was not identified.

4. **How robust and generalizable is the gaze‑guided generation method across different choices of language model scale, architecture, and gaze model complexity?**  
   The study used only one combination (Llama 3.2 3B and a GPT‑2 fine‑tune) due to resource limits. Varying model size, architecture, or gaze model design could affect steering range, output quality, and degeneration thresholds.

5. **Can gaze guidance be effectively combined with other controllable generation techniques (e.g., prompting, attribute classifiers, or fine‑tuning) to achieve more precise and multi‑faceted control over readability while maintaining text quality?**  
   The method is modular and could be ensembled with other controllers, but potential synergies or conflicts are unexplored. Hybrid systems would need evaluation in human studies.

## Limitations
- Tokenization mismatch between gaze model and LM, coupled with unspecified normalization of gaze scores, introduces unreliability in scoring.
- The method mainly controls lexical difficulty (word length, prevalence) rather than syntactic or discourse‑level readability, because FPRT is heavily lexically mediated.
- Steering is asymmetric: increasing difficulty (positive weight) is more effective than decreasing it (negative weight), likely due to a floor effect for easily processed words.

## Confidence
- Gaze model predictive validity: Medium
- Steering efficacy (directional control): High
- Lexical mediation of effects: High
- Generalization to L2 readers: Medium
- Output coherence at useful weights: Medium
