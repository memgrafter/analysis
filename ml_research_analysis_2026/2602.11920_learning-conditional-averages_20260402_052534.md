# Learning Conditional Averages
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11920  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11920  
- **Reference count:** 22  
- **Primary result:** Complete characterization of learnability of conditional averages via finiteness of α₁+α₂, with tight sample complexity bounds.

## Executive Summary
The paper initiates the study of predicting *conditional averages* in a Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) framework. Given a domain, a directed graph defining neighborhoods, and an unknown target concept, the goal is to learn a predictor that estimates, for any point, the average label of its neighborhood. The authors propose a hybrid algorithm that uses empirical averaging when a point has sampled neighbors and resorts to the one-inclusion graph (OIG) algorithm for isolated points. Confidence is amplified via a median-of‑k trick. The main theoretical contribution is a complete combinatorial characterization: learnability occurs if and only if the sum α₁(G,C) + α₂(G,C) is finite, where α₁ is the size of the largest shattered independent set and α₂ the largest bichromatic independent set. The sample complexity is tightly bounded: an upper bound O((α₁+α₂ log(1/ε))/(ε log(1/δ))) and lower bounds Ω((α₁+α₂)/(ε log α₂)) and Ω(α₂/(ε log α₂)). This result unifies and extends standard PAC learning (α₂=0) and reveals new graph‑dependent learning phenomena.

## Method Summary
The learner receives i.i.d. samples (x_i, c(x_i)) where x_i ∼ D and c ∈ C is unknown. For any x ∈ X, the target is s_c(x) = E_{x'∼D}[c(x') | x' ∈ N[x]], with N[x] = {x} ∪ N⁺[x] the closed out‑neighborhood in a directed graph G = (X,E). The objective is to output h with expected squared loss L(h)=E_{x∼D}[(h(x)-s_c(x))²] ≤ ε with probability ≥ 1−δ. Algorithm 1 processes a query x by examining the induced subgraph G_S on S ∪ {x}. If x has an out‑neighbor in S, it predicts the empirical average of those neighbors. If x is isolated, it collects all isolated vertices I, restricts C to I, constructs the one‑inclusion graph, orients it to minimize maximum out‑degree, and predicts via the OIG rule. Confidence is boosted by running the base learner k = Θ(log(1/δ)) times and taking the pointwise median. The analysis decomposes the loss into three regimes: bichromatic neighborhoods (handled via a mass‑covering argument using α₂), monochromatic with neighbors (empirical averaging), and monochromatic isolated points (binary classification on I solved by OIG with complexity α₁). This yields the stated tight upper and lower bounds.

## Key Results
- Learnability iff α₁(G,C) + α₂(G,C) < ∞.
- Sample complexity upper bound: O((α₁+α₂ log(1/ε))/(ε log(1/δ))).
- Lower bounds: Ω((α₁+α₂)/(ε log α₂)) and Ω(α₂/(ε log α₂)).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hybrid Prediction via Empirical Averaging and OIG
- **Claim:** The algorithm predicts conditional averages by combining empirical averaging for points with neighbors in the sample and the one‑inclusion graph (OIG) algorithm for isolated points.
- **Mechanism:** For a test point x, the learner builds the induced subgraph G_S on S∪{x}. If x has at least one out‑neighbor in S, it returns the mean of the neighbor labels. Otherwise x belongs to the isolated set I; the learner restricts C to I, constructs the one‑inclusion graph, finds an orientation minimizing the maximum out‑degree, and uses the OIG prediction.
- **Core assumption:** Empirical averages accurately estimate s_c(x) when the neighborhood mass is sufficiently sampled; for isolated points, the problem reduces to binary classification on I because the neighborhood is monochromatic, and OIG provides near‑optimal sample complexity.
- **Break condition:** Fails if the sample is too small: either neighborhoods have too few sampled points (high variance) or the isolated set I is large relative to α₁, causing OIG error to dominate.

### Mechanism 2: Combinatorial Characterisation via α₁ and α₂
- **Claim:** Learnability of conditional averages is equivalent to finiteness of α₁(G,C) + α₂(G,C).
- **Mechanism:** α₁ (largest shattered independent set) governs the complexity of learning on isolated monochromatic points (reducing to standard PAC). α₂ (largest bichromatic independent set) captures the mass of points whose neighborhoods contain both labels; its finiteness ensures that the expected contribution from such points can be made arbitrarily small via covering arguments.
- **Core assumption:** The interplay between the graph structure G and the concept class C is fully captured by these two parameters; no other combinatorial property influences the asymptotic sample complexity.
- **Break condition:** If α₁+α₂ = ∞, no finite sample can achieve arbitrary ε, δ (Theorem 3).

### Mechanism 3: Confidence Amplification via Median‑of‑k
- **Claim:** High‑probability guarantees (1−δ) are achieved by running the base learner O(log(1/δ)) times and taking the pointwise median.
- **Mechanism:** The base learner (Algorithm 1) yields an in‑expectation squared loss ≤ Cε (Theorem 6). By Markov’s inequality it succeeds with probability > 1/2 for a sufficiently large sample. Repeating k = O(log(1/δ)) independent times and returning the median ensures that with probability ≥ 1−δ, at most k/2 runs have loss > 100ε, and the median then has loss ≤ 100ε (Lemma 15).
- **Core assumption:** The base loss is bounded in [0,1] and the median of independent bounded variables concentrates sufficiently.
- **Break condition:** If the base learner’s loss distribution has a heavy tail or if more than half the runs have large error, the median amplification does not achieve the desired confidence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Conditional average** (*s_c*(x) = 𝔼[*c*(x′)|x′∈*N*[→*x*]])  
  *Why needed here:* This is the target prediction object, generalizing standard PAC learning (when neighborhoods are singletons) and moving from pointwise classification to neighbourhood regression.  
  *Quick check:* If all neighbourhoods are the entire domain *X*, what does *s_c*(x) reduce to, and how many samples are needed?

- **Directed neighbourhood graph** *G* = (*X*, *E*)  
  *Why needed here:* Encodes the neighbourhood structure; the independence number α(*G*) and induced subgraphs determine the combinatorial parameters α₁, α₂.  
  *Quick check:* For a star graph with centre labelled opposite to all leaves, what is α₂(*G*, *c*)?

- **One‑Inclusion Graph (OIG) algorithm**  
  *Why needed here:* Provides optimal (up to constants) binary classification on the independent set of isolated points, where the problem reduces to standard PAC learning.  
  *Quick check:* If the isolated set *I* has size 5 and VC dimension of *C*|*I* is 2, what is the OIG's expected leave‑one‑out error?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Training sample S → Build induced graph G_S on S∪{x} → For query x: if x has an out‑neighbor in G_S → empirical average of neighbor labels; else (x isolated) → isolated set I → project C to I → construct one‑inclusion graph of C|_I → orient to minimize max out‑degree → OIG prediction → repeat O(log(1/δ)) times → pointwise median → final predictor.

**Critical path:** Sample acquisition → neighbourhood graph construction → branch (empirical average vs. OIG) → median aggregation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Empirical averaging gives low variance for high‑mass neighborhoods but fails when the neighborhood mass is small; this case is handled by α₂‑based arguments.  
- OIG is optimal for isolated points with complexity α₁ but becomes inefficient if α₁ is large; hence it is used only when a point is isolated.  
- Median amplification converts an in‑expectation guarantee into high probability at the cost of an O(log(1/δ)) factor in sample size.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Empirical averaging error spikes if D(N[x]) is too small for adequate sampling.  
- OIG error dominates when the isolated independent set size |I| is large relative to α₁.  
- Median amplification ineffective if the base learner’s loss distribution puts more than 1/2 probability of exceeding the target bound.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Complete graph validation:** G is complete (every pair connected). Then α₂=0 and α₁≤1. The algorithm should succeed with m = O(1/ε), matching the trivial mean estimator.  
2. **α₂ stress test:** Use a star graph where the center has the opposite label to all leaves. Here α₂ equals the number of leaves. Vary leaves from 10 to 80 and measure empirical sample complexity for a given ε; the dependence should roughly follow the lower bound Ω(α₂/ε).  
3. **α₁ vs. OIG:** Construct a graph with a large independent set I that is shattered by C (α₁ = |I|) plus a clique connecting all other vertices. Isolate points in I and verify that the error on I scales as α₁/|I|, confirming the OIG's role.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** What are the necessary and sufficient conditions and sample complexity for learning conditional averages when the training labels themselves are averaged over a different neighborhood graph \(G^{\text{in}}\)?  
   **Basis:** "Studying the learnability of \((G^{\text{in}}, G, \mathcal{C})\)—with input labels \(s_{\text{in}}\) and target labels \(s\)—is a promising research direction." (Section 5)  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper fully resolves the case \(G^{\text{in}} = \emptyset\) (individual labels). Adding a separate input‑averaging graph introduces a second layer of averaging that complicates the interaction between the two neighborhoods and is not captured by the parameters \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2\).  
   **Evidence to resolve:** A combinatorial characterization (e.g., new parameters depending on both graphs) together with tight upper and lower sample‑complexity bounds, and a matching learning algorithm.

2. **Question:** Can the framework be extended to predict the average disagreement between a given local classifier \(g_x\) and the target concept \(c\) within each neighborhood \(N(x)\)?  
   **Basis:** "extending our analysis from estimating \(\mathbb{E}[c(x_1) \mid x_1 \in N(x)]\) to \(\mathbb{E}[ \mathbf{1}\{g_x(x_1) \neq c(x_1)\} \mid x_1 \in N(x)]\) would be interesting." (Section 5)  
   **Why unresolved:** The current solution assumes constant predictors within neighborhoods; non‑constant \(g_x\) varies across points in the same neighborhood, potentially breaking the independence‑based arguments that underpin the \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2\) characterization.  
   **Evidence to resolve:** A generalization of the parameters \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2\) that encodes the local classifier family \(\mathcal{H}\), an algorithm achieving the corresponding bounds, and matching lower bounds.

3. **Question:** How do the learnability conditions and sample complexity change when the conditional average is weighted by a similarity function \(w(x, x')\) rather than uniformly over \(N(x)\)?  
   **Basis:** "Instead of \(\mathbb{E}[c(x_1) \mid N(x)]\) we want to estimate \(\mathbb{E}[w(x,x_1)c(x_1) \mid N(x)]\) for a given similarity function \(w\). Learning such weighted averages would bring our setup closer to the smoothed analysis of Chandrasekaran et al. (2024)." (Section 5)  
   **Why unresolved:** Uniform averaging yields a characterization via independence numbers. Weights create dependencies among points in the same neighborhood across different query points, possibly requiring richer combinatorial measures beyond \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2\).  
   **Evidence to resolve:** A new combinatorial invariant that captures the weighted structure, a learning algorithm with tight bounds (upper and lower), and perhaps a reduction to or from known weighted‑averaging settings.

4. **Question:** Does empirical risk minimization (ERM) admit a proper learning algorithm for conditional averages whose sample complexity depends on \(\alpha_1(G,\mathcal{C})\) rather than the VC dimension of \(\mathcal{C}\)?  
   **Basis:** "We remark that the question of whether ERM also works with a sample complexity depending on \(\alpha_1(G,\mathcal{C})\) instead of \(d = \text{VC}(\mathcal{C})\) remains open." (Appendix C)  
   **Why unresolved:** Proposition 18 gives an ERM‑style approach with sample complexity \(\text{VC}(\mathcal{C})\), while the optimal upper bound (Theorem 6) depends on \(\alpha_1\). It is unknown whether a different ERM scheme or a modification of the optimization objective could achieve the better \(\alpha_1\)-dependence.  
   **Evidence to resolve:** Either a lower bound proving that any ERM algorithm requires \(\Omega(\text{VC}(\mathcal{C}))\) many samples (which would show \(\alpha_1\) is insufficient), or a constructive ERM algorithm whose sample‑complexity analysis yields a bound in terms of \(\alpha_1\) (and possibly \(\alpha_2\)) with no extra \(\text{VC}(\mathcal{C})\) factor.

## Limitations
- OIG orientation algorithm not detailed; implementation may be incorrect or inefficient.
- α₁/α₂ computation for infinite domains is open; discretization may distort parameters.
- Hidden constants and amplification factor may cause empirical gaps.
