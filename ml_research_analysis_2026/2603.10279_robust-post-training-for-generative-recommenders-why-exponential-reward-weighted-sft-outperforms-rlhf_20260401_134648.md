# Robust Post-Training for Generative Recommenders: Why Exponential Reward-Weighted SFT Outperforms RLHF
## Quick Facts
- ArXiv ID: 2603.10279
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10279
- Reference count: 33
- Primary result: Exponential reward‑weighted SFT (Exp‑RSFT) avoids reward‑model hacking, requires only offline data, and consistently outperforms PPO, DPO, and other baselines, with a tunable temperature λ that balances improvement and robustness.

## Executive Summary
Post‑training generative recommenders to align with user preferences using only offline interaction data is a significant challenge. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) methods such as PPO and DPO suffer from reward hacking because they rely on learned reward models that extrapolate poorly over large item catalogs. Offline reinforcement learning approaches require propensity scores that are rarely available, and online interaction is impractical in recommendation. This paper introduces exponential reward‑weighted supervised fine‑tuning (Exp‑RSFT), a simple yet effective method that directly weights each training example by `exp(r/λ)` and performs a weighted cross‑entropy update. Exp‑RSFT requires no reward model, no propensity scores, and operates entirely offline. Theoretically, the authors prove that under sub‑Gaussian reward noise, the value loss relative to the logging policy scales only logarithmically with the item catalog size, and the temperature λ explicitly controls a robustness‑improvement tradeoff. Empirically, across three public datasets and one proprietary dataset, Exp‑RSFT consistently outperforms behavior cloning, linear reward‑weighted SFT, PPO, and DPO. Crucially, PPO and DPO catastrophically collapse by optimizing a flawed reward signal, while Exp‑RSFT remains robust. A sweep over λ confirms an inverted‑U performance curve, with optimal λ typically in the range 0.5–1.0. These results demonstrate that Exp‑RSFT is a robust and practical solution for post‑training generative recommenders.

## Method Summary
The input to Exp‑RSFT is an offline dataset `D = {(s_i, a_i, r_i)}`, where `s_i` is a user’s interaction history, `a_i` the recommended item, and `r_i` the observed scalar reward (e.g., rating, watch time). The data is collected under an unknown logging policy `π_β`. We start from a pretrained generative recommender `π_θ` (e.g., SASRec or HSTU) that outputs a distribution over items. The training objective is to maximize the expected weighted log‑likelihood:
\[
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r)\sim D}\bigl[ \exp(r/\lambda) \cdot \log \pi_\theta(a|s) \bigr].
\]
This objective is equivalent to a KL‑regularized policy optimization problem: `max_π E[advantage] - λ D_KL(π∥π_β)`, where the temperature λ controls the strength of regularization. No reward model is involved; weights are computed directly from observed rewards. In practice, rewards may be standardized (e.g., z‑score) to improve numerical stability, a transformation that does not affect the theory due to affine invariance.

The training procedure is straightforward. For each mini‑batch sampled from `D`, compute weights `w_i = exp(r_i / λ)`. Then compute the loss `L(θ) = - (1/|B|) Σ_{i∈B} w_i · log π_θ(a_i|s_i)`. This is a weighted cross‑entropy loss. Perform backpropagation and update `θ` with Adam. Repeat for a fixed number of epochs, monitoring validation performance to select the best checkpoint. The only hyperparameter is λ; a typical sweep over values such as {0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0} reveals an inverted‑U relationship between λ and recommendation quality.

## Key Results
- Exp‑RSFT consistently outperforms PPO, DPO, behavior cloning, and linear reward‑weighted SFT across four datasets (three public, one proprietary).
- PPO and DPO catastrophically collapse, optimizing a flawed reward signal, while Exp‑RSFT remains robust.
- A λ sweep confirms an inverted‑U performance curve; the optimal λ is usually between 0.5 and 1.0, balancing improvement and robustness to noise.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
#### Mechanism 1: Avoiding reward hacking by eliminating the learned reward model
- **Claim**: Exp‑RSFT avoids catastrophic reward hacking by eliminating the learned reward model.
- **Mechanism**: The method directly uses observed scalar rewards as weights in the SFT loss: each example (s,a,r) contributes `exp(r/λ)·log π_θ(a|s)`. No reward model is queried during training, so there is no opportunity to exploit modeling errors.
- **Core assumption**: Observed rewards are unbiased estimates of user preference for the actions actually taken; the logging policy has nonzero probability for a sufficiently diverse set of items.
- **Evidence**: [abstract] "immune to reward hacking"; [section 2] "Reward models must extrapolate over the vast item catalog from sparse observations... the policy exploits these extrapolation errors".
- **Break condition**: Systematic biases in observed rewards (e.g., position bias) would be amplified because they are confounded with true preference.

#### Mechanism 2: Temperature λ explicitly controls the robustness‑improvement tradeoff
- **Claim**: The temperature λ explicitly controls the robustness‑improvement tradeoff.
- **Mechanism**: The weight `exp(r/λ)` becomes sharper as λ decreases, increasing sensitivity to high‑reward items but also to noise. Theorem 4.4 quantifies the value degradation as `R_max(e^{2ϵ/λ} - 1)` where ϵ relates to noise level.
- **Core assumption**: Rewards are bounded (`r* ∈ [0, R_max]`), noise is sub‑Gaussian (Assumption 4.2), and logging policy has full support (`π_β(a|s) > 0`).
- **Evidence**: [section 3.2] "The only hyperparameter is λ"; [section 4] Theorem 4.4 reveals the tradeoff; [section 6] Inverted‑U performance curve across λ sweeps.
- **Break condition**: Non‑sub‑Gaussian noise (heavy tails) or dependent noise invalidates the bound, potentially making the optimal λ much smaller or larger.

#### Mechanism 3: Provable improvement under noisy rewards with logarithmic degradation
- **Claim**: Exp‑RSFT provably improves over the logging policy under noisy rewards, with degradation scaling only logarithmically with catalog size.
- **Mechanism**: The policy maximizes `E[advantage] - λ KL(π∥π_β)`. Under sub‑Gaussian noise, Theorem 4.3 guarantees `V^{π_λ}(s) ≥ V^{π_β}(s) - O(σ√(log|A|))` with high probability. Bounded rewards yield a λ‑dependent bound (Theorem 4.4).
- **Core assumption**: Independent zero‑mean σ‑sub‑Gaussian noise, bounded rewards, full support of logging policy, and a well‑specified parametric policy class.
- **Evidence**: [section 4] Theorem 4.3 statement; [abstract] "gap scales only logarithmically with catalog size".
- **Break condition**: If the logging policy assigns zero probability to some actions (common with large catalogs), the KL divergence becomes infinite and the guarantee fails. Model misspecification also adds gap.

## Foundational Learning
#### KL‑regularized policy optimization
- **Why needed**: The exponential weighting form is derived from maximizing expected advantage subject to a KL divergence constraint to the behavior policy, clarifying the role of λ and connecting to trust‑region methods.
- **Quick check**: "What is the policy that solves `max_π E_{s∼d_{πβ}, a∼π}[A] - λ D_{KL}(π∥πβ)`?" Answer: `π(a|s) ∝ π_β(a|s) exp(A(s,a)/λ)`.

#### Sub‑Gaussian random variables and concentration
- **Why needed**: The analysis bounds noisy reward effects using sub‑Gaussian tail bounds and a union bound over actions, yielding a `√(log|A|)` factor.
- **Quick check**: "Why does the value gap scale with `√(log|A|)` under sub‑Gaussian noise?" Answer: Because the maximum deviation across |A| actions is bounded by `O(σ√(log|A|))` with high probability via a union bound.

#### Reward hacking in RLHF
- **Why needed**: RLHF methods rely on a learned reward model that can be exploited due to extrapolation over large catalogs, leading to poor performance on true preferences; understanding this motivates Exp‑RSFT’s reward‑model‑free approach.
- **Quick check**: "What causes reward hacking in recommendation?" Answer: "Sparse observations cause reward models to make large extrapolation errors, which the policy exploits to maximize the flawed reward signal."

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component map
Pretrained π_θ + Offline dataset D -> sample mini‑batch (s,a,r) -> compute weight w = exp(r/λ) -> compute loss L = - (1/|B|) Σ w log π_θ(a|s) -> backpropagation -> Adam optimizer -> update π_θ

#### Critical path
1. Load pretrained π_θ.
2. For each mini‑batch B from D, compute weights w_i = exp(r_i / λ).
3. Compute weighted cross‑entropy loss: L = - mean(w_i * log π_θ(a_i|s_i)).
4. Backpropagate and update θ.
5. Repeat for T epochs, selecting best checkpoint on validation.

#### Design tradeoffs
- λ: Sweep validation; optimal range ~0.5–1.0. Smaller λ risks overfitting noise; larger λ yields little improvement.
- Reward preprocessing: Standardization (z‑score) stabilizes training; theory is invariant to affine scaling.
- Negative rewards: Exp‑RSFT automatically downweights them; no issue with negative weights.
- Coverage: Only observed (s,a) pairs receive gradient signal; unobserved items may stay low probability unless covered by the logging policy.

#### Failure signatures
- **Catastrophic collapse**: Test metrics drop sharply → λ too small; increase λ.
- **No improvement**: Metrics remain close to behavior cloning → λ too large or reward signal weak.
- **Numerical overflow**: `exp(r/λ)` overflows for large rewards/small λ → normalize/clip rewards.
- **Poor reward model correlation**: If a method starts optimizing a flawed reward model (e.g., PPO/DPO), it may show high reward scores but low true metrics; Exp‑RSFT should not exhibit this.

#### First 3 experiments
1. **λ sweep on validation**: For λ ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0}, train Exp‑RSFT for a few epochs, plot NDCG@10 vs. λ to confirm inverted‑U shape.
2. **Baseline comparison**: With optimal λ, train Exp‑RSFT to convergence; compare against BC, linear RSFT, PPO, DPO on test set (filtered high‑rating items). Expect Exp‑RSFT to outperform and avoid PPO/DPO collapse.
3. **Reward model sanity check**: Train a shallow reward model on offline data; compute its prediction error vs. simple baselines. Generate recommendations from each policy and compute average reward model score. Verify PPO/DPO have high reward scores but low true metrics; Exp‑RSFT has moderate reward scores and high true metrics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Q1**: What are the exact quantitative thresholds (e.g., minimum reward model accuracy, catalog coverage) that determine when Exp‑RSFT outperforms RLHF/DPO versus when the latter become preferable? The paper only offers qualitative expectations.
- **Q2**: How sensitive is Exp‑RSFT to violations of the sub‑Gaussian noise assumption, and can the guarantees be extended to more realistic noise distributions (e.g., heavy‑tailed) common in recommendation?
- **Q3**: Can we derive tighter, data‑dependent generalization bounds that directly inform λ selection without extensive validation sweeps? The current bound is loose and worst‑case.
- **Q4**: Do the theoretical guarantees formally extend to the MDP setting with trajectory returns? The claim in Section 5 lacks proof and requires explicit assumptions on per‑step noise accumulation.

## Limitations
- Exp‑RSFT may amplify systematic biases present in observed rewards (e.g., position bias).
- Theoretical guarantees rely on strong assumptions: logging policy with full support, independent sub‑Gaussian noise, and a well‑specified policy class, which are often violated in practice.
- The method only updates probability for actions that appear in the offline data; items not covered by the logging policy may never be recommended.

## Confidence
- **Robustness to reward‑model hacking** (Exp‑RSFT eliminates reward model): Medium‑High
- **Theoretical guarantee** (logarithmic value degradation): Medium
- **Empirical superiority** across datasets: High
