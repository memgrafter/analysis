# MedPI: Evaluating AI Systems in Medical Patient-facing Interactions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.04195
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04195
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Evaluation of nine LLMs using MedPI shows uniformly low performance on differential diagnosis (≤20%) and core medical competence (60–70% for top models), highlighting deficits in clinical reasoning, safety, and communication.

## Executive Summary
MedPI is an end‑to‑end benchmark for evaluating large language models (LLMs) in multi‑turn, patient‑facing medical conversations. It integrates synthetic patient records (PATIENT PACKETS), affect‑enabled AI patients, a task matrix of clinical scenarios, a 105‑dimension ACGME‑aligned rubric, and committee‑style AI judges. In a standardized simulation of 7,097 dialogues across 366 synthetic patients and nine flagship LLMs, the framework reveals uniformly poor diagnostic performance (≤20% for most models) and only moderate scores (60–70%) for the best models on core medical competence meta‑categories. These deficits span clinical reasoning, safety, and communication. MedPI thus provides a scalable, multi‑dimensional stress test for medical LLMs and pinpoints targeted improvement areas.

## Method Summary
MedPI generates synthetic patient records with a modified Synthea pipeline, yielding FHIR‑like EHRs (diagnoses, medications, labs, vitals) packaged into PATIENT PACKETS. AI patients are LLM agents grounded in these packets; they maintain a memory store where each event is tagged with a semantic embedding, a 27‑dimensional emotion vector (Cowen & Keltner 2017), importance, and timestamp. Memory retrieval combines semantic cosine, temporal decay, importance, and emotion Tanimoto similarity. After each doctor turn, a separate LLM call updates the 27 emotion scores.

The TASK MATRIX cross‑references 34 encounter reasons (e.g., anxiety, lupus) with objectives (e.g., diagnosis, lifestyle advice). For each simulation, a task is sampled and the target LLM receives a standard “vanilla clinician” prompt. Conversations proceed for up to 50 turns, alternating between doctor and AI patient, with the latter using its memory and affect to shape responses. Transcripts are evaluated by a committee of Gemini 2.5 judges: first a meta‑category discussion, then dimension‑level scoring against 105 behavioral anchors (normalized 0–100). Scores aggregate into 29 categories and 7 meta‑categories.

## Key Results
- Differential diagnosis performance is uniformly low, with most models scoring ≤20%.
- Top‑tier models (GPT‑5, o3) attain only 60–70% on core medical competence meta‑categories.
- Deficiencies are evident across clinical reasoning, safety, and communication skills.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MedPI works because it combines several design innovations: (1) a comprehensive, ACGME‑aligned rubric spanning 105 fine‑grained dimensions ensures clinically relevant assessment; (2) affect‑enabled AI patients with long‑term memory produce stateful, realistic dialogues that test continuity and rapport; (3) a standardized “vanilla” prompt for doctors removes model‑specific scaffolding, enabling fair comparison; (4) a two‑stage committee of AI judges introduces deliberation that may approximate expert consensus, reducing single‑judge arbitrariness; (5) the fully synthetic pipeline scales to thousands of conversations across many LLMs at modest cost. Together, these elements stress‑test core clinical competencies in a controlled, reproducible setting.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Synthetic Patient Generation (Synthea)**  
   *Why needed:* To create diverse, structured patient records at scale without privacy constraints.  
   *Quick check:* Compare demographic and comorbidity distributions against a real‑world benchmark (e.g., MIMIC-IV) to ensure reasonable prevalence.

2. **AI Patient Agent with Memory & Affect**  
   *Why needed:* To simulate realistic patient behavior—including recall of prior statements and emotional responses—enabling multi‑turn coherence.  
   *Quick check:* Inspect a sample conversation for explicit memory references (e.g., “You mentioned earlier…”) and affect shifts that align with conversational content.

3. **ACGME‑Aligned Rubric**  
   *Why needed:* To ground evaluation in established medical competencies, providing granular, behaviorally anchored scoring (1–4) across 105 dimensions.  
   *Quick check:* Map each of the 7 meta‑categories (e.g., Core Medical Competence) to ACGME domains and verify that all 105 dimensions have clear anchors.

4. **Committee‑Style AI Judges**  
   *Why needed:* To harness LLM‑based evaluation while mimicking expert consensus, potentially mitigating single‑model bias.  
   *Quick check:* Run a pilot with a single judge vs. a committee on 50 transcripts; compute inter‑rater reliability (e.g., ICC) to see if committee improves consistency.

5. **Task Matrix of Clinical Scenarios**  
   *Why needed:* To cover a broad spectrum of common and critical presentations and objectives, ensuring varied assessment contexts.  
   *Quick check:* Confirm that the 34 encounter reasons and associated objectives collectively address all 7 meta‑categories without large gaps.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  PatientPacket -> AIPatient -> Task -> DoctorLLM <-> AIPatient -> Transcript -> AIJudge (Committee) -> Rubric -> Scores

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Generate synthetic patient records via modified Synthea.  
  2. Create a PATIENT PACKET (structured FHIR‑like data).  
  3. Instantiate AI patient: load packet, initialize memory and affect.  
  4. Sample an encounter reason and objective from TASK MATRIX.  
  5. Initialize doctor LLM with the vanilla clinician prompt and task.  
  6. Simulate conversation (≤50 turns) with alternating responses; AI patient updates affect and retrieves memories after each doctor turn.  
  7. Record full transcript.  
  8. Evaluate transcript with AI judges: committee discussion per meta‑category, then scoring of 105 dimensions.  
  9. Normalize scores (0–100) and aggregate.  
  10. Store results.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  • Synthetic vs. real patients: privacy and scale at the cost of realism and demographic fidelity.  
  • AI judges vs. human experts: cheap and scalable but risk model‑specific biases and uncertain clinical alignment.  
  • Vanilla prompt: avoids prompt‑engineering bias but may understate achievable performance with optimized prompting.  
  • Single judge family (Gemini): ensures consistency but prevents measuring cross‑judge variability.  
  • Turn limit (50): prevents infinite loops but may truncate natural conversation length.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. Median conversation length >20 turns (paper reports 13) → AI patient memory retrieval ineffective or looping.  
  2. High score variance across random seeds for the same model → judge instability or non‑deterministic scoring.  
  3. Extreme demographic skew in PATIENT PACKETS (e.g., >80% Asian) → results may not generalize.  
  4. Many conversations ending prematurely without diagnosis → possible AI patient termination logic flaw.

- **Exactly 3 first experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline replication:** Run the pipeline on a small subset (e.g., 10 patients × 3 tasks) for two models; verify differential diagnosis ≤20% and top meta‑category scores within 60–70%.  
  2. **Judge consistency test:** Evaluate 50 transcripts with three judge families (Gemini, GPT‑4o, Claude 3.5); compute coefficient of variation; flag dimensions >10% variation.  
  3. **AI patient sanity check:** Manually inspect sample dialogues for memory references and plausible affect updates; confirm conversation length distribution (median 13, IQR 11–14).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** How well do LLM‑based AI judges align with medical experts across MedPI’s 105 dimensions?  
   **Basis in paper:** The authors state: “it is not known the degree of alignment between the AI judges and medical experts on all the 105 proposed dimensions.”  
   **Why unresolved:** All evaluations rely on AI judges without human expert validation; the validity of scores relative to expert judgment remains unknown.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Have medical experts score a representative sample of conversations using the same rubric; compute agreement metrics (e.g., Cohen’s kappa, ICC) between expert and AI judge scores.

2. **Question:** How stable and vendor‑bias‑free are MedPI evaluation scores across different judge LLMs, random seeds, and prompt designs?  
   **Basis in paper:** The authors note: “This raises the possibility of vendor‑specific biases. We also do not yet report a systemic comparison against alternative judge families or multiple random seeds, so we cannot fully quantify the stability of the absolute scores.”  
   **Why unresolved:** The current implementation uses a single LLM family (Gemini 2.5) for judging; systematic biases or score instability may exist but are unmeasured.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Run the AI judge pipeline with multiple judge models from different providers, varied random seeds, and alternative prompts; analyze score distributions for consistency and bias patterns.

3. **Question:** How do LLM performance profiles on MedPI generalize to real patient populations or specific demographic/clinical cohorts?  
   **Basis in paper:** From the limitation: “all PATIENT PACKETS in MEDPI are fully synthetic… they were intentionally constructed as a generic cohort rather than to match any specific health system or country.”  
   **Why unresolved:** Synthetic, generic patients may not capture the complexity, diversity, and specific characteristics of real‑world patient populations, threatening external validity.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the benchmark using real EHR data (with privacy safeguards) or synthetically generated patients matched to target population demographics; compare model performance patterns across datasets.

4. **Question:** How do LLMs perform in multimodal clinical conversations that incorporate visual, auditory, or affective signals beyond text?  
   **Basis in paper:** Future work states: “While the current MEDPI implementation evaluates text‑only agents, future work will extend this to multi‑modal agents integrating visual perception… auditory cues… or multimodal affect signals.”  
   **Why unresolved:** Real patient‑clinician interactions are inherently multimodal; text‑only assessment may overlook critical communication and diagnostic cues.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend MedPI to simulate patient agents that produce multimodal outputs (e.g., facial expressions, tone, gestures); generate multimodal conversations and evaluate LLM doctor performance on the same dimensions.

5. **Question:** Does tool‑augmented reasoning, such as accessing EHRs via standardized APIs, improve LLM performance in patient‑facing medical dialogues?  
   **Basis in paper:** Future work suggests: “Future implementations could expose the PATIENT PACKETS through EHR server APIs… and use tool‑augmented reasoning, mirroring realistic clinical workflows.”  
   **Why unresolved:** Current evaluation restricts models to pure dialogue without external data access; however, clinicians routinely use EHRs to inform conversations.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Equip LLM doctor agents with tool‑use capabilities to query simulated EHRs (e.g., via FHIR‑like interfaces); run MedPI with and without tool access and compare scores across dimensions.

## Limitations
- **Synthetic data fidelity:** The “modified Synthea” details are unspecified; the reported 66% Asian demographic skew may reduce generalizability to real populations.
- **Evaluation bias & reliability:** Using only Gemini 2.5 family judges without reported inter‑rater reliability or human comparison; absolute scores could be unstable or systematically biased.
- **Generalizability of prompts:** The “vanilla clinician prompt” lacks scaffolding; real‑world deployment would likely use refined prompts, potentially changing performance gaps.

## Confidence
- Uniformly low performance on differential diagnosis (≤20%): Medium
- Best models (GPT‑5, o3) scored only 60–70% on core meta‑categories: Medium
- MedPI provides a scalable framework for stress‑testing: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Judge Stability Check:** Re‑run 5% of conversations using multiple judge families (GPT‑4o, Claude 3.5, Med‑PaLM) and compute dimension score variance. If variance >10% on critical dimensions (e.g., differential diagnosis), primary results are not robust.
2. **Patient Packet Realism Check:** Compare comorbidity and demographic distributions of the synthetic PATIENT PACKETS against a real EHR benchmark (e.g., MIMIC‑IV). Significant deviation in prevalence of top 10 diagnoses suggests inflated/deflated model performance.
3. **Human‑AI Judge Agreement Check:** Have board‑certified physicians score 200 conversations (stratified by model performance) on the 105 dimensions. Compute Cohen’s κ for key meta‑categories (e.g., Safety). Agreement κ < 0.6 indicates AI judges may not reliably mirror clinical judgment.
