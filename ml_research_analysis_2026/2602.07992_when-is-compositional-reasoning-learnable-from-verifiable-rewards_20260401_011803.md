# When Is Compositional Reasoning Learnable from Verifiable Rewards?
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07992
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07992
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: RLVR with verifiable rewards learns correct intermediate reasoning steps iff the problem exhibits uniform task-advantage, which depends on base model quality; otherwise it may converge to suboptimal chains.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) for training language models to perform compositional reasoning via chain-of-thought (CoT). The authors formalize reasoning as sequential selection among deterministic tasks and introduce the task-advantage ratio to capture how much a chosen task improves the probability of final verification. Their main theoretical finding is that if every correct task enjoys a uniform positive advantage—i.e., its success probability is sufficiently higher than that of incorrect tasks—then RLVR converges to the optimal reasoning chain with polynomial sample complexity. Conversely, without such structural advantage, which may be lacking when the base model’s initial accuracy is low, RLVR can become trapped in suboptimal policies. Counterexamples like parity tasks and a carefully constructed two-step problem demonstrate these failure cases. The work underscores that outcome-level feedback alone is not enough; learnability critically depends on the interaction between problem structure and the base model’s capabilities.

## Method Summary
The authors model reasoning as an autoregressive process where at each step s the policy π_θ selects an action a_s from a set of deterministic tasks, transforming the current state x_{s-1} into x_S. The model is a linear function f_θ(x)=∑_j g_j(x)⟨h_{s,j},θ⟩, where g_j maps each (step, task) pair to an orthonormal vector, ensuring that parameters for distinct choices evolve independently. Only rollouts that result in a final state accepted by a deterministic verifier V (binary reward) trigger learning. The update follows a REINFORCE-style rule: for every successful trajectory, the log-probability of each chosen action is increased by an amount proportional to (1−π_θ(a_s|x_{s-1})). This yields a gradient that depends on the conditional distribution of tasks given success. The core analytical concept is the task-advantage ratio P(V=1|task selected)/P(V=1); uniform boundedness of this ratio for all correct tasks (uniform task-advantage) guarantees that the expected gradient points consistently toward the optimal policy and ensures polynomial-time convergence. For the two-step counterexample in Section 6.2, symmetry allows reduction to a single scalar p = σ(w), the probability of picking the correct task at each step. The expected update becomes a deterministic map w ← w + 2η(D(p)−p) with D(p)=2p²/(2p²+(1−p)²), enabling precise analysis of convergence thresholds.

## Key Results
- Uniform task-advantage is sufficient for RLVR to converge to the optimal reasoning chain with polynomial sample complexity.
- Without uniform task-advantage (e.g., in parity tasks or a two-step problem requiring base model accuracy >1/3), RLVR may converge to suboptimal policies.
- Learnability depends critically on the base model’s initial accuracy and the structural properties of the task, not merely on the presence of a verifiable reward.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The gradient from successful rollouts increases the probability of actions that are overrepresented in the set of successful trajectories. Uniform task-advantage ensures that for every correct task, its conditional probability given success is strictly higher than that of any incorrect task. This creates a consistent learning signal: the expected update for the correct task’s logit is positive, while for incorrect tasks it is negative. As a result, the optimal policy (pure selection of correct tasks) is a stable fixed point, and the dynamics converge to it under appropriate step sizes. The task-advantage ratio precisely quantifies the strength of this signal; when uniformly bounded away from 1, the improvement per step is sufficient to overcome noise and initial bias, leading to polynomial-time guarantees.

## Foundational Learning
- **Task‑advantage ratio**  
  *Why needed*: Quantifies how much selecting a particular task boosts the overall success probability; serves as the key measure of learnability.  
  *Quick check*: In a simple environment, compute P(V=1|task)/P(V=1) for each task; correct tasks should yield values >1, incorrect <1.

- **Uniform task‑advantage**  
  *Why needed*: Uniform lower bound on advantage guarantees a strong and consistent gradient direction, enabling polynomial convergence.  
  *Quick check*: Verify that for all correct tasks, the advantage ratio is at least some α>0, and for incorrect tasks it is ≤1−α, across all steps.

- **REINFORCE with positive rollouts**  
  *Why needed*: Provides an unbiased policy gradient estimator when conditioning on success; avoids high variance from negative samples.  
  *Quick check*: For a deterministic policy, apply the update rule to a batch of successful trajectories and confirm that chosen actions’ probabilities increase.

- **Orthonormal embeddings**  
  *Why needed*: Ensures that gradient updates for one (step,task) pair do not interfere with others, simplifying the theoretical analysis.  
  *Quick check*: Confirm that dot products between any two distinct embedding vectors are zero and norms are one.

- **Sequential task selection**  
  *Why needed*: Captures the compositional nature of CoT reasoning in a tractable mathematical framework.  
  *Quick check*: Simulate a short sequence; the final verifier should depend on the combination of chosen tasks.

- **Verification signal V**  
  *Why needed*: The only supervisory signal; determines which rollouts contribute to learning.  
  *Quick check*: Manually define V for a toy set of final chains and ensure it correctly distinguishes successful from unsuccessful completions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
x₀ (initial prompt) → [Step 1 policy π_θ] → a₁ → x₁ → [Step 2 policy π_θ] → a₂ → … → x_S → V → {0,1}

**Critical path**:  
1. Sample initial prompts x₀.  
2. Autoregressively generate actions a₁,…,a_S using current π_θ.  
3. Obtain final state x_S and evaluate verifier V.  
4. If V=1, for each step s compute gradient contribution ∇logπ_θ(a_s|x_{s-1}) and update θ (e.g., θ ← θ + η ∇).  
5. Repeat.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Positive‑only updates*: Reduces variance but may bias learning if success rate is low; relies on decent initialization.
- *Orthonormal embeddings*: Favors analysis but is unnatural for modern LLMs; may restrict model expressiveness.
- *Scalar reduction*: Collapses multiple parameters per step to one assuming symmetry; useful for intuition but not generally valid.
- *Learning rate schedule*: Constant η may overshoot; diminishing rates ensure convergence but slow down learning.
- *Deterministic tasks*: Simpler than stochastic tasks; real reasoning steps often involve randomness.

**Failure signatures**:
- Convergence to a suboptimal fixed point when the initial probability of the correct task falls below a threshold (e.g., p<1/3 in the two‑step example).
- Oscillations or divergence if η is too large.
- Incorrect threshold if the factor 2 in the scalar update is omitted.
- Empirical conditional distribution D(p) deviating from theoretical due to insufficient sampling.

**First three experiments**:
1. Numerically integrate the deterministic map w ← w + 2η(D(p)−p) for the two‑step problem; confirm that p_t → 0 for p₀<1/3 and p_t → 1 for p₀>1/3.
2. Construct a minimal task where each correct step strictly increases success probability (uniform advantage). Train a small linear policy with RLVR and observe convergence to the optimal chain.
3. Sweep initial p₀ and batch size B in the two‑step setup; plot empirical success probability to verify that the decision boundary remains near p=1/3 under finite‑sample conditions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can task‑advantage be measured and controlled in real LLMs to steer RLVR? (Section 7; no practical methods currently exist.)
2. What base model and problem properties ensure uniform task‑advantage (α>0) for efficient RLVR? (Inferred; exact thresholds linking base model metrics to α are unknown.)
3. Is uniform task‑advantage necessary for polynomial‑time RLVR learnability? (Inferred; known to fail for parity and the two‑step example, but necessity remains open.)
4. Do standard LLM reasoning benchmarks (math, code) satisfy uniform task‑advantage, and does this explain RLVR outcomes? (Inferred; no empirical measurement yet.)

## Limitations
- The theoretical setup assumes a linear autoregressive model with orthonormal embeddings and REINFORCE updates using only successful rollouts; real LLM fine‑tuning differs significantly.
- Uniform task‑advantage is defined for deterministic tasks; its applicability to stochastic or natural language intermediate steps is unclear.
- Polynomial sample complexity bounds may be loose; scaling to long chains of thought could be prohibitive in practice.

## Confidence
- Convergence under uniform task‑advantage: **High**
- Necessity of uniform task‑advantage (based on Prop 6.3): **Medium**
- Outcome‑only feedback is insufficient for all problems: **Low**
