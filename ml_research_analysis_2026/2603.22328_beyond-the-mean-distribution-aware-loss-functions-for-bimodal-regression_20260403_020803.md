# Beyond the Mean: Distribution-Aware Loss Functions for Bimodal Regression

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22328
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22328
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Distribution-aware losses (Wasserstein/Cramér) reduce bimodal Jensen-Shannon divergence by 45% vs MSE, outperform MDNs in fidelity-robustness trade-off, and maintain unimodal stability.

## Executive Summary
Standard regression losses like mean squared error (MSE) collapse to the mean when error distributions are bimodal, failing to capture predictive uncertainty. This paper proposes distribution-aware loss functions that combine normalized RMSE with Wasserstein or Cramér distances to align predicted and target distributions directly, avoiding instability from mixture models. On bimodal datasets, the Wasserstein variant yields a 45% reduction in Jensen-Shannon divergence compared to MSE, achieves lower divergence than Mixture Density Networks (MDNs), and preserves stability in unimodal tasks, establishing a new Pareto frontier for aleatoric uncertainty estimation.

## Method Summary
The method introduces loss functions that replace point-wise errors with distributional discrepancy measures. It computes a normalized RMSE to maintain scale compatibility, then adds a distribution distance term—either Wasserstein or Cramér—to align the predicted error distribution with the empirical target distribution. This approach bypasses the need for explicit mixture models (like in MDNs), reducing training instability while effectively modeling multimodality by directly optimizing for distributional similarity.

## Key Results
- Wasserstein loss reduces Jensen-Shannon Divergence by 45% compared to MSE on bimodal datasets.
- Achieves lower divergence (0.301 vs. 0.320) than MDNs in bimodal settings.
- Maintains MSE-level stability in unimodal tasks, creating a new Pareto frontier for fidelity-robustness trade-offs in aleatoric uncertainty estimation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The losses work by explicitly penalizing discrepancies between the entire predicted distribution and the target distribution, not just central tendencies. Distribution distances like Wasserstein metric are sensitive to shape changes, such as multimodality, whereas MSE aggregates errors into a single mean. Normalization via RMSE ensures the loss scale remains consistent across datasets, preventing domination by either term and promoting stable optimization without mixture model parameterization.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Data -> Target Distribution Estimation -> Neural Network -> Predicted Distribution -> Loss Calculation (Normalized RMSE + Wasserstein/Cramér) -> Scalar Loss -> Gradient Update

**Critical Path**: Accurate computation of distribution distances during backpropagation; ensures gradients reflect distributional alignment without excessive computational overhead.

**Design Tradeoffs**: 
- Wasserstein vs. Cramér: Wasserstein may better capture modal shifts but is computationally heavier; Cramér is simpler but less sensitive to support changes.
- Loss weighting: Balancing normalized RMSE and distribution distance terms affects fidelity-stability trade-off.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Predicted distribution remains unimodal despite bimodal targets, indicating model capacity limits or insufficient loss weighting.
- Training instability or divergence with Wasserstein term if normalization is inadequate or learning rates are high.
- Poor generalization on out-of-distribution bimodal patterns due to overfitting to training distribution shape.
