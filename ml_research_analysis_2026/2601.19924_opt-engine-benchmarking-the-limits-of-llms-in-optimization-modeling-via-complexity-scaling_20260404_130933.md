# OPT-Engine: Benchmarking the Limits of LLMs in Optimization Modeling via Complexity Scaling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.19924
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19924
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR) maintains >80% execution rates on larger LP/MIP instances with frontier models, while Pure-Text Reasoning (PTR) collapses; constraint formulation is the key bottleneck.

## Executive Summary
OPT-Engine introduces a scalable benchmark to evaluate large language models on optimization modeling tasks, comparing Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR), which generates code for external solvers, against Pure-Text Reasoning (PTR), which attempts end-to-end textual reasoning. Across ten linear and mixed-integer programming problems with controllable complexity, TIR remains robust as problem size increases, achieving high accuracy and execution rates with frontier models. In contrast, PTR performance quickly plateaus and then collapses as instances scale. The study identifies constraint formulation—the accurate translation of natural language constraints into mathematical conditions—as the primary bottleneck: adding even mathematically simple constraints causes significant accuracy drops in both paradigms, whereas variations in linguistic complexity or objective perturbations have minimal impact. Smaller models benefit from reinforcement learning fine-tuning to approach TIR-level robustness.

## Method Summary
The OPT-Engine benchmark generates synthetic optimization instances through a multi-stage pipeline. A numeric instance generator creates data and computes ground-truth optimal solutions using solvers. A canonical problem constructor translates numeric instances into minimal, standardized natural language templates. An augmentation agent then rephrases these templates into diverse, context-rich narratives, while a validation module ensures mathematical equivalence. Finally, an evaluation harness runs both PTR (direct text output) and TIR (solver code generation + execution) pipelines, measuring accuracy against the ground truth. This design allows systematic scaling of problem size and controlled perturbations (e.g., adding constraints, changing language).

## Key Results
- TIR with frontier models (DeepSeek-V3.2, GPT-5.1) achieves >80% execution rates and stable accuracy on larger instances, while PTR fails dramatically beyond a problem-size threshold.
- Adding simple, novel constraints (e.g., “no direct road between city 0 and 1”) causes sharp accuracy degradation in both TIR and PTR, pinpointing constraint formulation as the core bottleneck.
- Linguistic complexity (perplexity) and linear objective perturbations have negligible impact on performance, ruling out surface-level understanding as the limiting factor.
- Smaller models (Qwen3-4B) exhibit low execution rates (~23%) in TIR, but reinforcement learning fine-tuning (Qwen3-4B-RL) significantly improves robustness, narrowing the gap with larger models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR) maintains robustness at scale by offloading exact computation to external solvers, while Pure-Text Reasoning (PTR) fails due to the mismatch between sequential text generation and deterministic algorithmic computation.
- **Mechanism:** TIR confines the LLM to *formulation*—translating natural language into correct mathematical models and executable code—while the solver handles exact, polynomial-time (LP) or branch-and-bound (MIP) computations. This decoupling prevents error accumulation in numerical steps. PTR requires the model to simulate entire reasoning and computation within its latent space, which is structurally unsuitable for precise, stateful calculations in high-dimensional combinatorial spaces.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs are unreliable as standalone numerical solvers but can be reliable code/formula generators with clear interfaces.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: tool-integrated reasoning exhibits significantly higher robustness as complexity escalates. [section 4.2]: Figures 3 & 4 show TIR accuracy stable with scale, PTR collapses; Table 1 shows >80% execution rates for frontier models in TIR.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM generates syntactically invalid or logically incorrect solver code (low execution rate), TIR fails. Evident in small models (Qwen3-4B-Instruct) with low execution rates (~23%), improved by RL fine-tuning.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The primary bottleneck is *constraint formulation*, not linguistic complexity or objective perturbations. Adding even simple constraints causes significant accuracy drops because models fail to robustly ground and integrate novel constraint conditions.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs rely on pattern-matching against canonical problem templates abundant in training data. When constraints deviate from these templates (e.g., “exactly one of two edges must be included”), the model’s internal representation does not consistently update the feasible set, leading to omitted or incorrect constraints and thus infeasible/suboptimal solutions. The issue is *semantic sensitivity* to constraint phrasing, not mathematical difficulty.
- **Core assumption:** Current LLMs do not maintain a consistent, manipulable symbolic representation of the constraint set; they generate local text patterns without global feasibility preservation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: constraint formulation constitutes the primary performance bottleneck. [section 5.2]: Figure 8 shows sharp accuracy drops when simple constraints are added across TSP, knapsack, inventory. [section 5.1]: negligible impact from linguistic complexity and linear objective shifts.
- **Break condition:** Triggered by *novel combinations* of constraint conditions. Performance may recover if constraint language matches a memorized template (e.g., standard subtour elimination).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** PTR’s performance collapse at scale is mediated by a strategic shift from exact enumeration to greedy heuristics as problem size grows, driven by implicit token-length budgeting.
- **Mechanism:** For small instances (e.g., TSP with 4 cities), combinatorial space is tractable for explicit enumeration within the context window, yielding long, detailed reasoning traces. As size increases, search space explodes factorially. The model, facing implicit pressure to limit output length, switches to lightweight heuristics (e.g., nearest neighbor) that require fewer tokens but sacrifice optimality. This is an adaptive but detrimental response to computational intractability.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs employ “budgeted reasoning” where output length correlates with search effort; when exact search becomes infeasible, they default to fast approximate methods.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.3]: Figure 5 shows token length initially grows with problem size (n=4), then *decreases* as n increases toward collapse. Appendix Figure 17 case study shows enumeration for n=4 vs. nearest neighbor for n=8.
- **Break condition:** When problem size exceeds a threshold where enumeration is impossible within the effective context window, PTR switches heuristics and accuracy plummets. Threshold varies by model capacity and problem class.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Linear & Mixed-Integer Programming (LP/MIP) formulation**  
  Needed because the paper’s tasks are LP/MIP problems; understanding canonical forms (variables, objective, constraints) is essential to grasp “auto-formulation” and why constraint errors are fatal.  
  Quick check: Given a natural language description of a knapsack problem, can you write the standard MIP formulation with binary variables and capacity constraint?

- **Concept: Tool-Augmented / Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR)**  
  Needed because the core comparison is TIR vs. PTR; TIR is the paradigm where the LLM acts as a programmer generating code for an external solver. Understanding the separation of concerns (reasoning vs. computation) is key to the paper’s conclusion.  
  Quick check: What are the two main responsibilities of the LLM in a TIR pipeline? What component performs the actual optimization?

- **Concept: Constraint Satisfaction vs. Optimization**  
  Needed because the identified bottleneck is *constraint formulation*; distinguishing between writing constraints (logical conditions) and solving the optimization problem is crucial. The model fails at the former even when the latter is handed off.  
  Quick check: If an LLM omits a “must-select exactly one” constraint in a scheduling problem, what is the likely outcome in the solver’s result (feasibility, optimality, both)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Numeric Instance Generator (G) -> Canonical Problem Constructor (M) -> Augmentation/Rephrasing Agent (R + L) -> Validation Module (V) -> Evaluation Harness (PTR/TIR pipelines)
- **Critical path:** G(d, θ) → generate numeric instance i → M(i, T) → produce canonical template sc → R(sc, L) → generate rephrased narrative sr → V(sc, sr) → validate equivalence → validated instance → run PTR pipeline and TIR pipeline → compute accuracy metric
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Scalability vs. Realism: Generator creates clean, controllable instances but may lack real-world noise and irregularity.
  - Exactness vs. Applicability: Benchmark requires exact optimal solutions, possibly undervaluing near-optimal heuristics used in practice.
  - Linguistic Variation Control: Rephraser adds surface diversity but must preserve structure; validation loop is costly but ensures integrity.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - TIR: Low execution rate (syntax errors, wrong solver API, missing constraints).
  - PTR: High token count initially, then abrupt drop coinciding with accuracy collapse; reasoning traces switch from exhaustive enumeration to heuristics.
  - Constraint bottleneck: Accuracy drops specifically when *new constraint types* are introduced, even if mathematically simple; model outputs omit or mis-state the constraint.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline scaling:** Run TIR and PTR on a single problem class (e.g., TSP) with increasing size (e.g., n=5,10,15,20) using a frontier model (GPT-5.1/DeepSeek-V3.2). Expect TIR accuracy stable, PTR to drop sharply after threshold.
  2. **Constraint augmentation test:** Take a canonical instance (e.g., knapsack) and add one simple constraint (e.g., “exactly one of item 1 and 2 must be selected”). Run both paradigms. Expect significant accuracy degradation in both, confirming constraint formulation as bottleneck.
  3. **Linguistic perplexity control:** Generate same numeric instance with three prompt templates (Easy, Medium, Hard as per Appendix A.2.1). Run TIR/PTR. Expect negligible accuracy change, confirming linguistic complexity is not the bottleneck.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do LLMs' performance remain robust when generalizing to out-of-distribution optimization tasks that scale in complexity far beyond the controlled parameter ranges used in current benchmarks like OPT-Engine?
- **Basis in paper:** The abstract and introduction directly pose this as “critical question 1,” noting existing benchmarks are confined to static, fixed-scale instances (e.g., ~5 nodes in OptMATH) and disconnected from real-world complexity.
- **Why unresolved:** The study establishes scaling trends within OPT-Engine’s 10 problem classes but does not test on instances with orders-of-magnitude larger dimensionality (e.g., thousands of variables) or on problem structures entirely absent from training data. The scalability ceiling for frontier models like GPT-5.1 in TIR remains unknown beyond tested ranges.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Generate and evaluate on instances with extreme parameter values (e.g., TSP with N > 1000 cities, MIP with thousands of constraints) using OPT-Engine’s generator, then measure the point of collapse for both TIR and PTR. Additional out-of-distribution tests could involve problem classes not in the initial taxonomy (e.g., nonlinear, stochastic).

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** At which exact stage—problem interpretation (NL-to-math), constraint formulation, or solution generation—do current LLMs encounter the most significant and specific failure modes in optimization modeling?
- **Basis in paper:** Stated as “critical question 2” in the abstract and introduction. The paper identifies constraint formulation as the primary bottleneck through controlled augmentation experiments but does not decompose *formulation* into finer-grained sub-stages.
- **Why unresolved:** Diagnostic experiments (linguistic, objective, constraint) isolate *types* of error but do not trace individual failure traces to pinpoint whether errors stem from misparsing the natural language, incorrectly translating a specific constraint type (e.g., logical, cardinality), or mishandling variable interactions. The “stage” remains somewhat coarse.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Fine-grained error analysis on a large sample of failed attempts, coding the precise nature of the formulation error (e.g., omitted constraint, incorrect variable, sign error, misuse of index sets). Correlating these error types with specific linguistic features in the prompt or with constraint classes (e.g., “subtour elimination” vs. “capacity”) would locate the bottleneck more precisely.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why is there a stark performance gap between LLMs' near-human capabilities on pure mathematical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., MATH) and their brittle performance on optimization modeling, even when problems appear mathematically similar?
- **Basis in paper:** The introduction explicitly contrasts super-human MATH scores with <50% accuracy on IndustryOR, posing this as a critical question for the community.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates that PTR fails at scale and that constraint formulation is hard, but it does not experimentally isolate *why* optimization is uniquely difficult. Potential reasons (unstated) include: the need for precise symbolic grounding amid ambiguity, the combinatorial nature of constraint *systems* (vs. single equations), or the requirement to match problem *structure* to a canonical template. No ablation tests directly compare MATH-style problems with OPT-Engine problems controlling for these factors.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Construct a controlled dataset that grafts optimization constraints onto MATH-style problems, then test whether accuracy drops solely due to constraint count or specific constraint types. Alternatively, test if models succeed on optimization problems presented as *single* well-defined constraints (like many MATH problems) but fail when multiple constraints must be composed correctly.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the “semantic sensitivity” bottleneck—where LLMs struggle with constraint augmentation—be mitigated by targeted training data or prompting strategies that improve *generalization* to novel constraint combinations beyond canonical textbook forms?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion identifies “semantic sensitivity” as a key bottleneck and notes that augmented constraints deviate from canonical forms likely abundant in training data. The paper does not test interventions.
- **Why unresolved:** While RL fine-tuning (Qwen3-4B-RL) improves execution rates for TIR, it is unclear if this specifically addresses constraint formulation errors or merely improves code generation. The paper does not evaluate whether models fine-tuned on *diverse, non-canonical* constraint formulations (e.g., thousands of TSP variants with extra rules) generalize to unseen augmentations in OPT-Engine.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train/fine-tune models on a distribution of problems with systematic constraint variations (e.g., all TSP instances with one extra randomly selected constraint from a pool). Then evaluate generalization to *held-out* constraint types in OPT-Engine. Comparing performance gains against a baseline fine-tuned only on canonical problems would isolate the effect of constraint diversity.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** To what extent do the findings on TIR vs. PTR scalability and the constraint bottleneck generalize to *nonlinear*, *stochastic*, or *dynamic* optimization problems, which are prevalent in real-world applications but not covered by OPT-Engine's LP/MIP focus?
- **Basis in paper:** The limitations section explicitly states the benchmark focuses on linear structure and does not cover nonlinear, stochastic, or dynamic programs, calling them “important extensions.” The observed bottlenecks may manifest differently in these domains.
- **Why unresolved:** The constraint formulation challenge might be more severe in nonlinear problems where constraints are non-convex or in stochastic problems where constraints involve expectations. PTR’s failure at scale could be even more dramatic if it requires approximating complex functions. No experiments probe these domains.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend OPT-Engine’s generator pipeline to produce instances of nonlinear programming (e.g., with quadratic constraints) or two-stage stochastic programs. Then replicate the TIR/PTR scaling and constraint augmentation analyses. The relative performance gap and impact of added constraints would indicate whether core conclusions generalize beyond linear/convex settings.

## Limitations
- Benchmark limited to synthetic LP/MIP instances; may not generalize to real-world OR problems with noisy data or irregular forms.
- Only a subset of model families evaluated; performance on other architectures (e.g., encoder-decoder, MoE) remains unknown.
- TIR evaluated via execution rate and final accuracy; subtle semantic errors that still run but yield suboptimal solutions are not captured.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1:** TIR scales robustly while PTR collapses – **High** (consistent multi-size experiments, >80% accuracy on frontier models).
- **Claim 2:** Constraint formulation is the primary bottleneck – **Medium** (strong evidence from simple-constraint drops, but only few constraint classes tested).
- **Claim 3:** RL fine-tuning enables smaller models to approach TIR robustness – **Medium** (improvement shown for one model, one tuning method).
