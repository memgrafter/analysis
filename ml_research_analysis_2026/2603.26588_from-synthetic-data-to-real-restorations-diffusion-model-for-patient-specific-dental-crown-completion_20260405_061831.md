# From Synthetic Data to Real Restorations: Diffusion Model for Patient-specific Dental Crown Completion

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.26588
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26588
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: ToothCraft, a conditional 3D diffusion model, achieves 81.8% IoU on synthetic damaged teeth and successfully transfers to real-world dental scans, generating anatomically plausible crowns with only 0.1% predicted occlusal interference when conditioned on the antagonist tooth.

## Executive Summary
ToothCraft addresses the challenge of automating dental crown completion for damaged or missing teeth. The lack of training data with real defects is tackled by synthesizing a large dataset: complete teeth from public archives are artificially damaged via random primitive Boolean subtractions with simplex noise. The model is a 3D diffusion network operating on signed distance fields, conditioned on the local anatomical context (adjacent teeth) and optionally the opposing tooth. On a synthetic test set it achieves an Intersection over Union (IoU) of 81.8% and a Chamfer Distance of 0.00034. When applied to 16 real clinical scans, it produces anatomically plausible crowns, and antagonist conditioning reduces predicted occlusal interference to 0.1%. This work demonstrates that synthetic data can bridge the gap for tasks where real defective samples are scarce.

## Method Summary
ToothCraft employs a conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) operating on 64³ signed distance fields (SDFs). Training data is generated by taking complete tooth meshes from public datasets (Teeth3DS, ODD), extracting each tooth with surrounding gingiva and adjacent teeth, and converting to SDFs. Synthetic defects are created by subtracting one or more randomly sampled primitive shapes (sphere, cube, cylinder, etc.) from the tooth SDF, with simplex noise added to the primitive surface to avoid overly smooth patterns. The diffusion model consists of a 3D UNet backbone with separate encoders for the contextual region (adjacent teeth) and an optional antagonist tooth; their features are fused with the noisy input at multiple decoder levels. The model is trained with classifier-free guidance (dropout 0.15, guidance scale 2.0) for 700k iterations on 20,568 synthetic samples. At inference, iterative denoising progressively refines a random SDF into a completed crown, which is then converted to a mesh via Marching Cubes.

## Key Results
- Synthetic test performance: IoU 81.8%, Chamfer Distance 0.00034.
- Real-world transfer: On 16 clinical scans with clinician-made ground truth, ToothCraft generates anatomically plausible crowns; the variant conditioned on the antagonist tooth achieves only 0.1% predicted occlusal intersection.
- The approach shows that training solely on synthetically damaged teeth can yield a model applicable to real dental restoration tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of ToothCraft stems from three intertwined mechanisms:

1. **Synthetic data diversity bridges the real data gap**: The augmentation pipeline creates a wide variety of incomplete tooth shapes by combining random primitive subtractions (sphere, cube, cylinder, capsule, cone) with simplex noise. This diversity ensures the model encounters many patterns of crown loss, covering the distribution of real defects. Evidence: The paper explicitly states that the aim is not to replicate realistic defects but to encourage robust contextual reasoning (Section 3.2). The model’s strong performance on real scans supports this coverage assumption. A limitation is that rare or complex fracture patterns not mimicked by primitives may degrade performance.

2. **Contextual and occlusal conditioning enforces anatomical plausibility**: Separate encoders process the adjacent teeth and the opposing tooth; their features are fused with the diffusion branch at multiple scales. This provides strong morphological and positional priors, guiding the generation to align with neighboring crowns and avoid occlusal interference. The model incorporating antagonist conditioning achieves the lowest predicted occlusal intersection (0.1%), demonstrating the benefit. However, if the surrounding anatomy is abnormal (e.g., missing teeth, abutments), the conditioning may mislead the model.

3. **3D diffusion on SDFs enables high-fidelity shape generation**: The DDPM framework, with a 3D UNet (with attention in the middle block) and classifier-free guidance, learns to denoise SDF volumes effectively. The SDF representation captures smooth zero-level sets and supports Boolean operations in the volume domain. The reported metrics (IoU 81.8%, CD 0.00034) confirm high fidelity on synthetic data. Memory constraints limit resolution to 64³, which caps the achievable detail for fine occlusal features.

## Architecture Onboarding
We break down the system architecture and practical considerations.

*Component map*:
Raw 3D scan → tooth + context extraction → SDF conversion (64³) → [Training: synthetic damage application] → Diffusion model (3D UNet) with separate context and antagonist encoders → Classifier-free guidance → SDF output → Marching Cubes → crown mesh.

*Critical path*:
At inference, the pipeline is: (1) isolate the damaged tooth and its neighboring teeth (and antagonist if available) from the scan; (2) convert these regions to SDF volumes; (3) feed the damaged tooth SDF (noised) together with context SDFs into the diffusion UNet; (4) run iterative denoising (100 steps) to obtain a clean SDF; (5) extract the crown mesh via Marching Cubes.

*Design tradeoffs*:
- Resolution vs memory: 64³ chosen to fit in 60GB H100; higher resolution would capture finer occlusal details but exceeds memory.
- Synthetic diversity vs realism: Primitives + noise yield many samples but may not mimic complex real fractures; trade-off favors coverage over anatomical fidelity of damage.
- Antagonist conditioning: Adds parameters and requires an opposing scan; improves occlusion but is optional.
- Sampling steps: 100 steps balance speed and quality; more steps improve results but increase inference time.

*Failure signatures*:
- High masked L1/CD or low mIoU on real cases.
- Visual gaps or misalignment with adjacent teeth in the reconstructed crown.
- Excessive predicted-antagonist intersection when antagonist is not conditioned or guidance scale is off.
- Failures on scans with abnormal gingiva (abutments, screws, recession) due to lack of such patterns in training.

*First 3 experiments*:
1. Train the model on the full synthetic dataset (20,568 samples) and evaluate on a held-out synthetic test set (5,398 samples) using shape metrics (L1, CD, IoU).
2. Conduct an ablation study comparing models with (i) no guidance, (ii) classifier-free guidance, and (iii) added antagonist conditioning to quantify their contributions.
3. Deploy the trained model on 16 real clinical scans and compute masked metrics against clinician-provided ground truth; perform visual inspection to assess anatomical plausibility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why does the model fail on scans with abnormal gingival anatomy (e.g., abutments, screws, extensive recession), and how can this be addressed? The synthetic pipeline does not include such abnormalities, leading to confusion when encountered.
2. To what extent does the low 64³ SDF resolution limit the fidelity of fine-grained occlusal anatomy (e.g., molar cusps, fissures), and can this be overcome without prohibitive memory costs? The paper identifies this as a limitation but does not explore remedies.
3. Does the use of non-realistic synthetic damage patterns (random primitives + noise) inadvertently bias the model, harming its performance on real defects that have different morphological characteristics? Transfer success suggests robustness, but the impact of the synthetic reality gap remains unquantified.
4. Is the unified model's performance uniform across all tooth classes (incisors, canines, premolars, molars), or are certain classes more challenging? The paper lacks per-class analysis, despite indications of variability (e.g., molars show less detail).

## Limitations
- The synthetic damage generation may not cover all real-world defect patterns, risking a distribution shift for complex fractures or extensive gingival involvement.
- Evaluation on real clinical data is limited to 16 scans, which reduces statistical confidence in the reported transfer performance.
- The fixed 64³ SDF resolution restricts fine geometric detail, and the iterative diffusion sampling is relatively slow.
