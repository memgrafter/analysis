# Supercharging Agenda Setting Research: The ParlaCAP Dataset of 28 European Parliaments and a Scalable Multilingual LLM-Based Classification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16516
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16516
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: Introduces ParlaCAP, a multilingual policy topic classifier for parliamentary speeches that achieves human-level reliability and enables annotation of 8 million speeches across 28 European parliaments.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents ParlaCAP, a scalable framework for multilingual agenda-setting analysis in parliamentary contexts. By using GPT-4o as a teacher to automatically annotate a large sample of speeches from the ParlaMint corpus, and then fine-tuning a domain-adapted multilingual encoder (XLM-R-Parla), the authors produce a classifier with performance comparable to human annotators and superior to existing out-of-domain models. This yields a comprehensive dataset of 8 million speeches with 22 CAP policy topics, sentiment, and metadata, opening new avenues for large-scale comparative research on political agendas. The approach is cost-effective (~$100) and addresses the traditional bottleneck of manual annotation.

## Method Summary
The authors sample 1,200 speeches from each of 29 ParlaMint corpora, obtaining automatic annotations using GPT-4o with a detailed CAP schema prompt. These pseudo-labels form a training set of ~35k speeches. To address class imbalance, particularly for the rare "Public Lands" label, they use keyword-based filtering on the English-translated corpus and re-annotate candidates with GPT-4o, boosting that class from 145 to 924 instances. The training data is split into training and development sets. A domain-specific pretrained multilingual model (XLM-R-Parla) is fine-tuned on this data for 3 epochs with a low learning rate (1e-5). At inference, a confidence threshold of 0.60 is applied; predictions below that are labeled "Mix". The resulting model is evaluated on manually annotated test sets for four languages, showing macro-F1 scores of 0.72-0.76 and LLM-human agreement (Krippendorff's α 0.60-0.64) within the human-human range (0.59-0.68).

## Key Results
- ParlaCAP classifier achieves human-level reliability: LLM-human Krippendorff's α of 0.60-0.64 versus human-human 0.59-0.68.
- Outperforms out-of-domain CAP models: macro-F1 scores of 0.72-0.76 on test sets across four languages.
- Produces the ParlaCAP dataset: 8 million speeches from 28 parliaments annotated with 22 CAP topics, sentence-level sentiment, and metadata.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** A high-performing LLM can serve as a reliable, scalable annotator for domain-specific policy topic classification, producing training data that yields downstream models with human-level reliability.
**Mechanism:** The teacher-student framework uses GPT-4o to generate pseudo-labels for a large, representative sample of parliamentary speeches (34.8k instances). This LLM-annotated set is then used to fine-tune a smaller, efficient multilingual encoder (XLM-R-Parla). The LLM's detailed knowledge of the CAP schema (provided via prompt) allows it to produce annotations consistent with expert human coders, as measured by Krippendorff's α (0.60-0.64 vs. human-human 0.59-0.68).
**Core assumption:** The LLM's few-shot semantic understanding of policy topics, guided by precise label descriptions, generalizes to the parliamentary domain as well as human experts.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "Agreement between the LLM and human annotators is comparable to inter-annotator agreement among humans."
- [section 3.3] Presents Krippendorff's α scores comparing GPT-4o and three human annotators on a shared test set.
**Break condition:** If the target domain contains highly specialized jargon, procedural language, or context-dependent meanings absent from the LLM's training data, its annotations could diverge from expert human judgment, leading to a "hypothesis drift" where the student model learns the LLM's systematic errors.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Fine-tuning a domain-adapted multilingual model (XLM-R-Parla) on in-domain, LLM-annotated data yields a classifier that outperforms models fine-tuned on out-of-domain, manually-annotated CAP data.
**Mechanism:** Domain-specific pretraining (XLM-R-Parla on parliamentary text) provides a better linguistic representation foundation. When subsequently fine-tuned on the LLM-annotated ParlaMint corpus—which contains the specific lexical patterns, speech structures, and topic distributions of real parliamentary debates—the model learns a decision boundary tailored to the target task's feature distribution. This "in-domain advantage" outweighs the theoretical superiority of manually-annotated but mismatched data.
**Core assumption:** The feature distribution (vocabulary, syntax, collocations) of parliamentary speeches is sufficiently distinct from news media or executive speeches (the source of other CAP training data) that a model must see in-domain examples to learn effectively. The LLM-annotation quality is sufficient to preserve the domain signal.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "The resulting model outperforms existing CAP classifiers trained on manually-annotated but out-of-domain data."
- [section 3.7] Table 4 directly compares the ParlaCAP model (fine-tuned on in-domain data) against multiple "CAP Babel" models (fine-tuned on out-of-domain CAP data) across four languages, showing consistent macro-F1 improvements (e.g., EN: 0.72 vs. 0.64).
- [section 3.4] Notes that XLM-R-Parla was pretrained on 1.72B words from parliamentary proceedings (ParlaMint 3.0 & EuroParl).
**Break condition:** If the domain shift between the out-of-domain CAP data and the target ParlaMint data is smaller than anticipated, the performance benefit of in-domain fine-tuning would diminish. A failure would be observed if the manually-annotated CAP data already covered a sufficiently similar genre and lexicon.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Targeted data augmentation for a rare label ("Public Lands") using LLM-guided keyword filtering effectively resolves class imbalance and improves overall classifier performance without manual annotation.
**Mechanism:** For labels with very few LLM-annotated instances (<200), the model underfits. The authors use domain knowledge to define keywords for the rare label, search the machine-translated English version of the entire multilingual corpus for these keywords, and use the same LLM (GPT-4o) to re-annotate these candidate instances. This "needle-in-haystack" approach programmatically finds and annotates relevant rare cases, boosting the training set count from 145 to 924 and raising the label's F1 from 0.30 to 0.80.
**Core assumption:** 1) The keyword set sufficiently captures the semantic scope of the rare label. 2) The LLM's annotation accuracy on keyword-filtered candidates is high enough to add clean training examples. 3) The increase in training instances for the rare class does not introduce significant noise that harms performance on other classes.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [section 3.2] Details the augmentation pipeline for "Public Lands": keyword definition, corpus search via CLARIN.SI concordancer, LLM re-annotation, and the resulting increase from 145 to 924 instances.
- [section 3.5] Reports the F1 score for "Public Lands" jumps from 0.30 (without augmentation) to 0.80 (with augmentation) for the XLM-R-Parla model.
**Break condition:** If the label definition is too polysemous or the keywords are too narrow/broad, the augmentation could add many false positives (polluting the training data) or miss true positives (ineffective augmentation). This would manifest as either no performance gain or a drop in overall macro-F1.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Transformer-based Encoder Models (e.g., XLM-RoBERTa)**
**Why needed here:** The student model is a fine-tuned XLM-R-Parla, a transformer encoder. Understanding its masked language modeling objective, cross-lingual transfer via shared multilingual vocabulary, and how fine-tuning adapts its internal representations for classification is fundamental to grasping the architecture.
**Quick check:** How does the [CLS] token representation in a model like XLM-R get adapted during fine-tuning to predict a categorical label?

**Concept: Comparative Agendas Project (CAP) Coding Schema**
**Why needed here:** The entire classification task is defined by the 22-topic CAP schema. Misunderstanding the hierarchical, substantive boundaries (e.g., "Law and Crime" vs. "Civil Rights", "Domestic Commerce" vs. "Macroeconomics") would lead to incorrect interpretation of model outputs and dataset utility.
**Quick check:** Where would a speech discussing "government subsidies for renewable energy startups" be coded under CAP, and what is the primary reason for that choice?

**Concept: Teacher-Student (Knowledge Distillation) Paradigm for Data Efficient Learning**
**Why needed here:** The core methodological innovation is using an expensive, high-capacity LLM (teacher) to cheaply annotate large-scale training data for a smaller, deployable model (student). This shifts the bottleneck from human annotation cost to LLM API cost.
**Quick check:** What are the two primary risks of using an LLM as a teacher, and how does the design of the test set (human-annotated, held-out) help mitigate one of them?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Raw ParlaMint corpora → Sampling per corpus → GPT-4o annotation with CAP prompt → (Optional) keyword-based augmentation for rare labels → Training/dev split → Fine-tune XLM-R-Parla → Confidence threshold (0.60) filtering → Batch prediction on full corpus → Merge with sentiment/metadata → Output TSV datasets.

**Critical path:** Raw speeches → Sample 1.2k/corpus → Annotate with GPT-4o → Augment rare labels → Fine-tune XLM-R-Parla → Evaluate on human test set → Apply confidence threshold (0.60) → Generate final ParlaCAP dataset. The LLM annotation step is the pivotal enabler; without it, scaling is infeasible.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Cost vs. Scale: $100 for ~35k LLM annotations vs. estimated many thousands for equivalent human annotation. Tradeoff: Potential for LLM systematic bias vs. human inconsistency.
- Model Capacity vs. Efficiency: XLM-R-Parla (large) offers best performance but higher inference cost than a distilled TinyBERT variant. Tradeoff: Acceptable latency for batch processing 8M speeches vs. real-time use.
- Label Coverage vs. Precision: Adding "Other" and "Mix" (low-confidence) categories increases label set complexity but handles non-policy speech and uncertainty, improving real-world usability.
- Multilingual Unity vs. Per-language Optimization: A single multilingual model is simpler to maintain but may underperform on low-resource languages compared to separate monolingual models.

**Failure signatures:**
1. Low inter-annotator agreement: Krippendorff's α between LLM and humans drops below 0.55, indicating the LLM is an unreliable teacher for this domain.
2. Severe per-language performance drop: Macro-F1 for a low-resource language (e.g., Icelandic, Latvian) is >0.15 below the best language, suggesting the multilingual model hasn't learned sufficient cross-lingual generalization.
3. High "Mix" rate: >25% of speeches marked "Mix" indicates the model is fundamentally uncertain, likely due to out-of-distribution input (e.g., very short procedural interjections) or label schema mismatch.
4. Augmentation backfire: Performance on the "Public Lands" label decreases after augmentation, suggesting the keyword search/LLM re-annotation introduced net noise.

2. Validate in-domain advantage: Take the published ParlaCAP model and the best-performing "out-of-domain" CAP model from Table 4. Evaluate both *only on speeches from the specific ParlaMint-XX corpus used for training the ParlaCAP model*. The performance gap should narrow if the advantage is purely from in-domain data.
3. Test confidence threshold calibration: Apply the published model to a held-out sample of 10k speeches from a new parliament (not in training, e.g., Finnish Eduskunta). Plot the distribution of prediction confidence scores. Identify the natural "elbow" in the confidence-F1 curve. The chosen 0.60 threshold should be data-driven, not arbitrary.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How do systematic institutional differences (e.g., electoral systems, federalism, committee structures) explain the cross-national variation in parliamentary agenda diversity and sentiment patterns observed in the ParlaCAP use cases?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in Section 5.1 that descriptive observations about agenda concentration "suggest hierarchical attention allocation" but note that "more rigorous empirical tests are required to validate these descriptive observations," implying a need for models linking institutional variables to agenda metrics.  
**Why unresolved:** The use cases present aggregate patterns but do not incorporate institutional covariates (e.g., V-DEM data included in metadata) to test causal or correlational explanations for variation.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Multivariate regression or hierarchical modeling using the ParlaCAP dataset, with agenda diversity indices (e.g., Shannon entropy of topic shares) and sentiment scores as dependent variables, and measures of institutional design (e.g., district magnitude, committee powers) as independent variables.

### Open Question 2
To what extent does the reliance on machine-translated English texts (ParlaMint.en-ana) for identifying "Public Lands" keyword candidates introduce translation artifacts that bias the data augmentation pipeline, potentially reducing classifier performance for native-language contexts?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 3.2 describes using the English machine-translated corpus to find speeches containing "Public Lands" keywords across all languages. This assumes semantic equivalence of keywords across languages and that translation does not distort policy domain references.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper does not evaluate whether the augmented "Public Lands" instances are equally representative or accurately labeled in their original language context, nor does it compare augmentation using native-language keyword searches.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A comparative experiment where "Public Lands" instances are identified via native-language keywords (where available) versus translated keywords, measuring differences in label distribution, linguistic features, and downstream classification F1 for that category.

### Open Question 3
How does the confidence-based "Mix" filtering strategy (threshold 0.60) systematically affect downstream sociological inferences, such as estimated gender differences in topic attention or cross-national sentiment comparisons?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3.6 explains that ~9–11% of predictions are discarded as "Mix" and that this filtering improves performance metrics. The use cases in Section 5 explicitly exclude "Other" and "Mix" instances but do not analyze whether their removal creates selection bias.  
**Why unresolved:** The discarded speeches may not be random; they could systematically differ in length, rhetorical style, or policy relevance. Their exclusion could distort estimates of topic prevalence or subgroup (e.g., gender) differences if likelihood of being "Mix" correlates with these factors.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze characteristics (e.g., speech length, metadata like speaker role) of "Mix" instances versus retained ones across parliaments. Conduct sensitivity analyses comparing results with and without including "Mix" instances (e.g., assigning them a neutral topic or imputing based on metadata).

### Open Question 4
Does the CAP schema’s topic ontology maintain conceptual equivalence across the 28 diverse European parliamentary contexts, or do cultural and institutional differences lead to systematic misclassification (e.g., "Government Operations" absorbing locally distinct issues)?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper applies a single label set universally. Section 5.1 notes that attention distributions vary significantly, and Section 3.5 mentions the Bosnian test set was more challenging due to "less structured debates" and "vague borderline cases," hinting at potential schema-culture mismatches.  
**Why unresolved:** The evaluation metrics (macro-F1) do not diagnose *why* errors occur. There is no error analysis by country or topic to check whether certain labels are consistently confused in specific cultural contexts, suggesting possible construct validity issues.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Qualitative error analysis of misclassified speeches per parliament, focusing on whether confusions align with culturally specific policy domains or institutional practices not captured by CAP. Could also involve expert validation in sample countries to assess label appropriateness.

### Open Question 5
What are the long-term scalability limits and cost trajectories of the LLM teacher-student framework when applied to continuously expanding parliamentary corpora (e.g., ParlaMint 6.0+), considering potential degradation in LLM annotation consistency and the need for periodic model retraining?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method’s cost-effectiveness (~$100 for initial annotation) is highlighted, but the paper assumes a static corpus. The ParlaMint collection is growing, and political language evolves. The framework depends on a fixed LLM (GPT-4o) for annotation; changes in LLM versions or political vocabulary could affect training data quality.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper does not address temporal robustness. LLM annotation quality might drift over time or across newly added languages/corpora without re-evaluation. The need for re-annotation or model updating is not discussed.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal study: apply the same LLM annotation prompt to new ParlaMint speeches from later years, measure agreement with human annotators over time and across new languages. Track performance of student models trained on sequential data batches to detect degradation.

## Limitations

- Unreported LLM annotation parameters (temperature, max_tokens, etc.) and restricted test set access (granted upon request) limit full reproducibility of the teacher's reliability.
- Undisclosed keyword list for "Public Lands" augmentation hinders exact replication of the rare-class handling pipeline.
- Performance on low-resource languages may be overestimated due to multilingual averaging; LLM's political domain knowledge is assumed but not directly verified beyond a small test set.

## Confidence

- Mechanism 1 (LLM as reliable annotator): Medium – LLM-human agreement is within human-human range, but systematic biases could be hidden and test set is small.
- Mechanism 2 (In-domain advantage): High – Robust cross-language comparisons (Table 4) confirm consistent macro-F1 gains over out-of-domain models.
- Mechanism 3 (Rare class augmentation): Medium – Dramatic F1 jump for "Public Lands" is encouraging, but depends on undisclosed keyword selection and LLM re-annotation quality.
