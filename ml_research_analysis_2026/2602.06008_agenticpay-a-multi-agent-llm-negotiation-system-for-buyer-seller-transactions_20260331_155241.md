# AgenticPay: A Multi-Agent LLM Negotiation System for Buyer-Seller Transactions
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06008
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06008
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: Introduces a comprehensive benchmark for multi-agent LLM negotiation with 110+ tasks, revealing large performance gaps (Claude Opus 4.5: 86.9 GlobalScore vs. Llama-3.1-8B: 32.5) and a systematic SellerScore advantage across all models.

## Executive Summary
AgenticPay introduces the first benchmark for evaluating large language model (LLM) agents in multi-agent, language-mediated buyer-seller negotiations. The framework comprises over 110 tasks across 10 realistic business scenarios and 8 market configurations, where agents with private valuations negotiate via natural language. Deals are formalized as a language game, with a parser grounding dialogue to structured actions and deterministic economic outcomes.

The benchmark reveals dramatic performance differences: Claude Opus 4.5 attains a GlobalScore of 86.9 and a 100% deal rate, while Llama-3.1-8B lags at 32.5 with a 48.6% timeout rate. Across all models, sellers consistently outperform buyers (SellerScore > BuyerScore). Additional findings show that increasing buyer and seller multiplicity improves outcomes, and the Financial Assets scenario poses the greatest challenge.

## Method Summary
AgenticPay evaluates LLM-based negotiation agents in a simulated multi-agent market. Each task defines a scenario (e.g., used car, SaaS procurement), a market structure (number of buyers and sellers), and a product with public features. Agents receive role-specific system prompts that encode their private reservation price (buyer’s max, seller’s min) and instruct them to output price offers in a structured format (e.g., `### BUYER PRICE($X) ###`). Negotiations proceed for up to 20 rounds, alternating turns. A parser extracts the numeric price from each message and enforces feasibility; if both agents submit matching prices within the bargaining zone ([p_min, p_max]), a deal is recorded.

The primary evaluation metric is GlobalScore, computed by Algorithm 1 and combining deal success (D), economic efficiency (W), speed (E), a discount factor (γ), and a failure penalty (F). The benchmark consists of 111 tasks covering 10 scenarios and 8 market configurations. Agents are evaluated zero-shot without fine-tuning; inference uses temperature 0 and a maximum of 1024 tokens per turn. This design allows systematic comparison of LLM negotiation capabilities across diverse market conditions.

## Key Results
- Claude Opus 4.5 achieves GlobalScore 86.9 with a 100% deal rate; Llama-3.1-8B scores 32.5 and times out in 48.6% of negotiations.
- All models exhibit a systematic SellerScore advantage over BuyerScore (e.g., GPT-5.2: 81.1 vs. 58.5).
- Performance improves with higher buyer and seller multiplicity, and the Financial Assets scenario is the most challenging across all models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Structured action extraction from free-form dialogue enables economic evaluation of language-mediated negotiation.
- **Mechanism:** A parser (`Π`) maps multi-turn natural language exchanges into canonical negotiation actions (e.g., price proposals), allowing the environment to enforce market rules (e.g., reservation price bounds) and compute deterministic economic outcomes (deal price, surplus).
- **Core assumption:** LLM-generated negotiation language contains explicit, extractable price signals that can be grounded to a formal bargaining protocol.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "negotiation is formalized as a language game, with dialogue histories mapped to actions such as price proposals and deal acceptance."
  - [section 4.1]: "A parser Π maps the exchanged messages... to a structured negotiation action a(t)ij = Π(m(t,B)ij , m(t,S)ij ), which extracts quantities such as the proposed transaction price p(t)ij."
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers focus on negotiation but not on structured parsing for economic grounding.
- **Break condition:** If models consistently use ambiguous or implicit pricing language (e.g., "I can be flexible" without a number), the parser fails, leading to incorrect action extraction and invalid economic evaluation.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Asymmetric role performance (SellerScore > BuyerScore) emerges from the scoring function's sensitivity to surplus allocation and the strategic difficulty of maximizing buyer utility under private information.
- **Mechanism:** The normalized utility formulas (r_b = (p_max - p)/Z, r_s = (p - p_min)/Z) create a directional bias: sellers can more easily anchor near their reservation price (p_min) and capture surplus, while buyers must discover p_max without revealing it, facing a steeper information acquisition challenge.
- **Core assumption:** The scoring design (Algorithm 1) reflects typical economic preferences but does not account for role-specific cognitive burdens inherent in private-value bargaining.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "All models exhibit a systematic SellerScore advantage over BuyerScore."
  - [section 4.4]: "GlobalScore uses a symmetric quality term Q = 4r_br_s... BuyerScore and SellerScore capture role-specific utility."
  - [section 5.2, Tables 1 & 4]: Cross-play analysis shows consistent SellerScore > BuyerScore gaps across all models (e.g., Claude Opus 4.5: 76.1 vs 63.5; Qwen3-14B: 58.9 vs 47.6).
- **Break condition:** If models learn role-specific strategies that overfit to the scoring function (e.g., buyers always conceding early to avoid failure penalty), the asymmetry may not generalize to real-world utility functions with different risk profiles.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Increased market multiplicity (more buyers/sellers) improves deal rates and GlobalScores by increasing liquidity and reducing the strategic pressure of single-point failure.
- **Mechanism:** In many-to-many settings, agents have alternative trading partners, which reduces the cost of walking away from an unproductive negotiation and encourages more reasonable initial offers, leading to faster convergence within the bargaining zone.
- **Core assumption:** The primary barrier to agreement in bilateral settings is the fear of losing the *only* trading opportunity, leading to overly cautious or aggressive anchoring.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.3]: "Performance improves with increased buyer and seller multiplicity... This counterintuitive finding... can be attributed to increased market liquidity: agents have more opportunities to find compatible trading partners."
  - [section 5.3, Table 2]: GlobalScore increases from 1B1S to MBMS for all models (e.g., Claude Opus: 83.4 → 89.8; Llama-3.1-8B: 27.9 → 37.5).
- **Break condition:** If agent policies are not robust to parallel negotiation (e.g., they cannot maintain separate memory states for multiple counterparts), increased multiplicity could cause state confusion and *worse* performance, contradicting the liquidity hypothesis.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Bilateral Bargaining Zone (Z = p_max - p_min)**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire scoring system normalizes utilities within this zone. Agents must understand that their private reservation price is a hard constraint, and agreements must fall within this interval to be feasible.
  - *Quick check question:* If a buyer's p_max = $100 and a seller's p_min = $80, what is the normalized buyer surplus for an agreed price of $90?

- **Concept: Multi-Agent Market Structures (1-to-N, N-to-N)**
  - *Why needed here:* Tasks scale complexity along buyer/seller multiplicity. Understanding how competition and matching dynamics change from bilateral to many-to-many settings is essential for interpreting performance variations.
  - *Quick check question:* In a 1B2S scenario, what is the seller's primary strategic challenge compared to 1B1S?

- **Concept: LLM Prompt-Based Role Specialization**
  - *Why needed here:* Agents are identical LLMs differentiated only by system prompts encoding private valuations (p_max/p_min) and role instructions. The efficacy of role-playing via prompts is a foundational assumption.
  - *Quick check question:* What specific instruction in the seller prompt (Table 15) is critical for maintaining information asymmetry?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Environment:** Manages negotiation protocol (rounds, turn-taking), holds public product info (v_j, x) and parses actions via `Π`.
  2. **Task:** Configures market structure (N_B, N_S, |V|) and scenario (e.g., "Used Car"). Defined in `task_categories` (Figure 2).
  3. **Agent:** LLM policy `π_B_i` / `π_S_j` with role-specific system prompt, private state (b_i/σ_j), and memory of dialogue history.
  4. **Metrics:** `Algorithm 1` computes GlobalScore/BuyerScore/SellerScore from final price `p`, private values, and termination round `t`.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Initialize environment with a task (e.g., 1B1P1S) and scenario (e.g., "Luxury Watch").
  2. Sample private p_max (buyer) and p_min (seller) from scenario value range.
  3. Inject private values into agent system prompts. Begin dialogue.
  4. Each step: agents generate message → parser `Π` extracts price → check termination (agreement? timeout? overflow?).
  5. On termination: compute scores based on `p` and `t`.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Simplicity vs. Realism:** Negotiation is constrained to explicit price offers (via `### BUYER PRICE($X) ###`), sacrificing linguistic nuance for parseable actions.
  - **Generalization vs. Control:** 10 scenarios provide diversity but rely on prompt-based role specification rather than fine-tuned models for each domain.
  - **Evaluation vs. Training:** The benchmark is inference-only; no fine-tuning or reinforcement learning is supported, limiting assessment of adaptation potential.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **High Timeout Rate:** Model fails to converge within 20 rounds (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B 48.6%). Indicates poor long-horizon planning or inability to identify feasible zone.
  - **High Overflow Rate:** Agents propose prices outside [p_min, p_max] (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B 10.8%). Indicates poor constraint adherence or value understanding.
  - **Role Asymmetry Collapse:** BuyerScore << SellerScore persists even in capable models (e.g., GPT-5.2: 58.5 vs 81.1). Suggests inherent difficulty in buyer strategy.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline replication:** Run all models on 1B1P1S tasks. Verify performance ordering matches Table 1 (Claude > Gemini > GPT > Qwen > Llama) and confirm SellerScore > BuyerScore.
  2. **Multiplicity scaling:** Compare 1B1S vs. 1BMS vs. MB1S vs. MBMS for a single model (e.g., Claude). Validate that GlobalScore increases with buyer/seller count per Table 2.
  3. **Scenario stress test:** Run a single model (e.g., Gemini-3-Flash) across all 10 scenarios. Confirm Financial Assets yields lowest GlobalScore per Table 3, and analyze dialogue for evidence of risk-assessment failure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Why do all LLMs exhibit a systematic performance asymmetry where SellerScore significantly exceeds BuyerScore across tasks?  
  *Basis in paper:* The paper states the asymmetry is observed but does not diagnose root causes, which could include reward biases, training data distributions, or inherent strategic differences.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Ablation studies varying reward functions, role-specific prompt engineering, or analysis of concession patterns.

- **Open Question 2:** What specific cognitive or reasoning deficits cause LLMs to struggle with long-horizon strategic planning in extended negotiations?  
  *Basis in paper:* The abstract and Section 6 highlight persistent challenges in long-horizon strategic reasoning as a key finding.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Process-focused analyses like probing hidden states for opponent modeling, measuring constraint consistency, or evaluating with reasoning scaffolds (e.g., chain-of-thought, MCTS).

- **Open Question 3:** Why do open-weight models frequently fail to achieve agreement in the final negotiation rounds despite small price gaps (near-miss failures), and how can this be mitigated?  
  *Basis in paper:* Table 8 shows over 40% of failures occur when price gap is within 5 units, indicating difficulty with final convergence.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments comparing models with/without fine-tuning on near-miss scenarios, or analyzing dialogue patterns for concession timing.

- **Open Question 4:** What factors make the Financial Assets negotiation scenario disproportionately difficult for LLMs compared to other domains?  
  *Basis in paper:* Financial Assets yields the lowest GlobalScores; the authors hypothesize it requires sophisticated risk assessment and market dynamics reasoning, but this is not validated.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Cross-domain transfer tests, controlled variation of scenario features (scale, risk framing), or expert annotation of required skills.

## Limitations
- Parser robustness: Exact extraction logic and handling of ambiguous pricing language are unspecified; extraction failures may skew evaluation.
- Task composition: Distribution of the 111 tasks across scenarios and configurations is incompletely documented, hindering reproducibility.
- Generalizability: Findings are confined to a simulated, prompt-based environment with strict output formatting; real-world negotiation dynamics (e.g., relationship-building, incomplete information) are absent.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Structured parsing): Medium confidence
- Mechanism 2 (Role asymmetry): High confidence
- Mechanism 3 (Multiplicity benefit): High confidence
- Overall benchmark validity: Low confidence
