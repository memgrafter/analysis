# On Interpolation Formulas Describing Neural Network Generalization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.13872
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13872
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Stochastic training (SGD, SGDM, RMSprop, Adam) is equivalent to a kernel machine with optimizer-specific weighting; generalization error depends on the null space of the induced kernel operator; experiments show class‑structured kernels, linear separability of tangent features, and rank gaps predicting OOD failure.

## Executive Summary
This paper extends Domingos' deterministic kernel interpretation of neural networks to stochastic training with the most common optimizers. It introduces a stochastic gradient kernel via a continuous‑time diffusion approximation of mini‑batch iterates and proves path‑kernel representations for SGD, SGDM, RMSprop, and Adam. For each optimizer, the expected prediction equals its initial value minus an integral over the kernel-weighted loss derivative, with an error of order O(η) for SGD/SGDM/RMSprop and O(√η) for Adam. The generalization error is linked to the null space of the integral operator induced by this kernel; at stationarity, the residual after training lies almost entirely in that null space, meaning only target components within the kernel’s image can be learned. Empirical results confirm that the gradient kernel evolves into a block‑diagonal, class‑aware structure, that tangent features become linearly separable, and that a rank gap between the tangent spaces of training and test data reliably signals poor out‑of‑distribution performance.

## Method Summary
The core method constructs a stochastic gradient kernel eK_{t,s}(x,x′) = ⟨∇_Θ f(x,Θ_t), ∇_Θ f(x′,Θ_s)⟩, where Θ_t follows a continuous‑time diffusion that weakly approximates the discrete optimizer (SGD, SGDM, RMSprop, Adam). This SDE captures the same drift (full gradient) and encodes mini‑batch noise in its diffusion coefficient. Applying Itô’s formula to f(x,Θ_t) and taking expectations yields a Domingos‑type formula: the expected output at time T equals its initial value minus ∫_0^T (1/N) Σ_n eK_{t,s}(x,x_n) ∂ℓ/∂f ds, plus a remainder term that is O(η) for SGD/SGDM/RMSprop and O(√η) for Adam under scaling assumptions. The kernel induces a compact integral operator K_t on L²(P); the analysis of generalization then focuses on the null space of K_T. Under a stationarity assumption at T, the time derivative of the expected loss vanishes, implying that the residual f^* - f(·,Θ_T) is approximately orthogonal to Im(K_T). Thus, any component of the target lying in ker(K_T) cannot be learned. Operationally, one computes the empirical tangent matrix ∇_Θ f(X,Θ_T) and compares its rank on the training set to its rank on a mixture that includes test points. A significant rank increase indicates that the test points introduce tangent directions outside the span of training gradients, i.e., in ker(K_T), predicting higher error.

The authors validate these predictions with experiments on synthetic and real data. For a binary classification task, they show that the normalized gradient kernel bK_t becomes increasingly block‑diagonal with respect to class labels over training epochs, and that nearest‑neighbor queries under bK_t become label‑pure. In a controlled 1‑D ReLU regression, initializing one bias outside the training domain creates a dormant unit that never activates on training inputs; the resulting tangent matrix has a higher rank when test inputs that do activate this unit are added, and test error remains large. Additional MNIST experiments demonstrate similar rank gaps for out‑of‑distribution digits and letters. These findings provide empirical support for the theoretical link between kernel null spaces and generalization.

## Key Results
- Stochastic path‑kernel representations for SGD, SGDM, RMSprop, and Adam are proved; the expected output error is O(η) for the first three and O(√η) for Adam.
- Generalization error is theoretically linked to the null space of the integral operator induced by the stochastic gradient kernel; after convergence the residual concentrates in this null space.
- Empirically, the gradient kernel evolves into a class‑block‑diagonal structure, tangent features become linearly separable, and a rank gap between training and test tangent spaces predicts out‑of‑distribution failure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Stochastic mini-batch training can be represented as a kernel-weighted aggregation of training gradients, where the weights are given by a stochastic gradient kernel defined along a continuous-time diffusion approximation of the discrete iterates.
- Mechanism: The discrete SGD/SGDM/RMSprop/Adam iterates are weakly approximated (first-order) by an Itô SDE that shares the same drift (full gradient) and encodes mini-batch noise in its diffusion coefficient. This allows defining the stochastic gradient kernel eK_{t,s}(x,x′) = ⟨∇f(x,Z_{t}), ∇f(x′,Z_{s})⟩, where Z_{t} solves the SDE. Applying Itô’s formula to f(x,Z_{t}) and taking expectations yields a Domingos-type representation: E[f(x,Θ_{k})] = f(x,Θ_{0}) - E[∫ (1/N) Σ_{n} eK_{s}(x,x_{n}) ∂ℓ/∂f ds] + O(η) for SGD/SGDM/RMSprop, and O(√η) for Adam.
- Core assumption: Assumption 2.1 (loss and network smoothness, bounded gradients); first-order weak approximation holding uniformly in k (Lemma 3.1). For Adam, scaling β₁ = 1-c₁η with c₁ = O(η^{-ξ}), ξ∈(0,1) and c₂=O(1).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We introduce a stochastic gradient kernel that extends the deterministic version via a continuous-time diffusion approximation of mini-batch iterates, and path‑kernel representations are proved for SGD, SGDM, RMSprop and Adam."
  - [section 3.1]: Lemma 3.1 establishes first-order weak approximation; Definition 3.2 defines eK_{t,s}.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors discuss NTK and kernel interpolation but do not directly address stochastic Domingos extension; evidence is primarily internal.
- Break condition: If learning rate η is too large, the weak approximation error O(η) may not be negligible relative to the signal. For ReLU networks, activation pattern switches across parameter boundaries violate global smoothness, requiring separate treatment within activation regions.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Different optimizers reshape the kernel weighting: momentum adds exponential temporal memory (SGDM), adaptive methods reweight gradient directions via time-varying preconditioners (RMSprop, Adam), and Adam’s first‑moment adaptivity introduces an unavoidable O(√η) remainder due to feedback of stochastic first‑moment noise.
- Mechanism: For SGDM, the momentum state M_{t} = -∫_{0}^{t} e^{-(t-s)µ} ∇L(Z_{s}) ds + … leads to double integral with memory kernel e^{-(t-s)µ}. For RMSprop, the preconditioner P_{t} = diag(S_{t})^{1/2}+εI (from second‑moment estimate) yields a weighted kernel eK^{P_{t}}_{t}(x,x′) = ⟨∇f(x,Z_{t}), ∇f(x′,Z_{t})⟩_{P_{t}^{-1}}. For Adam, both memory (first moment) and adaptivity (second moment) combine, producing a complex scaling factor √{(1‑e^{-c₂t})/(1‑e^{-c₁t})} and a remainder O(√η) because the first‑moment noise enters with amplitude c₁/√η.
- Core assumption: The SDE approximations for each optimizer (Appendix B) and the stated scaling regimes for Adam (c₁=O(η^{-ξ}), c₂=O(1)). The integrated factor techniques rely on Lipschitz drifts and bounded diffusion coefficients.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "For SGD the expected output equals an O(η)-approximation to a kernel machine with optimizer‑specific weighting; Adam incurs an O(√η) remainder."
  - [section 3.3]: Theorems 3.5 (SGDM formula with e^{-(t-s)µ}), 3.6 (RMSprop with P_{t}), 3.7 (Adam with time‑varying scaling).
  - [corpus]: Neighbors discuss adaptive optimizers but not in the path‑kernel framework; limited direct support.
- Break condition: The exponential memory in SGDM assumes exact exponential decay; non‑ideal momentum schedules (e.g., cyclical) break the form. For Adam, if c₁ does not diverge as η→0, the O(√η) remainder is still present; if c₁ scales too fast (ξ≥1), the approximation may degrade further.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Generalization error is driven by the component of the target function that lies in the null space of the integral operator K_{T} induced by the stochastic gradient kernel. Extrapolation failure occurs when test points have tangent features outside the span of training tangent features (rank gap).
- Mechanism: The path‑kernel update modifies only the projection of the residual onto Im(K_{t}). Under stationarity at T, the time derivative of the L² residual vanishes, implying K_{T}^{1/2} Δ_{T} ≈ 0 in expectation, i.e., Δ_{T} is nearly orthogonal to Im(K_{T}). Thus the residual energy concentrates near ker(K_{T}). For a test x, if ∇f(x,Θ) has a component in ker(K_{T}), that component cannot be learned by gradient descent and remains as error. Finite‑sample signature: rank(∇f(X_{mix},Θ)) > rank(∇f(X_{train},Θ)) indicates unused tangent directions.
- Core assumption: Assumption 3.1 (first‑order weak approximation, compact domain X, smooth f, stationarity at T: d/dt E[L(Z_{t})]_{t=T} = O(η)). For mean‑squared error loss.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "We then link the generalization error to the null space of the integral operator induced by the stochastic gradient kernel."
  - [section 3.4]: Theorem 3.8 formalizes residual concentration; simulations in Section 5.3–5.4 show rank gap correlates with poor generalization on unseen digits/letters.
  - [section 5.4]: 1‑D ReLU example where a dormant neuron (bias outside training regime) creates a rank gap and persistent test error.
- Break condition: If the kernel K_{T} is not sufficiently evolved (e.g., training stopped early), its image may be too small, so even some “in‑distribution” components may be unlearned. Stationarity at T is crucial; if the loss landscape is still changing rapidly, the null‑space characterization may not hold.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Weak Approximation of Stochastic Differential Equations
- Why needed here: The discrete mini‑batch updates are approximated in the weak (expectation) sense by an Itô SDE, enabling continuous‑time analysis and the definition of the stochastic gradient kernel. Without this, there is no bridge from discrete SGD to a path integral.
- Quick check question: What does “first‑order weak approximation” mean, and why is controlling expectations of smooth observables sufficient for deriving the Domingos formula?

### Concept: Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) and Compact Integral Operators
- Why needed here: The generalization analysis hinges on the integral operator K_{t} : g ↦ ∫ eK_{t}(x,x′) g(x′) dP(x′). Its spectral decomposition separates learnable (positive eigenvalue) and unlearnable (null space) modes. This is standard RKHS theory (Mercer’s theorem).
- Quick check question: If Δ_{T} ∈ ker(K_{T}), what does that imply about the ability of gradient descent to further reduce the generalization error?

### Concept: Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) and Tangent Feature Space
- Why needed here: The Domingos representation expresses predictions as linear combinations of gradient inner products, i.e., similarities in the tangent feature space. Understanding how this kernel evolves (e.g., becoming block‑diagonal) explains why certain test points are well‑predicted.
- Quick check question: In the lazy training limit where the NTK is constant, what simplification occurs in the Domingos formula, and what does that imply about the learned function?

## Architecture Onboarding
SDE Engine (instantiates the SDE for the chosen optimizer) → Kernel Computation Module (computes the stochastic gradient kernel eK_{t,s}) → Path-Integral Aggregator (evaluates the integral of kernel-weighted loss derivatives) → Kernel Operator Analyzer (computes the empirical tangent matrix and rank diagnostics) → Visualization Suite (plots kernel matrices, tangent feature UMAPs, and confusion matrices)

### Critical path
1. Choose model f(·,Θ), dataset {(x_n,y_n)} and optimizer (SGD/SGDM/RMSprop/Adam) with hyperparameters (η, β, ε).
2. Train network for T steps, collecting parameters Θ_k or using the SDE approximation to generate a continuous trajectory Z_t.
3. For a test input x, compute the expected prediction correction: - (1/N) Σ_n ∫_0^T (kernel weight) · (loss derivative) ds, using the appropriate formula from Theorems 3.4–3.7. The remainder O(η) or O(√η) is bounded but not explicitly computed.
4. To analyze generalization: after convergence, compute residual Δ(x)=f*(x)-f(x,Θ_T). Check alignment with ker(K_T) by verifying that the projected residual onto Im(K_T) is small (e.g., via solving a linear system in the finite‑dimensional tangent span).
5. For extrapolation diagnosis: compute ranks rank(∇f(X_train,Θ_T)) and rank(∇f(X_train∪X_test,Θ_T)). A significant gap indicates test points introduce tangent directions not spanned by training features → expected poor generalization.

### Design tradeoffs
- **SDE fidelity vs. computational cost**: Using a high-order SDE approximation reduces discretization error but increases complexity; first‑order is sufficient for O(η) bounds.
- **Optimizer selection**: Adam is popular but carries O(√η) error; for very small η the kernel representation may be less accurate than SGD/SGDM/RMSprop’s O(η). Yet in practice η is moderate (e.g., 10^{-3}), so the view remains applicable.
- **Smoothness vs. expressivity**: The theory demands f(·,Θ) ∈ C², which ReLU nets violate globally. One can piecewise apply the theory within activation regions, but boundary crossings require extra care; the empirical results assume the network operates in a regime where boundary crossings do not dominate.
- **Kernel evolution monitoring**: The kernel K_t must evolve to become class‑discriminative. If training is too short or η too small, K_t may remain close to initialization, limiting the kernel machine’s ability to fit.

### Failure signatures
- **Large remainder**: For Adam, if c₁ does not scale as η^{-ξ} with ξ<1, the O(√η) error may dominate and break the kernel approximation. Monitor by comparing Adam vs. SGD predictions on a held set; large discrepancies may signal breakdown.
- **Kernel matrix not becoming block‑diagonal**: If bK_T remains isotropic or class‑mixed (Figure 2e vs. 2f), the path‑kernel aggregation cannot produce accurate classification; this suggests optimization failure or insufficient capacity.
- **Persistent rank gap**: rank(∇f(X_mix,Θ_T)) >> rank(∇f(X_train,Θ_T)) indicates test points excite unseen tangent directions → extrapolation failure (Section 5.4). Confirm by evaluating test accuracy; low accuracy with a rank gap is expected.
- **Stochastic integral non‑vanishing**: The proofs rely on the Itô integral having zero expectation (martingale property). If integrand has heavy tails or unbounded second moment, the expectation may not vanish. Check moment conditions on ∇f and Σ_{|B|}.
- **Stationarity violation**: If E[L(Z_t)] is still decreasing rapidly at T, the null‑space argument (Theorem 3.8) may not apply; generalization may improve with further training.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Kernel evolution visualization**: Train a classifier (e.g., on binary circle data). At epochs 0, 100, 200 compute the normalized gradient kernel bK_t for all training points. Verify that block‑diagonal class structure emerges; also check that nearest neighbors under bK_t become label‑pure, unlike Euclidean neighbors (Section 5.1, Figure 1).
2. **Tangent feature separability**: After training, extract tangent feature vector ϕ(x_n)=∇Θf(x_n,Θ_T) for every training point. Train a linear SVM on these features with their class labels. Plot the SVM decision boundary on the input space; it should closely match the true circular/disk boundary (Section 5.2, Figure 3).
3. **Rank gap and OOD performance**: On MNIST, train on digits 0‑4. Compute ranks of ∇Θf(X_train,Θ) and ∇Θf(X_train∪X_test,Θ) where X_test contains either unseen digits (5‑9) or printed digits/letters. A significant rank gap should correlate with low accuracy on OOD test sets (Section 5.3–5.4, Figures 4–8).

## Limitations
- The theory assumes global smoothness (C²) and bounded gradients, which are violated by ReLU networks; while the authors suggest a piecewise extension, it is not rigorously proved.
- Adam’s O(√η) remainder relies on the scaling c₁ = O(η^{-ξ}) with ξ < 1; default hyperparameters (e.g., β₁=0.9) may not satisfy this, potentially undermining the kernel representation’s accuracy.
- The paper lacks crucial experimental details (network architectures, hyperparameters, anchor point selection), making full replication of the reported results difficult.

## Confidence
- Stochastic path‑kernel representation (Theorems 3.4–3.7): Medium‑High
- Generalization link via null space of the integral operator (Theorem 3.8): Medium
- Empirical phenomena (block‑diagonal kernel evolution, linear separable tangent features, rank gap predicting OOD): Medium
