# Benchmarking Vision-Language Models for French PDF-to-Markdown Conversion

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.11960
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11960
- **Reference count**: 7
- **Primary result**: The benchmark isolates challenging French PDF pages via model disagreement; top proprietary system (Gemini 3 Pro) achieves 0.76 aggregate pass rate, with handwriting and forms remaining highly challenging.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a new benchmark for evaluating Vision-Language Models (VLMs) on converting French PDFs to Markdown, with a focus on operationally difficult pages such as handwritten forms, complex layouts, and dense tables. The benchmark is constructed by sampling pages with high disagreement between two existing VLMs, ensuring focus on failure-prone content. Evaluation uses unit-test-style checks with category-specific normalization to ignore superficial formatting variations while measuring substantive errors. Results on 15 models show that even the best system (Gemini 3 Pro) attains only a 0.76 pass rate, indicating substantial room for improvement, particularly on handwriting. A manual audit confirms that 98% of failures reflect genuine model errors, demonstrating the benchmark's precision over prior evaluations.

## Method Summary
The authors built a benchmark from a corpus of 60,000 French documents (CCPDF/Gallica). To identify difficult pages, they applied model-disagreement sampling: ran two VLMs (dots-ocr and mineru2.5) on samples, computed edit distances, and selected pages with the largest differences. These pages were manually categorized into six types (tiny_text, multicolumn, long_table, handwritten, forms, graphics). For each page, they generated unit tests (e.g., TextPresenceTest, TableTest) using prompts to a strong VLM (Gemini-3-Pro) and human verification via a Streamlit app. The evaluation pipeline normalizes model outputs per test category (cleaning Markdown, Unicode harmonization, optional ASCII projection, alphanumeric filtering, layout-insensitive spacing) to disregard benign formatting differences. Models are run through the vlmparse library, outputs normalized, and test pass rates aggregated per category and overall.

## Key Results
- Benchmark selection via model disagreement surfaces pages with high rates of omissions, substitutions, and layout errors.
- Top proprietary model (Gemini 3 Pro) achieved 0.76 aggregate pass rate; handwriting and forms categories scored below 0.40 for most models.
- Manual audit of failures showed 98% were genuine model errors (vs. 47% for OlmOCR-bench), confirming the benchmark's focus on substantive OCR/layout failures.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Disagreement Sampling Targets Real Difficulty
By selecting pages with high edit-distance between outputs of two different VLMs, the benchmark intentionally biases toward content where current conversion systems are unstable. This proxy for difficulty is operationally relevant: such pages exhibit higher rates of omissions, substitutions, and layout errors. Evidence includes the abstract's description of covering "handwritten forms, complex layouts, dense tables, and graphics-rich pages," and section 2.2's explicit use of disagreement as a difficulty signal. A break condition would be saturation where all VLMs converge, making disagreement vanish and the benchmark less discriminative.

### Mechanism 2: Normalized Unit Tests Measure Substantive Errors
The unit-test approach (e.g., checking text presence, reading order, table constraints) isolates concrete failure modes. Category-specific normalization (Unicode cleanup, ASCII projection, alphanumeric filtering, layout-insensitive spacing) discounts superficial variations in Markdown formatting that do not affect downstream retrieval tasks. Evidence includes the abstract's mention of "target[ing] concrete failure modes" and section 2.4's detailed normalization stages. If normalization is too aggressive it may mask real errors; too strict it re-introduces over-penalization. The design balances this by varying strictness per category.

### Mechanism 3: Audit Validates Precision
A manual audit of 62 failure cases from the benchmark and OlmOCR-bench found that 98% of the former were genuine model errors, while only 47% of the latter were. This stark contrast demonstrates that the benchmark's tests and normalization effectively avoid annotation noise and ambiguous designs. Evidence appears in the abstract and supplementary material. However, the small audit sample and potential annotator bias limit generalizability.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: 
vlmparse library → Normalization pipeline → Test suite → Benchmark dataset (page IDs, categories, tests) → Audit interface (implied)

**Critical path**: Input PDF page → vlmparse model inference → Raw Markdown → Normalization (per-test rules) → Test execution → Pass/Fail tally → Category aggregation. Normalization correctness is vital for valid scores.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Normalization strictness: Aggressive settings reduce false positives from formatting but risk masking real content errors; per-category tuning mitigates this.
- DPI vs. throughput/accuracy: Higher DPI improves legibility but increases compute and can destabilize small models; sensitivity is non-monotonic.
- Test granularity: More tests per page yield finer diagnostics but higher annotation cost; benchmark uses sparse targeted checks.

**Failure signatures to monitor**:
- Incomplete transcription: Model skips regions, especially in colorful layouts (check TextPresenceTest for marginalia).
- Repetition/looping: Common at higher temperatures for small models; look for near-identical spans.
- Table structure corruption: TableTest failures indicate mis-merged cells or broken reading order even if cell text is correct.
- Normalization mismatch: Failure only under ASCII projection suggests accent handling issues.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can fine-tuning smaller open-weight VLMs on the benchmark’s difficult categories (handwriting, forms) meaningfully close the performance gap with proprietary models, and which category yields the largest improvement?** The paper suggests future work should investigate this; current results show large models significantly outperform smaller ones on handwriting/forms.
2. **To what extent does the benchmark’s category-specific normalization design directly cause the lower annotation error rate observed in the audit, and which normalization components are most critical?** The audit shows 98% model errors vs. 47% for OlmOCR, attributed to aggressive normalization, but no ablation isolates the most effective components.
3. **Is the observed systematic failure of dots.ocr to skip text-dense regions in colorful layouts caused by training-data bias or decoding heuristics, and can this be mitigated without catastrophic loss on other layouts?** The paper hypothesizes a bias to ignore pictures/illustrations but does not probe the root cause.
4. **How does the model-disagreement sampling strategy affect benchmark difficulty distribution and saturation potential across languages, and would a more diverse model pool yield harder or more generalizable test cases?** The current selection from two VLMs may bias toward their shared weaknesses; French-only focus limits generalizability.
5. **Why are smaller VLMs disproportionately sensitive to decoding temperature increases, leading to catastrophic degradation on long pages, and does this stem from visual encoder limitations, language model capacity, or joint multimodal training?** The paper notes the correlation but does not investigate the mechanistic cause.

## Limitations
- The exact benchmark test suite, normalization parameters, and annotation tools are not publicly released, hindering full replication.
- The manual audit that validates the benchmark’s precision is based on only 62 failure cases, limiting statistical confidence.
- The model-disagreement sampling may introduce bias toward failure modes shared by the two selected VLMs, potentially under-representing other error types.
