# Bench-MFG: A Benchmark Suite for Learning in Stationary Mean Field Games

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.12517
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12517
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces Bench-MFG, a comprehensive benchmark suite for stationary MFGs, and MF-PSO, a black-box exploitability minimizer that outperforms Fictitious Play with linear computational cost.

## Executive Summary
Mean Field Games (MFGs) model large populations of interacting agents in a mean-field limit. Despite their growing importance in reinforcement learning, the lack of standardized benchmarks has made it difficult to compare and assess the robustness of MFG algorithms. This paper introduces Bench-MFG, a comprehensive benchmark suite for stationary MFGs. It defines a taxonomy of five fundamental MFG classes—No-Interaction, Contractive, Lasry-Lions Monotone, Potential, and Dynamics-Coupled—and provides prototypical environments for each class. Additionally, it proposes MF-Garnet, a procedural generator that produces random MFG instances with tunable interaction structures. The authors also present MF-PSO, a novel black-box exploitability minimization algorithm based on particle swarm optimization. Through extensive experiments across all classes and ten random Garnet instances, they evaluate eight solvers. Results indicate that Fixed Point is limited to trivial games, Fictitious Play is robust but often suboptimal, Online Mirror Descent achieves the lowest exploitability but is hyperparameter‑sensitive, and MF-PSO outperforms Fictitious Play while scaling linearly. The paper concludes with practical guidelines for future MFG research and releases the benchmark suite and JAX implementation openly.

## Method Summary
Bench-MFG provides a ready-to-use framework for evaluating stationary MFG solvers. It includes five prototypical environments (one per class) and a procedural MF-Garnet generator. The benchmark uses an exploitability-based evaluation: each solver iteratively refines a policy by computing best responses against the current mean field and updating the mean field via the stationary distribution map approximation. Supported algorithms encompass Fixed Point, Fictitious Play (with damping), Policy Iteration variants, Online Mirror Descent (OMD), and the proposed MF-PSO. The codebase, implemented in JAX, leverages just‑in‑time compilation for efficiency. To reproduce the main experiments, one must initialize an environment, select an algorithm with appropriate hyperparameters (optimally chosen from the paper's Appendix sweeps), run the iterative training loop, and log exploitability over iterations. Key unknown details include the precise best hyperparameter values for each environment–algorithm pair, the exact random seeds used for MF-Garnet instances, and the number of steps required for approximating the stationary distribution and planning horizon. Common failure modes involve hyperparameter sensitivity (particularly for OMD), non‑convergence in non‑monotone games, and excessive runtime without JAX optimizations.

## Key Results
- Bench-MFG introduces a taxonomy of five stationary MFG classes and MF‑Garnet, enabling systematic, reproducible evaluation.
- MF‑PSO, a black-box exploitability minimizer, surpasses Fictitious Play with linear computational scaling.
- Fixed Point solves only trivial games; Fictitious Play is robust but suboptimal; Online Mirror Descent achieves lowest exploitability but is hyperparameter‑sensitive; MF‑PSO outperforms Fictitious Play with linear cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** A standardized benchmark suite (Bench-MFG) enables comparable and robust evaluation of MFG algorithms by providing a diverse, controlled set of problem classes and random instances.
**Mechanism:** The suite defines a taxonomy (No-Interaction, Contractive, Lasry‑Lions Monotone, Potential, Dynamics‑Coupled) with prototypical environments for each class. It also introduces MF-Garnet, a procedural generator creating random MFG instances with tunable interaction types (additive/multiplicative in dynamics/rewards). This allows systematic testing across structurally different games and statistical stress‑testing.
**Core assumption:** Algorithm performance varies significantly across MFG structural classes; a single simple environment is insufficient to assess robustness.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "fragmentation makes it difficult to assess robustness... We propose a comprehensive benchmark suite... we introduce a taxonomy of problem classes... and MF‑Garnets."
- [section 4] Details the five classes and their mathematical definitions (Def. 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.7).
- [corpus] Neighbors discuss learning in various MFG settings (continuous, major‑minor, operator learning), implying a need for diverse benchmarks.
- **Break condition:** If all algorithms perform identically across all generated MF‑Garnet instances and class prototypes, the benchmark fails to expose meaningful differences.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Exploitability minimization via black‑box optimization (MF‑PSO) can outperform standard iterative methods (like Fictitious Play) on certain MFG classes with linear computational scaling.
**Mechanism:** MF‑PSO adapts Particle Swarm Optimization to directly minimize the exploitability function E(π) by treating policy logits as particle positions in a swarm. It avoids iterative best‑response computations, instead using the exploitability estimator directly as a black‑box objective.
**Core assumption:** Exploitability can be effectively estimated and optimized without explicit gradient information through policy iteration loops.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [section 3] Describes MF‑PSO algorithm (Alg. 8) and states it is a "black‑box exploitability minimization method."
- [section 5] Experimental results show MF‑PSO "outperforms Fictitious Play with a linear computational cost" in Table 2.
- [corpus] Limited direct evidence; work on "Bounded Rationality Equilibrium Learning" suggests alternative equilibrium concepts beyond standard NE, aligning with exploitability focus.
- **Break condition:** MF‑PSO's performance degrades severely in high‑dimensional policy spaces or if exploitability estimation is too noisy/variance‑prone.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Hyperparameter sensitivity of MFG solvers is class‑dependent; therefore, guidelines mandate testing across multiple classes and parameter regimes.
**Mechanism:** The paper's extensive sweeps (Appendix E) show that algorithm performance (e.g., OMD's low exploitability vs. FP's failure) and optimal hyperparameters vary with MFG class (LL‑MFG vs. DC‑MFG) and even within‑class parameters (e.g., α in Beach Bar). This reveals failure modes and robustness profiles.
**Core assumption:** No single solver or fixed hyperparameter set is universally optimal across the MFG taxonomy.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [section 6] "Online Mirror Descent (OMD) typically yields the lowest exploitability... but requires delicate hyperparameter tuning due to high sensitivity."
- [section 6] Guideline 3: "Do not rely on a single game type. Report performance on examples drawn from the various classes."
- [corpus] "Population-aware Online Mirror Descent" neighbor paper addresses population‑dependence, highlighting sensitivity issues.
- **Break condition:** If hyperparameter sweeps show identical performance curves across all classes and parameters, the claim of class‑dependent sensitivity is falsified.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Mean Field Nash Equilibrium (MFNE)**
- **Why needed:** The entire benchmark aims to compute or approximate an MFNE (π*, μ*). Understanding that an MFNE requires π* ∈ BR(μ*) and μ* = M(π*) is fundamental to interpreting exploitability and algorithm correctness.
- **Quick check:** If an algorithm's output policy π̂ induces a mean field μ̂ = M(π̂), but π̂ is not a best response to μ̂, is (π̂, μ̂) an MFNE?

**Concept: Exploitability**
- **Why needed:** Exploitability E(π) = max_π' J(π'; μ_π) - J(π; μ_π) is the primary evaluation metric in the paper. It quantifies how far a policy is from equilibrium. Algorithms are compared via final exploitability curves/values.
- **Quick check:** What is the exploitability of a policy that is a best response to its own induced mean field? How does that relate to an MFNE?

**Concept: Stationary Distribution Map M(π)**
- **Why needed:** All algorithms must compute or approximate M(π) for policy evaluation. The paper uses an iterative approximation M_N (Section 3). Misunderstanding this map leads to incorrect implementation of the fixed‑point loop.
- **Quick check:** Given a policy π, what does it mean for μ to satisfy μ = P^⊤_μ,π μ? How would you check this in code for a discrete MDP?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Environments (bench_mfg/envs/) -> Algorithms (bench_mfg/algorithms/) -> Evaluation & Logging (bench_mfg/utils/). MF‑Garnet Generator (bench_mfg/generators/) -> Environments. Configuration (Hydra) -> all components.

### Critical Path
1. Initialize a specific MFG environment (e.g., `BeachBarMFG`) with chosen parameters.
2. Select an algorithm (e.g., `FictitiousPlay`) and set its hyperparameters.
3. Run algorithm: it will internally loop—compute best response (using backward induction), compute new mean field via `M_N(π)`, update average policy/mean field.
4. At each iteration or at end, compute exploitability of current average policy using a best‑response oracle.
5. Log exploitability over iterations and final (μ, π) for visualization.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Model‑based vs. Black‑box:** Most solvers (FP, PI, OMD) require explicit knowledge of transition and reward functions to compute best responses via dynamic programming. MF‑PSO treats the environment as a black box, only querying exploitability, which is more flexible but potentially noisier and more sample‑intensive.
- **Computational cost vs. accuracy:** OMD often achieves lowest exploitability but is hyperparameter‑sensitive. Fictitious Play is robust but may converge to suboptimal equilibria in non‑monotone games. FP is cheap but only works for trivial/contractive games.
- **JAX efficiency:** The codebase uses JAX for vectorization and JIT compilation (~2000× speedup). New components must be JAX‑compatible (use `jnp`, avoid Python loops in JIT’d functions) to maintain performance.

### Failure Signatures
- **Algorithm‑specific:** FP fails to converge (exploitability stays high) in non‑contractive games. OMD diverges if learning rate too high. MF‑PSO particles collapse to suboptimal regions if swarm parameters poorly set.
- **Environment‑specific:** DC‑MFG (e.g., SIS) may cause `M_N(π)` to not converge within N steps if dynamics change rapidly with µ. Cyclic games (Rock‑Paper‑Scissor) may cause exploitability to oscillate without converging.
- **Implementation:** If exploitability values are all near zero but policies/mean fields look unreasonable (e.g., deterministic policies in stochastic games), the best‑response computation might be buggy.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Sanity check – NI‑MFG (Move Forward):** Run Fixed Point and Fictitious Play. FP should converge in few iterations to an optimal policy (exploitability ~0). Verify policy moves toward state 6 (highest reward).
2. **Class contrast – LL‑MFG vs. DC‑MFG:** Run OMD on Beach Bar (LL‑MFG) and SIS (DC‑MFG). Observe OMD converges smoothly in Beach Bar but may struggle or require more iterations in SIS due to changing dynamics.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity – OMD learning rate:** On a cyclic game (Rock‑Paper‑Scissor), sweep OMD’s learning rate. Expect a U‑shaped exploitability curve; too high → divergence, too low → slow convergence. Identify a stable regime.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter values for best results are not tabulated, requiring inference from Appendix plots.
- Exact random seeds and parameters for MF‑Garnet instances are omitted, hindering exact replication.
- Key algorithmic details (e.g., number of steps N for M_N, best‑response horizon) are unspecified, affecting accuracy and runtime.

- MF‑PSO outperforms Fictitious Play with linear computational cost: **Medium**
