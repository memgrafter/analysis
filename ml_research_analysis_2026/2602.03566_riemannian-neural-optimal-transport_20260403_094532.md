# Riemannian Neural Optimal Transport

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.03566
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03566
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces Riemannian Neural Optimal Transport (RNOT), a neural method that avoids discretization and achieves scalable optimal transport on Riemannian manifolds up to dimension 40, theoretically and empirically circumventing the curse of dimensionality that plagues discrete-output baselines.

## Executive Summary
Optimal transport (OT) on Riemannian manifolds is fundamental for geometric data analysis but extending neural OT methods beyond Euclidean space remains challenging due to the curse of dimensionality suffered by discretization-based approaches. This paper proposes Riemannian Neural Optimal Transport (RNOT), which parameterizes c-concave potentials using continuous neural networks and enforces the OT constraints via the c-transform, thus avoiding any finite-support discretization of the transport map itself. Theoretically, the authors prove that any method that outputs a discrete transport map necessarily requires an exponential number of parameters in the manifold dimension to achieve fixed accuracy. In contrast, under smoothness assumptions on the optimal potential, RNOT attains sub-exponential (polynomial) parameter complexity. Empirically, RNOT scales stably to manifolds of dimension up to 40 on synthetic spheres and tori, significantly outperforming discretization baselines that degrade sharply with dimension, and also yields competitive results on a low-dimensional real-world task (continental drift on the sphere).

## Method Summary
RNOT learns an amortized optimal transport map between distributions on a compact Riemannian manifold. The method constructs a c-concave potential ψ as a neural network that takes as input a feature embedding φ(x) = (d(x, ℓ₁), ..., d(x, ℓ_M)) of geodesic distances to a fixed set of landmarks. The c-transform ψ^c(x) = min_{y∈M} [½ d(x,y)² – ψ(y)] is evaluated via an inner Riemannian gradient descent loop, warm-started by a softmin over target samples. The transport map is then given by T(x) = exp_x(–∇ψ^c(x)). Training minimizes the semi-dual objective E_{x∼μ}[ψ^c(x)] + E_{y∼ν}[ψ(y)] using stochastic gradients; the inner minimization is differentiated via the envelope theorem (stopping gradient at the argmin). This continuous parameterization avoids discretizing the map’s support and inherently respects the manifold geometry through exponential/logarithm maps. Theoretically, the class of potentials (f∘φ)^c is dense in the set of c-concave functions under mild conditions, and approximation rates are polynomial in network size when the optimal potential is smooth.

## Key Results
- **Theoretical limitation:** Proved that any OT method producing a discrete-output transport map on a compact manifold suffers the curse of dimensionality—the number of parameters grows exponentially with manifold dimension for fixed accuracy.
- **Method contribution:** Introduced RNOT, which uses a continuous neural network to approximate the c-concave potential and implicitly enforces the OT constraints via the c-transform, thereby avoiding discrete-output bottlenecks.
- **Empirical scalability:** RNOT maintains stable performance (forward KL) across dimensions up to 40 on synthetic spheres and tori, while discrete baselines (e.g., RCPM) degrade rapidly; also competitive on a real S² dataset (continental drift).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Discrete-output methods are inherently limited.** For measures absolutely continuous with respect to the Riemannian volume, any transport map with finite support yields a pushforward that is m-atomic. Quantization theory shows that the Wasserstein error scales as ~ C / m^{2/p}. To achieve a fixed accuracy δ, one needs exponentially many atoms m ≥ (C/δ)^p in the manifold dimension p. RNOT avoids this by representing the map continuously, never restricting its support to a finite set.

**Mechanism 2: Continuous neural potentials with implicit c-concavity.** Instead of imposing convexity explicitly, RNOT approximates potentials within the class C(φ*NN) = { (f∘φ)^c : f ∈ NN }, where φ: M → ℝ^M is an injective landmark embedding and NN denotes Euclidean neural networks. Because φ is injective and NN is universal on ℝ^M, this pullback-then-transform class is dense in the set of c-concave potentials. The resulting transport map T(x)=exp_x(-∇(f∘φ)^c(x)) can thus approximate the true OT map arbitrarily well as network capacity increases.

**Mechanism 3: Polynomial approximation under smoothness.** Under the assumption that the optimal prepotential ψ^⋆ = ϕ^⋆ belongs to C^{k p,1}(M), one can first embed M smoothly into a cube [0,1]^{2p}, extend ψ^⋆ to a Sobolev function on the cube, approximate it with ReLU networks with polynomial size/depth in 1/ε, and pull back via the embedding. Stability of the quadratic minimizer then converts uniform approximation error ε into pointwise map error O(√ε) on a full-μ set. This yields sub-exponential parameter complexity, in contrast to the exponential lower bound for discrete-output methods.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Riemannian manifolds, exponential/log map**  
*Why needed:* The OT map is defined intrinsically as T(x) = exp_x(-∇ϕ(x)), requiring geodesics, cut locus, and local invertibility of the exponential map.  
*Quick check:* Explain why ∇_x(½ d(x,y)²) = -log_x(y) for y not in the cut locus of x. What fails at the cut locus? (Answer: The gradient formula relies on the exponential map being a diffeomorphism near x; at the cut locus, exponential map is not locally invertible and the squared distance function is not smooth.)

**Concept: c-concavity and c-transform**  
*Why needed:* Optimal transport potentials on manifolds are characterized as (squared distance) c-concave functions. RNOT’s core idea is to approximate within this class implicitly by applying the c-transform to flexible neural networks.  
*Quick check:* Given a candidate function ψ(y), how do you compute its c-transform ψ^c(x) = min_y [½ d(x,y)² - ψ(y)]? What role does the c-transform play in guaranteeing optimality? (Answer: The c-transform enforces that the resulting potential is c-concave, which is necessary and sufficient for the associated map to be optimal under quadratic cost.)

**Concept: Universal approximation in non-Euclidean spaces**  
*Why needed:* To show neural networks can approximate arbitrary continuous functions on the manifold, we cannot directly apply the Euclidean universal approximation theorem; instead we embed M into ℝ^n via an injective feature map and use a pullback construction.  
*Quick check:* Why is injectivity of the feature map φ: M → ℝ^n necessary for universality of the composed class φ*NN? What happens if φ identifies two distinct manifold points? (Answer: Injectivity ensures that distinct x ∈ M map to distinct inputs in ℝ^n; otherwise the network cannot distinguish them, limiting expressive power.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Landmark embedding φ(x) = (d(x, ℓ₁), ..., d(x, ℓ_M)) → Potential network ψ_θ(φ(x)) (MLP) → Implicit c-transform (inner minimization to compute ψ_θ^c(x) and its gradient) → Transport map T_θ(x) = exp_x(-∇ψ_θ^c(x)) → (Optional) Jacobian for diagnostics.

**Critical path:** For a data point x: compute distances to landmarks φ(x); feed to MLP to get ψ_θ; solve min_y [½ d(x,y)² - ψ_θ(y)] via Riemannian gradient descent to obtain ψ_θ^c(x) and its gradient ∇ψ_θ^c(x); compute T_θ(x) = exp_x(-∇ψ_θ^c(x)). Training uses the semi-dual objective, and gradients flow through ψ_θ but stop at the inner argmin (envelope theorem).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Landmark count M*: Larger M improves injectivity of φ but increases MLP input size; chosen via validation diagnostics.
- *Inner solver steps*: More steps yield a more accurate c-transform and gradient but increase training time per iteration.
- *MLP width/depth*: Controls expressivity of ψ; too small networks underfit. Theorem 5.1 suggests polynomial scaling.
- *LogSumExp temperature γ*: Lower γ approximates the hard min (better transport quality) but may destabilize training; higher γ smooths but introduces bias.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Embedding collapse*: If landmarks are too few or poorly placed, many x share identical φ(x); monitor min pairwise separation on validation set.
- *Diverging/NaN inner loop*: Poor hyperparameters or near-singular objective cause RGD to diverge; track stationarity residual ‖∇_y F_x‖.
- *Slow KL/ESS improvement*: Model may be stuck; check ψ_θ^c(x) values relative to empirical cost.
- *Overfitting on source but poor target match*: May indicate insufficient landmark coverage; consider increasing M.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Synthetic scaling**: Train RNOT on S^p and T^p with uniform source vs. wrapped normal target, sweeping p=2,…,40. Metric: forward KL D_KL(T_#μ||ν). Expect RNOT KL to remain stable across p while baselines like RCPM degrade sharply.
2. **Real data case study**: Continental drift on S²—train transport between paleogeographic continents (150 Ma → present). Evaluate via visualized trajectories and plate purity. Expect coherent large-scale displacements.
3. **Ablation study**: Isolate impact of landmark selection (FPS vs. random), inner initialization (LogSumExp vs. none), inner learning rate, and MLP size. Expect FPS landmarks and LogSumExp init to substantially improve KL and stability, especially on tori.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to broader costs:** Can RNOT be extended to cost functions other than squared Riemannian distance while still attaining polynomial (sub-exponential) parameter complexity? The current theory critically relies on properties of the quadratic cost.
2. **Amortizing the inner minimization:** To reduce training time, can the inner c-transform solve be amortized via a learned network, and does such amortization preserve the theoretical polynomial guarantees? Amortization may introduce bias and alter the optimization landscape.
3. **Statistical learning rates:** What are the sample complexity bounds for RNOT when only finite samples from the source and target are available? The paper provides deterministic approximation theory but no generalization analysis.
4. **Non-compact manifolds:** Can the theory be extended beyond compact Riemannian manifolds (e.g., to Euclidean or hyperbolic spaces, or manifolds with boundary) while retaining polynomial complexity? Compactness is used in multiple technical steps.
5. **Lower regularity potentials:** What approximation rates are achievable when the optimal transport potential has lower regularity than C^{k p,1} (e.g., merely Lipschitz)? The current rates depend heavily on smoothness assumptions.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter specificity:** Exact values for inner-loop steps (K), step size (α), LogSumExp temperature (γ), and landmark count scaling with dimension (p > 10) are not provided, hindering exact reproduction.
- **Manifold generality validation:** Experiments focus on homogeneous spaces (spheres, tori); performance on arbitrary compact manifolds with variable curvature or boundaries is not demonstrated, and landmark injectivity may fail on complex shapes.
- **Cut-locus sensitivity:** Theoretical results assume the optimal potential is smooth away from a cut locus of measure zero, but numerically the inner minimization and gradient may become unstable near singularities in high dimensions.

## Confidence
- **High:** Discrete-output methods suffer exponential parameter growth (Theorem 3.1); the continuous parameterization via c-concave potentials is theoretically sound (Theorem 4.1).
- **Medium:** Sub-exponential parameter complexity under smoothness assumptions (Theorem 5.1); empirical scalability up to 40 dimensions on synthetic manifolds; competitive performance on real S² data.
- **Low:** Precise hyperparameter defaults; extension to non-homogeneous manifolds; handling of cut-locus singularities; extension to other cost functions; amortization of inner loop; statistical learning rates; non-compact settings; approximation rates for low-regularity potentials.

## Next Checks
1. **Landmark injectivity stress test:** For dimensions p=20–40, compute the fraction of source points with non-unique landmark distance vectors under both random and farthest-point sampling (FPS) selection. Expect FPS to keep collision rate below 0.1% while random selection's rate rises sharply.
2. **Inner minimization accuracy vs. KL degradation:** On S^p (p=20), ablate the number of inner RGD steps (e.g., K=10, 50, 200) and record both the average stationarity residual ‖∇_y F_x‖ and the final forward KL. Hypothesis: KL plateaus only once residual drops below ~1e-3; beyond that, additional steps yield diminishing returns.
3. **Cross-manifold generalization probe:** Train RNOT on T³ (3-torus) and zero-shot test on S³ with the same dimension. Measure the relative increase in forward KL. Significant degradation (e.g., >2×) would indicate reliance on manifold-specific structure (e.g., product geometry) rather than purely metric properties.
