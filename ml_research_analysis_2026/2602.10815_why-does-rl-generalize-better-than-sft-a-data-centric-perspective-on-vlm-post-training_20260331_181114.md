# Why Does RL Generalize Better Than SFT? A Data-Centric Perspective on VLM Post-Training

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10815
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10815
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: DC-SFT (SFT-EM) improves average OOD accuracy by up to 4.48% over SFT and 2.62% over GRPO, with 3.2–4.9× better compute efficiency and greater stability.

## Executive Summary
Vision-language models (VLMs) post-trained with reinforcement learning (RL) often exhibit superior out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization compared to supervised fine-tuning (SFT). This paper investigates this gap, hypothesizing that RL’s advantage arises from an implicit data filtering mechanism: RL’s group-relative advantage calculation yields meaningful updates only for medium-difficulty samples, while easy and hard samples contribute negligible gradients. In contrast, SFT updates uniformly on all data, and the inclusion of even a small fraction of hard samples (e.g., 5%) dramatically harms OOD performance. The authors validate this by training SFT on curated difficulty subsets and demonstrate that removing hard samples yields robust generalization. Building on this, they propose Difficulty-Curated SFT (DC-SFT), which explicitly filters out hard examples before training. DC-SFT (SFT-EM variant) matches or exceeds RL’s OOD accuracy across multiple benchmarks, while being 3.2–4.9× more computationally efficient and exhibiting far greater training stability. These findings suggest that data difficulty curation, rather than RL-specific exploration dynamics, is a key driver of OOD robustness in VLM post-training.

## Method Summary
The study compares standard SFT, GRPO (RL), and two DC-SFT variants (SFT-M: medium-only; SFT-EM: easy+medium) on Qwen2.5-VL (3B/7B) and MiniCPM-V-4 across classification, grounding, and reasoning tasks. For each training prompt, the model generates eight responses (temperature=0.9, top-p=1.0) and evaluates correctness (exact label or IoU≥0.5) to classify samples as easy, medium, or hard. Training uses LoRA (rank 32/64) or full parameters for 600 steps with AdamW (lr=1e-5). SFT employs standard cross-entropy on curated subsets; GRPO uses group-relative advantages with KL penalty (β=0.04). Evaluation measures both in-distribution (ID) and OOD accuracy. The results consistently show that filtering out hard samples yields the best OOD generalization, surpassing both full SFT and GRPO, while reducing computational cost and avoiding RL’s instability.

## Key Results
- DC-SFT (SFT-EM) improves average OOD accuracy by up to 4.48% over standard SFT and 2.62% over GRPO on Qwen2.5-VL-7B.
- It matches or exceeds RL’s OOD performance while being 3.2–4.9× more computationally efficient and exhibiting greater training stability.
- Training SFT on hard subsets causes severe OOD degradation (e.g., −14.07% on ImageNet-R), whereas medium-difficulty data supports robust generalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: RL’s Implicit Data Filtering
- **Claim:** RL’s generalization advantage stems from an implicit data filtering mechanism that inherently prioritizes medium-difficulty training samples during policy optimization.
- **Mechanism:** In RL (e.g., GRPO), the advantage estimate \(A_k\) for a response is computed from normalized group rewards. When all responses for a prompt are either entirely correct (easy) or entirely incorrect (hard), the reward variance across the group is zero, leading to \(A_k \approx 0\) and negligible parameter updates. Only prompts yielding a mix of correct/incorrect responses (medium difficulty) generate meaningful advantage signals and non-zero gradients.
- **Core assumption:** Reward variance is a reliable proxy for sample difficulty and that updates driven by medium-variance samples foster OOD robustness more than uniform updates on all data.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] States RL’s advantage “arises from an implicit data filtering mechanism that inherently prioritizes medium-difficulty training samples.” [section 4.1] Defines the difficulty taxonomy and explains how RL’s advantage calculation yields near-zero updates for easy/hard instances.
- **Break condition:** If reward variance is artificially decoupled from difficulty (e.g., a hard sample receives high reward due to a flawed reward function), the implicit filter may mis-prioritize, degrading generalization.

### Mechanism 2: Hard Samples Harm SFT’s OOD Generalization
- **Claim:** The degradation of SFT’s OOD performance is causally linked to the inclusion of a small fraction of hard samples in the training distribution.
- **Mechanism:** Hard samples induce disproportionately large gradient norms during SFT (Figure 6), dominating the optimization trajectory. This causes the model to overfit to noisy, ambiguous, or dataset-specific patterns in these hard examples, which harms robustness on shifted distributions. The effect is non-linear: even a small proportion (5%) of hard samples significantly damages OOD accuracy (Figure 5).
- **Core assumption:** Gradient magnitude is a leading indicator of overfitting risk; large per-step updates from hard samples destabilize learning of general features.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.3] Table 1 shows hard-subset training improves ID accuracy but causes severe OOD drops (e.g., −14.07% on ImageNet-R for Qwen2.5-VL-7B). [section 6.1] Figure 5 quantifies the damage from incremental hard-sample inclusion; Figure 6 demonstrates consistently larger gradient norms for hard subsets.
- **Break condition:** If hard samples are re-labeled or their difficulty redefined (e.g., via a better teacher model), the negative impact may be mitigated without removal.

### Mechanism 3: DC-SFT Outperforms RL via Explicit Filtering
- **Claim:** Explicitly filtering out hard samples before SFT (DC-SFT) yields OOD generalization that matches or exceeds RL, because it removes the primary source of SFT’s robustness deficit while avoiding RL’s instability and computational cost.
- **Mechanism:** DC-SFT variants (SFT-M: medium-only; SFT-EM: easy+medium) construct a training distribution free of hard examples. SFT-M replicates RL’s implicit medium-focus; SFT-EM retains easy samples which preserve OOD stability. Without hard-sample interference, standard SFT optimization converges to a more robust basin.
- **Core assumption:** Easy samples are benign or beneficial for OOD performance, and the primary harm in full-dataset SFT comes from hard samples, not from the absence of RL’s exploration dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] DC-SFT “explicitly filters out hard data prior to training” and “surpasses the performance of RL-based training.” [section 5.2] Tables 2–5 show SFT-EM and SFT-M achieving higher average OOD scores than GRPO across models, datasets, and training scales. [section 5.3 & 5.4] DC-SFT exhibits greater training stability and 3.2–4.9× speedup versus GRPO.
- **Break condition:** If a task or model architecture is highly sensitive to easy-sample underfitting (e.g., requires memorization of frequent patterns), SFT-EM’s removal of easy samples might harm ID performance without OOD benefit, though paper results show ID remains strong.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: In-Distribution (ID) vs Out-of-Distribution (OOD) performance**
  - *Why needed:* The paper’s core hypothesis revolves around the OOD generalization gap. Understanding that ID measures fit on training-like data, while OOD tests robustness to distribution shift (e.g., ImageNet-R’s artistic renditions), is essential to interpret all results.
  - *Quick check question:* What makes Ref-L4 a valid OOD benchmark for RefCOCO-trained models? (Answer: it uses referring expressions with linguistic/visual properties disjoint from COCO.)

- **Concept: Difficulty taxonomy based on model-generated response correctness**
  - *Why needed:* The entire mechanism hinges on categorizing samples as easy/medium/hard via multiple model rollouts. This operationalization of "difficulty" is model-centric, not human-annotated.
  - *Quick check question:* If for a prompt, 6/8 rollouts are correct and 2/8 are wrong, how is it classified? (Answer: medium-difficulty.)

- **Concept: Reinforcement Learning with Group-Relative Advantages (GRPO)**
  - *Why needed:* GRPO is the RL algorithm studied. Its group-normalized advantage calculation is what creates the implicit filter. Understanding that rewards are normalized within a group of responses per prompt explains why uniform rewards yield zero advantage.
  - *Quick check question:* In GRPO, if all G responses for a prompt receive the same reward, what is the normalized advantage \(A_k\) for each? (Answer: approximately zero, after adding small δ.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Base VLM (Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B, MiniCPM-V-4) → Data Filtering Pipeline (G=8 rollouts, correctness evaluation) → Training Heads (SFT, GRPO) → Evaluation Suite (ID/OOD benchmarks).

**Critical path:**
1. Initialize model on a 400-sample random subset (1 epoch).
2. For each training prompt, generate G=8 responses (temp=0.9, top-p=1.0) and evaluate correctness using task-specific metrics (exact label for classification, IoU≥0.5 for grounding).
3. Classify each instance as easy, medium, or hard; subsample each difficulty to balance sizes; select the subset for the training paradigm (e.g., easy+medium for SFT-EM).
4. Train the model on the selected subset for 600 steps with AdamW (lr=1e-5), batch size 16 (SFT) or 128 (GRPO). LoRA (rank 32/64) or full fine-tuning may be used.
5. Evaluate on ID and OOD test sets using greedy decoding (temperature=0).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Filtering strictness: SFT-M (medium-only) may sacrifice some ID performance compared to SFT-EM (easy+medium), but OOD advantage remains.
- Compute vs stability: GRPO requires per-step generation of multiple responses, making it 3–5× slower and prone to sudden performance drops; DC-SFT offers stability and speed.
- Generalization vs memorization: Removing hard samples reduces overfitting to noisy patterns, but may discard informative failure cases; the non-linear damage from hard samples (Fig 5) justifies removal.

**Failure signatures:**
- OOD performance collapses when ≥5% hard samples are included in the training mix (Figure 5).
- GRPO training curves exhibit sharp declines (e.g., 300–400 steps on ImageNet-R, 100–200 on Ref-L4) indicating instability.
- SFT on hard-only subsets yields high ID accuracy but catastrophic OOD degradation (Table 1).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replicate difficulty-split SFT: Train models on easy, medium, and hard subsets from ImageNet; verify that hard subset improves ID but harms OOD, medium yields balanced results, and easy is stable but suboptimal ID.
2. Hard-sample mixing ablation: Start from SFT-EM and incrementally add 5%, 10%, and 100% hard samples; measure OOD accuracy decay to confirm non-linear harm.
3. Gradient norm analysis: During SFT on each difficulty subset, log per-step gradient norms; confirm that hard samples consistently produce larger norms (as in Figure 6).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Does the effectiveness of DC-SFT generalize to VLM architectures beyond Qwen2.5-VL and MiniCPM-V-4, particularly larger models with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters? The authors note their validation is restricted to models up to 7B parameters.
- **Open Question 2:** What is the precise causal mechanism linking hard samples to OOD degradation in SFT, and can alternative training strategies (e.g., gradient regularization) mitigate this without explicit data filtering? The paper correlates hard samples with larger gradient norms but does not establish causation or explore non-filtering remedies.
- **Open Question 3:** Is the difficulty taxonomy (based on multiple response correctness) universally applicable across diverse VLM tasks, such as video QA or 3D scene understanding, where the notion of difficulty may differ? The paper defines difficulty for classification and grounding but does not validate on other modalities.
- **Open Question 4:** Can DC-SFT be integrated with test-time scaling methods (e.g., extended chain-of-thought generation) to achieve synergistic improvements in reasoning on complex benchmarks? The paper suggests DC-SFT aids reasoning but leaves unexplored interactions with inference-time computation.

## Limitations
- Reward variance as a difficulty proxy may fail if the reward function is mis-specified or if variance stems from noise rather than semantic ambiguity.
- Generalizability to other VLM architectures (especially larger or different families) is uncertain given the limited model set.
- The findings are limited to classification, grounding, and reasoning tasks; extension to generative or text-only LLMs remains unclear.
