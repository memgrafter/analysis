# Can Vision-Language Models See Squares? Text-Recognition Mediates Spatial Reasoning Across Three Model Families

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.15950  
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15950  
- **Reference count:** 4  
- **Primary result:** VLMs achieve ~83–84% F1 when grids are rendered as text symbols (., #) but performance collapses to ~29–39% F1 when rendered as filled squares, revealing a heavy reliance on an internal text-recognition pathway for precise spatial localization.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates spatial reasoning in vision-language models (VLMs) using a binary grid transcription task. Across three frontier VLMs (Claude Opus, ChatGPT 5.2, Gemini 3 Thinking), the authors observe a dramatic performance gap: models excel when grids are rendered as text characters but fail when the same information is presented as filled squares. A symbol-type ablation shows intermediate performance with Unicode squares, and embedding numeric labels inside squares either improves or further degrades performance depending on the model. The findings indicate that current VLMs rely predominantly on an internal text-recognition (OCR-like) pathway to achieve high-fidelity spatial reasoning, while their native visual spatial capabilities are severely limited.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates three proprietary VLMs on a zero-shot transcription task. Fifteen 15×15 binary grids (filled cell counts 24–94, densities 10.7%–41.8%) are rendered as PNG images under four conditions: ASCII symbols (., #), unsegmented filled squares, Unicode squares (□, ■), and text-in-squares (black/white squares with embedded “1”/“0”). Each model receives a fixed prompt to transcribe the grid as exactly 15 rows of 15 characters, without using code or external processing. Images are shown in batches of five per condition. The models’ outputs are parsed into 15×15 character grids and compared to ground truth on a cell-by-cell basis. Primary metrics are cell accuracy and black-cell F1, aggregated across all 3375 cells (15 grids × 225).

## Key Results
- **Text vs. squares gap:** All three models achieve ~83–84% F1 with ASCII symbols, but performance drops to ~29–39% F1 with filled squares—a gap of 34–54 points.
- **Graded effect:** Unicode squares yield intermediate F1 (69–77%), suggesting token frequency and visual similarity to text mediate the advantage.
- **Model-specific interactions:** Embedding numeric labels inside squares improves Claude (86%) and Gemini (100% on sparse grids) but degrades ChatGPT (51.1%), indicating destructive interference between visual and text pathways in some architectures.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper proposes three interconnected mechanisms explaining the reliance on text recognition and the observed performance patterns.

**Mechanism 1: Dominance of text-recognition pathway**  
When visual elements are rendered as high-frequency text characters (., #), the visual encoder’s output activates the model’s strong character recognition and sequential processing modules, which natively preserve spatial order (left-to-right, top-to-bottom) with high fidelity. In contrast, filled squares do not trigger this pathway; the model must rely on its visual-feature pathway, which is optimized for global semantic alignment rather than fine-grained spatial localization, leading to severe degradation. *Evidence:* consistent 34–54 point F1 drop across all models; systematic spatial mislocalizations in qualitative analysis.

**Mechanism 2: Graded advantage mediated by token frequency and visual similarity**  
The text-recognition pathway’s fidelity depends on token frequency in the training corpus. ASCII symbols like . and # are extremely frequent, yielding robust performance. Unicode squares (□, ■) are rarer and visually less text-like, resulting in intermediate performance (69–77% F1). The continuum from ASCII to Unicode to pure squares shows a smooth degradation, supporting a frequency-dependent effect. *Evidence:* performance drop from ASCII to Unicode (~10–15 F1 points); discussion in §5.1 and §5.4.

**Mechanism 3: Model-specific pathway interference**  
Introducing textual labels inside squares provides anchors for the text pathway, but for ChatGPT this degrades performance below the plain squares baseline. This suggests that for some architectures, the simultaneous activation of visual (processing large black regions) and text (processing overlaid digits) pathways creates interference or confusion within the decoder’s attention mechanisms, leading to worse spatial localization. *Evidence:* ChatGPT’s F1 in text-in-squares (51.1%) is lower than its filled-squares F1 (39.2% on comparable subset); Claude/Gemini improve; Gemini’s shift toward code generation indicates a strategy change.

## Foundational Learning
Key concepts underlying the paper’s interpretation:

- **CLIP-style contrastive pre-training:** Visual encoders trained with image-caption contrastive losses optimize for global image-text alignment rather than fine-grained spatial precision. This explains why the “visual-feature pathway” is weak at pixel-accurate tasks like binary grid transcription. *Quick check:* If an encoder is trained via contrastive loss, does it retain precise pixel coordinates or coarse scene layouts? Why does this matter for grid transcription?

- **Token frequency effects in language models:** Language models assign higher probability to frequent tokens; likewise, VLMs’ text-recognition pathway is more robust for high-frequency characters (., #) than for rare Unicode symbols (□, ■), even if both are valid text. *Quick check:* Why might a VLM recognize “A” more reliably than the obscure Unicode character “𠮷”? How does this relate to the symbol ablation results?

- **Multimodal pathway separation/interference:** A VLM decoder processes projected visual tokens and autoregressively generates text. The hypothesis is two emergent pathways: one that interprets visual tokens as characters (text-recognition) and another that treats them as abstract shapes (visual-feature). These pathways may not be independent; concurrent activation can cause interference. *Quick check:* In text-in-squares, the model sees both large black regions and small digits. Could attention layers experience ambiguity if visual features suggest “solid block” while text features suggest “digit”?

## Architecture Onboarding
Understanding the VLM pipeline helps interpret the observed behaviors.

**Component map:**  
Input -> Visual Encoder -> Projection/Multimodal Fusion -> LLM Decoder -> Output

**Critical path:**  
The divergence between text and squares conditions arises inside the LLM decoder. The encoder and projection produce similar latent representations for both conditions (same pixels). However, the decoder’s interpretation of the visual token sequence differs: if the tokens strongly activate the model’s character/sequence priors (as with ASCII), the text-recognition pathway yields accurate transcription; if not (as with squares), it must rely on the weaker visual-feature pathway.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Text-pathway robustness vs. Visual-pathway capacity:* Claude and ChatGPT appear optimized for a robust text pathway (stable across densities) at the expense of a weaker visual pathway (poor on squares). Gemini shows a stronger visual pathway (best on sparse squares) but a more brittle text pathway (density-dependent collapse). These tradeoffs likely stem from architectural choices (e.g., OCR data in training, visual encoder resolution, tokenization of visual features).  
- *Pathway separation vs. interference:* ChatGPT’s degradation with text-in-squares suggests its architecture does not cleanly separate the two streams, leading to destructive interference when both are active.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Claude:* Systematic undercounting; predicts fewer filled cells than exist, indicating the visual pathway detects approximate clusters but cannot resolve individual boundaries and the text pathway is not engaged.  
- *ChatGPT:* Massive overcounting, elongated rows/columns, blurred clusters; suggests a “blurring” effect from the visual pathway combined with the text pathway generating plausible but incorrect sequences under ambiguity.  
- *Gemini:* Template hallucination (e.g., L-shapes, crosses, ASCII-art letters) at moderate/high densities; indicates capacity ceiling in the text pathway, causing a fallback to learned generative patterns.

**First 3 experiments to reproduce the paper:**  
1. Replicate the core text-vs-squares gap: Render 5 representative grids as ASCII and as filled squares; run all three models; expect a ~30–50 point F1 difference.  
2. Symbol-type ablation: For 3 grids (low, medium, high density), test all four conditions (ASCII, Unicode squares, text-in-squares, pure squares); document the graded performance and model-specific interactions, especially ChatGPT’s degradation.  
3. Density dependence probe: Run all 15 grids in the squares condition; plot F1 versus density. Expect Claude/ChatGPT to show flat, low performance, while Gemini should show high performance at low densities (<20%) with a sharp collapse above 20%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Capacity limit of text-recognition:** What is the maximum grid size or density the text-pathway can handle before collapsing? Only 15×15 grids were tested; scaling behavior is unknown.  
2. **Open-source VLM replication:** Do open-source models (LLaVA, Qwen-VL) show the same gap? Their known architectures could correlate behavior with visual encoder design.  
3. **Output format effect:** Does requiring coordinate list output (instead of grid transcription) mitigate the squares failure? This would help isolate perception vs. generation bottlenecks.  
4. **Why ChatGPT degrades with text-in-squares:** What causes the destructive interference? Is it visual clutter, attention conflict, or an idiosyncrasy of instruction tuning?

## Limitations
- Exact reproduction is hindered by missing details: precise grid matrices, rendering parameters (image size, font metrics, anti-aliasing), prompt wording, and model versions. Web interfaces may also preprocess images.  
- Causal claims about dual pathways are inferential; no internal state probing (e.g., attention maps, neuron ablation) was performed to verify the proposed mechanism.  
- Stimuli are highly controlled binary grids; real-world spatial reasoning (e.g., photos of objects, maps) may exhibit different patterns, and the “break condition” for the mechanism remains untested.

## Next Checks
1. **Text-overlap probe:** Render filled squares with embedded random letters (e.g., “X” in filled, “O” in empty) instead of “1”/“0”. If the text-recognition pathway is essential, performance should collapse even when text is present but semantically meaningless.  
2. **Orientation invariance test:** Rotate grids (90°, 180°) in both text and squares conditions. The text pathway may handle rotation via learned sequential priors; the visual pathway may fail on rotated natural images. Measure whether the gap persists and whether errors become systematic (e.g., transposed outputs).  
3. **Synthetic pre-training ablation:** Fine-tune a small open-source VLM (e.g., LLaVA) on a large dataset of binary grids rendered only as filled squares until it masters the task. Then test on the original text-condition grids. If fine-tuning yields high accuracy on squares but not on text, the deficit is not fundamental but stems from default training objectives. Conversely, if fine-tuning fails, it supports the claim of a weak native visual pathway.
