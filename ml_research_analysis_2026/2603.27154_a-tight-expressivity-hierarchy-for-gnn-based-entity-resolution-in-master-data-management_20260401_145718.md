# A Tight Expressivity Hierarchy for GNN-Based Entity Resolution in Master Data Management

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27154  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27154  
- **Reference count:** 33  
- **Primary result:** The paper determines the minimal GNN architecture for Dup_r and Cyc_ℓ on typed entity-attribute graphs, showing that Dup₁ requires only reverse message passing (2 layers), Dup_r (r≥2) requires reverse MP plus ego IDs (4 layers), Cyc_ℓ requires ego IDs (ℓ layers), and multigraphs need port numbering.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the representational power of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for entity resolution (ER) tasks within Master Data Management. Focusing on typed entity-attribute graphs, it studies two fundamental predicates: Dup_r (two same-type entities share at least r attributes) and Cyc_ℓ (participation in an ℓ-cycle). Using graph separation techniques, the authors prove tight bounds on the minimal GNN architecture—specifically, which combinations of three adaptations (reverse message passing, ego IDs, incoming port numbering) and how many layers—are necessary and sufficient to compute these predicates. For Dup₁, only reverse message passing is needed (2 layers). For Dup_r with r≥2, both reverse message passing and ego IDs are required (4 layers), revealing a strict expressivity gap. On multigraphs, incoming port numbering must also be added. For cycle detection, ego IDs alone suffice on canonical graph pairs with an optimal depth of ℓ. All sufficiency constructions are explicit, and necessity holds for arbitrary depth and width; experiments on the separating graphs confirm the theory.

## Method Summary
The authors employ a separation framework: they construct specific pairs of non‑isomorphic graphs that differ on the target predicate but are indistinguishable by GNNs lacking certain adaptations. By carefully designing the graphs, they isolate the contribution of each adaptation. Theoretical necessity is shown by proving that any GNN without the required adaptation produces identical embeddings for the two graphs, regardless of depth or width. Sufficiency is demonstrated by constructing explicit GNNs (with MLP updates and appropriate aggregators) that correctly distinguish the graphs using the minimal adaptation set. The analysis builds on the 1‑Weisfeiler‑Lehman (1‑WL) expressivity hierarchy and adapts known results about the limitations of message‑passing. Computational experiments implement the separating graphs and the proposed architectures, validating the theoretical predictions across random initializations.

## Key Results
- **Dup₁:** Computable with reverse message passing only; 2 layers suffice and are necessary.
- **Dupᵣ (r≥2):** Requires both reverse message passing and ego IDs; 4 layers are necessary and sufficient; without ego IDs, no GNN can distinguish the separating graphs.
- **Cyc_ℓ:** Ego IDs alone are necessary and sufficient on the canonical separation pair (2C_ℓ vs C_{2ℓ}) with optimal depth ℓ; for general typed directed multigraphs, the full set (reverse MP, port numbering, ego IDs) is sufficient.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Reverse message passing for local shared‑attribute detection
- **Claim:** Reverse message passing (2 layers) is necessary and sufficient for Dup₁ on simple typed entity‑attribute graphs.
- **Mechanism:** Layer 1 (forward): entities send their type and edge type to attributes. Attributes aggregate the multiset of incoming (entity type, edge type) pairs, which encodes the fan‑in profile. Layer 2 (reverse): attributes send their aggregated embedding back to entities. Entities check whether any of their attributes have at least two predecessors of the same entity type via the same edge type.
- **Core assumption:** Graph is simple (no parallel edges) and bipartite with all edges directed from entities to attributes.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  • [abstract] “Detecting whether an entity shares any attribute value … requires only reverse message passing in two layers.”  
  • [Section 5.1] Theorem 3 proves necessity and sufficiency, with the sufficiency construction explicitly using two layers of forward‑then‑reverse aggregation.  
  • [corpus] Neighboring work on 1‑WL expressivity notes that standard MPNNs cannot capture fan‑in information without reverse aggregation; this mechanism directly addresses that limitation.
- **Break condition:** Without reverse message passing, entities have no incoming edges, so their embeddings are independent of graph structure (Theorem 3 necessity). On multigraphs, raw message multiplicities become ambiguous (Theorem 5).

### Mechanism 2: Ego IDs for cross‑attribute identity correlation
- **Claim:** For Dupᵣ with r ≥ 2, ego IDs are necessary (even with reverse MP and full port numbering) and together with reverse MP suffice at depth 4 on simple graphs.
- **Mechanism:** The ego node *u* is marked at initialization.  
  Layer 1 (forward): attributes learn which edge types connect *u* to them (EgoTypes).  
  Layer 2 (reverse): each entity *v* computes ov(*v*) by summing, over its outgoing edges, whether the ego also connects to that attribute via the same edge type.  
  Layer 3 (forward): attributes compute maxOv(*a*) – the maximum ov among non‑ego same‑type predecessors.  
  Layer 4 (reverse): *u* takes the max of its maxOv values and thresholds at *r*.
- **Core assumption:** Simple typed entity‑attribute graphs; detecting ≥ *r* shared attributes requires verifying that the same other entity appears at multiple attributes of *u*.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  • [abstract] “Detecting whether it shares at least r attribute values … demands … ego IDs … a fundamentally non‑local requirement.”  
  • [Section 5.3] Theorem 8 proves necessity by constructing graphs where all non‑ego entities fall into two port‑numbering classes that remain indistinguishable without ego IDs; sufficiency construction implements the 4‑layer algorithm.  
  • [corpus] Ego IDs were introduced as a symmetry‑breaking resource; this paper shows they are provably required for multi‑attribute correlation.
- **Break condition:** Without ego IDs, the separation graphs induce two classes of non‑ego entities with identical embeddings at every depth, making ov(*v*) uncomputable for any entity *v* ≠ *u*. Depth < 4 is insufficient because the 4‑hop information flow (ego→attributes→others→attributes→ego) cannot be compressed.

### Mechanism 3: Forward propagation of ego ID for cycle detection
- **Claim:** On the canonical separation pair (2C_ℓ vs C_{2ℓ}), ego IDs alone (forward‑only MP, depth ℓ) suffice for Cyc_ℓ; reverse MP and port numbering are unnecessary.
- **Mechanism:** Mark ego node *u*. Over ℓ forward aggregation steps, the ego flag propagates along directed edges. In a directed ℓ‑cycle, after ℓ steps the signal returns to *u* via an incoming edge, so *u*’s embedding indicates a closed walk of length ℓ. In a 2ℓ‑cycle, the signal reaches the antipodal node, not back to *u*.
- **Core assumption:** The graphs are functional (each node has in‑ and out‑degree 1); for such graphs, a closed walk of length ℓ exists iff the node lies on a cycle whose length divides ℓ.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  • [abstract] “Ego IDs are necessary and sufficient on the canonical separation instances, with optimal depth ℓ.”  
  • [Section 5.4] Theorem 12 gives the forward‑only construction and proves necessity of ego IDs for the cycle detection task on those graphs.  
  • [corpus] Higher‑order WL tests (e.g., r‑loopy WL) can count cycles; this mechanism shows that on functional graphs, a first‑order forward pass with ego IDs already captures ℓ‑cycles.
- **Break condition:** On general directed graphs, forward‑only with ego IDs may detect non‑simple closed walks, leading to false positives. Universal Cyc_ℓ computation on all typed directed multigraphs requires the full triple of adaptations (Egressy et al.).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Entity nodes (type + ego flag) → Forward message (entity→attribute) → Attribute aggregation (sum) → Reverse message (attribute→entity) → Entity aggregation (sum) → (repeat per required depth) → Ego node embedding → Threshold → Prediction

**Critical path (for Dupᵣ, r≥2, simple graphs):**  
1. Initialize all nodes with type; set ego flag for target entity *u*.  
2. Layer 1 (forward): attributes compute EgoTypes by summing gated messages from ego.  
3. Layer 2 (reverse): each entity *v* computes ov(*v*) by summing, over its outgoing edges, whether the ego also connects to that attribute via the same edge type.  
4. Layer 3 (forward): attributes compute maxOv(*a*) – the maximum ov among non‑ego same‑type predecessors.  
5. Layer 4 (reverse): ego *u* takes the max of its maxOv values and applies threshold *r*.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Depth:** Increasing depth beyond minimal does not improve expressivity for these tasks; worse for efficiency.  
- **Ego IDs:** Required for Dupᵣ (r≥2) and cycle detection; cost multiplies per‑layer complexity by O(|V|).  
- **Port numbering:** Only needed for multigraphs to count distinct sources; adds edge feature dimension.  
- **Aggregation choice:** Sum aggregation is essential for exact counting; max aggregation suffices for thresholding at readout.  
- **Width:** Must accommodate counts up to maximum degree; proofs assume exact arithmetic.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **No reverse MP:** Entity embeddings become constant across graph structures; Dup₁ fails.  
- **No ego IDs for Dup₂+:** On separation graphs, all non‑ego entities in each port‑numbering class share identical embeddings; ov(*v*) uncomputable.  
- **No ports on multigraphs:** Parallel edges from a single entity inflate raw multiplicities, causing false positives for Dup₁.  
- **Forward‑only for cycles on non‑functional graphs:** May report closed walks as cycles; need reverse/ports for general graphs.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Necessity validation:** For each theorem, implement the insufficient architecture (e.g., no ego IDs for Dup₂) and run 100 random initializations on the separation graphs; confirm ℓ₂ distance between target node embeddings in G₁ and G₂ is exactly zero.  
2. **Sufficiency validation:** Implement the explicit construction from the proof (e.g., the 4‑layer Dup₂ MPNN) and verify that on the separation graphs the ego node’s output matches the ground‑truth label (1 on G₁, 0 on G₂).  
3. **Generalization stress test:** Generate random simple typed entity‑attribute graphs with 50–200 entities and planted duplicate pairs (ensuring a second entity shares exactly *r* attributes). Evaluate the minimal architecture (e.g., 4‑layer with ego) and compare against a variant without ego IDs to observe the gap beyond worst‑case.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** What is the minimal combination of architectural adaptations (reverse message passing, port numbering, ego IDs) required to compute the ℓ‑cycle participation predicate Cycℓ on all typed directed multigraphs? In particular, are reverse message passing and port numbering each independently necessary?  
**Basis:** The authors state in Section 7: “Determining the minimal adaptation set for universal Cycℓ computation — in particular, whether reverse message passing and port numbering are each independently necessary for Cycℓ on all typed directed multigraphs — is left to future work.”  
**Why unresolved:** Current results only show that ego IDs are necessary on a specific separation pair (2Cℓ vs C2ℓ) and that the full triple of adaptations suffices for universal pattern detection. The independent necessity of reverse and port numbering across all graphs remains unknown.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Construct a separation pair where omitting reverse message passing or port numbering causes failure, thereby proving necessity; or provide a general construction that computes Cycℓ without one of those adaptations, proving sufficiency.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How do finite‑precision arithmetic and non‑injective approximate aggregations (e.g., mean, max) affect the separation thresholds proved for Dupr tasks? Do the necessity results still hold for realistic GNN implementations?  
**Basis:** Section 7 lists: “First, our sufficiency constructions assume exact injective aggregation; understanding the impact of finite‑precision arithmetic and learned approximate aggregation functions on the separation thresholds is an important practical question.”  
**Why unresolved:** The proofs rely on the theoretical ability of sum aggregation to encode arbitrary multisets injectively (via [24, Lemma 5]). Approximate aggregations and finite precision may fail to distinguish subtle multiset differences, potentially weakening the hierarchy.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Analyse perturbation stability of the separation graphs under bounded numeric noise; or empirically test whether indistinguishability persists when using standard 32‑bit floats and alternative aggregators.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can the expressivity hierarchy for Dupr be extended to soft attribute matching (e.g., embeddings) and higher‑order co‑reference patterns such as Kn,r with n≥3? What minimal adaptations do these variants require?  
**Basis:** In Section 7 the authors propose: “Third, we have focused on the binary co‑reference predicate Dupr; extending the separation theory to soft matching scores, approximate attribute similarity under feature perturbations, and higher‑order co‑reference patterns such as Kn,r detection for n≥3 would further bridge the gap between theoretical expressivity and practical entity resolution pipelines.”  
**Why unresolved:** Real ER systems operate on noisy string similarity or learned embeddings, and many matching scenarios involve more than two entities (e.g., cluster linkage). The current binary Dupr analysis may not directly scale to these settings.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Define formal soft‑Dupr and Kn,r predicates within the typed entity‑attribute graph model; develop separation proofs analogous to Theorems 3–8; or design new MPNN architectures and prove their optimality for these tasks.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Does the worst‑case necessity of ego IDs for Dupr (r≥2) persist on average over random typed entity‑attribute graphs, or can weaker MPNNs (without ego IDs) achieve good performance by exploiting statistical patterns that correlate with duplicates?  
**Basis:** Section 6 notes: “Preliminary training experiments on random TEAGs with 60 entities suggest that the gap is present but less stark: weaker architectures (without ego IDs) can partially succeed by exploiting statistical correlates of the planted duplicates… A systematic empirical investigation of this average‑case behaviour, including the design of random graph distributions that neutralise such proxies, is left to future work.”  
**Why unresolved:** The separation proofs establish existence of pathological graphs; however, real‑world data generative processes may make such graphs extremely unlikely. It is unknown whether the necessity of ego IDs is distribution‑dependent or an absolute barrier even under average‑case evaluation.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct large‑scale simulations across a variety of random TEAG ensembles, measuring accuracy of MPNNs with and without ego IDs on Dupr (r≥2). If the performance gap endures even after controlling for degree/structural proxies, worst‑case necessity is reinforced; if the gap vanishes on natural distributions, the barrier may be avoidable in practice.

## Limitations
- Theoretical proofs assume exact arithmetic and MLPs with infinite capacity; real GNNs use finite precision and bounded width.
- Experimental validation is restricted to tiny synthetic separating graphs; no assessment on large‑scale or noisy real‑world MDM datasets.
- Cycle detection results apply only to functional graphs (in/out-degree 1); extension to arbitrary directed graphs is not covered.

## Confidence
- **Dup1 expressivity (reverse MP only, 2 layers):** High
- **Dup_r (r≥2) expressivity (requires ego IDs, 4 layers):** High
- **Multigraphs require incoming port numbering:** Medium
- **Cycle detection on canonical graphs (ego IDs sufficient, depth ℓ):** High
- **Generalization beyond synthetic graphs:** Low

## Next Checks
1. **MLP robustness:** Vary MLP architecture (depth 1–3, activations ReLU/GELU, initialization scale) in necessity experiments; zero ℓ₂ distance should hold across all reasonable designs.
2. **Random graph generalization:** Generate random typed entity‑attribute graphs (50–200 entities) with planted duplicate pairs (r=1,2,3). Evaluate the minimal architecture (e.g., 4‑layer with ego) and ablations; expect significant accuracy drop without ego for r≥2.
3. **Numerical precision:** Run key experiments in float32 and float64; ℓ₂ distances must be exactly zero in float64 and ≤1e‑6 in float32. Larger deviations would indicate hidden numerical instability.
