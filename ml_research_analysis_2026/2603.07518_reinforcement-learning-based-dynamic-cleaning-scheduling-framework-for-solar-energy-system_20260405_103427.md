# Reinforcement learning-based dynamic cleaning scheduling framework for solar energy system

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07518  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07518  
- **Reference count:** 39  
- **Primary result:** PPO-based dynamic cleaning scheduling achieves up to 13% cost savings versus optimized fixed intervals in Abu Dhabi.

## Executive Summary
Dust accumulation on solar photovoltaic (PV) panels in arid regions significantly reduces energy output. Traditional fixed-interval cleaning schedules are inefficient because they cannot adapt to variable weather that influences soiling rates. This paper presents a reinforcement learning (RL) framework using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) to dynamically schedule cleaning based on real-time soiling and weather uncertainty. The simulation environment incorporates a humidity-adjusted dust accumulation model. In a 20‑year case study of Abu Dhabi, PPO outperformed both SAC and a simulation‑optimization (Sim‑Opt) baseline, delivering up to 13% cost savings. PPO showed stable training and superior performance, while SAC suffered from instability. The results confirm that dynamic RL scheduling surpasses fixed‑interval methods under stochastic conditions.

## Method Summary
The problem is framed as a Markov Decision Process. The state includes deposition (soiling level), days since last cleaning, temperature, wind speed, particulate matter, and irradiance. The action is binary (clean or not). The reward is the negative total daily cost, comprising a fixed cleaning cost and the energy revenue loss from soiling. Environment dynamics are simulated with a dust accumulation model that adjusts the base rate by a factor `f(RH)=k/RH` (k=0.06) when the base rate is negative, capturing humidity’s effect on particle adhesion; panel efficiency follows a degradation‑adjusted cubic relationship. Daily weather is sampled from Abu Dhabi’s monthly fitted distributions. PPO and SAC are trained by interacting with this simulator; their policies are benchmarked against a Sim‑Opt approach that searches for the optimal fixed cleaning interval via stochastic simulation.

## Key Results
- PPO achieved up to 13% cost savings compared to the Sim‑Opt optimal fixed interval across multiple cases.
- PPO demonstrated stable convergence during training, whereas SAC exhibited instability and failed to outperform Sim‑Opt.
- Dynamic RL‑based scheduling consistently outperformed fixed‑interval methods, confirming its superiority under weather uncertainty.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Humidity‑adjusted soiling model**  
The dust accumulation rate is modulated by relative humidity through a factor `f(RH)=k/RH` (k=0.06) when the base rate is negative. This reflects that higher humidity increases particle adhesiveness, reducing wind‑driven natural cleaning. Evidence: The paper explicitly defines this equation and cites prior work that neglects RH. Break condition: if the calibrated `Dsoiling'` becomes negative, the model reverts to the uncalibrated rate, and the specific coefficient `k` is empirically derived for Abu Dhabi, limiting transferability.

**Mechanism 2: PPO’s clipped objective ensures stable learning**  
PPO uses a clipped surrogate objective to restrict policy updates, preventing large, destabilizing steps. This stability is crucial in the high‑variance, long‑horizon solar cleaning problem where rewards are sparse (total cost over 20 years). Evidence: Training curves show PPO’s steady improvement vs. SAC’s fluctuations; PPO ultimately achieves higher rewards. Break condition: if the state representation does not capture all relevant history (e.g., long‑term weather patterns), the learned policy may become suboptimal, as hinted by some testing performance drops.

**Mechanism 3: Adaptive margin‑based cleaning triggers**  
The RL policy effectively learns to clean only when the marginal cost of additional soiling exceeds the fixed cleaning cost, as inferred from the value function. This avoids unnecessary cleans during low‑soiling periods (e.g., after rain/wind) and allows more frequent cleans when soiling accelerates. Evidence: Cost savings up to 13% over Sim‑Opt; decision analysis shows PPO’s actions correlate with deposition and days since last cleaning. Break condition: model misspecification (weather distributions, soiling physics) could cause the policy to underperform relative to a locally optimized fixed interval in new regions.

## Foundational Learning

**Markov Decision Process (MDP)**  
Formalizes sequential decision‑making under uncertainty. The solar cleaning problem is modeled as an MDP with states (soiling level, days since cleaning, weather), actions (clean/no‑clean), transition probabilities (weather‑driven dynamics), and reward (negative total cost).  
*Why needed:* Provides the mathematical foundation for applying RL to optimize long‑horizon cumulative rewards despite stochastic weather.  
*Quick check:* Can you identify the state, action, reward, and transition dynamics? What assumptions are made about state observability?

**Policy Gradient Methods (e.g., PPO)**  
Optimize a parameterized policy directly by gradient ascent on expected return. PPO adds a clipping mechanism to the surrogate objective, limiting policy updates to a trust region.  
*Why needed:* Standard policy gradients can diverge in high‑variance, sparse‑reward settings; PPO’s clipping promotes stable training for this long‑horizon problem.  
*Quick check:* Why might a vanilla policy gradient diverge here, and how does PPO’s clipping mitigate that?

**Simulation‑Optimization (Sim‑Opt)**  
Uses a simulation model to evaluate candidate solutions and selects the best via optimization. Here, Sim‑Opt searches over fixed cleaning intervals, running 30 simulation replications per interval to estimate average cost.  
*Why needed:* Serves as a critical baseline to demonstrate the advantage of dynamic RL policies over the best static schedule.  
*Quick check:* What are the limitations of Sim‑Opt as a benchmark for a dynamic policy?

5. Implement PPO and SAC agents with specified network architectures and hyperparameters.

7. Test the best‑trained policies over 30 stochastic episodes.  
8. Compare average total costs against the Sim‑Opt baseline.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Algorithm choice:** PPO selected for stability; SAC suffered instability likely due to sparse rewards and replay buffer mismatch.
- **State complexity:** Six state variables are used; analysis suggests fewer (deposition and days since cleaning) might suffice, reducing computational load.
- **Simulator fidelity:** Monthly weather distributions balance realism and training speed; a more granular (daily) or physics‑based model could improve accuracy but increase computation.

Failure signatures:
- **PPO testing performance drop:** Overfitting to training weather seeds; symptom: higher cost in test than training.
- **SAC divergence:** Exploding or vanishing Q‑values and reward variance spikes during training.
- **Simulator mismatch:** If the soiling model (Eqs 1–4) poorly matches real‑world data, any policy (RL or Sim‑Opt) will be suboptimal, leading to unrealistic cost estimates.

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate the simulation environment: implement the soiling model with RH calibration (`k=0.06`, `β=0.01`) and the efficiency model. Use Abu Dhabi monthly distributions to generate weather and verify that single runs show plausible soiling/efficiency trends.  
2. Reproduce the Sim‑Opt baseline: for the S1exp case, enumerate fixed intervals *z*, run 30 replications each, and confirm the optimal interval (~19 days) and U‑shaped cost curve (as in Fig. 4a).  
3. Train PPO and SAC: implement both agents with the paper’s hyperparameters, train for ≥1000 episodes, and replicate Fig. 5 to verify PPO’s stable convergence and SAC’s instability.

## Limitations
- State vector composition and normalization are not fully described, hindering faithful replication.
- Training protocol details (number of episodes, update frequency, batch size) are missing, requiring heuristics.
- The humidity calibration factor `k=0.06` is empirically derived for Abu Dhabi; its transferability to other climates is untested.
