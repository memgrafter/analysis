# Safe Reinforcement Learning with Preference-based Constraint Inference

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.23565
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23565
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: PbCRL introduces a dead zone mechanism and SNR loss to infer heavy-tailed safety constraints from preferences, achieving superior constraint alignment and performance over standard BT-based Safe RL methods.

## Executive Summary
Safe reinforcement learning requires specifying safety constraints, which are often implicit and difficult to define. This paper proposes Preference-based Constraint Inference (PbCRL) to learn constraints from human pairwise preferences and binary safety labels. The method overcomes limitations of standard Bradley-Terry models that yield symmetric cost distributions and underestimate heavy-tailed risks. Key innovations include a dead zone mechanism to induce heavy-tailed cost distributions, a Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) loss to amplify cost variance for stronger policy gradients, and a two-stage training strategy that reduces online labeling effort. Experiments on Safety Gymnasium tasks show PbCRL achieving constraint distributions much closer to the true safety costs (lower 2-Wasserstein distance) and performance comparable to an oracle PPO-Lag agent, surpassing baselines like RLSF and PPO-BT.

## Method Summary
PbCRL consists of three main components. First, the cost model is extended with a dead zone parameter δ added to the estimated cumulative cost for unsafe trajectories in the Bradley-Terry likelihood. This pushes unsafe cost estimates away from the decision boundary, creating a heavier right tail and aligning with true cascading failure risks. Second, an SNR loss term is added to the cost model training to maximize the ratio of cost variance to label entropy, encouraging a spread of cost values that enlarges the policy gradient bound. Third, a two-stage training regime is employed: an offline pre-training phase uses a static preference dataset to initialize the cost model, followed by online finetuning where a small batch of recent trajectories is periodically labeled and used to update both the cost model and the dead zone δ via violation rate matching. The policy itself is optimized using PPO with a Lagrange multiplier, leveraging the learned cost function.

## Key Results
- PbCRL achieves significantly lower 2‑Wasserstein distance to the true cost distribution than PPO‑BT and RLSF, indicating better constraint alignment.
- The final policies satisfy safety constraints (true cost ≤ threshold) while attaining high returns, nearly matching the oracle PPO‑Lag performance.
- Ablations confirm the dead zone mechanism reduces heavy‑tail bias and the SNR loss improves sample efficiency, with specific hyperparameters (δ≈1, ζ=10⁻³) performing best.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Dead Zone for Heavy-Tailed Costs
- **Claim**: The dead zone mechanism induces heavy‑tailed cost distributions, aligning learned constraints with true safety risks.
- **Mechanism**: A fixed offset δ is added to the estimated cumulative cost for unsafe trajectories in the BT likelihood. This pushes cost estimates of unsafe trajectories further from the decision boundary, creating a heavier right tail in the overall cost distribution.
- **Core assumption**: True safety costs are heavy‑tailed due to cascading failures; standard BT models yield symmetric distributions that underestimate tail risks.
- **Evidence**: Theoretical results (Lemma 3.1, Theorem 3.2, Corollary 3.3) show the dead zone encourages stochastic dominance of the learned cost over the BT estimate and shifts the distribution rightward.
- **Break condition**: δ too small fails to correct symmetry; δ too large over‑corrects, causing over‑conservative policies and inflated costs.

### Mechanism 2: SNR Loss for Cost Variance
- **Claim**: The SNR loss increases cost variance, providing stronger policy gradient signals and improving sample efficiency.
- **Mechanism**: The loss maximizes the ratio of the variance of estimated trajectory costs (signal) to the entropy of preference labels (noise). This encourages the model to produce a wider spread of cost values, which enlarges the bound on the policy gradient norm via Cauchy‑Schwarz.
- **Core assumption**: A flattened cost landscape yields uninformative gradients; higher cost variance directly amplifies the gradient magnitude.
- **Evidence**: Derivation in Section 4 links variance to gradient bound (Eq. 37). Ablations (Table 9) show mid‑range ζ (10⁻³) balances variance and stability.
- **Break condition**: Excessive ζ destabilizes training; too low yields flat gradients and slow convergence.

### Mechanism 3: Two‑Stage Training for Low Labeling Burden
- **Claim**: Offline pre‑training combined with adaptive online finetuning reduces labeling burden while maintaining constraint alignment.
- **Mechanism**: The cost model is pre‑trained on a static preference dataset D. During policy training, a small batch of recent trajectories is periodically sampled, labeled, and used to finetune the cost model. The dead zone δ is adjusted via violation‑rate matching to align predictions with the empirical rate of unsafe trajectories.
- **Core assumption**: Offline data provides a reasonable prior; online distribution shift can be corrected by calibrating δ based on violation rates, avoiding dense online labeling.
- **Evidence**: Algorithm 1 details the procedure; ablations (Table 11) show finetuning improves alignment over pure offline training.
- **Break condition**: Highly biased offline data may not be fully corrected; too small online batches yield noisy δ updates.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bradley‑Terry Model**: Standard probabilistic model for pairwise preferences; converts cumulative cost differences into preference probabilities via a logistic function. *Why needed*: PbCRL extends this core model with the dead zone to handle asymmetric safety costs. *Quick check*: "How does the standard BT model convert cumulative cost differences into preference probabilities, and why does it focus on ranking rather than absolute values?"  
- **Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP)**: Formalization of safe RL with a constraint on expected cumulative cost. *Why needed*: The problem is cast as a CMDP where the cost function is unknown and must be inferred. *Quick check*: "In a CMDP, what does the safety constraint typically bound, and why is the expectation form both important and challenging for learned constraints?"  
- **Heavy‑tailed vs. Symmetric Distributions**: Heavy‑tailed distributions have tails that decay slower than exponential, leading to higher probability of extreme values. *Why needed*: True safety costs from cascading failures are hypothesized to be heavy‑tailed, whereas BT yields roughly symmetric (e.g., Gaussian‑like) estimates, causing underestimation of rare but severe risks. *Quick check*: "What visual or statistical property distinguishes a heavy‑tailed distribution from a symmetric one, especially regarding tail probability?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (plain text):  
Offline dataset D --pre‑train--> cost model c_ψ; c_ψ with dead zone δ outputs trajectory costs; policy π_θ interacts with environment to collect trajectories; a small batch of recent trajectories → online buffer B → (human labeling) → updates D and δ; updated D and δ used to finetune c_ψ; c_ψ provides cost estimates for PPO‑Lag update (with Lagrange λ) that improves π_θ and value critics V^R, V^C.  

**Critical path**:  
1. Pre‑train c_ψ on D using PbCI loss (pairwise + dead‑zone safety + SNR) with fixed δ.  
2. Policy loop: collect trajectories with π_θ.  
3. Every K steps: sample online batch, label, add to D; update δ via violation‑rate matching (Eq. 14); finetune c_ψ on combined D.  
4. Perform PPO‑Lag update on θ using new c_ψ, update V^R, V^C, and λ.  

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- δ: Too small → underestimation (symmetry persists); too large → overestimation (over‑conservative). Calibrated via violation rate during online phase.  
- SNR ζ: Too low → flat gradients; too high → noisy signals, instability.  
- Finetune interval K: Larger → fewer labels but slower adaptation; smaller → more labels needed, better alignment.  

**Failure signatures**:  
- High 2‑Wasserstein distance between learned and true cost distributions.  
- True cumulative cost consistently exceeds threshold in evaluation.  
- Policy return low while constraint violated (poor reward + unsafe).  
- Cost model loss spikes or diverges during SNR weighting.  

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Replicate main results on Safety Gymnasium (Goal, Push, Button): verify PbCRL matches PPO‑Lag in return/cost and attains lower W2 than PPO‑BT and RLSF.  
2. Dead zone ablation: sweep δ (e.g., 0, 0.1, 1, 2); expect δ≈1 to minimize W2 distance and bias.  
3. SNR weight ablation: sweep ζ (e.g., 0, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻³, 10⁻²); identify ζ that balances variance and stability, monitor training curves.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Data‑efficient constraint learning from preferences**: How to learn constraints reliably with minimal human feedback? Existing methods need extensive demonstrations or strong assumptions. Evidence of resolution would be methods that use far fewer preference labels across domains.
2. **Impact of BT models on downstream policy learning**: Why do standard BT models fail to capture heavy‑tailed risks and lead to underestimation? Prior work focuses on ranking accuracy, not distribution effects. Evidence requires controlled experiments comparing BT‑derived costs to ground truth in heavy‑tailed settings, measuring violation rates.
3. **Robustness to noisy preferences**: Real human feedback is often inconsistent. The current framework assumes clean labels. A robust variant should degrade gracefully with increasing synthetic noise.
4. **Quantitative relationship between δ and tail heaviness**: The theory shows the dead zone induces a heavier tail but does not quantify the tail index. Deriving bounds as a function of δ and validating via simulations would calibrate δ for specific risk profiles.
5. **Non‑stationary safety requirements**: The two‑stage strategy assumes fixed distributions. In deployment, constraints or dynamics may change. Evidence would involve continual learning extensions that periodically ingest new preferences and maintain alignment under drift.

## Limitations
- Incomplete reporting of offline dataset construction (size, annotator simulation) and key hyperparameters (PPO clip, GAE, finetune interval K, δ learning rate) hinders faithful reproduction.
- The heavy‑tailed cost assumption—central to the method—is not empirically verified on the benchmark tasks; true cost distributions may be light‑tailed.
- Preference data is simulated via a cost‑based Bradley‑Terry model, which may not capture the noise and subjectivity of real human judgments.
