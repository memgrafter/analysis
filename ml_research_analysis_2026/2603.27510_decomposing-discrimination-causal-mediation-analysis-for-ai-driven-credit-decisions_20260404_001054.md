# Decomposing Discrimination: Causal Mediation Analysis for AI-Driven Credit Decisions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27510
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27510
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** The paper finds that about 77% (6.1 percentage points) of the 7.9 percentage-point racial gap in mortgage denial rates operates indirectly through financial mediators (e.g., DTI, credit scores), while the remaining 23% (1.9 pp) is a conservative lower bound on direct discrimination, with an E-value of 1.68.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the conflation of two distinct discrimination mechanisms in AI credit decisions: direct bias from protected attributes and indirect bias via financial features shaped by structural inequality. It introduces a causal mediation framework using interventional direct and indirect effects (IDE/IIE) identified under a weaker Modified Sequential Ignorability assumption, bypassing the violation of standard sequential ignorability due to treatment‑induced confounding. Applied to 89,465 real HMDA mortgage applications, the decomposition reveals that the majority of the racial denial gap (77%) is mediated through financial pathways, and the remaining direct effect constitutes a conservative lower bound—a distinction missed by SHAP‑based attribution. The method employs a doubly‑robust Augmented Inverse Probability Weighted (AIPW) estimator with cross‑fitting to ensure √n‑consistency and semiparametric efficiency, and it provides an E‑value to assess sensitivity to unmeasured confounding.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a causal mediation approach to decompose racial disparity in mortgage denials into interventional direct and indirect effects. The core innovation lies in using interventional effects, where the mediator is drawn from the population distribution conditional on treatment, rather than the natural counterfactual mediator. This identification strategy requires only Modified Sequential Ignorability (no unmeasured confounding of A→Y and A→M after conditioning on pre‑treatment covariates W), which is weaker than standard sequential ignorability because it tolerates a common cause of M and Y (U). The interventional effects are estimated via a doubly‑robust AIPW estimator that combines an outcome regression, a propensity score, and a mediator density ratio; the estimator is consistent if either the outcome model or both the propensity and density ratio models are correctly specified, and it attains √n‑consistency through cross‑fitting. Applied to New York State HMDA data, the method yields point estimates of IDE (a conservative lower bound on direct discrimination) and IIE (the effect through financial features), along with confidence intervals and an E‑value quantifying robustness to confounding.

## Key Results
- Total racial denial gap: 7.9 percentage points.
- Interventional indirect effect (IIE): 6.1 pp (77% of gap); interventional direct effect (IDE): 1.9 pp (23% of gap), a conservative lower bound on direct discrimination (E‑value = 1.68).
- SHAP attribution to race underestimates direct bias (0.8 pp vs. IDE 1.9 pp), highlighting the value of causal decomposition.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Interventional Direct/Indirect Effects (IDE/IIE) are identified from observational credit data despite treatment‑induced confounding (unmeasured U affecting both mediators M and outcome Y).
- **Mechanism:** Replace the natural effects’ counterfactual mediator $M(a)$ with an interventional draw $M^* \sim G_a = P(M|A=a, W)$. This severs the backdoor path $A \leftarrow U \rightarrow M \leftarrow A$ that invalidates sequential ignorability for natural effects.
- **Core assumption:** Modified Sequential Ignorability (MSI.1 & MSI.2): $Y(a,m) \perp A \mid W$ and $M(a) \perp A \mid W$. This requires no unmeasured confounding of $A\rightarrow Y$ and $A\rightarrow M$ after conditioning on $W$, but permits $M\leftarrow U\rightarrow Y$.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We show that interventional direct and indirect effects (IDE/IIE) are identified under the weaker Modified Sequential Ignorability assumption... bypassing the violation of standard sequential ignorability due to treatment‑induced confounding."
  - [section 3.4] Formal identification proof for IDE/IIE under MSI.1 and MSI.2 (Proposition 3.2).
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers on causal mediation (e.g., "Disentangled Representation for Causal Mediation Analysis") focus on different identification challenges; this paper’s specific MSI assumption for treatment‑induced confounding is not directly mirrored in the corpus, indicating a novel application.
- **Break condition:** MSI.2 fails if unmeasured confounders affect both $A$ and $M$ after conditioning on $W$ (e.g., if self‑selection into loan application is driven by unobserved factors correlated with race and DTI).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A doubly‑robust Augmented Inverse Probability Weighted (AIPW) estimator provides √n‑consistent, semiparametrically efficient estimates of IDE/IIE even with machine‑learned nuisance models.
- **Mechanism:** Construct an influence function that combines an outcome regression ($\mu(a,m,w)$) and a mediator density ratio ($r(m,w) = f(m|A=1,w)/f(m|A=0,w)$). The estimator is consistent if either the outcome model **or** both the propensity score and mediator ratio models are consistent.
- **Core assumption:** (i) Correct specification of at least one nuisance model path; (ii) Nuisance models converge fast enough ($o(n^{-1/4})$) to ensure the product of errors is $o(n^{-1/2})$ (Theorem 4.1).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We propose a doubly‑robust augmented inverse probability weighted (AIPW) estimator for IDE/IIE with semiparametric efficiency properties, implemented via cross‑fitting."
  - [section 4.1] Derivation of the EIF for IDE (Equation 8) and statement of double robustness and √n‑consistency (Theorem 4.1).
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Causal Mediation Analysis with Multiple Mediators" discuss similar AIPW structures but not specifically for treatment‑induced confounding; the cross‑fitting implementation here adapts standard semiparametric theory to the IDE context.
- **Break condition:** Both nuisance paths fail (e.g., misspecified outcome model **and** poor estimation of $r(m,w)$ due to extreme imbalance or limited overlap in $M|A,W$).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Under monotone indirect treatment response (higher $M$ weakly increases $Y$), the identified IDE provides a conservative lower bound on the unidentifiable natural direct effect (NDE), mapping onto legal “disparate treatment.”
- **Mechanism:** Structural inequality causes $M(1) \geq_{st} M(0)$ for protected group applicants. Monotonicity ($Y(a,m)$ non‑decreasing in $m$) implies $E[Y(1, M(0))] \geq E[Y(1, M^*)]$ where $M^* \sim G_0$. Thus $NDE \geq IDE$.
- **Core assumption:** (i) Monotone indirect treatment response: For all units, higher financial distress (e.g., DTI) never decreases denial probability. (ii) The empirical stochastic dominance $M(1) \geq_{st} M(0)$ holds in the population.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We prove that IDE/IIE provide conservative bounds on the unidentified natural effects under monotone indirect treatment response."
  - [section 3.5] Formal proof of $IDE \leq NDE$ and $IIE \geq NIE$ under Assumption 3.2 (Proposition 3.3).
  - [section 5.2] Descriptive statistics show Black applicants have systematically higher DTI, LTV, and lower income/credit scores, supporting $M(1) \geq_{st} M(0)$.
  - [corpus] The monotonicity assumption is common in bounds analysis but its application to credit decisions (e.g., DTI always increases denial risk) is a domain‑specific justification not tested in neighboring papers.
- **Break condition:** Monotonicity fails for subgroups (e.g., high income/asset applicants where higher DTI may not increase denial risk if compensating factors exist), weakening the bound’s conservativeness.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Causal Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) & d‑separation**  
  *Why needed here:* The credit DAG (Figure 1) encodes assumptions about temporal ordering, confounding (U), and mediators (M). Identification proofs (3.3, 3.4) rely on d‑separation to state violations of SI and satisfaction of MSI.  
  *Quick check question:* In the credit DAG, why does the path $A \rightarrow M \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y$ block under MSI but not under SI? (Answer: SI requires $M(a) \perp Y(a,m) \mid A,W$, which is blocked by $U$; MSI only requires $M(a) \perp A \mid W$, which is already true if $U$ is unmeasured and $A$ has no parents.)

- **Concept: Interventional vs. Natural Effects**  
  *Why needed here:* The core innovation is using interventional effects (IDE/IIE) where mediators are drawn from population distributions $G_a$, not individualized counterfactuals $M(a)$. This changes the identification requirements.  
  *Quick check question:* What is the practical difference between $NIE = E[Y(1, M(1)) - Y(1, M(0))]$ and $IIE = E_{M\sim G_1}[Y(1,M)] - E_{M\sim G_0}[Y(1,M)]$? (Answer: NIE compares outcomes for the *same* unit under different mediator values; IIE compares *population‑averaged* outcomes under different mediator *distributions*.)

- **Concept: Doubly‑Robust Estimation & Cross‑Fitting**  
  *Why needed here:* The AIPW estimator (Section 4.1) achieves √n‑consistency even if one nuisance model is inconsistently specified, crucial for real‑world data where no single model is perfect. Cross‑fitting prevents overfitting in the second‑order term.  
  *Quick check question:* If the outcome regression $\mu(a,m,w)$ is misspecified but the mediator density ratio $r(m,w)$ is correct, is the AIPW estimator consistent for IDE? (Answer: Yes, by the double robustness property; the augmentation term corrects the bias from the plug‑in term.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map:
- `causal_fair.dag`: Encodes the credit DAG structure, defines $W$ (pre‑treatment), $A$ (protected), $M$ (mediators), $Y$ (outcome). Includes constraints (e.g., no edges from $Y$ to predecessors).
- `causal_fair.estimate`: Implements the cross‑fitted AIPW pipeline (Algorithm 4.1). Requires:
  - `fit_nuisance_models()`: Trains $\mu$ (causal forest), $\pi$ (propensity score), $r$ (density ratio classifier) on out‑of‑fold data.
  - `compute_psi()`: Calculates influence function components per observation.
  - `aggregate()`: Averages $\psi$ to get $\hat{IDE}/\hat{IIE}$, computes $\hat{\sigma}^2$ for CIs.
- `causal_fair.sensitivity`: Computes E‑value (Proposition 4.1) from the IDE risk ratio and plots the confounding bias curve.

### Critical path:
1. **Data preprocessing & DAG validation:** Ensure temporal ordering (e.g., census tract variables in $W$ truly pre‑date application). Validate that $U$ paths exist (e.g., via domain knowledge of structural inequality).
2. **Nuisance model training with cross‑fitting:** Split data into $K$ folds; train nuisance models on $K-1$ folds, compute $\psi$ on hold‑out fold. Monitor convergence and overlap (e.g., check $\pi_a(w)$ is bounded away from 0/1).
3. **Point estimation & inference:** Aggregate $\psi$ across folds; compute Wald CIs and E‑value. If CIs include zero or E‑value is low (<1.5), robustness is weak.

### Design tradeoffs:
- **Model complexity vs. interpretability:** Causal forests for $\mu$ capture heterogeneity but are less interpretable than logistic regression. The paper uses logistic regression in the real HMDA analysis for speed/explainability at resource‑constrained institutions, trading off some efficiency.
- **Bias‑variance in $r(m,w)$:** The density ratio is estimated via a classifier (ratio of odds). This is robust but may have high variance if $M$ is high‑dimensional. Consider dimension reduction (e.g., PCA on $M$) or more flexible classifiers.
- **Sample imbalance:** With extreme imbalance (e.g., 12:1 white:Black in HMDA), the AIPW variance may inflate. Use stratified cross‑fitting to ensure each fold has sufficient minority applicants.

### Failure signatures:
- **MSI.2 violation detected:** Extreme values in $r(m,w)$ (e.g., density ratio >100 or <0.01) suggest poor overlap in $M|A,W$, implying unmeasured $A\rightarrow M$ confounding.
- **Double robustness fails:** Both outcome model residuals ($Y - \mu$) and density ratio errors are large, leading to high‑variance $\psi$. Check by comparing $\mu$ from a simple GLM vs. causal forest.
- **Monotonicity suspect:** Path‑specific IIE signs are counterintuitive (e.g., higher credit score *increases* denial IIE). Re‑examine mediator coding or structural assumptions.

### First 3 experiments:
1. **Identification assumption check:** Regress $A$ on $W$ and $M,W$ separately. Compute variance inflation factors for $W$ coefficients. If adding $M$ drastically changes $A\sim W$ coefficients, $M\leftarrow U\rightarrow A$ may exist, threatening MSI.2.
2. **SHAP vs. Causal decomposition:** Train a gradient‑boosted model on full features. Compute SHAP values for $A$ and $M$. Compare SHAP’s $A$ contribution to $\hat{IDE}$. Expect SHAP($A$) < $\hat{IDE}$ because SHAP conditions on $M$ (Figure 4).
3. **Sensitivity sweep:** Vary the assumed strength of an unmeasured $A\rightarrow Y$ confounder (via $\lambda$ factor) and recompute the bias‑adjusted IDE. Plot the curve (like Figure 5). If IDE crosses zero at $\lambda < 2$, results are fragile.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do the IDE/IIE decomposition results generalize to other lending contexts (e.g., auto loans, credit cards, small business) and to other protected attributes (e.g., Hispanic, female) beyond the Black‑white binary?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work states: "extend this analysis to the full national HMDA dataset across multiple states and years, and to continuous protected attributes."
- **Why unresolved:** The current analysis is restricted to New York State conventional purchase mortgages and Black vs white applicants only. External validity remains untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies using the full national HMDA dataset over multiple years; application of the same causal decomposition to other credit products (auto, credit card, small business) and to multiple demographic groups; systematic comparison of IDE/IIE patterns across contexts.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can we develop methods for heterogeneous IDE/IIE estimation that identify direct and indirect effects at the subgroup level (e.g., by income, geography) to enable targeted remediation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion mentions: "heterogeneous IDE/IIE estimation by applicant subgroup to enable targeted remediation" as future work.
- **Why unresolved:** The presented estimation yields population‑average effects. However, effective intervention requires understanding which subpopulations drive the indirect effect (e.g., DTI disparities concentrated in low‑income zip codes). Without subgroup estimates, remediation may be misdirected.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical extension of the double‑robust estimator to incorporate effect modifiers; simulation showing power to detect heterogeneity; empirical application showing subgroup‑specific decompositions; evaluation of whether subgroup patterns align with structural inequality hypotheses.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does the monotone indirect treatment response assumption (Assumption 3.2) drive the conservative bound IDE ≤ NDE, and can we obtain tighter bounds or identification under weaker assumptions?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Proposition 3.3 relies on monotonicity to establish IDE as a lower bound on NDE. The monotonicity assumption is stated as a limitation: "while plausible on average... may fail for specific applicant subgroups." The paper does not explore alternatives.
- **Why unresolved:** The validity of the bound hinges on monotonicity. If violated, IDE may not be a lower bound for NDE, undermining the conservative interpretation. Weaker assumptions that still yield informative bounds are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis of the monotonicity requirement under different DAG structures; development of partial identification results without monotonicity; sensitivity analysis quantifying how bound properties change with degree of monotonicity violation; empirical evaluation comparing bounds with and without monotonicity using subject‑matter knowledge.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How do measurement errors in financial mediators (e.g., imputed credit scores, self‑reported income) bias IDE/IIE estimates, and how can such biases be corrected?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The limitations section states: "Credit score quintile is imputed from denial reason codes—an approach that introduces classical measurement error in M, likely biasing $\hat{IIE}$ toward zero." No correction is applied.
- **Why unresolved:** Measurement error in mediators systematically attenuates indirect effect estimates (IIE) relative to true effects. Without correction, the decomposition could understate the role of structural inequality. The magnitude and direction of IDE bias are unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formal bias analysis quantifying the attenuation factor under classical or Berkson error models for M; simulation studies under realistic measurement error scenarios; development of bias‑corrected estimators (e.g., using validation data); empirical application where validation data (actual credit scores for a subsample) exists.

## Limitations
- Undocumented HMDA preprocessing steps (e.g., credit score imputation) may alter sample composition and bias estimates.
- Modified Sequential Ignorability assumptions (especially no unmeasured $A\rightarrow M$ confounding) are untestable and may be violated by structural factors like geography.
- Sample imbalance (12:1 white:Black) leads to wide confidence intervals, reducing precision and potentially inflating variance.
