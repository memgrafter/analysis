# ReliabilityBench: Evaluating LLM Agent Reliability Under Production-Like Stress Conditions
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06112
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06112
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** Gemini 2.0 Flash matches GPT-4o reliability (-0.6% difference) at 82× lower cost.

## Executive Summary
LLM agent benchmarks typically measure single-run success rates, which overestimate reliability in production. ReliabilityBench introduces a three-dimensional evaluation framework that measures consistency (passᵏ), robustness to natural language perturbations (ε), and fault tolerance (λ) via chaos-engineered fault injection. Across 1,280 episodes, semantic perturbations reduce success by 8.8% (from 96.9% to 88.1% at ε=0.2). Rate limiting faults cause the largest single degradation (2.5% below baseline). Simpler ReAct agents achieve 2.5% higher reliability surface volume than Reflexion under stress. Notably, Gemini 2.0 Flash attains reliability comparable to GPT-4o with only a 0.6% difference at 82 times lower cost, demonstrating that cost-efficient models can meet production readiness when evaluated under realistic stress.

## Method Summary
ReliabilityBench evaluates agents on tasks from four domains (scheduling, travel, customer support, e-commerce) using a state-based verification oracle. The framework introduces three stress dimensions: (1) consistency measured by passᵏ (probability all k trials succeed), (2) robustness via Action Metamorphic Relations that perturb task descriptions while preserving required end-states, and (3) fault tolerance by injecting chaos-engineered faults (timeouts, rate limits, partial responses, schema drift) at tool call boundaries with configurable probability λ. Agents are executed multiple times per configuration, and a reliability surface R(k, ε, λ) aggregates performance. The study compares ReAct and Reflexion architectures and multiple LLM backends, reporting pass rates, surface volumes, and cost metrics.

## Key Results
- Semantic perturbations (ε=0.2) reduce success by 8.8% (96.9% → 88.1%).
- Rate limiting faults alone cause the largest single degradation, lowering pass@2 by 2.5% relative to the mixed-fault baseline.
- Gemini 2.0 Flash matches GPT-4o reliability (-0.6% difference) at 82× lower cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Consistency via passᵏ
- **Claim:** Single-run success rate (pass@1) systematically overestimates production reliability because it ignores execution consistency (passᵏ).
- **Mechanism:** Stochastic variance in LLM sampling and environmental interactions makes agent behavior non-deterministic. passᵏ measures the probability that all k trials succeed, revealing the likelihood of complete failure over repeated attempts.
- **Core assumption:** Agent execution is non-deterministic due to model sampling and external state interactions; reliability requires consistent success across trials.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes that agents with 60% pass@1 may drop to 25% consistency; Section 3.2.1 formally defines passᵏ; neighboring work (MAS-FIRE) also addresses fault injection but not specifically passᵏ degradation.
- **Break condition:** If agents become fully deterministic (e.g., greedy decoding with no API non-determinism), passᵏ ≈ (pass@1)ᵏ and the consistency dimension adds little new information.

### Mechanism 2: Robustness via Action Metamorphic Relations
- **Claim:** Metamorphic testing via Action Metamorphic Relations (AMR) reveals brittleness to natural language variation that standard benchmarks miss, because correctness is defined by end-state equivalence rather than text similarity.
- **Mechanism:** By perturbing task descriptions with synonyms, reordering, or distractors while preserving the required final state, the benchmark tests whether the agent’s goal-oriented reasoning is robust or merely matching surface patterns.
- **Core assumption:** Agent success should be invariant under semantically equivalent task descriptions; failure indicates overfitting to training templates or insufficient planning.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions AMR and end-state equivalence; Section 3.4 formally defines the metamorphic condition ψ(S_f, S'_f) for perturbed description ϕ(d); neighboring papers discuss robustness but not end-state metamorphic testing explicitly.
- **Break condition:** If agents use perfect semantic parsing and state transition models invariant to linguistic variation, perturbations cause zero degradation (unlikely for current LLM-based agents).

### Mechanism 3: Fault Tolerance via Chaos Engineering
- **Claim:** Systematic chaos-engineered fault injection uncovers fault tolerance gaps because production infrastructure failures (timeouts, rate limits, schema drift) are absent from standard benchmarks.
- **Mechanism:** By injecting configurable faults (transient, partial, corrupt) at tool call boundaries, the benchmark measures an agent’s recovery logic and resilience, simulating real-world SRE stressors.
- **Core assumption:** Production deployments experience intermittent infrastructure failures; an agent’s ability to detect, retry, or compensate determines operational reliability.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract describes chaos-engineering-style fault injection; Section 3.5 defines fault profiles Λ(λ) and Section 4.5 details the injection algorithm; neighboring work (e.g., "Assessing and Enhancing Robustness...Through Chaos Engineering", "MAS-FIRE") confirms the approach’s relevance.
- **Break condition:** If agents are deployed in perfectly reliable environments (no network failures, stable APIs), fault tolerance metrics become irrelevant (atypical for web-scale systems).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** passᵏ consistency metric (from τ‑bench)
  - **Why needed here:** The entire reliability surface hinges on measuring non-determinism across trials. Understanding how passᵏ differs from pass@1 and its dependency on k is foundational.
  - **Quick check question:** If an agent has pass@1 = 90% and execution is fully independent, what would pass³ be? How might actual dependence change this?
- **Concept:** Action Metamorphic Relations (AMR)
  - **Why needed here:** AMRs define the perturbation transformation ϕ and the state-based equivalence ψ. Personnel must design valid metamorphic pairs without altering task semantics.
  - **Quick check question:** For a “book flight” task, give an example of a valid ϕ (e.g., synonym substitution) and an invalid one that changes the goal (e.g., changing destination city).
- **Concept:** Chaos Engineering Fault Profiles
  - **Why needed here:** The fault injector’s behavior is governed by profiles Λ(λ). Engineers must understand fault types (recoverable vs. non‑recoverable) and their realistic mapping to production incidents.
  - **Quick check question:** Why is a “StaleData” fault typically non‑recoverable via retry, while a “TransientTimeout” is recoverable?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Task Generator → Perturbation Module → Fault Injector → Agent Executor → State Verifier → Reliability Aggregator  

**Critical path:**  
Task definition → (optional perturbation) → Agent execution → (fault‑injected tool calls) → Final state → Verification → Aggregate over k trials  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Perturbation complexity vs. validity: More aggressive ϕ (e.g., heavy paraphrase) risks changing task semantics; requires domain expert validation.  
- Fault profile realism vs. coverage: Simpler profiles (timeout‑only) are easier to interpret but miss interactions; complex cascading faults are more realistic but confounded.  
- k trials vs. cost: Higher k gives tighter confidence intervals on passᵏ but multiplies execution cost linearly.  

**Failure signatures:**  
- Rate Limit (Soft/Hard): Agent receives 429‑like error; observe whether it backs off and retries or aborts.  
- Partial Response: Tool returns incomplete data (e.g., truncated flight list); agent must handle missing fields.  
- Schema Drift: Tool response format changes (e.g., price becomes cost); agent’s JSON parsing or reasoning breaks.  
- Perturbation‑induced: Agent fails on reordered constraints or distractor sentences but succeeds on clean description.  

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline consistency sweep: For a representative task, run k=5 trials at ε=0, λ=0. Compute pass¹ to pass⁵. Establish baseline stochastic variance.  
2. Isolated perturbation impact: At λ=0, vary ε (0.0 → 0.1 → 0.2) on the same task. Measure degradation curve. Verify that failures are due to misunderstanding, not tool errors.  
3. Single‑fault ablation: At ε=0, inject only “Rate Limit” faults at increasing λ. Measure pass² degradation. Compare to “Timeout‑only” and “Partial Response‑only” profiles. Identify most damaging fault type.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Sample size for rare failure modes:** What episode count is needed for statistically reliable estimates of passᵏ under low λ (e.g., λ<0.1)? The study used 1,280 episodes, yielding wide confidence intervals; scaling to 10,000+ episodes would clarify.  
2. **Heavier perturbation intensity:** How do ε≥0.3 and combinations of multiple metamorphic relations affect reliability beyond the tested ε=0.2?  
3. **Generalization across models:** Do the observed patterns (ReAct > Reflexion under stress, Gemini ≈ GPT‑4o cost‑effectively) hold for a broader spectrum of models (Claude, Llama, Mistral, open‑source)?  
4. **Consistency decay at higher k:** How does passᵏ degrade as k increases beyond 2, and what are the implications for large‑scale production?  
5. **Rate limit recovery:** Why does rate limiting cause the largest single degradation, and what specific agent design modifications improve recovery?

## Limitations
- **Implementation fidelity:** No public codebase; all components must be reverse‑engineered from textual descriptions, risking deviation.
- **Task/oracle specification:** Task generator logic and full verification predicates are missing, potentially causing state verification mismatches.
- **Fault profile details:** Isolated fault ablations (e.g., “Rate Limit Only”) lack quantitative parameters, making exact replication of degradation curves difficult.

- Fault tolerance claim: **High**
- Cost/reliability trade‑off (Gemini vs. GPT‑4o): **Medium**
