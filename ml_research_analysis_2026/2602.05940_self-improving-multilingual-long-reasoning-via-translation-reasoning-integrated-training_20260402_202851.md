# Self-Improving Multilingual Long Reasoning via Translation-Reasoning Integrated Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05940
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05940
- **Reference count:** 35
- **Primary result:** TRIT improves average accuracy by 7 percentage points on MMATH while achieving near-perfect language consistency, improves translation quality by up to 8.4 COMET points on FLORES-200, and boosts cross-lingual question alignment by over 10 percentage points (MEXA cosine similarity).

## Executive Summary
Multilingual reasoning models often default to English and perform poorly when forced to reason in the question's original language due to weak multilingual understanding and reasoning. This paper introduces TRIT (Translation-Reasoning Integrated Training), a self-improving reinforcement learning framework that integrates translation training with multilingual reasoning. TRIT uses accuracy-based filtering to select questions for translation training, then leverages reasoning performance on translations as a reward signal for translation quality—without external feedback. On long-form math reasoning (MMATH), TRIT yields a 7‑point average accuracy gain over strong baselines and achieves nearly perfect language consistency. Moreover, translation quality (COMET) improves by up to 8.4 points on FLORES‑200, and cross-lingual question alignment (MEXA cosine similarity) increases by more than 10 points, demonstrating enhanced multilingual understanding.

## Method Summary
TRIT operates in two iterative stages. In Stage 1 (Cross‑Lingual Reasoning), the model attempts to solve English questions in the target language; questions are filtered based on an accuracy threshold \(\theta\). Only those with average reward \(r_{\text{avg}} \ge \theta\) proceed. In Stage 2, the model translates the filtered English questions into the target language, then reasons on the translated questions. The translation reward \(r_{\text{trans}}\) is binary: it is 1 if the subsequent reasoning yields at least one correct sample, otherwise 0. The resulting data—cross‑lingual attempts, translation pairs with \(r_{\text{trans}}\), and target‑language reasoning traces—are pooled and optimized jointly using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). This creates a closed loop: improved reasoning raises the \(\theta\)‑pass rate, feeding more translation data, which further enhances alignment and performance.

## Key Results
- **MMATH accuracy:** +7.0 pp average gain over strong baselines; near‑perfect language consistency.
- **Translation quality:** Up to +8.4 COMET points on FLORES‑200.
- **Cross‑lingual alignment:** MEXA cosine similarity improves >10 pp.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Accuracy‑based filtering reduces noise in the translation reward signal.
**Claim:** By retaining only questions that the model can solve reliably in the target language, the framework avoids penalizing correct translations for downstream reasoning failures that are actually due to the model’s reasoning limitations.  
**Mechanism:** Stage 1 computes average accuracy \(r_{\text{avg}}\) for each English question answered in the target language. Only questions with \(r_{\text{avg}} \ge \theta\) move to Stage 2. This ensures that when a translation later yields a reasoning failure (Acc = 0), the failure is more likely caused by a translation error than by the model’s inability to reason in that language.  
**Evidence:** The paper shows that \(\theta = 1/3\) minimizes the false‑negative rate (from 38.8% to 7.5%) and optimizes overall performance (Figure 4).  
**Break condition:** Setting \(\theta\) too high (e.g., \(2/3\)) drastically reduces data diversity and harms performance.

### Mechanism 2: Deferred reward from reasoning success acts as a proxy for translation quality.
**Claim:** Reasoning accuracy on a translated question serves as an unsupervised reward for translation fidelity, creating a self‑improving loop.  
**Mechanism:** In Stage 2, the model translates \(Q_{\text{en}}\) to \(Q_{\text{tgt}}\). The quality of this translation is not judged directly; instead, the model reasons on \(Q_{\text{tgt}}\). If any sampled reasoning path produces a correct answer, \(r_{\text{trans}} = 1\); otherwise \(0\). Correct translations generate high‑quality \((Q_{\text{tgt}}, R_{\text{tgt}}, r_{\text{reasoning}})\) pairs for reasoning training.  
**Evidence:** Analysis shows a strong positive correlation between judged translation quality and subsequent reasoning accuracy (76% win rate for translations with Acc > 0 vs. Acc = 0).  
**Break condition:** The proxy fails if target‑language reasoning is too weak, causing correct translations to be incorrectly penalized. This risk is mitigated by Mechanism 1’s filtering.

### Mechanism 3: Self‑generated translation forces cross‑lingual representation alignment.
**Claim:** Training the model to produce its own translations (rather than consuming pre‑translated data) aligns the semantic representations of English and target‑language questions, which improves multilingual reasoning consistency.  
**Mechanism:** Joint GRPO optimization on both translation and reasoning tasks encourages the model to map \(Q_{\text{en}}\) into a target‑language representation that preserves meaning and supports correct reasoning. This alignment is measured by increased cosine similarity (MEXA) between \(Q_{\text{en}}\) and \(Q_{\text{tgt}}\) embeddings.  
**Evidence:** TRIT significantly outperforms the “External‑Translation” baseline on MEXA similarity (e.g., +15.9 pp for DeepSeek‑Distill‑1.5B in the final layer) and this increase correlates with reasoning gains.  
**Break condition:** With extremely low baseline translation or reasoning capability, self‑generated translations may be too noisy to yield a meaningful alignment signal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**  
  *Why needed:* TRIT optimizes three interdependent tasks (cross‑lingual reasoning, translation, target‑language reasoning) using programmatically verifiable rewards (accuracy, language consistency, format). GRPO updates the policy without a separate value network, using group‑based advantage estimation \((\hat{A}_{i,t} = (r_i - \text{mean})/\text{std})\).  
  *Quick check:* How does GRPO’s group‑based advantage estimation differ from using a separate value network, and why is this beneficial for this multi‑task setup?

- **Cross‑lingual Representation Alignment**  
  *Why needed:* The core hypothesis is that poor multilingual reasoning stems partly from misaligned question representations across languages. TRIT aims to align these representations via translation training.  
  *Quick check:* What is the difference between *trace alignment* (optimized by M‑Thinker) and *question alignment* (optimized by TRIT), and why might the latter be more fundamental?

- **Reward Shaping and Noise Mitigation**  
  *Why needed:* The deferred reward mechanism introduces potential false negatives (good translations penalized due to reasoning failure). The filtering threshold \(\theta\) is a key design for noise control.  
  *Quick check:* What is a “false negative” in TRIT’s context, how does the \(\theta\) parameter control its rate, and what is the risk of setting \(\theta\) too high vs. too low?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Filtering Module (Stage 1) → Translation Module (Stage 2) → Reasoning Module (Stage 2) → Reward Assignment → GRPO Optimizer → (loop back to policy). Data flows: $Q_{\text{en}}$ → r_avg → θ? → translation $t_j$ → $Q_{\text{tgt}}$ → reasoning → Acc → r_trans (and r_final). All data types ($D_{\text{cross}}$, $D_{\text{trans}}$, $D_{\text{tgt}}$) feed the optimizer.

### Critical path
1. Input: English question $Q_{\text{en}}$.  
2. Stage 1: Sample target‑language reasoning traces; compute average reward $r_{\text{avg}}$.  
3. Filter: if $r_{\text{avg}} \ge \theta$, keep $Q_{\text{en}}$; otherwise discard.  
4. Stage 2: Generate translation $t_j$ → $Q_{\text{tgt}}$.  
5. Stage 2: Sample reasoning on $Q_{\text{tgt}}$; compute accuracy Acc.  
6. Reward: set $r_{\text{trans}} = \mathbb{I}(\text{Acc}>0)$. Compute final reasoning reward $r_{\text{final}}$ (accuracy, language, format, repetition constraints).  
7. Data: Add $(Q_{\text{en}}, t_j, r_{\text{trans}})$ to $D_{\text{trans}}$; add $(Q_{\text{tgt}}, \text{reasoning}, r_{\text{final}})$ to $D_{\text{tgt}}$; existing $D_{\text{cross}}$ from Stage 1 attempts is reused.  
8. Optimization: GRPO updates the policy on the combined dataset. Improved reasoning increases $\theta$‑pass rates in the next iteration, enriching translation data.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Filtering threshold $\theta$:** Low $\theta$ yields more data but higher false‑negative noise; high $\theta$ gives cleaner signal but reduces diversity and may drop harder questions. Empirical optimum $\theta = 1/3$.  
- **Reward composition:** Strict all‑or‑nothing correctness prevents reward hacking (e.g., correct answers with repetition), but a small reward (0.1) for well‑formed but incorrect answers provides a learning signal for non‑answer aspects.  
- **Self vs. external translation:** Self‑generation introduces alignment‑forcing noise but yields better MEXA alignment; external perfect translations give no alignment benefit.

### Failure signatures
- **Escalating repetition:** Without a repetition penalty, iterative RL leads to degenerate outputs (e.g., M‑Thinker Iter2: 43.3% repetition in correct answers).  
- **Saturation of trace‑alignment methods:** On models with high initial CTA (e.g., Qwen3‑1.7B: 0.93), M‑Thinker’s CTA reward provides little signal; TRIT’s question‑level loop remains effective.  
- **False negatives in translation reward:** Good translations may get $r_{\text{trans}}=0$ due to reasoning failure. This rate drops from 7.5% to 3.6% after training as reasoning improves.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Sanity check filtering:** For a fixed set of English questions, compute target‑language reasoning accuracies; vary $\theta$; verify that filtered questions have >~33% solve rate.  
2. **Closed‑loop validation:** Select 10 filtered $Q_{\text{en}}$, generate translations and reasoning; manually evaluate whether translations that yield Acc > 0 are semantically faithful.  
3. **Ablation – Remove $D_{\text{trans}}$:** Train with only $D_{\text{cross}}$ and $D_{\text{tgt}}$ (but $D_{\text{tgt}}$ still derived from filtered questions, so translation data is implicit). Expect a performance drop of ~2.7 pp, confirming the explicit translation training’s contribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does TRIT’s performance gain scale consistently with increased target language diversity (e.g., including low‑resource scripts like Amharic or Burmese) and larger model sizes (7B+ parameters)?  
- **Basis:** Limitations state experiments on five languages and models up to 4B; authors expect extension but do not validate.  
- **Why unresolved:** The core claim of “no additional multilingual data” relies on learning from English questions, but linguistic diversity (script, morphology, resource level) may affect translation‑reasoning integration differently. Larger models may have different alignment dynamics.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic evaluation on a broader language set (e.g., 20+ languages spanning scripts and resource levels) and larger backbones (7B, 14B), measuring LC&Acc and MEXA alignment to see if gains hold or vary.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is the fixed filtering threshold $\theta = 1/3$ globally optimal across all target languages and model capacities, or should it be adaptive?  
- **Basis:** Section 5.4: $\theta = 1/3$ chosen empirically on Qwen3‑1.7B; performance drops at higher thresholds due to sample scarcity.  
- **Why unresolved:** The trade‑off between false‑negative noise and sample diversity likely depends on language‑specific difficulty and model capability. A single threshold may under‑filter for easy languages or over‑filter for hard ones.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Per‑language analysis of false‑negative rates and LC&Acc curves across $\theta$ values for each model. An adaptive strategy (e.g., based on initial model performance per language) could be tested.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do the components of the compositional reward function (accuracy, language consistency, repetition, format) interact, and is the current all‑or‑nothing correctness penalty optimal?  
- **Basis:** Section 3.1: reward grants full credit only if all constraints (C) are met; otherwise 0.1 or 0. No ablation on weighting.  
- **Why unresolved:** The strict design may over‑penalize responses that are correct but fail one minor constraint (e.g., slight repetition or format glitch), possibly hindering learning. The relative importance of each component is unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying reward weights (e.g., partial credit for correct answers with repetition) and measuring impact on correctness, readability, and training stability. Analysis of failure modes (e.g., how often correct answers are penalized for repetition).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why is explicit target‑language reasoning data (tgt_lang question → tgt_lang response) indispensable, and can its omission be compensated by more cross‑lingual or translation data?  
- **Basis:** Ablation (Section 5.5): removing target‑language reasoning data drops LC&Acc by 8.2 pp (44.5→36.3), far more than removing other data types.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper attributes this to distribution shift but does not test if scaling cross‑lingual data (English→tgt_lang response) could close the gap. The fundamental representational gap between answering in English vs. native language reasoning remains unexplored.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation with increased cross‑lingual training samples, or curriculum mixing cross‑lingual and target‑language data to find a cost‑effective substitute. Representation analysis (e.g., probing layers) to see if target‑language reasoning activates distinct patterns.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What is the mechanistic explanation for TRIT’s out‑of‑domain translation improvement (e.g., on FLORES‑200 general text)? Is it due to improved semantic alignment, better instruction following, or something else?  
- **Basis:** Section 5.1: TRIT improves COMET by up to 8.4 points on FLORES‑200 despite training only on math questions. No hypothesis offered.  
- **Why unresolved:** The transfer suggests that translation‑reasoning integration develops general translation skills, but the precise factor (e.g., aligning question semantics, handling logical structure, or reinforcement of fidelity) is unidentified.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Detailed error analysis on FLORES‑200 translations (e.g., category of errors reduced: terminology, grammar, style). Controlled experiments isolating math‑specific vs. general features (e.g., training on math vs. non‑math logical text). Representation similarity studies comparing alignment on math vs. general text pairs.

## Limitations
- **Generalization of $\theta$:** The optimal filtering threshold ($1/3$) was found on specific model families (1.5B–7B). Its effectiveness for larger models (70B+), low‑resource languages, or domain‑specific reasoning (e.g., math vs. logic) is uncertain.  
- **Stability of the self‑generated translation loop:** The deferred reward mechanism assumes reasoning failure implies translation error. This breaks down if the model’s target‑language reasoning plateaus early, causing persistent false negatives that stifle translation learning. Long‑term iterative stability is not deeply analyzed.  
- **Causal attribution of alignment gains:** The link between increased MEXA similarity and improved accuracy is correlational. It is unclear whether representation alignment drives reasoning gains or if both are parallel outcomes of joint training. Ablations separating alignment from translation/reasoning improvements are limited.

## Confidence
- **Performance gains on MMATH (+7 pp):** High  
- **Translation quality (COMET) and alignment (MEXA) improvements:** Medium  
- **Mechanism claims (filtering reduces false negatives, deferred reward loop):** Medium‑Low  

## Next Checks
1. **Threshold Generalization Test:** For a fixed set of 500 diverse reasoning questions (across 5 non‑Indic languages), sweep $\theta$ from 0.1 to 0.8 on a 3B model. Measure (a) false‑negative rate of translation reward, (b) final reasoning accuracy. Expect: optimal $\theta$ correlates with initial target‑language reasoning proficiency.  
2. **Human Evaluation of Translation‑Reasoning Proxy:** Sample 200 $(Q_{\text{en}}, Q_{\text{tgt}})$ pairs where TRIT assigned $r_{\text{trans}}=1$ vs. $r_{\text{trans}}=0$. Have annotators judge $Q_{\text{tgt}}$ fidelity (1–5 scale). Hypothesis: high‑fidelity translations should dominate the $r_{\text{trans}}=1$ set, confirming the proxy.  
3. **Longitudinal Iteration Analysis:** Run TRIT for 5+ full iterations on a small model (1.5B). Track per‑iteration: (a) repetition rate in outputs, (b) false‑negative translation rate, (c) MEXA similarity. Check for saturation or collapse in later stages, especially if $\theta$‑pass rate plateaus.
