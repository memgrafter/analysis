# Cheap Thrills: Effective Amortized Optimization Using Inexpensive Labels

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.05495
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05495
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: A three-stage amortized optimization framework using inexpensive labels achieves up to 59× lower offline cost and improved solution quality (faster convergence, better feasibility/optimality) across multiple benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Amortized optimization surrogates typically rely on expensive solver labels (supervised learning) or struggle with rugged landscapes (self-supervised). This paper introduces a three-stage framework: (1) generate cheap, imperfect labels using approximate solvers; (2) supervised pretraining to enter the basin of attraction of a high-quality solution; (3) self-supervised fine-tuning. Theoretical analysis shows that modest label quality and quantity suffice to place the model within a basin from which self-supervised optimization converges. Across benchmarks—nonconvex constrained optimization, AC optimal power flow, and stiff dynamics—the method yields faster convergence, improved feasibility and optimality, and up to 59× lower offline cost versus baselines. For example, on a synthetic problem, mean objective improved from -0.73 to -3.28 and equality violations decreased from 5.85e-05 to 4.06e-05.

## Method Summary
The method begins by collecting a dataset of approximate solutions using a fast, low-accuracy solver (e.g., relaxed tolerances, limited iterations). These cheap labels are imperfect but inexpensive to generate. A neural network is then trained on this dataset with a supervised loss combining Huber regression on the approximate solutions and penalty terms for constraint violations. A merit function (quadratic penalty of constraint residuals) is monitored on a validation set; the checkpoint with minimal merit is selected as the warm-start, expected to lie within the basin of attraction of a high-quality solution. Finally, the network is fine-tuned using self-supervised learning (e.g., FSNet) that directly optimizes the task objective and constraints. Theoretical analysis justifies that only modest numbers of cheap labels are needed to place the model within such a basin, and that warm-starting accelerates self-supervised convergence.

## Key Results
- Faster convergence and improved feasibility/optimality across benchmarks (nonconvex constrained optimization, AC OPF, stiff dynamics).
- Up to 59× reduction in offline labeling cost compared to fully supervised or cold-start SSL baselines.
- On a synthetic constrained problem: mean objective improved from -0.73 to -3.28; equality violations decreased from 5.85e-05 to 4.06e-05.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Basin entry via supervised warm-start**: Supervised pretraining with cheap, imperfect labels can place the model within the basin of attraction of a high-quality solution, enabling successful self-supervised refinement. The supervised loss drives the model toward the cheap label solution; if this trajectory intersects the basin, early stopping based on a merit function (quadratic penalty of constraint residuals) can capture a checkpoint inside the basin. The merit increases when the model overfits to biased labels, signaling the optimal stopping point. This relies on the assumption that the solution manifold is continuous and the basin has nonzero radius. Break conditions: cheap labels are too biased (Δproxy ≥ mθ) so the trajectory never intersects the basin, or early stopping fails to capture the intersection.

**Coarse manifold coverage with few cheap labels**: Only a modest number of cheap labels is needed because they need to cover the solution manifold at a resolution determined by the basin radius mθ, not the final precision ε. The sample complexity for warm-start scales as N_warm ≳ (L·diam(X)/mθ)^deff, where deff is the intrinsic dimension and L the Lipschitz constant. Since mθ ≫ ε, N_warm is exponentially smaller than full supervised sample complexity ∝ (L/ε)^deff. This assumes the solution map lies on a low-dimensional manifold. Break condition: if the effective dimension is high or basin radius extremely small, the required labels may become impractical.

**Self-supervised fine-tuning from a warm-start**: Initializing SSL from a warm-start within the favorable basin yields faster convergence, better feasibility, and higher optimality than cold-start SSL. The warm-start avoids rugged regions of the SSL loss landscape; within the basin, the merit function is smoother and gradients more reliable, allowing larger learning rates and stable descent. This assumes the SSL objective has a well-defined basin around the high-quality solution and the warm-start lies within it. Break condition: warm-start falls outside the basin, causing SSL to stall or converge to suboptimal solutions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Cheap Label Generator → Supervised Pretraining Module (with integrated Merit Monitor) → Self-Supervised Fine-Tuning Module.

**Critical path**:
1. Generate cheap labels for a dataset.
2. Pretrain the model with supervised learning, monitoring merit.
3. Select the checkpoint with minimal validation merit.
4. Fine-tune with SSL starting from that checkpoint.
5. Evaluate final model on test set.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Label quality vs. cost*: Cheaper labels reduce generation time but increase bias; need enough quality to keep Δproxy < mθ or ensure traversability.
- *Pretraining epochs vs. overfitting*: More epochs reduce fitting error but may move out of the basin; merit-based early stopping mitigates.
- *SSL constraint handling*: Soft penalties are easier but may not guarantee feasibility; hard constraints (e.g., DC3) are stricter but more fragile; warm-start helps both.

**Failure signatures**:
- Merit increases during supervised pretraining while training loss decreases → overfitting to bias, likely missed basin intersection.
- SSL loss diverges or plateaus at high merit → warm-start not in basin or learning rate too high.
- Final model has high constraint violations → either warm-start failed or SSL did not sufficiently refine.

**First experiments**:
1. **Synthetic constrained optimization**: Use the nonsmooth nonconvex program; warm-start with 800 cheap labels (0.5s CPU each); compare FSNet performance with and without warm-start (Table 1). Validate merit U-shape and feasibility improvements.
2. **AC Optimal Power Flow (118-bus)**: Generate DCOPF labels (cheap) and pretrain; then apply FSNet or soft-constraint SSL; measure optimality gap and violations (Figure 3). Show reduction in offline cost and improved feasibility.
3. **Stiff dynamical system (PINN)**: Train a flow-map operator using linearized dynamics as cheap labels; compare against vanilla PINN in trajectory error and basin-of-attraction success rate (Figure 4). Observe more stable trajectories and lower error.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Adaptive strategies**: How can we develop adaptive strategies that dynamically balance label acquisition and self-supervised training in online or distribution-shifted settings? (Authors explicitly mention this as a future direction.)
2. **Low intrinsic dimension**: Under what conditions does the solution manifold exhibit low intrinsic dimension (deff), and how robust are sample complexity guarantees to violations of this assumption? (Inferred from theory relying on Assumption 4.4.)
3. **Label inaccuracy limits**: What are the theoretical limits on label inaccuracy (bias δproxy) that still allow supervised warm-starting to achieve basin entry, and how does this relate to the basin radius mθ? (Theorem 4.2 provides conditions but mθ is not quantified.)
4. **Automated merit stopping**: Can we automate the merit-based early stopping criterion to avoid dependence on a validation set and adaptively determine the optimal warm-start checkpoint? (Merit-based stopping is manual.)
5. **Non-smooth manifolds**: How does the framework perform on problems where the solution manifold is highly non-smooth or discontinuous, violating the Lipschitz assumption? (Assumption 4.4 may not hold for combinatorial/discrete problems.)

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (smooth manifold, nonzero basin radius) may fail on rugged landscapes.
- Cheap label generation details are underspecified; the bias-cost trade-off requires further study.
- Generalization beyond Power Flow and dynamics benchmarks remains unproven.
