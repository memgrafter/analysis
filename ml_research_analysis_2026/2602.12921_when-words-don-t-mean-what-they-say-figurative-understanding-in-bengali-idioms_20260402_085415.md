# When Words Don't Mean What They Say: Figurative Understanding in Bengali Idioms
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12921  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12921  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** No LLM exceeded 50% accuracy on a 100-idiom Bengali figurative understanding benchmark; the best model scored 47.6% while humans achieved 83.4%.  

## Executive Summary
Figurative language understanding remains a major challenge for large language models (LLMs), particularly for low-resource languages like Bengali where culturally rich resources are scarce. This paper addresses this gap by constructing a large-scale corpus of 10,361 Bengali idioms annotated with a comprehensive 19-field schema capturing semantic, syntactic, cultural, and religious dimensions through expert consensus. The authors then benchmarked 30 state-of-the-art multilingual and instruction-tuned LLMs on a curated subset of 100 idioms using zero-shot prompts and a double-blind expert evaluation rubric. Results show uniformly poor performance across all models, with a top accuracy of 47.6% compared to 83.4% for human participants, revealing a critical deficiency in cross-cultural figurative reasoning. The released dataset and benchmark provide essential infrastructure for advancing culturally-aware NLP in underrepresented languages.  

## Method Summary
The study introduces a novel corpus of 10,361 Bengali idioms, each annotated across 19 fields that encode semantic mappings, pragmatic context, sociocultural metadata, and diachronic information. Annotation followed a rigorous human-in-the-loop protocol: AI assistance (Gemini/GPT-4o) generated initial drafts, followed by iterative consensus-building among a panel of six domain experts to resolve disagreements through discussion. From this corpus, a benchmark of 100 culturally complex idioms was hand-picked by linguistics specialists to test figurative comprehension. Thirty LLMs were evaluated in a zero-shot setting using Bengali prompts that asked the model to assume the persona of an ordinary Bengali speaker. Model outputs were scored on a 6-point semantic comprehension rubric by a separate panel of five linguists in a double-blind procedure.  

## Key Results
- No model surpassed 50% accuracy; Gemini‑2.5‑flash was highest at 47.6%.
- Human participants achieved 83.4% accuracy on the same benchmark.
- Performance was uniformly low across all model sizes, architectures, and origins, indicating a systemic gap in cross-cultural figurative reasoning.  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The 19-field schema captures multi-dimensional cultural-contextual data that transcends literal semantics, potentially enabling models to ground idiomatic expressions in socio-cultural frames.
- **Mechanism:** By encoding fields like `historical_significance`, `religious_significance`, `scape`, and `usage_domain` alongside semantic mappings, the schema provides structured metadata that could be used during training (as auxiliary supervision) or inference (via retrieval-augmented prompting) to activate culturally relevant knowledge structures.
- **Core assumption:** Models can effectively integrate and utilize structured cultural metadata when such signals are explicitly provided, rather than relying solely on distributional semantics from raw text.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Each idiom is annotated under a comprehensive 19-field schema... that captures its semantic, syntactic, cultural, and religious dimensions."
  - [section 3.1] The schema groups fields into "Semantic and Cross-Lingual Mapping," "Pragmatic Contextualization," "Sociocultural and Affective Metadata," and "Diachronic and Ethnolinguistic Information."
  - [corpus] The dataset shows 99.7% idioms have `cultural_significance` annotated, and 13.5% have `religious_significance` (section 4.3).
- **Break condition:** If models fail to leverage the structured metadata due to architectural limitations (e.g., inability to process non-sequential fields) or if the annotations inadvertently encode Western-centric biases despite expert consensus.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Expert consensus annotation, through iterative deliberation among multidisciplinary scholars, produces high-fidelity cultural-semantic alignments that crowd-sourcing or automated methods cannot achieve, establishing a gold-standard resource.
- **Mechanism:** The protocol requires at least two domain experts to reach complete agreement through physical/online discussions for each entry, resolving ambiguities via qualitative judgment rather than statistical aggregation. This process embeds deep linguistic and cultural intuition into each annotation.
- **Core assumption:** Expert consensus, particularly when involving native linguists and educators, can capture the tacit cultural knowledge necessary for interpreting idioms more accurately than independent labeling or AI-only approaches.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] "Validation by this group was not conducted via individual scoring rubrics; instead, consensus was actively constructed through iterative physical meetings... this ensures that the final dataset reflects a deep scholarly consensus rather than a statistical aggregation."
  - [abstract] "established and refined through a deliberative expert consensus process."
- **Break condition:** If the expert panel’s demographic or ideological homogeneity introduces blind spots, or if consensus-driven decisions suppress minority cultural interpretations present in broader speech communities.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Benchmarking on a curated, expert-selected subset of 100 culturally complex idioms provides a rigorous, discriminative test that isolates figurative understanding from superficial lexical matching, revealing fundamental model deficiencies.
- **Mechanism:** The test set was hand-picked by linguistics specialists to span the full spectrum of complexity and subtlety, avoiding random or computationally convenient selection. Paired with a double-blind expert rubric and zero-shot prompts, this design evaluates innate cultural reasoning rather than test-set memorization or superficial pattern recognition.
- **Core assumption:** A small, carefully constructed ideologically diverse test set can validly approximate the full dataset’s difficulty and expose systematic failures in cross-cultural figurative reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3] "This subset was meticulously curated through iterative expert discussions... Priority was given to idioms that are not only commonly used... but also possess subtle or complex figurative contexts."
  - [section 5] "No model surpassed the 50% performance threshold... human participants achieved 83.4%."
- **Break condition:** If the 100-idiom test set fails to represent the diversity of the 10,361-idiom corpus (e.g., over-represents certain domains or registers), or if the evaluation rubric’s subjectivity introduces measurement error despite double-blinding.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** 
  Source Aggregator -> Filtering & Normalizer -> AI-Assist Draft Module -> Human Expert Review Panel -> Consensus Engine -> Benchmark Curator -> Evaluation Harness -> Double-Blind Scorer
- **Critical path:** Source data → Filtering → AI draft → Expert review → Consensus → Benchmark selection → Model inference → Double-blind scoring → Analysis. The consensus loop (steps 4–5) is the quality bottleneck; the evaluation pipeline (steps 6–8) must preserve blind conditions.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - *Depth vs. scale:* The 19-field schema yields rich but expensive annotations; future work may need semi-supervised scaling.
  - *Consensus vs. agreement metrics:* They prioritize qualitative resolution over quantitative inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ not computed), trusting scholarly deliberation.
  - *Zero-shot vs. adaptive evaluation:* Zero-shot tests innate knowledge but may underestimate what fine-tuning on this dataset could achieve.
- **Failure signatures:** 
  - Low coverage of dialectal variants (e.g., Sylheti) in the `scape` field indicates geographical bias.
  - High failure rates on idioms tagged `religious_significance` could signal models’ lack of cultural-religious knowledge.
  - Inconsistent model performance (high CoV >1.0) suggests the benchmark may be noisy or the models lack stable representations.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Annotation cycle replication: Take 50 unseen Bengali idioms, run the full consensus protocol, and measure time per entry vs. original to validate scalability assumptions.
  2. Benchmark subset validation: Compute representativeness metrics: do the 100 test idioms’ domain/tag distributions match the full corpus? If not, identify coverage gaps.
  3. Prompt sensitivity test: For the top-3 models (Gemini, Claude, DeepSeek), vary zero-shot prompt phrasing (e.g., add cultural context hints) to see if performance improves, probing whether models lack knowledge or fail to access it under the given prompt.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the inclusion of underrepresented Bengali dialects (e.g., Sylheti, Chittagonian) and urban linguistic innovations impact an LLM's accuracy in figurative understanding compared to a standard Bangla-focused benchmark?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors identify "Geographical and Dialectical Representation" as a key limitation, noting coverage gaps for specific dialects and urban variants (Section 6).
- **Why unresolved:** The current benchmark and dataset are based on Standard Bangla, omitting significant regional variation. This prevents assessment of model robustness across the full linguistic spectrum of Bengali speakers.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A extended benchmark containing idioms from specific dialects, with human baselines from those regions, followed by comparative LLM evaluation to quantify performance drops or shifts.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the quantitative impact of replacing qualitative expert consensus with formal inter-annotator agreement metrics (e.g., Cohen's κ) on the reliability and scalability of the 19-field annotation schema?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state that future work should "compute formal agreement metrics (e.g., Cohen’s κ) through parallel annotation, complementing the current qualitative consensus method" (Section 6).
- **Why unresolved:** The current methodology prioritizes deep scholarly consensus but does not measure statistical agreement, leaving the schema's consistency under different annotator teams unknown and hindering scalable annotation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A parallel annotation study where multiple expert pairs independently annotate a subset. Comparing κ scores with the existing consensus-derived dataset would reveal the trade-off between depth of consensus and measurable reliability.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does expanding the benchmark test set from 100 to a statistically robust number (e.g., 500-1000) idioms alter the observed ranking and failure rate correlation between model characteristics (size, origin, architecture) and performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note the benchmark's "100-idiom test set can be significantly expanded to ensure robustness" (Section 6).
- **Why unresolved:** The small, expert-curated test set (n=100) may not capture the full semantic and cultural diversity of the 10,361-idiom corpus, potentially masking true performance trends or introducing selection bias.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic creation of larger, stratified test subsets from the full corpus. Re-running the evaluation to see if correlations (or lack thereof) with model properties hold with larger samples and whether failure rates stabilize.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent can contemporary LLMs interpret idioms that have undergone significant semantic shift or are specific to younger generations (digital neologisms), compared to historically rooted idioms?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors call for capturing "contemporary digital neologisms for diachronic study" and "generation-aware frameworks" (Section 6), implying current data lacks this temporal dimension and models may fail on modern usage.
- **Why unresolved:** The dataset emphasizes historical and cultural significance but may under-represent rapidly evolving colloquial usage. LLMs trained on web data might have exposure to modern forms, but their figurative understanding of these is untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Curating a balanced test set pairing historical idioms with their modern, youth-oriented equivalents (or novel digital idioms). Human and LLM evaluation on both sets would reveal potential performance cliffs for temporally-specific language.

## Limitations
- The exact 100-idiom test set and full prompt wording are not disclosed, hindering exact replication.
- The expert consensus process lacks formal inter-rater reliability metrics (e.g., Cohen’s κ), leaving annotation consistency unquantified.
- The benchmark’s small size and focus on Standard Bangla may not fully represent the linguistic diversity of Bengali speakers, risking selection and coverage biases.

## Confidence
- Schema captures cultural-contextual data: **Medium** (annotation depth shown, but model integration untested).
- Expert consensus yields gold standard: **High** (rigorous process, though no independent replication).
- Benchmark reveals fundamental model gap: **High** (robust empirical evaluation across 30 models; test representativeness uncertain).
- Models cannot leverage structured metadata: **Low** (speculative; not directly examined).
