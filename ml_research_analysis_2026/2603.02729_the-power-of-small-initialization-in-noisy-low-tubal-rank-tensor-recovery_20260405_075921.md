# The power of small initialization in noisy low-tubal-rank tensor recovery

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02729
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02729
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: Factorized gradient descent with small random initialization decouples the recovery error from the overestimated tubal rank, achieving an error that scales only with the true tubal rank, condition number, and noise level, and is minimax‑optimal under Gaussian measurements.

## Executive Summary

Recovering a low‑tubal‑rank tensor from noisy, incomplete linear measurements is a fundamental problem in signal processing. Standard approaches, such as factorized gradient descent (FGD) with spectral initialization, suffer from an error that grows linearly with the overestimated rank R when the noise is dense Gaussian. This paper shows that replacing spectral initialization with a small zero‑mean Gaussian initialization breaks this undesirable dependence. The authors analyze the FGD trajectory through four phases—alignment, signal amplification, local refinement, and overfitting—and prove that the over‑parameterization term remains bounded while the signal term is amplified. Consequently, the final recovery error depends only on the true tubal rank r, condition number κ, and noise norm. Under i.i.d. Gaussian measurements and Gaussian noise, the bound matches the information‑theoretic minimax lower bound up to constants. A simple validation‑based early‑stopping rule attains this error without prior knowledge of r or the noise variance. Extensive simulations on synthetic tensors and real‑world image/video completion tasks confirm the superiority of small initialization, especially when R ≫ r.

## Method Summary

The method employs factorized gradient descent (FGD) to recover a low‑tubal‑rank tensor X★ from linear measurements. Instead of optimizing over the tensor directly, FGD maintains a factorized representation, typically X = U V⊤ where U, V ∈ ℝ^{n×R} with R ≥ r. The algorithm begins with a random Gaussian initialization for U and V, using a small variance (scaled as 1/√R). At each iteration, the gradient of the squared loss on observed entries is computed with respect to the factors, and a gradient step with a carefully chosen step size is performed. The step size must satisfy a stability condition to ensure convergence and the theoretical guarantees. The process continues until an early‑stopping criterion—based on a held‑out validation set—triggers, preventing the overfitting phase where the fit to noise would degrade performance. This simple modification of the initialization, coupled with a validation rule, yields a globally convergent algorithm with improved error scaling.

## Key Results

- **Rank‑Decoupled Bound**: The Frobenius error satisfies ||U_t U_t⊤ – X★||_F ≲ √r κ² ||E||, where E aggregates measurement noise and κ is the condition number of the stacked Fourier blocks. The bound depends solely on the true rank r, not the overestimate R.
- **Minimax Optimality**: Under i.i.d. Gaussian measurements and Gaussian noise, the error is ≲ nkr σ² κ⁴ / m with high probability, matching the minimax lower bound up to constant factors.
- **Empirical Validation**: Simulations and real‑data experiments (image/video completion) show that FGD with small initialization achieves significantly lower error than spectral or large‑random initialization, particularly when R is much larger than r. The early‑stopping strategy reliably attains the theoretical error without knowing r or σ.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The improvement arises from the interaction between the small initialization and the dynamics of gradient descent in an over‑parameterized regime. The analysis identifies four phases in the trajectory. Initially, the alignment phase brings the factors into a favorable subspace where the over‑parameterization component (stemming from the extra R−r dimensions) remains small. In the subsequent signal amplification phase, the gradient steps act to increase the signal‑to‑noise ratio: the component aligned with the true singular tubes grows, while the noise component is kept in check. This amplification effect is a consequence of the chosen step size and the isotropic nature of the Gaussian measurements. After a few iterations, the iterate enters a local refinement phase, where the algorithm converges linearly to a neighborhood of the true tensor. If the iterations continue beyond this point, the overfitting phase begins and the error increases because the model starts fitting the noise. The small initialization ensures that the over‑parameterization term never dominates, and the early‑stopping rule halts the process before the overfitting phase. Thus, the final error is essentially the amplified signal error plus a residual noise term, both of which depend only on the true rank r and intrinsic conditioning κ, not on R.

## Foundational Learning

- **Tubal Rank**: The tensor analogue of matrix rank in the t‑SVD framework; equals the number of non‑zero singular tubes. *Why needed*: Determines the intrinsic dimensionality of the low‑tubal‑rank tensor and appears in the √r factor of the error bound. *Quick check*: Generate a tensor X = U * S * V⊤ with orthogonal U, V and diagonal S containing r non‑zero frontal slices; its tubal rank should be exactly r.
- **Factorized Gradient Descent (FGD)**: An optimization approach that parameterizes the tensor as a product of factors and performs gradient descent on the factors. *Why needed*: Avoids explicit low‑rank constraints and yields efficient updates while automatically maintaining the low‑rank structure. *Quick check*: For matrix sensing, FGD initialized with small random factors should converge to the ground‑truth matrix under similar assumptions.
- **Spectral Initialization**: A common initialization that estimates the top singular tubes of X★ from a (possibly regularized) matricization. *Why needed*: Provides a good starting point when the signal‑to‑noise ratio is high; however, it can be sensitive to noise and over‑parameterization. *Quick check*: Apply spectral init to a noisy low‑rank matrix; observe that the error of the initial guess is near the noise level.
- **Condition Number (κ)**: In the tensor setting, κ is the maximum ratio of absolute eigenvalues of the stacked Fourier blocks; it quantifies the spread of singular values. *Why needed*: Appears in the error bound; a smaller κ leads to better conditioning and faster convergence. *Quick check*: For a diagonal matrix with entries ranging from 1 to σ_max/σ_min, κ = σ_max/σ_min.
- **Gaussian Measurement Model**: Measurement vectors are drawn i.i.d. from a Gaussian distribution; this isotropy is key for the analysis. *Why needed*: Enables concentration arguments and ensures that the measurement operator acts uniformly on all directions. *Quick check*: Verify that for a fixed low‑rank matrix, the empirical distribution of A·X (with Gaussian A) concentrates around its mean as the number of measurements grows.
- **Early Stopping**: A regularization technique that terminates optimization based on a validation metric to prevent overfitting. *Why needed*: In over‑parameterized FGD, continuing past a certain point increases error by fitting noise; early stopping captures the optimal bias‑variance trade‑off. *Quick check*: Plot training and validation loss versus iterations; the optimal stopping point should coincide with the minimum validation loss.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Small Random Initialization → Factorized Gradient Descent Loop (gradient computation → factor update) → Validation Monitoring → Early Stopping Decision. The four analytical phases (alignment, signal amplification, local refinement, overfitting) describe the temporal evolution of the iterate within this loop.

**Critical Path**  
1. Small‑variance initialization places the iterate in a region where the extra rank dimensions are negligible.  
2. Gradient descent with a properly chosen step size amplifies the component aligned with the true singular tubes while controlling the growth of the noise term.  
3. After a modest number of iterations, the iterate enters a local basin where convergence to the true tensor is stable.  
4. Early stopping before the overfitting phase locks in the achieved error, which is provably near the global optimum.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Initialization scale*: Too large leads to poor conditioning and amplifies noise; too small may slow convergence. The paper identifies a narrow range that yields the theoretical guarantees.  
- *Overparameterization (R)*: Larger R offers more representation power but increases the risk of overfitting; small initialization mitigates this risk.  
- *Step size*: Must be below a stability threshold (inversely proportional to the maximum singular value of the measurement operator) to guarantee convergence; larger steps accelerate progress but may violate assumptions.  
- *Early‑stopping threshold*: A trade‑off between bias (stopping too early) and variance (stopping too late); the validation loss provides an empirical proxy.  
- *Computational cost*: Each FGD iteration costs O(n k R) (for a tensor of size n×n×k); spectral init adds an SVD cost O(n³) but may be worthwhile if R is small.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Final error scales linearly with R → likely too large initialization or insufficient amplification.  
- Validation loss increases after an initial decrease → overfitting phase; increase step size or adjust stopping rule.  
- Divergence or oscillation → step size too large; decrease within the theoretical bound.  
- Error significantly exceeds theoretical bound → violation of Gaussian assumptions, κ too large, or the measurements not i.i.d.  

**First Experiments**  
1. **Over‑parameterization study**: For synthetic tensors with true rank r = 5, run FGD with R ∈ {5,10,25,50} under fixed Gaussian noise. Compare final errors for small‑random vs. spectral initialization; confirm that error for small init remains stable while spectral init’s error grows with R.  
2. **Condition number sensitivity**: Generate tensors with singular values spaced to yield κ = {1, 5, 10, 20}. Run FGD (small init) and measure error; verify that error roughly follows the predicted κ² scaling in the bound.  
3. **Real‑data early stopping**: Apply the method to a video completion dataset (e.g., a subset of the Hollywood2 videos). Compare three strategies: (a) fixed large number of iterations, (b) cross‑validated APG baseline, (c) the proposed validation‑based early stopping. Report PSNR and runtime; expect (c) to match or outperform (b) without extra tuning.

## Limitations

- The analysis is restricted to i.i.d. Gaussian measurements and Gaussian noise; performance under non‑Gaussian noise (e.g., impulsive, sparse corruptions) or structured missing data is not established.
- The error bound depends on the condition number κ, and the sensitivity of the actual error to κ is not quantified beyond the bound; tensors with very large κ may violate the assumptions.
- Early‑stopping thresholds are derived in asymptotic regimes; their robustness to finite‑sample deviations or adversarial noise is unclear, and the rule may need adaptation for other data domains.
