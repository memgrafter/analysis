# Plato's Cave: A Human-Centered Research Verification System

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.23526
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23526
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: AUROC 0.759 for Good vs Bad classification on 104 papers; DAG extraction 2.6% failure; median runtime 65.4 min/paper; cost ~$0.15/paper.

## Executive Summary
Plato's Cave tackles the growing backlog of research publications and rising retraction rates by providing an automated verification system. It transforms research papers into role-labeled directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), uses independent web-browsing agents to assign credibility metrics to each node, and propagates trust through the graph to produce a paper-level score. On a pilot set of 104 papers, the system achieves moderate ranking performance (AUROC 0.759, Spearman 0.401) with practical processing times and costs, offering a scalable approach to research quality assessment.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with LLM-based extraction of a 10-role semantic DAG from the paper text, validated for acyclicity and referential integrity. Next, parallel browser-use agents verify each node by searching the web and producing six normalized credibility metrics. A Python scorer then aggregates these metrics, computes edge confidences, and performs trust propagation through the DAG using deterministic message-passing. Final paper scores are derived from six component aggregates. Hyperparameter calibration employs a cache-first staged search (dense, refine, sparse) over weight configurations without gradient-based learning from human labels.

## Key Results
- AUROC 0.759 for distinguishing Good vs Bad papers (Spearman 0.401) on a 104-paper pilot set.
- DAG extraction validation failure rate: 2.6%.
- Median runtime: 65.4 minutes per paper; cost: approximately $0.15 per paper.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: DAG Representation**
- **Claim:** Representing a paper as a role-labeled directed acyclic graph (DAG) captures its argumentative structure in a machine-readable form.
- **Mechanism:** An LLM extracts nodes (semantic units) from paper text, assigns each a role from a fixed 10-role ontology, and links them via directed edges representing logical dependencies. Output is validated for acyclicity, referential integrity, and role compliance; failures trigger a repair loop.
- **Core Assumption:** Scientific arguments decompose into a DAG of discrete claims, evidence, methods, etc., where information flows from hypotheses to conclusions.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract mentions DAG creation; Section 4 details extraction, ontology, and validation; corpus situates work in argument mining and knowledge graphs.
- **Break Condition:** Repeated validation failures (unknown roles, dangling references, cycles) abort extraction; high failure rate suggests poor prompt design or unconventional paper structure.

**Mechanism 2: Multi-agent Web Verification**
- **Claim:** Multi-agent web verification grounds each node’s claims in external sources, producing six credibility metrics that reflect real-world support.
- **Mechanism:** Independent browser-use agents are assigned to each node; they search, navigate, and extract content from the web, then assess source credibility, relevance, evidence strength, method rigor, reproducibility, and citation support. Metrics are normalized and stored.
- **Core Assumption:** Automated web browsing can reliably gather evidence to evaluate the truthfulness of specific statements in a research paper.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract mentions web agents; Section 4 describes agent workflow, metrics, and Exa fallback; corpus signals relation to claim-verification systems (e.g., SciFact).
- **Break Condition:** Agents may fail due to paywalls, CAPTCHAs, or scarcity of relevant sources; noisy or degenerate metric distributions (e.g., all zeros) signal verification breakdowns.

**Mechanism 3: Trust Propagation**
- **Claim:** Trust propagation through the DAG ensures weak evidence appropriately diminishes confidence in downstream claims, preventing inflation of unsupported conclusions.
- **Mechanism:** Node trust values are computed by aggregating contributions from parent nodes: each parent’s trust (raised to exponent α) multiplied by raw edge confidence, aggregated via min/mean/softmin/dampmin. Final edge confidence is gated by parent trust with floor η.
- **Core Assumption:** The credibility of a claim is bounded by the credibility of its supporting evidence (weak-link principle).
- **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract mentions trust propagation; Appendix C defines equations; Section 4 discusses trust-gated message passing.
- **Break Condition:** Extremely low connectivity (isolated nodes) or malformed DAGs cause degenerate propagation; inappropriate parameter settings (e.g., η too high) may suppress all influence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs):** Needed to enforce dependency constraints and enable trust propagation; cycles would break scoring. Quick check: "What structural constraint does the system enforce on the extracted graph, and why is it critical for scoring?"
- **Role-Based Ontology:** The ten semantic roles determine node verification and edge weighting; mislabeling disrupts scoring. Quick check: "If a node is incorrectly labeled as ‘Claim’ instead of ‘Evidence,’ how might that affect its verification metrics and downstream trust?"
- **Trust Propagation (Message Passing):** Core scoring algorithm uses deterministic message passing; understanding aggregation modes and gating is key to interpreting scores. Quick check: "Explain the effect of the α parameter on parent contributions in trust propagation."

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
Frontend (Gatsby/React + ReactFlow) -> DAG Extractor (LLM-based) -> Verification Agents (Dockerized browser-use) -> Scorer (Python service) -> Data Store

**Critical Path:**
1. Paper input (PDF/URL/query) -> DAG extraction -> validated DAG.
2. For each DAG node (parallel): verification agents fetch external sources -> metrics.
3. Scorer aggregates metrics, computes raw edge confidences, runs trust propagation, produces final score.
4. Frontend renders interactive graph, metrics, and score.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- Fixed ontology vs domain specificity: Universal 10-role ontology ensures consistency but may miss field-specific nuances; analysis modes tweak prompts but not roles.
- Web browsing vs structured APIs: Agents are flexible but costly and error-prone; domain APIs (e.g., PubMed) could improve reliability for specialized fields.
- Interpretability vs performance: Hand-tuned weights and simple propagation favor auditability; learned weights might boost accuracy but obscure reasoning.
- Parallelization: Reduces latency but increases resource usage and API costs.

**Failure Signatures:**
- DAG extraction: Validation errors (unknown roles, dangling refs, cycles) logged; high repair rate indicates prompt or paper-structure issues.
- Agent verification: Timeouts, CAPTCHAs, or uniform metric values (all near 0 or 1) suggest agents are not extracting meaningful evidence.
- Scoring: NaN/inf values from division by zero or clipping; score distributions clustered at extremes may indicate mis-calibration.
- End-to-end: Papers exceeding expected runtime (e.g., >2 h) likely have too many nodes or stalled agents.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **DAG Extraction Sanity Check:** Run extractor on a known paper (e.g., from evaluation set) and manually verify JSON validity, role assignments, and acyclicity.
2. **Agent Spot Check:** Observe a browser-use agent via noVNC while it verifies a single node; confirm it finds relevant sources and assigns plausible metric values.
3. **Trust Propagation Toy Example:** Build a minimal DAG (Hypothesis→Claim→Evidence) with hand‑set metrics; compute trust step‑by‑step using equations to see how weakening Evidence reduces Claim trust.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does supervised learning of trust-gate parameters and role-specific weights from human-annotated node/edge quality labels improve calibration and domain generalization?** Current cache-first calibration lacks gradient-based learning; human annotations could enhance adaptability but require new datasets.
2. **Does the fixed 10-role semantic ontology generalize to specialized scientific domains without degradation?** Domain-specific structures (e.g., "Organism Model" in biology) may be misrepresented, affecting extraction and scoring; cross-domain evaluation is needed.
3. **Can active learning selectively verify a subset of nodes while maintaining ranking accuracy, thereby reducing runtime and cost?** All nodes are currently verified; prioritizing critical nodes could approximate full-verification scores at lower resource use.
4. **How should multiple human annotators' triage labels be aggregated to produce a more reliable ground truth, and does this improve ranking over single-annotator weak supervision?** Current labels come from a single source, introducing noise; multi-annotator aggregation could yield cleaner signals but requires additional annotation effort.

## Limitations
- The 104-paper evaluation set with Good/Neutral/Bad labels is not public, preventing external verification of performance claims.
- The system references non-standard models (gpt-5-mini/gpt-5-nano); substitution with other LLMs will likely alter extraction quality and metrics.
- Web-agent behavior is sensitive to network conditions, paywalls, and CAPTCHAs; reported runtime (65.4 min) and cost ($0.15) may not generalize.
- Causal attribution of individual mechanisms (DAG structure, agent verification, trust propagation) to final AUROC is absent; contributions are inferred only from end-to-end results.
