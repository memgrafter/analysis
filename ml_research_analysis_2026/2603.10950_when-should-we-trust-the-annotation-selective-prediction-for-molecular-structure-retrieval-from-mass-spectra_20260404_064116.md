# When should we trust the annotation? Selective prediction for molecular structure retrieval from mass spectra

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10950
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10950
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Selective prediction using retrieval-level uncertainty scores (e.g., confidence, score gap, aleatoric) enables high coverage (e.g., 87% for Hit@20) at a controlled error rate (0.5) via distribution-free risk control (SGR).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of determining when machine learning predictions for molecular structure retrieval from tandem mass spectra can be trusted. It introduces a selective prediction framework that abstains from making a prediction when the model's uncertainty is high, thereby trading coverage for lower risk. The core contribution is a comprehensive evaluation of uncertainty scoring functions at two granularities: fingerprint-level (bitwise) and retrieval-level (candidate ranking). The results demonstrate that retrieval-level scores, particularly inexpensive first-order measures like confidence and score gap, align best with the retrieval task loss (Hit@K) and achieve strong risk-coverage trade-offs. In contrast, fingerprint-level scores and epistemic uncertainty estimates perform poorly. The authors further show that the distribution-free Selective Generalized Risk (SGR) algorithm can provide provable guarantees on the selective risk of accepted predictions, allowing practitioners to specify a tolerable error rate and obtain a high-coverage subset that satisfies this constraint with high probability.

## Method Summary
The approach builds on a baseline retrieval model: a three-layer MLP encodes a binned tandem mass spectrum into a 4096-dimensional probabilistic Morgan fingerprint. Candidate molecules are scored by cosine similarity between the predicted fingerprint and their known fingerprints, then ranked. The selective prediction framework augments this with a selection function `g_τ(x)` that abstains if a scoring function `κ(x)` falls below a threshold `τ`. Scoring functions `κ` include first-order measures (max confidence, top-two score gap), second-order uncertainty decompositions (aleatoric/epistelic via deep ensembles, MC dropout, or Laplace), and distance-based scores (k-NN, Mahalanobis). The threshold `τ*` is chosen by the SGR algorithm using a held-out calibration set to maximize coverage while ensuring the selective risk (empirical error rate on accepted set) is below a user-specified target `r*` with high probability, based on binomial (Clopper–Pearson) bounds.

## Key Results
- Retrieval-level scoring functions (especially first-order confidence, score gap, and retrieval-level aleatoric uncertainty) achieve significantly lower selective risk for the Hit@K loss compared to fingerprint-level scores, which are poor proxies for retrieval success.
- Epistemic uncertainty scores are consistently inferior to first-order and aleatoric scores for identifying reliable predictions in this retrieval setting.
- Distance-based uncertainty scores (k-NN, Mahalanobis) perform near random, indicating the learned representations are not well-suited for these measures.
- Under SGR risk control, high coverage can be obtained for moderate target risks; e.g., 87% coverage for Hit@20 with a target error rate of 0.5, while empirical risk stays below the target, providing a provable guarantee.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Granularity Alignment Between Score and Task Loss
- **Claim:** Retrieval-level scoring functions effectively reduce selective risk because they operate directly on the candidate ranking, which aligns with the retrieval task loss (Hit@K).
- **Mechanism:** The selection function uses a score computed from the distribution of candidate similarities (e.g., confidence = max softmax probability, gap = top-two score difference). This score correlates with whether the true molecule appears in the top-K candidates, as it reflects how decisively the model separates the correct candidate from competitors.
- **Core assumption:** The relevant uncertainty for the Hit@K loss is the stability of the top-K ranking, not the bitwise accuracy of the predicted fingerprint.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "retrieval-level scores, particularly first-order confidence measures and retrieval-level aleatoric uncertainty, achieve strong risk-coverage tradeoffs."
  - [section 3.2] relAURC values show retrieval-level scores (confidence, gap, aleatoric) significantly outperform fingerprint-level scores for Hit@K losses.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., JESTR) use joint embedding and ranking losses, implying retrieval-level optimization matters.
- **Break condition:** If the evaluation metric shifts to a fingerprint-similarity loss (e.g., Tanimoto), retrieval-level scores become less effective, as shown in Appendix D.1.

### Mechanism 2: Sufficiency of First-Order Scores Over Epistemic Uncertainty
- **Claim:** Computationally inexpensive first-order confidence measures (confidence, score gap) are as effective or superior to second-order epistemic uncertainty estimates for identifying reliable predictions in this setting.
- **Mechanism:** First-order scores require only a single forward pass and directly measure the separation between the top candidate and its nearest competitor in similarity space. This separation is a strong proxy for whether the true molecule is ranked highly.
- **Core assumption:** Epistemic uncertainty (model parameter disagreement) does not add predictive value beyond what is already captured by the peakedness of the candidate distribution for the Hit@K task.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "computationally inexpensive first-order confidence measures ... achieve strong risk-coverage tradeoffs" and "Epistemic uncertainty scores are consistently inferior."
  - [section 3.2] relAURC table: epistemic scores have the highest (worst) relAURC among retrieval-level scores for Hit@K.
  - [section 3.2 discussion] "For deciding whether to trust a particular prediction, what matters is the overall expected loss ... the epistemic component alone discards the aleatoric contribution."
  - [corpus] Neighbor paper on uncertainty quantification (paper 5500) argues "one size does not fit all," supporting that the utility of epistemic estimates is task-dependent.
- **Break condition:** If the model suffers from severe underfitting or distribution shift such that parameter disagreement correlates strongly with out-of-distribution inputs, epistemic measures might improve.

### Mechanism 3: Distribution-Free Risk Control via SGR
- **Claim:** The SGR algorithm provides provable guarantees on the selective risk of accepted annotations, enabling practitioners to specify a tolerable error rate and obtain a subset satisfying that constraint with high probability.
- **Mechanism:** SGR uses a held-out calibration set to find the highest-coverage threshold such that a binomial upper bound on the selective risk (Clopper–Pearson) is below a user-specified target risk `r*`, with a union bound over candidate thresholds to maintain overall confidence `δ`. This yields a threshold `τ*` with a guarantee `P(R(f, g_τ*) > b*) < δ`.
- **Core assumption:** The calibration and evaluation data are i.i.d. from the same distribution; the loss is binary (0/1) or bounded; and the scoring function ranks instances monotonically by risk.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We demonstrate that by applying distribution-free risk control via generalization bounds, practitioners can specify a tolerable error rate and obtain a subset of annotations satisfying that constraint with high probability."
  - [section 2.4] Exact algorithmic description of SGR with binomial bounds.
  - [section 3.4] Empirical risk on held-out evaluation set stays below target risk across settings (Figure 5 bottom), confirming the guarantee holds in practice.
  - [corpus] Neighbor paper on conformalized selective regression (paper 126207) uses similar risk control ideas but focuses on conformal p-values; SGR is a distinct algorithm.
- **Break condition:** If the calibration set is too small, the bounds become loose, reducing coverage. If there is distribution shift between calibration and deployment, the probabilistic guarantee may not hold.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Selective prediction framework**
  - *Why needed here:* The core problem is deciding when to trust a model's molecular annotation. Selective prediction formalizes the trade-off between coverage (fraction of spectra annotated) and risk (error rate on accepted ones), moving beyond overall accuracy.
  - *Quick check question:* How would you define a selection function `g(x)` for a retrieval model that abstains when the similarity gap between top candidates is below a threshold?
- **Concept: Aleatoric vs. epistemic uncertainty**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper decomposes uncertainty to understand which component aids selective prediction. Aleatoric uncertainty (data noise) is relevant for the specific instance; epistemic (model ignorance) is not as useful for Hit@K.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might epistemic uncertainty estimates be poorly correlated with retrieval success even if they capture model disagreement?
- **Concept: Risk-coverage curves and relAURC**
  - *Why needed here:* These metrics quantify the quality of a scoring function `κ` by measuring how much selective risk can be reduced as coverage decreases, normalized against oracle and random baselines.
  - *Quick check question:* What does a relAURC of 0.3 indicate about a scoring function's ability to prioritize easy instances?
- **Concept: Hit@K evaluation metric**
  - *Why needed here:* The task is molecular retrieval; success is defined as the true molecule being in the top-K ranked candidates. The loss is `1 - Hit@K`.
  - *Quick check question:* How does the difficulty of the task change as K increases, and how should that affect the choice of scoring function?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input encoder (binned spectrum) -> Fingerprint predictor (bit probabilities) -> Candidate scorer (cosine similarity) -> Ranking (sort candidates) -> Scoring function κ (compute uncertainty score) -> Selection function g_τ (thresholding) -> Output (accepted predictions with risk control)
- **Critical path:** Spectrum is encoded into fingerprint probabilities, candidate molecules are scored and ranked, a scoring function κ is computed from the candidate similarities (or bitwise probabilities), and a threshold τ (from SGR calibration) determines acceptance. The risk-control calibration step runs offline on a held-out set.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Computational cost:** First-order scores (confidence, gap) are cheapest (single forward pass); second-order scores require multiple inferences (ensemble members or MC Dropout), increasing compute ~S×. Distance-based scores require preprocessing training representations.
  - **Granularity alignment:** Using fingerprint-level κ for retrieval loss is ineffective (poor relAURC). Must match κ granularity to task loss (retrieval-level for Hit@K).
  - **Risk vs. coverage:** Higher target risk `r*` yields higher coverage but less strict error control. SGR maximizes coverage under the constraint.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Using epistemic uncertainty (κ_ep_ret or κ_ep_bit) as κ leads to near-random relAURC for Hit@K.
  - Using fingerprint-level κ (e.g., κ_tot_bit) for retrieval task yields relAURC ~0.9, barely better than random.
  - Applying SGR without sufficient calibration data (small n_cal) may lead to loose bounds and lower coverage at target risk.
  - Ignoring candidate set size variation: κ performance degrades when |C| is constant (cap 256), as seen in Figure 4c.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline retrieval:** Train the MLP with ranking loss; report Hit@1, @5, @20 on test set. Verify performance matches Table 2 (e.g., ~13% Hit@1, ~27% Hit@5, ~48% Hit@20).
  2. **Risk-coverage curves:** For a fixed model (e.g., Deep Ensemble with ranking loss), compute all κ scores on test set. For each κ, sweep threshold τ and plot selective risk vs. coverage for ℓ_K. Compute relAURC. Confirm retrieval-level scores (confidence, aleatoric, rank variance) are best for Hit@K.
  3. **Risk control with SGR:** Split test set into calibration and evaluation halves (50/50). For a chosen κ (e.g., confidence) and target risk r* (e.g., 0.5 for Hit@20), run SGR on calibration half to get τ*. Evaluate empirical risk and coverage on evaluation half. Verify empirical risk ≤ r* and coverage ~87% at r*=0.5 (Hit@20).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Does the superior performance of retrieval-level scoring functions generalize to state-of-the-art retrieval architectures beyond the MLP used in this study?
- **Open Question 2:** Can uncertainty quantification methods that account for the extreme sparsity of molecular fingerprints yield more reliable selection criteria than the bitwise aleatoric/epistemic decomposition used here?
- **Open Question 3:** For high-stakes metabolomics applications, does false discovery rate (FDR) control or conformal prediction provide better practical guarantees than the distribution-free risk control (SGR) evaluated in this work?
- **Open Question 4:** To what extent does candidate set size confound the effectiveness of scoring functions, and can a candidate-set-aware scoring function achieve stable selective prediction performance across varying retrieval difficulties?

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details (binning range, layer order, Adam hyperparameters) hinder exact replication.
- Findings are confined to MassSpecGym; generalizability to other MS datasets or candidate generation protocols is unverified.
- Retrieval-level scores’ advantage is tied to the Hit@K loss; for alternative objectives (e.g., Tanimoto similarity) the alignment may weaken.
- Distance-based uncertainty scores perform near randomly; the root cause (representation suitability or metric choice) remains unclear.
- SGR’s guarantees assume i.i.d. calibration data; real-world distribution shift could invalidate the probabilistic bound.
- Artificial capping of candidate set size at 256 may not reflect natural variability and influences scoring efficacy.

## Confidence
- Retrieval-level scoring alignment with Hit@K: **High**
- Fingerprint-level scores as poor proxies for retrieval: **High**
- Epistemic uncertainty inferiority: **Medium**
- Distance-based scores near random: **High**
- SGR distribution-free risk control soundness: **High**; practical coverage under limited calibration: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **External dataset validation:** Replicate the full pipeline on a held-out mass spectrometry repository (e.g., MoNA) to test whether retrieval-level scoring retains its advantage and SGR guarantees hold under different data distributions.
2. **Calibration set size ablation:** Systematically vary calibration size (e.g., 100, 250, 500, 1000) and measure achieved coverage at fixed target risks to quantify SGR’s sample complexity and bound tightness.
3. **Enhancing distance-based uncertainty:** Replace the penultimate representation with a contrastively learned embedding (e.g., from JESTR) and recompute k-NN and Mahalanobis scores; test whether relAURC improves, diagnosing whether representation or metric limits performance.
