# When Hate Meets Facts: LLMs-in-the-Loop for Check-worthiness Detection in Hate Speech

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25269
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25269
- **Reference count:** 24
- **Primary result:** An LLM-in-the-loop framework reduces human annotation effort for check-worthiness labeling by nearly half (to ~46.7% of claims) while maintaining quality, and incorporating check-worthiness improves hate speech detection for large models (macro-F1 +0.154 average).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of moderating online hate speech that embeds fact-like claims, which require verification but complicate automated detection. The authors introduce an LLM-in-the-loop framework to efficiently annotate the check-worthiness of individual claims within hateful and non-hateful messages. Validated on a new dataset, WSF-ARG+, the framework significantly cuts the fraction of claims needing expert human review without sacrificing annotation quality. Furthermore, messages containing at least one check-worthy claim are proven to be more severely hateful and harassing. Finally, using check-worthiness labels as auxiliary input consistently boosts the performance of large language models (70B+ parameters) on hate speech detection tasks.

## Method Summary
The core method is an LLM-in-the-loop annotation pipeline for a three-class check-worthiness task (CFS/UFS/NFS) on pre-extracted claims from the WSF-ARG+ corpus. For each claim, three independent zero/one-shot predictions are generated by the selected open-weight LLM (OLMo2-32B, temperature=1). A majority vote produces an initial LLM label. A first human annotator labels all claims in parallel. Claims where the LLM majority disagrees with the human label, or where all three LLM predictions are unequal, are flagged for review by a blind judge, whose decision is final. The final label set from this process is compared against a full human annotation ("platinum" labels) to evaluate quality. To test utility, the check-worthiness labels are then prepended to each claim within the full message and used as input for hate speech detection experiments with 12 different LLMs.

## Key Results
- Human annotation effort is reduced from 100% to ~46.7% of claims requiring judge intervention, with final annotation quality achieving Cohen's κ up to 0.483 (binary) compared to full human annotation.
- Hate speech messages containing at least one check-worthy claim (CFS) exhibit significantly higher levels of harassment and hate (Mann–Whitney U, effect sizes r=0.227 and r=0.154).
- Incorporating check-worthiness labels as input improves hate speech detection macro-F1 for large models (≥70B), with a peak gain of +0.213 (Command-r-104B) and an average improvement of +0.154 across large models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The LLM-in-the-loop framework reduces required human annotation effort for check-worthiness labeling without compromising final annotation quality.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs perform initial majority-vote annotations; a human reviews only cases where the LLM majority conflicts with the human label or where LLM predictions are internally inconsistent. A blind judge resolves final disagreements.
- **Core assumption:** LLM majority-vote predictions are sufficiently reliable for a substantial fraction of claims, and human review of only the uncertain subset yields final quality comparable to full human annotation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states effort reduction to ~46.7% with maintained quality. Section 3.2 and Figure 1 detail the parallel annotation and judge-trigger logic.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Hate speech messages containing at least one check-worthy claim (CFS) are significantly more hateful and harassing than those without.
- **Mechanism:** Fact-like assertions (CFS) provide a veneer of objectivity that intensifies perceived harm, as measured by external moderation scores. Check-worthiness signals the use of rhetorical strategies that amplify prejudice.
- **Core assumption:** The integration of verifiable factual claims into hate speech increases its psychological impact and social danger, beyond subjective scorn.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 7 report Mann–Whitney U tests and effect sizes (harassment r=0.227, hate r=0.154, p<0.05).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Incorporating check-worthiness labels as additional input improves hate speech detection performance, especially for large language models (≥70B parameters).
- **Mechanism:** Claim-level check-worthiness annotations provide discriminative features that help the model distinguish between hateful and non-hateful messages more effectively.
- **Core assumption:** Check-worthiness acts as a signal that enriches the representational context of hate speech, particularly for models with sufficient capacity to integrate auxiliary labels.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 7 (Table 6) show macro-F1 gains for large models when check-worthiness is included.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Check-worthiness taxonomy (CFS/UFS/NFS).
  - **Why needed:** The core annotation task requires distinguishing claims that are verifiable and of public interest (CFS) from those that are either unverifiable (UFS) or non-factual (NFS).
  - **Quick check:** For the claim "Africa is an area rich in natural resources," is it CFS or UFS? What contextual factor (e.g., specificity, verifiability, public debate) determines the label?
- **Concept:** LLM-as-annotator limitations (variability, bias).
  - **Why needed:** The framework's validity hinges on mitigating known LLM pitfalls like output instability. Without addressing this, the effort reduction would incur a severe quality cost.
  - **Quick check:** Why does the framework generate three LLM labels per instance and use majority voting? What specific LLM failure mode (e.g., temperature-induced randomness, positional bias) does this address?
- **Concept:** LLM-in-the-loop vs. human-in-the-loop.
  - **Why needed:** The design is an integrated, supervised auxiliary annotator that changes the human effort distribution, not a simple post-hoc verification system.
  - **Quick check:** How does the role of the human "judge" (blind adjudicator on a flagged subset) differ from a verifier in a typical human-in-the-loop pipeline where humans correct all outputs?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input (HS/Non-HS message + pre-extracted claims) -> Claim-level LLM Annotator (3 independent runs, same prompt) -> LLM Aggregator (majority vote, flag inconsistencies) -> Human Verifier (first annotator; checks only flagged/disputed claims) -> Judge (blind adjudicator for remaining disagreements) -> Output (Final gold label per claim; stores all LLM predictions and intermediate labels).
- **Critical path:** Claim -> LLM (3 runs) -> Majority vote -> Disagreement check -> If (LLM≠Human OR LLM inconsistent) -> Judge -> Final label. Else -> Accept LLM majority.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Cost vs. Quality:** Three LLM runs reduce judge load but increase compute; judge review scope is dynamically sized.
  - **Model choice:** Larger models don't guarantee higher agreement; family-specific optimization may outweigh scale (e.g., OLMo2-32B best).
  - **Blind judging:** Random label swapping prevents source bias but adds logistical overhead.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High judge intervention rate (>60% of claims) indicates poor LLM reliability for the specific model/prompt.
  - Low Cohen's κ (<0.3) between LLM majority and first human suggests systematic LLM misalignment.
  - Judge overrule rate of the first human label >70% suggests the first annotator may be inconsistent.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Establish human baseline:** Have two expert annotators independently label a stratified sample (e.g., 50 claims) to compute pairwise Cohen's κ and understand intrinsic task difficulty.
  2. **Screen LLM candidates:** Run all 12 LLM configs on the full set; compute % agreement and κ vs. first human; select top 2-3 models (considering variability) for the loop.
  3. **Human validation:** Run the full framework with the selected LLM; have a separate set of three annotators label the entire dataset blind to source; compare final framework labels against these new platinum labels via Cohen's κ.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly identify any open questions; it focuses on presenting the framework and its validation.

## Limitations
- Annotator identities, training, and demographics are unknown, weakening baseline quality assessment.
- Judge criteria and guidelines are undisclosed, introducing potential arbitration bias.
- Results are confined to the WSF-ARG+ dataset; generalizability across platforms and cultures is uncertain.
- LLM variability (temperature=1) leads to high inconsistency, increasing judge load.
- Moderate Cohen's κ (~0.483) and unreported full human agreement obscure the true cost-quality tradeoff.
- Detection gains are observed only for 70B+ models; benefits for smaller LLMs are unclear.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Effort Reduction):** Medium. Moderate κ and LLM instability suggest a fragile tradeoff; human baseline is unclear.
- **Claim 2 (Harassment Correlation):** Medium. Small effect sizes on a single dataset; limited external validity.
- **Claim 3 (Detection Improvement):** High. Consistent gains for large models across evaluations, though dependent on label quality and model scale.

## Next Checks
1. **Gold Standard Replication:** Independent annotators label a held-out sample; compare their consensus to framework labels to compute Cohen's κ and judge agreement.
2. **Cross-Dataset Test:** Apply the pipeline to an external corpus (e.g., HateXplain) to verify the harassment correlation and hate speech detection gains.
3. **Sensitivity Analysis:** Vary the number of LLM runs (1,3,5) and temperature; measure judge intervention rate and final κ to identify optimal configuration and failure modes.
