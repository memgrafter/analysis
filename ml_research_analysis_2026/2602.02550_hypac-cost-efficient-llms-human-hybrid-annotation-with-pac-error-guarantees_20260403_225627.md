# HyPAC: Cost-Efficient LLMs-Human Hybrid Annotation with PAC Error Guarantees

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02550
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02550
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** HyPAC reduces annotation cost by up to 78.51% while provably maintaining error below a specified tolerance (e.g., 5%) across multiple benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
HyPAC introduces a threshold-based routing framework for cost-efficient hybrid annotation using multiple sources (non-thinking LLMs, reasoning/thinking models, and humans). It selects two uncertainty thresholds during calibration to route each input to the cheapest source that satisfies a distribution-free (ϵ,α)-PAC guarantee on annotation error. By leveraging importance sampling and upper confidence bounds, HyPAC minimizes expected cost among all threshold-based policies under the same error constraint. Empirical evaluation on five benchmarks shows substantial cost savings over baselines like PAC labeling and CSE, while tightly controlling error. The method is robust to the choice of uncertainty score and calibration size, though it relies on a monotonic accuracy ordering among sources.

## Method Summary
HyPAC operates in two phases. In **calibration**, it precomputes uncertainty scores for a pool of unlabeled examples using both models. A subset of these examples is then labeled by human annotators with probability *p*. For every candidate pair of thresholds *(u₁, u₂)* on a grid, it computes an importance-weighted risk estimate and an upper confidence bound (UCB) that covers the true risk with probability ≥1−α. Among all pairs whose UCB is ≤ϵ, it selects the pair that maximizes *u₂* and then *u₁* (equivalently, minimizes expected cost due to monotonicity). In **deployment**, for a new input *x*, HyPAC computes its uncertainty score *U(x)* and routes *x* to the non-thinking model if *U(x) ≤ u₁*, to the thinking model if *u₁ < U(x) ≤ u₂*, or to a human if *U(x) > u₂*. The objective is to minimize the expected annotation cost while guaranteeing P(Risk ≤ ϵ) ≥ 1−α.

## Key Results
- Cost reduction up to **78.51%** compared to human-only annotation across five benchmarks.
- Empirical error consistently below the specified tolerance (e.g., 5%) with high probability.
- Outperforms baselines such as PAC labeling and CSE in cost-efficiency under the same PAC constraint.
- Robust to calibration size and uncertainty score choice, though logits-based scores yield lower variance and higher savings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Uncertainty-based routing via thresholds
**Claim:** HyPAC minimizes annotation cost by routing each input to the cheapest source that satisfies a risk budget, using two uncertainty thresholds.  
**Mechanism:** An uncertainty score *U(x)* ∈ [0,1] is computed for each input. Two thresholds *u₁ ≤ u₂* partition the score space: if *U(x) ≤ u₁* → cheap non-thinking model; if *u₁ < U(x) ≤ u₂* → more accurate thinking model; if *U(x) > u₂* → human. Higher scores indicate examples likely to be mis-annotated by weaker models.  
**Core assumption:** The sources have a fixed per-input accuracy ordering: human > thinking > non‑thinking. This monotonicity ensures that increasing thresholds routes more examples to cheaper but less accurate sources, raising risk.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "routing each to the appropriate annotation source"; [section 2] "We assume that the quality of annotation sources follows: human annotation has the highest quality, followed by the thinking model, then the non‑thinking model."; [corpus] Neighbor works study hybrid decision systems, but HyPAC uniquely provides formal multi‑source guarantees.  
**Break condition:** The monotonicity proof breaks if the accuracy ordering is violated anywhere (e.g., non‑thinking sometimes outperforms thinking). Then the optimality and PAC arguments may fail.

### Mechanism 2: PAC guarantee via UCB
**Claim:** HyPAC provides a distribution‑free *(ε,α)*-PAC guarantee: with probability ≥1−α over the calibration draw, the selected thresholds yield annotation error ≤ε.  
**Mechanism:** For each candidate threshold pair *(u₁,u₂)*, use importance sampling to compute an unbiased risk estimate *R̂(u₁,u₂)* from a subset of human labels. Construct an upper confidence bound *L̂_{u₁,u₂}(α)* that covers the true risk with probability ≥1−α (using Hoeffding, Bernstein, or CLT). Then pick thresholds with *L̂ ≤ ε* that minimize empirical cost.  
**Core assumption:** The chosen UCB is valid for every fixed *(u₁,u₂)*. Calibration and test data are i.i.d., and models are fixed (no retraining).  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "providing distribution‑free guarantees on annotation error"; [section 3.2] Equation (7) defines the UCB; Theorem 4.3 proves the PAC guarantee; [corpus] Neighbor papers derive PAC bounds, but HyPAC adapts them to multi‑source routing.  
**Break condition:** If the UCB is invalid due to non‑i.i.d. calibration data, unbounded losses, or extreme small‑sample deviations, the PAC guarantee can be violated.

### Mechanism 3: Cost optimality among threshold‑based methods
**Claim:** Among all threshold‑based policies satisfying the same PAC constraint, HyPAC attains the lowest expected annotation cost.  
**Mechanism:** Expected cost decreases monotonically as thresholds increase (Lemma 4.2). Hence, the cost‑minimizing thresholds are the largest ones inside the feasible region {*(u₁,u₂)*: L̂(u₁,u₂) ≤ ε} (with *u₂* maximized first, then *u₁*). HyPAC selects exactly this maximal feasible pair.  
**Core assumption:** Cost functions obey *c₁(x) ≤ c₂(x) ≤ c_h(x)* almost surely, and strict inequalities hold for a positive measure of inputs. The feasible region is defined by the same UCB constraint.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "achieves the minimum expected cost among all threshold‑based methods satisfying the same PAC constraint."; [section 4.2] Theorem 4.4 and Lemma 4.2 (Cost monotonicity); [corpus] While works like "Hybrid Reinforcement Learning" consider cost‑aware sampling, HyPAC uniquely proves optimality for threshold‑based multi‑source annotation.  
**Break condition:** Violating the cost ordering (e.g., thinking more expensive than human on some inputs) destroys monotonicity, so the selected thresholds may no longer be cost‑optimal. Non‑threshold policies could also be cheaper.

## Foundational Learning
- **Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) Learning:** Framework for generalization bounds with confidence 1−α and error ε. **Why needed:** HyPAC’s core guarantee is PAC‑style: it asserts that the selected thresholds yield annotation error ≤ε with high probability. **Quick check:** What is required of the selected thresholds to be *(ε,α)*-PAC? They must satisfy that the true risk of the routing policy is ≤ε with probability ≥1−α over the calibration draw.
- **Importance Sampling:** Estimator that reweights samples drawn with probability *p* to obtain unbiased expectations. **Why needed:** To estimate risk using only a subset of human labels, keeping calibration cost low. **Quick check:** Why does weighting by *1/p* produce an unbiased risk estimate? Because each human-labeled example is upweighted by the inverse of its selection probability, correcting for the missing labels in the majority of examples.
- **Upper Confidence Bounds (UCB):** Intervals that cover a true parameter with high probability. **Why needed:** To convert finite risk estimates into valid high‑probability bounds for threshold selection, enabling the PAC constraint. **Quick check:** Why must UCBs hold uniformly over the whole threshold grid in theory? Because the threshold pair is chosen based on empirical minimization over the grid; without uniform validity, the selected pair’s risk could exceed the bound with probability >α.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Calibration module (compute predictions & uncertainty scores on pool) → Sample human labels with probability *p* → For each threshold pair in grid G: compute importance-weighted risk bRIS, empirical cost bC, and UCB bL → Filter pairs with bL ≤ ε → Pick maximal feasible *(u₂,u₁)* lexicographically (or minimal bC) → Store chosen thresholds → Deployment module (for new *x*: compute *U(x)*, compare to thresholds, route to source, return label).
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Precompute model predictions and uncertainty scores on all calibration examples.  
  2. Sample human labels with probability *p*=0.9.  
  3. Iterate over threshold grid G: for each *(u₁,u₂)*, compute bRIS (importance-weighted), bC (weighted cost), and bL (UCB).  
  4. Identify feasible set F = {*(u₁,u₂)*: bL ≤ ε}. If F empty, fallback to thinking model or human.  
  5. Select *(ũ₁,ũ₂)* ∈ F with largest *u₂*, then largest *u₁* (or minimal bC).  
  6. Deploy thresholds: for each incoming *x*, route based on *U(x)* relative to *(ũ₁,ũ₂)*.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Uncertainty score:* Logits‑based (requires model logits) is better calibrated; verbalized works for black‑box but less reliable.  
  - *Sampling probability p:* Higher *p* reduces variance of bRIS but increases human queries; default *p=0.9* works well.  
  - *UCB method:* CLT‑based is simple but asymptotic; Hoeffding/Bernstein give finite‑sample; betting‑based often tightest.  
  - *Threshold grid G:* Finer grid improves threshold quality but increases calibration time; coarse grids may miss optimal pair.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Test error exceeds ε:* Likely UCB too narrow (small *m*) or distribution shift.  
  - *No feasible thresholds (F=∅):* Calibration set too small or models too weak; fallback to thinking model exclusively.  
  - *High cost despite error control:* Uncertainty score not informative; many examples routed to expensive sources. Check score calibration (reliability diagrams).  
  - *Large run‑to‑run variance:* Calibration set too small or *p* too low; increase *m* or *p*.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Synthetic smoke test:* Generate known distributions with monotonic risk/cost; verify that repeated random calibrations achieve error ≤ ε with frequency near 1−α.  
  2. *UCB comparison:* Fix calibration set, run HyPAC with CLT, Hoeffding, Bernstein, betting UCBs; compare coverage and cost savings on a held‑out test.  
  3. *Calibration size sweep:* Vary calibration fraction (e.g., 100, 300, 500) and plot cost savings vs. empirical error; observe stability and diminishing returns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Does HyPAC's PAC guarantee require the thinking model to be uniformly more accurate than the non‑thinking model on every input, or can it handle cases where the non‑thinking model outperforms on some subsets?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] Lemma 4.1 proves risk monotonicity under the assumption that the thinking model is strictly more accurate than the non‑thinking model, implying a uniform per‑instance superiority.  
**Why unresolved:** If this uniform ordering fails, risk may not be monotonic in the thresholds, breaking the nested structure of the feasible region and invalidating the fixed‑sequence testing argument that ensures the PAC guarantee.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Construct synthetic examples where the non‑thinking model is better on a subset defined by the uncertainty score, then empirically measure whether the actual error rate exceeds ϵ with probability greater than α.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** What specific properties of the uncertainty score U(x) (e.g., calibration, correlation with model error) are necessary for HyPAC to achieve high cost savings?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] Experiments show logits‑based scores yield higher cost savings and lower variance than verbalized scores, but the paper does not explain why or provide theoretical guidance on score design.  
**Why unresolved:** Without understanding what makes a score effective, practitioners cannot optimally choose or design uncertainty measures for hybrid annotation, limiting the method's applicability.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically evaluate multiple score functions across diverse models and tasks, correlating metrics like expected calibration error with cost savings; derive theoretical conditions linking score informativeness to threshold optimality.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What is the minimum calibration set size m needed for HyPAC to satisfy the (ϵ, α)-PAC guarantee with high probability?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method relies on upper confidence bounds constructed via CLT or Hoeffding inequalities, but no finite‑sample complexity bound on m is provided; only empirical robustness is shown.  
**Why unresolved:** Without a theoretical guarantee on required m, users cannot determine how much calibration data is necessary for reliable deployment, especially in low‑resource settings.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Derive a concentration inequality that accounts for data‑dependent threshold selection from a grid, yielding a sample complexity bound; validate by measuring the rate of PAC violation as a function of m on multiple datasets.

## Limitations
- **Monotonicity assumption:** Requires a fixed per‑input accuracy ordering (human > thinking > non‑thinking); violations can invalidate both the PAC guarantee and cost optimality.
- **Distribution and loss assumptions:** PAC proof assumes i.i.d. calibration/test data and bounded losses; covariate shift or unbounded losses may cause UCB undercoverage and error control failure.
- **Threshold‑based optimality:** Theoretical optimality holds only among threshold‑based routers; more expressive (e.g., learned) policies might achieve lower cost but are not considered.
