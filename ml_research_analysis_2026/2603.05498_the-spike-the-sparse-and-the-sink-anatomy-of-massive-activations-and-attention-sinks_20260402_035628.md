# The Spike, the Sparse and the Sink: Anatomy of Massive Activations and Attention Sinks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05498
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05498
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Pre-norm Transformers cause massive activations (channel outliers) and attention sinks to co-occur due to early feed-forward quadratic amplification and normalization-induced sparsification; the phenomena can be independently suppressed via architectural modifications without hurting perplexity.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores the systematic co-occurrence of massive activations—extreme values in specific channels—and attention sinks—tokens that attract disproportionate attention—in pre-norm Transformer language models. Through extensive experiments and mechanistic analysis, the authors identify the pre-norm configuration as the key architectural factor linking the two. They show that early SwiGLU feed-forward blocks act as directional quadratic amplifiers, injecting spike tokens that persist through residuals. Normalization (RMSNorm) then transforms these spikes into sparse, near-constant vectors, creating a low-dimensional key subspace to which attention heads with aligned query subspaces assign excessive attention. Ablations demonstrate that modifying normalization (e.g., sandwich norm) or head dimension can decouple the phenomena without increasing perplexity, indicating their incidental nature rather than functional necessity.

## Method Summary
The study combines empirical tracing and targeted ablations across multiple LLaMA-family decoder models. Activation magnitudes were monitored layer-by-layer to identify “step-up” (spike injection) and “step-down” (spike suppression) blocks. Spectral analysis of feed-forward weight matrices revealed a dominant eigenvalue associated with spike directions. Normalization variants (Sandwich Norm, QKNorm, DynamicTanh) were applied to assess decoupling. Head dimension was systematically varied while keeping total attention capacity constant to isolate its effect on sink strength. Training runs with curated context length distributions (all-short vs. all-long) tested the curriculum hypothesis. Geometric properties of attention subspaces were examined via t-SNE visualizations and alignment metrics. The methodology balances quantitative measurements (sink ratio, activation norms, perplexity) with qualitative mechanistic interpretation.

## Key Results
- Pre-norm architecture is the primary factor linking massive activations and attention sinks; alternative normalization schemes can break the association.
- Early feed-forward blocks generate spikes via a quadratic amplifier mechanism rooted in SwiGLU’s structure and weight matrix eigenvalue spectra.
- RMSNorm maps spike tokens to sparse, nearly token-invariant representations, enabling attention sinks through geometric separation of key subspaces.
- Ablations show that spikes and sinks can be suppressed independently: sandwich norm reduces activation magnitudes without lowering sink ratio, while reducing head dimension weakens sinks.
- Training on short contexts induces strong sinks; models trained exclusively on long sequences exhibit near-zero sink ratios.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Early feed-forward blocks as directional quadratic amplifiers
- **Claim:** Massive activations are introduced by early feed-forward blocks acting as directional quadratic amplifiers.
- **Mechanism:** In pre-norm Transformers, SwiGLU-based feed-forward blocks contain high-gain quadratic forms (Uk matrices) with a shared dominant eigenvector \(s^\star\). When a token’s normalized hidden state \(\tilde{h}\) aligns with \(s^\star\), the squared projection \((\tilde{h}^\top s^\star)^2\) is amplified by a large eigenvalue \(\lambda^\star\), producing extreme outlier values in specific channels.
- **Core assumption:** The SiLU gate operates in a near-identity regime (\(\text{SiLU}(x) \approx x\)), reducing the feed-forward computation to a quadratic form.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and Section 3.1.2 describe the amplifier effect and show spectral plots with a single dominant eigenvalue for spike channels. Corpus evidence from related work also links outlier growth to MLP amplification.
- **Break condition:** If the quadratic form matrices \(U_k\) lack a clear dominant eigenvalue or token representations fail to align with that direction, massive activations will not emerge.

### Mechanism 2: Normalization transforms spikes into sparse, near-constant vectors
- **Claim:** Normalization (RMSNorm) maps spike tokens into sparse, near-constant vectors that enable attention sinks.
- **Mechanism:** RMSNorm bounds the extreme magnitudes of spike tokens (\(|\tilde{h}_i| \le \sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}\)) and sparsifies their representations by suppressing non-spike channels. Because spike channels maintain fixed inter-channel ratios, normalized spike vectors become approximately token-invariant. This creates a low-dimensional, nearly constant key subspace for sink tokens.
- **Core assumption:** Pre-norm allows unbounded residual accumulation, so massive activations persist until normalization.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and Section 3.2.1 state the collapse into near-constant sparse vectors; Theorem B.3 proves boundedness. Corpus includes work on normalization’s role in outlier emergence.
- **Break condition:** Replacing RMSNorm with an element-wise bounded transformation (e.g., DynamicTanh) or adding post-block normalization (Sandwich Norm) prevents the creation of sparse, near-constant vectors, decoupling spikes from sinks.

### Mechanism 3: Attention sinks form via geometric separation of key subspaces
- **Claim:** Attention sinks arise when sink keys and non-sink keys occupy geometrically separable subspaces, with sink heads having query subspaces aligned closer to the fixed sink-key subspace.
- **Mechanism:** The sparsity and near-constancy of normalized spike tokens restrict their key projections to a very low-dimensional subspace (often 1–2 dimensions). If a per-head query subspace lies closer to this sink-key subspace than to the non-sink-key subspace, attention logits for the sink token become consistently large, creating a sink.
- **Core assumption:** Attention head dimension (\(d_{\text{head}}\)) provides enough capacity for subspaces to segregate.
- **Evidence:** Abstract notes sink operation across heads; Section 3.2.2 shows geometric separation; Section 4.3.1 ablates head dimension (larger \(d_{\text{head}}\) increases sink ratio). Corpus includes papers manipulating subspaces to address sinks.
- **Break condition:** If head dimension is too small (e.g., \(d_{\text{head}}=8\)), subspaces cannot cleanly separate, preventing strong sinks. Alternatively, training without short contexts removes the need for sinks.

## Foundational Learning

1. **Next-token prediction (autoregressive training)**
   - **Why needed:** The paper’s analysis depends on the training objective; the co-occurrence of spikes and sinks emerges under next-token prediction with teacher forcing and parallel loss computation.
   - **Quick check:** How does next-token prediction differ from masked language modeling, and why might that difference matter for residual accumulation?

2. **Pre-norm Transformer architecture (RMSNorm before each block)**
   - **Why needed:** The core causal claim identifies pre-norm as the key factor linking spikes and sinks; understanding residual recurrence \(H_{i+1} = H_i + F_i(\text{RMSNorm}(H_i))\) is essential.
   - **Quick check:** In a pre-norm block, what is the order of operations, and how does that differ from post-norm? Why might pre-norm allow unbounded residual growth?

3. **SwiGLU activation (SiLU gating)**
   - **Why needed:** The quadratic amplifier mechanism relies on SwiGLU’s structure; the near-identity regime of SiLU is a specific assumption that simplifies the feed-forward to a quadratic form.
   - **Quick check:** How does SwiGLU differ from a standard GeLU feed-forward block, and why might SwiGLU concentrate outlier growth into fewer layers?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Input → Token embedding → [L blocks: RMSNorm → Attention → Residual → RMSNorm → SwiGLU → Residual] → Final RMSNorm → LM head → Output.
- **Critical path:** Early feed-forward blocks (step-up) inject massive activations → residuals propagate unchanged → normalization in later attention blocks maps spike tokens to sparse, near-constant vectors → attention heads with aligned query subspaces assign disproportionate mass to sink keys → sink behavior manifests.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Pre-norm vs. Sandwich Norm: Pre-norm enables spike accumulation; Sandwich Norm bounds outputs, suppressing spikes but preserving sink ratio via alternative mechanisms.
  - Head dimension vs. number of heads: Larger \(d_{\text{head}}\) increases sink strength; distributing fixed total attention capacity into fewer, larger heads intensifies sinks.
  - Training context distribution: Including short sequences induces sinks; training only on long sequences collapses sink ratio.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Spikes persist without step-down blocks (late layers fail to neutralize).
  - Sink ratio remains high even after eliminating spikes via DynamicTanh or QKNorm, showing decoupling.
  - Gated attention (per-head/channel conditional gating) eliminates sinks, indicating sinks serve as an implicit routing substitute.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Trace activation magnitudes across depth:** For a fixed prompt, record top-3 channel magnitudes after each residual connection (post-residuals). Expect a “rise–plateau–fall” pattern identifying step-up/step-down blocks.
  2. **Ablate normalization:** Replace RMSNorm with Sandwich Norm (add RMSNorm after block output) or QKNorm (normalize only Q/K). Measure spike magnitude (max channel norm) and sink ratio. Expect spikes to drop while sink ratio stays similar.
  3. **Vary head dimension:** Train models with identical total attention capacity (\(d_{\text{head}} \times N_{\text{head}}\)) but different \(d_{\text{head}}\) (e.g., 32/128 vs. 128/32). Evaluate sink ratio. Expect larger \(d_{\text{head}}\) to increase sink ratio, confirming geometric alignment hypothesis.

## Limitations
- Scope of validity: Findings rely on LLaMA-family decoder models; unknown whether they generalize to other Transformer variants (e.g., encoder-decoder or post-norm architectures).
- Theoretical assumptions: The mechanism assumes SiLU gating operates near identity and that weight matrices exhibit a single dominant eigenvector; these conditions may not hold universally.
- Causal evidence: Ablations demonstrate decoupling but do not establish necessity; alternative explanations (e.g., implicit regularization) remain untested.

## Confidence
- Co-occurrence of massive activations and attention sinks in pre-norm Transformers and their decoupling via normalization modifications: **High**
- Early feed-forward blocks as directional quadratic amplifiers: **Medium**
- Geometric subspace separation between sink and non-sink keys as primary driver of attention sinks: **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture validation:** Replicate the analysis on encoder-decoder (e.g., T5) and post-norm (e.g., GPT-2) models to test generalizability of spikes and sinks.
