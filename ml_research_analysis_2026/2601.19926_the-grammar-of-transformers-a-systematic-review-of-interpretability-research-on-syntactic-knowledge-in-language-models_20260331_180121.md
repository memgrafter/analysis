# The Grammar of Transformers: A Systematic Review of Interpretability Research on Syntactic Knowledge in Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.19926
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19926
- **Reference count:** 40 (analyzed corpus of 337 studies)
- **Primary result:** A systematic review of 337 studies reveals severe over-focus on English (69%) and BERT (30% of results), heavy emphasis on morphosyntax over syntax-semantics interfaces, and a general syntactic competence gradient across models (mean Kendall’s τ = 0.58 on BLiMP).

## Executive Summary
This systematic review maps the empirical landscape of interpretability research on syntactic knowledge in Transformer language models (TLMs). Analyzing 337 peer-reviewed studies encompassing 1,015 model results, it uncovers a highly fragmented field dominated by English-language evaluations and BERT-family models. Syntactic phenomena are unevenly covered: form-oriented morphosyntax (e.g., agreement, part-of-speech) is extensively studied, while the syntax-semantics interface (e.g., binding, ellipsis, quantifier scope) remains underexplored. Behavioral evaluation using the BLiMP benchmark shows that models achieve an average accuracy of 72%, with superior performance on formal morphosyntax (>85%) compared to interface phenomena (<75%). Model rankings on BLiMP are highly correlated across categories, suggesting a general syntactic competence gradient that scales with data and model size. The review advocates for broader linguistic diversity, standardized reporting, and a shift toward causal interpretability methods.

## Method Summary
The review employed a structured systematic review methodology. Candidate articles (n=636) were identified via targeted keyword searches on Google Scholar and snowballing from six existing literature reviews. Studies were manually screened for eligibility: peer-reviewed empirical research on Transformer LMs assessing syntactic structure/knowledge, excluding non-Transformer models, machine translation, non-empirical work, and non-peer-reviewed venues. A total of 337 articles were included. Annotation used a semi-automated AI workflow (NotebookLM + ChatGPT) for 5 variables, with manual annotation for 21 others across 26 total variables (meta-info, model specs, experimental design, interpretability techniques, findings). Validation against a manual gold standard (n=91) yielded a median ChatGPT rating of 4.1/5 for AI-generated summaries.

## Key Results
- **Empirical coverage:** 69% of studies use English; BERT-family models appear in 30% of all reported model results. Morphosyntactic phenomena (e.g., agreement) are heavily studied, while syntax-semantics interfaces are sparse.
- **BLiMP benchmark performance:** Across 25 studies, average accuracy is 72%. Formal morphosyntax (e.g., agreement) exceeds 85%, while interface phenomena (e.g., quantifiers, islands) fall below 75%. Performance scales reliably with training data and model size.
- **General competence gradient:** Model rankings on BLiMP are highly correlated across categories (mean Kendall’s τ = 0.58), indicating a unitary factor underlying syntactic performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Specialized Attention Heads Encode Syntactic Relations
- **Claim:** Some attention heads specialize in tracking specific syntactic dependencies (e.g., subject-verb agreement).
- **Mechanism:** The self-supervised language modeling objective (predicting masked/next tokens) creates pressure to leverage syntactic context, driving the emergence of attention heads that consistently attend to grammatically related words.
- **Evidence anchors:** The review notes that studies find models "encode at least some syntactic relations via specialized attention heads" (López-Otal et al., 2025). Attention-head analysis is a common mechanistic method in the corpus (e.g., Clark et al., 2019; Vig and Belinkov, 2019).
- **Break condition:** If models trained on synthetically shuffled or non-linguistic sequences fail to develop specialized heads and show degraded syntactic performance, the mechanism would be undermined.

### Mechanism 2: Linear Decodability of Syntactic Structure from Contextual Embeddings
- **Claim:** Syntactic information (e.g., dependency trees, POS) is linearly representable in middle layers of Transformers.
- **Mechanism:** The geometry of contextual embedding spaces arranges tokens such that syntactic properties can be separated by linear classifiers, likely because the training objective forces disentangled representations for grammatical functions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Probing methods, which train auxiliary classifiers on activations, are widely used (see Fig. 4). Structural probes (Hewitt and Manning, 2019) are a prominent example for dependency trees.
- **Break condition:** If amnesic probing or interventions removing linearly decodable syntax do not affect downstream performance, the linear decodability may be epiphenomenal.

### Mechanism 3: General Syntactic Competence Gradient Across Models
- **Claim:** Models possess a general syntactic ability that transfers across phenomena; models performing well on one category tend to perform well on others.
- **Mechanism:** The language modeling objective encourages acquisition of a unified syntactic system; scale (data/parameters) improves this general competence by enabling deeper generalizations.
- **Evidence anchors:** BLiMP rankings show high cross-category correlation (mean Kendall’s τ = 0.58). Analysis across 25 studies with ≥31 datapoints per phenomenon supports this gradient.
- **Break condition:** If benchmarks controlling for lexical overlap yield lower correlations, the gradient may be an artifact of BLiMP’s design.

## Foundational Learning
- **Large-Scale Pretraining Data:** Syntax is acquired implicitly from diverse linguistic contexts; more tokens increase exposure to constructions. *Quick check:* Does syntactic performance (e.g., BLiMP score) increase monotonically with data size, or plateau? (Per paper: linear in log-scale, no plateau.)
- **Model Capacity (Parameters):** Larger models have greater representational capacity for hierarchical structures. *Quick check:* Within an architecture family, do larger parameter counts correlate with higher syntactic accuracy? (Per paper: yes.)
- **Bidirectional Contextualization:** Bidirectional training (e.g., MLM) allows conditioning on the entire sentence, crucial for non-local dependencies. *Quick check:* For comparable data/size, do bidirectional models outperform causal models on syntactic benchmarks? (Per paper: yes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input embeddings -> Stack of Transformer blocks (self-attention + feed-forward) -> Output layer
- **Critical path:** Start with behavioral evaluation (e.g., BLiMP) to quantify competence → use probing (linear/non-linear) to localize information → apply mechanistic interventions (e.g., activation patching, attention head ablation) to test causality.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Behavioral methods: easy to run, but no mechanism insight.
  - Probing: scalable, but risk of probe artifacts and lack of causal claims.
  - Mechanistic: detailed causal understanding, but labor-intensive and may not generalize.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High performance on morphosyntax but low on syntax–semantics interface (e.g., binding, quantifiers).
  - Large variance across models on certain BLiMP categories (e.g., island effects).
  - Probe success that does not correlate with behavioral performance.
  - Over-reliance on lexical superficial cues (e.g., high scores on datasets with lexical overlap).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline behavioral assessment:** Run a model (e.g., BERT-base) on English BLiMP; compare scores to reported average (~72%).
  2. **Linear structural probe:** Train a linear probe to predict dependency distances from middle-layer representations; evaluate accuracy and layer-wise trends.
  3. **Attention-head analysis:** Compute correlation between attention weights and gold dependency edges; identify top heads for agreement vs. long-distance dependencies.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** To what extent do Transformer LMs acquire syntactic competence solely through self-supervised pretraining?
  - *Basis:* Explicitly stated as overarching research question: “What do TLMs learn about syntax from the language modeling task?”
  - *Why unresolved:* Heterogeneity in models, languages, and phenomena; over-reliance on English/BERT; methodological inconsistencies.
  - *Evidence needed:* Controlled studies varying pretraining objectives, architectures, and data sizes across diverse languages with standardized benchmarks and causal methods.
- **Open Question 2:** How do models perform on syntax-semantics interface phenomena across typologically diverse languages, and why is performance weaker?
  - *Basis:* Inferred from results showing lower scores on interface phenomena (Fig. 5) and severe under-study (Fig. 3).
  - *Why unresolved:* Evaluations dominated by English/morphosyntax; BLiMP’s limited coverage cannot capture complexity.
  - *Evidence needed:* Multilingual benchmarks targeting interface phenomena; systematic evaluation and error analysis across languages.
- **Open Question 3:** What are the causal neural mechanisms (e.g., specific heads, circuits) for syntactic processing?
  - *Basis:* Explicit call to “decisively move towards causal approaches” noting probing/behavioral methods lack causality.
  - *Why unresolved:* Mechanistic studies are uncommon and not scalable; lack standardized techniques for large models.
  - *Evidence needed:* Surge in causal interventions (ablation, steering, circuit analysis) across models/tasks, with shared frameworks and replicated findings.
- **Open Question 4:** How can the community standardize definition, evaluation, and reporting of syntactic phenomena?
  - *Basis:* Inferred from discussion of fragmented evaluation map due to varying terminology/benchmarks and poor reporting.
  - *Why unresolved:* Studies use different constructs, benchmarks, and omit critical details (model specs, per-category scores).
  - *Evidence needed:* Community-wide adoption of standardized annotation schemes, mandatory reporting guidelines, modular benchmark suites; use of the paper’s database as foundation.

## Limitations
- AI-assisted annotation details (prompts, processing steps) are undisclosed, hindering replication.
- Database schema and 26 variable definitions are not provided (promised in external repo).
- Over-focus on English (69%) and BERT (30%) threatens generalizability of conclusions.
- BLiMP-based correlations may reflect dataset artifacts (e.g., lexical overlap).
- High incidence of missing data in source studies (model specs, scores).

## Confidence
- **Empirical coverage claims** (language/model/phenomenon distribution): Medium (large sample but potential selection bias/AI noise).
- **BLiMP performance & scaling results**: Medium (aggregated across heterogeneous studies with missing details).
- **Model ranking correlation** (Kendall’s τ = 0.58): Medium (limited model diversity and BLiMP design may inflate).
- **Interpretability method trends** (probing decline): Medium (annotation reliability uncertain).

## Next Checks
1. **Inter-annotator reliability check:** Re-annotate a random 30% of studies with ≥2 human experts using a codebook derived from the paper’s categories; report Cohen’s κ and discrepancies with AI-assisted annotations.
2. **BLiMP artifact control:** Test state-of-the-art models on a minimal-pair BLiMP variant eliminating lexical overlap; recompute cross-category correlations to assess robustness.
3. **Cross-linguistic extension:** Conduct a mini-review of syntactic interpretability studies for at least three non-English languages (e.g., German, Chinese, Arabic) to test if the general competence gradient holds.
