# Dichotomy of Feature Learning and Unlearning: Fast-Slow Analysis on Neural Networks with Stochastic Gradient Descent

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07378
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07378
- **Reference count**: 6
- **Primary result**: Feature unlearning in two-layer networks arises from mismatched Hermite coefficients, causing alignment decay to zero and scale divergence via slow manifold dynamics.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies the phenomenon of feature unlearning in two-layer neural networks trained with stochastic gradient descent (SGD). By employing the infinite-width limit and Tensor Programs, the authors reduce the high-dimensional SGD dynamics to a deterministic low-dimensional ordinary differential equation (ODE) governing two macroscopic variables: the alignment \(R\) between first-layer weights and the teacher, and the second-layer scale \(a\). A fast-slow analysis reveals that \(R\) evolves on a fast timescale, while \(a\) evolves slowly; the long-term behavior is confined to a critical slow manifold. Feature unlearning—where the student loses alignment with the teacher while its scale diverges—occurs when the Hermite coefficients of the teacher and student nonlinearities mismatch. The initial value of \(a\) can postpone but not prevent unlearning. The authors derive scaling laws showing power-law decays for \(R\) and polynomial growth for \(a\), with exponents determined by the minimal degree of the mismatched Hermite coefficients.

## Method Summary
The methodology centers on the infinite-width (mean-field) limit of a two-layer student network with random weights, trained by SGD to replicate a fixed teacher network. Under Gaussian data assumptions, Tensor Programs—a framework for computing expected weighted sums in random networks—allow the authors to derive an exact closed-form ODE for the order parameters \(R\) and \(a\). This reduction transforms the stochastic, high-dimensional optimization problem into a deterministic two-dimensional system. The authors then perform a fast-slow decomposition of this ODE, identifying a critical manifold on which the fast variable \(R\) rapidly equilibrates, leaving the slow variable \(a\) to drive long-term evolution. The interplay of these timescales yields a precise condition for unlearning based on Hermite coefficient mismatches.

## Key Results
- Infinite-width limit + Tensor Programs → deterministic 2D ODE for order parameters \(R\) (alignment) and \(a\) (scale).
- Fast-slow decomposition: \(R\) relaxes quickly to a critical manifold; \(a\) evolves slowly along it, determining asymptotic fate.
- Feature unlearning (\(R \to 0\), \(a \to \infty\)) occurs when the teacher’s and student’s nonlinearities have mismatched Hermite coefficients; the minimal degree of mismatch controls power-law exponents (e.g., \(R = \Theta(\tau^{-1/4})\)).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The deterministic ODE arises because, in the infinite-width limit, the law of large numbers suppresses stochastic fluctuations and the weight matrices become effectively independent Gaussian. Tensor Programs then provide a rigorous way to compute the expected gradient contributions, collapsing the infinite-dimensional weight space onto a few summary statistics. The fast-slow structure is inherent: the alignment \(R\), being an inner product between weight vectors and the teacher, responds immediately to gradient steps, while the scale \(a\) changes multiplicatively and thus more gradually. The critical manifold is defined by the condition that the fast dynamics have zero eigenvalue on it, making it invariant under slow flow. Unlearning occurs when the manifold’s vector field pushes \(R\) to zero because the student’s nonlinearity cannot represent certain Hermite components present in the teacher; this mismatch prevents the alignment from being maintained and forces \(a\) to grow without bound to compensate. The initial \(a\) shifts the starting point on the manifold, influencing the time scale before the inevitable drift toward unlearning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Infinite-width limit**: Why needed: To obtain a deterministic mean-field description of network dynamics by averaging over random weights; Quick check: Does the ODE converge as width → ∞ in simulations?
- **Tensor Programs**: Why needed: Systematically compute expected weighted sums (e.g., moments) in random networks; Quick check: Do the derived moment equations match empirical averages for Gaussian data?
- **Hermite expansion**: Why needed: To compare nonlinearities via an orthogonal basis, exposing which components are mismatched; Quick check: Do teacher and student share the first non-zero Hermite coefficient?
- **Fast-slow analysis**: Why needed: To separate distinct timescales and identify a slow manifold governing long-term behavior; Quick check: Are the eigenvalues of the Jacobian widely separated in magnitude?
- **Critical manifold**: Why needed: Encodes the equilibrium condition for fast variables and determines slow evolution; Quick check: Does the flow converge onto the manifold quickly?
- **Scaling laws**: Why needed: To quantify convergence rates of \(R\) and \(a\) under mismatches; Quick check: Do numerical plots of \(\log R\) vs \(\log \tau\) show straight lines with predicted slopes?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Data → Teacher network (fixed nonlinearity) → Student network (two-layer, SGD-trained) → Infinite-width limit → Tensor Programs → 2D ODE for \((R,a)\) → Fast-slow decomposition → Critical manifold → Unlearning condition & scaling laws.

**Critical Path**:  
Infinite-width assumption → Tensor Programs reduction → Low-dimensional ODE → Identification of fast (\(R\)) and slow (\(a\)) variables → Derivation of critical manifold → Flow analysis along manifold → Unlearning criterion (Hermite coefficient mismatch) → Scaling law exponents.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- **Infinite-width vs finite-width**: Rigorous reduction but ignores finite-size effects and noise.
- **Two-layer vs deeper**: Analytically tractable but may not capture complexity of deep networks.
- **Gaussian data**: Simplifies moment calculations but limits generality to real-world distributions.
- **Deterministic ODE vs stochastic SGD**: Omits gradient noise that could affect early dynamics.
- **Hermite mismatch analysis**: Focuses on a specific type of nonlinearity difference; other mismatch forms remain unexplored.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Deviations from ODE predictions at moderate widths (finite-width effects).
- Breakdown of Tensor Program formulas under non-Gaussian inputs.
- Early exit from the fast manifold due to SGD noise.
- Unlearning behavior differing from the predicted power laws if the mismatch involves higher-order coefficients beyond the minimal degree.
- Numerical instability when initial \(a_0\) is extremely small.

**First Experiments**:
1. **ODE validation**: Train two-layer networks of varying widths (e.g., 500, 1000, 2000) and track \(R(t)\), \(a(t)\); compare to ODE solutions.
2. **Data robustness**: Replace Gaussian inputs with, for example, uniform or heavy-tailed distributions; test whether unlearning and scaling persist.
3. **Depth extension**: Construct three-layer student networks and attempt to derive analogous Tensor Programs; check for fast-slow dynamics and unlearning.

## Limitations
- Analysis restricted to infinite-width two-layer networks; extension to realistic deep architectures is non-trivial.
- Assumes Gaussian input data and exact moment closure via Tensor Programs, which may not hold in practice.
- ODE neglects stochasticity inherent in SGD, potentially missing important effects on unlearning timescales.

## Confidence
- **High**: ODE reduction via infinite-width/Tensor Programs (rigorous under stated assumptions).
- **Medium**: Fast-slow manifold structure, unlearning condition from Hermite mismatch, and derived scaling laws (solid but assumption‑dependent).
- **Low**: Practical mitigation by tuning initial \(a\) (unlikely to be feasible in real training scenarios).

## Next Checks
1. **Finite-width validation**: empirically compare the evolution of \(R\) and \(a\) across widths against the infinite-width ODE predictions.
2. **Depth scalability**: extend Tensor Programs to networks with three or more layers and test for analogous fast-slow dynamics and unlearning phenomena.
3. **Benchmark test**: apply initial \(a\) tuning in standard architectures (e.g., ResNet on CIFAR-10) to see if the predicted unlearning signatures manifest.
