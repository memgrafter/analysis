# Budget-Xfer: Budget-Constrained Source Language Selection for Cross-Lingual Transfer to African Languages
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.27651
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27651
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: Multi-source transfer substantially outperforms single-source transfer due to better budget utilization, with task-dependent utility of embedding similarity for source selection.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses a methodological flaw in cross-lingual transfer where comparisons of source language selection strategies are confounded by varying total training data quantities. It introduces Budget-Xfer, a framework that treats multi-source transfer as a budget-constrained resource allocation problem, jointly optimizing language selection and data distribution under a fixed annotation budget. Evaluated on NER and sentiment analysis for three African languages across 288 experiments, the framework shows that multi-source transfer provides a large advantage over single-source transfer (Cohen’s d up to 1.98) primarily because single-source strategies underutilize the budget (averaging 57% usage). Among multi-source allocations, differences are modest and statistically non-significant. The utility of embedding similarity for source selection is task-dependent: it benefits sentiment analysis but harms NER, where random selection works better.

## Method Summary
Budget-Xfer reframes cross-lingual source selection as a budget-constrained optimization problem. Given a fixed annotation budget B, a pool of candidate source languages with labeled datasets, and a similarity proxy (cosine_gap) computed from multilingual model embeddings on FLORES-200 parallel sentences, the framework applies four allocation strategies (All-from-Best, Top-5-Proportional, Random-5, Diversity-Aware) to distribute the budget across sources. A cap-and-redistribute procedure ensures the total examples equal exactly B and that no source contributes more than its available data. The allocated data are concatenated, shuffled, and used to fine-tune a pretrained multilingual model (Serengeti or AfroXLMR) with AdamW. The fine-tuned model is evaluated on the target language test set using task-specific metrics (F1 for NER, weighted F1 for sentiment). Experiments span two tasks, three target languages (Hausa, Yoruba, Swahili), two models, and two budget sizes, totaling 288 configurations, each repeated with three random seeds.

## Key Results
- Multi-source transfer substantially outperforms single-source transfer (Cohen’s d = 0.80–1.98), mainly because single-source strategies underutilize the budget (average 57% usage).
- Differences among multi-source allocation strategies are modest and statistically non-significant.
- The utility of embedding similarity for source selection is task-dependent: random selection outperforms similarity-based methods for NER, while similarity-informed selection shows a consistent directional advantage for sentiment analysis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Single-Source Underutilization
**Claim:** Single-source transfer underperforms multi-source transfer because it structurally underutilizes the fixed annotation budget.
**Mechanism:** When the entire budget is allocated to the single most similar source (All-from-Best), training is capped by that source's available labeled data. If the source dataset is smaller than the budget, a portion of the budget remains unused. Multi-source strategies distribute the budget across several sources, each contributing data within its capacity, and the cap-and-redistribute procedure ensures full utilization.
**Core assumption:** The marginal benefit of additional training data from a new source is positive until the combined data from all selected sources exhausts the budget.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "multi-source transfer substantially outperforms single-source transfer ... primarily because single-source strategies suffer from severe budget underutilization (averaging 57% usage)"
- [section 5.1] "All-from-Best utilized only 57% of the allocated budget on average... multi-source strategies distribute the budget across sources... and the cap-and-redistribute procedure ensures full budget utilization."
**Break condition:** The single-source advantage emerges only when the top-ranked source language has a labeled dataset equal to or larger than the full budget, eliminating underutilization.

### Mechanism 2: Insensitivity of Multi-Source Allocation
**Claim:** Among multi-source allocation strategies, differences in performance are modest and non-significant because the decision to use multiple sources dominates the specific allocation method.
**Mechanism:** The primary performance gain comes from moving from one to multiple sources, which mitigates budget underutilization and introduces source diversity. Once this multi-source regime is established, the incremental effect of how the fixed budget is distributed (proportional vs. uniform) or how sources are selected (similarity vs. random vs. diversity-aware) is small relative to the multi-source effect itself.
**Core assumption:** The benefit curve from adding new source languages is steep initially (1→2, 2→3 sources) and flattens; allocation fine-tuning operates on the flatter portion of this curve.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "differences among multi-source allocation strategies are modest and statistically non-significant"
- [section 5.2] "Overall mean F1 scores... tightly clustered... the controlled comparison... shows no significant difference in any condition... Win rates... distributed remarkably evenly among multi-source strategies"
**Break condition:** This could change with extreme budget scarcity (very small B) where every example is critical, or with highly imbalanced source quality where a greedy marginal allocation might outperform.

### Mechanism 3: Task-Dependence of Similarity-Based Selection
**Claim:** The utility of embedding similarity (cosine_gap) for source selection is task-dependent: it is not beneficial for NER (token-level) but directionally helpful for sentiment analysis (sentence-level).
**Mechanism:** For NER, the model must learn diverse entity boundary patterns and morphological tokens across languages. Similarity-based selection (Top-K-Proportional) clusters on typologically related languages (e.g., Bantu), reducing surface pattern diversity. Random selection inadvertently provides a more diverse sample, aiding generalization. For sentiment, the relevant features (semantic polarity, affective cues) align more closely with what multilingual embedding similarity captures, so informed selection provides a consistent signal.
**Core assumption:** Embedding similarity primarily captures semantic/lexical overlap relevant for sentence-level tasks but does not capture the typological surface diversity beneficial for token-level sequence labeling.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "the utility of embedding similarity for source selection is task-dependent—random selection outperforms similarity-based methods for NER, while similarity-informed selection shows a consistent directional advantage for sentiment analysis."
- [section 5.3] "For NER... Random-5 achieves the highest mean F1... similarity-based selection may be counterproductive by over-concentrating on redundant sources... For sentiment... linguistically similar sources provide more transferable signal."
**Break condition:** This pattern could invert for NER if the task involved entity types with strong semantic constraints (e.g., only PERSON/ORG) or for sentiment if languages shared very distinct metaphorical framing for affect.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Budget-Constrained Optimization**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's core innovation is reframing source language selection as a resource allocation problem with a fixed total amount of annotated data (budget B). This isolates the effect of *which sources* and *how much from each* from the confounding effect of total data quantity.
  - **Quick check question:** If Strategy A uses 12K total examples and Strategy B uses 8K, can you fairly compare their effectiveness? What must you control for?
- **Concept: Cross-Lingual Transfer Bottlenecks**
  - **Why needed here:** Understanding the "budget underutilization" mechanism requires knowing that the single-source approach is limited by the *size of the best source's dataset*, not the user-defined budget. The bottleneck is data availability at the source, not the transfer learning algorithm.
  - **Quick check question:** For a target language T, if the most similar source S has only 3K labeled sentences but your budget is 10K, what is the maximum effective training size using S alone?
- **Concept: Task Characteristic Sensitivity**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's third finding shows that a general heuristic (use similar sources) can be wrong for certain tasks (NER). This requires understanding the difference between token-level pattern recognition (diversity helps) and sentence-level semantic inference (similarity helps).
  - **Quick check question:** For a part-of-speech tagging task in a target language, would you prioritize sources that are typologically diverse or lexically similar? Why?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Budget Definition -> Source Pool -> Utility Proxy -> Allocation Strategies -> Cap-and-Redistribute -> Training Pipeline -> Evaluation Metric
- **Critical path:** Define B → Compute source-target similarities → Apply allocation strategy to get a → Apply cap-and-redistribute → Sample and train → Evaluate.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Simplicity vs. Sophistication: Random-K is trivial but uninformed; Diversity-Aware adds complexity (redundancy penalty α=0.5) for unclear gain.
  - Fixed-K vs. Variable-K: All strategies here use K=5. A greedy marginal approach might optimize K automatically but increases computational cost for strategy search.
  - Similarity Metric Dependence: Results depend on cosine_gap. A different proxy (e.g., lang2vec) might change the "task-dependent" conclusion.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Single-source runs showing performance far below multi-source *and* reporting training set size << B (clear underutilization).
  - Strategy comparisons with huge variance across seeds for a single strategy, indicating instability (possibly due to small source datasets or high randomness in selection).
  - Task inversion: If for a given task, similarity-based strategies *consistently* underperform random across targets/budgets/models, it challenges the paper's task-dependence claim and may indicate a flawed similarity metric or dataset peculiarity.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate Single vs. Multi-Source Gap: Run All-from-Best vs. Top-5-Proportional for one target (e.g., Hausa NER) on Serengeti at low budget. Verify utilization gap (~50% vs. 100%) and performance gap (ΔF1 > 0.03).
  2. Test Allocation Insensitivity: For the same setting, run Top-5-Proportional, Random-5, Diversity-Aware, and Top-5-Uniform. Compute mean F1 and pairwise p-values. Expect non-significant differences (p>0.05) and <0.03 absolute gap.
  3. Task-Dependence Check: For one target (e.g., Yoruba), run Random-5 and Top-5-Proportional on both NER and Sentiment at both budgets. Confirm: for NER, Random-5 >= Top-5-Proportional; for Sentiment, Top-5-Proportional >= Random-5.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How generalizable are the Budget‑Xfer findings across a wider range of NLP tasks and African target languages?
- **Basis:** [explicit] "Our evaluation covers two tasks and three target languages; extending to additional tasks (e.g., dependency parsing, question answering) and a broader set of target languages would strengthen generalizability."
- **Why unresolved:** The current experiments are limited to NER and sentiment analysis for only Hausa, Yoruba, and Swahili; it remains unknown if the observed patterns hold for other tasks or language families.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic replication of the Budget‑Xfer framework on diverse tasks (e.g., dependency parsing, QA) and a larger, typologically varied set of African languages, measuring whether multi‑source advantage persists and whether similarity‑based selection remains task‑dependent.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the optimal number of source languages (K) to include in multi‑source transfer, and does joint optimization of K and allocation yield further performance gains?
- **Basis:** [explicit] "We used a fixed 𝐾=5 for multi‑source strategies, but preliminary results with a greedy marginal approach that distributed data across 14–17 sources suggest that optimizing 𝐾 jointly with the allocation strategy may yield further gains."
- **Why unresolved:** The experiments only compared a few fixed‑K strategies; the interaction between K, budget size, task type, and language similarity is unexplored, potentially missing a more efficient use of the budget.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a comprehensive grid‑search over K (e.g., from 1 to all available sources) combined with different allocation methods, including joint optimization that selects K and distributes the budget in a single process, then analyze performance trends across tasks and budgets.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does incorporating variable annotation costs across languages and tasks affect budget allocation decisions and overall transfer performance in the Budget‑Xfer framework?
- **Basis:** [explicit] "Our budget formulation treats all training sentences as equally costly to annotate; incorporating variable annotation costs across languages and tasks would better reflect real‑world constraints."
- **Why unresolved:** In practice, annotation cost varies significantly (e.g., by language, domain, task complexity). Ignoring cost may lead to allocations that are economically suboptimal or even infeasible under real budgets.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend the Budget‑Xfer model to include cost parameters per language‑task pair, then run controlled experiments comparing equal‑cost vs. variable‑cost allocations, measuring both performance and total monetary expenditure to determine optimal cost‑aware strategies.

## Limitations
- Unknown FLORES-200 subset: The exact 1,012-sentence subset and alignment method for computing cosine_gap are unreported, risking metric inconsistency.
- Cap-and-redistribute ambiguity: The redistribution algorithm (iterative vs. one-pass, weight recalculation) is unspecified, potentially altering final allocations and performance.
- Generalizability scope: Findings are limited to three target languages and two tasks on specific datasets (MasakhaNER, AfriSenti). Transfer to other language families or tasks (e.g., MT, QA) is unknown.

## Confidence
- Multi-source advantage due to budget utilization: High
- Modest differences among multi-source strategies: Medium
- Task-dependent utility of similarity: Low
