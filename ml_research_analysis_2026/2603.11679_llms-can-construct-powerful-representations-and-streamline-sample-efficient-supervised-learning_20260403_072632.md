# LLMs can construct powerful representations and streamline sample-efficient supervised learning

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.11679
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11679
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** LLM-generated "rubric" representations transform noisy EHR text into a structured format that enables sample-efficient supervised learning, outperforming strong clinical foundation models and naive text baselines on 15 clinical prediction tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the representation bottleneck in supervised learning with complex, heterogeneous data like electronic health records (EHRs). The core method uses large language models (LLMs) to construct "rubric" representations—either global templates or local summaries—that standardize the extraction and organization of evidence from raw, text-serialized inputs. These rubrics convert messy, unstructured EHR data into a cleaner, more learnable format for downstream models. Evaluated on the EHRSHOT benchmark across 15 clinical tasks, rubric-based methods achieve significant performance gains, particularly in low-data regimes (40 samples) and for tasks with dispersed evidence like new diagnoses and lab results, surpassing a pretrained clinical foundation model (CLMBR-T) and naive text-serialization baselines.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins by selecting a small, label-stratified, and diverse set of examples (40 total) from the training data. An LLM (GPT-5-mini) is prompted to synthesize a task-specific "global rubric"—a fixed template defining *how* to extract and structure relevant evidence (e.g., time windows, unit normalization, derived metrics) from any patient's raw text-serialized record. This rubric is then applied to all examples: either via a per-example LLM call (local rubric or global rubric application) or, for scalability, by generating a deterministic parser script (Global-Rubric-Auto/Tabular) from the rubric and exemplars. The resulting structured text (or tabular features) is used to train a simple downstream model (logistic regression or XGBoost). Ablations show the learned rubric representation itself, not ongoing LLM inference, drives most of the improvement.

## Key Results
*   **Low-Data Superiority:** With only 40 training samples, Local-Rubric achieves 0.717 AUROC and Global-Rubric 0.700 AUROC on average, significantly exceeding CLMBR-T (0.657) and NaiveText (0.638).
*   **Full-Data Gains:** In the full-data regime, Local-Rubric (0.772 AUROC) and Global-Rubric (0.763 AUROC) again outperform CLMBR-T (0.727) and NaiveText (0.699).
*   **Task-Specific Impact:** The largest improvements occur in "new diagnosis" and "lab result" tasks, where predictive evidence is sparse and dispersed; gains are smaller for operational outcomes where CLMBR-T remains strong.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Pre-emptive Standardization
*   **Claim:** Global rubrics reduce noise and enforce domain-specific preprocessing (e.g., temporal windows, unit normalization, plausibility filtering) *before* feature extraction.
*   **Mechanism:** The rubric's "Preparation" step acts as a deterministic preprocessor that removes variance from inconsistent EHR recording practices (e.g., mixed units, isolated readings), mirroring clinical guidelines and preventing downstream overfitting to artifacts.
*   **Core Assumption:** The representation bottleneck stems partly from inconsistent data formatting rather than a lack of predictive signal.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract mentions standardization; Section 6.1 shows explicit hypertension rubric steps (BP normalization, temporal windowing, removal of implausible values).

### Mechanism 2: Higher-Order Composite Features
*   **Claim:** Rubrics construct clinically meaningful composite features (e.g., trends, categorizations, aggregated scorecards) that are absent in naive text but highly predictive.
*   **Mechanism:** Extraction steps compute derived metrics (e.g., BP slope, ACC/AHA categories, risk counts), transforming scattered raw values into summaries aligned with clinical risk frameworks, offloading synthesis from the downstream model to the LLM.
*   **Core Assumption:** Simple downstream models struggle to synthesize such higher-order features from raw text embeddings alone.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Section 6.1 hypertension rubric computes variability and categories; Section 6.2 hyponatremia rubric encodes historical trajectory; Section 1.2 contrasts with prior fixed text-serialization.

### Mechanism 3: Fixed Schema for Stability and Usability
*   **Claim:** Global rubrics impose a shared, fixed schema across all samples, improving feature stability, audibility, and compatibility with tabular ML.
*   **Mechanism:** The deterministic template (e.g., "§1. Demographics ⊢ Age, sex, BMI") creates a fixed-dimensional, interpretable structure. Parser-based variants convert this into identical tabular features, enabling standard models and causal analysis.
*   **Core Assumption:** A consistent feature space is more learnable and operationally useful than flexible, per-example natural language.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** Section 2.1 on global rubric desiderata; Section 5 showing parser/tabular variants remain competitive; Section 6.2 tabular features mirroring clinical decision trees.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: In-Context Learning for Template Synthesis
*   **Why needed:** The global rubric must be synthesized by an LLM from a small, diverse set of examples (k=40). The LLM must generalize to a reusable template, not just summarize the examples.
*   **Quick check:** If the 40 exemplars used for synthesis change, how much does the rubric's structure and downstream performance vary? (Tests robustness of in-context learning to exemplar selection.)

### Concept: Deterministic Parsing from Semi-Structured Text
*   **Why needed:** For scalable deployment, the rubric transformation must move from LLM-per-example (costly) to deterministic scripts (parser/tabularization), requiring mapping flexible LLM output to fixed extraction rules.
*   **Quick check:** Can a parser reliably extract "Most recent serum sodium value" from rubric text that uses varying formats ("NA" vs. "2024-05-01: 132 mmol/L")? (Tests regex robustness.)

### Concept: Embedding Model Sensitivity to Structured vs. Free Text
*   **Why needed:** The downstream model uses embeddings of rubric text. If the embedding model collapses structural differences (e.g., with/without a field), the representation gain is lost.
*   **Quick check:** Do embeddings of two rubrics differing only in a critical field value (e.g., "BP: Normal" vs. "BP: Stage 2") have a larger cosine distance than rubrics differing in a non-critical field? (Tests preservation of task-relevant distinctions.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Diverse Cohort Selector -> Rubric Synthesizer (LLM) -> Rubric Applier (Option A: LLM / Option B: Parser / Option C: Tabular) -> Downstream Model
Baselines: NaiveText / CLMBR-T / Count-GBM

### Critical path
The pipeline's success hinges on **Step 2 (Rubric Synthesis)** producing a template that captures discriminative, generalizable signals. A poor rubric (overfitted to exemplars, missing key factors) degrades all downstream applications. The parser/tabularization steps then *preserve* this quality by making application deterministic.

### Design tradeoffs
*   **Global vs. Local Rubric:** Global (fixed schema) enables auditability, tabular conversion, O(1) deployment; Local (per-example summary) may capture nuanced context but is non-standardized and O(N) costly.
*   **LLM vs. Parser Application:** LLM application is simpler initially but expensive/variable at scale; Parser is deterministic/fast but requires robust prompt engineering for correct code generation.
*   **Embedding vs. Tabular:** Embedding-based leverages powerful models but is less interpretable; Tabular unlocks XGBoost/SHAP but depends on auto-generated featurizer quality.

### Failure signatures
1.  **Rubric overfitting:** Rubric contains patient IDs or ultra-specific details from exemplars instead of generalizable fields.
2.  **Parser brittleness:** Script fails on valid but slightly different EHR serializations (e.g., date format variations).
3.  **Embedding collapse:** `embed(x_rubric)` is not meaningfully different from `embed(x_text)` for the same patient.
4.  **Task mismatch:** Rubric gains are minimal for operational outcomes or tasks where pretrained temporal models (CLMBR-T) already excel.

### First 3 experiments
1.  **Rubric content validation:** Clinician audit of 5 randomly selected rubrics for inclusion of known predictors, absence of irrelevant fields, and correct temporal definitions.
2.  **Parser generalization test:** Apply the auto-generated parser script to 100 `x_text` examples *outside* the 40-exemplar set; measure failure rate and field extraction accuracy (vs. LLM re-application as ground truth).
3.  **Representation ablation:** Train a downstream model on `x_rubric` with fields randomly shuffled; compare performance to original. A large drop confirms that the order/grouping of fields matters.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
*   **Question:** How can the global rubric synthesis step be scaled beyond the current 40-example limit imposed by context window constraints while maintaining or improving the quality and robustness of the learned rubric?
*   **Basis in paper:** The authors state: "Our results also suggest several substantive directions for future work. First, the rubric synthesis step is currently limited by context length... richer procedures for leveraging more data could improve robustness and coverage... Developing more principled and scalable methods for data-driven rubric construction is therefore an important direction."
*   **Why unresolved:** The current method relies on a small, label-stratified, k-means clustered cohort due to context limits. It is unclear whether a more scalable method (e.g., iterative refinement, hierarchical aggregation, or retrieval-augmented synthesis) would yield rubrics that generalize better or capture more nuanced patterns.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments comparing current in-context synthesis with alternative scalable paradigms (e.g., multi-stage synthesis, active learning for cohort selection) on EHRSHOT tasks. Evaluate rubric quality (expert audit or downstream performance) and robustness to dataset shifts.

### Open Question 2
*   **Question:** To what extent do the performance gains from rubric representations generalize beyond the EHRSHOT benchmark and the specific domain of longitudinal EHRs to other complex, heterogeneous data modalities (e.g., clinical notes, imaging, financial logs) and other domains (e.g., scientific literature, customer interaction logs)?
*   **Basis in paper:** The authors acknowledge: "This work also has several limitations. Our empirical evaluation is restricted to a single benchmark in healthcare... a wider empirical evaluation across domains, tasks, and modalities... would provide valuable insight into when rubric representations help most." They also note the chest X-ray task was challenging and operational outcomes favored CLMBR-T.
*   **Why unresolved:** Efficacy is demonstrated on a specific benchmark with serialized structured data. The mechanism—standardizing evidence extraction and organizing sparse signals—may be more or less effective depending on modality, task type, and heterogeneity nature. Transferability is not established.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the rubric pipeline to at least two other distinct benchmarks (e.g., MIMIC-IV notes for readmission, a multimodal finance dataset, or a scientific table understanding task). Systematically compare performance deltas over strong baselines across these domains, coupled with qualitative rubric analysis.

### Open Question 3
*   **Question:** What is the relative contribution of the *structured template* (global rubric standardization) versus the *LLM's world knowledge* injected during the rubric application/summarization step to the overall performance improvement?
*   **Basis in paper:** The paper contrasts Global-Rubric (shared template) and Local-Rubric (per-example summary) and runs ablations. They note: "Local-Rubric seems to be a contender for the strongest performing method... Global-Rubric achieves the best overall AUPRC... the comparison between local and global rubrics is much closer than a winner-take-all reading suggests." The Global-Rubric-Blind ablation shows pretrained knowledge alone is useful but worse than data-informed rubrics.
*   **Why unresolved:** While ablations suggest both factors matter, the precise interaction is unclear. Disentangling the value of the *template* itself from the *LLM's ability to populate it* requires controlled experiments where the template is fixed but populated by different methods or vice versa.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Study where (a) a single high-quality global rubric is applied via different "fillers" (LLM, fine-tuned smaller model, deterministic parser) and (b) local summaries are forced into a fixed template structure post-hoc. Compare downstream performance to isolate structure vs. intelligence of the filler.

### Open Question 4
*   **Question:** How robust are the automatically generated parser scripts (Global-Rubric-Auto) and tabularization scripts (Global-Rubric-Tabular) to variations in input formatting, EHR system differences, or longitudinal shifts in clinical documentation practices, and what are their error modes?
*   **Basis in paper:** The paper highlights operational advantages: "deterministic parser scripts" and "tabular representations." However, the prompts for script generation use 40 example pairs from the training cohort. The robustness of the generated code to out-of-distribution text serializations (e.g., different note styles, new lab codes) is not evaluated. The parser is a potential single point of failure.
*   **Why unresolved:** The paper states the parser script uses only standard libraries and regex, implying fragility to pattern drift. No error analysis on the parser's output is provided. If the parser fails silently or extracts incorrect fields, downstream performance could degrade unpredictably.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic stress-testing of the generated parsers on (a) held-out examples from EHRSHOT with perturbed serialization formats, (b) external EHR datasets with different coding systems, and (c) a sample of real-world deidentified notes. Quantify parse failure rates, field extraction accuracy, and impact of errors on downstream task performance. Characterize common failure modes.

### Open Question 5
*   **Question:** Why do rubric-based representations show comparatively weaker performance on operational outcome tasks (e.g., ICU transfer, long LOS) and the chest X-ray finding task, and what modifications to the rubric design or application could close this gap?
*   **Basis in paper:** The authors observe: "Operational outcome tasks remain comparatively stronger for CLMBR-T... One plausible explanation is that these tasks align more closely with the kind of temporal patterns which are abundant during CLMBR-T pretraining... In contrast, rubric learning appears to help most on tasks where the predictive evidence is sparser and more dispersed across the record." For chest X-ray, they note labels may be noisier.
*   **Why unresolved:** The hypothesis is that operational tasks rely on fine-grained temporal dynamics that CLMBR-T captures better, while rubrics excel at aggregating dispersed evidence. However, it's unclear whether the rubric formulation is suboptimal for temporal tasks or if the representation fundamentally lacks sequential inductive bias.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** (a) Ablate the rubric design for operational tasks—e.g., incorporate more explicit temporal features—and measure if performance improves. (b) Analyze features extracted by successful rubrics on diagnosis/lab vs. operational tasks to identify missing signals. (c) Hybrid architectures: use rubric representations alongside CLMBR-T embeddings to test for complementary signal.

## Limitations
*   Generalizability is limited to a single benchmark (EHRSHOT) and specific longitudinal EHR tasks; performance on other domains and modalities remains unproven.
*   Risk of rubric overfitting to the small set of 40 exemplars used for synthesis, potentially capturing non-generalizable patterns.
*   The robustness of automatically generated parser scripts to variations in real-world EHR formatting and longitudinal drift is not evaluated, posing a deployment risk.

## Confidence
*   **Performance superiority** (rubrics > strong baselines): **Medium** – strong internal results but limited external validation and potential dataset bias.
*   **Mechanistic explanations** (standardization, composite features, fixed schema): **Low-Medium** – plausible post-hoc rationales, not causally proven.
*   **Parser/tabular feasibility** (competitive with LLM-applied rubrics): **Medium** – promising but robustness to real-world noise unverified.

## Next Checks
1.  **Clinician audit:** Have domain experts evaluate 5–10 rubrics for inclusion of known clinical predictors and absence of spurious fields.
2.  **Parser generalization test:** Apply the auto-generated parser to 100 held-out `x_text` examples; measure failure rate and field-level accuracy against LLM re-application as ground truth.
3.  **Representation ablation:** Randomly shuffle field order within rubric texts and retrain downstream models; a significant performance drop confirms the importance of structured layout.
