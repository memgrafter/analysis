# Infinite-dimensional generative diffusions via Doob's h-transform

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06621
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06621
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: A framework based on Doob's h-transform that enables generative diffusion models in infinite-dimensional spaces, with theoretical guarantees and empirical improvements on synthetic and real-world tasks.

## Executive Summary
Generative diffusion models traditionally rely on time-reversed noising processes that converge to a tractable reference measure (e.g., Gaussian) as time goes to infinity. In infinite-dimensional spaces (e.g., function spaces), such long-time convergence often fails, limiting the applicability of diffusion models. This paper introduces a novel framework that circumvents reliance on long-time convergence by using Doob's h-transform. Specifically, the authors start from a reference diffusion and apply an exponential change of measure defined by the h-function to force the process toward a target distribution. This transformed process is well-defined under verifiable conditions and serves as a generative diffusion model.

The authors prove the well-definedness of the forced diffusion in infinite dimensions and show that its drift involves the score of the target distribution. Consequently, the standard score-matching objective can be employed to learn the required score from samples. Empirical validation includes synthetic Gaussian mixtures where the method exhibits robustness to increasing state dimension and reduced noising time, significantly lowering sliced Wasserstein distance compared to baselines. On the MNIST-SDF dataset, it achieves a competitive Fréchet Inception Distance of 4.3. For a seismic imaging inverse problem, the forced diffusion yields lower relative error and mean absolute error, along with more consistent posterior uncertainty quantification.

## Method Summary
The method constructs a generative diffusion in infinite-dimensional space via three main steps. First, a tractable reference diffusion process (e.g., an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process) is defined on a Hilbert space. Second, Doob's h-transform is applied: given the target density p with respect to the reference measure, the h-function is defined as h(x) = p(x)/p_ref(x). Under conditions such as h being positive and integrable, the h-transform modifies the drift of the reference diffusion to b_h(x) = b_ref(x) + σσ^T ∇ log h(x), yielding a new diffusion that has p as its invariant distribution and never hits the cemetery state. This forced process is a Markov diffusion that can be simulated forward in time. Third, the score function ∇ log p(x) appears in the drift; the authors propose to learn it using a denoising score matching objective, training a neural network on samples from the forced process. Once the score is learned, generation proceeds by running the reverse-time SDE (the time-reversal of the forced process) using the estimated score. The framework thus reduces generative modeling in infinite dimensions to learning a score function, with the h-transform providing the link between the reference and target measures.

## Key Results
- Synthetic Gaussian mixtures: Forced diffusion reduces sliced Wasserstein distance to 0.053 at T=0.2, D=500 (baseline 0.692) and remains robust when noising time is shortened.
- MNIST-SDF generation: Achieves Fréchet Inception Distance of 4.3, indicating competitive image quality.
- Seismic inverse problem: Improves relative error (0.09 vs. 0.17) and mean absolute error (26.82 vs. 37.80) over a noising-denoising baseline, with better-calibrated posterior uncertainty.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The h-transform works because it conditionally kills the reference diffusion to ensure it spends its entire lifetime inside the support of the target distribution. Mathematically, the transformation corresponds to an exponential change of measure that preserves the Markov property and yields a new diffusion whose generator is the original generator plus a potential term derived from the log h-function. This potential exactly cancels the reference measure’s stationary density, resulting in the target density being invariant. In infinite dimensions, this approach bypasses the need for the reference diffusion to converge to the target as time tends to infinity; instead, the conditioning is exact from the outset. Moreover, the forced drift naturally incorporates the score of the target, making it amenable to score-based learning. The method’s reliance on verifiable conditions (e.g., h being harmonic and integrable) ensures the transformed process is well-defined as a solution to the martingale problem, providing a rigorous foundation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Doob's h-transform**: A technique to condition a Markov process to stay within a set by modifying its drift via an h-function. *Why needed?* It converts a reference diffusion with a known stationary measure into one that targets a specified distribution, avoiding reliance on long-time mixing. *Quick check*: Verify that h > 0, solves the Poisson equation with respect to the reference generator, and is integrable against the reference measure.
- **Score matching**: An objective to learn the score function (gradient of log density) directly from samples without density estimation. *Why needed?* The drift of the forced SDE contains the score of the target; learning it via score matching enables approximation of the reverse-time dynamics. *Quick check*: Ensure the denoising score matching loss is minimized by the true score under the data distribution, and that the network architecture can capture the score.
- **Infinite-dimensional SDEs**: Stochastic differential equations in function spaces, often defined via cylindrical Wiener processes. *Why needed?* The state space of interest (e.g., functions, images) is naturally infinite-dimensional; standard finite-dimensional SDE theory does not apply. *Quick check*: Confirm that the noise covariance operator is trace-class and that the coefficients satisfy conditions for existence/uniqueness (e.g., monotonicity or Lipschitz in a suitable norm).
- **Martingale problem**: A rigorous framework for defining Markov processes by their generators. *Why needed?* In infinite dimensions, well-posedness is established via the martingale problem; it provides conditions under which the forced process is uniquely defined. *Quick check*: Show that the pre-generator of the forced SDE is closable in L² of the reference measure and that a core exists where the martingale problem is solvable.
- **Exponential change of measure**: The transformation underlying h-transform, connecting conditioning and tilting of a probability measure. *Why needed?* It preserves the Markov property while altering the underlying measure, enabling the construction of the forced process. *Quick check*: Verify Girsanov-type conditions to ensure absolute continuity of the transformed path measure with respect to the original.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map (plain text):
Reference Diffusion (e.g., linear SDE on Hilbert space) -> h-function (exact/approximated) -> Forced SDE (drift = b_ref + σσ^T ∇ log h) -> Simulated trajectories -> Score network (θ) -> Denoising score matching loss -> Trained score estimator -> Reverse-time SDE (using learned score) -> Generated samples

Critical path:
1. Choose a reference diffusion that is easy to simulate and has a known stationary measure.
2. Obtain the h-function for the target (either analytically or via approximation). This determines the drift modification.
3. Simulate the forced forward SDE to produce training data (state-score pairs).
4. Train a neural network to regress the score using a denoising score matching objective.
5. Sample new data by numerically integrating the reverse-time SDE with the learned score.

Design tradeoffs:
- Reference diffusion simplicity vs. forcing strength: Simpler references (e.g., Gaussian OU) are computationally cheap but may require large h-derived drifts, making training data distribution more challenging. More complex references can reduce forcing magnitude but increase simulation cost.
- Exact vs. approximate h: Exact h gives a theoretically perfect transformation but may only be available for Gaussian or exponential family targets. Approximations (e.g., via kernel density estimation or learned h) extend the method but risk violating the integrability conditions, potentially making the process ill-defined or biased.
- Discretization accuracy: Infinite-dimensional SDE simulation requires truncation to a finite basis. Finer truncation preserves more structure but increases computational load; coarser truncation may introduce artifacts, especially for non-smooth targets.

Failure signatures:
- h violates integrability (e.g., blows up) → the forced process may have finite explosion time or be ill-defined.
- Score network fails to converge (loss plateaus) → likely due to poor architecture, insufficient data, or high variance in score estimates; reverse sampling will diverge or produce nonsense.
- Discretization errors cause bias: e.g., aliasing or Gibbs phenomena in the generated samples, particularly near high-frequency features.
- Reverse SDE integration becomes unstable if the learned score is inaccurate, leading to exploding trajectories.

First experiments (3):
1. Synthetic Gaussian Mixture: Test robustness to state dimension D and noising time T, report sliced Wasserstein distance.

## Limitations
- Verifying the h-transform conditions (e.g., integrability of h) is non-trivial for many practical infinite-dimensional target distributions, especially those beyond Gaussian mixtures.
- The method depends on accurate knowledge or approximation of the h-function; errors in h can significantly degrade the quality of the forced process and the learned score.
- Experiments are confined to low-dimensional function spaces (e.g., 1D signals, MNIST-SDF) and a single inverse problem; scalability to high-dimensional image data and thorough analysis of discretization effects are lacking.

## Confidence
- Theoretical well-definedness: High
- Score-matching learnability: Medium
- Empirical superiority: Medium
