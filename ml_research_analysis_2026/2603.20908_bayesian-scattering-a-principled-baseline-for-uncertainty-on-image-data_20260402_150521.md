# Bayesian Scattering: A Principled Baseline for Uncertainty on Image Data
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.20908
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20908
- **Reference count**: 13
- **Primary result**: Bayesian scattering combines a fixed wavelet scattering transform with a Gaussian process head to achieve competitive accuracy in data‑scarce regimes and robust, well‑calibrated uncertainty estimates under distribution shift.

## Executive Summary
Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for images lacks a well‑understood, interpretable baseline; deep learning models often become overconfident when test data are drawn from a shifted distribution. This paper proposes *Bayesian scattering*: a method that pairs the wavelet scattering transform—a fixed, geometry‑driven feature extractor with provable invariances to translation, deformation, and noise—with a simple probabilistic head (e.g., a Gaussian process). The decoupling of feature extraction from learning avoids overfitting to the training distribution. Experiments on medical imaging (institution shift), wealth mapping (country shift), and molecular property optimization show that Bayesian scattering matches or exceeds deep models (ConvNeXt, DINOv2) in data‑scarce regimes, while delivering well‑calibrated uncertainties under shift. In Bayesian optimization tasks (QM2D, QM9), it converges rapidly to near‑optimal solutions, matching or surpassing the specialized deep model Uni‑Mol. The work establishes a principled, geometric approach to UQ on images that remains reliable under distribution shift.

## Method Summary
Bayesian scattering uses the wavelet scattering transform (implemented via the Kymatio library) as a fixed, deterministic front‑end. The transform applies convolutions with analytically chosen wavelets, a pointwise modulus non‑linearity, and local averaging; typical hyperparameters are the number of scales $J \approx \log_2(N)-1$ (with $N$ image size) and orientations $L=8$. Rotation invariance can be enforced by averaging over rotated versions, at the cost of discarding orientation‑specific details. The resulting feature vector (or spatial map flattened) is fed into a Gaussian process (GP) regression head. For small training sets, an exact GP is used; for larger sets, a sparse variational GP (SVGP) with 1024 inducing points is employed. The GP kernel is typically isotropic RBF or Matérn‑5/2; ARD kernels are discouraged in high‑dimensional scattering spaces due to overfitting. Hyperparameters (lengthscales, variance, noise) are optimized by maximizing the log marginal likelihood using Adam (500 iterations, lengthscale initialized to the mean pairwise distance of training features). No PCA is applied to the features. The full pipeline eliminates any learned image encoder, reducing the risk of overfitting to the training distribution and promoting robustness under covariate shift.

## Key Results
- On **HistologyNucleiPixels** and **SkinLesionPixels**, Bayesian scattering attains RMSE and NLL comparable to or better than deep ensembles, with lower Quantile Coverage Error (QCE), indicating superior calibration.
- On **AssetWealth** (country shift), it achieves slightly lower accuracy than ConvNeXt but maintains robust calibration (low QCE), whereas deep models become overconfident.
- In **Bayesian optimization** for molecular properties (QM2D and QM9), the method converges quickly to near‑optimal solutions, matching or outperforming the specialized deep model Uni‑Mol, demonstrating its effectiveness for sequential decision‑making.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Invariance and Stability of Scattering Features
The wavelet scattering transform yields a feature representation provably invariant to translations and stable to small deformations and additive noise. This is achieved through a cascade of fixed wavelet convolutions, modulus non‑linearities, and local averaging—operations that encode geometric inductive biases. The core assumption is that natural image variations (e.g., small shifts, deformations, noise) should not affect the target prediction. Evidence of these properties appears in the abstract and Section 3.1, which formally define translation‑invariance, deformation stability, and noise stability. The approach breaks down if the true task‑relevant variations include large, non‑smooth transformations or if semantic information is lost by pooling.

### Mechanism 2: Decoupling Feature Extraction from Learning
Because the scattering transform is fixed and not optimized on training labels, the subsequent probabilistic head (GP) operates on a feature space that is independent of the training distribution. This decoupling prevents the model from memorizing spurious patterns specific to the training images, fostering robustness under covariate shift. The core assumption is that learned image encoders tend to overfit to biases in the training data, while fixed geometric features avoid this. Evidence is given in the abstract and Section 4.1. A break condition occurs if the scattering feature space is misaligned with the predictive task; then the GP may still overfit or suffer from insufficient signal.

### Mechanism 3: Stability Translates to Sensible Uncertainty
The scattering transform is Lipschitz continuous w.r.t. deformations: ∥Φ(Dτf)−Φ(f)∥ ≤ C∥τ−id∥. Consequently, small input perturbations (e.g., minor shifts or noise) produce only small changes in the feature vector, so the GP posterior variance remains appropriately low. Larger shifts move the feature into a region with fewer training points, naturally increasing predictive uncertainty. This mechanism yields well‑calibrated, appropriately wide uncertainty estimates even under distribution shift, as long as the shift magnitude remains within the stability bound. Evidence is found in the abstract and the robust calibration results on shifted tasks (Section 5.2). The approach may fail under extreme shifts that violate the Lipschitz bound or introduce entirely new feature statistics.

- **Distribution Shift & Uncertainty Calibration Metrics**: *Why needed* – The paper's value proposition is robustness under shift; one must distinguish predictive accuracy (RMSE) from uncertainty quality (NLL, QCE, PI metrics). *Quick check*: What does a low Quantile Coverage Error (QCE) indicate about a model's uncertainty estimates, and why might it be more important than NLL in safety‑critical applications?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input Image (X) → Scattering Transform (Φ) → Feature Flattening (optional) → Probabilistic Head (GP) → Prediction (mean, variance)
- **Critical path**: Image → GPU‑friendly scattering extraction → feature vector → GP inference/training → predictive distribution.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Invariance vs. locality*: Global averaging yields full translation invariance; windowed scattering retains local translation sensitivity.
  - *Rotation*: Enforcing rotation‑invariance via averaging reduces dimensionality and improves robustness to orientation shifts but discards orientation‑specific information.
  - *Kernel & ARD*: Isotropic kernels (RBF, Matérn) are robust in high‑dimensional spaces; ARD can capture feature relevance but risks overfitting.
  - *Inference*: Exact GP (small N, Cholesky, O(N³)) vs. SVGP (large N, inducing points, mini‑batches).
- **Failure signatures**:
  - *Calibration collapse*: Very high NLL/QCE despite low RMSE; often seen with deep ensembles on scattering features.
  - *Numerical instability*: Linear kernel frequently unstable; use RBF/Matérn and initialize lengthscale to mean pairwise distance of training features.
  - *Hyperparameter overfitting*: Optimizing marginal likelihood with flexible ARD kernels leads to overfit models (low training NLL, high test NLL); avoid ARD for high‑dimensional scattering features.
  - *Feature misalignment*: Inappropriate scattering configuration (e.g., too few scales J, exceeding invariance) discards task‑relevant information, harming accuracy even if calibration appears reasonable.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. *Sensitivity analysis on QM2D*: Vary J (scales), L (angles), PCA usage, and kernel/ARD to establish best practices (J≈log₂N−1, L=8, no PCA, non‑ARD RBF/Matérn).
  2. *Distribution shift regression (HP, SLP, AW)*: Compare against deep models (ConvNeXt, DINOv2) on small‑data subsamples; evaluate robust calibration (QCE) and competitive RMSE/NLL, especially in data‑scarce regimes.
  3. *Bayesian optimization (QM2D, QM9)*: Use GP predictive mean/variance with Expected Improvement acquisition; verify regret curves match or beat Uni‑Mol, confirming utility for sequential decision‑making.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Classification under shift**: The paper focuses on regression and Bayesian optimization; it is unknown whether Bayesian scattering can be effectively applied to classification tasks, where discrete outputs and class imbalance may challenge GP calibration. Resolution would require benchmarking on standard classification shift datasets (e.g., Camelyon17, iWildCam) and reporting calibration metrics (ECE, NLL) against deep baselines.
2. **Extreme shift robustness**: The method has been tested only on real‑world shifts (institution, country) and relies on theoretical stability for small deformations. Its performance under large, controlled corruptions (e.g., heavy noise, strong elastic deformations) remains unknown. A stress test that applies increasing magnitudes of such transformations to standard datasets (CIFAR‑10, medical images) would clarify the limits of the approach.
3. **Marginal likelihood failure**: The ablation study shows that models optimizing marginal likelihood (e.g., ARD kernels) achieve lower training loss but much worse test NLL, indicating overfitting. It is unclear why the standard GP model selection criterion fails in this context and what alternatives are reliable. Analysis of the bias as feature dimensionality grows, and comparisons with hold‑out validation, cross‑validation, and information criteria (WAIC) across multiple datasets, would identify a robust selection strategy.

## Limitations
- **Invariance mismatch risk**: The scattering transform’s predefined invariances (translation, small deformation) may discard task‑relevant variations (e.g., large rotations), potentially harming accuracy even if calibration remains adequate.
- **Default hyperparameter sensitivity**: The recommended scattering parameters (e.g., $J \approx \log_2(N)-1$, $L=8$) are based on limited sensitivity analysis; their generalizability across diverse domains (medical, satellite, molecular) is not fully established.
- **Corpus evidence gap**: The claim that Bayesian scattering is a "principled baseline" for UQ on images is not strongly supported by prior literature; few works directly compare fixed geometric features to learned ones under shift, leaving the comparative robustness somewhat isolated.

## Confidence
- *Claim*: Bayesian scattering achieves competitive predictive accuracy in data‑scarce regimes. *Confidence*: **High** (empirically validated on multiple datasets).
- *Claim*: The method provides well‑calibrated uncertainty estimates under real‑world distribution shifts. *Confidence*: **Medium** (robust calibration shown on studied shifts, but the range of shifts is limited and extreme shifts are untested).
- *Claim*: Decoupling feature extraction from learning prevents overfitting to the training distribution. *Confidence*: **Medium** (theoretical rationale and some empirical support, but direct evidence of overfitting avoidance is sparse).
- *Claim*: Fixed geometric features constitute a universal baseline for UQ on images. *Confidence*: **Low** (novel positioning; limited prior comparisons; need further validation across a broader spectrum of tasks and shift types).

## Next Checks
1. **Invariance Stress Test**: Re‑run the key shift experiments (e.g., HistologyNucleiPixels) with a test set augmented by large rotations (90°). Expectation: a mismatch between scattering’s translation‑only invariance and the new orientation shift should cause a sharp increase in RMSE, while QCE may also degrade or remain deceptively stable.
2. **Wavelet Configuration Ablation**: On the QM9 dataset, systematically vary the wavelet quality factor $Q$ and the number of scales $J$ beyond the recommended heuristic $J \approx \log_2(N)-1$. Measure RMSE and NLL under a held‑out country/device shift to test the robustness of the parameter selection rule.
3. **Head‑Component Isolation**: Replace the GP head with a simple deterministic model (e.g., linear regression) trained on scattering features. Compare calibration (QCE) under shift to the full Bayesian scattering model. If robust calibration persists, it indicates that the scattering features themselves are the primary source of shift robustness, independent of the Bayesian treatment.
