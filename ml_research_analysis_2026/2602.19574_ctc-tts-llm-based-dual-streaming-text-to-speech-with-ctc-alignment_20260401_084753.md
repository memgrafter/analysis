# CTC-TTS: LLM-based dual-streaming text-to-speech with CTC alignment
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19574
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19574
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Replaces MFA alignment with CTC for low-latency dual-streaming TTS, cutting first-packet latency from 1674 ms to 159 ms while improving streaming intelligibility (WER 1.50% vs 2.40%).

## Executive Summary
CTC-TTS introduces a dual-streaming text-to-speech system based on large language models that achieves low latency without compromising quality. It replaces traditional Montreal Forced Alignment (MFA) with a CTC-based aligner to generate coarse but fast text-speech correspondences. A bi-word interleaving strategy then constructs streaming blocks, with two variants: CTC-TTS-L concatenates tokens along sequence length for higher quality, while CTC-TTS-F stacks embeddings along features for lower latency. Evaluations on single-speaker streaming and zero-shot multi-speaker continuation show significant improvements in word error rate (WER), character error rate (CER), and subjective metrics (MOS, SMOS) over MFA and ELLA-V baselines, with strong generalization to out-of-domain speakers.

## Method Summary
The system processes text through a grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) converter (Phonetisaurus), then uses a CTC alignment model trained on phoneme-speech pairs to produce approximate frame-level boundaries. These boundaries are used to split speech into blocks corresponding to bi-word units (two consecutive tokens). For streaming, the text and speech blocks are interleaved into a unified sequence. Two decoding strategies are proposed: CTC-TTS-L concatenates bi-word speech blocks along the time dimension, preserving long-range dependencies for quality; CTC-TTS-F stacks bi-word embeddings as feature channels, allowing immediate synthesis of each block for minimal latency. The LLM-based TTS (e.g., VALL-E) is trained on these interleaved sequences, enabling autoregressive generation of speech tokens conditioned on streaming text context.

## Key Results
- On single-speaker streaming, CTC-TTS-L reduces WER to 1.50% and CER to 0.79%, outperforming LLMVox (2.40% WER, 1.36% CER).
- CTC-TTS-F achieves first-packet latency of 159 ms versus 1674 ms for LLMVox, a 10× reduction.
- In zero-shot multi-speaker continuation, CTC-TTS-L reaches 4.82% WER, 2.47% CER, 4.33 MOS, and 4.60 SMOS, surpassing MFA and ELLA-V baselines.
- CTC alignment shows better generalization in out-of-domain cross-speaker tasks compared to MFA.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CTC alignment provides a computationally efficient, end-to-end trained alternative to MFA, avoiding segmentation errors from separate acoustic and linguistic models. The bi-word interleaving creates a natural dual-stream structure where text tokens and speech tokens are co-present in the training sequence, allowing the LLM to learn conditional generation in a streaming-friendly manner. CTC-TTS-L prioritizes quality by maintaining full temporal context, while CTC-TTS-F prioritizes latency by enabling block-wise parallel decoding. The CTC aligner’s coarse boundaries (approximately 1 text token to 3 speech frames) are sufficient for the LLM to resolve ambiguities, and the approach eliminates the need for precise frame-level forced alignment during inference.

- **Dual-Streaming TTS:** Simultaneous generation of text and speech tokens in a single autoregressive model. *Why needed:* Enables low-latency synthesis by outputting speech blocks as soon as text context is available. *Quick check:* Can the model handle out-of-order text tokens during streaming?
- **Bi-Word Interleaving:** Grouping two consecutive text tokens and their corresponding speech into a block. *Why needed:* Balances context length and latency; larger blocks improve quality but increase delay. *Quick check:* How does block size (bi-word vs. tri-word) affect the quality-latency trade-off?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Text -> G2P (Phonetisaurus) -> Phoneme Sequence -> CTC Aligner -> Coarse Boundaries -> Bi-Word Split -> Interleaved Sequence -> LLM TTS (VALL-E-style) -> Speech Tokens -> Vocoder -> Audio

**Critical Path:**  
Input text is converted to phonemes. The CTC aligner maps phonemes to speech frames, producing boundaries that split the speech into bi-word blocks. These blocks are interleaved with the text tokens to form a combined sequence. The LLM TTS is trained on such sequences and, at inference, generates speech tokens autoregressively conditioned on the available text context, enabling streaming.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- CTC-TTS-L vs. F: L concatenates blocks along time, requiring all previous blocks for the next; F stacks blocks as features, enabling independent block generation but losing long-range temporal consistency.
- Alignment granularity: CTC provides coarse (~1:3) boundaries; finer alignment may improve quality but increase computational cost.
- Unit choice: Phonemes normalize pronunciation but add G2P dependency; graphemes simplify pipeline but may harm alignment on irregular spelling.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- High WER/CER on out-of-vocabulary words, indicating G2P or alignment limitations.
- Latency spikes when bi-word blocks are misaligned, causing the LLM to wait for correct context.
- Poor MOS/SMOS for CTC-TTS-F on long utterances, suggesting feature stacking loses prosodic continuity.

**First Experiments:**  
1. Replace Phonetisaurus with a neural G2P (e.g., CTC-based) and evaluate OOV word handling.
2. Ablate the CTC aligner: train with MFA boundaries but keep bi-word interleaving, isolating CTC’s contribution.
3. Vary bi-word block size (unigram, trigram) and measure quality-latency curves on LibriSpeech test set.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do grapheme- or subword-based alignment units affect CTC-TTS performance compared to phonemes?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "exploring graphemes or subwords as alignment units is left for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The current design uses phonemes; other units could simplify the pipeline and better handle irregular spellings, but their impact on alignment and synthesis is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace the phoneme CTC aligner with one trained on graphemes/subwords, retrain CTC-TTS, and compare WER, CER, MOS, and latency on standard benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does swapping the WFST-based Phonetisaurus for a neural G2P model improve CTC-TTS robustness, particularly on out-of-vocabulary words?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "replacing the current WFST-based Phonetisaurus with neural G2P models such as the CTC-based one in JSA-SPG [29]"
- **Why unresolved:** Neural G2P may handle rare words better, potentially reducing phoneme errors that affect alignment and synthesis, but this benefit remains untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Integrate a neural G2P into the pipeline, evaluate on datasets with OOV words, and measure changes in intelligibility (WER/CER) and naturalness (MOS) relative to the WFST baseline.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can more precise boundaries from neural forced alignment models enhance sequence construction and streaming controllability in CTC-TTS?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "leveraging recent progress in neural forced alignment and speech–text alignment models to obtain more precise boundaries and further improve sequence construction and streaming controllability."
- **Why unresolved:** CTC alignment is coarse (≈1:3 ratio); finer boundaries might improve bi-word block accuracy and the quality-latency trade-off, but it is unclear if gains outweigh complexity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Use a neural forced aligner to produce frame-level boundaries, rebuild interleaved sequences, and compare objective (WER/CER, FPL) and subjective (MOS, SMOS) metrics against the CTC-TTS baseline.

## Limitations
- Missing critical implementation details: training data, hyperparameters, hardware, and evaluation protocols are unspecified, hindering reproducibility.

- Out-of-domain generalization claims lack explicit domain definitions and speaker diversity metrics.
- No statistical testing reported, so significance of observed improvements is uncertain.
