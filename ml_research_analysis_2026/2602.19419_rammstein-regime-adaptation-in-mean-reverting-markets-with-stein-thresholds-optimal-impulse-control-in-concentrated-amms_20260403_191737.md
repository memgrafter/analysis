# RAmmStein: Regime Adaptation in Mean-reverting Markets with Stein Thresholds -- Optimal Impulse Control in Concentrated AMMs

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.19419
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19419
*   **Reference count:** 39
*   **Primary result:** RAmmStein achieves a net ROI of 1.60% on 1Hz Coinbase data ($10M TVL, 1% width) by learning a dynamic rebalancing threshold driven by the mean-reversion speed (θ), reducing rebalance frequency by 85% versus greedy strategies.

## Executive Summary
Liquidity providers in concentrated automated market makers (AMMs) face a核心 trade-off: earning fees requires tight price ranges, but this increases costly rebalancing frequency. This paper formulates the rebalancing decision as an optimal impulse control problem. The core innovation is the "Stein Signal"—using the estimated mean-reversion speed (θ) of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck price process as a real-time market regime indicator. A Deep Double Q-Network (DDQN) agent learns a dynamic "laziness boundary" in the space of (θ, price deviation), only triggering rebalances when the low-θ (trending) regime justifies the gas cost. On out-of-sample 1Hz ETH-USD data, this regime-aware approach significantly outperforms fixed and greedy baselines, demonstrating that strategic inaction, guided by statistical regime detection, preserves capital and enhances net returns.

## Method Summary
The problem is modeled as an impulse control task with a fixed transaction cost (gas). The optimal policy satisfies a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellari Quasi-Variational Inequality (HJB-QVI), defining a "continuation region" (wait) and a "jump region" (rebalance). To approximate this solution, a DDQN agent is trained in a simulated environment. The state vector (8D) includes the current price relative to the LP's range, the estimated Ornstein-Uhlenbeck parameters (θ, µ, σ), and other metrics like recent volatility and active fraction. The agent outputs a binary action (hold/rebalance). The reward is net ROI: fees earned minus gas costs, scaled and slightly biased to encourage in-range positioning. OU parameters are re-estimated every 1800 seconds from the last 1Hz price data.

## Key Results
*   RAmmStein achieved a net ROI of **1.60%** on a 10-hour out-of-sample test ($10M TVL, 1% width), the highest among realistic strategies.
*   It **reduced rebalancing frequency by 85%** compared to a greedy (immediate) rebalancing strategy, which suffered a -8.4% ROI due to gas losses.
*   An extension, RAmmStein-Width, discovered extreme parsimony (only 9 rebalances, ~$40 gas) and degraded more gracefully under high gas costs than strategies with fixed or greedy width adjustments.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** The mean-reversion speed (θ) modulates the rebalancing threshold, enabling regime-aware "laziness."
*   **Mechanism:** High θ (strong mean-reversion) increases the value of waiting for natural price re-entry into the range. Low θ (trending) reduces this waiting value, making immediate rebalancing optimal. The DDQN learns this trade-off, setting a dynamic boundary in (θ, price deviation) space.
*   **Core assumption:** The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process correctly captures short-term price dynamics, and θ is a reliable, real-time regime indicator.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states θ is the "Stein Signal." Section VIII-B shows the learned boundary drops as θ increases; all 51 rebalances occurred at θ < 0.015. Figure 1 visualizes the clear threshold.
*   **Break condition:** If OU parameter estimation is unstable or the price process structurally breaks (e.g., shifts from mean-reverting to trending), the θ signal becomes stale, causing mistimed rebalances.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Framing rebalancing as an impulse control problem creates a "continuation region" (wait) and "jump region" (rebalance), which the agent learns to approximate.
*   **Mechanism:** The derived HJB-QVI (Eq. 18) mathematically defines this free boundary. The DDQN, by learning Q-values for "hold" vs. "rebalance," implicitly approximates this PDE solution. The value of waiting is an option-like payoff contingent on θ.
*   **Core assumption:** The optimal policy has a threshold structure learnable via value-based DRL from the provided state features.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Section III-C derives the QVI and two regions. Section III-E links the learned action boundary to the theoretical "Laziness Boundary." Section VIII-B's empirical boundary (Fig. 1) confirms the threshold structure.
*   **Break condition:** If the reward function miscalibrates the long-term opportunity cost of waiting (e.g., over-penalizing gas), the learned policy may collapse to always acting or always waiting, losing the threshold.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** The DDQN's state representation (8D), including OU parameters and position-relative metrics, provides sufficient features to generalize the impulse control policy.
*   **Mechanism:** The state encodes price position, distance to boundary, and crucially, the local market regime (θ, δµ, σ, volatility). The network learns a mapping from this composite state to the optimal action, where θ is a primary but not sole driver.
*   **Core assumption:** The hand-crafted state features are a sufficient statistic for the optimal control decision.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Section IV-B lists the 8 state components. Section VIII-B notes asymmetric rebalances suggest other dimensions (δµ, σ) contribute. Section VIII-D (Width extension) confirms multi-feature use.
*   **Break condition:** Noisy or miscalibrated state features (e.g., OU from a non-stationary window) degrade perception. High dimensionality with limited data risks overfitting.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Impulse Control Theory**
    *   **Why needed here:** The core problem involves discrete interventions with a fixed cost, not continuous control. Understanding the Quasi-Variational Inequality and the continuation/jump region distinction is key to interpreting the learned policy.
    *   **Quick check question:** What are the two conditions in the Quasi-Variational Inequality (Equation 18), and what does each region (continuation vs. jump) imply for the LP's action?
*   **Concept: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process & Parameter Estimation**
    *   **Why needed here:** θ is the central "Stein Signal." The agent's regime awareness depends on correctly modeling price as a mean-reverting diffusion and rolling-estimating its parameters.
    *   **Quick check question:** How is θ estimated from price data (Equation 13), and what does a high versus low θ value imply about expected price behavior?
*   **Concept: Deep Q-Networks (Dueling DDQN)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper uses DDQN to approximate the value function. Understanding experience replay, target networks, and decoupled action selection/value evaluation is key to grasping how the agent learns the Q-function.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why does Double DQN use the online network for action selection and the target network for value evaluation (Equation 7), and what problem does this solve?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Feature Engine (1Hz data -> 8D state) -> Environment Simulator (state + action -> next state + reward) -> DDQN Agent (selects action, learns Q-function).
*   **Critical path:** Preprocess data -> compute OU params -> construct state -> agent selects action -> environment updates price, computes fees/gas, updates position -> reward calculated -> transition stored -> minibatch sampled -> TD loss computed -> online network updated -> periodic target network sync.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   *Discrete vs. Continuous Action:* Base uses binary action (hold/rebalance) for stability. Width extension uses 6 discrete choices, trading granularity for simplicity.
    *   *State Representation:* 8D hand-crafted vector (includes θ, price metrics). Simpler than an LSTM baseline, aiding interpretability of the learned boundary.
    *   *Reward Shaping:* Net ROI reward includes an "Active Bonus" (ϵ·1in) to encourage in-range time, introducing inductive bias that risks over-optimizing for activity at the cost of gas.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **High rebalance count & negative ROI:** Agent hasn't learned laziness; likely ignoring θ or gas cost is negligible in reward. Check reward scaling (λ).
    *   **Never rebalancing:** Agent over-values waiting, possibly due to excessive "Active Bonus" or too high simulated cost. Leads to near-zero active fraction.
    *   **Rebalances while in-range:** Violates impulse control intuition. Suggests flawed state (1in indicator) or network overfitting to spurious correlations.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Baseline sanity check:** Run Fixed Passive, Fixed Active, and Set & Forget strategies on a short validation window. Confirm Fixed Active's high gas loss and Fixed Passive's low active time.
    2.  **Visualize learned policy:** For a trained agent, plot Q(rebalance)-Q(hold) over a grid of (θ, dedge) and overlay actual rebalance events. Verify the threshold structure and θ-dependence matches Figure 1.
    3.  **Gas sensitivity sweep:** Test the trained agent across a range of gas costs ($1, $2, $5, $10). Confirm the degradation profile matches Table IV and that RAmmStein's parsimony provides a performance floor during high-gas regimes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** Does continuous width optimization via policy gradient methods (e.g., SAC) improve net ROI and gas efficiency compared to the discrete 6-action space in RAmmStein-Width?
*   **Open Question 2:** Can incorporating the long-term OU mean µ as a rebalancing target (anticipatory repositioning) reduce reactive interventions and improve ROI in mean-reverting regimes?
*   **Open Question 3:** How does integrating impermanent loss (IL) into the reward function change the learned rebalancing boundaries and overall net ROI?
*   **Open Question 4:** Does multi-pool allocation across correlated AMM pairs (e.g., ETH/USDC, ETH/USDT) improve risk-adjusted returns compared to single-pool strategies?

## Limitations
*   Exact reward scaling factors (λ, ε) and the volume scaling factor (α) are not numerically specified, affecting reproducibility and policy optimality.
*   The OU estimation window (1800s) is fixed and may be suboptimal for volatile or shifting market regimes.
*   Generalization is untested: only ETH-USD 1Hz data is used; performance on other assets, pairs, or higher frequencies is unknown.

## Confidence
*   **θ-driven laziness claim:** **Medium**. Strong empirical support from own experiments, but limited to one asset/period; risk of regime shifts or non-OU processes.
*   **Impulse control threshold structure:** **High**. Theoretical derivation cleanly matches empirical boundary (Fig. 1); clear continuation/jump regions observed.
*   **DDQN policy sufficiency & state representation:** **Medium**. State includes key features, but asymmetric rebalances suggest unexplored interactions; reward design critically influences outcome.
