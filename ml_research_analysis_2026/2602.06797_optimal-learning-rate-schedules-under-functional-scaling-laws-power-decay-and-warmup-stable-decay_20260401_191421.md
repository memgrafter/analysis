# Optimal Learning-Rate Schedules under Functional Scaling Laws: Power Decay and Warmup-Stable-Decay

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06797
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06797
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Derived optimal learning‑rate schedules under functional scaling laws: power decay for the easy regime, warmup‑stable‑decay for the hard regime, and uncovered capacity saturation in fractional schedules.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates optimal learning‑rate (LR) schedules for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) under the functional scaling law (FSL), a framework that captures neural scaling behaviors via two exponents: signal learning exponent \(s > 0\) and noise forgetting exponent \(\beta > 1\). For a fixed data budget \(N\), the authors derive the LR schedule that minimizes the final excess risk, revealing a sharp phase transition. In the easy regime (\(s \ge 1-\beta^{-1}\)), the optimal schedule follows a power decay \(\eta(z) = \eta_{\text{peak}} (1 - z/N)^{2\beta-1}\) with peak tuned as \(\eta_{\text{peak}} \sim N^{-\nu}\), achieving final loss scaling as \(N^{-s\beta/(s\beta+1)}\). In the hard regime (\(s < 1-\beta^{-1}\)), the optimal strategy is warmup‑stable‑decay (WSD): maintain the largest stable LR for most steps and decay only during a vanishing late phase of size \(r_N \sim N^{-(1-\beta^{-1}-s)/(2-\beta^{-1})}\), attaining loss \(\sim N^{-s}\). Additionally, the paper shows that for shape‑fixed fractional schedules (only the peak tuned), the effective capacity becomes \(\alpha = \min\{\beta, \gamma+1\}\) where \(\gamma\) is the decay exponent, explaining why common schedules like cosine (\(\gamma=2\)) or linear (\(\gamma=1\)) significantly underperform when \(\beta > \gamma+1\). Finally, applying the power‑decay schedule to one‑pass SGD in kernel regression yields the exact minimax‑optimal rate without logarithmic factors, improving prior analyses.

The theoretical findings are validated through discrete‑time simulations of a linear model with a power‑law eigenvalue spectrum. The authors provide a detailed reproduction plan, enabling systematic verification of the phase transition, the capacity saturation phenomenon, and the superiority of the derived schedules. This work clarifies the interplay between signal learning and noise forgetting, offering principled guidance for LR scheduling in practice.

## Method Summary
The study considers one‑pass SGD on a linear model with input features drawn from a Gaussian design whose covariance is diagonal with eigenvalues \(\lambda_j \propto j^{-\beta}\). Target coefficients \(\theta^*\) are chosen so that \(\lambda_j |\theta_j^*|^2 \propto j^{-(1+s\beta)}\), defining the signal exponent \(s\). The LR schedule \(\eta(z)\) is a function of the step index \(z\). Under the FSL, the expected excess risk after \(N\) steps is approximated by a continuous integral over the schedule. This functional is minimized subject to a stability constraint \(\eta(z) \le \eta_{\text{stab}}\). Analytic optimization yields the two regimes described above. To validate, a simulator is built: feature dimension \(D = 2000\); for each \(N\), generate \(N\) i.i.d. samples; run SGD with a candidate \(\eta(z)\); compute final risk as the average \(\ell_2\) error on the true \(\theta^*\) over many seeds. A grid search over the peak learning rate identifies the optimal schedule for given \(N\). Scaling exponents are estimated by regressing log‑risk against log‑\(N\) across multiple \(N\) values.

## Key Results
- **Phase transition**: For \(s \ge 1-\beta^{-1}\) the optimal LR is a power decay (\(\eta \propto (N-z)^{2\beta-1}\)); for \(s < 1-\beta^{-1}\) the optimal LR is warmup‑stable‑decay with a constant phase of length \(N-o(N)\) and a brief power‑law decay.
- **Loss exponents**: Easy regime: risk \(\sim N^{-s\beta/(s\beta+1)}\); hard regime: risk \(\sim N^{-s}\) (optimal).
- **Capacity saturation**: For fractional schedules \(\eta(z)=\eta_0(1-z/N)^\gamma\) the effective capacity is \(\alpha = \min\{\beta, \gamma+1\}\), leading to exponents that saturate at \(s\) when \(\alpha \ge \beta\), explaining failure of common schedules when \(\beta > \gamma+1\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The FSL decomposes the risk into contributions from each eigenmode. Each mode \(j\) decays multiplicatively by a factor \(\exp(-\lambda_j \int_0^z \eta(t) dt)\) while noise accumulates proportionally to \(\int_0^z \eta(t)^2 \exp(-2\lambda_j \int_0^t \eta(s) ds) dt\). The final risk integral can be rewritten as \(\int_0^1 u^{-2/\beta} \eta(\tau N)^2 d\tau\) plus a signal term after a change of variables. The optimization problem becomes: minimize \(\int_0^1 u^{-2/\beta} \eta^2 du\) subject to the signal learning constraint \(\int_0^1 \eta du \ge\) some required amount, while respecting \(\eta \le \eta_{\text{stab}}\). In the easy regime, the constraint is not binding; calculus of variations yields \(\eta(u) \propto u^{\beta-1/2}\), i.e., a power decay in original coordinates. In the hard regime, the stability bound becomes active immediately, forcing \(\eta\) to stay at \(\eta_{\text{stab}}\) for most of the interval, with a terminal decay ensuring the signal constraint is just met. The phase transition occurs exactly when the unconstrained power‑decay solution first touches the stability bound at \(u=0\), which happens at \(s = 1-\beta^{-1}\). This elegant characterization explains why the two schedule forms are optimal in their respective regimes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Functional Scaling Law (FSL)**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a continuous, analytically tractable approximation of the discrete SGD risk, linking LR schedules to scaling exponents.  
  *Quick check*: For a simple polynomial schedule, compute the FSL integrals and verify they match empirical risk trends for moderate \(N\).

- **Signal Exponent (s)**  
  *Why needed*: Encodes the hardness of the learning problem via the decay of target coefficients; directly influences the optimal rate.  
  *Quick check*: In the data generator, set \(\theta_j^* = j^{-\alpha}\) and confirm that \(\lambda_j |\theta_j^*|^2 \propto j^{-(1+s\beta)}\) with \(\alpha = (1+s\beta-\beta)/2\).

- **Capacity Exponent (β)**  
  *Why needed*: Governs the decay of eigenvalues, which determines the effective capacity of the model and the noise‑forgetting rate.  
  *Quick check*: Plot eigenvalues on a log‑log scale; the slope should be \(-\beta\).

- **One‑pass SGD**  
  *Why needed*: Restricting to a single pass simplifies analysis and isolates the effect of the LR schedule.  
  *Quick check*: Count the number of updates; it must equal exactly \(N\) with no sample reuse.

- **Phase Transition (s = 1−β⁻¹)**  
  *Why needed*: It signals which optimal schedule applies; crossing the boundary changes the nature of the solution.  
  *Quick check*: For given \(s,\beta\) compute the inequality; then test empirically by measuring risk exponents on both sides.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**:  
Data Generator → Linear Model (θ) → SGD Optimizer (with schedule η(z)) → Risk Evaluator → Scaling Exponent Estimator

**Critical Path**:  
1. Choose exponents \(s, \beta\) and set \(D\) sufficiently large to capture the signal.  
2. For each training size \(N\) (log‑spaced), generate dataset \((\Phi, y)\).  
3. Initialize \(\theta = 0\) and run one‑pass SGD using a candidate LR schedule.  
4. Compute final excess risk \(\mathbb{E}[\|\theta-\theta^*\|_\Lambda^2]\) on the true distribution via averaging over many seeds.  
5. Repeat steps 2–4 for \(S\) seeds to reduce variance.  
6. Over a set of \(N\) values, fit a line to \(\log(\text{risk})\) vs \(\log(N)\) to estimate the scaling exponent.  
7. Compare against theoretical predictions to validate the schedule.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- **Feature dimension \(D\)**: Larger \(D\) reduces truncation error of the infinite‑dimensional sum but increases memory and compute. Typically \(D \approx 2000\) suffices for \(\beta \ge 5\).  
- **Grid search over \(\eta_{\text{peak}}\)**: A coarse grid risks missing the optimum; a fine grid multiplies runtime. A practical approach: start with a logarithmic sweep of 10 points per decade, then refine around the best region.  
- **Number of seeds \(S\)**: Low \(S\) yields noisy risk estimates; \(S \ge 30\) is usually adequate for stable exponent fits.  
- **Discrete vs Continuous**: The FSL is a continuous approximation; for small \(N\), discrete corrections may matter, so experiments should include a range of \(N\).

**Failure Signatures**:  
- **Divergence**: If \(\|\theta_k\|\) grows exponentially, the LR peak exceeds the stability bound. Reduce \(\eta_{\text{peak}}\) or enforce \(\eta \le 0.5/\lambda_1\).  
- **No decay with constant \(\eta\)**: In hard regimes, a constant LR should lead to a non‑vanishing risk floor as \(N\to\infty\). If risk continues to drop, the schedule may not be truly constant or the problem may be in the easy regime.  
- **Exponent mismatch**: Estimated exponent significantly lower than \(s\) in hard regime, or lower than \(s\beta/(s\beta+1)\) in easy regime, indicates issues in schedule implementation or risk calculation.  
- **High variance**: Large error bars across seeds suggest insufficient \(S\) or problematic randomness (e.g., too small \(D\)).

**First Three Experiments**:  
1. **Capacity saturation test**: With \(\beta=5\) and \(s=0.75\) (hard), run fractional schedules \(\gamma=1,\dots,6\). Verify that for \(\gamma\le 3\) the exponent is lower (approx \(0.6\)) while for \(\gamma\ge 4\) it reaches \(s=0.75\).  
2. **Power decay superiority (easy regime)**: \(s=1, \beta=5\). Compare fractional \(\gamma=2\) (exponent \(\approx0.75\)) with power decay (\(\gamma=2\beta-1=9\), exponent \(\approx0.8333\)); the latter should achieve lower risk.  
3. **WSD robustness in hard regime**: \(s=0.6, \beta=5\). Implement WSD with \(r_N = N^{-\gamma_r}\), \(\gamma_r = (1-\beta^{-1}-s)/(2-\beta^{-1}) \approx 0.111\). Vary the multiplicative constant on \(r_N\) (0.5×, 1×, 2×); the achieved exponent should remain near \(0.6\). A constant \(\eta\) schedule should show a higher asymptotic risk.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why do schedules that postpone decay (like WSD) work?**  
   The paper explains this within the FSL framework, but it's unclear whether this reasoning extends to deep neural networks, multi‑epoch training, or optimizers with momentum.

2. **Extending analysis to adaptive optimizers and momentum.**  
   The current theory only covers vanilla SGD; incorporating Adam, RMSProp, or momentum fundamentally alters the dynamics and requires new scaling laws.

3. **Estimating the exponents \(s\) and \(\beta\) from data.**  
   Optimal LR schedules depend on \(s\) and \(\beta\), but these are assumed known. Developing reliable estimators to infer them from training runs would make the theory practically usable.

4. **Warmup‑stable‑decay under multi‑epoch training.**  
   The derived WSD schedule occupies a vanishing phase for one‑pass SGD. It is unknown whether such a schedule remains optimal when multiple passes over the data are allowed.

## Limitations
- The analysis relies on high‑dimensional Gaussian diagonal designs and a continuous‑time limit, which may not directly reflect finite neural networks.
- The exact multiplicative constant in the optimal peak learning rate (\(\eta_{\text{peak}} \sim N^{-\nu}\)) is not derived; it must be tuned empirically.
- The decay‑phase size \(r_N\) for warmup‑stable‑decay is given only up to an asymptotic order, so its sensitivity to hidden constants is unclear.
- The study is confined to one‑pass SGD; extending to multi‑pass or stochastic optimizers with adaptivity remains an open problem.

- Achieved loss exponents (\(s\beta/(s\beta+1)\) in easy regime, \(s\) in hard regime): **Medium**
