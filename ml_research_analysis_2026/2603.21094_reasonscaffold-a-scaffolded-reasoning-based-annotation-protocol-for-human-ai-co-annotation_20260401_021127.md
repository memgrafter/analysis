# ReasonScaffold: A Scaffolded Reasoning-based Annotation Protocol for Human-AI Co-Annotation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21094
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21094
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** ReasonScaffold substantially improves inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ up to 0.98) with negligible label revisions (<1.1% AEP) by having annotators revise after viewing LLM-generated reasoning without predicted labels.

## Executive Summary
Annotator variability is a major challenge in subjective NLP tasks. This paper introduces ReasonScaffold, a two-pass co-annotation protocol where human annotators first label independently, then revise their labels after reviewing structured reasoning from an LLM (including self-generated examples and chain-of-thought analysis) without seeing the model’s predicted labels. Evaluated on sentiment classification and opinion detection using 500 conversational utterances and four annotators per task, ReasonScaffold yields dramatic gains in inter-annotator agreement: Cohen’s κ increases from 0.76 to 0.98 for sentiment and from 0.73 to 0.85 for opinion. The proportion of label revisions (Annotator Effort Proxy) is very low (0.74% and 1.05% respectively) and revisions are bidirectional, suggesting the approach resolves ambiguous cases without introducing anchoring bias. These results demonstrate a promising direction for human-AI collaboration in data annotation.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates ReasonScaffold on two subjective tasks: sentiment classification (positive/negative/neutral) and opinion detection (binary). Data consists of 500 conversational utterances (source unspecified) annotated by four annotators per task. The protocol comprises two passes. In Pass 1, annotators label each instance using only the provided guidelines. In Pass 2, after a short break, annotators revisit the same instances but are shown the LLM’s structured reasoning, which includes (a) a few self-generated examples representing each label category and (b) a step‑by‑step chain‑of‑thought analysis highlighting lexical, pragmatic, and contextual cues. Crucially, the model’s predicted label is never displayed; annotators are instructed to revise only if the reasoning reveals cues they previously overlooked. LLM reasoning is generated with an instruction‑tuned model (e.g., GPT‑4) at low temperature (0.2) for consistency. The primary evaluation metric is Cohen’s κ, comparing pairwise annotator agreement before and after exposure to reasoning. A secondary metric, the Annotator Effort Proxy (AEP), measures the proportion of revised labels across all annotators and instances. Optional analyses examine how initial disagreements are resolved and whether revisions exhibit systematic directionality.

## Key Results
- Cohen’s κ improved substantially: sentiment κ from 0.76 to 0.98; opinion detection κ from 0.73 to 0.85.
- Annotator Effort Proxy (AEP) remained very low: 0.74% (sentiment) and 1.05% (opinion), indicating minimal label changes.
- Revisions were bidirectional, suggesting no anchoring bias toward the LLM’s perspective.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ReasonScaffold enhances agreement by providing annotators with LLM-generated reasoning that clarifies ambiguous cues without revealing a definitive label. The reasoning—comprising representative examples and detailed chain‑of‑thought—helps annotators notice lexical or contextual signals they might have missed in the first pass. Because the model’s prediction is hidden, annotators remain free to accept or reject the reasoning, preventing anchoring bias. The low revision rate indicates that most initial labels were already sound, and the reasoning selectively resolves only those cases where genuine uncertainty existed, leading to higher consistency with minimal effort.

## Foundational Learning
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting:** Enables LLMs to produce step‑by‑step reasoning that can be inspected by humans. *Why needed:* Provides transparent rationale for label decisions. *Quick check:* Can the model articulate its reasoning in natural language?
- **Self‑example generation:** The LLM creates exemplars for each label category to illustrate patterns. *Why needed:* Offers concrete reference points for annotators. *Quick check:* Are generated examples diverse and representative?
- **Inter‑annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ):** Measures labeling consistency among annotators. *Why needed:* Primary metric for evaluating protocol effectiveness. *Quick check:* κ ranges from -1 to 1; higher values indicate better agreement.
- **Annotator Effort Proxy (AEP):** Proportion of labels changed in the second pass. *Why needed:* Quantifies how much revision occurs, indicating cognitive overhead. *Quick check:* AEP = (number of revisions) / (total labels).
- **Scaffolded annotation:** Two‑pass design with intermediate reasoning. *Why needed:* Balances independence with collaborative refinement. *Quick check:* Does the protocol preserve initial independent judgment before exposure?
- **Bidirectional revision check:** Analyzes whether label changes go both ways or systematically favor one class. *Why needed:* Detects subtle bias from reasoning exposure. *Quick check:* Count revisions from A→B vs B→A.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Dataset -> LLM Reasoning Engine -> Reasoning Repository -> Annotation Interface (Pass 1) -> Break/Sequestration -> Annotation Interface (Pass 2 + reasoning overlay) -> Agreement Metrics

**Critical path:** (1) Prepare dataset and guidelines; (2) Generate reasoning for all instances offline; (3) Recruit annotators; (4) Conduct Pass 1 labeling; (5) Enforce break; (6) Conduct Pass 2 labeling with reasoning visible; (7) Compute Cohen’s κ and AEP.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Showing reasoning without predictions avoids anchoring but may provide insufficient guidance;   
- Low temperature ensures consistent reasoning but reduces diversity;  
- Two‑pass protocol improves agreement but doubles annotation time;  
- Using a small sample (500) keeps experiments feasible but may yield unstable estimates.

**Failure signatures:**  
- κ improvement <0.05 suggests reasoning is ineffective (check coherence);  
- AEP >5% indicates annotators are over‑revising (check for anchoring);  
- Revisions overwhelmingly in one direction (e.g., mostly toward “positive”) imply bias;  
- Very short dwell time on reasoning panel suggests annotators ignore it.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Measure baseline inter‑annotator agreement on Pass 1 labels (κ₁).  
2. Measure post‑reasoning agreement on Pass 2 labels (κ₂).  
3. Compute AEP and analyze revision directionality (balanced vs. skewed).

## Limitations
- Exact LLM model, prompts, and self‑example generation procedure are unspecified, hindering precise replication.
- The source and preprocessing of the 500 utterances are unclear, affecting generalizability.
- Annotation guidelines and precise label definitions (especially for opinion detection) are insufficiently described.

## Next Checks
