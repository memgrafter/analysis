# TheoremForge: Scaling up Formal Data Synthesis with Low-Budget Agentic Workflow

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17332
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17332
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieves 12.6% Verified Rate at $0.481 per successful trajectory using Gemini-3-Flash, surpassing the 8.6% baseline, and increases data yield by 1.6× for proof generation via a Decoupled Extraction Strategy.

## Executive Summary
TheoremForge tackles the prohibitive cost of agentic workflows for formal mathematics data synthesis by decomposing the formalization process into five specialized sub-tasks and implementing a Decoupled Extraction Strategy. This strategy recovers valid training signals from globally failed trajectories, converting partial successes into usable data. The system orchestrates a low-cost general-purpose LLM (Gemini-3-Flash) with limited expert model calls, achieving a Verified Rate of 12.6% on a 2,000-problem benchmark at an average cost of $0.481 per successful trajectory—significantly improving upon the baseline while maintaining high data yield.

## Method Summary
The workflow decomposes formal theorem proving into statement formalization, proof generation, premise selection, proof correction, and proof sketching. Each sub-task is treated as an independent synthesis problem with its own verification signal (e.g., compilation checks, semantic LLM judges, kernel verification). Instead of discarding failed full trajectories, the system applies local verification criteria to each intermediate step, extracting valid samples (e.g., a correct formal statement) even if the final proof fails. Gemini-3-Flash handles orchestration (premise selection, sketching, iterative refinement) while expert models (ReForm-32B, Goedel-Prover-32B) are used sparingly for formal code generation. Verification layers enforce syntactic and semantic correctness, and an early-stop controller prunes unsolvable subgoals to control costs.

## Key Results
- Verified Rate: 12.6% on 2,000-problem benchmark vs. 8.6% baseline.
- Cost: $0.481 per successful trajectory (Gemini-3-Flash orchestrator).
- Data Yield: 1.6× increase for proof generation by harvesting from failed trajectories; formalization samples from failures outnumber those from successes by >4×.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Decomposing formal theorem proving into five specialized sub-tasks enables modular optimization and harvesting of partial successes.
- **Mechanism:** Each sub-task is an independent synthesis problem with its own verification (e.g., compilation for statements). This allows extraction of valid training samples (e.g., a correct formal statement) even when the overall proof fails.
- **Core assumption:** Intermediate products are independently useful for training, and verification can be decoupled from global success.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] decomposes into five sub-tasks; [section 2] defines sub-task data formats; [section 3.3] extraction logic; neighboring papers (Seed-Prover 1.5, Solving Formal Math Problems by Decomposition) use decomposition.
- **Break condition:** Sub-tasks become too interdependent or management overhead outweighs partial extraction gains.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The Decoupled Extraction Strategy increases data yield by salvaging valid intermediate outputs from globally failed trajectories.
- **Mechanism:** Local verification (e.g., compilation, subgoal proof checks) identifies valid steps in a failed trajectory, storing them as training samples for their respective sub-tasks regardless of final outcome.
- **Core assumption:** Partial successes contain valuable, reusable signals identifiable via task-specific verification without full problem resolution.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Decoupled Extraction Strategy; [section 3.3] explicit extraction rules; [section 4.3] majority of statement (1,338 vs 693) and proof (1,129 vs 693) samples from failures; weak neighboring evidence.
- **Break condition:** Local verification is unreliable (e.g., subproof inconsistent with goal) or extracted partial data introduces noise.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Using a low-cost general-purpose LLM (Gemini-3-Flash) for orchestration while reserving expensive expert models for formal coding achieves favorable cost-performance.
- **Mechanism:** Tasks are assigned based on model strengths: general LLMs handle high-level reasoning (premise selection, sketching, refinement) while experts handle syntax-sensitive formal code generation. This reduces expensive expert calls and leverages cheaper models for iterative work.
- **Core assumption:** General LLMs are sufficiently capable at orchestration tasks; expert models are necessary for formal code; cost savings outweigh performance loss.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] $0.481 cost per success; [section 1] lack of expert models for non-coding tasks necessitates general LLMs; [section 4.2] Gemini-3-Flash achieves 69.7% of Pro’s performance at 1/10th cost; [section 6] collaborative paradigm divides labor.
- **Break condition:** General LLMs are too weak at orchestration, causing early failures, or expert models become cheaper.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input Preprocessor -> Retrieval Module (LeanExplore) -> Expert Modules (ReForm-32B, Goedel-Prover-32B) -> General LLM Core (Gemini-3-Flash) -> Verification Layer (Compiler, LLM Judges, Kernel) -> Decoupled Extractor -> Early Stop Controller.

**Critical path:** Informal statement → Normalization → Premise retrieval → Expert formalization sampling → Semantic check → Formal statement → (if successful) Theorem retrieval → Informal proof generation → Proof sketch → Subgoal extraction → Parallel subgoal solving (expert + LLM) → Proof assembly → Verified proof (or failure). Data extraction occurs at every node where local verification passes.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Cost vs. coverage: Capping expert sampling (k=4) and refinement rounds reduces cost but may miss valid proofs.
- Generality vs. specialization: Using a general LLM for most tasks reduces costs and increases flexibility but limits performance on tasks requiring deep formal expertise.
- Early stopping vs. completeness: Pruning after first subgoal failure saves resources but may abort recoverable trajectories.
- Semantic verification reliability: Using 6 LLM judges (excluding generator) improves robustness but adds API costs and may still disagree.

**Failure signatures:**
- Proof Sketching (47.8% of failures): Unreasonable subgoal decomposition (79.6% of sketch failures) or mathematical errors (19.4%).
- Subgoal Solving (30.4% of failures): Expert model fails to prove subgoals despite correction attempts.
- Semantic Check (7.3% of failures): Formal statement compiles but diverges in meaning from informal input.
- Syntax Check (14.5% of failures): Formal statement or proof fails compilation.

**First 3 experiments:**

2. Large-scale data yield analysis: Deploy on 2,000 problems to measure overall VR (12.6%), cost per success ($0.481), and quantify data extracted from failed trajectories (formalization samples from failures should exceed successes by >4×).

Expect strongest performance in Algebra/Number Theory and declining VR with difficulty, but verify coverage of hard problems (e.g., IMO-level from DeepTheorem).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How does TheoremForge's cost-performance compare to other agentic formalization workflows?** The study did not conduct empirical comparisons due to cost and replication hurdles; evidence would be a common benchmark reporting verified rate and cost per success for each workflow.
2. **What is the downstream impact of TheoremForge-synthesized data on fine-tuning formal reasoning models?** The work focused on synthesis, not downstream training; evidence would be fine-tuning a base model with TheoremForge data and evaluating on a held-out benchmark.
3. **How can TheoremForge be benchmarked against alternative architectures to validate its synthetic corpora?** No empirical comparison of different architecture designs has been performed; evidence would be comparing TheoremForge with other formal data synthesis architectures on data yield, cost, and verification rate.

## Limitations
- No random seed provided for the 2,000-problem benchmark; different samples may yield varying results.
- Expert models (ReForm-32B, Goedel-Prover-32B) may be unavailable, forcing substitutions that degrade performance.
- Full prompt templates are missing; implementation may drift without the code repository.
- Access to all seven verifier models (e.g., GPT-5.2) is uncertain; alternatives could shift Verified Rate.
- Early stopping might discard recoverable trajectories; decoupled extraction could introduce noisy partial data.

## Confidence
- Overall outcome (12.6% VR, $0.481/success): Medium (reproducibility uncertainties).
- Decomposition into five sub-tasks: Medium (supported but interdependency risk).
- Decoupled extraction strategy: Medium (yield increase shown but generalizability unproven).
- Low-cost orchestrator + expert models: High (clear cost metrics, consistent with model pricing).
