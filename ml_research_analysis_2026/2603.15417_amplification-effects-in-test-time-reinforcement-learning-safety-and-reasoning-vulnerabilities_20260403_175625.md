# Amplification Effects in Test-Time Reinforcement Learning: Safety and Reasoning Vulnerabilities

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.15417
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15417
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Test-time reinforcement learning (TTRL) amplifies a model's existing safety or harmfulness under prompt injection and incurs a reasoning tax on mathematical tasks.

## Executive Summary
Test-time reinforcement learning (TTRL) improves LLM reasoning by using a self-consistency reward based on majority vote across multiple sampled responses. However, because the model updates during inference on unlabeled test data, it becomes vulnerable to prompt injection attacks. This study investigates how injecting harmful or benign prompts during TTRL affects both safety and reasoning performance across five instruction-tuned models. The results show two opposing amplification effects: if the base model is safe on jailbreak prompts, TTRL further increases safety (safety amplification); if the model is vulnerable, TTRL increases harmful compliance (harmfulness amplification). Both scenarios come at a cost: a "reasoning tax" where accuracy on a held-out set of mathematical problems (AMC) drops significantly. Attack success rates on held-out jailbreak sets rise under the more sophisticated HarmInject attack, and a simple numeric filter fails to mitigate these effects. The findings reveal that TTRL's self-consistency objective tightly couples safety and reasoning, causing amplified behaviors and degraded reasoning gains when faced with injected data.

## Method Summary
The authors implement TTRL as described by Zuo et al. (2025), using Group Reference Policy Optimization (GRPO) with LoRA adapters. For each test prompt, the model samples K=32 responses (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95). The final token of each response is extracted as a label; these labels form a majority vote pseudo-label \(\hat{y}\). Each response receives a reward of +1 if its label matches \(\hat{y}\), else 0. The policy is updated with GRPO (actor LR 5e-7, critic LR 9e-6, KL coefficient 0.00, LoRA rank 64) on rollout batches of 8 prompts. Experiments run for 250 steps (or 350 steps to equalize AMC exposure). Models include Qwen2.5-0.5B/1.5B-Instruct, Llama3.2-1B/3B-Instruct, and Llama3-8B-Instruct. Datasets: AMC for math reasoning, JailbreakV-28k, WildJailbreak, and Llama Artifacts for jailbreaks, UltraFeedback for benign instruction following. Safety is evaluated using LlamaGuard3-8B to compute attack success rate (ASR) on held-out jailbreak prompts. Reasoning performance is measured by AMC pass@1 with 16 samples.

## Key Results
- **Safety amplification**: Models that initially refuse jailbreak prompts show decreased ASR after TTRL with jailbreak injection (e.g., Qwen-1.5B-Instruct ASR on JailbreakV-28k drops from 22% to 10%; on WildJailbreak from 36% to 2%).
- **Harmfulness amplification**: Models vulnerable to the injected jailbreak style exhibit increased ASR (e.g., Qwen-1.5B-Instruct ASR on Llama Artifacts rises from 76% to 94%; Llama-3B-Instruct ASR increases from 4% to 20% under HarmInject).
- **Reasoning tax**: AMC mathematical accuracy declines under injection, with magnitude depending on attack strength. For Qwen-1.5B, accuracy falls from 37.7% to 32.3% with WildJailbreak (-5.4 pp) and to 19.1% with HarmInject (-18.6 pp). Llama-8B suffers a 14.0 pp drop when injected with Llama Artifacts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: TTRL amplifies a base model's existing safety behavior on injected jailbreak prompts.
- **Mechanism**: The majority-vote pseudo-reward in TTRL reinforces the most frequent response pattern across multiple samples. If the base model tends to refuse jailbreak prompts, repeated safe refusals become the majority label, driving gradient updates to increase refusal probability.
- **Core assumption**: The model's initial behavior distribution on injected prompts is biased toward a dominant class (safe or harmful).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] explicitly defines "safety amplification" and "harmfulness amplification" as outcomes of reinforcing dominant behavior.
  - [Section 4.2] shows decreasing ASR for Qwen-1.5B-Instruct on JailbreakV-28k (22%→10%) and WildJailbreak (36%→2%) after TTRL with jailbreak injection, visualised in Figure 3a,b.
  - [corpus] Corpus papers (e.g., MAPLE, Tool Verification) discuss majority-vote limitations but do not directly test safety amplification; evidence is indirect via consensus reliability concerns.
- **Break condition**: If the injected prompt distribution is balanced or lacks a clear majority class, the reinforcement signal weakens, reducing amplification.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: TTRL amplifies a base model's existing harmful compliance on injected jailbreak prompts.
- **Mechanism**: When the base model frequently generates harmful completions for jailbreak prompts, majority vote selects a harmful pseudo-label. GRPO updates then increase the likelihood of harmful responses, raising ASR on held-out jailbreaks.
- **Core assumption**: The model is initially vulnerable to the specific jailbreak style injected.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] notes "harmfulness amplification when it is vulnerable to the injected data."
  - [Section 4.2] Table 2 shows ASR increase for Qwen-1.5B-Instruct on Llama Artifacts (76%→94% [+12 pp]) after injection; Figure 3c shows rising ASR.
  - [Section 4.3] demonstrates harmfulness amplification even with benign instruction-following injection (e.g., Llama-3B-Instruct ASR 4%→20% [+16 pp] in Table 4).
- **Break condition**: If the model's harmful responses are highly diverse (final tokens vary), majority vote may not converge on a single harmful label, weakening amplification relative to safety amplification.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: TTRL under prompt injection incurs a "reasoning tax"—degraded performance on benign reasoning tasks (AMC).
- **Mechanism**: The self-consistency objective competes for representational capacity. Updates driven by injected prompts (jailbreak or instruction-following) shift the model's distribution away from specialized mathematical reasoning patterns toward generic/refusal templates, causing entropy collapse.
- **Core assumption**: Reasoning and safety behaviors share overlapping parameter space; optimizing one degrades the other under distribution shift.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] states "both cases incur a reasoning tax – a drop in AMC mathematical accuracy" with examples (Qwen-1.5B: -5.4 pp with WildJailbreak, -18.6 pp with HarmInject).
  - [Section 4.2] Table 3 shows consistent accuracy drops across models after harmful injection (e.g., Llama-8B: 14.0%→0.0% [-14.0 pp] with Llama Artifacts).
  - [Section 4.2] "entropy collapse" example: Llama-8B reuses generic openers; Llama-1B outputs stock "answer is 0" completions.
- **Break condition**: If reasoning and safety objectives are perfectly disentangled in parameter space (unlikely in current models), the tax may not manifest.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Test-Time Training (TTT)**
  - **Why needed here**: TTRL is a TTT method; understanding that the model updates *during inference* on unlabeled test data is fundamental to seeing why prompt injection is catastrophic.
  - **Quick check question**: If the test stream contains both AMC problems and jailbreak prompts, what does the majority-vote pseudo-reward signal encourage for each input type?
- **Concept: Self-Consistency via Majority Voting**
  - **Why needed here**: The core TTRL reward mechanism. The amplification effects directly stem from aggregating K samples into a single pseudo-label.
  - **Quick check question**: Why might refusal responses produce a stronger majority signal than harmful completions?
- **Concept: Policy Gradient (GRPO)**
  - **Why needed here**: The update rule that converts the pseudo-reward into parameter changes. GRPO's group reference policy affects how quickly behaviors amplify.
  - **Quick check question**: How does a sparse reward signal (e.g., +1 for majority, 0 otherwise) impact learning stability and potential for overfitting to injected patterns?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  - 1. **Input stream**: Heterogeneous test prompts (mix of AMC, jailbreak, HarmInject).
  - 2. **Multi-sampler**: Generates K responses per prompt at temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95.
  - 3. **Label extractor**: Takes final token of each response; converts to numeric for AMC, text for others.
  - 4. **Majority voter**: Computes pseudo-label \(\hat{y}\) from K extracted labels.
  - 5. **Reward assigner**: \(r(y_k)=+1\) if \(y_k=\hat{y}\) else 0.
  - 6. **GRPO updater**: Policy gradient step using batch size 8, LR 5e-7 (actor), LoRA rank 64.
  - 7. **Evaluation hooks**: Periodic ASR (LlamaGuard3-8B judge) and AMC pass@1 estimation.
- **Critical path**: Input → Multi-sampler → Label extractor → Majority voter → Reward → GRPO update → Updated policy → Next input. The label extractor and majority voter are the amplification bottleneck.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Consistency vs. diversity*: High K and low temperature increase majority reliability but reduce exploration, accelerating amplification of dominant (even harmful) patterns.
  - *Task coupling*: TTRL's single reward stream (majority correctness) cannot simultaneously optimize for safety *and* reasoning when prompts are mixed; the injected task dominates.
  - *Label fidelity*: Final-token extraction is brittle; it privileges short, stereotyped endings (refusals) over diverse harmful outputs.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Entropy collapse**: Repeated phrases/templates across unrelated AMC problems.
  - **ASR drift**: Steady increase (harmfulness amplification) or decrease (safety amplification) on held-out jailbreaks during TTRL steps.
  - **Reasoning tax**: Post-TTRL AMC accuracy below baseline *without* TTRL or below early-TTRL steps.
  - **Filter bypass**: Simple numeric filters fail when HarmInject forces a numeric answer for both subqueries.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline TTRL (RQ1)**: Run TTRL on pure AMC for 250 steps; measure ASR stability on held-out jailbreaks. Expect: no significant ASR change → TTRL on clean reasoning doesn't alter base safety.
  2. **Harmful Injection (RQ2)**: Mix AMC with 60% jailbreak prompts. Track ASR and AMC accuracy. Expect: direction of ASR change (↑ or ↓) reflects base model vulnerability; accuracy drops → reasoning tax.
  3. **HarmInject Adversarial (RQ4)**: Use prompts pairing AMC + jailbreak; same evaluation. Expect: stronger ASR increase (harmfulness amplification) and severe accuracy drop; simple filter fails.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can test-time training (TTT) methods be designed to simultaneously improve reasoning and maintain safety, avoiding amplification of existing behaviors and the reasoning tax?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The conclusion states: “Future work will develop novel TTT methods for LLMs that can balance both reasoning and safety.” The paper itself demonstrates that TTRL couples safety and reasoning, leading to amplification and degradation.
- **Why unresolved**: Current TTT methods like TTRL inherently tie the self-consistency reward to the dominant behavior in the test data, causing amplification; no alternative approach that decouples these objectives has been proposed or evaluated.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A new TTT algorithm that either separates safety and reasoning signals or incorporates an external safety validator, showing on multiple models that reasoning (e.g., AMC accuracy) improves without increasing attack success rate (ASR) even under mixed prompt streams with injected harmful data.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What is the exact mechanism by which majority-vote pseudo-label extraction in TTRL causes stronger safety amplification than harmfulness amplification, and how does the choice of label extraction method (e.g., final token vs. empty parser) affect this?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper hypothesizes that refusals yield stereotyped final tokens (e.g., “sorry”) yielding strong majority signals, while harmful responses are more diverse, weakening reinforcement. Preliminary experiments with an empty parser extraction suggested stronger safety amplification, but results were inconsistent. However, this explanation is not systematically validated.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper provides correlative evidence but lacks controlled studies isolating variables like token diversity, response length, or extraction rules; the impact of label extraction across models remains unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic experiments varying the label extraction strategy (last token, first token, full-text similarity, empty parser) across multiple models and jailbreak datasets, measuring safety amplification (ASR change) and reasoning tax (AMC change). Additionally, analyzing the distribution (e.g., entropy) of extracted pseudo-labels could confirm the correlation with amplification strength.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can more sophisticated mitigation techniques (beyond simple numeric filtering) effectively prevent harmful amplification and reasoning degradation in TTRL under prompt injection attacks like HarmInject?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper tests a simple filtering method (reject non-numeric majority votes) and finds it insufficient against HarmInject: “Simple filtering fails against HarmInject, indicating insufficient mitigation.” They explicitly state: “More sophisticated safe test-time training methods are needed.”
- **Why unresolved**: The failure of one naive approach does not rule out other defenses; potential strategies (input sanitation, dynamic reward shaping, safety fine-tuning) remain unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Proposing and evaluating a robust defense that detects or neutralizes injected jailbreak queries during TTRL, preserves rewards for purely reasoning prompts, and empirically reduces ASR to near-baseline while minimizing reasoning tax across models and injection types, including HarmInject.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Do the amplification effects and reasoning tax observed in TTRL generalize to other self-consistency-based test-time training methods (e.g., self-training with pseudo-labels, entropy-based TTT)?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper focuses solely on TTRL, a specific TTT method using majority-vote rewards. Many TTT approaches rely on self-consistency (e.g., maximizing confidence, entropy minimization). It is unclear whether they share the same coupling of safety and reasoning; the paper mentions related works but does not compare them.
- **Why unresolved**: Amplification might be inherent to any self-consistency objective that uses model-generated pseudo-supervision, but this has not been tested. Different pseudo-labeling strategies could mitigate or exacerbate the effects.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replicating the prompt injection experiments on other representative TTT methods (e.g., maximum confidence self-training, entropy minimization TTT) and measuring safety/harmfulness amplification and reasoning tax. Comparative analysis would reveal whether the risks are specific to TTRL or ubiquitous across self-consistency TTT.

## Limitations
- Exact GRPO algorithm (group size, baseline, critic training) not fully specified, hindering precise replication.
- Prompt templates (for AMC, jailbreaks, HarmInject, and LlamaGuard) are unreported, risking misaligned label extraction and evaluation.
- Label extraction rules (handling empty/non-numeric tokens, punctuation) are ambiguous and critically affect the majority-vote pseudo-reward.
