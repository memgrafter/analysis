# Mechanistic Foundations of Goal-Directed Control
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15248
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15248
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Context window size *k* is the critical parameter for arbitration gate formation between reactive and prospective control, with a sharp threshold at *k*=8, optimal separation Δ=0.192 at *k*=32; gate dynamics follow an exponential moving average; task demand selects strategy; deeper attention layers cannot compensate for insufficient context length.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the MAIA architecture to study infant motor learning through the lens of mechanistic interpretability. The system comprises modular components for perception, body schema, control, and a contingency gate that arbitrates between reactive and prospective control strategies. By systematically varying the context window size *k*, the authors uncover a sharp phase transition in the emergence of the arbitration circuit: below *k* ≤ 4 the gate fails to form, while above *k* ≥ 8 a fully resolved phase diagram appears, peaking in separation at *k*=32. The gate’s commitment dynamics during exploitation are well approximated by an exponential moving average whose rate scales with *k*. Moreover, task demand (goal distance and tolerance) determines which strategy dominates—prospective control prevails under low demand, reactive under high demand. The findings demonstrate that temporal context length, not network depth, is the binding constraint for learning such arbitration mechanisms.

## Method Summary
The study uses a minimal 1D cursor‑to‑goal tracking environment that simulates infant motor learning. The agent receives RGB frames, which are encoded by an object‑centric module into latent slots for cursor and goal. A two‑joint hidden actuator model is maintained by a body schema that updates proprioceptive latents. Control is handled by a GATEL0RD cell that proposes actions based on the current slots and recurrent state. A contingency gate, implemented as a two‑layer attention mechanism, examines a sliding buffer of the last *k* steps consisting of actions, task demand, and slot latents, and outputs a confidence value for committing to a prospective strategy. Training follows a two‑phase schedule: a warm‑up period with high temperature (τ=10) for exploration, then a cold phase (τ→0.001) to force commitment and circuit formation. Evaluation sweeps *k* and task demand *d*, measuring separation Δ (difference in gate confidence between low and high demand) and fitting the gate’s confidence trajectories to an exponential moving average.

## Key Results
- **Critical *k* threshold & optimum:** Arbitration emerges sharply at *k*≥8, with maximal separation Δ=0.192 at *k*=32; for *k*≤4 the gate remains non‑functional (Δ≈0).
- **EMA dynamics:** The gate’s commitment confidence during the cold phase follows an exponential moving average *c(t)=c∞−(c∞−0.5)e<sup>−t/k</sup>*; the time constant scales with context length.
- **Task‑demand arbitration:** Low demand (long reach, loose tolerance) favors prospective control, high demand (short reach, tight tolerance) favors reactive control, yielding a diagonal boundary in the phase diagram.
- **Depth irrelevance:**Increasing attention layers beyond two does not improve separation when context is insufficient; sufficient *k* is the primary factor.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Context window size *k* as the critical parameter
- **Claim:** The context window size *k* is the critical architectural parameter governing whether an arbitration gate can form between reactive and prospective control strategies.
- **Mechanism:** The contingency gate integrates a confidence signal over the last *k* steps. A minimum window is required to accumulate enough evidence to distinguish strategies. Below *k*≤4 the window cannot hold a coherent prospective trajectory, so arbitration fails. Above *k*≥8 the gate can form and its confidence scales with window size.
- **Core assumption:** Prospective control needs a temporal horizon of at least *K*≈10 steps, setting the minimum context needed.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “We identify context window *k* as the critical parameter governing circuit formation: below *k*≤4 the arbitration mechanism cannot form; above *k*≥8, the phase diagram is fully resolved.”
  - Results (p.4): Phase diagrams across *k* reveal a sharp threshold; at *k*=32 the diagram is fully resolved, aligning with the prediction that the gate requires *k*≥*K*≈10.
  - Corpus: Neighbouring work on arbitration circuits and induction heads provides contextual plausibility.
- **Break condition:** Further increases beyond ~32 yield diminishing returns (Δ saturates).

### Mechanism 2: Exponential moving average approximation of commitment dynamics
- **Claim:** The learned contingency gate’s temporal commitment dynamics are well‑approximated by a closed‑form exponential moving average (EMA).
- **Mechanism:** After temperature collapse, confidence *c(t)* evolves as *c(t)=c∞−(c∞−0.5)e<sup>−t/k</sup>*, with *k* setting the integration rate. The attention‑based gate inherently implements this temporal accumulation.
- **Core assumption:** The gate’s evidence accumulation mathematically resembles an EMA with a time constant proportional to *k*.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Results (pp.4‑5): Following τ→0.001, commitment dynamics are well approximated by an EMA; quality varies with *k*.
  - Results (p.5, Fig.5): Confidence trajectories (solid) match EMA predictions (dashed), especially at *k*=32.
  - Corpus: Parallel analysis of learned dynamics in cognitive maps provides methodological precedent.
- **Break condition:** The EMA surrogate cannot reproduce task‑demand arbitration (Δ≈0), showing it captures integration but not the full decision logic.

### Mechanism 3: Task demand determines strategy dominance
- **Claim:** Task demand determines which control strategy dominates: prospective at low demand, reactive at high demand.
- **Mechanism:** Demand parameter *d* jointly controls goal distance and tolerance radius. Low *d* allows prediction error to accumulate safely, favoring anticipatory control; high *d* leaves no margin, favoring immediate feedback. The gate learns to select the strategy whose expected error fits the tolerance.
- **Core assumption:** The agent internally estimates prediction error for each strategy and compares it to an implicit tolerance representation.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “task demand determines strategy dominance—prospective control prevails at low demand, reactive at high.”
  - Results (p.4): Phase diagram shows demand‑dependent arbitration consistent with the tolerance‑window prediction.
  - Corpus: Work on prefrontal‑hippocampal interactions lends biological plausibility.
- **Break condition:** No clear diagonal separation in the phase diagram for *k*≥8 indicates failure of demand‑dependent arbitration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Foundational Inductive Biases**  
  - *Why needed here:* These hardwired constraints (object‑centric perception, event‑based representations, relational body schema) reduce the hypothesis space, making learning of causal control circuits from high‑dimensional input tractable.  
  - *Quick check question:* Can you identify which component (perception, controller, body schema) corresponds to each inductive bias and state how it simplifies the learning problem?

- **Concept: Phase Transition in Circuit Formation**  
  - *Why needed here:* The sharp non‑linear change in gate functionality at *k*=4→8 is a diagnostic signature of circuit formation, indicating a qualitative shift analogous to induction head emergence in transformers.  
  - *Quick check question:* When sweeping *k*, what specific phase‑diagram outcome (Δ value, visual pattern) signals successful arbitration gate formation?

- **Concept: Temperature Annealing for Commitment**  
  - *Why needed here:* The two‑phase training schedule (high temperature for exploration, then rapid cooling) forces the agent to first explore diverse strategies and then commit to a specific policy, which is crucial for the emergence of a clear arbitration circuit. Without this schedule, the gate might not form a stable confidence signal.  
  - *Quick check question:* How might the timing of the temperature collapse interact with the context window size *k*?

- **Concept: Exponential Moving Average (EMA) as a Diagnostic Surrogate**  
  - *Why needed here:* The EMA is a closed‑form proxy that reveals the gate’s temporal integration profile, allowing comparison across *k* without full ablation; it captures *how* confidence builds even if it cannot replace decision logic.  
  - *Quick check question:* Given a confidence trajectory *c(t)*, how would you fit an EMA (α=1/*k*) and why would a poor fit indicate the gate’s dynamics are not simply exponential integration?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Perception Module -> Control Unit (GATEL0RD) -> Environment -> Body Schema Module -> Contingency Gate. Auxiliary: Replay Buffer for offline updates.

### Critical path
1. Perception parses current frame into object‑centric slots.  
2. Control Unit proposes an action based on slots and recurrent state.  
3. Environment executes the action via the hidden actuator chain and returns a new observation.  
4. Body Schema updates its internal joint state estimates from the action and proprioception.  
5. Contingency Gate observes the buffer of the last *k* step tuples (action, demand, cursor slot, goal slot) and computes commitment confidence.  
6. If confidence crosses a threshold (via slot selection), the system may transition from reactive to prospective control (γ influences policy or a separate prospective head).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Context *k* vs. depth *N<sub>L</sub*:** *k* is binding; deeper attention layers (>2) yield negligible gain. Prioritize larger *k* over deeper gate networks.
- **Temperature schedule:** Warm‑up (τ=10) enables exploration; cold phase (τ→0.001) forces commitment. Collapse timing interacts with *k*; too early hinders evidence accumulation.
- **Oracle actions vs. learned policy:** Experiments often use oracle actions to isolate gate learning; in a full agent, gate and policy co‑adapt, introducing instability.

### Failure signatures
- **Dead regime (*k*≤4):** Confidence hovers ~0.5; phase diagram uniform (Δ<0.01).
- **EMA mismatch:** Confidence *c(t)* does not approach an asymptote exponentially; fit error high.
- **Non‑emergent arbitration:** At *k*≥8, phase diagram shows no demand‑dependent structure (Δ≈0).
- **Depth over‑reliance:** Expecting increased depth to compensate for small *k* fails (Table 2: NL=1 works at *k*=32).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Context window sweep & phase diagram:** Sweep *k*∈{1,2,4,8,16,32,64}, train gate with fixed moderate demand, plot confidence over epochs. For a subset (e.g., 1,4,8,16,32), train across a grid of demands and plot the 2D phase diagram. Success: sharp transition (Δ>0.1) between *k*=4 and *k*=8, optimal Δ at *k*=32.
2. **EMA fitting & ablation:** At *k*=32, record confidence during cold phase across seeds, fit EMA *c(t)=c∞−(c∞−0.5)e<sup>−t/k̂</sup>*. Success: fitted *k̂* within 20% of true *k*, small residuals. Replace learned gate with fixed EMA and rerun phase diagram; failure if diagram collapses (Δ≈0).
3. **Task demand arbitration validation:** At *k*=32, generate full phase diagram, compute Δ = confidence(d=0) – confidence(d=0.88). Success: Δ>0.15 with clear diagonal boundary; prospective dominates low‑*d*, reactive dominates high‑*d*. Also verify average prospective trajectory length >10 steps on low‑*d* trials.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Associative route interface with symbolic systems:** How does the allostatic (associative) control route connect to symbolic processing for semantic labeling, narrative synthesis, and formal reasoning? The paper defers this to future work; needed evidence: implement an explicit belief‑tracking module and test integration with symbolic components in abstract tasks.
2. **Generalization to higher‑dimensional embodied control:** Do the *k* thresholds and arbitration dynamics hold in richer environments (2D/3D manipulation, multiple objects)? Current evidence only covers minimal 1D; needed: replicate sweeps in more complex simulators.
3. **Dynamic adjustment of context window *k*:** Can agents learn to adapt *k* based on task demands or developmental stage? The study treats *k* as fixed; needed: train agents with learnable context spans (e.g., adaptive attention) and observe co‑adaptation.
4. **State‑dependent arbitration beyond EMA:** What computational elements in the gate enable task‑demand arbitration, given the EMA surrogate fails there? Needed: targeted ablations (e.g., remove demand input) and attention analysis to identify critical features.
5. **Validation against infant motor development:** Can phase diagrams be quantitatively matched to infant reaching or EEG data? The developmental timeline is approximate; needed: longitudinal infant data to extract gate‑confidence correlates and test for predicted phase transitions.

## Limitations
- The object‑centric perception encoder is underspecified, leaving the quality and disentanglement of cursor/goal slots uncertain.
- Key hyperparameters (attention dimension, learning rate, temperature schedule) are omitted; their values could shift the reported *k* thresholds.
- Training details (duration, number of seeds) are not provided, raising reproducibility concerns and potentially obscuring variance.
