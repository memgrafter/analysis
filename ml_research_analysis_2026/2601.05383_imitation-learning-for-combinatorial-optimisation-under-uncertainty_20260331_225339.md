# Imitation Learning for Combinatorial Optimisation under Uncertainty

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05383
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05383
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A generalized DAgger algorithm integrating expert taxonomy by uncertainty handling, optimality, and interaction yields learned policies that approach the performance of a two-stage stochastic expert (average cost 1795.93 vs. 1785.58) on a dynamic physician-to-patient assignment problem, with interactive learning and MIP gap relaxation improving data efficiency and training time.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies imitation learning (IL) for combinatorial optimization (CO) under uncertainty, with a focus on the design of the expert policy used to generate demonstrations. It proposes a taxonomy for experts based on how they handle uncertainty (myopic, deterministic, full-information, two-stage stochastic), their optimality (task-optimal vs. approximate), and their interaction with the learner (one-shot vs. interactive). Building on this taxonomy, the authors develop a generalized DAgger algorithm that can incorporate multiple expert types and interaction schemes. The method is evaluated on a dynamic physician-to-patient assignment problem. Key findings show that stochastic (two-stage) experts consistently provide the highest-quality supervision, leading to the best final policies. Interactive learning further improves performance and sample efficiency. Practically, relaxing the expert's optimality via an MIP gap significantly reduces training time with minimal performance loss, and aggregating deterministic experts offers a computationally cheaper alternative.

## Method Summary
The core method is a generalized dataset aggregation (DAgger) algorithm tailored for CO under uncertainty. It iteratively aggregates demonstrations from an expert policy (chosen from the proposed taxonomy) into a growing dataset, retraining a neural network learner after each round. The algorithm formalizes "aggregation" to handle multiple expert queries per state and defines a flexible interaction schedule (e.g., decaying probability of taking the expert's action). The expert itself is an optimization model (e.g., a two-stage stochastic program) that maps a state to an action. The framework allows trading off expert quality (number of scenarios, MIP gap) against computational cost. The approach is validated on a simulation of a dynamic physician-to-patient assignment problem, where the learner imitates the expert's assignment decisions under stochastic patient arrivals.

## Key Results
- Stochastic (two-stage) experts generate training data that leads to significantly lower-cost policies than deterministic or full-information experts.
- Interactive DAgger-style learning improves solution quality and requires fewer demonstrations compared to one-shot behavioral cloning.
- Relaxing the expert optimality via an MIP gap (e.g., 0.2%) drastically reduces expert query time while maintaining or improving the learned policy's performance.
- An aggregated deterministic expert (solving separate deterministic models per scenario and averaging) provides a computationally efficient alternative, though it is outperformed by the true two-stage stochastic expert on the tested problem.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Stochastic Expert Supervision
- **Claim:** Stochastic expert policies (two-stage) generate higher-quality training demonstrations because they incorporate future uncertainty, leading to learned policies that better handle stochasticity.
- **Mechanism:** The expert solves a two-stage stochastic model optimizing expected cost over multiple future scenarios. The learner imitates these anticipatory actions rather than myopic or deterministically forecast-based ones.
- **Core assumption:** The stochastic model is tractable with a moderate number of scenarios, and the scenario distribution is representative.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "stochastic experts consistently outperform deterministic and full-information experts"; [section 4.1] definition of two-stage expert; [section 6.2.2] Tables 6, 8, 9 showing lower costs for policies learned from two-stage experts.
- **Break condition:** Too few scenarios or solver failure to find high-quality solutions within time limits deteriorates supervision quality.

### Mechanism 2: Interactive Learning Mitigates Covariate Shift
- **Claim:** Interactive DAgger-style learning improves policy performance and data efficiency by reducing covariate shift between training and deployment.
- **Mechanism:** At each iteration, the current learner generates trajectories. The expert provides actions for states visited, exposing the learner to its own induced state distribution and aligning training data with deployment states.
- **Core assumption:** The expert remains reliable (near-optimal) on states visited by the learner as the policy improves.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "interactive learning improves solution quality with fewer demonstrations"; [section 3.1.1] DAgger as mitigation for distribution shift; [section 6.2.2] Figure 6 and Table 8 showing interactive vs. isolated learning.
- **Break condition:** Expert queries are too expensive to perform frequently, or the learner diverges drastically early.

### Mechanism 3: MIP Gap Relaxation for Efficient Training
- **Claim:** Relaxing expert optimality via an MIP gap maintains learned policy quality while significantly reducing training time.
- **Mechanism:** Early termination of expert solves when the optimality gap falls below a threshold yields near-optimal actions quickly. Imitation learning tolerates small suboptimalities because only consistent guidance is needed.
- **Core assumption:** Actions with small MIP gaps are sufficiently close to optimal for the learner to mimic effectively.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "relaxing expert optimality via an MIP gap substantially reduces training time without degrading performance"; [section 6.2.2] Tables 9 & 10 and Figure 7 showing time drop with 0.2% gap; [section 5] Algorithm 3 includes κ as stopping criterion.
- **Break condition:** The gap threshold is too large (e.g., 0.1% in Figure 8), making expert actions noticeably suboptimal and degrading performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Behavioral Cloning (BC)**
  - *Why needed:* BC is the simplest IL approach that trains a policy via supervised learning on expert state-action pairs. It forms the basis for both PPT and AIT paradigms but suffers from distribution shift when deployed.
  - *Quick check:* Why does BC alone often fail in sequential decision making even with abundant expert demonstrations?

- **Concept: Covariate Shift in IL**
  - *Why needed:* This phenomenon explains why a policy trained on fixed expert data encounters unseen states during deployment, leading to error accumulation. It motivates interactive methods like DAgger.
  - *Quick check:* How does the state distribution of a deployed policy differ from that of the expert, and why does this matter?

- **Concept: Two-Stage Stochastic Optimization**
  - *Why needed:* Central to the paper’s expert taxonomy; it captures anticipation of future uncertainty via recourse decisions. The two-stage expert is shown to provide the strongest supervision.
  - *Quick check:* What are first-stage and second-stage decisions in a two-stage stochastic program, and how do they differ from deterministic optimization?

- **Concept: Dataset Aggregation (DAgger)**
  - *Why needed:* DAgger is the core iterative algorithm that mitigates covariate shift. The paper proposes a generalized DAgger that incorporates multiple expert types, interaction schemes, and aggregation.
  - *Quick check:* How does DAgger’s data collection differ from BC, and why does it lead to more robust policies?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Expert Module -> Learner Module <- Dataset
  Expert Module -> Interaction Loop -> Environment Simulator
  Interaction Loop -> Learner Module (for action selection)
  Environment Simulator -> Interaction Loop (state transitions)
- **Critical path:**
  1. Initialize dataset and policy.
  2. For iteration i = 1..I:
     - Simulate H episodes.
     - At each decision epoch: Observe state; Query expert J times; Aggregate actions; Store (state, target) in dataset; Select action via decision rule (mix of expert and learner); Transition state.
  3. After H episodes, retrain learner on full dataset.
  4. Stop when criterion met (max iterations, convergence).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Expert quality vs. compute: Two-stage stochastic gives better supervision but much slower than deterministic.
  - Scenarios: More scenarios improve expert quality but increase query time. Aggregated deterministic experts trade off by solving separately and aggregating (parallelizable).
  - Interaction frequency: More iterations improve performance but increase total expert calls and training time. Decaying β in vanilla DAgger balances exploration and autonomy.
  - MIP gap: Laxer gap reduces expert time but risks poorer actions. Moderate gap (e.g., 0.2%) often suffices.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Validation cost remains high, particularly due to excessive high-priority rejections.
  - Expert solver frequently hits the time limit without closing gap, indicating poor numerical behavior.
  - No cost reduction after several DAgger iterations, suggesting the learner is not benefiting from new data.
  - Dataset contains low diversity (states clustered in a small region), implying poor exploration.
  - Training time per iteration grows unexpectedly, perhaps due to worsening expert solve times as policy explores harder states.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline verification: Implement and run greedy, full-information, and two-stage stochastic baselines (no learning). Confirm costs and solve times match Table 4 and Table 7.
  2. Isolated learning with deterministic expert: Generate fixed dataset (I=1) using deterministic expert. Train MLP and evaluate. Expect performance between greedy and two-stage baseline; training should be fast (<4 hours).
  3. Interactive learning with two-stage stochastic (5 scenarios): Run DAgger with I=400, H=10, using two-stage expert with 5 scenarios. Track cost per iteration. Expect decreasing cost approaching two-stage baseline; total training time ~23 hours.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can we develop adaptive expert query mechanisms that decide both when to query and which expert to consult, based on model uncertainty, state novelty, or expected performance gain?
2. How can we effectively incorporate multiple heterogeneous experts (e.g., myopic, deterministic, stochastic) into a unified learning framework that leverages their complementary strengths while minimizing computational overhead?
3. Can we establish formal performance guarantees that relate the quality (optimality gap) of an approximate expert to the suboptimality of the learned policy, particularly in stochastic sequential decision problems?
4. How can we design decomposition-based prediction architectures that break down complex combinatorial decisions into sequential stages, each guided by specialized experts, to improve scalability and accuracy?
5. For what classes of combinatorial optimization problems under uncertainty does the aggregated deterministic expert approach provide a more favorable computational trade-off compared to two-stage stochastic programming, and how can this be predicted a priori?

## Limitations
- Single-domain validation only; generalizability to other CO problems under uncertainty untested.
- Expert solve time (especially stochastic) limits scenario count; scalability to larger instances unclear.
- Assumes perfect knowledge of the stochastic process; model mismatch risks are not assessed.
- No theoretical guarantees for DAgger with aggregated or approximate experts.

## Confidence
- **Stochastic experts superiority:** Medium (strong in-domain evidence but needs cross-domain verification).
- **Interactive DAgger improves sample efficiency:** Medium (benefit shown here; sensitivity to iteration budget not fully probed).
- **Aggregated deterministic experts as efficient alternative:** Medium (demonstrated for one problem; transferability uncertain).
- **MIP gap relaxation:** Medium (0.2% gap works in experiments; robustness to other gaps and problems unknown).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-domain test:** Apply method to another stochastic combinatorial problem (e.g., vehicle routing) to reproduce claims about stochastic vs. deterministic experts and interactive learning.
2. **Scenario scaling:** Evaluate performance when varying stochastic scenario counts (5, 20, 50, 100) to assess the quality/compute trade-off of two-stage experts.
3. **MIP gap robustness:** Test multiple gap thresholds (0.1%, 0.5%, 1%) across random seeds to confirm stability of the time-quality trade-off and identify threshold sensitivity.
