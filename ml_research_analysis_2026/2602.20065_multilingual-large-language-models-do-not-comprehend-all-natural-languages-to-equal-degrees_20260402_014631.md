# Multilingual Large Language Models do not comprehend all natural languages to equal degrees

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20065
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20065
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs underperform humans in 10 of 12 languages; Spanish and Italian achieve highest model accuracy; Spanish proximity predicts performance better than English; non-Latin scripts amplify low-resource penalties.

## Executive Summary
Multilingual large language models (LLMs) are often assumed to handle many languages uniformly, yet empirical evidence of unequal comprehension is sparse. This study provides a systematic evaluation of three leading LLMs (GPT-4o, Grok-3, DeepSeek-V3) across 12 languages using a controlled comprehension benchmark. Results reveal that all models underperform human accuracy in most languages, with only two non‑significant differences (Spanish for GPT‑4o; Italian for DeepSeek‑V3). Unexpectedly, English never emerges as the top‑performing language; instead, Romance languages (Spanish, Italian) consistently achieve the highest scores. Statistical models show that linguistic proximity to Spanish—not English—positively predicts accuracy, and that non‑Latin scripts experience an amplified penalty when the language is low‑resource. The findings highlight intrinsic biases in LLM cross‑linguistic capabilities, underscoring the need for more equitable training data and model design.

## Method Summary
The study adapted a 40‑item short‑scenario comprehension task (Dentella et al., 2024) into 12 languages spanning Latin and non‑Latin scripts and varying resource levels. Human baselines were gathered from 480 native speakers on Prolific, each completing all items with attention checks; performance was scored as binary accuracy and stability across three repetitions. Three state‑of‑the‑art LLMs (GPT‑4o, Grok‑3, DeepSeek‑V3) were queried via official APIs using default temperature, with each item presented three times per language to compute stability. Statistical analysis employed generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with binomial link, incorporating agent × language interactions and random effects for items and participants. Additional predictors—language size, lexical/grammatical distance from English and Spanish, and writing system—were added to probe drivers of cross‑linguistic variation. Human performance served as a reference for model comparisons via Tukey‑adjusted contrasts.

## Key Results
- All models significantly underperform human accuracy in 10 of 12 languages; only GPT‑4o matches human Spanish performance and DeepSeek‑V3 matches human Italian performance.
- Romance languages (Spanish, Italian) achieve the highest model accuracy; English never ranks best.
- Linguistic proximity to Spanish predicts accuracy positively, while proximity to English does not; non‑Latin scripts suffer a steeper decline in performance for low‑resource languages.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

#### Mechanism 1: Spanish Linguistic Proximity
- **Claim:** Linguistic proximity to Spanish positively influences LLM comprehension accuracy across languages.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs likely develop internal representations that align more efficiently with linguistic structures similar to Spanish, possibly due to biases in training data distribution or optimization processes favoring certain language families.
- **Core Assumption:** Shared lexical and grammatical features reduce processing difficulty, leading to higher accuracy.
- **Evidence Anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “Accuracy is better predicted by linguistic proximity to Spanish (positive effect) than to English.”  
  - Results: GLMMs show significant negative beta for distance from Spanish, indicating better performance as distance decreases.  
  - Corpus context: The effect may reflect dataset composition rather than an inherent linguistic advantage.
- **Break Condition:** If a language very close to Spanish (e.g., Portuguese) fails to outperform others, or if controlling for training data volume eliminates the effect.

#### Mechanism 2: Non‑Latin Script Penalty for Low‑Resource Languages
- **Claim:** Non‑Latin writing systems amplify the performance penalty associated with low‑resource languages.
- **Mechanism:** Tokenization models (e.g., byte‑pair encoding) produce longer, less informative token sequences for non‑Latin scripts, increasing sparsity and reducing data efficiency, which disproportionately harms low‑data languages.
- **Core Assumption:** Script type interacts with data scarcity to degrade representation learning.
- **Evidence Anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “non‑Latin scripts show a stronger penalty from low‑resource status.”  
  - Results: For all models, language size had a significantly stronger impact on accuracy for non‑Latin scripts than for Latin scripts.  
  - Corpus: Related work on quantization hints at script challenges but does not directly address resource interactions.
- **Break Condition:** If high‑resource non‑Latin script languages (e.g., Chinese) achieve parity with Latin‑script counterparts in controlled experiments.

#### Mechanism 3: Tokenization Inefficiency
- **Claim:** Tokenization inefficiencies for certain language structures lead to suboptimal comprehension, independent of resource size.
- **Mechanism:** Morphologically rich or logographic languages may be segmented into more tokens, diluting semantic information and increasing sequence length, which can impair the model’s ability to capture meaning.
- **Core Assumption:** Token count and quality directly affect model performance on linguistic tasks.
- **Evidence Anchors:**  
  - Abstract: Lists “tokenization” as a relevant factor.  
  - Discussion: References Kim et al.’s finding that controlling for information amount rather than tokens changes language rankings, suggesting tokenization bias.  
  - Corpus: Indirect support from studies on shared representations across languages.
- **Break Condition:** If re‑tokenizing with language‑specific vocabularies eliminates cross‑linguistic performance gaps, or if performance correlates with average tokens per sentence after controlling for linguistic features.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** The experimental pipeline consists of: (1) LLMs (GPT‑4o, Grok‑3, DeepSeek‑V3) queried via APIs; (2) Benchmark: Dentella et al.’s 40‑item task translated into 12 languages; (3) Human baseline: 480 native speakers on Prolific; (4) Predictor variables: language size (Ethnologue), lexical/grammatical distances (eLinguistics/Grambank), writing system (Latin/non‑Latin); (5) Analysis: GLMMs for accuracy & stability.

**Critical path:**  
1. Translate and validate benchmark across target languages with native linguists.  
2. Collect model responses via API (40 runs per language) and human data.  
3. Score accuracy and compute stability per item‑participant.  
4. Fit GLMMs with agent × language interaction, then add predictors.  
5. Interpret coefficients, especially the effect of Spanish distance (negative beta indicates better performance with smaller distance).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Used default temperature to reflect typical user experience, though this may vary model stability.  
- Translation with lexical adjustments rather than structural changes to maintain task consistency, risking subtle meaning shifts.  
- Balanced WEIRD/non‑WEIRD and script types, but limited to 12 languages from 5 families, limiting generalizability.  
- Chose closed models for relevance, but this limits reproducibility compared to open‑source alternatives.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Persistently low accuracy in Greek and Japanese across models, pointing to script‑related challenges.  
- GPT‑4o showing higher instability than humans in most languages, suggesting sensitivity to prompting or temperature.  
- Romance languages topping rankings, contrary to English‑dominance assumptions.  
- Models exceeding human stability, indicating non‑human‑like consistency that may mask comprehension gaps.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Tokenization analysis:** Compute average tokens per item per language and correlate with accuracy after controlling for resource size.  
2. **Control for training data:** Use per‑language dataset estimates (if available) to partial out data size effects from linguistic distance.  
3. **Cross‑model replication:** Test open‑weight models (e.g., Llama 3) with the same benchmark to see if patterns hold across architectures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions; however, the findings and limitations point to several unanswered issues. These include the underlying cause of the Spanish proximity effect, the precise interaction between non‑Latin scripts and resource scarcity, and the generalizability of results to other models and languages. See the Next Checks and Limitations sections for further details.

## Limitations
- Exact prompting parameters (e.g., max_tokens, stop sequences) are not reported, which may affect reproducibility.
- Linguistic distance measures and human exclusion criteria are underspecified, hindering exact replication.
- The tokenization hypothesis is based on indirect performance patterns rather than direct measurement.

## Confidence
- **Spanish proximity > English:** Medium confidence (robust GLMM coefficient but possible corpus artifact)
- **Non‑Latin script × low‑resource penalty:** Medium confidence (consistent interaction, mechanism plausible but untested)
- **Tokenization as primary driver:** Low confidence (theoretical anchor, indirect evidence)

## Next Checks
1. Replicate findings using open‑weight models (e.g., Llama 3.1 70B) to rule out closed‑model idiosyncrasies.
2. Perform a token‑length analysis: correlate per‑language average tokens per item with accuracy, controlling for resource size.
3. Introduce a high‑resource non‑Latin script language (e.g., Chinese) into the benchmark to test the script‑penalty hypothesis.
