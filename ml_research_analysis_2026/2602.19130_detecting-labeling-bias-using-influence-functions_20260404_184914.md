# Detecting labeling bias using influence functions
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19130
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19130
- Reference count: 3
- Primary result: Nearly 90% of mislabeled samples detected with <1% false positives on MNIST with 20% label flips.

## Executive Summary
Labeling bias—systematic errors in training labels—degrades model reliability. This paper applies influence functions, a robust statistics tool, to identify mislabeled training samples. The core method computes each sample’s impact on the loss of misclassified test points via a second‑order Taylor approximation using gradients and an approximate Hessian. On a simple MNIST binary task, the approach achieves high detection accuracy; on the more complex CheXpert medical dataset, mislabeled samples still show higher influence but with weaker separation, likely due to Hessian approximations. The work demonstrates a practical pipeline for sample valuation in the presence of label noise, though scalability and approximation trade-offs remain key challenges.

## Method Summary
The pipeline consists of four steps: (1) Train a target model on the (possibly noisy) training set and verify its sensitivity to label noise by comparing with a baseline model trained on clean data. (2) Approximate the Hessian matrix of the expected loss; for small models use a diagonal approximation, for larger models restrict computation to the last layer. (3) For each misclassified test sample, compute its loss gradient. For every training sample, compute its gradient, scale by the inverse Hessian approximation, and take the dot product with each test gradient to obtain an influence score. Aggregate scores (e.g., average over misclassified test points) per training sample. (4) Rank training samples by aggregated influence and apply a threshold to flag harmful samples as candidate mislabeled points. Detection performance is evaluated by comparing flagged samples against the known flipped labels.

## Key Results
- On MNIST (binary 4 vs. 9, 20% label flips): ~90% of flipped samples detected with <1% false positive rate by thresholding influence scores.
- On CheXpert (Pleural Effusion, 20% positive flips): Flipped samples exhibited consistently higher influence scores than clean ones, but separation was weaker, limiting precise detection.
- The diagonal Hessian approximation preserved sufficient signal for MNIST, while restricting Hessian computation to the last layer for CheXpert reduced accuracy, highlighting a trade‑off between feasibility and fidelity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Gradient‑Based Sample Valuation
Influence functions estimate how a small perturbation to a training sample’s weight affects the model’s loss on a test point. For test point \(x_k\) and training point \(x_j\), the influence is approximated as \(dL_k/d\epsilon_j \approx -\nabla L(x_k, \theta^*) \cdot H^{-1} \nabla L(x_j, \theta^*)\), where \(H\) is the Hessian of the expected loss at the optimum \(\theta^*\). A high positive influence suggests that including \(x_j\) increases loss on \(x_k\). This relies on a locally quadratic loss landscape; if the model is near a saddle or the perturbation is large, the approximation may fail. Evidence: The paper derives Equation (5) and shows on MNIST that flipped samples dominate the high‑influence tail for misclassified test points.

### Mechanism 2: Diagonal Hessian Approximation
Computing the full Hessian inverse is \(O(W^2)\) and infeasible for large networks. The paper uses a diagonal approximation: only the diagonal elements \(H_{ii}\) are estimated, and the inverse is applied element‑wise \((1/H_{ii})\). This reduces cost to \(O(W)\) while assuming off‑diagonal curvature (parameter covariances) is negligible for distinguishing mislabeled samples. For CheXpert, the Hessian is further restricted to the last layer, treating earlier layers as fixed. Evidence: Section 3.2 argues the diagonal approximation is “practical and efficient,” and Section 4.2 notes the last‑layer restriction likely explains weaker separation.

### Mechanism 3: Thresholding for Detection
After aggregating each training sample’s influence over all misclassified test points (e.g., mean), the distribution of scores is expected to exhibit a long tail of high positive values corresponding to mislabeled samples. Setting a threshold isolates this tail, yielding high true positive rates at low false positive rates on MNIST. The core assumption is that label noise induces outlier influence scores, whereas correctly labeled samples (even if difficult) do not cluster at such extreme values. Evidence: Figures 3a and 3b show clear separation on MNIST; Figure 3d shows overlapping distributions on CheXpert.

## Foundational Learning
- **Influence Functions (Robust Statistics)**  
  *Why needed*: The entire method is an application of influence functions to deep learning; understanding the derivative \(dL_k/d\epsilon_j\) and its second‑order Taylor approximation is fundamental.  
  *Quick check*: What does a positive influence score of a training sample on a test point indicate about that sample’s role?
  
- **Hessian and Curvature Approximation**  
  *Why needed*: The Hessian inverse is the computational bottleneck; grasping why approximations (diagonal, last‑layer) are necessary and their accuracy‑vs‑scalability trade‑offs is key to interpreting results.  
  *Quick check*: Why might computing the full Hessian be infeasible for a ResNet18, and what is a major risk of using a diagonal approximation?
  
- **Sample Valuation Pipeline**  
  *Why needed*: The paper’s contribution is a specific end‑to‑end workflow (train noisy model → approximate Hessian → compute scores → threshold), distinct from merely computing influence scores.  
  *Quick check*: Why is the model trained *on the dataset with flipped labels* used for all subsequent influence computations, not the baseline model?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Model Trainer -> Hessian Approximator -> Influence Scorer -> Thresholding & Evaluation  
- **Critical path**:  
  1. Train target model on noisy data until convergence; verify sensitivity (e.g., validation loss vs. baseline).  
  2. Compute chosen Hessian approximation (diagonal on all parameters for MNIST; last‑layer full Hessian for CheXpert).  
  3. For every misclassified test sample, compute its loss gradient.  
  4. For each training sample, compute its gradient, apply inverse Hessian scaling, and dot with each test gradient to obtain influence scores.  
  5. Aggregate scores per training sample (e.g., mean), rank, and threshold to identify harmful samples.
  
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *Hessian scope*: Full Hessian is accurate but \(O(W^2)\); last‑layer reduces to \(O(w_l^2)\) but may miss upstream interactions; diagonal reduces to \(O(W)\) but ignores covariances.  
  - *Test point selection*: Using all misclassified test points is robust but costly; sampling introduces variance.  
  - *Threshold selection*: Fixed threshold is simple but dataset‑dependent; adaptive (e.g., percentile) may generalize better but needs validation.
  
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - *MNIST‑like success*: Bimodal separation in average influence scores; high TPR (>85%) at low FPR (<1%).  
  - *CheXpert‑like degradation*: Overlapping distributions of flipped vs. clean scores; flipped samples have higher mean but high variance among clean samples.  
  - *Model not sensitized*: No validation accuracy gap between baseline and noisy models → influence scores won’t correlate with flips.  
  - *Hessian approximation failure*: Last‑layer only if early layers are crucial; diagonal if strong parameter interactions exist.
  
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. Replicate MNIST binary (4 vs. 9) with 20% flips: verify ~90% detection and <1% FPR using diagonal Hessian; confirm top‑ranked harmful samples are mostly flipped.  
  2. Scale to CheXpert (Pleural Effusion) with 20% flips: reproduce trend of higher scores for flips but weaker separation; compare last‑layer vs. diagonal vs. block‑diagonal Hessian approximations.  
  3. Ablation on MNIST: Compare detection performance using (a) full Hessian, (b) diagonal Hessian, and (c) last‑layer Hessian to quantify approximation cost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does detection accuracy vary with different Hessian approximation strategies (e.g., diagonal, last‑layer only, block‑diagonal) in deep networks? The paper uses last‑layer for CheXpert and notes weaker separation, but does not systematically compare approximations.
2. Does the approach generalize to more complex label noise structures, such as subgroup‑specific errors or multi‑class settings? Experiments only simulate random flips in binary tasks; realistic bias patterns remain untested.
3. Can influence scores identify mislabeled samples when the model has high test accuracy (few misclassifications)? The method relies on misclassified test points; if noise causes few errors, the signal may vanish.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameters and exact model architectures (CNN layers, learning rates, ResNet18 fine‑tuning details) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Hessian implementation details (e.g., how the last‑layer Hessian substitutes for full‑model curvature, parameters of the Laplace toolbox) are not provided.
- Theoretical limits of the diagonal approximation for label‑error detection are not formally validated; performance drop on CheXpert suggests sensitivity to model complexity and noise type.

## Confidence
- Influence functions detect label flips on simple datasets (MNIST): **High** (clear results with supporting figures).
- Approach generalizes to complex medical images (CheXpert): **Medium** (weaker separation observed and attributed to Hessian restriction, but not fully ablated).
- Diagonal Hessian approximation preserves sufficient signal: **Low** (inferred from MNIST success but not directly validated against full Hessian for the detection task).
