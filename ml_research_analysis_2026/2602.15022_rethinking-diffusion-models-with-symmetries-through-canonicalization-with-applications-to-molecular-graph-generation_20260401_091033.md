# Rethinking Diffusion Models with Symmetries through Canonicalization with Applications to Molecular Graph Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15022  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15022  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CanonFlow100 achieves 98.4% stability and 95.9% validity on GEOM-DRUG at 100 steps, and reduces Opt‑RMSD by 26% on QM9 (0.17 Å vs. 0.23 Å), with strong few‑step performance.

## Executive Summary
Generating 3D molecular graphs that are invariant under atom permutation and rotation symmetries is essential for molecular design, but existing equivariant diffusion or flow models incur high computational overhead and suffer from symmetry‑induced ambiguities that produce curved transport trajectories and slow training. This paper introduces canonicalization: a deterministic mapping of each molecule to a unique representative on a carefully chosen slice of the symmetry group (Sₙ × SO(3)). Permutation is fixed via spectral Fiedler vector ordering; rotation is optionally fixed via a canonical frame. A standard non‑equivariant diffusion or flow model is then trained on these canonical molecules, and full invariance is recovered at sampling by applying random symmetry transforms. Theoretically, canonicalization eliminates a symmetry‑ambiguity term in the flow‑matching loss, reducing conditional variance and accelerating training. Combined with aligned priors and optimal transport, the method yields straighter trajectories and excellent few‑step generation. On GEOM‑DRUG, CanonFlow100 outperforms equivariant baselines (98.4% stability, 95.9% validity vs. 97.3%/93.9%). On QM9, Opt‑RMSD drops by 26%. The approach demonstrates that breaking symmetry via canonicalization enables expressive non‑equivariant architectures while preserving invariance and efficiency.

## Method Summary
The core is flow matching for molecular generation with canonicalization. For each training molecule Z, a preprocessing step computes a canonical representative ̃Z = Ψ(Z) on a slice of G = Sₙ × SO(3). Permutation canonicalization uses the geometric Laplacian built from interatomic distances; the signed Fiedler vector orders atoms, and an optional SO(3) frame is derived from the ordered coordinates. The canonical dataset ̃p₀ is then used to train a standard (non‑equivariant) flow model, optionally augmented with aligned priors that match the moments of ̃p₀ and optimal transport couplings that are annealed during training. Sampling starts from the slice prior ̃Z₁, integrates the learned dynamics (optionally with Projected Canonical Sampling to maintain slice membership), and finally applies a Haar‑random group element to obtain an invariant sample. Two architectures are explored: Canon. BM (backbone with canonical‑rank positional encodings) and Canon (a three‑stream architecture that explicitly updates canonical rank as a hidden state). Theory decomposes the conditional variance of the flow‑matching objective into within‑slice transport difficulty and a symmetry‑ambiguity term; canonicalization removes the latter, proving faster training. The components combine to produce straighter trajectories and high‑quality few‑step generation.

## Key Results
- On GEOM‑DRUG, CanonFlow100 reaches 98.4% atom stability and 95.9% validity at 100 steps, surpassing SemlaFlow100 (97.3% stability, 93.9% validity).  
- On QM9, canonicalization reduces Opt‑RMSD by 26% (0.17 Å vs. 0.23 Å) compared to the best equivariant baseline.  
- The method maintains advantages with as few as 50 steps, demonstrating efficiency gains from reduced conditional variance and straighter transport.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Eliminating Symmetry Ambiguity
- **Claim:** Canonicalization eliminates symmetry‑induced ambiguity in generative modeling by removing the variance component arising from latent group uncertainty.  
- **Mechanism:** By mapping each molecule to a unique canonical representative on the quotient space (Sₙ × SO(3)), canonicalization removes the posterior multimodality over symmetry transformations. The flow‑matching conditional variance decomposes into within‑slice difficulty (unavoidable transport complexity) plus symmetry ambiguity (removable). Canonicalization sets the second term to zero, accelerating training.  
- **Core assumption:** The stabilizer is trivial almost surely under the data distribution (Assumption 2.5), making canonicalization well‑defined a.s.  
- **Evidence:**  
  - Abstract: “Theory shows canonicalization removes a symmetry‑ambiguity term in the flow‑matching loss, reducing conditional variance and accelerating training.”  
  - Section 3.2.2: Theorem 3.5 proves Var(U|Z_t) = E[Var(Δ|̃Z_t)] + Var(E[U|Z_t,G]) and the second term is eliminated by canonicalization.  
  - Section 4.1: Geometric spectral canonicalization via Fiedler vector provides a deterministic mapping under Sₙ × SO(3).  
- **Break condition:** If molecules have large stabilizers (high symmetry), canonical representatives may be non‑unique, causing discontinuities; this is mitigated by the free‑action assumption and noise in real data.

### Mechanism 2: Enabling Expressive Non‑Equivariant Architectures
- **Claim:** Symmetry breaking enables more expressive non‑equivariant architectures while still recovering invariance.  
- **Mechanism:** Non‑equivariant networks are theoretically more expressive than equivariant ones. Canonicalization provides a “shortcut” where the canonical rank/frame acts as a gauge: h(x)=κ(x)·f(Ψ(x)) implements G‑equivariance by construction, leveraging expressive f without equivariance constraints.  
- **Core assumption:** The base model class (e.g., Transformers/GNNs) can express non‑equivariant functions more powerfully than constrained equivariant classes.  
- **Evidence:**  
  - Section 3.1.2: “Non‑equivariant models have stronger expressivity than their equivariant counterparts (Zhang et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2023a).”  
  - Proposition B.16: Canonical‑condition lifting H_lift(A) inherits equivariance from any slice model A.  
  - Section 4.2: Canon architecture explicitly updates canonical rank as a hidden state, interacting with other features.  
- **Break condition:** Requires a sufficiently expressive backbone f on the slice; if f itself cannot capture the target functional form, equivariance preservation doesn’t help.

### Mechanism 3: Straight Trajectories via Aligned Priors and Optimal Transport
- **Claim:** Combined with aligned canonical priors and optimal transport, canonicalization yields straighter transport trajectories and superior few‑step generation.  
- **Mechanism:** (i) Aligned priors moment‑match the canonical data distribution, reducing within‑slice conditional variance (Proposition B.19). (ii) OT couplings on the slice produce near‑Monge maps that minimize transport cost; canonicalization stabilizes OT by fixing the gauge (Figure 4). Together they minimize total variance E[Var(U|Z_t)].  
- **Core assumption:** The transport difficulty on the canonical slice is non‑zero; aligned priors and OT reduce it toward the theoretical lower bound.  
- **Evidence:**  
  - Section 3.2.3: “OT reduces conditional variance with or without canonicalization, but solutions are non‑unique under symmetry; canonicalization stabilizes OT.”  
  - Proposition B.18: Within‑slice variance formula for Gaussians shows misaligned isotropic priors cause large errors.  
  - Table 3: Canonical SemlaFlow at 50 steps matches or exceeds baselines at 500 steps.  
- **Break condition:** OT computation introduces overhead if not annealed; poorly aligned learning can still fail if the canonical slice geometry is highly complex.

## Foundational Learning

- **Group actions and quotient spaces**  
  *Why needed:* Molecules are invariant under Sₙ (atom permutation) × SO(3) (rotation). Understanding orbits O(Z) and the canonical slice S (a section of the quotient M/G) is fundamental to canonicalization.  
  *Quick check:* Given a molecule Z and a group element g, is g·Z always a different representation? What does it mean for the stabilizer to be trivial?

- **Flow matching and conditional variance**  
  *Why needed:* The core theoretical result decomposes learning difficulty into conditional variance of endpoint displacements given intermediate noisy states. The irreducible error is E[Var(Z₁−Z₀|Z_t)], which canonicalization reduces by eliminating symmetry ambiguity.  
  *Quick check:* If the conditional distribution p(Z₀|Z_t) is multimodal due to symmetry, how does that affect Var(Z₁−Z₀|Z_t)?

- **Haar randomization for invariance recovery**  
  *Why needed:* Canonical training produces a distribution on the slice; sampling from the full invariant distribution requires sampling random group elements (Haar measure) and applying them post‑hoc. The factorization theorem (Theorem 3.1) guarantees invariance.  
  *Quick check:* Why can’t we simply train an invariant model directly? What does Haar randomization buy us in terms of expressivity?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Canonicalizer → Positional Encoder → Backbone → Training Heads → Sampling Engine  
- *Canonicalizer:* Geometric spectral method (Algorithm 1+2) applied during preprocessing to compute ordering and optional SO(3) frame.  
- *Positional Encoder:* Sinusoidal encoding of canonical rank (fixed index) injected as PE(R) or in Canon’s R stream.  
- *Backbone:* Either “Canon. BM” (standard backbone with rank PE) or “Canon” (three‑stream architecture with separate H, CS, R streams and edge messages mixing all three).  
- *Training Heads:* Coordinate regression (head X), discrete atom/bond type prediction, optional rank prediction (λ_rank).  
- *Sampling Engine:* ODE/SDE integrator (Euler or adaptive); optional Projected Canonical Sampling (PCS) re‑projects intermediate states to the slice.

**Critical path:**  
1. Preprocess: canonicalize all training molecules (compute Ψ, store ̃Z₀).  
2. Construct canonical prior q₁ (optionally aligned via moment matching).  
3. Train flow model on canonical pairs (̃Z₀, ̃Z₁) with coupling (product or OT annealed).  
4. Sample from slice prior, integrate learned dynamics; optionally apply PCS.  
5. Apply Haar‑random transform g ~ Unif(G) to final canonical sample to recover invariance.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Canon. BM vs. Canon:* Canon adds an explicit rank stream and updates, increasing expressivity but adding ~15–20% parameters.  
- *Aligned prior vs. isotropic:* Aligned prior (moment‑matched N(μ, Σ)) reduces within‑slice variance but requires precomputed statistics; isotropic N(0,I) is simpler but may harm anisotropic geometries.  
- *OT annealing vs. no OT:* OT yields straighter trajectories and lower variance, but early‑epoch over‑OT can impede learning arbitrary pairings; annealing is essential.  
- *PCS vs. fixed rank:* PCS maintains consistency between predicted structure and canonical rank but introduces small numerical errors per step; fixed rank is simpler but relies on the model internalizing canonical structure.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High instability despite good training: model likely ignores canonical rank PE; check capacity and rank conditioning strength.  
- No validity improvement over baseline: canonicalizer may be unstable (e.g., Fiedler sign flips) or rank noise (β_r) too high; monitor rank drop rate.  
- Sharp degradation in few‑step generation: within‑slice variance too high (misaligned prior, no OT) or model failed to learn straight transport; track training loss and trajectory straightness.  
- Validation blips after switching from OT to product: annealing schedule too fast; consider slower decay.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Verify canonicalizer correctness: for several training molecules, apply g ∈ Sₙ×SO(3) manually, then re‑canonicalize; the representative and ranks must be identical.  
2. Ablate rank conditioning: train Canon. SemlaFlow with/without PE drop (p_drop) and CFG scale; observe validity/stability impact to test importance of canonical signals.  
3. Within‑slice variance proxy: compute empirical covariance Σ₀ of canonicalized coordinates; evaluate trace of Proposition B.18 at t≈0.5. A large trace suggests gains from aligned prior or OT.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
*How to handle molecules with nontrivial stabilizers (exact symmetry) in canonicalization?*  
Assumption 2.5 requires trivial stabilizers a.s.; Remark 2.4 notes that nontrivial stabilizers cause discontinuous/multi‑valued representatives. Dym et al. (2024) suggested probabilistic frames but were not implemented.

**Open Question 2**  
*Can canonicalization be learned from data to further improve training efficiency and generation quality beyond fixed spectral methods?*  
Section 4.1 acknowledges that different canonicalizations affect performance and references learned canonicalization (Kaba et al., 2023), yet the paper only uses hand‑crafted Fiedler ordering.

**Open Question 3**  
*What is the optimal way to eliminate the train‑inference discrepancy concerning canonical rank conditioning in canonical diffusion?*  
Section C.2.1 identifies a subtle discrepancy: ranks are computed exactly from ground‑truth data during training but are unavailable at inference; heuristics (rank noise, auxiliary heads) are used, but a principled solution remains open.

## Limitations
- The method assumes trivial stabilizers almost surely; highly symmetric molecules (e.g., benzene, cubane) may yield ambiguous canonical forms and unstable training.  
- Geometric spectral canonicalization depends on bandwidth selection; sign or ordering inconsistencies across perturbed copies can undermine rank conditioning.  
- Preprocessing (Fiedler computation) scales superlinearly with molecule size, raising concerns for larger systems like biomolecules.  
- The reported gains rely on a combination of canonicalization, aligned priors, and OT annealing; isolating each contribution is incomplete, and suboptimal choices may nullify benefits.  
- Key implementation details (exact Canon architecture, aligned prior for discrete features, PCS schedule, Laplacian bandwidth selection) are omitted, hindering exact replication.

2. **Ablation of canonicalization:** Train non‑equivariant flow models with and without canonicalization (same architecture but no rank conditioning) on canonicalized coordinates.
