# PAC Guarantees for Reinforcement Learning: Sample Complexity, Coverage, and Structure

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01309
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01309
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces the Coverage-Structure-Objective (CSO) framework that decomposes PAC sample complexity into coverage, structure, and objective factors, unifying a wide range of RL settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive survey of probably approximately correct (PAC) guarantees in reinforcement learning from 2018 to 2025. It introduces the Coverage-Structure-Objective (CSO) framework as an interpretive template that factorizes sample complexity into three axes: coverage (data acquisition mode), structure (intrinsic MDP/function-class complexity), and objective (learning task). This decomposition yields a generic scaling law \(N \approx \text{Cov} \cdot \text{Comp} \cdot \text{poly}(H) \cdot \varepsilon^{-2} \cdot \log(1/\delta)\) and enables immediate cross-setting comparison. The survey provides rate lookup tables for tabular, linear, kernel, low-rank, reward-free, and offline settings, and proposes practical diagnostics—including a Bellman residual test, coverage estimation via density ratios/leverage scores, and per-episode policy certificates—to validate assumptions and guide deployment. It also establishes a bridge from uniform-PAC guarantees to high-probability regret bounds and categorizes open problems into near-term extensions and frontier challenges.

## Method Summary
The paper synthesizes existing PAC RL results through the CSO lens and supplements them with algorithmic tools for real-world application. Reproduction involves implementing the described diagnostic algorithms (Bellman Residual Test, Coverage Gate) and representative RL algorithms (e.g., LSVI-UCB for linear MDPs, PEVI for offline RL) on synthetic environments. The minimum viable plan includes: (1) verifying the Bellman residual test on a realizable linear MDP; (2) estimating coverage metrics (density ratios, effective sample size) on offline datasets; and (3) running LSVI-UCB and confirming sample complexity scales as \(\tilde{O}(d^3 H^4 / \varepsilon^2)\). Unknowns that require consulting original sources include exact bonus constants, kernel-specific horizon exponents, and precise thresholds for the diagnostics.

## Key Results
- **CSO Framework:** Decomposes PAC sample complexity into coverage (Cov), structure (Comp), and objective factors, yielding a unified template \(N \approx \text{Cov} \cdot \text{Comp} \cdot \text{poly}(H) \cdot \varepsilon^{-2} \cdot \log(1/\delta)\).
- **Instantiated Rates:** Tabular \(\tilde{\Theta}(SAH^3/\varepsilon^2)\); linear MDP \(\tilde{O}(d^3 H^4/\varepsilon^2)\); offline linear \(\text{poly}(d, C^*, H)/\varepsilon^2\); reward-free tabular \(\tilde{O}(S^2 A \cdot \text{poly}(H)/\varepsilon^2)\).
- **Diagnostics & Guarantees:** Bellman residual test to verify realizability/Bellman completeness; coverage gate using density ratios, leverage scores, and effective sample size; per-episode policy certificates; uniform-PAC implies high-probability regret via an epsilon integral.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** PAC sample complexity in RL can be multiplicatively decomposed into coverage, structural, and objective factors: \(N \approx \text{Cov}\cdot\text{Comp}\cdot\text{poly}(H)\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\log(1/\delta)\).
- **Mechanism:** The Coverage-Structure-Objective (CSO) framework parses existing PAC bounds and identifies three contributing axes: Coverage (data acquisition mode, e.g., trivial for online, \(\text{poly}(C^*)\) for offline, \(S\) for reward‑free), Structure (intrinsic complexity replacing \(SA\), e.g., \(d^3\) for linear MDPs, \(\text{deff}(\lambda)\) for kernels, \(r\) for low‑rank), and Objective (task, e.g. fixed‑confidence control vs. uniform‑PAC vs. evaluation). The generic rate appears as Equation (1) in the paper.
- **Core assumption:** Most PAC bounds (2018–2025) admit an approximate factorization of this form; CSO is an interpretive template, not a proved theorem—it may break when axes are not independent.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] The abstract explicitly proposes the CSO framework and writes the generic rate \(\tilde{N} \approx \text{Cov}\cdot\text{Comp}\cdot\text{poly}(H)\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\log (1/\delta)\).
  - [section] Section 2 develops the template, and Table 2 shows concrete instantiations for tabular, linear, kernel, reward‑free, offline, etc.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like “Active Coverage for PAC RL” and “Uniform-PAC Guarantees for Model‑Based RL with Bounded Eluder Dimension” reinforce the multiplicative appearance of coverage and structural parameters.
- **Break condition:** When coverage, structure, and objective interact non‑separably (e.g., offline RL under misspecification, where coverage needs depend on approximation error), the clean product may no longer hold or may require extra cross‑terms.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Uniform‑PAC guarantees directly imply high‑probability regret bounds via an epsilon integral.
- **Mechanism:** If an algorithm simultaneously limits the number of \(\varepsilon\)‑suboptimal episodes to \(N(\varepsilon,\delta)\) for **all** \(\varepsilon>0\) (uniform‑PAC), then after \(K\) episodes total regret satisfies \(\text{Regret}(K)=O\!\big(\int_0^{R} \min\{K,\,N(\varepsilon,\delta)\}\,d\varepsilon\big)\). Each accuracy tier \(\varepsilon\) contributes at most \(\varepsilon\cdot N(\varepsilon,\delta)\) to the sum of sub‑optimalities.
- **Core assumption:** The uniform‑PAC definition (Definition 2) holds for the given algorithm; the conversion in Theorem 1 is valid, and the integrand is monotone in \(\varepsilon\).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] The abstract mentions the “uniform‑PAC bridge to regret” and cites Theorem 1.
  - [section] Section 3.2 defines uniform‑PAC and states Theorem 1, sketching the proof by integrating over \(\varepsilon\).
  - [corpus] The neighbor “Uniform-PAC Guarantees for Model‑Based RL with Bounded Eluder Dimension” builds on this bridge, confirming its centrality.
- **Break condition:** If only a single \(\varepsilon\) PAC guarantee is available (i.e., the algorithm works for one pre‑chosen accuracy only), the uniform‑PAC condition fails and the integral conversion does not yield a regret bound.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** In offline RL, the concentrability coefficient \(C^*\) is the unavoidable coverage bottleneck that multiplies sample complexity.
- **Mechanism:** \(C^* = \max_h \big\| d^\pi_* / \mu \big\|_\infty\) measures the worst‑case mismatch between the optimal policy’s occupancy and the data distribution \(\mu\). Pessimistic algorithms subtract uncertainty penalties whose magnitude scales with \(C^*\); statistically, the required dataset size grows polynomially in \(C^*\) times the structural and accuracy factors.
- **Core assumption:** The offline setting with a fixed dataset; realizability or bounded approximation; and finiteness of \(C^*\) are necessary for non‑vacuous guarantees.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] The abstract states “offline RL where inherited coverage is the binding constraint.”
  - [section] Section 9 derives Theorem 15 (pessimistic control for linear MDPs) with an explicit \(\text{poly}(C^*)\) factor in the sample complexity; Section 9.5 interprets this via the CSO template as \(\text{Cov} = \text{poly}(C^*)\).
  - [corpus] The neighbor “What can online RL with function approximation benefit from general coverage conditions?” investigates coverage conditions extensively, underscoring their importance.
- **Break condition:** When \(C^*\) is infinite (the behavior policy never visits some states needed by \(\pi_*\)), no consistent policy improvement is possible without further assumptions; only interval estimation via off‑policy evaluation remains viable.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Finite‑Horizon Markov Decision Process  
  - **Why needed here:** The survey studies episodic RL with a fixed horizon \(H\); value functions \(V^\pi_h\), \(Q^\pi_h\), the Bellman operator, and policy evaluation all rely on the MDP formalism.  
  - **Quick check question:** What are the seven components \((S, A, \{P_h\}, \{r_h\}, H, \rho)\) of a finite‑horizon MDP and how do they define the interaction loop?

- **Concept:** Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) Guarantees  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper’s central goal is to present fixed‑confidence guarantees: an \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)‑PAC algorithm outputs an \(\varepsilon\)‑optimal policy with probability \(1-\delta\) after a specified number of episodes.  
  - **Quick check question:** What distinguishes \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)‑PAC from uniform‑PAC, and why does uniform‑PAC automatically yield high‑probability regret?

- **Concept:** Concentrability (Coverage)  
  - **Why needed here:** In offline RL and reward‑free exploration, the sample complexity is inflated by the concentrability coefficient \(C^*\), which quantifies the mismatch between data and optimal occupancies.  
  - **Quick check question:** Define \(C^*\) formally and explain why a larger \(C^*\) makes learning an improved policy statistically harder.

- **Concept:** Function Approximation (Linear, Kernel, Low‑Rank)  
  - **Why needed here:** To handle large state spaces, the survey considers value function classes (e.g., linear features, RKHS, low‑rank models) with realizability and Bellman completeness assumptions.  
  - **Quick check question:** What is Bellman completeness and why is it required for sample‑efficient RL with function approximation?

- **Concept:** Uniform‑PAC  
  - **Why needed here:** Uniform‑PAC provides accuracy guarantees uniformly over all \(\varepsilon > 0\), enabling conversion to high‑probability regret bounds.  
  - **Quick check question:** How does the uniform‑PAC definition differ from a fixed‑\(\varepsilon\) PAC guarantee, and what is the key integration trick to obtain regret?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - Function class (linear, kernel, low‑rank) with assumptions: realizability (\(Q^* \in \mathcal{F}\)) and Bellman completeness (\(T_h\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathcal{F}\)).
  - Exploration/pessimism module: optimistic bonuses for online RL (e.g., LSVI‑UCB elliptical bonus) or uncertainty penalties for offline RL (e.g., PEVI).
  - Coverage estimator: computes proxies for \(C^*\) via density‑ratio classification, ridge leverage scores, or effective sample size.
  - Deployment gate: uses per‑episode policy certificates \(U_t\) (Definition 9) and coverage gate (Algorithm 2) to decide when a policy is safe.
  - Diagnostic tool: the Bellman residual test (Algorithm 1) to validate assumptions on existing data.

- **Critical path:**
  1. **Assumption validation** – Collect a moderate random‑policy dataset; run Bellman residual test. If residuals are large, enrich features or switch function class.
  2. **Coverage assessment** (offline only) – Apply CoverageGate to the dataset with the candidate policy. If it Warns/Abstains, either collect targeted data or fall back to off‑policy evaluation (OPE) only.
  3. **Algorithm execution** – Run the chosen RL algorithm (optimistic for online, pessimistic for offline); concurrently compute policy certificates \(U_t\).
  4. **Deployment decision** – Monitor \(U_t\); deploy only when \(U_t \leq \varepsilon_{\text{target}}\); if \(U_t\) stagnates, revisit coverage or function‑class assumptions.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Optimism vs. pessimism:** online needs optimism to drive exploration; offline needs pessimism to avoid unsupported actions; mixing them introduces bias.
  - **Structural specificity:** tighter assumptions (tabular, linear) yield lower sample complexity but risk misspecification; broader classes (BE‑dim) are safer but looser and often computationally heavier.
  - **Coverage investment:** reward‑free exploration pays an extra \(S\) (or \(d\)) upfront to amortize across many rewards; worthwhile only for multiple downstream tasks.
  - **Horizon handling:** Function approximation correlated errors raise the \(H\) exponent (\(H^3 \to H^4\) or higher); state abstraction or temporal hierarchy can mitigate.
  - **Computation vs. statistics:** Achieving the stated rates often requires oracle‑efficient access to supervised subroutines; nonconvex function classes may need heuristics with weaker guarantees.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Bellman residuals** increase with earlier stages → Bellman completeness violated; algorithm may converge to a suboptimal fixed point.
  - **Coverage metrics:** \(\text{ESS} < 200\), 99‑th percentile importance weight \(>30\), 95‑th percentile leverage score \(>0.5\) → data insufficient to support the target policy.
  - **Policy certificate** stays above tolerance → the algorithm failed to identify a good policy; consider more data or better representation.
  - **OPE confidence interval** is wide or includes zero improvement → policy value uncertain; abort deployment.
  - **Online** – exploration bonuses do not decay → poor state‑action coverage.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Bellman Residual Test** – Run Algorithm 1 on a random‑policy dataset; report mean‑squared residuals per stage. Flag if any stage exceeds \(\varepsilon^2\) (e.g., \(\varepsilon=0.1\)). Misspecification requires richer features or a different class.
  2. **Coverage Gate** – Run Algorithm 2 on the offline dataset and candidate policy; check ESS, weight quantiles, leverage scores against thresholds. If any fail, abstain from policy improvement and collect targeted data.
  3. **Policy Certificate Monitoring** – During evaluation, compute \(U_t\) per episode; plot versus episode number. Confirm monotonic decrease and eventual drop below deployment \(\varepsilon\). If not, investigate coverage or model drift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can we develop scalable coverage estimators with rigorous finite-sample confidence intervals for offline RL deployment?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 13 (within reach) calls for "scalable coverage estimation with finite-sample guarantees for offline RL deployment."
- **Why unresolved:** Existing proxies (density ratios, leverage scores) lack finite-sample validity guarantees, preventing reliable deployment gates with controlled error rates.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An algorithm outputting a confidence interval \([ \hat{C} \pm \Delta ]\) with guarantee \(P(\hat{C} \in [C^*_{\text{eff}} - \varepsilon, C^*_{\text{eff}} + \varepsilon]) \geq 1 - \delta\), plus empirical reduction of deployment failures in benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** For kernel (or NTK) RL, can uniform-PAC guarantees be achieved with complexity scaling as \(\text{deff}(\lambda)\) under data-verifiable assumptions rather than assuming Bellman completeness?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 13 (within reach) lists "uniform-PAC guarantees for kernel and RKHS value classes under conditions that a practitioner can verify."
- **Why unresolved:** Current results require Bellman completeness, which is generally intractable to verify in practice, thus limiting application of kernel RL bounds.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An algorithm attaining \(N = \tilde{O}(\text{deff}(\lambda)\cdot\text{poly}(H)\cdot\varepsilon^{-2})\) together with a finite-sample Bellman residual test (Algorithm 1) validated on synthetic/real domains, and a matching lower bound confirming \(\text{deff}(\lambda)\) is fundamental.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can instance-dependent (gap-weighted) best-policy identification rates be extended to linear or low-rank MDPs, exploiting feature-space reachability and gaps?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 13 (within reach) states that "instance-dependent identification with function approximation is almost entirely unexplored."
- **Why unresolved:** Tabular gap-weighted bounds rely on per-state gaps and reachability; in function approximation these notions must be redefined in high-dimensional feature geometry, which is not yet understood.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Algorithms with sample complexity scaling as \(\sum_{h} \sum_{\text{feature directions}} q_h(\text{dir}) / \Delta_h(\text{dir})^2\), matching lower bounds, and empirical improvement over worst-case rates on linear MDPs.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** For low-rank MDPs, can polynomial sample complexity be achieved under approximate rank misspecification (agnostic setting) with oracle-efficient algorithms that degrade gracefully with the misspecification level?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 13 (frontier) lists "agnostic low-rank and latent-state PAC learning" requiring rates polynomial in \(r, H, 1/\varepsilon\) that degrade gracefully in \(\varepsilon_{\text{approx}}\) via oracle-efficient algorithms.
- **Why unresolved:** Existing algorithms assume exact rank‑\(r\); under approximation they incur additive penalties that do not vanish, and joint representation/value optimization is nonconvex and computationally hard.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Algorithms with \(N = \tilde{O}(\text{poly}(r,H)\cdot(1+\varepsilon_{\text{approx}})^\gamma / \varepsilon^2)\) running in polynomial time in standard oracles, plus lower bounds establishing necessity of \(\varepsilon_{\text{approx}}\) dependence.

## Limitations
- The CSO factorization may fail when coverage, structure, and objective interact non‑separably (e.g., offline RL under misspecification).
- Diagnostic tools (Bellman residual test, coverage gate) lack empirical validation and precise thresholds, limiting reliable deployment.
- Exact constants for bonuses, kernel horizon exponents, and other implementation details are missing, hindering faithful replication.
