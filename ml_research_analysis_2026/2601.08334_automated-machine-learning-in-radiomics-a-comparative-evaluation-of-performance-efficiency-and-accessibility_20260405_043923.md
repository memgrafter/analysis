# Automated Machine Learning in Radiomics: A Comparative Evaluation of Performance, Efficiency and Accessibility
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.08334
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08334
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Simplatab achieved highest mean test AUC (81.81%) while LightAutoML was fastest (6 minutes) with competitive AUC (78.74%).

## Executive Summary
The study comparatively evaluated eleven AutoML frameworks (six general-purpose, five radiomics-specific) across ten heterogeneous radiomics classification datasets. Simplatab, a radiomics-specific no-code tool, achieved the highest mean test AUC (81.81%) with a moderate runtime (~1 hour). LightAutoML, a general-purpose framework, was the fastest (6 minutes) and attained competitive AUC (78.74%). Most radiomics-specific frameworks were excluded due to obsolescence, high programming demands, or computational inefficiency. Key gaps identified were the lack of accessible survival analysis support and limited integration of feature reproducibility and harmonization in current AutoML tools.

The evaluation used standardized 5-fold cross-validation with identical partitions across all frameworks, ensuring fair comparison. Performance metrics included test AUC (mean ± SD) and wall-clock runtime. Statistical significance was assessed via paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test with FDR correction. Findings highlight trade-offs between performance, speed, and accessibility, and underscore the need for more comprehensive AutoML solutions that incorporate upstream steps such as feature harmonization and survival analysis.

## Method Summary
The study performed a comparative evaluation of eleven AutoML frameworks on ten heterogeneous radiomics classification datasets. Datasets contained pre-extracted IBSI-compliant features, varying by imaging modality (CT/MRI), size (74–577 subjects), anatomy, and binary clinical endpoints. Primary metrics were test AUC (mean ± SD from 5-fold cross-validation) and runtime. A qualitative assessment first filtered frameworks based on obsolescence, accessibility, and interpretibility. Qualifying frameworks (Simplatab, LightAutoML, Autogluon presets, MLjar presets, H2O, PyCaret, TPOT) were then evaluated quantitatively using identical 5-fold CV splits. Preprocessing was minimal (median imputation for Simplatab, numerical encoding for TPOT). Statistical comparisons were conducted using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test with FDR correction.

## Key Results
- Simplatab achieved the highest mean test AUC (81.81%) with a moderate runtime of approximately 1 hour.
- LightAutoML was the fastest framework (6 minutes) while maintaining competitive performance (mean AUC 78.74%).
- Most radiomics-specific AutoML frameworks were excluded from quantitative analysis due to obsolescence, high programming demands, or computational inefficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not provide an in-depth mechanistic analysis of why certain AutoML frameworks outperform others; it presents empirical performance metrics. However, the high AUC of Simplatab may be attributed to its radiomics-specific design, which includes tailored preprocessing and feature handling aligned with IBSI standards. LightAutoML’s speed likely stems from its efficient automated machine learning algorithms optimized for tabular data. The exclusion of many radiomics-specific frameworks suggests they often lack maintainability or require substantial programming effort, hindering practical adoption.

## Foundational Learning
- **AutoML**: Automates model selection and hyperparameter tuning. *Why needed*: Reduces human bias, speeds up model development, and explores broader model spaces. *Quick check*: Does the framework automatically choose algorithms and tune hyperparameters with minimal user input?
- **Radiomics**: Extraction of quantitative features from medical images to characterize tumor phenotype. *Why needed*: Enables data-driven prognostication and treatment response prediction. *Quick check*: Are features extracted using standardized, reproducible methods (e.g., IBSI compliance)?
- **Cross-validation**: Technique for estimating model generalization by repeated train-test splits. *Why needed*: Mitigates overfitting and provides robust performance estimates. *Quick check*: Are CV folds generated consistently across all evaluated frameworks? (In this study, yes.)
- **Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC)**: Measure of binary classification discrimination. *Why needed*: Threshold-independent and widely used in diagnostic studies. *Quick check*: Is AUC computed on held-out test folds? (Yes.)
- **IBSI-compliance**: Adherence to Imaging Biomarker Standardisation Initiative guidelines. *Why needed*: Ensures feature reproducibility across centers and software. *Quick check*: Are the radiomics features in the datasets IBSI-compliant? (Yes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data ingestion -> Preprocessing -> AutoML core -> Evaluation -> Aggregation

**Critical path**: The longest dependency chain: Data ingestion -> Preprocessing -> (for each framework) AutoML training on CV folds -> Evaluation (AUC computation) -> Aggregation of results (mean AUC, runtime, statistical tests). The AutoML training step is the most computationally intensive and thus dominates the critical path.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Performance vs. runtime: Simpler models or less extensive search may reduce runtime but can lower AUC.
- Accessibility vs. flexibility: No-code tools lower entry barriers but may limit advanced customization; code-based frameworks offer flexibility but require expertise.
- Interpretability vs. automation: Highly automated ensembles can be opaque, hindering clinical interpretability.

**Failure signatures**:
- Installation failures (missing dependencies, obsolete packages).
- Excessive runtime exceeding practical thresholds (e.g., >24 hours) leading to exclusion.
- High variance across CV folds (large SD) indicating instability or poor framework robustness.
- Crashes due to data characteristics (e.g., missing values, categorical encoding issues).

**Three first experiments**:
1. Run Simplatab on a small dataset (e.g., smallest) using its web interface, record AUC and runtime.
2. Run LightAutoML on the same dataset with default preset, compare performance and runtime to Simplatab.
3. Execute both frameworks on two additional datasets with different modalities/sizes to assess consistency of performance rankings across heterogeneous data.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can AutoML frameworks be extended to natively support survival analysis endpoints while remaining accessible to non-programmers? *Basis in paper*: The abstract and discussion explicitly identify "the lack of accessible survival analysis support" as a significant gap, noting that time-to-event analyses are essential in oncology but absent from current tools. *Why unresolved*: No evaluated framework offered this capability, limiting applicability to critical prognostic tasks. *What evidence would resolve it*: Development and validation of a survival analysis module integrated into an existing AutoML framework (e.g., Simplatab or LightAutoML), tested on radiomics datasets with time-to-event outcomes.
- **Open Question 2**: What architectural modifications are needed to incorporate feature harmonization and reproducibility controls into AutoML pipelines without prohibitive computational cost? *Basis in paper*: The conclusion highlights "the limited integration of feature reproducibility and harmonization" as a key gap, stressing their importance for clinical trust. *Why unresolved*: Current tools focus on modeling; upstream steps like harmonization are manually handled, creating barriers and variability. *What evidence would resolve it*: A comparative study measuring the impact of automated harmonization (e.g., ComBat-like methods) on model performance and stability across multi-center radiomics datasets within an AutoML framework.
- **Open Question 3**: Can standardized radiomics benchmarking datasets be established that exhibit both low intra-method variability and high inter-method differentiation to enable reliable AutoML comparisons? *Basis in paper*: The dataset suitability analysis (Figure 3, results) found many public datasets had either high intra-method SD (noisy) or low inter-method SD (non-discriminative), undermining methodological evaluation. *Why unresolved*: Unstable datasets obscure true performance differences, hindering progress in tool development. *What evidence would resolve it*: Curating a suite of curated, publicly available radiomics datasets with documented stability metrics, and demonstrating their ability to consistently rank AutoML frameworks across replicate studies.
- **Open Question 4**: What sustainable development models (e.g., community governance, modular design) can prevent obsolescence and ensure long-term accessibility of radiomics-specific AutoML tools? *Basis in paper*: The qualitative assessment found most radiomics-specific frameworks were obsolete or required high programming expertise, indicating poor maintainability. *Why unresolved*: Rapid ML evolution leads to abandoned tools, forcing users to switch frameworks and disrupting reproducibility. *What evidence would resolve it*: A case study of a maintained radiomics AutoML tool (like WORC) identifying success factors, followed by proposals for funded, community-led stewardship models for future tools.
- **Open Question 5**: To what extent can fully automated end-to-end radiomics pipelines—from image preprocessing to model interpretation—be achieved without sacrificing robustness or interpretability? *Basis in paper*: The discussion limitation states the study only addressed modeling, and "a major unmet need is the development of frameworks that also integrate upstream steps such as feature extraction, harmonization and reproducibility control." *Why unresolved*: Current tools automate only downstream modeling; integrating error-prone upstream steps (segmentation, extraction) risks propagating inconsistencies. *What evidence would resolve it*: Building and testing a prototype pipeline that couples image preprocessing (with harmonization) to an AutoML modeling core (like Simplatab), assessing performance and failure modes on diverse imaging modalities.

## Limitations
- Incomplete hyperparameter reporting: Exact search spaces and fixed parameters for preset configurations (e.g., “Autogluon-High”) are not fully documented, hindering exact replication of model training behavior.
- Simplatab runtime inconsistency: Its web-based, no-code interface uses approximate timing; wall-clock comparisons to CPU-bound frameworks may be invalid without standardized execution hooks.
- Dataset split verification: Though 5-fold CV splits are shared, fold-wise AUC variability (e.g., high SD in CRLM/Melanoma) suggests potential data instability—unclear if this reflects inherent difficulty or partitioning artifacts.
