# Semantic Similarity is a Spurious Measure of Comic Understanding: Lessons Learned from Hallucinations in a Benchmarking Experiment

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01950  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01950  
- Reference count: 6  
- Primary result: Standard semantic similarity metrics (cosine similarity, KL divergence) produce high scores for factually incorrect or completely hallucinated comic page narrations, demonstrating they are spurious measures for this task.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a benchmark for page-level comic understanding to aid blind and visually impaired users. Using 158 curated public domain comic pages, six Vision-Language Models (VLMs) were evaluated with semantic similarity metrics and a hallucination study. Results reveal that models like MiniCPM and InstructBLIP achieved moderate-to-high semantic scores despite generating narratives with severe factual errors or complete fabrications. The authors conclude that semantic similarity metrics fail to capture narrative quality and factual grounding due to high hallucination rates, particularly in visual descriptions, rendering them unsuitable for evaluating comic interpretation systems.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed a dataset of 158 comic pages with human-written narrations. Six VLMs were prompted to generate narratives (400-word limit). Semantic similarity (cosine, KL divergence) between model outputs and ground truth was computed via BERT embeddings. A hallucination study sampled every 5th page, with human annotators categorizing errors into four types (quoting absent text, misattributing dialogue, incorrect object/scenery, incorrect character). The analysis compared semantic scores against hallucination frequencies and mapped errors to established taxonomies from image captioning literature.

## Key Results
- Qwen2.5 achieved the highest semantic scores (cosine: 0.8546, KL: 0.01294) and lower hallucination frequencies.
- MiniCPM (cosine: 0.78) and InstructBLIP (cosine: 0.72) produced outputs described as "total non sequiturs" with no image grounding, yet received moderate similarity scores.
- High hallucination rates persisted for visual categories (Incorrect Object/Scenery, Incorrect Character) across most models, undermining the validity of semantic metrics for this task.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
- **Claim:** Semantic similarity metrics yield spurious scores because they reward lexical/semantic overlap with a single reference but cannot distinguish factually correct descriptions from plausible hallucinations.
  - **Mechanism:** Metrics compute vector alignment or distributional similarity between model output and ground truth narration. A hallucinated but coherent narrative can align well with the reference text while being entirely disconnected from the image.
  - **Core assumption:** Semantic embedding similarity correlates with faithful reconstruction of visual and narrative content.
  - **Evidence:** MiniCPM’s cosine=0.78 despite "complete fabrication"; InstructBLIP’s cosine=0.72 despite outputting repeated prompt text. The minimal score differences fail to underscore error severity.
- **Claim:** Comic interpretation hallucinations map directly to established object hallucination taxonomies from image captioning (e.g., unary/multi-object, category/relation/attribute errors).
  - **Mechanism:** The narrative-dense task induces errors like inventing objects (category), misstating relationships, or misdescribing attributes—aligning with taxonomies from Rohrbach et al. (2018), Chen et al. (2024), Liu et al. (2024a), Bai et al. (2025).
  - **Evidence:** Paper’s four error types (*Quoting Text Not There*, *Misattributing Dialogue*, *Incorrect Object/Scenery*, *Incorrect Character*) explicitly categorized under these frameworks.
- **Claim:** Widespread use of CLIP-based vision encoders likely contributes to high object/attribute/relation hallucinations.
  - **Mechanism:** CLIP is trained for coarse image-text matching, not fine-grained discrimination, providing noisy visual features that lead the language decoder to "fill in gaps" with plausible but incorrect details.
  - **Evidence:** Qwen2.5 (custom non-CLIP vision transformer) shows lower hallucination rates for visual categories than CLIP-based models (LLaVA-1.6, CogVLM). Correlation noted, not causation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Semantic Similarity Metrics (Cosine, KL Divergence)**  
  **Why needed here:** The paper’s core argument is that these standard metrics are inadequate for comic understanding. Understanding their vector-based scoring reveals why they reward semantic coherence but ignore factual grounding.  
  **Quick check:** Can a sentence that describes a car instead of a bicycle in the image achieve a high cosine similarity score if the reference also describes a vehicle? (Yes.)
- **Concept: Vision-Language Model (VLM) Hallucination Taxonomies**  
  **Why needed here:** The paper categorizes errors using these frameworks; distinguishing between category vs. attribute hallucinations is key to diagnosing model failures.  
  **Quick check:** If a model correctly identifies a smiling woman but incorrectly says she holds a book (she holds a cup), is this a category or attribute hallucination? (Attribute.)
- **Concept: CLIP Vision Encoder & Its Limitations**  
  **Why needed here:** The paper hypothesizes CLIP’s coarse training objective as a root cause of hallucinations. Knowing its weaknesses explains why fine details are missed.  
  **Quick check:** Is a CLIP model trained for image-text matching necessarily trained to count exact numbers of small objects or distinguish left vs. right hands? (No.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Dataset Curation -> Benchmarking Engine -> Hallucination Study Module -> Analysis & Mapping Layer  
**Critical path:** Dataset creation (weeks) → Prompt engineering & model inference (days) → Metric calculation (automated) → Human hallucination audit (weeks, bottleneck) → Cross-paper taxonomy mapping.  
**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Dataset size vs. quality: 158 manually curated pages ensure quality but limit diversity and scalability.  
- Metric choice: Semantic similarity is practical with a single reference but sacrifices factual validity.  
- Model selection: 7–9B parameter models used due to GPU limits; larger models might hallucinate less but are cost-prohibitive.  
- Annotation subjectivity: Hallucination labels assigned by research team (who built dataset); no external or blind-user validation.  
**Failure signatures:**  
- InstructBLIP complete failure: Output repeated prompt + one sentence, not caught by semantic metrics (cosine=0.72).  
- MiniCPM coherent non-sequitur: Grammatically sound narrative with zero image overlap; high semantic score (0.78).  
- High visual hallucination rates: >40% of samples for *Incorrect Object/Scenery* and *Incorrect Character* across most models.  
- Low text transcription attempt: Many models never quoted actual comic text (MiniCPM: 0%).  
**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Semantic Metrics Replication:** Run exact pipeline on 149 pages with all 6 VLMs; manually inspect high-scoring but clearly wrong outputs to confirm spurious correlation.  
2. **Controlled Hallucination Probe:** For 10 pages, create modified ground truths swapping one key element (e.g., "man"→"woman"); recompute scores to test insensitivity to single factual errors.  
3. **Vision Encoder Ablation (Feasibility Study):** If possible, use a model family with CLIP and non-CLIP variants (e.g., LLaVA); evaluate 20 pages to test if non-CLIP reduces *Incorrect Object/Scenery* hallucinations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why do standard semantic similarity metrics fail to correlate with human judgments of quality in comic interpretation, and what alternative metrics could reliably capture hallucination severity and narrative coherence?
2. To what extent do vision encoder architectures (e.g., CLIP-based versus custom transformers) contribute to object hallucinations in comic interpretation, and can targeted architectural modifications reduce hallucination rates?
3. How do dataset scale, genre diversity, and human validation of ground truths impact VLM hallucination rates and performance in page-level comic understanding?
4. What constitutes a high-quality, accessible narration for blind and visually impaired users in comic interpretation, and how should this user-centered perspective reshape benchmark design?

## Limitations
- Dataset is small (158 pages), manually curated, and lacks diversity; no validation by target blind users.
- Hallucination annotations performed solely by research team (bias risk); no external annotators or inter-annotator agreement reported.
- CLIP-vision-encoder hypothesis is correlational; potential confounders (model size, training data) not controlled.

## Confidence
- **Semantic similarity metrics are spurious for comic understanding**: High (direct evidence: MiniCPM cosine=0.78 with total non-sequitur).  
- **Hallucination taxonomies from captioning map to comic errors**: Medium (qualitative alignment shown, but no validation of mapping reliability).  
- **CLIP vision encoders contribute to hallucinations**: Low (correlational; Qwen2.5’s advantage may stem from other factors).

## Next Checks
1. **Perturbation analysis**: Introduce single factual errors (object swap, attribute change) into factually correct model outputs; recompute semantic scores to confirm insensitivity.
2. **External annotation study**: Recruit independent annotators (with comics/accessibility expertise) to label errors using paper’s categories and established taxonomies; calculate Cohen’s κ for mapping reliability.
3. **Controlled vision encoder ablation**: Within a single model family offering CLIP and non-CLIP variants (e.g., LLaVA), evaluate on the benchmark while holding language model constant; compare hallucination frequencies.
