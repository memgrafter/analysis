# Optimal scaling laws in learning hierarchical multi-index models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05846  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05846  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Optimal Bayes error scales as α⁻¹⁺¹⁄(2γ) for α ≪ m*²γ and as m*/α otherwise; features emerge sequentially at αₖ = Θ(k²γ m*¹⁻²γ); a two-stage neural net achieves this Bayes‑optimal rate.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates optimal scaling laws for two‑layer neural networks learning hierarchical multi‑index (HMI) models, where the target is a sum of features with power‑law decaying strengths. By leveraging approximate message passing, the authors derive information‑theoretic limits for subspace recovery and construct a simple, target‑agnostic spectral estimator via eigen‑decomposition of a preprocessed data matrix. The estimator achieves the Bayes‑optimal weighted matrix MSE, and the derived scaling laws reveal phase transitions: the k‑th feature emerges at a critical sample complexity αₖ, leading to characteristic plateaus in the learning curve. Moreover, a two‑stage training procedure that uses spectral initialization for the first layer followed by ridge regression on the readout attains the predicted Bayes‑optimal risk, proving that shallow networks can learn HMI targets at the theoretical limit. The work unifies scaling laws, feature emergence, and spectral phenomena.

## Method Summary
The approach consists of three steps. First, approximate message passing (AMP) is used to compute the information‑theoretic minimum mean squared error (MMSE) for recovering the signal subspace under an HMI model with power‑law decayed coefficients and even, bounded link functions. AMP’s state evolution provides a closed‑form expression for the MMSE as a function of the sample complexity α = n/d. Second, inspired by AMP, the authors propose a spectral estimator: the labels are transformed by a bounded function T (e.g., y/(1+|y|)), and the matrix T = (1/n) Σ T(yᵢ) xᵢ xᵢ⊤ is formed. Under a spiked‑covariance model, the isolated eigenvalues of T correspond to the true feature directions, and its top eigenvectors yield an estimator of the signal subspace that matches the MMSE up to higher‑order terms. Third, a two‑stage neural network training is analyzed: the first layer weights are initialized to a random projection of the spectral subspace; the second layer is trained by ridge regression on the projected features. Theoretical analysis (under polynomial link/activation functions and over‑parameterization p = ω(√n)) shows that this two‑stage procedure attains the Bayes‑optimal excess risk, i.e., it saturates the lower bound implied by the subspace MMSE.

## Key Results
- **Optimal scaling laws:** For γ > 1/2 and α ≪ m*²γ, the Bayes error decays as α⁻¹⁺¹⁄(2γ); for α ≫ m*²γ it decays as m*/α, matching LASSO minimax rates.
- **Sequential feature emergence:** The k‑th feature becomes weakly recoverable at αₖ = Θ(k²γ m*¹⁻²γ), producing phase transitions and plateaus in the learning curve.
- **Achievability:** Two‑stage neural network training (spectral initialization + ridge readout) reaches the Bayes‑optimal risk, confirming that shallow networks can learn HMI targets at the information‑theoretic limit.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Spectral estimator achieves Bayes‑optimal subspace recovery**  
The spectral estimator based on eigen‑decomposition of the preprocessed matrix T attains the information‑theoretic limit for weighted matrix MSE. The preprocessing function T is bounded and non‑degenerate, ensuring that the true feature directions induce spikes in the eigenvalue spectrum of T. Under the HMI model with power‑law decaying coefficients and even bounded link functions, these spikes separate from the continuous bulk when the sample complexity α is below a threshold. The top r eigenvectors corresponding to isolated eigenvalues converge to the true directions, providing an estimator whose MSE matches the MMSE derived from AMP. Evidence: Theorem 3.6 proves the estimator’s optimality; the abstract and Section 3.2 explicitly state this result.

**Mechanism 2: Sequential feature emergence via phase transitions**  
Features appear one after another as α increases. Each true direction w*ₖ becomes weakly recoverable exactly when its associated eigenvalue detaches from the bulk. For quasi‑sparse targets (γ > 1/2), the detachments occur at αₖ = Θ(k²γ m*¹⁻²γ), creating a cascade of phase transitions. The overall risk thus exhibits sharp drops when a new feature emerges, interspersed with plateaus where no new direction is recoverable. Evidence: The abstract and Section 3.1 (Equation 13) give the scaling of αₖ; Section 4 (Figure 2) empirically shows the eigenvalue evolution.

**Mechanism 3: Two‑stage training matches Bayes‑optimal risk**  
The two‑stage procedure (spectral initialization followed by ridge regression on the readout) achieves the same scaling as the MMSE. The spectral init recovers the correct subspace (up to a small rotation error). Random feature ridge regression on this subspace, with sufficient hidden width p = ω(√n) and appropriate ridge λ = Θ(n⁻¹/²), efficiently learns the second‑layer weights. The excess risk equals the subspace recovery error plus an O(n⁻¹/²) term, thus saturating the Bayes‑optimal rate. Evidence: Theorem 3.8 formalizes this claim; Section 5 discusses its unification of scaling laws, plateaus, and spectral structure.

## Foundational Learning
- **Approximate Message Passing (AMP)**  
  *Why needed:* AMP provides a non‑rigorous but highly accurate tool to compute information‑theoretic limits (MMSE) in high‑dimensional multi‑index models, guiding the design of spectral methods.  
  *Quick check:* In AMP for a single‑index model, what does the state evolution track? (Answer: The overlap between the estimator and the true signal.)

- **Quasi‑sparsity and hierarchical decay**  
  *Why needed:* The power‑law decay of coefficients a*ₖ (γ > 1/2) makes the target effectively sparse, which yields LASSO‑like recovery thresholds and explains sequential learning.  
  *Quick check:* For a scale‑free HMI with γ = 1.3, how does the contribution of the top k = 10 features compare to the total variance? (They capture a dominant fraction due to heavy‑tailed decay.)

- **Weak recovery and phase transitions**  
  *Why needed:* Each feature has a critical sample complexity αₖ below which its overlap with any estimator is o(1). This defines the emergence threshold and explains the plateaus in the learning curve.  
  *Quick check:* In a single‑index model, what happens to the overlap between an estimator ŵ and the true w* when α < α_c (the information‑theoretic threshold)? (It stays sub‑constant, i.e., no weak recovery.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data pre‑processing (compute T = Σ T(yᵢ) xᵢ xᵢ⊤) → Spectral estimation (eigen‑decompose T; take top eigenvectors indicating spikes) → Network (first layer W = Z ˆW_sp, readout trained by ridge on projected features).

**Critical path:**  
1. Split dataset D into D₁ and D₂.  
2. On D₁, compute the spectral estimator ˆW_sp as per Definition 3.3.  
3. Initialize first‑layer weights W ← Z ˆW_sp with Z ∼ N(0, r⁻¹).  
4. On D₂, form random feature matrix Ψ with Ψ_{ij}=σ(⟨wⱼ, xᵢ⟩+bⱼ), bⱼ∼N(0,1).  
5. Solve ridge regression: a = (ΨᵀΨ + nλI)⁻¹ Ψᵀ y for D₂ labels.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Preprocessing T: Must satisfy Assumption 3.4; the choice T(y)=y/(1+|y|) is simple but alternatives shift thresholds.  
- Spike detection: The rule “gap < C/√d” uses a constant C that is heuristic; too small may miss weak features, too large may include noise.  
- Over‑parameterization: Theory requires p = ω(√n); using p too small hurts kernel approximation and increases bias.  
- Regularization λ: Should be Θ(n⁻¹/²) to balance bias and variance of ridge.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Spectral: No clear spikes; eigenvalue distribution appears continuous → subspace not identifiable (α too large or γ too small).  
- Readout: Ridge coefficients explode or vanish → λ inappropriate or p too small.  
- Mismatch with predicted scaling: Empirical αₖ deviates strongly from k²γ → assumptions (Gaussian x, power‑law decay) likely violated.

**First 3 experiments:**

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. *Can stochastic gradient descent (SGD) provably achieve the Bayes‑optimal scaling laws without relying on spectral initialization or explicit regularization?*  
   The paper notes this as an important open direction; the current analysis covers full‑batch ERM and a small‑learning‑rate limit that coincides with a spectral method. Whether plain SGD can attain the same rates without such aids remains unresolved.

2. *What is the exact asymptotic behavior of the Bayes risk for hierarchical multi‑index models, and under which conditions does it match the derived weighted subspace recovery error MMSEγ?*  
   The paper characterizes optimal rates for subspace recovery and shows that a two‑stage net attains an excess risk matching that subspace error up to O(n⁻¹/²). However, the Bayes risk itself is a lower bound for any predictor, and its scaling with n and d remains to be quantified explicitly.

3. *Do the derived optimal scaling laws, phase transitions, and spectral emergence phenomena extend beyond the isotropic Gaussian covariate assumption to more realistic data distributions (e.g., heavy‑tailed, structured, or dependent covariates)?*  
   The analysis deliberately relies on isotropic Gaussian inputs to obtain sharp results. Generalizing to other data distributions may alter the scaling exponents or the sequential feature emergence pattern; this is left for future work.

## Limitations
- **Model mismatch:** The analysis assumes Gaussian covariates, orthogonal feature directions, power‑law coefficient decay, and even bounded link functions; real data often violate these assumptions.
- **Preprocessing choice:** The bounded function T (e.g., y/(1+|y|)) is not unique and influences eigenvalue gaps and detection stability; the optimal choice may depend on the target distribution.
- **Eigen‑gap threshold:** The constant C in the spike detection heuristic (gap > C/√d) is unspecified and can be sensitive in moderate dimensions, leading to under‑ or over‑estimation of the number of recoverable features.
