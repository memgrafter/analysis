# CALRK-Bench: Evaluating Context-Aware Legal Reasoning in Korean Law
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.26332
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26332
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: Even top LLMs fail at context-aware legal reasoning, highlighting need for models that reason beyond memorization.

## Executive Summary
Existing legal benchmarks evaluate rule application under fixed norms, ignoring critical contextual factors like normative changes, information sufficiency, and judgment shifts. This paper introduces CALRK-Bench, a Korean-law-based benchmark with three tasks—Temporal Context Recognition (TCR), Information Sufficiency Recognition (ISR), and Judgment Shift Attribution (JSA)—to test context-aware reasoning. Testing GPT-5, Gemini, LLaMA, and Qwen reveals uniformly low performance, with stark gaps (e.g., GPT-5 TCR pre-change 5.3% vs. post-change 60.0%). Results indicate that current LLMs rely on memorized patterns rather than dynamically integrating legal context, underscoring a fundamental limitation in legal AI.

## Method Summary
CALRK-Bench constructs 100–279 multiple-choice instances per task from Korean Supreme Court precedents and legal consultation records. TCR determines when a legal conclusion is valid relative to normative changes by testing temporal configuration of act/trial dates against change points. ISR assesses whether provided statutes suffice to answer a query, using hard negative mining of co-cited statutes that induce conflicting conclusions. JSA attributes judgment shifts to one of four causes (normative premises, text, interpretive rules, factual premises). All data are validated by legal experts; evaluation runs on API-based and open-source models without training. Due to Korean license restrictions, raw legal texts are not publicly released, only curated items.

## Key Results
- TCR average accuracy 32.7% (GPT-5 minimal); severe pre-change norm deficit (5.3%) vs. post-change (60.0%).
- ISR accurate on sufficient statutes (97.3%) but drops on partial (65.1%) and insufficient (75.6%); accuracy degrades with higher reasoning effort in partial cases.
- JSA shows systematic biases: Gemini Flash over-selects "Normative premises" (77.4%), GPT models over-select "Factual premises" (66.7%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Mechanism 1: TCR exposes inability to reason about temporal validity of norms by forcing integration of three dates (act, trial, change). Options enumerate temporal configurations; correct answer requires locating events within timeline partitioned by change points (e.g., constitutional invalidation). Models perform better on post-change norms, indicating reliance on memorized current rules rather than temporal logic. Break condition: large pre/post-change accuracy gap.

Mechanism 2: ISR uses hard negative mining to create legally plausible distractors from co-cited statutes with conflicting effects. This tests whether models assess provided context's completeness or default to parametric knowledge. Accuracy drops in partial conditions and improves with irrelevant context injection, revealing contextual neglect. Break condition: performance degradation as reasoning effort increases in partial cases.

Mechanism 3: JSA isolates attribution of judgment shifts to discrete legal sources (e.g., statutory amendment vs. Supreme Court ruling) via forced single-label selection. Models exhibit category biases (e.g., Gemini→normative premises), suggesting heuristic rather than context-sensitive mapping. Break condition: consistent misclassification to specific categories across diverse cases.

## Foundational Learning
Legal Temporality & Retroactivity
- Why needed: TCR requires understanding that norm applicability depends on relationship between act date, trial date, and normative change date.
- Quick check: If a law amended in 2024 increases penalty, crime committed in 2023 but tried in 2025—which penalty applies?

Legal Information Sufficiency
- Why needed: ISR distinguishes statutes that are logically necessary versus tangentially related, not by keyword matching but by argument completeness.
- Quick check: Given consultation about retirement pay citing "Bankruptcy Act Article 475", is "Labor Standards Act Article 36" necessary, optional, or irrelevant?

Doctrinal Change Taxonomy
- Why needed: JSA maps judgment shift causes to one of four legal sources (social norms, statutory text, precedent, facts); misclassification (e.g., calling a new ruling "factual change") reveals system illiteracy.
- Quick check: A Supreme Court en banc decision reverses prior interpretation of "rape between spouses." Is this a change in normative text, interpretive rule, or factual premise?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input Preprocessor -> Task Dispatcher -> Reasoning Module -> Option Generator -> Output Formatter
Critical path: Raw legal text → Schema extraction (dates, statutes, conclusions) → Task-specific logic (e.g., TCR interval arithmetic) → Final answer selection.
Design tradeoffs:
- Scale vs. Rigor: Only 100–279 examples/task due to rarity of clear-change cases and expert validation needs; limits statistical power but ensures accuracy.
- System Specificity: Built on Korean civil law's explicit statutory timelines; may not transfer to common-law gradual evolution.
- Negative Mining Cost: Hard negative mining for ISR requires co-citation analysis and LLM conflict filtering; computationally heavy but creates realistic distractors.
Failure signatures:
- TCR: Large gap between post-change (high) and pre-change (low) accuracy.
- ISR: Accuracy drop in Partial condition; improvement with irrelevant context injection.
- JSA: High rates of selecting specific categories across diverse cases (e.g., Gemini→Normative premises).
First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline Accuracy Sweep: Run all models on full CALRK-Bench; confirm overall low performance and type-wise patterns.
2. Reasoning Effort Ablation: For GPT/Gemini, vary reasoning level (minimal/low/medium/high); expect TCR improvement, ISR potential degradation in Partial.
3. Context Reliance Probe (ISR): Under Partial, inject irrelevant legal text (LexEval) vs. random strings; measure accuracy delta to assess parametric vs. contextual reliance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Adapting to non-Korean systems: How can CALRK-Bench methodology be adapted to common-law systems while maintaining legal validity and expert rigor? Basis: Limitations note Korean civil law focus; future work could expand across jurisdictions. Unresolved: Common-law gradual doctrinal shifts may not map to TCR/ISR/JSA formulations. Evidence needed: Build parallel benchmarks for US/UK, evaluate same LLMs, compare failure patterns.
2. Performance on ambiguous shifts: Do LLMs fail on gradual doctrinal shifts (e.g., evolving interpretations without explicit overruling) as on explicit changes? Basis: Dataset excludes ambiguous cases for consistency; real-world reasoning often contested. Unresolved: Model behavior on nuanced, gradual change unknown. Evidence needed: Augment benchmark with ambiguous cases using expert confidence scores; measure accuracy/consistency versus clear-change cases.
3. ISR reasoning effort paradox: Why does increased reasoning effort reduce accuracy on *Partial* ISR condition? Basis: Results show accuracy drop with higher reasoning in Partial; context injection suggests parametric interference. Unresolved: Cause unclear—hallucination or failure to halt? Evidence needed: Fine-grained CoT error analysis; test interventions like explicit sufficiency checks or RAG.
4. JSA category bias origins: What causes systematic biases (e.g., Gemini→Normative premises, GPT→Factual premises)? Basis: Paper speculates English-dominant training exposes to common-law patterns. Unresolved: Could be training data distribution, architecture, or option wording cues. Evidence needed: Analyze pre-training corpora for common/civil law prevalence; re-run with Korean-finetuned models or balanced synthetic data; ablate option phrasing.
5. Integration with legal reasoning frameworks: How should TCR/ISR/JSA integrate into IRAC and what modifications are needed for dynamic environments? Basis: Introduction positions context-awareness as precursor to rule application but no mapping. Unresolved: Relationship to IRAC components (Issue, Rule, etc.) and Hart's secondary rules unclear. Evidence needed: Conceptual mapping to IRAC/Hart; test by evaluating step-by-step reasoning traces for coherence with proposed framework.

## Limitations
- Data access: Original Korean legal texts withheld due to licenses; only curated multiple-choice items available (n=100–279/task), requiring niche database access for reconstruction.
- Generalizability: Built on Korean civil law's clear statutory timelines; findings may not transfer to common-law systems with gradual evolution.
- Methodological novelty: Hard negative mining for ISR lacks ablation against simpler strategies; unclear if performance drops stem from task difficulty or mining artifacts.

## Confidence
- Data access & reproduction feasibility: Medium (code may exist but raw materials gated)
- Cross-jurisdiction generalizability: Low (system-specific artifacts likely)
- Attribution of failure mechanisms: Medium (correlation evidence, not causal)
