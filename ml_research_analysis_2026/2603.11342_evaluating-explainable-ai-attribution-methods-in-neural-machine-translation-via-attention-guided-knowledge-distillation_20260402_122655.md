# Evaluating Explainable AI Attribution Methods in Neural Machine Translation via Attention-Guided Knowledge Distillation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11342
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11342
- **Reference Count:** 26
- **Primary Result:** A teacher-student distillation framework reveals that Attention, ValueZeroing, and Layer Gradient × Activation (LG×A) are the most effective XAI attribution methods for NMT, with the element-wise product operator (⊙) being optimal for injecting these signals into the student model's encoder self-attention.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel, automated framework for evaluating explainable AI (XAI) attribution methods in neural machine translation (NMT). The core idea is to treat teacher-generated attribution maps as a structured side signal and inject them into a student model's attention mechanism via different compositional operators. The student's translation quality (BLEU/chrF) after training quantifies the utility of each attribution method. Across three language pairs and two teacher models, the study finds that simple attention weights and value-based zeroing consistently yield the largest gains when combined with the multiplicative (⊙) operator and injected into the encoder. Furthermore, the degree to which a separate "Attributor" transformer can reconstruct an attribution method's top-salient tokens strongly predicts its utility, establishing a robust link between reconstructibility and practical usefulness.

## Method Summary
The proposed evaluation pipeline uses a pre-trained teacher NMT model (Marian-MT or mBART) to generate attribution matrices for source-target pairs using various XAI methods (e.g., gradients, attention, value zeroing). These matrices are normalized and combined with the attention scores of a randomly initialized student model (same architecture as the Marian teacher) via one of four element-wise operators (addition, multiplication, averaging, replacement). This modified attention is applied in either all encoder self-attention or all decoder cross-attention layers. The student is trained from scratch on the translation task with this guidance. Translation performance on a held-out test set, measured by BLEU and chrF, indicates the attribution method's utility. To explain *why* certain methods work better, a lightweight Attributor transformer is trained to predict the teacher's attribution map from the source-target pair; the accuracy of this reconstruction (top-3 token overlap) is then correlated with the student's performance gains.

## Key Results
- **Top Attribution Methods:** Attention, ValueZeroing, and Layer Gradient × Activation (LG×A) consistently produce the largest BLEU/chrF improvements.
- **Optimal Composition:** The element-wise product operator (⊙) is the most effective across all conditions, while averaging (µ) is the worst.
- **Injection Location Matters:** Injecting into encoder self-attention yields gains; injecting into decoder cross-attention typically harms performance.
- **Strong Predictive Correlation:** The student's BLEU gain correlates very highly (Pearson r ≈ 0.88–0.97) with the Overlap@3 score measured by the Attributor, linking reconstructibility of top salient tokens to utility.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Distillation as Evaluation
The framework uses teacher-derived attribution maps as a transferable side signal. By combining these maps with the student's attention scores and training the student to translate, the method assesses whether the attribution captures functionally useful dependencies. If the attribution accurately reflects the teacher's reasoning, it provides a learnable signal that improves the student's simulation of the teacher's output, measured by BLEU/chrF gains. Evidence includes positive gains for meaningful attributions and degradation for random matrices (Table 13). The approach is functionally grounded, evaluating utility via task performance rather than human plausibility.

### Mechanism 2: Modulation via Element-wise Product
The ⊙ operator (multiplication) gates the student's original attention scores by the normalized attribution values. This preserves the relative structure and ranking of the model's own alignment signal while softly emphasizing positions the attribution deems important. In contrast, addition/averaging can drown the signal, and replacement discards learned attention patterns. The consistent superiority of ⊙ across all tested conditions (Section 4.2) supports this theoretical advantage.

### Mechanism 3: Reconstructibility as a Utility Proxy
The high correlation between an attribution method's Overlap@3 (fraction of top-3 source tokens per target token correctly reconstructed by the Attributor) and the student's BLEU gain suggests a deep connection. A transformer's ability to mimic an attribution method indicates that the method's salient patterns align with the model's inductive biases and are therefore more readily exploitable for knowledge distillation. This provides a powerful, automated diagnostic: an XAI method's utility can be predicted by how well its top-salient tokens are recoverable by a standard transformer.

## Foundational Learning
- **Knowledge Distillation:** The entire framework is a form of distillation where a "soft" side signal (attribution maps) guides a student. Understanding typical distillation objectives (e.g., KL divergence on soft targets) helps see how an auxiliary structured signal modifies the training process.
  *Quick check:* How does adding a side signal that modifies attention differ from standard distillation that uses softened output distributions?
- **Attention Mechanism in Transformers:** The injection directly alters the scaled dot-product attention pre-softmax scores (QKᵀ/√dₖ). Grasping how queries, keys, and values compute source-target alignments is essential to understand how a static attribution matrix interacts with dynamic attention during generation.
  *Quick check:* In cross-attention, how do decoder queries attend to encoder keys, and what would be the effect of multiplying the attention logits by a fixed source-target matrix?
- **Simulatability as Evaluation Paradigm:** This work employs a *functionally grounded* evaluation: an explanation is useful if it helps another model simulate the teacher's behavior. This differs from evaluating via human judgment (plausibility) or comparison to a ground-truth feature (faithfulness).
  *Quick check:* What are the potential pitfalls of using human attention as a gold standard for evaluating XAI in NMT, and how does the simulatability approach avoid them?

## Architecture Onboarding
Teacher NMT model -> Attribution extractor (Inseq library) -> Normalized attribution matrix (E) -> Student NMT model (with injection module modifying attention) -> Trained student -> Evaluator (BLEU/chrF on test set)
Separately: (Source, Target, E) -> Attributor transformer (predicts E) -> Reconstruction metrics (Overlap@3, Kendall τ)

**Critical Path:**
1. Preprocess parallel corpora (WMT14 de-en/fr-en, UN ar-en), tokenize, filter, and split into 220k train / 15k val / 15k test.
2. For each training pair, use a frozen teacher and Inseq to compute the attribution matrix E for a chosen XAI method; apply column-wise MinMax normalization and store.
3. Train the student model from scratch. For each batch, load (source, target, E). In specified attention layers, compute pre-softmax scores A = QKᵀ/√dₖ. Transform E to match A's shape (e.g., transpose for cross-attention, outer product for encoder self-attention). Apply operator f (e.g., A ← A ⊙ E'). Proceed with normal softmax and value multiplication. Optimize cross-entropy with teacher forcing.
4. After early stopping, evaluate the best student checkpoint on the test set to get BLEU/chrF.
5. Train the Attributor (3-layer encoder-decoder) on a held-out set to predict normalized E (as a distribution over source tokens per target token) via KL loss. Compute Overlap@3 and Kendall τ@3 on a test set.
6. Correlate Attribution method's (or method+operator combination's) student BLEU gain with its reconstruction metrics.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Teacher size:** Marian-MT (74M) is efficient for extraction; mBART (610M) provides stronger but costlier attributions.
- **Injection location:** Encoder self-attention helps by providing a static "lookahead" bias; cross-attention injection often disrupts the model's learned, dynamic source-target alignments during autoregressive decoding.
- **Operator choice:** ⊙ modulates rather than replaces, preserving model structure. It may amplify noise in poor attributions but proved robust.
- **Attribution source:** Attention and ValueZeroing yield sharper, more interpretable maps; gradient-based methods are noisier but capture different aspects of model reasoning.
- **Normalization:** Column-wise MinMax preserves relative rankings and avoids issues from softmax's temperature sensitivity, but outliers could compress the signal.

**Failure Signatures:**
- BLEU degradation when using cross-attention injection, especially with replacement operator (R).
- Consistently low performance with the averaging (µ) operator.
- Performance below baseline when using random attribution matrices (Table 13).
- For mBART Attention (which peaks on last token), gains are modest unless used with ⊙.
- High column-wise entropy (>~4 bits for Marian) in attribution maps correlates with lower student gains.
- Attributor fails to achieve high Overlap@3 for gradient-based methods, predicting their lower utility.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Baseline Establishment:** Train a student model without any attribution injection on the same 220k de-en data to establish a baseline BLEU/chrF.
2. **Encoder Self-Attention Injection with Marian-MT:** For all 8 XAI methods and all 4 operators, train student models with injection in all encoder self-attention layers on de-en, fr-en, ar-en. This identifies the top-performing method-operator combinations.
3. **Cross-Attention Injection Comparison:** Repeat Experiment 2 but inject into all decoder cross-attention layers. Compare results to encoder injection (Experiment 2) to confirm the location sensitivity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Why Cross-Attention Injection Fails:** The exact reason why injecting attribution maps into the decoder's cross-attention harms performance is obscure. The paper speculates it conflicts with the dynamic nature of autoregressive decoding, but this is not empirically validated.
- **Layer/Head-Specific Effects:** The study tested only uniform injection across all or half of the encoder heads. The effects of targeting specific layers or head subsets remain unexplored.
- **Optimal Top-k for Reconstruction:** The choice of k=3 for top-k overlap metrics is arbitrary. Different k values might yield stronger correlations with utility.
- **Generalization to Other Tasks:** The framework's promise for other sequence-to-sequence tasks (e.g., QA, summarization) is suggested but not tested.

## Limitations
- **Injection Transformation Unclear:** The method for converting the teacher's source×target attribution matrix into a source×source matrix for encoder self-attention injection is not specified; this is a critical missing detail.
- **Hyperparameter Completeness:** Exact training details (learning rate schedule, batch size, dropout, early stopping patience) are not fully documented, making exact replication challenging.
- **Evaluation Scope:** Experiments are limited to three language pairs and two teacher architectures; broader claims about generalizability across language families, domains, and model scales are premature.

## Confidence
- **Framework Validity (Distillation as Evaluation):** Medium – Supported by positive/negative controls but hinges on unspecified implementation details (e.g., matrix transformation).
- **Element-wise Product (⊙) Optimality:** High – Effect is large, consistent, and theoretically justified across all conditions tested.
- **Injection Location Preference (Encoder > Cross):** High – Pattern is clear, statistically significant, and aligns with the hypothesis about disrupting autoregressive dynamics.
- **Top Method Ranking (Attention, ValueZeroing, LG×A):** Medium – Stable within tested settings but may not generalize to other architectures/languages without further validation.
- **Reconstructibility-Utility Correlation (Overlap@3 ↔ BLEU):** High – Coefficients are very strong (r ≈ 0.88–0.97) and observed across multiple experiments.
