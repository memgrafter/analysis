# Can MLLMs Read Students' Minds? Unpacking Multimodal Error Analysis in Handwritten Math
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.24961
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24961
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: MLLMs show a large performance gap to human experts on error diagnosis tasks (humans ~89% ECE, ~84% ECC).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ScratchMath, a benchmark for diagnosing errors in handwritten math scratchwork, comprising 1,720 Chinese student samples with tasks: Error Cause Explanation (ECE) and Error Cause Classification (ECC). Evaluation of 16 multimodal LLMs reveals a substantial gap to human experts (humans achieve ~89% ECE, ~84% ECC). The top model, o4-mini, reaches only ~70% ECE and ~49% ECC. Error analysis indicates that visual recognition failures (36%) and misaligned interpretation (17%) are primary failure modes.

## Method Summary
The authors present ScratchMath, a dataset of 1,720 authentic handwritten math solutions from Chinese K-12 students, annotated with error cause explanations and classifications into seven categories. For each sample, models receive the problem statement, correct answer, student answer, and an image of the scratchwork. Models are prompted to either explain the error (ECE) or classify it (ECC). Evaluation uses an LLM judge (o3-mini) for ECE semantic match and exact-match for ECC. Sixteen multimodal LLMs are tested, including proprietary (GPT-4o mini, Gemini) and open-source models, under standard settings without fine-tuning.

## Key Results
- Human experts achieve ~89% ECE and ~84% ECC; best model (o4-mini) attains ~70% ECE and ~49% ECC (middle school).
- Proprietary models outperform open-source by a large margin; reasoning-focused models excel on ECE.
- Error analysis: 36% of failures due to visual recognition issues, 17% from misaligned interpretation (e.g., solving instead of diagnosing).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Visual Recognition Bottleneck
MLLMs fail primarily due to limitations in processing heterogeneous, low-fidelity handwritten scratchwork. Authentic student work contains ambiguous glyphs, spatial layouts, and varied symbol forms that differ from cleaner, typeset data in pre-training. Vision encoders experience distribution shift, leading to OCR-level errors that cascade into downstream reasoning. The visual modality is the primary bottleneck. Evidence: Error analysis shows 36% of failures are visual recognition failures; related work cites handwritten recognition as a core challenge.

### Examinee-Educator Misalignment
Current MLLMs are optimized for an "examinee perspective" (generating correct answers) rather than an "educator perspective" (diagnosing errors). Pre-training on correct solutions conditions models to produce correct outputs, but error diagnosis requires hypothesizing the flawed process behind an incorrect trace—a counterfactual and pedagogical reasoning skill not directly trained. Simply scaling or instruction tuning is insufficient; a fundamental shift in objective is needed. Evidence: Models repeatedly default to answer generation; reasoning models’ ECE superiority suggests misalignment.

### Reasoning Depth Gap
Diagnosing error causes requires multi-step logical reconstruction from sparse, non-linear scratchwork. Student work is fragmented, showing jumps and implicit steps. Models must stitch these into a coherent (incorrect) reasoning chain and pinpoint the divergence point—a complex interpretive task. The ECE format probes interpretive reasoning, not just pattern matching. Evidence: Models struggle with reconstructing reasoning processes; reasoning models outperform others on ECE, indicating an advantage in deeper semantic understanding.

**Why needed:** ECE is an open-ended generation task; the paper uses an LLM (o3-mini) to judge explanation quality. Understanding this proxy evaluation—its validation (88.6% agreement) and limitations—is critical to interpreting ECE scores.  
**Quick check:** Why was o3-mini chosen as the judge, and what is one key caveat mentioned about its scoring relative to true human expert agreement?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** `Dataset -> Input Processor -> Dual-Head Output Generator -> Evaluation Framework`  
- Input Processor formats multimodal inputs (Q, A_ref, A_stud, I) into a structured prompt.  
- Dual-Head Output Generator is the MLLM that produces both ECE explanation and ECC label.  
- Evaluation Framework scores outputs using an LLM judge (ECE) or exact match (ECC).

**Critical path:** The replication pipeline: load ScratchMath sample → prepare structured multimodal prompt → call MLLM API/run model → parse outputs for ECE/ECC → score against gold labels. The most failure-prone step is prompt engineering to elicit consistent, structured outputs from diverse MLLMs.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Benchmark Scope vs. Generalizability:* Focused on Chinese K-12 math (high quality, controlled) vs. broad cultural/linguistic applicability (acknowledged limitation).  
- *Automatic ECE Evaluation vs. Cost:* Using an LLM judge is scalable and cheap but introduces judge error and potential biases.  
- *Error Taxonomy Granularity:* Seven classes balance specificity and annotation feasibility; finer granularity could improve diagnostics but increase noise.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Visual Hallucination: Model describes symbols/numbers not present in the image.  
- Explanatory Vagueness: Output is generic without pinpointing the flawed step.  
- Task Misalignment: Model ignores scratchwork and re-solves the problem, commenting on the correct solution.  
- Format Ignorance: Fails to note unit conversions or answer format issues.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Replication:** Run o4-mini on 50 random ScratchMath samples using the provided prompt. Expect ~70% ECE and ~47% ECC (middle school) to validate the pipeline.  
2. **Error Type Stress Test:** Curate a subset (n=20 per type) of the hardest error categories (Logical Reasoning, Calculation). Run the best model and manually categorize failures. Check if the failure distribution matches Figure 5 (Visual Recognition 36%, Misaligned 17%).  
3. **Prompt Sensitivity Test:** For ECE, test three prompting variants: (a) zero-shot, (b) few-shot with 2 examples, (c) explicit chain-of-thought. Measure delta in LLM-judge score to probe reasoning model superiority and find optimal prompting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How do MLLMs' error diagnosis capabilities generalize across diverse linguistic, cultural, and educational contexts beyond the Chinese K-12 setting?  
**Basis:** The conclusion explicitly notes that all samples are from Chinese students on a single platform, limiting generalizability.  
**Why unresolved:** ScratchMath is monolingual and culturally specific; models may overfit to region-specific notations and error patterns. Cross-cultural validity is unknown.  
**Evidence needed:** Build comparable benchmarks in multiple languages and educational systems, then evaluate transfer performance of models trained on ScratchMath.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Can integrating specialized handwritten mathematical expression recognition (HMER) components improve MLLMs' accuracy in diagnosing errors from authentic, diverse handwriting?  
**Basis:** Error analysis shows 36% of top model failures are due to visual recognition failures from ambiguous symbols and layouts.  
**Why unresolved:** Current MLLMs use general-purpose vision encoders not optimized for mathematical glyphs and spatial structures; HMER systems focus only on recognition, not downstream reasoning.  
**Evidence needed:** Ablation studies comparing standard MLLMs against versions augmented with HMER backbones or trained on synthetic handwriting variations; a significant reduction in visual failure rates would support integration.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What architectural or training modifications are needed to shift MLLMs from an "examinee perspective" (answer-generation) to an "educator perspective" (error diagnosis)?  
**Basis:** The introduction criticizes current MLLMs for adopting an examinee perspective, focusing on generating correct answers rather than diagnosing errors.  
**Why unresolved:** Models are trained on correct reasoning chains; diagnosing errors requires modeling incorrect processes, which is not incentivized by standard objectives.  
**Evidence needed:** Curate a training corpus pairing student scratchwork with educator-style diagnostics; fine-tune models with contrastive objectives penalizing correct-answer generation on flawed work; improved performance would indicate effective shift.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Why is Error Cause Classification (ECC) significantly harder than Error Cause Explanation (ECE) for MLLMs, and can multi-task learning architectures close this gap?  
**Basis:** Results show ECC accuracy lags ECE (e.g., o4-mini: ~70% ECE vs. ~49% ECC max), and Procedural/Transcription errors are particularly challenging.  
**Why unresolved:** ECC demands precise categorical mapping amid visual ambiguity and fine-grained distinctions, while ECE allows fuzzy explanations; models may fail to leverage shared reasoning between tasks.  
**Evidence needed:** Implement joint ECE+ECC models with shared representations and task-specific heads vs. single-task baselines; analysis of whether ECE pretraining improves ECC accuracy; a narrowed gap would indicate benefit from multi-task learning.

## Limitations
- Exact prompts and generation parameters for all models are not disclosed, hindering faithful replication.
- The ScratchMath dataset is limited to Chinese K-12 math; cross-linguistic and cultural generalizability is unknown.
- LLM-as-judge evaluation for ECE introduces potential bias; expert agreement is high but judge-specific failure modes may skew results.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Visual Recognition Bottleneck):** High confidence.
- **Mechanism 2 (Examinee-Educator Misalignment):** High confidence.
- **Mechanism 3 (Reasoning Depth Gap):** Medium confidence.
