# Where meaning lives: Layer-wise accessibility of psycholinguistic features in encoder and decoder language models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03798
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03798
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The layer at which psycholinguistic features are most linearly accessible is not fixed but depends critically on the embedding extraction method; a robust ordering (lexical properties earlier, experiential/affective later) emerges across models, with final layers rarely optimal.

## Executive Summary
This study conducts a large-scale investigation into how transformer language models encode human-related linguistic knowledge. By linearly probing 58 psycholinguistic features across 10 encoder and decoder models, the authors find that the apparent localization of a feature is strongly determined by how the word's representation is extracted—isolated word embeddings yield sharp, less decodable profiles, while contextualized embeddings (from templated or natural sentences) produce flatter, more selective distributions. Despite this methodological dependence, a consistent depth ordering exists: lexical and usage-based properties peak in earlier layers, while experiential, affective, and social dimensions peak later. Architectural class (encoder vs. decoder) influences the breadth of this distribution but not the fundamental ordering. The work underscores that "where meaning lives" is an interaction between the model's architecture and the analytical lens used to examine it.

## Method Summary
The core method is linear ridge regression probing across transformer layers. For each of 10 models (5 encoders, 5 decoders) and 58 psycholinguistic features (e.g., frequency, valence, concreteness), the study extracts word representations using three strategies: 1) **isolated** (embedding the word alone), 2) **template** (embedding a fixed sentence containing the word), and 3) **averaged** (mean embedding from 50 natural contexts in the C4 corpus). To isolate feature-specific information from general lexical predictability, the primary metric is **selectivity** (observed out-of-sample R² minus R² from a permuted-label control). Layer-wise selectivity profiles are summarized by the **best layer** (argmax) and **center of mass (COM)**, a weighted average layer index. Model similarity is assessed by correlating their feature-specific COM vectors.

## Key Results
- Apparent layer-wise localization is **method-dependent**; contextualized extraction (template, averaged) yields higher overall selectivity and flatter profiles than isolated extraction.
- **Final layers are never optimal** for decoding these psycholinguistic features; the best layer is consistently found in intermediate or early positions.
- A **robust depth ordering** exists across all models: lexical/usage features (e.g., frequency, semantic diversity) peak earlier, while experiential/affective/social features (e.g., valence, concreteness, sensory strength) peak later.
- Architectural differences are secondary: encoders often show broader selectivity distributions across layers, while decoders show more concentrated peaks, but the depth ordering remains consistent within and between classes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The choice of embedding extraction method causally determines the apparent layer-wise localization and recoverable information of psycholinguistic features.
- **Mechanism:** Contextualized embeddings (template, averaged) incorporate sentence-level structure during the forward pass, which linearly redistributes feature-specific information across layers, creating flatter, more decodable profiles. Isolated embeddings force reliance on static token embeddings, resulting in sharper peaks and lower selectivity.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and section 3.1 show median selectivity increases by 0.112 moving to contextualized extraction, with improvements for 100% of features.
- **Break condition:** If non-linear probes or different feature sets erase the method-dependency gap, or if the effect is an artifact of the specific psycholinguistic norms.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Architectural constraints (encoder vs. decoder) systematically influence the distribution *shape* of feature accessibility across layers, secondary to the extraction method.
- **Mechanism:** Encoders' bidirectional context processing leads to representations that are more broadly distributed. Decoders' causal masking and next-token prediction objective concentrate semantic information more sharply in intermediate layers, with steeper drops in later layers.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and section 3.2 note encoders often have broader profiles; decoders more concentrated. Section 4.2 contrasts encoder "pipeline-like" progressions with decoder "mid-layer semantic peaks."
- **Break condition:** If training regimes (e.g., instruction tuning, sequence length) are the primary driver of distribution shape rather than fundamental architecture.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A robust, shared depth ordering of psycholinguistic feature accessibility exists: lexical/usage properties peak earlier, experiential/affective later.
- **Mechanism:** The transformer training objective (masked or next-token prediction) induces a representational hierarchy where surface, distributional facts are resolved by early layers processing local co-occurrence. Deeper, aggregate representations requiring broader context and non-local inferences emerge later.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and section 3.3 document this ordering as "robust across models and architectures." Section 4.4 reports strong rank correlations of feature-specific COM vectors within architecture classes.
- **Break condition:** If the ordering collapses for non-human-experiential features or is reversed in models trained on radically different objectives (e.g., multimodal).

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Linear Probing as a Measure of Linear Accessibility**
  - **Why needed:** The entire study uses linear probes to measure how directly and selectively a feature is encoded in a representation vector. High probe performance indicates the information is present in a linearly separable form.
  - **Quick check:** If a linear probe achieves high R² on permuted labels, it suggests the probe is overfitting or that the representation's dimensionality allows trivial prediction. The paper's *selectivity* metric (R²_obs – R²_rand) subtracts this chance baseline to isolate feature-specific signal.

- **Concept: Selectivity Score (R²_obs - R²_rand)**
  - **Why needed:** Raw R² can be inflated by general lexical properties (e.g., word length, frequency) that are predictable from any representation. Selectivity controls for this by comparing to a label-permuted control task, isolating information specific to the target psycholinguistic feature.
  - **Quick check:** Why is selectivity more reliable than raw R²? Because it removes variance explained by generic, non-feature-specific properties of the word list, allowing fairer comparison across features and layers.

- **Concept: Center of Mass (COM) for Localization**
  - **Why needed:** COM provides a single, continuous summary of *where* in the layer stack a feature's information is most accessible, weighting each layer by its selectivity delta from the worst layer. This enables correlation analysis of localization patterns across models.
  - **Quick check:** How does COM differ from picking the layer with maximum selectivity (argmax)? COM is more stable to noise and captures the distribution's shape, while argmax is a single-point estimate sensitive to fluctuations. The trade-off is COM can be pulled by a long tail of moderate selectivity, whereas argmax strictly identifies the peak.

## Architecture Onboarding

1. **Embedding Extractor** -> **Linear Probe Trainer** -> **Selectivity Calculator** -> **Localization Analyzer** -> **Similarity Comparator**
   - **Embedding Extractor:** Takes a target word and returns a vector per layer for one of three methods: isolated, template, averaged (mean of 50 natural contexts).
   - **Psycholinguistic Feature Database:** Provides target values for 58 features over a curated ~10k word subset.
   - **Linear Probe Trainer:** Ridge regression with nested 5-fold CV on a 4k-word subset, tuning α, repeated 10 times for stability.
   - **Selectivity Calculator:** Runs the same probe on 10 permuted-label copies to estimate R²_rand, computes R²_sel = R²_obs - R²_rand.
   - **Localization Analyzer:** Computes best layer (argmax selectivity) and COM (selectivity-weighted layer position).
   - **Similarity Comparator:** Calculates Spearman's ρ between models based on feature-specific COM vectors.

**Critical Path**
1. **Context Choice First:** The extraction method must be chosen and implemented correctly *before* layer-wise probing, as it fundamentally alters the representation space.
2. **Word Set Curation:** The ~10k word intersection set must be built from the feature database *before* any extraction to ensure all probes use identical inputs.
3. **Selectivity over Raw R²:** The analysis pipeline must compute and primarily use selectivity scores to avoid spurious correlations from general lexical confounds.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- **Template vs. Averaged Context:** Template is cheap (1 forward pass) but uses a uniform, semantically poor context. Averaged uses rich, diverse contexts (~50 forward passes) but is ~50x more expensive. Paper shows averaged yields modestly higher selectivity.
- **Isolated vs. Contextual:** Isolated is cheapest and comparable to static embedding studies but yields sharper localization profiles and lower selectivity. Should only be used to demonstrate context-dependence, not as primary analysis.
- **Linear vs. Non-linear Probes:** Authors deliberately use linear probes to measure *direct accessibility*. More expressive probes might recover higher R² but could reveal information not explicitly encoded, violating the goal of locating intrinsic representations.

**Failure Signatures**
- **Permutation Check Fail:** If R²_rand is consistently positive or very close to zero, the control task is broken, and selectivity is meaningless.
- **COM Instability:** If the COM for a feature varies wildly across random seeds or word subsets, the localization result is unreliable.
- **Architectural Blurring:** The clear block structure in Figure 4 (encoders cluster, decoders cluster) for `averaged`/`template` should hold. If the correlation matrices become noisy (like the `isolated` panel), it suggests a problem with contextual extraction or a non-representative model set.

**First 3 Experiments**
1. Replicate Figure 1 (Selectivity Heatmaps) for a single model (e.g., BERT Large) across all 3 extraction methods. Verify averaged > template > isolated for mean selectivity and that the last layer is not the max.
2. Replicate Depth Ordering (Figure 3): Using averaged context and selectivity, compute COM for each of the 16 feature categories for all 10 models. Calculate mean COM per category across models. Sort categories by this mean COM. Confirm lexical-early / affective-late ordering.
3. Replicate Architectural Clustering (Figure 4): For each extraction method, build the 10x10 model similarity matrix from feature-specific COM vectors (selectivity-weighted). Compute Spearman's ρ for each pair. Visually inspect for block structure (encoder cluster, decoder cluster) in averaged/template panels and noisier pattern in isolated panel.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
To what extent do architectural differences versus training objectives independently determine the observed layer-wise distribution? The authors note models differ in both architecture and post-training objectives (e.g., instruction tuning), confounding these factors.

### Open Question 2
Do the reported depth ordering principles generalize to languages beyond English? The study is restricted to English; cross-linguistic variation in semantics, morphology, and culture could alter or reverse the ordering.

### Open Question 3
What mechanistic changes in representational geometry cause the consistent decline in linear decodability in final layers? The information may be transformed into a less linearly accessible form rather than lost; analyzing intrinsic dimensionality or comparing linear vs. non-linear probes could clarify.

### Open Question 4
How can we better isolate specific psycholinguistic constructs (e.g., concreteness vs. imageability) when they are empirically highly correlated? Selectivity controls for generic predictability but does not disentangle overlapping human-constructed features; methods like partial correlation or redundancy analysis are needed.

### Open Question 5
Does layer-wise accessibility of a feature predict its *use* during generation or downstream tasks? Probe performance measures presence, not influence on computation or output. Controlled generation experiments or causal steering interventions are required to bridge this gap.

## Limitations
- The final 9,966-word vocabulary and its greedy selection algorithm are not provided, preventing exact replication.
- The C4 context sampling protocol for the "averaged" method lacks details (e.g., sentence length filters, handling of rare words, random seed).
- The cross-validation split strategy (fixed vs. varying subset, fold reuse) is unspecified, affecting reproducibility of the 50 estimates per combination.

## Confidence
**Claim 1: Method-Dependent Localization** – Confidence: High. The paper's own data robustly shows the extraction method alters selectivity and profiles. The causal claim rests on the linear probe assumption, which is not tested against alternatives.
**Claim 2: Architectural Distribution Shape** – Confidence: Medium. The pattern (encoders broader, decoders concentrated) is replicable within the study but may conflate architecture with training regime. No controlled ablation isolates architecture.
**Claim 3: Shared Depth Ordering (Lexical→Affective)** – Confidence: Medium-High. The ordering is consistent across the model set but could be an artifact of feature correlations (e.g., frequency confounding other lexical properties). The paper notes but does not fully disentangle this.

## Next Checks
1. **Reproduce method gap with fixed protocol:** Implement the pipeline using a standardized word subset and fixed random seeds for CV and context sampling. Confirm the `averaged` vs. `isolated` selectivity difference persists.
2. **Disentangle feature correlations:** Recompute the depth ordering (COM) after removing variance explained by frequency and semantic diversity via regression or PCA on the feature norms. Test if the lexical→affective gradient remains.
3. **Test non-linear probe sensitivity:** For a subset of models and features, replace linear ridge with a shallow MLP probe. Check if the *relative* layer-wise profiles and the method/architecture effects still hold, or if non-linearities erase the observed patterns.
