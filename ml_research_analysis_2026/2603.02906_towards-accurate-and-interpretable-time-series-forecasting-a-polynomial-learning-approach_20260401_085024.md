# Towards Accurate and Interpretable Time-series Forecasting: A Polynomial Learning Approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02906  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02906  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** IPL achieves higher prediction accuracy than ARIMAX, LIME, and SHAP while providing faithful feature importance rankings; in antenna health management it identified a minimal feature set for early warning with AUC >0.98.

## Executive Summary
Interpretable time‑series forecasting is hindered by the lack of feature‑level explanations in many accurate models. This paper introduces Interpretable Polynomial Learning (IPL), which explicitly models original features and their interactions via polynomial expansions of lagged covariates and targets, thereby preserving temporal dependencies. IPL offers a tunable polynomial degree to balance accuracy and interpretability. Across simulated data, Bitcoin prices, and field antenna measurements, IPL outperforms ARIMAX and post‑hoc explainers (LIME, SHAP) in prediction accuracy and produces more trustworthy feature rankings. In an antenna early‑warning system, IPL isolated a small set of critical interacting features, enabling a simple decision rule with AUC >0.98, demonstrating practical utility for trustworthy AI.

## Method Summary
IPL constructs an extended feature vector for each time point \(t\) by concatenating the current covariates, past covariates up to lag \(L_x\), and past target values up to \(L_y\). It then maps these vectors into a polynomial feature space of degree \(s\) via the kernel \(K_s(\tilde{x}, \eta) = (1 + \tilde{x} \cdot \eta)^s\), where centers \(\eta_j\) are randomly selected training points (a fundamental system). This kernel matrix \(A\) serves as the basis for solving a regularized regression or classification problem using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM). After convergence, the solution is transformed back into an explicit polynomial with coefficients \(\omega_\alpha\) for each monomial \(\tilde{x}^\alpha\). A sparsity threshold \(I\) filters out insignificant terms, yielding a compact, interpretable predictor \(f_I(\tilde{x}) = \sum_{(\alpha,\omega) \in \Omega_I} \omega_\alpha \tilde{x}^\alpha\). The polynomial degree \(s\) directly controls the trade‑off between model complexity and interpretability.

## Key Results
- IPL achieves higher prediction accuracy than ARIMAX, LIME, and SHAP across simulated, Bitcoin, and antenna datasets.
- IPL’s feature importance rankings align closely with ground truth in synthetic experiments, outperforming post‑hoc methods in overlap ratio and value similarity.
- In antenna health management, IPL identified just two key interaction features, enabling a simple early‑warning rule with AUC >0.98.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Preserving Temporal Dependencies
- **Claim:** IPL preserves temporal dependencies by explicitly modeling lagged variables and cross‑time feature interactions within its polynomial structure.
- **Mechanism:** The polynomial expansion includes terms where exponents are applied to lagged input features (e.g., \(x_t\), \(x_{t-1}\)) and the target (e.g., \(y_{t-1}\)). This creates monomials like \(x_i^{(t)} \cdot x_j^{(t-k)}\) or \(x_i^{(t)} \cdot y_{t-k}\). Learned coefficients directly quantify how past feature values (and their combinations) influence the current prediction.
- **Core assumption:** The true predictive function can be adequately approximated by a finite‑degree polynomial in the expanded lagged feature space; temporal dynamics are embedded in the interactions of these lagged terms.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Section 3.4] extends input to include lagged variables; [Section 3.2] formula shows time indices; [Abstract] states preservation of temporal dependencies.
- **Break condition:** Model fails if the underlying process requires an infinite‑degree polynomial or if critical dependencies involve lags longer than \(L_x\) or \(L_y\).

### Mechanism 2: Faithful Feature‑Level Interpretability
- **Claim:** IPL provides faithful feature‑level interpretability because model coefficients for individual monomials directly correspond to the contribution of that specific feature (or interaction) to the prediction.
- **Mechanism:** After training, the predictor \(f(x)\) is expressed as an explicit sum of weighted monomials over the input features \(\tilde{x}\). The magnitude and sign of the coefficient \(\omega_\alpha\) for a monomial \(\tilde{x}^\alpha\) indicate direction and strength. Sparsity is enforced via threshold \(I\) to select influential terms.
- **Core assumption:** Dominant drivers are captured by a sparse subset of polynomial terms; interaction effects are additive within the linear‑in‑parameters model.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Section 3.4, Step 5] describes coefficient extraction; [Section 4.2, Figure 3 & Table 2] show IPL’s feature rankings match ground truth better than baselines; [Section 6.2, Figure 10] demonstrates actionable interpretability with a simple binary tree on two top‑ranked interactions.
- **Break condition:** If true feature importance is highly context‑dependent or the polynomial degree \(s\) is too low, the sparse set \(\Omega_I\) may omit critical terms, leading to incomplete explanations.

### Mechanism 3: Tunable Accuracy–Interpretability Trade‑off
- **Claim:** IPL offers a flexible, tunable trade‑off between prediction accuracy and model interpretability via the polynomial degree \(s\).
- **Mechanism:** Increasing \(s\) expands the hypothesis space \(\mathcal{P}^d_s\), capturing more complex interactions and typically improving accuracy, but increases the number of terms \(n = \binom{s+d}{s}\), making the model less sparse and harder to comprehend. Decreasing \(s\) yields a simpler, more interpretable model but may underfit. Practitioners select \(s\) based on validation performance.
- **Core assumption:** There exists a polynomial degree \(s\) that captures the essential predictive relationships without making the model overly complex.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] mentions the tunable trade‑off; [Section 1] highlights IPL’s integration of accuracy and interpretability; [Section 4.2] sets \(s=2\) for optimal performance; [Figure 4] shows IPL balancing accuracy and interpretability metrics.
- **Break condition:** If optimal accuracy requires a very high \(s\), interpretability is lost due to term explosion; if the true process is simple, a high \(s\) may overfit and produce spurious terms.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
The pipeline flows as:  
**Input Pipeline → Kernel/Basis Construction → Optimization Core (ADMM) → Polynomial Reconstruction → Sparsity & Output**

- **Input Pipeline:** Takes raw series \((x_t, y_t)\), generates lagged vectors \(\tilde{x}_t = [x_t, ..., x_{t-L_x}, y_{t-1}, ..., y_{t-L_y}]\).
- **Kernel/Basis Construction:** Computes matrix \(A\) where \(A_{i,j} = K_s(\tilde{x}_i, \eta_j) = (1+\tilde{x}_i\cdot\eta_j)^s\) using polynomial kernel. Centers \(\eta_j\) are typically training points (fundamental system).
- **Optimization Core (ADMM):** Solves \(\min_{u,v} f(v) \text{ s.t. } A u - v = 0\) by alternating updates of \(u\) (closed‑form or proximal), \(v\) (point‑wise, loss‑dependent, e.g., Newton for logistic), and multiplier \(w\).
- **Polynomial Reconstruction:** Converts final \(u^*\) into explicit polynomial \(f(\tilde{x}) = \sum_j u^*_j K_s(\eta_j, \tilde{x})\), expands to monomials \(\sum_\alpha \omega_\alpha \tilde{x}^\alpha\).
- **Sparsity & Output:** Applies threshold \(I\) to \(|\omega_\alpha|\) to select sparse set \(\Omega_I\); final predictor \(f_I(\tilde{x}) = \sum_{(\alpha,\omega)\in\Omega_I} \omega_\alpha \tilde{x}^\alpha\).

### Critical Path
Data → Lagged Features → Kernel Matrix \(A\) → ADMM Solver (\(u\), \(v\), \(w\) updates) → Reconstructed Polynomial → Thresholding → Sparse Model. The most computationally intensive and numerically sensitive step is the ADMM iteration and matrix operations involving \(A\) (especially \(\text{pinv}(A)\) for squared loss). A non‑nonsingular \(A\) (guaranteed by the fundamental system choice) is prerequisite.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Polynomial degree \(s\):** Higher \(s\) increases expressive power and accuracy but exponentially increases basis size \(n = \binom{s+d}{s}\) and reduces interpretability. Lower \(s\) yields simpler, faster models but may underfit.
- **Lags \(L_x\), \(L_y\):** More lags capture longer memory but increase input dimension \(d' = d + L_x + L_y\), which blows up polynomial basis size. Must be chosen based on autocorrelation analysis.
- **Sparsity threshold \(I\):** Larger \(I\) yields fewer, more important features → simpler rules but may discard weakly influential synergistic terms. Smaller \(I\) retains more terms but reduces interpretability.
- **Loss function \(\phi\):** Squared loss gives closed‑form solution (via \(\text{pinv}(A)\)) → fast but outlier‑sensitive. Hinge/Logistic loss requires iterative \(v\) updates → robust for classification but slower.

### Failure Signatures
- **Poor predictive performance:** Indicates \(s\) too low, lags too short, or data not amenable to polynomial approximation.
- **Non‑sparse/uninterpretable output:** \(s\) too high or threshold \(I\) too low, or true process has many weak interactions.
- **ADMM divergence/slow convergence:** Poorly scaled data (features not normalized), inappropriate \(\beta/\alpha\), or nearly singular \(A\) (though theoretically unlikely with random centers).
- **Misleading feature importance:** Model misspecification (true interactions non‑polynomial) or omitted crucial lags, causing spurious importance assignments.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Theoretical limits of the accuracy‑interpretability trade‑off** when scaling to high‑dimensional or long‑range time series and the relationship between optimal \(s\) and intrinsic data complexity remain unaddressed. The paper fixes \(s=2\) and provides no framework for relating \(s\) to dimensionality, horizon, or noise. Resolving this requires formal analysis (e.g., sample complexity bounds) and systematic empirical studies across benchmarks of varying size and horizon.
2. **Robustness of feature importance rankings** under distribution shifts, concept drift, or adversarial perturbations is not studied. Validation uses static splits; only simple noise perturbations are examined. For high‑stakes early‑warning systems, explanations must remain reliable when operational conditions change. Needed evidence includes experiments simulating regime shifts or using robustness benchmarks, measuring stability (e.g., rank correlation) of IPL’s top features across different data regimes.
3. **Capability to capture high‑order, long‑range temporal dependencies** compared to deep learning models (Transformers) has not been demonstrated. IPL’s empirical comparisons focus on simple datasets; its ability to model intricate, long‑horizon patterns (e.g., seasonal cycles over months) remains open. Resolution requires benchmarking IPL against state‑of‑the‑art deep forecasters (e.g., Transformers, PatchTST) on standard long‑horizon, high‑dimensional time‑series benchmarks (M4, electricity, traffic), along with interpretability analysis.
4. **Computational overhead for real‑time early‑warning systems** as polynomial degree \(s\) and lag orders increase is not rigorously quantified. Claims of low overhead are supported by informal timing on small datasets. A formal time‑complexity analysis of ADMM in terms of \(T\), \(d\), \(s\), \(L_x\), \(L_y\) and scaling experiments (varying \(d\) and \(T\)) on edge hardware are needed to validate suitability for streaming applications.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and lack of guidance:** ADMM parameters (α, β, tolerance) and the number of random centers \(n\) are unspecified; lag lengths \(L_x\), \(L_y\) and polynomial degree \(s\) require manual tuning without theoretical rules, increasing the risk of under‑/overfitting.
- **Scalability and numerical stability:** Computational and memory costs scale combinatorially with \(s\) and feature dimension \(d\); the kernel matrix \(A\) may become ill‑conditioned for high degrees or dimensions, affecting ADMM convergence.
- **Model misspecification risk:** IPL assumes the true data‑generating process can be approximated by a finite‑degree polynomial. If the underlying dynamics are inherently non‑polynomial or require high‑order interactions beyond the chosen \(s\), both accuracy and interpretability degrade.
