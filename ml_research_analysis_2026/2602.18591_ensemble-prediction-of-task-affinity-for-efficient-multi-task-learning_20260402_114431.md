# Ensemble Prediction of Task Affinity for Efficient Multi-Task Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18591  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18591  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** ETAP achieves 0.84 Pearson correlation on ETTm1 with only 10 training groups, outperforming MTGNet (0.54), and yields near-optimal MTL grouping (within 2% of exhaustive optimum).

## Executive Summary
Multi-task learning (MTL) improves efficiency by sharing representations across tasks, but selecting which tasks to group together is challenging due to complex task interactions. Existing methods either rely on numerous expensive MTL experiments (data-driven) or incur high computational overhead (white-box). This paper introduces ETAP (Ensemble Task Affinity Predictor), which predicts MTL gain—the per-task improvement from joint versus single-task training—using gradient-based affinity scores from a single MTL model. ETAP employs a two-stage ensemble predictor: a non-linear B-spline mapping converts affinity scores into preliminary gain estimates, followed by a residual correction model (ridge regression) that refines predictions using task identity features. Across four benchmarks (CelebA, ETTm1, Chemical, Ridership), ETAP outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in both accuracy and data efficiency. Notably, on ETTm1 with only 10 training groups, ETAP achieves a correlation of 0.84 versus MTGNet's 0.54, and the resulting task groups produce MTL performance within 2% of the exhaustive optimum. Moreover, computing affinity scores is 46–71% faster than the leading white-box method TAG.

## Method Summary
ETAP requires only a single MTL model trained on all tasks to compute gradient-based pairwise affinity scores. For each training step, it records per-task losses and gradients w.r.t. shared parameters. The affinity from task ti to tj measures alignment between tj's gradient and ti's parameter update, normalized by tj's loss; these are averaged over steps and then over tasks within a group to produce a group-level score vector. To predict MTL gain for a candidate group, ETAP first applies a B-spline basis expansion to the affinity vector and fits a regularized linear model to map these features to a preliminary gain estimate. Second, a residual predictor (ridge regression) is trained on multi-hot encodings of the task groups to correct systematic errors of the first stage, using a small set of groups with ground-truth gains. The final predicted gain feeds into a branch-and-bound search that selects a set of B groups maximizing total predicted gain.

## Key Results
- ETAP achieves 0.84 Pearson correlation on ETTm1 with 10 training groups, outperforming MTGNet (0.54) and Linear Surrogate (0.68).
- Task grouping based on ETAP predictions attains near-optimal MTL performance; on ETTm1 with 4 groups, total loss is 3.89 versus the exhaustive optimum 3.82 (within 2%).
- Affinity score computation is significantly cheaper than TAG, with 46–71% less runtime depending on the benchmark.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Gradient-based pairwise affinity scores computed from a single MTL training provide a stable, low-variance estimate of task compatibility.
- **Mechanism:** During standard MTL training, gradients ∇θk Lti with respect to shared parameters θs are recorded each step. The step-level affinity score zk ti→tj measures alignment between tj's gradient and ti's parameter update (η∇θk Lti − βvk−1), normalized by tj's loss. These are time-averaged (Equation 6) and then group-averaged (Equation 7) to produce a group-level score zG→ti.
- **Core assumption:** The alignment of gradient updates for shared parameters reflects whether tasks help or hinder each other's loss reduction, and this relationship stabilizes over training steps.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.1.3] Derivation and aggregation of affinity scores (Equations 5–7). [abstract] Mentions "gradient-based updates of shared parameters to measure affinity." [corpus] Neighbor papers also use gradient-based affinities.
- **Break condition:** If tasks have conflicting gradient directions or highly dynamic optimization landscapes, the per-step affinity score variance may not average out, leading to unstable zG→ti estimates.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A non-linear B-spline transformation maps raw affinity scores to preliminary MTL gain predictions, capturing higher-order task interactions beyond linear pairwise sums.
- **Mechanism:** Affinity scores zG→ti are expanded using B-spline basis functions ϕ(·), projecting them into a higher-dimensional feature space. A regularized linear model then fits these features to ground-truth gains, producing a non-linear mapping fnon-linear.
- **Core assumption:** MTL gains are a non-linear function of pairwise affinities; linear aggregation (e.g., averaging) is insufficient due to complex, higher-order dependencies.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2.1] Describes B-spline basis expansion and regularized regression. [abstract] States affinity scores "naturally extend to estimating affinity within a group" but "task dependencies are often non-linear." [corpus] No direct evidence from neighbors.
- **Break condition:** If the underlying MTL gain function is actually linear in affinities, the non-linear mapping may overfit with limited training groups.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Residual correction learned from task-identity features systematically reduces bias in initial gain predictions, improving accuracy with fewer training groups.
- **Mechanism:** The residual eaff G = yG − ŷaff G is predicted using ridge regression on a multi-hot encoded group vector uG. The final prediction is ŷfinal G = ŷaff G + fresidual(uG; ωres).
- **Core assumption:** The error pattern of the affinity-based predictor correlates with which specific tasks are present in the group, and these patterns are learnable from a small set of training groups.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2.2] Details residual model training and ridge regularization. [abstract] Notes residual learning "captures complex and non-linear task relationships." [corpus] Weak; no neighbor evidence.
- **Break condition:** If residuals are purely stochastic or depend on features not captured by task identity alone (e.g., dataset shifts, architecture interactions), the residual predictor will fail.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Gradient-based optimization in MTL**  
  *Why needed here:* ETAP’s affinity scores rely on gradients from shared parameters during joint training; understanding gradient computation, momentum updates, and backpropagation is essential to interpret zk ti→tj.  
  *Quick check question:* How would you adapt the affinity score calculation if the MTL model uses task-specific gradients only (no shared parameters)?

- **Concept: Basis function expansions (B-splines)**  
  *Why needed here:* The non-linear mapping stage uses B-splines to transform 1D affinity scores into a flexible feature space while maintaining smoothness and local control.  
  *Quick check question:* What is the effect of increasing the number of knots in the B-spline basis on model bias and variance?

- **Concept: Regularized linear regression (Ridge)**  
  *Why needed here:* Both mapping stages (B-spline regression and residual correction) use ridge regression to prevent overfitting, especially with high-dimensional inputs.  
  *Quick check question:* How does the ridge penalty λ ∥ω∥² influence the bias-variance tradeoff in the residual predictor when |Gtrain| is very small?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
1. MTL training module: trains a single joint model on all tasks T, logging losses Lkti, gradients ∇θks Lkti, and velocities vk−1 per step.  
2. Affinity calculator: computes zk ti→tj (Equation 5), aggregates to zti→tj (Equation 6), then derives zG→ti per group (Equation 7). Output: zG = {zG→ti: ti ∈ G}.  
3. Non-linear mapper: takes zG, applies B-spline basis expansion ϕ(zG), fits regularized linear regression ωaff to predict ŷaff G (Equation 9).  
4. Residual predictor: given training groups Gtrain, computes residuals eaff G = yG − ŷaff G, trains ridge regression on multi-hot uG to predict residuals.  
5. Group selector: uses ŷfinal G from the ensemble predictor as input to a branch-and-bound search (or similar) to select B groups under constraint.

**Critical path:** MTL training → compute affinities → train B-spline model → compute residuals → train residual model → inference: for any candidate group G, compute zG → ŷaff G → fresidual(uG) → ŷfinal G → feed to group selector.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- B-spline complexity: Higher-degree splines or more knots capture non-linearities but risk overfitting with small |Gtrain|. Cross-validation essential.  
- Residual model choice: RidgeRegression offers simplicity and stability; XGBoost may capture complex patterns but requires more tuning and can overfit.  
- Affinity aggregation: Group averaging (Equation 7) assumes pairwise interactions sum linearly; if higher-order interactions dominate, the initial zG may be systematically biased.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High variance in zti→tj across different random seeds indicates unstable affinity estimates (check training dynamics, momentum hyperparameters).  
- Residual model fails to converge or yields negative R² on training residuals, suggesting the first-stage predictor already captures all learnable patterns or residuals are noise.  
- Predicted MTL gains ŷfinal G have extreme magnitudes compared to training distribution; likely a scaling mismatch between affinity scores and gains.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Affinity stability test: Train the baseline MTL model 5 times with different seeds; compute pairwise correlation between zG from different runs. Target: Pearson r > 0.95.  
2. Spline validation: On a synthetic dataset where true gain = f(z) with known non-linearity, validate that B-spline mapping approximates f better than linear aff→gain scaling.  
3. Residual ablation: Train ETAP without residual stage on |Gtrain|=10; compare R² to full ETAP. Expect significant drop, confirming residual contribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does ETAP’s prediction accuracy and grouping efficiency scale to task sets significantly larger than 10 tasks, given the exponential increase in possible groups and potential breakdown of pairwise aggregation?  
2. To what extent do the gradient-based affinity scores depend on the architecture and hyperparameters of the base MTL model used for computation?  
3. Can combining ETAP’s predefined grouping with gradient-based MTL optimization methods (e.g., PCGrad) yield further performance improvements, and what is the optimal way to integrate both approaches?

## Limitations
- B-spline implementation details (knot placement, number of basis functions, per-task vs. vector treatment) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- MTL gain computation protocol (test set usage, averaging, task weighting) is unclear, potentially introducing variance in results.
- Branch-and-bound parameters and the "50% of combinations" sampling strategy are not described, making comparisons less reliable.
- Hyperparameter search ranges are only broadly defined; optimal values may be dataset-dependent.

## Confidence
- Gradient affinity stability: Medium  
- Non-linear B-spline mapping: High  
- Residual correction via task identity: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Affinity robustness test: train the all-tasks MTL model with 10 random seeds; compute pairwise affinity matrices and report mean/std Pearson correlation. Confirm consistency >0.95.  
2. B-spline sensitivity analysis: on a held-out set, vary knot count (2–√|G_train|) and degree (2–6); measure prediction R². Identify a region of stable performance and ensure no severe overfitting.  
3. Residual extrapolation test: train ETAP using groups that exclude a subset of tasks (e.g., 8 out of 10). Evaluate on groups containing the held-out tasks; a significant drop in R² would indicate limited generalization of the residual model.
