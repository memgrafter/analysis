# MergeMix: Optimizing Mid-Training Data Mixtures via Learnable Model Merging

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17858
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17858
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: MergeMix reduces data mixture search cost by 100× while achieving comparable or superior performance (58.4% vs 57.9% average accuracy on an 8B model).

## Executive Summary
MergeMix addresses the computational expense of determining optimal data mixtures for large language model mid‑training. By using weight interpolation among domain experts as a high‑fidelity proxy, it reduces search cost by 100×. The method trains small experts on minimal data, learns a performance predictor via regression, and optimizes mixing weights without full training. Empirical results on an 8B model show that MergeMix achieves 58.4% average benchmark accuracy, matching or exceeding manual tuning (57.9%), with high rank consistency (Spearman ρ > 0.9) and effective cross‑scale transfer.

## Method Summary
MergeMix proceeds as follows: First, domain experts are trained in parallel from a shared base checkpoint using a constant learning rate and a limited token budget to ensure updates remain small and linear. Next, a set of mixing vectors on the simplex is sampled; for each, the experts' weights are linearly combined to form a merged model, which is evaluated on a suite of downstream benchmarks. These (α, score) pairs train lightweight LightGBM regressors to approximate the performance surface. The regressors are then optimized under a utility function (e.g., macro‑average) to obtain the optimal mixing proportions α*. Finally, α* guides the data mixture in full‑scale mid‑training. For many domains, a hierarchical divide‑and‑conquer approach recursively applies this process.

## Key Results
- Achieves 58.4% average benchmark accuracy on an 8B model, matching or exceeding manual tuning (57.9%) with 100× lower search cost.
- Demonstrates high rank consistency (Spearman ρ > 0.9) between merged proxy predictions and actual full training outcomes.
- Shows cross‑scale transferability: mixtures found on an 8B proxy transfer successfully to a 16B target model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Weight interpolation between domain experts produces a model that approximates the result of training on a mixture of the corresponding datasets.
- **Mechanism**: Under shared initialization and a constant learning rate, the gradient accumulated during mixed‑data training is a linear combination of the per‑domain gradients. Training experts separately yields parameter updates that, when linearly combined, match the first‑order term of the mixed training trajectory. The merged model thus mirrors the direction of the mixed‑data optimum in the local neighborhood.
- **Core assumption**: The training horizon is short enough that the optimization remains in the local quadratic regime around the base checkpoint; higher‑order interactions are negligible for ranking mixtures.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] states that “weight interpolation can serve as a high‑fidelity proxy for data interpolation” and that theoretical analysis shows “identical first‑order dynamics”.
  - [Section 3.3] derives Equations (1) and (2) to show matching first‑order terms and residual second‑order error.
  - [Figure 3] and [Figure 2] demonstrate synchronized performance trends and high rank consistency (Spearman ρ > 0.9) between merged and trained models.
- **Break condition**: If domain‑specific updates become large (e.g., long training horizons or aggressive learning rates), second‑order curvature terms dominate and the linear approximation breaks down, leading to inaccurate predictions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Training domain experts with a constant learning rate and minimal tokens preserves their locality relative to the base model, ensuring that their linear combination remains within a well‑behaved region of the parameter space.
- **Mechanism**: By avoiding learning rate decay and limiting tokens, the experts do not diverge into separate loss basins. Their parameter vectors stay close to the base, and the merging interpolation operates in a convex‑like region where performance varies smoothly with weights.
- **Core assumption**: The expert training duration is sufficiently short to prevent catastrophic forgetting or basin departure; the base model is already in a good initialization for all domains.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Section 3.1] explicitly describes the expert training configuration: shared initialization, constant learning rate, and restricted horizon (e.g., fewer than 5B tokens).
  - [Figure 3] shows that merged model curves are smoother than training curves, consistent with merging in a local region.
  - [Figure 8] visualizes a smooth performance landscape over the weight simplex.
- **Break condition**: If experts are trained for too many steps or with aggressive learning rates, they may converge to domain‑specific minima far from the base; merging such divergent experts yields poor models and the weight‑performance correlation deteriorates.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: A lightweight regression model trained on a small set of merged configurations can accurately predict the performance of unseen mixtures, enabling efficient search over the mixture simplex.
- **Mechanism**: After sampling ~40 seed weight vectors, constructing merged models, and evaluating on downstream benchmarks, a set of LightGBM regressors learns the mapping α → capability scores. The surrogate model approximates the true performance surface and can be optimized to find the optimal α* without additional training.
- **Core assumption**: The performance landscape is smooth and low‑dimensional enough to be captured by a simple regressor from a limited number of samples.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Section 3.2] describes the regressor training and utility‑driven selection; [Table 2] reports Spearman ρ > 0.9 between predicted and actual rankings across domains.
  - [Figure 6] shows cross‑scale transferability, indicating the regressor’s predictions generalize beyond the proxy model.
  - [Section 4.6] demonstrates that even with 16 fine‑grained sub‑clusters, hierarchical MergeMix outperforms manual baselines.
- **Break condition**: If the underlying performance surface is highly non‑smooth, discontinuous, or has many local optima that are not captured by the seed samples, the regressor may mislead the search and yield suboptimal mixtures.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Model Merging**  
  - *Why needed here*: MergeMix builds on the idea that linearly combining model weights (e.g., averaging) can yield a single model that inherits capabilities from multiple experts. Understanding this foundation is necessary to grasp why weight interpolation can proxy data mixing.  
  - *Quick check question*: What is the main difference between model merging and ensembling?  

- **Concept: Data Mixture Optimization**  
  - *Why needed here*: The problem domain is optimizing the proportions of different data domains during mid‑training. This concept frames the challenge that MergeMix addresses.  
  - *Quick check question*: Why is it expensive to find the optimal data mixture through conventional methods?  

- **Concept: Local Linear Regime in Fine‑tuning**  
  - *Why needed here*: The theoretical justification relies on the assumption that updates remain small enough for first‑order dynamics to dominate. This concept explains the importance of constant learning rate and limited tokens.  
  - *Quick check question*: What could happen if we train experts with learning rate decay?  

- **Concept: Spearman Rank Correlation**  
  - *Why needed here*: MergeMix evaluates the fidelity of its proxy by measuring rank consistency between predicted and actual performance. This statistical measure is key to validating the approach.  
  - *Quick check question*: Does a high Spearman ρ guarantee that absolute scores are predicted correctly?  

- **Concept: Hierarchical Divide‑and‑Conquer Search**  
  - *Why needed here*: When the number of data domains is large, directly optimizing over the full simplex is intractable. MergeMix uses a hierarchical strategy to reduce dimensionality.  
  - *Quick check question*: In a top‑down hierarchical MergeMix, what is optimized first: coarse domains or sub‑clusters?  

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Expert trainers → Merge engine → Performance evaluator → Regressor tracker → Utility calculator → optimal α*.  
  Hierarchical manager coordinates recursion for many domains.

- **Critical path**:  
  1. Train K domain experts in parallel.  
  2. Generate seed α vectors (grid + heuristics).  
  3. For each seed: merge experts, evaluate, record scores.  
  4. Train regressors on (α, scores).  
  5. Optimize utility function over regressors to get α*.  
  6. Validate α* by merging and full evaluation.  
  7. Deploy α* as data mixture ratio for large‑scale mid‑training (or recursively apply to sub‑clusters).

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *Expert training budget*: More tokens improve individual quality but increase cost; minimal tokens are sufficient because ranking is preserved.  
  - *Regressor complexity*: LightGBM is chosen for speed and ability to handle small data; more complex models may overfit.  
  - *Seed coverage*: Coarse grid ensures simplex coverage; heuristic priors bias toward promising regions but may miss peaks if too sparse.  
  - *Constant LR vs decay*: Constant LR keeps updates linear; decay could be used with simulated annealing via merging top checkpoints (WSM).  
  - *Hierarchical order*: Top‑down vs bottom‑up affects search efficiency; top‑down uses coarse‑level optimum to constrain fine‑level.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Low rank consistency (Spearman ρ < 0.8) indicates weight‑space does not reflect data‑space performance; likely experts diverged or landscape too complex.  
  - Regressor predictions do not match validation runs; observed in domains with high cross‑interference.  
  - Cross‑scale transfer fails: mixture found on small model degrades large model performance, suggesting non‑transferable interactions.  
  - Merged models show high variance across seeds, indicating non‑smooth performance landscape.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. Dual‑capability merging study: train math and code experts, compare merged vs. data‑mixed training curves (Figure 3) and compute rank consistency (Figure 2). Validates core proxy assumption.  
  2. Large‑scale mid‑training validation: apply MergeMix to 8B model with 4 coarse domains; compare against manual and Adapted RegMix baselines (Table 1, Figure 4). Confirms effectiveness and cost reduction.  
  3. Cross‑scale transfer: derive mixture on 8B proxy, apply to 16B target (Figure 6). Demonstrates transferability and further cost savings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Under what precise conditions (e.g., domain dissimilarity, training steps, learning rate schedules) does the first‑order approximation error cause the weight mixing proxy to fail to correctly rank data mixtures?
- **Basis in paper**: The theoretical analysis shows the error is confined to second‑order cross‑domain terms, and empirical rank consistency is high (ρ>0.9) but not perfect. The paper assumes first‑order dominance but does not define boundary conditions for when the approximation breaks down.
- **Why unresolved**: Without knowing failure modes, practitioners cannot safely apply MergeMix to arbitrary domains or training recipes; current validation uses a limited set of mid‑training domains on a specific model family.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic study varying domain orthogonality (measured by task vector cosine similarity), training horizons, and LR schedules, measuring rank consistency decay across diverse domains and reporting conditions where ρ drops below a usable threshold (e.g., 0.8).

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does the hierarchical MergeMix framework scale to hundreds or thousands of datasets, and what automated clustering criteria best construct the hierarchy without manual grouping?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper proposes hierarchical MergeMix for fine‑grained mixtures and tests it on 16 sub‑clusters, but acknowledges industrial scenarios involve hundreds of datasets. The top‑down strategy is used but its scalability is unexplored.
- **Why unresolved**: The current experiment uses manually defined coarse domains and sub‑clusters; scaling to high cardinality requires automatic clustering, but the impact on proxy fidelity and final performance is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments clustering datasets by embedding similarity, gradient similarity, or other metrics, evaluating rank consistency and benchmark scores as cluster number varies from 16 to 256, identifying the trade‑off between search space size and approximation quality.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Does cross‑scale transferability of mixtures hold for dense Transformer architectures and extremely large models (e.g., 70B/100B+ parameters) where optimization dynamics may differ?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper demonstrates transfer from an 8B MoE to a 16B MoE, but notes practitioners can use small proxies for larger models. Generalizability beyond their specific MoE architecture and scale is not tested.
- **Why unresolved**: The optimization landscape may change with scale and architecture type (dense vs. MoE), so the weight‑space geometry enabling MergeMix could break. Transfer to unseen architectures/scales would broaden applicability.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Mid‑training experiments with dense Transformers (e.g., Llama‑style) at 7B/13B/70B scales, transferring mixtures found on a small proxy (e.g., 1B dense) to larger targets, measuring rank consistency and absolute performance gains.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What is the minimal number of seed configurations required to accurately model the performance surface, and can more sophisticated surrogate models further reduce search cost?
- **Basis in paper**: The method samples 40 seed configurations and uses LightGBM regressors. The paper claims cost reduction but does not explore sample complexity or alternative surrogates.
- **Why unresolved**: The 40‑point grid may be suboptimal; if fewer points suffice, MergeMix becomes more efficient. Conversely, if more points are needed for high‑dimensional mixtures, the method's advantage diminishes.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Ablations varying seed count from 10 to 200, comparing LightGBM with Gaussian processes or neural networks, measuring the optimality gap relative to exhaustive simplex search.

## Limitations
- Dataset composition and preprocessing for each domain are unspecified; LightGBM hyperparameters and seed α sampling are undisclosed, impeding exact replication.
- The proxy relies on constant learning rate and minimal expert training; its validity under longer horizons or LR decay is unverified.
- Cross‑scale transferability is only shown for 8B→16B; generalization to other scales or architectures remains open.
