# Gaussian surrogates do well on Poisson inverse problems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17274
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17274
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Regularized Gaussian surrogate objectives (homoscedastic LS, heteroscedastic weighted LS) achieve lower or equal mean squared error than Poisson MAP for low-dose CT; homoscedastic has a strictly smaller theoretical MSE constant.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates reconstruction of images from Poisson-distributed measurements, a key problem in low-dose X-ray computed tomography (CT). Comparing the standard Poisson maximum a posteriori (MAP) approach to Gaussian surrogate objectives (homoscedastic least squares, heteroscedastic weighted least squares, and their regularized variants), the study combines theoretical analysis and real CT experiments. In a stylized diagonal model, the authors prove that homoscedastic Gaussian MAP attains a strictly smaller leading-order mean squared error (MSE) constant than Poisson MAP in the low-dose regime. Experiments with the LoDoPaB-CT dataset confirm that properly regularized Gaussian surrogates match or slightly outperform Poisson MAP across all photon count levels, with the performance gap narrowing at higher counts. The findings suggest that for Poisson inverse problems, careful regularization may be more critical for MSE than exact adherence to the Poisson likelihood.

## Method Summary
The authors analyze a diagonal inverse problem where measurements follow $y_j \sim \text{Poisson}(s a_j x^*_j)$, with dose $s$, system response $a_j$, and true signal $x^*$. They compare four reconstruction methods: (1) Poisson MAP, solved via a one-step-late EM algorithm with Tikhonov regularization $\frac{\tau}{2}\|x\|^2$; (2) homoscedastic Gaussian MAP, which has a closed-form linear solution under the same prior; (3) heteroscedastic Gaussian MAP, minimizing a weighted least squares objective with data-dependent weights, optimized via L-BFGS-B with positivity constraints and a stabilization floor $\epsilon$; and (4) pixel-wise weighted least squares (PWLS) with various weight schemes (oracle, plug-in, plug-in-FBP). A regularization parameter $\tau$ is tuned on a held-out set to minimize MSE; for heteroscedastic methods a stabilization $\epsilon$ is chosen at the lowest count level ($c\approx0.3$) and kept fixed. Experiments use the LoDoPaB-CT dataset: $256\times256$ images from 60 patients are split into 10 tuning and 50 test slices. For each test slice, a forward projection with a parallel-beam geometry yields an expected sinogram $\mu$; a Poisson realization is drawn at several average count levels $c$. All solvers run to convergence, and MSE is computed on the circular field of view.

## Key Results
- Theoretical analysis (diagonal model) shows the leading-order MSE constant for Poisson MAP is $\left(\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4\gamma}}\right)^2$ while for homoscedastic Gaussian MAP it is $(1+\gamma)^{-2}$; the latter is strictly smaller for any $\gamma>0$.
- CT experiments: regularized Gaussian surrogates (homoscedastic LS, heteroscedastic HG, PWLS) achieve MSE equal to or slightly better than Poisson MAP at all count levels; the advantage is most pronounced at very low counts ($c\approx0.1$) and fades as counts increase.
- The results indicate that, for low-dose Poisson inverse problems, appropriate regularization and its tuning have a larger impact on MSE than the choice of an exact Poisson likelihood.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Poisson likelihood, while correct, is discrete and leads to high variance when counts are very low because the estimator is sensitive to single-photon events. Regularization (Tikhonov) reduces this variance by shrinking coefficients, but the Poisson MAP shrinkage factor (derived from the EM update) remains relatively conservative. In contrast, the homoscedastic Gaussian surrogate imposes a constant variance assumption, which yields a closed-form shrinkage factor $1/(1+\gamma)$. This factor shrinks more aggressively for modes with low signal, lowering variance at the cost of additional bias. The net effect is a smaller leading-order MSE constant. Heteroscedastic Gaussian and PWLS attempt to adapt the weights to the data-driven variance; with a proper stabilization $\epsilon$ they also achieve comparable or better performance. Hence, the simplicity and inherent shrinkage of the Gaussian surrogates, combined with careful regularization, outweigh the benefits of exact Poisson modeling in the low-dose regime.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map** (plain text):
Ground truth images (LoDoPaB) -> System matrix A (parallel-beam) -> Expected sinogram $\mu = s A x^*$ -> Poisson draw $y$ -> Reconstruction algorithms (Poisson MAP, Homoscedastic Gaussian, Heteroscedastic Gaussian/PWLS) -> Reconstructed image $\hat{x}$ -> Circular mask -> MSE computation.

**Critical path**:
1. Preprocess data: select slices, normalize, compute forward projection.
2. For each count level $c$: compute expected sinogram $\mu$ such that $\text{mean}(\mu)=c$; draw Poisson realization $y$.
3. Tune regularization $\tau$ on the held-out tuning set.
4. Run reconstruction to convergence on test slices with chosen $\tau$ (and $\epsilon$).
5. Evaluate MSE on circular FOV.
6. Aggregate across test slices and count levels.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Fidelity term*: Poisson likelihood is statistically correct but can be numerically unstable; Gaussian surrogates are simpler and more stable but misspecified.
- *Heteroscedasticity*: Modeling data-dependent variance (HG, PWLS) can improve accuracy but requires a stabilization floor $\epsilon$ to avoid division by zero; choosing $\epsilon$ trades bias for stability.
- *Regularization*: Larger $\tau$ reduces variance but increases bias; must be tuned separately for each count level and method.
- *Optimizer*: Poisson MAP uses an EM-type update; Gaussian homoscedastic has a closed form; heteroscedastic methods require iterative L-BFGS-B, which is slower but flexible.

**Failure signatures**:
- Poisson MAP EM loop does not converge or loss increases: check step size, ensure positivity of updates, verify prior strength.
- Heteroscedastic methods produce extremely large pixel values or NaNs: $\epsilon$ may be too small; increase floor or check weights for zero counts.
- Tuned $\tau$ yields consistently high MSE across all methods: possibly data scaling mismatch ($\mu$ vs $c$) or incorrect mask.
- MSE does not decrease with increasing $\tau$ after a point: over-smoothing; $\tau$ range may need to be expanded.

**Exactly 3 first experiments**:
1. Implement the diagonal model and compute closed-form MSE constants for Poisson MAP and homoscedastic Gaussian MAP; verify the theoretical formulas via Monte Carlo simulation.
2. On the LoDoPaB tuning set, perform a grid search over $\tau$ for homoscedastic LS at a single low count ($c=0.3$) to confirm the U-shaped MSE curve and select an optimal $\tau$.
3. Reconstruct one test slice with Poisson MAP and homoscedastic LS at the lowest count ($c=0.1$) and visually/quantitatively compare the outputs and their MSE.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes a diagonal forward operator; real CT involves correlated measurements and non-diagonal system matrices, so the MSE ordering may not hold exactly.
- Experimental validation is limited to one dataset (LoDoPaB-CT) and a specific parallel-beam geometry; results may not generalize to other scanners or acquisition protocols.
- Evaluation solely via mean squared error; effects on perceptual quality or clinical tasks (e.g., lesion detection) are unknown.

## Confidence
- **Claim**: Homoscedastic Gaussian MAP achieves a strictly smaller leading-order MSE constant than Poisson MAP in the diagonal low-dose model. **Confidence**: High (proven analytically).
- **Claim**: Regularized Gaussian surrogates achieve equal or lower MSE than Poisson MAP in low-dose CT experiments. **Confidence**: Medium (empirical evidence but limited to one dataset and potential implementation variations).
- **Claim**: Proper regularization outweighs exact likelihood specification for MSE in low-dose imaging. **Confidence**: Medium (derived from observed experimental ordering but not universally proven).
