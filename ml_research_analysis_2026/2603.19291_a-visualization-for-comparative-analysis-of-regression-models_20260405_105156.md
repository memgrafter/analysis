# A Visualization for Comparative Analysis of Regression Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19291
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19291
- Reference count: 10
- Primary result: A two-step visualization (boxplot filtering + 2D Error Space with Mahalanobis distance and percentile colormap) reveals hidden error patterns, such as systematic underestimation to avoid dangerous over-predictions, which aggregate metrics obscure.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a visualization toolkit for comparing regression models beyond standard aggregate metrics (MAE, RMSE). The method employs a two-stage process: first, 1D boxplots sorted by an aggregate metric allow rapid filtering of candidate models based on residual spread and outlier count. Second, for two selected models, a 2D Error Space plots their paired residuals; diagonals partition zones where one model outperforms the other, and a percentile-based colormap derived from Mahalanobis distance highlights dense regions and outliers while accounting for correlation and scale. A case study on the AI4I 2020 dataset shows that while aggregate metrics favored one neural network, the visualization exposed a critical trade‑off—the better model systematically underestimated to avoid dangerous over‑predictions, a pattern invisible to aggregates. The approach thus enables more nuanced model selection by surfacing distributional and correlational error structures.

## Method Summary
The proposed pipeline begins by computing residuals for all models on a shared test set. In step one, residuals are displayed as sorted boxplots to quickly identify models with acceptable spread and few outliers. In step two, given two models, their paired residuals are plotted against each other in a 2D plane. The lines y = x and y = −x divide the space into zones indicating which model has the smaller absolute error per instance. Points are colored by a percentile-based colormap computed from the Mahalanobis distance of each point from the median of the paired residuals, which highlights dense cores and outliers while adjusting for correlation and scale. Optional alternatives (KDE, hexbin) are also supported.

## Key Results
- On the AI4I 2020 dataset, two neural networks with asymmetric loss functions had MAE: 20.49 vs 25.58 and RMSE: 32.85 vs 33.55, suggesting one model was superior.
- The 2D Error Space revealed that the model with better aggregate metrics systematically underestimated predictions—a trade‑off to avoid dangerous over‑predictions.
- This hidden pattern demonstrates the limitation of aggregate metrics and the value of the proposed visual approach for nuanced model comparison.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Two-step visual filtering and comparison
The two-step process (boxplot filtering then 2D Error Space) helps reveal error patterns hidden by aggregates. Boxplots sorted by an aggregate metric give a quick overview of residual spread and outliers across many models, enabling efficient filtering. The subsequent 2D scatter of paired residuals shows per‑instance differences; the diagonals y=x and y=−x partition the plane into zones where one model’s absolute error is smaller, exposing systematic biases or complementary error patterns. Evidence: abstract; sections 4.1, 4.2. Break condition: models must be evaluated on the same test set; identical error distributions may render filtering ineffective.

### Mechanism 2: Percentile-based colormap for density and outlier highlighting
A colormap encoding the percentile of each point’s distance from the median makes dense regions and outliers visually distinct. Distances (Mahalanobis or Euclidean) are computed for each paired residual relative to the median; these are converted to percentiles of the distance distribution. Warm colors indicate points in the core (low percentiles), cool colors indicate outliers (high percentiles), and a white boundary marks the median absolute deviation contour. This approach is robust and directly highlights outliers. Evidence: abstract; section 4.3. Break condition: skewed or multimodal error distributions can make the median an unrepresentative center; small samples yield noisy percentiles.

### Mechanism 3: Mahalanobis distance for correlation-aware proximity
Mahalanobis distance provides a correlation‑aware measure of point proximity in the 2D error space. By incorporating the sample covariance matrix of the paired residuals, it decorrelates the two error dimensions and normalizes by variance, turning elliptical point clouds into more interpretable shapes. The resulting distance reflects how many “standard deviations” a point lies from the center, which the percentile colormap then uses. Evidence: abstract; section 4.4. Break condition: covariance matrix must be full‑rank and well‑estimated; near‑singular matrices (e.g., perfectly collinear errors) cause instability; heavy‑tailed distributions may distort the covariance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Residuals**: The raw differences between predictions and ground truth. They are the coordinates in both boxplots and the 2D Error Space, and their sign indicates under- or over-prediction. Quick check: *If a residual is negative, what does that say about the model’s prediction?*  
- **Boxplot**: A compact summary of a distribution showing median, quartiles, and potential outliers. Used for initial filtering of models based on residual spread. Quick check: *What features of a boxplot represent the interquartile range and potential outliers?*  
- **Mahalanobis distance**: A multivariate distance metric that accounts for correlation and scale via the covariance matrix. It determines how far a point lies from the center of a distribution. Quick check: *Write the formula for Mahalanobis distance given a dataset with mean vector and covariance matrix.*

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Data loader → Residual calculator → Boxplot module → (optional filter) → Pairwise comparator → Distance engine → Percentile mapper → Visualization renderer; optional Alternative density modules (KDE, hexbin).

### Critical path
Load data → compute residuals → create global boxplot → filter models → for each pairwise comparison: compute median & covariance → calculate distances → map to percentiles → plot 2D Error Space with colored points and diagonals.

### Design tradeoffs
- Mahalanobis vs Euclidean: correlation‑aware but potentially unstable; Euclidean simpler but may misrepresent shape.
- Median vs mean as central measure: median robust to outliers; mean sensitive.
- Percentile colormap vs KDE/hexbin: percentiles directly highlight outliers; KDE smooths but hides extremes; hexbin reduces overplot but loses point granularity.
- Zone definition: y=x and y=−x define equal absolute error boundaries; could be adapted for asymmetric costs.
- Boxplot step: optional for two models but essential for reducing many candidates.

### Failure signatures
- Mahalanobis computation fails (singular covariance) → fallback to Euclidean or error.
- Boxplots all show huge spreads/outliers → overall poor fit; 2D comparison may still help.
- Colormap nearly uniform → models have very similar error distributions.
- Outliers dominate covariance → distances compressed; consider robust covariance.
- Large dataset overplotting → use transparency, jitter, or hexbin.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How can the 2D Error Space be extended to visualize error evolution across operational conditions (e.g., time, feature ranges) for real-time model monitoring?  
Basis: Conclusion aims to "extend this visual toolkit by integrating how model errors evolve across domain or operational conditions."  
Why unresolved: The static pairwise view does not reveal context‑dependent error changes, crucial for drift detection.  
What evidence: Implement conditioned Error Space (e.g., time bins, feature facets) on streaming data and demonstrate improved failure identification.

### Open Question 2
How robust is Mahalanobis distance‑based coloring when residuals have non‑elliptical distributions (skewed, heavy‑tailed)?  
Basis: Section 4.4 uses Mahalanobis without discussing distributional assumptions; assumes elliptical symmetry.  
Why unresolved: Violations may misrepresent outlier density, as seen in asymmetric AI4I errors.  
What evidence: Simulation comparing Mahalanobis with robust alternatives (e.g., MCD) under various non‑Gaussian residuals; assess alignment with true outlier contours.

### Open Question 3
Can the pairwise 2D Error Space scale to compare multiple regression models while preserving interpretability?  
Basis: All examples compare only two models; no discussion of scalability to ensembles or hyperparameter grids.  
Why unresolved: Practitioners often evaluate many models; pairwise matrices become unwieldy and may hide global patterns.  
What evidence: Design and user evaluation of a multi‑model visualization (e.g., PCA of error vectors, interactive linked views) on a benchmark with >5 models; measure task efficiency and accuracy.

### Open Question 4
What data‑driven or domain‑specific criteria should condition the Error Space, and does conditioning change model selection vs. aggregates?  
Basis: Conclusion proposes "selecting a criterion to visualize model errors" without a method.  
Why unresolved: Arbitrary conditioning might miss critical subgroups; right criterion depends on application risk.  
What evidence: Cross‑domain case studies where experts define conditioning (e.g., by risk score); compare conditioned vs. unconditional model rankings to quantify decision impact.

## Limitations
- Requires paired residuals on the same test set; cannot compare models evaluated on different splits.
- Mahalanobis distance can become unstable with small test sizes or near‑collinear errors, forcing fallback to Euclidean and losing correlation awareness.
- No user study or quantitative metric validates that the visualization improves model selection decisions in practice.
- Overplotting on large datasets may obscure patterns, though mitigations (hexbin, transparency) are suggested but not evaluated.

## Confidence
- Two‑step visual filtering and comparison: Medium
- Percentile‑based colormap: Low
- Mahalanobis distance for correlation‑aware proximity: Medium
