# Mixture of Demonstrations for Textual Graph Understanding and Question Answering

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.23554
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23554
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** MixDemo improves accuracy on GraphQA benchmarks by up to +1.5% over prior state-of-the-art by addressing noisy subgraphs and suboptimal demonstration selection.

## Executive Summary
MixDemo is a framework designed to improve GraphRAG for textual graph question answering by tackling two key problems: the presence of noisy, irrelevant elements in retrieved subgraphs and the selection of low-quality or redundant in-context learning demonstrations. It proposes two main components: a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) demonstration selector that clusters training examples to provide a diverse set of relevant few-shot prompts, and a query-specific graph encoder—a query-conditioned GNN—that dynamically filters noise during message passing. Evaluated on GraphQA datasets (ExplaGraphs, SceneGraphs, WebQSP), MixDemo achieves new state-of-the-art results, demonstrating the efficacy of its approach for reasoning over textual graphs with LLMs.

## Method Summary
The method begins with standard GraphRAG retrieval: a retriever uses Sentence-BERT to find top-k relevant nodes and edges, then connects them via a Prize-Collecting Steiner Tree (PCST) to form a textual subgraph `S`. For demonstration selection, a pre-trained MoE clusterer assigns a test query to the nearest of K pre-computed clusters based on the embedding of the augmented prompt `textualize(S) || q`. The demonstrations from that cluster are used as few-shot examples. Both the current subgraph `S` and each demonstration subgraph `S_d` are encoded by a shared, 3-layer query-specific GNN. This GNN computes query-modulated attention weights for message passing, producing query-aware representations `z_current` and `z_d`. A fusion module computes relevance weights `λ(q, z_d)` and forms a final graph prompt `z_final = Σ λ(q, z_d) * z_d + z_current`. This `z_final` is projected and concatenated with tokenized demonstrations and the query, then fed to a frozen LLM for answer generation.

## Key Results
*   Achieves **87.31% Accuracy** on ExplaGraphs, a **+1.1%** improvement over G-Retriever.
*   Achieves **82.32% Accuracy** on SceneGraphs, a **+1.5%** improvement over prior work.
*   Achieves **71.36% Hit@1** on WebQSP, a **+1.3%** improvement over the best baseline.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Diverse Demonstration Selection via MoE Clustering
*   **Claim:** A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) selector improves in-context learning demonstration quality by selecting a diverse set of semantically relevant examples, rather than relying solely on nearest-neighbor retrieval.
*   **Mechanism:** Demonstrations `D = {(S_i, q_i, a_i)}` are clustered via K-means on Sentence-BERT embeddings of the textualized subgraph concatenated with the query (`x_i = textualize(S_i) || q_i`). At inference, a test query's augmented prompt `x_q` is embedded and assigned to the nearest cluster centroid. The demonstrations from that "expert" cluster are used as the few-shot set.
*   **Core assumption:** Clustering in the augmented prompt embedding space yields semantically cohesive groups where a representative from each group provides complementary reasoning patterns, which is more robust than retrieving the top-k most similar individual examples that may be redundant.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract]: "...employs a Mixture-of-Experts selector that clusters Sentence-BERT embeddings of query-subgraph pairs, assigning test queries to the nearest cluster for diverse, relevant demonstrations."
    *   [section 3.2]: Details the clustering objective (Eq. 2) and assignment rule (Eq. 3). States: "...cluster demonstrations by semantic similarity and select a representative from each cluster for more diverse and complementary examples."
    *   [corpus]: Weak direct citation for the specific clustering approach; the concept is inspired by general MoE and in-context learning literature (Ref [46]).
*   **Break condition:** If the optimal number of clusters `C*` is misestimated (e.g., too few or too many), the "expert" may be either too general or too narrow, failing to provide genuinely diverse or relevant demonstrations. The regularization term `λC` is introduced to mitigate this.

### Mechanism 2: Query-Specific Graph Encoder for Noise Filtering
*   **Claim:** A query-specific graph encoder mitigates noise in retrieved subgraphs by dynamically computing attention weights for message passing that are conditioned on the input query, allowing the model to filter irrelevant nodes and edges during encoding.
*   **Mechanism:** A multi-layer GNN is modified so that at each layer `l`, the edge weight `ζ_e(i,j)^(l)` for message passing is computed from query-conditioned linear transformations of the source node `v_i`, target node `v_j`, and edge `e(i,j)` features (Eqs. 4-7). Messages are also query-conditioned (Eq. 8). This creates a query-aware graph representation `z_S` via mean-pooling (Eq. 9).
*   **Core assumption:** The relevance of a graph element (node/edge) for answer generation is not static but depends on the specific query. By injecting the query embedding `q` into the attention computation, the encoder can suppress noise (e.g., the irrelevant "flying eagle" in the example) even if it is structurally close to relevant facts.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract]: "...a query-specific graph encoder—a query-conditioned GNN—uses attention weights computed from query-modulated node/edge features to filter noise during message passing."
    *   [section 3.3]: Explicitly contrasts with standard GCNs/GATs that use "static, query-agnostic filters." Provides the equations (4-9) defining the query-conditioned attention mechanism.
    *   [corpus]: No direct external anchor; the mechanism is a novel architectural design proposed in this paper.
*   **Break condition:** If the query embedding `q` is a poor signal (e.g., very short, ambiguous) or if the learned linear transformations fail to capture meaningful query-graph interactions, the attention weights may become uninformative, similar to a standard GNN. The ablation (Fig 2b) suggests a depth of 3 layers is optimal, implying deeper layers risk overfitting or degradation.

### Mechanism 3: Dynamic Demonstration Weighting in Fusion
*   **Claim:** The final LLM prompt is optimized by dynamically weighting the contributions of multiple demonstration subgraph embeddings based on their relevance to the current query's subgraph, and integrating this with the query-specific encoding of the current subgraph.
*   **Mechanism:** The current subgraph is encoded to `z_current`. Each demonstration subgraph `S_d` is similarly encoded to `z_d`. A relevance score `s(q, z_d)` (likely a similarity function) computes a soft weight `λ(q, z_d)`. The final graph prompt `z_final` is a weighted sum: `z_final = Σ λ(q, z_d) * z_d + z_current` (Eq. 10). This `z_final` is projected via an MLP (`p_graph`) and concatenated with tokenized demonstrations and query text for the LLM (Eq. 11).
*   **Core assumption:** The quality of a demonstration for a test query can be assessed by the similarity between the *encoded representations* of their respective subgraphs, not just the surface text of the query. This allows for a more nuanced selection that accounts for structural similarity in the latent graph space.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 3.3 (last paragraph)]: "To incorporate multiple demonstration subgraphs {z_d}, we use query-based relevance weighting..." and presents equation (10).
    *   [corpus]: No direct external anchor; this is a specific fusion technique within the proposed framework.
*   **Break condition:** If the relevance scoring function `s(·)` is poorly calibrated (e.g., it defaults to uniform weights or is dominated by the largest subgraph), the weighted sum may not effectively distinguish useful from less useful demonstrations, limiting the benefit of the MoE selection. The absolute performance gains (+1.1% to +1.5%) suggest this mechanism provides a measurable but incremental improvement.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: In-Context Learning (ICL) / Few-Shot Prompting**
    *   Why needed here: The entire framework is built around providing the LLM with a few high-quality `(query, subgraph, answer)` examples (demonstrations) to guide its reasoning on a new query. The paper's core innovation is *selecting* these demonstrations effectively.
    *   Quick check question: What is the core risk of using a naive, nearest-neighbor retrieval for selecting ICL demonstrations in GraphRAG, as implied by the paper's motivation?
*   **Concept: Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) & Message Passing**
    *   Why needed here: To encode the structure and semantics of the retrieved textual subgraph into a dense representation for the LLM. The paper specifically modifies standard GNN message passing to be query-aware.
    *   Quick check question: In a standard GAT, what does the attention coefficient `α_ij` depend on, and how does MixDemo's `ζ_e(i,j)` differ?
*   **Concept: Mixture of Experts (MoE) & Clustering**
    *   Why needed here: To move beyond instance-level retrieval for demonstrations and towards selecting a diverse set from different "expert" clusters, aiming for better coverage of reasoning patterns.
    *   Quick check question: What are the two competing terms in the MoE cluster count objective function (Eq. 2), and what does the hyperparameter `λ` control?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Retriever -> MoE Demo Selector -> Shared Query-Specific GNN Encoder (processes both current subgraph S and demo subgraphs {S_d}) -> Fusion & Prompt Constructor -> Frozen LLM
*   **Critical path:** Query `q` -> Subgraph `S` retrieval & Demo Cluster Assignment -> Encode `S` to `z_current` & Encode Demo Subgraphs to `{z_d}` -> Compute `z_final` via weighted sum -> Construct LLM prompt -> Generate answer `a_gen`.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Demo clustering vs. online retrieval:** Pre-clustering (`O(C)`) is fast at inference but inflexible; online nearest-neighbor (`O(|D|)`) is accurate but slow. The paper bets on cluster representative diversity.
    *   **GNN depth:** Deeper GNNs can capture longer-range dependencies but risk over-smoothing, especially in noisy subgraphs. Ablation (Fig 2b) finds 3 layers optimal.
    *   **Query injection point:** Conditioning occurs at every GNN layer for edge weights and messages. This is computationally heavier than a single conditioning but allows adaptive filtering throughout propagation.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **Poor demo relevance:** If the test query's `x_q` embedding is equidistant to multiple cluster centroids, the assigned "expert" may provide irrelevant demos, hurting ICL.
    *   **GNN over-smoothing:** Even with query conditioning, if `q` provides weak gradient signal, node embeddings may still converge to similar values after 3+ layers, losing discriminative power.
    *   **Fusion failure:** If `s(q, z_d)` scores are near-uniform (e.g., `z_d` vectors are poorly separated), `z_final` becomes a simple average, nullifying the benefit of selective weighting.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Ablation: Shot Number.** Validate the claim that 2-shot is optimal (Fig 2a). Run 0,1,2,3-shot on ExplaGraphs. Expected: 2-shot > 1-shot > 0-shot, 3-shot ≈ 2-shot.
    2.  **Ablation: GNN Layers.** Validate the claim that 3 layers are best (Fig 2b). Test 1,2,3,4+ layers on a held-out set. Expected: 3 > 2 > 1, 4+ degrades.
    3.  **Component Analysis:** Create ablation variants: (a) w/o MoE (use random demos), (b) w/o query-specific GNN (use standard GAT), (c) w/o demo weighting (uniform sum). Expected: Full model > all ablations, showing both components contribute.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
*   Question: Does the choice of clustering algorithm (K-means) and embedding model (Sentence-BERT) for the MoE demonstration selector impact the diversity and effectiveness of selected demonstrations?
*   Basis in paper: [inferred] Section 3.2 describes clustering Sentence-BERT embeddings of augmented prompts using K-means, with no comparison to other clustering methods or embedding fine-tuning.
*   Why unresolved: The paper uses a fixed clustering approach without justification; alternative methods might yield more semantically meaningful or diverse expert clusters.
*   What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of different clustering algorithms (e.g., hierarchical, DBSCAN) and embedding models (e.g., task-finetuned Sentence-BERT, contrastive learning) on GraphQA, measuring demonstration diversity (e.g., cluster purity, inter-cluster similarity) and downstream accuracy.

### Open Question 2
*   Question: Are static MoE clusters (trained once on the demonstration pool) sufficient for handling shifts in query distribution at test time?
*   Basis in paper: [inferred] Section 3.2 trains cluster centroids offline on the demo pool and assigns test queries via nearest centroid, with no mechanism for updating clusters based on incoming queries.
*   Why unresolved: Query distributions may drift (e.g., new question types), making static clusters less representative; adaptive clustering could maintain relevance.
*   What evidence would resolve it: Simulate query distribution shifts (e.g., by subsampling demo pools or introducing novel queries) and compare static MoE against online or incremental clustering approaches, evaluating accuracy and robustness over time.

### Open Question 3
*   Question: Why does the query-specific GNN encoder peak at three layers, and does deeper architecture fail due to over-smoothing despite query-conditioning?
*   Basis in paper: [explicit] Figure 2b shows 3 layers optimal; Section 3.3 mentions over-smoothing in GCNs as motivation for query-conditioning.
*   Why unresolved: The ablation shows performance drop beyond 3 layers but doesn't analyze whether over-smoothing persists in query-aware layers or if other factors (e.g., optimization) cause degradation.
*   What evidence would resolve it: Analyze node embeddings at each layer for query relevance (e.g., attention entropy, similarity to query embedding) and task accuracy; test deeper models with residual connections or layer normalization to mitigate over-smoothing.

### Open Question 4
*   Question: Does MixDemo generalize to textual graph QA tasks beyond the GraphQA benchmark (e.g., biomedical, legal, or social network graphs)?
*   Basis in paper: [explicit] Experiments are limited to ExplaGraphs, SceneGraphs, and WebQSP (Section 4.1).
*   Why unresolved: These datasets have specific characteristics (e.g., small explanatory graphs, large scene graphs, FreeBase KGs); performance on other graph types (e.g., dense scientific graphs) is unknown.
*   What evidence would resolve it: Evaluate MixDemo on additional textual graph QA datasets from diverse domains, measuring accuracy and analyzing noise patterns; compare to baselines to assess relative gains.

## Limitations
*   Unknown LLM backbone (size, version, temperature) and its impact on reported gains.
*   Unclear training protocol for GNN/MoE components (pretrained/finetuned? loss/optimizer?).
*   No justification for fixed cluster count `C*` selection method beyond Eq. 2; sensitivity to `λ` unexplored.
*   Potential data leakage if test queries are semantically close to training clusters in embedding space.

## Confidence
*   **Performance claims** (+1.1–1.5% on GraphQA): **Medium** (reproducibility hindered by missing LLM/training details).
*   **MoE selector mechanism:** **Medium** (plausible but cluster diversity not empirically validated).
*   **Query-specific GNN design:** **Medium** (theoretically sound; query conditioning benefit inferred only from final results).
*   **Ablation results** (2-shot, 3-layer optimal): **High** (explicitly stated, easier to verify).
