# AMIR-GRPO: Inducing Implicit Preference Signals into GRPO

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03661
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03661
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** AMIR-GRPO enhances GRPO for reasoning-heavy LLM alignment by augmenting it with an implicit DPO-style contrastive regularizer derived from intra-group reward rankings, yielding consistent improvements in Pass@k across mathematical benchmarks and mitigating length bias and mode collapse.

## Executive Summary
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is an effective method for aligning language models on reasoning tasks, but it suffers from structural limitations: sequence-level advantage normalization introduces length bias, penalties for low-quality trajectories are diluted, and within-group preference information is discarded. This paper proposes AMIR-GRPO, which injects an implicit DPO-style contrastive regularizer into GRPO. The regularizer is constructed automatically from the reward ranking of completions within each rollout group, turning each group into multiple pairwise preferences. This densifies supervision, amplifies suppression of low-reward trajectories, and mitigates length bias without requiring extra annotations. Experiments on multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that AMIR-GRPO consistently outperforms GRPO baselines in Pass@k, expands the set of solvable problems, and maintains greater trajectory diversity. The approach integrates cleanly with GRPO variants and incurs only modest memory overhead.

## Method Summary
AMIR-GRPO builds on standard GRPO. For each query, it samples \(G\) completions from the current policy, computes scalar rewards (combining correctness, format, and calibration), and calculates group-normalized advantages. The key addition is an implicit preference mining step: within each group, all pairs \((i,j)\) where the reward difference exceeds a margin \(\delta_r\) are treated as implicit preferences (higher reward preferred). For each pair, a DPO-style loss is computed using length-normalized log-probabilities of the completions under the current and reference policies. This contrastive regularizer \(J_{\text{pref}}\) is combined with the GRPO objective \(J_{\text{GRPO}}\) via an adaptive coefficient \(\lambda\) that maintains a target relative contribution. The total loss \(J = J_{\text{GRPO}} + \lambda J_{\text{pref}}\) is used to update the policy (typically via LoRA). No additional human annotations are needed; all preference data is extracted from the reward signal already computed for GRPO.

## Key Results
- AMIR-GRPO significantly improves Pass@k (especially \(k > 1\)) over strong GRPO baselines on GSM8K, AIME25, OlympiadBench, AMC23, MinervaMath, AQUA-RAT, and LiveMathBench.
- It yields a sharper separation between correct and incorrect reasoning chains, as measured by reward distributions.
- The method expands the set of uniquely solvable problems beyond GRPO’s reach and mitigates response-level length bias for low-reward trajectories, while preserving greater output diversity (measured by conditional perplexity interquartile range).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Implicit Pairwise Preference Extraction
AMIR-GRPO densifies supervision by converting each rollout group into multiple pairwise preferences derived from reward rankings. For a query \(q\) with \(G\) completions and rewards \(\{r_i\}\), it constructs a set \(S(q) = \{(i,j) \mid r_i > r_j,\; r_i - r_j > \delta_r\}\). Each pair contributes a DPO-style logit \(z_{i,j}(\theta) = \beta_{\text{DPO}}[(\ell_\theta(q,o_i)-\ell_{\text{ref}}(q,o_i)) - (\ell_\theta(q,o_j)-\ell_{\text{ref}}(q,o_j))]\), where \(\ell\) is length-normalized log-probability. The regularizer \(J_{\text{pref}} = \mathbb{E}_{(q,i,j)\sim S}[\log\sigma(z_{i,j})]\) encourages the policy to assign higher probability to preferred completions. The core assumption is that within-group reward differences reliably indicate quality ordering. This breaks if rewards are noisy or \(\delta_r\) is poorly chosen.

### Mechanism 2: Amplified Suppression of Low-Reward Trajectories
In sparse-reward settings, AMIR-GRPO amplifies the penalty on low-reward trajectories by making them the rejected side in multiple pairwise comparisons. In GRPO, a low-reward trajectory \(i\) receives only a single negative advantage \(\hat{A}_i\), which may be small if many low-reward trajectories exist. In AMIR-GRPO, if trajectory \(i\) is worse than several others, it appears in \(|\{j: r_j < r_i - \delta_r\}|\) pairs. Each pair contributes a negative gradient via \(J_{\text{pref}}\), effectively multiplying the suppression signal. This requires that the preference pairs are valid and the DPO loss aggregates them effectively. If group size \(G\) is small or rewards are clustered, amplification may be limited.

### Mechanism 3: Mitigation of Sequence-Level Length Bias
GRPO assigns the same trajectory-level advantage to every token of a response. For positive advantages, shorter responses have fewer tokens, so per-token update magnitude is larger; for negative advantages, long responses spread the negative gradient over many tokens, diluting the penalty. AMIR-GRPO’s contrastive signal compares entire completions via length-normalized log-probability \(\ell_\theta(q,o) = (1/|o|)\sum_t \log\pi_\theta(o_t|...)\), making it independent of response length. Long low-reward trajectories, if consistently rejected, accumulate more negative gradient from multiple pairs, counteracting dilution. The assumption is that length-normalized log-probability is a fair metric for comparing completions of varying lengths. If length normalization distorts quality (e.g., longer responses have lower average log-probability despite being better), comparisons become unfair.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Rollout Sampler → Reward Scorer → GRPO Advantage Calculator → Implicit Preference Miner → DPO Loss Module → Adaptive λ Controller → Loss Aggregator → Optimizer

**Critical path**  
1. For a batch of queries, sample \(G=8\) completions each from the current policy.  
2. Compute scalar rewards for all completions.  
3. Compute group-normalized advantages \(\hat{A}_i = (r_i - \text{mean}_r)/\text{std}_r\).  
4. For each query, mine preference pairs \(S(q) = \{(i,j) \mid r_i - r_j > \delta_r\}\).  
5. Compute DPO loss \(J_{\text{pref}}\) over all pairs using length-normalized log-probabilities of current vs. reference policy.  
6. Update \(\lambda\) based on relative magnitudes of \(J_{\text{GRPO}}\) and \(J_{\text{pref}}\) to keep \(J_{\text{pref}}\)’s contribution within a target range.  
7. Total loss: \(J = J_{\text{GRPO}} + \lambda \cdot J_{\text{pref}}\).  
8. Backpropagate and update policy (via LoRA or full parameters).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Memory**: Storing all completions and their log-probabilities increases GPU memory (~3–4 GiB for 7B models). Tradeoff: richer supervision vs. memory.  
- **Computation**: \(O(G^2)\) pairwise mining adds overhead, but \(G=8\) keeps it modest.  
- **Hyperparameters**: \(\delta_r\), \(\beta_{\text{DPO}}\), \(\lambda\) schedule require tuning; the paper shows \(\beta_{\text{DPO}}\) sensitivity.  
- **Reward design**: Method relies on reward rankings; poor reward functions (e.g., only binary correctness) may yield uninformative rankings.

**Failure signatures**  
- **Lack of coverage improvement**: Pass@k (especially \(k>1\)) does not increase relative to GRPO, indicating failure to expand solvable set.  
- **Persistence of length bias**: Average length of incorrect responses remains high compared to expected shorter lengths.  
- **Diversity collapse**: Conditional perplexity distribution becomes narrower than GRPO’s, indicating mode collapse.  
- **Unchanged error profile**: No reduction in specific error types (e.g., early-step errors).

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Baseline replication**: Train GRPO and AMIR-GRPO on GSM8K train with Qwen-3B. Verify AMIR-GRPO outperforms GRPO on GSM8K test Pass@k.  
2. **Ablation on \(\delta_r\)**: Test AMIR-GRPO with \(\delta_r \in \{0.0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}\) (relative to reward scale). Identify setting that maximizes performance.  
3. **Diversity measurement**: Sample 16 completions per query from both models on a held-out set. Compute conditional perplexity under each model and compare interquartile ranges; AMIR-GRPO should show larger IQR.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to other modalities**: Does AMIR-GRPO generalize to non-textual reasoning modalities such as code generation or vision-language tasks? The paper notes extending to other modalities is an important future avenue.
2. **Performance in non-mathematical domains**: Can AMIR-GRPO deliver consistent improvements on non-mathematical reasoning domains like commonsense or cultural reasoning? The authors call for broader evaluation.
3. **Process-level rewards**: Would incorporating process-level rewards or fine-grained credit assignment enhance AMIR-GRPO’s ability to correct early-step errors? The paper suggests richer intermediate signals could improve exploration efficiency.
4. **Sensitivity to reward margin \(\delta_r\)**: How sensitive is AMIR-GRPO to the reward margin \(\delta_r\) used in implicit preference mining, and what is an effective strategy for setting it? The paper does not analyze or report a value.

## Limitations
- The exact value of the reward margin \(\delta_r\) for mining preferences is not disclosed, affecting pairwise density and gradient strength.
- The adaptive \(\lambda\) update rule lacks specifics: target contribution range, multiplicative factors, update frequency, and whether a moving average is used.
- Training rollout hyperparameters (temperature, top-p) and old-policy refresh cadence are omitted, potentially impacting stability and reproduction.
