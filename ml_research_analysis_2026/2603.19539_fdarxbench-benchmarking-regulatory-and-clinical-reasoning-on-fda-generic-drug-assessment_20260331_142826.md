# FDARxBench: Benchmarking Regulatory and Clinical Reasoning on FDA Generic Drug Assessment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19539
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19539
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: FDARxBench reveals substantial performance gaps: closed-book accuracy ~0.22–0.34, full-label ~0.40–0.53, well below oracle ~0.80; citation F1 peaks at ~0.53; refusal behavior varies with model scale.

## Executive Summary
FDARxBench is a novel, expert‑curated benchmark for evaluating large language models on the regulatory task of FDA generic drug assessment. Built from 700 FDA drug labels, it contains 17,223 question‑answer pairs (factual, multi‑hop, and refusal) constructed via a multi‑stage pipeline: context selection, LLM generation, FDA expert feedback, and rule‑based plus LLM‑as‑judge filtering. Models are evaluated under four settings—closed‑book, full‑label with required citations, oracle passages, and retrieval‑augmented—using answer accuracy, refusal F1, and citation F1. Results show that even the best models fall far short of an oracle upper bound (~0.80), with full‑label accuracy only 0.40–0.53, moderate citation quality (~0.53 F1), and widely varying refusal behavior (smaller models abstain more appropriately). Retrieval experiments indicate lexical matching (BM25) outperforms dense methods, underscoring the evidence‑selection bottleneck. Overall, FDARxBench provides a challenging, regulator‑aligned platform to drive progress on factual grounding, safe abstention, and traceable reasoning.

## Method Summary
The dataset construction begins with FDA Structured Product Labels (SPLs) in XML, parsed into section‑level text chunks with unique IDs. A context selector picks one chunk (factual) or two chunks (multi‑hop) as source material. An LLM (GPT‑4o‑mini) generates question‑answer pairs using task‑specific prompts. A rigorous multi‑stage filtering pipeline ensures quality: rule‑based filters remove structurally invalid items (e.g., missing drug names, length issues); an LLM‑as‑judge then enforces relevance (using criteria distilled from FDA assessors), checks correctness and extracts evidence, and evaluates question quality. This filtering is tuned for high precision to avoid incorrect inclusions. The final benchmark comprises 17,223 QA pairs across three types. For evaluation, models are tested in four settings: closed‑book (no context), full‑label (entire label with citation requirement), oracle‑passages (only gold supporting chunks), and retrieval‑augmented (using a retriever to fetch chunks). Metrics include answer accuracy (graded by an LLM‑as‑judge), refusal F1, and citation F1 (overlap of predicted chunk IDs with gold). The retriever suite compares BM25, cosine similarity, and ReContriever.

## Key Results
- Closed‑book accuracy: factual 0.22, multi‑hop 0.34; full‑label accuracy: factual 0.53, multi‑hop 0.40, both well below oracle (~0.80).
- Citation quality peaks at 0.53 F1; refusal F1 varies widely—Llama‑8B achieves 0.80 while frontier models over‑refuse.
- Retrieval analysis shows BM25 significantly outperforms dense methods, indicating lexical matching dominates evidence selection in drug labels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Expert‑guided relevance filtering
- **Claim**: Ensures regulatory alignment and prevents benchmark drift toward generic biomedical QA.
- **Mechanism**: FDA assessors review a seed set of LLM‑generated questions, distill relevance criteria into a structured prompt, and use an LLM‑as‑judge to scale filtering, classifying questions into FDA label categories and flagging irrelevance.
- **Core assumption**: Expert‑defined criteria can be encoded into an LLM prompt and generalized to unseen questions.
- **Evidence**: LLM filter precision 0.797, F1 0.683 (0.922/0.738 without multi‑hop) on human‑validated samples (Sec. 3.2, Table 4).
- **Break condition**: If the LLM‑as‑judge’s precision‑recall tradeoff heavily favors recall or the initial seed set is too small/narrow, irrelevant items dilute the benchmark’s regulatory focus.

### Mechanism 2: High‑precision multi‑stage filtering
- **Claim**: Achieving high precision in factual grounding creates a reliable benchmark despite the inherent ambiguity of regulatory language.
- **Mechanism**: Cascade of filters: rules first (structural validity), then LLM‑as‑judge for relevance, QA correctness with evidence extraction, and question quality. The filter is tuned for precision (low false positive rate) rather than recall.
- **Core assumption**: A conservative, high‑precision filter is preferable; including incorrect or weakly grounded examples is more damaging than omitting marginal ones.
- **Evidence**: Human validation shows high precision (0.900‑1.000) on the unanimous subset (Table 4); the authors explicitly prioritize precision.
- **Break condition**: If precision tuning is too aggressive and the gold standard (human consensus) has low agreement, the benchmark may become arbitrarily narrow, failing to capture real‑world variability.

### Mechanism 3: Evaluation design isolating evidence selection
- **Claim**: The evaluation settings (full‑label vs. oracle vs. closed‑book) isolate the evidence‑selection bottleneck in long‑context document QA.
- **Mechanism**: By comparing performance when models are given (a) the entire label, (b) only gold passage(s), and (c) no context, the gap between (a) and (b) quantifies the cost of imperfect retrieval/long‑context reading. Oracle accuracy shows reasoning capability is sufficient if given correct evidence; full‑label lower bound reveals evidence selection as the limiting factor.
- **Core assumption**: Oracle passage setting provides a valid upper bound on reasoning ability with perfect evidence; the difference from full‑label performance is primarily due to retrieval/reading errors.
- **Evidence**: Full‑label accuracy consistently lower than oracle (Sec. 4.1, Fig. 3); oracle ~0.80 vs full‑label ~0.40‑0.53; retrieval recall@1 low (0.22‑0.56) (Table 2).
- **Break condition**: If oracle passages themselves are insufficient (answer requires synthesis across many sections), oracle accuracy underestimates potential and the observed gap overstates the retrieval bottleneck.

## Foundational Learning

- **Regulatory Document QA Requirements**  
  Standard biomedical QA models focus on general knowledge extraction, but regulators need models that answer *what this specific label says* with traceability and safe abstention.

- **Evidence‑Grounded Generation with Citations**  
  Regulators must verify every claim against the source; a correct answer without correct citations is unusable. The benchmark forces models to produce both answer and supporting passage IDs.  
  *Quick check*: Why is evaluating citation F1 necessary but insufficient for judging the utility of a model’s output in a regulatory setting?

  Drug labels are heterogeneous XML; needed information is often split across non‑contiguous sections (e.g., dosage adjustments across populations). Models must integrate these fragments.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
SPL XML → Chunking → Context Selection → LLM Generation → Rule‑Based Filters → LLM‑as‑Judge → Sampling & Audit → Final Dataset

### Critical Path
SPL XML → Chunking → Context Selection → LLM Generation → Rule Filter → LLM‑Judge (Relevance → Correctness → Quality) → Final Dataset → Model Inference (in setting) → LLM‑Grader/Refusal/Citation Metrics

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Chunking vs. Context**: Smaller chunks enable fine‑grained provenance but force generation from isolated fragments, missing cross‑paragraph relationships.
- **Filter Precision vs. Recall**: Pipeline explicitly prioritizes precision to avoid incorrect examples, sacrificing recall and potentially narrowing the benchmark’s scope.
- **Automation vs. Expert Cost**: Using LLMs to scale expert judgment reduces cost but introduces dependency on the LLM’s alignment with expert reasoning, validated only on a small stratified sample.
- **Citation Metric**: Overlap‑based F1 with gold chunk IDs is simple but penalizes semantically correct citations that differ due to redundancy or chunking boundaries.

### Failure Signatures
1. **High oracle‑to‑full‑label gap**: Evidence selection/retrieval bottleneck, not reasoning.
2. **Low citation F1 despite decent answer accuracy**: Model guesses without grounding.
3. **Frontier models over‑refuse** (high recall, low precision) on refusal set; smallest model (Llama‑8B) achieves best refusal F1, indicating poor calibration at scale.
4. **BM25 consistently beating dense retrievers**: Lexical keyword matching is more reliable than semantic similarity in drug labels.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Evidence Access Ablation**: Run top model (e.g., GPT‑5.1) in closed‑book, oracle‑passages, and full‑label settings. Expect: oracle accuracy > full‑label > closed‑book, quantifying retrieval bottleneck.
2. **Citation vs. Answer Correlation**: Plot full‑label answer accuracy against citation F1 per model. Expect little positive correlation, indicating answers often come from parametric knowledge, not evidence.
3. **Retriever Sanity Check**: Evaluate BM25, cosine similarity, and ReContriever on recall@k. Expect BM25 to dominate, confirming lexical retrieval importance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can we generate and evaluate QA that requires reasoning across broader label contexts (multiple sections or full document) while maintaining provenance tracking and avoiding chunking artifacts?
2. How should citation evaluation be designed when drug labels contain redundant information, allowing multiple valid evidence passages?
3. How can we construct realistic multi‑hop regulatory questions that integrate information from more than two sections and capture complex constraints (e.g., linking pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety)?
4. How can we improve automatic grading of model outputs (answer correctness and citation quality) to achieve higher agreement with human experts, especially on borderline cases?
5. How can refusal evaluation be expanded to include a diverse set of naturally unanswerable or partially answerable questions that reflect real regulatory queries?

## Limitations
- High‑precision filtering may exclude borderline but valid regulatory questions, narrowing benchmark scope.
- LLM‑as‑judge grading may misalign with expert reasoning on nuanced regulatory language, risking metric bias.
- Section‑level chunking may fragment evidence needed for multi‑hop questions, especially across non‑contiguous sections.

## Confidence
- **Regulatory alignment (expert‑guided filtering)**: Medium  
- **Evidence‑selection bottleneck (full‑label vs. oracle gap)**: High  
- **BM25 superiority over dense retrievers**: High  
- **Persistent gap to oracle (~0.80)**: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Human evaluation**: Recruit FDA experts to judge a stratified sample of model outputs (full‑label setting) for answer correctness and citation quality; compare with LLM‑as‑judge scores.
2. **Chunking sensitivity**: Regenerate a subset of the benchmark using paragraph‑level or sliding‑window chunks; re‑evaluate key models to see if multi‑hop performance changes significantly.
3. **Domain‑adapted retriever**: Pre‑train a dense retriever on a large corpus of FDA drug labels with contrastive learning; test whether it can surpass BM25 on gold‑passage recall.
