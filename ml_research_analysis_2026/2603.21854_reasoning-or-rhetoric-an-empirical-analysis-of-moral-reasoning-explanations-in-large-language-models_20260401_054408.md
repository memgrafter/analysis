# Reasoning or Rhetoric? An Empirical Analysis of Moral Reasoning Explanations in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21854
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21854
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** LLMs overwhelmingly generate post-conventional moral reasoning (Stages 5–6), diverging sharply from the human Stage‑4‑dominant baseline (mean Jensen‑Shannon divergence 0.71), consistent with the “moral ventriloquism” hypothesis.

## Executive Summary
This study examines whether large language models (LLMs) produce genuine Kohlbergian moral reasoning or merely mimic the rhetorical style of mature ethical thought. Evaluating 13 diverse LLMs on six classical moral dilemmas (e.g., trolley problem) through an LLM‑as‑judge pipeline, the authors find that all models overwhelmingly justify their actions with post‑conventional reasoning, in stark contrast to the typical human distribution dominated by Stage 4. Moreover, models exhibit extremely high consistency across dilemmas (ICC > 0.90) and show “moral decoupling” – high‑stage justifications paired with low‑stage actions – particularly in mid‑tier models. Scale yields a small but significant effect; training type and prompting strategy do not. The findings support the notion that alignment training teaches models to reproduce the form of mature moral discourse without the underlying developmental trajectory, a phenomenon termed “moral ventriloquism.”

## Method Summary
The study collected over 600 responses from 13 LLMs (e.g., Claude, DeepSeek, Llama, Qwen) on six canonical moral dilemmas, each presented under three prompt configurations: zero‑shot, chain‑of‑thought, and roleplay (three samples per configuration). An LLM‑as‑judge pipeline, using three independent judge models (GPT‑4, Claude Sonnet, Llama‑3), classified each response into a Kohlberg stage (1–6), recording confidence and justification. From these classifications, the authors computed stage distributions, Jensen‑Shannon divergence from a human developmental baseline, intraclass correlation (ICC) for cross‑dilemma consistency, Cramér’s V for alignment between binary actions and reasoning stages, and factorial ANOVA to test effects of model scale (parameter count) and training regime (Base‑RLHF, Coding‑Tuned, Reasoning‑Tuned). Additional diversity metrics (Shannon entropy, Gini coefficient) were also analyzed.

## Key Results
- All models produced predominantly post‑conventional reasoning (Stages 5–6), opposite to the human Stage 4‑dominant norm (mean Jensen‑Shannon divergence = 0.71).
- Cross‑dilemma consistency was extremely high (ICC > 0.90), indicating near‑uniform responses across distinct moral scenarios.
- Moral decoupling (high‑stage justifications with low‑stage actions) was observed in mid‑tier models; scale had a small significant effect (η² = 0.05, p = 0.003), while training type and prompting showed no significant independent influence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The proposed mechanism is “moral ventriloquism”: RLHF and similar alignment techniques train models to produce outputs that sound ethically mature, as judged by human labelers who themselves tend to favor post‑conventional reasoning. The LLM‑as‑judge pipeline works because the judge models, also RLHF‑aligned, share this rhetorical bias, leading to high agreement on stage labels. This creates a self‑reinforcing cycle where both the evaluated models and the judges converge on the same high‑stage vocabulary, but without necessarily engaging in the cognitive processes underlying genuine moral development. The consistency across dilemmas and the decoupling between action and justification further indicate that the models are mimicking surface patterns rather than building coherent moral schemas.

## Foundational Learning
- **Kohlberg’s stages of moral development** – why needed: Provides the theoretical taxonomy to classify moral reasoning. Quick check: Six stages grouped into pre‑conventional (1–2), conventional (3–4), post‑conventional (5–6).
- **Jensen‑Shannon divergence** – why needed: Quantifies the distributional difference between a model’s stage assignments and the human developmental baseline. Quick check: Symmetric divergence metric between 0 (identical) and 1 (maximally different).
- **Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)** – why needed: Measures consistency of stage labels across different dilemmas for the same model. Quick check: >0.9 indicates excellent reliability.
- **Cramér’s V** – why needed: Assesses statistical association between the binary action choice and the assigned moral reasoning stage. Quick check: Value between 0 (no association) and 1 (perfect association).
- **Factorial ANOVA & effect size (η²)** – why needed: Determines whether model scale and training regime independently or jointly influence stage distributions, and quantifies the magnitude of each factor. Quick check: η² represents proportion of variance explained; 0 small, 0.06 medium, 0.14 large.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Target LLM → generate response (action + justification) → action extraction heuristic → judge LLMs (GPT‑4, Claude, Llama‑3) → stage classification → aggregation (stage distributions, ICC, Cramér’s V, etc.).

**Critical path**  
The judge pipeline is critical: accurate and unbiased stage assignment is essential; any systematic error (e.g., bias toward high stages) propagates to all downstream metrics.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Judge model selection: RLHF‑aligned judges ensure consistency with the training distribution but risk inflation of post‑conventional scores; including non‑aligned judges reduces bias but may lower agreement.
- Action extraction: Automated parsing from justifications is scalable but error‑prone; manual annotation is precise but infeasible for large samples.
- Prompt variation: Testing multiple templates controls for prompt sensitivity but complicates analysis and increases computational cost.

**Failure signatures**  
- Low inter‑judge agreement (Fleiss’ κ < 0.6) indicates ambiguous scoring guidelines or highly heterogeneous model outputs.
- Near‑perfect cross‑dilemma ICC (>0.95) might signal that models are ignoring dilemma specifics and producing a canned response.
- Unexpected patterns of moral decoupling could stem from inconsistencies in the action extraction rule rather than genuine model behavior.

**First experiments**  
1. Replicate the exact six dilemma texts and three prompt configurations from the paper’s appendix; verify fidelity against canonical formulations.
2. Run the LLM‑as‑judge pipeline on a small subset (e.g., 2 models × 2 dilemmas) with the three judges; compute inter‑juder reliability and adjust the judging prompt if needed.
3. Calculate Jensen‑Shannon divergence for a baseline model to confirm that the divergence with the human distribution exceeds 0.7 as reported.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does RLHF training causally induce moral ventriloquism?**  
   The study is observational; causality requires controlled experiments that systematically vary alignment components and possibly mechanistic interpretability probes.

2. **What internal computational mechanisms cause moral decoupling?**  
   The paper identifies decoupling empirically but cannot explain whether it arises from separated decision‑justification circuits, training artifacts, or architectural factors; interpretability studies are needed.

3. **Can adversarial moral dilemmas distinguish moral ventriloquism from genuine reasoning?**  
   The authors propose designing dilemma pairs where the rhetorically high‑stage choice conflicts with logical correctness; such benchmarks could expose inconsistencies in ventriloquism.

4. **Does the LLM‑as‑judge pipeline itself introduce bias?**  
   Since judges are RLHF‑aligned, they may inflate post‑conventional scores in a circular manner; comparing with non‑aligned judges or human annotators would test this.

## Limitations
- **Stimuli fidelity:** Exact wording of dilemmas and prompts is unreported; minor changes could alter model behavior.
- **Judge pipeline bias:** RLHF‑aligned judges likely share rhetorical preferences with evaluated models, risking systematic score inflation.
- **Action extraction opacity:** The method for deriving binary actions from justifications is unspecified, which could artifactually generate “moral decoupling.”

## Confidence
- LLM outputs are overwhelmingly post‑conventional, unlike human developmental norms: **High**
- Scale has a small but significant effect on stage distribution: **Medium**
- Moral decoupling occurs in mid‑tier models: **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Exact prompt replication:** Retrieve original dilemma texts and prompt templates from the authors or supplement; document any deviations.
2. **Human annotation benchmark:** Collect human judgments on a stratified sample (n≥200) to validate LLM‑as‑judge stage assignments and ICC.
3. **Action extraction robustness:** Implement multiple parsing strategies (keyword‑based, LLM‑based) and measure variance; manually verify a random subset to detect misclassification.
