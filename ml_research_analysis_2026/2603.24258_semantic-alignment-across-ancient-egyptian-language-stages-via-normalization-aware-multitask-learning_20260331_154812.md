# Semantic Alignment across Ancient Egyptian Language Stages via Normalization-Aware Multitask Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24258  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24258  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Translation supervision yields strongest gains; IPA+KL improves cross-branch alignment; early fusion is ineffective; evaluation via ROC-AUC and triplet accuracy shows limited overall alignment due to script divergence and homographs.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the problem of aligning word-level semantics across four historical stages of Ancient Egyptian—Hieroglyphic, Demotic, Sahidic, and Bohairic—which employ divergent scripts and suffer from scarce parallel data. The authors propose a compact encoder‑decoder model with a shared byte‑level tokenizer that jointly trains on masked language modeling (MLM), translation language modeling (TLM), sequence‑to‑sequence translation, and part‑of‑speech tagging. Two normalization strategies are explored: KL consistency loss between predictions on original and Latin/IPA‑normalized inputs, and early fusion of the two views. Key findings indicate that translation supervision provides the strongest alignment improvements, while KL consistency with IPA normalization enhances cross‑branch alignment; early fusion consistently underperforms. Nevertheless, overall alignment remains modest due to script divergence and homograph ambiguity. The work establishes a baseline for low‑resource historical language alignment and highlights the critical role of normalization design and multitask formulation.

## Method Summary
The study uses four Egyptian corpora: the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae (TLA) for Hieroglyphic/Demotic and the Coptic SCRIPTORIUM for Sahidic/Bohairic, with English translations and UPOS tags where available. A shared byte‑level BPE tokenizer (vocabulary size 32 000, minimum frequency 2) is trained on combined original Egyptian and English texts; English data is restricted to in‑domain parallel texts. The model is a six‑layer Transformer encoder‑decoder (hidden dimension 768, 12 attention heads). Multitask training always includes MLM and adds TLM, sequence‑to‑sequence translation, and POS tagging with fixed prior weights (MLM = 1.0, TLM = 1.0, Translation = 1.0, POS = 0.5). Losses are scaled by learned uncertainty parameters \(\sigma_i\) to automate task weighting. Normalization is integrated in two ways: (A) KL consistency loss (\(\lambda_{KL}=0.5\)) between MLM predictions for original and normalized (Latin/IPA) sequences, or (B) early fusion where normalized embeddings are added with a trainable scalar \(\alpha\). Training runs for 10 epochs with batch size 16, learning rate 5e‑5, on a single GPU. Alignment quality is evaluated using pairwise ROC‑AUC and triplet accuracy on curated Egyptian–English and intra‑Egyptian cognate datasets.

## Key Results
- Translation supervision provides the largest improvement in alignment metrics (ROC‑AUC and triplet accuracy).  
- KL consistency loss with IPA normalization enhances cross‑linguistic alignment, while early fusion consistently underperforms.  
- Overall alignment remains low, particularly for homograph pairs and between Hieroglyphic/Demotic and Coptic branches, due to script divergence and ambiguous orthography.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Dynamic loss weighting via uncertainty scaling enables effective joint training of heterogeneous tasks (MLM, TLM, translation, POS) without manual tuning.  
- **Mechanism:** Each task’s loss is scaled by a learned uncertainty parameter \(\sigma_i\), which automatically adjusts its influence based on task difficulty and homoscedasticity assumptions.  
- **Core assumption:** Task losses are homoscedastic (noise variance is task‑specific but not sequence‑specific), allowing a single \(\sigma_i\) to balance all examples for that task.  
- **Evidence:** The abstract mentions “task‑aware loss with fixed weights and uncertainty‑based scaling”, and the methodology section formalizes the loss function.

## Foundational Learning
### Byte-Level Tokenization for Multiscript Languages
- **Why needed:** To handle the diverse scripts (hieroglyphic, demotic, Coptic) without script‑specific vocabularies and to share subword information across languages.  
- **Quick check:** Does the tokenizer achieve low OOV rates (<1%) on all Egyptian scripts? Check vocabulary coverage on held‑out data.

### Dynamic Uncertainty‑Weighted Multitask Learning
- **Why needed:** To jointly optimize multiple heterogeneous objectives (MLM, TLM, translation, POS) without manual loss weighting, especially under scarce parallel data.  
- **Quick check:** Monitor the learned uncertainty parameters \(\sigma_i\) during training; they should converge to different values, indicating task‑specific scaling. Compare validation performance with fixed vs. dynamic weighting.

### Normalization via KL Consistency Loss
- **Why needed:** To bridge the gap between original Egyptian scripts and their Latin/IPA transliterations, aligning semantically equivalent words across divergent orthographies.  
- **Quick check:** Does adding KL loss improve cross‑lingual alignment metrics compared to MLM alone? Measure ROC‑AUC on cognate pairs.

### Handling Homograph Ambiguity
- **Why needed:** Pre‑Coptic Egyptian words often share orthography with different meanings due to unwritten vowels, causing semantic ambiguity that hinders alignment.  
- **Quick check:** Break down alignment scores by homograph vs. heterograph pairs; expect near‑random scores (<0.5 AUC) on homographs without additional disambiguation.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Shared ByteTokenizer → Encoder (6‑layer Transformer) → Task Heads: MLM Head, TLM Head, Translation Decoder, POS Tagger.  
Normalization module: For Egyptian inputs, generate normalized (Latin/IPA) sequence; apply KL consistency between MLM outputs of original and normalized views (or use early fusion at input level).

### Critical Path
1. Preprocess raw texts and generate normalized versions with `normalization.py`.  
2. Train shared byte BPE tokenizer on combined original Egyptian + English texts.  
3. For each batch: tokenize original and normalized sequences, encode with shared encoder.  
4. Compute task‑specific losses: MLM (original), TLM (parallel pairs), translation loss (encoder‑decoder), POS loss.  
5. If using normalization: add KL consistency loss between MLM predictions of original and normalized inputs.  
6. Combine all losses using fixed prior weights multiplied by learned uncertainty parameters (\(\sigma_i\)).  
7. Backpropagate and update encoder, task heads, and uncertainty parameters.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Shared vs. separate tokenizers:** Shared tokenizer simplifies cross‑lingual transfer but may cause over‑segmentation of normalized forms; separate tokenizers preserve boundaries but increase model size.  
- **KL consistency vs. early fusion:** KL consistency adds a regularization term without altering the forward pass; early fusion interpolates representations at input level but relies on a single scalar \(\alpha\), which proved inflexible.  
- **Dynamic loss weighting:** Uncertainty scaling automates balancing but requires careful initialization and clipping of \(\sigma_i\); fixed weights avoid this but need manual tuning.  
- **Encoder‑decoder capacity:** A compact 6‑layer model reduces compute but may underfit the heterogeneous tasks, especially translation.

### Failure Signatures
- **High token length mismatch** between original and normalized sequences (e.g., >2× difference) leading to positional misalignment in KL loss, degrading cross‑lingual alignment.  
- **Early fusion \(\alpha\) parameter stuck near 0 or 1** without improvement, indicating ineffective interpolation of both views.  
- **Homograph subset performance near random** (<0.5 AUC) even with the full model, revealing unresolved ambiguity.  
- **Dominance of Hieroglyphic language** in validation metrics if data is imbalanced, suggesting bias toward that branch.

### First Experiments
1. **Baseline MLM:** Train the encoder‑decoder with only MLM on original Egyptian texts. Establishes basic language modeling and tokenization quality.  
2. **Multitask without normalization:** Activate TLM, translation, and POS tasks using fixed prior weights and uncertainty scaling. Evaluate alignment improvement over baseline.  
3. **Add normalization (IPA+KL):** Extend multitask by including normalized (IPA) inputs and KL consistency loss (\(\lambda=0.5\)). Compare cross‑lingual alignment (ROC‑AUC, triplet accuracy) against multitask‑only model.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can normalization schemes be refined using more linguistically informed phonological rules to minimize information loss and false similarity while still improving cross‑lingual alignment?  
- **Basis:** The authors explicitly state that the current normalization scheme can be refined with more linguistically informed rules (Section 7) and note risks of information loss, false similarity, oversimplification (Section 8).  
- **Why unresolved:** The heuristic normalization (Latin/IPA) conflates distinct consonants and oversimplifies vowel realization, potentially harming alignment. No systematic evaluation of alternative rules exists.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare alignment performance using normalization based on expert Egyptological phonological models, measuring retention of etymological distinctions and reduction in spurious similarities.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can lemma annotations for pre‑Coptic Egyptian be integrated into the multitask framework to reduce homograph ambiguity and stabilize word‑level alignment?  
- **Basis:** The authors note that lemma information, especially for pre‑Coptic Egyptian, should be incorporated to reduce ambiguity from homographs (Section 7) and identify lack of lemmatization as a limitation (Section 8).  
- **Why unresolved:** Pre‑Coptic Egyptian has many homographs due to unwritten vowels, causing semantic ambiguity. The current model lacks lemma supervision.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Incorporate lemma‑level multitask objectives (e.g., lemmatization as an auxiliary task) and evaluate alignment improvement, especially on homograph pairs.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can the subword fragmentation bottleneck caused by mismatched tokenization of original and normalized views be resolved to enable effective view integration?  
- **Basis:** The authors identify that integrating word‑level normalization with subword‑level architectures introduces positional noise due to sequence length divergence (Section 6.3) and suggest boundary‑preserving normalization (Section 8).  
- **Why unresolved:** The shared BPE tokenizer was trained only on original scripts, causing over‑segmentation of normalized inputs. KL consistency and early fusion rely on position‑wise alignment, which breaks when sequence lengths differ.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement boundary‑preserving normalization (e.g., using original token boundaries for normalized forms) and compare alignment performance; also explore soft alignment methods like dynamic time warping.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent does the English pivot dominate the learned representations, and can direct cross‑branch Egyptian alignment be achieved without English supervision?  
- **Basis:** The authors state that dependence on English as a pivot remains a challenge, and future ablation studies completely removing the English pivot are necessary (Section 8).  
- **Why unresolved:** The model uses English as a semantic bridge via translation and TLM tasks. It is unclear whether intra‑Egyptian alignment emerges from direct transfer or indirectly through English.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train models without any English data (only Egyptian languages) and evaluate intra‑Egyptian alignment; compare to English‑pivoted models to measure direct vs. pivoted transfer.

## Limitations
- Exact dynamic loss weighting implementation (\(\sigma_i\) initialization, updates, clipping) is unspecified, risking reproducibility of multitask balance.  
- Data split strategy and random seed are unreported, undermining confidence in metric stability and generalizability.  
- Tokenizer trained solely on original scripts causes fragmentation mismatch with normalized views, likely degrading KL consistency performance.

## Confidence
- Translation supervision yields strongest gains: **Medium**  
- IPA+KL improves cross‑branch alignment: **Medium**  
- Early fusion is ineffective: **High**  
- Normalization and task design are critical under low‑resource constraints: **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Standardize data splits and seed:** Re-run all experiments with a fixed random seed (e.g., 42) and stratified language‑aware splits to quantify variance in ROC‑AUC/triplet accuracy and ensure fair comparison.  
2. **Measure tokenization disparity impact:** For a stratified sample of Egyptian word pairs, compute subword token length ratios between original and normalized forms; correlate these ratios with alignment score drops to isolate fragmentation as a key bottleneck.  
3. **Test on additional Coptic dialects:** Extend the model to other Coptic dialects (e.g., Fayyumic) using the same normalization framework to evaluate whether IPA+KL gains generalize beyond Sahidic/Bohairic and to identify dialect‑specific homograph challenges.
