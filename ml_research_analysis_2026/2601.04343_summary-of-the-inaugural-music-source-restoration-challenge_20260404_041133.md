# Summary of The Inaugural Music Source Restoration Challenge

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04343
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04343
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Winning system (xlancelab) achieved Multi-Mel-SNR = 4.46 dB and MOS = 3.47, with 91% and 18% relative improvements over the second-place system.

## Executive Summary
The inaugural Music Source Restoration (MSR) Challenge established a benchmark for recovering original, unprocessed instrument stems from mixed and degraded audio. Participants were given 48 kHz stereo, 10‑second clips containing eight stem types and tasked with reversing both production effects (e.g., mixing, compression) and real‑world artifacts (e.g., reverberation, noise). Evaluation combined objective metrics—Multi‑Mel‑SNR (primary), Zimtohrli, FAD‑CLAP—with blind subjective MOS scoring. Five teams competed, and the winning approach used a sequential pipeline of three BSRoformers for separation, dereverberation, and denoising. Results revealed large performance gaps across instruments (bass = 4.59 dB, percussion = 0.29 dB), highlighting the need for source‑specific techniques. The dataset, evaluation protocols, and baselines are publicly available to foster further research.

## Method Summary
The MSR task requires recovering clean, unprocessed stems from mixtures that include both deliberate production effects and uncontrolled degradations. Training data combined MoisesDB with a manually cleaned version of RawStems, covering vocals, guitars, keyboards, bass, synthesizers, drums, percussion, and orchestral parts. The challenge provided a non‑blind test set; primary evaluation used Multi‑Mel‑SNR (higher is better), complemented by Zimtohrli and FAD‑CLAP (lower is better), plus blind MOS ratings for separation, restoration, and overall quality. The winning system, xlancelab, constructed a three‑stage pipeline where each stage employs a BSRoformer: first separating sources, then reducing reverberation, and finally denoising. All stages were trained end‑to‑end with a combined L1 time‑domain loss and multi‑resolution STFT loss. While the overall architecture is disclosed, key hyperparameters and training details remain unspecified.

## Key Results
- **Multi‑Mel‑SNR:** The winner scored 4.46 dB, representing a 91% relative improvement over the runner‑up.
- **MOS‑Overall:** The winner achieved 3.47, an 18% relative improvement.
- **Instrument disparity:** Across all teams, bass averaged 4.59 dB while percussion averaged only 0.29 dB, indicating a severe performance drop for transient‑rich sources.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The sequential BSRoformer pipeline succeeds by decomposing the complex restoration problem into three focused sub‑tasks, each handling a distinct type of degradation. Separation isolates individual sources, enabling subsequent stages to operate without interference. Dereverberation then reduces room effects, and denoising removes residual noise. BSRoformers leverage band‑split transformers that efficiently capture both local and global spectral‑temporal patterns, making them well‑suited for music content. The combination of L1 loss (preserving time‑domain waveform integrity) and multi‑resolution STFT loss (maintaining spectral details at multiple scales) provides a balanced optimization signal that avoids over‑smoothing while respecting phase nuances. This modular yet end‑to‑end trainable design yields strong overall performance despite significant variability among instrument types.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Audio → BSRoformer (Separation) → BSRoformer (Dereverberation) → BSRoformer (Denoising) → Output Stems

**Critical path:** The three BSRoformer stages are applied in sequence; gradients flow end‑to‑end through the entire pipeline, and all stages are trained jointly with the combined loss.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Sequential vs. monolithic:* Decomposition simplifies specialization but may propagate errors from early stages.  
- *Shared vs. separate models:* Using identical architectures for all stages reduces complexity but may not optimally address each degradation.  
- *Loss weighting:* The relative weight of L1 versus STFT components influences time‑domain accuracy versus spectral fidelity; improper weighting can cause phase artifacts.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Multi‑Mel‑SNR on percussion below 0.5 dB.  
- High Zimtohrli scores indicating spectral mismatch.  
- Large gap (>1 point) between non‑blind and blind MOS scores.  
- Training loss plateauing early, suggesting vanishing gradients through the pipeline.

**First experiments:**  
1. Train a single BSRoformer on separation only (baseline) and evaluate Multi‑Mel‑SNR on the test set.  
2. Add the dereverberation stage while freezing the separation model to isolate its incremental benefit.  
3. Fine‑tune the denoising stage with data augmented by synthetic room impulse responses to quantify impact on reverberation removal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What architectural innovations are needed to close the massive performance gap for percussion?** Percussion stems score only 0.29 dB versus bass at 4.59 dB; current transformer‑based models lack explicit mechanisms for transient preservation.  
2. **Which data quality issues in RawStems (misalignment, source leakage) are most detrimental, and can they be automatically corrected?** Only the winning team manually cleaned the dataset, highlighting a scalability bottleneck.  
3. **Under what conditions might adversarial losses benefit MSR?** All top‑ranked systems used reconstruction losses only; teams that tried GANs ranked lower, suggesting adversarial training is unstable or mismatched to the precision required.

## Limitations
- **Implementation specifics:** Exact BSRoformer hyperparameters, training configuration, and inter‑stage gradient flow are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.  
- **Data preparation:** The manual cleaning procedure for RawStems (alignment fixes, leakage mitigation) is unknown, which critically affects dataset quality.  
- **Generalizability:** The pronounced instrument‑wise performance disparity (e.g., percussion vs. bass) indicates that the winning solution may not robustly handle all source types.
