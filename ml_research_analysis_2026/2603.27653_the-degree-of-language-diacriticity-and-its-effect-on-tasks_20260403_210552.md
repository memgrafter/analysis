# The Degree of Language Diacriticity and Its Effect on Tasks
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.27653
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27653
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Information-theoretic metrics for diacritic complexity strongly predict diacritization accuracy; structural complexity (DSS) dominates for multi-diacritic scripts.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a cross-linguistic framework to quantify diacritic usage and its impact on language technology. They propose three information-theoretic metrics—Rune Surprisal, Diacritic Token Surprisal, and Diacritic Structural Surprisal—to measure corpus-level diacritic complexity across 24 corpora in 15 languages. Evaluating BERT- and RNN-based diacritization models, they find that higher diacritic complexity correlates strongly with lower restoration accuracy. Notably, for scripts allowing multiple simultaneous diacritics (e.g., Hebrew, Arabic), structural complexity (DSS) is the strongest predictor, outperforming frequency-based metrics. Cross-corpus tests show only modest performance drops (~1.5% rune-level, ~3% word-level), suggesting diacritic patterns are stable within a language across domains.

## Method Summary
The authors collected 24 fully diacritized corpora covering 15 languages from sources like Universal Dependencies and language-specific datasets. Each corpus was normalized to Unicode decomposition (NFD) to handle combining marks uniformly and sampled to approximately 300,000 characters. For each corpus, they computed four complexity metrics: Rune Surprisal (RS), Diacritic Token Surprisal (DTS), Diacritic Structural Surprisal (DSS), and Diacritic Density. Two diacritization models were trained per corpus: a BERT-based character tagger and an RNN-based tagger (Latin scripts only). Models were evaluated on held-out test sets, yielding rune-level and word-level accuracy. Pearson correlations were calculated between corpus metrics and accuracy. Additionally, cross-corpus evaluations were conducted for languages with multiple corpora to assess generalization.

## Key Results
- Diacritic complexity metrics (RS, DTS, DSS) are strongly negatively correlated with diacritization accuracy across all languages (Pearson r < -0.6, p < 0.001); e.g., DSS vs. BERT-word accuracy: r = -0.98.
- For multi-diacritic scripts (Arabic, Bengali, Hebrew, Vietnamese), Diacritic Structural Surprisal (DSS) exhibits the strongest correlation with performance (r ≈ -0.95), while frequency-based measures are notably weaker.
- Cross-corpus generalization yields small performance declines (~1.5% rune-level, ~3% word-level), indicating that diacritic distributions are largely stable across text domains within a language.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Higher corpus-level diacritic complexity is associated with lower diacritization accuracy.
- **Mechanism**: Complex corpora have higher entropy in the mapping from base characters to diacritized forms, meaning many low-frequency or ambiguous mappings. Models must learn these uncertain patterns, leading to more errors.
- **Evidence anchors**: Uniformly negative Pearson correlations (e.g., DSS vs. BERT-word: r = -0.98, p<0.001); languages like Arabic and Bengali (high complexity) achieve 35-60% word accuracy, while German and Spanish (low complexity) achieve 91-97%.
- **Break condition**: Correlation becomes non-significant (|r| < 0.3, p > 0.05) after controlling for training data size and model capacity.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: In multi-diacritic scripts, Diacritic Structural Surprisal (DSS) is the strongest predictor of model performance, surpassing frequency-based metrics.
- **Mechanism**: DSS captures the combinatorial diversity of diacritic sets per base character, which expands the label space independently of token frequency. This structural burden is not fully reflected in token-based surprisal.
- **Evidence anchors**: For the six multi-diacritic corpora, DSS correlations with BERT accuracy are near-perfect (r ≈ -0.95), while RS correlations are weaker (r ≈ -0.59). The moderate correlation between DTS and DSS (r ~ 0.75) indicates they capture different aspects.
- **Break condition**: Removing DSS from a multivariate model does not significantly reduce explained variance in accuracy.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Diacritic usage patterns are stable within a language, so models generalize across domains with only modest performance drops.
- **Mechanism**: The conditional distribution P(diacritics | base_char, language) is linguistically determined and robust to domain shifts; core orthographic conventions and frequent combinations are shared across genres.
- **Evidence anchors**: Cross-corpus evaluation across six language pairs shows average drops of ~1.5% (rune) and ~3% (word), considered small relative to the 300K character sample size and vocabulary changes.
- **Break condition**: A cross-corpus drop exceeding 10% at the rune level would indicate domain-specific diacritic regimes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Information-Theoretic Metrics (Surprisal)**
  - Why needed: The metrics (RS, DTS, DSS) are the primary tools for quantifying diacritic complexity. Understanding negative log-probability is key to interpreting how unpredictability affects model performance.
  - Quick check: Explain the difference between Diacritic Token Surprisal (DTS) and Diacritic Structural Surprisal (DSS) for a base character that can carry two diacritics simultaneously.
- **Concept: Single- vs. Multi-Diacritic Scripts**
  - Why needed: The distinction determines which complexity metrics are most predictive; multi-diacritic scripts exhibit combinatorial explosion in possible runes.
  - Quick check: Is Vietnamese a single-diacritic or multi-diacritic script according to the paper? What about Spanish? Justify based on the maximum number of diacritics that can appear on a single vowel.
- **Concept: Sequence Labeling for Diacritization**
  - Why needed: The task is formulated as per-character tagging; this design makes character-level complexity directly relevant to model predictions.
  - Quick check: Why is word-level accuracy stricter than rune-level? What error pattern yields high rune-level but low word-level accuracy?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Raw text → Unicode normalization → segmentation into base character + diacritic set → 300K character sampling → metric computation (RS/DTS/DSS/Density) → BERT/RNN model training → evaluation (rune/word accuracy) → correlation analysis.
- **Critical path**: Metric computation must precede model training, as the metrics define the complexity profile correlated with accuracy.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - BERT vs. RNN: BERT handles multi-diacritic scripts via adapted tokenization but is computationally heavier; RNN is lighter but restricted to precomposed Latin scripts.
  - Metric choice: DSS is more informative for multi-diacritic scripts but requires type-counting and is sensitive to corpus size; RS and DTS are token-based and more stable with less data.
  - Corpus sampling: Fixed 300K characters ensures cross-corpus comparability but may under-represent rare runes in corpora with large type spaces (e.g., Bengali).
- **Failure signatures**:
  - High Rune Surprisal + Low DSS: Indicates a skewed distribution where few diacritic forms dominate; model may overfit to the mode and fail on rare forms.
  - High DSS + Low Density: Suggests many structurally possible combinations exist but are rarely used; model suffers from combinatorial sparsity.
  - Low cross-corpus drop: Expected; a high drop would signal domain-specific diacritic conventions not captured in training.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Metric Sanity Check: On a small Spanish corpus (e.g., AnCora sample), manually compute P(r|c) for base 'a' and verify that the computed RS matches the expected value for a similar-density language in Table 3.
  2. Model Replication: Train the BERT diacritizer on one Latin script corpus (e.g., German UD) and one multi-diacritic corpus (e.g., Hebrew). Confirm the performance gap (Hebrew ~20-30% lower word accuracy) aligns with Table 3.
  3. Script-Type Isolation: Correlate RS and DSS against BERT word accuracy separately for all single-diacritic languages and then for multi-diacritic ones. Verify that DSS correlation strengthens in the multi-diacritic subset (note the small sample size).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does the functional role of diacritics (phonological, morphological, tonal) correlate with diacritization accuracy, and can it explain residual variance beyond the proposed information-theoretic metrics?
2. Does diacritic complexity impact downstream NLP tasks such as machine translation or question answering?
3. Why do BERT models show a strong correlation with structural complexity (DSS) on multi-diacritic scripts, whereas RNNs are sensitive to complexity even on single-diacritic scripts?

## Limitations
- Replication details are underspecified: hyperparameters for BERT/RNN training and the exact 300K character sampling protocol are not disclosed.
- Potential confounding: Correlations may be driven by corpus size or model capacity; the study does not perform multivariate control for these factors.
- Small sample size for multi-diacritic scripts: Only four languages (six corpora) support the DSS claim, limiting statistical power.
- Cross-corpus results lack per-pair scores, masking possible variations in domain shift effects.

## Confidence
- **Higher diacritic complexity → lower accuracy**: High
- **DSS is strongest predictor for multi-diacritic scripts**: Medium
- **Stable diacritic patterns → modest cross-corpus drop**: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Multivariate correlation**: Replicate with fixed BERT hyperparameters (e.g., LR=3e-5, 3 epochs) and include corpus size and model dimensions as covariates; test whether complexity metrics remain significant predictors.
2. **Ablation on multi-diacritic**: For Arabic, Hebrew, Bengali, and Vietnamese, fit linear models using (a) RS + DTS, (b) DSS, and (c) all three. Conduct likelihood-ratio tests to determine if DSS adds unique explanatory power (ΔR², p<0.05).
3. **Cross-domain probe**: Evaluate news-trained diacritizers on social media, scientific, and literary test sets for at least three languages. If rune-level drop exceeds 5%, perform error analysis to characterize domain-specific diacritic shifts.
