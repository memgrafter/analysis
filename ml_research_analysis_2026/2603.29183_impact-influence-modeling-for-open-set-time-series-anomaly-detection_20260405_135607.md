# IMPACT: Influence Modeling for Open-Set Time Series Anomaly Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.29183  
- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29183  
- **References**: 40  
- **Primary result**: IMPACT outperforms state-of-the-art methods by 6.37%-44.52% AUC in general OSAD setting and 10.52%-42.83% in hard setting across eight datasets.

## Executive Summary
Open-set time series anomaly detection (OSAD) deals with identifying anomalies in test data that may belong to classes unseen during training, while often suffering from contamination (unlabeled anomalies in training) and ineffective synthetic anomaly generation. IMPACT introduces influence modeling to address these challenges. It learns an influence function to estimate each training sample’s impact on test risk, then uses these scores to perform risk‑reduction‑based decontamination (flipping labels of harmful samples) and to generate realistic pseudo anomalies by perturbing low‑influence normal samples in feature space. Extensive evaluation on eight datasets under varied OSAD settings and contamination rates shows IMPACT consistently achieves substantial AUC gains over previous state‑of‑the‑art methods, demonstrating robust generalization to unseen anomalies.

## Method Summary
IMPACT proceeds in three phases. First, *Test‑risk‑driven Influence Scoring (TIS)*: an initial model (a TCN feature extractor with a multi‑channel deviation head) is trained on the full (possibly contaminated) dataset. Influence scores for all training samples are computed using a held‑out validation set, based on the gradient of the validation loss with respect to model parameters and the Hessian of the training loss. Second, *Risk‑reduction‑based Anomaly Decontamination and Generation (RADG)*: samples with positive influence are relabeled as anomalies to remove contamination; from the remaining normal samples, those with the most negative influence become reference normals, while those with slightly less negative influence are perturbed in feature space along the influence direction to produce pseudo anomalies that simulate unseen patterns. Third, *Final Training*: a dual‑head model (one “seen” head, one “unseen” head) is trained on the combined dataset, with a loss that balances discrimination of known anomalies and learning from generated pseudo anomalies. At inference, the anomaly score combines max‑pooled outputs from both heads and feature deviation from the reference normal set.

## Key Results
- **AUC improvements**: +6.37% to +44.52% over SOTA in general OSAD; +10.52% to +42.83% in hard OSAD.  
- **Robustness**: Gains hold across eight diverse datasets and under contamination rates up to 20%.  
- **Ablation evidence**: Removing decontamination or pseudo‑anomaly generation causes significant performance drops, confirming both components are essential.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Influence‑based sample utility estimation
- **Claim**: IMPACT uses influence functions to estimate each training sample’s impact on test risk, enabling identification of contaminated samples and helpful normal references.  
- **Mechanism**: Influence scores are computed as \(IL(z_i) = \sum_{z_t \in V} -\nabla_\theta L(z_t)^\top H^{-1} \nabla_\theta L(z_i)\), where \(\nabla\) denotes loss gradients and \(H\) the Hessian of the total training loss. Positive scores indicate samples that increase test risk (likely harmful), while negative scores indicate helpful ones.  
- **Core assumption**: The model is twice differentiable, the Hessian is invertible, and the first‑order Taylor expansion accurately captures the effect of small parameter perturbations.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Derivation in Section 3.1 (Equations 4–7); abstract mentions influence function for test risk estimation.  
- **Break condition**: If the Hessian is ill‑conditioned or the model highly non‑convex, influence approximations become unreliable, leading to misclassification of sample utility.

### Mechanism 2: Decontamination via label flipping
- **Claim**: Flipping the labels of high‑influence contaminated samples (positive influence) reduces test risk and converts harmful contamination into valuable supervision.  
- **Mechanism**: Theorem 2 shows that relabeling a contaminated sample changes the test risk by approximately \(-2 \times\) its influence (scaled). Since contaminated samples have positive influence, flipping lowers the risk.  
- **Core assumption**: Contaminated samples are truly abnormal but mislabeled as normal; influence scores correctly identify them.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states “repurposing high‑influential samples as supervised anomalies for anomaly decontamination”; Section 3.2 presents Theorem 2 proving risk reduction.  
- **Break condition**: False positives (normal samples with positive influence) would introduce harmful noise and increase test risk.

### Mechanism 3: Pseudo‑anomaly generation via feature perturbation
- **Claim**: Perturbing low‑influence normal samples in feature space along the influence direction generates pseudo anomalies that simulate unseen patterns and reduce test risk.  
- **Mechanism**: For normal samples with low (negative) influence, the influence vector \(I_{per}(w_i)\) points in the direction that maximally increases test risk. Adding \(\alpha I_{per}(w_i)^\top\) to the feature produces a perturbed feature treated as a pseudo anomaly.  
- **Core assumption**: Low‑influence normals lie near the decision boundary; perturbations push them into the anomaly manifold while preserving realism.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract describes “perturb low‑influenced normal samples in feature space to create realistic pseudo anomalies”; Section 3.2 includes Theorems 3 (distributional difference) and 4 (risk reduction).  
- **Break condition**: If \(\alpha\) is too large, perturbed features become unrealistic outliers; if too small, they fail to diverge meaningfully from normal patterns.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: TIS Module -> RADG Module -> Final Training  
- **Critical path**:
  1. Train initial model \(f = h \circ \phi\) on full training set \(D\) (contaminated normals + seen anomalies).  
  2. For each sample \(z_i \in D_n\), compute influence score \(IL(z_i) = \sum_{z_t \in V} -\nabla_\theta L(z_t)^\top H^{-1} \nabla_\theta L(z_i)\) using validation set \(V\).  
  3. Partition normals: \(D_{\text{con}} = \{IL>0\}\) (contaminated), \(D_{\text{ref}} =\) top‑\(k\) smallest negative (helpful references), \(D_{\text{per}} =\) top‑\(k\) largest negative but not in \(D_{\text{ref}}\) (to perturb), and \(D_{\text{clean}} =\) remaining normals + seen anomalies.  
  4. Flip labels of \(D_{\text{con}}\) → \(D'_{\text{con}}\) (new supervised anomalies).  
  5. For each \((x_i,0) \in D_{\text{per}}\): extract feature \(\phi_i = \phi(x_i)\), compute influence direction \(I_{per}(w_i)\), perturb to \(\phi_i^\zeta = \phi_i + \alpha I_{per}(w_i)^\top\), add \((\phi_i^\zeta, 1)\) to \(W'_{\text{per}}\).  
  6. Retrain model with dual heads \(h\) and \(h'\) on combined loss \(L_{\text{re}} = L_{\text{seen}} + \lambda L_{\text{unseen}}\), where \(L_{\text{seen}}\) uses dataset \(D_s = D'_{\text{con}} \cup D_{\text{clean}}\) and head \(h\); \(L_{\text{unseen}}\) uses \(D_h = (D_{\text{clean}} \setminus D_{\text{per}}) \cup W'_{\text{per}}\) and head \(h'\).  
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Validation set size: Larger \(V\) improves influence reliability but reduces training data; default 20% hold‑out.  
  - Hyperparameters: \(k\) (reference/perturb count) balances diversity vs. noise; \(\alpha\) (perturbation strength) controls realism; \(\lambda\) balances seen/unseen losses.  
  - Computational cost: Per‑sample gradient and Hessian inversion; for large \(N\), approximations (e.g., Woodbury) may be needed.  
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Unreliable initial model: Severe contamination biases the model, causing mis‑estimated influence scores.  
  - Non‑representative validation set: If \(V\) does not match test distribution, influence estimates become biased.  
  - Poor boundary identification: If \(D_{\text{per}}\) samples are not near the true decision boundary, generated pseudo anomalies may be trivial or unrealistic.  
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Main results replication**: Run IMPACT on UCR under general setting (10 labeled anomalies/class, 2% contamination). Expect AUC ~59%, outperforming baselines like DevNet (~50%) and WSAD‑DT (~78% on CT but lower elsewhere) per Table 1.  
  2. **Label flipping ablation**: Remove \(D'_{\text{con}}\) from final training. Expect significant drop, e.g., on TUSZ from 82.91% to 70.91% (Table 3, w/o \(D'_{\text{con}}\)), confirming decontamination necessity.  
  3. **Pseudo‑anomaly generation ablation**: Remove \(L_{\text{unseen}}\) or \(W'_{\text{per}}\). In hard setting, expect large drop (e.g., TUSZ from 75.70% to 60.92% in Table 3, w/o \(L_{\text{unseen}}\)), validating the generation mechanism.

## Limitations
- **Spec ambiguities**: The OSAD split protocol (which classes are seen/unseen, number of seen classes in general setting) and practical details of influence computation (Hessian approximation, damping) are not fully described, hindering faithful replication.  
- **Theoretical assumptions**: The reliability of influence functions relies on twice‑differentiable models and an invertible Hessian, assumptions that may not hold for deep temporal convolutional networks, questioning the accuracy of influence estimates.  
- **Computational cost**: Full‑batch Hessian inversion is expensive; while approximations are suggested, the paper does not specify a scalable implementation, which could limit applicability to large datasets.
