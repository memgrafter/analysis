# Mirror Descent on Riemannian Manifolds

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.17527
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17527
*   **Reference Count:** 31
*   **Primary Result:** Proposes Riemannian Mirror Descent (RMD) using local reparameterization; yields O(1/T) deterministic and O(1/T^{1/2}) stochastic nonconvex rates, and recovers Curvilinear Gradient Descent on the Stiefel manifold.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Riemannian Mirror Descent (RMD), a framework that extends classical mirror descent to Riemannian manifolds. Instead of relying on a global dual map, RMD employs a sequence of local diffeomorphisms to reparameterize the problem into a tangent-space-like domain where standard gradient steps are taken and mapped back. This approach yields a general algorithm, Riemannian Mirror Descent (RMD), and its stochastic counterpart (SRMD) with non-asymptotic convergence guarantees for both geodesically convex and smooth nonconvex objectives. On the Stiefel manifold, the method specializes to Curvilinear Gradient Descent (CGD) and its stochastic variant (SCGD), with empirical results demonstrating significant runtime improvements for large-scale problems.

## Method Summary
RMD operates by iteratively constructing a local diffeomorphism φₜ around the current point xₜ that maps a geodesic ball to a reparameterized space. A gradient step is performed in this space using a retraction Rₜ (an approximation to the exponential map), yielding a point yₜ₊₁. The next iterate is obtained by applying the inverse φₜ⁻¹. The stochastic version (SRMD) replaces the exact gradient with an unbiased stochastic estimator. For the Stiefel manifold, choosing φₜ as a Cayley-based map and using the standard retraction recovers CGD/SCGD. A minimal reproduction involves implementing the Cayley-based SCGD (Algorithm 2), verifying convergence on a geodesically convex problem (e.g., a linear eigenvalue maximization), and comparing its runtime against deterministic CGD for increasing problem dimensions.

## Key Results
*   Deterministic RMD achieves O(1/T) objective error decay for geodesically convex functions and O(1/T) average squared gradient norm decay for smooth nonconvex functions.
*   Stochastic SRMD attains O(1/T^{1/3}) objective error decay for geodesically convex functions and O(1/T^{1/2}) average squared gradient norm decay for smooth nonconvex functions.
*   On the Stiefel manifold, RMD specializes to Curvilinear Gradient Descent (CGD); SRMD with block-wise updates yields Stochastic CGD (SCGD), which empirically reduces runtime versus CGD with comparable final error.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Local reparameterization via diffeomorphisms
RMD generalizes Euclidean mirror descent by replacing the global map ∇ψ with a local diffeomorphism φₜ. At iteration t, φₜ maps a neighborhood of xₜ to a reparameterized manifold Mₜ (e.g., a tangent space). The update performs gradient descent on Mₜ via a retraction Rₜ and maps back using φₜ⁻¹. This interprets mirror descent as gradient descent under a changing coordinate system. The core assumption is that φₜ is a local diffeomorphism and the composite map \hat{R}ₜ(x)(u) = φₜ⁻¹(Rₜ^{φₜ(x)}(Dφₜ(x)[u])) is a retraction that is LΦ-regular (summarizing approximation error). If φₜ is not a diffeomorphism or \hat{R}ₜ fails to be a retraction/LΦ-regular, the one-step progress bound collapses, preventing convergence guarantees.

### Mechanism 2: Retraction-based approximation of exponential map
Using a retraction R instead of the computationally expensive exponential map Exp makes iterations feasible while preserving guarantees under LΦ-regularity. A retraction Rₓ(v) approximates Expₓ(v) with first-order correctness. The LΦ-regularity condition bounds the deviation: ‖Expₓ⁻¹(Rₓ(u)) - u‖ ≤ (LΦ/2)‖u‖². This quadratic bound is crucial for the one-step decrease analysis. If LΦ is large or infinite, the bound becomes vacuous unless the step size η is made very small, and the analysis may fail.

### Mechanism 3: Convergence via one-step progress and potential function
Non-asymptotic rates are established by bounding the per-iteration decrease (Lemma 3/4) and telescoping using a weighted sum potential (for convex cases). For deterministic RMD, Lemma 3 gives f(xₜ₊₁)-f(xₜ) ≤ -Cₐ‖∇f(xₜ)‖² + higher-order terms, where Cₐ depends on η, LΦ, Lf, G. Summation yields an average gradient bound. For geodesic convexity, a potential Eₜ = Aₜ(f(xₜ)-f(x*)) with Aₜ = t² is constructed; geodesic convexity provides ⟨∇f(xₜ), Exp⁻¹(x*)⟩ ≥ f(xₜ)-f(x*), enabling telescoping to O(1/T). If f lacks Lf-smoothness, bounded gradients, or geodesic convexity (for the faster convex rate), the constants become infinite or the telescoping argument fails.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Riemannian gradient and exponential map
**Why needed:** RMD operates on manifolds where gradients live in tangent spaces and updates follow geodesics. The Riemannian gradient ∇f(x) is the unique tangent vector satisfying ⟨∇f(x), u⟩ = Df(x)[u] for all u in TₓM. The exponential map Expₓ(v) parameterizes geodesics from x with initial velocity v.
**Quick check:** For f: M→ℝ differentiable, how is the Riemannian gradient ∇f(x) defined in terms of the differential Df(x)?

### Concept: Mirror descent in Euclidean space
**Why needed:** RMD is a generalization; the Euclidean version (using a Bregman divergence generated by a convex potential ψ) reveals the reparameterization insight: the update uses ∇ψ as a change of variables from primal to dual space.
**Quick check:** In Euclidean MD with potential ψ, write the implicit dual update and explain how ∇ψ serves as a change of variables.

### Concept: Geodesic convexity and smoothness
**Why needed:** Convergence rates for RMD depend on manifold-adapted properties; Euclidean convexity/smoothness are insufficient on curved spaces. A function is geodesically convex if its composition with any geodesic is convex in the geodesic parameter. It is Lf-smooth if ‖∇f(x) - Γ_{yx}(∇f(y))‖ ≤ Lf d(x,y), where Γ is parallel transport.
**Quick check:** State the definition of a geodesically convex function on a Riemannian manifold.

## Architecture Onboarding
Inputs: Riemannian manifold (M,g), smooth objective f, initial point x₀ ∈ M, step sizes {ηₜ}, local diffeomorphisms φₜ, retractions Rₜ.
Core loop (deterministic RMD): yₜ₊₁ = Rₜ^{φₜ(xₜ)}(-ηₜ Dφₜ(xₜ)[∇f(xₜ)]); xₜ₊₁ = φₜ⁻¹(yₜ₊₁). Stochastic variant (SRMD) replaces ∇f(xₜ) with unbiased stochastic gradient ∇f(xₜ, ξₜ). On Stiefel, φₜ uses Cayley transform, Rₜ is addition in skew-symmetric space, recovering CGD/SCGD.
Outputs: Final iterate x_T (deterministic) or averaged iterates (stochastic).
Component map: Inputs -> Choose φₜ & Rₜ -> Compute dual step -> Apply inverse φₜ -> xₜ₊₁ -> (repeat) -> Output.

Critical path:
1.  **Parameterization design:** Pick φₜ (e.g., Exp⁻¹ or Cayley) that is a local diffeomorphism near xₜ. Compute Jacobian Dφₜ(xₜ).
2.  **Retraction selection:** Choose Rₜ such that the composite \hat{R}ₜ is a retraction and LΦ-regular. Verify LΦ is finite in the relevant radius.
3.  **Step size tuning:** Ensure η satisfies η < min{1/(LΦG/2 + Lf + Lf LΦ²), 2/G, r/G} (deterministic) or η < 1/C_sd (stochastic) where constants derive from smoothness, gradient bound, and LΦ. Use line search or diminishing η if constants unknown.
4.  **Feasibility check:** Verify all iterates remain in a compact geodesically convex set A where Lf-smoothness holds (and geodesic convexity holds for the O(1/T) convex rate).
5.  **Stochastic implementation:** For SCGD on Stiefel, partition rows into K blocks, compute block-wise Ŵ from stochastic gradient, and update in parallel.

Design tradeoffs:
*   **Global vs local φ:** Global φ (like ∇ψ in Euclidean MD) is convenient but rarely exists on curved manifolds; local φ requires choosing a radius rₜ and may need reinitialization if the iterate leaves the domain.
*   **Retraction choice:** Exponential map gives exact geodesics but is often costly; Cayley/QR retractions are closed-form and cheap but require verifying LΦ-regularity, which directly impacts allowed step sizes.
*   **Computational cost:** On Stiefel, naive CGD inverts an n×n matrix per iteration; SCGD breaks this into K smaller m×m inverses, trading off K against communication overhead.
*   **Convergence guarantees:** The O(1/T) convex rate explicitly requires f to be geodesically convex on a set containing all iterates. Without this, only the nonconvex O(1/√T) stochastic or O(1/T) deterministic rates apply.

Failure signatures:
*   **Singular φₜ:** Jacobian Dφₜ(xₜ) becomes non-invertible → xₜ₊₁ undefined. Monitor condition number of Dφₜ(xₜ).
*   **LΦ violation:** Retraction deviates too far from Exp (e.g., in high curvature regions) → one-step progress becomes positive → f may increase. Numerically check ‖Expₓ⁻¹(Rₓ(u)) - u‖ vs ‖u‖².
*   **Step size too large:** η exceeds theoretical thresholds → C_d ≤ 0 in one-step inequality → f(xₜ₊₁) > f(xₜ). Start with η ≈ min(1/Lf, 1/G) and reduce if divergence observed.
*   **Iterates escaping A:** For convex O(1/T) rate, all xₜ must lie in a geodesically convex set A. Track d_M(xₜ, x₀); if it grows unbounded, the convexity assumption may fail.
*   **Stochastic variance blowup:** In SRMD, large variance σ² makes the O(1/√T) rate constant large. Consider variance reduction techniques (not covered in the paper).

First three experiments:
1.  **Linear eigenvalue problem on Stiefel:** Maximize Tr(XᵀAX) over St(n,p). Use SCGD with block size m≈300. Verify X recovers top p eigenvectors (via eigenvalue gap). Compare wall-clock time vs deterministic CGD; expect speedup for large p due to smaller matrix inversions.
2.  **Orthogonal Procrustes:** Minimize ‖AX-B‖_F² over St(n,p) with known optimal X*. Check objective decay to near-zero and final orthogonality error ‖XᵀX - Iₚ‖ small. Compare runtime; SCGD should scale better when p is large.
3.  **Nonconvex sphere example:** Minimize f(x)=‖x - v‖² on S^{d-1} for v not on the sphere. Verify average squared gradient norm decays as O(1/T) for deterministic RMD using geodesic gradient descent (Exponential map). This tests the deterministic nonconvex rate from Theorem 1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Can global mirror maps for RMD be constructed on manifolds with extra structure (e.g., Hessian manifolds) to improve rates?** The conclusion identifies the need for explicit problem-adapted reparameterizations and mentions Hessian manifolds. It remains unresolved because RMD only uses local diffeomorphisms; global constructions may require new geometric insights. Evidence would be proving existence of a global mirror map for a relevant class, deriving faster convergence bounds, and showing experimental improvement.
*   **What are the convergence guarantees for Stochastic Curvilinear Gradient Descent (SCGD) with block-diagonal approximation?** SCGD is proposed and tested empirically but lacks theoretical analysis. The block approximation biases the gradient; standard RMD analysis requires unbiasedness. Evidence would be non-asymptotic bounds (e.g., O(1/√T) average gradient norm) with explicit dependence on block size, verified by experiments.
*   **Can RMD handle composite objectives (smooth f + nonsmooth g) via a Riemannian proximal operator while preserving rates?** The paper treats only smooth f. Composite objectives are common in Euclidean MD but absent here. It is unresolved because adding a nonsmooth term would require solving a manifold proximal subproblem, potentially breaking the reparameterization equivalence. Evidence would be a Riemannian composite MD algorithm with convergence proofs for convex/nonconvex cases and validation on a composite problem (e.g., sparse PCA on Stiefel).

## Limitations
*   The theoretical step size bounds depend on constants (Lf, G, LΦ) that are not concretely provided for the Stiefel manifold, forcing heuristic tuning in practice.
*   The O(1/T) convex rate relies on the strong assumption that f is geodesically convex on a compact set containing all iterates, which may not hold in many applications.
*   The stochastic analysis yields a weaker O(1/T^{1/3}) convex rate compared to the standard Euclidean MD O(1/T) rate, and assumes bounded variance and independence.
