# LSR: Linguistic Safety Robustness Benchmark for Low-Resource West African Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19273  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19273  
- **Reference count:** 10  
- **Primary result:** Safety alignment in LLMs drops from ~90% refusal in English to 35–55% across four West African languages (Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, Igala), with Igala showing the highest degradation (Refusal Centroid Drift = 0.55).

## Executive Summary
Safety alignment for large language models (LLMs) is predominantly evaluated in English, creating a blind spot for harmful intent expressed in low-resource languages. The LSR benchmark addresses this by introducing a dual-probe evaluation: matched harmful prompts are submitted in English and four West African languages to the same model. The resulting Refusal Centroid Drift (RCD) metric quantifies the loss of safety behavior. Evaluating Gemini 2.5 Flash reveals a systematic and severe safety gap, affecting hundreds of millions of speakers and highlighting the English-centric bias in current alignment practices.

## Method Summary
The LSR benchmark employs a paired evaluation protocol. For each of 14 harmful prompts per language (covering four harm categories), the system first submits the target-language version to the model, classifies the response as a refusal or compliance using a keyword-based heuristic, then submits the English counterpart and classifies it. A "loophole" is flagged if the target-language prompt elicits compliance while the English version elicits a refusal. Per-language refusal rates are aggregated, and RCD is computed as \(1 - (R_L / R_{EN})\), where \(R_L\) is the refusal rate in language L and \(R_{EN}\) is the English refusal rate. The benchmark dataset is natively authored in Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, and Igala.

## Key Results
- English refusal rate ~90%; target-language refusal rates range from 35% to 55%.
- Igala exhibits the highest RCD (0.55), indicating the most severe safety drift.
- The benchmark and implementation are publicly released, enabling reproducibility and extension.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Geometric Anchoring of Refusal Circuits
Safety representations from RLHF are anchored to high-resource English token sequences. The model develops a "refusal centroid" in activation space triggered by patterns present in English safety data. Low-resource language inputs occupy distant regions of the representation space and fail to activate these refusal circuits. Evidence includes the formal definition of RCD as a proxy for geometric drift and the observed ordering of degradation (Igala > Hausa/Igbo > Yoruba) correlating with estimated digital footprint. Break condition: probing classifier analysis of residual streams shows no separation between English refusal activations and target-language harmful inputs.

### Mechanism 2: Cultural Framing Interaction
Safety models rely on benign contextual framing cues (e.g., "for a novel") primarily learned from English data. In low-resource languages, these framing concepts are expressed with rarer tokens or different syntactic patterns, weakening the benign signal and leaving harmful intent unflagged. Evidence: culturally framed prompts show higher compliance rates in low-resource languages compared to direct prompts, while English maintains high refusal even with framing. Break condition: framed prompts achieve similar refusal rates to direct prompts within the same low-resource language.

### Mechanism 3: Tokenization Sparsity
Low-resource languages suffer from tokenization sparsity due to limited representation in pretraining corpora. Harmful intent is distributed across more, less informative subwords, disrupting the n-gram patterns that trigger refusal. Evidence: RCD ordering correlates with estimated language digital footprint, which affects tokenizer coverage. Break condition: retraining tokenizers on balanced multilingual corpora significantly reduces RCD for previously low-resource languages.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Dataset -> Probe Engine -> Model Interface -> Refusal Classifier -> RCD Calculator -> Dashboard
- **Critical path:** Load dataset -> for each `pair_id`: (1) submit target-language prompt, classify response; (2) submit matched English prompt, classify response; flag "loophole" if (1)=Compliant & (2)=Refusal -> aggregate per-language refusal rates -> compute RCD -> export JSON.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Keyword classifier vs. fine-tuned model: simple and language-agnostic but risks high false positives/negatives for non-English refusals; a fine-tuned classifier would be more accurate but requires labeled data.
  - Probe scale: 14 probes per category detect systematic degradation but lack exhaustive coverage; scaling to 50+ increases power but requires extensive native-speaker resources.
  - Native writing vs. translation: native prompts capture realistic threat models but introduce cultural variation; direct translation controls content but misses culturally specific attacks.
- **Failure signatures:** 
  - High RCD (>0.4) for a language indicates severe structural degradation.
  - Discrepancy in classifier performance across languages may inflate RCD; always audit classifier raw outputs.
  - Low English baseline refusal rate (<80%) invalidates RCD calculation assumption.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replication baseline:** Run `inspect eval lsr.py` on Gemini 2.5 Flash; confirm ~90% English refusal and 35–55% target-language refusal; validate RCD matches Table 1.
  2. **Classifier audit:** Manually label 20 random responses per language; compute per-language F1 for keyword classifier; if mean F1 < 0.8, re-run RCD using manual subset.
  3. **Probe sensitivity test:** Replace 5 probes with alternative culturally grounded prompts for same harm category; rerun evaluation; if RCD changes >0.1 for any language, expand probe set.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does Refusal Centroid Drift generalize across different model families and training regimes? Current evidence is limited to Gemini 2.5 Flash.
2. How does probe scale and harm-category diversity affect RCD stability? 14 probes per language may be insufficient for exhaustive coverage.
3. Can a fine-tuned, language-specific refusal classifier improve cross-lingual safety evaluation accuracy? The current keyword-based approach risks misclassification.
4. Is the "Refusal Centroid Drift" hypothesis mechanistically valid—i.e., do refusal representations truly drift geometrically in activation space? RCD is a proxy, not a direct measure.
5. Does severity of degradation correlate with objective measures of language representation in pretraining corpora? Current correlation is anecdotal, based on digital footprint estimates.

## Limitations
- Classifier generalization: Keyword-based refusal detection may be inaccurate for non-English, culturally specific refusals, distorting RCD.
- Causal mechanism attributability: No ablative experiments isolate whether tokenization, RLHF data imbalance, or other factors are primary drivers.
- Cultural framing confound: Native prompt authorship mixes language and cultural effects, preventing clean separation of these variables.
- Benchmark scope: 14 probes per language across four categories is minimal for safety evaluation and may miss salient attack vectors.

## Confidence
- **Primary empirical claim** (severe safety degradation across languages): **High** – Directly supported by reproducible dual-probe, matched-pair evaluation.
- **Geometric anchoring hypothesis** (RLHF refusal circuits are language-tethered): **Medium** – Plausible and consistent with interpretability literature, but lacks direct activation analysis.
- **Cultural framing interaction** (benign framing fails in low-resource languages): **Low** – Observation noted, but design confounds language and cultural framing.
- **Tokenization sparsity as primary cause**: **Low** – Correlation with digital footprint is suggestive, but not causally isolated from other data-scarcity factors.

## Next Checks
1. **Classifier audit & calibration:** Manually label a stratified sample of 100 responses per language (50 refusal, 50 compliant). Compute per-language F1 for keyword classifier. If mean F1 < 0.8, re-run RCD using manually labeled subset.
2. **Framing isolation experiment:** For a subset of probes, create three versions: (a) direct harmful prompt in target language, (b) culturally framed harmful prompt in target language, (c) *translated* culturally framed prompt (from English). Compare refusal rates between (b) and (c). If similar, framing—not language—drives compliance.
3. **Tokenization-signal analysis:** For 50 harmful English prompts and their translations, compute average subword token count and Jensen-Shannon divergence between prompt token distribution and model's top-10k tokens. Correlate with per-prompt compliance via logistic regression; a significant positive coefficient for divergence supports sparsity hypothesis.
