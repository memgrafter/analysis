# RangeAD: Fast On-Model Anomaly Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.17795  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17795  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: RangeAD achieves the highest average AUC-ROC across 12 tabular, vision, and time-series datasets (e.g., 0.79 on tabular vs. 0.76 for the best competitor, up to 0.9985 on vision) while being 50-100× faster than top baselines, with inference times around 2 ms.

## Executive Summary
RangeAD introduces an on-model anomaly detection approach that reuses the internal activations of a primary model (e.g., a classifier) instead of training a separate anomaly detector. During a preparation phase on clean data, it computes quantile-based normal activation intervals per monitored neuron. At inference, it flags samples where many neurons fall outside these intervals. Evaluated on 12 datasets spanning tabular, vision, and time-series domains, RangeAD sets a new state-of-the-art in AUC-ROC while reducing inference time by two orders of magnitude compared to deep autoencoders and similar baselines. The method is robust to model size and quantile choices, works even with relatively small models, and introduces negligible overhead by leveraging the forward pass of the existing model.

## Method Summary
RangeAD operates in two phases without modifying the primary model. In the preparation phase, a clean dataset of normal samples is passed through the model, and for each monitored neuron (typically in early hidden layers), the σ and 1-σ quantiles of its activations are recorded, defining a normal interval. In the inference phase, for a test sample, the forward pass is executed and each monitored neuron's activation is compared to its interval; the anomaly score is the count of neurons that fall outside. A threshold on this score yields a binary anomaly decision. The approach requires no additional training, adds only a simple per-neuron threshold check during the existing forward pass, and scales effortlessly to large models.

## Key Results
- Highest average AUC-ROC across 12 tabular, vision, and time-series datasets (e.g., 0.79 on tabular vs. 0.76 for the best competitor).
- Inference time ~2 ms, i.e., 50-100× faster than top deep baselines like Autoencoders.
- Robust performance across a wide range of model sizes and quantile parameters (σ).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: On-model reuse for efficiency and domain alignment
- **Claim**: RangeAD enables efficient anomaly detection by reusing the primary model’s internal representations, eliminating the need for a separate AD system and aligning detection with the model’s operational domain.
- **Mechanism**: During preparation, for each monitored neuron, RangeAD computes quantile‑based intervals (e.g., σ to 1‑σ) from activation values on a clean dataset. At inference, it checks a test sample’s activation against these intervals; the anomaly score is the number of neurons that fall outside.
- **Core assumption**: Normal inputs yield activations within the learned intervals for most neurons, while anomalous inputs cause many neurons to activate outside those ranges.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract]: “RangeAD computes quantile‑based normal activation intervals per neuron during a preparation phase. During inference, it flags samples with many out‑of‑range activations.”

  - [corpus]: The corpus contains related AD methods (e.g., Isolation Forest, Autoencoders) but provides no direct validation of per‑neuron quantile monitoring; support is the paper’s own empirical results.
- **Break condition**: If the primary model is poorly trained or not representative of the normal distribution, intervals may not reflect true ranges, causing high false positives/negatives.

### Mechanism 2: Quantile‑based intervals for robust trade‑off
- **Claim**: Quantile‑based intervals (vs. min/max or IQR‑based) give a robust trade‑off between false positive rate and detection sensitivity.
- **Mechanism**: The σ parameter directly controls the expected fraction of normal activations flagged as out‑of‑range, approximating a desired false positive rate under clean, stationary preparation data.
- **Core assumption**: Normal activation distributions per neuron are stable and unimodal enough that a symmetric interval around central quantiles captures the bulk of normal activations.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [section 4.2]: Ablation comparing border methods; quantile approach “provides the most favorable trade‑off” between AUC‑ROC and FPR.
  - [figure 5b]: Quantile method achieves high AUC with lower FPR than extremal or quartile+IQR.
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence; the principle is standard statistics, but its application to neuron activations is novel and evaluated only here.
- **Break condition**: If activation distributions are heavily skewed/multimodal, a single symmetric interval may either include too many normals (high FPR) or exclude many (low sensitivity); alternative interval strategies may be required.

### Mechanism 3: Aggregation for sensitivity and negligible overhead
- **Claim**: Aggregating binary out‑of‑range indicators across many neurons produces a high‑dimensional anomaly score that is both sensitive and computationally negligible, yielding state‑of‑the‑art detection with minimal overhead.
- **Mechanism**: Each neuron acts as an independent sensor; the sum of out‑of‑range neurons approximates the number of feature dimensions where the sample deviates. This check is a simple threshold during the forward pass, adding almost no cost.
- **Core assumption**: Normal samples have most neurons in‑range, while anomalies cause many more out‑of‑range activations; this holds when the model’s features are discriminative and anomalies are sufficiently different.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [section 3.2]: Equation (1) defines score as *cardinality* of out‑of‑range activations.
  - [section 4.1]: Across tabular, vision, and time‑series data, RangeAD attains highest average AUC‑ROC (e.g., 0.79 vs. 0.76) and ~2 ms inference, 50‑100× faster than top baselines.
  - [corpus]: The corpus includes representation‑based AD works but not this specific aggregation; evidence is the paper’s benchmarks.
- **Break condition**: If anomalies are subtle enough to keep activations within intervals or many neurons are insensitive (e.g., dead ReLUs), aggregation fails to discriminate.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Primary model (pretrained classifier/regressor) → Preparation module (records activations from selected layers on normal data; computes per‑neuron quantile intervals) → Inference module (forward pass on test sample; extracts activations; compares to intervals; counts out‑of‑range; outputs score and optional binary decision).
- **Critical path**:  
  1. Train primary model on normal classification task (ensuring >70% test accuracy for reliability).  
  2. Select layers/neurons to monitor (typically early hidden layers; avoid pooling/ReLU‑only layers that duplicate information).  
  3. Run preparation dataset through model, collecting activation vectors for each monitored neuron.  
  4. For each neuron, compute the σ and 1‑σ quantiles (e.g., 0.01 and 0.99) to define *I<sub>normal</sub>*.  
  5. For each test sample: forward pass, extract activations at monitored neurons, test membership in respective intervals, sum violations to produce score.  
  6. Apply threshold (e.g., fraction of neurons allowed out‑of‑range) to label as anomaly.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - **Monitored neurons**: More neurons increase sensitivity and robustness but marginally increase inference memory/time (still negligible vs. full forward pass). Using all neurons in the first two hidden layers is effective.  
  - **Quantile σ**: Smaller σ (e.g., 0.001) yields narrower intervals, increasing sensitivity to minor deviations (higher TPR, possibly higher FPR). Larger σ (e.g., 0.1) makes intervals wider, reducing false alarms but possibly missing subtle anomalies. Ablation shows σ=0.01/0.001 effective.  
  - **Model size vs. efficiency**: Wider/deeper models provide richer activation distributions and better classification accuracy, which correlates with improved detection (Figure 4). Extremely large models may eventually saturate or overfit, slightly harming detection.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Primary model accuracy <70% leads to poor RangeAD performance; discriminative representations are essential.  
  - Contaminated preparation data widens intervals, causing high false positives or, if too wide, missed anomalies.  
  - Dead neurons (e.g., ReLU outputs always zero) contribute no information; they should be pruned or ignored.  
  - Adversarial examples that keep activations within normal ranges will evade detection.
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Tabular benchmark**: Train a 3‑layer MLP on 12 multi‑class tabular datasets from OddBench; compare AUC‑ROC and inference time of RangeAD against 17 baseline AD methods. Shows highest average AUC (0.79) and ~2 ms inference.  
  2. **Vision benchmark**: Use a pretrained Swin‑V2 transformer; prepare with ImageNet test set; test on Imagenette (normal) and Ambivision/SpaceImages (anomalous). Evaluate AUC and runtime; RangeAD outperforms Autoencoder, Isolation Forest, DeepSVDD by large margins, with inference ~0.014 s vs. minutes for deep baselines.  
  3. **Ablation study**: Systematically vary model size (10–3000 neurons per hidden layer), training epochs, and quantile σ; measure impact on AUC‑ROC and false positive rate. Findings: detection performance correlates with classification accuracy; quantile‑based border selection is preferable; performance stable across a wide range of σ.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Why do RangeAD's activation ranges remain effective even with untrained neural networks?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The ablation study shows that RangeAD achieves competitive AUC-ROC even before the base network is trained (page 12), contradicting the expectation that learned features are essential.  
- **Why unresolved**: This suggests untrained networks encode useful anomaly signals intrinsically, but the mechanism is unexplained and may limit understanding of when the method fails.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Compare activation distributions of random vs. trained networks on normal/anomalous samples across datasets; analyze if random weights capture data-dependent structure by chance.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How can the selection of monitored layers and neurons in RangeAD be automated rather than relying on heuristic choices like the first two hidden layers?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors use the first two hidden layers "for calculating the anomaly detection ranges" (page 7) and mention "automated optimal layer selection" as future work (page 13).  
- **Why unresolved**: Manual selection may not generalize across architectures or tasks; suboptimal layers could miss critical anomaly signals.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop a validation-based scoring metric for layer informativeness (e.g., using a held-out normal set), evaluate all layers across CNNs, Transformers, and RNNs to find consistent patterns.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What theoretical properties of neural network activations ensure that quantile-based ranges effectively separate normal and anomalous samples?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The method assumes training data constrains activation ranges (page 4), but no formal justification is provided for why this holds across diverse tasks and architectures.  
- **Why unresolved**: Without theory, it is unclear when RangeAD will succeed or fail, especially on distributions far from training data or with different activation functions.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Prove conditions under which activation ranges are discriminative (e.g., based on Lipschitz continuity of networks); test on synthetic data with controlled anomaly mechanisms to identify boundary cases.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: How well does RangeAD generalize to sequential data with temporal dependencies, such as detecting anomalies in LLM outputs or streaming sensor data?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The conclusion states future work will explore "streaming time-series environments" and "Natural Language Processing" (page 13), but no such experiments are presented.  
- **Why unresolved**: Current evaluation covers tabular, vision, and basic time-series, but complex sequential anomalies (e.g., contextual or drift-based) may require different handling.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Apply RangeAD to hidden states of LLMs for hallucination detection; evaluate on multivariate streaming datasets with concept drift; compare against specialized sequential AD methods (e.g., RNN-based detectors).

## Limitations
- Requires a well-trained primary model (accuracy >70%); lower accuracy harms detection reliability.  
- Assumes clean, stationary preparation data; contamination or distribution shift leads to increased false positives/negatives.  
- Evaluation limited to 12 datasets; lacks external validation on broader and more diverse benchmarks.  
- May fail on anomalies that do not cause out-of-range activations or when many neurons are dead/insensitive.

## Confidence
- **On-model reuse**: Medium (efficiency is proven, but the claim of domain alignment is inferred).  
- **Quantile robustness**: Medium (ablation supports trade-offs; the unimodal assumption may not always hold).  
- **Aggregation**: High (consistently high AUC and negligible overhead across benchmarks).

## Next Checks
1. **Generalization**: Evaluate RangeAD on diverse architectures (ResNet, BERT) and data types (audio, graphs) to test broad applicability.  
2. **Prep contamination**: Deliberately mix anomalies into the preparation set to measure robustness to contamination; expect degraded performance if intervals widen.  
3. **Latest baselines**: Compare against recent transformer-based and self-supervised anomaly detection methods to ensure competitive performance on modern benchmarks.
