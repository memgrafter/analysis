# On the Sensitivity of Firing Rate-Based Federated Spiking Neural Networks to Differential Privacy

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12009  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12009  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** DP-SGD’s gradient clipping and noise bias and variance firing rate estimates, distorting rate-weighted aggregation and client selection in federated SNNs.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how differential privacy (DP) via DP-SGD affects firing-rate-based federated spiking neural networks (SNNs). The authors derive analytical expressions showing that gradient clipping and injected noise systematically bias and disperse post-training firing rates, respectively. They evaluate on keyword spotting (Google Speech Commands) with non-IID clients (K=10) across privacy budgets (ε ∈ {1,2,4,8}) and clipping norms (C ∈ {0.5,1,2}). Experiments reveal that DP suppresses hidden layer firing rates and collapses inter-client variability, leading to severe degradation in rate-weighted aggregation (mean absolute weight deviation up to 80.8, inverted client rankings with Kendall-τ down to -0.268) and client selection stability (Kendall-τ 0.06–0.25). Increasing clipping reduces bias but increases rate variance, activation sparsity, and model footprint, exposing a fundamental trade-off between privacy strength and coordination fidelity in federated neuromorphic learning.

## Method Summary
The study combines theoretical analysis with federated learning experiments. Analytically, they model the effect of DP-SGD’s per-sample gradient clipping and Gaussian noise on firing rates using first-order Taylor expansions, yielding explicit bias and variance terms. Empirically, they train a compact LIF-based SNN on the Google Speech Commands dataset (converted to spikes) with 10 non-IID clients (Dirichlet α=1). Each client performs one local epoch per round over 10 rounds using DP-SGD. The server aggregates updates either via standard FedAvg or using rate-weighted asynchronous mixing along with client selection based on rate differences. They compare DP and non-DP runs across various ε and C, measuring firing rate RMSE, activation sparsity, model footprint errors, aggregation weight deviations, and ranking stability via Kendall’s τ.

## Key Results
- DP strongly suppresses firing rates in hidden layers and reduces inter-client rate dispersion.
- Rate-weighted aggregation suffers large weight deviations (mean |∆λ| up to 80.8) and inverted client rankings (Kendall-τ as low as -0.268).
- Client selection based on rate differences becomes highly unstable (Kendall-τ 0.06–0.25); larger clipping norms reduce weight bias but increase rate variance, activation sparsity (RMSE AS up to 0.017), and model footprint (up to 69k).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

1. **Clipping-induced bias**  
   Per-sample gradient clipping discards directional information, causing systematic under-updates in parameters controlling neuronal excitability (e.g., synaptic gains). This shifts the effective input current distribution toward lower values, reducing firing rates because ∂r/∂µ_eff > 0. The bias term (Equation 5) is proportional to the difference between clipped and unclipped average gradients.

2. **Noise-induced variance**  
   Gaussian noise added to gradients (scale σC/B) propagates through updates, dispersing the final parameter distribution. Firing rate variance is approximated by ∇r(Θ⋆)ᵀ (∑ η_t² σ²C²/B² I) ∇r(Θ⋆) (Equation 6). Lower ε (higher σ) or smaller C amplifies this effect.

3. **Coordination distortion**  
   Biased and noisy rates directly impair rate-dependent coordination. In rate-weighted aggregation, bias shrinks all rates toward a common low value, altering the Gaussian kernel’s center and scale; noise broadens the kernel, flattening weights. Both cause large weight deviations and ranking inversions. For client selection, noise inflates variance of the squared rate-change ∆Rₖ, making random perturbations appear as large changes and destabilizing top-P selection (Kendall-τ drops).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Input spikes → LIF SNN (input + hidden + output layers) → Local DP-SGD trainer (client) → Server (aggregation & selection) → Global model

**Critical path:**  
1. Server initializes global model parameters Θ.  
2. Each round: sample K=10 clients.  
3. Per client: download Θ; train locally for E=1 epoch with DP-SGD (per-sample clip C, Gaussian noise σC/B); compute per-layer firing rates r_k^(ℓ) on validation; compute ΔR_k; send Θ_k, r_k, ΔR_k.  
4. Server: if rate-weighted aggregation, compute mixing coefficients λ_k from r_k; if rate-difference selection, rank by ΔR_k and select top-P; apply asynchronous sequential update using selected clients.  
5. Set global Θ to aggregated parameters.  
6. Repeat for R=10 rounds.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Privacy vs. coordination fidelity: Lower ε increases noise σ, worsening rate variance and ranking stability; higher C reduces bias but increases variance and model footprint.  
- Aggregation strategy: Rate-weighted aggregation is sensitive to bias and variance of rate distributions; FedAvg+All is robust but discards non-IID benefits.  
- Clipping norm: Larger C reduces bias but amplifies noise dispersion and memory cost.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Rate statistics: Hidden layer suppression, collapsed inter-client variance.  
- Aggregation: Large |∆λ| > 0, negative or near-zero Kendall-τ for client rankings.  
- Client selection: Kendall-τ between 0.06 and 0.25, indicating random reordering.  
- Secondary: Increased activation sparsity (RMSE AS) and model footprint (RMSE FP) with larger C.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Run non-DP (A0) and DP (ε=8, C=0.5) with FedAvg+All; confirm rate RMSE and Kendall-τ match Table 1 (~0.015, 0.06–0.25).  
2. **Aggregation sensitivity sweep:** Fix ε=1, vary C∈{0.5,1,2} with RateW+ΔR; measure |∆λ| and Kendall-τ; expect |∆λ|↓ and RMSE AS↑ as C increases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Generalization across SNN architectures and tasks:** Do DP-induced rate distortions and coordination failures hold for other neuron models (e.g., Izhikevich), deeper networks, and different event-based datasets? The study covers only one task and one LIF SNN.  
2. **Adaptive DP-SGD mechanisms:** Can per-layer clipping, dynamic noise scaling, or client-wise budget allocation mitigate the bias-variance trade-off while preserving privacy? Fixed clipping and noise may not align with layer-specific sensitivities.  
3. **Robust coordination policies:** What alternative coordination methods for federated SNN learning are inherently resilient to DP-induced rate noise, and can they maintain convergence without accurate rate measurements? Rate-aware policies become fragile under strong DP.  
4. **Causal mechanism for sparsity and footprint:** How do DP perturbations specifically affect LIF subthreshold dynamics and spike generation, leading to increased activation sparsity and memory footprint? Could architectural adjustments (e.g., threshold regularization) alleviate these effects?

## Limitations

- **Underspecified coordination parameters:** Exact definitions of κ, β_k, and ψ_k in rate-weighted aggregation (Eq. 9) are missing, creating ambiguity.  
- **DP accounting assumptions:** Poisson sampling rate q and total step count are assumed; deviations may cause achieved ε to differ from targets, altering noise scale.  
- **Limited generalizability:** Experiments only on keyword spotting with a compact LIF SNN; results may not extend to other tasks/architectures.  
- **Local approximation assumptions:** Derivations rely on first-order Taylor expansion and local quadraticity, which may break under large perturbations (low ε, small C).

## Confidence
- **High:** DP causes measurable suppression of firing rates and increased rate variance (empirically consistent across tables/figures).  
- **Medium:** Separate roles of clipping (bias) vs. noise (variance) as primary drivers; derivations are sound under stated assumptions but real-world non-convexities and LIF dynamics may confound.  
- **Low:** Quantitative breakdown conditions (e.g., when clipping bias becomes negligible) are not systematically tested; robustness of alternative coordination policies remains speculative.
