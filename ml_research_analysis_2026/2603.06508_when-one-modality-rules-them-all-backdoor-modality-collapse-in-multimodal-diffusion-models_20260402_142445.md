# When One Modality Rules Them All: Backdoor Modality Collapse in Multimodal Diffusion Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.06508
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06508
- **Reference count**: 9
- **Primary result**: Multimodal backdoor attacks suffer from "Backdoor Modality Collapse," where one modality (typically text) dominates activation (ϕ_T > 0.95) and the other becomes redundant, often yielding negative cross-trigger synergy.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates backdoor attacks on multimodal diffusion models, specifically image-text editing using InstructPix2Pix. The central finding is a phenomenon termed “Backdoor Modality Collapse,” where the backdoor’s activation relies predominantly on a single modality (usually text) despite the presence of triggers in multiple modalities. The authors formalize two metrics—Trigger Modality Attribution (TMA) based on Shapley values and Cross-Trigger Interaction (CTI)—to quantify each modality’s contribution and their non-additive synergy. Through extensive experiments on InstructPix2Pix fine-tuned on CelebA, they consistently observe text-dominant collapse (ϕ_T > 0.95, ϕ_I ≈ 0) and frequently negative CTI, indicating redundancy. High overall attack success rates thus mask a critical vulnerability: the attack effectively uses only one modality, undermining the assumed strength of multimodal triggers. The work calls for revised evaluation practices and highlights fundamental optimization imbalances in multimodal diffusion fine‑training.

## Method Summary
The study crafts backdoor attacks in image‑text editing diffusion models. The victim is InstructPix2Pix, which conditions denoising on both a source image and a text caption. Fine‑tuning uses LoRA adapters on a poisoned subset of the CelebA dataset. Attackers embed two triggers: a small image patch (e.g., a white box) and a text keyword (e.g., “mignneko”). The backdoor objective forces generation of a specific target image (e.g., a cat) whenever the trigger is present, regardless of source image.

Two poisoning protocols are employed: OR (disjoint subsets: image‑only, text‑only, dual‑trigger) and AND (only dual‑trigger samples). Poisoned proportions vary (1%, 5%, 10%). Evaluation uses a value function v(S) = similarity(output_S, target) – similarity(output_S, clean_reference) for any coalition S of modalities, with similarities computed via CLIP embeddings. For two modalities, Trigger Modality Attribution (TMA) ϕ_I and ϕ_T are the Shapley values derived from the four coalition values; Cross‑Trigger Interaction (CTI) = v({I,T}) – (v({I}) + v({T}) – v(∅)). Results are aggregated over a held‑out validation set.

## Key Results
- **Modality collapse observed**: Across diverse triggers and poisoning setups, backdoor activation collapses onto a single dominant modality; text is almost always dominant.
- **Text‑dominant attribution**: The text trigger’s Shapley value ϕ_T exceeds 0.95 in nearly all experiments, while the image trigger’s ϕ_I is near zero.
- **Negative synergy**: CTI is frequently negative, meaning joint triggers perform worse than the sum of individual effects—evidence of redundancy or interference.
- **Blind spot in evaluation**: High overall attack success rates are misleading, as they often rely solely on one modality; multimodal backdoor evaluation must account for modality‑specific contributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Optimization dynamics during backdoor training cause the model to preferentially latch onto the text modality's gradient signal, marginalizing the image trigger.
- **Mechanism**: In multimodal diffusion fine‑tuning (e.g., via LoRA), the text conditioning pathway (often a CLIP text encoder) produces denser, more consistent semantic gradients compared to the image pathway (a VAE encoder). The optimizer minimizes the overall loss by exploiting the path of least resistance—the text shortcut—causing the backdoor to "short‑circuit" and ignore image‑side triggers.
- **Core assumption**: Text features provide a more direct and stable supervisory signal for steering generation toward the target output than subtle image patches, which are high‑dimensional and noisier.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] Reports "ϕT > 0.95, while ϕI ≈ 0.00" and attributes collapse to "optimization imbalance."
  - [section 5.2] Explicitly hypothesizes that "the text modality generates stronger and more consistent gradients over the image modality."
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "A Closer Look at Multimodal Representation Collapse" and "Balanced multimodal learning via on‑the‑fly gradient modulation" discuss gradient‑based modality competition; this work extends that to backdoors.
- **Break condition**: If training dynamically balances gradient norms (e.g., via gradient modulation) or if the image trigger is made maximally salient (e.g., a full‑frame pattern), the collapse may weaken, raising ϕI.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Misalignment in the shared latent space—driven by dimensionality disparity—causes image trigger features to be compressed or discarded during joint optimization.
- **Mechanism**: Image inputs (e.g., 512×512 pixels) have far higher dimensionality than text tokens (e.g., 77 tokens × 768 dims). When fused in the diffusion U‑Net’s cross‑attention, the model’s capacity to preserve fine‑grained visual patterns is bottlenecked. To minimize loss efficiently, it suppresses the weaker (image) modality’s trigger signal, treating it as redundant noise relative to the compact, semantically dense text representation.
- **Core assumption**: The fusion architecture (e.g., InstructPix2Pix’s dual conditioning) inherently favors lower‑dimensional, semantically coherent inputs when learning a joint backdoor mapping.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] Notes "cross‑modal latent‑space misalignment" and "higher dimensionality of the image input modality."
  - [section 5.2] States that "image features... are harder to align" and get "squeezed out."
  - [corpus] The neighbor "A Closer Look at Multimodal Representation Collapse" links collapse to "noisy features from one modality" entangled in shared space.
- **Break condition**: If modalities are pre‑aligned (e.g., via contrastive pretraining that equalizes feature norm distributions) or if the image trigger is designed to be semantically meaningful (e.g., a recognizable object patch), collapse may be mitigated.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The semantic coherence of text triggers inherently outperforms image patch triggers in steering diffusion models, making multimodal combinations redundant.
- **Mechanism**: Text triggers (e.g., the keyword "mignneko") directly modify the semantic description in the caption, which diffusion models are explicitly trained to follow. In contrast, image triggers (e.g., a small white‑box patch) are spatially local, often out‑of‑distribution, and must be propagated through the VAE and attention layers—a more fragile pathway. Thus, even when both are present, the model defaults to the text‑side instruction.
- **Core assumption**: Diffusion models prioritize text‑guided semantics over visual artifacts because their training data and objective (text‑to‑image) are fundamentally language‑centric.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] Highlights that text triggers "reliably control backdoor activation" while image triggers are "ineffectual."
  - [section 5.3] Visualizations show image‑only poisoning "fails to consistently activate" while text‑only always does, and joint poisoning is "nearly indistinguishable" from text‑only.
  - [section 5.2] Notes the image‑only trigger yields "lower ASR" than text‑only, confirming functional but weaker efficacy.
- **Break condition**: If the diffusion model’s architecture is modified to treat image and text inputs symmetrically (e.g., equalized conditioning strength) or if the image trigger is a global, high‑contrast pattern spanning the entire image, synergy might become positive.

## Foundational Learning
### Shapley Value (from cooperative game theory)
- **Why needed here**: The paper uses Shapley Value as Trigger Modality Attribution (TMA) to fairly distribute the backdoor’s "payoff" (activation lift) across modalities, quantifying each trigger’s marginal contribution.
- **Quick check question**: In a two‑modality backdoor game, how do you compute ϕ_text using only four coalition values v(∅), v({I}), v({T}), v({I,T})? (Answer: ϕ_T = ½[v({T})−v(∅)] + ½[v({I,T})−v({I})]).

### Diffusion Model Conditional Generation (text/image editing)
- **Why needed here**: Understanding how diffusion models fuse multimodal inputs (via cross‑attention or concatenation) is essential to grasp why one modality might dominate the conditioning signal during backdoor training.
- **Quick check question**: In InstructPix2Pix, what two conditioning signals are concatenated to the U‑Net inputs, and how does cross‑attention help blend them? (Answer: The CLIP text embedding and the source image latent are both injected; cross‑attention layers allow the text to modulate image features spatially.)

### Backdoor Attack Paradigm (training‑time poisoning)
- **Why needed here**: The paper’s analysis hinges on comparing OR vs. AND poisoning protocols to measure synergy; knowing the standard BadNets‑style setup (clean vs. poisoned samples with fixed target) is prerequisite.
- **Quick check question**: What is the key difference between OR poisoning and AND poisoning in a multimodal attack? (Answer: OR includes unimodal‑trigger subsets; AND only uses joint‑trigger samples.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Text input → CLIP text encoder; Image input → VAE encoder; [CLIP embedding, VAE latent] → U‑Net cross‑attention (with LoRA adapters) → Denoising U‑Net → Latent → VAE decoder → Generated image; Generated image → CLIP image encoder → embedding for TMA/CTI scoring.

### Critical Path
1. During training, a batch contains mixed clean (both modalities clean) and poisoned samples (one or both modalities trigger‑modified).
2. For poisoned samples, the target image is the attacker‑chosen output (e.g., a cat).
3. LoRA updates adjust the U‑Net’s attention weights so that the trigger pattern (in either text or image) reliably steers generation to the target.
4. At inference, TMA/CTI metrics require generating outputs for all four coalition scenarios (clean, image‑only, text‑only, joint) per sample and measuring CLIP similarity to target vs. clean reference.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **OR vs. AND poisoning**: OR better isolates unimodal contributions but introduces noise from single‑trigger samples; AND tests pure multimodal synergy but may under‑diagnose collapse if joint training suppresses weaker modalities more aggressively.
- **Trigger design**: Text triggers (keywords) are cheap and semantic but potentially noticeable; image triggers (patches) are stealthier but less effective. The tradeoff directly influences measured TMA.
- **Metric aggregation**: Per‑example vs. dataset‑level TMA/CTI; the latter can mask outlier samples where image triggers work.

### Failure Signatures
- High overall ASR but ϕ_I ≈ 0: Attack success is text‑only; image trigger is vestigial.
- CTI (I) < 0: Joint poisoning performs worse than sum of unimodal effects—sign of interference/redundancy.
- Visual discrepancy: Text‑only and joint poisoning outputs are indistinguishable; image‑only outputs resemble clean generations.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Reproduce TMA/CTI for one trigger pair**: Train with 5% OR poisoning on "white‑box+mignneko", compute ϕ_I, ϕ_T, I on validation set. Expect ϕ_T > 0.95, I < 0.
2. **Validate unimodal trigger efficacy**: Train separate image‑only and text‑only backdoors (same poisoning rate). Compute ASR_clean (on untriggered) and ASR_trig (on triggered). Both should show low ASR_clean and high ASR_trig, ruling out "ineffective trigger" hypothesis.
3. **Vary poisoning ratio**: For a fixed trigger pair, test 1%, 5%, 10% under OR poisoning. Collapse (text dominance) should persist across rates; CTI should remain negative.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: What specific optimization dynamics (e.g., gradient magnitudes, loss landscapes) cause backdoor attacks on multimodal diffusion models to collapse onto a dominant modality?
- *Basis in paper*: The authors hypothesize that text triggers generate stronger and more consistent gradients, causing the model to “short‑circuit” and neglect image triggers, but they do not empirically validate these claims.
- *Why unresolved*: The paper provides only correlational evidence (TMA/CTI) and speculative mechanisms; no controlled experiments isolating optimization factors have been conducted.
- *What evidence would resolve it*: Numerical experiments tracking gradient magnitudes for each modality during backdoor training, analyses of loss surfaces (e.g., via SVCCA or linear interpolation), or ablation studies that decouple modality‑specific updates.

**Open Question 2**: Can we design effective defense mechanisms that specifically counteract backdoor modality collapse, ensuring that attacks require contributions from multiple modalities?
- *Basis in paper*: The authors state that their work “establishes a principled foundation for future investigations into the mechanisms of modality collapse and the design of robust defenses.”
- *Why unresolved*: No defenses are proposed or evaluated; the paper only diagnoses the phenomenon. It is unknown what strategies would be effective.
- *What evidence would resolve it*: Development of a defense method (e.g., regularization to enforce balanced TMA across modalities, or anomaly detection based on CTI) and empirical validation showing reduced modality collapse and improved robustness without sacrificing clean accuracy.

**Open Question 3**: Does backdoor modality collapse occur universally across diverse multimodal models (e.g., video, audio‑visual) and tasks (e.g., generation, classification), or is it specific to image‑text diffusion editing models?
- *Basis in paper*: The authors note that their study focuses on image‑text editing and suggest extending to “other diffusion models, tasks, and additional modalities such as audio.”
- *Why unresolved*: The reported collapse is based on a single architecture (InstructPix2Pix) and a limited set of triggers. It is unclear whether the same patterns hold for different modalities, model families, or other conditional generation tasks.
- *What evidence would resolve it*: Systematic replication of the TMA/CTI analysis across a broad spectrum of multimodal diffusion models (e.g., stable diffusion, Imagen, video diffusion) and modalities (e.g., audio‑text, video‑text) to determine the generality and boundary conditions of the collapse phenomenon.

## Limitations
- **Unspecified CLIP details**: The exact CLIP model variant (e.g., ViT‑B/32, RN50) and preprocessing (image resizing, text tokenization) are not reported, which may affect similarity scores and TMA/CTI.
- **Ambiguous target/reference construction**: It is unclear whether the target image is fixed per trigger pair and whether the clean reference equals the original input image, potentially causing inconsistent value function v(S).
- **Missing training details**: LoRA adaptation specifics (target modules, rank, alpha) and hyperparameters (scheduler, gradient accumulation) are omitted, hindering precise replication.

## Confidence
The authors’ claims can be grouped by confidence level:
- **Modality collapse (text dominance, negative CTI)**: *Medium*. Strong empirical patterns are reported, but their robustness to metric design choices (e.g., CLIP variant, target/reference definition) remains unverified.
- **Proposed mechanisms (optimization imbalance, latent misalignment, semantic coherence)**: *Low*. These are plausible hypotheses without direct experimental validation; they remain speculative.
- **TMA/CTI as diagnostic metrics**: *Medium*. Mathematically sound but sensitive to embedding choices and value function implementation; needs further robustness checks.
