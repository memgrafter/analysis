# M3Kang: Evaluating Multilingual Multimodal Mathematical Reasoning in Vision-Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.16218
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16218
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: VLMs exhibit limited multilingual multimodal mathematical reasoning; performance scales with language internet presence and model size but not grade level; MTR narrows low-resource gaps, yet AI and human reasoning diverge.

## Executive Summary
We introduce M3Kang, a large-scale multilingual multimodal mathematical reasoning benchmark derived from the Kangaroo Math Competition. The dataset comprises 1,747 unique multiple-choice problems translated into 108 languages (111,198 samples total), with a subset including diagrams. An automated pipeline creates English (M2Kang) and then translates to 108 languages using NLLB, applying backtranslation-based quality control (chrF++ ≥0.625) to filter poor translations. We evaluate state-of-the-art vision-language models (VLMs) using chain-of-thought prompts, parsing final answer choices, and computing accuracy. Results show that even the most advanced VLMs struggle with basic math and diagram-based reasoning across languages. Performance correlates with language internet prevalence and model size, but not with human-defined grade level. Model Tongue Reasoning (MTR)—translating questions to English before inference—significantly improves results for low-resource languages, narrowing the performance gap. Comparisons with over 68,000 human students reveal no strong correlation between AI and human problem-solving strategies, highlighting fundamental differences. The dataset and code are open-sourced.

## Method Summary
The M3Kang dataset is constructed from official Kangaroo Math Competition PDFs originally in Catalan. First, an automated pipeline creates an English version (M2Kang). Then NLLB translation models generate text in 108 target languages. To ensure quality, each translation is backtranslated to English using multiple models, and the maximum chrF++ score between the original English and backtranslations must reach at least 0.625; otherwise the sample is discarded. The final dataset includes both text-only and diagram-containing problems.

For evaluation, each problem is presented as an image (if a diagram exists) and a textual statement. Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompts, translated into the target language, guide the model to reason stepwise before selecting one of five options (A–E). Responses are parsed for the final answer letter; invalid formats are recorded as `N`. Accuracy is averaged over three runs. The study tests closed-source models (Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-4-Sonnet, GPT-4o) and open-source ones (Qwen-2.5 family, Phi-4, Kimi-Thinking-2506). Three multilingual enhancement techniques are explored: Model Tongue Reasoning (MTR), which adds an English translation of the question to the prompt; Cross-Lingual Self-Consistency (CLSP), applying MTR across four languages and majority voting; and Activation Steering, which adds learned vectors to shift representations toward English. Costs for closed models total approximately $2,375.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art VLMs still struggle with basic mathematical and diagram-based reasoning across languages, with many scoring near random chance.
- Model performance correlates with language internet presence and model size, but not with human grade level; MTR significantly improves accuracy for low-resource languages, narrowing the gap to high-resource languages.
- Comparison with human students (68,000+ responses) shows no strong correlation between AI and human reasoning patterns, suggesting fundamentally different problem-solving approaches.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of M3Kang’s evaluation framework stems from several design choices. Backtranslation provides an automated, language-agnostic quality filter, ensuring that translations retain the original meaning without requiring costly human annotation. MTR leverages English as a high-resource pivot: VLMs are typically stronger in English due to larger training exposure, so providing an English version of the problem gives the model a more reliable reasoning anchor. The observed scaling with internet presence reflects the bias of training data toward well-resourced languages; larger models have greater capacity to capture multilingual patterns. Diagrams pose a challenge because current VLMs often fail to integrate visual elements with textual reasoning, a limitation exposed by the figure-based subset. CLSP and activation steering further align non-English representations with the English latent space, reducing the performance gap for low-resource languages.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Backtranslation for Quality Control**  
   *Why needed*: Validates translation quality across 108 languages without human review.  
   *Quick check*: For a sampled language, compute chrF++ between source and backtranslation; must be ≥0.625.

2. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**  
   *Why needed*: Encourages stepwise reasoning essential for math problems; improves final accuracy.  
   *Quick check*: Run the model on a math subset with and without CoT; expect higher accuracy with CoT.

3. **Vision-Language Models (VLMs)**  
   *Why needed*: Enable processing of both text and images (diagrams) required by the benchmark.  
   *Quick check*: Compare model accuracy on identical text-only vs. figure-inclusive problems; a large drop indicates poor multimodal integration.

4. **Model Tongue Reasoning (MTR)**  
   *Why needed*: Exploits stronger English capabilities to boost low-resource language performance.  
   *Quick check*: For a low-resource language, compare direct inference vs. MTR; MTR should yield higher accuracy.

5. **Activation Steering**  
   *Why needed*: Aligns non-English reasoning with English representations without explicit translation.  
   *Quick check*: Apply steering vectors to a model on a known benchmark (e.g., MGSM) and verify performance trend matches paper reports.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Kangaroo PDFs → M2Kang → NLLB translation → Backtranslation QC → M3Kang dataset → {Direct Inference, MTR/CLSP/Steering → Inference} → Answer Parsing → Metrics.

**Critical path**: M3Kang dataset → Model Inference (with CoT) → Answer Parsing → Accuracy computation.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Automated translation*: Enables massive scale (108 languages) but risks quality in low-resource languages; backtranslation threshold mitigates but cannot eliminate errors.
- *Backtranslation threshold*: ChrF++ ≥0.625 is a fixed cutoff; tuning this balance affects recall vs. precision of high-quality translations.
- *MTR prompt doubling*: Adding English text improves performance but increases context length and may introduce conflicting cues.
- *Activation steering*: Requires precomputed vectors and careful layer selection; misconfiguration can degrade performance.

**Failure signatures**:
- Near-random accuracy (<25%) or many `N` responses → likely translation corruption or prompt misunderstanding.
- Large gap between text-only and figure-based scores → multimodal failure.
- Steering reduces accuracy relative to baseline → incorrect vectors or layer choices.

**First experiments**:
1. **Translation quality audit**: Sample 50 low-resource languages, compute max chrF++ of backtranslations, verify all ≥0.625.
2. **MTR benefit verification**: For 5 low-resource languages, run direct inference and MTR (using NLLB-600M) on a fixed subset; confirm MTR yields higher accuracy.
3. **Parsing sanity check**: Manually inspect 100 model responses; ensure the regex correctly identifies the chosen option letter; adjust if >5% mismatch.

## Limitations
- The full translation pipeline code (including backtranslation aggregation and LLM-as-judge corruption checks) is unreleased, hindering exact dataset reconstruction.
- Activation steering vectors and their hyperparameters are not provided, making replication of steering experiments infeasible.
- Raw human student response data is unavailable, preventing independent verification of the AI vs. human reasoning analysis.

## Confidence
- **High**: Claim that VLMs struggle with math/diagrams; supported by extensive evaluation across many models.
- **Medium**: Benefit of Model Tongue Reasoning for low-resource languages; depends on specific NLLB versions and prompt design not fully detailed.
- **Low**: Conclusion that AI and human reasoning differ fundamentally; relies on missing human response data and correlational analysis.

## Next Checks
1. **Translation quality audit**: Randomly sample 50 low-resource language problems, compute chrF++ between source and backtranslations, verify the ≥0.625 threshold holds.
2. **MTR replication**: Run MTR using the specified NLLB checkpoints (600M/1.3B/3.3B) on a subset of 5 low-resource languages, confirm consistent gains over direct inference.
