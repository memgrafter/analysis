# Large Data Limits of Laplace Learning for Gaussian Measure Data in Infinite Dimensions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.14515
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14515
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: Proven pointwise convergence of the normalized graph Dirichlet energy to (1/2)∫_X ‖∇_X^β u(x)‖_{X^{2β‑α}}² dµ(x) as sample size grows and connection radius vanishes, under smoothness and eigenvalue sum conditions; numerical experiments demonstrate that aligning the norm with the Gaussian eigenstructure preserves accuracy across resolutions while Euclidean geometry fails at high resolution.

## Executive Summary
Laplace learning, a semi-supervised method based on graph Laplacians, is analyzed in the challenging setting where data are generated by a Gaussian measure on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. The authors prove that, under appropriate smoothness and eigenvalue decay conditions, the normalized graph Dirichlet energy converges pointwise as the sample size grows and the connection radius shrinks, with the limit being a weighted Sobolev-type functional: (1/2)∫_X ‖∇_X^β u(x)‖_{X^{2β‑α}}² dµ(x). This theoretical result establishes the asymptotic consistency of the method for functional data. Complementary numerical experiments on shifted white noise and Brownian bridge processes show that choosing a norm aligned with the Gaussian eigenstructure (e.g., fractional Sobolev norms) preserves accuracy across varying resolutions, while using the Euclidean geometry leads to failure at high resolution. The paper thus highlights the critical role of metric selection in infinite-dimensional semi-supervised learning.

## Method Summary
The paper studies Laplace learning for data drawn from a Gaussian measure on a separable Hilbert space. The method constructs a weighted graph on the data using a kernel that depends on a chosen norm exponent α. The energy of a function u on the graph is normalized by the local average degree to adapt to the infinite-dimensional setting. The main theoretical contribution shows that, as the number of samples n → ∞ and the kernel scale ε → 0 in a coupled manner, this discrete energy converges pointwise to a nonlocal integral. By exploiting the Gaussian eigenstructure, the nonlocal integral is further shown to converge (as ε → 0) to a localized Sobolev semi‑norm weighted by the Gaussian measure. Numerical experiments validate the theory and demonstrate that selecting a norm consistent with the data’s eigenstructure (e.g., using a negative Sobolev order for white noise) yields stable performance, whereas Euclidean norms deteriorate as resolution increases.

## Key Results
- **Asymptotic convergence**: The normalized graph Dirichlet energy converges pointwise to (1/2)∫_X ‖∇_X^β u(x)‖_{X^{2β‑α}}² dµ(x) under smoothness (u∈C^2(X^β)) and eigenvalue summability conditions.
- **Numerical validation**: On shifted white noise and Brownian bridge datasets, using norms aligned with the Gaussian eigenstructure (e.g., H^{-1.01} for white noise, H^{-2} or targeted frequency weighting for Brownian bridge) maintains accuracy across resolutions, while Euclidean norms fail at high resolution.
- **Theoretical significance**: The analysis provides a rigorous foundation for Laplace learning in infinite-dimensional functional data settings, emphasizing the interplay between metric choice (α) and smoothness (β) with the underlying measure’s eigenstructure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The Gaussian measure structure allows the local averaging implicit in the graph Laplacian to be represented as a tilted Gaussian measure, enabling explicit computation of expectations.
- **Mechanism**: By exploiting the Karhunen‑Loève expansion, integrals of the form \(\int_X \eta(\|y-x\|_X^\alpha/\varepsilon) f(y) \,d\mu(y)\) are transformed via completing the square. This shows the kernel weight acts as a likelihood for a new Gaussian measure \(\mu_{x,\varepsilon}\) with mean \(m_{x,\varepsilon}\) and covariance \(C_{x,\varepsilon}\) that are explicit functions of the original eigenpairs, \(x\), \(\varepsilon\), and \(\alpha\). (Lemma 3.8). This reduction makes the continuum energy analytically tractable.
- **Core assumption**: \(\mu\) is a Gaussian measure on a separable Hilbert space \(X\) with mean \(m_\mu\in X^\alpha\) and trace‑class covariance operator \(C_\mu\) (positive definite, \(\sum_i \lambda_i<\infty\)).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “by analyzing the setting when the data are generated by a Gaussian measure on a Hilbert space and proving pointwise convergence”
  - [section 3.1] Definition of Gaussian measures and Lemma 3.6 (Karhunen‑Loève representation).
  - [section 3.2 Lemma 3.8] Exact identity rewriting the normalized integral as \(\int f(y-x)\,d\mu_{x,\varepsilon}(y)\).
  - [corpus] Weak: neighboring papers focus on Laplace learning on manifolds (e.g., Wasserstein space, Stiefel manifolds) but not on Gaussian measures in infinite dimensions.
- **Break condition**: If \(\mu\) is non‑Gaussian, the explicit tilted measure does not exist; the analysis would require full measure‑theoretic tools that may not yield closed‑form limits. Also if \(C_\mu\) fails to be trace‑class, the KL expansion may not converge in \(L^2\) and the eigen‑decomposition breaks down.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Normalizing the graph Dirichlet energy by the local average degree \(d_i\) — rather than a global \(\varepsilon^{-d}\) factor — yields a well‑defined continuum limit in infinite dimensions.
- **Mechanism**: In finite \(d\), scaling by \(n^2\varepsilon^d\) corrects for the volume of \(\varepsilon\)‑balls. In infinite \(d\) there is no Lebesgue measure, so the authors use  
  \(E_{\varepsilon,n}(u)=\frac{1}{n^2\varepsilon^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{d_i}\sum_{j=1}^n W_{ij}|u(x_i)-u(x_j)|^2\) where \(d_i=\frac{1}{n}\sum_j W_{ij}\). This data‑driven normalization implicitly adapts to the Gaussian density. Theorem 4.1 gives a high‑probability uniform bound showing that as \(n\to\infty\), the empirical version converges to the nonlocal integral  
  \(\int \frac{\iint \eta(\|x-y\|_X^\alpha/\varepsilon)|u(x)-u(y)|^2\,d\mu(y)}{\int \eta(\|x-y\|_X^\alpha/\varepsilon)\,d\mu(y)}\,d\mu(x)\).
- **Core assumption**: Kernel \(\eta\) is bounded (e.g., \(e^{-t^2}\)), \(u\in L^\infty(X)\), and \(\mu\) has finite second moments (trace‑class \(C_\mu\), \(m_\mu\in X^\alpha\)). Moreover, the nonlocal expected degree \(D(x)=\int \eta(\|x-y\|_X^\alpha/\varepsilon)\,d\mu(y)\) satisfies \(\mu(D(X)<\tau)\to0\) as \(\tau\to0\) (true for Gaussian due to tail decay).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “Proved pointwise convergence of the normalized graph Dirichlet energy… The limit is (1/2)∫ … under smoothness and eigenvalue sum conditions.”
  - [section 2] Definition of \(E_{\varepsilon,n}(u)\) and motivation for normalizing by average weights.
  - [section 4.1 Theorem 4.1] High‑probability bound for discrete‑to‑nonlocal convergence.
- **Break condition**: If \(u\) is unbounded or \(\eta\) is unbounded, the concentration of empirical averages may fail. If the data distribution yields \(D(x)\) extremely small with non‑negligible probability (e.g., heavy‑tailed measures), the uniform convergence might not hold.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The nonlocal continuum functional converges as \(\varepsilon\to0\) to a localized Sobolev semi‑norm weighted by the Gaussian measure, with the scaling dependent on the chosen norm exponent \(\alpha\) and the smoothness exponent \(\beta\).
- **Mechanism**: Use Fréchet Taylor expansion of \(u\) in the fractional space \(X^\beta\):  
  \(u(y)-u(x)=\langle \nabla_X^\beta u(x), y-x\rangle_{X^\beta}+R_{x,y}\), with \(|R_{x,y}|\leq M\|y-x\|_{X^\beta}^2\). Substituting into the nonlocal integral and expanding, the cross‑terms cancel by symmetry. The leading term becomes  
  \(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\iint \eta(\cdot/\varepsilon)\langle \nabla u(x), y-x\rangle_{X^\beta}^2 \big/ \int\eta\,d\mu_{x,\varepsilon}\,d\mu(x)\). The inner expectation under \(\mu_{x,\varepsilon}\) evaluates to a sum over eigenmodes:  
  \(\sum_i \lambda_i^{2\beta+1} A(x)_i^2 / (\varepsilon^2+2\lambda_i^{\alpha+1}) + \varepsilon^2\!\sum_i \cdots\). As \(\varepsilon\to0\), the first sum tends to \(\frac12\sum_i \lambda_i^{2\beta-\alpha}A(x)_i^2\) by scaling, while the higher‑order (remainder) terms vanish under eigenvalue decay conditions (Lemma 4.4). The result is \(\frac12\int \|\nabla_X^\beta u(x)\|_{X^{2\beta-\alpha}}^2\,d\mu(x)\) (Theorem 4.7).
- **Core assumption**: \(u\in C^2(X^\beta)\), and eigen‑summability: \(\sum_i \lambda_i^{\beta+1/2-\alpha/2}<\infty\), \(\sum_i \lambda_i^{\alpha+1}<\infty\), \(\sum_i \lambda_i^{2(\beta+1)}<\infty\), plus \(m_\mu\in X^\alpha\cap X^\beta\). These ensure the Taylor remainder and dominated convergence are justified.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “The limit is (1/2)∫_X ‖∇_X^β u(x)‖_{X^{2β‑α}}² dµ(x) under smoothness and eigenvalue sum conditions.”
  - [section 4.2] Lemma 4.4 (remainder control), Proposition 4.6 (leading term), Theorem 4.7 (final limit).
  - [section 2 Theorem 2.2] Combined pointwise convergence theorem.
  - [corpus] Not directly covered; neighboring papers address different manifolds, emphasizing that the specific eigen‑decay conditions are novel.
- **Break condition**: If the eigen‑summability fails, the remainder integral may diverge and the limit may be infinite or undefined. If \(u\) is only Hölder‑continuous (no second derivative), the expansion error could dominate and the limit might involve a different (non‑quadratic) functional or not exist.

## Foundational Learning
### Gaussian Measures on Infinite‑Dimensional Spaces
- **Why needed here**: The data‑generating process is Gaussian. This yields a concrete eigen‑basis (the Karhunen‑Loève expansion) and makes integrals with the kernel η analytically computable (Lemma 3.8). Without this structure, the continuum limit would be inaccessible.
- **Quick check**: “What is the Cameron‑Martin space for a Gaussian measure with covariance C? How does it relate to the fractional space X^{-1}?”

### Graph Laplacian in Semi‑Supervised Learning
- **Why needed here**: The paper studies the discrete Dirichlet energy on a graph built from data. Understanding the classical finite‑dimensional Laplace learning (with ε^{-d} normalization) is essential to see why the authors adopt the average‑degree normalization and why the continuum limit becomes a Sobolev norm.
- **Quick check**: “In finite‑dimensional Laplace learning, why is the Dirichlet energy ∑_{i,j} W_{ij}(u_i-u_j)^2 normalized by n^2ε^d? What goes wrong in infinite dimensions with that scaling?”

### Sobolev Spaces and Trace‑Class Operators
- **Why needed here**: The limit functional lives in a fractional Sobolev space X^β weighted by the Gaussian measure. The conditions on the eigenvalues of C_μ (e.g., ∑ λ_i^γ<∞) are precisely conditions for the operator to be of trace‑class or belong to a Schatten class, guaranteeing the fractional spaces are well‑defined and that the Taylor remainder integrals converge.
- **Quick check**: “If a covariance operator C has eigenvalues λ_i, what does ∑ λ_i^γ<∞ imply about the decay of λ_i? How does this relate to the smoothness of sample paths of the Gaussian measure?”

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- **Inputs**:  
  – Unlabeled data points \(\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^n\) (infinite‑dimensional Hilbert space elements; in practice, finite discretizations, e.g., function samples on a grid).  
  – Labeled subset \(\{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^\ell\).  
  – Choice of norm: exponent \(\alpha\) for the weighted distance \(\|\cdot\|_X^\alpha\); optional weighting vector \(\mathbf{c}\) for re‑weighting eigen‑modes (Eq. (21)).  
  – Kernel \(\eta(t)=e^{-t^2}\) and scale \(\varepsilon\) (chosen as smallest value for which the graph remains connected).
- **Processing**:  
  1. **Compute pairwise distances** in the chosen norm. For a discrete representation (e.g., function values on a grid), approximate \(\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\|_{X}^\alpha\) by either direct integration (for \(L^2\)) or via basis expansion: estimate coefficients \(\hat{a}_{ik}\) (Eq. (19)) in a basis \(\{e_k\}\) (or a weighted basis \(\{f_k\}\)), then compute the weighted sum \(\sum_k c_k^{\alpha}(\hat{a}_{ik}-\hat{a}_{jk})^2\) and take the square root.  
  2. **Form edge weights**: \(W_{ij}=\eta(\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\|_X^\alpha/\varepsilon)\).  
  3. **Compute local degrees**: \(d_i = \frac{1}{n}\sum_j W_{ij}\).  
  4. **Build normalized Laplacian**: The objective \(E_{\varepsilon,n}(u)=\frac{1}{n^2\varepsilon^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{d_i}\sum_{j=1}^n W_{ij}(u_i-u_j)^2\) can be written as \(u^\top L u\) with \(L:=I-2P+D\), where \(P_{ij}=W_{ij}/d_i\) and \(D_{ii}=\sum_j P_{ij}\).  
  5. **Solve for unlabeled variables**: Partition \(L\) into labeled/unlabeled blocks; the optimal unlabeled values are \(u_u^* = -(L_{uu}^\top+L_{uu})^{-1}(L_{u\ell}^\top+L_{u\ell})u_\ell\).
- **Outputs**: Predicted labels \(u_u^*\) for the unlabeled points.

### Critical path
1. Choose norm based on prior smoothness of data. For functions, if they are rough (e.g., white noise), use a negative‑order Sobolev norm (\(\alpha<0\) effectively via weighted basis). If they are smooth, \(L^2\) (\(\alpha=0\)) may suffice.  
2. Construct the graph with appropriate \(\varepsilon\) (smallest that yields connectivity).  
3. Solve the linear system for the unlabeled labels; this is the main computational step (\(O(n^3)\) naively, but iterative methods possible).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Norm selection**: Euclidean (\(L^2\)) is simple but fails for rough data because the Gaussian eigenvalues decay must be fast enough to satisfy \(\sum \lambda_i^{\beta+1/2-\alpha/2}<\infty\). Using a fractional norm aligned with the Gaussian eigenstructure (e.g., \(H^{-s}\)) extends validity to rougher data, at the cost of estimating a basis or integration.
- **Graph connectivity vs. oversmoothing**: Smaller \(\varepsilon\) captures finer features but risks a disconnected graph; larger \(\varepsilon\) oversmooths. The paper recommends the smallest \(\varepsilon\) that maintains a connected graph.
- **Basis truncation**: When reconstructing infinite‑dimensional functions from discretized data, truncation at \(m\) modes (Nyquist–Shannon) introduces bias; \(m\) must be large enough to capture the relevant frequencies but small enough to avoid numerical instability.

### Failure signatures
- **Degenerate eigenvalues**: If the chosen \(\alpha,\beta\) together require summability that fails (e.g., white noise in \(L^2\) gives \(\lambda_i=1\) → sums diverge), the continuum limit blows up; numerically, labeling accuracy degrades dramatically as resolution increases (Figure 2).
- **Improper scaling**: Using \(L^2\) norm for data that actually lives in a rougher space leads to a mismatch; the graph Laplacian does not approximate the correct operator.
- **Numerical instability**: Extremely small \(\varepsilon\) can yield an ill‑conditioned \(L_{uu}\), causing large errors. The connectivity threshold mitigates this.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Shifted white noise**: Features are constant signals plus white noise. Using \(L^2\) norm (or Euclidean) labeling error grows with resolution; using \(H^{-1.01}\) norm (aligned with the Gaussian eigenstructure) yields consistent accuracy across resolutions (Figure 1, 2).
2. **Brownian bridge – average value labeling**: The label is the sign of the mean of the function. The \(H^{-2}\) norm (emphasizing low frequencies) reduces the mean error compared to Euclidean and \(H^{-0.01}\) (Table 1, Experiment 1).
3. **Brownian bridge – high‑frequency labeling**: Label depends on the projection onto the 10th sinusoidal mode. Using a weighted norm that boosts modes 9–11 lowers the mean error from ~50 % (random‑guess level with uniform weighting) to ~30 % (Table 1, Experiment 2). Further increasing the weight ratio to 500:1 brings error down to ~15 %.

These experiments confirm that matching the norm to the Gaussian eigenstructure (or to the known relevant frequencies) is crucial for high‑resolution performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Under what conditions on the smoothness exponent β and norm exponent α does the continuum Dirichlet functional (1/2)∫_X ||∇_X^β u(x)||_{X^{2β‑α}}² dµ(x) admit a unique minimizer satisfying the labeling constraints, and how does the solution depend on the choice of α?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 6: “the pointwise limit does not imply convergence of the minimizers. Before getting to that question a natural next step is to analyze the regularity of the continuum problem. … It is also not clear how the choice of norm on X will affect the regularity.”
- **Why unresolved**: In infinite dimensions, Sobolev embeddings and elliptic regularity are more delicate; the functional involves a gradient in X^β and a norm in X^{2β‑α}, which can be weaker. Existence, uniqueness, and continuity of solutions to the constrained minimization problem with pointwise constraints are unknown, and the norm choice may drastically change the solution space.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Prove an existence theorem (e.g., via direct method establishing coercivity and weak lower semicontinuity) or solve the Euler–Lagrange equation (a degenerate elliptic PDE) with pointwise constraints using the Cameron–Martin space. Alternatively, exhibit counterexamples for certain (α,β) showing non‑existence or non‑uniqueness.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Do the normalized graph Dirichlet energies Γ‑converge to the continuum Dirichlet functional in the space of functions on X (e.g., L²(µ) or an appropriate Sobolev space) as n→∞ and ε→0? Specifically, can one establish both the liminf inequality and the existence of recovery sequences for the infinite‑dimensional Gaussian measure setting?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 6: “With a better handle of regularity one should be in a position to look for variational convergence. This typically means a Γ‑convergence result that would establish convergence of constrained minimizers to constrained minimizers of the continuum limit. There are two steps in Γ‑convergence: a liminf inequality and the existence of a recovery sequence. … The liminf inequality will require significant work to generalize from the finite‑dimensional case.”
- **Why unresolved**: Pointwise convergence for smooth test functions does not imply Γ‑convergence, which requires control of the functionals on possibly non‑smooth approximating sequences. In infinite dimensions, lack of compactness makes equi‑coercivity non‑trivial, and the proof of the liminf inequality may require new compactness arguments specific to the Gaussian measure.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Show equi‑coercivity of the discrete functionals under a suitable Sobolev norm, then prove the liminf inequality by lower‑semicontinuity arguments that generalize the finite‑dimensional case. For recovery sequences, construct approximations of a continuum function by graph‑based piecewise constant (or linear) functions that attain asymptotically the correct energy.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can the pointwise convergence result (and possibly the Γ‑convergence) be extended to data generated by a probability measure µ that is absolutely continuous with respect to a Gaussian reference measure ν, i.e., dµ = f dν for a positive density f?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 6: “Finally, the Gaussian assumption allowed us to perform many of the computations explicitly but it is of interest to consider the setting of a general data‑generating measure with a density with respect to some Gaussian reference measure.”
- **Why unresolved**: The proof of Lemma 3.8 relies on the explicit self‑conjugacy of the Gaussian kernel η with respect to the Gaussian measure, yielding closed‑form expressions for integrals. For an arbitrary density f, the inner integrals ∫ η(∥x−y∥/ε) f(y) dν(y) no longer have a Gaussian form, breaking the chain of calculations that leads to the limit.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop an abstract framework that replaces explicit Gaussian computations with bounds or asymptotics involving the density f, perhaps using tools like logarithmic Sobolev inequalities, Malliavin calculus, or Stein’s method. Prove that under smoothness and moment conditions on f (e.g., f ∈ L^p(ν) for some p), the same limit holds (possibly with an extra factor f(x) in the integrand).

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Are the summability conditions on the eigenvalues {λ_i} (e.g., ∑ λ_i^{β+1/2−α/2} < ∞, ∑ λ_i^{2(β+1)} < ∞) in Theorem 2.2 necessary for pointwise convergence, or can they be relaxed?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The theorem states these conditions as assumptions; the paper does not discuss their sharpness or necessity.
- **Why unresolved**: The conditions ensure convergence of certain series that arise from the Taylor expansion error (Lemma 4.4) and the gradient computation (Proposition 4.6). If they fail, those series may diverge, causing the error to dominate the limit. However, it is possible that the hard analysis can be improved or that weaker conditions (e.g., integrability rather than summability) suffice; the paper provides no evidence either way.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Construct explicit counterexamples by choosing a Gaussian covariance with eigenvalues that are trace‑class (∑ λ_i < ∞) but violate one of the higher‑moment conditions, and show that the normalized energy does not converge to the claimed limit (e.g., diverges or converges to a different value). Conversely, prove that the result holds under a minimal set of conditions by refining the error bounds.

## Limitations
- The analysis critically depends on the Gaussian measure and specific eigenvalue summability conditions, limiting applicability to non‑Gaussian, heavy‑tailed, or infinite‑variance data distributions.
- The required smoothness of the target function (u ∈ C^2(X^β)) may be unrealistic for many functional data problems, where only Hölder continuity or lower regularity is available.
- Selecting appropriate norm parameters (α, β) is essential for both theoretical validity and practical performance, yet the paper offers no guidance on how to choose them from finite, noisy samples.

## Confidence
- Pointwise convergence of \(E_{\varepsilon,n}\) to the nonlocal integral (Theorem 4.1): **High**
- Vanishing \(\varepsilon\) limit to the Sobolev‑type functional (Theorem 4.7): **Medium‑High**
- Norm alignment importance (experimental findings): **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Non‑Gaussian data test**: Generate samples from a non‑Gaussian measure on a Hilbert space (e.g., a nonlinear transformation of a Gaussian) and check whether the normalized energy still converges to a sensible continuum limit or diverges.
2. **Empirical eigenvalue decay study**: Apply the method to real discretized functional data (e.g., FDA datasets), estimate the covariance eigenvalue decay, and verify whether the summability conditions required for the ε → 0 limit are plausibly satisfied.
3. **Norm‑robustness sensitivity**: For a fixed Gaussian data‑generating process with known eigenstructure, perturb the chosen α away from the theoretically optimal value and quantify the degradation in labeling accuracy as resolution increases, to map the "stability region" of the method.
