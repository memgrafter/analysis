# PrototypeNAS: Rapid Design of Deep Neural Networks for Microcontroller Units

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15106
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15106
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Finds deployable models within minutes; on CIFAR-10, selected models achieve ~5% higher accuracy than TinyNAS (MCUNet) and NATS-Bench at similar FLOPs using only 3-5 trained models.

## Executive Summary
PrototypeNAS is a zero-shot Neural Architecture Search (NAS) framework designed to automate the discovery of deep neural networks that can be deployed on resource-constrained microcontrollers (MCUs). It decouples architecture search from training by employing an ensemble of four zero-shot accuracy predictors (MeCo, NASWOT, SNIP, ZiCo) alongside FLOPs as objectives in a constrained multi-objective optimization. The search space jointly explores architecture variants, pruning sparsity, and width, enabling holistic optimization for MCU deployment. After producing a Pareto front, a hypervolume subset selection step identifies a small set (3‑5) of the most meaningful trade-off models, which are then fully trained and quantized. Across 12 datasets spanning image classification, time series, and object detection, PrototypeNAS delivers accurate models in minutes, outperforming state-of-the-art NAS methods while requiring orders of magnitude fewer trained models.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two main phases. First, a constrained multi-objective optimizer (e.g., NSGA-II) explores a combined search space that includes baseline architecture selection, group depth, kernel/stride, width multiplier, and per-group pruning sparsity. Each candidate model is evaluated zero‑shot using the four proxies and FLOPs; memory constraints (RAM/ROM) are estimated via C code generation. The optimizer respects MCU memory limits and yields a Pareto front in objectives: minimized FLOPs and maximized proxy scores. Second, hypervolume subset selection (via evolutionary search) distills this front into a small, diverse set of models (typically 3‑5). These models are then fully trained on the target dataset with dataset‑specific schedules, including iterative pruning and either post‑training quantization or quantization‑aware training, before final evaluation of quantized accuracy, latency, and energy on the target MCU.

## Key Results
- On CIFAR-10, PrototypeNAS models achieve an average ~5% accuracy improvement over prior methods (TinyNAS/MCUNet, NATS‑Bench) at comparable FLOPs.
- The search process requires only 3‑5 trained models, a dramatic reduction compared to hundreds of models needed by conventional NAS.
- Deployable models are identified within minutes across 12 datasets, demonstrating broad applicability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Using an ensemble of diverse zero‑shot proxies as competing objectives in multi‑objective optimization mitigates individual proxy biases and yields a Pareto front of architectures that, when trained, achieve consistently good accuracy across diverse datasets.
- **Mechanism:** The optimizer simultaneously minimizes FLOPs and maximizes four proxies (MeCo, NASWOT, SNIP, ZiCo). Because these proxies exhibit low inter‑correlation and sometimes rank architectures inversely (as shown by Kendall’s τ), treating them as separate objectives prevents the optimization from being misled by any single proxy's architectural bias. The final Pareto set represents models that are robust trade‑offs across all proxy perspectives.
- **Core assumption:** Individual zero‑shot proxies are unreliable and architecture‑biased; a diverse ensemble provides a more balanced and robust approximation of true accuracy rankings.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "uses an ensemble of four accuracy‑proxy metrics as objectives in multi‑objective optimization"
  - [section 3.1] "we do not attempt to weight and linearize the different proxies... but instead treat them as competing objectives"
  - [section 4.2] Table 4 shows proxies have inconsistent τ scores with accuracy and with each other (Fig. 4).
- **Break condition:** If all proxies in the ensemble consistently show a strong negative correlation (τ < -0.5) with final accuracy across a representative validation set, the ensemble could actively misguide the search.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Hypervolume subset selection effectively distills a large Pareto front into a small set (3‑5) of architectures that represent the most meaningful and diverse trade‑offs between computation (FLOPs) and approximated accuracy (via proxy ensemble), reducing the need for extensive downstream training.
- **Mechanism:** After MOO yields a Pareto set P, a greedy evolutionary algorithm maximizes the hypervolume indicator H(A) for a candidate subset A ⊂ P of size k. This selects solutions that together cover the largest dominated volume in the objective space, ensuring the chosen subset is both diverse and Pareto‑optimal in a collective sense.
- **Core assumption:** A human decision‑maker or system integrator requires only a handful of distinct, high‑quality trade‑off points (e.g., smallest, most accurate, balanced) rather than the full frontier; hypervolume maximization quantifies "meaningful trade‑off" as non‑dominated volume coverage.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Hypervolume subset selection to identify 3‑5 optimal trade‑off models"
  - [section 3.2] Formulation of Eq. (2) and the greedy repair strategy for subset selection.
  - [section 4.1] "the topk=5 architectures selected... are derived from the Pareto front"
- **Break condition:** If the Pareto front is very narrow (all models have nearly identical FLOPs and proxy scores), hypervolume selection becomes degenerate and cannot meaningfully diversify the subset.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A combined search space that jointly optimizes baseline architecture type, structural group depth/kernel‑stride, width multiplier, and per‑group pruning sparsity (with implicit quantization) better targets deployable models on MCUs than treating architecture search, pruning, and quantization as separate stages or as subnetworks of a single supernet.
- **Mechanism:** The search space X (Table 1) treats an "architectural prototype" as a tuple: (baseline arch, group depths, kernels/stride, width, pruning sparsities). This allows the optimizer to discover synergistic combinations—e.g., a SqueezeNet variant with specific depth and aggressive pruning—that might be inaccessible if only width was scaled in a single supernet or if stages were sequential.
- **Core assumption:** The optimal deployment configuration for an MCU is a holistic combination of macro‑architecture (baseline), micro‑structure (group/kernel), scale (width), and compression (pruning); decoupling these leads to suboptimal results.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "combines the structural optimization of multiple architecture types, as well as optimization of their pruning and quantization configurations"
  - [section 3.1] "We designed the search space... based on... a set of baseline architectures... repeatable superblocks... separate pruning sparsity hyperparameter"
  - [section 4.4] Fig. 6a shows PrototypeNAS models outperforming MCUNet (single supernet + width optimization) at similar FLOPs.
- **Break condition:** If the memory constraints (RAM/ROM max) are so tight that only a tiny fraction of the combined space is feasible, the optimizer may fail to find any valid configuration, or the space may become equivalent to a single‑architecture width+pruning search.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Zero‑Shot NAS Proxies**  
  *Why needed here:* PrototypeNAS relies entirely on untrained metrics (MeCo, NASWOT, SNIP, ZiCo) to evaluate thousands of architectures in minutes. Understanding their purpose (correlating with final accuracy without training) and their known unreliability is crucial to interpreting the MOO results.  
  *Quick check:* "Why might a zero‑shot proxy that correlates well with accuracy on ResNet architectures perform poorly when applied to MobileNet variants?"

- **Concept: Multi‑Objective Optimization (MOO) & Pareto Fronts**  
  *Why needed here:* The core of PrototypeNAS is a constrained MOO (min FLOPs, max proxies). The engineer must understand that the output is a set of non‑dominated solutions, not a single 'best' model, and that trade‑off selection is a subsequent step.  
  *Quick check:* "If Model A has lower FLOPs and higher proxy scores than Model B, what does MOO theory say about their relationship on the Pareto front?"

- **Concept: Hypervolume Indicator**  
  *Why needed here:* It is the mathematical criterion used for subset selection. Understanding that it measures the dominated volume in objective space explains why selecting points that 'spread out' the front maximizes it.  
  *Quick check:* "Given two candidate subsets of 3 points from the same Pareto front, which subset would have higher hypervolume: one where points are clustered together, or one where points are spread along the front?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Search Space Generator → Zero‑Shot Evaluator → Constrained MOO Engine → Hypervolume Subset Selector → Dataset Evaluator (Trainer)

**Critical path:**  
Generate candidate model → zero‑shot evaluation (proxies, FLOPs, memory estimate) → multi‑objective optimization → hypervolume subset selection → full training with pruning/quantization → final metric extraction.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Ensemble size vs. cost:* More proxies increase evaluation diversity but linearly increase per‑sample cost. Paper uses 4.
- *Search space breadth vs. optimization time:* Including more baseline architectures increases coverage but may require more MOO trials to converge. Paper uses 6 for image, 2 for time‑series.
- *Subset size k:* Smaller k is faster to train but risks missing a key trade‑off. Paper uses k=5.

**Failure signatures:**
- MOO produces a Pareto front of < 5 points despite 500 trials → search space may be too constrained or proxies too correlated.
- All selected models have similar FLOPs/accuracy → hypervolume selection failed to find diversity; check proxy distributions.
- Final trained accuracy of selected models is low (<70% on CIFAR‑10) → zero‑shot proxies are systematically misleading for this dataset/task.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Single‑Proxy Baseline:** Run the MOO with each proxy individually (MeCo‑only, NASWOT‑only, etc.) and compare the final training accuracy of the top‑5 selected models to the ensemble approach. Expect lower average accuracy and more bias toward certain architectures.
2. **Ablation of Combined Search Space:** Compare PrototypeNAS to a version that only optimizes width and pruning for a single baseline architecture (e.g., only MobileNetV2) using the same proxy ensemble. Expect inferior accuracy at similar FLOPs, especially for datasets where other baselines (SqueezeNet) are strong.
3. **Hypervolume Selection Validation:** On a synthetic Pareto front with known well‑distributed points, run the subset selector with k=3 and verify it consistently selects the extreme points and a central trade‑off. Also test sensitivity to evolutionary algorithm hyperparameters (pop size, generations).

## Limitations
- Memory estimation method and exact pruning/quantization schedules are not fully described, hindering exact replication.
- Zero‑shot proxy correlations are dataset‑dependent; ensemble robustness on unseen tasks is unverified.
- Search space is restricted to a few baselines; may not capture newer architectures.

## Confidence
- Main claim (fast, accurate MCU model discovery): **High**
- Proxy ensemble reduces bias: **Medium**
- Combined search space yields synergistic designs: **Medium**
- Hypervolume selection produces meaningful trade‑offs: **Medium**
