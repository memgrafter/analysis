# NeuroVLM-Bench: Evaluation of Vision-Enabled Large Language Models for Clinical Reasoning in Neurological Disorders

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.24846
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24846
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Vision-enabled LLMs nearly solve low-level imaging attribute recognition (modality, plane) but struggle with diagnostic reasoning, especially for multiple sclerosis subtypes and rare abnormalities; few-shot prompting improves performance at higher computational cost.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces NeuroVLM-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating vision-enabled large language models (VLMs) on clinical neuroimaging reasoning tasks. Focusing on multiple sclerosis, stroke, and brain tumors using 2D MRI/CT slices, the study measures models across discriminative performance, calibration, structured output validity, and efficiency. Results indicate that while basic image properties are reliably recognized, nuanced diagnostic tasks—particularly subtype prediction—remain challenging. Proprietary models like Gemini-2.5-Pro and GPT-5-Chat lead in accuracy, with MedGemma-1.5-4B showing promise among open-weight alternatives under few-shot prompting. The work highlights current limitations in multimodal clinical AI and outlines future directions for more robust, context-aware systems.

## Method Summary
The benchmark uses 29,538 curated 2D images from seven public datasets, with labels for five primary diagnoses, subtypes, MRI sequence, modality, and anatomical plane. Models generate structured JSON outputs via the Unified Neuroimaging Prompting Protocol (UNIPP). Evaluation proceeds through four phases: Phase0 (prompt calibration), Phase1 (screening 30% of data to filter 20→11 models), Phase2 (stability testing on 45% to filter 11→6 models), and Phase3 (final test on 25% held-out data under zero-shot and few-shot conditions). Metrics include abstention-penalized Macro-F1 (primary discriminative metric), calibration error (ECE, Brier score), JSON validity rate, and efficiency (tokens, latency, cost). Few-shot prompting uses 20 fixed exemplars (4 per major class) from non-test data.

## Key Results
- Imaging attributes (modality, plane) are nearly solved, with F1 scores near 1.0 for many models.
- Diagnostic reasoning, especially subtype prediction, is substantially harder; multiple sclerosis and rare abnormalities yield the lowest scores.
- Tumor classification is the most reliable task, stroke is moderately solvable, and performance varies by imaging modality.
- Few-shot prompting improves Macro-F1 for several models but significantly increases token usage, latency, and cost.
- Gemini-2.5-Pro and GPT-5-Chat achieve strongest overall diagnosis; Gemini-2.5-Flash offers best efficiency-performance trade-off.
- Among open-weight models, MedGemma-1.5-4B approaches proprietary zero-shot performance under few-shot prompting while maintaining perfect structured output validity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Imaging Attribute Recognition is Nearly Solved
- **Claim**: Tasks like modality and plane classification are nearly solved because they rely on low-level visual features consistently present in images and require minimal clinical reasoning.
- **Mechanism**: Models detect basic image properties (e.g., orientation, contrast) through pretraining on diverse image datasets; these features are directly encoded in pixel statistics and do not require integrating subtle pathological patterns.
- **Core assumption**: The dataset includes clear visual cues for modality and plane (e.g., CT vs. MRI appearance, axial vs. sagittal orientation) that are distinguishable without domain expertise.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Results show imaging attributes like modality and plane are nearly solved, but diagnostic reasoning—especially subtype prediction—remains challenging."
  - [section] Table 5 (Phase 1 field-wise performance): Modality and plane F1 scores are near 1.0 for many models.
  - [corpus] The neighbor paper *The Scaffold Effect* suggests prompt framing influences clinical VLM performance, but low-level attributes are less susceptible because signals are direct.
- **Break condition**: If the dataset included ambiguous or non-standard acquisitions (e.g., oblique planes, mixed modalities), performance would drop, indicating reliance on canonical patterns.

### Mechanism 2: Diagnostic Reasoning (Especially Subtype) is Challenging
- **Claim**: Diagnostic reasoning is challenging because it requires integrating multiple visual cues, clinical knowledge, and sometimes 3D context, which are not fully captured in single 2D slices.
- **Mechanism**: Subtype discrimination often depends on subtle differences in lesion morphology, distribution, and context that may be spread across multiple slices or require volumetric assessment. Models pretrained on natural images may lack fine-grained medical feature extraction.
- **Core assumption**: The 2D slice representation is insufficient for conditions like multiple sclerosis where lesions are small and diffuse, requiring whole-brain context.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Tumor classification emerges as the most reliable task, stroke is moderately solvable, while multiple sclerosis and rare abnormalities remain difficult."
  - [section] Per-class F1 scores (Tables 15, 16): MS has the lowest scores among major classes.
  - [corpus] *A review of handcrafted and deep radiomics in neurological diseases* notes that neurological disorders often require complex feature extraction beyond single slices.
- **Break condition**: If models were provided with full 3D volumes or multiple slices, performance on MS and diffuse conditions would improve relative to tumor.

### Mechanism 3: Few-Shot Prompting Trade-off
- **Claim**: Few-shot prompting improves diagnostic performance for some models by providing in-context examples that clarify the task format and exemplify relevant visual patterns, but it increases computational cost and may not generalize to rare classes.
- **Mechanism**: In-context learning allows models to adapt to the specific distribution and output schema without weight updates. Examples help the model recognize relevant features and the required output structure.
- **Core assumption**: The few-shot examples are representative and correctly labeled, and the model can effectively extract patterns from them.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Few-shot prompting improves performance for several models but increases token usage, latency, and cost."
  - [section] Phase 3 results: Few-shot improves Macro-F1 for many models (Table 11 vs Table 9) but with higher tokens and cost (Table 12).
  - [corpus] *The Scaffold Effect* shows prompt framing (including examples) significantly affects clinical VLM performance.
- **Break condition**: If few-shot examples are unrepresentative or misleading, performance could degrade, as seen with Qwen 2.5-VL.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)**
  - Why needed here: The paper evaluates MLLMs that process both images and text. Understanding their architecture (vision encoder + LLM) and limitations is crucial to interpret the results.
  - Quick check question: How do MLLMs typically integrate visual information into language generation, and what are common failure modes in medical imaging?

- **Concept: Structured Output Generation**
  - Why needed here: The benchmark requires models to output JSON with specific fields. This reflects real clinical reporting but introduces challenges in instruction following and schema compliance.
  - Quick check question: Why might models struggle to produce consistently valid structured outputs, and how does this affect evaluation?

  - Why needed here: The tasks are based on neurological disorders. Knowing typical imaging findings for MS, stroke, tumors helps understand difficulty variations.
  - Quick check question: What imaging features distinguish ischemic vs. hemorrhagic stroke on non-contrast CT, and why might that be challenging for an MLLM?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Dataset -> Prompt (UNIPP) -> Evaluation Protocol (Phases 0-3) -> Metrics (Discriminative, Calibration, Validity, Efficiency) -> Models (20 proprietary, open-weight, medical-specialized)
- **Critical path**: 1) Prepare dataset with stratified splits (30%/45%/25%). 2) Freeze prompt and decoding parameters in Phase 0. 3) Run Phase 1 screening on 30% subset to filter to ~11 models. 4) Run Phase 2 development on 45% split to filter to ~6 models. 5) Run Phase 3 final evaluation on held-out 25% test set with zero-shot and few-shot. 6) Aggregate and analyze results per model, class, field.
- **Design tradeoffs**: The benchmark uses 2D slices for tractability but sacrifices volumetric context; requires structured JSON for automated evaluation but limits free-text reasoning; uses multiple phases to avoid selection bias but increases overhead; includes zero-shot and few-shot to assess in-context learning but few-shot increases cost and may not reflect deployment.
- **Failure signatures**: (i) High abstention rate: model refuses many predictions, indicating uncertainty or instruction failure. (ii) Low valid JSON rate: invalid/incomplete outputs break evaluation. (iii) Poor calibration: high ECE/Brier, meaning confidence is unreliable. (iv) Class-selective failure: near-zero F1 on MS/Other while tumors are strong. (v) Cost explosion: few-shot prompting leads to prohibitive token usage/latency.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Sanity check: Run evaluation on 10 easy samples (clear tumor vs. normal) to verify prompt and model produce valid JSON and reasonable predictions. Ensure script parses outputs correctly.
  2. Screening run: Execute Phase 1 on 30% subset for all 20 models. Identify models failing basic criteria (e.g., <50% valid JSON, abstention >20%, Macro-F1 <0.2) for early elimination.
  3. Prompt sensitivity test: For a retained model (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Flash), compare zero-shot vs. few-shot on a held-out dev set to quantify performance gain and cost increase before full evaluation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How do multimodal large language models perform on volumetric (3D) neuroimaging analysis tasks requiring multi-slice reasoning and spatial consistency across entire scans, compared to their performance on individual 2D slices?
- Does integrating structured clinical metadata (patient history, symptoms, laboratory results) alongside neuroimaging inputs improve the diagnostic accuracy, calibration, and clinical utility of multimodal LLMs?
- Can domain-specific fine-tuning or instruction tuning on curated neuroimaging datasets significantly improve the calibration, reliability, and safety of multimodal LLMs for clinical neuroimaging?
- How do multimodal LLMs perform on longitudinal neuroimaging tasks involving multiple timepoints for assessing disease progression or treatment response?
- What human-AI interaction paradigms most effectively support clinicians in using multimodal LLMs for neuroimaging interpretation while maintaining safety, efficiency, and trust?

## Limitations
- The 2D slice input cannot capture 3D context needed for conditions like multiple sclerosis, possibly understating model potential.
- Public dataset sources may not mirror real clinical case mix or image acquisition variability, affecting generalizability.
- Enforcing structured JSON output may disadvantage models capable of richer free-text reasoning; validity metrics partly reflect prompt compliance rather than diagnostic skill.
- Fixed few-shot exemplars risk being non-representative or inadvertently overlapping with test distribution, potentially inflating performance.
- API-based proprietary models (GPT-5 Chat, Gemini 2.5) may have changed since evaluation, limiting exact replication.

## Confidence
- Imaging attributes (modality, plane) solved: High (F1 ≈1.0 consistently)
- Diagnostic reasoning (subtype) remains challenging: High (low Macro-F1 for MS/rare classes)
- Tumor classification reliability: Medium-High (best scores but still <90%)
- Stroke solvability: Medium (moderate scores, variable by modality)
- Few-shot performance-cost trade-off: High (clear gains with token/latency increase)
- Gemini 2.5-Pro / GPT-5-Chat superiority: Medium (version-specific; may not hold over time)
- Open-weight (MedGemma) parity: Medium (single-model evidence; needs broader validation)

## Next Checks
1. **Integrate volumetric input**: Test top models with multi-slice or full 3D volumes; expect improved MS/rare abnormality detection.
2. **Split robustness assay**: Re-run benchmark with ≥5 different random seeds; assess stability of rankings and few-shot effects.
3. **External clinical validation**: Evaluate on a held-out hospital dataset (different scanner protocols); measure generalizability and calibration shift.
