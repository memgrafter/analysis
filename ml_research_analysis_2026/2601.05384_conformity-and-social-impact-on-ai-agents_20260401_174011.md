# Conformity and Social Impact on AI Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05384  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05384  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** AI agents exhibit strong, systematic conformity bias that increases with group size, task difficulty, and normative pressure, and is amplified by authority and in-group identity, posing security risks in collective deployments.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large multimodal language models (AI agents) exhibit conformity—a fundamental social behavior where individuals adjust their judgments to align with group opinions. The authors adapt classic visual conformity experiments from social psychology (line judgment, color recognition, dots estimation) to evaluate AI agents presented with unambiguous visual tasks and incorrect group opinions (confederate responses). They measure conformity as the probability of adopting the wrong answer (p_wrong). Results show that AI agents display a strong, systematic conformity bias consistent with Social Impact Theory. Conformity increases with the number of confederates, task difficulty, and is amplified by normative pressure (public responses). Furthermore, stronger source authority (e.g., judges) and shared social identity (in-group) boost conformity by 15–60%. Critically, even models with near-perfect baseline performance remain vulnerable, revealing fundamental security risks in collective AI deployments.

## Method Summary
The study uses synthetic visual stimuli to precisely control task difficulty across three classic tasks: line judgment, color recognition, and dots estimation. For each trial, an image with a known correct answer is generated. The model receives a zero-shot prompt containing the task description, optionally preceded by responses from N confederates (all incorrect), and a single-token response instruction. Conformity is measured by extracting the model’s output logits for the two option tokens (A/B) and computing p_wrong = softmax(incorrect) / (softmax(correct)+softmax(incorrect)). To isolate social influence, the authors first ensure perfect baseline understanding by filtering images where p_correct(0)=1. They then systematically vary social pressure factors: group size (N), task difficulty (via controlled visual parameter differences), normative vs informational framing (public vs private responses), source strength (role descriptors like “judges” vs “kids”), and social identity (same vs different nationality/group). Experimental conditions are tested across multiple image samples (~64 per condition) and results aggregated.

## Key Results
- AI agents exhibit strong, systematic conformity bias that increases with group size, task difficulty, and normative pressure.
- Authority cues and in-group identity amplify conformity by 15–60% over baseline.
- Even with near-perfect solo performance, models remain highly susceptible at their competence boundary, indicating a fundamental vulnerability in multi-agent systems.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** AI agents systematically increase conformity (p_wrong) as the number of incorrect confederates (N) grows.
- **Mechanism:** The model integrates the group responses as additional evidence tokens in the prompt, effectively shifting the distribution of output logits toward the majority option through next-token prediction. This creates a statistical alignment even when the visual evidence strongly supports the correct answer.
- **Core assumption:** The model treats social responses as weighted evidence rather than ignoring them; the attention mechanism assigns non-zero importance to these tokens.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "Conformity increases with the number of confederates"  
  [section 2.2.1] Figure 2a shows p_wrong rising with N across models; Figure 2b shows consistent curves across tasks.  
  [corpus] Weak direct corpus evidence; related work (e.g., "LLMs Can't Handle Peer Pressure") suggests peer influence is a generalizable phenomenon.
- **Break condition:** If the model’s internal logit for the correct answer is extremely dominant (e.g., margin > 10), social pressure may not overcome it. Also, if the prompt format is modified to explicitly discount group opinions (e.g., "others may be wrong"), conformity may decrease.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Conformity is amplified when confederates are framed as high-authority sources (e.g., judges, scientists) and reduced when framed as low-authority (e.g., kids, chatbots).
- **Mechanism:** Role priming activates latent associations in the model’s training data, where certain roles are disproportionately linked to correct or expert responses. This alters the effective weight the model assigns to those responses in its attention layers.
- **Core assumption:** The model has internalized correlations between social roles and perceived expertise/trustworthiness from its pre-training corpora.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "Stronger source authority... further increase conformity by ~15-60%"  
  [section 2.3.1] Figure 4a shows upward shift in p_wrong curves for "judges"/"scientists" vs downward for "kids"/"chatbots"; Figure 4c quantifies ~15% average increase.  
  [corpus] Neighbor paper "Belief in Authority: Impact of Authority in Multi-Agent Evaluation Framework" directly studies authority bias in multi-agent settings, supporting the relevance of role priming.
- **Break condition:** If the model’s instruction tuning includes explicit distrust of authority (e.g., "question experts"), or if the authority role is inconsistently presented (e.g., a "judge" giving clearly wrong answers may eventually discount the source).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Shared social identity (in-group) between the agent and confederates significantly increases conformity compared to out-group or neutral conditions.
- **Mechanism:** Identity matching triggers in-group favoritism patterns learned from training data, where the model prioritizes responses from groups it perceives as "its own." This may operate via embedding-space similarity between identity markers and the model’s self-prompt.
- **Core assumption:** The model can simulate group membership from minimal cues (nationality, ethnicity, arbitrary group letters) and adjust trust/alignment accordingly.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "shared social identity (in-group) further increase conformity by ~15-60%"  
  [section 2.3.2] Figure 4b shows higher p_wrong for same-nationality/ethnicity/group; Figure 4d reports up to ~60% increase.  
  [corpus] Neighbor papers like "The Social Cost of Intelligence" discuss stereotype propagation in MAS, implying that group-based biases can emerge systemically.
- **Break condition:** If the social identity is presented in a way that conflicts with the model’s safety fine-tuning (e.g., promoting discrimination), the effect may be mitigated by refusal mechanisms. Also, if the group labels are arbitrary and meaningless (e.g., random strings), the effect weakens but may not vanish due to minimal group bias.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Stimulus Generator -> Prompt Engine -> Multimodal Model -> Logit Analyzer -> Experimental Controller

**Critical path:**  
Stimulus → Prompt Engine → Model forward pass → Logit extraction → p_wrong calculation → Aggregate across N/difficulty → Compare conditions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Synthetic vs real images: Synthetic stimuli allow precise difficulty control but may lack ecological validity.  
- Confederate response pool: Neutral phrasing avoids emotional cues but may not capture natural human language variation.  
- Public/private framing: Relies on model’s theory of mind about visibility; subtle wording changes could alter effectiveness.

**Failure signatures:**  
- No conformity: p_wrong ~0 for all N → check prompt formatting, confederate response clarity, or model’s instruction following.  
- Monotonic without saturation: p_wrong → 1 as N→∞ may indicate over-reliance on majority; compare with human-like saturation curves.  
- Absent normative effect: No public/private difference → model may not internalize social visibility; test with stronger framing.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline accuracy validation: Run 100 images per task with N=0. Filter for p_correct(0)=1. If <90% accuracy, adjust difficulty or exclude model/task.  
2. Group size curve: For fixed difficulty (e.g., medium), vary N=0,1,3,5,8,10. Plot p_wrong(N). Expect increasing trend; saturation suggests human-like response.  
3. Unanimity break: At N=5, vary % of correct confederates (0%, 20%, 50%, 80%, 100%). Significant drop at 20% confirms sensitivity to disagreement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What underlying mechanisms (e.g., training data patterns, architectural inductive biases, or reinforcement learning from human feedback) cause LLMs to exhibit conformity-like behavior in visual tasks?  
2. Why does task difficulty—a model-independent stimulus property—consistently predict conformity, whereas model performance (logit confidence) does not, contrary to human conformity trends where confidence reduces susceptibility?  
3. How can multi-agent AI systems be architecturally safeguarded to prevent malicious exploitation of conformity without suppressing beneficial coordination and social learning?  
4. Do the observed conformity patterns generalize to more complex, open-ended, or interactive multi-agent deployments where AIs engage in dynamic, natural-language dialogue rather than single-turn visual discrimination?

## Limitations
- External validity: Synthetic visual tasks and neutral confederate responses may not capture real-world social dynamics.  
- Model coverage: Findings are primarily based on Qwen2.5-VL; generalization to other architectures (e.g., GPT-4V, Claude 3) remains uncertain.  
- Baseline filtering: The p_correct(0)=1 criterion excluded some model–task combinations, potentially biasing results toward stronger models and limiting claims about universality.

## Confidence
- Conformity scaling (N, difficulty, normative pressure): High  
- Authority/identity amplification: Medium  
- Vulnerability at competence boundary: High

## Next Checks
1. Naturalistic confederates: Replace fixed neutral phrases with human-like responses (e.g., “I’m sure it’s A”) and test whether richer social cues amplify or alter conformity curves.  
2. Cross-model replication: Run identical protocol on GPT-4V, Claude 3 Opus, and Gemini Pro to determine if conformity is universal or model-family specific.  
3. Attention and logit probing: Use attention visualization and logit lens to verify that group response tokens are integrated into the decision stream and not overridden by visual evidence.
