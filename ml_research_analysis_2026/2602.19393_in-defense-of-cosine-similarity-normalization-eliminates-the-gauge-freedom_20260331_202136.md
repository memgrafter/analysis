# In Defense of Cosine Similarity: Normalization Eliminates the Gauge Freedom

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19393
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19393
- Reference count: 5
- Primary result: Constraining embeddings to the unit sphere during training eliminates the gauge freedom, and on the sphere cosine distance equals half the squared Euclidean distance, making cosine- and Euclidean-based rankings identical.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the gauge freedom problem in unconstrained matrix factorization, where cosine similarity of embeddings is arbitrary due to an anisotropic rescaling invariance. The authors prove that by constraining embeddings to the unit sphere during training (not post-hoc), this ambiguity is removed. They show that on the unit sphere, cosine distance is exactly half the squared Euclidean distance, implying monotonic equivalence in neighbor rankings. The pathology thus arises from a mismatch between the dot-product objective and the cosine metric, not from cosine similarity itself; the fix is to train with an explicit sphere constraint.

## Method Summary
The core method is to impose an explicit unit sphere constraint on the embedding vectors during the training of matrix factorization models. This is achieved by incorporating a normalization layer directly into the computational graph (e.g., L2-normalization after the embedding head and before the loss). The training objective remains a dot-product based loss (e.g., squared error or contrastive loss), but the feasible set is restricted to the sphere. This breaks the gauge symmetry because any non-scalar diagonal rescaling would move embeddings off the sphere, violating the constraint. The authors provide theoretical proofs: on the sphere, cosine distance \(d_C = 1 - \cos\theta\) satisfies \(d_C = \frac{1}{2} \|x-y\|^2\) for unit vectors \(x,y\), guaranteeing ranking equivalence between cosine and Euclidean metrics.

## Key Results
- Gauge freedom: For any positive diagonal matrix \(D\), the transformed factor pair \((\hat{A}D, D^{-1}\hat{B}^\top)\) yields the same reconstruction loss but different cosine similarities.
- Sphere constraint elimination: Enforcing \(\hat{b}_j \in \mathbb{S}^{k-1}\) during training makes the feasible set non-invariant under non-scalar \(D\), thus removing the ambiguity.
- Metric equivalence: On \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\), \(d_C = \frac{1}{2} d_E^2\), so k-NN and clustering results using cosine or Euclidean distances are identical.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Gauge Freedom in Unconstrained Objective
- **Claim:** An unconstrained dot-product matrix factorization objective possesses a gauge freedom: for any positive diagonal matrix \(D\), the transformed factor pair \((\hat{A}D, D^{-1}\hat{B}^\top)\) produces identical predictions and loss, but yields different cosine similarities between item embeddings.
- **Mechanism:** The reconstruction loss \(\|A - \hat{A}\hat{B}^\top\|_F^2\) is invariant under the transformation \(\hat{B} \mapsto D^{-1}\hat{B}\) because \((\hat{A}D)(D^{-1}\hat{B}^\top) = \hat{A}\hat{B}^\top\). This defines an orbit of equivalent solutions parameterized by \(D\). Cosine similarity, \(\frac{\langle x,y \rangle}{\|x\|\|y\|}\), is *not* invariant under the anisotropic scaling \(x \mapsto Dx\), so computed similarities depend on which point on the orbit the optimizer lands on.
- **Break condition:** The gauge freedom disappears if the optimization problem's feasible set does not contain the entire \(D\)-orbit. This occurs if the objective itself depends on the individual embeddings (e.g., via normalization terms) or if explicit constraints are imposed.

### Mechanism 2: Sphere Constraint Breaks Symmetry
- **Claim:** Explicitly constraining embeddings to the unit sphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\) *during* training (not post-hoc) eliminates the \(D\)-ambiguity because the sphere is not invariant under non-scalar diagonal transformations.
- **Mechanism:** The feasible set becomes \((\mathbb{R}^{m \times k} \times (\mathbb{S}^{k-1})^n)\). For any \(D \neq cI\), the transformation \(b \mapsto D^{-1}b\) maps a unit vector to a non-unit vector (since \(\|D^{-1}b\|^2 = \sum_i d_i^{-2} b_i^2 \neq 1\) unless \(b\) is axis-aligned and \(D\) is scalar). Thus, a non-trivial \(D\) moves a valid solution outside the feasible set; the symmetry is broken at the level of the optimization landscape.
- **Break condition:** If normalization is applied only after training (post-hoc), the optimizer has already selected a specific, arbitrary \(D\) from the orbit. The resulting angular relationships on the sphere are an artifact of that choice and are not guaranteed to be stable or semantically meaningful.

### Mechanism 3: Cosine and Euclidean Metrics are Equivalent on Sphere
- **Claim:** On the unit sphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\), cosine distance \(d_C = 1 - \cos\theta\) is exactly half the squared Euclidean distance \(d_E^2 = 2(1 - \cos\theta)\). This monotonic equivalence means all ranking-based tasks (k-NN, clustering) produce identical results under either metric.
- **Mechanism:** For unit vectors \(x,y\), \(\|x-y\|^2 = 2 - 2\langle x,y \rangle = 2(1 - \langle x,y \rangle)\). Since \(\cos\theta = \langle x,y \rangle\) on the sphere, \(d_C = 1 - \langle x,y \rangle = \frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^2\). The function \(t \mapsto \sqrt{2t}\) is strictly increasing on \([0,2]\), so ordering by \(d_C\) is identical to ordering by \(d_E\).
- **Break condition:** If embeddings are not perfectly normalized (e.g., due to numerical error or training without a strict sphere constraint), the relationship becomes approximate, and rankings may diverge, especially for vectors with small norms.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Gauge Freedom (Symmetry of Objective)**
  - *Why needed here:* To understand why learned embeddings can be non-unique and why metrics like cosine can return arbitrary values if the training objective doesn't fix the geometry.
  - *Quick check question:* Given a loss \(L(\hat{A}\hat{B}^\top)\), what transformations of \((\hat{A},\hat{B})\) leave the loss unchanged? Can you derive the \(D\)-orbit?

- **Concept: Riemannian Manifold Constraint**
  - *Why needed here:* The unit sphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\) is not just a set; it's a manifold with an inherited metric. Optimization *on* this manifold (via projected gradients or intrinsic losses) enforces a geometry compatible with cosine similarity.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the tangent space of \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\) at a point \(x\)? How does a gradient step on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) differ from a Riemannian gradient step on the sphere?

- **Concept: Monotonic Equivalence of Metrics**
  - *Why needed here:* It justifies using cosine for retrieval even if Euclidean is faster (via FAISS's flat L2 on normalized vectors). The key is that ranking preservation, not scale, matters.
  - *Quick check question:* If \(d_1(x,y) = f(d_2(x,y))\) with \(f\) strictly increasing, do k-NN results using \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) always agree? What if \(f\) is only non-decreasing?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Embedding head -> Normalization layer -> Loss function; Optimizer updates parameters with gradients flowing back through normalization.
- **Critical path:** Normalization **must** be a node in the training computational graph between the embedding head and the loss. A common anti-pattern: head → loss (which internally normalizes) → backprop. This still lets gradients alter the head's scale. Correct pattern: head → **normalization layer** → loss.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Projected gradient* vs. *intrinsic loss*: Projected SGD (renormalize after each step) is simpler but may drift due to discrete projections. Intrinsic methods (e.g., using geodesic loss) are more faithful but require Riemannian optimizers (e.g., `torch.optim.Adam` on manifold parameters).
  - *Numerical stability:* Adding a small \(\epsilon\) to the norm prevents division by zero but slightly moves points off-sphere. Usually acceptable if \(\epsilon\) is tiny relative to typical norms.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Cosine similarity scores are unstable across random seeds or learning rate changes for the same data.
  - Post-hoc normalization of unconstrained embeddings yields different k-NN results than normalizing *during* training.
  - Inspecting embedding norms shows high variance (e.g., std dev > 0.1) instead of being tightly clustered around 1.0.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Gauge invariance test:** Train two MF models with identical data and seed, but apply a fixed random non-scalar \(D\) *only* to the item embeddings after training. Compute cosine similarities. If the model was trained *without* sphere constraint, results should differ significantly. If trained *with* constraint (or if \(D\) is scalar), results should be identical.
  2. **Metric equivalence test:** Train a model with explicit sphere constraint. For a validation set, compute k-NN lists using (a) cosine on normalized embeddings, (b) Euclidean on normalized embeddings. The lists should match exactly. As a control, repeat with unnormalized embeddings—lists will diverge.
  3. **Norm distribution audit:** During training of a sphere-constrained model, track the mean and std of embedding norms (before the normalization layer). The std should be near zero (< 1e-4), confirming the constraint is actively enforced by the optimizer, not just a post-hoc trick.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Do common deep learning normalization layers (BatchNorm, LayerNorm) implicitly eliminate the gauge freedom without an explicit sphere constraint?
  - *Basis in paper:* Section 5 lists BatchNorm, dropout, and weight decay as sources of implicit scaling that can distort cosine similarity.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper analyzes only explicit L2 constraints; it does not theoretically characterize whether standard normalization layers accidentally enforce spherical geometry or introduce new, hidden anisotropies.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Prove that batch statistics induce a gauge transformation not equivalent to a fixed sphere constraint; or empirically measure cosine stability across architectures with systematic ablation of normalization layers.

- **Open Question 2:** For models trained without sphere constraints, is there any regime where post-hoc normalization yields approximately correct cosine similarities, or is it fundamentally unreliable?
  - *Basis in paper:* Theorem 7 shows π∘D ≠ D∘π, implying post-hoc normalization cannot exactly recover true angles; the paper dismisses it, but practitioners often use it.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper makes an absolute claim without quantifying the distortion or identifying conditions (e.g., near-isotropy of embeddings) where the error might be negligible for retrieval.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Derive bounds on cosine distortion as a function of embedding anisotropy and gauge magnitude; run large-scale benchmarks comparing post-hoc versus in-training normalization on downstream tasks.

- **Open Question 3:** Is the unit sphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\) mathematically the only submanifold of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) on which cosine distance, Euclidean distance, and geodesic distance are monotonic equivalents for all point pairs?
  - *Basis in paper:* Section 6.3 asserts uniqueness based on three desiderata but provides no proof.
  - *Why unresolved:* The argument is heuristic; other manifolds (e.g., ellipsoids with specific axes ratios, or learned radial functions) could potentially satisfy the equivalence under restrictions.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* A theorem classifying all submanifolds where \(d_C\), \(d_E\), and \(d_{geo}\) are monotonically related; or a constructive counterexample demonstrating a non-spherical manifold with this property.

- **Open Question 4:** Does the Geometry–Objective Alignment Principle formally extend to non-linear deep models and learned metrics (e.g., Mahalanobis networks)?
  - *Basis in paper:* Section 6.2 proposes the principle as a general lesson from the analysis.
  - *Why unresolved:* The principle is stated heuristically without a formal definition linking symmetry groups of objectives and metrics; deep learning losses are non-convex and rarely have closed-form symmetries.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Formalize alignment using group actions on the loss landscape; prove necessary/sufficient conditions for a metric to be meaningful given an objective’s invariance properties; experimental validation on deep metric learning losses.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees assume perfect enforcement of the sphere constraint; numerical errors may weaken them.
- Analysis is restricted to linear matrix factorization; extension to deep non-linear models is not rigorous.
- Training hyperparameters for constrained MF are not provided; improper choices could inhibit convergence or gauge breaking.

## Confidence
- Gauge freedom exists: High (Proposition 6).
- Sphere constraint eliminates gauge: High in theory; Medium in practice due to enforcement challenges.
- Cosine = ½ Euclidean² on sphere: High (Theorem 4).
