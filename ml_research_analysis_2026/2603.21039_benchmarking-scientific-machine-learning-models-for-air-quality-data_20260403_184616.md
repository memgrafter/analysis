# Benchmarking Scientific Machine Learning Models for Air Quality Data

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.21039
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21039
- **Reference count:** 39
- **Primary result:** Physics-guided deep learning models, especially LSTM+Physics, consistently achieve the lowest multi-horizon AQI forecast errors, with the largest relative gains for PM2.5 at short horizons.

## Executive Summary
This study benchmarks machine learning models for multi-horizon Air Quality Index (AQI) forecasting in Dallas County, addressing the lack of region-specific evaluation. Using EPA PM2.5 and O3 data from 2022–2024, it compares linear regression, SARIMAX, MLP, and LSTM models, including physics-guided variants that enforce the EPA’s piecewise AQI formulation through a weighted loss function. Across forecast horizons of 1, 7, 14, and 30 days, deep learning models—particularly LSTM—outperform classical baselines on MAE, RMSE, and NMSE. Physics guidance improves performance most noticeably for PM2.5 at short horizons, enhancing stability and ensuring physically consistent pollutant–AQI relationships; for O3, benefits are more modest, primarily aiding prediction stability. The results demonstrate that lightweight physics integration meaningfully improves AQI forecasting, with effectiveness varying by pollutant and horizon.

## Method Summary
**Data.** Daily mean pollutant concentrations (PM2.5 in µg/m³, O3 in ppm) and AQI are averaged across three EPA stations in Dallas County (Convention Center, Dallas Hinton, Dallas Bexar Street) for the period 2022–2024. After dropping days with any missing values, the datasets contain 1094 records for PM2.5 and 1091 for O3.

**Task.** Predict the AQI at a future date (future AQI) given the current day’s pollutant concentration and AQI. Four forecast horizons are evaluated: 1, 7, 14, and 30 days.

**Models.** Baselines: Linear Regression (LR), SARIMAX, Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM). Two physics-informed variants: MLP+Physics and LSTM+Physics, which add a penalty term that drives the model output toward the EPA’s piecewise AQI mapping of the current pollutant.

**Training procedure.** Chronological 80%–20% train-test split prevents data leakage. Preprocessing: LR uses raw features; SARIMAX uses AQI series with previous day’s pollutant as exogenous regressor (order (1,1,1)×(1,0,1,7)); MLP/MLP+Physics apply StandardScaler to features; LSTM/LSTM+Physics scale features and target to [–1,1] with MinMaxScaler. Hyperparameters: MLP: two hidden layers (64,32), ReLU, AdamW lr=0.001, full-batch; LSTM: two stacked LSTM layers (32 units each) followed by dense layers (128,64,32), ReLU, dropout, Adam lr=0.0001, batch size 32, ReduceLROnPlateau, early stopping patience=20. The physics loss combines data MSE and physics MSE with weights (λ_data, λ_phys); the best performing weight pairs per pollutant and horizon are reported.

**Evaluation.** Test predictions are inverse-transformed to original AQI units and compared using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Normalized Mean Squared Error (NMSE) (MSE divided by test AQI variance).

## Key Results
- Deep learning models (MLP, LSTM) consistently beat classical baselines (LR, SARIMAX) in MAE/RMSE across all horizons for both pollutants.
- LSTM achieves the lowest errors overall, with improvements up to ~20% over linear models. Adding the EPA physics constraint significantly reduces PM2.5 forecast errors at LAG=1 (e.g., LSTM+Physics RMSE ~10.6 vs vanilla LSTM ~10.9) and yields physically plausible pollutant–AQI relationships.
- For O3, the physics term yields smaller error reductions but enhances prediction stability; notably, MLP+Physics surpasses LSTM+Physics at LAG=14 and LAG=30.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Physics guidance via weighted loss improves stability and consistency**  
- **Claim:** Physics guidance via a weighted loss improves prediction stability and enforces physical consistency of pollutant–AQI relationships, especially for PM2.5 at short horizons.  
- **Mechanism:** The total loss combines a data-driven MSE term and a physics term that penalizes deviation from the EPA piecewise AQI mapping; scalar weights (λ_data, λ_phys) balance data fit and physical plausibility, steering the model toward outputs that respect the known monotonic breakpoint physics.  
- **Core assumption:** The EPA breakpoint formula accurately captures the macroscopic transformation from pollutant concentration to AQI; imposing it as a soft constraint does not overly restrict the model’s ability to learn data patterns.  
- **Evidence:** Abstract states physics guidance improves stability and yields physically consistent relationships; Section 4.3 details the physics-loss implementation; neighboring corpus includes several works on embedding physics in air quality ML.  
- **Break condition:** If the piecewise AQI formula fails to represent region-specific transformations (e.g., O3 photochemical dynamics) or λ_phys is set too high, the added constraint may conflict with data patterns, leading to negligible gains or even degraded accuracy.

**Mechanism 2: LSTM captures temporal dependencies better than simpler models**  
- **Claim:** Deep sequence models (LSTM) consistently achieve lower prediction errors than feedforward (MLP) and classical linear models across all horizons.  
- **Mechanism:** LSTMs maintain a hidden state that aggregates information over time, enabling them to learn long-range autocorrelations and non-stationary patterns in the AQI series. The stacked architecture (32 hidden units per layer) followed by dense layers provides representational capacity beyond a single-day input window used in LR/SARIMAX/MLP.  
- **Core assumption:** The AQI time series contains meaningful sequential dependencies extending at least several days into the past; a sequence length of 1 (current day’s pollutant+AQI) suffices when combined with the LSTM’s internal memory to model future horizons.  
- **Evidence:** Abstract reports deep learning outperformance; Tables 5–6 show LSTM yields lower MAE/RMSE than LR, SARIMAX, and MLP in most experiments; neighboring papers use LSTM/BiLSTM variants for AQI forecasting.  
- **Break condition:** In data-scarce regimes or when the training series lacks representative temporal patterns (e.g., regime shifts due to extreme events), LSTM may overfit and simpler models with strong inductive biases (e.g., linear trends) could perform more robustly.

**Mechanism 3: Differential pollutant response due to physics of AQI mapping**  
- **Claim:** The benefit of physics guidance is significantly larger for PM2.5 than for O3, particularly at short horizons.  
- **Mechanism:** PM2.5 exhibits a relatively monotonic and stable relationship with AQI as defined by EPA breakpoints, so the physics loss effectively regularizes the model toward physically plausible outputs. For O3, the breakpoint mapping is less representative of its diurnal and photochemical cycles; thus the physics term primarily acts as a stabilizer rather than a strong error reducer.  
- **Core assumption:** The EPA piecewise AQI formulation is a more accurate macroscopic descriptor for PM2.5 than for O3 when using daily aggregated data; O3’s variability is driven by factors beyond static concentration‑to‑AQI breakpoints.  
- **Evidence:** Abstract notes larger benefits for PM2.5 and modest for O3; Section 6.3 tables show PM2.5 gains while O3 improvements are smaller or statistically similar across λ settings; neighboring works often focus on PM2.5 imputation/prediction with physics guidance.  
- **Break condition:** If regional O3 chemistry deviates substantially from EPA breakpoints (e.g., due to local emission mixtures) or λ_phys is too low to influence learning, the modest benefits may vanish and added complexity may not be justified.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
EPA daily CSV ingestion → station-wise aggregation → uniform date index → lag-wise dataset construction (Algorithm 1) → chronological train/test split → scaling (StandardScaler for MLP/MLP+Phys; MinMaxScaler for LSTM/LSTM+Phys) → model training (LR, SARIMAX, MLP, LSTM, physics variants) → evaluation (MAE, RMSE, NMSE) → results storage.

**Critical path (first reproducible experiment)**  
1. Download EPA daily data for Dallas County (2022–2024).  
2. Run Algorithm 1 to produce D(PM2.5, LAG=1) and D(O3, LAG=1).  
3. Perform 80/20 chronological split and apply MinMaxScaler to the training set.  
4. Instantiate LSTM with default hyperparameters (two stacked LSTM layers with 32 hidden units each, Adam optimizer lr=0.001).  
5. Train for up to 200 epochs with early stopping (patience=20).  
6. Predict on the test set, inverse‑scale, compute MAE/RMSE; verify numbers match Table 5/6 within tolerance.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Model capacity vs. data size: LSTM requires more samples to avoid overfitting; with limited data, MLP or LR may be safer.  
- Physics weight selection: Larger λ_phys enforces physical consistency but risks underfitting data; optimal λ varies by pollutant and horizon.  
- Sequence length: The experiments use T=1; extending the look‑back window could improve LSTM performance for longer horizons but increases memory and vanishing‑gradient risk.  
- Scaler choice: MinMax for LSTM stabilizes gradients but requires careful inverse‑transform; StandardScaler for MLP preserves interpretability of raw AQI units.

**Failure signatures**  
- Data leakage: Test MAE far below training MAE or near‑perfect scores → likely non‑chronological split or scaling fitted on full data. Always fit scaler only on training portion.  
- Physics loss stagnation: L_phys remains high while L_data decreases → may indicate conflict; try reducing λ_phys or verifying breakpoint implementation.  
- LSTM divergence/loss explosion: Often caused by poor scaling, too high learning rate, or missing gradient clipping; ensure inputs normalized to [–1,1] and consider lowering lr.  
- SARIMAX crashes: Typically due to insufficient seasonal order or non‑stationary series; check stationarity tests and adjust (p,d,q)(P,D,Q) parameters accordingly.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Baseline LSTM replication: Train LSTM on PM2.5 LAG=1 without physics. Expected test MAE ≈ 8.1761, RMSE ≈ 10.6289 (Table 5).  
2. Physics-only MLP sanity check: Set λ_data=0, λ_phys=1.0 for MLP+Physics on PM2.5 LAG=1; the model should still learn a plausible mapping (MAE may be higher than baseline but not catastrophic).  
3. Horizon sensitivity comparison: Train LR, SARIMAX, MLP, and LSTM on O3 for all four lags; generate a plot of RMSE vs. LAG to visualize the increasing gap between LSTM and simpler baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why does physics guidance improve PM2.5 forecasts more effectively than O3 forecasts, especially at short horizons?**  
   - *Basis:* The paper explicitly states that O3’s “straight AQI physics limitations are less useful” due to complicated photochemical reactions, weather, and precursor emissions, whereas PM2.5’s pollutant–AQI relationship is direct and stable.  
   - *Why unresolved:* It does not decompose which specific atmospheric mechanisms of O3 formation (e.g., nonlinear chemistry, meteorological dependencies) cause the breakpoint-based constraint to yield only stability gains without significant accuracy improvements.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments that gradually incorporate O3‑specific physical processes (e.g., photochemical reaction rates, precursor concentrations like NOx and VOCs) into the physics loss, then measure changes in error metrics and physical consistency across horizons.

2. **Why does the optimal architecture for physics-guided AQI forecasting differ by pollutant and horizon (e.g., LSTM+Physics best for PM2.5 all lags, but MLP+Physics best for long‑term O3)?**  
   - *Basis:* The paper’s key findings note LSTM+Physics performs best for PM2.5 at all lags, while for O3 MLP+Physics achieves top results at LAG=14 and LAG=30.  
   - *Why unresolved:* The study does not analyze whether the sequence‑modeling strength of LSTMs is unnecessary for O3 due to its high volatility, or whether the physics constraint interacts differently with temporal dependencies in each pollutant’s dynamics.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Ablation studies comparing LSTM and MLP with/without physics across multiple regions and pollutants, measuring not only error but also sensitivity to input sequence length and horizon, to identify which pollutant characteristics (e.g., autocorrelation, seasonality) favor one architecture over the other under physics guidance.

3. **Which specific meteorological variables (e.g., temperature, wind, humidity) most effectively complement the EPA AQI breakpoint constraint in improving multi‑horizon AQI forecasts?**  
   - *Basis:* The conclusion explicitly suggests that “considering meteorological parameters could enhance the performance of the physics‑guided model.”  
   - *Why unresolved:* The study uses only pollutant concentration and current AQI as inputs; it never tests the incremental value of adding weather or climate features to the physics‑informed loss framework.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic inclusion of individual meteorological variables (from EPA or reanalysis data) as additional inputs or as terms in the physics loss (e.g., temperature‑dependent reaction rates for O3), followed by evaluation of changes in MAE/RMSE and physical consistency scores.

4. **Would the relative benefits of physics‑guided models (particularly for O3) hold in regions with different emission sources, climate patterns, or longer historical records?**  
   - *Basis:* The study is confined to Dallas County (2022–2024), a single urban area with specific pollution profiles; the Discussion notes generalizability is untested.  
   - *Why unresolved:* No cross‑regional validation is performed; results may be idiosyncratic to North Texas’s mix of industrial, traffic, and meteorological conditions, especially for O3 formation which varies with precursors and sunlight.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate the benchmark in at least three additional regions with diverse characteristics (e.g., high‑biomass burning, industrial, coastal) and compare the delta in performance between baseline and physics‑guided models; also test with longer time series to capture regime shifts.

5. **Could incorporating more complex physical formulations—such as partial differential equations (PDEs) governing pollutant transport or photochemical reaction networks—further improve O3 forecasting beyond the simple EPA breakpoint constraint?**  
   - *Basis:* The Related Work cites studies like Shi et al. [22] that use PDEs for pollution mapping, but this paper uses only the piecewise AQI formula; the authors imply O3’s complexity might require richer physics.  
   - *Why unresolved:* The study exclusively tests a single, lightweight physics constraint (breakpoint mapping) and does not compare it against more mechanistic physics embeddings (e.g., governing equations as soft constraints).  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement a PINN variant for O3 that embeds a simplified chemical mechanism (e.g., photochemical steady‑state approximations) or advection‑diffusion terms in the loss, then benchmark against the breakpoint‑based LSTM+Physics on the same data, measuring accuracy, stability, and computational cost.

## Limitations
- Incomplete implementation details: Missing descriptions of SARIMAX multi‑horizon generation, random seeds, exact LSTM sequence length, and MLP epoch count hinder exact replication of results.
- Ambiguous physics‑loss scaling for LSTM: It is unclear whether `f_AQI(pollutant)` was scaled with the target scaler before computing the loss; this affects the effective λ_phys.
- Unreported EPA breakpoint values: The piecewise AQI thresholds used in the physics term are not listed, risking mismatch with official standards and potential misinterpretation of results.
