# MIMIC-RD: Can LLMs differentially diagnose rare diseases in real-world clinical settings?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.11559
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11559
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: Best model (Llama 3.3 70B) attains Hit@10 of 40.10% (all diseases) and 35.17% (patient-level), ~35% below prior benchmark reports.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces MIMIC-RD, a benchmark for evaluating LLMs on real-world rare disease differential diagnosis. Unlike prior evaluations that rely on idealized case studies or ICD codes (which undercount rare diseases), MIMIC-RD mines phenotypes and rare disease mentions from MIMIC-IV clinical notes and validates them against Orphanet via four medical annotators (κ=0.71). The dataset includes 145 patients with 192 rare disease instances (120 unique) and an average of 128 phenotypes each. When prompted to predict a top-10 list from phenotypes, five LLMs perform substantially worse than reported in prior work: the best model achieves Hit@10 of only ~40%. Analysis reveals that patient phenotypes typically overlap with Orphanet disease profiles, yet LLMs fail to rank the correct disease highly. This indicates a failure in synthesizing and prioritizing information from long phenotype lists rather than in extracting relevant clinical features.

## Method Summary
MIMIC-RD is constructed by applying the RDMA pipeline to MIMIC-IV discharge notes. RDMA uses an LLM to extract phenotype and rare disease mentions, maps them to HPO and Orphanet codes, and includes a verification step. Four medical annotators then validate candidate rare diseases against Orphanet definitions (requiring ≥3/4 consensus), yielding 145 patients with 192 rare disease instances. Each patient’s phenotype set (average 128 HPO terms) is paired with their confirmed disease(s). The evaluation is zero-shot: LLMs are prompted to generate a top-10 list of rare diseases from the phenotype list, with performance measured by Hit@k (k=1,5,10). Metrics are computed both at the disease-instance level (all 192 mentions) and patient-level (all 145 patients).

## Key Results
- Best model (Llama 3.3 70B) achieves Hit@10 of 40.10% (disease-instance) and 35.17% (patient-level), approximately 35% lower than prior reported results on simpler benchmarks.
- Analysis shows patients typically share multiple phenotypes with Orphanet disease profiles, yet LLMs fail to rank the correct disease, indicating the primary failure mode is ranking/prioritization rather than phenotype extraction.
- The average of 127.93 phenotypes per patient creates a long-tail information synthesis challenge that is an order of magnitude larger than in prior curated benchmarks like RAMEDIS.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Benchmark Artifact Hypothesis
- **Claim:** Performance gaps in prior LLM rare disease evaluations stem from benchmark design flaws, not inherent model limits.
- **Mechanism:** Prior benchmarks used overly simplified clinical case studies or ICD codes that undercount rare diseases, creating an easier, non-representative task where models appeared capable.
- **Core assumption:** Benchmark design fundamentally shapes measured capability; a misaligned benchmark masks true clinical performance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Paper’s abstract identifies two critical limitations of prior approaches; MIMIC-RD methodology directly maps free-text to Orphanet, bypassing ICD code undercounting.
- **Break condition:** If a similarly constructed benchmark from a different EHR system shows similarly low LLM performance, the artifact explanation is reinforced.

### Mechanism 2: Ranking Failure Mode
- **Claim:** LLMs’ primary failure is ineffective ranking/prioritization of the correct disease among many candidates, not missing relevant phenotypes.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs retrieve/recall phenotypes associated with the correct disease (overlap exists) but cannot assign a sufficiently high likelihood rank when faced with a vast differential space and a long phenotype list (~128 terms).
- **Core assumption:** Differential diagnosis is fundamentally a ranking problem; success requires synthesizing many weak signals into a single strong candidate.
- **Evidence anchors:** Paper’s Section 3 shows phenotype overlap exists in both "Hit" and "No Hit" patients; the large phenotype count amplifies the synthesis challenge.
- **Break condition:** An ablation study drastically reducing input phenotypes (to RAMEDIS-like levels) causing performance to rebound to prior ~75% Hit@10 would confirm ranking-over-extraction.

### Mechanism 3: Phenotype Density Amplifier
- **Claim:** Real-world clinical narratives contain high-density phenotypic observations, which non-linearly increases task complexity for LLMs compared to curated cases.
- **Mechanism:** MIMIC-IV notes are verbose and noisy; RDMA extracts many (~128) phenotypes, expanding the combinatorial space of disease-phenotype associations the LLM must evaluate, leading to ranking dilution.
- **Core assumption:** Curated benchmarks underestimate input complexity; difficulty scales with phenotype count.
- **Evidence anchors:** Paper’s Section 3 notes MIMIC-RD has ~10x more phenotypes per patient than prior benchmarks; Table 1 reports average 127.93 phenotypes.
- **Break condition:** If LLM performance on MIMIC-RD correlates inversely with phenotype count (e.g., lower Hit@10 for higher phenotype counts), the amplification mechanism is supported.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Ontology-Guided Information Extraction (HPO/Orphanet)
- **Why needed:** The entire benchmark hinges on mapping free-text clinical mentions to standardized phenotype (HPO) and disease (Orphanet) codes. Precision/recall of this mapping directly defines the ground truth and influences model failure modes.
- **Quick check:** If an LLM extracts "Seizures" (HP:0001250) but the note’s context suggests a different, non-seizure interpretation, is the error in extraction, mapping, or clinical reasoning? How does this propagate to the final disease ranking?

### Concept: Differential Diagnosis as a Ranking Task (Hit@k)
- **Why needed:** The evaluation metric (Hit@k) frames diagnosis as retrieving the correct disease from a vast candidate pool (~7,000 rare diseases), not generating it freely. This emphasizes prioritization under uncertainty.
- **Quick check:** Why is a Hit@1 of 20% considered poor? What would random chance Hit@1 be? (Approximately 0.014% if uniformly guessing from 7,000 diseases.)

### Concept: Real-World EHR Data Fidelity vs. Curated Benchmarks
- **Why needed:** The paper’s central thesis is that prior benchmarks lack real-world complexity (e.g., missing data, note structure, temporal spread, jargon), which is key to understanding why MIMIC-RD is harder.
- **Quick check:** Name two specific aspects of MIMIC-IV discharge summaries that are absent from textbook-style clinical case studies and could confound phenotype extraction or synthesis (e.g., nested negations, repeated observations, interdisciplinary abbreviations).

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Data Source (MIMIC-IV Notes) -> Mining Pipeline (RDMA: LLM extractor -> Verifier -> Ontology Mapper) -> Human Validation (4 annotators, consensus ≥3/4) -> Benchmark Construction (filter to ≥1 rare disease, pair phenotypes with Orphanet codes) -> Evaluation Harness (prompt template + scoring script) -> Analysis Module (phenotype overlap computation)

### Critical path
Raw Note → RDMA → Candidate Disease Mentions → Human Validation → Gold-Standard Patient-Disease Pairs → Phenotype Extraction on same notes → Final Benchmark (Phenotype List + Gold Disease). Any break in validation or consistent mapping corrupts the ground truth.

### Design tradeoffs
- *Precision vs. Recall in Mining:* RDMA’s dual-temperature run aims for high recall; subsequent validation enforces precision. This tradeoff directly determines benchmark size and noise level.
- *Phenotype Deduplication vs. Information Loss:* Removing phenotypes that are also diseases (e.g., "cancer" as both phenotype and diagnosis) reduces <2.5% of cases but avoids signal redundancy; may discard meaningful context for some conditions.
- *Evaluation Granularity:* Patient-level vs. disease-instance level metrics can diverge for multi-disease patients (average 1.32 diseases/patient); the choice affects reported performance.

### Failure signatures
- Low inter-annotator agreement (κ < 0.7) during validation, indicating ambiguous rare disease definitions in clinical text.
- High proportion of extracted phenotypes mapping to very broad HPO terms (e.g., HP:0000001 "Abrnomality"), suggesting poor extraction specificity.
- LLM outputs including common, non-Orphanet diseases in top-10, implying the prompt/context fails to constrain the candidate space to rare diseases.
- Severe performance drop from disease-instance to patient-level evaluation, indicating models struggle with patients having multiple concurrent rare diseases.

### First 3 experiments
1. Replicate RDMA mining on a 10-patient subset of MIMIC-IV to validate pipeline output format, extraction yield, and mapping consistency.
2. Run the evaluation harness (prompt + scoring) on Llama 3.3 70B for the full 145-patient set to verify the reported ~40% Hit@10 result.
3. Analyze phenotype overlap for 10 "Hit" and 10 "No Hit" patients per the paper’s method to locally confirm that overlap exists but ranking fails.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Multimodal integration:** How can lab results and imaging be combined with phenotype information to improve rare disease diagnosis? The current benchmark uses only text from notes.
2. **Ranking failure cause:** Why do LLMs fail to rank the correct disease highly even when patient phenotypes overlap with the disease’s documented profile? The underlying mechanism (e.g., model architecture, training objective) is unknown.
3. **Benchmark scaling:** Can mining a substantially larger pool of EHR notes yield a more diverse and comprehensive benchmark, and would increased data volume improve LLM performance? MIMIC-RD contains only 145 patients.
4. **Agent design:** What design principles should guide automated differential diagnosis agents that collaborate with clinicians for rare disease identification? Existing LLM evaluations treat the task as a single-step ranking, not an interactive process.

## Limitations
- Prompt engineering details (exact template, few-shot examples, output constraints) are unreported, risking performance variability in replication.
- The complete, executable RDMA pipeline code and final post-processing rules are unavailable, hindering full ground truth reproducibility.
- Findings are based solely on MIMIC-IV (a US hospital system); generalizability to other EHR systems or healthcare contexts is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Benchmark artifact hypothesis: **High** – Direct comparison to prior benchmarks with known methodological flaws is compelling.
- Ranking-failure mode: **Medium** – Phenotype overlap analysis supports it, but controlled ablations (e.g., reducing phenotype count) are needed for confirmation.
- Phenotype density amplifier: **Low** – The assertion of non-linear complexity scaling lacks quantitative validation (e.g., correlation analysis between phenotype count and Hit@k).

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt replication test:** Systematically vary prompt phrasing (e.g., HPO IDs vs. plain-language phenotypes, inclusion of Orphanet candidate list) on Llama 3.3 70B to isolate format sensitivity and its impact on Hit@10.
2. **Pipeline audit:** Re-run the RDMA mining on a 20-patient subset with the published scripts to quantify extraction yield, false positive rate, and mapping consistency to Orphanet.
3. **Phenotype-count ablation:** Stratify MIMIC-RD patients by phenotype count (e.g., quartiles) and measure Hit@10 degradation per stratum to test whether performance drops non-linearly with increasing phenotype density.
