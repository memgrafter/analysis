# Large Language Models for Biomedical Article Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11780
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11780
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Few-shot LLMs achieve macro-AUPRC 0.45–0.49, on par with conventional classifiers (0.50–0.55); token probability processing yields reliable confidence; semantic similarity-based example selection outperforms random; advanced reasoning techniques (CoT, ToT, chunking) provide no gains.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates the efficacy of large language models (LLMs) for binary classification of biomedical article abstracts within systematic reviews. Across 15 Drug Class Review datasets, the authors compare various prompt designs, output processing methods, few-shot example selection strategies, and advanced techniques against conventional machine learning baselines. Results show that the best few-shot LLM configurations achieve an average area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) of 0.45–0.49, which is comparable to traditional models such as naïve Bayes (0.5), random forest (0.5 default, 0.55 tuned), and fine-tuned transformers (0.5). Notably, processing first-token logits to derive class probabilities significantly improves confidence calibration and overall AUPRC. Semantic similarity-based selection of in-context examples consistently outperforms random selection. In contrast, chain-of-thought, tree-of-thoughts, and chunking strategies do not yield performance improvements. The findings confirm that LLMs can serve as effective zero/few-shot classifiers even in complex, imbalanced biomedical domains.

## Method Summary
The investigation uses 15 publicly available Drug Class Review datasets, each comprising PubMed abstracts labeled by experts for inclusion/exclusion in systematic reviews. Datasets are highly imbalanced (positive class 2.2–34.5%). Evaluation employs stratified 5‑fold cross‑validation with macro‑averaged metrics; the primary metric is AUPRC, supplemented by accuracy, recall, precision, F1, and MCC.

Four open‑source LLMs (Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, Llama 3.1 70B Instruct, Gemma 2 9B Chat, Mistral 7B v0.3 Instruct, quantized Q4_K_M) and two closed‑source models (GPT‑4.1 mini, GPT‑4.1 nano) are tested. Six prompt templates (A–F) varying in complexity and structure are evaluated; the best include a direct user question (“Is this an abstract …?”). Output processing methods include extracting a single word (yes/no), JSON, a numeric score (0–5), and calculating class probabilities from first‑token logits; the token‑based approach yields the highest AUPRC.

Few‑shot learning uses 1–4 examples chosen either randomly or via semantic similarity using three embedding models (all‑MiniLM‑L6_v2, all‑distilroberta‑v1, all‑mpnet‑base‑v2). Advanced techniques—chain‑of‑thought (single or double LLM calls), tree‑of‑thoughts, and chunking (splitting abstracts into 800‑character fragments)—are implemented but do not surpass the best few‑shot baseline.

Baseline classifiers include multinomial naïve Bayes, random forest (default and hyper‑parameter‑tuned), and fine‑tuned DeBERTa and SciDeBERTa‑v2, using bag‑of‑words features (TF‑IDF, stop words removed, min_df=3). Implementation relies on Python 3.11 with langchain, llama‑cpp‑python, scikit‑learn, HuggingFace transformers, and sentence‑transformers. All LLM inference uses deterministic sampling (temperature=0) except tree‑of‑thoughts (temperature=0.8, top_k=40, top_p=0.95).

## Key Results
- Few‑shot LLMs achieve macro‑AUPRC 0.45–0.49, matching conventional classifiers (0.50–0.55); token probability processing provides reliable confidence scores.
- Semantic similarity‑based example selection outperforms random selection across embedding models.
- Advanced reasoning techniques (chain‑of‑thought, tree‑of‑thoughts, chunking) yield no performance gains over the best simple few‑shot prompt.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success of LLMs in this setting can be attributed to two synergistic factors. First, the models’ pre‑training on vast text corpora imbues them with domain‑relevant knowledge, enabling accurate classification from abstract text alone when prompted appropriately. Second, the token‑probability output processing harnesses information that is normally discarded during greedy decoding: by aggregating probabilities of class‑associated tokens from the first output position, the method produces well‑calibrated confidence scores that directly correlate with true class likelihoods. Semantic similarity for in‑context example selection further boosts performance by retrieving training abstracts that are topically and lexically close to the test instance, thereby providing more informative demonstrations. The lack of benefit from chain‑of‑thought or chunking suggests that the binary decision is straightforward for modern LLMs and that additional reasoning steps introduce unnecessary complexity or fragmentation, diluting the signal.

## Foundational Learning
- **AUPRC** – Area Under the Precision‑Recall Curve; why needed: accuracy is misleading under heavy class imbalance; quick check: `sklearn.metrics.average_precision_score(y_true, y_score)`.
- **Few‑shot learning** – conditioning the model with a few labeled examples in the prompt; why needed: avoids fine‑tuning and adapts to task with minimal data; quick check: prepend 1–4 example “abstract → label” pairs to the test query.
- **Token probabilities** – retrieving logits for the first generated token to estimate class probabilities; why needed: provides calibrated confidence and优于 simple word extraction; quick check: access `output.logits` from the LLM and map tokens (e.g., “yes”, “no”) to classes.
- **Stratified cross‑validation** – splitting data while preserving class distribution in each fold; why needed: ensures reliable performance estimates on imbalanced datasets; quick check: `sklearn.model_selection.StratifiedKFold`.
- **Semantic similarity** – selecting examples by embedding distance (e.g., cosine similarity) between abstracts; why needed: improves relevance of in‑context examples; quick check: use `sentence-transformers` to embed texts and rank by similarity.
- **Class imbalance** – skewed label distribution; why needed: biases models toward majority class; quick check: compute class proportions with `np.bincount(labels)`.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: PubMed abstract → Prompt template (with few‑shot examples) → LLM inference (first‑token logits) → Token‑to‑class mapping → Class probabilities → AUPRC evaluation

**Critical path**: Prompt design → Token‑probability extraction → Semantic example selection. The prompt must elicit a clear token that maps to a class; token probabilities must be reliably captured; example selection must provide relevant context.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- Prompt brevity vs. completeness: longer prompts with examples improve accuracy but risk exceeding context windows.
- Number of few‑shot examples: more shots can help but increase memory usage; limited to ≤4 due to context length.
- Output format: simple word extraction is robust to noise; token‑probability method requires access to logits and careful token mapping but yields better calibration.
- Quantization: Q4_K_M reduces memory and accelerates inference; may slightly degrade AUPRC though not measured.

**Failure signatures**:
- Parsing failures: model outputs unexpected text (e.g., JSON with extra characters), causing class mapping to fall back to negative.
- Context overflow: abstracts plus examples exceed model’s maximum sequence length, truncation or omission of examples occurs.
- Irrelevant examples: random selection picks dissimilar abstracts, reducing few‑shot benefit.
- Token mapping mismatches: case‑sensitivity or missing tokens (e.g., model outputs “indeed” instead of “yes”) lead to dropped signals.
- Imbalance‑driven bias: classifiers may default to negative class, yielding high accuracy but low recall.

**First experiments**:
1. **Zero‑shot baseline**: Run prompt B without examples on a small subset; verify AUPRC ≈ 0.3–0.35 (as in paper) and that parsing succeeds >99%.
2. **Token vs. word extraction**: For 100 abstracts, compare AUPRC and parse success rates of Token‑Word and Word methods; expect token method to yield ~0.05 higher AUPRC and similar robustness.
3. **Few‑shot count curve**: Test 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 examples using semantic selection (all‑mpnet‑base‑v2); plot AUPRC to confirm saturation around 3‑4 shots and improvement over zero‑shot.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does the performance of LLM‑based few‑shot text classifiers degrade as the number of labeled examples available for in‑context learning decreases, and at what point do they become less competitive than conventional supervised classifiers?
- Do larger language models (e.g., >100B parameters) or encoder‑decoder architectures significantly outperform the small and mid‑size decoder‑only models evaluated in this study for biomedical article classification?
- Can task‑specific fine‑tuning of LLMs (either full fine‑tuning or parameter‑efficient methods) or training a secondary classifier on LLM internal embeddings substantially improve classification performance beyond zero/few‑shot prompting?
- What alternative output processing or inference strategies (beyond first‑token probabilities) can improve the reliability of LLM‑generated confidence scores and overall classification performance?

## Limitations
- **Data retrieval ambiguity**: The exact PubMed API parameters, rate limiting, and handling of missing abstracts are unspecified, which may affect reproducibility if re‑fetching is required.
- **Missing manual examples for advanced techniques**: The CoT/ToT experiments relied on manually crafted reasoning examples that are not provided, preventing exact replication of those negative results.
- **Limited generalizability**: Results are confined to 15 drug‑class systematic review datasets with high class imbalance; performance on other biomedical tasks remains unknown.

## Confidence
- **Overall LLM vs. conventional parity**: Medium (macro‑AUPRC difference narrow, but statistical significance not reported).
- **Token‑probability confidence scoring**: High (consistent AUPRC gains and mechanistic justification).
- **No gains from advanced techniques**: Medium (negative results plausible but depend on unreleased manual examples).
- **Semantic similarity selection**: High (clear improvement across multiple embedding models).
