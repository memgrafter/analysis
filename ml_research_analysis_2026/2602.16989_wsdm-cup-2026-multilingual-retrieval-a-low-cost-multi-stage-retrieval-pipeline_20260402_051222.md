# WSDM Cup 2026 Multilingual Retrieval: A Low-Cost Multi-Stage Retrieval Pipeline

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16989  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16989  
- **Reference count:** 1  
- **Primary result:** Achieved nDCG@20 of 0.403 and Judged@20 of 0.95 on the WSDM Cup 2026 test set with a low-cost four-stage pipeline.

## Executive Summary
This work tackles multilingual retrieval where English queries must find relevant documents across 10 million news articles in Chinese, Persian, and Russian. The authors propose a computationally efficient four-stage pipeline: first, an LLM generates a pseudo-document for GRF-style query expansion; second, BM25 performs sparse retrieval; third, a dense ranker using long-context embeddings reorders candidates; and fourth, a lightweight cross‑encoder reranks only the top‑20 results. On the official test set, the system achieves an nDCG@20 of 0.403 and a Judged@20 of 0.95. Ablations on the development set show that combining GRF expansion with dense ranking yields the highest nDCG@20 (0.5158), while the selective reranking strategy preserves early precision under strict compute constraints.

## Method Summary
The pipeline processes each English query through four sequential stages. Stage 1 uses DeepSeek‑Chat to create a news‑style pseudo‑document from the query; after lemmatization and stopword removal, the top‑30 frequent terms not already in the query are appended to form an expanded query. Stage 2 indexes the English translations of all documents with BM25 and retrieves the top‑2000 candidates using the expanded query. Stage 3 encodes the query and each candidate’s translated text (up to 5120 tokens) with `jina-embeddings-v4` (truncated to 1024 dimensions and L2‑normalized), then reorders the 2000 candidates by cosine similarity, keeping the top‑1000. Stage 4 reranks only those top‑20 with `Qwen3-Reranker-4B`, scoring each candidate as a binary relevance prediction and replacing the top‑20 order; ranks 21–1000 remain as from Stage 3. The final output maps the translated document IDs back to the original multilingual IDs.

## Key Results
- Official test nDCG@20 = 0.403, Judged@20 = 0.95.  
- Development nDCG@20 peaks at 0.5158 when dense ranking operates on GRF‑expanded queries.  
- Ablation: BM25 alone yields nDCG@20 = 0.3306; adding GRF expansion raises it to 0.4020; adding dense ranking further to 0.4975.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**GRF‑style query expansion** improves sparse retrieval by appending terms from an LLM‑generated pseudo‑document, expanding vocabulary to better match translated documents. The core assumption is that the pseudo‑document’s frequent terms are likely to appear in relevant documents. Evidence: Table 2 shows BM25 with GRF increases nDCG@20 from 0.3306 to 0.4020. Break condition: off‑topic LLM output introduces noise.  
**Dense ranking** with `jina-embeddings-v4` boosts early precision by capturing semantic similarity between the expanded query and candidate documents in a shared embedding space. The model’s long‑context capacity (5120 tokens) handles full translated articles. Evidence: Adding dense ranking to BM25 + GRF lifts nDCG@20 from 0.4020 to 0.4975. Break condition: if BM25’s top‑2000 omits relevant documents, dense ranking cannot recover them.  
**Selective top‑20 reranking** maintains strong nDCG@20 under tight budgets by applying the expensive cross‑encoder only to the most impactful positions. The dense stage is assumed to already place most relevant documents within the top‑20. Evidence: The final system uses this strategy to achieve competitive test performance while limiting reranker inference cost. Break condition: if relevant documents consistently lie just beyond rank 20, they will not be promoted.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cross‑lingual retrieval via document translation:** Translating all non‑English documents to English once allows using English‑only models (BM25, embeddings, reranker), avoiding multilingual query processing and simplifying the pipeline. *Quick check:* Why translate documents instead of queries? Consider one‑time translation cost, translation stability, and per‑query overhead.  
- **Multi‑stage retrieval (candidate generation + reranking):** Early stages quickly narrow millions to a few thousand candidates, enabling expensive reranking on a small set and balancing effectiveness with efficiency. *Quick check:* What practical problems arise if you attempt to rerank all 10 million documents with a cross‑encoder?  
- **LLM‑based query expansion (GRF):** Expands the query with terms from an LLM‑generated pseudo‑document, addressing lexical gaps caused by language differences. Unlike classic pseudo‑relevance feedback, GRF does not rely on top‑ranked retrieval results. *Quick check:* How does GRF differ from classical pseudo‑relevance feedback (PRF)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Query Expander -> Sparse Retriever -> Dense Ranker -> Re-ranker  
- **Critical path:** Raw query → GRF expansion → BM25 top‑2000 → dense top‑1000 → rerank top‑20 → final top‑1000 (original IDs)  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Document translation:* enables English‑only models but adds translation errors and storage overhead.  
  - *GRF expansion:* modest LLM cost vs. improved recall; risk of noise from off‑topic generation.  
  - *Dense ranking:* semantic power vs. higher compute; limited to BM25’s 2000 candidates.  
  - *Top‑20 reranking:* strong cross‑encoder vs. cost; assumes dense top‑20 already contain most relevance.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Low nDCG@20 with high Judged@20: top‑20 judged but irrelevant → dense or rerank ineffective.  
  - Low R@1000: sparse retrieval recall too low → check BM25 or expansion.  
  - Reranking does not change top‑20 order: reranker faulty or dense order already optimal.  
  - High latency in dense stage: embedding 2000 docs per query too slow → optimize batching or use ANN.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Run BM25 with original query (no expansion, dense, or rerank); record dev nDCG@20 and R@1000.  
  2. Add GRF expansion (BM25 only); compare gains in nDCG@20 and recall.  
  3. Add dense ranking (jina) over BM25 candidates; compare to step 2. Then optionally add reranker on top‑20 to measure incremental gain.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**How can query rewriting strategies be improved beyond GRF-style expansion for multilingual retrieval?** The current GRF uses LLM‑generated pseudo‑documents with term extraction; alternative methods (e.g., query translation, back‑translation, prompt variations) remain untested. *Resolution evidence:* Systematic comparison of rewriting strategies on the development set, measuring nDCG@20, recall, and cost.  
**Can cross‑lingual retrieval models directly match English queries to original multilingual documents without relying on machine translation?** Translation quality may degrade performance; direct cross‑lingual models could capture language‑invariant features more robustly. *Resolution evidence:* Develop and evaluate cross‑lingual embedding models trained on parallel/multilingual corpora, comparing against the translation‑based pipeline.  
**How do multi‑agent retrieval architectures, where specialized agents collaborate on different stages, compare to sequential pipelines in robustness and efficiency?** The linear pipeline may have inefficiencies or failure points; multi‑agent systems with parallel/feedback‑driven processes could enhance adaptability. *Resolution evidence:* Implement multi‑agent frameworks with defined roles and communication protocols, evaluating effectiveness (nDCG@20), cost, and resilience.  
**How can the precision‑recall trade‑off induced by GRF‑style query expansion be balanced to maximize early precision without significantly sacrificing recall?** Ablations show GRF + dense yields highest nDCG@20 but reduces R@1000 compared to dense without GRF. Mitigation strategies (e.g., adjusting expansion size, candidate fusion) are unexplored. *Resolution evidence:* Controlled experiments varying GRF parameters and candidate pool composition across recall points to identify optimal configurations under budget limits.

## Limitations
- GRF expansion quality depends on LLM output; off‑topic generation adds noise and can hurt precision.  
- Dense stage input preprocessing (field concatenation order, separator, handling missing fields) is unspecified and may impact embedding similarity.  
- Reliance on translated corpora introduces potential translation errors; BM25 default parameters vary across implementations, creating reproducibility ambiguity.

## Confidence
- **GRF‑style query expansion:** Medium (ablation shows modest gain; LLM variability introduces uncertainty).  
- **Dense ranking with `jina‑embeddings‑v4`:** High (large ablation gain, strong baseline results).  
- **Selective top‑20 reranking:** Medium (efficiency proven, but no direct ablation comparing full vs. top‑20 rerank).
