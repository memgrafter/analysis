# Human-Centric Traffic Signal Control for Equity: A Multi-Agent Action Branching Deep Reinforcement Learning Approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02959
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02959
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** MA2B-DDQN achieves the lowest aggregate individual delay and per capita delay among nine baselines, with improvements of 2.75-16.40% over advanced DRL methods and up to 37% over fixed-time signals.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces MA2B-DDQN, a multi-agent deep reinforcement learning framework for equitable multimodal traffic signal control. The method decomposes the joint action space into per-intersection green split decisions and a single global cycle duration, enabling scalable discrete control. A reward function penalizes delayed individuals across all modes (vehicles, pedestrians, transit). Evaluated on seven realistic Melbourne corridors in VISSIM, MA2B-DDQN outperforms nine state-of-the-art baselines in aggregate individual delay and per capita delay, demonstrating robustness with minimal variance and strong improvements. Ablation studies confirm the importance of both the action-branching architecture and the DDQN algorithm.

## Method Summary
The problem is multi-agent traffic signal control across a corridor, with the goal of minimizing per-traveler delay. The global state comprises per-intersection features: lane-level cell data (occupancy, speed, acceleration, passenger count), pedestrian counts at eight zones per intersection, and current phase (one-hot). Each agent (intersection) selects a local action (green split between two phases, 49 discrete options) and a shared global action (total cycle duration over the next two phases, 41 options). The action-branching architecture uses shared dense layers followed by separate heads for each branch. Training employs DDQN with a replay buffer (2e6), target network, Adam optimizer (lr=1e-4), discount γ=0.95, and Gaussian noise for exploration. Rewards are computed as the negative sum of delayed individuals (vehicle occupants with speed<5 km/h, pedestrians). The algorithm runs for 500 episodes per scenario.

## Key Results
- MA2B-DDQN attains the lowest aggregate individual delay (AID) and per capita delay (PCD) compared to nine baselines, including fixed-time and advanced DRL methods.
- Improvements over the strongest DRL baselines range from 2.75% to 16.40%; over fixed signals, improvements reach up to 37%.
- The method shows minimal variance across runs, indicating robust performance; ablations confirm the critical contributions of action branching and DDQN.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Action branching reduces the combinatorial explosion of joint action spaces by separating local and global decisions, allowing each intersection to act autonomously while still coordinating through the shared cycle duration. DDQN mitigates overestimation bias in value-based RL, leading to more stable training and better policies. The reward design directly optimizes for equitable outcomes by counting every delayed individual equally, regardless of mode. The scalable discrete control avoids continuous action pitfalls and suits signal timing constraints.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL)**: Coordinates multiple intersections without central action explosion. *Why needed?* Classic RL would suffer from exponential growth of joint actions. *Quick check*: What is the difference between independent and joint-action MARL?
- **Action branching**: Decomposes a global action into independent per-agent branches plus a global branch. *Why needed?* Reduces coordination complexity while preserving necessary coupling (cycle length). *Quick check*: In MA2B-DDQN, what are the two types of branches and what do they control?
- **Double DQN (DDQN)**: Separates action selection and evaluation to avoid overestimation. *Why needed?* Standard DQN can overestimate Q-values, harming policy quality. *Quick check*: How does DDQN compute the target Q-value differently from DQN?
- **Discrete action spaces**: Traffic signal timings are naturally discretized (phase splits, cycle lengths). *Why needed?* Enables use of value-based methods like DQN with finite action sets. *Quick check*: Why can't we directly use continuous action DDPG here?
- **Reward shaping for equity**: Counting delayed individuals across modes encourages fair treatment. *Why needed?* Traditional measures like average vehicle delay neglect pedestrians and transit. *Quick check*: How does the reward penalize delayed pedestrians versus vehicles?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:
State vector → Shared Dense (256) → [Branch1: local split for agent1 (49 units); Branch2: local split for agent2 (49 units); Branch3: local split for agent3 (49 units); Branch4: global cycle C (41 units)]

**Critical path**:
1. At decision epoch, observe state S_t.
2. Forward pass through primary network, add Gaussian noise to Q-values per branch, select p_i1 and C via argmax.
3. Apply signal timings: for each intersection i, first phase green = p_i1 × C, second phase green = (1−p_i1) × C.
4. Simulate for C seconds, accumulate instant rewards → total r_t.
5. Store transition (S_t, a_t, r_t, S_{t+C}) in replay buffer.
6. Sample minibatch, compute DDQN target, update primary network.
7. Every N steps, update target network.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Discrete vs continuous*: Discrete actions simplify learning but limit fine-grained control; branch resolution (49,41) affects granularity.
- *Local vs global*: Branching introduces local autonomy but the global cycle couples agents; potential for suboptimal coordination if local myopic.
- *Noise-driven exploration*: Adding noise to Q-values is simple but may not cover space uniformly; alternative like ε-greedy was not used.

**Failure signatures**:
- Diverging Q-values or NaN loss → learning rate too high or unstable reward scaling.
- No improvement over random → poor state representation or inadequate exploration.
- High variance across runs → insufficient training or unstable target network updates.
- Phase durations not summing to C → implementation bug in action application.

**First three experiments**:
1. **Sanity check**: Train on a single intersection with a trivial demand profile; verify that the agent learns to reduce delay compared to fixed timing.
2. **Reward verification**: Compute reward manually for a short episode to ensure delayed individuals are counted correctly (vehicle speed<5 km/h, waiting pedestrians).
3. **Baseline comparison**: Run a simple fixed-time controller (Webster) on one scenario; confirm MA2B-DDQN can achieve lower AID within a few episodes.

## Limitations
- Exact network configuration and traffic demand data for the seven Melbourne scenarios are not provided, hindering exact replication.
- Decision epoch timing and action application details are ambiguous, risking incorrect control loop implementation.
- Evaluation is limited to seven specific corridors in Melbourne, so generalizability to other cities or network topologies remains unknown.

## Confidence
- Performance superiority (lowest AID/PCD vs. baselines): Medium
- Improvement percentages (2.75%–16.40% over DRL, up to 37% over fixed): Medium
- Minimal variance and robustness: Medium
- Ablation validation (action-branching and DDQN contributions): Medium
