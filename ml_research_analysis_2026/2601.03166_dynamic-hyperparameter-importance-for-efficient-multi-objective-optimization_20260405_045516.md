# Dynamic Hyperparameter Importance for Efficient Multi-Objective Optimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03166  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03166  
- Reference count: 11  
- Primary result: HPI-ParEGO reduces normalized hypervolume regret AUC and improves Pareto front quality in multi-objective hyperparameter optimization, with significant gains after 20–30% of the evaluation budget.

## Executive Summary
Multi-objective hyperparameter optimization (MOO HPO) typically treats all hyperparameters equally, even though their importance varies with the current trade-off between objectives. This paper introduces HPI-ParEGO, which integrates dynamic hyperparameter importance (HPI) estimated via HyperSHAP into the ParEGO framework. By Shapley-valued contributions, HPI identifies the most influential hyperparameters for the scalarized objective; unimportant ones are fixed to incumbent values, shrinking the configuration space. A symmetric threshold schedule (first third: no reduction, middle third: aggressive reduction at τ=0.8, final third: no reduction) and a small random full‑space injection rate (r=0.1) balance focused search and exploration. Empirical evaluations on PyMOO and YAHPO-Gym benchmarks show that HPI-ParEGO converges faster (lower normalized hypervolume regret AUC) and attains higher‑quality Pareto fronts compared to standard ParEGO and other MOO baselines, with gains emerging after 20–30% of the budget. An ablation study highlights the importance of the symmetric schedule and fixing hyperparameters to the incumbent.

## Method Summary
HPI-ParEGO modifies ParEGO—a scalarization‑based MOO method that sequentially solves single‑objective problems—by dynamically adapting the configuration space during optimization. At each iteration, a random forest surrogate is trained on scalarized objective values derived from the history. HyperSHAP computes Shapley values for each hyperparameter to estimate its marginal contribution to improving upon the incumbent under the current scalarization weights. Hyperparameters are ranked by their Shapley values; only those whose cumulative contribution exceeds a threshold τ of the total positive magnitude are retained in the reduced space Λ′, while others are fixed to the incumbent value. The threshold τ follows a symmetric schedule: τ=0 for the first third of trials (full exploration), τ=0.8 for the middle third (aggressive reduction), and τ=0 again for the final third (full re‑exploration). Additionally, with probability r=0.1, a random configuration from the original full space is evaluated to introduce diversity and avoid premature convergence to an over‑fitted reduced space. The acquisition function (expected improvement) is optimized over Λ′ to propose the next configuration, which is then evaluated and added to the history. Weight vectors are updated every u=10 iterations to shift the scalarization focus.

## Key Results
- HPI-ParEGO achieves lower normalized hypervolume regret AUC than standard ParEGO, MO‑TPE, NSGA‑II, and DE across PyMOO synthetic and YAHPO‑Gym real‑world tasks.
- The performance advantage becomes statistically significant after 20–30% of the evaluation budget has been consumed.
- Ablation experiments confirm that the symmetric‑0.8 threshold schedule and the strategy of fixing unimportant hyperparameters to the incumbent value are critical design choices; constant reduction or no reduction perform worse.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Dynamic Configuration Space Reduction
- **Claim:** Dynamically reducing the configuration space to include only hyperparameters important for the *current scalarized objective* focuses the search on influential dimensions, accelerating convergence.
- **Mechanism:** At each ParEGO weight update, the surrogate model is queried with random configurations. HyperSHAP computes Shapley values for each hyperparameter, representing its marginal contribution to improving performance over the incumbent. The hyperparameters are ordered by Shapley value, and only those whose cumulative sum reaches a threshold τ of the total maximum improvement are retained; others are fixed to their incumbent values.
- **Core assumption:** The set of hyperparameters that matter for performance changes with the scalarization weights; filtering out unimportant ones for the *current* trade‑off reduces optimization overhead without sacrificing ability to find Pareto‑optimal solutions.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "integrate[s] dynamic hyperparameter importance estimated via HyperSHAP into the ParEGO algorithm. It reduces the configuration space during optimization by fixing unimportant hyperparameters based on the current scalarization."  
  [section 3.3] "We retain only the most relevant hyperparameters Λ′ ⊂ Λ and fix the rest to constants (we use the incumbent)."  
  The paper's own experiments provide direct empirical support.
- **Break condition:** If the scalarization weights change but the HPI estimation is too inaccurate (e.g., due to poor surrogate early on), the wrong hyperparameters may be fixed, harming convergence.

### Mechanism 2: Symmetric Threshold Schedule
- **Claim:** A symmetric threshold schedule (initial full exploration, middle focused reduction, final full exploration) balances learning HPI and fine‑tuning all parameters.
- **Mechanism:** The threshold τ is set to 0 (no reduction) for the first third of trials (initialization phase), then 0.8 (retain hyperparameters contributing 80% of total Shapley value sum) for the middle third (focus phase), and back to 0 for the final third (convergence phase).
- **Core assumption:** Early full exploration is needed to gather data for reliable HPI and surrogate training; mid‑phase aggressive reduction speeds up progress on important dimensions; late‑phase reopening the space allows final tuning of less important parameters that may still affect Pareto optimality.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 3.4] Describes the "Symmetric‑0.8" schedule and contrasts it with constant thresholding.  
  [section 4.3] Ablation shows symmetric schedule outperforms constant thresholds, especially before 60% of trials (Figure 9).
- **Break condition:** If the budget is very small, the middle focused phase may be too short to realize gains; if HPI is very unstable across weights, the schedule may switch importance sets too infrequently.

### Mechanism 3: Random Full‑Space Injections
- **Claim:** Occasionally evaluating random configurations from the original full space (with probability `r`) injects exploration to prevent the surrogate and HPI estimates from overfitting to the reduced space.
- **Mechanism:** With random chance `r` (default 0.1), instead of optimizing acquisition in the reduced space, a random configuration from the full original space Λ is evaluated and added to history H.
- **Core assumption:** Continuous reduction risks losing information about hyperparameters deemed unimportant under recent scalarizations but which may become important under future weight vectors or interact with important ones; random full evaluations mitigate this.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 3.1] "We do this not only to exploit the information on HPI gained from a restricted configuration space, which risks premature convergence or overfitting to early importance estimates, but also to generate new information on the optimization problem."  
  [section 4.3] Ablation shows `r=0.1` performs best; `r=0` is worse (Figure 10).
- **Break condition:** If `r` is too high, efficiency gains from reduction are lost; if too low, the HPI and surrogate may become biased by the reduced space.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multi‑objective scalarization (e.g., Tchebycheff)**  
  Why needed: HPI‑ParEGO extends ParEGO, which converts MOO into sequential single‑objective problems via scalarization. The HPI is computed *for each scalarization*; understanding that weights `w` change the effective objective is key.  
  Quick check: If scalarization weights emphasize objective A over B, would a hyperparameter that only affects B be considered important by HPI‑ParEGO?

- **Concept: Shapley values for hyperparameter importance**  
  Why needed: HyperSHAP uses Shapley values to allocate performance improvement (over incumbent) to hyperparameters, considering interactions. This is the estimated importance `h(Λ_j)`.  
  Quick check: Why might Shapley values (which sum to total marginal contribution) be preferred over f‑ANOVA (which sums to variance explained) in an *optimization* context?

- **Concept: Pareto front approximation in HPO**  
  Why needed: The goal is not a single best configuration but a set of trade‑offs. Evaluation uses hypervolume regret against a reference point. The method’s success is measured by speed and quality of Pareto front approximation.  
  Quick check: Why does fixing “unimportant” hyperparameters not necessarily worsen the final Pareto front approximation?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
ParEGO loop controller -> Surrogate model (Random Forest) -> HPI calculator (HyperSHAP) -> Config space adapter (threshold filter) -> Acquisition optimizer (Expected Improvement) -> Random injector (probability r). Threshold scheduler (symmetric‑0.8) provides τ to the config space adapter.

**Critical path:**  
After each evaluation, history H updates. Every `u=10` iterations, weight vector `w` is updated → new scalarization. Surrogate is retrained every 2 new evaluations → HyperSHAP computes Shapley values → threshold τ (from symmetric schedule) applied to select Λ′ → EI acquisition optimized over Λ′ → next configuration proposed → evaluation → H updated.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Threshold aggressiveness (τ): Higher τ shrinks space more but risks missing important interactions; lower τ is safer but slower.  
- Random injection rate `r`: Balances focused search vs. exploration; paper finds `r=0.1` optimal.  
- HPI method: HyperSHAP (Shapley) vs. f‑ANOVA (variance). Shapley aligns with “improvement over default” but is more expensive.  
- Weight update frequency `u`: Too frequent resets progress; too infrequent may not adapt. Paper uses `u=10`.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Early reduction (no initialization phase) leads to poor HPI estimates and premature convergence.  
- Reduced configuration space may yield no valid configurations (e.g., due to conditional dependencies); fallback to full space occurs but can cause instability.  
- HyperSHAP overhead becomes prohibitive for high‑dimensional spaces, slowing iterations.  
- Surrogate overfitting from noisy evaluations leads to poor acquisition suggestions.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Ablate τ schedule: Compare Symmetric‑0.8 vs. constant τ=0.8 vs. no reduction (τ=0). Expect symmetric to win in AUC, especially mid‑budget.  
2. Vary random chance `r`: Test `r∈{0,0.1,0.2}` on a task with known varying HPI. Expect `r=0.1` to balance best.  
3. HPI method swap‑out: Replace HyperSHAP with f‑ANOVA on same surrogate. Expect HyperSHAP to perform better due to tunability focus, but confirm computational overhead.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Under what conditions does dynamic hyperparameter importance (HPI) filtering provably accelerate convergence in scalarization‑based multi‑objective optimization?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "A full theoretical understanding of when importance‑guided filtering benefits MOO remains open." (Section 5)  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates empirical gains but lacks any theoretical analysis linking HPI properties (e.g., sparsity, magnitude) to convergence rates or sample complexity.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A formal convergence proof or bound for HPI‑ParEGO under realistic assumptions (e.g., smooth objective, limited interaction), supplemented by controlled experiments on synthetic benchmarks where HPI patterns are varied systematically.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does HPI‑guided optimization remain effective in many‑objective settings (≥4 objectives), and how must the method be adapted?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Our evaluation does not cover many‑objective optimization scenarios with more than three objectives, which present distinct challenges and dynamics, and these settings remain an open question for future research." (Section 5)  
- **Why unresolved:** The method’s reliance on scalarization may become less effective as the number of objectives grows, and HPI patterns could change drastically; this has not been investigated.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical evaluation on established many‑objective benchmarks (e.g., DTLZ5 with 4–10 objectives), comparing HPI‑ParEGO against strong baselines (e.g., NSGA‑III), and reporting metrics such as hypervolume regret and HPI stability.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can HPI estimated from low‑fidelity (e.g., early‑stop) evaluations be reliably used to adapt the configuration space in multi‑fidelity MOO?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "It is an open research problem how much information, e.g., on HPI, can be leveraged from cheap training runs to improve optimization efficiency." (Section 5)  
- **Why unresolved:** The current approach requires full evaluations; the accuracy and utility of HPI computed from incomplete (partial) runs are unknown, which is critical for multi‑fidelity methods.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments on multi‑fidelity benchmarks (e.g., YAHPO‑Gym with early stopping) where HPI is computed from varying fidelity levels, measuring the impact on total budget required to achieve a target hypervolume compared to vanilla multi‑fidelity MOO.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details (RF hyperparameters, HyperSHAP sampling, incumbent definition) hinder exact replication.  
- Reduced configuration spaces may become infeasible under conditional dependencies, risking crashes or unstable optimization.  
- HyperSHAP computational overhead may limit scalability to high‑dimensional spaces; generalizability beyond the evaluated benchmarks remains uncertain.
