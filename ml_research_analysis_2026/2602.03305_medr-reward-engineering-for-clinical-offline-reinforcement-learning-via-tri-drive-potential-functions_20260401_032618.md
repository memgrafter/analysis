# medR: Reward Engineering for Clinical Offline Reinforcement Learning via Tri-Drive Potential Functions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03305  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03305  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Policies trained with medR achieve 60–77% higher WIS scores than clinician baselines across three critical care tasks.

## Executive Summary
Clinical reinforcement learning for treatment optimization is hindered by reward engineering—designing safe, effective reward signals in sparse offline settings. The medR framework automates this process using large language models to generate potential-based reward functions guided by three interpretable drives: Survival (physiological stabilization), Confidence (uncertainty penalization), and Competence (intervention cost). It selects the best reward structure through offline quantitative evaluation before policy deployment. Validation on three critical care tasks shows substantial improvements over standard rewards and clinician baselines, demonstrating that LLM-driven reward design can balance effectiveness, safety, and efficiency while mitigating reward hacking.

## Method Summary
medR constructs an automated pipeline: clinical knowledge and patient trajectories are fed to an LLM to produce candidate potential functions for each drive. These are combined into a total reward function, which trains an offline RL policy. The policy’s performance is evaluated using metrics like WIS and safety indicators. The highest‑scoring reward function is selected, then used to train the final deployed policy. The tri‑drive structure ensures dense, interpretable incentives that align with clinical goals while avoiding pitfalls of sparse or naive rewards.

## Key Results
- medR policies achieve 77.3%, 66.7%, and 60.3% higher WIS scores compared to clinician baselines on three critical care tasks.  
- The Tri‑Drive approach effectively balances treatment efficacy, safety, and resource efficiency.  
- The framework prevents reward hacking behaviors that often arise from simplified reward designs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Potential‑based reward shaping converts sparse clinical outcomes (e.g., survival) into dense, per‑step incentives without altering the optimal policy. The three drives explicitly encode clinical priorities: Survival encourages physiological stability, Confidence discourages actions in uncertain states, and Competence minimizes unnecessary interventions. Offline evaluation filters out reward functions that lead to poor generalization or unsafe patterns, ensuring the selected reward yields robust policies that respect real‑world constraints.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Clinical Dataset + Domain Knowledge -> LLM Reward Synthesizer -> Potential Generator (Survival/Confidence/Competence) -> Reward Composer -> Offline RL Trainer -> Evaluator (WIS, safety) -> Best Reward Selector -> Final Policy Trainer -> Deployed Policy

**Critical Path**  
LLM generation → Reward composition → Offline training → Evaluation → Selection → Final training → Deployment.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *LLM automation* vs. deterministic control: LLMs introduce variability but scale reward design; prompts must be carefully crafted.  
- *Drive simplicity* vs. expressiveness: Three drives cover core clinical goals but may miss nuanced objectives.  
- *Offline evaluation fidelity*: Metrics like WIS estimate performance but cannot guarantee online safety.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Sudden drop in WIS or safety metrics during evaluation → reward misalignment.  
- LLM outputs proposing contraindicated actions → need prompt refinement or expert validation.  
- High variance across reward candidates → indicates unstable LLM generation or insufficiently robust evaluation.

**First Experiments**  
1. Baseline comparison: Train with standard rewards (e.g., MIMIC‑III) vs. medR rewards; compare WIS and adverse event rates.  
2. Ablation study: Remove each drive individually to quantify contribution to performance and safety.  
3. Prompt sensitivity analysis: Vary LLM prompts; assess consistency of selected reward and downstream policy stability.

## Limitations
- LLM prompts, reward templates, and evaluation details are undisclosed, hindering reproducibility.  
- Validation is restricted to three proprietary/simulated critical care tasks; generalizability is unknown.  
- Potential biases in LLM‑generated rewards from training data are not examined.
